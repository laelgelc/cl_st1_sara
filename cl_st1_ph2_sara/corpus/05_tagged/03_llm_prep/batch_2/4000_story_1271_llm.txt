### I

Two men sat on opposite sides of a table which
was too large for the room and too small for
the work upon it.

Rudolph Starkey, proprietor of the Imperial Collegiate Correspondence Bureau, wore
a velvet smoking-jacket, a clean collar, and slippers embroidered with
tarnished gold thread. His chair was tilted backward; one hand
lay in his pocket, while the other held a cigarette
from which he drew at intervals expressive less of enjoyment
than of grievance. Before him stood a cup of cold
coffee and an unopened newspaper.

Topham had no cigarette, and his coat was shiny at
the elbows. He bent over a pile of exercise-books with
a wooden penholder between his fingers. On his right were
letters awaiting tactful answers; on his left, manuscripts requiring correction;
beneath his elbow, half concealed by a grammar, lay the
proof of an advertisement announcing that personal attention was bestowed
by Mr. Rudolph Starkey, M.A., upon every student entrusted to
his distinguished care.

"Have you finished the commercial arithmetic?" asked Starkey.

"No."

"You said you would finish it this morning."

"I said I might."

"Your tone is quite unnecessary."

Topham made no answer. He had discovered, after nine months
of experiment, that silence was the only reply which Starkey
could neither misquote nor charge with insolence.

Starkey picked up the advertisement and read a line of
it with approval.

"'A unique method, combining the ripe scholarship of the university
with the practical requirements of modern life.' That is good.
I wrote that, I think."

"You dictated 'a good method.'"

"The conception was mine."

"The adjectives were mine."

"My dear fellow, a business man cannot stop to divide
hairs over literary trifles. What matters is the directing intelligence."

The directing intelligence yawned and reached for the newspaper.

Almost all the business of the Imperial Collegiate Correspondence Bureau
was performed by Topham. He answered inquiries, prepared lessons, corrected
papers, composed testimonials, devised courses, kept the accounts when there
were accounts to keep, and pacified those pupils whose faith
had not survived six months of Mr. Starkey's personal attention.
Starkey supplied a degree, a name with an academic sound,
and that confidence which comes naturally to a man who
has never considered the possibility of his own inadequacy.

He was not wholly idle, for complete idleness demands a
certain strength of purpose. He began many things, spoiled some,
abandoned most, and complained of the ingratitude of the world
toward strenuous men. He did everything by portions, and resented
the poverty of the total. If a pupil left, it
was because education had fallen into contempt; if a creditor
called, it was because commerce had become rapacious; if Topham
objected to working fourteen hours a day without wages, it
was because educated men no longer possessed loyalty.

Topham had once possessed more than loyalty. He had possessed
prospects. He had been well taught, had read widely, and
had seemed likely to take an honourable place in a
profession which rewards discretion more regularly than intelligence. Then had
come a quarrel, a reckless letter, a public accusation which
he could not prove and would not withdraw. The accusation
may have been true; truth, unsupported by documents, is among
the least negotiable forms of property. His friends regretted him,
his acquaintances avoided him, and the institution which had employed
him discovered a moral objection to continuing to do so.

For a while he had lived by occasional teaching. Then
the teaching became more occasional and the meals less so.
When Starkey found him, he offered friendship, partnership, and an
immediate improvement in circumstances. The friendship proved imperative, the partnership
prospective, and the improvement consisted of a bed in the
attic and as much food as Starkey's landlady could be
persuaded to provide without payment.

A gentleman who has been poor for a week speaks
lightly of the open road; one who has been poor
for a year understands the value of a roof.

Thus Topham remained.

Starkey turned a page of the newspaper.

"I shall require five shillings this afternoon," he said.

"There are three and ninepence in the cash-box."

"There ought to be more."

"There would be, if Mrs. Pelling of Bath had paid
her second instalment."

"You should have written firmly."

"I wrote three times."

"Then you should have written effectively."

Topham put down his pen.

"You may write the fourth letter yourself."

Starkey stared at him. "Really, Topham, your temper is becoming
intolerable. I have treated you as a friend."

"You have treated me as a clerk who cannot give
notice."

"You eat at my expense."

"At your landlady's."

"And you occupy a room for which I am responsible."

"For which you are three weeks in arrears."

Starkey lowered his chair with a thump. Conflict disturbed him
when it ceased to be one-sided.

"I refuse to discuss money in this sordid fashion. You
know that when the bureau develops—as it must develop—you will
benefit. I have always intended that."

"The bureau has developed. It has developed from two pupils
to thirty-seven."

"Exactly."

"And my benefit has developed from supper to supper and
breakfast to no breakfast."

Starkey's face expressed injury. He liked gratitude when he could
obtain it cheaply, and regarded the want of it as
a personal fraud.

"You asked me for sixpence yesterday," he said.

"I did."

"And I lent it."

"Yes."

"That does not suggest oppression."

Topham looked toward the mantelpiece, where the sixpenny piece lay
beside a cracked ink-bottle. He had borrowed it to buy
tobacco, and had not bought the tobacco. The coin had
become too valuable to spend and too humiliating to touch.

"No," he said. "It suggests generosity."

Starkey, missing the irony, was mollified.

"I knew you would see matters reasonably. Now get on
with the Reverend Merton's Latin. His examination is in a
fortnight, and he is most dissatisfied."

"So he ought to be. You promised to teach him
Latin in six weeks."

"An energetic man could learn it in six weeks."

"Then why did you never learn it?"

Starkey rose. "I am going out."

"So I inferred."

"I have an important appointment."

Topham glanced at the slippers.

"I shall change my shoes."

He did so, after a leisurely search for a bootlace.
At the door he paused.

"If anyone calls, I am engaged at the university."

"You have not been inside the university for twelve years."

"That is beside the point. A professional establishment must preserve
appearances."

The door closed behind him. Topham heard his steps descend,
heard a dispute with the landlady in the passage, and
then heard the front door slam.

He sat alone among the papers. The mantel clock ticked
with officious precision. From the street came the cry of
a seller of flowers; from below, the landlady's voice explaining
to someone that promises were not legal tender.

Topham picked up the sixpence, turned it over, and put
it into his waistcoat pocket.

For ten minutes he corrected a clergyman's Latin. Then he
rested his forehead in his hands.

He was thirty-four, hungry, able to translate three dead languages,
and afraid of the rain.

### II

At twelve o'clock the landlady entered without knocking.

"There's a gentleman asking for Mr. Starkey."

"Mr. Starkey is out."

"So I told him. He says he has come from
a distance."

"Then he has had a wasted journey."

"He looks like a man who can pay."

This final circumstance altered the case. Mrs. Dawlish distrusted education,
but she respected solvency wherever she could recognise it.

"Show him up," said Topham.

The visitor who entered was a broad, healthy man of
perhaps fifty-five, dressed with a care which did not conceal
the provincial origin of his clothes. His boots were excellent
but heavy; his gloves new and uncomfortable. A gold watch-chain
crossed a waistcoat of generous dimensions, and a small silver
fruit-knife hung among its seals. His face was ruddy, open,
and troubled. He carried a tightly rolled umbrella and a
parcel wrapped in brown paper.

"Mr. Starkey, sir?"

Topham was about to deny the name. Then he observed
the parcel, the boots, and the deferential anxiety with which
the stranger awaited his answer.

A denial would send the man away. An explanation would
lead to questions. Besides, Topham had written every letter which
bore Starkey's signature for the past eight months.

"I am Mr. Starkey," he said.

The words, once spoken, produced less alarm than he had
expected. Respectability often rests upon no stronger foundation than a
prompt answer.

The visitor brightened.

"Wigmore, sir—Abraham Wigmore. You may recall the name."

"Certainly. Elementary History, English Composition, and Introductory Latin."

"That is me, sir. That is—I am the pupil."

Topham recalled him well. Mr. Wigmore wrote on thick, scented
paper and formed his capital letters with painful magnificence. He
had once translated *agricola* as "a gentleman farmer," and had
apologised in three pages.

"I did not expect a personal visit," said Topham, acquiring
Starkey's most professional manner with surprising ease.

"No, sir. I hope it is not a liberty. I
had business in town, and I thought—being so near, or
near enough—that I might ask your advice private."

"Pray sit down."

Wigmore sat upon the edge of the chair and placed
the parcel on his knees.

"A little something from my own establishment," he said. "Pears.
Not common pears, sir. I picked them out myself."

"That was very kind."

"I am in fruit."

"So I understood."

"Wholesale and retail. Wigmore and Son, though the son is
no longer with us, poor boy. Fever, six years ago."

"I am sorry."

"Thank you, sir. Business is good. I cannot complain of
business. Three shops, two vans, and the warehouse. A man
might do worse."

Topham agreed that a man might. Indeed, looking at the
visitor's substantial coat and thinking of the empty cupboard downstairs,
he felt that a man very often did.

"But there are things," Wigmore continued, "which business does not
give."

"Leisure?"

"Standing, sir."

He uttered the word softly, as if it belonged to
a vocabulary which he had paid to enter.

Topham waited.

"I have two daughters. Good girls, both of them. I
deny nobody that. Emily married Mr. Chalfont, who is in
the law—not a solicitor exactly, but connected with legal affairs.
Rose married a gentleman whose uncle is a canon. They
have moved among different people since."

"Different from fruiterers?"

Wigmore flushed.

"I do not say better. No, sir, I will not
say better. There are honest men behind counters and rogues
in drawing-rooms, as my poor wife used to tell me.
Still, there is a manner. An education. A man feels
it."

"And your daughters have made you feel it?"

The question was direct, but Wigmore did not resent it.
Sincere people are often grateful for the wound which saves
them the labour of making an incision.

"They are affectionate," he said quickly. "They write. Emily sends
a hamper at Christmas, though what a fruiterer wants with
preserved ginger I cannot say. Rose has asked me to
visit, more than once. But when her husband's friends are
there she calls the business 'importing.' Last summer, at the
seaside, I heard her tell a lady that I had
retired."

"Had you?"

"No, sir."

"Did you correct her?"

Wigmore looked down at his umbrella. "It would have embarrassed
her."

There was a short silence. Topham's first amusement had disappeared.
Vanity in a prosperous man may be comic, but a
father's shame before his children has an inconvenient dignity.

"That is why I took up the course," Wigmore said.
"A man cannot alter his beginnings, but he may improve
his ending. I have worked. Perhaps not well."

"You have worked very diligently."

The pupil's face shone at this scrap of praise.

"I knew you would speak honest, sir. Your remarks in
the margins have been strict, but helpful. 'This sentence possesses
enthusiasm rather than grammar'—that was yours."

"It was."

"I copied it into a book."

Topham felt a discomfort which was almost remorse, though he
had not yet profited by the deception.

"What exactly do you wish to ask me?"

Wigmore shifted in his chair.

"It is difficult."

"Most important questions are difficult until they are stated."

"That is like one of your printed lessons."

"It may become one."

The pupil laughed obediently, then grew solemn.

"I want to leave the business."

"Can you afford to?"

"Yes. The warehouse is worth something, and Perkins, my manager,
would take the shops. I have put money by. Not
a fortune, but comfortable. Two thousand a year, perhaps a
shade under after all is settled."

Topham's hand moved involuntarily toward the frayed cuff of his
coat.

"And after leaving business?"

"That is what I require your opinion upon. Whether a
man of my age, if he laboured under competent direction—daily,
I mean, not merely by post—might make himself fit for
a position."

"What position?"

Wigmore's ruddy face became redder.

"It may sound foolish."

"Say it."

"A curate."

Topham stared.

Wigmore hurried on. "Not a rector, sir. I know my
place. I would not expect a living, nor a grand
pulpit, nor anything of that kind. But a curate in
some quiet parish—a useful man. Visiting the sick, reading the
lessons, helping the poor. I have always respected the Church.
My wife's father was a verger."

"And you wish to be ordained?"

"If it could be managed."

"For the sake of your daughters?"

"Not altogether." He hesitated. "Not more than partly. I should
like Rose to say, 'My father is in the Church,'
without lowering her voice. I admit that. But I have
other reasons. A man who has spent thirty-five years persuading
people to buy sound apples knows something of human nature.
There are lonely people in back streets. I have seen
them. I have given fruit away when it would not
keep, but that is not the same as giving comfort."

It was an odd mixture of social ambition, wounded affection,
practical benevolence, and ignorance. Yet mixtures are the usual condition
of human motives; purity is claimed chiefly by those who
have not examined theirs.

Topham leaned back.

"The difficulties would be considerable."

"I expected that."

"Your Latin is weak."

"Very weak."

"Your Greek does not exist."

"No, sir."

"Your history is uncertain, your composition heavy, and your theology
at present confined to good intentions."

Wigmore received each blow with a grave nod.

"But," Topham continued, "you are industrious. You accept correction. You
have sufficient means to obtain books and competent aid. With
intensive private tuition, and if the necessary authorities viewed your
age and circumstances favourably, I see no reason for despair."

"Then you think it possible?"

"I think many men have entered professions with less ability
and worse motives."

Wigmore rose in his excitement, upsetting the umbrella.

"I knew it! I said to myself, 'Go to Mr.
Starkey. He will not flatter you. He will say yes
or no.' And you say yes!"

"I said possible."

"Possible is yes with work attached."

Topham looked at him sharply. The fruiterer was not such
a fool as his grammar suggested.

"Where would you study?" he asked.

"At my house. I have a good room over the
garden, quite separate. Quiet. My housekeeper is an excellent cook.
I thought perhaps there might be a gentleman—a university gentleman—who
would live in and direct me."

Topham heard the mantel clock. He heard also, with remarkable
distinctness, Starkey's voice ordering five shillings, Mrs. Dawlish demanding rent,
and the rain which had not yet begun striking upon
an imaginary pavement under which he might shortly sleep.

"A resident tutor," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"That would be expensive."

"I am prepared for expense."

"What sum had you considered?"

Wigmore named one.

Topham preserved the calm of Mr. Rudolph Starkey, M.A., but
the impoverished Topham within him sprang up and shouted at
his own stupidity. He had spent months believing himself cheap
because a cheap man had told him so.

"The sum," he said slowly, "might secure a junior tutor.
Your case requires experience, patience, and a breadth of instruction
not commonly found in one person."

"I could increase it."

He named a second sum.

Topham folded his hands.

"And an allowance for books?"

"Of course."

"Travelling expenses?"

"Certainly."

"A month's salary in advance is customary."

"If you say so, sir."

The ease of it made Topham suspicious. Years of poverty
teach a man that every open door is likely to
belong to someone else's house.

"You would entrust the selection to me?"

"Entirely."

"I may know a suitable person."

"Could he begin soon?"

"Very soon."

"Within a month?"

"Within a day."

Wigmore blinked.

Topham stood and went to the window. Below, a butcher's
boy splashed through the first drops of rain. Across the
street, a clerk in a neat overcoat entered a dining-room
where the legend "Ordinary, One Shilling" was displayed behind clean
glass.

He had only to remain silent and return to the
Reverend Merton's Latin. He had only to continue being honest
for the benefit of a man who lived by dishonest
promises. There are moments when principle speaks clearly; unfortunately, hunger
speaks at the same time.

He turned.

"The gentleman I have in mind has suffered a reverse,"
he said. "His qualifications are excellent, but certain circumstances have
prejudiced conventional employers against him."

Wigmore nodded sympathetically. "Drink?"

"No."

"Money trouble?"

"No."

"A woman?"

"Nothing so respectable. He told the truth at an inconvenient
moment."

"Then I should like him."

"You have not seen him."

"I take your recommendation."

Topham almost laughed.

"My dear sir, confidence is admirable, but must be tempered
by judgement."

"That is what I pay you for."

For once, commercial rhetoric had answered itself.

### III

The arrangement was completed in less than half an hour.
Mr. Wigmore drew a cheque at the table, blowing upon
the ink before he folded it. He wrote the amount
with the care of a man to whom money was
important but not mysterious.

"The tutor's name?" he asked.

"Topham."

"Christian name?"

"Edmund."

"Mr. Edmund Topham." Wigmore repeated it as though committing a
lesson to memory. "And you answer for him?"

"I answer for his teaching."

"That is enough."

"No," said Topham, more sharply than he intended. "It may
not be. You should understand that he is proud, impatient
under injustice, and not always scrupulous when driven into a
corner."

Wigmore considered this.

"Will he work?"

"Yes."

"Will he teach me honest?"

"Yes."

"Then we will try each other. A man must be
judged where he stands, not where he stumbled. You said
something like that in Lesson Seven."

Topham had, in fact, invented the sentence to fill a
blank space at the bottom of a page.

"When do you return home?" he asked.

"By the four-ten."

"Mr. Topham will accompany you."

"Today?"

"Today."

Wigmore's delight was almost boyish.

"Then we shall begin tomorrow! Greek, perhaps?"

"English syntax."

"Ah. Yes. We must not run before we can walk."

"Nor seek ordination before we can punctuate."

Wigmore laughed again. He tied up the parcel of pears,
then pushed it across the table.

"You keep those, sir. With my thanks."

"I shall."

At the door he paused.

"You will continue to supervise the course, Mr. Starkey?"

"In a sense."

"That comforts me. I have come to depend on your
judgement."

When he had gone downstairs, Topham remained motionless. The cheque
lay before him; beside it stood the pears, golden beneath
their brown wrapping. Both looked improbable.

Then action took possession of him.

He went first to the bank indicated by Wigmore, presented
the cheque, and received notes and gold. The clerk counted
the money without asking whether Topham deserved it. Banks, unlike
moralists, are satisfied by signatures.

He next purchased a serviceable portmanteau, two shirts, a collar,
and a second-hand coat which fitted him sufficiently well to
suggest that it had once belonged to a thinner optimist.
He paid Mrs. Dawlish what Starkey owed her on his
behalf, though she protested that half would do until Saturday.

"Are you coming into money, Mr. Topham?" she asked.

"I am going out of Mr. Starkey."

"That is much the same thing."

He packed his books, his razors, and the few letters
which survived from better years. The whole of his possessions
entered the portmanteau without argument. On the table he placed
a note addressed to Starkey.

He wrote it twice. The first version was eloquent, accusatory,
and therefore dangerous. The second contained four lines.

> Dear Starkey,—I have obtained employment elsewhere and leave today.
I enclose the sixpence which you lent me yesterday, with
sincere gratitude for all the generosity it represents. You will
find the Reverend Merton's exercise corrected to page sixteen. The
remainder may afford exercise to you.
>
> E. Topham.

He laid the sixpence upon the paper.

After reflection, he added another envelope, stamped and addressed to
the Imperial Collegiate Correspondence Bureau. Inside it was a letter
purporting to come from Wigmore.

> Sir,—Owing to a sudden and serious failure of health,
I regret that I am compelled to abandon my studies
indefinitely. I must therefore request that no further lessons be
sent and no further fees charged. With thanks for past
attention, yours faithfully, Abraham Wigmore.

Topham imitated the pupil's laborious capitals, his unnecessary commas, and
the flourish beneath his name. The forgery was excellent. Education
had at last produced a practical result.

He posted the letter at a box near the station.

At three-forty he met Wigmore under the clock. The fruiterer
had bought an evening paper and appeared to have read
none of it.

"You are Mr. Topham?"

"I am."

Wigmore examined him with frank curiosity.

"Mr. Starkey described you."

"Did he?"

"He spoke highly of your ability."

"That was handsome of him."

"He also said you had met with injustice."

"On that subject he is well informed."

Something in the reply puzzled Wigmore, but the platform bell
rang before he could pursue it. They entered a second-class
compartment. Wigmore talked for forty miles, chiefly about his house,
his daughters, the late Mrs. Wigmore, and the proper storage
of citrus fruit. Topham listened with a patience which surprised
him. A full stomach, a railway ticket, and a definite
destination had already improved his character.

At the junction Wigmore produced sandwiches.

"Ham," he said. "I hope you do not object."

"I object to very little that is edible."

"You have been badly placed, perhaps?"

"I have been placed where meals were treated as an
encouragement to idleness."

Wigmore looked at him, then handed over a second sandwich
without comment.

They reached his town after dark. A dog-cart waited outside
the station. Twenty minutes later they entered a square red
house behind high laurels, with lamps burning in three windows
and a smell of roast beef in the hall.

The room over the garden contained a broad desk, two
armchairs, shelves newly cleared for books, and a blackboard which
Wigmore had ordered in anticipation. A fire burned brightly. On
the desk stood fresh ink, a box of pens, and
a silver bell.

"For anything you require," said Wigmore.

Topham touched the bell but did not ring it.

"What I require first is a timetable."

His pupil beamed.

### IV

For three days Topham postponed his confession. Cowardice played some
part in the delay, but practical considerations played more. He
wished to begin work before Wigmore could decide that no
work was possible under a liar.

At eight each morning they read English prose. At ten
they took Latin. After luncheon they walked, and Topham required
his pupil to describe accurately what he had seen, an
exercise which Wigmore considered childish until he discovered that he
could not do it. In the evening they studied history
or Scripture. Wigmore attacked irregular verbs with the resolution of
a man who had once built a business from a
barrow and nine shillings. He forgot slowly, remembered obstinately, and
never pretended to understand.

On the fourth evening, after an hour spent upon the
ablative absolute, Topham closed the book.

"There is something I must tell you."

Wigmore's face fell. "You are leaving?"

"No."

"Then it cannot be the worst."

"It may appear worse."

Topham walked to the fireplace.

"When you called in London, the man to whom you
spoke was not Rudolph Starkey."

Wigmore stared.

"It was you?"

"Yes."

"You said you were him."

"Yes."

"And recommended yoursel."

"Yes."

The west-country turn of the last word, usually softened by
effort, emerged under agitation.

Topham explained. He omitted neither the impersonation nor the forged
letter; necessity may excuse a deception to the deceiver, but
confession which conceals the profitable portions is merely a second
fraud.

Wigmore listened with his hands upon his knees.

When the account was finished, he rose and walked twice
around the room. Then he stopped before the blackboard, upon
which *Caesar, having crossed the river,* remained written in chalk.

"This Mr. Starkey," he said, "did he teach me anything?"

"He did not teach you. I prepared the lessons and
corrected the papers."

"And the remarks in the margins?"

"Mine."

"'Enthusiasm rather than grammar'?"

"Mine."

"And he kept the money?"

"Yes."

"What did he pay you?"

"Food and lodging, when available."

Wigmore's astonishment hardened into anger.

"Then he is a rogue."

"He would call himself a man of ideas."

"I have met the sort in business. They call the
cart an organisation and forget to pay the horse."

Topham smiled despite himself.

"You do not propose to send me away?"

"I have not decided that."

"That is reasonable."

"Do not hurry me, sir. I am angry, and I
do not always think best when angry."

Topham waited.

Wigmore went to the window, though the curtains were drawn.
After several moments he said, "You lied to me."

"I did."

"You took my money under a false name."

"Yes."

"But you are the man who did the work I
paid for before?"

"Yes."

"And you can teach what I need?"

"If anyone can."

"That sounds proud."

"It is meant to."

Wigmore turned. "Then we will settle it this way. You
will remain for one month. At the end of the
month, if I am satisfied, you remain longer. If not,
you go—with the month paid, because I agreed it. But
there will be no more false names and no more
letters written as me."

"Agreed."

"And if Mr. Starkey comes here?"

"He may."

"What shall I say?"

"The truth."

Wigmore's eyes brightened.

"I have often wished to say the truth to a
gentleman with a degree."

Starkey did not come. He wrote six letters, however: two
imperious, two conciliatory, one threatening legal proceedings, and one suggesting
that Topham return as a partner on revised terms. Topham
answered none of them.

The Imperial Collegiate Correspondence Bureau declined rapidly. The Reverend Merton
failed his examination; Mrs. Pelling of Bath requested the return
of her fees; three pupils received identical essays upon the
causes of the French Revolution, though one was studying bookkeeping.
Starkey attributed these reverses to Topham's treachery, never reflecting that
a business destroyed by the departure of an unpaid subordinate
may have mistaken the identity of its proprietor.

At Wigmore's house matters advanced more slowly and more soundly.

The pupil never became a classical scholar. Greek verbs oppressed
him, and the finer distinctions of theology sometimes appeared to
him less reasonable than the grading of oranges. Yet he
learned. His sentences lost weight without losing substance; his reading
acquired intelligence; his charity, which had formerly been impulsive, became
informed. Topham took him into poor streets and showed him
that benevolence requires attention as well as a basket. Wigmore,
in return, taught Topham that regular meals do not necessarily
corrupt the intellect.

The daughters heard of the plan with alarm. Emily wrote
that the Church was not a refuge for persons tired
of commerce. Rose came in person, accompanied by her husband,
and asked whether her father had considered the social difficulties.

"I have," said Wigmore. "They are the reason I began,
but not the reason I shall finish."

Topham, overhearing from the adjoining room, allowed himself a small
smile.

Whether Wigmore would ultimately be ordained remained uncertain. There were
examinations, authorities, and prejudices still to be encountered; institutions which
willingly accept a young fool often scrutinise an elderly man
of sense. But a sympathetic clergyman was found, a course
was arranged, and the former fruiterer persevered with a steadiness
which made predictions unsafe.

Topham also prospered. He became better dressed, less bitter, and,
when not reminded of Starkey, almost honest in his estimate
of mankind. He knew that his escape had begun in
fraud, and at times the knowledge troubled him. At such
moments he reflected that he had rescued himself from servitude,
delivered Wigmore from an incompetent impostor, punished a scoundrel, and
placed a sincere man upon the road to useful work.

He further reflected that, had he told the truth at
once, none of these excellent consequences would have occurred.

Such arguments gave him considerable peace.

### The Wild Flowers

At half-past one, when the dining-room of the Anchor and
Crown had reached that stage of activity in which the
waitresses had ceased to run and had begun to collide,
Mr. Whiston entered with his daughter.

He paused inside the door, partly that his short sight
might accommodate itself to the comparative dusk, and partly to
survey the company. The latter operation was neither hasty nor
agreeable. He discovered a commercial traveller eating cold beef with
a knife; two young women in red blouses, whose laughter
had already crossed the border between vivacity and freedom; an
elderly couple too respectable to be interesting; and, at a
table beside the window, a broad-shouldered young man with red
hair, a sunburnt forehead, and the largest mug of ale
that Mr. Whiston had ever seen outside a picture of
rustic depravity.

"We might," he said, "have done better."

Rose, who had followed him with her head a little
bent, made no reply. The room was warm after the
wind upon the cliffs, and she wished to sit down.
She carried a small bunch of wild flowers, gathered during
their morning's walk: scabious, sea-pink, and two yellow blossoms whose
name neither she nor her father knew. Their gaiety looked
almost impertinent against her grey dress.

A waitress approached.

"Table for two, sir?"

"If you please. Somewhere quiet."

There was nowhere quiet. They were placed at the table
adjoining the red-haired young man, who had now added a
plate of steak and onions to his ale, and who
was speaking in a loud, cheerful voice to the waiter.

"No, no, my lad; mustard, not melancholy. I've enough melancholy
in my room. The paper's coming off the wall."

The waiter grinned. Mr. Whiston did not.

Rose sat with her back partly turned to the offender,
but she could not help hearing him. He had walked
fifteen miles before luncheon; he had found a spring above
Denby Cove; he had lost his tobacco-pouch somewhere near the
coastguard station; and he considered the local beer "a good
honest drink, though a thought too anxious to please." None
of these remarks was addressed to the Whistons. Yet Mr.
Whiston received each of them as a personal affront.

The young man, having been supplied with mustard, glanced towards
Rose's flowers.

"You've been up along the west cliff, miss?" he said.

Rose looked up, startled.

"We have," replied Mr. Whiston.

"Thought so. That yellow fellow only grows beyond the old
lime-kiln. Fine view there."

"It is tolerable."

"Wind enough to take the buttons off your coat, though."

Mr. Whiston's hand went, by instinct, to the middle button
of his frock-coat.

The young man smiled, but not, Rose thought, at her
father. His face was plain, freckled, and exceedingly alive. There
was dust upon his sleeve and a small cut across
one knuckle. He looked like a person who accepted the
existence of weather, appetite, hills, buttons, and other vulgar necessities
with a gratitude that required expression.

"Excuse me," he added. "Those flowers took me back. I
was up there yesterday."

Rose murmured something. Mr. Whiston examined the menu as though
it contained a difficult moral choice.

They ordered sole, potatoes, and lemonade. The young man ordered
cheese.

He ate heartily, humming under his breath between mouthfuls. Presently
he took out a pipe, discovered that it was empty,
slapped each of his pockets in turn, and laughed.

"There's the punishment of carelessness! Half an ounce of the
best bird's-eye scattered over His Majesty's coast."

Smoking was not permitted in the dining-room, nor did he
attempt it; but the mere mention of bird's-eye completed Mr.
Whiston's estimate.

When they had gone upstairs to prepare for the afternoon,
he said, while passing a pocket-comb through the remnants of
hair above his ears:

"That is the sort of person one is compelled to
meet at these places."

Rose was arranging the flowers in a tumbler.

"He was civil."

"Civility, my dear, is not always good breeding."

"No."

"And good spirits are very easily mistaken for good character."

"Yes, Father."

Mr. Whiston regarded her reflection in the glass. She had
always been amenable to principles, and principles, unlike persons, gave
him no trouble. He therefore proceeded with the calmness of
one who strengthens a structure not because it is weak,
but because strength is meritorious.

"A stranger who talks across public tables about beer and
tobacco is, to say the least, lacking in reserve. One
has to lay down rules. Particularly for a woman."

Rose put one yellow blossom a little higher.

"Was it wrong of him to speak about the flowers?"

"Wrong is perhaps too strong a word. Unsuitable."

"He knew where they grew."

"That does not alter the social aspect of the matter."

"No," said Rose.

She was twenty-nine, and had learnt that a word might
be unreasonable without being answerable.

Mr. Whiston was not a tyrant. Tyrants possess imagination, if
only in the service of cruelty; he possessed affection, solicitude,
and a series of conclusions. His wife had died when
Rose was seven, leaving him with a child, a modest
income, and an enlarged sense of duty. He had discharged
that duty with undeviating conscientiousness. Rose had attended a small
private school until sixteen, had taken music lessons until the
teacher married, and had since remained at home, where she
read to her father in the evenings, supervised the maid,
and accompanied him to church, to the lending library, and
once a year to the sea.

The house at Norwood was narrow, clean, and respectable. Its
curtains were renewed before they became shabby; its front step
was whitening-stoned every Friday; and its drawing-room, by reason of
having been preserved for callers, gradually acquired the air of
a chamber in which callers had been extinct for centuries.
Mr. Whiston's acquaintances were chiefly elderly men. Rose's girlhood companions
had married, moved away, or committed themselves to occupations of
which he could not wholly approve. One taught singing; another
had become a hospital nurse and used, he believed, expressions
of professional indelicacy. A third had married a manufacturer of
enamelled baths.

"You may know people," Mr. Whiston would say, "without being
obliged to adopt their circle."

The practical result was that Rose knew scarcely anybody.

Yet loneliness, like damp, may spread for years before it
stains the wallpaper. She had not called herself lonely. Her
father loved her; the servant respected her; shopkeepers knew her
preferences; the vicar bowed in the street. She performed many
little offices, was necessary in many little ways, and lay
awake only at intervals with a sense that life was
proceeding in some adjoining room to which she had misplaced
the key.

The annual holiday was her chief adventure. Mr. Whiston believed
in sea air as firmly as he distrusted those who
came to breathe it. They walked at fixed hours, chose
the less frequented paths, and avoided amusements advertised in coloured
bills. That morning, however, Rose had persuaded him to go
beyond the west cliff. He had tired sooner than usual
and sat upon a stone, while she wandered among the
coarse grasses and picked flowers in the wind.

For twenty minutes she had been alone under an immense
sky.

Now, in the bedroom of the Anchor and Crown, the
unknown young man's remark seemed to belong to that sky.
Fine view there. Wind enough to take the buttons off
your coat. Coarse? Perhaps. Yet he had seen the flowers,
and no one else had seen them—not even her father,
who had merely warned her that their stalks would drip
upon her glove.

They spent the afternoon upon the pier. Mr. Whiston read
the newspaper. Rose watched the gulls and wondered whether the
red-haired man had found his tobacco.

At five they returned to the inn for tea.

As they reached the door, the stranger hurried after them,
carrying her wild flowers.

"I beg your pardon, miss. You left these in the
dining-room."

Rose stopped.

The blossoms had been removed from the tumbler and wrapped
in a piece of damp newspaper. This precaution, insignificant to
any person of sense, moved her absurdly.

"Oh! Thank you. I thought—I had forgotten—"

"So had everybody else, nearly. They were clearing the table.
It seemed a pity."

He offered the flowers without touching her glove. His manner,
though brisk, was perfectly respectful.

"You are very kind," she said.

Mr. Whiston inclined his head.

"We are obliged to you."

"Not at all, sir."

The young man raised his hat and went away towards
the sea. He did not seek to prolong the encounter;
indeed, before they had entered the inn, he had begun
to whistle.

Rose looked down at the flowers. The newspaper was part
of a financial page, and the ink had smudged her
glove. She did not mind.

"Quite civilly done," her father conceded.

"Yes."

"But such little incidents do not justify acquaintanceship. One may
acknowledge an attention without putting oneself under an obligation."

"I don't think he meant to put us under one."

Mr. Whiston's lips compressed themselves. Rose had not contradicted him
precisely; nevertheless, a small obstruction had appeared in the smooth
channel of paternal instruction.

"My dear, I have lived longer in the world than
you."

"I know, Father."

It was not an argument, but he accepted it as
submission.

Their holiday ended two days later. The flowers remained fresh
enough to travel, and Rose carried them to the station
in a paper parcel. Mr. Whiston disliked railway refreshment rooms
and had arranged that they should eat sandwiches in the
train. He also disliked changing carriages, draughts, corner seats facing
the engine, corner seats with their backs to the engine,
and fellow passengers who had the appearance of changing their
minds. These preferences converted departure into a military operation.

They arrived thirty-five minutes early.

The station was crowded. Porters shouted, children dragged wooden spades
along the platform, and a dog in a hamper expressed
violent opinions upon imprisonment. Rose stood beside the luggage while
her father questioned three officials about a connection already printed
in his timetable.

Then, through the steam, she saw the red-haired young man.

He came along the platform with a brown bag in
one hand and a rolled mackintosh under his arm. His
hat was pushed back, and he appeared both hurried and
pleased with the world. Rose's first impulse was to turn
away; her second, to remain exactly as she was. Between
these impulses lay the whole discipline of her life.

He saw her.

For a moment his face showed open pleasure. Then he
noticed Mr. Whiston returning and reduced the pleasure to a
bow.

"Good afternoon, sir. Good afternoon, miss."

"Good afternoon," said Rose.

Mr. Whiston bowed more remotely. "Good afternoon."

"You're travelling up?"

"We are going to London."

"So am I. At least, if this train ever makes
up its mind to come in."

"It is advertised for three-sixteen," said Mr. Whiston.

"Yes, sir. But it doesn't look a punctual sort of
train."

A whistle sounded. Mr. Whiston frowned, not because the stranger
was wrong, but because the train had vindicated him in
a vulgar manner.

It entered with smoke, clatter, and the indiscriminate authority of
machinery. Doors opened. Passengers descended into passengers who intended to
ascend. Mr. Whiston secured a first-class compartment, installed Rose in
one corner, distributed rugs, papers, umbrellas and luncheon-basket according to
a plan invisible but sacred, and then stood guard at
the door.

The stranger passed.

He was evidently travelling third class. Rose felt a little
contraction of disappointment, followed by shame at the disappointment. What
had she expected? That her father would invite him to
sit opposite them? That red hair, bird's-eye tobacco, and a
large mug of ale should be transmuted by railway steam
into eligibility?

A porter hurried up.

"Third's full forward, sir. You'll have to get in here
and pay the difference."

The young man laughed. "My extravagance is settled for me."

He entered their compartment.

Mr. Whiston sat more upright. Opposite him were an old
lady with a basket and a curate reading a theological
review; beside Rose, the newcomer. There was no room elsewhere.

For the first half-hour scarcely a word was spoken. Mr.
Whiston read his newspaper with a severity ordinarily reserved for
refuting it. The curate turned pages. The old lady slept.
Rose watched fields slide past the window, each hedge appearing
to hurry backward from some place towards which she had
not the courage to look.

The young man was very near. His coat-sleeve sometimes touched
hers with the movement of the train. Whenever this happened
he drew it away at once. He neither hummed nor
spoke of beer. He looked out of the window, smiling
occasionally at nothing visible to Rose; once he picked up
the old lady's ball of wool before it had rolled
beneath the seat.

At the first stop the curate descended. At the second,
the old lady woke, assembled her basket and wool, and
left them. The compartment contained only the Whistons and the
stranger.

Mr. Whiston lowered his paper.

"I believe this train is losing time."

"Seven minutes at least," said the young man, consulting his
watch.

"You know the line?"

"I travel a good bit, sir."

"Indeed."

"For Empson and Blythe. Agricultural fittings. I cover Kent, Sussex,
and part of Hampshire."

A clerk—or something worse, a travelling clerk. Yet he did
not seem abashed by the revelation.

"Commercial employment," said Mr. Whiston.

"That's it. Not a prince in disguise, I'm afraid."

Rose's lips moved.

The young man saw, and his eyes brightened. Mr. Whiston
also saw, and folded his newspaper.

"There is nothing discreditable in honest trade," he observed.

"I hope not, sir. It keeps me hungry."

There it was again: appetite, cheerfully admitted. Mr. Whiston looked
through the window.

A little later the train stopped outside a station. It
remained among sidings, where empty trucks stood beneath a pale
afternoon sky. The young man leant forward.

"Would you like the window up, miss? There's smoke coming
in."

"Thank you."

He raised it. Rose noticed that the cut upon his
knuckle had opened slightly. She wanted to ask whether he
had found his tobacco-pouch, and despised herself for the triviality;
yet human intimacy is perhaps more often desired through trivial
questions than through profound ones.

Mr. Whiston opened the luncheon-basket. Eating in the presence of
a stranger displeased him, but not eating would have meant
waste. He offered Rose a sandwich.

The young man turned resolutely to the window.

Rose knew that he must be hungry.

"Would you—" she began.

Her father glanced at her.

"Would you like the newspaper?" she finished.

"Very much, thank you."

He accepted it gravely and read an article on municipal
drainage with such attention that she knew he had understood
her kindness and her fear.

At Ashford there was a delay of twelve minutes. Mr.
Whiston, who could endure delay but not uncertainty, went in
search of the guard.

"Remain here, my dear. I shall not be long."

He left the carriage.

The door closed.

Rose and the young man did not look at one
another. Steam drifted outside the window; feet passed upon the
platform; a boy cried newspapers. They had perhaps ten minutes,
perhaps one. The extraordinary opportunity was as terrifying as a
precipice and as commonplace as a railway seat.

The young man spoke first.

"My name is Charles Burroughs."

Rose's heart struck once, heavily.

"I oughtn't to tell you mine."

"No."

"It would be wrong."

"I suppose so."

She turned then. His face was serious, though not solemn.
He seemed unwilling to pretend that rules did not exist,
but equally unwilling to suppose that their existence made them
wise.

"You were very kind about the flowers," she said.

"I wanted to speak to you before that."

"Why?"

He reddened beneath his freckles.

"I don't know. Because you looked as if no one
did."

This was too direct. It was also so exactly true
that Rose's eyes filled with tears.

He continued quickly: "I don't mean you looked neglected. I
don't mean anything disrespectful. I only thought—well, there you were."

There she was. In four words he had described twenty-two
years.

"My name is Rose Whiston," she said. "We live at
seventeen Belgrave Road, Norwood."

He repeated neither name nor address, but she saw him
receive them.

"I'm at thirty-four Landor Street, Brixton."

"Are you?"

"Yes. With my mother and sister. My father's dead."

There was not time to ask how long he had
been dead, or whether the sister was older, or if
Landor Street had trees. These facts pressed upon Rose with
the fascination of contraband.

"I shouldn't have told you," she whispered.

"I won't write if you don't want me to."

She did not answer.

"Miss Whiston—may I?"

The platform bell rang.

"My father would be very angry."

"I don't want to make trouble for you."

"He believes—we do not know you."

"That's true."

"He thinks you are—"

Charles waited.

Rose could not repeat the words coarse and vulgar. They
had become indecent.

"He thinks strangers should be kept at a distance."

"Most strangers, perhaps."

Footsteps approached.

Charles sat back. "I'll do nothing underhand," he said.

Mr. Whiston opened the door.

"The guard assures me," he announced, "that we shall leave
almost immediately."

The train moved before he had settled himself. He accepted
this as confirmation of his influence.

During the remaining journey Rose scarcely spoke. A new, hot
consciousness possessed her. She had given her name and address
to a man whom she had seen three times. She
had done so without permission, without precedent, and almost without
reflection. The fact shone within her like a lamp in
a forbidden room.

At Victoria, Charles assisted with the luggage. Mr. Whiston could
not prevent him from carrying the larger bag to the
cab, though he attempted to reduce the courtesy to an
impersonal transaction by offering sixpence.

Charles looked at the coin, then at Rose.

"No, thank you, sir."

"A porter would have been paid."

"Then it's fortunate I'm not one."

The answer might have been insolent. His smile made it
merely firm.

"Good evening, Miss Whiston."

"Good evening."

He turned away.

In the cab, Rose felt for one intoxicating minute that
London had altered. Every street led somewhere; every lit window
contained a possible life. Omnibuses thundered past like declarations of
liberty. Men and women walked beneath the lamps, choosing their
directions without consultation. She had acted. She, Rose Whiston, had
given an address.

Then her father tucked the rug around her knees.

"You are cold, my dear."

His familiar solicitude pierced her excitement. She had deceived him.
The lamp went out.

That night the flowers, placed in water beside her bed,
seemed no longer innocent. They were evidence. Their bent stalks
accused her; their faint salt smell recalled the railway carriage;
the yellow petals, beginning to curl, had the dreadful brightness
of pleasures obtained improperly.

Suppose Charles wrote? The post came at eight. Her father
usually opened the box; occasionally the maid did. A letter
in a masculine hand would be remarked. Suppose he did
not write? That possibility, at once proper and unbearable, made
her turn upon the pillow.

She had known him for perhaps two hours in all.
He sold agricultural fittings. He drank ale. He smoked bird's-eye.
He used expressions that Mr. Whiston would not have permitted
at table. What did she know of his principles? His
income? His associates? Whether he attended church? Whether he had
debts? A woman who gave her address under such circumstances
deserved—

What did she deserve?

Here upbringing supplied many shadows but no precise punishment. Loss
of self-respect was the phrase most often employed. Yet self-respect,
when Rose examined it, seemed to consist chiefly in accepting
her father's estimate of other people. Was there no self-respect
in judging for herself?

Charles had returned the flowers. He had protected her from
smoke, read municipal drainage rather than embarrass her, and refused
a coin he might honourably have accepted. Coarse? Gross? No.
Not gross. Whatever else he was, he was not that.

But she had deceived her father.

At three in the morning she decided she would confess
everything at breakfast. At eight, Mr. Whiston complained that the
egg was underdone, and confession seemed theatrical. At luncheon she
determined to speak after the meal; he remarked that young
women who acted without parental guidance frequently repented at leisure,
having read an instance in the newspaper. Rose turned cold
and remained silent.

No letter came.

On the second day, none came. On the third, Rose
heard the postman's knock while dressing and sat motionless until
the maid carried the letters into the breakfast-room. There were
two circulars and an account.

Relief and disappointment had become so intermingled that she could
not distinguish virtue from pain.

A week passed. The flowers died. Rose kept them in
a drawer.

Charles Burroughs had, in fact, written on the day after
his return; but not to Rose.

His letter, addressed to Mr. Algernon Whiston, arrived on Thursday
morning. Mr. Whiston read it once beside the breakfast table,
again at the window, and a third time after polishing
his spectacles. Rose, pouring tea, knew only that something had
disturbed him. His thin beard moved before he spoke.

"Extraordinary."

She set down the pot.

"Is anything wrong?"

"Wrong? That depends upon the standard one applies. An incident
may be in itself merely irregular, yet indicate tendencies that
are—" He paused. His pregnant silence, after a decent interval,
delivered an admonitory axiom. "One cannot be too cautious in
permitting strangers to make advances."

Rose's hands grew cold.

Mr. Whiston read aloud:

"'Dear Sir,—You may possibly remember me as a fellow-passenger from
Seaborough on Monday last. I hope you will pardon my
writing. I should esteem it a favour if you would
allow me to call upon you, as I very much
desire to make your acquaintance, and, with your permission, that
of Miss Whiston. I am employed by Messrs. Empson and
Blythe, of Coleman Street, and can give references as to
my character and position. I wish to act openly and
respectfully, and shall not communicate with Miss Whiston unless I
have your consent.'"

Rose could hear the clock.

Her father continued:

"'As to your address, I obtained it by following your
cab from Victoria. I am aware that this may appear
an unwarrantable liberty, and can only say that my intentions
are honourable. Yours faithfully, Charles Henry Burroughs.'"

Mr. Whiston laid the letter upon the table.

"There, my dear."

Rose did not move.

"You perceive the danger."

"He followed us?"

"So he asserts."

But he had not. He had lied.

The lie struck her first as an injury. Charles had
promised to do nothing underhand, and almost immediately had invented
a falsehood. Then, beneath her confusion, she saw the shape
of it. He had not lied to obtain access; he
had lied to conceal her part in granting it. He
had made himself appear intrusive in order that she might
appear innocent.

Her father tapped the letter.

"Following a private cab through London! One hesitates to employ
a strong expression, but the proceeding is scarcely that of
a gentleman."

"Perhaps he—"

"Well?"

"Perhaps he wished to know us."

"That is evident. Desire, however, does not constitute a right."

"No."

"Nor does an offer of references transform presumption into propriety."

Mr. Whiston folded the letter exactly. He was shocked, yet
not wholly displeased to find reality corroborating a principle. The
red-haired stranger had spoken of beer and tobacco; now he
pursued cabs. Cause and consequence had seldom behaved so neatly.

"I shall not answer."

Rose looked up.

"He wrote respectfully."

"Respectful language may be used in a disrespectful act."

"Would it be respectful not to answer him?"

This was the second obstruction. Mr. Whiston regarded his daughter
with surprise.

"I might acknowledge the letter, merely to terminate the matter."

"Then he would know."

"Know what?"

"That you had received it."

Mr. Whiston finger-combed his beard.

"I shall consider."

He considered for four days. Charles wrote again.

The second letter was shorter. He apologised for troubling Mr.
Whiston, repeated that he sought no clandestine correspondence, and begged
only for the opportunity to answer any inquiries. He enclosed
the address of his employer, the name of his clergyman,
and that of a married cousin in Camberwell.

"This persistence," said Mr. Whiston, "is most unfortunate."

Rose, who was hemming a cloth, pushed the needle through
her finger.

He read the letter aloud. She listened to Charles's phrases—stiff
phrases, unlike his speech, evidently composed with labour. She pictured
him at a small table in Landor Street, rejecting words,
beginning again, perhaps asking his mother whether "esteem it a
favour" was sufficiently proper. The image was so tender that
she could not trust herself to speak.

Mr. Whiston wrote a refusal.

Charles replied once more. He would not presume again, he
said, but wished Mr. Whiston to understand that his admiration
for Miss Whiston was sincere, though he had no claim
upon her attention. If his conduct in following them had
caused offence, he regretted it. He had preferred to risk
appearing foolish rather than approach Miss Whiston secretly.

That sentence broke the structure of Rose's silence.

Her father read it after dinner, standing beneath the gas-bracket.
He had reached "approach Miss Whiston secretly" when she rose.

"Father."

"One moment, my dear."

"No. I must tell you."

Her voice was not loud, but something in its simplicity
stopped him.

She clasped her hands, then unclasped them. The room, with
its dark furniture and framed engravings, had never looked so
fixed. Even the bronze shepherdess upon the mantelpiece appeared to
know that Rose was about to move while everything else
remained still.

"He did not follow us."

Mr. Whiston stared.

"Not follow us?"

"No."

"But he states—repeatedly—that he did."

"I gave him the address."

The gas made a small sound.

"You?"

"Yes."

"You gave this person our address?"

"Yes, Father."

"When?"

"In the train. At Ashford. When you went to speak
to the guard."

Mr. Whiston sat down. He did not do so weakly;
he did so with the care of a man whose
entire social philosophy has become unexpectedly heavy.

"You gave a complete stranger—"

"I told him my name and where we lived."

"But why?"

The question, stripped of maxims, was almost childlike.

Rose's answer came slowly.

"Because I wanted him to know."

"Wanted him—Rose, you had exchanged perhaps six sentences with the
man."

"He returned my flowers."

Mr. Whiston blinked.

"Your flowers?"

"At the inn. The flowers I gathered. He brought them
after I forgot them in the dining-room."

"That was he?"

"Yes."

"You did not tell me."

"You were there."

Mr. Whiston searched his memory. The courteous young man at
the door, hat in hand, flowers wrapped against injury; the
noisy fellow at luncheon; the commercial clerk in the railway
carriage; the pursuer of cabs—these had been separate offenders, conveniently
labelled. They now converged into one human being, and the
convergence disturbed him.

"You knew, all this time, that his statement was false?"

"Yes."

"And permitted me to correspond under a misapprehension?"

Rose bent her head. "I was ashamed."

"As indeed—" he began; but affection frustrated doctrine. Her face
was very pale. "My dear, why did you not confide
in me?"

"I was afraid."

"Of me?"

"Of what you would think."

"What else could I think? A young woman does not
give her address to an unknown man."

"I did."

The plainness of the words was more revolutionary than defiance.
Mr. Whiston opened his mouth, closed it, and placed Charles's
letter upon the table.

Rose continued, trembling now that she had begun:

"I know it was against everything you have taught me.
I know I had no right, if you say I
had none. But I believed he was good. I still
believe it. He did not write to me. He wrote
to you. And he said he followed us so that
you would blame him instead of me."

Mr. Whiston looked at the letter.

"That does not excuse an untruth."

"No."

"Truth is not a matter of convenience."

"No."

"A person of principle would have stated the facts."

"Then you would have blamed me."

"I should certainly have—" He stopped.

Charles had foreseen precisely this certainty. The lie was indefensible;
its delicacy was equally undeniable. Mr. Whiston disliked moral complications.
A falsehood should be selfish, a courtesy should be truthful,
and a clerk who discussed tobacco should not display finer
consideration than persons of breeding. Classification was the first defence
of respectability, and Charles Burroughs had an alarming way of
escaping his compartment.

Mr. Whiston paced to the window and back.

"Are you attached to this man?"

Rose's colour rose.

"I hardly know him."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer I have."

"Do you wish to know him?"

"Yes."

The word was quiet.

Mr. Whiston's eyes rested upon her. He saw, perhaps for
the first time, not the child left to his guardianship,
nor the dutiful companion arranged by Providence for his widowerhood,
but a woman almost thirty, frightened and resolute, whose desires
had grown in the house without asking permission. That they
had grown at all seemed vaguely ungrateful; that he had
never observed them seemed vaguely shameful.

"Have you communicated with him since?"

"No."

"Have you attempted to?"

"No."

"Has he communicated with you?"

"No."

"You give me your word?"

Rose's head came up.

"Yes."

He was immediately sorry for the question.

"My dear, I do not doubt your word. I am
endeavouring to understand an extremely painful situation."

"It need not be painful."

"To you, perhaps."

"To you either, if you would see him."

Mr. Whiston assumed his most formal expression.

"I can make no promise."

"I am not asking for one."

"Nor can emotional considerations be permitted to override prudence."

"No."

"There are differences of upbringing, habits, associations—possibly of position."

"Yes."

"He may be a perfectly honest young man and yet
wholly unsuitable."

"Yes, Father."

Her submission no longer reassured him. It had ceased to
be surrender and become patience.

For the next fortnight Mr. Whiston investigated Charles Burroughs.

He began reluctantly, telling himself that inquiry was necessary only
to close the affair upon an informed basis. Messrs. Empson
and Blythe replied that Mr. Burroughs had been in their
employment for six years, was energetic, trustworthy, and likely to
advance. The clergyman described him as "a young man of
sound heart, though not conspicuously ecclesiastical in manner." The cousin
in Camberwell wrote three crowded pages, of which Mr. Whiston
disapproved, but which established that Charles supported his widowed mother,
had paid his father's remaining debts, belonged to a cricket
club, and once carried an injured errand-boy two miles in
the rain.

These particulars were annoyingly favourable.

Mr. Whiston called at Coleman Street. He did not tell
Rose beforehand.

He found Charles behind a high desk in a warehouse
where iron implements of obscure agricultural purpose stood in ordered
ranks. Deprived of seaside dust and railway accident, he appeared
younger; in a dark suit, with his red hair brushed
flat by unsuccessful discipline, he was almost presentable.

Charles came forward at once.

"Mr. Whiston, sir?"

"I have called to ask certain questions."

"Of course. Will you come in here?"

The private room belonged to a senior clerk, who surrendered
it upon Charles's request. Mr. Whiston noted the courtesy with
which this was done. He also noted a faint smell
of tobacco upon Charles's coat, but by then tobacco had
lost some of its evidentiary value.

"You understand," Mr. Whiston began, "that my daughter has informed
me of the actual circumstances under which you obtained our
address."

Charles's freckles darkened.

"I hoped she wouldn't have to."

"You admit that your statement was untrue?"

"Yes, sir."

"Deliberately untrue?"

"Yes."

"You place me in some difficulty."

"I know."

"Why did you not write the facts?"

"Because they were her facts as well as mine."

Mr. Whiston frowned.

"I don't wish to be clever, sir," Charles added. "She
trusted me with the address. I wasn't going to repay
her by telling you she had done something you might
be angry about."

"Yet your present application depends upon my considering you trustworthy."

"That's awkward, I grant you."

"Awkward?"

"Very. But if I had it to do again, I
believe I'd do the same."

This was not the repentance Mr. Whiston desired.

"You appear to regard deception lightly."

"No, sir. I've been sick enough over those letters. But
there are lies to save oneself and lies to save
somebody else a thrashing. They may both be lies, but
I don't feel the same about them."

"My daughter is not subject to thrashings."

"No. I beg your pardon. It was a figure of
speech."

"A regrettable one."

"Yes, sir."

Mr. Whiston was silent.

Charles folded and unfolded a piece of blotting-paper.

"I know I spoke too freely at Seaborough," he said.
"I was on holiday and full of wind and food.
I daresay you thought me an awful bounder."

"The expression had not occurred to me."

"But the thought had."

Mr. Whiston ignored this.

"I am bound to consider my daughter's happiness."

"So am I."

"You are not bound at all, as yet."

"No, sir. But I should like to be."

There was no answering such a statement without entering territory
Mr. Whiston had come expressly to survey from the frontier.

He asked about salary, prospects, family, residence, church attendance and
associates. Charles answered directly. His salary was adequate but not
large; his prospects were good; his mother let two rooms;
his sister was training to teach; he attended church irregularly
but not, he hoped, hypocritically; his associates included clerks, salesmen,
a schoolmaster, two warehousemen, and a dentist's assistant who played
the cornet.

"The cornet?" said Mr. Whiston.

"Only on Saturdays."

At the end of forty minutes Mr. Whiston rose.

"I shall communicate with you."

"Thank you for coming, sir."

"This must not be interpreted as encouragement."

"I'll interpret it as your having come."

Outside, Mr. Whiston walked past the same shop three times
before discovering that he was going in the wrong direction.

At home Rose asked no questions.

This reticence first gratified and then disconcerted him. He had
expected anxiety, persuasion, perhaps tears. He was prepared to resist
them with tenderness. Instead, Rose went about her duties; she
ordered groceries, darned his socks, read aloud the parliamentary intelligence,
and accompanied him to church. Yet something had altered. Her
obedience, once soft from habit, had become deliberate. She deferred
to him in actions while reserving judgment. Her silence contained
a liberty he could neither rebuke nor ignore.

One evening he remarked:

"I saw Mr. Burroughs last Tuesday."

Rose's needle stopped, then continued.

"Did you?"

"He answered my inquiries frankly."

"I knew he would."

"You could not know."

"No. But I believed it."

Mr. Whiston waited for more. Nothing came.

"He has certain good qualities," he conceded.

Rose looked at him, and the happiness in her face
was so immediate that he felt both generous and alarmed.

"Good qualities do not necessarily ensure compatibility."

"No, Father."

"Nor would a single interview justify conclusions."

"No."

"He is somewhat impulsive."

"Yes."

"And his manner is occasionally lacking in restraint."

Rose smiled. "Yes."

"You perceive it yourself?"

"I have always perceived it."

This was another complication. He had imagined her blinded by
romance; instead she saw Charles's faults and liked him through
them. It occurred to Mr. Whiston that affection might not
be an error of classification, but a form of knowledge.

The matter remained unsettled for another week.

During that week Mr. Whiston observed his daughter. He noticed
how rarely she laughed, though she had laughed in the
railway carriage. He noticed that no letters came for her,
that no friend called, and that her days, so serviceable
to him, contained almost nothing that was hers. The house
had long seemed to him a fortress of decorum; now,
at moments, it appeared merely small.

He did not surrender his principles. He revised their application.

On Saturday morning he entered the drawing-room, opened the writing-desk,
and selected a sheet of cream paper. He rejected one
nib as too broad and another as frivolously fine. The
third satisfied him.

He wrote:

"Dear Mr. Burroughs,—With reference to our recent conversation, I have
decided that no harm can arise from your taking tea
with my daughter and myself on Thursday next, at four
o'clock, when we shall be pleased to see you. I
need hardly add that this invitation must not be understood
as committing either my daughter or myself to any future
course. Yours faithfully, Algernon Whiston."

He read the note twice. The phrase "no harm can
arise" seemed optimistic; "pleased to see you" seemed excessive. He
altered neither. Having addressed and sealed it, he carried it
into the morning-room.

Rose was arranging fresh flowers in a vase.

They were not wild flowers. They had come from the
florist and possessed, in Mr. Whiston's opinion, the advantages of
cultivation, regularity, and freedom from insects. Nevertheless, they were yellow.

"I have written to Mr. Burroughs," he said.

Rose turned.

"I have invited him to tea on Thursday."

For a moment she did not speak. Then she came
to him and kissed his cheek.

"My dear," he murmured, embarrassed by gratitude where he had
expected approval, "it is merely an opportunity for further acquaintance."

"Yes, Father."

"No conclusions should be drawn."

"No."

"And I trust he will understand that punctuality is important.
Four o'clock means four, not ten minutes past."

"I am sure he will understand."

Mr. Whiston gave her the envelope to post.

She carried it herself to the pillar-box at the end
of Belgrave Road. The morning was bright, and a breeze
moved above the houses, stirring the thin suburban trees. Rose
walked slowly, though not from hesitation. At the box she
held the letter for a second before letting it fall.

The small sound it made upon dropping was scarcely audible
amid the carts and cries of the street.

Yet it bore results.

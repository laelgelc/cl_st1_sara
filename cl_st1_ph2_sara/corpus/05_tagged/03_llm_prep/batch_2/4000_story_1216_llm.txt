# The Thanksgiving Wager

It is likely that girls will care most for this
story because there is a doll in it, and boys
because there are bullets; but since there are also a
turkey, a wager, and a confession, perhaps no sensible child
need be left out. The events occurred in New England,
when the smoke of the Revolutionary War had scarcely blown
away from the fields, and when a handful of old
bullets might lie in a cupboard beside the best pewter
porringer.

On the Wednesday before Thanksgiving, Submit Thompson sat upon the
stone wall dividing her father's pasture from the Adams orchard.

The day was gray and sharp. Dry blackberry vines caught
at her red petticoat, and the wind puffed her hood
out behind her head. There was not a leaf on
the apple trees, and the brown grass bent all one
way, as if it were tired of standing upright. Submit,
perched above it in her scarlet hood and homespun skirts,
looked like some strange late flower which November had forgotten
to kill.

Sarah Adams stood on the other side of the wall.

Sarah was a slender, prim little girl, with a high
forehead, a pointed chin, and grave blue eyes. She held
her doll Thankful against her breast, although Thankful was almost
too large to be carried comfortably. The doll had a
painted face, real flaxen curls, a sprigged muslin gown, and
tiny red shoes. Sarah's aunt in Boston had sent her,
and there was no other doll like her for many
miles.

"Our turkey is heavier than yours," said Submit Thompson.

"It ain't," said Sarah Adams.

"It is."

"It ain't."

Submit swung one stout shoe against the stones. "Father says
ours is a monstrous bird."

"My father says ours is the greatest turkey ever raised
in our barnyard."

"He was speaking playful, mebbe."

"He was not speaking playful."

Sarah's thin arm tightened around Thankful. The doll stared past
Submit with a mild, painted smile, as though turkeys and
quarrels were equally beneath her notice.

"Our turkey has a breast on him as broad as
the meeting-house door," Submit declared.

"Our turkey can scarce walk through the woodshed door."

"Ours eats more corn at one swallow than yours does
in a day."

"You never saw ours eat."

"I saw his tracks."

"You did not, Submit Thompson."

"I did, Sarah Adams."

They stared at each other. Submit's black eyes sparkled. Sarah's
blue eyes grew narrow and steady. The families were fast
friends, and so were the girls, but friendship does not
always prevent rivalry. Indeed, with certain children it gives rivalry
a better opportunity.

"To-morrow they'll be weighed," Submit said at last.

"I know it."

"And then you'll see."

"You'll see."

"If ours weighs the most, you must say before everybody
that you were wrong."

"So must you."

Submit considered this. Merely saying that she was wrong seemed
a small victory. Her gaze fell upon Thankful.

Sarah turned a little, but not soon enough to hide
the doll.

"Let us wager," said Submit.

Sarah's pointed chin lifted. "Wager what?"

"My work-box against Thankful."

Now Submit's work-box was no mean possession. Her grandfather had
brought it from Boston. It was covered with blue paper,
and inside were bright scissors, a silver thimble, a strawberry
emery cushion, and six spools of colored silk. Sarah had
long admired it.

Still, it was not Thankful.

Sarah looked down at the doll. "You hear, Thankful," she
murmured. "Submit Thompson wants me to wager you, but I
shall not do it unless you are a mind to."

Thankful continued smiling. Sarah turned her so that her painted
eyes appeared to meet Submit's.

"She does not look unwilling," said Submit.

"She looks certain our turkey will win."

"Then wager."

Sarah hesitated. Her mother had told her that wagers were
unseemly. Her grandfather had once preached for nearly half an
hour about men who staked silver at a tavern horse
race. But Sarah knew that the Adams turkey was enormous.
She had seen him lifted, protesting, into the woodshed. Surely
there could be no wrong in promising a prize when
one was certain not to lose it.

That is one of the small doors through which mischief
enters. A child says, "Surely it can do no harm,"
and before she has turned around the harm is sitting
by the hearth like an invited guest.

"I will," Sarah said.

"Truly?"

"Truly. If your turkey weighs more, you may have Thankful.
If ours weighs more, I shall have your work-box."

They clasped hands over the stone wall.

Just then Mistress Thompson called from the back doorway, "Submit!
Where be you? The apples are waiting."

"And marm says I must stone the raisins," Sarah said.

They parted, each with a last confident nod. Sarah went
between the bare trees, carrying Thankful carefully. Submit jumped down
among the dead leaves and ran toward the gambrel-roofed Thompson
house like a red robin flying into its nest.

The Thompson kitchen was warm enough to redden her cheeks.
A great fire roared in the chimney oven. Copper kettles
shone on the wall, the spinning wheel had been pushed
into a corner, and every table seemed covered with Thanksgiving
preparations.

There were yellow pumpkins, red apples, bowls of cranberries, brown
nuts, and a row of pies with crimped edges. Warm,
spicy odors of cinnamon, ginger, molasses and roasting squash floated
through the room.

Mistress Thompson stood at the table in a checked apron.
Grandmother Thompson was sorting sage, and ten-year-old Ebenezer was cracking
walnuts with more noise than success.

"Wash your hands, Submit," said her mother. "Then pare those
apples fine."

Submit obeyed. She sat near the window, knife in hand,
and peeled apple after apple. The skins fell in long
curling ribbons into her lap.

"Did father say how much our turkey might weigh?" she
asked carelessly.

"Near twenty pounds, I should judge," said her mother.

"Only twenty?"

Mistress Thompson looked surprised. "Only! He is a noble bird."

"Is the Adams turkey bigger?"

"Mrs. Adams tells me theirs may be two-and-twenty."

Submit's knife stopped.

"Mind your thumb," said Grandmother.

But the warning came too late. The blade slipped, and
a bright bead of blood appeared.

"There now," cried her mother.

"It does not signify," Submit said bravely, although her eyes
watered.

Grandmother wrapped the thumb in linen. "A Thompson must bear
a small cut stoutly. Your grandfather carried a musket at
Saratoga with a wound in his shoulder."

Submit was fond of hearing about Saratoga, but at present
she thought less of her grandfather's wound than of the
two pounds by which Sarah's turkey might exceed theirs.

Two pounds! Thankful seemed to retreat from her across an
immeasurable distance.

All afternoon the work went forward. Submit carried apples to
the pantry, fetched flour from the bin, picked over cranberries,
and polished pewter plates. Yet whatever her hands did, her
thoughts returned to the turkeys.

The Thompsons' bird hung in the cool woodshed. Submit had
been forbidden to meddle with it, but she found reasons
to pass the door. Once she looked in.

The turkey was plump and heavy, with his legs tied
neatly. He seemed a very large bird until Submit remembered
the Adams turkey, who could scarce walk through a door.

At dusk her father came from the barn.

"Neighbor Adams says we shall weigh the turkeys together in
the morning," he announced. "He maintains his bird will best
ours."

Submit's heart sank.

Father laughed. "I told him I would not believe it
until the steelyard said so."

"Could ours grow heavier by morning?" Submit asked.

"Not unless he dines in his present condition," said Ebenezer.

Everyone laughed except Submit.

After supper Grandmother brought out a basket of mending. The
candle flames shivered in the draft, and the fire settled
into a red glow. Submit took a pair of stockings
and sat beside the corner cupboard.

The cupboard door stood a little open. Behind a stack
of linen she saw a small wooden box.

She knew that box.

It contained bullets which Grandfather Thompson had carried during the
war. They were dull, round lumps of lead, some battered,
some marked by teeth where soldiers had bitten the paper
cartridges open. Grandfather sometimes showed them to visitors and told
how liberty had been bought with courage, hunger, cold and
blood.

Submit stared at the box.

A thought came into her mind.

It was a very little thought at first, no bigger
than a mouse peeping from a hole. She drove it
away.

Then it came again.

The bullets were heavy.

She bent over her stocking. Her needle went in and
out, in and out, but the thought remained.

If a few bullets were put inside the turkey, no
one would see them. The bird would weigh more. The
Thompsons would triumph, Sarah would surrender Thankful, and afterward the
bullets might perhaps be removed before cooking.

Submit's face grew hot.

It would be cheating.

She knew that as plainly as she knew the Ten
Commandments. Yet naughty covetousness had taken hold of her. Thankful's
flaxen curls, red shoes and sprigged gown shone before her
imagination. Submit had never possessed a proper doll. She had
a rag baby named Experience, but Experience's face was made
with charcoal and had spread sadly in damp weather.

A doll from Boston was another matter.

"Why do you prick your needle into the air?" asked
Grandmother.

Submit started. "I was thinking."

"Then think through your fingers. That stocking will not mend
itself."

Submit finished her seam, but the temptation followed her upstairs
and lay down beside her. She heard the wind rub
a branch against the roof. She heard the clock strike
nine, then ten.

At last she slept, and dreamed that Thankful sat on
one side of the bed while a gigantic turkey sat
on the other, and both asked where Grandfather's bullets had
gone.

Before sunrise the Thompson household was awake. Thanksgiving Day began
in darkness, for there were fires to kindle, pies to
warm, kettles to fill, and a turkey to roast many
hours.

Submit dressed quickly. She tied on her red petticoat, pulled
her gown over it, and slipped downstairs.

No one was in the kitchen. Her father and Ebenezer
were at the barn. Her mother and grandmother were in
the milk room.

The cupboard seemed to wait for her.

Submit stood still, listening. Then she opened the door, moved
the linen, and took out the bullet box. It was
heavier than she expected.

Even then she might have put it back.

Instead she carried it to the woodshed.

Gray light showed through the cracks in the boards. The
turkey lay on a bench, ready for weighing. Submit's fingers
trembled as she untied the cloth which covered it.

She opened the cavity prepared for stuffing.

One bullet dropped in with a dull thud.

Submit nearly snatched it out. But footsteps sounded in the
yard, and fear hurried her where conscience might have stopped
her. She added another bullet, then another. Six went in.
She paused, imagined the Adams turkey outweighing theirs, and put
in four more.

She replaced the cloth, retied it, and hid the empty
space in the bullet box by shaking the remaining shot
level. Then she ran indoors.

"Why are you abroad without your hood?" her mother asked.

"I—I went to see if father was coming."

"Your teeth chatter. Stand by the fire."

Submit stood by the fire, but no warmth could prevent
her from shivering.

The weighing was to take place in the Thompson barn
after breakfast. By eight o'clock the two families had gathered
there, breath steaming in the frosty air.

Mr. Adams carried his turkey. It was indeed enormous. Sarah
walked beside him with Thankful in her arms. She wore
a brown cloak and a quilted bonnet, and her face
was pale with solemn expectation.

Mr. Thompson hung the steelyard from a beam. Grandfather adjusted
the hook while Ebenezer hopped from foot to foot and
offered unnecessary advice.

"The Adams bird first," said Mr. Thompson.

The turkey was secured in a cloth sling and lifted.
The iron bar dipped. Everyone leaned nearer.

"Two-and-twenty pounds and six ounces," Grandfather announced.

"Aha!" cried Mr. Adams.

Sarah's blue eyes shone. She hugged Thankful and looked across
at Submit.

Submit could not look back.

"Now ours," said Mr. Thompson.

The Thompson turkey was placed in the sling. The steelyard
dropped with a heavy jerk. Grandfather slid the weight along
the bar, farther and farther.

"Well!" he exclaimed. "Here is a wonder."

"How much?" asked Mistress Thompson.

"Four-and-twenty pounds, lacking an ounce."

Mr. Thompson laughed aloud. Mr. Adams removed his cap and
scratched his head.

"I would have sworn yours was the lesser bird."

"So would I," said Mistress Thompson. "I scarce lifted it
yesterday."

"Our corn must possess uncommon virtue," said Ebenezer.

Only Submit was silent.

Sarah stared at the turkey, then at the steelyard, then
at Thankful. Her pointed chin quivered.

"Sarah," whispered Mrs. Adams, "what troubles you?"

"Nothing, marm."

She pressed her lips together. She would not cry before
Submit. Without one word about the wager, she turned and
walked out of the barn.

Victory ought to have tasted sweet. Instead it was like
a green persimmon in Submit's mouth.

The families returned to their kitchens. The Thompsons' turkey was
laid upon the table to be stuffed with bread, onions
and sage.

Submit hovered near the door.

Her mother untied the cloth. "What in the world—"

A bullet rolled across the table.

Then came another, and another, each landing upon the scrubbed
boards with a heavy knock.

The kitchen grew still.

Grandfather picked one up. He knew it by the flattened
side.

"These came from my box."

Submit's mother looked at Ebenezer.

"I never touched 'em!" he cried.

Grandfather looked at Submit.

She could not speak.

Ten bullets lay on the table among the sage leaves
and crumbs. They appeared larger than musket balls had any
right to appear. In Submit's eyes they were as large
as cannon shot.

Before anyone questioned her, there came a knock at the
back door.

Sarah entered alone.

Her cheeks were white from the cold, and her eyes
were red, but she carried herself very straight. Thankful was
wrapped in a little shawl.

"I came to bring Submit what belongs to her," she
said.

Mrs. Thompson glanced from the doll to Submit. "What does
this mean?"

Sarah swallowed. "We wagered. If her turkey weighed the most,
she was to have Thankful. If ours weighed the most,
I was to have her work-box."

Grandfather's brows came down.

"A wager!" he said. "Gambling! Like unto the brawlers in
taverns and ale-houses, who cast away their substance and bring
sorrow upon their households!"

Sarah's courage failed. Tears slipped down her cheeks.

"I am sorry, marm," she whispered to Mrs. Adams, who
had followed her to the door.

"And the bullets?" asked Mr. Thompson quietly.

Submit looked at Thankful. The doll's painted smile had never
altered, but it no longer seemed mild. It seemed dreadful.

Truth stood before Submit like a steep, icy hill. She
feared to climb it. Yet behind her there was nowhere
safe to remain.

"I put them in," she said.

No one moved.

Submit's voice shook, but she went on. "Marm said the
Adams turkey might weigh two-and-twenty pounds. I wanted Thankful. I
took Grandfather's bullets and put ten inside our turkey before
it was weighed."

Sarah gave a little cry and clasped the doll.

"You cheated me, Submit Thompson!"

"Yes."

"You let me believe Thankful was yours!"

"Yes."

"You wicked, wicked girl!"

"I know it."

Submit's black eyes filled. She gripped her bandaged thumb until
the cut smarted.

"I wanted her so," she said. "I thought I could
take the bullets out afterward. I thought—" She stopped. There
was no thought which could mend what she had done.
"I am sorry."

Confession was not pleasant. It stung like balm in a
wound. But, like good balm, even while it smarted it
began its healing work.

Mr. Thompson took Thankful gently from Sarah and placed her
back in the child's arms.

"The doll is yours, Sarah. The false weight settles no
honest wager."

Sarah buried her face in Thankful's curls.

"And the wager itself," said Mrs. Adams, "was disobedient and
wrong."

Sarah's face emerged. "But I lost!"

"You did not lose, because there was no proper wager
to begin with. You knew my judgment concerning such bargains."

"Yes, marm."

"You will go to your chamber and remain there until
you are called."

Sarah looked toward the dining room, where the long table
was set with pewter dishes and the red berries of
the wintergreen. The smell of turkey had begun to mingle
with the odors of pie and spiced cider.

"On Thanksgiving?" she whispered.

"On Thanksgiving," said her mother.

Mrs. Thompson turned to Submit. "You also will go to
your chamber."

"Before dinner?"

"Certainly before dinner. You gambled, stole Grandfather's bullets, deceived your
family, and cheated your friend."

Each offense seemed to add another flight of stairs to
the way up to Submit's room.

She removed her apron and went without protest. Sarah left
by the back door and crossed the strip of frozen
ground between the two houses.

The girls' chambers were in opposite gables. Their windows faced
each other across the stone wall, and in summer they
sometimes exchanged signs after they had been sent to bed.

Submit sat on a wooden stool by her window. Below,
smoke rose from both chimneys. Wagons passed on the road
carrying families to Thanksgiving gatherings. Once she heard the meeting-house
bell, cheerful and commanding.

From downstairs came the clatter of dishes.

Submit thought of the dinner: the great brown turkey, bowls
of onions and squash, cranberry sauce, hot rolls, mince pies,
pumpkin pies, apples, walnuts and cider. But she felt too
miserable even to be properly hungry.

The cold pane blurred under her breath. She rubbed it
with her sleeve.

In the Adams window opposite, Sarah appeared.

Thankful was in her arms.

Submit drew back. She could not bear to see the
doll. Then Sarah tapped upon the glass.

Submit looked again.

Sarah raised the sash. Cold air rushed into her chamber
and fluttered the muslin cap on her head.

Submit opened her own window.

For a moment neither spoke. The distance between the houses
was not great, but their voices had to cross the
orchard path and the stone wall.

"My mother says I am a gambler," Sarah called mournfully.

"So does my family."

"Grandfather Thompson said we were like tavern brawlers."

"I know."

"I ain't like a tavern brawler."

"Neither am I."

Sarah considered. "Mebbe we were a little like them."

"Mebbe."

The wind lifted Submit's hair around her face. "I am
sorrier than you are, Sarah Adams."

"You ought to be."

"I am. I did not only wager. I cheated."

"Yes."

"And I took Grandfather's bullets."

"Yes."

"And I made you cry."

Sarah looked down at Thankful. "I cried all the way
home. Then I cried because I was sinful enough to
wager her. Then I cried because I called you wicked."

"I was wicked."

"But I ought not to have called you so. Marm
says we must condemn wickedness but be sparing of names."

Submit nodded. "I wanted Thankful more than I wanted to
be honest."

"I wanted the work-box."

"Not as much."

"No. But enough to wager."

"I will give you the work-box," Submit said impulsively.

Sarah shook her head. "Marm would make me return it."

"I will let you sew with the colored silks."

"All six colors?"

"All six."

Sarah's solemn face softened.

"And you may hold Thankful sometimes," she offered.

Submit's eyes widened. "After what I did?"

"Afterward. Not to-day, mebbe."

"That is fair."

They stood with their elbows on the sills, the cold
reddening their noses.

"Are we friends again, Submit Thompson?" Sarah asked.

"If you can be."

"I can."

"So can I, Sarah Adams."

They stretched their arms through the open windows, though they
were much too far apart to touch. It was a
foolish gesture, but it satisfied them. Then Sarah hugged Thankful,
and Submit hugged herself, and both began to cry a
little from relief.

Belowstairs the Thanksgiving company had arrived at the Thompsons'. Submit
heard Aunt Rose's clear laugh among the deeper voices. Aunt
Rose was Sarah's mother's unmarried sister from Boston, and she
possessed three accomplishments which the children particularly admired: she could
paint flowers, make dolls, and tell a story without forgetting
the ending.

Presently Grandmother Thompson came up the stairs.

Submit turned from the window.

"Grandmother," she said quickly, "I am truly sorry. Not because
I am missing dinner. I was sorry before I came
upstairs. I knew about the turkey as soon as it
weighed more, and it felt dreadful."

Grandmother sat on the bed. Her brown gown rustled, and
a scent of lavender came from her kerchief.

"Why did you not confess at the barn?"

"I was afraid."

"And why did you confess in the kitchen?"

"I was more afraid not to."

Grandmother's mouth twitched, though she did not smile. "That is
sometimes the beginning of courage."

"Was Grandfather brave at Saratoga?"

"So I have heard him say many times."

"I was not brave."

"You became brave enough to tell the truth when a
lie might still have hidden you a little longer. That
does not undo the wrong, but it begins the undoing."

Submit stared at her bandaged thumb.

"I put his brave bullets to a cowardly use."

"That is so."

"I suppose I shall never be forgiven."

"Do not suppose beyond your years," Grandmother said. "Forgiveness is
not the same as pretending no wrong was done. Your
father requires you to return the bullets, clean the woodshed,
and surrender your work-box for one month."

Submit's face fell at the last penalty, but she nodded.

"Your mother requires obedience and honest conduct."

"Yes, Grandmother."

"And I require you to shut that window before you
freeze."

Submit hurried to obey.

Grandmother rose. "Mercy may temper justice on Thanksgiving Day. Since
you have confessed, you may come down to dinner."

Submit looked up, hardly believing.

"Now?"

"After you wash your face."

Across the way another sash banged shut, and Sarah disappeared
from her window. A few moments later Submit saw her
running downstairs through the Adams house. Mercy, it appeared, had
crossed the stone wall.

The two families dined together at the Thompsons' long table.
The turkey was lighter than had been reported but quite
large enough for everyone. Grandfather carved it and remarked that
sage made a better stuffing than ammunition.

Even Mr. Adams laughed.

Submit sat beside Sarah. Neither girl spoke much at first.
Thankful had been left safely at home, and the blue
work-box remained on Submit's shelf under sentence. But when the
cranberry bowl reached them, Sarah gave Submit the larger spoonful.

After the turkey came the pies, and after the pies
came apples and nuts. Candles shone upon the pewter. The
wind moved around the old house, but indoors the fire
glowed, dishes passed from hand to hand, and every face
appeared kinder than it had in the morning.

Aunt Rose watched the two girls whisper together.

When dinner was over, she called Submit to her.

"I have heard about the wager," she said.

Submit hung her head. "Everybody has."

"And about the bullets."

"Yes, Aunt Rose."

"And about the confession."

Submit looked up.

Aunt Rose's eyes were smiling, though her voice remained grave.
"Thankful was made from a pattern I have kept."

"Was she?"

"She was. I could make another doll with flaxen curls,
a muslin gown, and red shoes."

Submit's breath caught.

"For me?"

"For you—on conditions."

"What conditions?"

"That you behave well, that you never wager her, and
that you remember lead bullets belong to history, not to
turkeys."

"I shall remember forever."

"Forever is a large promise. Remember to-morrow first, and then
the day after."

Submit nodded earnestly.

"What will you name her?" Sarah asked.

Submit thought of the sharp pain of confession, the open
windows, the returned dinner, and the friendship restored across the
cold November air.

"I shall name her Verity," she said.

Grandfather approved the name. Sarah declared that Thankful and Verity
would be cousins. Aunt Rose promised to begin sewing before
Christmas.

Outside, the fields lay gray and still, and the bare
orchard branches clicked together in the wind. Inside, the girls
cracked walnuts by the hearth, taking turns and dividing the
meats honestly.

And if Submit glanced now and then toward the cupboard
where the Revolutionary bullets had been restored, it was not
because she wished to touch them. She had learned that
old relics may carry the honor of a family, but
truth carries something heavier still—and needs no false weight to
make itself felt.

At half-past six on a wet November evening, when the
cobblestones of Prytania Street shone like black scales and every
gutter hurried toward the river, a little girl entered Tony
Bellotti's shop and laid a nickel upon the counter.

"Gimme fi' cents candy," she said, pointing a damp finger
at the tallest jar.

Tony, who was opening oysters with a knife broad enough
for a pirate, did not look up.

"Which kind?"

"Red ones."

"Red ones cost ten."

"Mamma says fi'."

"Then your mamma can sell 'em."

From behind the charcoal stove came a stifled sigh. The
woman whom everybody called Mrs. Bellotti rose, wiped her hands
upon her apron, and counted six scarlet candies into a
twist of paper.

"There," she whispered. "One is lagniappe."

Tony's head came up. It was a magnificent head, black-bearded,
bull-necked, and angry; the head of a minor Caesar condemned
by commerce to sell onions, lamp oil and liquor by
the glass.

"What you give away my candy for?"

"It is only one, Tony."

"Only one! Only one!" he roared. "Give away one all
day, and at night I got an empty jar."

The child fled. The woman resumed her seat, drew a
gray stocking from beneath her shawl and began to knit.
Her fingers were thin and restless. They moved even when
she watched Tony, even when customers came, even when the
stove smoked and the rain rattled against the panes. It
seemed that if those fingers ceased their poor employment, her
heart also might stop from lack of habit.

The neighbourhood called her Tony's German wife, though nobody knew
when they had married. Prytania Street, you must admit, had
other matters to occupy it. The refined families above the
avenue required oysters for supper, charcoal during cold snaps, candy
for their children and credit until Saturday. They could not
be expected to investigate the sacraments of the people who
supplied these necessities.

Tony himself was Italian, and therefore, according to local science,
naturally excitable, thrifty and armed with a knife. The woman
was German, and was consequently supposed to endure. Thus nationality
explained everything and excused nearly as much.

She had come to him fifteen years before, when his
shop consisted of one room, three shelves and a barrel
of potatoes. She scrubbed the floor, cooked his meals, mended
his shirts, carried coal, salted fish, kept accounts and slept
in a cupboard behind the kitchen. In time the business
prospered. Jars multiplied; sausages hung from hooks; pyramids of oranges
guarded the doorway; and Tony, nourished by success, grew until
he seemed a Hercules upholstered in greasy blue flannel.

The woman diminished in equal proportion.

No matter what the weather, she went at dawn to
the French Market. No matter how late Tony drank, she
waited to unlace his shoes. No matter what he broke,
she swept it up. And no matter whether his anger
sprang from a dishonest customer, a sour oyster, a losing
card or the mere insolence of Tuesday following Monday, it
descended upon her.

Sometimes it was a word. Sometimes it was his open
hand. Sometimes it was the leather strap with which he
sharpened his razor.

The neighbours heard.

"Foreign folks is different," said Madame Lavigne, who bought sugar
there on credit.

"He don't mean nothin'," said the iceman.

"He'd never dare hit me," declared Mrs. Flaherty, the daughter
of Erin, whose husband had not dared do anything in
twelve years.

The woman offered no complaint. The following morning she appeared
behind the counter with a dingy shawl over her shoulders
and perhaps a bruise beneath one eye. She measured beans,
wrapped candles and gave the children their lagniappe. Her needles
clicked, clicked, clicked. Life went on pretty much as before.

There were holidays, certainly. Once or twice a year Tony
suffered from gout.

Then Samson fell.

He lay in bed with his mountainous foot enclosed in
flannel, roaring that the sheet was too heavy, the broth
too weak, the doctor a thief and the saints negligent.
He could throw spoons, but he could not pursue. During
those blessed intervals the woman moved almost lightly. She stood
in the yard beneath the narrow strip of sky and
breathed as if air were a novelty. Once she even
hummed a German song while hanging Tony's enormous stockings upon
the line.

"What you sing for?" he called from the bedroom.

She stopped at once.

But a smile, small and secret, survived at the corners
of her mouth.

Years passed in this fashion, which is to say they
passed like loaded carts over a weak bridge. Tony's beard
became streaked with iron-gray; the woman's hair faded to the
colour of dust. Children who had spent pennies at his
counter returned as servants, labourers, mothers and young gentlemen too
proud to remember their debts. The shop grew darker and
more crowded. Behind flour bins, under loose boards, within tobacco
tins and even in the hollow leg of an old
table, Tony hid money.

He trusted no bank. Banks failed, presidents lied, priests ate
too well, and women—above all women—wanted a man's property.

This opinion he expressed frequently while the woman darned his
socks.

One August Tony took to bed and did not rise.

At first he blamed bad sausage. Then the heat. Then
the city water, Congress and a Corsican peddler who had
cursed him in 1894. The doctor, a dry little man
who had long attended Tony's gout and feared neither his
muscles nor his language, examined him and afterward spoke to
the woman in the passage.

"It may be weeks," he said. "It may be less."

She stared at him.

"Less?"

"He is very ill."

The needles slipped from her lap. A sound escaped her—half
sob, half laugh. The doctor looked sharply at her, but
she bent to recover the stocking. When she stood again
her face was properly miserable.

That night she knelt before a wooden crucifix in the
kitchen. Whether she thanked Heaven or begged pardon is not
for us to say. Human prayers, like shop accounts, often
contain entries on both sides.

For several days Tony weakened. The fallen giant could no
longer raise himself without assistance. His hands, which had once
lifted barrels, clawed feebly at the sheet. His voice dwindled
from thunder to a harsh muttering. Yet his eyes remained
fierce, and when the woman approached with medicine he watched
her as if she had poisoned it.

Then John arrived.

John Bellotti came from a grocery near the French Quarter,
wearing a black coat, a red necktie and a gold
watch chain fat enough to restrain a dog. He resembled
Tony reduced by one-third and polished for Sunday. He kissed
his brother upon both cheeks, glanced once at the woman
and immediately began opening drawers.

"What he look for?" she asked.

"Papers," Tony whispered.

John stayed. He ate at Tony's table, slept in the
little front room and questioned customers concerning the value of
the shop. He tapped walls. He lifted boards with his
heel. When he thought himself unobserved, he weighed the flour
tins in his hands.

The woman's new hope began to shiver.

On the fourth evening a storm rolled in from the
river. Thunder pressed against the windows; the gas flame bent
and recovered. Tony lay beneath a red quilt, his face
yellow against the pillow. John had gone out for wine.

The woman sat by the bed knitting the same gray
stocking she had begun weeks before. At last she put
it aside.

"Tony?"

His eyelids stirred.

"Tony, you send for priest?"

"No priest."

"You are sick. Very sick."

"I know I'm sick, fool."

She folded and unfolded her worn hands.

"Send for Father André. He come quick."

"No."

"For confession, Tony."

"I got nothin' to confess."

At this even the thunder appeared embarrassed.

She leaned nearer. "Tony, listen. We must marry."

His eyes opened fully.

"Marry?"

"Before you die. The priest can do it here. He
bring witness. It takes little time."

For fifteen years the entire street had called her Mrs.
Bellotti. Fifteen years she had answered to the name, paid
his bills under it, been struck beneath it and worn
it like an iron collar. But no church register held
it. No civil book joined her life to his. Tony
had always postponed the ceremony—until business improved, until Easter, until
the weather cooled, until the pope himself should find leisure,
perhaps, to come to Prytania Street.

Tony's cracked lips drew back from his teeth.

"So. Now you want marry."

"I wanted always."

"You want shop."

"No, Tony."

"You want money."

"No. Only what is right. I work all these years.
I stay. I am your wife to everybody."

"To fools."

She recoiled as though he had struck her, and indeed
old habit made the motion unnecessary.

"Please," she said. "Please, Tony. I cook, I wash, I
keep shop. When you hit me, I stay. When you
are sick, I stay. I got nowhere."

"Go Germany."

"I have nobody there."

"Then go nowhere."

Her pale face seemed to collapse inward. Yet necessity, which
lends courage even to mice when the room is burning,
forced her on.

"If no marriage, John takes all."

At his brother's name Tony smiled.

"John is blood."

"And I—what am I?"

He turned his face toward the wall.

She seized his wrist. It was the first time she
had touched him without command in all those years.

"Tony! What am I?"

He shook her off with surprising strength.

"You are woman I keep."

She remained bent over him. The storm swept down the
street, rattling signs and setting every loose shutter to clapping.
Her mouth opened twice before words came.

"Send priest," she whispered. "For mercy."

"No."

"For my old age."

"No."

"For God."

"No!"

The last refusal rose almost to his former roar. Then
he coughed, choked and sank back. She stood trembling beside
the bed until John returned, shaking rain from his hat
and carrying two bottles beneath his coat.

"What happen?"

"Nothing," said Tony.

Near dawn his breathing changed. The woman knew the sound.
She had heard it from her mother, from a child
in the next room of a lodging house, from an
old fruit seller who died one winter behind his cart.
She sent John for the doctor, though it was useless.

"Tony," she pleaded once more. "Say I am your wife.
Say it before John."

John came to the bedside.

Tony's gaze travelled from the woman's face to his brother's.
He could no longer speak. She bent so low that
her shawl brushed his beard.

"Me, Tony? Your wife?"

His hand crawled from beneath the quilt. For one foolish,
glorious instant she thought he meant to touch her. Instead
the thick forefinger lifted, trembled and pointed across her shoulder.

At John.

Then Tony died.

The funeral was considerable. Three carriages went to the cemetery,
and the shop closed for half a day. People spoke
of Tony's strength, his enterprise and the excellence of his
oysters. Madame Lavigne said the widow looked "strangely composed," by
which she meant that the woman had no money for
proper mourning.

The next morning John opened the shop.

By noon he had found seven hundred dollars beneath a
floorboard, two hundred in the stove pipe and a roll
of banknotes sewn inside Tony's winter coat. The gold watch
chain shone splendidly across his satisfaction.

"You go," he told the woman.

She looked toward the counter. "I keep shop."

"My shop now."

"I can work."

"I got cousin coming."

She gazed about the cramped room: the candy jars, the
oyster knife, the scales, the charcoal stove, the barrels rubbed
smooth by her hands. Even the dust appeared to belong
to her more justly than it belonged to John.

"You got paper?" he asked.

She had none.

Thus, while Prytania Street watched from doorways and found the
affair unfortunate, Tony Bellotti's wife—who had never been Tony Bellotti's
wife—was turned into the morning with a bundle of clothes
beneath her arm.

No one followed. Customers still required sugar; children still had
pennies; commerce, unlike pity, cannot pause indefinitely.

She walked toward the river, thin and gray beneath her
dingy shawl. Her empty hands moved against the bundle, the
fingers bending and straightening as though seeking two familiar needles
and the endless stocking by which she had measured out
her life.

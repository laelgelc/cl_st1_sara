### The Islington Papers

The papers from which the following particulars are taken came
into my possession in a manner too ordinary to deserve
concealment. In the spring of 1907, a dealer in Clerkenwell
sold me, with several parish books and two volumes of
sermons by the admirable but seldom consulted Dr. South, a
narrow ledger bound in black calf. Upon its back, in
ink now browned, were the words **DISBURSEMENTS—ST. MARY'S—1716–1723**.

The first forty leaves contained accounts of candles, bell-ropes, tiles,
wine for communions, and the destruction of polecats. There followed
sixteen blank pages, after which certain papers had been sewn
into the book. They are of several sizes and in
several hands. Some bear the name of the Reverend Thomas
Pratt, rector of a parish near Islington; others are copies
of depositions, medical memoranda, and an unfinished letter from one
Luke Jennett. A leaf has been cut away between pages
97 and 98. The stub carries, in Pratt's hand, these
words:

> Jennett's full account touching the bedstaff, not to be
shown except upon necessity.

No such account is now present.

I have arranged the remainder by date. A name occurring
in the papers of a noble family I have suppressed,
not from fear of living representatives (there are none), but
because the identification adds little to the history and might
give pain to persons connected only remotely with that house.
In other respects I have altered nothing beyond spelling and
punctuation where obscurity demanded it.

Whether the documents record a crime, a delusion, or—as Pratt
seems reluctantly to have believed—an exercise of powers not commonly
admitted, the reader may decide.

#### I

In 1718 there lived in Islington, at a house on
the north side of Lower Street, one Erasmus Abell, doctor
of physic, aged about fifty-three. He had studied for a
time at Leyden, though no record has been found of
his taking a degree there. His practice was never large,
but among his patients were several families of substance who
valued him for the gravity of his manner, the variety
of his reading, and his unwillingness to promise what physic
could not perform.

Pratt's recollection of him is not unfriendly.

> Dr. Abell was of a dry person, with dark
eyes and a beard cut nearer than was then usual.
His discourse, when not medical, inclined to theology, natural philosophy,
and the properties of those beings which the heathen named
fauns, lemures, and so forth. He was no Papist, as
some affirmed, but had read many Popish books. Of witches
he spoke contemptuously, saying that an old woman could no
more command a spirit than a child command a colonel
of horse; yet he did not from this conclude that
spirits were not to be commanded.
>
> Once at my table he asked whether motion were
an accident bestowed only upon living bodies.
>
> "A rolling stone moves," said I.
>
> "By an impulse not its own," says he.
>
> "Then you would have stones roll themselves?"
>
> "I would know," he replied, "whether energy may be
communicated to matter without the continued presence of him who
communicates it."
>
> I told him this was a question for the
Royal Society, who would no doubt set a subscription on
foot and obtain a moving coal-scuttle within ten years.
>
> He did not laugh.

Abell's servant at this period was Luke Jennett, who had
entered the house as a boy and remained nearly twenty-seven
years. Jennett purchased medicines, cleaned instruments, admitted patients, and slept
in a small room beyond the dispensing closet. His fidelity
was generally acknowledged. Mrs. Hester Bale, who washed for Abell,
stated that the doctor trusted Jennett "beyond any brother," though
he cursed him sharply when powders were damp or bottles
misplaced.

The earliest surviving statement by Jennett was made before Pratt
on 11 March 1718.

> I do declare that I left Dr. Abell's service
not for wages, nor for drunkenness, nor through any practice
of Dr. Quinn, as has been maliciously given out. I
carried away only my apparel, two razors, a Bible, and
seventeen shillings owing to me. I spread no report against
my master. What I saw in the upper chamber I
told first to Mr. Pratt, he being a minister, and
afterward to Mr. Justice Bellamy when commanded.
>
> It is false that I was sleeping. I was
awake and had a candle.

Here the statement ends. The next leaf, as already observed,
has been removed. On the following page Pratt has written:

> The particular concerning the bedstaff appeared to me at
first the effect of fright, yet J. could not be
brought to repeat it without trembling and a flux of
tears very unsuitable to his years. He maintained always that
the thing came from beneath the bed, though he had
himself set it upright in the corner. He further said—but
this belongs to the missing examination—that his master did not
seem surprised.

A bedstaff, it may be recalled, was a short wooden
rod used to keep bedclothes in order and sometimes, less
properly, as a domestic weapon. No other explanation is given.

Jennett left Abell's house that night. Within a fortnight he
entered the service of Dr. Gideon Quinn, a physician of
Canonbury Lane.

#### II

Quinn was ten years younger than Abell and of a
disposition almost ostentatiously plain. He prescribed sparingly, attended poor patients
without fee on Thursdays, and is said to have called
most foreign medicines "dear names for old dirt." This opinion,
whether wise or not, recommended him to households which had
formerly employed Dr. Abell. Three such families transferred their custom
during the summer of 1718.

It would be unjust to say that Jennett procured this
result. Mrs. Bale, who continued to wash for both establishments,
swore that he never spoke against his old master.

> He would say only, "I am better where I
am," and if asked what passed at Dr. Abell's, he
would take up the basket or go from the room.
Once I said, to tease him, "Luke, was it a
ghost?" He answered, "No ghost would need to hide in
a stick," and afterward would say no more.

Abell's resentment is recorded by Pratt, who met him near
St. John's Gate in October.

> He complained that Quinn fed upon another man's reputation
as a rook upon seed-corn. I answered that patients, like
parishioners, had souls of their own, though both physicians and
rectors sometimes forgot it.
>
> "Quinn has my servant," said he.
>
> "He has also a red door," I replied, "but
I do not understand that either was stolen."
>
> At this he struck the iron rail beside him
with his cane and asked whether I considered Quinn an
honest practitioner. I said that I did.
>
> "Then you are easily satisfied," says he.
>
> We walked some way in silence. At the corner
he asked if I had lately read any account of
the Telchines, who, as he said, could put life or
malignity into manufactured things. I told him that I had
enough malignity among my churchwardens and needed none from Rhodes.
>
> He smiled then, but I observed that the ferrule
of his cane was broken, though he had not struck
the rail with great force.

A more curious passage follows.

> On the 2 nd of November I drank a
dish of tea with Abell. A fire being newly kindled,
the poker stood within the fender. During our talk it
fell outward, not sideways as might be expected, and travelled
near the length of the hearthrug. I supposed a coal
had displaced it. Abell said, "You see, Pratt, how little
persuasion matter requires."
>
> "Persuasion?" said I.
>
> "The word passed my lips without design."
>
> He restored the poker, taking it in a cloth
though it was cold.

Pratt adds that he thought nothing of the incident until
later.

#### III

On Tuesday, 18 November, Dr. Quinn visited a patient in
Highbury and returned at six. Jennett had gone to purchase
lamp oil. The maid, Alice Ferry, admitted a gentleman in
a brown cloak who gave no name but said he
would wait in the study.

Her deposition was taken after Quinn's death.

> The gentleman was Dr. Abell, whom I knew by
sight, though he held his hat before him when first
entering. I told him my master might not return before
seven. He said, "All the better; I have papers to
leave."
>
> I showed him into the study. Afterward, being below,
I heard the door of the dispensing room, which sticks
in wet weather. I went up and found no person
in the study. The gentleman came out of the dispensing
room and said he had mistaken the door.
>
> I said, "Sir, that door is not near the
stairs."
>
> He looked at me in such a fashion that
I wished I had not spoken.
>
> I went down. Presently I heard him above again,
though I had not heard his foot upon the stair.
When I followed, he stood in my master's bedchamber, at
the foot of the bed. He had turned back the
coverlet.
>
> I asked what he did there. He answered that
Dr. Quinn had once complained of a pain occasioned by
a bad mattress, and he desired to examine it. I
said the mattress was not my master's complaint, nor had
Dr. Abell been called.
>
> He then laughed and gave me sixpence, which I
laid upon the study table after he was gone.

Nothing was stolen. Quinn, when informed, treated the matter lightly,
saying that physicians were as inquisitive about one another's beds
as old women about christenings. Jennett did not share his
amusement and begged him to change the lock of his
chamber. Quinn refused.

That same night his dreams began.

The account is contained partly in Quinn's own notebook and
partly in a letter addressed to his sister at Ware.
The notebook entry for 19 November reads:

> Slept ill. Dreamed I walked at night in a
churchyard unknown to me, yet knew where I must go.
A man behind compelled me, not by touching but by
intending. I had a spade. Dug beside a stone without
inscription. Found at four feet a white case resembling the
winter lodging of an insect, but of a man's length.
Woke before opening it. Pulse quick, tongue dry.

On the 23 rd he records the dream again.

> The same place. Ground wet. Hands blistered. The case
was bound at head and feet, not sewn like a
shroud but smooth and somewhat shining. I was required to
cut it. Heard breathing behind me. Could not turn.

On the 30 th:

> Opened the upper part. Within was a face. Mine,
but dead, and older by perhaps six days.

This last phrase puzzled Pratt, who asked Quinn what he
meant.

> He said, "A corpse changes hourly, as you know.
This face had been dead six days. Do not ask
how a dream computes."
>
> I advised a purge and less wine.
>
> "I take one glass at dinner."
>
> "Then take two," said I, "and disappoint the faculty."

Quinn laughed, but Jennett, who was in the room, dropped
a tray. Pratt notes that the servant afterward followed him
into the passage and asked whether dreams might be sent
through "things a man lies upon."

"I told him," writes Pratt, "that dreams were commonly sent
through indigestion. He answered that some persons dig where another
directs, though the director remain at home."

#### IV

About the middle of December, Quinn bought a bedstead and
bedding at the sale-room of Josiah Pounce in Moorfields. The
bedstead proved too broad for his chamber and was returned,
but he kept two pillows, three linen sheets, a quilted
under-cover, and a bolster. They were of a fineness beyond
what Quinn habitually purchased and had apparently seen little use.

Pounce's books list them as:

> Lot 44. Bed furniture, French ticking, Holland sheets, feather
pillows, very handsome, second-hand, from a gentleman's country lodging. £3
12 s.

The words "country lodging" have been crossed through and "effects
removed" written above.

Jennett disliked the purchase. His unfinished letter gives the reason.

> I told my master the linen was burial linen,
though it had no smell of earth. He asked how
I could know such a thing. I said I knew
by the breadth and by the bands at the corners,
which were not for a living bed. Also, upon each
pillow, beneath the seam, there was worked in white a
bird with open wings and over it a coronet. The
mark had been picked out in places but not wholly.
>
> My master said, "Then I sleep like a lord
at a tradesman's price."
>
> I desired him to burn all, and he was
angry, saying I had brought fancies from Dr. Abell's house
and must leave them outside his own. I begged pardon.
I did not spread any report.

Alice Ferry stated that the new pillows were exceptionally soft
and heavy. They did not spring back readily when pressed,
but retained the impression of the hand. On the first
night Quinn slept upon them for eleven hours, though his
custom was to rise before seven. Jennett and Alice knocked
repeatedly. At last Quinn called out in a thick voice
that he was awake but could not "get free of
the warmth."

When he came down, a red line encircled his neck,
as if a cord had lain there. He attributed it
to the collar of his nightshirt.

For the next three weeks Quinn's sleep deepened. He ceased
to hear the bells, street cries, or morning traffic. Once
Jennett entered at nine and found both pillows drawn close
against his master's ears, though Quinn had gone to bed
with one beneath his head and the other upon a
chair. Quinn explained that he must have moved it in
sleep.

On 6 January he told Pratt that the dream had
altered.

> I no longer dig. I am in the white
covering, and something outside folds me by degrees. There is
no pain, only great certainty. The worst is that my
hands are crossed and I cannot uncross them, because they
seem to belong to one already dead.

Pratt asked whether he had disposed of the bedding.

> "Why should I?"
>
> "Because you sleep badly upon it."
>
> "On the contrary, I sleep too well."
>
> "That may be the less healthy fault."
>
> He replied that a clergyman should not encourage servant's
tales. I said that a physician should not value economy
above breath. This displeased him, and we parted with some
coldness.

Pratt did not see him alive again.

#### V

On the morning of 14 January 1719, Alice Ferry found
the bedchamber door fastened. At half past eight she called
Jennett, who knocked and received no answer. The key was
inside. A carpenter was fetched from the next house and
removed a panel.

The room was orderly. The window had been bolted and
its inner shutter secured. Ashes lay undisturbed upon the hearth.
A glass of water stood beside the bed. Quinn's watch,
purse, and rings were present.

Quinn lay on his back beneath the sheets. His arms
were straight at his sides, but his hands had been
brought together upon the breast and placed palm over palm.
The upper sheet passed across both shoulders and was drawn
so tightly beneath the mattress that two men were required
to release it.

The pillows presented a still more singular appearance. The larger
lay under Quinn's head. Its two ends had bent upward
and inward, meeting over the mouth and nose. The second
pillow, which the maid had left on a chair, was
upon his chest, pressed under the chin. Its corners appeared
twisted together behind the neck.

Jennett would not touch them.

Mr. Samuel Hobb, surgeon, arrived at ten and made the
following memorandum:

> Body not cold. No wound. Face livid, eyes prominent,
tongue bitten. Marks about mouth consistent with forcible stopping of
breath. No cord-mark. Feather pillow much compressed over countenance. Bed-linen
unusually constricting. I cannot conceive deceased to have so disposed
it, especially the lower sheet being secured beneath him. No
person discovered in room, chimney too narrow, window and door
fastened within. Death by suffocation, cause uncertain.

At the coroner's inquiry, Hobb was asked whether an apoplexy
might have caused the position of the body. He answered
that apoplexy did not commonly tuck in sheets or knot
pillows. This reply was judged impertinent and omitted from the
formal record.

The jury returned: "Death by a sudden Visitation of God."

Jennett was examined for six hours. Suspicion fell upon him
because he had first raised the alarm and had formerly
served Abell. Nothing could be proved. The carpenter testified that
the door was fastened by a strong bolt, not merely
locked. Dust upon the window sill was unmarked. No passage
existed behind the wainscot.

There is among the papers a copy of a question
put to Jennett:

> Did you ever hear Dr. Erasmus Abell affirm that
he could cause any article, instrument, or furniture to move
or perform an action without the hand of man?

Jennett's answer:

> I heard him say many things. He said a
corpse was but matter from which the tenant had gone,
and clothes about a corpse might learn more of death
than clothes about a living man learned of life. I
did not understand him. I spread no report.

Asked whether Abell had ever attempted such a practice, Jennett
replied:

> The answer is in my first declaration concerning the
bedstaff.

That declaration was produced at the inquiry but, as noted,
is no longer in the collection.

#### VI

Dr. Abell attended Quinn's funeral on 19 January and stood
at a distance from the other physicians. Pratt, after the
service, approached him.

> I said, "You have heard the manner of his
death."
>
> "Every cookmaid in Islington has heard it."
>
> "You were in his chamber in November."
>
> He looked at me and said, "Do you propose
to accuse me of hiding beneath the bed for two
months?"
>
> "I propose nothing. I ask why you went there."
>
> "To satisfy curiosity."
>
> "Concerning the mattress?"
>
> "Concerning Quinn."
>
> I then asked whether motion might be communicated to
matter without the continued presence of him who communicates it.
>
> He regarded me for some while. "You remember old
conversations too exactly, Pratt."
>
> "Clergymen must store what learning they cannot use."
>
> At this he turned away. I caught his sleeve
and asked directly whether the dead could infect what covered
them.
>
> He answered, "Everything instructs by contact."
>
> "And may the lesson be repeated?"
>
> "A sermon may."
>
> "By a sheet?"
>
> He smiled, though I observed that his face was
grey. "You are now a believer in satyrs, moving coal-scuttles,
and all the nursery of the Platonists."
>
> "No," said I. "But I believe Dr. Quinn was
murdered."
>
> "Then find the murderer."

No charge was brought against Abell. His practice declined, partly
owing to gossip, though Jennett repeatedly denied originating it. In
April Abell removed to a house near Barnet. He died
there in 1721 of a fever attended by delirium. A
surgeon who visited him reported that the room had been
stripped of curtains, carpets, cushions, and all loose furniture, by
Abell's own command. He slept upon boards with a folded
coat beneath his head.

During the fever he complained that objects retained grudges. He
demanded that his cane be burned and cried out whenever
the poker was moved.

This evidence, being second-hand, need not be pressed.

#### VII

The final papers in the ledger concern Josiah Pounce, from
whom Quinn bought the bedding. In February 1719 Pounce was
charged with receiving goods stolen from a burial place. The
indictment is damaged, but enough survives to show that the
goods included silver coffin fittings, velvet, a sword, linen, two
feather pillows, and "divers ornaments of a funeral bed."

The principal thief, one Nathaniel Rusk, had been employed in
repairs at a mausoleum upon an estate north of London.
He discovered that a side door could be opened after
the steward's men departed. On three nights he removed articles
from the vault and conveyed them in a cart under
spoiled hay.

The deceased whose coffin was disturbed had been a person
of rank, dead less than a year. Certain furnishings had
been placed not merely in the vault but about the
body itself according to an old family custom. The arms
of that family included a bird displayed beneath a coronet.

Pounce maintained that he believed the articles came from a
dismantled country house. He was fined, pilloried, and sentenced to
imprisonment. Rusk's confession, which appears once to have accompanied the
papers, is absent. Pratt supplies a summary but refuses particulars.

> The account of the opening of the coffin is
blunt and terrible, and I see no profit in copying
it. Rusk says that the wrapping clung to the body
and made a sound when pulled away, though the vault
was dry. He says also that one pillow, being cast
down, was afterward found lying again across the face. This
may be the lie of a frightened criminal. He sold
the linen and pillows to Pounce on 9 December.
>
> I examined the mark described by the maid Ferry
and found it agreeable to the arms named in the
indictment. The family shall not be set down here.
>
> It is possible that Abell, knowing Pounce or Rusk,
arranged the sale. It is possible he merely learned of
it and made use of what chance provided. It is
possible that he had no part in Quinn's death, and
that sheets, being sheets, do not remember the offices for
which they have been employed.
>
> Yet I cannot forget his expression: that clothes about
a corpse may learn more of death than clothes about
a living man learn of life.

Pratt's memorandum ends with a passage written several years later:

> Luke Jennett came to me in May 1724, being
then in service at Hackney. He asked whether I still
possessed his declaration. I told him it was safe. He
desired that it be burned.
>
> "Why now?" said I.
>
> He answered that Dr. Abell was dead and Dr.
Quinn beyond help, while he himself wished to sleep without
imagining a stick upright in the corner.
>
> I asked once more what had occurred in that
chamber.
>
> "Sir," says he, "you read it."
>
> "I did."
>
> "Then you know it moved."
>
> "Many things move by settling, vibration, rats, or an
uneven floor."
>
> "It stood across the room in the morning."
>
> I confess this shook me, though I did not
show it. "And what did your master say?"
>
> Jennett replied, "He said it had not yet learned
enough."
>
> I promised to destroy the paper, but did not.
Whether another has since done what I lacked the resolution
to do, I cannot tell.

Here the dossier properly concludes. There is, however, one last
circumstance.

When I obtained the ledger, a thin strip of linen
was caught between the sewn papers and the calf binding.
It is yellow with age and of a quality much
finer than any parish rag used for repair. Worked upon
it in white thread, almost invisible except when turned toward
the light, is the upper point of a coronet and,
beneath it, what may be the tip of a bird's
wing.

I have not attempted to detach it.

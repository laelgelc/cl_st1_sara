We were twenty-six men beneath the earth.

The cellar had windows, if three oblong wounds at pavement
level may be called windows. Iron bars crossed them outside;
inside, the panes were furred with flour and damp. Through
them we saw boots, wheels, the dragging hems of women's
skirts, dogs nosing the gutter, and, in winter, the gray
weight of snow pressed almost to the glass. We never
saw a whole human being. The world passed us in
fragments from the knees downward.

From five in the morning until ten at night we
made hard biscuits for taverns, barracks, riverboats, prisons, and poor
households. We kneaded, rolled, cut, boiled, baked, stacked. Twenty-six pairs
of hands repeated the same movements until the hands appeared
wiser than the men to whom they belonged. Our backs
bent before the benches; our shoulders rose and fell; sweat
cut pale channels through the flour on our faces. Looking
along the room, one might have thought that some miserly
sculptor had fashioned twenty-six clay figures and set them turning
in a machine.

The ceiling was low and green with moisture. The brick
walls sweated in spring and crusted with ice in winter.
Flour hung in the air like a sickness. It entered
the nose, settled on the tongue, clung beneath the eyelids,
and mixed with sweat inside our shirts until our skin
felt coated with paste.

At one end of the cellar stood the oven.

It was broad, black, and swollen, with an iron mouth
that opened upon trembling red depths. Two round vents above
the door looked like eyes without pity. When the logs
cracked, sparks sprang out like teeth, and a wavering shine
crawled across the wall, mocking us with the color of
daylight. Beside it the copper kettle purred. The shovel scraped.
Dough slapped the boards. Trays rang. These sounds accompanied every
thought we had, until even our dreams seemed to scrape,
purr, slap, and ring.

Our employer called us lazy.

He was a small, dry man with pale lashes and
rings on two fingers. He visited the cellar holding a
scented handkerchief beneath his nose, as though our breath were
an insult designed especially for him. He locked the outer
window grates because he feared we would steal the biscuits
we made. He fed us gristle, sour cabbage, lungs that
smelled of medicine, and soup in which gray fat floated
like dead insects. If one of us complained, he smiled.

"You are not here to feast."

That was true. We were not there to feast, to
sleep, to think, or to live. We were there to
turn flour into biscuits.

Above us, in the clean shop facing the street, four
men baked white bread, rolls, braided loaves, and sweet buns.
Their walls were limewashed. Their windows opened. They earned more
money and worked fewer hours. We hated them, though we
hardly knew them. They hated us more easily, because those
who stand one step above the bottom are often most
anxious to kick downward and prove the distance between themselves
and the pit.

We smelled their bread sometimes.

Warm milk, browned crust, butter, poppy seeds—such smells descended through
cracks in the floor and mingled with smoke, sweat, and
dampness. They were smells from another life. We imagined clean
tables, bright cloths, knives shining beside plates, children breaking soft
rolls, women brushing crumbs from their lips. Then somebody cursed,
the shovel scraped, and the cellar closed around us again.

There was only one thing in our days that did
not belong to the machine.

Every morning, a little after six, there came three quick
taps at the narrow window beside the passage. A fresh
face appeared behind the glass, pink from the air outside,
with dark eyes shining beneath a scarf.

"Good morning, little prisoners!"

It was Tanya.

She was sixteen and worked as a maid for the
embroiderers on the second floor. She ran errands, scrubbed stairs,
carried coal, heated irons, and endured the sharp tongues of
women who themselves endured the sharper tongues of customers. Yet
she came to us smiling.

"Have you saved my biscuits?"

Someone always had.

Even when the employer counted every tray, even when hunger
twisted our bellies and the crisp brown rounds lay under
our hands, we hid the best ones for Tanya. We
selected those that had risen evenly and colored golden at
the edges. We wrapped them in a scrap of clean
paper if any could be found. Then one of us
climbed upon a flour bin and passed the little bundle
through the window.

She received it solemnly, as if it were a treasure.

"Thank you! All twenty-six of you!"

"Mind you don't let the mistress see."

"She sees less than an old hen."

"Don't run on the stairs."

"I never fall."

"Put on a warmer shawl."

"You put on warmer shirts!"

This always made us laugh. Some of us owned no
second shirt. Tanya knew this, but her answer was not
mockery; it was the quick defense of youth against sadness.
She could not mend our lives, so she tossed a
bright word into the cellar, and we warmed our hands
over it.

She did small things for us. She brought thread for
a torn cuff, medicine for a swollen eye, an old
newspaper, once even a sprig of lilac which she had
found fallen beside a garden wall. We placed that lilac
in a bottle on the window ledge. By evening the
heat had wilted it; by morning the petals were brown.
Still, for one day, the cellar smelled faintly of rain.

We tried to do things for her in return. One
repaired the clasp of her basket. Another carved a tiny
bird from wood. We warned her against the porter, who
drank, and against the ice near the pump. We asked
whether she had eaten breakfast and scolded her when she
had not. Our concern was clumsy and noisy, but it
was tender.

We never spoke obscenely to her.

This was remarkable, because obscenity was the mud in which
our speech ordinarily walked. We flung filthy jokes across the
benches, discussed women with the brutal imagination of men deprived
of them, and laughed at things that were not funny
because laughter, even ugly laughter, proved the throat had not
become part of the machine. But when Tanya's face appeared
at the window, our words washed themselves.

Among ourselves we called her our little sun, our swallow,
our clean one. Most often we said simply: our Tanya.

There is danger in that word "our," though we did
not know it then.

A man crushed every day must raise something above himself
or sink wholly into the dirt. Some worship a saint,
some a leader, some a woman, some a memory of
home. The object matters less than the need. We had
no church but the oven, no holy picture but a
soot-dark wall, and no festival except the first warm wind
that reached our grating in April. So we set Tanya
in the bright empty place within us and gave her
all the purity we wanted to believe had survived our
degradation.

We did not ask whether she wished to stand there.

Why should a godling object to a pedestal? Why should
an idol wish to step down and become human?

Sometimes, during the heaviest hour of afternoon, when the air
was so hot that our hearts seemed wrapped in wet
cloth, one of us would begin to sing.

At first the song emerged low and uncertain, hardly more
than breath shaped into sound. Another voice joined it from
the rolling table, then another from beside the kettle. Soon
all twenty-six were singing. Our voices were rough, cracked, badly
matched, yet together they gathered force. The song climbed the
walls, pressed against the ceiling, and searched for an opening.
It rolled around the oven and over the noise of
the shovel. It was like a broad river seeking the
sea; like a road shining far away beneath a sky
none of us could see.

We sang of fields.

Few of us had owned fields, but we knew how
fields looked at sunset. We sang of mothers, though some
had forgotten their mothers' faces. We sang of girls waiting
at gates, though no girl waited for us. The song
gave us what life withheld, and for a few minutes
the cellar widened. Beyond the bars there were forests, roads,
blue distances, and clean winds. We lifted our heads.

Then the last note died.

The shovel scraped.

The kettle purred.

The oven stared.

And we bent again.

Tanya once heard us singing. She stood in the passage
until we finished, her face quiet, almost frightened.

"That was beautiful," she said.

We laughed because praise embarrassed us. One man struck the
bench with his fist and shouted that we could sing
better if our throats were not full of flour. Another
offered her two extra biscuits. For the remainder of that
day we felt taller. Beauty had been seen in us;
Tanya had seen it, and therefore it existed.

So passed the months—morning after morning, dough after dough, song
after song, Tanya after darkness.

Then the soldier came.

He was not a soldier any longer, though he wore
his past as proudly as another man wears a medal.
He had served in a cavalry regiment and claimed to
have known officers, actresses, countesses, smugglers, and women of every
hair color between black and gold. The proprietor hired him
for the white-bread shop after dismissing a baker for drunkenness.

We first saw him in the yard.

He was tall and straight, with yellow mustaches curled at
the ends. A blue waistcoat fitted tightly across his chest,
and a bright watch chain lay over it. His boots
were polished enough to catch the thin autumn light. He
walked with his elbows held away from his body, as
if the world ought to make room for the width
of him.

He came down to inspect us on his second day.

"Well!" he said, stopping on the bottom step. "So this
is where the dead bake food for the living."

We looked at him in silence.

His uniform was gone, but his manner still expected salutes.
He smiled, displaying small, strong teeth, and twisted one mustache.

"Why so gloomy, brothers?"

"Because we rejoice inwardly," someone answered.

He laughed loudly.

He had brought tobacco, and this softened us toward him.
Within half an hour he sat on a flour sack,
telling stories. Every story concerned a fight he had won,
a superior he had outwitted, or a woman who had
begged him not to leave. He named them by trade
or feature: the red-haired innkeeper, the officer's widow, the seamstress
with dimples, the butcher's broad daughter.

"Women," he said, tapping his chest, "know a man. A
woman may pretend modesty, may turn her eyes away, may
call you a devil—but she knows. Here is strength, she
thinks. Here is courage. Here are arms that can lift
her."

He rolled up his sleeve. The muscles beneath the pale
skin rose hard and smooth.

We admired him unwillingly. We were bent, gray, flour-coated creatures;
he was well-fed, clean, shining, and free in his movements.
Even his vanity seemed a kind of wealth.

"How many women have you had?" one of us asked.

He narrowed his eyes as if calculating regiments.

"I stopped counting."

We laughed, and he enjoyed our laughter.

After that he often visited the cellar during pauses in
the white shop. He boasted, smoked, criticized our oven, and
complained that the four bread bakers were dull fellows. He
preferred us because we listened. A vain man loves an
audience more than companionship and will descend even into a
pit if applause rises from it.

He began watching the women who crossed the yard.

The embroiderers passed in pairs, holding their skirts above the
mud. A laundress came for hot water. The cook's assistant
carried baskets from the market. The soldier leaned at the
door and judged each one.

"Too narrow in the hips."

"Bad teeth."

"That one would be fiery."

"She pretends not to see me."

We responded with jokes. It entertained us, and entertainment was
scarce.

One rainy morning Tanya came as usual. Her scarf was
wet, and little drops shone in the loose hair above
her forehead.

"Good morning, little prisoners! Quickly—I'm late."

The soldier was sitting near the window. He looked up.

For once he did not speak.

We passed Tanya her biscuits. She thanked us, saw the
stranger, and glanced at him with simple curiosity.

"Who is that?"

"A hero," we said.

"A conqueror."

"A prince from the white-bread kingdom."

The soldier rose and made her a sweeping bow.

"At your service."

Tanya laughed, and before he could say more, she vanished
from the window.

He remained standing.

"Who is she?" he asked.

The question irritated us. We heard possession in it before
any right to possession had been challenged.

"She is Tanya."

"I heard the name."

"She works upstairs."

"Is she promised to anyone?"

"That is none of your concern."

He looked from face to face, surprised by our tone,
then smiled.

"Ah. So the cellar has a pet bird."

"Watch your tongue."

He lifted both hands.

"No offense, brothers! She is pretty, that is all."

"She is not like the others," said the chief kneader,
a broad man with broken veins across his nose. "Leave
her alone."

The soldier's smile sharpened.

"Not like the others? Are there two species of women?"

"You wouldn't understand."

"I understand more about women than twenty-six monks underground."

"We are not monks."

"No," he said, surveying us. "That is plain."

The laughter this time was strained.

For several days nothing happened. Tanya came, took her biscuits,
and ran away. Sometimes the soldier happened to be nearby;
sometimes he did not. When he greeted her, she answered.
When he complimented her eyes, she told him his mustaches
looked painted. We took delight in that answer and repeated
it all day.

"You see?" we told him. "She has your measure."

He twirled his mustaches more often after that.

A small hostility grew between him and our chief kneader.
The kneader disliked clean-handed men, soldiers, boasts, and curled mustaches.
More than this, he believed himself guardian of Tanya's sanctity.
We all did, but he spoke most fiercely.

One noon the soldier came below after quarreling with his
companions. He was angry and eager to restore himself by
boasting.

"Women are flies," he declared. "Show them honey, and they
come."

The kneader threw a lump of dough onto the table.

"Not every woman."

"Every one."

"Tanya wouldn't."

The soldier glanced at him.

"Wouldn't what?"

"You know."

"Come to me?"

"She'd spit in your painted mustaches."

Some laughed. The soldier's face reddened beneath its healthy color.

"Would you wager?"

There are moments when an evil thing enters quietly and
appears to be only a word. No thunder announces it.
No wall cracks. A man issues a challenge, another accepts,
and those nearby smile. Yet afterward one sees that the
word opened a door through which shame walked in.

The kneader wiped his hands upon his apron.

"You couldn't win her."

"How long?"

"You couldn't in a year."

"Give me a month."

"A month! Hear him!"

"Two weeks," the soldier said.

We stopped laughing.

The kettle purred in the silence.

"Two weeks," he repeated. "And she will come to me
willingly."

"You lie."

"Then watch."

He went up the stairs, and his polished heels flashed
once before disappearing.

We crowded around the kneader.

"What have you done?"

"Nothing. Let him boast."

"You shouldn't have named her."

"He'll get his nose pulled."

"What if—"

"Shut up! Tanya is not some alley cat."

We all agreed loudly. We praised her good sense, her
pride, her cleanliness of heart. We recalled how she had
rebuked the porter, how she had slapped an apprentice who
pinched her, how she laughed at the soldier's mustaches. Our
faith became noisy because something beneath it had begun to
tremble.

The next morning Tanya came.

We watched her too closely.

She noticed.

"Why are you staring?"

"No reason."

"Has flour turned your heads?"

"Has anybody troubled you?"

"Who would trouble me?"

"That new baker—"

"The peacock?" She laughed. "He talks like a barrel rolling
downstairs."

Relief passed among us so visibly that her brows rose.

"What is wrong with you today?"

"Nothing, Tanya. Take another biscuit."

She went away, and we congratulated one another as if
a battle had been won.

The soldier came down later.

"Well?" we demanded.

"Well what?"

"She called you a peacock."

"A peacock is a handsome bird."

"She says you talk like a barrel."

"Then she has been listening."

His confidence angered us. We jeered until he left, yet
the seed of his answer remained.

Then began the fourteen days.

Never had time moved so slowly in our cellar. Usually
days were indistinguishable, mixed together like dough in the trough.
Now each had a number. First day. Second. Third. We
measured Tanya's looks, words, pauses, and blushes. If she came
early, we wondered why. If late, we imagined where she
had been. If she laughed, we asked what secret happiness
fed the laugh. If she seemed tired, we feared she
had lain awake thinking of him.

We called this concern for her.

In truth, curiosity had already placed its cold fingers upon
us.

The soldier reported nothing. That made things worse. He no
longer boasted about Tanya. When questioned, he smiled and said,
"There is time." Once we saw him speaking to her
beside the pump. She held a bucket between them. He
leaned close; she threw back her head and laughed.

"She is laughing at him," we said.

"Certainly."

"Look how foolish he appears."

"Yes."

But the bucket stood on the ground for a long
time.

On the fifth day she did not come for biscuits.

Our work went badly. One man scorched a tray; another
spilled boiled dough; the kneader cursed everyone. At noon we
learned that Tanya had been sent across town with a
parcel.

"So that is all," we said, ashamed of our uneasiness.

On the sixth day she came cheerful and hungry. We
questioned her about the errand until she grew impatient.

"You are worse than old women. Must I account for
every step?"

"No, no. We only missed you."

Her face softened.

"Did you?"

"All twenty-six."

She looked at us through the little window, and something
like sadness moved in her eyes.

"Poor prisoners," she said gently.

That name had always pleased us. This time it stung.

The soldier appeared behind her in the passage.

"Good morning, Tanya."

She turned.

"Good morning, peacock."

"You forgot something yesterday."

"What?"

"To see me."

She told him not to be stupid, but she smiled
when she said it. Then they went in opposite directions.

We argued for an hour over that smile.

By the eighth day our songs ceased. No one proposed
that we stop; we simply had no room in us
for singing. Suspicion occupied the place where music had lived.
The shovel scraped more sharply, the kettle purred with sly
contentment, and the oven's eyes glowed as though it knew
how the wager would end.

Why did we not warn Tanya?

We asked ourselves this later, many times. We could have
told her plainly: the soldier boasts that he will conquer
you; you are the stake in a filthy game. Had
we respected her, that is what we would have done.

But we wanted the trial.

We wanted to watch virtue fight temptation. We wanted Tanya
to prove our judgment, justify our worship, and humiliate the
splendid man whose strength mocked our weakness. Her danger excited
us. We spoke of protecting her while withholding the one
fact that might protect her, because knowledge would spoil the
experiment.

Thus affection, when mixed with vanity, may become more cruel
than hatred. Hatred at least sees its enemy. We saw
only our image shining upon her and loved that image
as misers love gold.

On the ninth day the soldier came down, ate one
of our biscuits without asking, and said nothing.

"Well?"

He chewed.

"Well?"

"She is charming."

The kneader seized his apron.

"Have you touched her?"

The soldier looked at the fist on his chest, then
at the kneader.

"Take your hand away."

"Answer."

"I answer questions from men, not jealous cellar rats."

We pulled them apart before blows were struck. The soldier
straightened his waistcoat.

"Five days," he said. "Be patient."

"Braggart!"

"Five days."

That evening we abused him for an hour after he
was gone. Yet nobody defended Tanya by saying that whether
she accepted or rejected him belonged to her. Such a
thought did not occur to us. We had divided the
world into two parties: the soldier and ourselves. Tanya stood
between them like a flag.

On the eleventh day she wore a red ribbon at
her throat.

We stared at it.

"Where did you get that?"

"What business is it of yours?"

"Did he give it to you?"

"Who?"

"You know who."

Her cheeks colored. Perhaps anger colored them; perhaps surprise. We
chose the meaning that frightened us.

"I bought it," she said. "With my own money."

She snatched the biscuits and went away.

"She lied," someone whispered.

"No."

"She blushed."

"Because we insulted her."

"Why should an honest girl blush?"

At this stupidity, no one laughed.

The next morning she did not call us little prisoners.
She merely asked for the biscuits and thanked us. Her
smile was absent. We blamed the soldier for stealing it,
though we had driven it away ourselves.

The twelfth day passed.

The thirteenth.

The cellar became a waiting room outside a courtroom. Every
footstep overhead silenced us. Every laugh in the yard drew
eyes toward the windows. Our work continued by habit, but
our minds climbed the stairs, lurked in passages, followed Tanya,
followed the soldier. At night we dreamed of them.

On the morning of the fourteenth day, rain began before
dawn.

It drummed on the yard doors and ran down the
panes in black threads. Water seeped beneath the threshold, spreading
across the floor until the flour dust became gray paste
beneath our boots. The chimney drew badly. Smoke thickened the
cellar, making our eyes burn.

Tanya came late.

Her face appeared pale behind the wet glass.

"Here," said the man at the bin, passing the packet.

She took it without opening the window fully.

"Thank you."

"Are you ill?"

"No."

"Will you come tomorrow?"

"Why shouldn't I?"

She glanced over her shoulder and disappeared.

The question tormented us. Why had we asked whether she
would come tomorrow? Had we sensed an ending?

The soldier did not visit us. At noon the kneader
went upstairs on the pretext of fetching yeast. He returned
saying the soldier was singing.

"Then he has lost," we decided.

"Or won," someone said.

The kneader struck him across the mouth. The blow was
sudden and heavy. The injured man raised a stool, others
intervened, and for several minutes we shouted and struggled in
the smoke. Then shame separated us. We went back to
work without apology.

Near evening, a boy from the yard came to tell
us that the proprietor wanted a count of the spoiled
flour in the rear storeroom. Two men went. They returned
breathless.

"There's a crack in the storeroom wall," one said.

We stared.

"It looks into the small coal cellar."

"So?"

"The soldier went in there."

"When?"

"Just now."

"With whom?"

He did not answer.

We knew.

The oven seemed suddenly to roar, though no new log
had been thrown inside.

We abandoned the benches.

All twenty-six could not fit into the storeroom, but we
tried. We crowded among sacks and broken crates, stepping on
one another's feet, pressing shoulders against damp stone. The crack
was narrow, where mortar had fallen between two bricks. Through
it one could see a slice of the coal-cellar door
and a patch of darkness beyond.

"Move!"

"I can't see!"

"Quiet!"

"Who saw her enter?"

"I did."

"You lie."

"I saw her skirt."

"Maybe it was someone else."

"It was Tanya."

The words passed backward through us in whispers.

It was Tanya.

It was Tanya.

Our Tanya.

Rain beat the yard above. Somewhere a drain gurgled. From
behind the wall came no clear sound—only once a low
murmur, then a laugh. We could not tell whose laugh
it was. Each man nearest the crack reported to those
behind him, and every report changed as it traveled.

"He kissed her."

"No, he spoke."

"She told him to stop."

"She laughed."

"She is crying."

"No—quiet!"

We waited.

Those minutes were longer than our years in the cellar.
Some prayed she would come out angry, with her ribbon
torn from his hand, after striking his handsome face. Some
hoped she had entered only to rebuke him. Yet beneath
these hopes lay another sensation, hot and shameful: the thrill
of witnessing a secret fall. We hated the soldier, feared
for Tanya, and desired the catastrophe all at once.

At last the door opened.

The soldier came out first.

He glanced around the yard, stretched his arms, and smoothed
one curled mustache. His face wore the lazy satisfaction of
a man rising from a good meal. Then he walked
away beneath the rain, not hurrying, his boots sinking neatly
into the mud.

A little later Tanya appeared.

We strained toward the crack.

She stood upon the threshold while rain whispered around her.
Her scarf had slipped back. Damp curls clung to her
forehead. Her cheeks were bright, her lips parted, and in
her eyes there shone a happiness so open, so defenseless,
that even the darkest among us understood it at once.

She looked upward.

There was no sun, only a low gray sky, but
she smiled at it.

Then she stepped into the yard.

Something broke inside us.

It was not love. Love, if it had been love,
would perhaps have trembled before her happiness, even if that
happiness caused pain. What broke was our pride, our invention,
our claim upon her. The sacred figure we had raised
above the cellar had stepped down of her own will.
She had not asked our permission. She had become a
girl—a living girl with blood, desire, curiosity, folly, and choice.

We felt robbed.

Men who possessed nothing imagined that their last possession had
been stolen. Men whose dignity was trampled daily thirsted to
trample someone. Men who were mocked as beasts discovered a
creature smaller and more exposed than themselves.

We rushed from the storeroom.

The rear door opened into the yard, and we poured
through it—twenty-six flour-white men in filthy aprons, blinking beneath the
rain. Tanya stopped when she saw us.

At first she smiled, thinking perhaps that we had come
for some ordinary reason.

Then she saw our faces.

We surrounded her.

The yard was narrow, fenced by high brick walls. Rainwater
lay in ruts, black with coal dust. Steam rose from
our clothes. Behind us the cellar door gaped like the
entrance to a grave.

"Well," said the kneader.

Only that.

"Well."

Tanya looked from one man to another.

"What is it?"

Someone laughed. It was a thin, hateful sound.

"So the peacock talks like a barrel, does he?"

Her brightness vanished.

"You were watching."

No one answered.

"You were spying on me?"

That accusation should have scattered us. Instead it drew us
tighter together. A crowd is often brave only against one
person.

Words began to fly.

Ugly words. Words we had kept from her for months,
as if storing them in darkness for this hour. We
called her false, shameless, dirty. We reminded her of the
biscuits, the ribbon, the warnings, the kindnesses. Each gift returned
as a debt. Each gentle word was sharpened into a
weapon.

"We treated you like a sister!"

"We believed in you!"

"For him? That strutting fool?"

"You sold yourself cheaply!"

"You deceived us!"

She backed away until her heel entered a puddle. Mud
splashed her skirt. Her face went white.

At first she tried to speak.

"Stop—"

We did not.

"You don't understand—"

We shouted louder.

"There was nothing to understand," one cried. "We saw!"

"What did you see?" she demanded.

This checked us for a heartbeat.

What had we seen? A door, a mustache, a smiling
face beneath rain. But our imagination had supplied the rest,
and wounded imagination does not tolerate questions.

We closed the silence with more abuse.

One man spat near her shoe. Another leaned forward and
hissed a word so vile that even now, remembering it,
we feel the cellar air enter our lungs again. The
kneader accused her of disgracing us.

Us!

As though her body carried our honor. As though the
twenty-six of us had placed our souls inside her and
she had no right to move without spilling them.

Tanya's head sank.

Rain beaded on her lashes. Her hands hung beside her
skirt, trembling. For a moment we tasted victory, and the
taste was bitter even while we swallowed it. We had
brought down the bright thing. We had dragged it to
our level. Now she stood in mud, surrounded, humiliated, small.

Then she lifted her head.

The trembling stopped.

She brushed a wet curl from her cheek and looked
at us—not quickly, not fearfully, but one by one, as
if counting.

Twenty-six.

Her eyes had changed. The affection was gone; the pity
was gone. What remained was clear and cold.

"You miserable prisoners," she said.

We fell silent.

Her voice was not loud. It did not need to
be.

"You give me biscuits and think you bought me. You
smile through a window and think my life belongs in
your hands. You hide in a wall like rats, and
then you judge me?"

Nobody moved.

She looked at the kneader.

"You—rascal."

At the man who had spat:

"You—coward."

Then at all of us:

"Rabble."

The word struck harder than any fist because it named
what we had become.

She stepped forward. We opened a path without deciding to.
Her shoulder brushed one apron, leaving a clean wet mark
through the flour. At the edge of the circle she
turned.

"Little prisoners?" she said. "No. You are prisoners because you
make a prison of everything you touch."

Then she walked away.

Her shoes splashed through the mud. The red ribbon shone
once at her throat. She reached the archway, passed beneath
it, and disappeared into the rain.

No one followed.

We remained in the yard, twenty-six men under the gray
sky. For years we had imagined that if only we
could stand outside together we would feel free. Yet now
the walls seemed higher than those of the cellar, and
the open air gave us no breath.

The soldier watched from an upper window.

He was smiling faintly. Perhaps he believed he had triumphed
over us. Perhaps he had. But we felt no hatred
for him then. He had boasted plainly; he had acted
according to the cheap law by which he lived. We
had claimed a higher law and betrayed it.

The proprietor found us in the yard and screamed that
the dough was spoiling.

We returned below.

What else could we do?

The oven received us with its red mouth. The kettle
still purred. The shovel lay where it had fallen, its
handle smeared with dough. On the tables, the unfinished biscuits
had formed dry skins. We took our places and resumed
the movements.

Knead.

Roll.

Cut.

Boil.

Bake.

Stack.

For several days we expected Tanya to return. We saved
the finest biscuits and wrapped them carefully. At six we
watched the window.

No tapping came.

At every quick footstep in the passage, twenty-six heads lifted.
A skirt would pass, or a bucket scrape the wall,
or one of the embroiderers would call to somebody above.
Then we bent again.

The biscuits hardened in their paper. On the third day
one man ate them. No one reproached him.

We heard that Tanya still worked upstairs. Once a boy
said he had seen her carrying coal. Once the laundress
mentioned that she was leaving at the end of the
month to live with an aunt across the river. We
did not ask whether the soldier continued to see her.

He visited our cellar only once more.

He came to borrow a shovel. His waistcoat was bright,
his boots polished, his mustaches curled. He looked around as
if searching for the audience that had once admired him.

"Well, brothers?" he said.

No one answered.

His smile failed. He took the shovel and went away.

Soon afterward he quarreled with the proprietor and left the
bakery. Another man replaced him in the white shop. The
rolls continued to bake. Soft bread continued to descend in
fragrance through the cracks. The world did not pause for
our shame.

Winter arrived.

Snow pressed against the barred windows. Moisture froze along the
walls. Our hands split and bled, and flour gathered in
the cracks. We worked from darkness before dawn into darkness
after evening. The proprietor still fed us tainted meat. The
oven still watched us with pitiless eyes.

Sometimes someone began a song.

It no longer rose as before. One or two voices
joined, then faltered. The sound struck the ceiling and fell
back upon us. Perhaps we had learned that imagination could
open no door; perhaps we feared any beauty we made,
knowing what our hands might do to it.

We spoke of Tanya less and less.

But silence is not forgetting. There are names that vanish
from the tongue only because they have entered too deeply
into the heart. When morning approached six, we always knew
it. We did not need a clock. The body remembered
the three taps that no longer sounded.

In spring, a narrow beam of sunlight entered through the
upper pane for the first time in months. It crossed
the flour-heavy air and made the dust shine like countless
golden points. The beam touched the ledge where the dead
lilac had once stood. All twenty-six of us saw it,
though none spoke.

It remained only a minute.

Then a cart rolled into the yard, its wheel blocking
the light, and the cellar darkened.

We bent over the dough.

We were twenty-six men beneath the earth, as before.

Yet not as before.

Before, we had believed that the brightest thing among us
was our love for Tanya. Afterward we understood—though understanding is
not the same as repentance, and repentance does not restore
what it destroys—that we had not loved her enough to
let her be human. We had worshipped her because worship
asked less of us than kindness. An idol need not
be heard. An idol need not choose. An idol must
only remain where suffering men have placed it and shine
upon them without turning toward a light of its own.

Tanya had turned.

For that we had punished her.

She never came again. No face appeared at the flour-dimmed
window. No cheerful voice called us prisoners with affection. Morning
followed morning, tray followed tray, year prepared to follow year.

The shovel scraped.

The kettle purred.

The fire trembled in the monster's mouth.

And we, who had once possessed nothing but one small
daily kindness, had destroyed even that with our own hands.

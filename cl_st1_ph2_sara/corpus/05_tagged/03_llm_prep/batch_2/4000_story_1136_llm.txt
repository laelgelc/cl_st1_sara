### MAYMEYS FROM CUBA

You have heard of the Hungry Girl in the Great
City.

Of course you have. She has been through every printing
press from Nineveh to New York, generally in a thin
dress, frequently in the snow, and always within smelling distance
of a restaurant where a millionaire is ordering another bird
under glass. If I told you I had invented her,
some conscientious librarian would produce a Babylonian tablet proving that
a young person named Ishtar once fainted outside a date
market. There is nothing new under the sun—not even starvation,
though the municipal authorities continue to treat each case as
an unexpected novelty.

No, I make no claim to the plot. I offer
merely a new slant upon it.

The girl's name was Jennie MacLaren, and she was Scotch
by birth, American by immigration, and nothing at all by
occupation. That last profession is overcrowded and poorly paid.

As for how she came to be walking along State
Street in Chicago on a bitter afternoon in January with
four cents in her pocket, no breakfast inside her, and
no certainty of supper—must we investigate? Must poverty submit references
before being pitied? Perhaps her employer had failed; perhaps she
had failed her employer; perhaps a man had promised something
and performed something else. You may choose whichever explanation makes
you most comfortable.

The fact was hunger.

Not the literary hunger that permits a heroine to turn
pale, say "I have eaten nothing since yesterday," and then
discuss her childhood for three chapters. Jennie's hunger had passed
beyond biography. It had become a large, active, private animal.
It clawed under her ribs, gnawed upward toward her throat,
and put strange thoughts into her head. It made the
elevated trains sound like frying bacon. It transformed the brass
buttons of a street-car conductor into butter pats. It caused
the red lamps of an automobile to resemble two hot
cherries on a pudding.

Chicago was feeding.

The whole city seemed to be lunching, dining, supping, sampling,
chewing, swallowing, or waiting for the waiter to bring the
check. Restaurant doors opened to let out warm gusts perfumed
with roast beef and coffee. Men in fur collars emerged
picking their teeth. Women stepped from taxicabs with the calm,
reserved expressions of persons who knew that somewhere a table
had been held for them. Even the pigeons looked prosperous.

Jennie stopped before a fruiterer's window.

Now, there are windows which display merchandise, and there are
windows which preach revolution. This was of the latter school.

Behind glass polished to invisibility rose pyramids, cascades, ramparts, and
battlements of fruit. Apples shone with the red respectability of
aldermen. Pears, yellow and swollen, leaned against one another like
dowagers exchanging confidences. Grapes spilled from baskets in purple torrents.
There were oranges with green leaves still attached, lemons bright
enough to light a tenement stair, strawberries in January—January, mind
you!—and peaches lying in cotton nests as though each had
been born to a private room and trained nurse.

There were pineapples, pomegranates, figs, dates, bananas, grapefruit, tangerines, kumquats,
and certain aristocratic vegetables masquerading among them under foreign names.
At the centre stood a silver dish containing six brownish-gold
objects, smooth-skinned, plump, and wholly unknown to Jennie.

A card announced:

**MAYMEYS FROM CUBA
75 CENTS EACH**

Jennie stared.

"Maymeys," she whispered.

She had never seen a maymey. Neither, perhaps, have you.
Do not be ashamed. Civilisation advances so rapidly that it
is impossible for the ordinary citizen to keep up with
all the things he cannot afford.

Seventy-five cents each! Three-quarters of a dollar for one lump
of tropical flesh imported from Cuba, carried by boat and
train, packed in straw, guarded from frost, arranged upon silver,
and illuminated by six electric bulbs—while Jennie had four cents
and an emptiness that hurt her when she breathed.

The wantonness of it!

One maymey would have bought bread, milk, oatmeal, potatoes, and
perhaps a little dripping. One maymey would have quieted the
animal for two days. Yet there they rested, six idle
foreigners, doing nothing useful, waiting for some gentleman whose dinner
required a comic epilogue.

The nearest maymey appeared to swell under Jennie's gaze. Its
skin seemed warm. She imagined cutting it—though she did not
know whether one cut maymeys—imagined juice running over her fingers,
imagined sweetness thick as honey.

Then the fruit moved.

Naturally it did not move. But hunger is a stage
manager with a taste for effects. The maymeys leaned forward.
The oranges winked. A pineapple raised its green crest in
derision. The whole display became a second feast of Tantalus,
except that Tantalus, having connections in mythology, at least secured
permanent publicity.

A clerk inside noticed Jennie and tapped the glass.

"Move along," he mouthed.

She moved.

At the corner she addressed a policeman. He was a
magnificent policeman, six feet of municipal certainty planted in the
snow. His coat was thick, his cheeks were red, and
his stomach described a curve suggesting faith in regular meals.

"Could ye tell me," Jennie began, "where there might be
a place—a charitable place—where a woman could get—"

"West Madison," he said.

"Which way is that?"

He pointed with the majesty of Columbus indicating America.

"Take a car."

"I've only four cents."

"Then walk."

"Is it far?"

The policeman had already turned to settle an argument between
a taxicab and a delivery wagon. Jennie remained beside him
for a moment, but there is no loneliness quite so
complete as that of a person standing next to official
authority which has finished speaking.

She walked.

Or thought she did. Streets crossed, doubled, widened, narrowed, and
changed names with the ingenuity of persons avoiding creditors. A
wind from the lake ran icy fingers through her coat.
The coat had once belonged to a larger woman, and
it hung upon Jennie as though disappointed in her. Her
shoes admitted snow at the toes. One stocking had slipped
down and wrinkled beneath her heel, but she lacked the
energy to repair it.

She asked a man carrying a briefcase where West Madison
lay.

"Search me," he said, and hurried on.

She asked an errand boy whose cap sat upon one
ear.

"Keep goin' till you get there," he advised.

She approached a stout woman coming out of a theatre
ticket office. The woman wore sealskin, a feathered hat, and
a small lorgnette suspended from a chain. Jennie selected her
because people in sealskin ought to be capable of philanthropy.
This is an error, but a popular one.

"Madam, could ye perhaps spare—"

The lorgnette rose. It inspected Jennie from wet shoes to
colourless face.

"My good girl, I never give in the street."

There are people who never give in the street, never
give at the door, never give through the mail, and
never give without having the amount engraved upon a marble
tablet. But they have principles, which are warmer than sealskin
and cost less.

Jennie tried a workman powdered white with lime.

"Ain't got a nickel, sister," he said, showing empty palms.
He looked honestly sorry, and for that reason she thanked
him.

She tried a young man with a carnation in his
buttonhole. He smiled before hearing her.

"Sorry, kid. I'm married."

This struck him as witty. He carried the joke half
a block and chuckled over it twice.

She tried nobody else.

By three o'clock the four cents had acquired a personality.
They lay in her pocket and argued. Two cents wished
to buy a morning newspaper at the wrong hour, one
cent favoured a stick of gum, and the fourth advocated
hoarding. Together they could not purchase a cup of coffee.
Jennie put her hand around them, less from affection than
to make sure they had not deserted.

She passed lunchrooms whose windows advertised beef stew, fifteen cents;
ham and eggs, twenty-five; wheat cakes with syrup, ten; coffee,
five. Five cents! The coffee might have been distilled from
rubies.

A cafeteria displayed baked apples swimming in amber syrup; custard
pies with trembling yellow centres; bowls of rice pudding dusted
with cinnamon; roast pork beneath a crust that crackled in
imagination; brown gravy shining over mountains of mashed potato. A
revolving ventilator pushed the smells into the street. This, I
submit, was assault. Had Jennie struck the restaurant with a
brick, any intelligent jury would have called it self-defence.

But she had no brick.

She came at last to a department store, one of
those enormous commercial temples in which a citizen may buy
a piano, a corset, a fishing rod, a funeral wreath,
and an imported sausage without once seeing daylight.

Warmth rolled through the revolving door.

Jennie entered.

Instantly civilisation received her. Perfume counters breathed upon her; elevators
clanged; cash registers rang; saleswomen in black moved briskly behind
counters; parcels travelled overhead on wires like captive birds. A
floorwalker, tall and waxed and parted down the centre, saw
everything in the store except Jennie.

She paused beside gloves. Gloves at two dollars, four dollars,
six dollars, twelve dollars—the hands of the rich evidently requiring
more protection than the bodies of the poor. Then came
ribbons, laces, silver, china, toys, furniture, lamps. A mechanical bear
beat a drum in the toy department with the fixed
cheerfulness of one who had never missed a meal.

Jennie saw a sign:

**GROCERY DEPARTMENT—BASEMENT**

She followed it.

The basement was no basement. It was a continent of
food.

Here the city's appetite had organised itself into aisles, counters,
showcases, bins, barrels, pyramids, jars, tins, baskets, and refrigerated palaces.
Coffee was ground with a rich, bitter roar. Tea rested
in lacquered boxes. Sugar stood in snowy sacks. Olives floated
green and black behind curved glass. Pickles glimmered in vinegar.
Honey shone like captured sunset.

There were cheeses white, cream, orange, blue-veined, red-rinded, round as
millstones, small as medals, soft as butter, hard as theology,
fragrant as meadows, and odorous as old socks. Sausages hung
in rosy chains. Hams lay broad and pink under frills
of paper. Turkeys, geese, ducks, and chickens appeared in such
numbers that one wondered whether Noah had misunderstood his instructions
and taken them aboard by the thousand.

Fish stared upward from beds of ice—silver fish, blue fish,
red fish, flat fish, fish with long noses and fish
whose faces suggested they had received bad news. Lobsters extended
helpless claws. Oysters dreamed in barrels. Caviar, black and solemn,
occupied tiny jars at prices befitting jewels.

And bread! White loaves, brown loaves, rye loaves, French sticks,
crescent rolls, Parker House rolls, poppy-seed twists, coffee rings, buns
glazed with sugar, cakes tiered and frosted, pies latticed and
golden, doughnuts powdered like comic burglars, and puddings dark with
raisins and social consequence.

Jennie's knees weakened.

A demonstrator stood behind a little table cutting a pale
cheese into cubes. She was a brisk woman with spectacles
and a cap. Before her, toothpicks rose from the samples
like miniature flagstaffs.

"Try our new cream cheese," she called. "Mild, nourishing, economical!"

Economical! The word reached Jennie like a personal invitation.

She approached with what she hoped was the careless air
of a householder possessing a pantry, a cook, and credit.
She took one cube.

The cheese touched her tongue.

For one second there was salt, softness, fat, life. Then
it was gone. Her whole body seemed to rush after
it.

"Very nice," she said faintly.

"Thirty-eight cents the pound," said the demonstrator. "Shall I wrap
some?"

"I'm looking about first."

"Certainly."

Jennie walked away slowly, though every nerve in her commanded
her to seize the plate and run. At the next
aisle a young man in a striped apron offered slivers
of smoked ham.

"Sample, lady?"

Lady! Bless commerce: it distributes titles more freely than food.

Jennie accepted. The ham was no larger than a postage
stamp, but it contained smoke, salt, sweetness, and an unbearable
memory of breakfast tables. She swallowed it too quickly.

"How much?" she asked, for the role required dialogue.

"Forty-two a pound. Special today."

"I'll come back."

"Surest thing you know."

At another counter she sampled a cracker spread with anchovy
paste. Then half an olive. Then a morsel of sausage.
Then a spoon-tip of marmalade offered by a girl in
a white apron who repeated, "Made from the finest Seville
oranges," as mechanically as the bear upstairs beat its drum.

You may suppose these samples relieved Jennie's hunger.

They did not.

Give a starving furnace one shaving of coal and observe
whether it becomes grateful. Each taste opened another door. Her
stomach, which had been protesting dully, now shouted specific demands.
Cheese! Ham! Bread! Butter! It called the roll of nourishment.
Saliva flooded her mouth. Her fingers trembled.

She circled back toward the cream cheese.

The demonstrator recognised her.

"Did you wish a pound?"

"I was wondering—"

"Thirty-eight cents."

"I thought perhaps another taste—"

The woman's mouth tightened.

"One sample to a customer."

There spoke the Constitution of Retail.

Jennie retreated. Two schoolgirls in fur-trimmed coats came up, tasted
three cubes each, giggled, said they hated it, and went
away chewing.

She found herself before a counter of prepared foods. Roast
beef glistened under gravy. Chicken pies lifted round, crisp shoulders
from silver pans. Beans steamed beside bacon. Macaroni lay beneath
browned cheese. Great casseroles breathed warmth. A cook in white
carved slices from a joint, his knife moving with priestly
calm.

"What'll it be?" asked the clerk.

Jennie gazed at the roast.

"Nothing."

"Then don't block the counter."

She moved again.

There is a stage of hunger in which pride becomes
a luxury article. Jennie considered begging outright. She selected a
grey-haired woman purchasing six jars of pâté.

"Excuse me, ma'am—"

The woman turned, and Jennie saw not cruelty but alarm:
the instinctive alarm of the fortunate when misfortune comes close
enough to touch their sleeve.

"Ask the store," she said quickly. "They have arrangements for
such cases."

Such cases! Jennie was a case now. She had ceased
to be a woman somewhere between the fruit window and
the anchovy paste.

She found the floorwalker of the grocery department, a majestic
individual with a red carnation and side whiskers.

"Could ye tell me if there's any work?" she asked.
"I can clean, or serve, or wash dishes. I've had
experience."

"Employment office, eighth floor."

"Would they be open now?"

"Closed at three."

"Could I—might there be something to eat while I wait
till morning?"

The floorwalker looked over her head. Floorwalkers are trained to
see the customer behind the person.

"You'll have to apply to the proper charities."

"Where?"

"I couldn't say."

"Could ye ask someone?"

"Madam, you are obstructing traffic."

A wagon loaded with purchases rolled between them. Caviar, preserved
ginger, biscuits in tins, a ham in a canvas jacket,
and a basket of oranges passed by like a millionaire's
parade.

Jennie turned.

Then she smelled Scotland.

That is not an item ordinarily stocked in department stores,
but there it was: warm oatmeal, browned flour, butter, pepper,
baked meat, and the faint smoky fragrance of a remembered
kitchen. The scent came from a counter decorated with tartan
ribbon and a cardboard Highlander. Above it hung a sign:

**AULD SCOTCH BAKERY
SPECIALTIES FROM THE LAND OF HEATHER**

The land of heather had been reduced to six feet
of counter space between Imported Oils and Fancy Poultry, but
exile cannot afford to be critical.

Jennie approached.

There were bannocks, oatcakes, shortbread stamped with thistles, currant cakes,
Dundee cake, bridies, meat pies, and scones—round, floury, split at
the side, brown on top and still hot from the
oven. Steam escaped when the girl behind the counter opened
a basket.

Jennie stopped breathing.

Scones.

Not the embellished American scone, frosted, perfumed, studded with fruits
unknown to the Covenanters. These were plain scones, honest and
substantial, meant for butter if butter existed and tea whether
it did or not. Her mother had made them on
a black iron girdle. Her mother—

No. We agreed to avoid biography. Yet hunger has a
treacherous memory. It can resurrect a kitchen more perfectly than
love.

Jennie saw a scrubbed table near a small window blurred
by Scottish rain. She saw flour on strong hands. She
heard the slap of dough, the click of a plate,
the kettle beginning to sing. Her own younger voice said
something from very far away. A chair scraped. Someone laughed.

The vision vanished.

The scones remained.

"How much?" Jennie asked.

"Three cents each," said the bakery girl.

Jennie's hand closed around the four pennies.

Three cents. She could buy one.

Salvation ought to be simple, but arithmetic interfered. If she
spent three cents, one remained. One cent could do nothing.
If she saved all four, the four could also do
nothing. Nevertheless, possession has a superstition. Those coins were her
final tie to lawful society. While she had four cents
she was poor; with none she would be destitute. The
distinction is absurd, but so are many distinctions guarded by
policemen.

"I'll take one," she said.

The girl reached for a paper bag.

At that instant a gentleman pushed in front of Jennie.

"Two dozen oatcakes, a pound of shortbread, and six meat
pies," he ordered. "Send 'em to the Lake Shore Drive
address."

The clerk turned to fill the order. Another clerk brought
a fresh tray. Scones, hot and fragrant, stood within Jennie's
reach.

She placed the four cents on the counter.

Nobody saw.

Perhaps she meant to pay. Indeed, she did pay, though
no receipt was issued and commercial jurisprudence might raise objections.
She took one scone.

It burned her fingers deliciously.

She drew it toward her coat.

A hand seized her wrist.

"Here! What are you doing?"

The hand belonged to a woman customer, a tall woman
fitted into purple velvet with enough whalebone to equip a
small maritime museum. She had observed the transaction—or the theft,
according to where one places the emphasis.

"She took that!" cried the woman.

The bakery girl turned.

Jennie tried to speak. "I left—four pennies—"

"Call the floorwalker."

"I paid—there—"

The coins had vanished beneath a paper doily or been
swept aside with crumbs. No one looked.

"Shoplifter!" said a boy joyfully.

The word travelled. A shoplifter is entertainment; a starving woman
is merely depressing. Heads turned. Customers gathered with the promptness
of citizens at a free accident. The striped-apron youth came
from the ham counter. The cheese demonstrator peered over shoulders.
A delivery boy climbed upon a crate.

"Give it here," said the bakery clerk.

Jennie held the scone.

Why? Because it was warm. Because it was Scotland. Because
every window in Chicago had told her no, every counter
had displayed enough food for an army and measured kindness
by the toothpick, every well-fed mouth had supplied directions except
the direction to mercy.

"Give it here!"

The purple woman tightened her grip.

Jennie's sight contracted. First the faces lost their bodies. Then
the faces became pale circles floating in smoke. The lights
swung downward. The tiled floor rose in a slow, polished
wave.

She heard someone say, "Look out!"

There was a closing in upon her—a whirl of hats,
aprons, spectacles, carnations, open mouths.

She fell.

Even then she did not release the scone.

For a moment the crowd was silent. Disaster, when it
refuses to perform noisily, disappoints spectators.

The floorwalker arrived and restored authority.

"Stand back. Give her air."

Everybody stepped half an inch closer.

"She stole a scone," said the purple woman.

"She paid four cents," said the delivery boy.

"You keep out of this," said the bakery clerk.

"Looks sick," observed the ham man.

"Drunk, maybe," suggested somebody.

"Drunk nothing," said the cheese demonstrator, kneeling at last. She
touched Jennie's cheek, then her wrist. "She's fainted."

"What nationality is she?" asked the grey-haired purchaser of pâté,
as though treatment depended upon the tariff schedule.

"Scotch, I'd say," said the bakery girl.

"Then she ought to know better," replied the purple woman.

A house detective appeared. He had the square shoulders and
suspicious eyes of a man who believed innocence should carry
identification.

"Ambulance has been called," he announced. "Anybody know her name?"

No one did.

Jennie stirred. Her fingers crushed the soft centre of the
scone. Crumbs clung to her glove.

The cheese woman bent lower.

"What's your name, dear?"

Jennie's lips moved.

"Speak up."

Her eyes opened, but they did not see the ring
of people. Perhaps they saw the fruit window again: the
silver dish, the electric lights, the six useless miracles brought
a thousand miles to tempt an appetite already dead.

"Maymeys," she whispered.

"What?"

"Maymeys—from Cuba."

The cheese woman frowned in concentration. Then her face brightened
with the satisfaction of one who has solved a puzzle.

"Mamie," she announced. "Her name's Mamie. She says she's from
Cuba."

"Cuba!" exclaimed the purple woman. "But she's so light."

"Plenty of light ones down there," said the ham man,
an authority by instant appointment.

"Maybe her father was American," offered the delivery boy.

The floorwalker took out an order pad and wrote carefully:

**MAMIE—CUBAN—ABOUT TWENTY-FIVE.**

And so the city obtained its facts.

The ambulance men came with a stretcher. They lifted Jennie,
still clutching the stolen, purchased, rescued scone. The crowd divided
reluctantly and closed behind her. Within two minutes the Scotch
counter resumed business. The gentleman from Lake Shore Drive received
his meat pies. The purple woman purchased shortbread. The cheese
demonstrator returned to her samples.

"Try our new cream cheese!" she called. "Mild, nourishing, economical!"

Outside, snow began to fall upon State Street, softening the
signs, whitening the roofs, dusting the fruiterer's awning. Behind the
glass the apples shone, the grapes overflowed, the strawberries glowed
red against green leaves.

And in the centre, under six electric bulbs, lay the
maymeys from Cuba.

Seventy-five cents each.

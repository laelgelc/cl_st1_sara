Phillips had concluded, after an evening of prayerful consideration, that
a man preparing himself for the ministry was not required
to perish merely to spare the feelings of Mrs. Betterson.
He had borne with the smoking stove, the broken sash,
the damp sheets, and a diet which, though differing from
fasting in name, agreed with it in most practical effects.
He had borne them meekly because meekness was becoming in
a theological student; but there were limits beyond which meekness
became suicide, and suicide, he believed, was expressly discouraged by
Scripture. His physician had said something about digestion. His mother
had written that he looked thin. His professor expected diligence.
Duty, health, filial affection, scholarship, and perhaps a merciful providence
therefore united in urging him to remove at once—and secretly.

The secrecy was painful to him. He would have preferred
a frank explanation, provided Mrs. Betterson had been elsewhere while
he made it.

At half past ten, therefore, Phillips stood beneath the rear
window of the boarding-house, ankle-deep in March mud, and threw
pebbles against the glass.

In the room above, Briggs raised his head.

"That's him."

"That," said Blakeley, without looking up from the newspaper, "is
sleet."

"It struck three times."

"Sleet often does. Nature is persistent."

Another pebble hit the window, passed through the triangular absence
in the lower pane, and dropped into Wallace's wash-basin.

Wallace, who was reading anatomy beside the empty stove, glanced
at it.

"The climate is becoming geological."

Briggs went to the window and looked down. "He's there.
Hand me the bag."

Phillips's carpet-bag stood by the bed, a lank and discouraged
article whose contents had not visibly affected its shape. The
room contained four young men, two beds, one table, a
stove incapable of fire, and three chairs, only one of
which could be trusted by a person with serious plans
for the future. Blakeley occupied that chair. He said it
enabled him to rise quickly in case of danger, though
no one had ever seen him rise quickly for any
reason.

Briggs pulled on his coat.

"You're not going down?" Wallace asked.

"I promised."

"You also promised Mrs. Betterson last Tuesday that Phillips had
not been packing."

"He hadn't finished packing."

"A theological distinction," said Blakeley. "Useful to a young man
entering the pulpit."

Briggs lifted the bag. "I don't like it."

"Then carry it by the other handle."

"I mean this whole proceeding. Sneaking a man's baggage out
the back door as if we were robbing the house."

"We are not robbing it," said Blakeley. "There is nothing
in the house worth the moral strain. We are rescuing
property. The case is Phillips versus Betterson, with the carpet-bag
as minor child."

"He owes her a week."

"Then it is unlawful flight, and much more interesting."

Wallace turned a page. "If you stand there discussing it
until morning, Mrs. Betterson will collect another breakfast from him."

The prospect of breakfast ended the debate. Briggs opened the
door. It emitted a long creak, paused as if gathering
breath, and completed its complaint in a higher key.

"Oil it," Blakeley whispered.

"With what?"

"The gravy."

Briggs disappeared into the hall. His high boots struck the
stairs with what seemed to him the delicacy of feathers
and to everyone else the measured tread of a constable.
A minute later the rear door grated; then came a
murmur, the soft collapse of Phillips's bag into the mud,
and Phillips's anxious whisper:

"Did she hear?"

"Go!"

"But my umbrella—"

"Go, I tell you!"

"I hate to leave in this manner, Briggs."

"Then come in and explain."

Phillips went.

Briggs had closed the outer door and was turning from
it when a candle appeared at the end of the
hall. Behind the candle was Mrs. Betterson, in a faded
wrapper and with her gray hair rolled tightly above a
face which appeared severe chiefly because anxiety had drawn it
thin.

"Mr. Briggs!"

He stopped.

"What are you doing down here?"

"Nothing, ma'am."

"Nothing generally keeps upstairs after ten o'clock."

"I thought I heard the door."

"You opened it."

"Yes, ma'am. To see whether I heard it."

She came nearer. "Was that Mr. Phillips?"

Briggs looked at the door, as though Phillips might return
and furnish a less complicated answer.

"Mr. Phillips has gone out."

"With his carpet-bag?"

"He—he desired air."

"Air!"

"For his health."

Mrs. Betterson set the candle on the hall table. "Young
man, don't pettifog with me. Has Mr. Phillips left my
house?"

Briggs had prepared several vague phrases, but none of them
would come past the sight of the candle flame wavering
beside her face.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Without notice?"

"Yes."

"And you helped him?"

"He asked me to hand out his bag."

"To hand out his bag! Is that what they call
it at the seminary?"

"I'm not at the seminary."

"You behave as if you were. Why has he gone?"

Briggs flushed. "You had better ask him."

"I am asking you."

"He didn't like it here."

"Didn't like what?"

The stairs above them gave a faint crack. Blakeley, Wallace,
and the only dependable chair were listening from the landing.

Briggs lowered his voice. "Please, Mrs. Betterson."

"What didn't he like?"

"The room."

"What is the matter with the room?"

"The window's broken."

"Mr. Blakeley broke it."

"Mr. Blakeley says the wind did."

"The wind did it with Mr. Blakeley's boot on."

Above, Blakeley whispered, "Circumstantial evidence."

Mrs. Betterson continued, "What else?"

Briggs's shame, finding retreat impossible, changed direction and became anger.
"The stove smokes."

"You young gentlemen never attend to the draught."

"There isn't any coal to make a draught with."

"I cannot furnish coal to be wasted at midnight."

"And the door won't latch, and two chairs are gone
in the legs, and the roof leaks over Phillips's bed."

"I told him to move the bed."

"Into the leak over mine?"

Her mouth tightened. "Was that all?"

"No," said Briggs.

"What else?"

"The grub."

There was a silence.

"The what?"

"The food, then. He couldn't stand it. None of us
can. The beef is sole-leather and the steak is the
same sole-leather cut crosswise. The molasses tastes like tar boiled
with a boot. The potatoes are lumps outside and water
within. And as for the coffee—"

Mrs. Betterson seized the candle.

"What about the coffee?"

"It ain't coffee. It's burnt-rye slops colored in mourning!"

The words rang up the stairway magnificently. Briggs heard them
after he had uttered them, and wished he could overtake
them before they reached the second floor. Mrs. Betterson's hand
trembled, making the shadows leap across the wall.

"I see," she said. "You have all been very merry
over my table."

"No, ma'am, I—"

"You need not spare me now. Perhaps you have composed
jokes about the carpets too."

"Mrs. Betterson—"

"And the piano, I suppose. And my daughters."

"We never—"

"You have said quite enough, Mr. Briggs."

She went into the front parlor and shut the door.

Briggs remained beside the hall table, staring at the black
ring where the candle had stood. Then he climbed the
stairs.

Blakeley was seated again with the newspaper raised before him.

"Well," he said, "the prosecution rested too soon. You had
not mentioned the mince pie."

"Shut up."

"I merely suggest that if the whole truth is required—"

"Shut up, Blakeley!"

Wallace looked at Briggs's face. "Sit down."

"There ain't a chair."

"Take the bed."

Briggs sat heavily. "I made a brute of myself."

"You made several just observations," said Blakeley.

"I needn't have made 'em to her."

"You were under examination. Boarders' rights include freedom from compulsory
testimony."

"Do they include carrying bags out behind a woman's back?"

Blakeley lowered the paper. "My dear Briggs, remorse is a
luxury. If Mrs. Betterson provided it, she would charge fifty
cents extra."

Wallace closed his book. "Deal gently with the erring, Blakeley.
You may owe her money someday."

"I already do. That is why I deal gently with
her."

From below came the first notes of the piano, thin,
uneven notes which appeared to search the parlor for a
tune and fail to find it. Then a girl began
to sing. Her voice was small and breathless, but determined.

Blakeley grimaced. "Miss Cora's evening assault."

"Thought she had a cold," Briggs muttered.

"She sings through it. Heroism is common in this house."

The song proceeded rapidly, as if anxious to reach the
end before the singer's breath did. Under it another sound
moved, too low at first to distinguish. Wallace raised his
hand. The young men listened.

The piano stumbled on. Beneath its notes a woman was
speaking.

"I can't help it, Cora. I've tried, and I ain't
fit to keep a boarding-house. He said what they all
think."

The piano struck several louder chords.

"Ma, don't cry," said another voice—a weak voice, interrupted by
coughing. "Mr. Briggs was mad. He didn't mean half."

"He meant it. The coffee was scorched this morning. I
knew it. But the good coffee's twenty-eight cents, and Mr.
Perry hasn't paid in six weeks. The butcher won't trust
us after Saturday, and Mr. Wallace has waited already for
what I owe him."

Wallace's expression changed slightly. Blakeley folded the newspaper without a
joke.

The singing grew shriller.

"If I was strong," the weak voice said, "I could
do the cooking."

"You can hardly get downstairs, Nellie."

"I could pare things in bed."

"Pare what? There's nothing to pare. Two boarders gone since
New Year's, and now Mr. Phillips. Mr. Briggs will go
next. Then Mr. Blakeley, though he stays mostly because nobody
else would let his account run. We'll lose the house."

"I'll speak to Mr. Briggs."

"No. You'll do no such thing. I'd sooner go to
the poorhouse than have you beg those tonguey fellows upstairs."

Blakeley's eyes remained on the folded paper.

The piano faltered. Cora began the chorus again, with desperate
brightness.

"Maybe Mr. Seldon will come tomorrow," Nellie said.

"If he comes. Your sister's been expecting him every Sunday
for two months."

"He has prospects."

"Prospects don't pay the butcher. But if Cora married well—if
she only married a man with something regular—"

The singer missed a note and stopped. After a moment
she began once more.

Briggs got up and walked to the window. The sleet
clicked against the glass, sometimes finding the broken corner and
tapping into the basin.

"They shouldn't talk so loud," Blakeley said at last.

"They think the piano covers it," Wallace replied.

"It nearly does. Nothing ought to be expected to survive
that piano."

No one smiled.

Briggs rubbed the mist from the pane with his sleeve.
Phillips was undoubtedly by now established at Mrs. Ducker's, whose
advertisement promised good fires and a generous table. Briggs had
intended to follow on Monday. He had even calculated the
saving: Mrs. Ducker charged twenty-five cents more a week, but
her breakfasts were said to include an egg which had
not previously appeared at dinner in another form.

"I ain't going," he said.

"Where?" asked Blakeley.

"To Ducker's."

"You were not invited to Ducker's. You planned to force
yourself upon her."

"I'm staying here."

Wallace regarded him. "Because the coffee is burnt?"

"Because I told her it was. I can't run away
after that."

"That is not logic," said Blakeley. "It may be Christianity,
but it is not logic."

"I don't care what it is. I'll stay till summer."

The door opened and Perry came in, bringing with him
a gust of cold air and the odor of wet
wool. Perry read law in the office of a justice
of the peace and dressed as nearly like a prosperous
attorney as tight trousers, an enlarged collar, and no money
permitted.

"What's the solemn conclave?" he asked. "Phillips executed?"

"Escaped," said Blakeley. "Briggs assisted and then delivered an address
on domestic economy."

Perry looked at the empty corner. "Gone, has he? Sensible.
I'm off myself."

"You owe six weeks," Wallace said.

"That," Perry replied, removing his gloves finger by finger, "is
the principal obstacle to an honorable withdrawal."

"You heard?" Briggs asked.

"Heard you? The justice heard you at his house two
streets away. 'Burnt-rye slops colored in mourning' is good. I
may send it to the *Local and Literary*."

"I mean downstairs."

Perry listened. The voices had ceased. The piano continued alone,
each note struck with a care that made its cheerfulness
more painful.

"What's happened?"

Blakeley told him briefly.

Perry's legal manner deserted him. "Does she think I can
pay six weeks all in a bunch? If I had
six weeks' board money, I wouldn't be boarding here."

"No one accused you of concealed wealth," said Wallace.

"I can pay two dollars Saturday. Three, maybe, if Harkins
settles for copying those deeds."

"She needs all of it," Briggs said.

"So do I. A man must eat."

"You've been doing that here only in a technical sense,"
Blakeley observed.

Perry paced the room. "I'll stay if my bill can
be arranged."

"Magnificent," said Blakeley. "You offer to continue receiving food on
condition that some benevolent person pay for what you have
already received."

"I didn't say pay it. I said arrange it."

"Law is a noble profession. It gives poverty a vocabulary."

Perry turned on him. "How much do you owe?"

"That is between Mrs. Betterson, myself, and a future more
prosperous than the present."

"Then don't be smart."

"I am never smart. I am merely unable to be
stupid silently."

Wallace stood and took his coat from the peg.

"Where are you going?" Briggs asked.

"Downstairs."

"You heard her. She owes you already."

"Yes."

"And you're going to ask for it now?" Perry said.

Wallace looked at him. "I am studying medicine, not debt
collection."

Blakeley leaned back. The chair protested. "Take care, doctor. A
distressed landlady is more dangerous than a fever patient. Fever
seldom presents an account afterward."

Wallace searched his pocket, counted something in his palm, and
put it back.

"Have you got money?" Briggs asked.

"A little."

"You can't lend her more."

"I can prescribe it."

"For what?"

"Acute want."

"And what will you do?"

"Develop the same complaint."

Wallace opened the door.

Blakeley said, "Wait."

Wallace waited. Blakeley examined his own pockets. From one came
a pencil, from another a button, from a third a
folded notice demanding payment for boots. At last he produced
a silver coin.

"This is reserved for tobacco," he said. "Tobacco being medicinal
in badly ventilated rooms, I surrender it only under protest."

Wallace held out his hand.

Blakeley dropped in the coin. "Tell her it is on
Perry's account. His moral improvement should be encouraged."

"Put it on yours," Perry said.

"No. Mine has passed beyond encouragement."

Briggs gave Wallace a dollar and a few cents. "Don't
say it's from me."

"Why?"

"She'll think I'm sorry."

"You are."

"That ain't the same as wanting her to know it."

Wallace nodded and went downstairs.

They heard his steps descend, firm where Briggs's had been
cautious. The parlor door opened. Cora's song stopped in the
middle of a phrase. There was a murmur, then Mrs.
Betterson's voice:

"No, Mr. Wallace, I couldn't. You've done too much now."

Wallace answered too quietly to be understood.

"I couldn't pay you."

Another murmur.

Then Nellie coughed, and Wallace's voice became professionally distinct. "You
should not be in that room without a fire."

"There's coal enough tomorrow," Mrs. Betterson said.

"There's coal enough tonight."

A little later the coal-scuttle scraped across the hall.

Upstairs, Perry sat on the foot of the bed. "He'll
never get through medical school lending his fees back to
his patients."

"He may," Blakeley said. "He never lends to people who
can afford a doctor."

Briggs stared at the cold stove. "We might bring up
less coal."

"We might," said Blakeley, "but let us not reform with
indecent haste."

The room was silent again. Presently warmth began to move
through the floor from the parlor fire, not enough to
heat anything, but enough to remind them that heat existed.

Perry cleared his throat. "If somebody could loan me four
dollars till Saturday, I could straighten part of that account."

"No," said Blakeley.

"I wasn't asking you."

"I know. I wished to save the others suspense."

Briggs laughed despite himself.

Below, the piano began once more, but softly now. Cora
played the same song; Nellie attempted a line with her
and lost it in a cough. Mrs. Betterson said something
that made them both laugh, and Wallace's dry voice joined
in.

Blakeley reopened his newspaper.

"There," he said. "The household is restored, Phillips is delivered
to better coffee, Briggs has acquired a conscience, Perry has
arranged his debts by creating new ones, and Wallace is
purchasing coal for a woman who owes him money. Providence
conducts its affairs curiously."

"You gave him your last quarter," Briggs said.

Blakeley held the paper higher. "A temporary error."

"You won't have tobacco tomorrow."

"I shall borrow some."

"From Wallace?"

"Certainly not," said Blakeley. "One should never kick a man
from whom one may shortly need to borrow. That is
the first principle of Christian fellowship—and, Perry, I advise you
to put it in your law books."

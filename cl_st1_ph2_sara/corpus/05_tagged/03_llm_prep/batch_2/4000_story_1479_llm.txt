It was in the latter part of December—when the year,
like an aged traveller, has nearly reached his inn, and
begins to shake the snow from his cloak—that I was
favored with a flying visit from my young friend, Eustace
Bright. He arrived at my cottage shortly after noon, in
a sleigh which had jingled all the way from the
railway station, and whose horse stood steaming at the gate,
with icicles depending from his bridle as if he had
brought along a portion of the winter for my especial
entertainment.

Eustace sprang out with the elasticity proper to youth, and
likewise with a green portfolio under his arm. This last
circumstance I observed immediately; for an elderly author acquires, by
long exposure to manuscripts, a perception of them almost as
instinctive as that by which a physician recognizes the approach
of an epidemic.

"My dear sir," cried Eustace, grasping both my hands, "you
have grown younger since I saw you!"

This was so manifestly contrary to fact that I accepted
it as one of those delicate fictions without which society
would become intolerably truthful.

"And you," I replied, "have grown into a celebrated man,
if your countenance may be trusted."

"Not yet," said he; "but I have brought the materials."

He tapped the portfolio, and I confess that my heart
sank a little. Nevertheless, as hospitality requires us to welcome
not only our friends, but their infirmities, projects, opinions, and
unpublished works, I conducted him into the cottage, where a
substantial fire and a less substantial luncheon awaited us. Eustace,
however, had scarcely removed the snow from his boots before
he insisted on surveying my estate.

Now, my landed possessions consisted of somewhat less than six
acres, partly meadow, partly orchard, and partly a rocky declivity
upon which Nature had spent a great deal of stone
with no very profitable result. Yet I had lived upon
them long enough to regard every apple tree as a
tenant, every juniper bush as a hereditary dependent, and a
small brook—which could be crossed without wetting the sole of
one's shoe—as the principal river of the domain. I therefore
wrapped myself in a greatcoat and led Eustace forth with
the gravity of a proprietor exhibiting ancestral lands.

The snow had softened all our humble boundaries. Walls, hollows,
cart-ruts, and the unambitious inequalities of the fields were rounded
into one quiet harmony, as if the earth had fallen
asleep and drawn a white coverlet over its imperfections. Here
and there a weed thrust up its brown head; the
brook muttered beneath a thin crystal ceiling; and the bare
apple branches, touched by sunlight, seemed to bear a momentary
fruitage of silver.

Eustace praised everything. He admired the distant hills, which were
not mine; the road, over which I possessed only the
right of crossing; and a neighboring barn, concerning whose ownership
I had no claim whatever. His enthusiasm, being unencumbered by
legal distinctions, annexed the whole landscape to my property.

At length we climbed the rocky slope behind the orchard
and came to what I called my summer house. In
a more exact inventory it would have been described as
a ruin composed of four posts, several decayed boards, and
a roof through which the rain enjoyed uninterrupted privileges. A
wild vine had climbed one side and stretched its withered
tendrils among the rafters; two benches remained, though neither could
be trusted by a prudent man; and the entrance, framed
by snow-laden bushes, possessed an air of mystery wholly disproportioned
to the edifice.

In summer the place was green, airy, and full of
wavering shadows. Now, with snow upon its broken roof and
pale sunshine entering through every crevice, it looked less like
a structure of timber than the fading recollection of one.
A cathedral, after centuries of ruin, may retain its sanctity;
but this poor summer house, after twenty winters, had attained
something even more difficult—a dream.

Eustace stood within it and gazed through the open framework
toward the meadow.

"This is precisely the place," he said.

"For what? To catch a rheumatism?"

"To tell a story."

I glanced at the portfolio, which he had carried faithfully
through our expedition. "Then the fatal hour has arrived."

"You remember," pursued he, without heeding my forebodings, "the tales
which I related to the children at Tanglewood?"

"I remember both the tales and the children. Indeed, certain
of the latter were more difficult to forget than any
work of literature."

"They have demanded others. Six others!"

Thereupon he opened the portfolio, and displayed six manuscripts, neatly
tied with blue ribbon. Their clean margins and imposing titles
showed that they had not yet undergone the wholesome humiliation
of criticism. I took one into my hand. It was
written in a vigorous, aspiring script, every capital letter seeming
desirous of becoming famous before the sentence had been completed.

"You have been industrious," I observed.

"And successful," said Eustace. "They are the best things I
have done."

An author always believes this of his newest production while
it retains the red heat of composition. Afterwards, when it
has cooled into print, he is occasionally less certain; though
the public, with singular generosity, often relieves him of uncertainty
altogether.

"And what service," I asked, "do you expect from me?"

"The same as before. You must be my editor."

"My dear Eustace, to edit a young writer is to
assume responsibility without authority, blame without profit, and labor without
gratitude."

"I shall be grateful."

"Until I alter an adjective."

"You will not alter one."

"Then my duties will be light indeed."

We returned to the cottage, Eustace discoursing all the way
upon his six narratives, and I carrying the manuscripts with
that respectful apprehension which ought always to attend the transportation
of another man's immortality.

After dinner he established himself before the fire, while I
drew my chair to a shaded lamp and began to
read. Outside, twilight spread a bluish obscurity over the snow;
within, the burning logs fell together from time to time,
sending up a transient palace of sparks. Eustace watched my
face so narrowly that I soon perceived I was reading
under criticism quite as severe as any I could bestow.

The tales were founded, as the former ones had been,
upon ancient legends. In their old shapes these fables contain
much that is beautiful, much that is terrible, and not
a little which an honest man would hesitate to repeat
before the youngest member of his family. Their gods are
too frequently wicked with the additional disadvantage of being immortal;
their heroes shed blood as readily as modern children spill
ink; and sorrow, treachery, vengeance, and unnatural crime hang about
them like poisonous vines upon the fragments of a marble
temple.

When I had finished the first manuscript, I looked over
my spectacles.

"Eustace," said I, "have you never feared that these antique
stories are scarcely fit for children?"

"No," he answered promptly.

The confidence of his monosyllable deserved a longer argument.

"Consider," I continued, "what dark matters you must touch. There
are fathers who devour their offspring, sons who destroy their
fathers, kings whose crowns are paid for in blood, and
ladies who turn their rivals into trees, stones, fountains, or
other articles of landscape. A nursery governed by such principles
would soon attract the attention of the magistrates."

"Those horrors do not belong to the stories," said Eustace.
"They have fastened themselves upon them."

"After two or three thousand years of possession, they might
plead a title."

"They are corruptions," he insisted. "The legends were young once.
They came into the world in the childhood of mankind,
when a cloud might be a chariot, a star a
maiden, and every fountain had a voice. Cruel ages took
them afterward and made them cruel. Learned men buried them
under explanations. Poets darkened them with their own grief. But
tell them to children, and the false parts fall away."

This doctrine appeared to me more pleasing than demonstrable; yet
the firelight was on the young man's face, and gave
to his conviction an authority which colder reason might have
lacked.

"How do the false parts fall away?" I asked.

"Because the storyteller must rise to the level of childhood."

"Rise?"

"Certainly. It is easier to descend into wisdom than to
rise into innocence. When I see Primrose listening with her
hands clasped, or Clover lying in the grass and staring
upward, or little Buttercup holding his breath lest he interrupt
the wonder—why, then I know what the story ought to
have been before the world spoiled it. I do not
alter it. I discover it."

This was said with so delightful a mixture of imagination
and vanity that I had not the heart to separate
them.

I resumed my reading. Eustace had indeed taken considerable liberties
with antiquity. He had opened prison doors which tradition kept
locked, reconciled enemies whom poets had devoted to eternal hatred,
and converted more than one fatal catastrophe into a moral
sunrise. Monsters retained enough deformity to be interesting, but not
enough to disturb a well-regulated sleep. Even the ancient divinities
had undergone a species of baptism, and behaved, upon the
whole, with a propriety which must have surprised them.

Yet I was compelled to acknowledge that the transformations were
attended with very desirable success. The old shapes remained visible,
as the lines of our summer house remained beneath its
snow and vines; but over them had grown a fresh
verdure, innocent and flexible, through which the broken structure looked
more picturesque than when it was whole. Eustace had not
preserved the letter of the myths. Perhaps he had preserved
something more evanescent—their morning light.

"Well?" he demanded, when I laid down the final page.

"You have committed grave offences against scholarship."

"I knew you would like them."

"You have contradicted authorities."

"The children approved."

"You have supplied motives of which no ancient writer appears
to have heard."

"Primrose understood them perfectly."

"You have, in short, made the stories your own."

"Then you will edit them?"

Such is the disadvantage of conversing with youth: it passes
triumphantly over all the qualifications in an elderly man's speech
and alights upon the single affirmative which he had intended
to conceal.

"Yes," I said. "I will constitute myself editor, though posterity
must judge which of us has incurred the greater peril."

Eustace rose and shook my hand with a solemnity suitable
to the foundation of a literary epoch. Having secured my
consent, he became willing to speak of less important matters—namely,
the children themselves.

The tales, he informed me, had been delivered in a
variety of places and under circumstances which no ordinary lecturer
could hope to command. One was told in the pine
woods, where the wind supplied an accompaniment; another beside the
lake, while the images of clouds drifted through the water
like thoughts through a dreamer's mind. A third had been
recited in the orchard, with three apples falling at the
most effective passages. The winter story was reserved for a
palace constructed wholly of snow, its pillars molded by the
older boys, its throne disputed by the younger ones, and
its roof prudently omitted. There Eustace, wrapped in a scarlet
cloak, had addressed an audience seated upon frozen benches until
their enthusiasm, or the temperature, drove them indoors.

"And how are my little acquaintances?" I inquired.

"Growing," said Eustace, with an air of reporting upon a
highly successful enterprise.

Primrose, it appeared, had become almost a young lady, though
she still cherished a doll whose complexion had long since
vanished under the pressure of affection. Clover had gained two
inches, lost one front tooth, and acquired a philosophical contempt
for shoes. Dandelion had survived the measles and wore the
distinction with modesty. Sweet-Fern continued to ask questions so rapidly
that no answer could overtake them. Periwinkle had been presented
with a velvet jacket, in consequence of which he considered
himself qualified to govern the household. As for little Buttercup,
he had lately fallen from a hayloft, escaped without injury,
and derived from the adventure a confidence in Providence which
his elders thought excessive.

I listened to these particulars with more interest than I
am willing to defend upon literary grounds. Children vanish so
quietly into grown people that we often fail to bid
them farewell. One winter they sit with red cheeks around
the fire, believing every wonder that love can invent for
them; another winter they have put aside the marvellous, and
are learning—poor little souls!—to call disbelief wisdom.

"And what is to become of you?" I asked.

Eustace looked into the fire.

"I shall graduate in the summer. Afterward, perhaps, I shall
study law."

"That is a severe punishment for storytelling."

"Or I may write."

"That is a dangerous and seductive business."

"I think," he said, disregarding me, "there are more legends
to be rescued. The old world is full of them.
They are like seeds found in an ancient tomb—dry and
apparently dead, yet capable, if planted in warm earth, of
putting forth leaves."

"Take care," I replied, "that you do not mistake your
own flowers for those of antiquity."

"If they bloom, what matter?"

There spoke the author; and perhaps, in spite of editors,
commentators, and all guardians of the authentic dust, there spoke
also the truth. A tale which cannot endure a new
spring was never wholly alive. We preserve the past not
by sealing it from the air, but by allowing each
innocent imagination to breathe upon it, though something of the
new breath must mingle with the old fragrance.

The hour had grown late. Eustace retired, leaving his six
manuscripts upon my table; and I sat awhile beside them,
listening to the wind around the cottage. The fire had
sunk low. Through the window I could see the meadow
shining beneath the moon, and on the hill the ruined
summer house stood in so unsubstantial a whiteness that it
might have been built by memory and destined to disappear
at dawn.

I thought of Primrose, Clover, Dandelion, Sweet-Fern, Periwinkle, and Buttercup—all
advancing, with that eager haste which children never suspect they
will regret, toward the gates of maturity. Whether I shall
ever again become their chronicler I cannot tell. Other voices
will summon them; other books will open; the world, with
its stern amendments and sorrowful footnotes, will undertake the editing
of their lives.

But may they keep, beneath all later knowledge, some green
and sheltered corner where wonder is still credible; and may
Eustace, too, through fame or failure, law or literature, preserve
enough of his youthful arrogance to believe that an old
story can be made young again. For without that faith,
neither books nor children would flourish long; and even the
wisest of us might find the winter landscape intolerably cold.

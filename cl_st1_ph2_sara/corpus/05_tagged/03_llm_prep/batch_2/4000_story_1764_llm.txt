I

My friend came in while I was correcting the proofs
of an essay in which I had demonstrated, with a
moderation that must have carried conviction even to the theatrical
managers, that the modern stage was at once ruinous to
the purse and injurious to the soul. He took up
one of the long slips, read a few lines, and
then, in seating himself, swept three review books from the
corner of my table.

"You will observe," I said, "that the books you have
just thrown down were sent me for serious consideration."

"They fell very seriously," he answered. "What is this? Another
attack upon the theatre?"

"Not an attack; a protest. I merely ask whether any
rational amusement can be worth two dollars, three hours, and
the spectacle of conduct which, if it occurred in a
private family, would cause the neighbors to send for the
police."

"That depends upon the family. But why do you always
suppose that the theatre costs two dollars?"

"Because a theatre which costs less is usually worse."

He looked at me with the patience one reserves for
those whose errors are organic rather than intellectual.

"Last Thursday," he said, "I amused myself for ten cents."

"I don't say it cannot be done. There are peanuts."

"I went to a dime museum."

"Ah! Then you did not amuse yourself; you investigated."

"I did both, which is more than you can say
of most improving books. You have no idea how respectable
a dime museum can be."

"I have every idea. Respectability is one of the things
it advertises in the largest type."

"You are unjust because you have paid two dollars so
often that you have acquired a financial conception of art.
You think excellence is something which begins after the first
dollar and improves with the orchestra chairs."

"I think there are distinctions."

"So does the ticket seller. He distinguishes between ten cents
and twenty-five, and for the latter sum allows you to
sit nearer the calamity."

I suggested that his democratic enthusiasm would be more persuasive
if it were less evidently enjoyed as a form of
intellectual dissipation.

"You don't think you're making yourself rather offensive?" he asked.

"Not more than you expect."

"Then let me tell you about the museum."

He established himself more deeply in the chair, crushing beneath
him a pamphlet on the spiritual significance of Scandinavian fiction,
and began.

The museum, it appeared, occupied a building whose front was
covered with paintings of natural objects in conditions of unnatural
animation. A sea serpent was swallowing a boat without first
removing the sailors; a lady with four arms was playing
two banjos; an African lion, considerably larger than the continent
from which he came, was disputing a child with its
mother; and a gentleman whose head had been severed from
his body was looking at the separation with an expression
of professional interest.

"These pictures," my friend said, "had the great merit of
preparing you for disappointment, which is the proper office of
all criticism."

Inside, he found the curiosities arranged in a hall smelling
of damp sawdust, oranges, and that peculiar sadness which belongs
to places open in the daytime for amusement. There was
a calf with two heads, each wearing an expression of
having suspected the other; there were coins, shells, weapons, stuffed
birds, and a mermaid whose upper portion showed the decay
of woman and whose lower portion showed the failure of
fish. Beside her sat the discoverer, or proprietor, prepared to
certify to her authenticity at any moment when disbelief became
audible.

In one corner an inventor exhibited a machine for preventing
railway collisions. It consisted chiefly of a bell which rang
violently whenever the inventor touched it.

"He explained," said my friend, "that if every locomotive were
provided with his apparatus, no two trains could approach each
other without the engineers knowing it."

"That seems reasonable."

"So it did until I asked him how the machine
knew. He replied that this was the secret of the
invention. There was another man with a perpetual motor, but
it had stopped for lunch."

"You were in a scientific atmosphere."

"Distinctly. Science was everywhere appealing for small loans."

Beyond the mechanical department he discovered an "Australian Family," seated
upon a platform beneath a painted landscape of palms, volcanoes,
and impossible blue birds. The family consisted of a father,
a mother, a girl of fifteen, and a little boy.
They wore loose garments of red and yellow cloth, and
the father held a spear with the embarrassed care of
a person entrusted with an umbrella not his own. A
placard declared them to be the sole survivors of a
savage island tribe, though the little boy spoke to his
sister with an accent my friend believed to be indigenous
to the eastern part of Brooklyn.

"They were very affecting," he said.

"As ethnology?"

"As tragedy. Nothing occurred. That was the tragedy. The father
stared before him; the mother mended a stocking behind a
painted shield; the girl looked at the door through which
people came and went; and the boy desired candy with
the universality of childhood. Every ten minutes the father rose,
struck the floor with his spear, and gave a cry
which had lost much through repetition. Then they all sat
down again."

"You remained?"

"I could not leave them at once. They were so
much more melancholy than anything at the fashionable theatres, where
misery is upholstered, lighted by electricity, and carried to its
carriage after the last act. Here sorrow sat on a
plank and received no flowers."

I accused him of extracting a refined pleasure from discomfort
which he would have been ashamed to inflict.

"Of course," he said. "That is what cultivated persons mean
by sympathy."

The performance hall was at the end of the museum,
through curtains once crimson and now reconciled to brown. It
held perhaps two hundred chairs, of which twenty were occupied.
A young woman at the piano played a march with
that stern independence of time by which the amateur sometimes
corrects the composer. The curtain rose upon a dramatic sketch
representing the interior of a miner's cabin.

"There was a miner," my friend continued, "who had been
betrayed by his partner, deserted by his wife, dispossessed of
his claim, and deprived of his child, all before we
learned his name. He was a very busy sufferer. His
former partner entered by the window, though the door was
open, and announced that he had come to finish his
work."

"What work?"

"I could not determine. Possibly the sketch. They fought with
knives, chairs, a tin lamp, and portions of the cabin.
Whenever they knocked down the stove, the young woman at
the piano played louder. Finally the child, who had been
hidden in a barrel, emerged and called the villain 'Father.'"

"Then the villain was the father?"

"No; that was explained afterward. She had called him father
because she had been brought up to respect age."

I said that this appeared to me defective construction.

"It was; but the defects were so courageously occupied by
the actors that you could not help admiring them. The
miner died twice—the first time prematurely, because the curtain caught;
but he revived without pettiness and died again when the
mechanism was ready. There was artistic conscience for you. I
have seen eminent actors refuse to die once unless the
notices were favorable."

The audience applauded, and the players, astonished into happiness, came
before the curtain. The little girl bowed with a gravity
that included each of the twenty spectators in a domestic
circle; the villain showed that he had been a harmless
young man all along; and the miner, still breathing heavily
from his second death, placed his hand upon his heart.

"You pitied them," I said.

"I respected them, which is more dangerous. Pity leaves the
social arrangement undisturbed; respect interferes with it."

He next described a dancer whose dress had been designed
for a more prosperous season. She smiled faithfully while the
pianist attacked a Spanish air, and performed with a weary
conscientiousness which made every gesture seem less an invitation to
pleasure than the payment of a debt. Once, in turning,
she caught her heel in a loosened strip of carpet;
but she recovered herself, finished the measure, and bowed as
if the accident had been an ornament privately arranged with
the management.

"Was she good?"

"No. But she wished to be good, and for three
minutes the wish was almost talent. You literary people receive
more consideration for less."

"You speak as if you were not literary."

"I am trying to reform."

Then came the comedian. He was a thin man with
a solemn mouth and one woe-begone eye which seemed to
have survived some private disaster unknown to the other. He
sang a song concerning a landlord, a widow, and a
missing pair of trousers; but the verses mattered little, for
between them he talked to the audience.

"He saw me at once," said my friend, with some
satisfaction.

"There were only twenty of you."

"That enabled him to see me artistically. He asked whether
I had come in to escape the weather. I said
I had. He looked toward the ceiling and said, 'Then
I hope it keeps fine in here.' After that he
consulted me upon every point. When a joke failed, he
asked if I had understood it; when one succeeded, he
said he had known I would. We established a relation.
In a large theatre he might have addressed the public;
in that little room he addressed humanity personally, and humanity
sat in the fourth row wearing my overcoat."

"You make him sound gifted."

"He was gifted. His material was ancient, his clothes were
shiny, and his salary, I dare say, was an insult;
but he had the divine faculty of making expectation pleasant.
Before he spoke, you wished to hear him; after he
spoke, you forgave him; while he waited, you loved him.
That is acting—or authorship."

"Authorship?"

"Certainly. He and you are in the same business."

I denied this with perhaps unnecessary warmth.

"You amuse an audience," he said.

"I hope to interest it."

"He hoped to interest us in the widow's trousers. The
difference is of subject, not of condition. If you cease
to interest, your editor drops you; if he ceases to
amuse, the manager drops him. You both make entrances, preserve
effects, conceal poverty, repeat successes, and bow to applause. You
merely perform in print, where the contortions are less visible."

"Literature addresses the mind."

"And he addressed mine. He made it happier than your
Scandinavian pamphlet has done."

"That is not a fair comparison."

"No comparison is fair which compares literature with anything people
like."

The final exhibition consisted of two contortionists, brothers, who appeared
in green tights and an atmosphere of family disagreement. The
elder bent himself backward until his head emerged between his
knees with an expression of having arrived there by mistake.
The younger tied his legs behind his neck and walked
upon his hands, pausing occasionally to smile upside down upon
the assembly.

"Was that art?" I asked.

"One saw that it was when an accident happened. The
younger brother attempted to pass his body through a hoop
while balanced upon the elder's shoulders, but the hoop caught,
and they descended in sections. Some boys laughed. The elder
sprang up, not angry at the pain, but wounded in
his professional pride. He repeated the feat at once, though
his wrist was bleeding, and when he succeeded he turned
toward the boys with the dignity of a tragedian vindicating
Shakespeare."

"That was courage."

"It was more: it was style. Courage would have borne
the failure; style required its correction. I tell you, the
ten-cent stage is full of artists whom the two-dollar stage
would spoil by giving them time to know it."

II

A week later the same friend persuaded me to go
with him to the circus. I consented because he represented
the circus as an institution essential to the study of
national character; afterward he admitted that he had been given
the tickets.

The immense tent was bright with gas, restless with music,
and filled by that mixed multitude which appears nowhere else
in precisely the same composition: children already intoxicated with anticipation,
parents pretending that they had come for the children, young
men who wished to see the equestriennes, old ladies who
disapproved of the costumes without looking away, and grave citizens
purchasing pink lemonade under the conviction that civic virtue may
properly relax in a tent.

The procession pleased me. Horses passed in silver harness; ladies
stood upon them with a serenity not found in ordinary
transit; elephants moved by with the large, slow tact of
creatures accustomed to making human importance ridiculous; and a small
pony, drawing a gilded chariot, received more applause than the
Roman emperor who occupied it.

"You see," my friend whispered, "the popular taste is not
wholly unsound. It recognizes proportion when the pony is smaller
than the pretension."

Then the performance began in all three rings and upon
two elevated stages.

At the left, a woman rode a white horse and
leaped through paper hoops; in the center, six acrobats built
themselves into a trembling architecture; at the right, a trained
seal played a horn; above them, two men exchanged trapezes
in the roof; while through every open space galloped clowns
whose mouths visibly uttered jokes that the brass band mercifully
prevented us from hearing.

I looked from one marvel to another until admiration became
a form of fatigue.

"This is magnificent," I said.

"It is barbarous," my friend answered.

I reminded him that he had brought me to praise
popular amusement.

"I praise the circus in its impulses and condemn it
in its arrangements. It has the greatest performers in the
world and the worst manners. It shows you three impossible
things at once, so that you cannot properly disbelieve any
of them."

A trapezist swung from the roof, missed a bar, fell
into the net, climbed out, bowed, and limped away while
the audience, having been occupied by the seal's horn, scarcely
knew that death had altered its mind.

"That man," said my friend, "has risked his neck before
four thousand people and enjoyed the privacy of his own
room."

"But the variety is part of the excitement."

"So is a fire in a library. Yet you would
hardly call it the best method of reading. Suppose your
publishers issued three novels on the same page, with a
poem across the top and a theological discussion swinging in
the margin. You would complain that the mind had rights."

"The eye can select."

"The eye selects, but remorse attends the selection. While I
watch the horsewoman, I know I am neglecting the acrobat;
while I watch the acrobat, the trapezist may perish unobserved;
if I attend to the seal, I sacrifice my self-respect.
There is no repose in admiration. The circus turns wonder
into anxiety."

The clowns especially offended him. In his youth, he said,
a clown had been a person who entered the ring
and was heard. He possessed a voice, a history, perhaps
a political opinion. He conversed with the ringmaster, disputed the
ownership of the horse, and made a joke which, whether
good or bad, could be carried home and repeated. The
modern clown rushed silently about the circumference, struck another clown
with an inflated bladder, and disappeared beneath the feet of
commerce.

"They are pantomimists," I suggested.

"They are suppressed citizens. No man can be comic at
a distance of three rings and against a brass band.
Humor needs confidence. That fellow may be saying something which
would reconcile the classes, but we shall never know it."

One clown approached our section, placed his hand upon his
heart, and moved his lips in an elaborate confidence. Not
a syllable reached us. The people laughed because they supposed
the remoter people had heard; the remoter people laughed upon
the same charitable assumption; and thus a perfect success was
created without the intervention of a joke.

"There," my friend said, "is the modern reputation."

I admitted the resemblance to certain literary successes, but begged
him not to press his advantage.

He pressed it.

"You object to calling literature show business because the author
does not wear green tights. But he does morally. Your
novelist twists coincidence behind its neck; your poet leaps through
a hoop of rhyme; your essayist balances a generalization upon
his nose; and your critic—"

"My critic?"

"The critic walks upon his hands and calls the posture
an attitude. All seek the same applause. The only honest
difference is that the circus performer knows when he has
fallen."

I observed that books may elevate, instruct, and console.

"So may a circus. That little girl who has just
seen a woman stand upon a galloping horse will possess
for one day a larger conception of possibility. The boy
who watches the acrobat climb again after falling may learn
fortitude. The father who pays for six seats learns renunciation.
Why must instruction be printed before you acknowledge it?"

"Because thought requires leisure."

"And yet you publish monthly magazines."

This was unanswerable except by a distinction, and I was
arranging one when a troop of riders entered, standing two
upon each horse and carrying flags. The band played; the
audience rose; the elephants trumpeted from behind the curtain; boys
shouted; vendors cried lemonade; and a gentleman in front of
us lost his hat under the seats. For several minutes
civilization was represented by noise alone.

When we came out, the street seemed singularly quiet, though
the elevated trains were passing overhead.

"Well," I said, "you prefer the dime museum?"

"For civilization, yes. It understands concentration. At the museum one
wretched thing happens at a time, and you may give
yourself wholly to it."

"You would put it above the theatre?"

"I would put nothing above anything until I knew what
it was trying to do. The expensive theatre wishes to
improve the rich by showing them elegantly furnished wickedness. The
improving book wishes to improve everybody by preventing him from
enjoying himself. The dime museum promises curiosities and gives you
a stopped perpetual motor, an Australian family from Brooklyn, a
miner who dies twice, and a comedian who knows you
when he sees you. There is at least a rough
equality between the promise and the performance."

"And the continuous variety show?"

"The final expression of democracy. You may enter when you
please, leave when you please, miss the beginning, anticipate the
end, approve one act and despise the next, without being
told that your soul has failed to grasp the unity.
It asks no surrender of personality. It says merely, 'Here
is a man who can turn himself inside out; if
you do not care for him, a lady with pigeons
will follow.'"

"That sounds like a magazine."

"Exactly. The editor understood the variety manager before either admitted
the acquaintance. A serial novel, an essay on municipal drainage,
six poems, a portrait of an actress, advice to young
mothers, and the reminiscences of a statesman—what is that but
three rings under paper covers?"

"And criticism?"

"Criticism must accommodate itself to the bill."

"You mean it must accept the popular taste?"

"I mean it must first discover that the popular taste
exists. You and I have treated the public as a
backward child because it would not sit still for what
bored us also. Perhaps it is not so ignorant as
it is impatient. Perhaps it prefers the dime museum because
its absurdities are brief, its pretensions transparent, and its artists
near enough to bleed where one can see them."

He had stopped beneath a poster announcing, in letters of
heroic size, THE MOST MORAL AND STUPENDOUS ENTERTAINMENT ON EARTH.

"There is your standard," I said. "Moral and stupendous."

"Precisely. Literature has always desired to be both, and usually
succeeds in being neither."

He left me at the corner before I had perfected
my reply. On reaching home, I found the Scandinavian pamphlet
still crushed in the chair where he had sat. I
opened it with a renewed sense of duty, but after
three pages my mind reverted, with an obstinacy I am
unable to defend, to the solemn comedian, the wounded contortionist,
and the Australian child who had desired candy.

I cannot say that my principles were altered. Principles survive
far more damaging experiences than conviction. But when my essay
against the immoral and expensive theatre appeared, I added a
footnote suggesting that the future of criticism might properly include
some consideration of the ten-cent public.

My friend wrote beside it, in the copy he returned
to me:

"Admission to the popular taste, ten cents. Reserved intellectual seats,
two dollars."

``Burns Carroll is a knocker!''

Billie Sheldon spoke the accusation in a low, fierce voice,
but in the quiet corner of the grand stand it
sounded as startling as the crack of a bat. His
face, ordinarily boyish and sun-browned, had gone white around the
mouth. One hand gripped the rail before him; the other
crushed his cap against his knee.

Madge Ellston turned quickly.

On the field below them the Kansas City club was
taking batting practice. The afternoon sun flashed on white uniforms
and burnished the great green diamond. Balls leaped from bats,
curved against the blue, and fell into waiting gloves. Above
the shouts of players and the rumble of the gathering
crowd came the deep, commanding voice of Burns Carroll.

``Cut that plate, Vane! Don't lob 'em! How's a man
to hit if you pitch like a girl?''

A laugh followed. Madge saw the young pitcher flush.

Then Carroll turned, swung two bats together, and walked toward
the plate with the powerful ease which had first made
her watch him. He was a magnificent figure--huge of shoulder,
narrow of hip, splendidly erect. His dark head was uncovered,
his heavy face handsome in a bold, conquering fashion. Even
among trained athletes he seemed a master animal.

Madge had often wondered whether she admired or feared him.
She knew only that when Carroll entered a room she
was conscious of him before she looked his way.

Now she glanced back at Billie.

``A knocker?'' she repeated. ``What do you mean?''

``Just that. He's forever knockin' the other fellows. He runs
'em down to the manager, to the newspapers, to anybody
that'll listen. If a pitcher wins, Burns says he caught
a wonderful game. If a pitcher loses, Burns says he
warned him what not to throw. If I make a
hit, it was an accident. If I strike out, I'm
no good in fast company.''

``Billie!''

The young player's bitterness shocked her more than the charge.
She had known him for two seasons. He was the
club's fleet center fielder, a graceful, eager boy who had
come up from a Missouri Valley league with the reputation
of being a ``comer.'' Last summer his daring catches and
sharp hitting had made him a favorite. This year he
had fallen into a slump. For six weeks his batting
had dwindled; lately even easy flies appeared to trouble him.

Madge had thought him worried. She had not known that
despair had seized him.

``It's true,'' he persisted. ``Carroll's been after me ever since
spring training. Says I can't hit a curve, says I
throw to the wrong base, says I'm yellow on a
close play. He gets you believing it after a while.''

``No one can make you believe such things unless they
are true.''

The words had scarcely left her lips before she regretted
them.

Billie winced as if she had struck him. ``Then you
believe him, too.''

``I didn't say that.''

``You didn't need to. Everybody believes Burns. He's the greatest
catcher in the league. Maybe he's the greatest in the
country outside the big show. When a man can play
ball like that, folks think he must know all about
everybody else.''

On the diamond Carroll drove a ball over the right-field
fence. It sailed high, diminished to a speck, and vanished.
The early spectators cheered. Carroll did not look after it.
He tossed away the bat as though such drives were
beneath his notice.

Madge's heart gave an unwilling leap.

Billie saw her watching.

``There!'' he said. ``That's Burns. He can do everything, and
he knows it. To hear him tell it, he wins
all our games alone.''

``You are jealous.''

For a moment Sheldon stared at her. The anger went
out of him. Something more painful took its place.

``Yes,'' he answered simply. ``I'm jealous.''

The frankness of it confused her.

``Billie, I did not mean--''

``Why shouldn't I be? I love you, Madge. I've loved
you ever since your uncle brought you to the training
camp two years ago. Burns wants you now. I can
see it. He takes what he wants--on the field or
off. And you--you look at him as if--''

``Stop!''

The tell-tale scarlet flooded her cheeks. Her eyes flashed, yet
the indignation she tried to summon would not wholly come.
She was angry because his words had touched a truth
she had refused to name.

Billie rose.

``I got my notice this morning,'' he said.

That banished every other thought.

In baseball a player's ``notice'' was not a compliment, nor
a warning to mend some small fault at leisure. It
meant that, after the period prescribed by contract, his services
would no longer be required. He might be sold, sent
to a smaller league, or cast adrift. To a young
man who had climbed painfully toward the high company of
professional ball, it could mean the ruin of his dearest
hopes.

``Your release! Oh, Billie!''

``Ten days. Pat says he hates to do it, but
the club can't carry a center fielder who hits one-eighty
and drops flies. He's right. I wouldn't carry myself.''

``You must not talk so!''

``Why not? I'm through. Burns says so. Pat's notice proves
it.''

He jammed the cap upon his head and started down
the aisle.

Madge caught his sleeve. ``Where are you going?''

``To pack. I've asked Pat to let me go now.''

``Before the Denver series?''

``What's the use of hanging on?''

She released him, but her brown eyes held his.

``Billie Sheldon, I never thought you were a coward.''

He stiffened.

``I am not.''

``Then don't run away before you are driven. Play out
your ten days. Make them take back the notice.''

A forlorn smile crossed his face. ``That's easy to say.''

``Is it harder than chasing a fly into the fence?
I have seen you do that without flinching. You used
to come to bat as if you owned the pitcher.
What has happened to you?''

``I told you. I found out I wasn't as good
as I thought.''

``Perhaps that is what someone wished you to find out.''

He searched her face, hope struggling against misery.

``Do you believe Burns is a knocker?''

``I don't know what a knocker is,'' she replied. ``But
I know that you are not to quit.''

From the field came Manager Pat Donahue's hail.

``Sheldon! Hey, you young funeral procession! Are ye playin' ball
to-day or attendin' your own wake?''

Billie straightened his shoulders.

``I'm playing,'' he called.

He looked once more at Madge.

``If I could think you cared--''

``I care enough to be ashamed of you if you
quit.''

``Is that all?''

``It is all I shall tell you now.''

His lips tightened. Then, with a sudden lightness that recalled
the old Billie, he vaulted the rail and dropped to
the field.

Madge watched him run toward the clubhouse. Her heart was
beating painfully. She had encouraged him because she could not
bear to see him defeated, but the memory of his
declaration filled her with tender alarm. She liked Billie--more than
she had realized until that moment. With him she was
natural, laughing, companionable. She could scold him and praise him
and forget to guard her words.

Burns Carroll was different. His nearness set something primitive trembling
within her. When his heavy eyes rested upon her, she
felt singled out and subdued. He had paid court to
her in his assured fashion for a month. He sent
flowers, called at her uncle's home, and spoke of their
future as if her surrender were merely delayed. He rarely
asked; he expected.

Was that love? Madge did not know.

``Aha!'' exclaimed a genial voice behind her. ``So this is
where the storm broke.''

Pat Donahue came puffing along the aisle. The manager was
short, broad, and round-faced, with a battered nose and humorous
blue eyes. He looked like an amiable innkeeper; he was,
in reality, one of the shrewdest judges of ballplayers in
the Western circuit.

``What storm?'' Madge asked.

``Young Sheldon. He came tearin' into the clubhouse with his
chin up an' fire in his eye. That's more expression
than I've seen in him since May. What did ye
do--promise to marry him if he hits three hundred?''

``Certainly not! And, Pat, why did you give him his
notice?''

``Because I've got to win ball games, darlin'. Your uncle
pays me for that peculiar purpose.''

``But Billie can play.''

``He could. He hasn't lately.''

``He says Carroll has ruined his confidence.''

Donahue's eyes narrowed. ``Did he say that?''

``He said Burns is a knocker. What does the word
mean?''

The manager sat beside her and pushed his straw hat
back from his forehead.

``Well, now, there's knockers an' knockers. A man's got a
right to say a fellow booted a grounder if he
booted it. That's fact. But the genuine article--the topnotch, copper-riveted
knocker--he's different. He can't praise another man without first takin'
the praise apart. He sees a youngster get two hits
an' says the pitcher gave him grooved balls. He sees
a fielder make a great catch an' says the wind
held it up. If the club wins, it won because
of him. If it loses, some dub crossed his signals.''

``Why does he do it?''

``Sometimes because he's sour. Sometimes because he's jealous. An' sometimes
because it's business--dirty business, but business. He cuts down the
other fellows to make himself look taller. Suppose he's catcher.
He tells the boss the pitchers are boneheads an' only
his coachin' saves 'em. Pretty soon the boss thinks, 'What
a catcher!' The pitchers think, 'Maybe we are boneheads.' Then
they begin pitchin' scared, which proves the knocker's case for
him.''

Madge gazed toward the diamond. Carroll had put on his
mask and protector. Crouched behind the plate, he seemed broader
and more formidable than ever.

``Is Burns such a man?''

Pat rubbed his nose.

``Carroll's a great catcher.''

``That is not an answer.''

``It's the answer that's kept him on this club. He
can hit, throw, block the plate, steady a pitcher, remember
every batter's weakness, an' run a game from behind that
mask like Napoleon run an army. A manager forgives a
lot in a man who can do all that.''

``Then Billie told the truth.''

``I've heard Burns talk. So have the boys.''

``And you permit it?''

``Permit it? Saints above, I've fined him, bawled him out,
threatened him, argued with him, an' once invited him to
step behind the grand stand. He quits for a day.
Then it begins again. The trouble is, when I'm ready
to ship him, he wins a game nobody else could
win.''

Madge considered this gravely.

``Perhaps Billie merely seeks an excuse for failing.''

``Perhaps. A knocker can hurt a man, but he can't
make him miss a ball that's straight over. Sheldon's let
Burns get inside his head. That's Billie's weakness. He's sensitive.
A ballplayer needs ears enough to hear signals and hide
enough not to hear the bleachers.''

``Can his confidence be restored?''

``Confidence is a queer bird. Scare it away an' all
the king's horses may not coax it back. Then a
word, a hit, a lucky bounce--or a girl's bright eyes--an'
there it is, roostin' on his shoulder.''

Madge ignored the last suggestion.

``Will you let Billie play against Denver?''

``I was goin' to bench him.''

``Please don't.''

Pat looked at her sharply. ``For baseball reasons?''

``Of course.''

``Shure,'' he said solemnly. ``An' I wear whiskers to my
knees. Very well. He plays center. But if he drops
another fly, your uncle'll be askin' why sentiment is runnin'
his ball club.''

Before she could reply, Carroll came striding from the field.
He had removed his mask, and his black hair clung
damply to his forehead. His gaze found Madge at once.

``Good afternoon,'' he said. ``You came early to see me
work.''

It was not a question.

``I came to see the team.''

``Same thing, nearly.'' He laughed and leaned his powerful arms
on the rail. ``Denver won't last five innings to-day. I've
got their signs, and old Harlan can't hit anything I
call for.''

Pat's expression changed slightly.

``Harlan's hittin' .342,'' he observed.

``Against fool pitching. He has a weakness.''

``What is it?'' Madge asked.

Carroll smiled. ``A high, fast one inside--if the pitcher has
nerve enough to put it there. Vane hasn't. Maybe I
can make him.''

``Vane looked very effective in practice.''

``He has speed and nothing else. Without a catcher to
think for him, he'd be back in the bushes before
July.''

``And Billie?'' Madge asked deliberately.

``Sheldon? He's finished. Never had much sand. The league found
out he pulls away from a curve.''

Carroll's contempt was casual, complete. Then his face softened as
he looked at her.

``Don't bother your head about ballplayers, Madge. After the game
we'll drive out to the club. I've something to say
to you.''

She knew what he meant, and the warm color came
again. Yet beneath her confusion stirred a small, cold doubt.

``Perhaps I shall have something to say to you,'' she
answered.

Carroll's eyes gleamed with triumph.

The opening game of the Denver series was of unusual
importance. Kansas City stood second in the pennant race, a
game and a half behind Omaha, with Denver crowding close
in third place. To lose might mean a tumble toward
the second division; to win would keep hope alive and
hearten a club that had lately played uncertain ball.

By three o'clock the park was packed. Men filled the
grand stand, boys clung like brown insects to poles beyond
the fence, and parasols made patches of color in the
boxes. The vendors shouted; a brass band blared; thousands of
voices rolled together in an immense, expectant murmur. The ballpark
seemed not a structure of wood and iron but a
living creature drawing breath before combat.

Madge sat beside her uncle in the owner's box. Pat's
batting order placed Sheldon second.

``You persuaded him to use the boy,'' Mr. Ellston said.

``Pat made his own decision.''

Her uncle smiled. ``Then Pat's own decision happens to wear
a white hat with blue roses.''

The Kansas City players took the field. Burns Carroll went
down to second base with his pitcher, Vane, warming the
ball between them. Each throw snapped like a pistol shot.
When Carroll fired to the bag, the ball seemed to
travel on a white string marvelously stretched across the diamond.

Billie trotted to center field. The crowd gave him a
thin scattering of applause, followed by a jeering cry from
the right-field bleachers.

``Don't drop it, Billie!''

He pretended not to hear. Yet Madge saw his shoulders
contract.

Denver's first batter fouled out. The second drove a sharp
single past third. Harlan, the dangerous left-handed slugger, came up.

Carroll signaled. Vane shook his head.

Again Carroll signaled. Again the pitcher refused.

Carroll marched to the box, pulled down his mask, and
spoke close to Vane's face. The crowd laughed and stamped.
Presently he returned to his crouch.

The first ball came high and inside. Harlan jerked backward.

``Ball!'' called the umpire.

The second was in the same place, but lower. Harlan
swung late.

``Strike!''

Carroll called for it again. Vane delivered with all his
speed. Harlan met the ball squarely.

It shot toward center--a low, savage liner falling fast.

Billie started in.

For one instant he hesitated.

The crowd saw it and groaned. That groan might have
stopped him a week before. Now, perhaps, another voice sounded
within him: I shall be ashamed if you quit.

He came on desperately. The ball sank. He dived headlong,
arms extended, and slid over the turf in a cloud
of dust.

For a breath the whole park waited.

Then Sheldon rose, grass-stained and grinning, with the ball in
his glove.

The crowd exploded. Cheers crashed from grand stand to bleachers.
Men rose and beat their hats upon the seats. Madge
found herself standing, clapping, calling his name.

Billie looked toward the box.

Across the distance their eyes could not truly have met,
yet he lifted his cap to her.

Carroll's throw to first doubled the runner, who had gone
halfway to second. The inning was over.

``Carroll made him play shallow,'' Mr. Ellston remarked.

Madge looked down. ``Billie made the catch.''

Kansas City came to bat. Denver's pitcher, Halpin, was a
crafty veteran whose slow curve had humiliated many eager youngsters.
The first batter struck out.

Sheldon walked toward the plate.

There were derisive calls. His slump was common knowledge. Halpin
smiled as he wound up and sent a curve breaking
away.

Billie swung wildly.

``Strike one!''

Carroll, coaching from the bench, shouted, ``Make him pitch, Sheldon!
Don't murder the atmosphere!''

Laughter rippled through the crowd.

Billie stepped out of the box. His face had reddened.
He rubbed dirt upon his hands, looked once toward Madge,
and returned.

Halpin tried another curve. Billie let it pass.

``Ball!''

A fast one crossed the outer edge.

``Strike two!''

The crowd became very still. Madge gripped the railing.

Halpin wasted one high, then another outside. The count was
full. He smiled again, certain that the frightened batter expected
a curve.

The pitch came straight and fast, waist-high.

Billie's bat met it with a crack that split the
afternoon.

The ball bounded between short and third and raced into
left field. Sheldon was around first like a streak. The
fielder, fumbling the pickup, looked toward second. Billie slid beneath
the throw in a storm of dust.

Safe!

The applause was not yet thunder, but it was warm
and surprised. Billie crouched on the bag, slapping dust from
his trousers, alive in every line.

Carroll came up next.

Halpin, wary of his power, pitched outside. Carroll waited until
the count favored him, then drove a tremendous fly toward
right. The fielder backed to the warning track and caught
it. Billie tagged and sped to third.

``Good hittin', Burns!'' cried Pat.

Carroll returned to the bench scowling.

``Wind killed it,'' he said.

The fourth batter, Lacey, lifted a sacrifice fly, and Sheldon
scored standing up.

Kansas City led, one to nothing.

For four innings the game remained a duel. Vane pitched
with speed and courage under Carroll's relentless direction. Halpin used
every trick of the veteran--curves that seemed to stop in
air, sudden fast balls, tantalizing pitches just beyond reach.

In the third Billie lined a single to right, stole
second on Carroll's advice, and died there when two batters
failed.

``He's comin' back,'' Pat said, almost to himself.

In the Denver fourth Harlan drove a ball to the
farthest corner of center. Billie raced with it, back to
the diamond, hearing the roar rise behind him. At the
last moment he turned, leaped, and caught it against the
fence. His body crashed into the boards. He fell.

Madge's cry was lost in the tumult.

Billie lay still. Players ran from every side. Then he
rolled over, sat up, and showed the ball.

Applause became thunder. It beat upon the roof, rolled across
the field, and came back from the outfield walls. The
park roared its forgiveness.

Sheldon rose unsteadily. Carroll reached him first.

From the box Madge saw the catcher seize Billie's shoulder.
For a hopeful instant she imagined him praising the catch.
Later Billie told her what he said.

``You started the wrong way. Lucky the fence stopped you.''

Billie's face tightened. Then he glanced toward Madge and laughed.

``Lucky I've got it, too,'' he answered.

In the fifth inning Denver scored twice. A scratch hit,
an error by the shortstop, and a double down the
line put Kansas City behind. Carroll quarreled with the umpire
over the double, declaring it foul, and was warned back
to his place.

The crowd's temper altered. The living ballpark muttered. Vane lost
control and filled the bases.

Pat hurried out.

``Steady, boy,'' he said. ``Ye've still got somethin' on the
ball.''

``He crossed me twice,'' Carroll complained. ``If he'd pitch what
I call--''

``He'll pitch what ye call if ye call sense. Now
get back there.''

Vane retired the side without further scoring, but he came
to the bench pale and shaken.

``I knew that would happen,'' Carroll said loudly. ``No nerve.''

Madge heard him from the box. She saw Vane sink
lower, his confidence leaking away under the words.

Pat heard, too.

``One more crack like that,'' he told Carroll, ``an' I'll
put Finney behind the bat.''

Carroll stared at him. Finney was a weak-hitting reserve catcher
with a sore arm. The threat was almost absurd.

``You wouldn't dare.''

``Try me.''

Carroll shrugged, but he said no more.

Kansas City tied the score in the sixth. Carroll singled;
Lacey sacrificed him to second; a sharp hit brought him
home. As he crossed the plate, Burns pointed toward the
press box, making certain the reporters had observed him.

Denver regained the lead in the seventh on Harlan's triple
and a long fly. The visiting players shouted from their
bench. Halpin, growing craftier as shadows lengthened, set Kansas City
down without a hit.

The eighth inning began with the score three to two
against the home club.

Their ninth batter rolled an easy grounder. One out.

At the top of the order, Mason fouled twice and
then struck out.

Two out.

Billie Sheldon came from the bench.

The crowd greeted him now with a great swelling cry.
It was not yet faith, perhaps, but it was hunger
for faith. Men who had jeered him two hours before
begged him to save them. Boys danced on the bleacher
benches. Madge leaned forward until her breast pressed the rail.

``Come on, Billie!'' she called.

He could not have heard a single voice in that
sea of sound. Yet he turned toward her, and the
set look upon his face softened.

Halpin threw a slow curve.

Billie did not offer at it.

``Strike!''

A roar of protest greeted the call.

The next pitch was outside. Then came a fast ball,
fouled into the stand. Two strikes.

Halpin walked from the box, wiped his hand on his
trousers, and looked toward center field. His outfielders moved back.

The old pitcher had studied Billie all afternoon. He had
seen him hit the fast ball and wait on the
curve. Now he decided upon his best weapon--a sharp drop
that appeared waist-high and fell suddenly across the knees.

He wound up.

Billie waited.

The ball came floating, white and innocent. Then it dipped.

His bat started down and forward in a smooth, furious
arc.

There was a crash--not the sharp crack of an ordinary
hit, but a deep report that seemed to shake the
air.

The ball went out on a rising line toward left
center. The Denver fielders ran together. For an instant the
white speck appeared to hang above them. Then it sailed
on, over their heads, over the racing shadows, over the
wall.

A home run!

The park exploded.

Sound became a physical thing. It struck Madge, swept through
her, lifted her to her feet. The grand stand shook
beneath stamping thousands. Hats, scorecards, cushions flew upward. The band
began one tune, lost it, and blared another. Men embraced
strangers. Women waved handkerchiefs and cried.

Billie rounded first in long, free strides. At second he
heard the thunder and his face shone. Passing third, he
saw his teammates massed around the plate.

He crossed with the tying run.

Hands pounded his back. Pat Donahue hugged him. Even the
bat boy leaped upon him. Only Carroll stood apart.

When the noise subsided enough to permit play, Carroll strode
to the plate.

He hit Halpin's first pitch against the right-field wall for
a double.

``That boy's drive rattled the pitcher,'' Mr. Ellston said.

Madge smiled. ``Then Billie helped Burns.''

Lacey followed with a single, scoring Carroll, and Kansas City
led four to three.

The Denver ninth opened beneath a sky turning gold beyond
the grand stand. Every spectator remained. No one thought of
supper, streetcars, or the work of to-morrow. The vast crowd
had become one tense creature with forty thousand eyes fixed
upon the diamond.

Vane walked the first batter on four pitches.

Carroll charged out, furious. ``Put 'em over!''

The next hitter bunted. Vane fielded the ball, wheeled toward
second, and hesitated. Too late! He threw to first, but
the runner beat it.

Two men on, none out.

Pat started from the bench. Carroll waved him back.

``I've got him!'' the catcher shouted.

He spoke to Vane for nearly a minute. Whatever he
said, it steadied the youngster. The next batter attempted to
sacrifice. Vane threw a high, hard pitch. The bunt popped
foul, and Carroll caught it near the Denver bench.

One out.

Harlan was next.

A murmur traveled through the park. The Denver slugger had
doubled and tripled already. One long hit would win the
game.

Carroll crouched and gave his sign.

The first pitch came inside. Harlan stepped away.

The second cut the corner for a strike.

On the third the runners broke--a double steal.

Carroll came out of his crouch like a released spring.
He threw to third. The ball shot across the diamond
like a bullet, but the runner slid beneath the tag.

Safe!

Men now stood on second and third, with only one
out.

Madge could scarcely breathe. A fly would tie the score.
A hit would send Denver ahead. Even a wild pitch
might destroy the afternoon's miracle.

Pat ordered the infield drawn close. Carroll looked toward him
and shook his head. Then he called time and ran
to the bench.

``Put Mason on the bag,'' he said. ``Sheldon shallow. Harlan'll
hit outside pitching to center.''

``An' if he pulls it?''

``He won't.''

Pat studied him. This was ``inside-ball''--not the mere catching and
throwing visible to every spectator, but the hidden game of
judgment, memory, signals, and calculated risk. Carroll knew Harlan's habits.
He was staking the game upon that knowledge.

``Do it,'' Pat decided.

The fielders shifted.

Carroll returned and crouched far outside. Vane pitched.

Harlan swung.

The ball leaped from the bat--a low line drive directly
over second!

Mason, playing close to the bag, sprang sideways. For a
fraction of a second the ball, his glove, and the
running figures seemed suspended against the green.

Mason caught it!

The runner from second, racing on the hit, was nearly
at third. The runner on third had started for home.

``Back! Back!'' shrieked the Denver coach.

Mason stepped on second base.

Two out!

The runner from third wheeled frantically toward the bag he
had left. Mason's throw came to Carroll, who had raced
up the line. Carroll caught it, spun, and fired to
third.

The ball flashed through the sunset.

Lacey received it with his foot on the base an
instant before the runner's desperate slide.

Out!

For one heartbeat there was silence, vast and unbelieving.

Then the umpire flung up his arm.

``Three out!''

A triple play!

The game was over.

The ballpark went mad. The roar surpassed all earlier sound,
mounting and mounting until it seemed the wooden walls must
burst. Carroll stood between home and third, mask thrown off,
chest heaving, while his teammates rushed upon him. He had
directed the shift, covered the throw, and completed the swift
chain of judgment that produced the rarest play in baseball.

Madge stared at him, dazzled.

He seemed then all that strength and courage could make
a man. Dust powdered his uniform; the lowering sun blazed
behind his dark head; players clung about his shoulders. He
looked not merely a catcher but a conqueror.

The doubt within Madge faltered. Billie had played gloriously, but
Carroll had saved the game with genius. Was it fair
to judge so great a man by sharp words spoken
in the heat of play? Perhaps power must be harsh.
Perhaps Burns saw weaknesses because his own standard was so
high.

As the players left the field, Billie paused before the
box.

``Was that good enough?'' he called up.

His face, alight with happiness, touched her deeply.

``It was splendid!'' she answered.

Then Carroll came behind him. At once the air seemed
charged by his presence.

``Wait for me,'' he said to Madge. ``I'll dress in
ten minutes.''

Billie's happiness dimmed. He glanced from Carroll to her.

``Good-by, Madge,'' he said.

``Not good-by. I shall see you to-morrow.''

``Maybe.''

He walked away before she could answer.

That evening Carroll called at the Ellston house. Madge received
him in the long, cool parlor, where the windows stood
open to the summer night. The lamps had not yet
been lit. Fading daylight softened the room, and the curtains
stirred with the scent of the garden.

Carroll wore a gray suit that emphasized his breadth of
shoulder. The strain of the game had left no mark
upon him. He seemed certain, triumphant, almost dangerously handsome.

``You saw a little baseball to-day,'' he said.

``I saw a great deal.''

``That triple play will make the front pages. I told
Mason where to stand. Nobody else in the league would
have thought of it.''

``Mason made a wonderful catch.''

Carroll laughed. ``It hit his glove. He couldn't help catching
it.''

Madge looked at him closely.

``But he had to be in the right place.''

``I put him there.''

``And his throw to you was perfect.''

``A child could have made it. All I had to
do was finish the play.''

The small, cold doubt returned.

Madge seated herself near the window. ``Vane pitched well after
the bases filled.''

``Vane nearly threw away the game. He's got the heart
of a rabbit. If I hadn't steadied him, Harlan would
have knocked the ball into the river.''

``Yet Vane threw the pitch you wanted.''

``Naturally. I made him.''

``What did you think of Lacey's hit in the eighth?''

``A scratch. Halpin was rattled and laid it over.''

``It scored the winning run.''

``Because I was on second. I read the play and
got a jump.''

She folded her hands, hiding their tremor.

``And Billie's home run?''

Carroll's expression hardened.

``Luck.''

``Luck! It went over the deepest part of the field.''

``Halpin grooved one. Any man in the club could have
hit it.''

``They did not.''

``Because Sheldon happened to be up.''

``He had two other hits.''

``One through the infield and one blooper. Don't let a
good afternoon fool you. He's still a minor leaguer.''

Madge's eyes flashed, but she held her voice calm.

``His catches?''

``Bad judgment. He started late on both. Spectacular fielders usually
do. A real outfielder makes hard catches look easy.''

``Burns, have you ever seen another ballplayer do anything well?''

He stared, surprised.

``Of course.''

``Tell me who.''

``Well--'' He frowned. ``Morrow at Omaha can pitch when he
isn't loafing. Reeves is a fair hitter against left-handers. Our
Lacey has a good arm, though he doesn't know where
to throw. Pat used to be a smart manager before
he got soft.''

Each compliment carried its barb. Each praise diminished the man
praised.

The pattern was suddenly plain. Madge remembered Vane's flushed face
in practice, Billie's contracted shoulders, Carroll's certainty that every success
belonged to him and every failure to another. She understood
what Pat had tried to explain. The knocker did more
than speak ill. He stole the courage by which other
men did their work. He clouded victories, sharpened defeats, and
made comrades ashamed of trusting themselves.

And Carroll was proud of it.

``You are severe,'' she said.

``I know players. That's why I'm valuable.''

``Do the men like you?''

He shrugged. ``What difference does that make? They're paid to
play. I don't ask them to love me.''

``A team must trust its catcher.''

``They trust me because I'm the best.''

``They may trust your arm and distrust your tongue.''

His heavy brows drew together. ``You've been talking to Sheldon.''

``I have been listening to you.''

He stepped nearer.

``Madge, I didn't come here to discuss a lot of
bushers. I'm through with this league after the season. Cincinnati
wants me; so does New York. I'll be in the
big show next spring, drawing twice what your uncle pays
me. I want you to marry me.''

His hand closed over hers.

The power of him swept upon her once more--the bold
face, the compelling eyes, the physical certainty that had so
often confused her. For one moment her will seemed to
bend beneath it.

Then she remembered Billie rising from the grass with the
caught ball in his glove. She remembered his face after
the home run--not proud of himself alone, but glad for
the club, for Pat, for the crowd, for her. Carroll's
attraction demanded surrender. Billie's affection had answered faith with growth.

She drew her hand away.

``No, Burns.''

He smiled as if she had misunderstood the question. ``You
needn't answer to-night.''

``My answer will be the same to-morrow.''

``You care for Sheldon?''

``Yes.''

The word surprised her by its ease. Once spoken, it
opened a door. Light and freedom entered.

Carroll's face darkened.

``That quitter! He'll be out of the league in ten
days.''

``Perhaps. But he will not quit.''

``He told you I was a knocker, I suppose.''

``He did. I did not believe him. So I asked
you about the men who helped win to-day. You could
not praise one of them without belittling him.''

``I told the truth.''

``No. You told only the part of truth that made
you seem greater. A great player should make other men
confident. You make them doubt themselves.''

``Sentimental nonsense! Ball is business.''

``It is also a team game.''

He laughed harshly. ``You sound like Pat.''

``I am glad.''

Carroll seized his hat. ``When Sheldon goes back to the
bushes, perhaps you'll learn the difference between a man and
a boy.''

``I think I have learned it to-night.''

For an instant anger made him ugly. Then pride restored
his composure.

``Good evening,'' he said.

``Good evening, Burns.''

He left without looking back.

Madge remained by the window, trembling now that the struggle
was done. There was pain in her, for an enchantment
had broken, and even a false dream leaves an ache
when it dies. Yet beneath the pain flowed a happiness
clean and warm.

She wanted to tell Billie.

At ten o'clock she telephoned the hotel where many of
the players lived. Billie was not there. He had gone
out alone after supper.

At midnight he had not returned.

In the morning Madge reached the ballpark before batting practice.
She found Pat in his office, surrounded by score sheets,
contracts, telegrams, and clouds of cigar smoke.

``Where is Billie?'' she demanded.

Pat blinked. ``Good mornin' to you, too.''

``Has he reported?''

``Not yet.''

Her heart sank. ``Then he has quit.''

``It appears he may have celebrated his resurrection.''

``Billie does not drink.''

``All ballplayers don't become drunk merely because they're late. Some
oversleep with perfect sobriety.''

A knock sounded, and Sheldon entered.

His uniform was on, but his hair was wet and
uncombed. He looked tired, grave, and determined.

``Sorry, Pat. I was at the station.''

``Station? Planning to desert before I can shoot ye?''

Billie laid an envelope on the desk.

``I came to ask for my immediate release again.''

Madge gave a little cry.

He turned and saw her. All the resolution in his
face wavered.

``Why?'' she asked.

``Because I can't stay.''

``After yesterday?''

``Yesterday doesn't change things.''

``It changes your batting average,'' Pat remarked. ``Which is the
only moral transformation I'm officially paid to notice.''

Billie ignored him.

``I saw you leave with Carroll,'' he said to Madge.
``I know what he meant to ask. I figured you'd
say yes. I can't play on the same club and
watch--''

``You figured!'' Her eyes began to shine dangerously. ``Did it
occur to you to ask me?''

``I didn't have the right.''

``Yet you had the right to decide my answer for
me?''

``Madge--''

``You talk of Carroll destroying your confidence, but you are
eager to do it yourself! Yesterday you fought for every
ball because I told you not to quit. Then, at
the first shadow, you pack your bag.''

Pat pushed back his chair.

``I'll just step outside,'' he said. ``The tactical situation appears
beyond a manager's duties.''

``Stay where you are, Pat,'' Madge ordered. ``This concerns the
club.''

``Shure. All pennant business.''

She faced Billie. Her cheeks were scarlet, but she no
longer cared.

``I refused Burns last night.''

Billie's lips parted.

``You--refused him?''

``Yes.''

``Why?''

``Because you were right. He is a knocker. And because--because
I discovered I care for someone else.''

Hope came into Billie's eyes so swiftly that it transformed
him.

``Who?'' he whispered.

Pat groaned. ``For the love of Mike, Sheldon, must she
diagram the play?''

Madge laughed, half angry and half near tears.

``A foolish center fielder,'' she said, ``who can catch impossible
flies but cannot recognize a very easy answer.''

Billie crossed the room in two strides.

``Madge!''

He caught her hands. For a moment they forgot the
manager, the club, the waiting game, and everything except the
astonishing happiness in each other's faces.

Pat coughed.

They sprang apart.

``Now that the batteries are agreed,'' he said, ``we'll transact
business. Sheldon, I withdraw your notice.''

Billie stared. ``You do?''

``Your release. It's canceled. Torn up. Dead an' buried. Any
man who hits Halpin over that fence, steals a base,
an' saves two runs in center is worth another chance.''

``But one game--''

``One game doesn't make a season. Neither does one slump.
You'll play until ye prove yesterday was luck or until
ye prove it wasn't. An' if I hear ye've quit
again without my permission, I'll fine ye fifty dollars for
desertion an' another fifty for bein' an idiot.''

Billie grinned. ``I haven't got a hundred dollars.''

``Then ye'll have to keep your courage.''

Madge squeezed Billie's hand.

``He will.''

Pat picked up a telegram from his desk.

``There's one more item. Carroll won't be with us after
to-day.''

Their happiness quieted.

``You are releasing him?'' Madge asked.

``Trade. Milwaukee offered me a catcher, an outfielder, an' cash.
I wired acceptance this morning.''

Billie looked incredulous. ``But Burns won yesterday's game.''

``He helped win it,'' Pat corrected. ``So did eight other
men. Carroll's the greatest catcher I ever managed. Maybe he'll
go to the big leagues, an' maybe he'll be a
star there. But this club has been walkin' on eggshells
all season. Vane's scared to shake off a sign. Mason
apologizes when he gets a hit. You forgot how to
catch a ball. A team can't win if every man
expects his own catcher to sneer at him.''

``Will Milwaukee take him if they know?''

``Their manager thinks he can cure Burns. Managers are an
optimistic race.''

At that moment the office door opened without ceremony.

Carroll entered.

He glanced at Billie and Madge, saw their joined hands,
and understood. His face became cold.

``What's this about Milwaukee?''

Pat offered him the telegram.

Carroll read it. The paper crackled in his fist.

``You traded me?''

``I did.''

``For Finney's cousin and a broken-down outfielder!''

``Also cash,'' said Pat. ``Don't overlook cash.''

``You're crazy. Without me this club will drop into fourth
place.''

``Perhaps.''

``Vane can't pitch without me.''

``He'll learn.''

``Mason's a fool. Lacey's a loafer. Sheldon had one lucky
day. Your whole infield is minor-league stuff.''

Pat's genial face hardened.

``There ye are, Burns. Ye can't even be traded without
knockin' the men ye leave behind.''

``I tell the truth.''

``Then find some truth that builds a man once in
a while. Ye know more baseball than any catcher in
this league, but ye've never learned the game. Nine men
play it, Burns. Nine--not one great man an' eight conveniences.''

Carroll laughed contemptuously. ``You'll beg to get me back.''

``If I do, ye may enjoy refusin'. Report to Milwaukee
Monday.''

For a moment Carroll seemed ready to strike him. Then
his pride rose like armor. He tossed the telegram upon
the desk.

``Good luck with your kindergarten,'' he said.

He turned to Madge.

``And good luck with your minor leaguer.''

Billie stepped forward, but Madge held him back.

``Thank you, Burns,'' she said quietly. ``Good luck to you,
too. I hope someday you find teammates whose greatness you
can see.''

Carroll's eyes met hers. Perhaps, for one brief instant, something
like regret broke through his vanity. But he lifted his
head, smiled, and went out.

Through the open window came the sounds of the players
gathering on the field--the smack of balls in gloves, the
rasp of spikes, the joking cries. Presently Vane's young voice
rose above them.

``Come on, boys! Let's take another from Denver!''

Another player answered. Then another. Laughter followed--free, rough, friendly laughter.

Pat listened and nodded.

``Hear that?'' he said. ``That's a ball club breathin' again.''

Billie picked up his cap.

``I'd better get out there.''

``Ye had,'' said Pat. ``Love may improve battin', but it
doesn't excuse a man from practice.''

At the door Billie hesitated and looked back at Madge.
There was a question in his eyes, no longer despairing
but boyishly eager.

She crossed to him.

``Play ball,'' she whispered. ``I'll be watching.''

He bent quickly and kissed her. Then he ran down
the passage toward the sunlight.

A moment later Madge saw him burst from beneath the
grand stand and race across the field. His step was
light; his head was high. The players hailed him. Vane
tossed a ball far into center, and Billie turned, sped
after it, and gathered it over his shoulder with the
old effortless grace.

The crowd had not yet entered, but the empty park
seemed to awaken. Sunlight lay broad upon the diamond. Flags
stirred above the roof. Somewhere beyond the fence a newsboy
cried the story of yesterday's victory and the miracle triple
play.

Madge stood at the rail, her face warm, her heart
full.

She knew that Billie would have other hitless days. He
would misjudge flies, strike out with men on base, and
hear the bleachers jeer again. Confidence was not magic, nor
love a guarantee against failure. But he had learned that
courage could return when summoned, and that honest faith asked
effort rather than perfection.

Burns Carroll, too, would play other brilliant games. His throws
would travel like bullets; his bat would terrify pitchers; crowds
would roar his name. Yet until he learned generosity, every
victory would leave something broken behind it.

Pat came to stand beside Madge.

``There goes your center fielder,'' he said.

``Yes.''

``Think he'll hit to-day?''

She watched Billie make another running catch, whirl, and throw
to the plate. The ball crossed the field on a
straight white line and struck the catcher's glove breast-high.

``I don't know,'' she answered. ``But he will try.''

Pat smiled.

``That's enough to start with.''

Below them Billie looked up, waved his cap, and shouted
something the distance carried away. Madge could not hear the
words, but she understood.

She waved in return.

Then the gates opened, the first spectators poured into the
stands, and the ballpark drew its mighty breath for another
game.

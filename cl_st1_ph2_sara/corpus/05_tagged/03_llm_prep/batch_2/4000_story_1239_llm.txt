Edward Glynn died on a Thursday morning, between the hour
at which the breakfast tray was carried upstairs and that
at which the postman passed the east gate. His death
was sudden, though not so sudden as to prevent Dr.
Henry Glynn, his younger brother, from pronouncing it the result
of a gastric seizure; nor was it unexpected in the
sense that Edward had been well. For several days he
had complained of a burning pain, a heaviness after food,
and an oppression which he described, with his accustomed asperity,
as being "like a hand laid upon the heart."

Still, nobody had supposed that he would die.

At half past nine he had been heard speaking loudly
in his chamber. At ten Caroline, the eldest of the
three Glynn sisters, entered with a cup of broth and
found him lying upon the pillows with his eyes half
open and his face already wearing that extraordinary look of
completed withdrawal which death bestows upon features familiar enough to
have seemed incapable of secrecy.

Henry stood at the foot of the bed.

"He is gone," he said.

Caroline did not scream. She set the cup upon the
little table, where it clicked once against the saucer, and
looked first at Edward, then at Henry.

"You were with him?"

"I came when he called."

"What did he say?"

"Nothing intelligible."

Henry spoke gently. His voice had always been one of
his charms, even when he was a boy—a low voice,
cultivated and reassuring, with a softness which sometimes appeared to
proceed from kindness and sometimes from disdain. He was a
handsome man of forty-six, lean and finely fashioned, with an
aquiline nose, a broad, pale forehead, and dark eyes set
rather deeply beneath level brows. There was austerity in his
face, but no coarseness; indeed, the discrepancy between his appearance
and his temper had often caused strangers to misjudge him.
He looked capable of noble endurance, whereas he was capable
chiefly of violent resentment.

Caroline remained beside the door. She was tall, thin, and
nearly colourless, with a rigid carriage and a narrow mouth
which seemed made less for speech than for the compression
of speech. She had ruled the household since their mother's
death, and the long exercise of authority had given to
her very silences the force of commands.

"What did you give him?" she asked.

Henry's eyes moved quickly to hers.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You said last night that you would give him something."

"I gave him what his condition required."

"What?"

"A draught."

There was a slight pause.

"Where is the glass?" said Caroline.

Henry smiled.

The smile altered him in an unpleasant fashion, not because
it was cruel, but because it was boyish. It suggested
that he regarded the question as a simplicity which he
might forgive.

"Really, Caroline," he said, "this is hardly the time for
an examination."

She looked again at Edward. One involuntarily followed her glance
to the dead man's hands, which lay outside the coverlet.
They were powerful hands, somewhat swollen at the knuckles, and
one of them was clenched as though upon a final
objection.

"No," she replied. "It is not the time."

Then she went downstairs to tell her sisters.

Rebecca received the news with a convulsive gasp and covered
her face with her handkerchief. She was the youngest, though
only by two years, and possessed that frail, faded prettiness
which sometimes survives middle age because it has never had
sufficient vigour to mature into beauty. Her eyes were light
and prominent; her gestures uncertain; her whole person seemed continually
on the point of retreating before some threatened unpleasantness. She
had feared Edward, feared Henry, and feared Caroline, though in
different degrees and for different reasons.

Emma, who sat by the window sewing a black braid
upon a grey gown, did not immediately comprehend.

"Gone where?" she asked.

"Edward is dead," said Caroline.

Emma's large white hands ceased their work. In her youth
she had been considered the beauty of the family, and
even now, at fifty, she preserved a full-blown magnificence, a
superb bulk of femininity, which mourning could diminish but not
subdue. Her smooth cheeks retained a faint bloom; her pale
hair was arranged with elaborate care; her black silk dresses
rustled with a soft authority. She looked at Caroline with
a majestic vacancy which slowly gave place to horror.

"Dead?"

"Yes."

"But Henry said—Henry said it was nothing dangerous."

"Henry was mistaken."

Rebecca dropped her handkerchief from her face.

"Was he?" she whispered.

Caroline turned sharply.

"What do you mean?"

"Nothing."

"Then do not say it."

"I only meant that Henry is seldom mistaken."

"He is a physician, not Providence."

Emma rose. Her work slid to the floor.

"Did Edward suffer?"

"I do not know."

"Was Henry there?"

"Yes."

At this, all three women looked at one another.

It was a brief look, and no spectator unacquainted with
the family could have read it; yet their common blood,
which ordinarily revealed itself only in the formation of the
nose and the peculiar grey of the eyes, became suddenly
and terribly apparent. It was as if one thought had
looked from three faces and recognized itself. Caroline's mouth hardened.
Rebecca shrank back. Emma's full chin trembled.

Then Caroline stooped, picked up the fallen sewing, and laid
it carefully on the chair.

"We must send for Mr. Baines," she said. "And the
undertaker."

Thus propriety resumed its dominion.

The Glynn house stood a little apart from the village,
behind elms which had been planted by their grandfather and
had outlived every member of his generation. It was a
square, substantial dwelling of grey brick, with a white portico,
long windows, and a shallow roof whose gutters required attention
every autumn. Nothing about it invited legend. The rooms were
large but not grand; the furniture was of excellent quality
and somewhat too abundant; the carpets had faded honourably; family
portraits, most of them bad, occupied the spaces which a
more fashionable household would have given to landscapes.

The study lay across the central hall from the south
parlour. It had belonged to their father, then to Edward,
and was the room in which the brothers had conducted
business, disputed accounts, read newspapers, and quarrelled.

They had quarrelled there on the night before Edward's death.

The sisters had been in the parlour. The door of
the study was not shut, because damp weather had swollen
it, and Henry, who had tried once to close it,
had abandoned the effort with an oath. Rain pressed softly
against the windows. Emma rocked in her broad chair, the
silk at her shoulders whispering with every motion; Rebecca worked
at an altar cloth for St. Jude's; Caroline answered a
letter from a widowed cousin.

At first the brothers' voices were indistinguishable from the ordinary
disturbance of masculine conversation. Edward's was heavier, Henry's more incisive.
Then a chair scraped violently.

"You have had enough," Edward said.

"That is no concern of yours."

"It is my concern while you expect me to pay."

"I expect only what is mine."

"What is yours! You have spent what was yours and
half of what was mine."

"You are a liar."

Rebecca's needle stopped. Emma ceased rocking. Caroline continued to write,
but the line of ink extended beyond the final letter
and made a black incision across the paper.

"You will not use that word in my house," Edward
said.

"Our house."

"My house, until I am dead."

There followed a silence so complete that the rain seemed
to move from the windows into the room.

Then Henry spoke in his gentle voice.

"That may be sooner than you think."

Rebecca uttered a little cry.

Caroline turned upon her. "Hush."

"But he said—"

"I heard."

"What did he mean?"

"Nothing."

In the study Edward laughed. It was a short, brutal
laugh.

"You would like that, would you not? You have calculated
it very prettily. But I have made a new will."

There was another sound—a blow, perhaps, or a hand struck
upon the desk.

"A new will?"

"Yes."

"In whose favour?"

"That you shall discover in due season."

"You cannot disinherit me entirely."

"I can secure you precisely what you deserve."

Henry said something which they did not hear. Edward replied
more loudly:

"Get out!"

The study door was dragged open; it resisted, then yielded
with a cracking noise. Henry crossed the hall without looking
into the parlour. His face was white and perfectly composed.
Only his right hand betrayed him; the fingers opened and
closed at his side as if they clutched an invisible
instrument.

He went upstairs.

Later, near midnight, Rebecca heard him descend. She heard the
study door complain upon its hinges, and after an interval
she heard his foot upon the stair again. She told
nobody until after the funeral.

Edward was buried on Monday.

There had been no inquest. Dr. Henry Glynn's certificate stated
that death had resulted from acute inflammation of the stomach
complicated by cardiac weakness, and the village physician, old Dr.
Shore, accepted the statement with professional deference. Edward had been
intemperate in food and temper; he had suffered previous attacks;
sudden death, though lamentable, was not incredible.

Yet during the days of mourning the sisters moved beneath
a suspicion which none acknowledged and none escaped. It accompanied
them through the customary duties: choosing the coffin, receiving condolences,
ordering black-edged cards, and determining whether the second-best bedroom ought
to be given to Cousin James or to the clergyman's
wife. It hovered above the dining table when Henry carved
the mutton. It passed from face to face when the
will was read and they learned that Edward had not
completed the threatened alteration. The estate, after provision for the
sisters, descended chiefly to Henry.

"How fortunate for him," Rebecca said that night.

Caroline, who was locking the desk, looked over her shoulder.

"What is fortunate?"

"That the old will stood."

"It is fortunate for all of us that Edward did
not leave the property in confusion."

"But especially for Henry."

Emma stirred the fire. "It is natural that a brother
should inherit."

"Of course," said Rebecca.

"Then why speak in that manner?" Caroline demanded.

"I did not speak in any manner."

"You did."

Rebecca's lips quivered. "You think it too."

Caroline came rapidly across the room.

"Think what?"

"You know."

"I know nothing."

"You heard him say—"

"Be silent."

Emma turned from the fire. The flames gave an unnatural
animation to her heavy, handsome face.

"We all heard it," she said.

Caroline's eyes flashed.

"Heard WHAT?"

"That it might be sooner than Edward thought."

"It was an angry expression."

"And the draught?" Rebecca whispered.

Caroline seized the back of a chair.

"Don't ask and don't speak."

"Then you do think—"

"I think nothing. I will have no wickedness uttered in
this house. Henry is our brother."

"So was Edward," said Emma.

The words appeared to remain in the air after the
voice had ceased. Caroline's grasp tightened upon the chair until
the joints of her fingers whitened.

At that instant Henry entered.

He wore black, which became him; the severe lines of
his coat heightened the distinction of his figure, and sorrow—or
fatigue—had given his face a delicacy almost spiritual. He looked
from one sister to another.

"What solemn court is this?" he asked.

"No court," said Caroline.

"Rebecca has been crying."

"I have not," Rebecca answered, while drying her eyes.

Henry smiled and took the chair opposite Emma.

"You should not sit up so late. Grief taxes the
nerves, and weak nerves create fancies."

"Do they?" said Emma.

"Frequently."

"What sort of fancies?"

"All sorts. Sounds, shapes, suspicions."

Nobody moved.

Henry leaned back, crossed one narrow foot over the other,
and surveyed them with affectionate amusement. In that moment his
ease was so complete that their fears seemed monstrous. Caroline
experienced an almost grateful revulsion against herself. He was their
brother; he had played with them beneath the east elms;
he had once carried Rebecca two miles when she sprained
her ankle; he had watched beside their mother for three
nights without sleep. To connect this cultivated, weary man with
a secret poison seemed an offence against nature.

Then his gaze passed to the study door, and his
face changed.

It was no more than a contraction about the eyes,
instantly corrected. Nevertheless, Emma saw it.

"What is the matter?" she asked.

"Nothing."

"You looked toward the study."

"Did I?"

"Yes."

"I suppose I did."

"Why?"

Henry's smile returned. "Must every glance have a motive?"

"No," said Caroline quickly. "Emma, ring for the tray."

That evening Henry did not enter the study.

The room had remained undisturbed since Edward's funeral. On Tuesday
morning Caroline went there to sort his papers. She found
the task more difficult than she had anticipated. Edward had
possessed the orderliness of a man who trusts no one,
but the order was peculiar to himself: receipts tied in
bundles of varying colours, letters placed inside books, legal documents
folded in newspapers, and memoranda written upon envelopes which contained
quite unrelated matter. His large writing table stood near the
centre of the room; behind it was a leather chair
with a high back. Two smaller chairs flanked the fireplace.
A glass-fronted bookcase occupied the north wall, and opposite it
hung a cabinet containing their father's collection of old weapons—two
pistols, an East Indian dagger, and a slender Italian sword
which tradition, unsupported by evidence, associated with an ancestor's duel.

The west wall was papered in a pale pattern of
silver-grey leaves. There had once been pictures upon it, but
Edward, declaring them poor, had removed them. Only a small
oval miniature of their mother remained near the corner.

Caroline worked until dusk. Because the November day had darkened
early, she brought in the brass study lamp and placed
it upon the centre of the writing table. She struck
a match, adjusted the wick, and bent to replace the
glass chimney.

Then she looked at the wall.

A shadow stood there.

Caroline straightened very slowly.

At first she supposed it to be her own. The
supposition was natural, and she held to it even after
perceiving that the dark shape upon the wall was broad-shouldered
while she was narrow, and that its head rose above
the level at which her shadow could reasonably be cast.
It appeared life-sized, neither sharply outlined nor altogether vague. The
shoulders were square; the head inclined a little to the
left. One arm hung at the side. The other was
bent across the body.

She moved.

The shadow did not.

A sensation of coldness began at the back of her
neck and descended along her spine, yet her mind, trained
through years of domestic vicissitudes to reject disturbance until it
had been properly named, continued determinately at work. There must
be something between the lamp and the wall: a chair,
the corner of the desk, her own figure combined with
the edge of the bookcase. Shadows deceived. Light enlarged trivial
objects into human semblances.

She took one step to the right.

The shadow remained unchanged.

She took up the lamp. Darkness leaped across the wall
and vanished; the figure was gone. She replaced the lamp.
The figure sprang again into being.

Caroline's breath caught audibly.

It looked like Edward.

The resemblance did not lie in any exactness of feature,
for the shadow had none; nor in any detail which
might have been weighed by reason. It lay in the
mass of the head, the abrupt breadth of the shoulders,
the slight droop of the left side caused by an
injury Edward had received when thrown from a horse at
twenty. That infirmity had been scarcely observable in life, but
Caroline recognized it in the shadow as one recognizes a
familiar voice through a closed door.

She did not scream. Her horror was too great for
such relief. She walked backward, keeping her face toward the
wall, until she reached the door. Then she turned, crossed
the hall, and entered the parlour.

Emma was rocking by the fire; Rebecca sat near her,
reading a devotional book.

Caroline closed the parlour door.

"Come with me," she said.

Rebecca looked up. "Where?"

"To the study."

"Why?"

"Come."

There was something in her voice which caused Emma to
rise without question. Rebecca rose also, but clutched the book
against her breast.

Caroline opened the door again. The study lamp threw its
steady yellow light across the hall.

"What is it?" Rebecca whispered.

"Nothing. I wish you to look."

"At what?"

"Come."

They crossed together. At the threshold Caroline stopped and pointed.

For several seconds neither sister spoke.

Emma's heavy body appeared to settle downward, as if its
superb substance had lost its supporting principle. Rebecca gasped once,
twice, and pressed the devotional book against her mouth.

"What is that?" Emma said.

"It is a shadow."

"Whose?"

"Hush," said Caroline.

"But it—it looks like—"

"Hush!"

They entered. Three living figures now stood between the lamp
and various portions of the room, and three natural shadows
shifted obediently upon floor and furniture. The fourth remained upright
on the pale wall.

Rebecca began to sob.

"There is something," Caroline said. Her own voice sounded to
her very far away. "Something in the room causes it."

"Of course," said Emma.

"Of course," Rebecca repeated.

They examined the intervening space. The lamp stood upon the
table. Between it and the wall were the inkstand, a
bronze letter weight in the form of a sleeping dog,
two packets of papers, and the edge of a wooden
tray. Caroline removed these objects one by one. Their little
shadows disappeared. The great shadow did not alter.

"Move the lamp," Emma said.

Caroline moved it to the right. The figure vanished from
the wall.

Rebecca uttered a broken sigh.

"There! It was the lamp."

Caroline placed it upon a smaller table near the window.

The shadow appeared again.

It occupied nearly the same position and preserved the same
shape, although the angle of illumination had changed.

Rebecca gave a shrill cry.

"Stop it! Take the lamp away!"

Caroline lifted the lamp. The wall became innocent, pale, and
empty.

"There," she said. "There is nothing."

"But when the lamp—"

"There is nothing."

Emma, whose pride resisted terror longer than Rebecca's nerves, approached
the wall. She touched the paper where the shadow's breast
had been. Her broad fingers moved over the silver-grey leaves.

"Nothing," she said.

"Of course," Caroline replied.

"Then put the lamp back."

Caroline looked at her.

"Why?"

"To prove it."

"There is no need."

"There is every need."

Emma took the lamp from Caroline and set it again
upon the table.

The shadow sprang out.

Emma recoiled so violently that the chimney rattled. Her face,
ordinarily warm with a settled and almost complacent colour, became
livid.

"It is Edward," Rebecca sobbed.

Caroline caught her arm.

"You shall not say that."

"It is!"

"It is a shadow."

"It is his shadow."

"The dead cast no shadows."

Rebecca stared at the wall. "That one does."

A step sounded in the hall.

Henry appeared at the doorway.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

Nobody answered. His glance followed theirs.

The alteration in him was awful. His fine face did
not merely whiten; it seemed emptied of blood, thought, and
identity. The mouth opened, and for an instant he looked
like a man who has received a mortal wound without
yet feeling pain.

Then he drew himself erect.

"What is that?" he said.

Caroline released Rebecca's arm.

"We do not know."

Henry advanced to the table, his eyes fixed upon the
wall.

"Who arranged this?"

"Arranged what?" Emma asked.

"This absurdity."

"Nothing was arranged."

He turned upon Caroline. "You have been meddling with the
room."

"I was sorting Edward's papers."

"And contrived that?"

Caroline's fear yielded to indignation.

"I contrived nothing."

Henry looked again. A muscle beat visibly in his cheek.

"It must be something before the lamp."

"We removed everything," said Emma.

"Not everything, evidently."

With sudden violence he swept the table clear. Papers, pens,
the tray, and the bronze dog fell upon the carpet.
The inkstand overturned, making a dark stain. The shadow remained.

Rebecca whimpered.

"Be quiet!" Henry cried.

He bent and dragged the table aside. He placed the
lamp upon the mantel. The shadow appeared again, longer now,
but unmistakable in its human suggestion and in the droop
of its left shoulder.

Henry stood motionless.

"Well?" said Emma.

He wheeled upon her. "Well—what?"

"You are a physician. What is it?"

"A shadow."

"We know that."

"Then why ask me?"

"What casts it?"

"There is no accounting for shadows."

His tone was contemptuous, but sweat had gathered upon his
forehead. Caroline watched him take out his handkerchief and wipe
it away. The handkerchief shook.

"Henry," she said.

He looked at her.

"What?"

She intended to ask a question. She did not know
what form the question would take, only that it had
waited in the house since Thursday morning and had now
risen to her lips. But his eyes held hers with
so terrible an appeal—or threat—that she could not utter it.

"Nothing," she said.

"Exactly. Nothing."

He picked up the lamp and thrust it into her
hands.

"Take this away."

She obeyed. The shadow vanished, and darkness folded over the
study.

They spent the remainder of the evening in the south
parlour. Henry read a medical journal. Caroline attempted to mend
a cuff. Rebecca's devotional book lay unopened upon her knee.
Emma rocked steadily, the black silk at her shoulders rustling
backward and forward with a rhythm which gradually became intolerable.

At ten Henry closed his journal.

"You will not repeat this foolishness to the servants," he
said.

"No," Caroline answered.

"Nor in the village."

"No."

"It was an effect of light."

"Of course," said Emma.

Henry looked at her.

"Do you doubt it?"

"Certainly not."

"You speak as if you did."

"I said 'of course.'"

His face darkened. "Your manner—"

"Henry," Caroline interrupted, "we are all tired."

He rose and went upstairs.

When his steps had ceased, Rebecca leaned toward her sisters.

"He knew."

Caroline did not look up from the cuff.

"Knew what?"

"What it was."

"Nobody knows."

"He did. You saw his face."

"He was startled."

"He was terrified."

"So were we," Emma said.

Rebecca's voice dropped to a whisper. "Not in the same
way."

Caroline thrust the needle through the cloth and drew the
thread so hard that it broke.

"Don't ask and don't speak," she said.

The next afternoon Henry entered the study alone. He carried
a second lamp, a measuring tape, and a small looking
glass. The sisters, though they had agreed not to observe
him, sat in the parlour with the door ajar.

They heard furniture dragged across the floor. Once there came
the crash of a falling chair, followed by an oath.
Henry went upstairs and returned with a candle. Later he
brought in a tall lamp from the dining room. Light
moved strangely beneath the study door and along the hall,
brightening, diminishing, crossing itself in bands.

At last he called, "Caroline!"

She rose.

Emma and Rebecca rose also.

"I called Caroline."

They remained standing while she crossed the hall.

The study was in disorder. The writing table had been
pushed against the bookcase; chairs stood upon chairs; the curtains
had been taken down and lay in a heap; books
were stacked in the centre of the carpet. Three lamps
burned at separate elevations.

No shadow marked the west wall.

"You see," Henry said.

"Yes."

"It was an accident of arrangement."

"What caused it?"

"A convergence of objects and light."

"Which objects?"

He hesitated.

"Several. It is impossible to isolate every contributing factor."

Caroline looked at the dismantled room.

"Then you have discovered nothing."

"I have demonstrated that there is nothing supernatural."

"I did not say there was."

"No, you do not say things."

She turned to him with an expression so like Edward's
in its harsh patience that Henry recoiled.

"What do you mean?" he demanded.

"Nothing."

For an instant they faced one another. His eyes burned;
hers remained cold.

Then Emma called from the parlour, "Caroline?"

"I am coming."

As Caroline reached the doorway, Henry took the lamp from
the mantel and placed it on the floor.

The shadow appeared above him.

Caroline stopped.

Henry saw her stop and turned.

The lamps stood at different points; furniture obstructed the room
in every direction; the west wall was crossed by wavering
shapes from chairs, books, and the naked curtain pole. Yet
among these distortions the upright figure preserved itself. It was
darker than the other shadows and horribly composed. The bent
arm seemed now almost to indicate the man beneath it.

Henry uttered a sound unlike speech.

He seized one lamp, carried it to the table, then
another to the window ledge, then the third to a
chair. The lesser shadows reeled and leaped. The figure did
not go.

"Henry," Caroline said.

"Get out."

"Henry—"

"GET OUT!"

She withdrew.

He shut the door, forcing it against the swollen frame
until the latch caught. For an hour they heard him
moving within. He paced; he dragged the table; he opened
and closed drawers. Once the door shook under a violent
blow.

Rebecca sat with both hands pressed over her ears.

"What is he doing?"

"Nothing," said Caroline.

"He will hurt himself."

"Let him alone."

"But if he—"

"Let him alone."

Near midnight Henry emerged. His hair was disordered, his collar
open. He carried the brass lamp, which he placed upon
the hall table with extraordinary care.

"I have put the room right," he said.

No one answered.

"I do not wish any of you to go in
there."

"Why not?" Emma asked.

"Because I forbid it."

"You have no authority to forbid it."

"It is now my room."

Emma's rocking ceased.

"It was Edward's."

"It is mine."

Again the shared thought moved among the sisters, and Henry
perceived it. His expression became convulsive.

"What are you thinking?" he cried.

"Nothing," Caroline said.

"You are always thinking nothing. Your nothing looks from every
corner. It sits at the table. It whispers after I
have left the room."

"Henry, control yourself."

"I am perfectly controlled."

He stood before them trembling from head to foot.

Emma resumed her rocking.

"Then go to bed," she said.

For four nights the same scene, in varying forms, repeated
itself.

Henry waited until the servants retired, then carried the lamp
into the study. Sometimes he shut the door; sometimes he
left it ajar, as if secrecy were more terrible than
observation. The sisters sat opposite, hearing him conduct his furious
experiments. He used candles, mirrors, screens, books, curtains, and lengths
of cord. He measured the distance from lamp to wall
and from floor to ceiling. He chalked marks upon the
carpet. He examined the wallpaper for stains, dampness, and hidden
devices. Once he removed a breadth of paper, exposing the
bare plaster beneath.

The shadow appeared upon the plaster.

On the fifth night he ordered Caroline, Rebecca, and Emma
to witness his final proof.

The study was almost bare. Every portable article except the
central table and lamp had been carried into the hall.
Even their mother's miniature had been removed. The pale wall,
scarred where Henry had torn away the paper, showed nothing
while the lamp remained unlighted.

"You observe," he said, "that the space is empty."

"Yes," Caroline replied.

"There is no object between this table and the wall."

"No."

"No trick, no painted figure, no alteration in the surface."

"No."

Henry lit the lamp.

The shadow stood on the wall.

Rebecca's knees failed. She caught the doorframe and sank against
it. Emma supported her, though Emma herself was breathing in
great, laborious gasps.

Henry stared.

"It is impossible," he said.

Nobody replied.

"It is IMPOSSIBLE."

He moved his body directly into the line between the
lamp and the apparition. His own shadow spread black and
enormous over the wall, but the other figure remained visible
through it, or upon it—one could not determine which. Henry
stretched out his arms. The shape of his arms crossed
the shadow's breast; the bent arm beneath did not change.

He sprang aside.

"It is Edward," Rebecca whispered.

Henry turned with such ferocity that Emma drew her sister
backward.

"What did you say?"

"Nothing."

"You said a name."

"No."

"You said Edward."

Rebecca began to sob.

Caroline placed herself between them.

"She is frightened."

"Of what?"

Caroline looked toward the wall.

Henry followed her gaze.

"Answer me! Of WHAT?"

"Of a shadow," Caroline said.

He laughed. The laugh had no mirth in it; it
was high, breathless, and appalling.

"A shadow! You women will drive yourselves mad over a
shadow. And me with you. Do you think I do
not know what you imply? Do you think I have
not seen your eyes?"

"We have implied nothing."

"You believe I killed him."

Rebecca screamed.

Caroline's hand shot out and gripped Henry's sleeve.

"Hush!"

He tore himself free.

"You believe I poisoned him. There—it is said. You have
sat night after night in your mourning clothes and believed
it."

"No," Caroline answered.

Emma looked down.

Rebecca covered her face.

Henry pointed from one to another.

"Liar. Liar. Liar."

His voice fell upon the third word into a whisper.

Then, with a suddenness which none could prevent, he crossed
to the weapon cabinet. It was empty, for he had
removed it from the room; but the old Italian sword
lay with the displaced articles in the hall. He snatched
it up, wrenched the blade from its velvet-lined sheath, and
returned.

"Henry!" cried Caroline.

The blade flashed in the lamplight.

He rushed upon the wall—not upon the shadow itself, but
upon the clear intervening space where any material obstruction must
have stood. He struck and thrust at the empty air.
Once, twice, again; the slender steel whistled. He passed the
blade horizontally from lamp to wall, cutting through every supposed
line of projection.

The shadow did not stir.

At last Henry drove the sword directly through the figure's
breast and into the plaster. The point struck with a
dry grating sound. The blade bent, then broke near the
hilt.

Henry remained with his arm extended.

A small fragment of plaster fell to the carpet.

The shadow stood over him.

He dropped the broken hilt and backed away. His face
had become terrible—not merely terrified, but naked with the rage
of a man before whom all disguises have been torn
away. For one instant Caroline saw in him the boy
who had struck a stable lad senseless because the lad
laughed; the youth who had cheated at cards and blamed
his companion; the elegant physician who could soothe a sick
child and afterward curse the mother for inability to pay.
She saw also, though she refused the vision even while
beholding it, a man bending above his brother's bed with
a glass in his hand.

Henry's lips moved.

"I did not," he said.

No one had accused him.

"I did not. He was ill. I gave him medicine.
He would have died. He—he threatened me. But I did
not."

Caroline's voice was scarcely audible.

"Did not what?"

Henry looked at her, and his expression closed as a
door closes.

"Nothing."

He took the lamp and left the room.

The shadow disappeared.

In the morning Henry announced that he must go to
the city.

"On what business?" Caroline asked.

"Professional business."

"You have no practice in the city."

"I have correspondents."

"When will you return?"

"Tomorrow evening."

Emma, who was pouring coffee, watched him over the silver
pot.

"Is the business connected with Edward?"

Henry set down his cup.

"What do you mean?"

"Nothing. I asked a question."

"You ask very curious questions."

"You give very few answers."

Caroline intervened.

"What train will you take?"

"The eleven-fifteen."

"Shall Thomas drive you?"

"No. I will walk to the station."

"It is raining."

"I prefer to walk."

Rebecca had not spoken. She sat with her hands folded
in her lap and her eyes fixed upon Henry. Her
face was so pale and meek that it might have
excited tenderness in anyone less conscious of being judged.

At last Henry said, "Why do you stare at me?"

"I wondered if you slept."

"Very well."

"Did you dream?"

"No."

"Not of Edward?"

His chair grated backward.

Caroline rose. "Rebecca!"

Henry's hand closed around the edge of the table.

"I am going away for one day," he said with
dreadful quietness. "When I return, this hysteria will cease. The
study will be repapered. The furniture will be replaced. We
shall hear no more of shadows, dreams, or Edward. Do
you understand?"

Rebecca bowed her head.

Emma folded her napkin.

Caroline replied, "We understand."

He went upstairs for his bag.

From the window they watched him cross the wet lawn.
The rain silvered his black coat and made his figure
indistinct among the elms. At the gate he stopped and
looked back at the house. Even at that distance they
could perceive the pallor of his face.

He seemed about to return.

Then he turned and disappeared down the road.

Henry did not arrive on the evening train.

Caroline sent Thomas to meet it, and Thomas came back
alone. There was no message. At nine she telegraphed the
hotel at which Henry ordinarily stayed. The reply stated that
Dr. Glynn had not taken a room there.

"He may have gone elsewhere," Emma said.

"Of course," said Caroline.

"Or missed the train," Rebecca added.

"Of course."

"Perhaps he will come at midnight."

"Perhaps."

They waited until one. Every sound upon the road caused
Rebecca to rise; every settling board in the hall made
Emma's black silk rustle as she turned. Caroline sat upright
beside the extinguished fire, her hands clasped so tightly that
the nails marked her palms.

Henry did not come.

The next day passed without news.

They did not enter the study. No prohibition had been
spoken at his departure, but the room seemed sealed more
effectually than by a lock. Its door had again swollen
in the damp and stood slightly ajar. Through the narrow
opening one could see the edge of the writing table
and a pale strip of wall. Daylight entered from the
west window, showing nothing extraordinary. Nevertheless, each sister avoided looking
directly across the hall.

On the second evening Emma said, "We cannot leave it
forever."

"Leave what?" Caroline asked.

"The room."

"There is no reason to enter."

"There are Edward's papers."

"They can wait."

"And Henry's disorder."

"That also can wait."

Rebecca lifted her head.

"He may never come back."

Caroline's mouth became rigid.

"Do not be foolish."

"I did not say he was dead."

"No one spoke of death."

"No."

They listened to the rain. It had begun again, a
slow, persistent rain like that of the night before Edward's
last illness.

At half past seven Thomas brought the lamp for the
parlour. The sisters had lately used only one lamp, though
in former winters two had stood upon the paired tables.
Economy had nothing to do with it. They disliked carrying
light across the hall.

Thomas trimmed the wick and withdrew.

Emma rose.

"Where are you going?" Rebecca asked.

"To the study."

Caroline stood at once. "Why?"

"Because I will not be ruled by an empty room."

"Sit down."

"No."

Emma's grandeur, which fear had subdued but not destroyed, asserted
itself in the lift of her head and the broad
advance of her person. She took up the lamp.

"Emma," Caroline said.

Emma crossed the threshold.

Caroline and Rebecca followed, because they could not remain behind.

The study door resisted. Emma pressed it with her shoulder.
It gave way grudgingly, its lower edge scraping the floor
with a long, protesting sound.

The room smelled of cold ashes, torn paper, and lamp
oil. Henry had restored little. Chairs stood in the hall;
books remained piled near the window; the broken sword lay
where it had fallen, its naked fragment darkened at the
point by plaster. Upon the west wall the wound made
by the blade appeared as a small black mark.

Emma carried the lamp to the table.

"Wait," Rebecca whispered.

Emma stopped.

"What?"

"Do not put it there."

"Why not?"

"You know."

Caroline's voice came hard and low.

"Put it down."

Emma set the lamp upon the table.

Light filled the room.

Two shadows stood on the wall.

For a moment the sisters did not comprehend what they
saw. The first was broad, massive in head and shoulder,
the left side drooping with the old familiar defect. Beside
it, slightly behind, rose another figure—taller, narrower, with an austere
inclination of the head. The outline of the nose was
fine and aquiline. One arm hung straight; the other appeared
to reach toward the first shadow, whether in accusation, appeal,
or everlasting restraint none could determine.

Rebecca's mouth opened, but no sound emerged.

Emma's hand remained upon the lamp chimney. The heat scorched
her fingers; she did not move them.

Caroline stood as rigid as a statue. Her eyes travelled
from the second shadow to the empty place in the
room where its living substance should have been. There was
nothing there. The intervening air shone clear and undisturbed.

Then the front bell rang.

The sound passed through the house with a harsh metallic
vibration.

Nobody stirred.

It rang again.

At last Caroline withdrew from the room. She moved slowly
across the hall, while behind her Emma and Rebecca continued
to face the two dark figures. Thomas had already gone
to the door. She heard low voices, the rustle of
paper, and the closing of the outer latch.

He approached with a telegram upon a salver.

"For Dr. Glynn, ma'am—or the family."

Caroline took it.

The envelope resisted her fingers. She tore it unevenly, unfolded
the sheet, and read. Her face did not change; perhaps
it had reached that extremity of pallor beyond which no
visible alteration was possible.

Rebecca appeared in the study doorway.

"What is it?"

Caroline made no answer.

Emma came behind her, one scorched hand pressed against her
breast.

"What does it say?"

Caroline looked past them into the room.

The lamp burned steadily. Upon the pale, injured wall, Edward's
shadow and Henry's stood side by side.

She lowered her eyes to the telegram.

"Henry is dead," she said.

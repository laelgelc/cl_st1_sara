**TO THE EDITOR.**

Mr. Editor,

There are certain philosophers who maintain that a name is
but an empty sound; a label tied about a man
at baptism, or stuck upon a town by legislative authority,
for no better purpose than to prevent the postman from
carrying our letters astray. I confess I have never been
able to subscribe to this frigid doctrine. A name, in
my opinion, is a subtle atmosphere. It surrounds the object
to which it belongs; it tinges our first conception of
it; it haunts the memory long after the object itself
has vanished; and it may confer dignity upon insignificance, or
bring the most respectable reality into perpetual ridicule.

Mr. Shandy, if I recollect him rightly, held that the
fortunes of a child might be elevated or blasted by
the syllables imposed upon it. Though his philosophy may have
run somewhat too far into the clouds, there was sound
observation at the bottom of it. Present two strangers to
your imagination—an Orlando Montrose and an Ebenezer Snubbs. Before either
has entered the room, Orlando comes sweeping in with plumes,
ancestry, and a melancholy passion; while poor Snubbs appears with
ink upon his fingers and an unpaid tavern account in
his pocket. The actual Orlando may be a dealer in
candles, and Snubbs a hero; but fancy has given judgment
before evidence can be heard.

If names exercise this tyranny over individuals, how much more
potent must they be when fastened upon mountains, rivers, lakes,
valleys, and cities! A landscape is not made altogether of
earth and water. It is composed partly of recollection, partly
of poetry, and partly of those venerable sounds by which
generations have summoned it before the mind. Troy, without Homer,
might have been an inconsiderable heap; yet who can hear
its name and not behold towers, helmets, flames, and the
sorrowful shade of Hector? There are syllables which contain whole
histories, as a seed contains the future forest.

It is on this account, Mr. Editor, that I contemplate
the nomenclature of our country with mingled grief and apprehension.
Nature has performed her part magnificently. She has poured out
rivers that might bear the commerce of kingdoms; lifted mountains
worthy to become the thrones of legend; spread lakes broad
enough to have inland tempests and moonlit solitudes of their
own; and then we—her officious godfathers—advance with a surveyor's chain
in one hand and a poll-list in the other, and
christen these wonders after the first alderman who has treated
us to dinner.

We began, naturally enough, by borrowing. The colonist, looking backward
over the ocean, planted the names of his former habitation
in the wilderness, as a homesick traveler plants flowers from
his native garden. Thus came our News—New England, New Hampshire,
New Jersey, New York, and a whole progeny of renovated
antiquities. The sentiment was amiable; but the expedient has grown
stale. "New" is a prefix which wears badly. Let a
town preserve it for two centuries, and it becomes as
engagingly juvenile as the ancient colt in the Vicar of
Wakefield. The Pont-Neuf itself has grown gray under the contradiction.

When European memory was exhausted, we invaded classical antiquity. Greece
and Rome were laid under contribution; and Troy, Utica, Syracuse,
Athens, Carthage, and Rome were transported by wagon-loads into the
back settlements. A man may breakfast in Macedon, dine in
Homer, and sleep in Pompey, without having encountered a ruin,
a philosopher, or even a tolerable bust. Lemprière is scattered
over the map as if his dictionary had fallen from
a stagecoach and been blown about the country leaf by
leaf.

Then came political gratitude—that most industrious manufacturer of appellations. Every
successful orator, governor, general, contractor, and dispenser of patronage must
have his immortality measured into lots. Counties, villages, and post
offices spring up behind him like mushrooms after rain. If
this fashion proceed unchecked, wo unto posterity! We shall be
buried beneath Tubbsvilles, Pottsylvanias, Higginbotham Counties, and districts dedicated to
gentlemen whose fame never traveled six miles beyond the tavern
where their nomination was arranged.

Nor is vulgar description less active. Because a bay has
cattle near it, it becomes Cow Bay; because mosquitoes inhabit
a marsh—as they have an unquestionable right to do—it is
condemned forever as Mosquito Cove; because some worthy settler lost
a boot in a creek, Boot Creek transmits the circumstance
to the latest ages. Odds my life! Is the accidental
discomfort of an individual to outweigh the majesty of nature?
Must every hill record a broken axle, every pond a
drowned pig, and every village the nickname of its first
inhabitant?

Meanwhile, the country possessed a nomenclature of its own—ancient, melodious,
and fitted to the soil—yet we have kicked it aside
to make room for this hum-drum riff-raff. The original names
of river, rock, and valley have often a grave music
which seems born of the wind in the forests and
the fall of distant waters. They are not imported ornaments.
They belong to the features they designate, as the murmur
belongs to the stream.

Consider Neperan. There is a word that winds pleasantly through
the ear; and the stream itself, gliding among quiet meadows
and wooded banks, appears to acknowledge it. Call it by
some municipal surname, and half its pastoral charm is gone.
Pocantico, again, has a wild and shadowy cadence suited to
those secluded regions where evening gathers early beneath the trees
and old traditions linger in the recesses of Sleepy Hollow.
What can "Smith's Brook" do in such company? It comes
among the spirits like a tax collector.

I would preserve these native appellations, Mr. Editor, not merely
as curiosities for antiquaries, but as reliques of a history
otherwise passing away. The people who bestowed them have faded
from many of their ancient seats; their paths are roads,
their hunting grounds farms, and their council fires are extinguished.
Surely the least we can do is refrain from erasing
the few syllables that remain upon the land. They are
inscriptions more venerable than those carved upon monuments, because the
mountain and river themselves are the tablets.

Some prudent gentleman will object that these words are difficult.
Difficult to whom? Every name is difficult until it becomes
familiar. We pronounce with composure the titles of European princes,
though they seem constructed of consonants packed together for exportation;
we master the terminology of law, medicine, cookery, and horse-racing;
yet we affect despair before four harmonious syllables inherited from
the valley in which we live. Let poets employ them,
let historians restore them, let schoolboys repeat them, and usage
will soon make them as domestic as bread.

Literature, indeed, is the great naturalizer of names. A single
song may do more than twenty statutes. Let Mr. Bryant
breathe over a neglected river; let some future Halleck strike
a spirited measure upon a mountain; let a novelist of
Mr. Cooper's faculty place love, danger, and adventure beside an
obscure lake—and presently the word, once strange, passes into every
household. Fancy adopts it; memory cherishes it; and the map
is no longer a register of distances, but a treasury
of associations.

I would therefore propose—without presuming, forsooth, to dictate to Congress—that
our historical, antiquarian, and literary societies enter into a friendly
coparceny in this matter. Let each examine the elder nomenclature
of its neighborhood; collect the surviving names from records, traditions,
and the memories of aged inhabitants; determine, as nearly as
scholarship permits, their pronunciation and acceptation; and recommend their restoration
wherever some vulgar or borrowed substitute has usurped the place.

Such a reform should not be conducted by a committee
of political godfathers. Keep candidates away from it, or every
doubtful township will be christened in expectation of an appointment.
Let taste, history, and local fitness preside. Let the societies
correspond with one another, so that we do not bestow
the same noble name upon twelve villages and three counties;
and let geographers lend their maps to the undertaking. A
national gazetteer might thus become almost a poem.

Having once established this confederacy of taste, I would set
it immediately against the tyranny of "New." The prefix had
meaning when our settlements were recent dependencies; it has none
now but habit. We are no longer the faint copy
of an elder world, nor an appendix printed across the
Atlantic. Our cities have acquired histories, our rivers have witnessed
events, our forests have furnished legends, and our people have
accumulated enough sins and glories to stand upon their own
account. Why should maturity continue to answer to a nursery
name?

Speaking of New York reminds me that both the state
and city furnish excellent subjects for experiment. The city has
already a proper and noble designation waiting at its very
threshold—Manhattan. It is compact, sonorous, historical, and inseparable from the
island itself. "New York" might belong anywhere; Manhattan can belong
nowhere else. Say Manhattan, and one sees the crowded harbor,
the bright bay, the long island thrust between its rivers,
the masts, roofs, bells, merchants, and that ceaseless human tide
which makes the place at once magnificent and fatiguing.

The state presents greater difficulty. "Ontario" has breadth and dignity,
though the lake might complain that its individual property had
been seized for public use. "Mohegan" is more romantic, and
carries with it an echo of forest lineage; yet scholars
might quarrel concerning boundaries, tribes, and orthography until the state
had grown old under discussion. I offer both merely as
hints. These are crude ideas; but a crude idea may
be the acorn of a stately reform, provided no legislative
squirrel carry it away.

And now, Mr. Editor, my subject, which began with a
baptismal syllable and wandered into brooks and villages, expands before
me to its full magnitude. We want more than proper
names for towns, states, and rivers. We want a NATIONAL
NAME.

The political title "United States of America" is accurate, I
admit; but accuracy is the humblest merit of language. It
is a description, not a name—a circumlocution stamped upon treaties.
It tells how certain states are associated and on what
quarter of the globe they happen to be situated; but
it gives the imagination nothing to grasp. It cannot be
easily converted into an adjective without ambiguity; and as to
a native designation, what are we? Americans? So are, geographically
speaking, the inhabitants from the northern ice to Cape Horn.
We have taken the family surname and appropriated it to
one branch of a very extensive household.

Ask a citizen abroad what he is. "A citizen of
the United States." The phrase marches respectably, but it marches
like a procession of officials. Ask him to speak poetically
of his country: what compact, glowing word can he employ?
Ask him to name his countrymen without disputing possession with
half a hemisphere: what can he make of it, either
in poetry or prose? France has Frenchmen, England Englishmen, Italy
Italians. We have a constitutional paragraph.

I have sometimes thought that "Alleghania" might answer our necessity.
The name rises from our own continent; it is connected
with a mountain chain which stretches like a mighty spine
through the land; it is musical without effeminacy, grand without
bombast, and capable of all grammatical service. The country would
be Alleghania; its people Alleghanians; its affairs Alleghanian. A poet
might invoke it without being obliged to drag a federal
inventory into his verse, and a soldier might utter it
in a moment of enthusiasm without pausing for breath midway.

Do not understand me as supposing that a new word
would annihilate factions, cure corruption, reconcile interests, and render every
citizen wise before breakfast. I am not so deep in
nomenclatural enthusiasm. Yet symbols have power. A distinctive name becomes
a vessel into which common memories, sacrifices, hopes, and honors
are poured. Men who quarrel as inhabitants of separate provinces
may feel themselves reunited when addressed by one ancient-sounding appellation.
The ear teaches the heart a lesson which logic repeats
in vain.

We have political union; let us cultivate imaginative union. We
have a flag; let us have a name fit to
be spoken beneath it. We have mountains, rivers, forests, and
traditions peculiar to ourselves; let our language cease dressing them
in cast garments from Europe or daubing them with the
temporary notoriety of village magnates. Let us rescue what is
harmonious, restore what is historical, reject what is vulgar, and
leave posterity a map over which taste may travel without
continually stumbling.

I lay these reflections before you with all proper humility.
Perhaps your readers will laugh; perhaps some committee will propose
that Alleghania be renamed after its chairman; perhaps Cow Bay
will defend its ancient privileges and Mosquito Cove send a
remonstrance. Be it so. Ridicule is often the toll paid
by an idea upon entering the public road. If I
have persuaded one village to examine its forgotten name, or
one poet to rescue a melodious word from oblivion, my
epistle will not have been written wholly in vain.

I remain, Mr. Editor, with profound consideration,

Your obedient servant,
**GEOFFREY CRAYON.**

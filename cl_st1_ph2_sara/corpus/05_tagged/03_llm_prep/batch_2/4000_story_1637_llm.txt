### The Major's Account

When Major Pendleton Talbot first saw the boarding house in
Washington, he stopped upon the brick pavement, planted his silver-headed
cane between his feet, and regarded the premises with the
expression of a man unexpectedly addressed in his native tongue.

The house stood behind four white pillars of a species
no architect had ever acknowledged, but which time, vines, and
a certain amiable sagging had made respectable. Two magnolias shaded
the yard. Box bordered the walk, and in April the
branches of an old cherry tree spread above the gate
like a pink parasol. A rusty iron bench rested beneath
it, surrounded by violets that paid no rent and were
therefore among the most prosperous tenants.

"My dear Lydia," said the major, "Providence has at last
directed us to a place of occupation not wholly destitute
of the associations to which we have been accustomed."

Miss Lydia Talbot looked at the cracked steps, the card
advertising Rooms with Board, and the curtain at an upper
window, behind which the landlady was plainly watching them.

"It is three dollars less a week than Mrs. Hewitt's,"
she said. "That is the association I like best."

The major lifted one hand in acknowledgment of a remark
which, while lacking elevation, contained undeniable merit. Then he ascended
the steps.

His coat ascended with him in a separate and more
leisurely manner. It was a remarkable coat—long, loose, glossy at
the elbows, narrow at the shoulders, and ample about the
skirts. A hostile critic might have called it obsolete. The
major called it a morning coat, though it had survived
more mornings than most governments. With it he wore a
low-cut waistcoat, trousers of a soft gray, gaiters, and a
black string tie whose ends had an inveterate tendency to
slip from beneath his rolling collar. His linen was faultless.
His hat was broad, high, and of a soft black
felt. His white hair flowed backward from a noble forehead,
and his small mustache and pointed imperial were arranged with
military precision.

He bowed to the landlady in three measured movements: inclination,
pause, recovery. Mrs. Ward, whose ideas of courtesy were generally
satisfied by receiving money on Saturday, was so impressed that
she gave them the front rooms at the rear-room price.

Thus Major Talbot and his daughter entered the Washington establishment
in which they were to remain for considerably longer than
Miss Lydia's arithmetic recommended.

They had come from Mobile with two trunks, a cedar
box, one hundred and forty-eight dollars, and a manuscript. Of
these possessions, the manuscript was by far the largest.

It was entitled *Anecdotes and Memories of the Old South,
with Particular Reference to the Hospitality, Chivalry, Agriculture, Oratory, and
Domestic Customs of the Gulf States*. The title occupied a
page; the work itself occupied the cedar box. The major
had labored over it for five years and expected from
its publication not merely pecuniary reward, but a respectful correction
of the national memory.

"You will observe, Lydia," he had said on the train,
"that the Northern mind is not deliberately unjust. It is
merely uninformed. I propose to furnish the information."

Miss Lydia, who had copied all four hundred and thirty-seven
pages in a clear hand, knew that the information included
descriptions of twenty-one barbecues, nine political duels that had not
taken place, seven that had, eleven race meetings, the correct
procedure for receiving a governor, and a list of the
Talbot silver before it ceased to be the Talbot silver.

She also knew that Northern publishers were people who paid
for what they believed others would buy.

But she loved her father, and affection is frequently obliged
to transact business with hope.

The other residents of Mrs. Ward's establishment soon became acquainted
with the major. They met him at breakfast, where he
addressed the butter as though requesting a favor from a
duchess; on the steps, where he raised his hat to
ladies, children, and occasionally to respectable-looking dogs; and in the
parlor, where he sat in the evening beneath a steel
engraving of Abraham Lincoln and discoursed upon the insufficiency of
modern manners.

The boarders included two clerks from the Treasury, a widow
seeking a pension, a dentist, a schoolteacher, a young lady
employed in a patent office, and Henry Hopkins Hargraves.

Hargraves was twenty-seven, lean, quick-eyed, and composed of the particular
nervous material from which actors, wire-walkers, and men who sell
mining shares are commonly made. He earned his living in
vaudeville, where he gave imitations of public figures, national types,
and persons who had not yet discovered that they were
national types. He possessed a flexible face, a memory for
phrases, and an ear that could steal an accent through
a closed door.

At his first dinner with the Talbots he listened while
the major explained the distinction between hospitality and entertainment.

"Entertainment, sir," said the major, "may be purchased. Hospitality is
conferred."

Hargraves put down his fork.

"That's good," he said. "That's very good."

The major inclined his head.

"It is not intended as an epigram, Mr. Hargraves. It
is a statement of social law."

"Sure," said Hargraves. "That's why it's good."

Miss Lydia looked at the actor with some attention. His
voice was brisk and modern, but his eyes were respectful.
He had not laughed. At least, he had not laughed
where it could be collected as evidence.

Within a week he had become the major's most attentive
audience.

This was no light distinction. Major Talbot required from a
listener endurance, memory, and a readiness to ask at the
right moment what Colonel Deering had then replied. Hargraves displayed
all three virtues. He learned the names of the great
families from Savannah to New Orleans. He could distinguish General
Talbot, the major's father, from Judge Talbot, his uncle, and
Cousin Randolph Talbot, who had ruined himself upon horses but
had done so, the major explained, without the least injury
to his standing.

He learned of the Talbot plantation, known as Bellemeade, though
no map had ever admitted the title. He saw it
through the major's recollection: the long white house above the
river; the galleries cooled by striped awnings; the road between
the live oaks; the cotton fields stretching toward the dark
line of cypress; the kitchen yard at dawn; hunting horns,
carriage lamps, blue china, brass andirons, roses climbing to the
second floor; Christmas tables bright with silver; summer nights shaken
by music and fireflies.

The major's past had excellent weather.

Rain fell when needed, servants sang at a picturesque distance,
horses were spirited but obedient, and no account ever came
due. Misfortune entered only in the dignified forms of war,
bereavement, and political outrage. Smaller failures had been edited out
by memory, an author more ruthless than any publisher.

Hargraves encouraged these recollections.

"And that fellow who trained the colts," he would say,
"what was his name?"

"Mose," replied the major. "Mose Carter. A most trustworthy hand
with animals. I once presented him with two mule colts—not
for any commercial purpose, you understand, but in recognition of
his devotion during my father's illness."

"And what became of him?"

"After the disturbance, Mose removed westward. Texas, I believe. Many
of our people were scattered. An unfortunate severance of ties."

Miss Lydia, sewing by the lamp, would raise her eyes
for a moment and lower them again. She was twelve
years younger than her father's memories, and therefore less able
to inhabit them comfortably.

Hargraves asked about the races, the dinners, the fox hunts,
the old servants, the political speeches, and the quarrels that
etiquette had refined into historical events. Sometimes he drew the
major into demonstration.

"You mean Colonel Barksdale just walked in and called him
a liar?"

"My dear sir! Colonel Barksdale entered, approached the mantel, placed
his gloves upon it, and remarked: 'Captain Hilliard, your communication
contains a degree of imaginative freedom which I cannot permit
to pass under the name of accuracy.'"

The major rose, crossed the parlor, set down an imaginary
pair of gloves, and delivered the sentence with calm fatality.

Hargraves watched his feet, hands, eyebrows, and the slight advance
of his chin on the word "accuracy."

"Then what?"

"Then, sir, the matter proceeded to a private adjustment."

"A duel?"

"A private adjustment," repeated the major.

Hargraves nodded as a pupil accepts a technical term.

Miss Lydia occasionally suspected that his admiration had a professional
appetite in it. Yet he was useful. He ran errands,
carried the cedar box to publishers, obtained seats for performances,
and once returned with a new ribbon for her hat
because, he said, the shopkeeper had given him too much
change and the money ought to be kept out of
circulation. Lydia did not believe this explanation, but the ribbon
was the exact shade she had admired.

When she asked where he was appearing, he answered vaguely.

"Working up a new turn. Character piece. Got to make
it right before I spring it."

The major approved.

"Preparation, Mr. Hargraves, is the courtesy talent owes to the
public."

"Major," said Hargraves, "if I could put your talk in
bottles, I'd own the theatre district."

The major smiled. He regarded the remark as a tribute
to rhetoric rather than a commercial estimate.

One warm evening in May, Hargraves discovered the ceremony of
the mint julep.

He had returned from rehearsal to find the major in
the dining room, where Mrs. Ward had reluctantly surrendered a
small table. Upon it stood a bowl of cracked ice,
a bundle of mint, sugar, a bottle, two glasses, and
a silver spoon with a twisted handle.

The major had removed his coat. His sleeves were rolled
back with solemn neatness.

"Come in, Mr. Hargraves," he said. "You arrive at an
appropriate moment."

"What's doing?"

"Nothing is 'doing,' sir. A julep is being composed."

Hargraves leaned against the doorframe.

"I thought you mixed them."

The major turned slowly.

"You may mix medicine. You may mix mortar. A julep
requires a more considerate process."

He selected two glasses and examined them against the light.
Into each he placed mint leaves—not bruised, he explained, but
persuaded with the back of the spoon until their fragrance
rose. Sugar followed, dissolved in a little water. Then came
a measured flow of bourbon, more ice, another touch of
bourbon, and a final crown of mint.

At every stage the major had a sentence.

"The leaf, sir, must surrender its perfume without being subjected
to indignity."

"The ice should be cracked, not crushed. Powdered ice belongs
to the commercial refreshment."

"The spirit is not poured in haste. It is introduced."

At last he wiped the outside of each glass until
frost filmed it. He adjusted the sprigs so that the
drinker must encounter the mint before the liquor.

"The first impression," he said, "should be pastoral. The second
may be of a more animated character."

Hargraves accepted the glass.

"Major," he said after tasting it, "that drink has a
first act, an intermission, and a smash finish."

The major's tie had slipped sideways during the composition. He
restored it and permitted himself a pleased chuckle.

From that evening Hargraves could make a julep with every
gesture of the major, including the rescuing of the tie
after the sugar was dissolved.

The weeks passed. The cherry blossoms fell; the violets retreated
from the heat; the white pillars continued to stand chiefly
because no one had informed them of their condition.

The manuscript visited seven publishers.

It returned from each with a letter expressing admiration for
its charm, regret for the state of the market, and
confidence that some other publisher would recognize its importance. One
editor suggested reducing it by two thirds. Another advised turning
it into a novel with a young heroine. A third
believed that a book of Southern recipes might find a
public if the historical discussion were omitted.

The major received these opinions as a Roman senator might
have received suggestions for improving the republic from a dealer
in fish.

"The literary place of occupation," he said, "appears to have
been invaded by tradesmen."

Miss Lydia kept the accounts.

At first she kept them in a small blue notebook.
Later, when the figures became less varied, she kept them
in her head. Their capital dwindled through rent, postage, laundry,
shoe repairs, and the expense of carrying the manuscript about
the city. The major paid the latter cheerfully. A book,
like a lady, could not be expected to go unattended.

Lydia began taking in copying from an attorney, but concealed
the amount of it. The major believed that she occasionally
assisted a friend with correspondence.

One Saturday morning Mrs. Ward sent up the bill.

Lydia found her father at the table revising a passage
about the reception given to a Hungarian count in 1858.

"We owe thirty-six dollars," she said.

The major looked up.

"For what, my dear?"

"For being housed and fed."

"Ah. Mrs. Ward's account."

"She would like twelve dollars today."

"Certainly. We shall arrange it."

"We have four dollars and seventeen cents."

The major removed his spectacles.

For a moment his face showed not surprise but a
wounded effort to place an unpleasant fact within the forms
of good breeding.

"Has the remittance from Mobile failed to arrive?"

"There is no remittance from Mobile."

"The insurance matter?"

"Settled against us in February."

"The note from Cousin Ambrose?"

"Worthless."

The major folded his spectacles and laid them upon the
manuscript.

"Then it will be necessary to negotiate a temporary financial
adjustment."

"With whom?"

The question was plain and short. It entered the room
without bowing.

The major rose and walked to the window. In the
yard Hargraves was showing the Treasury clerks how a famous
senator blew his nose before delivering a speech.

"I have not yet exhausted," said the major, "the possibilities
connected with the publication."

"You have exhausted the money connected with them."

He turned. Lydia's face was pale, and the severity of
her words had frightened her more than him.

"My dear child," he said gently, "you have borne these
inconveniences with a fortitude that would have honored your mother.
I have perhaps permitted my confidence in the work to
delay considerations of a more immediate nature."

That was the nearest Major Talbot could come to saying
he had failed. Lydia crossed the room and kissed him.

"We will manage," she said.

That evening he went out alone. When he returned, he
wore the air of one who had concluded affairs of
importance, but Lydia saw that his watch chain hung without
a watch.

He placed two theatre tickets on the table.

She stared at them.

"Father!"

"My dear, we have allowed the vulgar pressure of finance
to narrow our existence. Tonight a new comedy is to
be presented at the Alcazar. Mr. Hargraves, I understand, is
in the cast. We owe it to his many civilities
to observe his performance."

"You sold your watch."

"I converted an idle article into two hours of recreation
and a modest balance."

"How modest?"

"Two dollars."

Lydia closed her eyes.

Then she put on her hat.

There are extravagances which can be prevented, and others which,
having already occurred, must be worn like flowers.

The Alcazar was crowded. Hargraves's new sketch had been advertised
as *The Last of the Cavaliers*, and the newspapers promised
a triumph of comic characterization. The Talbots sat well forward,
the major erect and expectant, Lydia uneasy without knowing why.

The curtain rose upon a hotel bar in a small
Southern town. Two traveling salesmen exchanged brisk insults with a
clerk. Then a servant announced "Colonel Calhoun Beauregard Fairfax, of
Suwanee County."

The audience applauded before the colonel entered.

He came through the door wearing a long-skirted coat, gray
trousers, gaiters, broad black hat, string tie, white hair, mustache,
and pointed imperial.

Lydia's hand closed upon her program.

The major did not move.

The actor paused, bowed in three measured movements, and requested
a chair with such elaborate courtesy that the request became
a public address. His tie slipped. He restored it. He
called the bar his "place of refreshment," referred to paying
his hotel bill as "a temporary financial adjustment," and informed
the salesmen that entertainment might be purchased but hospitality must
be conferred.

The house laughed.

Every phrase was the major's. Every gesture had been taken
from him and enlarged just enough to make recognition painless
for everyone except the original.

The colonel told the story of Captain Hilliard and Colonel
Barksdale. He placed imaginary gloves upon the mantel. He accused
the captain of "imaginative freedom." He described a private adjustment
and refused to call it a duel.

The house rocked.

The major sat frozen, his hands upon the silver head
of his cane.

Lydia leaned toward him.

"Let us go."

He made a slight movement, then stopped. To rise in
that coat would be to send a second copy of
the joke walking up the aisle.

"No," he whispered. "We shall not advertise the copy by
exhibiting the original."

The sketch proceeded.

The comic colonel spoke of his ancestral estate, Belle Grove,
where the roses climbed to the second gallery, the horses
possessed pedigrees longer than those of congressmen, and servants sang
at a convenient distance. He named no Talbots, but there
was no need. Even the colonel's method of adjusting his
cuffs belonged to the man in the fourth row.

Then came the julep.

A table was brought out. The actor rolled back his
sleeves, selected the glasses against the light, persuaded the mint,
introduced the spirit, condemned powdered ice, and promised a pastoral
first impression followed by one of more animated character.

The audience applauded sentence by sentence. At the end it
shouted.

Hargraves stood beneath the lights, holding the frosted glass, and
gave the major's pleased chuckle.

The curtain fell and rose six times.

Major Talbot remained seated until the theatre had nearly emptied.
His face had the gray stillness of old paper. Lydia
touched his arm.

"Father, it was cruel."

"No," he said. "Cruelty requires passion. This was commerce."

They returned to the boarding house in silence.

Hargraves came to their rooms the next afternoon.

He had removed the stage makeup, but to the major
it seemed still upon him. The young actor stood just
inside the door, his hat in his hand.

"Major," he began, "I came to say I'm sorry you
were hurt."

The major was seated beside the cedar box. He rose.

"Miss Talbot," he said, "will you kindly remain? I prefer
that no misunderstanding should arise concerning this interview."

Hargraves flushed.

"I didn't mean to make a fool of you."

"Your intention, sir, is immaterial beside the excellence of your
success."

"I used things you told me. I know that. But
the character isn't you—not all you. It's a type. Audiences
know the type."

"A type," said the major, "is the coward's method of
insulting an individual while pretending to discuss mankind."

Hargraves took a step forward.

"Now, hold on. You've talked for two months about Northerners
being tradesmen without manners, publishers being ignorant, politicians being thieves,
actors being vagabonds—"

"I do not recall describing actors in that manner."

"You didn't have to. Your eyebrows did it."

Lydia turned toward the window.

Hargraves continued more quietly.

"You tell stories about everybody. You make a character of
Colonel Barksdale and Captain Hilliard and Cousin Randolph. You give
their voices, their walk, their little foolishnesses. You enjoy it.
But when somebody uses your voice, that's dishonor."

The major's face reddened.

"The persons of whom I speak belong to my own
society. Their peculiarities are preserved with affection."

"That's what I did."

"The laughter of nine hundred strangers is not affection."

"No," Hargraves admitted. "But it pays the rent."

The words entered the room and found their mark. Lydia
looked at him sharply.

He put his hat on the table.

"The sketch is a hit. They've raised me to a
hundred and fifty a week. I owe you for it.
I came to offer you half of the first month—three
hundred dollars."

The major drew himself up. His coat seemed to gather
dignity from every crease.

"You propose to compensate me for the privilege of presenting
my person, my family traditions, and my private conversation as
a public burlesque?"

"I propose to share what I earned from material you
gave me."

"Mr. Hargraves, if my necessities were such that I must
choose between starvation and accepting that money, I should require
little time for the decision."

Lydia spoke.

"Father—"

He raised his hand, not harshly.

"No, my dear. There are economies which preserve life, and
economies which make its preservation unnecessary."

Hargraves's jaw tightened.

"All right. I played it wrong. But I wasn't sneering
at you, Major. I like you. I like the way
you talk and walk and make a ceremony out of
everything. Most people go through life as if they were
late for a train. You act as if life had
invited you and put your name on the card."

The major's expression wavered, but pride repaired it.

"You possess persuasive gifts, sir. They do not alter the
nature of the offense."

"So that's final?"

"It is."

Hargraves took his hat.

"Miss Lydia," he said.

She gave him a small, troubled bow.

At the door he looked back.

"For what it's worth, Major, the audience laughed, but they
liked the old colonel. He was the only gentleman in
the piece."

The major turned away.

Hargraves left the house that afternoon. Mrs. Ward said he
had taken rooms nearer the theatre.

During the next ten days, the Talbots descended through the
quieter degrees of poverty.

The major sent the manuscript to one final publisher. Lydia
sold a brooch. Mrs. Ward accepted six dollars and a
promise, then another promise without the six dollars. Breakfast became
a matter of coffee and dignity. Dinner was taken downstairs
so that dignity might observe what little there was of
it.

The major attempted to secure a clerical position through an
acquaintance at the War Department. After an interview, he returned
with the information that the government had become excessively particular
concerning age, handwriting, and experience.

"They desired a younger man," he said.

"They pay forty dollars a month," Lydia replied.

"Youth is notoriously inexpensive."

On Thursday their resources amounted to twenty-eight cents.

That afternoon Mrs. Ward called Lydia into the hall.

"My dear," she said, in the soft voice landladies reserve
for threats they dislike making, "I can carry you to
Monday. I truly can't go beyond it."

Lydia thanked her and returned upstairs.

The major sat at his desk composing a letter to
a former senator. He had written: *My dear sir, I
trust you will pardon the liberty I take in calling
your attention to a temporary embarrassment of a purely accidental
character—*

He stopped when Lydia entered.

"I have been considering," he said, "the possible disposition of
the manuscript."

"No."

"It has a certain value as waste paper, though naturally—"

"No," she repeated.

He smiled faintly.

"Your defense of literature is gratifying."

"It is five years of your life."

"It has also consumed a noticeable portion of yours."

There was a knock.

Lydia opened the door.

An elderly Black man stood in the passage. He was
bent but broad-shouldered, with a fringe of white beard and
a battered hat held respectfully in both hands. His clothes
were clean, old, and carefully brushed. A pair of spectacles,
one lens cracked, rested low on his nose. He looked
past Lydia into the room.

"Beg pardon, miss," he said. "Is this where Major Pendleton
Talbot is staying?"

The major rose at once.

"That is my name."

The visitor's face opened into delight.

"Well, sir! I'd have known you anywhere. Older, maybe, but
standing just the same."

The major searched the face.

"You have the advantage of me."

"I expect I do. Time changes a man. I'm Moses
Carter, sir. Folks used to call me Mose, down at
Bellemeade."

The major stared.

"Mose?"

"Yes, sir."

"Mose Carter, who had charge of the lower stable?"

"And got thrown by that red mare near every Thursday
till I learned which side she favored."

The major came nearer.

"Good heavens. Mose!"

He took the old man's hand in both of his.

Lydia watched from beside the door. Something in the visitor's
voice stirred a faint uncertainty in her, but the major's
pleasure swept the room clean of doubt.

"Mose, sit down. Lydia, my dear, this is Mose Carter,
one of the most trusted people connected with our household.
You remember hearing me speak of him."

"Many times," said Lydia.

Mose bowed.

"I remember Miss Lydia when she wasn't higher than that
table. Used to come down by the paddock with a
blue sash and ask more questions than a judge."

"I had a blue sash," Lydia said slowly.

"Indeed you did. Lost it once by the springhouse, and
everybody searched till old Aunt Millie found it caught on
the quince bush."

Lydia had no memory of the event. But she had
once owned a blue sash, and there had been a
quince hedge near the springhouse. Hargraves had heard of both.

The thought came and went too quickly to take form.

The major drew up a chair.

"Tell us everything. Where have you been? What has life
done with you?"

Mose sat with his hat upon his knees.

"Life's done fair, Major. I went to Texas after the
war. Worked cattle first. Saved a little. Lost it. Saved
again. Bought land outside Fort Worth. Then the railroad came
close enough to make the land worth something. My boys
handle most of it now. I've got grandchildren who read
better than I do and tell me so."

The major laughed with profound satisfaction.

"I am delighted, Mose. Truly delighted. Your advancement reflects credit
upon your character."

"It reflects some credit on luck too," said Mose. "But
I won't argue with you, Major. Never could."

He asked after names from Bellemeade. The major supplied histories.
Some had died; some had scattered; some had descended into
business. For half an hour the lost plantation assembled in
the poor room. Doors long burned swung open. Horses dead
for forty years stamped in their stalls. Dinner bells sounded
across lawns now divided by roads and warehouses.

Mose remembered enough to keep every ghost in its place.

At last he leaned forward.

"Major, I came on business."

The major's face assumed polite attention.

"Business, Mose?"

"Yes, sir. Been carrying something on my mind a long
while."

He reached inside his coat and drew out a thick
envelope.

"You remember giving me two mule colts?"

"Perfectly. A gray and a bay. The bay had a
white muzzle."

"That's them. Fine animals. I took them west. Traded up
from them, then traded again. You might say they started
me. I always held that I owed you for those
colts."

"Nonsense," said the major. "They were a gift."

"That may be how you kept it in your books.
Not how I kept it in mine."

He laid the envelope on the table.

"I've figured the price of the colts, and some interest—not
bank interest, because I'd never get through that arithmetic. My
own sort. There's three hundred dollars."

No one spoke.

The envelope rested upon the manuscript, small, square, and weightier
than the cedar box.

The major stepped back.

"I cannot accept it."

Mose's shoulders drooped.

"I was afraid you'd say that."

"The colts were bestowed freely. There is no debt."

"Then take it as my thanks."

"Gratitude is not purchased."

Mose looked toward Lydia. His eyes behind the cracked lens
were moist.

"Miss Lydia, maybe you can talk to him. I've carried
this a good many years. A debt don't always sit
in the account book. Sometimes it sits on a man.
I came all this way to get clear of it."

The major's gaze fell upon the envelope.

There are forms in which pride will refuse charity, and
forms in which it may receive rescue as a tribute.
Hargraves had offered payment for ridicule; Mose offered repayment of
an ancient obligation. One injured the major's identity. The other
confirmed it.

Still he hesitated.

Mose rose.

"If you send me away with it, Major, I'll feel
I haven't settled honest."

The major placed a hand on his shoulder.

"My dear Mose, I would not willingly burden you with
such a feeling."

"Then take it."

The major drew a breath.

"Very well. I accept—not as recompense for any kindness, but
as the discharge of an obligation which, though legally nonexistent,
has evidently acquired moral substance in your mind."

Mose smiled.

"That sounds settled enough to me."

The major opened the envelope. Inside were fifteen twenty-dollar bills.

Lydia sat down.

The visitor declined refreshment, saying he had a train to
catch. At the door the major embraced him. Mose accepted
this with a grave tenderness that brought tears to Lydia's
eyes despite the small, persistent uneasiness in her mind.

"You must write," said the major. "You must send me
the names and ages of all your grandchildren."

"I will, sir."

"And when circumstances permit, Lydia and I shall visit you
in Texas."

"My door will stand open."

The old man descended the stairs, crossed the yard, and
passed beneath the magnolia trees. His gait was bent and
slow until he reached the gate.

There, for one instant, beyond the boarding house windows, he
straightened.

Lydia did not see it.

The financial adjustment was immediate.

Mrs. Ward received her thirty-six dollars and became affectionate. The
major recovered his watch from the pawnbroker. Lydia purchased food,
stamps, and a pair of shoes whose need had become
a private emergency. Fifty dollars was reserved for the publication
campaign, though Lydia succeeded in reducing the sum to twenty-five
by describing the remaining publishers as "those who have not
yet refused us."

The atmosphere of the rooms changed. Poverty had not departed
forever, but it had been paid through the month and
instructed not to call without notice.

The major revised the chapter concerning loyalty among the former
workers of Bellemeade. He added, with restrained emotion, the story
of Moses Carter and the mule colts.

"It is not merely the money," he told Lydia. "The
incident constitutes a vindication of human attachment against the vulgar
theory that all relations are commercial."

"Yes, Father," said Lydia.

"Hargraves would have understood everything in terms of the box
office."

At the name, Lydia's needle paused.

"Perhaps."

The major looked at her.

"You do not defend him?"

"No. I only think he understood more than we allowed."

"My dear, generosity cannot repair an insult when the generosity
itself assumes the form of payment."

"No," said Lydia. "Not in that form."

Two mornings later a letter arrived. The envelope bore a
New York postmark and was addressed to Miss Lydia Talbot.

She took it to the window while the major worked
at his desk.

The letter was brief.

> Dear Miss Lydia,
>
> I hope the financial adjustment passed without hurting the
Major's feelings. I had to leave town at once, as
I've got a six-week booking in New York.
>
> Tell me privately, if you can, how I played
Uncle Mose. I was shaky on the voice, but I
think the cracked spectacle carried it. The three hundred belongs
to the Major. He earned it, though he would not
take it under the right name.
>
> Please burn this.
>
> Yours,
>
> H. H. Hargraves

Lydia read the letter twice.

Behind her, the major cleared his throat.

"From whom, my dear?"

She folded the paper along its existing crease.

"A theatrical acquaintance," she said.

"Mr. Hargraves?"

"Yes."

The major's pen stopped.

"I trust he is prospering."

"He has a six-week engagement in New York."

"I am gratified," said the major stiffly. "Ability, even when
misdirected, merits recognition."

Lydia held the letter over the small flame of the
alcohol lamp. The corner blackened, curled, and caught. Hargraves's question
vanished first, then his name. She dropped the ash into
a saucer and crushed the last bright edge with the
point of her scissors.

"What did he want?" asked the major.

"To know whether his performance was successful."

The major resumed writing.

"The public," he said, "has already answered him."

Lydia looked at the cedar box, the paid bill upon
the mantel, and the restored watch in her father's pocket.
Then she looked at the new paragraph beneath his pen,
where Mose Carter's fidelity was being preserved for posterity in
language no publisher had yet agreed to print.

"Yes," she said. "I suppose it has."

Outside, the magnolias stirred in the Washington heat. The major
adjusted his slipping tie, dipped his pen, and continued his
account of the old order—supported, for the present, by three
hundred dollars earned in the new.

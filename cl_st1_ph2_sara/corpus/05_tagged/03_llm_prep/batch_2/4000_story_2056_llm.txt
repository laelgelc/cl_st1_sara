## The Man Who Made Things Easier

There are men who cannot see a fellow-creature carrying a
parcel without taking hold of the string, with the result
that the parcel opens between them and its contents are
distributed over the roadway. Septimus Bolder was such a man.

He was born helpful. At six he undertook to teach
the family cat to swim, arguing that the accomplishment might
one day save its life. The cat survived the lesson,
but his aunt, who was bathing at the time, never
entirely recovered her confidence in children.

At eight he organised the smaller boys of his school
into a fire brigade. He appointed himself chief officer, purchased
two whistles, and selected his father's tool-house for the inaugural
rescue. There being no fire, he made one. The brigade
behaved with spirit, though without judgment. They extinguished the gardener,
damaged the pump, and burned down the tool-house.

At eleven he taught a neighbour's pony to jump. The
pony had previously led a blameless, if unambitious, existence. Under
Bolder's tuition it learned to refuse gates, climb banks, and
enter conservatories. After a fortnight the neighbour sold it to
a circus, where, I believe, its peculiar education was appreciated.

At school he was generous with information. During examinations he
would whisper answers to boys who had not asked for
them. His answers were invariably wrong; but as he supplied
them with firmness, and sometimes with documentary references, they carried
conviction. Three boys were punished on one occasion for copying
from Bolder, while Bolder himself escaped because no master could
believe that anybody would deliberately copy from a paper so
eminently unsatisfactory.

He also instructed his companions in boxing. His theory was
that the true boxer should never strike where his opponent
expected. His pupils, misunderstanding him, struck Bolder. He complained that
they had not grasped the system; nevertheless, their execution was
admitted to be effective.

His whole youth was spent in doing for people what
they either could have done better for themselves or had
wisely decided not to do at all. He settled quarrels
by introducing new points of dispute; he returned lost dogs
to the wrong owners; he advised boys concerning their choice
of profession, generally recommending the sea to those who were
sick upon ponds, and the Church to those who had
been expelled for profanity.

Such a disposition, unlike measles, does not disappear with maturity.
At thirty-five Bolder was still engaged in improving the condition
of mankind, and mankind was still endeavouring, with imperfect success,
to avoid him.

I first met him upon an omnibus travelling westward on
a wet November evening. The vehicle was crowded, and everyone
was tired enough to desire peace. A stout woman with
three baskets had just paid the conductor sixpence for a
fourpenny fare. The conductor gave her twopence change. The transaction
was simple, complete, and satisfactory to both parties. This was
the moment selected by Bolder for intervention.

"You have given that lady too little," he said.

The conductor looked at the two pennies in the woman's
hand.

"She give me sixpence," he replied, "an' 'er fare's fourpence.
Two from six is two."

"Precisely," said Bolder, "but you are forgetting the child."

There was no child.

"What child?" asked the woman.

"The child who got down at the corner."

"Nobody got down with me," said the woman. "I got
in at the corner."

Bolder smiled patiently. "That is what I mean. The child
got down as you got in."

"Well, what's 'e got to do with me?"

"Possibly nothing," admitted Bolder. "But the conductor charged you for
him."

"I never," said the conductor. "I charged 'er fourpence."

"Then," said Bolder, "you owe her fourpence."

The conductor considered this.

"Look 'ere," he said at last, "if I charged 'er
fourpence, and she give me sixpence, and I give 'er
twopence, where do I owe 'er fourpence?"

"You are confusing the refund with the change," explained Bolder.
"The child's fare was twopence."

"What child?" demanded the woman again.

By this time the passengers had begun to take an
interest. A thin gentleman opposite said that he had seen
a child. A young woman said it had been a
little girl. An elderly man at the end declared that
no child had travelled upon the omnibus, but that a
short person in a cape had alighted near the bank.
The thin gentleman said that was the child. The elderly
man replied that it had a moustache.

"Whether the child had a moustache," said Bolder, "is beside
the point. The principle remains."

The conductor rang the bell and stopped the omnibus.

"Now then," he said, "who's been done?"

"I have," said the stout woman, who had gradually come
to feel that an injustice must have occurred, though she
was doubtful of its nature.

"How much?"

She looked at Bolder.

"Fourpence," he prompted.

"Fourpence," she said.

"Two," corrected the thin gentleman.

"Eight," said the elderly man. "If the person in the
cape was charged from the bank."

The conductor turned upon Bolder.

"Did you pay for the child?"

"I am not personally concerned."

"Then what are you talking about?"

"I am defending this lady's inalienable rights."

"'Er rights is two pennies, and she's got 'em."

The stout woman, discovering that everybody was looking at her
hand, closed it.

"I haven't," she said.

"You 'ave," said the conductor.

"I saw him give them to her," said the young
woman.

"Then he must have taken them back," suggested Bolder.

This wounded the conductor. He accused Bolder of calling him
a thief. Bolder denied the accusation, but added that the
accounts required investigation. The conductor demanded Bolder's name and address.
Bolder offered to take the conductor's. The stout woman said
she wished to go home. The elderly man said nobody
would get home while public vehicles were administered on their
present basis.

A constable arrived. Each person explained the affair to him
separately and at length. The constable understood the conductor to
have charged the child sixpence and given the stout woman
fourpence; he therefore ordered somebody to restore twopence to somebody
else. The thin gentleman paid the money in order to
proceed. Bolder objected that acceptance would prejudice the case. The
stout woman began to cry. Her baskets were removed from
the omnibus, though she remained inside. Then she got out
to fetch them, and the omnibus went on without her.

Bolder sat down beside me, glowing with the consciousness of
duty performed.

"One must interfere," he said. "People are imposed upon because
no one will take the trouble to understand their business."

I agreed that this was frequently the explanation.

He walked with me from the omnibus, came in for
a cup of tea, stayed to supper, and from that
evening regarded me as a friend whose affairs had been
placed, by Providence, under his personal supervision.

A month later he found upon my desk a demand
for water-rate amounting to one pound, seven shillings and fourpence.

"You do not intend to pay this?" he asked.

"I had regarded payment as among the possibilities."

"My dear fellow, look at the assessment."

I looked at it. It appeared much as water assessments
commonly do—severe in tone, obscure in detail, and confident of
legal support.

"They have charged you for a cistern," said Bolder.

"I possess a cistern."

"That is not the point. The question is whether a
cistern constitutes storage."

"It stores water."

"Accidentally. Its primary function is to contain it."

I failed to detect the distinction, but Bolder had already
detected a principle. He explained that thousands of householders were
waiting for one courageous man to resist the tyranny of
the Metropolitan Water Authority. By refusing to pay one pound,
seven shillings and fourpence, I should establish a precedent, purify
local administration, and possibly reduce the burdens of generations yet
unborn.

I asked why he did not undertake the matter himself.

He said his own water was included in his rent.

The first letter he composed on my behalf occupied eight
pages and contained the expressions "arbitrary exaction," "constitutional privilege," and
"the liberties of Englishmen." The Water Authority replied on a
postcard to the effect that the account was correct.

Bolder regarded the postcard as a tactical error.

"They are frightened," he said.

He then advised me to tender one pound, seven shillings
and twopence, thereby admitting the water but denying the cistern.
The Authority returned the money and added costs. Bolder said
this exposed their weakness. At his suggestion I consulted a
solicitor, who listened to the history, looked at the documents,
and advised me to pay.

"Solicitors always advise surrender," said Bolder. "It saves thought."

We found another solicitor. He advised resistance, which Bolder considered
evidence of a powerful legal mind. Proceedings followed. Experts inspected
my premises. One measured the cistern; another photographed it; a
third asked whether it had ever been empty. I replied
that it had, during repairs.

"Excellent!" whispered Bolder. "Then it does not necessarily contain water."

In court he sat behind me and supplied suggestions upon
folded slips of paper. Most were illegible; the remainder were
irrelevant. Our counsel argued that a receptacle which occasionally contained
nothing could not properly be taxed as storage. The Authority's
counsel inquired whether a purse ceased to be a purse
when empty. Our counsel said a purse was movable. The
judge asked whether my cistern was movable. Bolder whispered, "Everything
is movable with sufficient force," and was requested to leave
the court.

Judgment was given against me. With fees, costs, inspections, and
a minor action arising from Bolder's removal, the vindication of
my inalienable rights amounted to forty-eight pounds, eleven shillings and
ninepence. Bolder remained cheerful.

"The point has not been properly tried," he said. "You
should appeal."

Our intimacy cooled after that, though I continued to observe
his public career.

At parties he compelled shy persons to play games. If
eight people were talking comfortably, Bolder divided them into sides,
hid household objects, and made them guess professions by gesture.
He once persuaded a retired undertaker to imitate his former
occupation before a widow who had buried her husband that
morning. He said afterwards that neither had entered into the
spirit of the thing.

At railway stations he was invaluable to elderly women, if
their destination was immaterial. He would seize their luggage, reject
the porter's advice, and install them triumphantly in a train
leaving from another platform. He was always particularly attentive to
women with only five minutes to spare. One lady wishing
to reach Brighton was discovered at Rugby, still clutching a
bun he had bought her for the journey.

Children feared him because he understood them. He approached them
with sudden noises, produced coins from their ears, and asked
what they intended to be when they grew up. If
they cried, he tossed them. If they cried harder, he
tossed them higher. He maintained that babies liked confidence.

In sickness his devotion was beyond praise. He brought grapes
to diabetics, lobster salad to fever patients, and improving books
to people who required sleep. Finding a friend under strict
medical orders to take no alcohol, Bolder persuaded him that
stout was food. The doctor arrived while he was proving
the proposition experimentally.

His excursions were planned with extraordinary completeness. He knew the
precise train, the shortest path, the cheapest inn, and the
hill from which a view could be obtained in clear
weather. The train had usually been discontinued; the path crossed
private land; the inn had lost its licence; and the
weather, resenting organisation, became violent. Yet Bolder never lost heart.
Soaked, hungry, and pursued by a farmer, he would call
upon the party to admit that the day had been
a change.

No wedding within his range escaped assistance. He corrected the
ushers, rearranged the carriages, instructed the photographer, and kept the
bride waiting while he searched for an aunt whom he
had himself sent to the wrong church. At one ceremony
he discovered that the ring was missing. After ten minutes
of anguish it was found in the best man's pocket,
where it had been before Bolder took charge of it.
His explanation was that no system could succeed without cooperation.

He was equally useful at funerals. He possessed a theory
that grief should not be encouraged, and sought to distract
the relatives with cheerful reminiscences. Unfortunately, his memory for identities
was uncertain.

"Well," he said to a bereaved husband, "she had a
happy life, except, of course, for that unfortunate business at
Margate."

There had been no business at Margate.

At another funeral he comforted a mother by observing that
her son's weak chest had always made a long life
improbable. The deceased had been killed by a falling chimney.
Bolder explained that this merely confirmed the uncertainty of human
expectations.

I saw him last in the house of a married
couple who had quarrelled over the colour of a drawing-room
carpet. Before his arrival they had agreed to differ. Within
an hour, owing to his tactful mediation, the husband had
insulted the wife's family, the wife had accused the husband
of secret drinking, the maid had given notice, and the
carpet had been thrown into the street. Bolder came away
saddened but not discouraged.

"They would have separated sooner or later," he told me.
"I have brought matters to a head."

And perhaps that was his true gift. Had he been
employed professionally to create disorder, estrange friends, misdirect travellers, prolong
illness, and increase legal expenses, he might have earned both
wealth and honour. Misfortune lay only in the fact that
he did it all from kindness.

# The Cuban Legacy

Miltonville lay asleep beneath the September sun, not with the
innocent repose of a child, but with the practiced slumber
of an old dog that has long ago discovered which
noises promise dinner and which may safely be ignored. The
courthouse clock struck when it pleased, the Baptist bell when
it was obliged, and the citizens, white and Black, pursued
their separate affairs with that leisurely regularity which they called
custom and outsiders called stagnation.

Nothing happened in Miltonville without having happened there before. A
birth recalled another birth; a marriage revived three old scandals;
a death furnished the town with an afternoon's occupation and
a week's conversation. Even the railway, which had been built
to connect Miltonville with the restless world, had fallen under
the influence of the place and arrived late with such
consistency that punctuality would have been taken as a sign
of disaster.

Thus, when the northbound train came wheezing into the station
on a Tuesday morning precisely eleven minutes before it was
due, Miltonville awakened in alarm.

From the colored waiting room emerged a gentleman whom nobody
remembered having seen before.

Miltonville remembered everybody. It remembered a peddler who had sold
blue spectacles in 1878, a dwarf who had exhibited two
educated geese behind the livery stable, and a pale young
man who had once claimed to tune pianos, though the
town possessed but one instrument and that had resisted him.
Yet nobody remembered this stranger, and therefore everybody looked at
him.

He was tall, broad in the shoulder, full in the
chest, and black as polished walnut. He wore a long
frock coat in spite of the heat, gray trousers, boots
of a brilliant and deliberate shine, and a silk hat
that seemed less a covering than a proclamation. A gold-headed
cane rested beneath one arm. In one gloved hand he
carried a leather valise; beneath the other he bore a
rectangular parcel wrapped in brown paper, crossed with red cord,
and sealed at every knot with crimson wax.

The station loungers rose without knowing why. Mr. Amos Pritchard,
who had been sitting on an express crate and chewing
tobacco with the steady melancholy of a man performing a
public duty, forgot to spit.

The stranger surveyed the platform, removed his hat, and bowed.

"Gentlemen," he said, in a voice deep enough to have
belonged to the courthouse itself, "may I inquire whether I
have the honor of addressing citizens of Miltonville?"

The loungers glanced at one another. They were citizens of
Miltonville every day, but they had never before been addressed
as if citizenship were a decoration.

"You have, suh," said Amos at last.

"I am gratified beyond expression."

This appeared to be good news, and the loungers accepted
it modestly.

"My name," continued the stranger, "is Joseph Augustus Scatters. I
come from Havana, by way of New Orleans, Mobile, and
the providence of God. My mission is private, delicate, and
honorable. I seek Mr. Isaac Jackson."

At this, the white station agent, who had been listening
through the ticket window, came out upon the platform. Isaac
Jackson was a colored carpenter, church deacon, property owner, and
payer of taxes. He possessed a neat frame house on
Willow Street, a wife named Martha Ann, and enough respectability
to make him an object of satisfaction to the white
citizens and of criticism to a few of his own
neighbors.

"What you want with Isaac?" asked Amos.

Scatters lowered his voice, though not so far that it
failed to reach the station agent.

"My business concerns an estate."

Now, the word *estate* enjoys a dignity denied to ordinary
property. A mule is a mule, a house is a
house, and money is money; but combine them under the
authority of death and the supervision of lawyers, and they
become an estate. Miltonville leaned nearer.

"A very considerable estate," Scatters added.

Someone was sent running for Isaac.

The messenger found him planing the edge of a coffin
in his shop behind Willow Street. Isaac was an earnest,
narrow-faced man whose spectacles lent him the appearance of continually
inspecting the world for faulty workmanship. He listened, laid down
his plane, removed his apron, put on his coat, and
went to the station accompanied by the messenger, two apprentices,
and six persons who had acquired the news along the
route.

Scatters advanced to meet him.

"Mr. Isaac Jackson?"

"I am."

Scatters clasped his hand in both of his own and
held it solemnly.

"My dear sir, I bring you tidings mingled of sorrow
and consolation. Your brother is dead."

Isaac's mouth opened. For twenty-three years he had spoken of
his brother Jeremiah as dead whenever Martha Ann complained that
Jeremiah never wrote; but this had been a domestic conclusion
rather than established fact.

"Jeremiah?"

"The same. Jeremiah Elias Jackson, lately of Havana, Cuba."

"You knowed him?"

"I had the privilege of serving him in confidential affairs
during the closing years of his distinguished and prosperous life."

The word *prosperous* passed through the listeners like an electric
current.

Isaac's eyes moved to the sealed package.

"He done well, then?"

Scatters looked toward the sky, as if asking Jeremiah's spirit
for permission to reveal how well he had done.

"Your brother," he said, "was a man of extraordinary commercial
genius. Beginning as a laborer upon the docks, he rose
by sobriety, courage, and the respect of influential gentlemen to
become interested in tobacco, shipping, sugar, and certain enterprises which
delicacy forbids me to specify. He died possessed of substance."

"How much substance?" asked Amos Pritchard.

Scatters turned upon him a gaze so courteous that Amos
felt himself rebuked by a cathedral.

"I should not presume, sir, to discuss the dimensions of
a private fortune upon a railway platform."

He tapped the parcel with one gloved finger.

"But as executor's representative, I am authorized to deliver to
Mr. Isaac Jackson a legacy of five thousand dollars."

For a moment Miltonville heard the ticking of the station
clock.

Five thousand dollars was not beyond the town's imagination, but
it was beyond its experience. Major Beauregard Taliaferro, who owned
the largest house in the county, was said to have
possessed ten thousand dollars before the war; yet this sum
existed chiefly in recollection, which, unlike a bank, pays handsome
interest. Five thousand dollars in present money was a fortune
solid enough to buy land, teams, lumber, respect, relatives, enemies,
and perhaps a seat close to the front of heaven.

Isaac removed his spectacles, polished them, replaced them, and looked
at the parcel again.

"Five thousand?"

"Five thousand dollars, less no fee, charge, commission, duty, tariff,
gratuity, or expense whatsoever. Your brother's final words to me
were, 'Mr. Scatters, see that Isaac receives every penny.' Sir,
I stand before you in obedience to the dead."

Martha Ann arrived in time to hear this declaration. She
was a large woman who wore her Sunday bonnet on
weekdays whenever there was a chance that Providence might call
without warning. Having pushed through the crowd, she seized Isaac's
arm.

"Is it true?"

"Madam," said Scatters, removing his hat again, "if truth possessed
a bodily form, it could wear no countenance more sacred
than that of a dying brother."

Martha Ann had not understood the sentence, but its grandeur
convinced her.

"Praise the Lord!"

The crowd murmured agreement.

There remained, however, a difficulty. Scatters explained that the will
had been certified in Cuba, translated in New Orleans, and
transmitted according to treaty; but before the parcel could legally
pass into Isaac's possession, the county court must recognize certain
seals. The judge would return on Thursday. Until then the
legacy must be lodged in a secure place.

Miltonville had one bank, but the bank received colored deposits
through a side window and did not encourage colored legacies
of international origin. Mr. Silas Gedge, proprietor of the principal
grocery, possessed a safe large enough for his accounts, two
sacks of silver, a pistol, and whatever confidence the neighborhood
had not deposited elsewhere. He volunteered its use.

The procession moved from the station to Gedge's store. Scatters
walked beside Isaac; Martha Ann followed beside the parcel; and
the population increased at every corner. By the time they
reached the grocery, children had been dismissed from no school
but were present nevertheless.

Mr. Gedge opened the safe with ceremonial reluctance. A safe
commands respect chiefly because it is closed; once opened, it
resembles any other iron box, and its owner feels exposed.

Scatters raised the parcel.

"Before these witnesses," he proclaimed, "I deposit the legacy of
the late Jeremiah Elias Jackson, valued at five thousand dollars,
pending the sanction of the honorable court."

He placed it inside. Gedge shut the door, spun the
dial, tested the handle twice, and put the combination into
his pocket, where everybody could see that it was safe.

Then Scatters turned to the crowd.

"Ladies and gentlemen, the nature of this affair compels me
to request your strictest secrecy."

By dinner, there was not a soul within five miles
who did not know that Isaac Jackson had inherited five
thousand dollars; by supper, the sum had become eight thousand;
and by bedtime, Jeremiah Jackson was remembered as the owner
of half the island of Cuba.

Secrecy, like seed corn, multiplies wonderfully when distributed among neighbors.

Scatters became Isaac's guest. The Jackson house was small, but
wealth enlarges the rooms of those who expect it. Martha
Ann put clean curtains in the spare chamber, borrowed a
washstand from her sister, and moved a portrait of Abraham
Lincoln from the parlor because she thought a gentleman from
Cuba might prefer something less political. She served fried chicken
on Wednesday, ham on Thursday, and chicken again on Friday,
there being few forms of honor which cannot be expressed
by poultry.

The court, unfortunately, did not sit on Thursday. Judge Wren
had been detained by a horse with principles of its
own. Friday brought a missing document. Saturday was not a
legal day for such business; Sunday belonged to the Lord;
Monday revealed an error in the translation; and Tuesday required
a certificate from New Orleans.

At each delay Scatters sighed with dignity.

"Law," he observed, "is a noble temple, my dear Isaac,
but its staircase is long."

Isaac agreed. A man waiting for five thousand dollars can
forgive the law much if the law keeps his money
respectable while detaining it.

Meanwhile, Scatters conquered Miltonville.

At the African Methodist church he delivered a discourse upon
"The Advancement of Our People Under the Twin Lamps of
Education and Character." At the Baptist church he spoke of
Moses. At the schoolhouse he examined the children in geography
and informed them that Cuba was a jewel upon the
bosom of the Caribbean. He quoted Shakespeare, the Constitution, the
book of Job, and two Latin expressions which nobody could
contradict. When challenged to explain the tariff, he explained it
for forty minutes, and Miltonville, having understood nothing, judged the
explanation profound.

His English was remarkable. Each sentence wore a stiff collar,
buttoned at the throat, and walked as if balancing a
book upon its head. Common speech bends, lounges, scratches itself,
and puts its feet upon the chair; Scatters' conversation sat
upright in a conversational splint. Yet the effect was impressive,
especially upon people who believed grammar to be a form
of property.

The women admired his manners. The men admired the admiration
of the women, though with less enthusiasm. Young persons copied
the angle at which he carried his cane. Old persons
approved his respect for old persons. He listened to every
story as though Congress had suspended business to hear the
conclusion, and when he laughed, he did so in three
rich notes, precisely enough to reward the teller without encouraging
him to continue.

White Miltonville heard of him soon enough. Major Beauregard Taliaferro
encountered Scatters outside the post office, where Isaac performed the
introduction.

The Major was a tall, silver-haired relic of a more
decorative age. He had inherited two hundred acres, a ruined
gin house, six silver spoons, an empty title, and memories
ample enough to support the family if memories could have
been mortgaged. He wore a broad hat and spoke of
Virginia as though he had personally invented it.

"Cuba, suh?" said the Major.

"Havana, Major."

"Fine city in its day. My uncle, Colonel Ambrose Taliaferro,
visited there in fifty-eight."

"I have heard the name pronounced with respect."

The Major straightened.

"You knew of him?"

"Who in the cultivated circles of the Caribbean has not
heard of the Taliaferros of Virginia?"

No cultivated circle in the Caribbean had ever performed this
duty; but the Major's face shone like a lamp newly
filled.

"You must dine with us, suh."

Scatters bowed.

"I could desire no greater privilege than to break bread
beneath a roof honored by so illustrious a lineage."

Thus he dined with the Taliaferros, attended tea at the
judge's house, and spent an evening discussing imperial policy with
Mr. Gedge, who had never before possessed one. His fame
crossed the racial boundary which Miltonville maintained in all practical
matters and ignored whenever entertainment required it. White gentlemen did
not receive colored strangers socially, as a rule; but rules,
like fences, are most admired by those who know where
the gate stands.

The first request for money came on the fourth night.

Isaac and Scatters sat in the parlor after Martha Ann
had gone to bed. A kerosene lamp smoked between them.
Scatters had been speaking tenderly of Jeremiah—his generosity, his lonely
exile, his last illness, his repeated longing for the red
clay of home. Isaac wiped his spectacles several times.

At length Scatters grew silent.

"My dear friend," he said, "I am embarrassed."

Isaac waited. Money itself was not yet his, but embarrassment
connected with money had already begun to treat him as
an equal.

"In my haste to execute your brother's wishes, I neglected
to exchange sufficient funds in New Orleans. I possess Cuban
notes in abundance, but local merchants display a provincial reluctance
to honor them."

"How much you need?"

"Merely fifty dollars until the court releases your legacy. I
would not ask it, except that certain telegraphic expenses—"

"You ain't got to explain."

Isaac had thirty-eight dollars in the house and borrowed twelve
from Martha Ann, who asked why he required it. He
said only that international business had expenses.

"Mr. Scatters said not to mention it," he added.

Martha Ann promised silence and went next morning to tell
her sister privately.

Two days later Scatters borrowed twenty dollars from the Reverend
Caleb Sweet, whose confidence he secured by describing the spiritual
needs of Havana. From Amos Pritchard he obtained fifteen for
a remittance to New Orleans. From Mrs. Louisa Bell, a
widow with a little money saved from laundry work, he
borrowed twelve dollars to pay a notary's fee. From Henry
Crowder, barber and lodge secretary, he obtained twenty-five upon the
confidential assurance that Henry was the only man in Miltonville
whose business judgment he trusted.

He borrowed from each person in a different manner, but
always with the same sorrowful reluctance and the same mention
of Cuban currency. He asked secrecy, not for himself, but
to preserve Isaac from harmful rumor. Each lender felt honored.
To be deceived privately is often more flattering than to
be told the truth in public.

It might have continued for some time had not human
vanity possessed a fatal need to compare decorations.

Henry Crowder mentioned at the barber shop that a gentleman
of international affairs had consulted him upon confidential financial matters.
Amos Pritchard, who was waiting for a shave, remarked that
some people considered twenty-five dollars important.

"How you know it was twenty-five?" Henry asked.

"I don't know nothin' about your twenty-five. I knows what
I lent."

"What you lent?"

The razor stopped halfway down Amos's cheek.

The Reverend Sweet, sitting near the stove, cleared his throat.

"Brethren, there may be an innocent explanation."

Henry turned.

"Reverend, he got money from you too?"

The Reverend's expression furnished the answer.

Within twenty minutes Mrs. Bell had been summoned from across
the street; within forty, Isaac had been informed; and within
an hour, six loans amounting to one hundred and forty-seven
dollars had assembled themselves in Gedge's grocery and begun to
look suspiciously at one another.

Isaac stood in the middle of the store, pale with
anger and shame. A man may bear the loss of
money with fortitude; it is the loss of superiority that
breaks his back. Yesterday he had been heir to five
thousand dollars and host to a distinguished traveler. Today Amos
Pritchard was looking at him as one looks at a
ladder that has collapsed under somebody else.

"He told me his money was Cuban," Isaac said.

"He told all of us that," said Mrs. Bell.

"Maybe it is," offered the Reverend.

"Then let him show it," Henry replied.

Scatters could not be found. He had left the Jackson
house after breakfast, carrying his cane but not his valise.
A boy had seen him near the station. Another boy
had seen him walking toward the river. A third, who
disliked being excluded from useful testimony, had seen him in
both places.

All eyes turned toward the safe.

Mr. Gedge planted himself before it.

"That parcel was deposited pending order of court."

"It belongs to me," said Isaac.

"Not yet, according to Mr. Scatters."

"Mr. Scatters is a liar."

This was the first concise description anybody had offered of
him, and its very simplicity seemed almost disrespectful.

The crowd increased. News ran through the colored quarter, crossed
Market Street, entered kitchens, workshops, porches, and yards, and returned
accompanied by women with aprons still on and men who
had left tools where they fell. White citizens gathered at
the rear of the store, not because the affair concerned
them, but because concern has never been the price of
admission to a spectacle.

At last Deputy Sheriff Larkin arrived with County Clerk Baines.
The clerk examined the seals, the red cord, the handwriting,
and the expectant faces.

"As clerk," he said, "I can't authorize the opening."

"What can you authorize?" Henry demanded.

"I can authorize waiting for Judge Wren."

"When he coming?"

"Tomorrow, perhaps."

A groan rose.

Isaac stepped forward.

"Silas, open that safe."

Gedge hesitated. He enjoyed possession of the mystery and hated
surrendering it. Yet a grocer knows the force of customers
gathered in a body.

He turned the dial.

The room became silent enough to hear the mechanism click.
Gedge opened the iron door. There lay the parcel, solemn,
square, and sealed, looking worthier of belief than any man
present.

The clerk cut the red cord. He broke the wax.
He unfolded the brown paper, then a layer of oiled
cloth, then another paper wrapper, then a sheet bearing official-looking
marks. The crowd leaned with each movement until it seemed
the entire town might fall into the safe.

Inside were newspapers.

Nothing more.

Old newspapers, folded to the dimensions of bank notes and
weighted with two pieces of cardboard.

No money. No bonds. No Cuban certificate. No will.

Newspapers.

For several seconds nobody spoke. Astonishment, when complete, has no
vocabulary.

Mrs. Bell gave a low cry. Isaac seized the heap
and tore through it, as though five thousand dollars might
be hiding in an editorial. Amos searched the wrapping. Henry
shook the oiled cloth. The Reverend Sweet inspected the cardboard
with theological severity.

Mr. Gedge, whose safe had been made ridiculous, became the
angriest man in the room.

"Fetch the sheriff!"

They found Joseph Scatters at the railway station, seated upon
his valise and reading a timetable.

He did not run. Running would have wrinkled his dignity
and, besides, the next train was not due for three
hours.

Deputy Larkin approached with half Miltonville behind him.

"Joseph Scatters, I arrest you for obtaining money under false
pretenses."

Scatters folded the timetable.

"Deputy, I submit to the majesty of the law."

"You got any weapons?"

"Only reason and eloquence."

"Hand over the cane anyway."

The jail stood behind the courthouse, and before sunset Scatters
occupied its cleanest cell. Crowds passed the barred window. Some
cursed him. Some demanded their money. Some merely stared, unwilling
to abandon completely the splendid gentleman they had admired that
morning.

He bowed to Mrs. Bell when she came.

"Madam, I regret your distress."

"You stole twelve dollars from me."

"I borrowed it."

"You lied to get it."

"That point, madam, belongs to the court."

"May the Lord deal with you."

"On that point," said Scatters, "I shall request a postponement."

His trial was fixed for Friday. No one knew how
it came to be fixed so quickly, since Miltonville law
ordinarily advanced with the stately caution of a cow approaching
a bridge. But public embarrassment is a swift horse, and
many citizens wished to see Scatters punished before the story
reached neighboring towns.

By nine o'clock Friday morning the courthouse was full.

The Black citizens of Miltonville packed the rear benches and
stood along the walls. Isaac sat near the prosecutor with
Martha Ann beside him, rigid in her best bonnet. Henry
Crowder, Amos Pritchard, Reverend Sweet, and Mrs. Bell waited to
testify. White citizens occupied the front benches. Major Taliaferro sat
within the rail near Judge Wren. Mr. Gedge had closed
his store, which suggested that justice, for one morning, might
compete successfully with trade.

The jury consisted of twelve white men: farmers, merchants, a
mill owner, and one gentleman who described his occupation as
"family interests." Nearly every one had praised Scatters during the
previous week. This made them severe at first, for men
are least merciful toward those who have caused them to
look foolish.

Scatters entered between two deputies.

He wore the same frock coat, freshly brushed. Someone had
supplied him with a white collar. He carried no cane,
but he walked as though a procession followed.

"Have you counsel?" asked Judge Wren.

"I shall represent myself, Your Honor."

"You understand the seriousness of the charge?"

"I understand both the seriousness of the charge and the
solemnity of the tribunal."

Judge Wren adjusted his face to conceal the pleasure this
gave him.

The prosecution's case was simple. Isaac testified to the package
and the promise. The lenders described the requests for money.
Mr. Gedge recounted the deposit in his safe. The clerk
produced the newspapers. Deputy Larkin told of the arrest.

Scatters cross-examined each witness with exquisite courtesy.

"Mr. Jackson, did I compel you to lend me fifty
dollars?"

"No."

"Did I threaten you?"

"No."

"Did I not tell you that law is uncertain?"

"You said the court would release my five thousand."

"Did I state the exact hour?"

"No, but—"

"Thank you."

To Reverend Sweet he said, "Reverend, did you not lend
me money after hearing my remarks upon missionary opportunity in
Cuba?"

"I did."

"Did those remarks contain spiritual value?"

"That ain't the point."

"Yet they had value?"

The Reverend hesitated. A preacher cannot easily declare a sermon
valueless merely because somebody else delivered it.

"Some value," he admitted.

Mrs. Bell proved less manageable.

"You told me you had Cuban money."

"I did."

"Did you?"

"I have had Cuban money at several periods of my
life."

"You said you had it then."

"Madam, I spoke of my financial condition in broad and
sympathetic terms."

"You spoke a lie in a fine coat."

Laughter moved through the room until Judge Wren struck his
gavel. Scatters bowed to Mrs. Bell as though she had
scored a point in a formal debate.

When the prosecution rested, Judge Wren asked whether he wished
to testify.

"I do."

Scatters took the witness chair, swore to tell the truth,
and gave his name.

The prosecutor approached.

"Did you come to Miltonville with a parcel containing newspapers?"

"I did."

"Did you represent that parcel as containing five thousand dollars
belonging to Isaac Jackson?"

"I permitted that belief to flourish."

"Did you tell him his brother had died wealthy in
Cuba?"

"I spoke of his brother's death and of wealth in
close association."

"Was any part of the tale true?"

"His brother may be dead. Cuba certainly exists."

A murmur ran through the courtroom.

"Did you borrow money from these people while pretending to
possess Cuban funds?"

"I borrowed money from persons who considered me distinguished."

"Answer the question."

"I have answered its substance, sir."

"Did you intend to repay them?"

Scatters paused.

"Intention is a traveler whose destination changes with the weather."

The prosecutor faced the jury.

"That is all."

Judge Wren leaned forward. "Mr. Scatters, you may make your
argument."

Scatters rose slowly. For a moment he said nothing. He
looked at Isaac, at Martha Ann, at the witnesses, at
the packed benches, at the jury, and finally at the
judge. Silence, which ordinary men endure, was to Scatters an
instrument; he tuned it before he played.

"Your Honor," he began, "gentlemen of the jury, citizens of
this ancient and honorable community: I stand before you accused
of theft by false pretense. The facts have been unfolded.
I shall not insult your intelligence by disputing what your
eyes have seen or what your ears have heard.

"I brought a parcel. The parcel contained paper. I spoke
of wealth. I borrowed money. These things are true.

"But truth, gentlemen, is not a stone lying solitary in
a field. Truth is a building, and a building must
be viewed from every side before judgment pronounces it a
palace or a prison."

The prosecutor frowned. Judge Wren did not.

"What did I take from these good people? One hundred
and forty-seven dollars. What did I give them? Hope, distinction,
excitement, conversation, importance. For one week Miltonville ceased to sleep.
For one week Mr. Isaac Jackson was not merely a
carpenter but the chosen heir of a merchant prince. For
one week his hospitable roof sheltered a representative of foreign
commerce. Men greeted him who had passed him silently for
twenty years. Women envied Mrs. Jackson's curtains. Children pointed at
his house. His name traveled beyond the county. Tell me,
gentlemen—is publicity without price? Is dignity without price? Is a
dream without price?"

Isaac half rose, but Martha Ann pulled him down.

"Reverend Sweet lent me twenty dollars. In return he received
an image of himself as the patron of missions across
the sea. Mr. Crowder lent me twenty-five and became, for
an afternoon, my confidential adviser in matters of international finance.
Mr. Pritchard purchased the privilege of hinting that he possessed
private information. Mrs. Bell—whose courage and intelligence I freely acknowledge—paid
twelve dollars for a lesson she will remember longer than
any hat, shawl, or household ornament she could have bought
with that sum."

Mrs. Bell's eyes flashed.

"I didn't ask for your lesson."

"No, madam. Humanity seldom requests instruction in advance."

Laughter came from the white benches. It did not come
from the rear.

Scatters turned toward the jury.

"Was there greed? I do not condemn it. The desire
for increase is the engine of civilization. Was there vanity?
I do not despise it. Vanity builds monuments, founds colleges,
supports churches, and causes men to wash their faces before
breakfast. Was there credulity? Gentlemen, faith itself is credulity made
respectable by the quality of its object.

"These citizens saw a sealed package and believed it full
of money. Why? Because they desired it to be full.
They heard of a rich man in Cuba and believed
him. Why? Because distant wealth is the most agreeable form
of wealth: it may be multiplied without examination. They looked
upon my coat, my voice, my manners, and my grammar,
and they surrendered not to falsehood, but to their own
imagination.

"Am I alone guilty?"

The question hung in the room.

"If I sold them moonshine bottled as medicine, punish me.
If I stole their savings while they slept, punish me.
If I put a pistol to honest labor and demanded
its wages, punish me. But I offered a performance, and
they applauded. I displayed a mirror, and they mistook the
reflection for treasure. They did not lend to Joseph Scatters,
a stranger. They lent to the noble personage they had
created. Shall the actor be imprisoned because the audience admired
the costume?"

The prosecutor rose. "Your Honor, this is no legal argument."

"Sit down," said Judge Wren. "He'll come to the law."

Scatters bowed.

"Your Honor's instinct for justice is matched only by your
patience."

Judge Wren's mouth softened.

"And now, gentlemen, I do come to the law—not merely
the law printed in books, but that greater law engraved
upon the traditions of this commonwealth."

His voice changed. It became warmer, slower, touched with sorrow.
He no longer spoke to the courtroom as it was,
with dusty windows, scarred benches, and a ceiling stained by
rain. He spoke to an imaginary hall of white columns,
ancestral portraits, silver swords, and gentlemen whose debts had long
since been forgiven because their names were remembered.

"I have traveled through many lands. I have seen wealth
without grace, authority without mercy, and law without honor. But
here—here in this Southern country—I find surviving that chivalric spirit
which once made the name of Virginia a trumpet of
courage and the name of Carolina a pledge of courtesy."

Major Taliaferro lifted his head.

"I see before me descendants of families whose sons rode
where duty called and whose daughters kept faith beside darkened
hearths. I see a Wren upon the bench."

Judge Wren blinked.

"Your Honor's grandfather, if history speaks correctly, was Colonel Josiah
Wren, who refused to abandon his wounded men at Seven
Pines."

The Judge's grandfather had been at Seven Pines, though in
a wagon containing flour. Time had promoted him.

"I see upon the jury a Cabell, a Mercer, a
Dabney, a Fitzhugh."

Four jurors straightened. One was not a Fitzhugh but had
a Fitzhugh grandmother, which served even better.

"I see Mr. Harlan, whose father opened his smokehouse to
hungry families during the terrible winter of sixty-five. I see
Mr. Poindexter, of a line that gave jurists to the
bench and soldiers to the field. I see Mr. Bland,
whose honored name has never been separated from fair dealing."

Mr. Bland, who sold fertilizer, looked overcome.

"These names are not merely names. They are guardians. They
stand unseen beside you. They ask whether justice shall be
reduced to arithmetic; whether mercy shall perish beneath technicality; whether
the gallant Bourbon spirit of the old South shall say
to a stranger, 'You amused us, you awakened us, you
revealed folly without bloodshed—therefore we shall cage you like a
thief.'"

The Black citizens at the rear began to stir. Scatters
heard them and raised his voice.

"I do not appeal to prejudice. God forbid! I appeal
above prejudice—to magnanimity. The weak man seeks revenge when embarrassed;
the great man smiles. The little community punishes the jester
who has exposed it; the noble community acknowledges the jest
and profits by the lesson.

"Gentlemen, I am Black. You are white. Let no man
say that I conceal the fact or seek advantage from
it. If my color condemns me, pronounce sentence and let
history record it. But if the blood of cavaliers still
moves within you; if the ancient virtues of courage, generosity,
humor, and mercy are not dead phrases carved upon neglected
stones; if a gentleman may yet recognize audacity even in
an adversary—then judge me not as a parcel, but as
a man."

He placed one hand upon his breast.

"I have taken money. I have given knowledge. The money
may be earned again. The knowledge will endure.

"Mr. Jackson now knows that inheritance without labor is a
dangerous dream. Reverend Sweet knows that eloquence and righteousness are
cousins who do not always inhabit the same house. Mr.
Crowder knows that confidential information is most confidential before it
is repeated. Mr. Pritchard knows that secrecy announced in public
has already packed its trunk. Mrs. Bell—wisest of them all—knows
that a liar may speak correct English."

Even Mrs. Bell's anger bent for an instant beneath the
accuracy of this.

Scatters spread his hands.

"If this lesson is worth nothing, convict me. If Miltonville
is poorer because I came, convict me. If your own
hearts contain no vanity, no appetite for wonder, no tenderness
toward splendid words, convict me.

"But if you, too, have sometimes believed because belief was
pleasant; if you, too, have bowed before a coat, a
title, a family name, a sealed package, a magnificent promise—then
remember that humanity sits beside me at the bar."

His final words dropped almost to a whisper.

"Gentlemen, I do not ask acquittal for Joseph Scatters. I
ask it for the frailty we share."

He sat down.

For several seconds the courthouse remained still. Then Major Taliaferro
struck his cane upon the floor and cried, "By heaven,
suh!"

Judge Wren rapped weakly for order.

The prosecutor attempted a reply. He spoke of statutes, evidence,
intent, property, and the necessity of protecting citizens from fraud.
His argument was correct in every particular and dead before
it reached the jury. Law had come in working clothes
after vanity had entered wearing a sword.

Judge Wren delivered instructions. He reminded the jurors that eloquence
was not evidence, that ancestry was not law, and that
false representation used to obtain money constituted an offense. Unfortunately,
his voice retained the glow with which he had heard
of Colonel Wren at Seven Pines.

The jury rose.

The foreman cleared his throat.

"Your Honor, I reckon we don't need to go out."

"Have you reached a verdict?"

"We have."

"How do you find?"

"Not guilty."

A roar burst from the rear benches—not of celebration, but
anger.

Isaac sprang up. Henry Crowder cursed aloud. Reverend Sweet demanded
justice. Mrs. Bell stood silent, and her silence was fiercer
than the rest. The white spectators applauded until Judge Wren
threatened to clear the room, though he showed no equal
haste in suppressing the protest behind them.

Scatters bowed to the jury.

"I shall strive to remain worthy of your confidence."

"You ain't got ours!" shouted Amos Pritchard.

Outside, word of the acquittal passed through the waiting crowd.
Men who had been unable to enter filled the courthouse
yard. Women gathered at the gate. The anger there was
not merely for one hundred and forty-seven dollars. It was
for the spectacle of plain evidence vanishing beneath fine phrases;
for the discovery that fraud became wit when it entertained
the right men; for the old, familiar lesson that justice
might be blind, but she could still recognize a family
name.

Deputy Larkin looked through the window and whistled.

"Mr. Scatters," he said, "I wouldn't advise you to walk
to the station."

Scatters glanced outside.

"Public opinion appears to have divided."

"It looks united to me."

Major Taliaferro came forward.

"This man has been acquitted by a jury of his
peers."

"Not ours," said Isaac.

The Major ignored him.

"The dignity of the court must be upheld. We cannot
permit mob violence in Miltonville."

There had been times when Major Taliaferro regarded a mob
as the spontaneous voice of civilization, but those mobs had
selected other objects. Principle, like a coat, is judged partly
by whom it fits.

He ordered his carriage brought around to the side entrance.
Two deputies climbed to the driver's box, and four white
men from the jury volunteered to ride beside it. Scatters
received his valise and gold-headed cane, which Deputy Larkin returned
with some regret.

As he passed Isaac, he stopped.

"My friend—"

"I ain't your friend."

"No," Scatters said, and for the first time his voice
lacked ornament. "I suppose not."

Mrs. Bell stepped between them.

"You going to pay us?"

Scatters looked at her. Beyond the windows the crowd pressed
against the iron fence; behind him stood the Major, the
judge, the jurors, and the law.

"Madam," he said quietly, "today I have no Cuban currency."

Then the magnificence returned. He put on his silk hat,
took the Major's offered arm, and descended by the side
stairs.

The crowd surged when it saw him. Deputies pushed people
back. Voices rose—demands, curses, appeals, names of those who had
lost money. Scatters entered the carriage and sat upright, one
gloved hand resting upon his cane. Major Taliaferro mounted beside
him.

"Drive on!" the Major commanded.

The horses started. Guards walked at either side. The carriage
moved through the shouting crowd, past Gedge's grocery and its
offended safe, past the church where Scatters had spoken of
character, past Isaac's house with its clean curtains, and down
the road to the station.

The southbound train arrived twenty-six minutes late, restoring the natural
order of Miltonville.

Scatters stepped aboard. From the platform he removed his hat
to Major Taliaferro.

"Major, the traditions of your house have this day been
nobly sustained."

The Major flushed with pride.

"Godspeed, suh."

The conductor called. Steam rose. The engine coughed, snorted, and
dragged its cars away toward Mobile and the wider world.

Miltonville watched until the last carriage disappeared.

Then it turned homeward to calculate its losses.

For a time the town remained angry. Isaac refused to
speak of Cuba. Martha Ann took down the spare-room curtains
and declared them faded. Reverend Sweet preached upon wolves in
fine clothing. Henry Crowder insisted that he had been suspicious
from the beginning. Amos Pritchard reduced his loan from fifteen
dollars to ten whenever he told the story. Mr. Gedge
bought a new safe and trusted it no more than
the old one. Mrs. Bell alone altered nothing in the
account. She had lost twelve dollars, she said, and no
amount of eloquence would make it eleven.

White Miltonville remembered the trial differently. Judge Wren quoted portions
of the speech at dinners. The jurors came to believe
they had performed an act of historic mercy. Major Taliaferro
spoke often of the extraordinary Black orator whom he had
saved from a mob, though he never mentioned what had
caused the mob to assemble.

In time, even the sealed package grew richer in recollection.
Some said it had contained Confederate newspapers of great rarity.
Others claimed that Scatters had hidden the real five thousand
dollars in his valise. A few insisted he was Jeremiah
Jackson himself, returned from Cuba to test his brother's character.
Miltonville preferred these inventions because truth, after exposing a community,
seldom receives permanent hospitality there.

Years later, when the courthouse had a new roof and
the railway no longer stopped except by request, old citizens
still told of Joseph Augustus Scatters: how he arrived in
a silk hat, how he made Isaac Jackson rich for
a week, how he sold Miltonville its own vanity, and
how twelve solemn men acquitted him because he remembered their
grandfathers better than they remembered justice.

The town laughed whenever the story was told.

Mrs. Bell did not.

And somewhere beyond Miltonville, upon another platform beneath another drowsy
sun, a tall gentleman may have descended from a wheezing
train with a sealed parcel beneath his arm, five thousand
dollars upon his tongue, and humanity waiting—hopeful, respectable, and eager
to be deceived.

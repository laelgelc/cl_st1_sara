# The Book of Canon Alberic

In the autumn of 1883—on the 12 th of September,
to be exact—my friend Edward Dennistoun arrived at St Bertrand
de Comminges, in the Haute-Garonne, with the intention of spending
two days upon an examination of the cathedral and, if
circumstances permitted, a third among the muniments of certain neighbouring
religious houses.

The expedition formed part of a larger journey, undertaken at
the expense of neither college nor learned society (a circumstance
which Dennistoun afterward had occasion to regret), for the purpose
of cataloguing such examples of pre-thirteenth-century glass and manuscript ornament
as had escaped the rather undiscriminating attentions of collectors under
the Empire. Of the results of that journey I need
say little. His notes upon the capitals at Moissac are
now at Cambridge; his drawings of the windows at Auch
have been several times reproduced; and the small ivory diptych,
which he acquired for forty francs at Toulouse and sold
twenty years later for a sum I shall not name,
remains a grievance with his executors.

St Bertrand, though magnificently situated, was not in 1883 a
place calculated to detain any traveller who lacked an ecclesiological
motive. A steep road ascended from the lower village between
walls of loose grey stone. The houses above were few,
shuttered and, in several cases, ruinous. One large mansion, displaying
the remains of Renaissance carving above its boarded doorway, appeared
never to have recovered from the Revolution. Grass pushed between
the paving-stones; a mountain stream sounded perpetually below the walls;
and over all rose the cathedral, disproportionately solemn, like a
great ship stranded upon a deserted shore.

Dennistoun reached the place shortly before noon and established himself
at the only inn which offered anything resembling accommodation. Its
sign bore, or had once borne, the effigy of St
Bertrand; rain and time had reduced the saint to a
mitre, one hand and a patch of blue. Having eaten
an omelette of unusual toughness, Dennistoun took his camera, notebook
and measuring tape, and went up to the cathedral.

The sacristan was waiting within the south door.

He was a small, bent man, very old, but not—as
Dennistoun at first supposed—deaf. His face had the yellowish transparency
seen in persons who have spent the greater part of
their lives in churches; his coat was rusty black, his
linen scrupulously clean, and upon his head he wore a
round cap of brown wool. He carried a ring of
keys large enough to have satisfied St Peter.

"You desire to see the treasury, monsieur?"

"In due course," said Dennistoun. "First, if you please, the
choir and the stalls."

The old man bowed. Yet before turning he glanced sharply
over his shoulder, not toward the open door, but into
the dim north aisle. There was nothing there. Dennistoun, whose
eyes were already occupied with the arrangement of the nave,
thought no more of the matter.

The examination began. The sacristan knew his building well and
had names, dates and anecdotes ready for everything. He indicated
the tomb of St Bertrand, the curious misericords, the episcopal
vestments, a chalice attributed without sufficient reason to Clement V,
and a fragment of embroidery said to have been worked
by a queen of Navarre. He spoke in a low
but distinct voice and displayed, beneath the ceremonial phrases proper
to his office, a certain shrewdness.

Only one peculiarity disturbed his usefulness. At irregular intervals he
stopped, listened and looked behind him. Once, while unlocking a
side-chapel, he crouched so suddenly that the keys struck the
pavement. On another occasion, when a pigeon fluttered in the
clerestory, he turned white and pressed himself against a pillar.

"You are unwell?" Dennistoun asked.

"No, monsieur. It is nothing."

"You heard something?"

"The birds make noises here."

"Surely that was not a bird."

The sacristan crossed himself.

"Old buildings have many voices, monsieur."

Dennistoun accepted the answer as one accepts a local superstition,
for the sake of proceeding. They entered the choir. Here
the antiquary's attention was soon absorbed by several pieces of
carving which had escaped description in the authorities he possessed.
He measured them, made sketches and argued—not altogether silently—with an
opinion of Viollet-le-Duc. The sacristan stood by, glancing from Dennistoun
to the darkness beneath the stalls.

At length Dennistoun asked whether there were manuscripts.

"Some service books, monsieur."

"Early?"

"One is of the fifteenth century. The others are later."

"Nothing from the old chapter library?"

The sacristan hesitated.

"Nothing belonging to the church."

The appellation struck Dennistoun as curious, but he did not
pursue it then. They went into the treasury, which proved
disappointing, and afterwards climbed a stair in the north-west tower
to inspect a chamber containing fragments of stained glass. The
stair was narrow, and the sacristan, who carried a lantern
although the afternoon was bright, ascended with painful slowness.

Halfway up, a sound came from above.

It was brief, metallic and singularly unpleasant: three or four
quick notes, not unlike laughter produced by striking thin plates
of iron together. The sacristan stopped dead. His lantern shook
against the wall.

"What was that?" said Dennistoun.

No answer.

"Is anyone in the tower?"

The old man whispered something which Dennistoun did not catch.

"Pardon?"

"The wind, monsieur."

"There is scarcely any wind."

"The owls, then."

Dennistoun tells me that he was at this point irritated
rather than alarmed. He took the lantern from the old
man and went up alone. The chamber above was empty.
Its single narrow window was barred; the floor held only
a broken chest, several crates of glass and a deal
of dust. No owl appeared, nor was there any opening
through which a person could have escaped.

When he returned to the stair, the sacristan had descended
to the bottom and was waiting in the church.

"You did not care to follow?" Dennistoun remarked.

"My breath is bad, monsieur."

"And your owls have an uncommon voice."

The old man made no reply.

They continued their survey until the light began to fail.
Near the west end stood a large, very indifferent picture
of St Bertrand subduing some pagan or heretic. It had
been darkened by smoke and injudicious varnish; the saint's face
possessed the expression of a displeased magistrate, and the prostrate
enemy might equally have represented Arius, Mahomet or an unsuccessful
taxpayer. As they approached it the sacristan again showed agitation.
He kept at a distance and refused to look directly
at the canvas.

Dennistoun, observing this, asked whether there was a legend connected
with it.

"No legend."

"It seems to distress you."

"Distress me? No."

"Then why do you avoid it?"

For a moment the sacristan's composure failed. He seized Dennistoun
by the sleeve.

"Do not stand there, monsieur."

"Why not?"

"Come away. It is late."

Dennistoun allowed himself to be drawn aside. The old man
immediately released him and began apologizing in a confused fashion,
pleading the hour, the dampness of the church and the
necessity of ringing the Angelus.

The bell sounded while they stood in the porch. Its
slow strokes went out over the roofs and the darkening
valley, and at each stroke the sacristan moved his lips.
When it ceased he appeared calmer.

"You asked about manuscripts," he said.

"I did."

"I have at my house a book. It is not
mine exactly—not made for my family, I mean—but it has
been in our possession for a long time. Perhaps monsieur
would consent to see it."

"With pleasure. What sort of book?"

The old man looked past him toward the church door.

"A collection. Very old things. Pages taken from books which
no longer exist."

"A scrapbook?"

"If monsieur chooses to call it so."

"Who assembled it?"

"Canon Alberic de Mauléon."

The name was unfamiliar to Dennistoun, though the family of
Mauléon was not. He repeated it and wrote it in
his notebook.

"When did your canon live?"

"He died in 1701."

"And the book?"

"It is earlier in its parts. Much earlier. Monsieur will
see."

"Can you bring it to the inn?"

At this suggestion the old man's eyes brightened with so
extraordinary a relief that Dennistoun noticed it at once.

"Yes. Certainly. This evening?"

"After supper. At eight, if that is convenient."

"At eight. And monsieur is not travelling alone?"

"My two friends are expected tomorrow."

The sacristan's relief increased.

"Tomorrow. Good. That is good."

"Why should that matter?"

"No reason, monsieur. The roads are lonely. It is better
to have companions."

They separated. Dennistoun returned to the inn, entered his notes
and ate an indifferent supper of trout. The weather, which
had been clear, changed after sunset. Clouds gathered over the
mountains, and now and then a gust came down the
street, rattling the shutters and stirring the ashes on the
hearth. The inn's upper rooms had already grown cold.

At a few minutes after eight there was a knock.
The sacristan entered, carrying beneath his cloak a large square
parcel wrapped in faded green cloth. With him came a
young woman of perhaps twenty-five, whom he introduced as his
daughter, Marie. She was grave, dark-eyed and, so far as
Dennistoun could judge, in considerable anxiety.

The innkeeper's wife led them to an upstairs sitting-room. A
fire burned there, though badly. Two candles had been placed
on the table. The sacristan shut the door, drew the
curtain and unwrapped the parcel.

Even before the volume was opened Dennistoun knew that he
had before him an object of exceptional interest. The binding
was of dark red leather over thick boards, much worn
and repaired at the corners with metal. Upon the upper
cover, almost obliterated by handling, were the arms of Mauléon
and the letters A. M. C. There was no title.

The sacristan pushed the book across the table.

"Examine it, monsieur."

Dennistoun did so.

The contents consisted of leaves cut from illuminated manuscripts and
mounted, with considerable skill, upon strong paper. It was a
barbarous proceeding; but the person responsible had possessed opportunities which
no modern collector could contemplate without envy. There were pages
from a Carolingian psalter; two leaves of an eleventh-century Apocalypse,
French in origin; an initial from a Bible of the
school of Winchester; several pieces of Italian work; and, most
remarkable of all, four miniatures apparently taken from a Spanish
manuscript of the ninth century.

Dennistoun forgot the church, the old man's manner and the
noise in the tower. He bent over the pages, uttering
those broken exclamations in which a collector's professional caution struggles
with his private rapture.

"Extraordinary—quite extraordinary. This border alone—yes, certainly before 900. You see
the beast forms? And here—this cannot be what I think
it is. Where did he obtain them?"

The sacristan sat with his hands upon his knees.

"Canon Alberic travelled."

"He must also have stolen."

"Perhaps."

"Or bought from those who did. This leaf is from
a sacramentary. Look here: *Te igitur, clementissime Pater*. The lower
half has been cut away. Monstrous! Yet—well, what is done
cannot now be mended."

It is proper to observe that Dennistoun's condemnation of Canon
Alberic's vandalism, though perfectly sincere, did not lessen his wish
to possess the result.

He proceeded leaf by leaf, making notes. Some fragments carried
old references in Alberic's hand: *Ex codice S. Martialis*, *Apud
Caesaraugustam*, *De bibliotheca fratrum minorum*. Others had only dates or
names. The canon's scholarship appeared respectable, his conscience less so.

Near the end was inserted a folded sheet of vellum
upon which a plan had been drawn. It represented, so
far as Dennistoun could determine, the cathedral precinct, the chapter
house and a portion of the hill beneath them. Lines
in red ink indicated a passage running from a crypt
or cellar to a point outside the northern wall. Beside
a cross were written the words:

**Custodia thesauri. Via sub terra.**

*The keeping-place of the treasure. The way underground.*

Below, in another hand or at another time, stood:

**Non aperiendum nisi cum sigillo.**

*Not to be opened except with the seal.*

"What treasure?" Dennistoun asked.

The sacristan shook his head.

"There are stories. The treasure of the Visigoths; the gold
of the bishops; the vessels hidden when the Moors came.
Every old church has such stories."

"And this seal?"

"I do not know."

The daughter, who had remained silent, said suddenly:

"Turn the page."

Her father rebuked her with a look, but Dennistoun had
already done so.

The last leaf differed from all the rest. It was
a drawing, or rather a painting in wash and body-colour,
executed upon vellum. Its date was uncertain. The border had
been imitated from a medieval manuscript, but the hand responsible
for the central design might have belonged to the seventeenth
century.

The subject was King Solomon seated upon a throne. He
wore a high eastern crown and held in one hand
a sceptre, in the other a circular seal engraved with
interlacing characters. Before him, crouched within a ring of flame,
was a creature.

Dennistoun remained silent for some time.

I have seen the photograph of that drawing; indeed, I
possess one of the three copies which he caused to
be made. It is no pleasure to look at it.
The photographer complained that the negative had twice fogged without
apparent reason, and the print, though technically clear, fails—Dennistoun always
insisted upon this—to communicate the most oppressive quality of the
original.

The creature was not much larger than a man, but
it was so meagre that its bones appeared to strain
against the skin. Coarse black hair covered the shoulders and
limbs in uneven patches. Its arms were long; its hands
rested flat upon the ground, and the fingers ended in
nails of extraordinary thickness and curvature. The feet were more
like those of a beast, though not of any beast
known to the draughtsman.

The head was the worst. It was turned backward toward
the spectator, while the body crouched toward Solomon. The skull
narrowed above; the ears lay close against it; the mouth,
stretched almost to the hinges of the jaw, displayed small
crowded teeth. The eyes were circular, pale about the pupils,
and possessed an intelligence beyond that of an animal yet
immeasurably below that of a man. They expressed recognition, hunger
and a patient malignity.

Dennistoun compared the general effect, inadequately but not absurdly, to
one of those bird-catching spiders which sit motionless upon a
branch until the instant of their spring. The creature in
the picture was still, but only because a command held
it so.

Around the border ran an inscription from the apocryphal *Testament
of Solomon*:

**Et interrogavi eum: Quis es tu? At ille dixit: Nomen
mihi non est unum, sed multi me vocant.**

*And I questioned him: Who art thou? But he said:
I have not one name, but many call me.*

Below the picture, nearly concealed by the frame into which
the vellum had been pasted, was a line in faded
brown ink:

**Cum videris, cave ne videat te.**

*When thou seest it, beware lest it see thee.*

Dennistoun drew back.

"A singularly detestable conception," he said. "Is this also Canon
Alberic's work?"

"I cannot tell," replied the sacristan.

"You have examined the writing?"

"No."

"You must have done so. The plan and this leaf
have not been kept secret for two centuries."

The old man rose.

"Monsieur wishes to buy the book?"

The abruptness of the question surprised him.

"You intend to sell?"

"Yes."

"But it belongs to your family."

"It belongs to me. I sell it."

Dennistoun looked again at the manuscript. He had already formed
a calculation—one which, as he afterwards admitted, did more honour
to his acquisitiveness than to his perception. The separate leaves,
if properly described, represented a small fortune; the Spanish miniatures
alone would command attention at any London sale. He was,
however, conscientious enough to warn the owner.

"You understand that this is valuable?"

"Yes."

"Very valuable."

"I understand."

"I cannot pay its full market price."

"Give me two hundred and fifty francs."

Dennistoun stared at him.

"That is absurd."

"Two hundred, then."

"No. I mean that the sum is absurdly low."

"One hundred and fifty."

The daughter caught her father's arm.

"Do not bargain. Let him take it."

Her face had become as pale as his.

Dennistoun, suspecting either madness or an undisclosed defect of title,
questioned them closely. Had the book been pledged? Was it
church property? Did any relative possess a claim? The answers
were clear and, later inquiry showed, correct. The volume had
descended to the sacristan through an ancestor who had served
Canon Alberic during the last years of his life.

At length they settled upon two hundred and fifty francs,
with the stipulation, insisted upon by Dennistoun, that a written
receipt and declaration of ownership should be furnished. The sacristan
signed both without reading them. When the money lay upon
the table he thrust it into his pocket, wrapped neither
book nor cloth, and uttered a long sigh.

It was the sigh of a man released from pain.

He moved toward the door. His daughter, however, remained.

"Monsieur," she said, "you will keep this near you."

She took from beneath her dress a small silver crucifix
attached to a cord. The workmanship was provincial and perhaps
a century old. She held it out to Dennistoun.

"I cannot accept it."

"You must."

"My dear young lady, I assure you—"

"You must keep it upon you tonight."

The sacristan said sharply, "Marie!"

But she continued:

"Do not put it down. Do not take it off.
When your friends come, you may return it."

Dennistoun, embarrassed and somewhat impatient, accepted the crucifix. The girl
herself placed the cord around his neck.

"Now," she said, "do not remove it."

As they went out, the old man turned upon the
landing.

"Your friends arrive early?"

"Before noon."

"And there are servants awake in the house?"

"I presume so."

"Good. Lock your door."

With that they departed.

The innkeeper's wife, who had seen the money change hands,
entered almost immediately under the pretence of mending the fire.
She asked what the old sacristan had sold. Dennistoun told
her it was a book.

"A book!" She gave a short laugh.

There was nothing supernatural in it; nevertheless, Dennistoun disliked the
sound. It reminded him of the noise in the tower.

"He has had that book many years?" he asked.

"All his life. His father had it before him. They
kept it in the locked room at the back of
the house."

"Why locked?"

"Because they are secret people. The old one never slept
well. Neither did his father."

"And Canon Alberic?"

At the name she stopped poking the fire.

"He was a bad priest."

"In what respect?"

"He looked for things under the church."

"Treasure?"

She shrugged.

"He found something, perhaps."

Then she laughed again, wished Dennistoun good night and went
away.

It was now about ten o'clock. The wind had increased,
but no rain fell. Dennistoun's room overlooked the street; through
the imperfect shutters came alternate breaths of cold air and
the subdued murmur of the stream below the hill. Somewhere
in the inn a child was being put to bed.
Doors closed, voices diminished, and presently the house settled into
silence.

He had no intention of sleeping. The discovery was too
considerable. He placed the book upon the table, arranged both
candles, lit a lamp and began a more systematic examination.

For an hour nothing occurred. He measured the leaves, transcribed
inscriptions and identified portions of texts. Once or twice he
heard steps in the passage, but these were explained by
the servants. At half-past eleven he came again to the
plan.

The crucifix irritated him. The cord was too short and
the silver lay cold against his skin whenever he bent
over the table. He took it off and placed it
beside the lamp.

"Superstitious nonsense," he said aloud.

Dennistoun had a habit of talking to himself during solitary
work, especially when he wished to assert that some matter
was beneath discussion.

"A very good girl, no doubt, but this is 1883."

He unfolded the plan. By comparing it with his measurements
of the cathedral, he concluded that the marked passage must
begin beneath the former chapter house, now used for storage.
The supposed treasure lay, if the drawing were accurate, under
the spot occupied by the large picture which had so
distressed the sacristan.

This coincidence gave him pause.

He looked toward the crucifix, smiled and returned to the
book.

The fire had sunk. Its light, passing through the legs
of the table, threw long moving shadows upon the boarded
wall behind him. One shutter tapped at intervals. The sound
of the stream seemed louder.

He turned the last page and confronted the figure of
Solomon and the crouching thing.

"No," he murmured. "Not a pleasant companion."

He began to examine the lower edge, where the faint
writing showed beneath the mounting. To see it clearly he
drew the book closer and lowered the lamp.

Something touched the back of his chair.

He sat upright.

The movement was slight—no more than might have been caused
by a fold of his coat catching upon the wood.
He listened. Nothing.

"A loose board," he said.

He looked beneath the table. There was nothing there.

When he raised his head, one of the shadows upon
the wall appeared to have altered. It was not the
shadow of the chair, nor of himself, nor of any
object upon the table. It lay low and wide, as
if something were crouching immediately behind him.

The flame of the lamp shortened.

Dennistoun did not turn. He watched the shadow.

It moved.

A penwiper? No. His travelling cloak? No; that hung beside
the door. A dog entered from the passage? The door
was locked. A trick of the fire?

The fire was nearly out.

There came a soft scraping upon the boards.

Then a breath touched his left ear: warm, rapid and
foul.

He turned.

At first he saw only the upper edge of the
chair and the darkness beyond it. Then two pale circles
rose slowly into the lamplight.

Eyes.

Below them opened a narrow mouth crowded with small teeth.

The thing's hands were upon the chair-back, one on either
side of him. The fingers curled over the wood. They
were black-haired, thin as bundles of cords, and armed with
nails that clicked softly as they tightened.

Dennistoun could neither rise nor cry out. He saw the
tendons move beneath the skin. He saw a shoulder, a
bent knee, the long backward slope of the skull. The
face approached his.

It was the creature in the drawing.

Not an apparition resembling it; not a shape suggested by
overstrained imagination. It was the same. The same starvation of
body, the same watchful malice, the same intelligence that understood
fear and delighted in delaying the instant of attack.

Its weight came against the chair. The wood creaked.

The mouth widened.

On the table, scarcely an arm's length away, lay the
silver crucifix.

Dennistoun snatched at it.

The creature sprang.

He remembered the cord winding about his fingers; remembered a
sensation as though hooked iron tore across his shoulder; remembered
the crucifix striking his palm. He thrust it backward without
looking and screamed.

There was a sound which he never succeeded in describing.
It was partly a shriek, partly the metallic laughter heard
in the tower, and partly the furious chattering of teeth.
The lamp went out. The chair overturned. Something rushed around
the room with a speed impossible to follow—floor, wall, ceiling—and
struck the locked door so violently that the panels split.

Dennistoun screamed again.

Below, the innkeeper and two servants were already awake. They
ran upstairs with lights. At the turn of the passage
all three felt a violent pressure pass them. The younger
servant was thrown against the wall; the candle in the
innkeeper's hand was extinguished; and the woman, who came last,
declared that a hairy arm brushed across her face. No
one saw a body. They heard claws—or what sounded like
claws—descending the stair and then a laugh from the empty
courtyard.

The door of Dennistoun's room remained locked.

They broke it open.

He lay unconscious beside the overturned chair, clutching the crucifix
so tightly that its edges had cut his hand. Three
long rents had been torn through the shoulder of his
coat and shirt, and beneath them were raised red marks,
though the skin was not broken. The book lay open
upon the table. The vellum drawing had been pulled partly
from its mount.

There was no one else in the room.

Dennistoun recovered consciousness shortly before dawn. His first words were:

"Cover it."

They did not understand.

"The picture. Cover the picture."

The innkeeper's wife shut the book. He then asked whether
the crucifix was still in his hand. Being assured that
it was, he allowed them to move him to another
chamber.

At sunrise a messenger was sent for the sacristan. The
old man arrived with his daughter before seven. On seeing
Dennistoun he began to weep—not noisily, but with the exhausted
resignation of one whose expected calamity has occurred.

"You knew," Dennistoun said.

The old man did not deny it.

"How many times?"

"Seen? Twice."

"And felt?"

"Many times. For forty years."

"Why did you sell me the book?"

The daughter cried out at this, but the sacristan raised
his hand.

"I am old. I was afraid. God forgive me, I
was afraid."

"It follows the book?"

"Yes."

"Why was I not told?"

"Would monsieur have believed?"

Dennistoun could not answer that.

The sacristan then gave an account which I abbreviate, though
I have before me the signed statement taken that morning
by Dennistoun and witnessed by the innkeeper. The creature had
first been seen by the sacristan when he was a
boy. His father had opened the book to show the
drawing to a visiting priest. That night something entered the
locked room and crouched beside the bed. The crucifix which
now protected Dennistoun had been placed there by the priest,
and the thing withdrew.

The second appearance occurred some thirty years later, after the
sacristan removed the drawing in order to examine writing concealed
beneath its edge. He saw the creature in a mirror,
standing behind him. Again the crucifix was near.

At other times he had not seen it, but heard
its steps in the house, its laughter from unoccupied rooms,
its breathing outside the locked door. It followed him in
the cathedral. It crouched behind pillars. It waited near the
picture over the supposed treasure. If he carried the crucifix,
it did not approach; if the book remained shut, it
was less active. Yet it was never wholly absent.

"Why not burn the volume?" Dennistoun asked.

The old man crossed himself.

"My father tried. The fire would not take it."

"The book or the picture?"

"The picture. He cut one corner and put it in
the flame. It bled."

The daughter protested that this was an old man's tale;
but she would not look at the volume.

Dennistoun's two companions arrived before noon and found him feverish
but rational. With their assistance he returned to the book.
The drawing was removed in daylight and in the presence
of six persons. Behind it, written upon the mounting sheet
in Canon Alberic's hand, was the following:

**Anno Domini MDCXCIV, die XII Decembris, viam aperui et sigillum
fregi. Thesaurum non inveni, sed custodem excitavi. Per signum Salomonis
eum coercere speravi. Non coercetur. Sequitur. Vigilat. Peccavi. Orate pro
me.**

The translation may be given:

*In the year of Our Lord 1694, on the twelfth
day of December, I opened the way and broke the
seal. I did not find the treasure, but awakened the
keeper. By the sign of Solomon I hoped to bind
him. He is not bound. He follows. He watches. I
have sinned. Pray for me.*

Beneath, in a later and less steady hand, appeared:

**Vidit me. Septem annos. Miserere mei, Domine.**

*It saw me. Seven years. Have mercy upon me, O
Lord.*

The cathedral register records the death of Canon Alberic de
Mauléon on 18 December 1701. The entry states that he
was found dead in his chamber, "having received no sacrament,
though apparently in health the previous evening." A marginal note,
added by another hand, says that the body was discovered
beneath the bed and that the face could not decently
be shown.

Dennistoun made inquiries concerning the underground passage. Its entrance, if
entrance there was, had been blocked during repairs in 1760.
He had no wish to reopen it. Scholarship has its
duties, but excavation is not invariably among them.

Before leaving St Bertrand he visited Canon Alberic's tomb, a
plain slab in the cloister, and arranged that Masses should
be said for the canon's soul. The sacristan suggested thirty.
Dennistoun, whose theological convictions were less definite than his gratitude,
paid for fifty; he afterwards observed that the additional twenty
were the cheapest insurance he had ever purchased.

As for the book, it remained in his possession. He
would not return it to the sacristan, nor did the
old man desire its return. The detached drawing was photographed
in Toulouse under Dennistoun's supervision. Three prints were made. One
is at Cambridge under restricted access; one was given to
the late Dr. H. L. Wraxall and cannot now be
traced; the third, as I have said, is in my
keeping.

After photography the vellum was burned.

This time it burned readily, though an unpleasant smell was
noticed and the ashes retained, for several minutes, the outline
of a crouching figure. Dennistoun scattered them in running water.

The manuscript collection, deprived of its final leaf, was bequeathed
to a college library, where it is catalogued under the
neutral title *Fragmenta varia ex collectione Alberici de Mauléon*. No

Dennistoun never again saw the creature. He did, however, refuse
throughout the rest of his life to sit with his
back to an open room; and when examining manuscripts after
dark, he kept upon his desk a small crucifix which,
though he commonly described it as "a useful piece of
southern metalwork," was never offered for sale.

## THE KEEPER OF THE KING'S KEY

It was in the winter of 1829—or perchance a year
earlier, for memory, when she has grown old, becomes as
careless of dates as she is tenacious of sorrows—that I
heard the story of Mistress Esther Dudley, last guardian of
the Province House. The narrator was himself an ancient man,
one Master Eben Pike, whose political opinions had never recovered
from the evacuation of Boston, though his bodily person had
survived many subsequent events of equal violence and greater benefit.
He sat in an oaken chair beside the chimney of
a chamber that looked upon the crooked roofs and steeples
of the town; his head trembled continually, his hands were
white and thin, and his eyes, though clouded by nearly
ninety winters, would kindle at the name of King George
as if a royal salute had flashed across their dimness.

A small lamp burned upon the table. The fire had
sunk into a red cavern among the ashes. On the
mantel-piece a clock, manufactured in London before the French war,
ticked with a grave and somewhat insolent deliberation, as though
it alone kept lawful time, while all the clocks of
republican America had fallen into sedition.

"Young sir," said Master Pike—for I was young then, and
democratic beyond the pardon of so loyal a relic—"you have
seen the Province House, doubtless?"

I acknowledged that I had seen it, and added, with
perhaps unnecessary levity, that its front was now disfigured by
signboards and its lower apartments profaned by trade.

"Profaned!" repeated he. "Ay, sir, that is the word. I
knew it when scarlet coats stood sentry at the door,
when a coach-and-six rolled beneath its arch, and when no
man entered the council chamber without remembering that Majesty dwelt
there by deputy. But I knew it afterward too—empty, dark,
and guarded by one poor gentlewoman, who had more loyalty
in her little finger than your whole Commonwealth in its
broad right hand."

Then did he relate the following history, with many digressions
concerning extinct families, disputed coats-of-arms, and persons whom he described
as honorable merely because they had been hanged in effigy.
His account was frequently broken by passion and occasionally contradicted
by itself; and I, being no lover of kings except
as antiquities, have ventured to correct its more extravagant prejudices.
Yet I would not willingly diminish the dignity of Mistress
Dudley's fidelity. A principle may be erroneous, and still the
heart that dies for it be true.

Thus amended—but not, I trust, dishonored—the old Loyalist's tale is
here set down.

---

On an evening of March, when the British power in
Massachusetts was crumbling like a sea-wall beneath the continual assault
of a storm, Sir William Howe stood alone in the
great audience chamber of the Province House. The town was
filled with confused and ominous noises. Drums rattled in the
streets; wheels groaned under military stores; hoarse voices shouted from
wharf to wharf; and now and then, above all these
sounds of departure, there came the boom of a distant
cannon from the American works, solemnly admonishing the king's general
that his hour was spent.

The chamber retained the pomp of government after government itself
had fled. Crimson hangings, though darkened by smoke, descended from
the cornice. The royal arms, carved and gilded, spread their
lion and unicorn above the governor's chair. Portraits of former
rulers—some proud, some placid, some magnificently stupid—looked down from tarnished
frames upon their successor. A long mirror, brought from England
in a more prosperous age, multiplied the candles into a
wavering procession; but its glass had grown dim at the
edges, so that each reflected flame seemed already entering a
fog.

Sir William walked slowly from window to window. He was
dressed for embarkation, yet wore his uniform with that ceremonious
exactness which military men sometimes preserve when all else is
disorder. His sword struck the furniture as he turned. Upon
a table lay dispatches, a pair of gloves, and a
heavy iron key with a silver bow—the key of the
principal entrance.

Preferable, perhaps, would he have deemed the loss of a
battle in some remote wilderness, where defeat might be hidden
under the leaves, to this public humiliation of yielding a
royal capital to farmers, mechanics, lawyers, and village captains. A
soldier can endure the overthrow of an army; but to
behold Authority itself packing its trunks, rolling up its banners,
and seeking passage in crowded transports is a spectacle to
make even philosophy ridiculous.

While he thus meditated, the door opened without announcement, and
there entered a woman so old, so erect, and so
singularly attired that she might have stepped from one of
the darkening portraits upon the wall.

She was Mistress Esther Dudley, a remote kinswoman of a
former governor and, in her own estimation, connected by blood,
marriage, or immemorial courtesy with half the noble houses of
the Province. Her fortune had vanished long before; her lands
had passed under mortgages; her servants were dead or scattered;
and of all the splendor to which she had been
born there remained only certain robes of faded brocade, a
few family trinkets, a poverty made stately by pride, and
an apartment in the Province House which official indulgence had
permitted her to occupy.

Her hair, white as the powder once worn in ballrooms,
was drawn beneath a cap of yellowed lace. A chain
of tarnished gold hung about her neck. She carried a
small ivory-headed cane, not for support—for she refused to confess
infirmity—but as the final sceptre of her rank.

"Your Excellency," said she, making a reverence that would have
become a court drawing-room, "the servants tell me that preparations
are making for departure."

Howe turned away from the window.

"They tell you truly, Mistress Dudley. His Majesty's forces will
leave Boston with the morning tide."

"For a day, no doubt," replied Esther. "Perhaps for a
week. The town hath grown unwholesome with rebellion, and a
prudent commander may withdraw only to return with strength sufficient
to chastise it."

The general regarded her with pity. Outside, another wagon thundered
over the pavement.

"We go to Halifax," said he.

"And thence?"

"Madam, war and ministers must answer."

"And the governor?"

"There will be no royal governor left in Massachusetts."

She stood motionless. The chamber seemed to enlarge around her,
as deserted rooms do when a single word has banished
their accustomed inhabitants.

"No royal governor?" she repeated. "Your Excellency speaks in bitterness.
Surely England does not abandon her own house because a
rabble hath broken the windows."

"England," answered Howe, with a sad smile, "has many houses,
and some are difficult to keep."

Then, with a sudden tenderness—whether arising from respect for her
years, shame at his own retreat, or that involuntary homage
which constancy exacts even from those who know it vain—he
took the heavy key from the table.

"Mistress Dudley, will you sail with us? You shall have
protection, a cabin, and such comforts as the voyage allows.
Halifax contains many loyal families who will welcome you."

"Leave the Province House!" cried she. "Leave the king's dwelling
to mice, rebels, and committees! No, Sir William. My fathers
served the Crown before these disturbers were born. I have
eaten the king's bread beneath this roof. Here I remain
until lawful government returns."

"Consider well. There may be danger."

"Danger is a word for those who have duties elsewhere."

"Poverty, then; solitude; perhaps insult."

"I have known poverty. Solitude is no stranger. As for
insult, it cannot reach me from beneath."

The general bowed his head. He placed the key in
her hand, closing her frail fingers over its cold weight.

"Keep it, then," said he, "until a royal governor shall
demand it."

Her face became radiant.

"I accept the charge in His Majesty's name."

"Guard the house, Mistress Dudley."

"With my life."

Howe raised her hand to his lips, not as a
commander saluting a subject, but as one doomed cause bidding
farewell to another. Then he departed. His steps sounded along
the corridor, descended the broad staircase, and were lost amid
the tumult below.

Esther remained beneath the royal arms. In the dusky mirror,
where the departing candlelight shook and divided, Rumor afterward affirmed
that she beheld, ranged behind her, all the governors who
had ever held commission from the Crown. One laid a
hand upon his sword; another clasped a parchment roll; another
wore the melancholy smile of a man who has ruled
much and governed little. But whether these were spirits, reflections,
or only the figures of Memory, who can say? Esther
herself never doubted that they had accepted her as their
successor.

At sunrise the transports moved down the harbor. The red
standards diminished upon the water; the glitter of arms became
a broken line; sails passed between the islands and were
swallowed by the pale eastern haze. Boston watched from housetop,
steeple, wharf, and hill. Some wept; many shouted; all understood
that an age was going forth with the tide.

In an upper window of the Province House stood Esther
Dudley. The key hung at her girdle. She lifted a
white handkerchief, not in farewell, but in confident salutation.

"They will return," said she. "Before the roses bloom—before the
roses bloom."

The roses bloomed. They withered. Snow whitened the garden; rain
washed the old brick walls; spring returned with rebellious birds
that built their nests beneath the royal cornice. The king's
troops did not come.

Yet Esther kept her charge.

---

There are lives in which outward incident ceases, while the
inward drama grows more intense with every silent year. Such
was hers. She inhabited the Province House as Memory inhabits
a ruined heart—not possessing it by law, perhaps, yet refusing
to surrender a single chamber.

At first she maintained the state of the mansion with
pathetic exactness. Each morning she unlocked the principal door, though
no official visitor was expected. She dusted the council table,
arranged chairs about it, and drew aside the curtains from
the governor's portrait. At sunset she fastened every shutter and
tried every bolt. Once a week she entered the banqueting
room, where cobwebbed bottles stood in a cabinet and two
dozen glasses awaited guests whose bones, for the most part,
were already mingled with English or provincial earth. On the
king's birthday she illuminated seven windows with tallow candles, drank
His Majesty's health in a spoonful of ancient Madeira, and
sang a verse of "God Save the King" in a
voice at once cracked and resolute.

As her small income diminished, the house surrendered room after
room to darkness. She could no longer heat the state
apartments. Their fireplaces became black mouths filled with cold ashes;
frost embroidered the panes; damp crept beneath the hangings and
touched the royal crimson with leprous stains. But in her
own chamber a little fire continued to burn, and beside
it, within reach of her chair, hung the key.

A few Loyalists visited her in the earlier years: ruined
merchants, widows whose sons had followed the army, superannuated officers
who had been unable to escape, and gentlemen whose devotion
to monarchy had prudently become inaudible. They entered by the
side door after dusk. Esther received them as if at
a levee. A worn carpet was spread; candles in silver
sockets—one of the sockets was plated, though she never acknowledged
it—were placed upon the mantel; and tea of suspicious ancestry
was served in china cups painted with crowns.

Then would they speak of the good old time.

They recalled governors' balls, proclamations read from balconies, scarlet uniforms
beneath elm trees, assemblies opened with formal speeches, and royal
birthdays when wine ran in the gutters. They named families
whose grandeur had been swept from the town like autumn
leaves. They wondered whether Lord North had at last formed
a wise design; whether fresh regiments were secretly approaching; whether
rebellion, so contrary to nature and Scripture, could possibly endure
another month.

Esther always possessed better intelligence than her guests.

"General Washington," she would say, "is retreating in Virginia."

"But, madam," some timid visitor might reply, "the printed account
calls it a victory."

"Printed by whom? Rebels."

Or she would announce, with solemn satisfaction, that the French
alliance proved the king's success.

"How so?" asked Captain Rudge, who had lost an estate
and two nephews to his principles.

"Because," said Esther, "France has ever been England's enemy. Whatever
she undertakes must therefore bring advantage to His Majesty."

No logic could pass the guarded door of that conclusion.

When news came of Saratoga, Esther interpreted the surrender as
a stratagem too profound for vulgar comprehension. When Cornwallis laid
down his arms, she declared that the ceremony must have
been arranged to deceive the French. When peace was signed
and Britain acknowledged American independence, she maintained that the document
was a temporary instrument, intended to soothe the colonies before
their complete restoration.

"The king," said she, "is merciful. He permits his children
to call themselves free until they are weary of the
word."

Thus each American triumph became, in passing through the twilight
of her understanding, a British triumph still more glorious. Facts
entered her mind as rebels and came forth wearing the
royal uniform.

My informant, Master Pike, insisted that this was not madness,
but loyalty purified from circumstance. Being myself less courtly, I
should say that madness and loyalty had entered into partnership,
and that each furnished what the other lacked. Yet let
us not mock too loudly. Most men compel the world
to flatter their opinions in some degree; Esther differed from
us chiefly in having a world more obedient because it
existed within her own breast.

The children of the neighborhood were her most frequent company.
They came first from mischief, daring one another to rap
upon the haunted door; afterward from curiosity; and at last
because they loved the strange old woman who spoke to
them as princes and ladies fallen temporarily into coarse garments.

She would lead them through the mansion, holding a candle
aloft.

"Here," she said, "the governor received messengers from the eastern
tribes. On that stair stood a sentry when the mob
filled the street. In this chamber a royal council sat
until midnight, and the fate of the Province trembled in
every word."

The children saw torn paper, mouse-holes, dust, and chairs with
broken cane seats; but Esther's voice clothed all in vanished
magnificence. The hall filled with plumes and swords. Coaches rolled
beneath the arch. Music sounded from the gallery. Jewels sparkled
upon white throats. For an hour, History became a fairy
tale, and decay itself wore gold lace.

"Did kings live here?" asked a little girl.

"The king lives wherever his authority is honored," Esther replied.

"Then he lives here still?"

The old woman pressed the key to her heart.

"Here last of all."

Sometimes the children asked about the ghosts. It was the
general belief among servants, apprentices, and such sober persons as
love a marvel while protesting against it, that the Province
House had never been empty. At midnight footsteps crossed locked
chambers. A bell rang in the council room, though its
cord had rotted away. Shadowy figures descended the staircase and
paused before the door, as if expecting coaches that no
longer came. One watchman swore that he had seen a
red-coated sentry pacing upon the roof; another, more temperate, admitted
only to a moving light.

Esther neither confirmed nor denied these reports.

"The dead," she said, "have better manners than to frighten
children. If they walk, it is because they have business
unfinished."

"Have you seen them, Mistress Dudley?"

"I have remembered them."

This answer, though less satisfactory to the young, may contain
the whole philosophy of apparitions.

There was especially a tale concerning the great mirror. Its
silver had decayed until living faces appeared faint and ancient
within it; but, behind those faces, other countenances were sometimes
descried—powdered heads, grave eyes, mouths formed for proclamations, and the
stiff collars of a former generation. Esther often stood before
the glass, arrayed in one of her old gowns, and
addressed those doubtful shapes.

"Patience, your Excellencies," she would murmur. "I keep the house.
The key is safe. He will return."

Whether she spoke to the dead governors, to her own
reflection, or to Hope—that most flattering and most dangerous of
ghosts—I do not pretend to determine.

Years went onward—onward. The child who had first knocked in
sport returned as a mother, leading her own little boy.
Young men who had marched barefoot in the revolutionary armies
became substantial citizens, broad in body and authority. New houses
arose. New streets pushed outward. Ships entered the harbor beneath
the flag of the republic, and merchants, who cared little
whether liberty wore homespun or velvet so long as she
paid her debts, filled warehouses with the wealth of distant
seas.

The Province House, meanwhile, sank deeper into age. Its masonry
cracked. Rain traced dark lines down the walls. The lion
and unicorn over the entrance lost snout, claw, and gilding.
In the courtyard weeds thrust themselves between the stones, republican
plants, needing no charter for possession.

Esther also decayed, though her ceremonial spirit yielded less readily
than her frame. Her brocade grew threadbare; her lace was
mended until the mending became its principal substance; her hands
shook when she lighted the candles. Yet every morning she
wound the clock in the governor's chamber. Every evening she
touched the key.

The town pitied her. Even those who laughed at her
politics sent food, fuel, and small gifts, often pretending that
they came from some surviving branch of her family. She
accepted charity only when disguised as tribute.

"His Majesty's allowance is irregular," she would say, "but doubtless
the treasury is burdened by the war."

One bitter winter she was found sitting before a fire
that had gone out, wrapped in a curtain taken from
the council room. A republican matron urged her to leave
the mansion and enter a warm household.

"Madam," said Esther, "would you ask the flame to leave
the lamp which it was appointed to keep burning?"

"The flame will perish for want of oil."

"Then let it perish at its post."

There was grandeur in the answer, though little prudence. Providence,
which sometimes nourishes imprudent grandeur by means of practical neighbors,
supplied the missing wood.

---

At length came a day when Boston put on festival
attire.

The morning sun shone from a sky of uncommon purity,
and every window along the principal streets displayed faces, flags,
or flowers. Bells answered one another from steeple to steeple.
Militia companies assembled in brave uniforms, not wholly regular in
color or cut, but worn with the confidence of men
who represented themselves. Artillery stood ready upon the Common. Citizens
crowded the pavements, while boys climbed trees, posts, and projections
forbidden alike by law and gravity.

A governor was coming.

The word passed through the town and penetrated, like a
trumpet blast, into the dimmest chambers of the Province House.

Esther had risen before dawn. For several days strange agitation
had possessed her. She had heard wheels in the night
and taken them for royal couriers. She had seen, or
believed that she saw, a crown of light above the
eastern horizon. The old clock, after stopping for twenty years
at nine minutes past eleven, had suddenly struck twelve—though a
rat within its case may have had more to do
with the miracle than King George.

Now the bells confirmed everything.

"O happy day!" she cried. "O blessed, blessed hour! He
comes at last!"

A charitable girl who attended her attempted an explanation.

"It is Governor Hancock, madam."

"A governor—yes! I knew it."

"John Hancock, chosen by the people."

But Esther had already turned away. The words "chosen by
the people" possessed no meaning in the language of her
hope.

She opened an ancient chest and took from it the
attire of her youth: a gown of flowered brocade, once
white and crimson, now resembling roses seen through autumn mist;
a stomacher embroidered with tarnished silver; gloves spotted by age;
and a necklace containing a miniature of the king in
his young manhood. Upon her hair she placed a structure
of lace and ribbon which had survived its century more
successfully than many constitutions.

The girl wept while assisting her.

"Why do you weep?" asked Esther.

"Because you are happy, madam."

"Ay," replied the old loyalist, with majestic simplicity. "Faith must
have its reward."

She ordered the shutters thrown open. Sunlight invaded the house,
startling dust from cornices and laying bare every wound that
darkness had charitably concealed. The portraits emerged from gloom; the
governor's chair stood revealed beneath a rent canopy; the mirror
gathered the bright room into its faded depths, where the
present instantly assumed the pallor of the past.

Esther moved from chamber to chamber, directing preparations as though
invisible servants surrounded her.

"Fresh candles in the sconces. Let the council table be
cleared. The silver inkstand must be placed at his right
hand. Open the main entrance—wide, wide! Massachusetts has been shut
against her king too long."

She polished the great key with her sleeve and hung
it from a blue ribbon. Then she descended the staircase.

Outside, the multitude thickened. The approaching procession could be heard
before it was seen: music, cheers, the measured tread of
soldiers, the clatter of horses, and, at intervals, cannon shaking
the old walls. To Esther these sounds were the returning
pulse of monarchy. The years between Howe's departure and this
triumphant hour shrank to a troubled dream. Yorktown, independence, the
treaty, elections, constitutions—all vanished like writing breathed upon a frosted
pane.

The procession entered the street.

At its center rode John Hancock, governor of the Commonwealth
of Massachusetts. Age and illness had touched him, but he
bore himself with the grace of one accustomed to public
observation. His dress was rich, his gestures ceremonious, and in
his countenance there remained something of that splendid audacity which
had once placed his name beneath a declaration no royal
pardon could safely overlook.

Beside him rode officers of the republic. Before him marched
citizens bearing no king's colors. Above him floated the new
flag.

Esther saw only the governor.

"Welcome!" she cried from the threshold. "Welcome, your Excellency, to
His Majesty's Province!"

The people nearest the house fell silent. Those behind, not
knowing the cause, continued to cheer, so that hush and
acclamation met strangely in the air.

Hancock checked his horse.

There, framed by the dark doorway, stood the aged woman
in her faded magnificence. The sunlight glittered upon the key
in her hand. Behind her extended the ruined hall, and
behind that, as imagination might conceive, the whole shadowy retinue
of departed authority.

Hancock recognized her. All Boston knew Esther Dudley.

He dismounted.

"Venerable lady," said he, approaching with uncovered head, "what service
may I render you?"

She made her profoundest reverence.

"Your Excellency has returned after long delay. Much hath suffered
in your absence, yet the house remains. I have preserved
it from rebel hands, from neglect, from time itself, so
far as mortal fidelity could prevail. Receive, therefore, the key
entrusted to me by Sir William Howe, and resume the
government of His Majesty's loyal Province."

She knelt and offered the key.

For one instant no person moved. The republican soldiers, the
citizens, the children upon the posts, even the restless horses
appeared arrested within a historical picture. Pity struggled with astonishment
upon many faces. Some would have laughed, but the laughter
died unborn.

Hancock took the key—not as a claimant seizing possession, but
gently, lest its weight break the hand that had borne
it so long.

"Mistress Dudley," he said, "your trust has been faithfully kept."

She raised her eyes.

"Then the king forgives his people?"

"The king," replied Hancock slowly, "has no Province here to
govern. Massachusetts is free."

The brightness remained upon her face, but became fixed and
unnatural.

"Free?"

"A sovereign Commonwealth, under laws made by her citizens."

"And you—your Excellency—by what commission do you come?"

"By the choice of the people."

The key slipped between their hands and struck the stone
threshold.

At that sound, small though it was, Esther seemed to
hear the fall of an empire.

She looked beyond Hancock to the flags, the militia, the
rejoicing multitude. Recognition came not gradually, but as lightning reveals
a landscape which darkness had made merciful. She beheld in
the dignitary before her not a servant of King George,
but John Hancock—the proscribed rebel, the signer, the chosen magistrate
of a nation founded upon the ruin of everything she
had loved.

"Thou!" she whispered.

Hancock bowed his head.

"John Hancock, madam."

"Never—never!" Her voice rose piercingly. "I have not kept the
king's house for thee! I have not watched while his
servants slept, and hoped while his armies tarried, that I
might open this door to treason crowned with office!"

She turned toward the dark interior.

"Your Excellencies! My lords! Why do you stand in the
mirror and give no warning? Sir William, take back your
key. I was faithful. Before God, I was faithful!"

Her hands groped at the empty ribbon. Then, with a
movement of extraordinary dignity, she straightened her bowed form and
faced the people.

"Bear witness," said she, "that Esther Dudley surrendered nothing. The
house was taken from her by Time and rebellion. Her
heart remained the king's."

The cannon fired.

Its concussion rolled through the hall, stirred the rotting curtains,
and shook a fragment of gilding from the royal arms.
Esther lifted both hands as though the report had been
a salute from the vanished fleet.

"God save King George!" she cried. "God save the king!"

She fell across the threshold.

When they raised her, life had already withdrawn beyond persuasion.
Her face, relieved of hope, prejudice, poverty, and age, wore
the calm of one who has delivered an account and
fears no earthly auditor. Hancock stood beside her, still holding
the iron key.

Some angry patriot muttered that the old woman had died
a traitor.

"No," said Hancock. "She died faithful."

He looked upon the crowd, and his voice, though not
loud, passed through the silence.

"My fellow-citizens, this venerable lady loved a cause which could
not live; but she loved it with a constancy that
might honor a better one. Let us neither inherit her
blindness nor deride her fidelity. The past has yielded us
its key. We must not use it to lock ourselves
within the past."

Then he placed the key upon Esther's breast.

"Close the old house gently," said he. "Its last guardian
is gone. For us there are doors not yet opened."

The procession moved onward—onward. Music rose again; banners passed in
the sun; the people followed their elected governor toward the
living business of the day. Behind them, servants bore Esther
into the Province House. The great door swung inward, slowly
and with a long complaint from its hinges, until only
a narrow blade of sunlight remained upon the floor.

Then that also vanished.

---

Here Master Eben Pike ended his recital.

The fire in our chamber had sunk meanwhile to a
single coal, whose red light, reflected in the brass fender,
resembled a distant beacon upon a dark coast. The lamp
flickered whenever the wind pressed against the windows. The London
clock ticked on, obstinately loyal to its own departed noon.

"You democrats," said the old man, after a silence, "have
never produced such faith."

"Perhaps," I replied, "because we ask men to be faithful
to what is still alive."

He frowned, yet not severely; for age, having lost the
strength to conquer an opponent, will sometimes consent to forgive
him. Then he leaned back and closed his eyes.

I remained beside the dying fire, considering Esther Dudley—not as
a mere madwoman, nor altogether as a heroine, but as
one of those mournful figures whom History stations at the
gates of a vanished world. To them the future appears
not as dawn, but sacrilege; change is only another name
for loss; and Time, which to the young opens roads
without number, comes to the old bearing a warrant of
eviction.

The clock struck midnight. At the first stroke Master Pike
started and murmured something that might have been a prayer
for the king; at the last, the remaining coal collapsed
into ash.

For a moment the mirror opposite the chimney seemed crowded
with pale, ceremonious faces. I saw governors, councillors, soldiers, powdered
ladies, and in their midst a tall old woman holding
out a key. But the lamp gave one upward leap
and expired; the mirror became merely darkness; and all that
stately company—whether spirits, memories, or shadows—passed away with the light.

Outside, through the sleeping republican city, I heard a watchman
calling the hour.

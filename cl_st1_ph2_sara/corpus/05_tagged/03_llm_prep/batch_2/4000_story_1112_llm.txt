### The Roman Ring

What feminine heart, though it beat beneath serge instead of
satin, does not quicken at the sound of wedding music?

Miss Sophie had entered the church only to escape the
rain. At least, this was what she told herself as
she slipped through the heavy door and stood, a small
black figure, behind the last pew. Outside, a November shower
silvered the banquette and beat against the roofs of the
waiting carriages; within, candle-flames trembled before the saints, flowers breathed
heavily about the altar, and the organ poured its swelling
tones upward among the gray arches toward the golden dome.

The church was warm. Miss Sophie had been cold all
day.

She held her little cape together with one thin hand
and watched the people turn expectantly toward the aisle. There
were whispers, the rustle of silk, a fragrance of violets
and damp wool. Then the bride came—a white-robed vision under
floating tulle, with orange blossoms at her breast and happiness
shining frankly from her young face.

Miss Sophie smiled. Poverty had not yet taken from her
the power to rejoice at another woman's joy.

Then she saw the groom.

The organ thundered; the tapers multiplied and swam before her
eyes; the painted Virgin above the side altar seemed to
bend through a mist.

"Louis!" she breathed.

No one heard. The bride moved forward, serene and radiant.
Louis Neale stood waiting at the altar, older than when
Miss Sophie had known him, broader in the shoulder, distinguished
by the careful black coat and grave moustache of prosperity.
Yet he had the same dark eyes, the same turn
of the head, the same impatient movement of the hand.

Miss Sophie had fainted.

They carried her into the vestibule, loosened her cape, and
sprinkled water upon her colorless face. A stout lady declared
it was the heat; a young girl said the poor
creature had probably eaten nothing; the sacristan wished her removed
before the recessional.

When Sophie opened her eyes, the vows had been spoken.

"Ma foi, madame," said an old Creole woman who had
remained beside her, "you are too weak to go about
in such weather."

"I am quite well," Sophie answered.

Through the open inner door she saw Louis turn toward
his bride. He smiled down at her, and the tenderness
of that smile pierced a chamber of Sophie's heart which
she had believed closed forever.

She rose without assistance and went out into the rain.

Years before, Louis had promised to marry her. That was
when her father's little shop still prospered, when her dresses,
though simply made, were of good material, and when there
had been music in the evenings at their cottage near
Bayou Road. Louis had been poor then, ambitious, handsome, and
easily wounded by delay. He had given Sophie a heavy
antique ring engraved with a Roman soldier's head, saying that
it had belonged to his mother's family and would one
day prove more valuable than either of them guessed.

Then Sophie's father failed in business. Illness entered the house;
debts followed it. Louis went away, at first writing every
week, then every month, then not at all. When the
father lay dying and there was no money for medicine,
Sophie carried the Roman ring to the Mont de Piété.

Afterward came silence.

She never blamed Louis aloud. The neighbourhood did that for
her, inventing a dozen romances because she denied them the
true one. Some said she had been deserted by a
sea captain; others, that she mourned a husband killed in
Cuba; Madame Joubert, her landlady, maintained that Miss Sophie had
once refused a millionaire.

"Why else she wear black toujours?" Madame would demand. "For
pleasure? Non!"

The truth was less picturesque. Sophie wore black because black
cloth did not betray repeated mending.

Her room was at the rear of Madame Joubert's house,
under a sloping roof. It contained a narrow bed, a
washstand with a cracked basin, two chairs, a wooden crucifix,
and the sewing machine by which she lived. Through the
single window she could see chimney pots, telegraph wires, and
one bent banana tree in a neighbouring yard. When the
north wind came, it found every crack in the walls.

On the evening after the wedding, Sophie sat before the
machine with a bundle of coarse shirts beside her.

Whirr, whirr—whirr, whirr.

Her foot drove the treadle; her transparent fingers guided the
cloth. Sometimes she stopped, hearing again the organ's triumph, seeing
again the bride's white face and Louis waiting in candlelight.
Then the room returned: bare boards, grimy wall, guttering lamp,
cold coffee.

"Well," she whispered, "he is happy."

That should have ended it.

Three days later she boarded a crowded streetcar to carry
finished work to a shop in Canal Street. The car
bumped along through damp streets, taking in clerks, labourers, market
women, and two gentlemen whose umbrellas dripped upon Sophie's skirt.
She sat between her bundle and the window, unnoticed.

"I tell you, Neale is in a desperate position," said
one of the gentlemen.

"The marriage brought him nothing?"

"Expectations only. The girl's family has suffered losses. Neale counted
upon the Caron legacy."

"And he cannot establish the claim?"

"Not without the ring. The will describes it precisely—a Roman
intaglio, used as the old gentleman's seal. Neale possessed it
once, but gave it away in some youthful love affair.
Foolish vanity! Unless he produces it before New Year's, the
estate passes to the Baton Rouge branch."

The other laughed softly. "Then let him ask the lady."

"He does not know where she is. Perhaps she sold
it. Perhaps she is dead."

The conductor called a street. Wheels shrieked against the rail.

Sophie's bundle slid to the floor.

"He must have it," she said.

"Beg pardon?" asked the gentleman.

She gathered the shirts. "Nothing, monsieur."

At the rabbe shop she received three dollars and forty
cents for six days' work. The pawnbroker's ticket lay hidden
in a little box beneath her crucifix. She knew the
amount required to redeem the ring: twenty-eight dollars, with interest.

He must have it; but how?

She could not go to Louis. Pride, that last clean
garment of the poor, wrapped itself closely about her. She
would not stand before his prosperous door, a faded remnant
of his youth; she would not let the white-robed bride
read old love in her face. No. The ring should
go without accusation, without claim, without even a name if
courage permitted.

Twenty-eight dollars.

Thus began the great labour.

She accepted every bundle the shop offered—shirts, drawers, children's dresses,
rough trousers that bruised the fingertips. At dawn the machine
commenced; after midnight it still answered the wind.

Whirr, whirr.

"Miss Sophie," Madame Joubert called one morning, "you make enough
noise to sew sails for all the ships!"

"I am sorry, madame."

"Sorry? It is not the noise. You kill yourself. Come,
take café au lait."

"I have had breakfast."

This was not precisely a lie. She had drunk hot
water with the memory of coffee in it.

The weeks passed, and the pile of work grew while
Miss Sophie diminished. Her cheeks drew inward; her shoulders sharpened
beneath the black dress; her hands, flying over the seams,
seemed scarcely heavier than the cloth. She ceased buying meat.
Then butter. Then milk. A sou saved was a sou
nearer the ring.

If one were given to pity, one might have pitied
the little figure bent under the smoking lamp while New
Orleans went brightly about its pleasures. Through her window came
the cries of vendors, the bells of streetcars, laughter from
supper tables, and, on clear evenings, music from houses where
young people danced. Sophie stitched.

Stitching, stitching, ceaselessly.

At night she dreamed of the wedding. But now the
bride's white gown had become a shroud of hunger, and
Louis stood helpless before a barred door. Between them shone
the ring—the Roman ring—red as a coal, heavy as sin.

"He must have it," Sophie would cry, waking.

December arrived with fog from the river and a damp
cold that entered the bones. Charcoal cost money. Sophie wrapped
her little cape about her knees and worked without a
fire.

Madame Joubert watched her with suspicious affection.

"There is a man in it," she announced downstairs.

"Always a man," said the baker's widow.

"Perhaps she prepares a trousseau," suggested young Célestine, whereupon they
all laughed, not cruelly, but with the easy laughter of
those to whom another's silence is an invitation.

Madame Joubert did not laugh long. "She look like one
of the saints after too much fasting."

On the twentieth of December, Sophie counted her savings: twenty-four
dollars and eleven cents.

Only four days remained before Christmas Eve, when the Mont
de Piété would close until after the deadline.

She took more work.

The shopkeeper shrugged. "You will not finish this by Thursday."

"I shall."

"You women always say so, and then comes fever, funeral,
baptism—"

"I shall finish it."

He gave her a bundle so large that only the
top of her black bonnet showed above it as she
carried it home.

Those last days lost their boundaries. Morning was gray light
upon cloth; evening was lamp smoke; night was the pain
between her shoulders. The machine's iron voice filled the room
and seemed at last to enter her body.

Whirr, whirr—the ring.

Whirr, whirr—the bride.

Whirr, whirr—Louis.

Once her foot slipped from the treadle, and she fell
beside the machine. She lay there listening to the wind
whistle through the wall. The crucifix above the bed blurred;
beyond it she seemed to see the gentle face of
the Virgin in the church, not crowned in gold now,
but poor, weary, and acquainted with sorrow.

"You gave Him," Sophie murmured. "Teach me to give."

She rose and resumed her work.

On Christmas Eve the city awakened to noisy gladness. Children
blew horns in the streets; firecrackers snapped beneath carriage wheels;
shop windows glowed with toys, fruit, cakes, and pyramids of
bonbons. The markets smelled of oranges, coffee, spices, roasting fowl.
Bells called through the mist, and every doorway seemed to
offer warmth.

At noon Sophie delivered the last bundle.

The shopkeeper examined the seams. "Good work. You are pale,
mademoiselle."

"The money, please."

He paid her. She counted twice. Twenty-eight dollars and a
few cents remained after the streetcar fare.

At the Mont de Piété, a clerk searched among numbered
drawers and returned with a small packet.

"Nearly lost it," he said. "Tomorrow it would have been
transferred."

Sophie's fingers shook so violently she could not untie the
string. When the paper opened, the ring lay upon her
palm: dull gold, broad and ancient, the Roman profile still
proud beneath its film of neglect.

"Ah, mon Louis," she whispered, "I have kept something for
you after all."

She walked home rather than spend the fare. The damp
evening closed about her. Crowds jostled along Canal Street; bells
rang; rockets broke in red and green stars overhead. No
one noticed the little woman in black pressing one hand
against her breast.

In her room she polished the ring with a scrap
of flannel until the Roman face gleamed. Then she found
paper and wrote slowly:

> Louis,
> I heard that you required this ring. I return
it because it belongs to your future, and I wish
no shadow upon your happiness. Do not try to find
or thank me. I hope it comes not too late.
> Sophie.

She folded the note around the ring, but after a
moment opened it again. Once—only once—she slipped the ring upon
her finger.

It was too large now.

A tender astonishment crossed her face. She remembered the girl
she had been, rosy and proud, holding out that same
hand while Louis laughed beneath the jasmine vine. For one
instant the wretched room filled with summer fragrance and music;
then the vision faded, leaving only the wind, the bare
boards, and distant Christmas bells.

Sophie placed the ring against her breast and lay down,
intending to rest before carrying it to Louis's house.

She did not rise.

Christmas morning came brilliant after the fog. Madame Joubert, offended
that her lodger had not appeared for coffee, climbed the
stairs.

"Miss Sophie! The Mass will be finished. Paresseuse!"

She opened the door.

The little room was extraordinarily tidy. Scraps had been swept
away; the machine stood silent; the black dress hung neatly
from a peg. Sophie lay upon the bed in her
white nightgown, one hand closed over her heart. Her face
had lost its pinched anxiety and wore the grave peace
of a sleeping child.

Madame touched her, then drew back.

"Célestine!" she cried. "Run for the doctor! Vite!"

But no doctor was needed.

When they opened Sophie's hand, they found the Roman ring
and the folded note. The neighbours gathered in frightened silence,
their old romances and merry explanations suddenly ashamed of themselves.

Madame Joubert read the name at the top.

"Louis Neale," she said. "So. It was a man."

Then, with a sob, she covered Sophie's thin body and
ordered Célestine to carry the ring at once.

Outside, bells pealed from every church; flowers crowded the altars;
white-robed children sang of mercy and miraculous gifts. Sunlight entered
the little room and rested upon Sophie's peaceful face.

Christmas had indeed dawned for Miss Sophie.

### THE TONIC AIR OF ISLINGTON

At the house of Mrs. Charman, in Bryanston Square, there
was frequent mention of a certain Mr. Tymperley, whose renunciation
of the world had become, during the last few months,
a subject of fashionable respect. People spoke of him after
dinner, when the servants had removed material evidence of a
meal which would have maintained Mr. Tymperley for a fortnight;
and ladies, reclining amid shaded lamps and hot-house flowers, wondered
whether the poor were grateful for the devotion of such
men.

"Not that he has taken orders," Mrs. Charman would explain,
with eager precision. "He is quite independent of any society—quite!
He simply lives among them, studies them, helps them in
his own way. Such courage! Such self-forgetfulness!"

Her daughter, Mrs. Weare, who had the misfortune to receive
most enthusiasms with a look of reflection, once asked what
form Mr. Tymperley's help assumed.

"My dear Agnes, how can I tell? One doesn't inquire
into such things. Money, advice, sympathy—everything, no doubt. I always
thought him singular; but Henry understood him. Henry used to
say that Tymperley had the most delicate conscience of any
man he knew."

The late Mr. Charman's opinions had acquired, through death, a
finality which they had seldom possessed during his lifetime. He
had been an energetic, sanguine person, very rich when money
came easily and very persuasive when it was to be
placed where more money might be expected. His portrait, over
the drawing-room fireplace, showed a broad countenance full of health
and commercial benevolence. Beneath it Mrs. Charman distributed her late
husband's judgments as an ecclesiastical widow might have quoted a
Father of the Church.

Mr. Tymperley appeared in Bryanston Square on a Wednesday evening
in November. He came late, having walked from Islington, and
entered the drawing-room with that sidelong hesitation which suggested either
excessive courtesy or a wish to escape unobserved. His shoulders,
always a little bowed, had sunk beneath an evening coat
of irreproachable cleanliness but distressing antiquity. The lapels shone; the
cuffs had received some ingenious repair; and the garment, cut
for a more expansive period of its owner's life, now
hung about him with funereal amplitude. His shirt-front, though carefully
polished, exhibited two minute fractures near the stud. His shoes
could be examined only with pain.

"My dear Mr. Tymperley!" cried Mrs. Charman, advancing upon him
with both hands. "At last! We had given you up.
Agnes, here is our saint from Islington!"

"Madam—pray—" murmured Tymperley, smiling as though desirous of obliterating himself
for having caused so much trouble. "The term is altogether—indeed,
I must beg—"

"You have walked!"

"The evening is remarkably—remarkably salubrious."

"In this fog?"

"A tonic quality," said Mr. Tymperley, glancing toward the curtained
windows, behind which the fog pressed upon London like soiled
wool. "The northern districts are frequently maligned."

Mrs. Weare gave him her hand and looked into his
face. She was a young widow, not beautiful, but possessed
of that quietness which makes nervous people at once grateful
and alarmed. She observed the whiteness of his temples, the
tremor of his long fingers, and the peculiar lustre of
eyes insufficiently fed. Mr. Tymperley, conscious of scrutiny, turned to
admire a Japanese cabinet.

There were six other guests. Among them was a baronet
who had lately spoken on the Housing Question; a lady
who subscribed to three missions; and a young man preparing
a book upon the Moral Awakening of the East End.
Each found occasion, before the evening ended, to press Mr.
Tymperley's hand.

"You are doing the real work," declared the baronet.

"The little that falls within my capacity—"

"Don't depreciate it. We want men on the spot."

"I am certainly on the spot," replied Tymperley, with melancholy
truth.

At supper he declined soup, fish, and cutlets, accepting a
fragment of bread and two olives. Mrs. Charman protested in
anguish.

"You eat nothing! Agnes, look at him. This is asceticism.
I will not permit it."

"Merely a system, dear lady. I have found that a
restricted diet conduces to mental clearness. Meat, in particular—"

"But you used to be devoted to game!"

"Our convictions ripen."

He spoke of uric acid, the discipline of appetite, and
the lamentable overfeeding of modern society. Happily, no one asked
how long he had held these views. The young man
who was writing about the East End made a note
on his cuff.

When coffee came, Tymperley accepted a cup, though it was
his habit to refuse this luxury even when alone with
one; and for twenty minutes warmth, sugar, and social recognition
combined to produce in him an almost painful animation. He
told a story of his old college days. It was
not a brilliant story, but Mrs. Charman laughed before the
end, and the baronet said, "Excellent!" Mr. Tymperley laughed too,
then suddenly remembered the room awaiting him in Islington. His
face contracted. Mrs. Weare saw the change.

"You work very late, I suppose?" she asked.

"Sometimes. My neighbours have needs which do not consult the
clock."

"And you never find the life lonely?"

"Lonely?" He repeated the word with an air of examining
some obsolete expression. "In such a multitude? My dear Mrs.
Weare!"

When he left, Mrs. Charman insisted on calling a cab.
He resisted with agitation. The walk was part of his
system; he needed air; vehicles made him ill; the extravagance
would offend his principles. At length he departed on foot,
carrying beneath his coat two sandwiches which Mrs. Weare, without
comment, had wrapped in a napkin and placed in his
hand.

"Magnificent!" said the baronet when the door closed.

"Heart-rending!" breathed the missionary lady.

Mrs. Weare stood apart. She had noticed that, while her
mother urged the cab upon him, Mr. Tymperley's eyes had
rested for one instant on a silver dish containing loose
coins for bridge.

He walked rapidly until he had turned the corner; then
his pace slackened. One shoe admitted damp at the toe,
the other rubbed his heel where a piece of cardboard,
inserted that morning, had shifted from its proper place. The
fog thickened as he went northward. It gave to shop
lights the appearance of submerged moons and transformed every passing
figure into a fugitive from some universal disgrace. Mr. Tymperley,
holding the sandwiches close beneath his coat, advanced through this
appropriate obscurity.

Poverty is the great secluder. Illness may summon sympathy; bereavement
has its ritual and even bankruptcy, if sufficiently large, admits
of public explanation. But the gradual decline of an educated
man through economies, evasions, relinquished acquaintances, and deteriorating linen is
a process for which society provides no decent ceremony. He
disappears by inches. Friends suppose him occupied, eccentric, altered in
his tastes. He, on his part, develops convictions. He discovers
that theatres are noisy, dinners injurious, correspondence burdensome, and the
outer suburbs full of philosophic charm.

Mr. Tymperley had now been a philosopher for eleven months.

His lodging stood in a street off the Caledonian Road,
where houses originally intended for respectable families had undergone repeated
subdivision without enlargement. A brass plate on the door announced
MRS. SUGGS, APARTMENTS; a card in the basement window offered
MANGLES TAKEN IN. The smell of boiled greens, paraffin, and
damp plaster met him in the passage.

Mrs. Suggs came from the back room, wiping her hands
upon her apron.

"There's another letter for you," she said. "And Mr. Suggs
says about Saturday—"

"Quite so, Mrs. Suggs. Pray assure him that the matter
has not escaped my attention."

"It escaped it last Saturday."

"A temporary embarrassment. I hope you will oblige me by
exercising for another day or two that patience which has
hitherto—"

"Well, patience don't pay the gas."

"No. Indisputably not."

She looked down at his shoes. Mrs. Suggs was neither
cruel nor obtuse; but daily commerce with small debts had
worn blunt the finer instruments of compassion.

"You'll catch your death with them things."

"I have enjoyed excellent health."

"You look it," she returned, and disappeared.

His room, on the second floor, measured eight feet by
seven, exclusive of a recess containing a washstand. A truckle-bed
occupied one wall; against another stood a deal table bearing
a bookbinder's press, some paste, thread, and a heap of
damaged volumes. There were two chairs, one unsafe, and a
strip of carpet which did not reach any object in
the room. Mr. Tymperley lit a candle-end, removed his evening
coat with reverence, and hung it upon a nail. From
the pocket of his ordinary jacket—greenish black at the seams—he
took the sandwiches.

He ate one slowly. The other he placed in a
cracked saucer for breakfast.

The letter was from Mrs. Charman.

"My dear, noble friend," it began.

He closed his eyes.

The letter thanked him for coming, reproached him for walking,
praised his "beautiful work," and begged him to dine again
on the following Sunday. There was a postscript: "Agnes is
deeply interested, though she says so little. She thinks you
must allow us to help."

Mr. Tymperley sat motionless before the candle. Presently he laughed—a
thin, brief sound, no more expressive of amusement than a
cough.

Eleven months earlier he had inhabited chambers near St. James's
Street, possessed an income of some eight hundred pounds, and
complained that life lacked an object. His father had left
him investments of unimpeachable dulness. Mr. Charman, who regarded four
per cent as an insult to Providence, awakened him to
opportunity.

The undertaking was concerned with timber, minerals, a concession in
South America, and rights whose value could be understood only
upon a map coloured by the company's promoters. Mr. Charman
had invested largely. More persuasive still, he had declared that
he would put in ten times as much if certain
negotiations were completed.

"My dear fellow, it is not speculation," he had said.
"Speculation implies uncertainty. This is foresight."

Tymperley, whose knowledge of commerce was limited to paying bills
with unnecessary promptitude, hesitated for three weeks and yielded in
twenty minutes. He sold the securities inherited from his father
and placed nearly the whole proceeds in the new enterprise.
A month later Mr. Charman died suddenly at a railway
station. Within another three months the company collapsed amid geographical
ambiguities and legal explanations. What remained to Tymperley, after the
payment of liabilities which he need not legally have discharged,
produced less than twenty pounds a year.

He made no accusation against the dead man. Indeed, as
disaster clarified his memory, he recalled qualifications in Charman's language,
warnings half jocularly uttered, and one letter in which risk
had been admitted. The recommendation had been reckless; it had
not been fraudulent. Mrs. Charman knew nothing. When Tymperley called
upon her after the funeral, saw her surrounded by flowers
and solicitude, and heard her speak of Henry's last concern
for his friends, confession became impossible.

"How glad I am," she had said, "that you and
he had that business interest together! It comforts me to
think his judgment may continue to benefit those he loved."

Tymperley bowed his head. The movement was interpreted as emotion.

He attempted, at first, to preserve his social existence. He
took cheaper chambers, dismissed his servant, sold wine and pictures,
and wrote to acquaintances inquiring, with elaborate carelessness, whether they
knew of remunerative occupation suited to a man of education.
One suggested secretarial work but had already promised the place
to a cousin. Another spoke of journalism, as though newspapers
maintained a door through which impoverished gentlemen might enter by
presenting a Latin quotation. A third advised him to go
abroad. All invited him to dinner.

But evening hospitality creates morning obligations. Gloves decay, shirt-fronts require
renewal, and the man who has dined must sooner or
later entertain, subscribe, travel by cab, or appear in daylight.
Invitations became terrors. Tymperley pleaded health, study, engagements, a growing
distaste for society. Then, with rent accumulating, he moved to
Islington and ceased to write.

Why bookbinding? The choice had in it both practicality and
illusion. He loved books; therefore, he reasoned, he could learn
to bind them. It did not occur to him that
affection for Sophocles provides no natural dexterity with paste, leather,
or the paring-knife. Yet he persevered. An old craftsman in
Clerkenwell, amused by his speech and touched by his punctuality,
allowed him to watch and practise for a few shillings
a week.

"You'll never be quick," said the man, "but you may
turn out tidy."

Tymperley accepted this estimate as encouragement.

The legend of his philanthropy began by accident.

One rainy afternoon, Mrs. Charman, driving home from a committee
meeting, saw him crossing a street near Pentonville Road. His
umbrella had collapsed; he carried a parcel of books; his
trousers were wet to the knee. She stopped the carriage
and called his name.

His first impulse was flight. His second, more gentlemanly and
therefore more disastrous, compelled him to approach.

"My dear Mr. Tymperley! What are you doing here? And
in this rain! Where are you living?"

He could not name the street. To do so would
be to expose the lodging, the arrears, the mangle in
the basement. He waved vaguely toward a region of soot-coloured
roofs.

"In the neighbourhood."

"But why? You never told us! Are you visiting someone?"

"I—my time is much occupied among the local population."

Mrs. Charman's eyes widened.

"The poor?"

There are moments when a lie is not invented but
conferred. The victim has only to lack courage to return
it.

"I have had occasion to observe certain conditions—"

"I knew it!" she exclaimed. "I always said you had
a vocation. Henry knew it too. You have given up
everything!"

"No, no; not everything."

"Your comfort, your friends, your whole old life! And you
never spoke of it!"

He tried to contradict her. He mentioned study, personal motives,
an experiment in living. But each embarrassed phrase furnished her
enthusiasm with new material. By the time the carriage drove
away, Mr. Tymperley had become a voluntary resident among the
poor, an independent social investigator, an anonymous benefactor, and possibly
a vegetarian apostle of sanitation.

The story spread. Invitations returned, now bearing the additional burden
of reverence. And because starvation does not invariably abolish vanity—because,
indeed, it may feed upon those fragments of vanity which
nothing else can nourish—Tymperley derived at first a mournful pleasure
from the mistake. He had failed as an investor, a
man of the world, and an applicant for employment; as
a saint, he succeeded without effort.

There was food, too, at these revived engagements.

He despised himself after every visit and accepted the next
invitation.

Three days after the dinner in Bryanston Square, a letter
came from Mrs. Weare. It contained a cheque for five
pounds, crossed, and a sheet of paper on which she
had written:

"Dear Mr. Tymperley,—Will you do me the kindness of using
this for one or two cases in your neighbourhood where
a little help may be useful? I should prefer that
no name be mentioned. My mother has told me how
quietly you work, and I know I may trust your
judgment."

Five pounds.

Mr. Tymperley read the letter standing by his window. Below,
a costermonger shouted the merits of bruised apples; rainwater trembled
upon a line of washing. The cheque, pale and crisp,
seemed to illuminate the room.

Five pounds meant a sovereign for Mrs. Suggs, thus reducing
the arrears to a sum which could be discussed without
violence; fifteen shillings for boots; perhaps ten for a serviceable
pair of trousers purchased second-hand; food—actual dinners—for several weeks; candles
without calculation; soap of a quality not destructive to the
skin. Five pounds meant, in short, temporary readmission to the
human species.

But the cheque was crossed.

This practical impediment first occupied him. He had no banking
account. The little branch near his former chambers had closed
his account with a politeness more chilling than reproof. To
convert the cheque, he must ask some tradesman or acquaintance.
Well, the clergyman in the next parish might undertake it.
Clearly the money was intended for charitable use.

Charitable use.

He placed the cheque on the table and addressed himself
to a volume of sermons which required resewing. His fingers
blundered. Twice the needle pierced his thumb.

What, after all, constituted a "case"? Mrs. Weare had left
the phrase deliberately broad. "One or two cases in your
neighbourhood where a little help may be useful." He lived
in the neighbourhood. Help would be useful. As to number,
one was explicitly permitted.

Absurd! Monstrous!

He rose and paced the three available steps between bed
and door.

But consider justice. From whom had Mrs. Weare inherited her
ample fortune? From Mr. Charman. By whose advice had Tymperley
been ruined? Mr. Charman's. Had he informed the family? No.
He had spared them—at what cost! If restitution had been
proposed, would five pounds have seemed excessive? It would have
been derisory. Morally regarded, the cheque represented not charity but
the smallest instalment of a debt.

Yet Mrs. Weare knew nothing of the debt.

Knowledge affects intention, no doubt; but can it affect justice?
If a man unknowingly returns one sovereign out of a
hundred which he possesses through another's loss, is the sovereign
less truly due because his hand acts under a misapprehension?

Merciful powers! He was constructing a philosophy of theft over
a piece of paper worth nothing until endorsed.

Mr. Tymperley folded the cheque and put it into his
breast pocket. The mere contact warmed him. He would consult
someone—not mentioning names, naturally. Or he would wait. Moral problems
often became transparent after a sufficient meal.

That afternoon, at the binder's workshop, old Mr. Clegg noticed
his preoccupation.

"You're pulling too tight," he said. "You'll have the back
all crooked."

"I beg your pardon."

"Beg the book's. What's wrong with you?"

"Nothing. A somewhat delicate question has arisen."

"Money?"

Tymperley stared.

"It generally is," said Mr. Clegg.

On his way home he stopped before a bootmaker's window.
Several pairs hung from hooks, thick-soled and practical. A card
announced: GENTS' STRONG WALKING BOOTS, 13/6. He contemplated them with
the intensity usually reserved for a masterpiece. His own left
foot was wet; the cardboard in the right shoe had
curled and now pressed like a blunt instrument against his
heel.

The shopkeeper came to the door.

"Want a pair, governor?"

"Not this evening. I was merely—"

"Fourteen bob, them. Card's wrong."

"I had supposed thirteen and sixpence."

"Leather's up."

Mr. Tymperley walked on, offended less by the additional sixpence
than by the ease with which the man had identified
desire beneath contemplation.

At home Mrs. Suggs presented him with half a loaf
and a basin of broth.

"From downstairs," she said. "Girl lodging there didn't fancy it."

"You are very good, Mrs. Suggs."

"Don't get notions. It was going out."

"I am obliged none the less."

She lingered.

"You doing any good with them books?"

"I anticipate shortly receiving remuneration."

"That means no."

Mr. Tymperley ate the broth. It contained a scrap of
mutton, and he swallowed it before remembering his vegetarian principles.

That night he slept badly. The cheque lay beneath his
pillow, an arrangement dictated, he assured himself, by the insecurity
of the bedroom door. He dreamed that he entered Mrs.
Charman's drawing-room wearing new boots of enormous size. Every step
resounded like a judge's hammer. The guests turned to look;
the boots, detaching themselves from his feet, strode across the
carpet and announced, in Mr. Charman's voice, "Foresight, my dear
fellow—not speculation!" Mrs. Weare then asked him to distribute five
golden loaves among starving children. He concealed them beneath his
coat, but they became stones, dragging him through the floor.

He woke before dawn, trembling.

For half an hour his resolution was absolute. At nine
he would take the cheque to the Reverend Mr. Milsom
of St. Jude's, explain the commission, and surrender every penny.
At seven he discovered that his shoe had split farther
during yesterday's walk. At eight he learnt that the volume
he hoped to finish had been spoiled by his crooked
sewing and would bring no payment. At half-past eight Mr.
Suggs knocked at the door.

Mr. Suggs was a square, taciturn man, professionally occupied in
moving furniture. He entered without removing his cap.

"About the rent."

"Mr. Suggs, I have already assured your wife—"

"She ain't me."

"The distinction, though in other circumstances superfluous, is at present
unhappily material. I expect a small sum—"

"When?"

"Within a week. Possibly earlier."

"You said that last week."

"And was mistaken. No man regrets the error more sincerely."

Mr. Suggs looked round the room, perhaps estimating the resale
value of the bookbinder's press.

"You're a queer sort," he said. "Talk like a lord
and owe nineteen bob."

The door closed.

Talk like a lord and owe nineteen shillings! There was
social criticism, concise and unanswerable.

At ten o'clock Tymperley left the house with the cheque
still in his pocket. He intended to go to St.
Jude's. His route led past the bootmaker's.

"Fourteen shillings," said the man. "Best you'll do in that
quality."

"I should require to know that they possess durability."

"They'll last you. Your present ones won't."

Mr. Tymperley sat down.

He had, in his purse, sixteen shillings and fourpence. Of
this, two shillings were due to Mr. Clegg for workshop
materials, and the remainder represented his entire available capital. To
spend fourteen shillings was madness. To spend Mrs. Weare's money
was dishonesty. But if he bought the boots from his
own purse, retaining the cheque untouched, his conscience could make
no objection. The rent would remain unpaid; food would become
conjectural; yet he would have preserved the distinction upon which
moral existence depended.

He tried on the boots. They were heavy, ugly, and
magnificent.

"A little capacious at the heel," he observed.

"Thick socks."

"I do not commonly wear— That is, the socks at
present—"

"Thirteen and six, if you take 'em now."

"The card price?"

"Need room."

Mr. Tymperley paid. Three bright coins and sixpence left his
hand. The shopkeeper wrapped the old shoes in brown paper.

"You won't want these."

"I do not readily discard articles which may retain some—some
subsidiary utility."

"They're done."

"Oblige me by including them."

He emerged in his new boots, taller by half an
inch and poorer by nearly all he possessed.

Their solidity at first delighted him. Pavements could no longer
penetrate; puddles had lost their menace. Yet with every step
came a thud of accusation. He had not stolen the
boots—assuredly not. He had used his own money. Still, would
he have dared to use that money had five pounds
not lain against his heart? The cheque had already corrupted
conduct without surrendering a farthing. He had borrowed from temptation.

At St. Jude's the curate informed him that Mr. Milsom
was visiting a sick parishioner.

"May I take a message?"

"The matter is financial," said Tymperley, and coloured.

The curate, a brisk young man with red wrists, looked
interested.

"I can deal with parish relief."

"No. That is—I am entrusted with a small donation. I
prefer to place it personally in Mr. Milsom's hands."

"Any particular case?"

"None. The donor requested my judgment."

The young man's interest became respect.

"Ah! You're Mr. Tymperley, aren't you? Mrs. Charman spoke to
Lady Bellhouse about your work."

So the legend had entered ecclesiastical circles. Tymperley murmured something,
bowed, and escaped.

He spent the day in a state of moral fever.
Because the cheque remained intact, he imagined at moments that
nothing had happened. Because his purse contained two shillings and
tenpence, he knew that something had. Hunger would arrive that
evening; rent, tomorrow; the purchase of paste, on Monday. Then
the cheque would cease to be a theory. He would
cash it "temporarily," intending replacement. He would take ten shillings,
then one pound. Accounts would be kept with scrupulous exactness.
Later, when his trade prospered, every penny would be restored
anonymously.

Thus do men of delicate conscience approach common dishonesty by
a private staircase, carpeted with intentions.

At six he returned to his lodging. On the table
lay an invitation to Bryanston Square and a note from
Mrs. Weare.

"I hope," she wrote, "you did not think my little
commission troublesome. If you happen to hear of some elderly
person too proud to ask assistance, I should especially like
the money to go in that direction. Pride makes suffering
so difficult to discover."

Tymperley sat down.

The words had not been designed for him. Their fitness
was accidental; and precisely because it was accidental, they broke
the elaborate machinery of his defence. An elderly person too
proud to ask assistance! He saw himself as Mrs. Weare
might have seen him had convention not interposed its kindly
screen: a hungry, shabby, mendacious gentleman carrying charitable money in
his pocket and debating whether Providence had addressed the envelope
incorrectly.

He took out the cheque.

For a long while he examined Mrs. Weare's signature. Then
he glanced downward at the new boots. They projected before
him with uncompromising respectability. Good boots, strong boots, boots for
a man returning to honest work. Why, then, did they
seem purchased at the price of his last illusion?

There remained a distinction, and it was a real one.
He had bought them with his own money. He clung
to this fact, not as an acquittal, but as a
handhold above some black descent. Poverty had lowered him far;
it had not yet compelled his fingers to endorse the
cheque.

He rose, put on his hat, and went again to
St. Jude's.

The Reverend Mr. Milsom received him in a study crowded
with pamphlets, boots for distribution, cricket bats, parish reports, and
an odour of tobacco. He was a large, untidy man
whose abruptness concealed neither sympathy nor fatigue.

"Mrs. Weare?" he said, glancing at the cheque. "Yes, yes.
We can use it. Plenty of cases. You wish to
select them?"

"I wish to select nothing."

Mr. Milsom looked up.

"The donor entrusted the choice to you."

"Under a misapprehension."

"About the district?"

"About me."

The clergyman waited. Tymperley's hand moved nervously along the brim
of his hat.

"I have no charitable work. I am not resident here
by choice. My presence in this neighbourhood is explained by
considerations wholly personal and—pecuniary. Mrs. Weare believes otherwise. I allowed
the belief to arise."

Mr. Milsom leaned back. If he felt surprise, his parish
had trained him not to exhibit it cheaply.

"And the cheque?"

"For several hours," said Tymperley, with increasing rigidity, "I contemplated
appropriating it. I discovered arguments—remarkably ingenious arguments. I have not
done so. Be good enough to take it before ingenuity
revives."

The clergyman accepted the paper.

"Are you in want?"

"Certainly not."

Mr. Milsom glanced at the obsolete coat, the hollow cheeks,
and the parcel containing dead shoes.

"I beg your pardon," Tymperley added. "That was untrue also.
I am in considerable want."

"Sit down."

"I prefer to stand."

"Have you eaten today?"

"The question—if you will forgive me—is not immediately relevant."

"It is relevant to most moral questions I meet with."

Mr. Milsom opened a cupboard and produced bread, cheese, and
a bottle of milk. Tymperley protested. The clergyman ignored him.

"You can regard it as payment for carrying the cheque."

"That would be disproportionate."

"Then eat without regarding it."

There are benefactions which degrade because they are offered with
ceremony, and others which preserve dignity because they permit no
time for its defence. Mr. Tymperley ate. He confessed nothing
further, and Mr. Milsom asked no questions until the bread
was gone.

"You said there was a misapprehension. You must correct it."

"I intend to write tonight."

"Good."

"The consequences will be unpleasant."

"Very likely."

"I shall appear contemptible."

Mr. Milsom shrugged.

"My dear sir, appearance has had too much attention already."

This blunt sentence accompanied Tymperley home.

He climbed to his room, lit the last inch of
candle, and spread paper upon the table. The composition occupied
three hours. Poverty had not impaired his command of circumlocution,
and shame repeatedly tempted him toward phrases which explained everything
while revealing nothing. He began:

"Dear Mrs. Weare,—Certain inaccuracies, for which circumstances rather than intention
may perhaps be held primarily responsible—"

He tore it up.

He tried again:

"My dear Mrs. Weare,—Before acknowledging the trust you have so
generously reposed in me, I must beg leave to correct
an impression—"

That too was destroyed.

At last, when the candle had begun to gutter, he
wrote plainly.

"Dear Mrs. Weare,—I have given your cheque to Mr. Milsom
of St. Jude's, who will use it for people in
need. I must tell you that I am not engaged
in charitable work and that I came to live in
Islington because I lost nearly all my money.

"The loss followed an investment which your father recommended to
me. I do not write this in blame. He invested
also, and I am convinced that he believed the undertaking
sound. After his death I could not tell your mother.
When she met me here unexpectedly, I was ashamed to
confess my poverty and allowed her to suppose that I
lived here by choice. Since then I have repeated the
falsehood by not denying it.

"Your cheque put me in a position I had deserved.
I urgently needed clothes, rent, and food. For some hours
I persuaded myself that I had a right to use
part of the money because my loss had arisen through
your father's advice. I did not use it, but I
came near enough to doing so that concealment would be
another dishonesty.

"I ask you to forgive the deception, and to tell
Mrs. Charman whatever you think right. I am learning bookbinding
and hope, when I have acquired greater skill, to earn
enough for a secure, though modest, living. I shall not
again accept hospitality under false pretences.

"Yours faithfully,

"Edmund Tymperley."

He read the letter twice. It seemed at once brutal
and insufficient. It failed to describe the ruined shoes, the
broth, Mr. Suggs, the eighteen hours during which five pounds
had altered the shape of the universe. But these details,
he perceived, were not evidence for the defence. If truth
was to restore him, it must not be dressed for
an evening visit.

He sealed the envelope and walked out to post it.

The fog had lifted. A cold wind moved along the
street, cleansing the air with none of the imaginary tonic
qualities to which he had formerly appealed. His new boots
struck firmly upon the pavement. At the pillar-box he hesitated
only once, then let the letter fall.

There was no immediate transformation. The heavens did not applaud;
his rent remained unpaid; breakfast consisted of water and the
crust left from Mrs. Suggs's loaf. In Bryanston Square, meanwhile,
a footman would carry the letter upon a silver tray
into a warm room. Mrs. Weare would read it; perhaps
her face would harden. Mrs. Charman would exclaim, weep, defend
Henry, accuse herself, and send for a cab. The legend
of Mr. Tymperley's sacrifice would expire before luncheon.

Yet, returning to the eight-by-seven-foot room, he experienced an unfamiliar
spaciousness. The walls had not receded; the cracked basin, unsafe
chair, and bookbinder's press retained their places. What had vanished
was the necessity of arranging each object as scenery in
a lie.

On the table lay the spoiled volume of sermons. Tymperley
sat down, cut the threads, and began patiently to unsew
his faulty work.

His fingers were cold and still insufficiently skilled. The task
would take hours; payment, if any, would be small. But
he bent over it with attention. In the street below,
carts began their morning traffic, children shouted, and Mrs. Suggs
disputed with the baker. London, that wilderness abounding in anchorites
who had not chosen their cells, offered no promise of
justice.

Mr. Tymperley asked none.

For that morning, it was enough that the boots upon
his feet were honestly his own, and that the next
book he bound, however clumsily, need conceal nothing.

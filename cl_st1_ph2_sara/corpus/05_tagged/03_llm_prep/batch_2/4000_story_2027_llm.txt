### The Anningley Impression

Those who knew the late Mr. Edmund Williams only in
his official capacity—as curator of the Ashcombe University Museum of
Art, secretary to two committees, and compiler of the catalogue
of the Harcourt bequest—may find it difficult to connect him
with any occurrence not susceptible of arrangement under a numbered
heading. Yet it was precisely his passion for order, and
not any tendency toward credulity, which made his account of
the Anningley mezzotint so troublesome to dismiss.

I knew Williams moderately well. He was a small, deliberate
man, with spectacles that seemed always to have slipped a
little farther down his nose than he intended, and with
the habit of looking over them when anyone used a
general term where a particular one would have served. If
you spoke of "an old print," he wished to know
the process; if you said "the seventeenth century," he asked
which decade; and if you described a thing as unique,
he became civil in a manner that warned you to
produce your evidence.

He occupied rooms in St. Martin's College, overlooking the west
court, though most of his day was spent at the
museum. His domestic arrangements were managed by a college servant
named Toller, who had attended him for twenty-three years and
regarded every object introduced into the rooms as a probable
source of dust, breakage, or unpaid labour.

It was Toller, oddly enough, who first expressed an intelligent
dislike of the print.

The mezzotint reached Williams on a Friday in February. It
had been sent on approval by Platt and Rowe, dealers
in prints and minor antiquities, whose shop in Berwick Street
was one of those establishments where a man may buy
a genuine article at twice its value or a doubtful
one at half, according to the state of Mr. Platt's
conscience and the extent of Mr. Rowe's information.

Their accompanying note was brief:

> Dear Sir,
> We enclose a topographical subject which may possibly interest
you. No great merit, but uncommon. We cannot trace another
impression. Price, two guineas. Kindly return if unsuitable.
> Yours faithfully,
> Platt & Rowe.

The engraving was enclosed between boards and brown paper, with
rather more string than seemed necessary. Williams opened it after
luncheon and placed it against a folio volume on his
writing table.

He has left a description of its appearance at that
time, and since the exact composition is material, I will
give the substance of it.

The plate measured approximately nine inches by six and three-quarters.
It represented a manor house seen from the front and
slightly from the right, under a night sky. The house
was Jacobean, or had at least a Jacobean central block,
with later wings and a low balustrade running above the
entrance. Eight windows were visible on the principal floor, four
on the upper, and three small attic openings beneath the
roof. The moon, obscured by streaks of cloud, stood over
a clump of elms at the left. From the foreground
a lawn ascended gently toward the house, interrupted by a
sundial and a stone basin. At the extreme right were
bushes, behind which a narrow path disappeared.

There were no persons in the scene.

The lower margin bore, in faint capitals, the words:

> A VIEW OF ANN—LEY HALL, IN THE COUNTY OF
———

The deficiencies were due not to abrasion but to imperfect
printing. Beneath, in smaller letters, appeared:

> A. F. delin. et sculpsit, 1802.

Williams's first judgment was unfavourable. The sky had been tolerably
managed, but the architecture was flat, the trees woolly, and
the reflections in the windows conventional. He thought the plate
the work of a gentleman amateur, an opinion strengthened by
the initials and by an uncertainty in the perspective of
the right-hand wing.

Still, the print had what collectors call atmosphere when they
cannot honestly praise execution. The moonlight lay uneasily on the
lawn; the windows, though merely black rectangles touched with grey,
conveyed the impression of a house occupied but not awake;
and the path at the right seemed to lead beyond
the edge of the plate rather than merely to end
there.

Williams wrote in pencil on the dealer's note, "Probably Anningley?
Identify county. Offer 25 s. if verified," and went to
the museum.

He returned at half past four, changed his coat, and
sat down to answer correspondence before Hall. It was then,
while reaching for a letter, that he glanced again at
the mezzotint.

A small mark had appeared near the bushes on the
right.

At first he supposed it to be a defect in
the paper—a fly-spot, perhaps, or a fragment of tobacco ash.
He lifted the print and held it nearer the window.
The February daylight was almost gone, and he lit the
lamp.

The mark was not on the paper. It belonged to
the impression.

It resembled the top of a human head, though so
little of it was visible above the bushes that no
certainty was possible. There was a pale oval, crossed by
a dark band, and below this an indistinct black mass.
Williams took out a magnifying glass. Under enlargement the pale
portion suggested neither face nor hair. It might equally have
been a stone, a flower, or a torn scrap of
white cloth lodged in the hedge.

He was annoyed with himself for not having noticed it
earlier. That he could have overlooked so obvious a detail
seemed improbable, but not impossible; curators are as liable as
other men to see what they expect, and Williams had
expected a house, not an incident.

At dinner he mentioned the purchase to Dr. Nisbet, Fellow
and lecturer in physiology, who sat opposite him.

"Two guineas for a bad mezzotint?" said Nisbet. "That appears
high."

"I shall not give two guineas."

"Then I withdraw the objection."

"There is a figure in it which I did not
observe at first."

"That often improves a landscape."

"I am not sure it was there at first."

Nisbet, who was carving mutton, paused only long enough to
ascertain that Williams was serious.

"You mean you failed to notice it."

"That is the reasonable interpretation."

"And the unreasonable one?"

"That the print has altered."

"Bring it to the common-room after Hall," said Nisbet. "We
will decide whether you require an oculist or merely dinner."

The common-room that evening contained, besides Williams and Nisbet, Mr.
Green of the library, the Bursar, and young Henslow, who
had lately returned from examining manuscripts in Spain and was
therefore thought capable of passing an opinion upon most European
matters. The print was placed beneath a reading lamp and
subjected to an examination rather more prolonged than its artistic
merit deserved.

All agreed that a small object was visible above the
bushes. Green called it a face. Nisbet thought it a
piece of drapery. Henslow, after viewing it upside down, pronounced
it a damaged urn.

The Bursar asked what the house was worth.

"The print, not the house," Williams corrected him.

"I was speaking of the house."

No one had an answer.

Green fetched the county gazetteers, and for twenty minutes the
five of them distributed England among themselves. Anningley was not
found, though an Anningby and an Annesleigh gave temporary encouragement.
Williams remarked that the printed title might be incomplete in
more than one place. Henslow suggested that the house was
imaginary. The Bursar said that if it were real it
would cost a fortune to heat.

At a quarter to ten the company broke up. Nisbet
accompanied Williams across the court and entered his rooms for
a whisky and soda. Toller had drawn the curtains, made
up the fire, and placed the mezzotint on the side
table.

"Ah," said Nisbet, "our lurking urn."

He carried the lamp nearer.

The pale mark was no longer above the bushes.

There was a figure on the lawn.

It was perhaps forty yards, in the scale of the
picture, from the house and a little to the right
of the sundial. Its posture was extraordinary. The body lay
so low that the chest seemed almost to touch the
ground, while the knees and elbows projected sharply. The head
was lifted. Over the shoulders hung a dark, loose covering,
and across the upper part of it was a pale
line which, whether intentionally or not, formed something like a
cross. The face could not be seen.

Williams said nothing for some seconds. Then he asked Nisbet
whether he remembered the object's former position.

"Perfectly."

"You would be prepared to state that it was behind
the bushes?"

"Yes."

"And now?"

"It is twenty or thirty feet from them, on the
grass."

"Could we have mistaken another mark for it?"

"No."

This was spoken without emphasis. Nisbet put down the lamp
and looked at his friend.

"We had better be exact," he said. "What time did
you first notice the original mark?"

Williams told him.

"And Green and the others saw it at about nine?"

"Yes."

"Then write that down. Include my recollection. No theories."

Williams drew a sheet of college paper toward him, and
the two men composed a short memorandum. They described the
position of the figure as it had appeared in the
common-room and as it now appeared. Both signed, and Williams
added the date and hour.

Nisbet then examined the back of the print, the mount,
and the frame, though the last was nothing more than
a removable card border. He rubbed a corner of the
image with a clean handkerchief, held it before the fire,
and even smelt it.

"You will injure it," said Williams.

"I hope so. Injury would be reassuring."

Nothing came away upon the cloth. The ink was dry
and old. There was no second sheet, no concealed flap,
and no sign that the paper had been chemically treated.

They decided to place the print in a drawer and
lock it up. This was done, though Williams confessed later
that he felt foolish in doing it. Before retiring he
reopened the drawer.

The figure had advanced.

It was beyond the sundial now, still crawling. Its limbs
appeared unnaturally thin below the black covering; one bare foot,
turned sideways, caught the moonlight. The head remained raised toward
the house.

Williams locked the drawer again and sported his outer door.
Neither precaution contributed much to his comfort.

At breakfast the next morning Nisbet found him pale and
irritable.

"You looked again," he said.

"Yes."

"And it moved?"

"Yes."

"Then we should have a photograph taken before it goes
farther."

The university photographer, Mr. Pullen, did not ordinarily open his
rooms on Saturday mornings. A note from the curator, however,
reinforced by the porter's statement that the matter concerned museum
business, brought him from his breakfast in a collar but
no tie. By ten o'clock the mezzotint had been photographed.

Pullen was told only that an exact record was required.
He noticed the crawling figure and said that it gave
the scene "a disagreeable narrative quality," a phrase Williams entered
in his notes because it was unsolicited. The developed negative
clearly showed the apparition halfway between the sundial and the
right-hand corner of the house.

The original was returned to Williams's rooms before luncheon. The
photograph remained with Pullen.

Here the duties of a conscientious narrator might require an
account of Williams's Saturday afternoon. I cannot provide one, except
to say that he attended a committee meeting, declined golf
on the ground of rain when it was not raining,
and visited Pullen twice to make sure that the negative
had not been confused with another. None of these actions
is heroic, but all are characteristic.

At four o'clock Green came to tea. He had spent
the morning among county histories and brought with him three
volumes, a railway guide, and a quantity of information about
houses that could not possibly have been the house in
the print.

Williams showed him the memorandum and the photograph before producing
the mezzotint.

When the print was laid on the table, Green adjusted
his glasses.

"Where is your figure?"

Williams bent over it.

The lawn was empty.

The bushes at the right were undisturbed; there was no
pale head at their margin; no black body crossed the
grass. The sundial stood alone in the moonlight.

Green looked from the photograph to the original.

"Well," he said, "Pullen has it, whatever it was."

Nisbet, who entered at that moment, heard what had happened
and immediately inspected the house.

"The window," he said.

One of the lower windows in the right-hand wing stood
open.

It had previously been shut. All three men were certain
of that, and the photograph settled the point. In Pullen's
image the row of windows was uniformly dark and closed.
In the mezzotint the casement nearest the corner had been
thrust outward. A pale oblong, which might have been a
curtain, hung from the opening.

There was no figure, and there was the open window.

Green sat down rather suddenly.

"My first inclination," he said, "is to suggest fraud. My
second is to object that fraud should have a motive."

"The dealer might wish to increase the value," said Nisbet.

"By selling a moving print for two guineas?"

"That is the difficulty."

Williams rang for Toller and asked whether anyone had entered
the rooms during his absence.

"Not through the door, sir."

"What do you mean by that?"

"Nobody's been in, sir."

"Did you touch this picture?"

Toller looked at it from a distance.

"No, sir. I don't care for that one."

"Why not?"

"Well, sir, it's the way 'e's going."

The three Fellows were silent.

"What way is who going?" Nisbet asked.

Toller's expression changed. He had clearly supposed that the figure
was still visible.

"Beg pardon, sir. Thought there was someone in it. On
the grass, like. On 'is 'ands."

"When did you see that?"

"This morning, sir, when I brought the coal. I didn't
meddle with it."

"You saw a man crawling toward the house?"

"I wouldn't say a man, sir. More like summat dressed
up. I didn't like 'is 'ead."

"What was wrong with the head?"

"Couldn't see none, properly. White round it, and black where
the face ought to be."

Toller was shown the photograph and identified the figure without
hesitation. He was then shown the open window in the
print. After a moment he said, "Then it's got inside,"
and requested leave to clear the tea things.

Green stopped him at the door.

"Did you ever see the picture before yesterday?"

"No, sir."

"Have you heard us speaking about it?"

"No, sir. Only Mr. Nisbet saying as how it ought
to be burnt."

"I said nothing of the kind," Nisbet protested.

"Not in those words, sir."

This distinction, I believe, was accurate.

They made another written statement, which Toller signed with some
reluctance. Pullen was sent for, and brought the glass negative
as well as two prints made from it. Comparison removed
any possibility that the crawling shape or the closed window
had been imagined.

Williams now insisted that the mezzotint should not leave his
sight. He carried it with him to Hall wrapped in
a clean cloth and kept it beneath his chair, which
caused the Dean to inquire whether he had developed an
attachment to a hot-water bottle. After dinner the four original
witnesses assembled again, and each examined the picture before signing
a description of the empty lawn and open casement.

Henslow proposed placing the print under continuous observation.

"For how long?" asked the Bursar.

"Until it changes."

"And if it does not?"

"Until breakfast."

The Bursar declined this programme, having whist elsewhere, but the
others agreed to divide the evening into watches. Their arrangements
need not detain us. Educated men can expend surprising ingenuity
upon sitting in a room, particularly when each wishes it
understood that he is not afraid to sit there alone.

From eight until ten no alteration occurred. At ten Green
went to fetch more whisky. At ten past, Henslow complained
that the lamp was smoking. At a quarter past, Nisbet
observed that the moon in the print appeared brighter. Williams
denied it. They compared the photograph and found that a
bank of engraved cloud had indeed shifted—or, to speak with
complete correctness, the light area around the moon had enlarged,
though no one could say how an engraved cloud might
shift.

At eleven the open window was darker than before.

At half past, something pale appeared inside it.

The watchers drew closer. It was not a face. It
was too low and too irregular. For several minutes no
one spoke, and during that interval the pale patch seemed
to advance, not by any perceptible movement, but as the
hand of a clock advances when watched badly.

Then Henslow whispered, "There are two of them."

He was mistaken. There was one figure, but it carried
something.

The black-draped shape had begun to emerge backward through the
casement. One arm extended behind it, the hand gripping the
stone sill. The other arm was clasped across a small
bundle. A child's bare leg hung below the fold of
cloth.

The figure's head was turned toward the room from which
it came, though the face remained concealed. The pale cross
on its back was now unmistakable: two strips of light-coloured
material sewn or bound upon the dark garment. Beneath the
hood, where features should have been, there was only a
granular shadow.

No one suggested that the thing was an urn.

The alteration proceeded in stages. At midnight the figure was
outside the window and clinging to the wall. At half
past twelve it was on the lawn, upright for the
first time. Its height was considerable, its legs very thin,
and its burden no longer wholly covered. The child appeared
to be an infant in a long nightdress. One hand
projected against the creature's shoulder.

Williams afterward said that this small hand disturbed him more
than any other detail, because the engraver—if engraver there was—had
represented its fingers with a delicacy quite absent from the
rest of the work.

At one o'clock the figure stood near the sundial, facing
away from the house.

The casement behind it was closed.

At twenty minutes past one it had reached the bushes.
Only the hood, the white cross, and one foot remained
visible.

At two it was gone.

The scene had returned almost to its first condition. The
lawn was vacant; the windows were shut; the moonlight had
contracted behind its cloud. There was no trace of pursuer,
captive, or intrusion.

The men continued watching until daylight, less from expectation than
from an unwillingness to be the first to leave. Nothing
further happened.

Sunday morning brought a practical difficulty. Williams had invited his
sister and her daughter to luncheon, and though he was
attached to them, he did not feel equal to explaining
why four gentlemen had spent the night observing a two-guinea
engraving. Nisbet therefore took the mezzotint to his own rooms,
while Williams attended chapel and discharged his family obligations with,
as his niece later remarked, an unusual tendency to ask
whether anyone had been missing from the house.

By Sunday afternoon the immediate horror had subsided enough to
permit investigation.

Green had meanwhile made progress with the title. In an
eighteenth-century itinerary he found a reference to "Anningley, vulgarly Annely,
a seat of the Francis family, nine miles north-west of
Wexton." The county name, omitted in the print, was Bradden.
A later gazetteer stated that the house had been largely
demolished in 1848, the surviving east wing being used as
a farmhouse.

More important was a note in the catalogue of Bradden
worthies:

> Francis, Arthur (1765–1809), landowner and amateur artist; executed several
plates of local seats, chiefly in mezzotint. His only child
disappeared from Anningley Hall in 1802 under circumstances never explained.
The loss is said to have affected his reason. The
male line became extinct at his death.

The initials, date, and subject agreed.

"Then A. F. is Arthur Francis," said Williams.

"And the engraving was made in the year of the
child's disappearance," Green replied.

"Before or after?"

"That we must discover."

On Monday letters were written to the rector of Anningley,
the Bradden County Museum, the present occupier of the surviving
wing, and Mr. Sayer Green—no relation to the librarian—whose privately
printed Notes on the Families of Northern Bradden was cited
in one of the gazetteers. Williams also sent Platt and
Rowe a cheque for twenty-five shillings, with a note accepting
the print at that price. Mr. Platt replied by return
that the price was two guineas and that, given its
uncommon character, no reduction could be entertained. Williams paid the
difference without argument, which perhaps conveys better than anything else
his state of mind.

The rector's answer arrived first. He knew little of Arthur
Francis but confirmed that the Francis vault contained no child's
coffin dating from 1802. The parish register recorded the baptism
of "John Arthur, son of Arthur and Caroline Francis," in
May 1801. There was no burial entry.

The county museum possessed three prints by Francis: a bridge,
a ruined priory, and a view of Wexton Market Cross.
All were competent and all dull. None exhibited any unusual
behaviour, though the curator's phrasing suggested that he had not
been asked that precise question before.

The decisive communication came from Mr. Sayer Green, who invited
Williams and any interested colleague to visit him. He lived
at Little Wexton, within twelve miles of Anningley, and claimed
to possess papers bearing upon "the Francis-Gawdy affair."

Williams, Nisbet, and Green travelled there the following Saturday. Their
host was an elderly solicitor with white whiskers, a warm
dining-room, and the enviable conviction that every local scandal acquired
moral value after a century. He gave them luncheon and
afterward produced a tin deed-box marked GAWDY—FRANCIS.

"You know, I suppose," he said, "that the Francises were
newcomers."

"I did not," said Williams.

"Bought Anningley in 1694. Wool money. The Gawdys had held
land there since King John, if you accept family tradition;
since Edward the Third, if you require documents. By 1770
they had nothing left but a cottage, shooting rights of
doubtful validity, and an uncommon talent for offending the magistrates."

The last of them was a man named Richard Gawdy.
He lived alone near Anningley Wood and supported himself by
trapping, odd labour, and poaching—though Mr. Sayer Green observed that
the distinction between inherited sporting rights and poaching was precisely
what the dispute concerned.

Arthur Francis, on succeeding to the estate, attempted to exclude
him from the woods. Gawdy ignored the notices. Traps were
destroyed, keepers assaulted, and timber burned. Francis prosecuted him repeatedly,
but local juries had an inconvenient respect for the old
name.

"At last," said their host, "a keeper was shot."

"By Gawdy?" Nisbet asked.

"Probably. Not certainly. The keeper lived long enough to name
him, though there were rumours that Francis had prompted the
statement. A pistol was found in Gawdy's cottage. Two witnesses
swore they had heard him threaten the man. One later
admitted to having been paid by Francis, but not until
the matter was over."

"He was hanged?"

"At Wexton Gaol in October 1801."

Mr. Sayer Green opened a packet and removed a copy
of the printed broadside sold at the execution. It contained
a crude woodcut of the condemned man. Gawdy wore a
black smock with a white cross sewn between the shoulders.

"What is that?" Williams asked, though he knew.

"Prison mark. Certain condemned prisoners wore it when taken to
chapel. Local usage, apparently."

The woodcut face was no more than a few strokes.
Even so, its high brow and narrow jaw possessed an
unpleasant vitality.

"There is a story," Mr. Sayer Green continued, "that when
Francis visited him before the execution—and he did visit him,
under pretence of urging repentance—Gawdy said, 'You have put out
my name, and I will put out yours.' That may
be broadside invention. Most good last words are."

Arthur Francis's son was five months old at the time.

The child vanished the following March. The nurse, sleeping in
an adjoining room, said she had heard the casement move
but supposed it to be wind. In the morning the
window was shut and the cradle empty. There was no
ladder mark outside, no footprint on the lawn, and no
demand for money. Ponds, wells, cottages, and woods were searched.
Nothing was found.

"Francis accused everyone," said their host. "Servants, tenants, gypsies, political
enemies. His wife died within the year. He then shut
himself up and took to engraving."

"Do you know when he made the plate?" Williams asked.

"After the disappearance, certainly. There is a letter here."

The letter, written by Francis to a cousin in August
1802, was brief and disordered. One passage read:

> I labour daily upon the View of the House,
having no other occupation that quiets me. It shall show
every stone and pane as they stood upon that night,
though no representation can restore what was taken. There are
times when I think the plate knows more than I
have put upon it.

That final sentence was underlined in pencil by some later
reader.

Green asked whether the original copper plate survived.

It did not. Francis's effects had been sold after his
death, and a catalogue mentioned "sundry engraved coppers, tools, acids
&c." without describing the subjects. The impression now owned by
Williams might have been unique, or merely the only one
preserved.

They drove to Anningley Hall before returning to the station.
Little remained of the house: a long brick wing incorporated
into a farm, a fragment of balustrade, and several foundation
lines visible in winter grass. Yet the site corresponded unmistakably
with the mezzotint. The sundial had gone, but its circular
base lay near an old stone basin. At the right,
a path descended toward a thicket occupying the edge of
what had once been Anningley Wood.

The farmer's wife showed them the interior. The room behind
the significant window had been a nursery, according to tradition.
Its casement, though renewed, still opened outward.

Nisbet measured the distance from the window to the bushes.
Williams made a sketch. Green questioned the farmer about bones
found during drainage work and received an account of two
horses, a dog, and what was probably a sheep. No
infant burial had been discovered.

As they were leaving, the farmer's wife said, "You gentlemen
aren't the first to ask about the lost baby."

"Who else has?" said Williams.

"People come now and then. There used to be a
tale that old Gawdy fetched it. My grandmother said you
mustn't leave a window unlatched in March, or the crossed
man would look in."

"Did she describe him?"

"Black all over, with white on his back. But that's
only a nursery tale."

Williams did not show her the print.

Upon returning to Ashcombe, the three men assembled their evidence.
There was no doubt that the picture's successive changes had
represented an approach from the direction of the wood, an
entry through the nursery window, and a withdrawal carrying a
child. Nor was there much doubt whom local tradition would
identify as the abductor.

It did not follow, Nisbet insisted, that Gawdy's ghost had
taken the child in 1802. The print might somehow embody
Francis's own suspicion, fear, or guilty imagination. Perhaps Francis believed
Gawdy had cursed him and engraved concealed states of the
scene under mental disturbance.

"Concealed how?" Green asked.

"I do not know."

"Displayed how?"

"I do not know that either."

This satisfied nobody, least of all Nisbet.

The mezzotint was transferred to the museum. Before accession it
underwent examination by Pullen, by a paper chemist from London,
and by an engraver experienced in historical printing methods. The
sheet was of the correct age. The ink contained nothing
anomalous. Infrared and raking light—then much less obliging instruments than
they have since become—revealed no palimpsest, overprinting, or mechanical substitution.
The surface showed one impression only.

The photograph and the signed statements were deposited separately in
the curator's files. Williams stipulated that the picture be hung
where attendants could observe it and that any alteration, however
slight, should be reported immediately.

For six months it was watched with more attention than
many finer works receive in fifty years. Students sketched it.
Experts enlarged details. One energetic lecturer proposed variations in humidity,
temperature, and illumination, all of which were tried under supervision.
Nothing occurred.

The lawn remained empty. The windows remained shut. The moon
stayed behind its cloud.

After a year the print was removed from public display
and placed in a drawer in the topographical collection, where,
so far as I know, it remains. Its catalogue entry
is exemplary:

> FRANCIS, Arthur, 1765–1809. *View of Anningley Hall by Moonlight.*
Mezzotint, 1802. Unique impression. Provenance: Platt & Rowe; E. Williams.
See Curatorial Papers 41 A–H.

There is no mention of Gawdy, the open casement, or
the child.

Some years afterward I discussed the matter with Professor Langthwaite,
who had succeeded Nisbet and had acquired, with his chair,
an obligation to disbelieve unusual things. He said that collective
suggestion, imperfect memory, and chemical instability would together account for
the affair.

"Would they account for the photograph?" I asked.

"Photography is not infallible."

"And the negative?"

"Still less so, in inexperienced hands."

"Pullen was the university photographer for thirty years."

"Then habit may have made him careless."

I confess I disliked that answer. It seemed to me
less rational than anything it was intended to replace.

Williams himself offered no final theory. In his private notes
he wrote only that the picture appeared to have completed
a narrative and thereafter lost either the power or the
necessity to repeat it. He wondered whether Francis, working upon
the plate after his child's disappearance, had impressed upon it
something not contained in line, ink, or conscious intention. He
wondered also why the sequence should have revealed itself in
Ashcombe, before witnesses, almost a century later. To neither question
did he propose a solution.

His last memorandum on the subject ends with a practical
instruction:

> If exhibited again, the print should not be placed
near an opening window.

That may strike you as inadequate. It strikes me as
sensible.

I saw the Anningley impression once, long after these events,
when Williams unlocked the drawer for me. The paper had
browned slightly at the edges, and a conservator had repaired
a short tear in the lower margin. The house was
as he had described it: architectural, commonplace, imperfectly drawn. The
stone basin caught a thread of moonlight. The path went
down behind the bushes. Every casement was closed.

There was no figure anywhere upon the lawn.

Yet I remember that, before Williams returned the sheet to
its portfolio, I asked him to wait while I examined
the right-hand thicket with his magnifying glass. I had thought
there was a fleck of white among the engraved leaves.

It proved to be only paper.

At least, it was not there when I looked a
second time.

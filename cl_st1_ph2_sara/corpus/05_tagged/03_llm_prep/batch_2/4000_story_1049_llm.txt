# THE GHOST OF ART

I am a barrister by profession, though neither my income
nor my habits have ever exposed the fact to public
suspicion. My name may be seen, certainly, upon a door
in the Temple; and behind that door there are books,
a wig-box, a table of formidable dimensions, and three chairs,
each possessing a distinct infirmity. These circumstances establish my legal
position as conclusively as circumstances establish anything in the Law.
But briefs avoid me. Solicitors, with a delicacy for which
I cannot sufficiently thank them, spare me the fatigue of
success; and I pass much of my time in court
listening to other men argue cases which I could have
lost with equal ability.

Being thus in the Law, but not, as I may
say, of it, I have cultivated the Arts.

I visit exhibitions. I know at a glance whether a
sunset is early Venetian or merely dirty varnish. I can
distinguish the noble gloom of a Spanish dungeon from the
accidental darkness produced by an economical frame-maker. I have written
an essay, still unpublished, upon The Moral Influence of Blue
Drapery; and I once possessed a proof engraving which three
Academicians admired until they discovered that it hung upside down.

These details are necessary. They prove that, in the affair
I am about to relate, I was neither an ignorant
observer nor a man likely to be deceived by appearances.
I was deceived, nevertheless—but by what? Let the candid reader
judge.

The subject of my present recollections first presented himself to
me on a wet October night, during a passage by
steamboat from Westminster to London Bridge.

The storm had descended upon the river with extraordinary violence.
Thunder rolled among the warehouses; lightning revealed the bridges for
an instant and then appeared to remove them; the rain
fell not in drops but in ropes, sheets, curtains, and
whole table-kivers of water. The deck shuddered. The funnel groaned.
The vessel, wrapped in steam, seemed now a phantom castle,
now an iron coffin, and now—as the steward came through
with tea—a refreshment room under supernatural management.

I had taken refuge beneath an awning, where I was
considering whether death by drowning would prejudice an action then
pending against my landlord, when I perceived a man seated
opposite me.

His hat was crushed over his eyes. Water ran from
his nose. His coat steamed. Yet there was in his
appearance something frightfully familiar.

Where had I seen that brow?

A flash answered me.

He was the Captive Prince awaiting execution in the last
spring exhibition.

Another flash—and he became the Roman Father condemning his son.

The boat lurched; his hat fell back; and I knew
him for the melancholy troubadour who, in no fewer than
six paintings, had wandered through Italy with a lute, though
there was that in his hands which convinced me he
could not have played "God Save the Queen" upon a
dinner-bell.

But this was not all. His throat! I recognised that
throat. It had been offered to the axe by Charles
the First, turned toward heaven by a Christian martyr, strained
over a balcony by a Venetian lover, and clasped by
the jeweled fingers of a remorseful duchess. His legs, too,
were old acquaintances. They had supported Crusaders, brigands, exiles, monks,
gamblers, Highland chieftains, and one singularly long-legged Cupid.

I stared.

He observed me.

"You knows me," he said.

The words, though simple, struck through me with the cold
precision of a subpoena.

"I have certainly," I replied, "had the honour of seeing
some portions of you."

"Ah!" said he bitterly. "My pints."

"Your—?"

"My pints. My forehead's a pint. My throat's a pint.
My calves is two pints. Them's what the artists takes.
They don't want me; they wants a bit here and
a bit there. I ain't a man to 'em. I'm
a collection."

He wrung the water from his cuff and contemplated the
result with professional melancholy.

"I sits," he continued, "for the historical, the religious, the
poetical, the military, the romantic, and the 'orrible. I've been
murdered in oil more times than any gentleman living. I've
died as Abel, Seneca, a Spanish nobleman, a Huguenot, and
a party as was stabbed behind a screen for knowing
too much. I've also repented as a miser, forgave as
a father, starved as a patriot, and waited for the
tide as a smuggler. But it's all over."

"Over?"

"Done with. Art's changed."

A blue glare enveloped him. The steam rose about his
shoulders. For one appalling second I believed the accumulated spirits
of his painted characters had claimed him.

"What," I asked, "has happened to Art?"

"Germans," he said.

I confess that I recoiled. The answer was so comprehensive.

"Germans and beards. Everything's got to be deep now. No
more clear chins. No more neat throats. No more calves—not
unless they belongs to a saint in a brown blanket.
A painter says to me yesterday, 'Your face is too
English.' Another says, 'Your jaw ain't mystical.' A third says,
'Where's your soul?' 'Same place as usual,' I says, 'and
sixpence an hour extra for expression.' But he didn't engage
me."

He struck his knee.

"Look at the things I've put up with. Tin armour
in January. Velvet cloaks in July. A carpet-broom for a
pilgrim's staff. A brass coal-scuttle for a Roman helmet. I've
leaned for hours on antique cabinets made last Tuesday. I've
drank grief out of pewter pots, and poison out of
papier-mâché goblets. I've stood beside stuffed 'orses, plaster lions, pasteboard
tombs, borrowed plate, sham fruit, tambourines, Davenportseseses, and Italian views
with Vesuvius smoking like a bad bedroom chimley. And now
they tells me I ain't wanted because I've got no
beard!"

His voice rose above the thunder.

"I'll grow one."

The words were scarcely audible; yet I heard them.

He leaned forward.

"I'll grow one," he repeated, "and when I do, you'll
see it everywhere. You'll see it in dreams. You'll see
it in galleries. You'll see it sticking out of Scripture,
'istory, poetry, and foreign parts. It'll follow you. It'll 'aunt
you. It'll do everything."

"Everything?"

"EVERYTHING."

At that moment the steamboat struck the landing with such
force that three passengers embraced without introduction. Bells rang, chains
rattled, porters shouted, and an umbrella entered my waistcoat. When
I recovered, the man was gone.

I searched the deck. I questioned the steward. I even
descended toward the engine-room, where a person blackened beyond recognition
advised me, in language more energetic than courteous, to return
upstairs. No trace remained of the mysterious model.

For two years I heard nothing of him.

I wish I could say that I forgot the incident.
I did not. His promise—"I'll grow one"—returned at inconvenient hours.
It whispered in the rustling of legal papers. It seemed
to issue from the bristles of my clothes-brush. Once, while
examining a portrait of an Assyrian monarch, I detected such
a malignancy in the royal whiskers that I left the
room.

Still, time softened the impression. I had nearly persuaded myself
that the whole encounter was the product of storm, steam,
and a sandwich eaten too rapidly at Westminster, when the
second event occurred.

It was again October. It was again midnight. It was
again thundering.

I sat alone in my chambers, which occupy an elevation
so considerable that the lower floors are inhabited by men,
the upper by clerks, and mine by tiles, sparrows, and
myself. The wind circled the chimney like a creditor unable
to find the door. Rain rushed from the roof in
mountain torrents. My lamp burned low, partly from atmospheric causes,
and partly because my laundress had removed the better candle
under the impression that it belonged to her.

I had been reading a case on the ownership of
a pig, but the arguments becoming painfully exciting, I closed
the volume and listened.

A footstep sounded below.

It ascended.

Our staircase is dark in daylight and geological at night.
Every landing represents a different period of decay. The step
rose from depth to depth, slow, heavy, inexorable.

It stopped outside my door.

Three knocks followed.

"Come in," I said, with the courage of a man
who knows the door is locked.

The handle turned.

I had forgotten the lock was broken.

The door opened, and a figure entered.

It was immense. It was black. Rain shone upon it.
Something vast flowed from its face to its waist, spread
over its chest, and trembled in the draught like funeral
foliage.

"My God!" I cried. "What are you?"

The figure removed its hat.

"Don't you know me?"

That brow—the Captive Prince! Those eyes—the Remorseful Bandit! That throat—no,
the throat had disappeared. Before me stood the model of
the steamboat, but altered by the most prodigious beard ever
permitted to grow upon a human countenance.

It was not hair. It was an event.

It descended in cataracts, divided into tributaries, curled back in
eddies, and lost itself beneath his coat. It had depths,
shadows, promontories. Small objects might have been misplaced in it
for weeks. A timid bird, deceived by its appearance, might
have nested there and reared a family.

He closed the door solemnly.

"I've come," he said.

"I see you have."

"Call me," he continued, spreading his arms, "the Ghost of
Art."

I pointed out, as calmly as my condition allowed, that
he cast a shadow.

"Anybody can cast a shadder," he replied. "It's what they
does with it as counts. Look 'ere."

He seated himself beneath the lamp and combed the beard
downward with his fingers. His face lengthened; his eyes became
fixed; his lips vanished in the forest.

"Severity," he said.

He separated the beard in the middle, brought two soft
portions over his breast, and inclined his head.

"Benevolence."

He then threw the whole mass across one shoulder, hollowed
his cheeks, and stared past me at eternity.

"Death."

My blood ran cold.

With an upward sweep he gathered the beard beneath his
chin, clasped his hands, and raised his eyes to the
ceiling.

"Adoration."

He twisted the ends sharply, thrust out his jaw, and
lowered his brows.

"Vengeance."

He loosened it, arranged one silvery lock—whether naturally silvery or
assisted by chalk I will not swear—beside his cheek, and
smiled toward an imaginary balcony.

"Romance."

A backward toss, a sideways glare, and two fingers clutching
the left-hand fork produced Jealousy. A complete collapse of the
beard upon the chest gave Despair. Rolled inward and stroked
with both palms, it became Avarice. Blown violently apart while
the eyes protruded, it represented Rage. Draped over the head,
it suggested Prophecy; wound round the neck, Martyrdom; spread upon
the table, Patriarchal Hospitality.

The beard did everything.

"You perceive," he said, resuming his ordinary appearance, "I'm in
work."

I could only bow.

"Never had such times. Artists fights for me. I sits
for three schools a day. Mornings I'm a German philosopher
discovering sorrow. Afternoons I'm a medieval burgomaster refusing his daughter's
hand to a young party in yellow. Evenings I'm something
Eastern with a brass dish. Sundays, when business is slack,
I'm a prophet."

"And is the beard," I asked faintly, "indispensable?"

"Indispensablest thing in the profession. They don't paint faces now.
They paints beards with features attached. Expression's troublesome; 'air's easy.
Suppose a painter wants Remorse. Does he study conscience? Not
him. He puts the beard over the mouth. Wants Wisdom?
Parts it. Wants Madness? Brushes it the wrong way. Wants
Domestic Affection? Lets a child pull it. Wants a 'ermit?
Adds leaves. Wants a king? Adds a crown. Wants a
saint? Adds a plate behind the head. There you are."

He rose and stalked about my room, growing more excited
with each turn.

"I've been the Spirit of the Rhine, the Last of
the Druids, the First of the Socialists, the Old Year,
the New Theology, and Autumn in the Tyrol. I've been
a dying composer listening to his unfinished symphony, though the
instrument was a pianer with no inside. I've been a
persecuted alchemist, a conscience-stricken cardinal, a fisherman blessing the sea,
and a Venetian senator cursing the Council. Same beard. Different
arrangements."

"This is monstrous," I said.

"This is Art," he corrected.

"But surely character—soul—individual truth—"

He laughed.

"Truth don't pay by the hour."

A clap of thunder shook the casement. The lamp shrank.
His enormous shadow leapt across the wall, and there, magnified
beyond all human proportion, the beard spread over my legal
shelves, covered the wig-box, swallowed the portrait of the Lord
Chancellor, and seemed about to engulf the entire profession.

"You remember what I told you?" he asked.

I did.

"I said I'd grow one."

"You did."

"I said it'd 'aunt you."

"You did."

"I said it'd do everything."

I could not speak.

He bent close. The beard touched my papers with a
dry and dreadful whisper.

"It does."

The lamp went out.

I sprang toward the door, struck my knee against the
defective chair, and called for assistance with a force that
ought to have awakened every lawyer in the Temple, had
lawyers ever been known to wake for anything except fees.
In the darkness I heard the door open. Then came
a slow rustling on the stairs, descending, descending, until it
was lost in the storm.

I found matches. I relit the lamp. The room was
empty.

On the table lay one long hair.

I preserved it in an envelope, sealed it, dated it,
and wrote across the front, "Exhibit A." The next morning
it had vanished. My laundress declared she had seen no
envelope, though she admitted sweeping up "a bit of rubbish."
I leave her statement to the contempt it deserves.

From that night my persecution began.

I went to the winter exhibition. The first picture was
Abraham Dismissing Hagar. Abraham's entire moral conflict was in his
beard, which pointed sternly toward the wilderness while curling regretfully
toward home. In the next room, A Norse Chief Hearing
of His Son's Death expressed grief by means of one
damp lock laid across the left wrist. A portrait of
an eminent brewer conveyed commercial integrity through two square-cut forks
resting upon a gold watch-chain.

I fled to the water-colours.

Beards.

I sought refuge among sacred subjects.

Beards!

I turned to landscapes—and there, upon an Alpine summit, sat
a solitary traveller whose beard streamed over the precipice and
supplied the picture with its only waterfall.

Everywhere the same device pursued me. History had grown it.
Religion stroked it. Poetry tangled flowers in it. Romance leaned
from windows to admire it. Remorse buried both hands in
it. Philosophy divided it into systems. Patriotism shook it at
tyrants. Hospitality tucked it into its waistcoat before carving.

And always I recognised him.

A change of costume could not deceive me. He was
a Babylonian beneath one beard, a Florentine beneath another, and
a melancholy Russian beneath a third. His throat was hidden,
his legs neglected, his forehead reduced to a minor point;
but the Beard reigned.

I no longer visit exhibitions willingly. Yet escape is impossible.
Engravings enter my rooms. Illustrated papers thrust bearded tragedies beneath
my breakfast cup. Friends ask me to admire portraits in
which every human sentiment has been delegated to whiskers. Even
in court, when an elderly witness strokes his chin before
answering, I tremble lest he should suddenly arrange himself into
Perjury or Benevolent Doubt.

You may smile, reader. Safe in your unpictured world, you
may attribute my sufferings to nerves, solitude, or professional leisure.
I make no demand upon your belief. I merely place
the facts before you, as a man of law and
a lover of truth.

But if, in some crowded gallery, you observe a vast
beard expressing Piety in one frame, Murder in the next,
and Maternal Tenderness in a third; if it appears alternately
as snow, smoke, foliage, drapery, waterfall, thundercloud, and moral character;
if beneath its transformations you detect the eyes of a
patient, injured, triumphant man—then remember my testimony.

He grew one.

It haunts me.

It does everything.

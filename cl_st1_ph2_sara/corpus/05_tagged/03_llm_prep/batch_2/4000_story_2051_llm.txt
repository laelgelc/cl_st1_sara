Thud. Thud. Thud-thud. Thud.

I sat up in bed and listened.

Thud.

There are sounds which, occurring at two o'clock in the
morning, suggest burglary, murder, or the collapse of the British
Constitution. This sound suggested all three. I rose, put on
a dressing-gown, and went to the window prepared to summon
the police, the fire brigade, and, if necessary, the Horse
Guards.

In the moonlit garden below stood Begglely, striking a tennis-ball
against the wall.

He wore white flannel trousers and a red blazer. His
hair, which was of that shade of red Nature generally
reserves for danger-signals, shone conspicuously.

"What are you doing?" I called.

"Practising my back-hand," he replied.

"At two in the morning?"

"It's the only time the wall's free."

I pointed out that, though the wall might be free,
the bedrooms behind it were occupied at considerable expense by
persons who required sleep.

"Awfully sorry," he said cheerfully. "Did I wake you?"

I said he had.

"Bad luck. Still, now you are awake, watch this. Renshaw
does it rather like this."

He struck the ball through the pantry window.

That was my introduction to Begglely.

We were staying at a boarding establishment at Eastbourne, presided
over by a widow who had once known a baronet
and could never entirely forget it. Begglely had arrived two
days before me. He was a pleasant, clean-looking young fellow
of twenty-three, with an open countenance, good teeth, and no
visible reason for being killed. It was only when one
came to know him that the idea presented itself.

He lived for tennis.

I do not mean that he was fond of tennis.
Many blameless people are fond of tennis. They play it
in the afternoon, change their clothes, and return to human
society. Begglely played tennis, practised tennis, talked tennis, dreamed tennis,
and read aloud from books about tennis. At breakfast he
discussed rackets; at luncheon he explained the service; at dinner
he described, with the assistance of the salt-cellars and a
bread-roll, Renshaw's position in a famous match.

Renshaw was his deity. Begglely could not pass the butter
without mentioning him.

"Renshaw," he would say, "never crooked his wrist when passing
anything across a table."

The first morning I sat opposite Begglely, he examined my
right arm.

"You have the reach," he said.

"The reach for what?"

"Tennis."

"I do not play."

He stared at me as a missionary might stare at
a savage who had never heard of trousers.

"Never played?"

"Never."

"Great Scott! Why not?"

I explained that no opportunity had arisen and that, if
it ever did, I intended to avoid it.

He laughed. He thought I was joking. Enthusiasts always think
one is joking when one speaks the truth.

Before luncheon he had placed a racket in my hand
and taken me to the public courts. For two hours
he sent balls at me with the force and accuracy
of machine-gun fire. Whenever one struck me he shouted encouragement.

"Better!"

Once, when a ball hit me beneath the ear, he
said my style was improving visibly.

At the end he asked if I had enjoyed myself.

"No," I said.

"You will when you get into it."

This theory—that an unpleasant thing becomes agreeable if repeated often
enough—has been responsible for much suffering. Begglely held it sincerely.
He persuaded an elderly vicar to "knock up quietly" before
tea. The vicar returned after sunset supported by two gardeners,
with one shoe missing and an expression which I had
previously seen only on persons leaving dentists.

A maiden lady named Miss Trivett was less fortunate. She
remarked at breakfast that she had always wished to understand
the game. By dinner she understood it thoroughly and wished
she had not. Begglely had made her run from side
to side of the court until she became uncertain which
side she belonged to.

Even when prevented by rain from playing, he remained active.
He moved the drawing-room furniture and demonstrated strokes with an
umbrella. Three ornaments, a lamp, and the widow's connection with
the baronet suffered during one overhead service.

I endured Begglely for six weeks. I do not claim
credit for this. I was younger then and had a
naturally sweet disposition. Still, toward the end I found myself
studying the history of the Renshaw family with an interest
not altogether sporting. I wished to learn whether there were
many of them, where they lived, and whether one powerful
but discreet explosion would be sufficient.

We parted in September. Begglely gripped my hand.

"Next year," he said, "we'll get your volley right."

I made no reply. There are moments when speech merely
weakens resolution.

When I met him the following spring, he had given
up tennis.

He said it was a childish game.

I was too deeply moved to answer. For one splendid
instant I believed he had become sane. Then I noticed
the camera.

It was a large affair of polished wood, brass fittings,
black cloth, and mysterious screws. It resembled a harmonium designed
by a military engineer. Begglely carried it everywhere.

"Photography," he explained, "is my life now."

I expressed surprise that his life should have altered without
notice.

"Tennis is all very well," he said, "but it produces
nothing permanent. Photography preserves character."

This was true in the sense that an accident may
preserve a facial expression.

Begglely's camera was not an ordinary camera. It possessed insight.
It saw beneath the surface. If a man had one
ear a trifle larger than the other, Begglely's camera detected
the fact, considered it the essential truth about him, and
produced a portrait consisting chiefly of that ear.

His first portrait of me represented a gloomy stranger with
enormous hands, no neck, and a stain upon his waistcoat.
I objected that I did not look like that.

"You don't know yourself," said Begglely.

"I hope not."

"The lens cannot lie."

I replied that it appeared to have devoted years to
learning.

His photograph of our landlady destroyed her peace of mind.
She had hitherto believed herself to possess an aristocratic profile.
Begglely photographed her eating a pear. The result suggested that
a camel had swallowed something disappointing. She burned the print,
but the negative remained, and from that hour she regarded
Begglely as a man holding compromising state papers.

He photographed Major Dibbs, who prided himself on his upright
carriage, while the major was bending to lace a boot.
The picture was exhibited at the local club under the
title "The Last Years of an Old Soldier." Dibbs was
forty-two.

A more remarkable work showed Mrs. Pomeroy with her nephew
upon her knee. The nephew was four years old and
quite small. Owing to some principle of perspective governed by
no law, human or divine, the child appeared eighteen feet
high, while Mrs. Pomeroy looked like a frightened doll beneath
him. Begglely called it "Maternal Affection." Mrs. Pomeroy called it
several other things.

His camera had a moral character of the severest kind.
It exposed vanity, revealed false teeth, detected baldness under arrangements
intended to conceal it, and brought to light stains which
had escaped servants, relatives, and intimate friends. It never photographed
a pretty girl without giving her the expression of one
who has just remembered a crime. It never photographed a
dignified man except at the instant when he sneezed.

Begglely maintained that he was merely an instrument in the
hands of science.

"If people object to the truth," he said, "that is
not my fault."

It was through him that young Wilkins lost Arabella Fray.

Wilkins had been engaged to Miss Fray for seven months.
Their attachment had survived parental opposition, financial uncertainty, and a
difference of opinion regarding Brussels sprouts. It appeared indestructible.

Begglely destroyed it in one-fiftieth of a second.

He photographed Wilkins reclining in a deck-chair. The young man
had closed his eyes against the sun and opened his
mouth in peaceful contemplation. Beside him, though at least thirty
yards distant, sat the cook's elderly mother. Begglely's camera drew
them together. The resulting picture represented Wilkins asleep on the
old lady's shoulder with an expression of abandoned contentment.

Miss Fray saw the photograph.

"Who is she?" she demanded.

Wilkins said he did not know.

"You appear to know her."

"I was nowhere near her."

"You are practically sitting in her lap."

Begglely was called as witness.

"It's perspective," he explained.

Miss Fray asked whether perspective was in the habit of
fastening gentlemen to strange women.

Begglely admitted that photography sometimes produced curious effects.

The engagement ended that evening. The cook's mother, who was
nearly seventy and extremely deaf, never learned of her triumph.

Soon afterward Begglely bought a portable camera.

This was a sinister development. The large machine had given
warning. A man could see Begglely approaching with it and
escape over a wall. The portable camera lurked beneath his
coat like an evil conscience. Privacy disappeared.

He photographed friends yawning in church, uncles asleep after dinner,
a magistrate climbing through a fence, and a respected solicitor
paddling in the sea with his trousers rolled above his
knees. No human weakness was safe. If a man slipped
on orange-peel, Begglely did not help him up; he adjusted
the light.

Society became nervous. Conversation ceased whenever he put his hand
into his pocket. Families searched him before admitting him to
picnics. One hostess required him to leave the camera in
the hall, but he smuggled it in beneath a cushion
and obtained a picture of her husband attempting to remove
a fish-bone.

It was at this period that we urged him to
travel in Turkey.

I will not pretend that our motives were entirely cultural.
Someone had told us that the East was rich in
colour, architecture, and men who objected strongly to being photographed.
We enlarged upon mosques, minarets, bazaars, brigands, janissaries, and the
peculiar sensitiveness of local officials.

"You would get wonderful studies," we said.

"Do they mind cameras?" he asked.

"Not at all," said Wilkins, whose engagement had not been
renewed. "They merely stab the photographer afterward as a form
of courtesy."

Begglely's eyes brightened.

"A genuine primitive reaction! Splendid!"

We subscribed toward his fare with remarkable generosity. Even the
landlady gave five pounds, though she ordinarily regarded half a
crown as money recklessly thrown into circulation.

Begglely departed, carrying two cameras, six dozen plates, and letters
of introduction to persons whom we did not like.

For three months we heard nothing.

I am not proud to confess that hope revived among
us. Wilkins bought a black tie. Major Dibbs drafted a
short obituary describing Begglely as "misguided but energetic." The landlady
said she had always felt there was something fatal in
his complexion.

Then, one wet afternoon, Begglely walked into my rooms.

He was brown, cheerful, and entirely unstabbed.

"What happened?" I asked.

"I've given up photography," he said.

The relief was so sudden that I had to sit
down.

"Why?"

"Too many photographers."

He described Turkey as a country in which every view
of interest was hidden behind a row of tourists with
cameras. If he aimed at a mosque, twelve strangers stepped
before it and photographed him. If he discovered a picturesque
old man, the old man produced a tariff. Children pursued
him, demanding payment for having been within possible range of
the lens. A solemn Albanian charged him twice—once for posing
and once for not being photographed in profile.

"At Constantinople," said Begglely bitterly, "a fellow took a snapshot
of me while I was taking a snapshot of another
fellow taking a snapshot of a dervish. It made the
whole thing seem futile."

"What are you doing now?" I asked cautiously.

"Golf."

The word chilled the room.

Begglely's golf period was violent but comparatively brief. He rented
a cottage with a lawn and converted the property into
a course. The cucumber-frame became a bunker, the flower-beds were
hazards, and the hole was placed beneath the drawing-room window.
His landlady complained at first, but after a ball entered
the kitchen and removed a saucepan from the stove she
confined herself to prayer.

He induced everyone to play.

Golf, according to Begglely, was suitable for all ages and
required no physical strain. He said this to Colonel Mapworth,
aged sixty-eight, and led him over eighteen improvised holes involving
two ditches, a gravel-pit, a railway embankment, and a field
occupied by an unsympathetic bull.

The colonel returned at dusk, muddy, limping, and full of
evil thoughts. He had lost seven balls, a club, and
his former belief in a benevolent Providence.

Begglely played through rain, fog, and domestic protest. At meals
he drew diagrams of "the beast of a fifth." He
practised swings in passages. He addressed every rounded object. I
once saw him studying an orange with such concentration that
I removed it before he could ask for a niblick.

Then I lost sight of him.

Two years later, while staying in Switzerland, I joined a
party climbing a modest mountain. Halfway up, we overtook a
man seated on a rock, holding a pack of cards.

It was Begglely.

"Golf?" I asked.

"Rotten game," he replied. "No intellectual element."

He showed me four cards arranged upon his knee.

"Whist," he said reverently. "That is the great thing. Now
suppose clubs are trumps, and your partner has led the
nine—"

The guide urged us onward. Snow had begun to fall,
the light was failing, and there was some question of
an avalanche.

Begglely gathered up the cards and walked beside me.

"Of course," he continued, "everything depends on whether Renshaw—no, I
mean Robertson—holds the queen."

And thus, three thousand feet above the valley, with death
on one side and Begglely on the other, I learned
the principles of whist.

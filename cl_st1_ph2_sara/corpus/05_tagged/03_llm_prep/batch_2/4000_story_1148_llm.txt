We began talking about Blanche Devine before the paint on
her cottage was dry.

The cottage stood on Sycamore Street, between the Dillons' yellow
house and the little gray place rented by the Very
Young Husband and the Very Young Wife. Sycamore Street was
considered respectable, though not fashionable. We had maples, cement walks,
porch swings, lace curtains, children with scabbed knees, and fathers
who came home at six carrying folded newspapers and looking
as if the nation had been waiting all day for
their opinions.

Blanche's old house stood at the far end of town,
beyond the freight tracks, where the street lamps thinned out
and respectable women never went. Men called it the House
with the Red Curtains when they were speaking among themselves,
and "that place" when their wives were near.

We knew all about it, naturally.

That is to say, no two of us knew the
same thing, but we had repeated our several versions so
often that the result possessed the solidity of Scripture.

Then Blanche sold it—or leased it, Alderman Mooney said; Mooney
always knew the legal side of wickedness—and bought the Sycamore
Street cottage for forty-two hundred dollars cash.

"She paid four hundred more than it's worth," said Mrs.
Dillion.

"Five hundred," corrected Mrs. Mooney.

"Money don't mean anything to that kind," said Mrs. Pritchard.

That was one of our first judgments against Blanche: she
had paid too much.

The second was her coat.

She came to inspect the cottage in a long brown
fur coat, though the day was only October-sharp and not
genuinely cold. The fur was soft and dark and had
a glistening ripple when she moved. Below it showed two
extremely small shoes with heels so high that Mrs. Pritchard
declared a Christian woman would break her ankles in them.
Blanche wore diamond drops in her ears, and when she
passed us there came with her a fragrance of violets,
warm skin, and some foreign powder.

"What's she want over here?" asked Mrs. Dillion, after Blanche
had gone through the cottage gate.

That was what troubled us. We could understand Blanche in
the shuttered house beyond the tracks. Wickedness ought to have
an address. It ought to stay in its district and
permit virtue to take another route. But Blanche, walking openly
beneath our maples at eleven o'clock in the morning, was
like a scarlet flower thrust into a row of white
kitchen gardens.

Alderman Mooney met the Very Young Husband at the hardware
store that afternoon.

"Heard who's bought next door to you?" Mooney asked.

The Very Young Husband had heard. He had also heard
that Blanche intended to live there alone and respectable.

"Can she do that?" he demanded.

"Bought the property, didn't she?"

"I don't mean legally. I mean—well, darn it, Mooney, I've
got a wife."

We smiled afterward when Mooney repeated this, because the Very
Young Husband had not always guarded female innocence with such
a flaming sword. Before his marriage he had been celebrated
for sowing enough wild oats to seed the county. Marriage,
however, had transformed him into a stern agricultural reformer.

"You know her?" Mooney asked him slyly.

"Know her! Certainly not."

"Thought maybe you might've seen her around."

"I may have passed her somewhere. A man passes all
kinds."

That settled the moral position of Sycamore Street.

Blanche took possession on the first of November.

For three weeks carpenters, paperhangers, plumbers, and painters went in
and out. She had the porch repaired, the sagging fence
straightened, and the shutters removed from every window. She had
the front room done in cream, the dining room in
a warm yellow, and the kitchen painted white—white walls, white
cupboards, white table, white enamel stove. Mrs. Dillion's hired girl
was engaged to help scrub, and afterward she told us
that Blanche had gone over the pantry shelves herself with
hot soda water until her hands were red.

"Acts like she never seen dirt before," the girl said.

"Or like she's seen too much," said Mrs. Dillion, and
we appreciated that remark.

Blanche's furniture arrived in vans. Some of it was flamboyant:
a carved mahogany bed, velvet chairs, a gilt mirror too
large for the parlor. But other pieces were plain and
curiously girlish—a white sewing table, a maple rocker, a little
desk with pigeonholes. There were pictures, too. Not the sort
we expected, but a field of poppies, a blue river
under willows, and a print of a mother bending above
a child.

She put muslin curtains at the kitchen windows and cream
net in the front room. By evening the cottage shone
clear and golden among the bare trees. One could see
Blanche moving inside, setting a lamp straight, arranging books, carrying
a bowl of apples from room to room until she
found the proper table for it.

We watched from behind our curtains.

She had opened all her windows.

We had closed ours.

The first morning after she moved in, Blanche appeared in
the yard at half past seven wearing a blue gingham
dress, a man's old sweater, and flat shoes. The diamonds
were gone. So was the perfume. Her hair, which we
had thought naturally arranged in shining waves, was tied up
in a cloth.

She swept the porch, shook rugs, polished the front windows,
and then attacked the back garden. The place had been
neglected. Dead stalks leaned over one another; burdock flourished against
the shed; last summer's beans hung in gray rattling pods.
Blanche pulled, dug, burned, and hauled until noon. She worked
with a fierce absorption, as though the roots under Sycamore
Street were enemies that could be defeated by strength.

"She'll tire of it," we said.

But she did not.

All winter she kept that cottage as if expecting the
governor's wife at any moment. Her steps were swept before
ours. Her windows flashed on the coldest mornings. Monday brought
white sheets snapping on the line; Tuesday, the fragrant steam
of ironing; Wednesday, the beating of rugs. By February Blanche
had learned to bake bread. Her first loaves were low
and stubborn, but she kept on until handsome brown domes
cooled beneath clean towels in her kitchen.

The smells that came from her house changed our opinion
of her only in detail.

At first there had been violets and expensive scent. Now
there were onions browning in butter, coffee, beeswax, cinnamon, yeast,
hot starch, and, once, the rich solemn odor of a
beef stew that caused Mr. Dillion to ask what they
were having next door.

"You'd better go over and find out," said Mrs. Dillion.

He did not ask again.

Blanche tried friendliness. That was embarrassing.

She nodded when she met us. The first few times
she even said, "Good morning." Her voice was lower than
we expected and without any of the bold laughter we
had imagined. Mrs. Mooney returned the greeting once, by mistake,
then spent the afternoon explaining that politeness meant nothing.

When Blanche encountered Mrs. Pritchard at the grocer's, she stepped
aside to let her pass.

"After you," Blanche said.

Mrs. Pritchard brushed by as though Blanche had proposed something
indecent involving the canned peaches.

The Very Young Wife was the nearest neighbor. She was
nineteen, soft-faced, easily frightened, and the mother of Snooky, who
was three and believed every fence built expressly for climbing.
Blanche could see Snooky playing from her kitchen window. We
knew because we often saw her standing there, hands in
dishwater, watching him with a look that made us uneasy.

Snooky had yellow hair, a stubborn lower lip, and a
red wool cap with a tassel. He called all dogs
"Buster" and all women "Lady." Before his mother learned to
prevent it, he waved at Blanche.

"Hello, Lady!"

Blanche would wave back with a wet hand.

The Very Young Wife began keeping him in the rear
yard.

In March Blanche appeared at the Methodist church.

We had not expected that. Our churches were open to
sinners in the theological sense, but we preferred them washed,
anonymous, and from some other street.

She came before the bell stopped ringing and sat near
the rear, wearing a dark gray suit and a small
black hat. No fur, no diamonds, no trace of violets.
Her gloves were plain. She carried a prayer book. If
she had entered loudly, or dressed too richly, or shown
embarrassment, we could have forgiven ourselves more easily. But she
slipped into the pew with such hopeful quiet that the
whole congregation seemed suddenly noisy with programs, coughs, and skirts.

Mrs. Pritchard was already seated at the other end of
that pew.

For perhaps ten seconds she remained. Then she saw Blanche.

Mrs. Pritchard gathered her handbag, hymn book, gloves, and offended
purity. She rose, squeezed past three people, and marched to
the opposite side of the aisle. We all observed it.
Blanche observed it too.

She looked down at her hands.

During the first hymn she sang. During the second her
lips scarcely moved. When the minister preached on the woman
taken in sin, several of us thought the selection awkward,
though afterward he maintained the sermon had been planned weeks
before. Blanche sat motionless beneath his phrases concerning repentance and
mercy.

She left before the benediction.

The following Sunday the back pew was empty.

Blanche did not come again.

Spring softened Sycamore Street. The maples put forth small green
fans. Rugs bloomed on clotheslines; screens were repaired; men came
out after supper to examine lawns as though conducting military
inspections. The intimate life of the street moved onto porches.
Spoons clicked in lemonade glasses. Babies fretted. Women called children
home. Radios sang through open windows. From yard to yard
drifted gossip, tobacco smoke, lilac scent, frying ham, and the
comfortable laughter of people who know exactly where they belong.

Blanche made a garden.

She planted sweet peas along the fence, nasturtiums beneath the
kitchen windows, and rows of lettuce, radishes, parsley, beans, and
tomatoes behind the house. She worked in a broad straw
hat and faded dress. Her arms browned. She grew stouter,
perhaps because of the bread and puddings she was always
experimenting with, and the sharp, glittering beauty she had possessed
in the fur coat became softer. Once Mrs. Dillion admitted
that Blanche looked almost motherly.

"Don't be fooled," said Mrs. Pritchard.

We were not.

Yet the garden flourished. Blanche tied her sweet peas with
strips of old stocking. She dusted the beans, pinched the
tomatoes, and went out at dusk with a tin can
to hunt cutworms. We knew every detail because our fences,
though excellent for exclusion, were poor protection against eyesight.

Sometimes she sat alone on the porch after dark. Her
hands rested idle in her lap while Sycamore Street hummed
around her. No one called across. No one asked whether
her peas needed rain. No child ran up her steps.
She was in the middle of us and farther away
than she had been beyond the tracks.

In June she made cookies.

We learned afterward that they were molasses cookies, cut in
scalloped circles, with half a walnut pressed into each one.
Blanche had put them on a wire rack by the
open kitchen window. Snooky smelled them, or perhaps merely saw
them. At three years old he had not yet acquired
our gift for ignoring what he wanted.

He came through a loose picket.

Blanche was kneeling beside the sweet peas when he appeared.

"Hello, Lady," said Snooky.

"Hello yourself."

"What you got?"

"Mud on my knees."

"No. In there."

"Cookies."

"I like cookies."

Blanche sat back on her heels. She looked toward the
Very Young Wife's house. No one was visible.

"Well," she said, "that's fortunate."

She went inside and returned with two cookies on a
blue saucer. Snooky took one in each hand. He had
bitten a perfect crescent from the first when his mother
came around the house.

"Snooky!"

The boy turned, his mouth full.

The Very Young Wife hurried across the grass. Her face
was pink with alarm. She seized Snooky by the arm,
struck the cookie from his hand, and then knocked away
the other. One landed in the dust. The second broke
against the walk.

"You come home this minute."

"Want it," Snooky wailed.

"You mustn't take things from strangers."

Blanche stood beside the sweet peas. She did not speak.

The Very Young Wife pulled the child through the pickets.
Snooky twisted backward, crying after the broken cookie. Before the
kitchen door slammed, Blanche had bent to pick up the
pieces.

We had watched all this.

Mrs. Dillion said later that the Very Young Wife had
done exactly right. Mrs. Pritchard said one could not be
too careful. Mrs. Mooney wondered what Blanche expected, offering sweets
to children.

I remember the blue saucer in Blanche's hand. I remember
how she brushed the dust from the walnut and dropped
the pieces, one by one, into her apron pocket.

That summer she preserved everything that would consent to enter
a jar. Strawberries in June, cherries in July, peaches and
tomatoes in August. Her kitchen steamed from morning until evening.
Rows of glass jars accumulated in the pantry, glowing ruby,
amber, and gold. We could see them when the back
door stood open.

"She can't eat all that," said Mrs. Dillion.

"She's used to feeding a crowd," said Mrs. Pritchard.

That remark traveled through the neighborhood and reached Blanche. We
knew it had reached her because the next morning her
back door was shut.

By autumn she had ceased saying good morning.

Not rudely. Blanche was never rude. She simply lowered her
head and passed. She ordered groceries by telephone. She changed
pews—then stopped going to church. She still kept the house
immaculate, still worked in the garden, still set a lamp
in the front window at dusk. But something had withdrawn
behind her face. The cottage remained open; Blanche herself had
begun to close.

Winter came early.

The first snow fell before Thanksgiving, wet and heavy, bending
the bare branches. By January, Sycamore Street lay between banks
of frozen gray snow, and smoke descended from the chimneys
instead of rising. The men complained of coal prices. Children
coasted on Emery Hill. Women compared colds.

One Saturday the Very Young Husband went to Centerville to
buy machinery for the feed business. He intended to return
by evening. At four o'clock the wind shifted north. Snow
began as a dry whisper and thickened until houses across
the street disappeared. By six the streetcars had stopped. By
eight every road out of town was blocked.

The storm did not fall; it attacked. It clawed under
doors, screamed at eaves, hurled handfuls of ice against windows.
Trees cracked. Telephone wires sagged and snapped. The electric lights
failed, returned weakly, and failed again.

We stayed inside beside our furnaces. We lighted lamps, brought
blankets downstairs, and said we had not seen such a
night since the blizzard of '09.

At the gray cottage, Snooky was sick.

He had awakened from his nap with a cold, and
by supper his cheeks burned. The Very Young Wife gave
him syrup and rocked him. At nine he began to
cough—a harsh, barking cough that seemed too large and terrible
to come from his small body.

She telephoned Doctor Hall.

The line was dead.

She tried Mrs. Dillion. No answer. The wind had torn
down the neighborhood wires. She wrapped Snooky in a blanket
and carried him from room to room, crying almost as
hard as he did. His breath came with a sawing
sound. He fought the blanket. His lips darkened.

The Very Young Wife knew only that her baby could
not breathe and that her husband was twenty miles away
behind walls of snow.

She ran onto the porch and screamed for help.

The storm swallowed her voice.

Mrs. Dillion's house was nearest on the other side, but
the snow between the two porches had drifted waist-high. No
light showed downstairs. The Dillions had gone to bed early
after banking the furnace.

Then the Very Young Wife saw a yellow square next
door.

Blanche's kitchen window.

She hesitated.

Even then, with Snooky gasping in her arms, she hesitated.
Habit is stronger than reason and sometimes stronger than love.
For months she had guarded her steps, her speech, her
child's hands, from crossing that property line.

Snooky coughed again.

She plunged into the snow.

Blanche opened the door before the young mother knocked. She
wore a red flannel wrapper and had a lamp in
one hand.

"What is it?"

"It's Snooky. He's—he can't—Oh, please, please come!"

Blanche set down the lamp. The Very Young Wife saw
her face change—not with surprise, but with an old recognition.

"Bring him in."

"No—the house—he's in his bed."

"Then get back to him. I'm coming."

Blanche pulled on boots, flung a shawl over her wrapper,
and followed through the storm. Inside the gray cottage Snooky
lay twisting in his mother's arms. The sound of his
breathing filled the room.

Blanche touched his forehead, opened his mouth, listened at his
chest.

"Croup," she said. "Put him down."

"Is he going to die?"

"Not if we can help it. Stop crying. He needs
you useful."

The Very Young Wife stared at her.

"Get every kettle you have on the stove. Fill the
wash boiler. Shut that window and open the door to
the kitchen. Have you kerosene?"

"Yes."

"Light all the burners. We need steam. Move!"

There was authority in Blanche's voice, and the girl obeyed.

Blanche carried Snooky into the kitchen. She wrapped him lightly
and held him upright against her shoulder. Between coughs she
spoke to him in a low, steady murmur.

"That's it, boy. Easy now. Don't fight every breath. Save
your strength. Lady's got you."

The kitchen became hot and wet. Kettles rattled. Steam clouded
the windows and curled along the ceiling. Blanche made a
tent of sheets over the table, placed steaming pans beneath
it, and sat inside with the child while his mother
heated more water.

"Doctor," the Very Young Wife kept saying. "We've got to
get a doctor."

"No one can get through that storm."

"We could try Mr. Dillion."

"Try him, then. But put your coat on."

The girl stumbled into the snow. She reached the Dillion
house, beat at the door, and roused the whole family.
Mr. Dillion attempted the telephone. Mrs. Dillion sent her eldest
boy toward Doctor Hall's on skis, though he returned after
two blocks, blinded and half frozen. We woke then, house
by house, and lamps bloomed along Sycamore Street.

We learned that Snooky was ill.

We learned that Blanche Devine was with him.

And, astonishingly, this second fact appeared almost as urgent as
the first.

"Her?" said Mrs. Pritchard over the party line, which had
revived fitfully.

"What does she know about sick children?"

Mrs. Mooney asked whether somebody respectable ought to go over.

But the storm was severe, and respectable people must consider
pneumonia.

Blanche stayed.

For an hour Snooky grew worse. His little chest labored.
He clawed at the neck of his nightshirt. The Very
Young Wife began to sway.

"Don't you faint," Blanche snapped. "If you faint, I'll put
you in the snowbank and leave you there."

The girl steadied herself against the stove.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Bring that basin."

Blanche tried steam, hot cloths, syrup of ipecac from the
medicine cupboard, and a poultice made with onions and something
pungent from her own house. She sent Mr. Dillion, who
had finally fought his way across, back for mustard, more
oil, and a bottle of whiskey.

"Whiskey!" Mrs. Dillion exclaimed.

"For rubbing," Blanche said. "Though if that husband of yours
keeps standing in the doorway, I may drink it."

Mr. Dillion moved.

Near midnight Snooky seemed to stop breathing.

His mother screamed.

Blanche laid the child across her knees, worked with quick
fingers at his throat, turned him, struck gently but firmly
between his shoulders, and bent her face close to his.
For several dreadful seconds there was only the wind and
the girl's sobbing.

Then Snooky drew a thin, tearing breath.

Blanche's shoulders sagged. She pressed her mouth against the child's
damp hair.

"The baby," she whispered. "Oh, the baby."

The Very Young Wife heard.

"So you—you've done this before?"

Blanche did not answer at once. She wiped Snooky's mouth
and shifted him upright.

"My little girl had it."

"Did she get well?"

"No."

The word lay quietly in the steaming kitchen.

"I was seventeen," Blanche went on. "There wasn't any doctor
where we were. Nobody came. She died before morning."

She said no more. She did not need to. We
had demanded explanations of Blanche for years—names, dates, sins, causes—and
received them with relish from anyone except Blanche. Yet here,
in the wet lamplight, she gave the Very Young Wife
the only history that mattered.

"My baby died."

The girl reached toward her, then drew back. Perhaps the
movement was habit. Perhaps fear. Perhaps only exhaustion.

Blanche saw it.

She adjusted the blanket around Snooky and said, "Heat more
water."

Toward two o'clock the storm began to weaken. The wind
still beat at the house, but less furiously. Snooky's breathing
eased by degrees so slight that only Blanche trusted them.
The terrible bark softened. His clenched hands opened.

At three he slept.

Blanche did not. She sat in the rocking chair with
Snooky against her breast and watched each breath. The Very
Young Wife knelt beside them, her head on Blanche's knee.

At dawn Doctor Hall arrived behind a team of plow
horses. He stamped snow from his boots, listened to Snooky's
chest, examined the steaming kitchen, and looked at Blanche.

"You've done the right things."

"Is he safe?"

"He will be."

The Very Young Wife began to cry again, but quietly
now. Blanche transferred Snooky into her arms. For a moment
the child stirred and clung to Blanche's wrapper.

"Lady," he murmured.

Blanche loosened his fingers one by one.

She went home through the blue morning. Her red wrapper
showed below the coat she had thrown over it. Her
hair hung down her back. She had no hat, and
the snow had stopped.

We saw her cross the yard.

Several of us were at our windows.

No one opened a door.

The Very Young Husband returned that afternoon on a freight
engine sent through after the plow. He rushed into the
gray cottage, heard the story, kissed Snooky, embraced his wife,
thanked Doctor Hall, and then asked several questions about Blanche.

"She stayed all night?"

"She saved him," said the Very Young Wife.

"The doctor saved him."

"The doctor said she'd done everything."

The Very Young Husband walked to the window. Blanche's cottage
stood bright against the snow. Smoke rose from its chimney.

"I don't like it."

His wife stared.

"You don't like what?"

"I don't like your being mixed up with her."

"Mixed up! Snooky was dying."

"You could've gone to the Dillions."

"I tried. Blanche was here first."

"Well, it's done. But that's the end of it."

The Very Young Wife's face changed. She had spent the
night obeying Blanche, watching Blanche fight for her child, hearing
about a dead baby no one had helped. For perhaps
half a minute she possessed a courage larger than her
upbringing.

"I'm going over to thank her."

"You already thanked her."

"I didn't. Not properly."

"I won't have it."

"She saved our boy!"

He lowered his voice, which men often do when saying
something brutal they wish to call reasonable.

"Listen. You're young. You don't understand these things. A woman
like that takes advantage of any opening. You speak to
her once, next she'll be coming over. People will talk.
I've got a position in this town."

The Very Young Wife looked down at Snooky.

Her courage lasted another few seconds.

Then it went out.

Three days later the young couple took Snooky for an
airing. He was bundled in blankets and seated in his
little sled. The sun shone on the snow with a
hard brilliance. Blanche sat on her porch, though the air
was cold. She wore a plain brown dress and knitted
something blue.

The family approached along the walk.

Blanche stood.

The Very Young Husband looked straight ahead. The Very Young
Wife bent to adjust Snooky's blanket, though it needed no
adjusting.

Snooky saw Blanche.

"Lady!" he cried.

His father pulled the sled faster.

Blanche remained standing until they passed. Then she sat down.
The blue knitting slid from her lap into the snow,
and for a while she did not pick it up.

I wish one of us had called to her then.

It would have required so little. A wave. A nod.
Two ordinary words sent across the white fence: Good morning.

We did not.

In March a wagon came for Blanche's furniture.

The maple rocker went first, then the white sewing table,
the cream curtains, the yellow dishes, the great carved bed.
The men carried out boxes of preserves. One dropped a
jar, and peaches burst upon the walk in a glitter
of syrup and broken glass.

Blanche sold the cottage for less than she had paid.

On her last morning she walked through the garden. Snow
still lay in the shaded corners, but green points of
tulips had pushed through beside the porch. She paused at
the fence where Snooky had entered for cookies. Then she
went inside and shut the door.

When she came out again she wore the dark fur
coat and the diamond earrings. Her shoes had high, narrow
heels. The scent of violets drifted after her.

A hired automobile carried her toward the freight tracks.

Within a month the red curtains were up again at
the old house. Men who would not bow to Blanche
on Sycamore Street found the road beyond the tracks passable
after dark. The Very Young Husband was seen there once,
though he explained that his automobile had developed engine trouble.

The cottage was sold to a bank clerk and his
wife. Mrs. Dillion took them a pie. Mrs. Pritchard invited
the wife to the church circle. The Very Young Wife
called with Snooky.

We welcomed them thoroughly.

As for Blanche, we said what respectable towns have always
said when they have pushed someone down and then discovered
her fallen.

"I knew she wouldn't stick it out," said Mrs. Pritchard.

"You can't change that sort," said Mrs. Mooney.

"They always go back," said the Very Young Husband.

Yes, we said.

They always do.

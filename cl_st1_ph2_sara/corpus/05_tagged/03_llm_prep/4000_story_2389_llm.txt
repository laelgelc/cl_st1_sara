### The Evil at Barwyke Hall

It is now thirty years since I had occasion, in
the exercise of my profession, to visit Barwyke Hall, a
place then little known beyond the boundary of its own
parish, and now, I believe, fallen altogether into ruin. The
business that carried me thither was neither romantic nor obscure.
Two elderly ladies, cousins and co-heiresses, had resolved upon a
division of the estate, which had descended to them after
a tedious litigation; and I was commissioned to examine the
lands, compare the ancient maps with the existing boundaries, and
prepare such a survey as their solicitors required.

At the market-town of T——, where I arrived late on
a wet October afternoon, I hired a room at the
George and Crown, intending to proceed next morning. The landlord,
a fat, florid man, whose person appeared constructed expressly for
the doorway he occupied, received me with all possible civility
until I mentioned my destination. At the words "Barwyke Hall,"
his expansive countenance underwent a contraction so sudden that I
could not but observe it.

"Is the road bad?" I asked.

"No, sir—not what you'd call bad. A bit lonely after
the common."

"And can I obtain a chaise?"

He rubbed his chin.

"A chaise can take you to Barwyke village, sir. Beyond
that, there's the woodland road; wheels don't favour it in
wet weather."

I told him that I intended to remain at the
Hall for several days, and that my instruments and baggage
must therefore be conveyed with me. He replied that a
cart might be procured, but advised that I should ride.
When I asked whether the house was inhabited, he became
exceedingly busy with a decanter.

"There's Tom Wyndsour, the bailiff," he said. "Him and his
wife keep it as well as two bodies may."

"That is sufficient. I have written to Wyndsour, and he
expects me."

The landlord bowed, but volunteered no grandiloquent eulogy upon the
hospitality of the place. On my inquiring whether the neighbourhood
afforded anything worthy of inspection—an old church, a Roman station,
a ruined priory—he recommended the trout in the north stream
and added, after a pause, that the Hall was "ancient
enough for most tastes."

I then asked its age.

"Older than's wholesome, some say."

He gave a short laugh, but the pleasantry did not
improve his looks. Perceiving that my curiosity distressed him, and
being at that hour more interested in supper than local
folklore, I let the subject fall. Later, under the communicative
influence of a tumbler of punch, I tried him again.
He would tell me nothing. He had never slept at
Barwyke, had no business there, and believed in letting sleeping
dogs lie—an excellent maxim which he repeated three times, though
I had made no mention of dogs.

The morning was clear, but cold. A pale sun shone
through detached masses of vapour, and the dripping roofs of
the town glittered cheerfully enough as I rode out. My
portmanteau, drawing-case, chains, and other impedimenta followed in a light
cart under the charge of the landlord's boy, whose red
hands and blue nose expressed no enthusiasm for the expedition.

For the first six miles the road traversed cultivated country.
The fields, newly ploughed, lay in long dark ridges; thin
smoke rose from cottages among orchards already stripped of fruit;
and here and there labourers, diminished by distance to black
moving points, went about their work in the mist. Beyond
a stone bridge the country altered. The hedges became neglected,
cultivated enclosures gave place to a wide heath, and the
road, breaking into two uncertain tracks, ascended a tract of
common where the wind met us without interruption.

From the summit I saw, some miles away, a thick
belt of woodland drawn across the lower country. Above it
rose two grey chimneys, touched at that moment by sunlight,
and beside them the ragged head of a cedar. This,
my guide informed me, was Barwyke Hall.

He did not speak again until we had reached the
village, which consisted of a church, an alehouse, a smithy,
and perhaps twenty cottages disposed along a stony brook. At
the smithy I was to find a lad acquainted with
the forest path. While the horses were watered, I went
into the alehouse for bread and cheese. The hostess, an
active little woman with bright eyes, asked whether I travelled
farther.

"To Barwyke Hall," said I.

Her hands became still upon the cloth she was folding.

"There's nout there but empty rooms."

"There is the bailiff."

"Ay, Tom's there."

"And Mrs. Wyndsour."

"Ay."

She folded the cloth again, though it was already folded
sufficiently, and carried it away. The smith's lad, when he
appeared, refused positively to accompany us beyond the lodge, alleging
that he had work. This was not strictly true, for
I had seen him playing at pitch-and-toss behind the forge;
but as the road was plainly marked, I did not
contest the point.

A broken wall, overrun by ivy and bramble, divided the
demesne from the village lands. Its gateway had once possessed
iron gates of some magnificence, but one hung crookedly from
a single hinge and the other lay half-concealed among nettles.
A small lodge stood within. Its windows were shuttered, and
the plaster had fallen in broad scales, exposing the reddish
stone beneath.

From this point the avenue penetrated the wood. The trees—oak,
beech, and Spanish chestnut—were of great age, and had not
been judiciously thinned. Their interlacing branches excluded much of the
day, while below them holly and yew formed walls of
glossy darkness. The late rains had softened the track; our
horses splashed through pools in which yellow leaves floated like
tarnished coins, and more than once the cart sank axle-deep
in mud.

Notwithstanding these inconveniences, I was at first pleased by the
solitude. The red and golden foliage, illuminated at intervals by
the declining sun, had a sombre richness; and the long
perspectives between the trunks offered, now a ferny hollow, now
a mossed statue without head or arms, now a fragment
of balustrade at which the wilderness appeared to have paused
before swallowing it entirely.

After nearly a mile, the ground descended. The trees opened
suddenly, and Barwyke Hall stood before us.

It was a large, irregular house of grey stone, built
at several periods, though principally, as I judged, in the
reign of Queen Anne. The central block, with its pediment
and tall sash-windows, retained a certain cold dignity; but one
wing was Jacobean, with clustered chimneys and narrow mullions, while
the other had been meanly altered by some later proprietor.
Most of the windows were shuttered. Grass grew between the
stones of the terrace, and the stone urns upon the
parapet were filled with weeds.

Yet it was not the house that first arrested my
attention, but the water below it.

A lake, or perhaps an enlarged mere, extended along the
foot of the slope. The woodland pressed close about it,
except where reeds invaded a marshy shore; and in its
centre lay a low island supporting three firs and the
remains of a small building. No breeze stirred its surface.
It reflected neither the autumn colours nor the strip of
sky above, but showed a dull metallic darkness, like the
cold polish of a snake. There are places which, by
some accident of proportion or association, impress us as having
a countenance; and this lake seemed to skulk from observation
with a guilty knowledge.

At sight of it I experienced a sensation which was
not exactly recollection, though it had something of that character.
A nurse in my childhood had frightened me with a
tale of a wicked squire who walked after burial beside
black water. I could recover neither the name of the
man nor the scene of the story, and I had
certainly never before beheld Barwyke. The association, therefore, was probably
no more than one of those tricks by which the
mind pretends to remember what it merely imagines.

A bell hung beside the entrance. I pulled it, and
heard a remote jangling within, as if the house resented
being awakened. After a considerable interval the door opened, and
a man of nearly sixty appeared, carrying a bunch of
keys.

He was broad-shouldered, weather-beaten, and dressed in a brown coat
of old-fashioned cut. His small eyes examined me cautiously; but
when I gave my name, his reserve vanished.

"Right glad to see you, sir. Tom Wyndsour, at your
service. We'd begun to think the wet might keep you."

He shouted for his wife, and together they set about
making us welcome. Mrs. Wyndsour was a spare, silent woman,
many years younger in appearance than her husband. She ordered
the boy and cart into the stable-yard, led me through
a stone hall hung with dim portraits, and conducted me
to a bedchamber in the inhabited wing.

The room was larger than I required, panelled in dark
oak, and furnished with a high bed, a walnut press,
two chairs, a washstand, and a small writing-table. A fire
had been lit, but the chamber remained chilly. Opposite the
bed was the door to the corridor; near its foot
another, bolted on my side, communicated with a dressing-room. The
curtains and counterpane, though faded, were scrupulously clean.

I asked Tom whether any difficulty had arisen concerning the
boundaries. He shook his head.

"Not difficulty, sir. Only old marks be gone, and folk
don't agree where they stood. There's West Meadow, and the
ash line, and the far coppice. You'll have walking enough."

"We will begin tomorrow."

"Ay, sir. Daylight's best."

His manner in saying this was enigmatical. I looked at
him, but he had turned to the fire.

At dinner the Wyndsours waited upon me in a small
parlour, where white linen, polished pewter, a cheerful blaze, and
a bottle of tolerable claret did much to counteract the
melancholy of the house. When I insisted that Tom should
sit down, his wife produced another plate. He ate heartily,
and his nose, already touched with red, deepened pleasantly under
the influence of the wine.

I asked how long the Hall had been unoccupied.

"Twenty-eight year come Candlemas."

"Since the death of the former owner?"

He glanced towards his wife.

"Ay. Since Squire Bowes died."

"And no tenant has been found?"

"Not one as stayed."

Mrs. Wyndsour made a small sound with a dish, and
Tom added hastily that the roof was costly to maintain,
the rooms inconvenient, and the neighbourhood remote. These reasons were
reasonable enough, though he delivered them as one who repeats
a lesson.

After dinner I examined some plans until fatigue compelled me
to desist. The journey and the close air of the
parlour had made me drowsy. Tom accompanied me upstairs with
a candle, wished me a good night, and paused at
the door.

"If you hear aught, sir, you'll call out."

"What am I likely to hear?"

"Nout, maybe. Boards stir in old houses."

"Then why should I call?"

He scratched his cheek and smiled.

"Only so we'll know you want us."

I laughed and told him that creaking timber seldom required
the assistance of a bailiff. He did not laugh in
return.

What interruption I experienced that night I shall tell you
presently.

The next morning was occupied by an examination of the
southern boundary. The weather had changed; the sky hung low,
and a fine rain moved among the trees. Tom proved
an intelligent guide, preserving in his memory the position of
banks and markers which had vanished before he was born.
We traced an old wall, crossed two sodden pastures, and
discovered beneath the roots of a fallen thorn a stone
bearing the almost obliterated initials of Sir Matthew Bowes, dated
1711.

At noon, while returning by a cattle-track, we saw a
man sitting upon a gate. He was tall and gaunt,
bareheaded despite the rain, with a ragged coat fastened by
cord. His hair, once dark, hung grey and wet about
a face of singular vacancy. As we approached, he descended
and stood in the track.

"That lad be Dickon," said Tom quietly.

The term "lad," applied to a man who looked fifty,
surprised me.

Dickon stared at us. His eyes moved from Tom to
me and remained fixed upon my face with a painful
intensity.

"You bain't him," he said.

"I hope not," I replied, though I did not know
whom he meant.

"He comes fine—oh, fine!—with his stick tap-tap, and his hand
hid under. But I know him. Bowes! Old Bowes! Devil
Bowes!"

He uttered the last words in a shrill cry and
flung himself backward against the hedge.

"Come, Dickon," said Tom. "This gentleman means no harm."

"Roof over him?" demanded the creature. "Will he sleep roofed?"

"I shall sleep at the Hall," I said.

Dickon's mouth fell open. He began to tremble, then laughed
so violently that he was forced to clutch the gate.

"At Hall! Hear that! He'll sleep where Bowes walks. Don't
turn, master. Don't you look behind. He's patient when he's
dead."

Tom took my arm and drew me onward. Dickon followed
for perhaps fifty yards, crying, "Bowes is the devil!" at
diminishing intervals. Then, as abruptly as he had begun, he
stopped. When I looked back, he was crouched in the
rain, scraping at the mud with both hands.

"Poor fellow," I said. "Has he long been so?"

"Twenty year and more."

"Where does he live?"

"Here and there. Folk give him meat. He won't sleep
under tiles nor thatch, not if snow's knee-deep."

"What frightened him?"

Tom's face closed.

"Best ask nout of Dickon. What's broken in him can't
be mended."

I reminded him that it was he who had spoken
first of calling out in the night, and asked whether
Dickon's madness had any connection with the Hall. Tom quickened
his pace.

"Let sleepin' dogs be, sir."

This echo of the landlord's maxim did not escape me.

Our work continued until dusk. On reaching the Hall, I
changed my wet clothes and spent an hour arranging notes.
I confess that Dickon's words had left an unpleasant impression,
although I attributed it to his wild voice and miserable
appearance rather than to their sense.

And now I must return to the preceding night.

I had gone to bed shortly after eleven, leaving my
candle upon the table beside me. The fire burned low
but had not expired. I slept soundly for some hours,
and awoke—I could not remember how—with the conviction that someone
was walking in the corridor.

The sound was faint and deliberate. Three steps; a pause;
then two more. The boards of the passage were old,
and I knew already that they complained beneath a foot.
Nevertheless, I lay still, supposing that Tom had risen to
attend to some household matter.

The steps ceased outside my door.

I looked towards it. A narrow band of light showed
beneath, though I had observed no lamp in the corridor
when I retired. This light did not resemble the yellow
illumination of a candle. It was pale and steady, but
unevenly distributed, as though partially intercepted by a person standing
outside.

I called, "Who is there?"

There was no answer.

The latch moved.

It rose slowly, remained suspended, and sank again. Then the
door opened inward to the breadth of an inch. I
remembered distinctly that Tom had advised me to lock it,
and I had as distinctly turned the key. Yet I
did not hear the lock withdraw.

I sat up. The opening widened by another inch, and
the pale light entered with it, tracing a line along
the floor and touching the edge of the bed-curtain. At
first I saw nothing beyond the door. Then a hand
came round and gripped its inner edge.

It was a man's left hand, large and discoloured, the
skin loose about the joints. The cuff above it was
black. What fixed my sight, however, was a deformity: the
middle finger had been shortened, either by injury or from
birth, and ended in a broad nail which curved outward
like a claw.

The fingers tightened upon the wood. The door opened farther.

I am not ashamed to say that my courage, though
sufficient for ordinary dangers, did not prompt me to await
the entrance of whatever stood there. I sprang from bed,
seized the heavy brass candlestick, and cried, "Come in, then,
and show yourself!"

The hand disappeared.

At the same instant the strange light was extinguished. The
door moved violently, but whether it closed or opened I
could not immediately perceive, for my candle, overturned by my
movement, went out, and the room was left to the
faint red glimmer of the fire.

I crossed the floor and found the door shut. The
key was turned. When I opened it, the corridor beyond
lay perfectly dark and empty.

I called Tom. After some delay he appeared at the
farther end, carrying a lamp and wearing breeches, boots, and
a coat hastily thrown over his nightshirt. His wife followed
with a candle.

"What is it, sir?" he asked.

I told him exactly what had happened. At the description
of the hand, the lamp shook visibly.

"The middle finger," he said. "Was it cut down so?
And the nail—"

"Like a talon," I answered. "Do you know it?"

Mrs. Wyndsour caught her husband's sleeve.

"Tom, don't."

He looked from her to me.

"You've seen Squire Bowes's hand."

I demanded an explanation, but neither would give one. Tom
searched the corridor and adjoining rooms, examined my lock, and
even descended to ascertain that the outer doors were secure.
There was no sign of an intruder. He offered to
remain outside my room, but I would not permit it.
Having relit my candle, I returned to bed; though I
may confess that I did not sleep, and that every
trivial noise of the house acquired until morning an individuality
which no amount of reasoning could subdue.

I had intended to question Tom at breakfast, but the
day's work and our meeting with Dickon delayed the matter.
That evening, after supper, I bolted the parlour door and
told him plainly that I would hear the whole history.

Mrs. Wyndsour rose.

"Don't rake it up."

"The gentleman's seen what he's seen," said Tom. "Hiding names
won't alter nout."

She departed, closing the door carefully behind her. Tom filled
his glass, pushed it away untouched, drew his chair nearer
the fire, and began.

Squire Bowes, he said, had been the last male owner
of Barwyke. In life he was accounted a quiet gentleman,
shy of company and little disposed to violence. Servants who
had known him declared that he spoke softly, paid punctually,
and could not abide the sight of an animal in
pain. Yet he possessed one settled hatred: he detested the
two female relations who stood to inherit his lands.

"He'd say they were waiting for his shoes," Tom continued,
"and watching every crumb he ate. They lived south, nigh
a hundred mile off, and scarce came here twice in
ten year, but he hated 'em as if they sat
at his table daily."

Bowes had repeatedly threatened to break the entail or dispose
of the property. Lawyers visited the Hall; papers were drafted
and destroyed; but his irresolution, or perhaps some defect in
the title, prevented action. In his last illness he sent
urgently for his solicitor, intending, it was believed, to execute
a final instrument. The solicitor arrived too late. Bowes died
at sunset and was buried in the family vault.

The co-heiresses took possession, but neither came to reside. A
steward was installed, the household dismissed, and the estate placed
largely under pasture. Within a month, strange reports began among
the labourers.

An old shepherd saw Bowes walking beside the lake at
dawn, wearing his broad hat and leaning on the blackthorn
stick he had carried in life. The shepherd addressed him,
thinking at first that death had been falsely reported. Bowes
turned, raised his left hand, and beckoned towards the water.
The man fled.

Soon afterwards a dairymaid met the same figure in the
stable-yard. She recognised the dragging step, the black coat, and,
when he lifted the latch of a stall, the misshapen
finger. The cow within sickened that night.

"Then another went," said Tom, "and another. First one, and
then another. They'd be well at sundown, chewing quiet; by
morn they'd stand all of a sweat, eyes staring, and
no power in their legs. The beast-doctor could make nout
of it. Little by little, the best cattle on the
place died."

"Disease," I suggested.

"May be. But the cows he touched were the cows
that took it."

"How could anyone know which he touched?"

"Because he was seen, sir. There'd be the old Squire,
moving down the byre with his stick under his arm,
laying that hand on a beast's neck. Shut doors did
not hinder him. Dogs would crawl flat and whine."

To guard the stock, two young herdsmen—brothers, Will and Dickon
Forth—were ordered to sleep in a room above the principal
cattle-house. Dickon was then eighteen, a merry, fearless youth, fond
of wrestling and known to laugh at every tale of
spirits. For several nights nothing occurred. The losses ceased, and
the steward congratulated himself upon having frightened away not a
ghost but a human mischief-maker.

On the sixth night the brothers heard the cattle moving
below. Will took a lantern and descended. He found the
animals crowded together at one end of the byre. At
the other stood Squire Bowes.

"He'd his back to Will," said Tom, speaking now with
the slow repetition of one who has often told a
tale in his own mind. "There was his coat, there
was his hat, there was the stick. Will couldn't stir.
Then Bowes turned his head—not his body, mind, only his
head—and looked across his shoulder."

Will dropped the lantern and ran upstairs. Dickon laughed at
him, but agreed to stay awake. They fastened the stair
door with a bar, locked their chamber, pushed a chest
against it, and lay down fully clothed, each with a
cudgel close to hand.

Near midnight Will heard a sound upon the stairs.

Tap—drag. Tap—drag.

The barred outer door opened below without crash or splinter.
The steps ascended. They crossed the landing and stopped outside
the chamber.

"Will shook Dickon," said Tom, "but the lad was dead
asleep. He'd never been a heavy sleeper, not before nor
since—if he sleeps now at all. Will called him. No
good. He pinched him, struck his cheek, pulled his hair.
No good."

The chamber door began to open. The lock remained turned,
and the chest did not move, but the door advanced
through it—or appeared to do so—until a tall figure stood
between the brothers' beds.

Will saw no face beneath the hat. He saw only
the left hand hanging beside the black coat: large, pale,
with the middle finger shortened and its nail bent like
a bird's claw.

Bowes leaned over Dickon.

Will attempted to rise, but could not. The figure passed
one arm beneath the sleeping youth and lifted him as
easily as a child. Then it turned towards the door.
As it crossed the room, Dickon's bare foot struck the
bedpost. He did not wake.

Bowes carried him out.

"For how long was Will unable to move?" I asked.

"He couldn't say. Might be minutes; might be till cockcrow.
When strength came back, candle was still burning. Door was
locked. Chest stood where they'd shoved it. Sure enough his
brother Dickon was gone."

A search began before dawn. The byres, barns, woods, and
fields were examined. Men dragged the lake margins and opened
disused wells. There were no footprints beneath the window, no
disturbance at either locked door, no sign of him.

At noon, a keeper on the eastern bank observed something
moving upon the island in the lake. A boat was
fetched. There they found Dickon crouched among the ruins of
the summer-house, soaked to the skin and bleeding from one
hand. The boat that ordinarily served the island was chained
at the shore; no other craft existed. Dickon could not
swim, and the water was deep.

He recognised no one. When the men approached, he fought
them, shrieking that Bowes was behind them. They brought him
ashore by force. For weeks he lay feverish, and when
his bodily health returned, his reason did not. He had
never again slept beneath a roof.

"Twenty year," Tom concluded. "Summer and winter. He'll lie in
ditch, stack-bottom, hollow tree—anywhere the sky's over him. And always
the same words when the fit takes him: Bowes be
the devil."

The fire had sunk while he spoke. A coal collapsed
softly, sending a brief illumination across the walls. I was
conscious of the great uninhabited house extending above and around
us—corridors without lamps, chambers sealed for decades, stairs upon which
no living foot had business at that hour.

"Why," I asked, "should Bowes torment the boy?"

Tom shook his head.

"Why kill cattle? Why show his spite after he'd lain
quiet enough in life? Some say hatred grew in him
secret, same as mould in a shut room. Some say
he meant the estate never to profit them ladies. I
know nout of dead men's reasons."

A scratching came at the window.

Tom started so sharply that his chair struck the floor.
I turned.

A face was pressed against the glass.

For an instant I saw only the flattened nose, the
open mouth, and two eyes shining in the firelight. Then
the face withdrew and a hand beat upon the pane.

"Bowes!" screamed Dickon from outside. "Bowes be behind you!"

I cannot adequately convey the effect of those words, uttered
at that hour and following the story I had heard.
I turned involuntarily towards the door. Nothing stood there. When
I looked again, Dickon was gesticulating wildly through the glass,
his wet hair streaming about his face.

"He's come in!" he cried. "Black coat—crooked hand—he's come for
thee!"

Tom hurried to the window, closed the inner shutter, and
dropped the bar.

"How did he enter the grounds?" I asked.

"Walls don't keep Dickon out."

"Open it. The poor wretch will perish in the cold."

"He won't come in, sir. Not him."

We went to the outer door, but by the time
Tom unfastened it there was no one upon the terrace.
We called and carried a lantern around the house. The
rain had ceased; the lake lay below us, without a
ripple, and the ruined summer-house on the island showed faintly
between the three black firs. Once I fancied I heard
laughter in the wood, but it might have been the
cry of a bird.

I spent the remainder of the night in the parlour.
No persuasion would have induced me to occupy the bedchamber
again, though I represented my decision to Tom as a
desire not to disturb the household should the noises recur.
At every sound I looked towards the door. Nothing appeared.

My horses, which had been ordered from T—— for the
following afternoon, arrived shortly before ten in the morning, the
groom having made better time than expected. I informed Tom
that the maps and notes already obtained would enable me
to complete most of my work elsewhere, and that any
remaining measurements could be supplied by a local assistant.

He received this announcement without surprise.

"You're right, sir," he said. "There's business better done by
daylight—and places better left when business is done."

As we rode from the Hall, I looked back once.
The upper windows reflected the pale sky, except that of
my late chamber, which appeared dark, though I could not
remember that its shutter had been closed.

Near the broken lodge we passed Dickon. He stood among
the brambles, bareheaded and motionless. He did not look at
me. His gaze was fixed upon the distant chimneys, and
as I went by I heard him muttering, with dreadful
patience:

"Tap—drag. Tap—drag. He's patient when he's dead."

I was rather glad when the woods closed behind us;
gladder still when we reached the common and saw the
smoke of Barwyke village. I never returned to the Hall.
The division of the property was concluded by others, and
the two old ladies, I believe, enjoyed the revenues without
once visiting their inheritance.

Whether the figure I saw was Squire Bowes, or whether
an impression received unconsciously from some forgotten tale shaped a
common nocturnal illusion, I do not pretend to determine. I
record only this: before Tom Wyndsour spoke the name, I
had described the hand; and before I heard the history
of Dickon Forth, I had seen that same shortened finger
close upon my door.

# The Edge of the Storm

The storm had eaten the trail.

All morning it came roaring down from the white shoulders
of the Coast Range, dragging sleet in its fists, wrenching
at the stunted spruce, and beating the tents flat until
the wet canvas slapped the sleepers' faces. Where yesterday a
man might have picked his way among stones and roots,
there now ran a brown, shoulder-deep river; where the trail
climbed, it vanished under sliding gravel; and where it skirted
the glacier, blue masses cracked and thundered like artillery behind
a curtain of driven snow.

In the lee of a granite ledge crouched a tent
which had once been white. It was patched with flour
sacks, smoke-blackened at the ridge, and held to the earth
by guy ropes made fast to axes, pack frames, spruce
roots, and one iron frying pan hammered edgewise into the
muck.

Within it were three people, their provisions, their bedding, and
the smell of wet wool.

Molly Travis sat upon a sack of beans and listened
to the gale.

She was not a woman accustomed to listening. She had
talked her way from San Francisco to Dyea, argued her
way over the Pass, and mocked, coaxed, hired, bullied, or
charmed every man who had tried to advise her between
salt water and the lakes. Five American-born generations stood behind
her narrow shoulders and objected, collectively and with violence, to
delay. Her chin was pointed, her gray eyes were hot,
and her auburn hair, loosened by the damp, curled in
little rebellious tendrils around her temples.

Across from her sat two Englishmen.

Dick Mowbray, lean and long-legged, was mending a moccasin with
waxed thread. He had the composed face of a man
who had once lost a ship's boat in the Southern
Ocean and considered most later inconveniences repetitions on a smaller
scale.

Tommy Hayward lay near the stove, smoking a short black
pipe which had gone out three times. He was thick
through the chest, bald at the crown, and red-bearded; and
he wore, even in camp, the expression of one who
suspected the universe of sharp practice.

The tent wall bellied inward. Sleet rattled against it with
the hard spite of thrown gravel.

"Well?" Molly demanded.

Neither man answered.

"Well?" she repeated.

Dick drew the thread through the leather and tested the
seam with his thumb.

Tommy sucked upon his dead pipe.

Molly rose. The tent was too low for dignity, and
the ridgepole forced her to bend her head, but she
contrived to look down upon both men.

"You said we should start at daylight."

"That was yesterday," said Dick.

"You said the trail would be bad."

"It is."

"You said we could make Dawson before the river locked."

"If the weather held."

"The weather has not held, and you sit there sewing!"

"Better than sitting with a hole in one's foot," Dick
replied.

She turned on Tommy. "And you?"

"I'm smokin'."

"You are doing nothing."

"'Tis difficult smokin' with no fire in the pipe," he
admitted. "But I'm perseverin'."

A gust struck the tent broadside. The ridgepole jumped; one
of the corner ropes groaned, and a trickle of icy
water ran beneath the ground sheet and touched Molly's boot.

She looked at it as though the two Britons had
arranged the matter.

"Our cache is three miles back," she said. "My other
clothes are there. My stockings, my skirts, my good boots—everything."

"Pots too," said Tommy. "And half the tea."

"I do not care about the pots."

"You'll care at supper."

"I mean to go back for my clothes."

"No," Dick said.

It was spoken quietly, which made it worse.

Molly's color rose. "You cannot order me."

"I can tell you the creek has risen six feet,
that the lower bridge went out before dawn, and that
a spruce a yard thick crossed the trail not twenty
minutes ago."

"You have not seen it."

"I heard it."

"You heard thunder."

"A tree makes one sort of noise when it falls,"
said Dick, "and a glacier another. A man who means
to live learns the distinction."

"And a man who means to do nothing learns a
reason for everything."

Tommy removed the pipe from his mouth.

"Look here, miss," he said. "There's no shame in waitin'.
Not in this country. Shame's for cities, where there's policemen
to drag a fool out from under the wheels. Up
here the weather's judge, jury, parson, and undertaker. When it
says lie down, you lie down."

"Because you are afraid."

Tommy's red beard stirred. "Ay. Because I'm afraid."

The ready admission checked her for half a breath; then
pride supplied fresh powder.

"An American would go."

"An American did go," said Dick. "Two of them. They
started at dawn."

Molly's eyes flashed triumphantly.

"And came back at nine," he continued. "One without his
pack, the other without three fingers' worth of skin."

"They were men, at least."

"A man may be a fool on two legs as
handily as a woman."

"If I were a man—"

Tommy groaned. "Gawd preserve us!"

"If I were a man, I would fetch those things
myself. I would not sit in a tent and call
cowardice wisdom."

Dick bit the thread and laid the moccasin aside.

"If you were a man," he said, "we might knock
you down and tie you to the bean sack."

She stared at him.

"And because I am a woman?"

"We must appeal to your sense."

"My sense tells me that three miles are three miles."

"In fair weather."

"A mile does not grow longer because it rains."

"No," said Dick. "But a minute does."

Outside, something went down the slope with a splintering rush.
The sound grew, passed, and vanished into the bellow of
water below.

Molly put on her oilskin.

The men watched her. Tommy's eyes narrowed, but Dick made
no movement.

"You will not go," Dick said.

"I am not asking leave."

She took up an empty pack sack.

"You'll not cross the creek," Tommy told her.

"I crossed it yesterday."

"Yesterday's dead."

"So, apparently, is English courage."

That brought Tommy to his knees, his face dark above
the beard. But Dick laid a hand on his shoulder.

"Let her speak."

"Oh, I shall speak," Molly said. "And when I reach
Dawson I shall speak more. I shall tell them how
two subjects of Her Majesty were held prisoners by a
shower."

"It will make fine hearing," said Dick. "Provided you are
there to tell it."

She thrust her arms through the pack straps.

Neither man helped her. That wounded her more deeply than
opposition. She wrestled with the twisted webbing while the tent
shook, the stove smoked, and the two Englishmen regarded her
with a patience that seemed almost judicial. At last Dick
reached over, freed the strap, and buckled it properly.

"Keep to the high ground," he said. "If the alder
begins moving beneath your feet, jump uphill. Do not try
the old bridge. There is a ford two hundred yards
above it, but if the water reaches your knees, turn
back."

"I need no instructions."

"Nevertheless, you have them."

Tommy crawled to the flap and held it.

The gale thrust a wet arm inside. The little stove
flame bent blue, loose papers flew, and the whole narrow
world of canvas and human warmth seemed about to be
ripped open and scattered across the Yukon watershed.

Molly hesitated no longer than pride permitted.

Then she went out.

The storm swallowed her in three steps.

Tommy dropped the flap and tied it savagely. For a
while the men listened to the rain drum overhead and
to Molly's boots sucking in the mud as she climbed.
The sound weakened, paused once, continued, and was gone.

"Bloody little fool," Tommy said.

"A spirited one."

"Same thing."

"No."

Tommy crawled to the stove and pushed in two damp
sticks. They hissed, blackened, and gave off a grudging flame.

Dick picked up Molly's abandoned blanket, spread it where the
heat could find it, then gathered her wet mittens and
hung them from the ridge line.

"She'll be back," he said.

"Ay."

"How long?"

"Soon, if she's clever. Longer, if she's stubborn."

"And if she's both?"

Tommy looked toward the flap.

"Then Gawd help the mountain."

For half an hour they worked without speaking. Dick repaired
a split pack strap. Tommy kneaded biscuit dough in a
tin plate, using less flour than the task deserved and
more profanity. He measured tea into the pot, added another
pinch, frowned, and added a third.

The storm tightened its grip.

Water burrowed under the tent; the stones of the floor
sweated cold; and from far beyond the ledge came the
vast, smothered booming of the glacier, which seemed to be
breaking its bones in the dark.

At last Dick said, "You were married once."

Tommy's hands stopped in the dough.

"Who told you?"

"You did, at Bennett. You were drunk enough to say
it and sober enough to refuse the rest."

"Then the matter's complete."

"Was she like this one?"

Tommy glanced at Molly's steaming mittens.

"No."

"Quiet?"

"No."

"Obedient?"

"Never."

Dick smiled. "Then she was like this one."

"She was Tlingit," Tommy said. "And there wasn't another like
her between here and the salt water."

He scraped dough from his fingers and set the plate
aside. His pipe came out again, though he did not
light it.

"Name was Tilly," he began. "White folk called her that
because her own name took too much tongue. She belonged
to Chief George's people down by the coast. I was
runnin' a little schooner then—forty tons, broad as a washtub
and twice as contrary. Carried flour north, salmon south, and
whisky nowhere at all, accordin' to the papers.

"I'd seen Tilly at the village. She could paddle straighter
than any man there, and she laughed at me because
I grounded the schooner in front of the whole tribe."

"You did?"

"Chart was wrong."

"Certainly."

"And the tide uncommon low."

"Naturally."

"And I may have had a glass."

"Impossible."

Tommy frowned. "If you want the story, keep your mouth
shut."

Dick settled back against the bean sack.

"Tilly came aboard when we floated," Tommy continued. "Walked the
rail barefoot, looked over my gear, and told me—through an
old trader—that my ship was sick, my crew lazy, and
my anchor fit for catchin' herring. Then she showed us
how to take the inner channel and saved a day's
beatin' against the wind.

"I gave her a red shawl. She gave it to
her grandmother. I gave her a knife. She tested the
edge, said it was poor, and sharpened it better than
I could. I gave her beads. She traded them for
fishhooks. After that I stopped givin' her pretty rubbish.

"For two seasons I came and went. Each time Chief
George watched me closer. He was not her father, but
he had influence, property, blankets, men. Also, he wanted Tilly.
He had one wife already, maybe two dependin' how the
reckonin' was made, and he intended another.

"Tilly had different arithmetic."

Outside, the wind lifted a fold of canvas and cracked
it like a sail. Tommy listened, then went on.

"The wedding was fixed for the autumn. There was feasting,
drums, painted faces, all the grand business a rich chief
puts on to show he can feed half the coast
and owe the other half. I came in with the
schooner on the same tide.

"Chief George met me at the beach.

"'You go,' says he.

"'Just arrived,' says I.

"'You go now.'

"'Wind's foul.'

"'I makum wind.'

"'Then you're wastin' your talent here,' says I. 'There's Admiralty
captains would pay you wages.'

"He did not laugh. Chief George was a great man
in his way, but not humorous. He pointed at the
schooner and then at the open water. Tilly stood behind
the women, wearin' a cedar-bark robe with shells at the
neck. She looked at me once. Not pleading—Tilly never pleaded.
She merely looked, and I knew she would rather swim
to Sitka than marry him.

"So I bowed to George and said I'd leave after
the feast."

"That satisfied him?" Dick asked.

"It did not. But he had fifty guests to impress,
and killin' a white skipper before supper might spoil trade.

"I went aboard and considered. We had three men: Sandy
McCrae, who could fight but not think; a half-breed cook
called Joe, who could think but preferred not to fight;
and me, who was young enough to believe I could
do both. There was a falling tide, a light southerly,
and a reef at the mouth of the bay. If
we left in haste, we'd likely pile up where George
could collect us at leisure.

"Then I remembered the lantern."

"The lantern?"

"Magic lantern. Belonged to a missionary who'd taken passage south
and forgotten it in the cabin. Tin box, glass slides,
lamp—showed pictures on a sheet. Daniel in the lions' den,
a steam locomotive, Queen Victoria, and some devils being cast
into a pit. Very educatin'.

"I sent Joe ashore to announce that, as a mark
of honor to the bridegroom, Captain Hayward would display white-man
medicine after dark. George mistrusted it, which was wise; but
his people wanted to see it, which was stronger. Pride
dragged him by the nose. He agreed.

"We hung a sail between two houses. I put the
lantern behind it. The whole village gathered, children for'ard, old
folk behind, George in the middle, and Tilly beside him
with two women watchin' her.

"First I showed the Queen. Everybody looked solemn. Then the
locomotive. That caused talk. Sandy worked a tin whistle from
behind a box, and three children ran away. Then Daniel.
George said the lions were small dogs and white men
had weak eyes.

"I saved the devils for last.

"While Joe changed the slides, I stepped out and told
them I possessed a power greater than Chief George's. Joe
translated and improved upon it, as he afterwards confessed. He
said I could make a living soul vanish from under
the sun and bring it back on another shore.

"That interested George.

"He said, 'Make Sandy go.'

"Sandy said he'd see him damned.

"I said the medicine required a woman."

Dick chuckled.

"Ay, you may laugh. I was sweatin' through my shirt.
One mistake and there'd be knives in us.

"George pushed forward an old widow. I said the spirits
demanded a young woman. He offered a child. I said
a bride. At that he understood enough to grow dangerous.

"'My bride strong,' he says. 'Your medicine no take.'

"'Then there is no fear,' says I.

"That touched him. A chief can be led by pride
where ropes won't haul him. He took Tilly by the
wrist and brought her out. She stood before the sheet,
face still, eyes on mine.

"I covered her with a blanket.

"Behind the sail, Joe lit the devil slide. Red shapes
leaped up ten feet high. Sandy blew the whistle, beat
on a flour tin, and fired one pistol into the
air. The people shouted. Dogs howled. Somebody overturned the lantern.
In the dark, I whipped the blanket aside.

"Tilly was gone."

"How?"

"Hole under the sail. We'd dug it shallow and covered
it with brush. She crawled through, dropped into the creek
bed, and ran for the shore. Joe kept the lamp
smokin'; Sandy bellowed that the spirits were angry; and I
stood with the empty blanket, pretendin' I commanded demons when
in truth I was prayin' to every saint my mother
ever named."

"And George?"

"Did what a sensible man does when his bride disappears.
He hit me."

Tommy rubbed his jaw at the memory.

"Good blow. Laid me down. But Sandy knocked the lantern
over proper, the sheet caught fire, and in the blaze
the devils looked to be dancin' loose among them. That
gave us twenty seconds.

"We ran.

"Tilly was already in the boat. She had thrown out
half the ballast, set the oars, and cut the painter.
We went down the creek bank with George's men behind.
Joe arrived first. I came second. Sandy came last, backwards,
swingin' a boat hook.

"They fired from the beach. Old muskets, most of them,
but a bad gun kills as final as a good
one. One ball went through the stern. Another broke an
oar. Sandy took a crease along his ribs and complained
for six months.

"We reached the schooner. The tide had fallen worse than
I feared, and she was touchin' mud. George's canoes shoved
off.

"'Sail,' says I.

"'No wind,' says Sandy.

"'Make some.'

"We sweated the anchor up, poled her round, and loosed
everything she carried. The sails hung like dead laundry. The
canoes came on.

"Tilly took the tiller.

"She studied the water, the mountains, and a little cat's-paw
darkenin' the bay. Then she pointed us toward the reef.

"'No,' says I. 'Channel's east.'

"She shook her head and pointed west, where the chart
showed rocks.

"I was skipper. I had responsibility, knowledge, official papers, and
white-man instruments. Therefore I wasted ten seconds arguin'. Tilly took
the tiller harder over.

"We went west.

"The breeze found us. Not much—enough to put life in
the jib and walk the schooner ahead of the canoes.
George's men were near enough to spit aboard when we
reached the reef. I waited for the bottom to tear
out.

"Instead there opened a narrow cut, black water between white
stones, with room for our beam and little else. Tilly
had gathered shellfish there as a child. She knew every
tooth of it.

"We slid through. The first canoe followed and split itself
from bow to belly. The others sheered off. Chief George
stood in the largest, shoutin' that he would follow us
to the end of the world.

"He did not. The wind freshened, the bay dropped astern,
and by morning we were at sea."

Tommy paused to fill the kettle. The water bucket was
low, and he tilted it carefully, wasting not a spoonful.
Dick watched him.

"You married her?"

"The captain of a trading steamer did it three weeks
later. Said he had no authority, but I told him
neither had the fellow at Fort Wrangel who'd married Sandy,
and Sandy's wife managed him legal enough. So he read
from a prayer book, Joe cried, Sandy got drunk, and
Tilly watched us all as if we were a pack
of performing seals."

"Was she happy?"

"Seven years," Tommy said.

The answer came too quickly and meant too much. The
wind filled the silence after it.

Tommy set the kettle on the stove and arranged the
biscuits around it. His hands were broad and scarred, but
he handled the little rounds of dough with peculiar care.

"Seven years," he repeated. "First aboard the schooner. Then in
a cabin up the Lynn Canal. She learned English from
sailors, which gave her a vocabulary not favored by missionaries.
Could reef a sail, skin a deer, set a broken
finger, and bargain a trader down till he wondered if
his own mother had cheated him at birth.

"White women would not visit her. White men did when
they wanted something. Her own people called her lost. Traders
called me a squaw-man and laughed behind their hands till
I bloodied enough noses to educate them.

"Tilly minded none of it—or said she did not.

"She kept house with a severity fit for a gunboat.
No boots beyond the door. No cards on Sunday. No
whisky ever, unless for sickness; and if the sick man
recovered too quick, she took the bottle away. She planted
potatoes in ground made chiefly of stone. She dried berries,
mended nets, and once walked thirty miles through snow to
bring medicine when I had the lung fever.

"I woke and found her boots cut through, her feet
bleeding, and her hands black with frost.

"'Why?' says I.

"She looked at me as if the fever had boiled
my brains.

"'You there,' says she. 'I come.'

"That was all."

Dick lowered his eyes.

"Later I took freight north. We had a bad year—ice
early, prices high, not enough grub. Men came to the
cabin hungry. Tilly fed them. I said we'd be short.

"'Then short together,' she says.

"When our flour ran low, she mixed bark in it.
When the tea went, she brewed spruce. When the bacon
was done, she trapped rabbit. She never complained. Not once.
But let me leave muddy boots on her floor, and
there was thunder enough to move a glacier."

He smiled, and for a moment the red-bearded, aging man
became the young skipper running under gunfire with a bride
in his boat.

"Brave?" Dick asked.

Tommy's smile vanished.

"More than brave. Men are often brave because someone watches.
They hear a cheer behind them or fear laughter if
they turn. Tilly could be brave alone. In the dark.
With no tale to tell after.

"One autumn I was caught across the inlet when a
gale came up. The boat turned over. I made a
rock and lay there from dusk till morning with the
spray freezin' on me. Tilly saw I was late. She
launched a canoe.

"No one would go with her. The sea was breakin'
white from point to point. She went alone.

"She found my cap floating near the rock. Could not
hear me for the wind, but she searched. Twice the
canoe filled. Once it rolled and she righted it in
water cold enough to stop a man's heart. At daylight
she came between the breakers and took me off.

"I was too stiff to move. She laid me in
the bottom like a dead salmon and paddled home."

"Still she never spoke of it?"

"She scolded me for losin' the boat."

The biscuits began to brown. Tommy turned them with a
knife.

"Then she wanted a child," he said.

The sentence lay heavily between them.

"She lost the first. Near died. The doctor at Juneau
said another would kill her. I told her that was
an end.

"She said no.

"I said yes.

"She waited a year and said no again.

"There are fights a man can win by strength, fights
he can win by patience, and fights where winning breaks
the thing he means to save. I learned that late.

"She carried the child through winter. At first she was
well. Worked as always. Sewed little shirts from flour sacking,
lined a basket with rabbit fur, carved a toy canoe
no longer than my hand. Come spring, she weakened. Her
ankles swelled. She could not hold food. Still she smiled
when I looked afraid.

"We had a feather bed by then—the first in the
district. Tilly had traded for it because she said white
wives slept soft and she meant to be equal to
any of them. She lay in it with her black
hair spread over the pillow and her face growing smaller
day by day.

"The child came in June, when the sun hardly went
down.

"There was no doctor. Only an old woman from the
village and the wife of a trader who had never
crossed our door before. Pain makes some people human who
have resisted every other argument.

"It lasted two days."

Tommy took the kettle from the stove though it had
not yet boiled. His pipe lay forgotten beside his knee.

"The baby was a boy. Lived one hour.

"I wrapped him in the little shirts she'd sewn. She
asked to see him. I said he was sleeping. She
knew I lied.

"'No sleep,' she says. 'Gone.'

"'Ay.'

"She held him. She did not cry. That was Tilly.
Grief had to work inward because she'd no habit of
show.

"Toward evening she called me close.

"'Tom,' she says, 'you marry again.'

"I told her to hush.

"'White woman,' she says.

"I swore at her. Not loud. I'd never sworn at
Tilly loud.

"She put her hand on my beard. Could scarcely lift
it.

"'White woman,' she repeated. 'Then doors open. Men sit with
you. Women come house. No shame.'

"I said there had never been shame.

"She smiled. She knew the world better than I did.
Knew every turned back, every laugh cut short when I
entered, every invitation that failed to come. She had borne
them quiet, but she had counted.

"'You lonely with me,' she says.

"'Never.'

"'After—lonely.'

"I told her she was not goin' anywhere.

"She looked toward the window. The inlet was smooth, and
the mountains stood upside down in it. Not a breath
moved. After two days of wind there came such stillness
I could hear the lamp flame.

"'My people say dead go where no cold,' she whispered.
'Your people say same?'

"'Ay.'

"'Good. Maybe one trail.'

"She made me promise."

Tommy's voice had flattened. He spoke as men report the
depth of water under a keel, knowing the reef has
already taken the ship.

"I promised because she would not rest else.

"Then she told me where the dried salmon was stored,
which trader owed us money, which potatoes must be planted
first. Practical to the end. She asked that her knife
go to her grandmother—the one I'd given her years before,
sharpened down half its width.

"Near midnight she said my name.

"I bent close.

"She said it again, though my ear was against her
lips. Then once more, after there was scarcely breath to
carry it.

"And that was all."

The tent seemed suddenly too small to contain the silence.

Rain pressed against the canvas. Water whispered underneath. From the
glacier came a remote detonation, rolled and crushed by distance,
but neither man looked up.

Dick reached for the kettle and placed it back on
the stove.

"Did you marry again?" he asked after a time.

"No."

"Because of her?"

"Because white women have sense."

Dick smiled faintly.

Tommy found his pipe, packed it, and held a match
to the bowl. The match flame shook between his fingers.
He cursed the damp and tried another.

"Molly has been gone two hours," Dick said.

"Hour and twenty minutes."

"You have been counting."

"No."

Dick added a stick to the fire.

"She has the wrong boots," Tommy said. "Soles are smooth."

"I noticed."

"Pack strap rubs her shoulder."

"You repaired it yesterday."

"Still rubs."

They listened.

No shout came through the weather, no stumble of boots,
no hand at the flap. The storm was a great
mill grinding rock, timber, water, and human intention into one
gray meal. Men might boast beside a stove, governments might
draw lines over maps, stampeders might swear Dawson waited only
three marches ahead; but the gale knew neither nation nor
sex, and the trail accepted no argument that could not
be paid in muscle, judgment, heat, and blood.

At two hours Tommy rose.

"Where are you going?" Dick asked.

"To look at the rope."

"The rope is sound."

"Then it won't mind inspection."

He crawled out.

The wind fell upon him instantly, flattening his oilskin to
his body. The air was full of water moving sideways.
He checked each guy line, drove one peg deeper, shifted
a rock which needed no shifting, and stood with a
hand over his eyes, staring up the trail.

Nothing.

He came back soaked.

"Well?" said Dick.

"Rope's sound."

Another half hour passed.

Dick brewed the tea. Tommy put the biscuits in a
covered pan and slid it near the stove. They laid
out dry stockings, Molly's blanket, a strip of linen for
cuts, and the small bottle of brandy which Tommy claimed
to carry for trade.

Then, very faintly, came a cry.

Both men raised their heads.

Again—a thin human note, snatched and torn by the gale.

Tommy reached the flap first. Dick was at his heels.
They fought into their coats and went out together.

Molly appeared below the ledge.

She did not walk so much as fall from one
standing position into the next. Mud covered her to the
waist. One sleeve was ripped from wrist to elbow. Her
hair hung across her face in wet ropes, and the
pack on her back had swollen into a monstrous hump
that dragged her shoulders down.

She reached the slope beneath the tent, tried to climb,
and slid to her knees.

Tommy descended without a word. He caught the pack and
lifted. Dick took her arm.

"I can manage," she gasped.

"Certainly," said Dick.

"I do not need—"

Her legs folded. Between them they brought her under the
ledge and through the flap.

Warmth, though miserable by civilized reckoning, struck her like a
blow. She stood dripping upon the ground sheet, gray-faced and
shaking, while Tommy cut the pack straps loose and Dick
pulled off her gloves.

The fingers beneath were white.

"Move them," Dick ordered.

"I am moving them."

"You are not."

"I—"

He took her hands beneath his shirt against the heat
of his body. She tried to pull away, but strength
had gone elsewhere.

Tommy removed her boots. Water poured from them. One stocking
was red at the heel.

"Creek?" he asked.

"Bridge gone."

"Told you."

"I went above."

"Water to your knees?"

She did not answer.

"To your waist, looks like."

"I slipped."

"Ay."

"I reached the cache."

"We see that."

"My clothes—" She swallowed. "They are safe."

"Good."

"I brought them."

Tommy looked at the pack.

It was heavy enough to hold a corpse. He dragged
it near the stove, opened the throat, and began taking
out its contents.

First came a cast-iron skillet.

Then a coffee pot.

Then two tin plates, a Dutch oven lid, six spoons
tied in a rag, a kettle, three cups, a bent
gridiron, and a sack of nails. Last of all came
a soot-blackened saucepan with a hole through the bottom.

No stockings. No skirts. No good boots.

The cookware accumulated between them, absurd, solid, and undeniable.

Molly watched.

"I—" she began.

Tommy held up the leaking saucepan.

"Particularly valuable, this."

"I thought—"

"Fine garment."

"The cache was dark."

"Lovely for a dance."

"I could not see!"

Dick turned away, but his shoulders moved.

Molly's face went scarlet. "Do not laugh."

No one answered.

"I said do not laugh!"

That was the command which destroyed them.

Dick sat down abruptly on the bean sack, covered his
face, and gave way. Tommy tried to preserve dignity, but
a sound escaped him—half cough, half bark. He looked at
the skillet, at Molly, and at the saucepan in his
hand; then he collapsed against the stove box and roared
until tears ran into his beard.

Molly stood between them, trembling with cold and rage.

"You cowards!" she cried. "You miserable, heartless—English—idiots!"

That made it worse.

She seized a tin cup and flung it at Tommy.
Her arm lacked force; the cup struck the tent wall
and rolled under the stove.

Then the wrath went out of her. Her knees failed,
and she sank upon the blankets.

The laughter stopped.

There was no more jest in the next ten minutes.
Dick stripped off her wet outer garments and wrapped her
in wool. Tommy rubbed her feet until color returned, ignoring
kicks and curses alike. They fed her brandy, then tea,
then a biscuit broken into small pieces because her teeth
chattered too violently to bite. Tommy examined the cut in
her heel, washed it, and bound it with the linen.

Molly submitted because resistance required power she no longer possessed.

When warmth began to hurt, she wept—not much, and angrily,
turning her face to the canvas.

The men pretended not to notice.

Tommy hung her clothes by the stove. Dick emptied water
from the cookware, nested the pots together, and placed the
ridiculous tower in a corner.

"You crossed the creek twice?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Lost the trail?"

"No."

She hesitated.

"Once."

"Which direction did you go?"

"Up."

"The creek runs down."

"I know that."

"Now you do."

She looked at him from within the blankets. Her eyes
had recovered some of their heat.

"I reached the cache."

"So you did."

"I came back."

"Yes."

"Alone."

"Yes."

There was challenge in each word, and Dick accepted all
of it without mockery.

Tommy glanced over. "She'll not try it again."

Molly stiffened.

"The storm has taught her," Tommy continued. "Knocked the foolish
edge off, I reckon."

"Has it?" Dick asked.

"Look at her."

Dick did.

Mud streaked Molly's cheek. Her lips were pale; her hands
were raw; one foot throbbed inside its bandage. She had
been frightened, confused, half drowned, beaten off the trail, and
humiliated by a pack full of ironmongery. Yet beneath the
exhaustion her pride still burned, small and hard, refusing extinction.

"No," Dick said. "The edge is there."

"Then the lesson's wasted."

"Not entirely."

Tommy grunted. "An edge like that cuts everyone near."

"True. But blunt it, and what have you? A poor
knife and a poorer woman."

Molly looked from one to the other.

Dick poured more tea.

"Sense tells a person when to stay," he said. "Spirit
brings her back when she has not. We need both
in this country."

"She might have died," Tommy muttered.

"So might you, going after Tilly."

"That was different."

"Every man's folly is different when he tells it himself."

Tommy considered this, disliked it, and reached for his pipe.

Dick handed Molly the tea. "Courage is troublesome stuff. It
kicks traces, breaks harness, chooses the wrong trail, and packs
saucepans instead of stockings. But it also crosses the creek
the second time."

Molly held the cup in both hands.

"I knew what was in the sack," she said weakly.

"Of course," said Dick.

"We required the pots."

"Desperately."

"The black one is cracked," Tommy observed.

She gave him a look sharp enough to shave with.

Tommy nodded toward Dick. "You're right. Edge ain't gone."

The storm raged through the afternoon and into the night.
The creek climbed higher; trees fell in the drowned valley;
and the glacier, invisible among racing clouds, continued its ancient
bombardment. The trail to Dawson remained erased, the north remained
far away, and the little tent under the ledge shuddered
as if each gust would carry it bodily into the
flood.

But the guy ropes held.

Inside, Molly slept wrapped in dry blankets. Her clothes steamed
above the stove. Her torn boot had been stitched, her
pack strap padded, and her rescued cookware stood in a
shining, foolish stack.

Dick kept the fire alive.

Tommy sat beside it with his dead pipe between his
teeth, staring at nothing the others could see: perhaps a
narrow schooner leaning toward open water; perhaps red spirits dancing
on a burning sheet; perhaps a dark-haired woman at the
tiller, finding a passage where the chart showed only rocks.

Near midnight Molly stirred.

"Tommy?"

"Ay?"

"Was she brave?"

He did not ask whom she meant.

"Ay," he said.

"Braver than I?"

Tommy took the pipe from his mouth and studied the
stove.

"Different."

Molly waited, but that was all he would give.

Dick drew the blanket higher over her shoulder.

Outside, the gale struck again, savage and enormous, demanding entry.
The tent bent nearly to the stove; the patched canvas
groaned; every rope came taut.

Then the gust passed.

The tent sprang upright, scarred, dripping, and unbroken.

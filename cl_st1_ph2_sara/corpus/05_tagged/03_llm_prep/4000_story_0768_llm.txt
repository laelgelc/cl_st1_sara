At the corner of Clark and Randolph Streets, where the
cable-cars clanged and shivered through ridges of blackened snow, two
very shabby-looking young men stood beneath the awning of a
closed cigar shop and watched Chicago prepare for Christmas.

The city was in no mood to notice them. Carriages
rolled northward toward warm houses; express wagons jolted among omnibuses;
sleigh-bells sounded fitfully where the slush permitted runners to scrape
the buried pavement. Women with bright cheeks and burdened arms
hurried under umbrellas, guarding parcels of toys, cakes, ribbons, books,
slippers, smoking-jackets, dolls, and the innumerable small absurdities by which
affection seeks to prove itself at the end of the
year. Every saloon door breathed light, tobacco smoke, wet wool,
and roast meat into the storm.

"Got anything left to hock?" asked the shorter of the
two men.

"Nothing portable," replied William Arden. "My education is extensive, but
the brokers refuse to advance on it."

His companion laughed without mirth. "You might bone Harrison for
a five."

"Harrison has been boned to the marrow."

"What about the newspaper fellows?"

"They cherish me as a memory."

"Then there ain't no show?"

"There is always Lake Michigan."

The shorter man glanced at him, uncertain whether the answer
were a joke. William's jokes had lately acquired an unpleasant
edge. They were like thin ice over deep water: a
man smiled at the glitter and hesitated to put his
foot upon it.

"Well," the other said, thrusting his hands deeper into his
pockets, "I'm going down to Billy Ryan's. They put out
a free lunch at six. Maybe I can stand off
the bartender."

"You owe him money."

"That's why I know him. Coming?"

William shook his head.

"You'd better. A man's got to eat."

"Yes," said William, "that is one of Nature's coarser arguments."

The young man moved away, was swallowed by the crowd,
reappeared beneath a lamp, and disappeared again. William remained under
the awning.

He was twenty-four years old that day.

The circumstance possessed no great importance, except that birthdays, like
milestones and gravestones, persuade a man to measure the road
behind him. At twenty-four Alexander had conquered something considerable; Byron
had made Europe discuss his wickedness; Keats had nearly completed
his brief bargain with immortality. William Arden had conquered a
second-hand overcoat, inspired several creditors to profanity, and written a
comic paragraph about a Polish alderman which had been cut
to three lines and printed without his initials.

He had not eaten since the previous morning.

Hunger, when it is mentioned at a comfortable table, appears
a simple appetite, almost a humorous inconvenience. Men who have
never lacked food talk of fasting as if the stomach
were a peevish child to be disciplined. But hunger prolonged
becomes first a pain, then a fever, then a cold
intelligence which occupies the whole body and turns every faculty
toward itself. It lodges behind the eyes; it trembles in
the fingers; it scents bread through brick walls; it makes
the universe revolve about a plate.

William had passed beyond the lively period of hunger. He
was weak, chilled, and curiously detached. The steaming windows of
restaurants no longer tortured him with distinct visions. Beef, oysters,
coffee, buckwheat cakes, turkey, mince-pie—all the vanished splendors of nourishment
had blended into one golden unreality, as the pleasures of
childhood blend into a summer afternoon which perhaps never existed.

He walked north on Clark Street because standing still required
more resolution than walking.

The snow had turned to rain, and the rain, meeting
the filth of traffic, became an adhesive gray substance which
splashed his trousers and entered through a separation in his
right shoe. His collar was clean only in intention; his
gloves had no fellowship with one another; his overcoat, once
black, had faded to the greenish hue of old bronze.
Yet he carried himself erect. There are men whose poverty
cries aloud from every seam, and others whose ruin wears
the air of an eccentric costume. William belonged to the
latter sort. His face was thin and cultivated; his mouth
sensitive, sardonic, and weak; his eyes dark with intelligence, sleeplessness,
and the habit of examining his own emotions while suffering
them.

He had failed at everything.

The thought did not come newly. It had been with
him for months, following him from lodging-house to lodging-house, waiting
beside him in newspaper offices, sitting opposite him in cheap
restaurants when he could still afford them. But on this
night it closed around him with the completeness of four
granite walls.

He had failed at college, not through stupidity, but through
a brilliant incapacity for submission. He had failed in his
father's office, because figures bored him and punctuality seemed a
conspiracy against the imagination. He had failed as an actor,
though for six intoxicating weeks he had carried a spear
and one sentence through Iowa. He had failed as a
poet, a lecturer, a clerk, a canvasser for an encyclopedia,
a teacher of elocution, a political reporter, and secretary to
a gentleman who sold patent furnaces and later removed to
Canada without taking his liabilities. He had loved two women:
one wisely declined to ruin herself; the other accepted a
jeweler's proposal while William was composing a letter explaining why
marriage was a bourgeois institution. He had borrowed, promised, postponed,
deceived, repented, and borrowed again.

His was a very common tragedy.

Because his tastes were refined, he had mistaken them for
character. Because he understood noble actions, he had assumed himself
capable of performing them. He could analyze courage in five
pages, discuss duty until midnight, and quote the martyrs over
beer; but when morning demanded that he rise, endure dullness,
keep faith, and do one small honest thing repeatedly, his
soul protested against the vulgarity of the arrangement.

At first he had called his wanderings experience. Then he
called them Bohemianism. After that he called them bad luck.
Tonight he called them by their name.

Failure.

The word walked beside him.

He turned eastward. From a church near Dearborn Street came
the practice of Christmas music, uncertain at first, then gathering
into a grave sweetness above the traffic. The sound brought
him, with cruel simplicity, a room he had not seen
for five years: holly over a mantel, wax candles upon
a tree, his mother's hands moving among packages, his father
pretending indifference while secretly shaking every parcel marked with his
name.

Five years! He had written during the first year, occasionally
during the second, twice during the third. His mother's letters
had followed him patiently from St. Louis to Omaha, from
Omaha to Denver, from Denver back to Chicago. He had
answered the last with a request for money. She sent
it. He had not replied.

Pride is sometimes the mask of dignity and sometimes merely
the last possession of a fool. William would not go
home until he could go triumphantly. Each failure increased the
grandeur required of his return. He would arrive with a
book, a fortune, a reputation, a wife, a vindicated life.
As these became less probable, return became impossible.

Besides, his family no longer lived in the old house.
He had heard that they had moved north, but did
not know the address. It was better so. A definite
door might have weakened him.

At the river he stopped. Black water showed between plates
of ice, and lamps stretched wavering ladders into the darkness.
Lake Michigan, he reflected, was broad, cold, and free. One
climbed a railing, surrendered a moment to gravity, and all
the ineffectual striving was concluded. No more rent, no more
hunger, no more interviews with city editors who said, "Leave
it; we'll let you know," no more waking to discover
that consciousness had renewed its lease.

Yet even suicide demands energy.

William looked down and imagined the plunge, the impact, the
strangling cold, the animal struggle which would disgrace his philosophical
conclusion. He had always disliked cold water. It was characteristic
that this trivial aversion should stand between him and eternity.

"Tomorrow, perhaps," he said.

Then his stomach contracted so violently that he bent against
the railing.

A man's got to eat.

The sentence returned with brutal authority. Death might be arranged
later; meanwhile the body mutinied. And if honest methods had
failed—if employers would not employ him, friends would not lend
to him, pawnbrokers would not accept him—there remained the ancient
redistribution of property by personal enterprise.

He would steal.

The decision, once made, warmed him. Not morally; he was
too well educated to obtain moral warmth from a sophistry
which he saw through while employing it. But action itself
has heat. Despair is cold because it is motionless.

He began at once to furnish the crime with arguments.
Society had abandoned him. Wealth displayed its insolence in every
window. Somewhere, at that very hour, a gentleman was eating
too much terrapin and complaining of indigestion, while William Arden,
who could explain the historical development of the sonnet, had
not a crust. Property was a convention. Hunger was a
fact. Jean Valjean had taken bread and achieved respectability in
several languages.

The arguments were flimsy, and he knew them to be
flimsy. But a man who sincerely desires temptation is seldom
delayed by logic.

On State Street the crowd thickened. William studied handbags, coat
pockets, unattended parcels. He selected a stout gentleman whose gold
watch-chain made an emphatic curve across his waistcoat and followed
him half a block. At the decisive instant the gentleman
turned into a brilliantly lighted shop, and William, seeing his
own white face reflected in the glass, abandoned the enterprise.

"The failure as a pickpocket," he murmured. "A brief but
representative episode."

Near Washington Street a young woman emerged from a confectioner's
carrying three packages. She was wrapped in dark fur and
walked rapidly, looking anxiously for a carriage. One of the
packages—a small square box tied with red cord—slipped from beneath
her arm and fell unnoticed into the slush.

William saw it. No one else appeared to see.

Here was Providence at last, neatly wrapped and delivered. Jewelry,
perhaps; or lace, gloves, silver, something salable before the shops
closed. He put his foot over the box.

The young woman continued toward the corner.

William stooped, lifted the package, and examined it. There was
no name upon it. His fingers tightened.

Then, with an oath directed against himself, he ran after
her.

"I beg your pardon," he said, touching his hat. "You
dropped this."

She turned with a startled look, saw the box, and
uttered a little cry.

"O, thank you! It is my sister's present. I should
not have known where I lost it."

She smiled and was gone.

William stood staring after her.

"That," he informed the surrounding metropolis, "was magnificent. I shall
starve with an approving conscience."

But beneath the bitterness something small and stubborn remained alive.
He had not taken the parcel. It was an inconvenient
discovery. A man preparing to become a criminal prefers not
to encounter evidence against his fitness.

He left the crowded business streets and wandered north, where
the houses grew larger and the intervals between saloons longer.
The rain ceased. A raw wind moved off the lake,
scraping clouds from a pale moon. Behind curtained windows families
gathered; pianos sounded; shadows crossed ceilings; Christmas trees burned in
little private heavens inaccessible to the pavement.

His hatred of wealth grew with his weakness. These houses
seemed not merely comfortable but guilty. Their doors were conspiracies,
their walls insults. Warmth was hoarded there, and food, and
laughter, and the careless security of those who expected tomorrow
to resemble today. What right had any man to possess
twenty rooms while another walked the street because he lacked
the price of one bed?

It did not occur to him that he himself had
once possessed such security and thrown it aside. Hunger is
a powerful advocate and seldom calls adverse witnesses.

On a quiet street east of Dearborn Avenue he observed
a house brilliantly illuminated. Carriages arrived in succession. A servant
under a striped awning opened doors; women in pale wraps
passed laughing into the vestibule; music flowed from within whenever
the great door swung back.

A party.

William paused beneath a bare elm. Houses are most easily
entered, he reasoned, when crowded with persons who assume that
everyone else has a right to be there. He crossed
the street, went down the side passage, and found a
service entrance opening as two waiters carried out a crate
of bottles.

Neither looked at him.

He entered.

The warmth struck him almost senseless. For several seconds he
stood in a corridor filled with the odor of coffee,
flowers, roasted meats, damp furs, and evergreen. Servants hurried past.
From beyond a baize door came the clatter of dishes
and the commanding voice of a cook. A maid glanced
at him, but his bearing, so obstinately superior to his
clothes, confused her.

"Gentlemen's dressing-room upstairs?" he asked.

"Second floor, front," she answered, and fled with a tray.

He removed his hat.

The action was instinctive. He had entered to rob the
house, yet somewhere beneath hunger, bitterness, and rags, the boy
trained by a careful mother still knew that a gentleman
did not wear his hat indoors.

He ascended the back stairs, his heart beating now with
great, almost joyous violence. This was action! This was the
Dance of Death, with an orchestra below and supper afterward
if the dancers were obliging. Childe Roland to the dark
tower came, though Childe Roland's boots were probably dry.

On the second floor he found a long hall. The
front rooms had been opened for wraps, and voices sounded
nearby. He moved in the opposite direction, trying doors until
one yielded.

It was a bedroom, large, quiet, and dimly lighted by
a shaded lamp. A fire burned low upon the hearth.
The noise of the party reached it as a softened
murmur.

William closed the door and stood listening.

No alarm.

He began his search rapidly. A jewel case lay upon
the dressing-table, but contained only hairpins. A drawer yielded handkerchiefs,
ribbons, letters. Another was locked. He tried the wardrobe, found
gowns, and searched their pockets with trembling hands. Shame had
become physical, a heat in his face; yet hunger drove
him on.

At last, beneath a folded lace collar, he found a
small leather box. Inside lay a brooch set with pearls,
two rings, and a gold bracelet.

He stared at them.

The moment had come. He was a thief now—not in
intention, not in rhetoric, but in fact. The distinction is
enormous. Many persons entertain sin as they entertain a notorious
visitor, amused by his conversation and confident of their power
to dismiss him. They are surprised when he removes his
coat and settles by the fire.

William thrust the box into his pocket.

A sound in the hall froze him. Someone passed. He
waited, scarcely breathing.

When silence returned he opened another drawer, thinking that, having
crossed the line, he might as well cross it profitably.
Silver glimmered beneath embroidered linen: a brush, a hand mirror,
several small articles. Among them stood a cup, tarnished about
the handle but polished across its face.

He took it up.

On the side, in worn Gothic letters, was engraved:

WILLIAM ARDEN
DECEMBER 24, 1872

The room tilted.

He carried the cup to the lamp. There was no
mistake. A shallow dent marked the rim where he had
dropped it upon the nursery hearth at seven years old.
He remembered his mother scolding the careless nurse and secretly
assuring him that the dent improved it. He remembered drinking
warm milk from it on birthdays; remembered floating a walnut-shell
boat in it; remembered packing it among his treasures before
he went to school.

His cup.

He looked about the room with new eyes. The carved
dressing-table had belonged to his grandmother. The watercolor above the
fire—a foolish blue Italian lake—had hung in the old guest-room.
Upon a chair lay his mother's gray shawl.

He had entered his father's house.

Coincidence, that sober persons call impossible and life employs whenever
it pleases, had led him through a city of more
than a million souls, through cold, hunger, pride, and crime,
to this room.

The cup fell from his hand upon the carpet.

The door opened.

A woman entered and stopped.

She was nearly fifty, though the light in her face
denied arithmetic. Her hair, once dark, had silvered at the
temples; her gown was black velvet, and at her throat
she wore the old cameo William remembered touching as a
child. She looked first at the cup, then at the
shabby stranger beside her dressing-table.

"William," she said.

He had imagined this meeting many times. In one version
he was famous and forgave them nobly; in another he
was dying and spoke with poetic restraint; in a third
he passed his father in the street and gave no
sign. Never had he imagined himself standing in his mother's
bedroom with her jewels in his pocket.

"Mother."

She crossed the room and put both arms around him.

He did not return the embrace. His hands hung at
his sides; his body became rigid with terror and shame.
But she held him with a strength which seemed to
deny the five vanished years.

"My boy," she said. "My boy—my boy!"

"Don't." His voice broke. "For God's sake, don't do that."

She drew back only far enough to see his face.
Her eyes moved over the hollow cheeks, the wet coat,
the frayed collar.

"You are cold."

"Mother, listen to me."

"And starving."

"I came here to rob the house."

"Yes, dear."

The simple answer confounded him.

"I did not know it was yours. I came in
from the street. I searched your drawers. I have your
jewelry in my pocket."

He took out the leather box and placed it upon
the table. His hand shook so violently that the bracelet
slipped to the floor.

"Now you understand."

She looked at the box and then at him.

"How could you rob your own house?"

He laughed—a harsh, brief sound. "That is sentiment, not law."

"It is truth."

"I am a thief."

"You are my son."

"I have lied to you. I took money from you.
I disappeared. I have done nothing—nothing worth doing. I have
failed at everything I touched, and tonight I decided to
add burglary to the list."

She lifted her hands to his face.

"You came home."

"By accident!"

"Perhaps," she said. "There are accidents that know more than
we do."

Then he began to struggle, not against her hands, but
against the tenderness in them. Accusation he could have met;
reproach would have restored his self-command; pardon might have permitted
some dignified abasement. But this immediate, unreasoning possession of him—this
refusal to recognize the barriers he had labored five years
to build—broke his defenses.

"You don't know what I have been."

"I know what you are."

"You know nothing!"

"I know you are hungry."

At that he bowed his head.

The party continued below. A waltz began, light and measured,
while in the quiet room the ruined young man stood
beneath his mother's hands and wept with the shameful abandonment
of a child.

She waited. Mothers learn early that certain griefs must expend
themselves like storms; to question the thunder is useless.

When he had grown quiet, she said, "Sit by the
fire."

"I cannot stay."

"You cannot go."

"Father—"

"Yes," she said.

The one word sobered him. He turned toward the door.

She caught his sleeve. "William."

"He will call the police, and perhaps he should."

"Your father will do no such thing."

"You do not know him."

A faint smile touched her mouth. "I have known him
for thirty years."

"He told me never to return until I could return
honestly."

"He has repented those words every day."

"He does not repent."

"He does not speak. Men often confuse the two."

She led him to a chair and rang the bell.
When a maid appeared, the mother stood so that William
was partly hidden.

"Bring a tray here at once—soup, meat, bread, coffee, whatever
is ready. And tell Mr. Arden I wish to see
him."

William rose.

"Sit down," she commanded.

The old authority remained in her voice. He sat.

His father entered five minutes later.

James Arden was a tall, heavy man with iron-gray whiskers
and a forehead that seemed constructed for opposition. He had
grown older more honestly than his wife; time showed in
every line. He closed the door, looked at William, and
became perfectly still.

No one spoke.

The silence between father and son contained five years of
anger: the abandoned position, the debts paid secretly, the insolent
letters, the unanswered appeals, the pride of one man and
the dissipation of the other. Love was there also, but
love in some masculine natures lies buried beneath so much
fear of expression that it resembles hostility.

Mrs. Arden went to her husband.

"I have found our boy."

His eyes did not leave William. "So I see."

"He is ill and hungry."

William stood. "I entered the house without knowing it was
yours. I stole jewelry from that room. I have returned
it. If you wish me to leave, I shall leave."

Mr. Arden's face hardened, perhaps because it was in danger
of doing something else.

"You chose an extraordinary manner of visiting us."

"I have developed a taste for dramatic entrances."

The old flippancy escaped before William could prevent it. His
father's brows contracted. Then, unexpectedly, the elder man sighed.

"Still making jokes when you are afraid?"

William stared at him.

His mother placed her hand upon her husband's arm. "James,
tonight is Christmas Eve."

"I am aware of the date."

"It is his birthday."

"I am aware of that also."

"He has come home."

"He says he came to steal."

"Then he chose his own property. We can hardly prosecute
him for bad judgment."

"This is not a matter for wit, Margaret."

"No. It is a matter for love."

Mr. Arden walked to the fire and stood with his
back to them. Below, the waltz ended in applause.

At last he said, "Leave us alone."

Mrs. Arden hesitated.

"Please."

She went to William, kissed his forehead, and whispered, "Do
not be proud now, my son. Pride has had five
years."

Then she left them.

The father and son remained standing.

"You look badly," Mr. Arden said.

"I am not at my best."

"Sit down."

William obeyed.

His father paced once across the room. "Your mother believes
I drove you away."

"I drove myself."

"I told you to go."

"I gave you reason."

"I expected you to come back within a week."

"I expected to come back within a year, with a
fortune."

"A very foolish expectation."

"Yes."

Another silence followed. Men who can negotiate contracts, command employees,
address committees, and dictate opinions upon every public subject may
find three honest words to a son beyond their eloquence.

Mr. Arden took up the silver cup.

"You used to drink from this."

"I remember."

"You threw it at the cat once."

"I missed."

"You broke a window."

"Yes."

"Your mother said the cat had jumped against the glass."

William looked toward him. "I never knew that."

"She lied badly. Your mother has many virtues, but no
talent for deception."

The father set down the cup.

"I followed your career as well as I could."

"There was not much to follow."

"I read your articles."

"Three of them?"

"More than three."

"Then you read some under names I no longer remember."

"One about the stockyards was good."

"The editor paid me seven dollars."

"It was worth ten."

William almost smiled.

His father turned suddenly. The sternness was still present, but
behind it stood fatigue—deep, accumulated fatigue.

"I was hard with you," he said. "You were idle,
vain, unreliable, and insolent."

"All true."

"But I was hard. I thought strength could be beaten
into a man as a nail is beaten into oak.
When you bent, I struck harder. When you broke away,
I called it proof of weakness."

"You were not responsible for what followed."

"I was responsible for being your father."

William lowered his eyes.

"I failed with you," the older man said.

The word passed between them with strange power. William had
considered failure his private brand, burned upon him alone. To
hear it from this successful, respected, inflexible man altered its
meaning. Perhaps failure was not a final species of being;
perhaps it was an act, a season, an error shared
even by the strong.

"I failed more thoroughly," William said.

"We need not compete in it."

His father approached and held out his hand.

"You may stay."

William looked at the hand but did not take it.

"On probation?" he asked.

"No."

"Until I find employment?"

"As long as you need a home."

"I have debts."

"We shall discuss them."

"I may disappoint you again."

"I dare say you will."

"And you may regret this tomorrow."

"I have regretted enough yesterdays."

William rose slowly. Their hands met. The father's grip was
firm, then tightened painfully.

"Your mother has suffered," he said.

"I know."

"No, you do not. But you may learn."

He released the hand and turned away. At the door
he stopped.

"One thing more. Did you take anything else?"

"No."

"Good. A man may begin again from many places. Prison
is an inconvenient one."

Then he went out.

William remained by the fire, looking at the closed door.
It had not been an embrace. It had not been
complete understanding, complete absolution, or the sudden conversion of two
unlike natures into harmonious affection. Life seldom supplies such completions.
Yet a hand had crossed the gulf.

That was enough.

The door opened almost immediately, and Mrs. Arden entered behind
a servant bearing a tray. She dismissed the girl, locked
the door, and arranged the food herself.

There was soup, clear and steaming; slices of turkey; potatoes
browned in butter; celery; bread; cranberry jelly; coffee; and a
wedge of mince-pie. The abundance was almost terrible. William looked
at it with the solemnity of a worshiper before an
altar.

"Slowly," his mother said.

He tried to obey. The first spoonful of soup caused
a pain so sharp that he stopped. She waited, watching
him, then gave him another. Gradually the frozen machinery of
the body accepted life. Warmth entered his chest, spread through
his limbs, softened the rigid muscles of his face.

He ate.

This simple act was the seal of his restoration. Men
may make promises, exchange pardons, utter creeds; but he who
sits at a family table and is fed has been
admitted again into the fellowship of the living.

His mother buttered bread as she had when he was
ten.

"Do you remember," she said, "the birthday when you demanded
oysters for breakfast, chocolate at noon, and plum-pudding for supper?"

"I was sick all night."

"Your father said it would teach you moderation."

"It did not."

"No," she answered gravely, "I observed that."

He glanced at her and saw the smile in her
eyes. For a moment the five years vanished so completely
that he expected to hear the nursery clock.

When he had eaten enough to make hunger less a
madness than an ache, shame returned.

"Mother, you must let me tell you everything."

"Not tonight."

"You do not know what you are taking back."

"My son."

"That is a word, not an account."

"It is the only account I require."

"I have been cowardly. I have lived on friends poorer
than myself. I have written things I did not believe,
praised men I despised, and borrowed money I knew I
could not repay. Once, in Denver—"

She laid a finger upon his lips.

"No."

"You cannot forgive without knowing."

"I do not forgive you."

He recoiled.

She drew his head against her shoulder.

"Forgiveness is for strangers," she said. "For persons who stand
outside and have wronged us. You are not outside. You
are mine. Do you ask your hand to pardon your
wounded foot? It suffers with it. I have suffered with
you, though you would not let me come near."

"But I wronged you."

"Yes."

"And you ask no explanation?"

"Love does not always need explanations. It waits beneath them.
It waits while anger talks, while pride travels, while folly
spends itself. It waits and waits and waits."

"Even when there is nothing worth loving?"

"My boy, love does not make inventories."

He could not answer. He knelt beside her, hiding his
face in her lap, and she bent over him, stroking
his wet hair.

After a time she made him remove his coat. She
found dry clothing that had belonged to a cousin staying
in the house, sent for stockings and slippers, and would
have summoned a physician had he not protested with genuine
alarm. When he was warm and clean, she led him
downstairs by a private stair to the library, away from
the guests.

The room had been furnished with objects from the old
home. His father's books stood behind glass; the brass clock
ticked upon the mantel; a faded rug retained the scorch
made by a Christmas spark when William was twelve. And
beside the hearth was the low leather chair in which
he had read Scott, dreamed of heroic careers, and fallen
asleep over Latin grammar.

He touched its worn arm.

"We kept it for you," his mother said.

"You kept everything."

"Not everything."

The words held no reproach. That made them harder to
bear.

He sat in the chair. It had become smaller, or
he larger, but it received him with the familiarity of
an old servant. His mother placed a rug over his
knees.

From the drawing-room came laughter, music, the pleasant confusion of
a prosperous Christmas party. No one there knew that the
lost son had entered through the kitchen and been born
again beside a stolen cup. Presently his father appeared at
the library door.

"The guests are leaving," he said to his wife. "They
are asking for you."

"Let them ask."

"You should go down for a few minutes."

She looked at William.

"I shall not vanish," he said.

She kissed him and went.

Mr. Arden remained. He adjusted a log with the poker,
though the fire required no adjustment.

"I have told the servants you arrived unexpectedly from the
West," he said.

"That is true in spirit, if not direction."

"Tomorrow we will decide what is to be said."

"Tomorrow," William repeated.

It was a word he had nearly surrendered at the
river.

His father glanced toward him. "There will be time."

"Yes."

He went out, closing the door softly.

William leaned back. The food had produced a delicious heaviness,
the fire a luxury almost painful. Safety, when it returns
after long absence, is not immediately believed. He listened for
the knock of a policeman, the command to leave, the
awakening in some frozen hallway. None came.

The clock ticked. The logs settled. Outside, carriage wheels diminished
one by one.

Near midnight the bells began.

They sounded from steeples across the city, some deep and
solemn, some thin in the windy distance, all confused by
space into one immense trembling music. They rang above the
lake and the river, above saloons and churches, above hotels
where strangers feasted, hospitals where strangers died, stations where men
departed, tenements where children slept hungry, and warm houses where
forgotten chairs waited through the years.

William opened his eyes. His mother sat beside him, her
hand resting upon his.

"Is it midnight?" he asked.

"Yes. Christmas Day."

"My birthday is over."

"No," she said. "I think it has just begun."

He smiled faintly and closed his eyes again.

Perhaps the life of man is not a straight account
in which every waste must remain waste and every error
be carried forward until the final sum. Perhaps there are
powers—human, divine, or so intermingled that only pride insists upon
distinguishing them—which gather broken things and refuse to call them
useless. The clay may be marred beneath its own foolish
pressure; yet the Potter does not therefore cast it from
the wheel. He wets it with tears, presses it again,
and from the distortion shapes some humbler vessel.

These are hopes, not certainties. But hope was enough for
William Arden that night.

He slept beside the fire, his face thin and tired,
his future unwritten. His mother watched him as she had
watched him in infancy, before ambition, failure, hunger, and shame
had acquired their names. Beyond the curtained windows the snow
began again, covering wheel-tracks, soot, and the black scars of
the street with a whiteness that would not last, but
was beautiful before morning.

And when dawn came pale over Chicago, it found him
still at home.

### The Golden Homecoming

When Lovell Stevens came down from the afternoon train at
Millbrook, after fifteen years in the West, he found the
station smaller than he remembered and the September sunshine just
as yellow.

He set his valise upon the platform and looked about
him with a pleased, expectant face. There was the dusty
road, the white steeple above the maples, and old Hiram
Pike's store with its porch leaning a little farther toward
the street. Beyond the village lay the brown-shingled house where
Aunt Sally and Uncle Tom had raised him from a
forlorn boy of nine.

"They'll be looking older," he said to himself. "But Aunt
Sally's biscuits won't have changed, I'll wager."

He would not send word ahead. He meant to surprise
them.

The mile seemed no distance until he reached the lane.
There he stopped.

The gate hung by one hinge. Weeds choked the path,
and the orchard grass stood long and gray beneath trees
which had not been pruned in seasons. Aunt Sally's morning-glory
vines straggled dead over the porch, and boards had been
nailed across the windows.

For a moment Lovell thought he had mistaken the place.
Then he saw the little wooden bird upon the barn
gable—one wing broken, but still pointing stubbornly into the wind—and
knew there could be no mistake.

He went to the door and knocked, though no one
could have answered.

"Hallo!" called a voice from the road. "You expecting ghosts
to let you in?"

Lovell turned. A stout woman in a brown bonnet was
peering at him over the gate.

"Why, Mis' Stetson!"

"Lovell Stevens!" she cried. "Mercy on us, Jonah, come here!
It's Lovell!"

Jonah Stetson appeared from behind the hedge with a basket
of apples on his arm. He shook Lovell's hand until
the young man's shoulder ached.

"Well, now," said Jonah, "if you ain't growed broader than
a barn door! Sally always said you'd come back."

"Where is she?" Lovell asked. "And Uncle Tom?"

The Stetsons exchanged a look.

"You'd best come along home with us," Mrs. Stetson said
gently.

"No, ma'am. Tell me here."

So, beside the broken gate, they told him.

Uncle Tom had been ill through one winter and most
of the next spring. There had been doctors' bills, medicine
bills, and a note upon the house. The crops failed
twice. One by one the cow, the wagon, and the
better pieces of furniture were sold. At last the mortgage
was foreclosed, and the old couple, having no children of
their own and no near kin remaining, went to the
county poorhouse.

"A poorhouse!" Lovell repeated.

"They fought against it," said Jonah. "Tom took odd jobs
when he could scarce stand, and Sally sewed till her
eyes give out. But there comes a time when pride
can't make bread."

Mrs. Stetson wiped her eyes. "They've been there three months.
Sally says they're treated kindly, but kindness given by rule
ain't the same as kindness given by love."

Lovell looked again at the boarded windows. Behind one of
them had been his narrow bedroom, with the patchwork quilt
and the shelf Uncle Tom made for his schoolbooks. He
remembered Aunt Sally sitting beside him through scarlet fever, and
Uncle Tom going without a winter coat to buy him
boots.

"When is their golden wedding?" he asked suddenly.

"Next Thursday," said Mrs. Stetson. "Fifty years. Sally spoke of
it afore she left. Said she'd hoped to sit once
more by her own hearth on that day."

Lovell picked up his valise.

"Mis' Stetson, can you give me supper?"

"I can, and a bed besides."

"And can you keep a secret?"

Her round face brightened beneath the tears. "I reckon I
can if it's a good one."

"It's going to be," said Lovell.

Before breakfast the next morning he visited Silas Crane, who
now held the house. By nine o'clock the two men
had shut themselves in the back room of the bank,
and by ten Lovell possessed a deed, a heavy key,
and considerably less money than he had brought east.

He put the deed in his pocket and sought Jonah.

"Now," he said, "I want the place cleaned, mended, painted
where it needs it, and made fit for Aunt Sally
by Thursday."

Jonah scratched his chin. "That's five days."

"You've got two hands, ain't you?"

"I have when my wife don't employ all four of
'em."

"And the village has more. Ask everybody who owes Uncle
Tom a favour."

"That'll be most everybody," Jonah said.

The secret spread with astonishing speed and, what was more
astonishing, did not reach the poorhouse. Men came with ladders,
hammers, and paint pots. Women scrubbed floors, washed curtains, and
set the neglected garden to rights. Children pulled weeds and
carried kindling with a solemnity they had never shown in
school.

Mrs. Stetson led an expedition through the countryside to recover
the household goods. The grandfather clock was found in the
parlour of Deacon Webb, who surrendered it gladly.

"I never did like its ticking," he declared. "Sounded as
if it knew how much time I wasted."

The pink-sprigged china came back from three different cupboards. Uncle
Tom's broad armchair had gone to the blacksmith, while Aunt
Sally's little rocker was discovered in Widow Bell's attic. Each
owner returned the purchased article and refused repayment until Lovell
insisted.

"No, sir," he said. "This is my affair. You folks
are giving labour and goodwill, and that's enough."

On Wednesday evening Jonah found him in the spare bedroom,
fitting a new latch to the window.

"House looks right royal," Jonah said. "Sally's feather bed is
aired, the clock's going, and my wife has travelled so
much after chairs and dishes that I've been a bachelor
as far as meals and buttons went."

Lovell laughed, but his eyes were tired.

"You ain't spent all that Western fortune, have you?" Jonah
asked.

"Not all."

"You was going into business, I heard."

"I am—some day."

Jonah regarded him shrewdly, but Lovell turned to the window.
Outside, sheaves of goldenrod stood in jars along the porch,
bright against the freshly painted boards.

Thursday dawned clear. Mrs. Stetson drove to the poorhouse with
a message that two old friends wished to take Mr.
and Mrs. Porter for a little anniversary outing.

Aunt Sally came down in her best black dress and
a bonnet whose ribbons had faded almost white. Uncle Tom
walked beside her with a cane. They looked smaller than
Lovell remembered, as old people sometimes do when sorrow has
taken away their familiar surroundings.

Aunt Sally saw him first.

"Lovell!"

She put both arms about his neck and wept against
his coat.

"There, there," he said, though his own voice was uncertain.
"Didn't I tell you I'd come back?"

"You wrote you would, but years go so fast, and
young folks have their own lives. Oh, Lovell, let me
look at you!"

Uncle Tom gripped his hand. "You've done well, boy."

"I've kept busy."

They drove toward Millbrook, speaking of small things at first.
Aunt Sally asked whether Lovell wore flannel in winter, and
Uncle Tom wanted to know if Western wheat was truly
higher than a horse's back. Neither asked to be taken
past the old house. Yet when the carriage turned into
the lane, Aunt Sally caught her breath.

"Oh, don't," she whispered. "I couldn't bear to see it."

"You must," said Lovell. "Just this once."

The gate stood straight. The orchard had been trimmed, the
windows shone, and goldenrod bordered the path like little flames.
Smoke rose from the chimney.

Uncle Tom stared at it.

"Somebody's living there."

"Reckon so," said Lovell.

Then the front door opened, and the neighbours poured out
upon the porch.

"Happy golden wedding!" they cried.

The old couple sat silent. Mrs. Stetson came down the
path and held out her hands.

"Come home, Sally."

Aunt Sally turned to Lovell. "What does she mean?"

He lifted her gently from the carriage.

"She means the house is yours again."

"No. No, Lovell, you mustn't make sport—"

He put the deed into Uncle Tom's trembling hands.

"There's no debt on it. Everything inside belongs to you.
Go and see."

They crossed the threshold together.

The grandfather clock ticked in the hall. The old chair
stood beside the hearth, polished but bearing the familiar scar
where young Lovell had once attacked it with a jackknife.
On the dresser waited the pink-sprigged china. A fire burned
in the stove, and the long table was spread with
roast chicken, preserves, biscuits, and a wedding cake crowned by
two golden candles.

Aunt Sally touched the dishes, the chair, and the clock
case, as if assuring herself they were solid. Then she
looked through the open door of the spare bedroom and
saw the patchwork quilt upon the bed.

"That was always your room," she said to Lovell.

"And I expect it still is when I come east."

She sat down suddenly in her rocker and covered her
face.

"I prayed," she murmured. "Even at the poorhouse I prayed
the Lord would let us have one day under our
own roof. But I didn't ask for all this. I
didn't know how."

"Well," said Lovell, kneeling beside her, "maybe Providence sometimes requires
a fellow with a pocketbook and a thick head."

That brought the laugh he wanted, though tears still shone
upon every face.

The celebration lasted until the harvest moon rose above the
orchard. There were speeches, hymns, and many stories of the
fifty years Aunt Sally and Uncle Tom had travelled together.
Before the neighbours left, Lovell gave Uncle Tom a bankbook.

"There's enough there to keep you comfortable for a good
while," he said. "And the garden will do the rest."

Uncle Tom looked at the sum and shook his head.

"We can't take it."

"You took me when I had nothing—not even a decent
surname to my back, so far as I knew. You
fed me, schooled me, and made me believe I was
worth raising. Don't you go refusing me now."

Aunt Sally laid one hand upon his head as she
had when he was a child.

"God bless you, Lovell."

He bent his face for a moment, and no one
pretended not to notice.

Later, when the house was quiet, Jonah found him alone
on the porch. The moonlight silvered the repaired gate and
the yellow flowers.

"What time's your train?" Jonah asked.

"Six in the morning."

"You going back to start that business?"

Lovell drew out his wallet and opened it. Within lay
his ticket and a few small bills.

"There's my business capital."

Jonah whistled softly. "You spent nigh everything."

"Nigh."

"And you ain't sorry?"

Through the window they could see Aunt Sally sleeping in
her chair, her hand resting near Uncle Tom's upon the
table. The firelight warmed the old room, and the clock
measured the peaceful minutes.

Lovell closed the wallet.

"Sorry? No, sir. Money's useful, but it ain't the only
capital a man can carry."

He held his hands out before him—brown, strong hands, marked
by years of work.

"I've got two of these. I've got health, and I
reckon I've got courage enough to begin again. Besides, there's
a blessing waiting for me in this house, and a
man would be poor indeed if he couldn't count that."

Jonah nodded.

Lovell looked once more through the window and smiled.

"Two hands," he said quietly to himself. "They made the
money once. They'll make it again."

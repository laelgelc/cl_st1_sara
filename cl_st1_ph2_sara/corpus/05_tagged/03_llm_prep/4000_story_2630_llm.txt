### The Sign Upon Hatheg-Kla

In the first years, when men were few and the
paths between kingdoms were no more than red threads beneath
the forests, the gods of earth dwelt upon mountains that
could be seen from the habitations of shepherds. At evening
their fires glimmered above the snows, and in the cold
hours before dawn their voices passed over the roofs like
a wind among bronze bells. Yet men multiplied, and curiosity
grew in them; they climbed where their fathers had knelt,
and measured with cords those cliffs which their grandmothers had
feared to name. Thus were the gods driven from summit
to summit, retreating ever northward, until they betook themselves to
cloud-hidden Kadath, where no mortal road ascendeth and no bird
returneth from the upper air.

There they were sheltered by powers not of earth, whose
names occur in neither prayer nor lawful manuscript. The priests
of Ulthar say that these greater beings came before the
moon and shall remain after all seas are dust; but
prudent priests say little more. It is enough that the
gods of earth obey them, and that the mists about
Kadath are not wholly mists.

Nevertheless, the gods remembered their former places. On certain nights
they left Kadath in pale procession, passing unseen above the
villages and the black rivers, and danced upon the peaks
they had loved in youth. Chief among those peaks was
Hatheg-Kla, lonely above the stony waste of Hatheg, with a
crown of ice that reddened at sunset like an altar
newly washed in blood. No man climbed Hatheg-Kla, for clouds
guarded its highest slopes, and within those clouds were sometimes
heard the flutes and measured feet of the homesick gods.

In the town of Hatheg dwelt Barzai the Wise, son
of a line of temple interpreters, and reader of books
which the prudent kept chained. He had studied the Seven
Tablets of Nath and the worm-eaten scroll brought by merchants
from forbidden Thraa; he knew the secret names of winds,
the pedigrees of spirits, and the syllables whereby certain small
demons may be compelled to answer. Old men bowed when
he passed, and young scholars listened beneath his window when
he recited the histories of the stars.

Barzai feared neither god nor ghost. He said that fear
was but the shadow cast by ignorance, and that he
whose lamp was learning might walk safely amid all shadows.
When the priests warned that wisdom itself hath boundaries, he
laughed softly and asked who had drawn them.

Only Atal, his young disciple, did not delight in that
laughter. Atal was the son of an innkeeper, grave beyond
his years, and learned enough to perceive the greatness of
what he did not know. He loved Barzai as a
son loveth a stern father, yet often he trembled whilst
the old scholar spoke of Kadath and the hidden dancers
upon Hatheg-Kla.

In the season when the moon would be full upon
the night of the gods' ancient festival, Barzai announced that
he would climb the mountain.

"I shall behold them," said he, "not in dream, nor
in the smoke of incense, nor through the riddles of
dead scribes, but with these eyes. For I have compared
all signs, and the gods of earth may conceal themselves
from peasants, but not from Barzai."

Atal begged him to abandon the purpose. The shepherds likewise
warned him, telling how their flocks crouched at certain moonrises
and how white shapes moved against winds that blew the
other way. One blind woman declared that Hatheg-Kla was no
mountain on those nights, but a stair raised for feet
too vast to touch the world.

Barzai heard all and was unmoved. Atal, perceiving that his
master would go alone if none accompanied him, prepared ropes,
lamps, woollen cloaks, and food for many days.

They departed beneath a waning sun. The people watched in
silence as the two figures crossed the plain, Barzai walking
before with his silver-headed staff, and Atal bearing the burdens.
None offered blessing, for there are journeys which a blessing
dare not follow.

For three days they climbed through woods of twisted pine,
where the roots gripped boulders like the claws of buried
beasts. No bird sang there. At night the trunks creaked
though no wind passed among them, and once Atal awoke
to behold a procession of faint lights moving far above
the branches. Barzai said they were stars seen through vapour,
though the vapour was thick and the lights moved in
a circle.

On the fourth day they came above the trees. Before
them rose slopes of grey stone, bleak and enormous, and
beyond those slopes stood the white wall of the summit.
The sky seemed nearer, yet not kinder; its blue was
deep as an ocean seen from beneath, and Atal felt
that the earth clung feebly to his feet.

Higher and higher they went. They crossed ravines whose bottoms
no cast stone could find, and shelves where a single
misplaced step meant silence forever. Ice coated the rocks in
spectral sheets. Sometimes the mountain groaned under them; sometimes there
came from above a soft, regular booming, as of drums
beaten in chambers many leagues away.

Barzai grew more joyful as the way grew cruel.

"Dost thou hear?" he asked. "The festival approacheth. The writings
were true."

Atal heard, but answered naught.

Higher and higher they climbed, and up and up, until
the plain of Hatheg was hidden by miles of cloud.
The world below became a memory. Peaks once mighty appeared
as little teeth through the vapours, and the sunset flowed
red beneath the climbers' feet. Then Atal understood why the
ancient songs called mountains the thoughts of sleeping earth; for
Hatheg-Kla seemed less a heap of stone than one vast
intention reaching toward forbidden regions.

On the seventh evening they found a narrow ledge beneath
the summit. Above it rose a final cliff, black, sheer,
and glazed with old ice. The full moon ascended, monstrous
and pale, making every pinnacle cast a shadow sharp as
a spear. There Barzai commanded that they wait.

"The gods will not dance under an uncovered heaven," he
said. "They remember the eyes of men. The clouds must
come."

No cloud had been visible, yet before midnight thin vapours
gathered from the north. They came like ships with torn
sails, one after another, joining and thickening about the crown
of Hatheg-Kla. Soon the summit vanished, though the moon still
poured its rays upon the lower slopes. Within the cloud
flickered colours for which Atal knew no names.

Then sounded flutes.

They were mournful at first, and sweet with a sweetness
older than mankind. Atal thought of ruined shrines in valleys
now beneath the sea, and of fields where the gods
had walked before the first house was built. The music
spoke of lost mountains, vanished forests, and worshippers whose bones
had become chalk beneath forgotten cities. For a little while
Atal wept, not from fear but from the vast sorrow
of immortal things condemned to remember.

Barzai sprang to his feet.

"They have come!" he cried. "The gods of earth dance
above us. Their steps are light upon the snow, and
their flutes lament the places men have stolen. Remain here,
Atal, if thy heart is weak. I go to stand
among them."

Before the youth could seize him, Barzai began ascending the
final cliff. He climbed without rope and with a swiftness
impossible in one of his years. The cloud lowered to
meet him, and soon only the glimmer of his lamp
could be seen, moving upward through the vapour.

Atal called after him, but the flutes grew louder.

Then from above came Barzai's voice, triumphant and ringing.

"I see their shadows! I know the rhythm! The signs
upon the Tablets of Nath are fulfilled. O gods of
earth, Barzai hath found you! Barzai, who readeth your laws
and feareth not your wrath!"

The mountain trembled. Snow hissed from the ledges into darkness.
Atal pressed himself against the rock and watched the moon.

A darkness touched its rim.

It was not a cloud, for the clouds lay below
the moon; nor was it the accustomed shadow of the
world. Something vast crossed the lunar face, something whose outline
was curved yet not round, jointed yet not fashioned like
any limb. The stars near it went out one by
one.

The flutes ceased.

For a breath there was absolute silence. Then Barzai laughed.

His laughter rang too loudly, as though shouted inside a
tomb of metal.

"I have passed the bounds!" he cried. "The peak is
beneath me, and Kadath itself cannot be far. The little
gods flee before my wisdom. I see them—pale, frightened, huddling
in the mist. They are not angry, Atal. They are
afraid!"

The cliff beneath Atal shifted. Not crumbled, not shook, but
shifted, as though the whole mountain had moved from its
place upon the earth. The horizon sank. Distant peaks descended
into cloud, and the moon appeared no longer above Hatheg-Kla,
but beside it.

From the upper darkness came a second music, if music
it may be called. It was a piping without melody,
joined by a beating so deep that Atal felt it
in his bones rather than heard it. Shapes moved behind
the clouds: immense, angular shadows whose motions contradicted distance, now
small as insects, now broader than the sky.

Barzai spoke again, and pride had departed from his voice.

"What cometh there? These are not the dancers named in
the scrolls. The gods kneel—ye powers, the gods of earth
kneel!"

A sound like hideous laughter rolled down from the unseen
height.

"No!" Barzai shrieked. "There are older thrones! There are abysses
above the stars! The guardians—the outer guardians—"

The moon vanished utterly.

Atal felt an upward wind. His cloak streamed toward the
black heavens, stones rose from the ledge and flew silently
into the dark, and his own body became light, as
if the earth had released him. Far overhead, or far
below—for direction had lost all meaning—the lamp of Barzai whirled
like a yellow star.

"I am falling!" cried the old man. "Atal, I fall
into the sky!"

Then came thunder, not from the air but from beyond
the air, and the lamp was extinguished.

Atal remembered no more until dawn, when shepherds found him
wandering barefoot among the lower foothills. His cloak was torn,
his hair white with frost, and in his hands he
clutched a length of rope burned black at one end.
He could tell them neither how he had descended nor
what path had borne him; he spoke only of a
mountain rising, a moon devoured, and a voice falling upward
through endless night.

Men searched for Barzai when the weather cleared. They found
no staff, no footprints, no fragment of clothing. Yet upon
the highest accessible stone, where no tool could readily have
been carried, there appeared a colossal symbol cut deep into
the granite. It resembled at once a crown, a talon,
and an open gate; but those who stared long perceived
other forms within it and afterward dreamed of black suns
revolving beyond creation.

The priests covered their sacred books, and Atal never prayed
for his master.

To this day the people of Hatheg bar their doors
during eclipses, and no hunter looketh upward when clouds conceal
the summit. Sometimes, beneath a full moon, flutes are heard
above the sleeping town, mournful and remote, and pale fires
move upon Hatheg-Kla. The gods of earth still dance there,
remembering the mountains they lost.

But they dance softly now, and often gaze toward the
outer dark.

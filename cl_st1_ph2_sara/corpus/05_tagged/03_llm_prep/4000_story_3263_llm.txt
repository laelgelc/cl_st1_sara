## The Winter Marriage

Toward the close of the year 1811 there lived upon
his estate of Nenaradovo a certain Gavrila Gavrilovich R——, a
landowner celebrated throughout the district for hospitality, a sound table,
and an inexhaustible patience at cards. His neighbours visited him
without ceremony; some to eat, some to play boston with
his wife, Praskovia Petrovna, and many to look at their
daughter, Maria Gavrilovna, a pale and slender girl of seventeen,
who was reputed to possess a large dowry and consequently
a still larger number of admirers.

Maria Gavrilovna had been educated, like most young ladies of
her time, by a French governess and a circulating library.
She had read of persecuted passion, hereditary enmity, secret vows,
moonlit meetings, cruel fathers, and heroines who died—usually with great
propriety—upon learning that happiness was impossible. Thus prepared, she quite
naturally fell in love.

The object of her attachment was Vladimir Nikolaevich, a young
army ensign who had come home on leave to a
small neighbouring property. He had a handsome face, an ardent
disposition, a threadbare uniform, and nothing else. This last circumstance,
which would scarcely have troubled the heroines of Maria's novels,
produced a disagreeable impression upon her parents. They received Vladimir
civilly when chance brought him to the house, but soon
made it plain that their daughter was not to be
thrown away upon a man whose income was insufficient even
for the maintenance of his horse.

Prohibition, as every experienced person knows, does not extinguish love;
it gives love occupation.

Maria and Vladimir began to correspond. Their letters were conveyed
by a maid with romantic sympathies and by a young
footman who had no sympathies whatever, but who liked silver.
The lovers met in the pine grove, beside an abandoned
chapel, and once in a kitchen garden during a rain
so persistent that both caught cold. They swore eternal constancy;
they lamented the tyranny of parents; they imagined poverty softened
by tenderness, military glory crowned by domestic felicity, and old
age passed beneath lindens planted with their own hands.

Needless to mention, they agreed at last that they could
not live apart.

Vladimir proposed flight and a secret marriage. Once they were
lawfully united, he argued, they would throw themselves at the
feet of her parents. Gavrila Gavrilovich, moved by paternal feeling
and the force of accomplished facts, would forgive them; Praskovia
Petrovna would weep; Maria would weep; Vladimir would perhaps also
weep; and the whole family would dine together. Nothing appeared
more reasonable.

Maria hesitated for several days, partly from fear and partly
because hesitation became the seriousness of her situation. She dreamed
alarming dreams. In one, her father seized her by the
hand and dragged her over the snow toward a bottomless
cellar; in another, Vladimir lay wounded beneath a fir tree
and begged her, in a voice of extraordinary sweetness, to
marry him before he died. She awoke trembling and interpreted
these visions precisely as her wishes required.

The plan was fixed for the evening of the fifteenth
of January.

Maria was to decline supper, retire early, and escape by
the back porch with her maid. Vladimir's sledge would wait
beyond the garden. It would carry her six versts to
the church in the village of Zhadrino, where Vladimir was
to meet her with the priest and witnesses. After the
ceremony there would be explanations, tears, forgiveness, and happiness.

Such arrangements are easy when discussed beside a stove.

During the appointed day Maria Gavrilovna was restless. She attempted
to read but understood nothing; she opened her workbox, broke
a needle, tangled her silk, and pricked her finger. At
dinner she ate scarcely a morsel. Her parents noticed her
pallor.

"What is the matter, Masha?" asked Praskovia Petrovna.

"My head aches, mamma."

"You were sitting too near the window yesterday," said her
father. "These young people imagine glass keeps out January."

Maria tried to smile. Their kindness oppressed her more than
severity would have done. She looked at her father's grey
hair, at her mother's familiar cap, at the old spaniel
asleep beneath the table, and was several times on the
point of abandoning the entire enterprise. But then she remembered
Vladimir, pale beneath the fir tree of her dream, and
recovered the invincible strength of passion.

By evening the weather had changed. A wind rose over
the fields, driving dry snow against the windows. The shutters
trembled; the chimney groaned; clouds of white powder swept across
the courtyard and buried the paths almost as quickly as
the servants could clear them.

"It will be a bad night," observed Gavrila Gavrilovich.

Maria shuddered. In novels, nature frequently sympathises with lovers; in
life she is not always so obliging.

At supper Maria declared herself unable to appear. Her mother
came to her room, felt her forehead, recommended raspberry tea,
and made the sign of the cross over her before
leaving. Maria nearly confessed everything. The words had reached her
lips when a clock struck ten in the drawing room.
She embraced Praskovia Petrovna with unusual tenderness.

"Sleep well, my darling," said her mother. "Tomorrow you will
be better."

When the house had grown quiet, Maria rose. Her maid
dressed her in a warm cloak and boots. Together they
tied up a small box containing linen, jewellery, a few
letters, and two volumes of French romances which Maria, even
at such a moment, could not resolve to leave behind.
She wrote a farewell to her parents in which she
called herself the most guilty and the most unhappy of
daughters, begged them not to curse her, and promised that
one day, when they saw the purity of her motives,
they would press her and her husband to their hearts.

This conclusion consoled her.

The maid opened the back door. Wind rushed into the
passage and extinguished the candle. They stepped outside.

The storm appeared to resist them deliberately. Snow struck Maria's
face and entered the collar of her cloak; the wind
pushed her backward; the darkness had swallowed the garden. They
reached the gate by feeling along the fence. Beyond it
stood the sledge, the horses restless, the driver stamping his
feet and muttering reproaches addressed equally to the weather, the
lovers, and his own foolishness.

Maria climbed in. The maid placed the box beneath the
seat and followed. The driver cracked his whip; the sledge
lurched forward and disappeared into the whirling night.

We will leave the young lady to the care of
Providence and a coachman who could hardly see his horses'
ears, and return to Vladimir Nikolaevich.

He had spent the whole day in activity. Early that
morning he visited the priest at Zhadrino. The good man
raised objections: the law, the parents, the impropriety of the
hour, the danger of scandal. Vladimir answered each objection with
eloquence and reinforced his eloquence with the assurance that, once
the affair was known, Gavrila Gavrilovich would consent. The priest
sighed, consulted his wife, sighed again, and at last yielded.

Witnesses were needed. Vladimir found three.

The first was Dravin, a retired cornet in his forties,
who wore a faded military coat, cultivated a great moustache,
and regarded every defiance of domestic authority as a noble
act. The second was the district surveyor, a small, timid
man who agreed because he had never attended a secret
wedding and wished to know how one was conducted. The
third was the son of the local police clerk, a
boy of sixteen who had recently learned to smoke and
thought himself capable of any crime.

Having secured these accomplices, Vladimir dined with them at the
cornet's house. They drank to love, courage, and the astonishment
of parents. At dusk, Vladimir sent his servant Tereshka with
the sledge for Maria; he himself intended to drive alone
to Zhadrino in a small one-horse sleigh. His road was
familiar, the distance no more than six versts, and the
whole journey ordinarily required less than half an hour.

At first all went well. The horse moved briskly; the
runners whispered over the packed road; lights from Nenaradovo vanished
behind him. Vladimir's heart beat with joy and anxiety. He
imagined Maria already on her way, her head bowed under
her hood, her hands clasped, her faithful maid whispering encouragement.
He imagined the church, the candles, the priest, the decisive
words. He imagined the return to her parents and prepared
a speech full of submission and dignity.

The wind strengthened.

Within ten minutes the road disappeared. Sky and earth became
one moving whiteness; the landmarks which Vladimir knew—the row of
willows, the black mound, the lonely haystack—had vanished. His horse
left the track and sank to the knees. Vladimir pulled
to the left, then to the right, then straight ahead,
guided by recollection, instinct, and hope, three counsellors of unequal
value.

He drove on.

The field appeared endless. Snowdrifts rose unexpectedly before the sleigh;
the horse struggled through them, breathing heavily. Twice the sleigh
overturned. Each time Vladimir fell into the snow, rose, shook
himself, righted the sleigh, and continued. His cap blew away;
he did not notice it. His hands became numb; snow
melted inside his collar and froze upon his coat. He
saw no village, no grove, no road.

Another quarter of an hour passed, or perhaps an hour.
Time in a snowstorm is measured not by clocks but
by despair.

Vladimir stopped and listened. Nothing was audible but the wind.
He urged the horse forward once more. Presently the ground
began to slope. He rejoiced, believing himself near the hollow
behind Zhadrino, but the descent led to a frozen stream
which he had never seen. The horse slipped and nearly
fell. Vladimir got out, led it up the opposite bank,
and discovered a dark line ahead.

"A wood!" he cried.

The wood was unfamiliar. Nevertheless, trees offered shelter from the
wind, and Vladimir entered among them, hoping to find a
path. The branches creaked overhead; snow fell from them in
soft, treacherous masses. The horse, encouraged by firmer ground, moved
faster. After some time they emerged into an open field.

No village appeared.

Vladimir's terror now became complete. Maria would reach the church;
the priest and witnesses would wait; every minute would expose
her to discovery. Perhaps she would think he had abandoned
her. Perhaps the driver had overturned. Perhaps she had frozen
upon the road. Each thought presented itself with the clarity
of a nightmare.

He drove on and on.

At length a small black shape rose before him. It
proved to be a haystack. Beyond it he saw a
light. He directed the horse toward it and presently reached
a miserable hamlet of three or four cottages. He jumped
down and hammered at the nearest window.

After a prolonged silence an old man in a sheepskin
coat opened the door.

"What do you want?"

"How far is Zhadrino?"

The old man considered.

"Zhadrino?"

"Yes, Zhadrino! How far?"

"Perhaps ten versts."

Vladimir leaned against the wall.

"Ten!"

"Maybe more, in this weather."

"Can you give me horses? I will pay anything."

"What horses? Ours are all out."

"A guide, then. For God's sake, find me a guide!"

The old man scratched his beard.

"My son could take you. But he is putting on
his boots."

"Let him come at once!"

"Boots cannot be put on before they are put on,"
replied the peasant, and closed the window.

Vladimir knocked again, promised money, threatened, implored. A light moved
inside the cottage with maddening calm. At last the door
opened and a young peasant appeared, wrapping a sash around
his coat.

"Come," said Vladimir, seizing him by the sleeve.

The peasant looked at the exhausted horse.

"We shall not go quickly."

"Go as you like—only go!"

They set off. The guide walked before the sleigh, searching
for the road with a stick, turning now one way,
now another. Vladimir asked repeatedly whether Zhadrino was near.

"Not far."

After half an hour he asked again.

"Not far now."

The horizon had begun to pale when they reached the
village. The storm was weakening; roofs and fences emerged from
the grey dawn. Vladimir saw the church. Its doors were
shut. No light shone from the windows. The witnesses' troika
was gone.

He sprang from the sleigh and ran to the priest's
house.

What he learned there we shall not yet repeat.

Let us return to Nenaradovo.

What had happened during the night? Nothing.

At least, nothing visible to Gavrila Gavrilovich and Praskovia Petrovna.
They awoke at the customary hour; he drank tea in
a dressing gown and discussed oats with the steward; she
complained that the storm had disturbed her sleep. At breakfast
they sent to ask after Maria. The maid answered that
the young lady had slept badly but was better. Toward
noon Maria appeared, pale and composed.

"How is your head?" asked her father.

"Better, papa."

"The storm has passed," said her mother. "I feared it
would tear away the roof."

Maria looked through the window at the buried garden.

"Yes, mamma. It was a terrible night."

She spoke no more. The household proceeded in its ordinary
course. Dinner was served; the priest did not call; no
scandalous husband appeared to demand forgiveness. Maria waited through the
day with an agitation which she concealed by silence. Evening
came. Still there was no message from Vladimir.

That night she developed a fever.

For two weeks her life was despaired of. The district
doctor prescribed powders, bleeding, mustard plasters, and quiet; the last
remedy alone could not be obtained. In delirium Maria spoke
continually of Vladimir, of a church, of snow, of a
black forest, and of a man who was not the
man expected. She begged someone to turn back the horses;
then she stretched out her hands and repeated the words
of the marriage service.

Her parents understood only that she loved Vladimir Nikolaevich and
that opposition had nearly cost them their daughter. Grief makes
even sensible people generous. Gavrila Gavrilovich reproached himself; Praskovia Petrovna
wept; together they decided that poverty was no sin, that
happiness did not depend upon rank, that Vladimir was of
good family, and that young officers sometimes advanced rapidly. In
short, they adopted all the arguments which the lovers had
previously found so convincing.

When Maria began to recover, her father wrote to Vladimir,
inviting him to Nenaradovo and offering his consent to the
marriage.

The answer was strange and violent.

Vladimir declared that he could never enter their house; that
death alone could end his misery; that they must forget
a man unworthy of Maria Gavrilovna; and that his only
hope was to seek danger as soon as possible. The
letter was confused, bitter, and marked by a despair too
sincere to be theatrical. Soon afterward they learned that he
had returned to his regiment.

Maria read the letter once and fainted. On recovering she
asked no questions. The maid who had accompanied her on
the fatal journey was sent away to distant relatives, and
no one in the house dared mention the night of
the storm.

We must now pass rapidly over events which history has
made familiar and private sorrow made indistinct. The year 1812
came. The French crossed the frontier; regiments marched; proclamations were
read in churches; provincial ladies scraped lint and spoke with
unexpected knowledge of military movements. Young men who had lately
disputed over cards now discussed death and honour. Russia seemed
to awaken, offended and magnificent.

Vladimir Nikolaevich distinguished himself at Borodino. He was severely wounded
and died some weeks later, before his name, printed among
those commended for bravery, reached Nenaradovo.

Maria found the notice in a newspaper. She remained long
motionless, then carried the paper to her room. For several
days her parents feared a return of the fever, but
grief had changed its character. It was no longer frantic;
it had become silent, disciplined, almost sacred.

Vladimir's letters, a miniature, several verses copied in his hand,
and a dried flower from the pine grove were kept
in a locked box. Maria arranged them with the care
of a widow, though no one knew by what right
she assumed that melancholy title. We are often strict concerning
the grief of others, but the household at Nenaradovo had
suffered enough and allowed her this harmless worship.

Not long after the war, Gavrila Gavrilovich died, leaving Maria
heiress to a considerable fortune. She and her mother removed
to another estate, situated in a quieter province. Suitors followed
them. Wealth, beauty, and a reputation for constancy form an
alliance rarely resisted by unmarried men.

There were landowners with excellent incomes and deplorable manners; officials
with excellent manners and deplorable incomes; cavalry officers who danced
well; civil councillors who did not dance at all; serious
widowers; lively young men; and one poet who addressed a
sonnet to Maria's sadness without having been introduced to her.
She refused them all.

Praskovia Petrovna mourned this obstinacy. She did not insist; she
sighed. Maternal sighs, when repeated daily, are a form of
argument, but Maria remained unmoved. She spoke of Vladimir rarely,
yet his memory seemed to stand between her and every
new attachment. The suitors departed, consoled by cards, travel, or
other heiresses.

Three years passed.

The war was over. Victorious regiments returned; officers appeared in
provincial drawing rooms with foreign decorations, wounded limbs, and stories
of Paris. Society welcomed them with enthusiasm. A man who
had crossed the Rhine could be forgiven much, and a
man wounded in battle was considered interesting even before he
spoke.

Among those who visited the neighbourhood was Colonel Bourmin of
the hussars. He was about twenty-six, tall, dark, and composed.
He wore the Cross of St George in his buttonhole
and carried one arm somewhat stiffly from an old wound.
His black eyes had a grave expression; an interesting pallor
softened the military precision of his features. He was neither
talkative nor silent, neither excessively modest nor offensively assured. In
short, he possessed that measured ease which society calls distinction
and which is sometimes no more than fatigue well concealed.

Bourmin was presented to Praskovia Petrovna and soon became a
frequent visitor.

Maria treated him at first with the calm courtesy she
had shown others. He did not recite verses, praise her
complexion, or question her upon the subject of eternal constancy.
He spoke of Italy, of regimental life, of books, and
of people whom he had observed without admiring too much.
His irony amused her because it was never loud; his
melancholy interested her because it was never displayed.

He, for his part, seemed drawn to her. In company
his attention followed her without importunity. If she spoke, he
listened; if she approached the piano, he turned the pages;
if she walked in the garden, he soon discovered that
fresh air was necessary to his wound. Their neighbours noticed
everything and understood everything, as neighbours always do.

Praskovia Petrovna began to hope. She discovered that Bourmin's estate
was respectable, his family ancient, his conduct excellent, and his
prospects brilliant. She spoke of him at breakfast, praised him
at dinner, and once casually ordered a guest room prepared,
though no one had proposed to occupy it.

Maria could not remain indifferent. Her first love had been
nourished by secrecy, opposition, and French novels; this second feeling
entered quietly, through confidence and companionship. She struggled against it.
Vladimir's miniature seemed to reproach her. She opened the old
letters, but their exclamations belonged to a girl she scarcely
recognised. Memory is faithful in its way: it preserves the
dead, but it does not always preserve us beside them.

Bourmin's behaviour, however, was inexplicable. No one could doubt his
attachment, yet he made no declaration. At moments, when they
were alone, he seemed about to speak; then he became
pale, changed the subject, or abruptly asked for his horse.
This reserve might have encouraged a less serious woman. Maria
was hurt by it.

"Perhaps," said Praskovia Petrovna one evening, "he fears refusal."

Maria bent lower over her embroidery.

"He has no reason to fear anything from me."

The mother understood this confession perfectly, though it had not
been addressed to her.

A week later Bourmin arrived earlier than usual. Praskovia Petrovna
received him in the drawing room, spoke for ten minutes
about the damp weather, and then remembered with admirable suddenness
that she had promised to inspect preserves in the pantry.
She left them alone.

Bourmin walked to the window. Maria continued working, but her
needle trembled. After a few ordinary remarks he asked whether
she would walk in the garden. The afternoon was warm;
autumn had only begun to yellow the birches. They went
out.

At the end of the garden lay a pond, dark
beneath old willows. A bench stood near the water. Maria
seated herself; Bourmin remained standing before her. His usual composure
had disappeared.

"Maria Gavrilovna," he said, "I have long wished to speak
to you."

She lowered her eyes.

"You cannot have failed to notice my feelings. I have
made little effort to conceal them."

Maria was silent. The willows stirred faintly; a leaf fell
upon the water and floated there with unnecessary deliberation.

"I love you," continued Bourmin. "I love you deeply. I
have never known a happiness equal to that which I
find in your presence. Do not turn away. Every moment
beside you has made silence more difficult—and yet I had
no right to speak."

Maria looked at him.

"No right?"

"None."

He sat at the other end of the bench and
covered his eyes for an instant.

"I know that you have suffered. I know you once
loved a man whose memory is dear to you. I
would never ask you to forget him. I would honour
your sorrow. If that were the only obstacle, I should
hope—perhaps presumptuously, but still I should hope. There is another
obstacle. An insurmountable one."

Maria's face lost its colour.

"You are married?" she asked.

Bourmin started.

"I do not know."

She gazed at him in astonishment.

"I must explain. What I shall tell you may appear
madness. It was madness. For four years I have carried
its punishment without knowing whom I injured or how to
repair it."

He paused, then began:

"In the winter of 1812, shortly before joining my regiment,
I was travelling to Vilna. I had been delayed on
the road and was in a wretched humour. Toward evening
a snowstorm came on. My coachman advised us to stop
at the next posting station, but I was young, impatient,
and confident that roads existed chiefly to obey travellers. I
ordered him to continue.

"The storm soon became violent. The road vanished. We wandered
across fields; the horses struggled; the carriage jolted into drifts
and twice nearly overturned. I should have turned back, but
obstinacy is a moral principle which youth adopts whenever prudence
becomes tiresome.

"After some hours we saw a light. It came from
a small village church. Several sledges stood outside. The windows
were illuminated, and people moved at the entrance. Before I
had asked a question, a man ran toward us.

"'At last!' he cried. 'Where have you been? Everyone is
waiting. The young lady has fainted twice. The priest scarcely
knows what to do.'

"I ought to have explained the mistake. Instead—"

Bourmin rose and walked several paces.

"Instead?" asked Maria almost inaudibly.

"Instead, some reckless spirit took possession of me. Perhaps it
was the strangeness of the night, the storm, fatigue, or
that fatal curiosity which persuades a man that consequences belong
to tomorrow. I stepped from the carriage and entered the
church.

"It was poorly lit. In one corner stood three men
whom I had never seen; they were anxious, cold, and
apparently half asleep. The priest came toward me with an
expression of reproach.

"'We were beginning to despair of you,' he said.

"I made some answer—I no longer remember what. They led
me forward.

"Near the altar sat a young woman in a travelling
cloak. Her maid and an elderly lady supported her. She
was extraordinarily pale. When I approached, she looked at me,
but the candlelight was uncertain and her eyes seemed clouded
by terror. One of the men whispered, 'Begin quickly; she
can scarcely stand.'

"I still had time to stop the whole affair. I
did not.

"The young woman was raised. I took my place beside
her. The priest began the service."

Maria's hands were clasped tightly in her lap.

"The church was silent except for the reading, the crackling
candles, and the storm beating against the windows. Several times
the bride swayed, and her maid held her upright. I
felt my absurd courage abandoning me, but shame prevented confession.
Imagine it: I feared looking ridiculous before strangers, and therefore
consented to become a criminal.

"The questions were put. I answered. She answered also, so
softly that I scarcely heard. Rings were produced. We walked
around the lectern. The final prayer was spoken. Then the
priest told us to kiss.

"I turned toward her.

"She looked fully into my face for the first time.
For a second she seemed unable to understand. Then she
gave a terrible cry.

"'It is not he!'

"She fell senseless.

"No one moved. The witnesses stared at me; the priest
dropped his book. I stepped back. Someone shouted, but confusion
gave me time. I ran from the church, threw myself
into my carriage, and ordered the coachman to drive."

"You left?" cried Maria.

"I fled. By morning I had recovered my reason, which
was no longer of much use. My servant, who was
with me, died during the campaign. I did not know
the name of the village. I had slept through part
of the journey before the storm and could not reconstruct
the road. I sent inquiries, but war had begun; the
district was disturbed, priests displaced, villages burned, records lost. Every
search failed.

"I do not know the name of the woman whom
I married. I do not know whether she lives. I
do not even know whether the ceremony can be proved
or dissolved. Yet before God and conscience I am bound
to her. That is why I cannot offer you my
hand, though I would give my life for you."

He ceased speaking.

Maria had risen. She was so pale that Bourmin moved
toward her in alarm, but she stopped him with a
gesture.

"The winter of 1812," she said. "A village church… The
bride had arrived in a sledge with her maid?"

Bourmin stared at her.

"Yes."

"There were three witnesses?"

"Yes."

"And when she saw your face, she cried out?"

He took her hand.

"Maria Gavrilovna—what are you saying?"

She looked at him, and through tears a strange smile
appeared—the smile of one who sees fate at last remove
its mask.

"My God," she whispered, "my God! Then it was you."

Bourmin fell at her feet.

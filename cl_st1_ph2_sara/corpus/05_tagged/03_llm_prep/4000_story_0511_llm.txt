## The Appointment at Four

On the morning of the eighth of January, in the
year 17—, Toza, chief of the brigands of Valdoro, stood
before Prince Lotarno in the great judgment-hall of the castle.

The hall was lofty and cold. Winter light entered through
narrow windows and lay in pale bars upon the pavement.
At the farther end, beneath a canopy embroidered with the
arms of the province, sat the Prince; beside him stood
his ministers, councillors, and captains. Toza, chained at wrist and
ankle, faced them with his black head held erect.

He had been taken three days before in a ruined
shepherd's hut, after a pursuit which had cost the Prince
twelve soldiers. For ten years he had plundered travellers in
the mountain passes, levied tribute upon villages, and defied every
officer sent against him. But these offences, though numerous, were
not the true cause of the solemnity with which his
trial had been conducted.

Six months earlier, Prince Lotarno's younger brother had fallen into
Toza's hands. A ransom had been demanded and paid. Nevertheless,
the young lord's body was afterward found beneath the Bridge
of San Falco, with a bullet through the heart.

The evidence had been brief and sufficient.

"Have you anything to say before judgment is pronounced?" asked
the Prince.

Toza smiled.

"I have much to say, Highness, but very little that
you will care to hear."

"You may say it."

"Then I say that this hall contains many thieves, though
I alone wear chains."

A murmur passed among the ministers. One of them stepped
forward angrily, but the Prince checked him with a motion.

"Continue," he said.

Toza glanced from face to face.

"A poor fellow takes ten crowns on the highway, and
you call him brigand. A prince takes ten thousand crowns
in taxes, and you call him ruler. The difference is
not in the taking, but in the number of men
who stand ready to defend the thief."

"You murdered my brother."

"I killed an enemy."

"You had received his ransom."

"To buy a lamb from the wolf does not make
the wolf a shepherd."

The Prince's face did not alter; yet those who knew
him observed that his right hand closed upon the carved
arm of his chair.

"My brother was unarmed."

"He was your brother," answered Toza. "That was weapon enough.
Had I set him free, he would have returned with
soldiers, and then you would have praised him for doing
to me what I did to him. Wolves do not
complain of cruelty until one of their own pack is
torn."

"You have the philosophy of your trade."

"And you, Highness, have the advantage of yours."

There was another murmur, louder than the first. Toza laughed
harshly. He knew that he must die, and the knowledge
gave him a savage liberty. He had expected torture, perhaps
the wheel, perhaps a cage hung upon the southern wall,
where hunger and ravens would divide him between them. Since
nothing could save him, he intended at least to rob
the Prince of the pleasure of seeing him plead.

Lotarno leaned toward his chief minister and spoke a few
words. Then he rose.

"Toza of Valdoro," he said, "you have been found guilty
of murder, robbery, rebellion, and many lesser crimes. Yet for
all these there shall be but one punishment. On the
fifteenth day of this month, at four o'clock in the
afternoon, you shall be beheaded."

For the first time the brigand's composure was disturbed.

"Beheaded?" he repeated.

"Beheaded."

"No rack? No hot irons? No public cage?"

"No."

Toza studied him suspiciously. Mercy in Prince Lotarno was less
credible than cruelty in a saint. The sentence was too
simple, too swift, almost honourable.

The Prince descended one step from the dais.

"You appear surprised."

"I am wondering," said Toza slowly, "why a cat with
so much leisure should kill the mouse at once."

A faint smile touched Lotarno's lips.

"You have seven days in which to discover the answer."

Then he turned to the captain of the guard.

"Take him away. Let the sentence be executed at the
appointed hour."

As Toza was led from the hall, he looked back.

"I shall keep the appointment, Highness," he called, "unless your
gaolers prove less honest than your judges!"

The Prince made no reply.

Toza was confined in the northern tower, in a chamber
whose single window looked down upon a profound valley. Far
below, a little river wound like a silver thread among
bare woods and fields. Beyond it rose the mountains, their
summits white with snow and their lower slopes dark with
pines. Freedom seemed visible from the window, but so distant
that the sight of it was an added cruelty.

His gaoler was a short, heavy man named Paulo, with
anxious eyes and a mouth that appeared made for whispering.
He brought the prisoner bread, soup, and wine, tested the
chain at his ankle twice each day, and never willingly
remained in the cell.

On the evening of the ninth, Toza said suddenly:

"How much are you paid?"

Paulo almost dropped the lamp.

"For what?"

"For locking doors, carrying soup, and watching men die."

"I am an officer of the Prince."

"Then you are badly paid."

Paulo placed the bowl upon the table.

"I have no complaints."

"Only poor men say that. Rich men complain of everything."

The gaoler moved toward the door.

"Wait," said Toza. "Do you know the ruined chapel above
Monte Nero?"

Paulo stopped.

"I may have seen it."

"Behind the altar is a stone marked with a cross.
Beneath that stone lies a leather sack."

The gaoler looked at him, then at the door.

"What is in the sack?"

"Enough gold to make an honest man of you."

Paulo licked his lips, though he attempted to hide the
movement.

"This is a trick."

"Certainly it is a trick. The only question is whether
I mean to trick you, the Prince, or the executioner."

"And why should you tell me?"

"Because gold under a stone cannot open a prison door.
You can."

Paulo shook his head.

"The northern tower has three guards below. The gate is
watched. The castle yard is full of soldiers."

"Every fortress has a door for men who know its
habits."

"And if I helped you, where would you go? The
roads are guarded. Your band is scattered."

"To the eastern mountains. There is an old tunnel beneath
the Pass of Orso. It leads beyond the Prince's territory."

Paulo's nervous eyes narrowed.

"I have heard of such a passage."

"You know it?"

"I know where it begins. My father took sheep through
it when I was a boy."

"Then Providence has placed us together."

"Providence has placed a chain on your leg."

"And greed has placed the key in your pocket."

For a long moment they stared at one another.

At last Paulo said, "How much gold?"

"Two thousand crowns, besides jewels."

The gaoler drew a sharp breath.

"Jewels?"

"Rings, Spanish doubloons, a ruby cross taken from the Bishop
of Marenza--objects of devotion which would improve your soul."

"And you would give me half?"

"I would give you all."

"All?"

"If I am free."

Paulo did not answer. He went out and locked the
door. But that night, a little before midnight, he returned.

His cloak was wet with rain.

"I went to the chapel," he whispered.

Toza sat up on his pallet.

"And?"

"The stone is there."

"You lifted it?"

"No."

The brigand laughed softly.

"You lie badly, Paulo."

"I did not touch the treasure!"

"Then you are either an honest gaoler or a very
cautious thief. In either case, I may trust you."

"Trust!" repeated Paulo. "Between you and me there is no
trust."

"Good. Trust makes men careless. Fear keeps them useful."

Paulo came close.

"There is a stair used by the servants. At two
in the morning the sentry in the lower court changes.
For perhaps three minutes the passage is unwatched."

"And the chain?"

"I have the key."

"And horses?"

"Impossible. Their hoofs would wake the gatehouse. We must go
on foot."

Toza examined his face.

"Why 'we'?"

"You cannot find the tunnel alone."

"You could draw me a map."

"There are no proper paths after the waterfall, and snow
has covered the old marks. Without me you would wander
until the soldiers found you."

The brigand considered. He did not like the man's insistence,
yet it appeared reasonable.

"When?"

"To-morrow night."

"That will leave us four days."

"Five," said Paulo. "The execution is on the fifteenth."

Toza smiled.

"You count carefully."

"A man risking his neck must count everything."

At two o'clock on the morning of the eleventh, Paulo
opened the cell. He had brought a coarse cloak, a
woollen cap, and a knife. He freed Toza's ankle but
left the iron ring upon it.

"The lock was sealed after the sentence," he explained. "I
cannot remove the ring without tools."

"It will remind me of the Prince."

"Speak lower."

They descended the servants' stair, crossed a store-room, and waited
behind a door while the sentries exchanged challenges in the
court. Rain drummed on the tiles. Somewhere a dog began
to bark.

"Now!" breathed Paulo.

They slipped along the wall. Paulo opened a narrow postern.
Beyond it lay a steep orchard, black beneath the winter
sky.

The dog barked again.

A soldier shouted.

The fugitives ran.

They plunged among the dripping trees, crossed a low wall,
and descended through thorn bushes toward the river. Behind them
the alarm-bell rang once, paused, then rang furiously.

"They have discovered us!" said Paulo.

"They have discovered an open door," answered Toza. "We shall
learn later whether they can discover men."

They reached the river at dawn, soaked and bleeding from
the thorns. The water, swollen by rain, rushed brown and
cold between its banks. A wooden bridge lay half a
mile downstream, but Paulo refused to approach it.

"There will be soldiers there."

"Then we swim."

"You may swim. I should drown."

Toza seized him by the collar.

"If you drown, little gaoler, I shall never find the
tunnel."

Using a fallen pine as a bridge, they crossed one
at a time. The trunk rolled beneath their weight; Paulo
slipped and hung for several seconds above the torrent, crying
out until Toza dragged him up. When they reached the
opposite bank, both men lay exhausted in the mud.

"You saved me," gasped Paulo.

"I saved my guide."

"Yes," said the gaoler. "I understand the distinction."

All that day they hid in a charcoal-burner's abandoned hut.
They had one loaf, a piece of cheese, and a
flask of wine between them. Toza wished to travel by
daylight, but Paulo would not consent.

"The valley roads will be filled with soldiers."

"Every hour brings us nearer the fifteenth."

"The tunnel will not vanish."

"The Prince's axe will not vanish either."

At sunset they set forth again. The rain became sleet,
and a bitter wind descended from the heights. They followed
cattle tracks, dry stream-beds, and paths known only to foresters.
Twice they saw torches moving upon the road below. Once
a patrol passed so close to their hiding-place that Toza
heard the leather of a soldier's saddle creak.

He gripped the knife beneath his cloak. Paulo lay beside
him, trembling.

The captain of the patrol halted.

"Search the thicket," he ordered.

Two soldiers dismounted.

Toza put his mouth to Paulo's ear.

"If you make a sound, I kill you first."

Paulo nodded.

The soldiers came among the bushes. One thrust his sword
into a heap of leaves within three feet of Toza's
hand. At that instant a fox broke cover farther up
the slope. The second soldier shouted, the horses started, and
both men hurried back to the road.

When the patrol had gone, Paulo whispered:

"You would truly have killed me?"

"A falling tower crushes friend and enemy alike."

"I am not your friend."

"Then you have answered your own question."

They continued before dawn, but hardship was beginning its work
upon Toza. In the mountains he had once been celebrated
for endurance. He had gone two days without food, had
slept upon snow, and had crossed flooded rivers with soldiers
at his heels. In those days, however, he had been
chief of forty armed men, feared by peasants and obeyed
by companions. Now every sound weakened him. A snapping branch
was a musket; a distant bell was an alarm; every
traveller upon the road seemed a spy.

Hope, which he had not possessed in the judgment-hall, had
made death terrible.

On the twelfth they entered the pine country. Snow lay
among the roots, and their food was gone. Paulo found
frozen berries and divided them carefully.

"You take the smaller share," Toza observed.

"I found them."

"I require greater strength."

"You require me alive."

The brigand glared at him, but accepted the answer. Paulo
had changed since their flight. In the prison he had
seemed a frightened, corrupt little man; in the wilderness he
displayed patience and an exact knowledge of the ground. He
never hesitated long at a fork in the path. He
knew where huts stood, where streams might be crossed, and
from which ridges the castle watch-fires could be seen.

This competence increased Toza's suspicion.

"You said your father brought sheep through these mountains," he
remarked.

"He did."

"He must have owned sheep in every valley."

"He was a wandering man."

"Like a soldier?"

"Like a shepherd."

That evening they saw horsemen behind them.

The fugitives had entered a narrow ravine bordered by walls
of grey rock. There was no cover except a line
of stunted firs. Paulo caught Toza's arm.

"Up there!"

They climbed a slope of loose stones and concealed themselves
beneath an overhang. The horsemen rode into the ravine--six soldiers
led by an officer in a red cloak.

Toza recognised him as Captain Bernado, who had captured him.

Bernado drew rein below the overhang.

"They cannot be far ahead," he said. "The peasant at
Luro saw two men before sunrise."

"We have searched the western track," replied a soldier.

"Then they have turned toward the Pass of Orso. Send
two men to the waterfall. The others come with me."

Toza looked at Paulo. The gaoler's face had become grey.

"They know," he mouthed.

Paulo pointed upward. There was a narrow ledge, almost invisible
in the rain. They crawled along it while the soldiers
waited beneath them. Once a stone came loose under Toza's
knee and bounded into the ravine. Bernado looked up.

"Who is there?"

A musket fired. The ball struck the rock, showering Toza
with fragments.

"Run!" cried Paulo.

They scrambled round the cliff as soldiers began to climb
after them. The ledge ended above a roaring waterfall. Mist
rose from the chasm, and an ancient bridge of three
planks spanned the torrent immediately above the drop.

Paulo crossed. Toza followed, the planks shaking under him.

"Cut the ropes!" he shouted.

"With what?"

Toza pushed him aside and slashed at them with his
knife. The first rope parted as a soldier stepped onto
the bridge. The planks swung violently; the man fell with
a scream and vanished into the white water. Toza cut
the second rope. The bridge collapsed against the cliff.

Captain Bernado stood on the opposite bank.

"You cannot leave the valley!" he called.

Toza raised his knife in salute.

"Then I shall remain long enough to bury you!"

Yet when he and Paulo had entered the woods beyond
the falls, his defiance deserted him. His hands shook, and
he was obliged to sit down.

"I cannot go farther to-night."

"We must," said Paulo. "Bernado will find another crossing."

"I said I cannot!"

"And I say the fifteenth is near."

Toza looked up sharply.

"You speak often of the date."

"Because you speak of it."

"Do I?"

"In your sleep."

The brigand rose. From that hour his hatred of Paulo
became fixed. He would use him to reach the tunnel;
afterward, he decided, the little gaoler should die. The treasure
under the chapel could then remain wholly his. He had
promised it in exchange for freedom, but promises made in
chains were not binding. A wolf escaping a trap did
not pay the trapper's dog.

On the thirteenth they ate nothing. On the fourteenth they
found a shepherd's shelter and slept for several hours among
mouldy straw. Toza awoke from a dream of the judgment-hall,
crying that the axe was falling. Paulo sat near the
entrance, watching him.

"What hour is it?" asked Toza.

"Past noon."

"What day?"

"The fourteenth."

"Why do you stare at me?"

"I wondered whether a man can feel the blade in
a dream."

Toza's hand moved toward his knife.

"Take care, Paulo."

"I meant no offence."

"You have meant none since we began, and yet I
have suffered many."

"So have I."

"You shall be paid."

"I depend upon that."

They started again beneath a lowering sky. The Pass of
Orso was now visible between two snowy peaks, but Paulo
turned away from it and led Toza down into a
wooded hollow.

"The tunnel lies east," said Toza.

"The entrance lies south. It bends under the mountain."

"How convenient."

"You may seek it alone if you prefer."

For an instant Toza thought of killing him then; but
the slopes were confused, dusk was approaching, and no entrance
could be seen. He lowered the knife.

"Lead on."

On the morning of the fifteenth, they reached a deserted
mill beside a frozen stream. Beyond it towered the mountain,
black and immense. Paulo searched beneath the broken wheel and
produced a small packet wrapped in oilskin. It contained bread,
meat, a candle, tinder, and a flask.

Toza stared at him.

"Who left these?"

"I did."

"When?"

"Before I opened your cell."

"So certain were you that we should come this way?"

"I was certain we must eat."

Toza drank from the flask and continued to watch him.

"You have prepared everything."

"A prudent man does."

"A prudent man does not abandon two thousand crowns."

Paulo smiled without humour.

"They are still beneath the stone."

"You expect me to believe that?"

"You may believe what you please."

"How far is the tunnel?"

"Three hours, if the snow has not blocked the path."

"And beyond it?"

"A road descends into the province of Carlano. Once there,
Prince Lotarno cannot touch you."

The words revived Toza. Three hours! He ate greedily, washed
his face in the stream, and even laughed at the
iron ring still encircling his ankle.

"At four o'clock," he said, "the Prince will enter his
execution chamber. His ministers will stand like crows about a
carcass. The headsman will sharpen his axe. But the block
will remain empty."

Paulo folded the oilskin.

"If we are across by four."

"We shall be."

"It is almost one."

"Then cease talking."

They climbed through deep snow. The wind had fallen, and
the mountains possessed that dreadful silence which makes a man
conscious of his own breathing. Toza drove himself forward, though
his feet were bleeding and fever burned behind his eyes.

At half-past two, Paulo stopped before a wall of thorn
bushes.

"Here."

He pulled the branches aside. Behind them appeared an archway
closed by stones. Together the men removed enough rubble to
uncover a black opening.

Toza peered within.

"This is the tunnel?"

"Yes."

"No soldiers?"

"Did you expect them?"

"I expect everything."

Paulo lit the candle.

"The passage is narrow at first. After twenty paces it
widens. At the farther end is an iron door, bolted
on this side."

Toza glanced at the knife beneath Paulo's belt. His own
blade had been lost while cutting the bridge. Yet a
stone, a blow from behind, or his bare hands would
suffice. Once they passed the door, he would kill the
gaoler and return later for the treasure.

"Go first," said Toza.

Paulo shook his head.

"You are the man escaping."

"You know the road."

"There is only one road."

Toza's eyes hardened.

"You distrust me?"

"Entirely."

"That is ungrateful."

"It is why I am alive."

After a moment Toza took the candle and entered. Paulo
followed, remaining several paces behind.

The tunnel descended steeply. Water dripped from the roof, and
the small flame cast enormous shadows on the walls. Their
steps echoed ahead of them as though other men walked
in the darkness.

"How long?" asked Toza.

"Less than a mile."

They went on. The passage turned repeatedly, sometimes narrowing until
their shoulders touched the rock. Toza's sense of direction soon
failed. He could not tell whether they travelled east, west,
or in a circle beneath the mountain.

"What hour is it?"

"Near four."

"Near?"

"Three minutes, perhaps five."

Toza quickened his pace.

Then the candlelight gleamed upon iron.

A low door stood at the end of the passage.
Its bolts were red with rust, but freshly oiled around
their sockets.

Toza noticed this. A warning rose within him--not a thought,
but the blind cry of instinct.

He turned.

Paulo stood ten feet away.

"Open it," said the gaoler.

"What lies beyond?"

"Freedom."

"Come here."

"No."

Toza's suspicion became certainty, though he could not yet understand
the trap.

"You open it."

Paulo drew a pistol from beneath his cloak.

"Draw the bolts, Toza."

The brigand's face changed. All the courage which had survived
hunger, rain, pursuit, and exhaustion deserted him before the small
black mouth of that weapon.

"Who are you?" he whispered.

"Your gaoler."

"You served the Prince from the beginning."

"I serve him still."

Toza sprang sideways, but the passage offered no shelter.

"Open it!" commanded Paulo.

The clock began to strike somewhere beyond the door.

One.

Toza stared at the iron panels.

Two.

He lifted the upper bolt.

Three.

He dragged back the lower.

Four.

The door swung inward.

Light burst upon him--not the grey light of a winter
afternoon, but the red blaze of twenty torches. He saw
a chamber hung with black cloth; a block upon a
raised platform; a masked executioner standing beside it with a
gleaming axe. Ministers, judges, and captains formed a solemn half-circle.

At their centre stood Prince Lotarno.

Toza remained upon the threshold, incapable of movement. The tunnel
had not passed beneath the mountain. It had wound back
through the northern ridge and entered the castle by an
ancient passage opening directly into the chamber of execution.

Paulo thrust him from behind. Toza stumbled forward. The iron
door slammed, and the bolts shot into place on the
other side.

The Prince consulted the clock.

Its last stroke still trembled in the air.

For seven days Toza had fled the scaffold. He had
crossed the river, hidden from patrols, starved in the woods,
climbed the waterfall, and dragged his exhausted body through the
snow. Hope had tormented him more skilfully than iron; fear
had humbled the man whom pain could not have humbled.
And all the while, the road of escape had been
the road appointed for his return.

Prince Lotarno descended from the platform.

"You promised to keep your appointment," he said quietly.

Toza looked once at the bolted door, once at the
block, and lastly at the masked man who raised the
axe to test its balance.

Then the Prince smiled.

"You are just in time."

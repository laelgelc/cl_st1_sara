# THE LAST HAMLET

## I

The news that Alexander Petrovitch Kostromsky was to appear in
the provincial theatre travelled through the town with that singular
rapidity which belongs only to reports of fires, murders, bankruptcies,
and the coming of celebrated actors.

The walls were covered with yellow bills. Kostromsky's name, printed
in enormous black letters, towered above those of the local
company as a church cupola towers above poor wooden cottages.
At the pastry-cook's, in the club, in the corridors of
the district court, and even beneath the whispering arches of
the cathedral porch, people spoke of him. Elderly ladies remembered
having seen him twenty years before in Petersburg; young ladies,
who knew him only from lithographs, repeated anecdotes of his
passions and his magnificence; officers wagered as to whether he
would be sober enough to finish the performance.

"He was a genius," said some.

"He is still a genius," objected others.

"He is a drunkard," declared the more practical.

Yet nobody intended to remain at home.

Kostromsky had begun his career in that very theatre. Forty
years earlier, a lean, dark-eyed youth with a hoarse voice
and ungovernable pride, he had crossed its stage carrying a
spear in a historical melodrama. There he had played his
first lover, his first villain, his first madman; there, after
a performance of Othello, the students had unharnessed the horses
from his sledge and dragged him through the snow; there
a governor's wife had sent him a diamond ring in
a billet-doux scented with violets. The town cherished these memories
as though his celebrity reflected honour upon every inhabitant.

Afterwards came Moscow, Petersburg, Warsaw, Kiev, triumphs without number, wreaths
with golden inscriptions, silver cups, velvet albums, money thrown away
by handfuls, women, suppers, quarrels, journeys, champagne. For thirty years
his name had drawn crowds. He had been compared with
Garrick, Mochalov, Salvini; critics had found in his voice thunder,
music, tears, prophecy. He himself had long ceased to doubt
that nature had created the drama principally in order that
he might interpret it.

But fame, which forgives almost everything while it is young,
becomes exacting in old age. During recent seasons reports had
grown malicious. It was said that Kostromsky forgot his words,
that his hands trembled, that his once flexible voice had
acquired a cracked metallic sound. In Kharkov he had fallen
asleep in a dressing-room while the orchestra repeated an entr'acte
three times; in Odessa he had struck a prompter; at
Kazan he had appeared as King Lear with one boot
unbuttoned and had addressed Cordelia by the name of Desdemona.

The reports reached him, but did not convince him. He
knew very well that he drank; he knew that his
memory had become treacherous and that, after the second act,
an intolerable weariness sometimes filled his bones. But genius—was not
genius immortal? Could wine drown what God had bestowed? Could
a few provincial fools judge Alexander Kostromsky?

He arrived at noon in a hired troika, wearing a
fur coat with a sable collar rubbed bare at the
edges. The station-master, two reporters, the theatrical manager, and a
deputation of admirers awaited him. Flowers were presented; speeches were
made. Kostromsky bowed with his old majestic negligence. His broad-brimmed
hat, grey locks, and aquiline profile produced an effect. Only
at close quarters could one perceive that his cheeks were
swollen, his eyelids inflamed, and the skin about his mouth
traversed by innumerable fine wrinkles.

"We welcome in you our own glorious son!" cried the
manager, Fedor Fedorovitch Babanov.

"My first cradle," replied Kostromsky, looking past him at the
station buffet. "Yes, my dear fellow, my artistic cradle."

He drank three glasses of brandy before entering the carriage.

By evening no seat remained unsold for either of the
two performances announced. Hamlet was to be given first, and
on the following night The Robbers. The manager rubbed his
hands, rushed through the theatre shouting at carpenters and dressers,
and calculated receipts with an agitation resembling religious ecstasy.

Kostromsky slept until five. When he awoke, the winter twilight
already pressed blue and heavy against the hotel windows. For
several minutes he did not know where he was. His
head ached; his tongue seemed coated with ashes; one hand,
lying upon the coverlet, trembled.

Then he saw the bouquet on the table and remembered.

Hamlet!

He rang and ordered tea, cognac, lemons, hot water, raw
eggs, and a bottle of champagne. Looking into the spotted
mirror above the washstand, he was startled by his own
face. It appeared old—not artistically old, not the noble pallor
of a soul exhausted by thought, but coarsely, pitilessly old.
He thrust back his grey hair.

"Nonsense!" he murmured. "A face is clay. Give me twenty
minutes before the mirror."

Yet uneasiness had entered him. It remained even after the
cognac had warmed his stomach and restored to his gestures
something of their former breadth.

He remembered the theatre—the low ceiling, the narrow stage, the
damp smell in the passages—and smiled. To return as Hamlet
to the boards where he had once carried a spear!
There was poetry in that. There was destiny. The public
would see that the old lion still possessed his claws.

"The rest are tradesmen," he said aloud. "They perform. I
create."

And, pleased by the phrase, he repeated it as he
dressed.

## II

Behind the curtain, two hours before the performance, the theatre
displayed none of that enchanted magnificence which the audience afterwards
ascribes to it. Bare brick walls rose above crooked flats;
ropes hung like gallows cords; a painted tomb leaned against
a woodland brook; Denmark's battlements were supported from behind by
cracked planks and flour barrels. Carpenters hammered, property-men quarrelled, chorus
girls ran through the passages half dressed, and somewhere a
child cried because a dresser had pinched her finger.

The gas burners hissed. The odour of dust, damp cloth,
glue, greasepaint, perspiration and cheap scent made the air heavy.

Kostromsky sat before his mirror in dressing-room number one. An
old barber named Semyon, who claimed to have shaved him
at his first benefit performance, was whitening his temples and
fastening a dark wig over the actor's grey hair.

"More shadow beneath the eyes," Kostromsky commanded.

"Yes, Alexander Petrovitch."

"And straighten the left eyebrow. You have made me look
like a frightened deacon."

"I beg pardon, Alexander Petrovitch. My hands—seeing you again after
so many years—my hands won't obey me."

"Then borrow another pair."

The old man stammered an apology. Kostromsky smiled at the
mirror. The paint was performing its merciful deception. Under its
warm brown tones the swollen flesh withdrew, wrinkles vanished, the
nose acquired greater nobility, and the eyes, outlined in black,
seemed larger and more melancholy. The Prince of Denmark looked
back at him.

There! What had he feared? The face alone could command
an audience.

On the table stood a glass and a bottle of
vodka. From time to time he drank, afterwards biting a
slice of apple. The alcohol did not intoxicate him; so
at least he believed. It merely drew a curtain between
him and fatigue, calmed the mutiny of his nerves, and
awakened in his breast that generous heat which he mistook
for inspiration.

The door opened without ceremony, and Babanov thrust in his
bald head.

"A misfortune, Alexander Petrovitch! A catastrophe! I am a ruined
man!"

"What is it?"

"Maria Andreyevna. Fever—chills—she cannot stand. She is lying on the
sofa like a corpse. Ophelia! We have no Ophelia!"

Kostromsky continued to inspect his eyebrows.

"Give the part to someone else."

"To whom? Surovtseva does not know it. The soubrette says
Shakspeare gives her neuralgia. We shall have to return the
money. Fourteen hundred roubles, not counting the boxes! My God,
why was I born?"

In the passage voices rose. Someone wept; someone ran for
the doctor. Kostromsky poured himself another vodka.

"Is there not a single young woman in this educational
metropolis capable of learning Ophelia?"

"There is Yureva," said Babanov suddenly. "But she—no, impossible."

"Who is Yureva?"

"A pupil. A student of the dramatic class. She has
never played before the public. She knows the role, I
think. She attends every rehearsal when she is permitted."

"Then let her play."

Babanov stared.

"Beside you?"

"Why not? A block of wood can play Ophelia if
Hamlet plays Hamlet."

At that moment another actor entered. This was Pavel Nikolaitch
Yakovlef, an elderly member of the company who had known
poverty, success, provincial winters, managerial swindles, and every humiliation of
his profession. His Claudius was considered sound, though Kostromsky had
privately described it as the work of a conscientious undertaker.

"Yureva can do it," Yakovlef said. "I have heard her
at the school."

"A schoolgirl?" asked Kostromsky.

"She has instinct."

"Instinct! Every cat has instinct."

"Not every actress."

Kostromsky shrugged.

"Bring her. But tell her not to scream. Young women
imagine madness is a toothache."

Ten minutes later Yureva stood at the dressing-room door. She
was dressed in a plain brown gown, for she had
been summoned from the gallery where she had come to
see her idol. She was scarcely twenty. Her figure was
slight; her fair hair was arranged without elegance, and her
face, pale from alarm, possessed no regular beauty. Only her
eyes were remarkable—grey, candid, and attentive, with that luminous seriousness
sometimes seen in children who have known sorrow too early.

She looked at Kostromsky, and her lips parted. For years
she had kept his portrait above her writing-table. His Hamlet,
known to her through descriptions, had become for her one
of those impossible summits toward which youth gazes without considering
roads, hunger, storms, or death.

"Do you know the part?" he asked.

"Yes, Alexander Petrovitch."

"All of it?"

"Yes."

"Have you played it?"

"Only at the class."

"Before how many?"

"Twelve."

He laughed.

"To-night there are nine hundred. But never mind; they are
only twelve people multiplied by seventy-five. Do as I direct,
keep your eyes on me, and do not seek effects.
Above all, do not interfere."

"I shall try not to disgrace you," she said softly.

The humility of the words pleased him.

"You will not disgrace me, child. No one can do
that but I myself—and I have not yet acquired the
habit."

Yakovlef glanced at him, but remained silent.

Yureva was led away to be dressed. In the corridor
Babanov wrung her hand and talked so rapidly that she
understood nothing. Costumes were fetched, rejected, pinned and altered. The
sick actress's shoes proved too small; her white gown was
too wide at the shoulders. Dressers pulled at Yureva as
though she were a doll to be repaired before presentation.

Only when she heard the orchestra tuning beyond the curtain
did terror seize her. Nine hundred persons! The thought had
no human proportions. It was a black sea waiting behind
a wall. She pressed her hands together and whispered the
first lines of Ophelia.

Yakovlef passed and paused beside her.

"Do not think of them," he said.

"How can I avoid it?"

"Think of the man to whom you speak. If he
is Hamlet to you, they will believe you are Ophelia."

"And if I fail?"

"Then you will have learned what cannot be taught in
twelve lessons."

He looked at her closely.

"Do not imitate Kostromsky."

She flushed, as though he had uttered blasphemy.

"Why should I not?"

"Because one Kostromsky is enough. And perhaps"—the old actor lowered
his voice—"more than enough."

## III

The bell rang.

That sound, which to a spectator is merely a warning
to settle into his chair, passes through an actor's nerves
like an electrical current. The confusion behind the scenes sharpened
and became purposeful. The players took their places; the prompter
descended into his wooden shell; Babanov peeped through a hole
in the curtain and came away radiant.

"Not an empty chair! The governor! The marshal of nobility!
Every student in town!"

Kostromsky stood in the wings, his black cloak falling in
severe folds. He could hear the audience: a vast, subdued
murmur, resembling the sound of the sea within a shell.
From this breathing darkness fame had come to him; from
it had come money, love, insolence, life itself. He knew
the creature. He had mastered it a thousand times.

The curtain rose upon Elsinore.

While the sentinels spoke, he waited calmly. His pulse was
even. He had taken enough vodka to steady himself, not
enough to cloud his articulation. A pleasant emptiness filled his
head, leaving room for the part.

His entrance was greeted first by silence and then by
applause which spread from the stalls to the gallery, gathering
force until the scene itself seemed to tremble. Kostromsky did
not bow. Hamlet, he held, must not acknowledge clapping. But
he delayed one instant, standing in profile beneath the blue
stage light, and allowed the audience to contemplate him.

The applause increased.

Ah! What fools had dared speak of decline?

He began.

His first words were uttered almost carelessly, with a dry,
inward bitterness which startled those who expected declamation. The voice,
though no longer full, carried to the remotest corner. In
the court scene he scarcely moved; a slight contraction of
the mouth, a lifted eyelid, the cold inclination of his
head toward the King—these sufficed. The spectators bent forward. When
he remained alone and grief at last broke through the
ceremonial restraint, his sorrow seemed enormous because it had been
so long confined.

The old enchantment was accomplished.

Backstage, even the carpenters ceased hammering. Babanov wiped his brow
and whispered, "A miracle." Yakovlef, waiting for his entrance, watched
without expression. Yureva stood behind a flat, pale and motionless.

Kostromsky felt the public yield. Its attention ran toward him
in invisible currents; nine hundred separate souls were fastened to
his gesture. He could pause as long as he pleased,
and no one coughed. He could lower his voice almost
to a breath, and the silence leaned toward him to
catch it.

When he came off, applause burst forth.

He walked to his dressing-room slowly, followed by congratulations.

"Well?" he said to the barber.

"Divine, Alexander Petrovitch! As in former days—no, greater!"

"As in former days?" Kostromsky repeated.

"I meant—your greatness cannot be measured by days."

"Idiot. Pour me some vodka."

He drank, sat down and closed his eyes. The act
had cost him more than he expected. His shirt clung
damply between his shoulders; his heart beat with small painful
knocks. Formerly, after such a scene, he had felt expanded,
refreshed, hungry for the next act. Now something within him
had been spent at once, like powder in a flash.

But this was natural. He had travelled; he had slept
badly. Besides, the first act was the heaviest.

In the corridor people were calling for Yureva. Her first
scene approached. Kostromsky opened his eyes and saw her pass
in the white gown, a wreath upon her fair hair.

"Come here."

She obeyed.

"Do not tremble," he said. "And remember: Ophelia loves Hamlet,
but she also fears him. Love pulls you forward, fear
holds you back. That is the whole mechanism."

"Yes, Alexander Petrovitch."

"Do not look at the audience."

"No."

"And do not weep too soon. Tears are the refuge
of actresses who have nothing to say."

She thanked him. Her grateful gaze again pleased his vanity,
and for a moment he felt almost paternal.

The scene began.

At first Yureva's voice was too low. Two or three
people coughed. Kostromsky, entering, perceived her terror and instantly made
use of it. His Hamlet approached with such concentrated anguish
that she forgot the footlights. She saw not the celebrated
actor who had spoken kindly to her in a dressing-room,
but a beloved man whom some invisible catastrophe had torn
away from her.

Her fear became Ophelia's fear.

"Tell me," he said, looking into her eyes.

She answered.

Something in the simplicity of her reply surprised him. There
was no theatrical sweetness, no carefully modulated pathos. She spoke
as a young girl would speak when truth is demanded
by one whom she cannot deceive. At the next phrase
her hand moved toward him and stopped midway, arrested by
shame and dread. The gesture was unconscious. A sigh passed
through the auditorium.

Kostromsky heard it.

For an instant he was irritated. Then professional instinct conquered.
Her sincerity supported him; against its clear light his Hamlet
grew darker and more terrible. He seized her wrist, rejected
her, returned, searched her face as though begging for salvation
while his words destroyed all hope. She responded to each
change, not with studied attitudes but with the helpless accuracy
of true feeling.

At the close of the scene there was applause. It
began hesitantly, then became warm and prolonged.

Yureva fled into the wings, blinded by tears.

"They like you," Babanov cried, embracing her shoulders.

She looked only for Kostromsky.

He came off more slowly. His breathing was laboured. She
hurried toward him.

"Alexander Petrovitch—"

"Not badly," he said. "For a beginning."

Those around them murmured approval. Yakovlef smiled.

"Instinct," he observed.

Kostromsky turned on him.

"She follows. That is all. A child can follow when
she has someone to lead her."

"Certainly," Yakovlef answered.

The agreement sounded to Kostromsky more offensive than contradiction.

## IV

During the interval the audience discussed Yureva.

At first her name was not known. Programmes were examined,
neighbours questioned, ushers summoned. The story of the sudden substitution
passed from box to box. That a pupil should have
faced the public without rehearsal and played with such truth
seemed marvellous. Young men in the gallery declared her a
discovery; ladies praised the purity of her face; an old
colonel said she reminded him of an actress whom he
had loved in 1862.

Kostromsky knew nothing of these conversations, yet by the obscure
instinct developed in those who live upon applause he felt
that attention had divided.

In his dressing-room he paced between table and door. The
fatigue had deepened. His muscles seemed filled with cold sand.
He poured vodka, found the bottle empty, and rang violently.

"Semyon!"

The barber appeared.

"Another bottle."

"Alexander Petrovitch, perhaps tea—"

"Did I ask what you drink? Bring vodka."

When alone, he looked into the mirror. The paint had
cracked beside his nostrils. Perspiration shone upon his forehead. He
repaired the damage with trembling fingers.

It was absurd to be disturbed by a schoolgirl. Her
success belonged to him. Had he not steadied her? Had
not his force drawn the feeling from her as a
magnet draws iron? Beside a mediocre Hamlet she would be
colourless. The audience applauded the scene, and the scene was
his.

Nevertheless, he remembered the sigh that had passed through the
theatre when she stretched out her hand.

Why had they sighed there? He had been speaking. He
had created the anguish. Yet the sigh had gone to
her.

Kostromsky drank as soon as the bottle arrived.

The next act began badly for him. The alcohol warmed
his limbs but loosened the rein upon his art. His
gestures became broader. He struck phrases which should have been
allowed to fall softly; he rolled his eyes in a
manner once admired by provincial critics; he prolonged pauses until
they ceased to be pregnant and became merely empty.

He did not know this. The actor who is declining
commonly becomes his own most indulgent spectator. He hears in
a worn voice its former resonance, sees in an habitual
gesture the passion that first produced it, and mistakes the
obedience of technique for the fire of creation. Memory applauds
while the public grows cold.

Kostromsky felt only that the audience resisted him. He therefore
increased his efforts. He shouted where he had once whispered,
rushed across the stage where he had once stood still,
and threw himself into attitudes whose beauty belonged to a
younger body.

The audience remained respectful. Respect is the first mourning garment
of dead enthusiasm.

There was applause at the recognised points, but it came
like payment of a debt. No spontaneous cry broke from
the gallery. No electric shudder ran through the house. The
mysterious chain had loosened.

Then Yureva entered.

Her first terror had vanished, but not her humility. She
had discovered the audience and forgotten it almost in the
same instant. Its attention no longer oppressed her; it sustained
her as deep water sustains one who ceases to struggle.
She thought only of Ophelia's bewilderment, of the beloved mind
into which she could no longer enter, of obedience to
a father she feared and loyalty to a man she
loved.

Kostromsky saw at once that she had grown.

This offended him as though the growth had taken something
from his own stature.

In their encounter he altered a piece of business without
warning, advancing upon her suddenly and speaking close to her
face. It was a test, almost a trap. A trained
but unimaginative actress would have lost the thread. Yureva recoiled;
then, reading cruelty and pain in his expression, she gave
a low cry so natural that the public stirred.

Again that murmur!

His fingers tightened about her arm.

"Go!" he said with real hatred beneath Hamlet's madness.

She turned white. The terror in her eyes delighted and
enraged him. For one hideous second he wished to crush
her arm, to make her cry not as Ophelia but
as herself, to punish the young blood in her cheeks,
the clear voice, the future opening before her.

And because the hatred was real, the scene flamed. The
audience believed it inspired.

When Yureva went off, applause followed her like a pursuing
storm. She returned for a bow at Babanov's frantic urging,
though the act was not yet ended. The audience called
her twice.

Kostromsky stood behind a flat and listened.

"Yureva! Yureva!"

Yesterday the town had not known her name.

He felt the cry pass through him like a knife.

Yakovlef approached, adjusting Claudius's crown.

"Take care," he said quietly.

"Of what?"

"You are tiring yourself."

"Attend to your king and leave Hamlet to me."

"I am speaking of the girl."

"What girl?"

"You frightened her."

"She is playing Ophelia. Ophelia must be frightened."

"Not of the actor."

Kostromsky laughed, but his lips were colourless beneath the paint.

"My dear Pavel Nikolaitch, when an old crow has acquired
a few grey feathers, he begins to imagine himself an
eagle. Do not teach me my art."

"No," said Yakovlef. "One could not teach you anything."

Again the calm agreement struck harder than insolence.

## V

The famous soliloquy came in the fourth act of the
provincial arrangement, after an interval during which Kostromsky drank twice
and sat for several minutes with his head between his
hands.

Once, "To be, or not to be" had been the
crown of his Hamlet. Audiences awaited it with almost physical
expectation. His interpretation was known throughout Russia. He began not
as a philosopher entertaining an abstract question, but as a
man to whom death had entered the room and taken
a chair opposite him. The thought developed gradually; fear contended
with longing; and at the end, when consciousness had made
cowards of all, he appeared to awake from the very
edge of the grave. Men who despised the theatre had
wept.

He remembered every triumph attached to those lines: flowers falling
from a box in Moscow, students shouting in Kiev, a
woman fainting in Vilna, the critic who wrote that Kostromsky
had "lifted the skull of mankind and looked beneath it."

Now, as he waited in the darkness, he repeated to
himself: This will restore everything.

The stage had been cleared. A cold light lay upon
the painted corridor. Beyond the footlights the auditorium was invisible,
yet he could feel its thousand eyes. It had applauded
Yureva; very well. In a moment it would remember whom
it had come to see.

He entered.

The silence was complete.

"To be, or not to be—"

His voice sounded strange to him, too near and too
dry. He paused.

Formerly the first sentence had opened within him an abyss.
Now he saw only a sentence, famous, dangerous, worn smooth
by repetition. He knew how to incline his head, when
to raise his hand, where to let the voice sink,
where to summon its bronze vibration. All was prepared, arranged,
classified. But behind the mechanism there was nothing.

He continued.

He spoke of death while fearing not death but indifference.
He spoke of the undiscovered country while thinking of the
gallery. He spoke of the whips and scorns of time,
and suddenly the words, long dulled by use, recovered an
atrocious personal meaning.

The whips and scorns of time!

Was not time standing before him now, pitiless, invisible, holding
up the young girl as evidence? Yureva had not defeated
him. Time had chosen her as its instrument. Youth had
entered in a borrowed white gown and, with one unconscious
gesture, overturned the throne built from thirty years of applause.

He reached the line where Hamlet meditates upon the proud
man's contumely. His breath failed. A cough sounded in the
stalls.

A cough!

In former days no one had breathed.

He made the great pause—the pause which had been described
in journals and imitated by dozens of actors. The audience
waited politely. From somewhere in the upper gallery came the
rustle of a programme.

He finished.

There was no applause.

Perhaps a few hands moved uncertainly, but the sound died
before it became clapping. The silence which followed was not
the silence of enchantment. It was vacancy. The audience had
listened because the words were Shakspeare's and the actor was
Kostromsky; it had waited for the celebrated effect; the effect
had not come.

It was a moment of awful enlightenment.

Kostromsky stood upon the stage, but another self, sober, merciless,
and all-seeing, stood apart and judged him. The paint, the
black cloak, the noble attitude—what were they? Rags hung upon
an exhausted old man. The voice? A broken instrument. The
gestures? Echoes. The grief? Memory of grief. He had spent
his gift years ago, squandered it in drinking rooms, at
card tables, in embraces without love, in quarrels undertaken to
nourish vanity. For a long time fame had concealed the
loss. Managers had advertised the name; audiences had applauded the
legend; critics had respectfully compared the ruin with the palace
once standing there. And he, fool, drunkard, sovereign of an
empty kingdom, had believed himself immortal.

Yes, it was all past!

Wreaths, diamonds, horses unharnessed, women at hotel doors, suppers until
dawn, banknotes thrust into waiters' hands, the roar of crowded
houses, young actors trembling in his presence—where were they? Painted
scraps, like the cardboard battlements of Elsinore, splendid from the
auditorium and propped behind by dirty planks.

He had thought himself Hamlet. He was only the ghost.

The thought struck him with such force that he swayed.
The prompter whispered the next line. Kostromsky looked toward the
little hooded box with hatred.

"I know!" he cried.

The words were not in the play.

The audience stirred. He recovered himself and continued, but now
consciousness pursued him. Every intonation appeared false before it left
his mouth. He anticipated each gesture and despised it while
making it. His own body had become an enemy which
parodied the actor he once had been.

When he came offstage, no one congratulated him.

Babanov busied himself with a property sword. Semyon pretended to
arrange the cloak. A young scene-shifter, not knowing whom to
fear, stared openly.

"Vodka," Kostromsky said.

No one moved.

"Are you deaf?"

The barber hurried away.

From the stage came Yureva's voice.

It was the mad scene.

## VI

Yureva had sat alone before her entrance, wounded and bewildered
by Kostromsky's cruelty. She could still feel the pressure of
his fingers upon her arm. She believed she had offended
him through inexperience. Perhaps she had stood in the wrong
place; perhaps her applause had delayed the performance; perhaps—and this
thought was most painful—her acting had been vulgar, and the
audience's approval had merely irritated a great artist who perceived
its falsity.

She wept quietly.

Then the call-boy summoned her.

Ophelia enters mad after a grief too great for her
frail understanding. Yureva had rehearsed the scene many times, adorning
it with broken laughter, wandering looks, and picturesque gestures copied
from celebrated actresses. Now all those devices seemed shameful. Her
own heart was bruised; admiration, fear, humiliation and pity had
confused it. She stepped before the footlights carrying this confusion
with her.

At first the spectators scarcely recognised her. Her fair hair
hung loose; bits of straw and withered flowers clung to
her white gown. She smiled.

The smile was not madness exhibited, but sanity trying with
pathetic courtesy to conceal its wound.

The theatre became still.

She sang. Her voice, small and without extraordinary beauty, trembled
once, steadied itself, and floated through the house with an
innocence more piercing than any lamentation. She distributed flowers which
were not there, naming them as though each invisible blossom
preserved a memory. At moments she seemed to listen for
someone who would never answer. Then she laughed, and in
that laugh there was the clear note of something broken
forever.

Behind the scenery Kostromsky listened.

Every phrase reached the place in him newly laid bare
by humiliation. He knew acting; whatever jealousy might say, he
could not be deceived in this. The girl possessed the
gift. Not discipline, not experience, not knowledge—these might come or
might not—but the sacred and terrible capacity to feel before
strangers without shame and to make their hearts beat in
obedience to hers. She had crossed the footlights. She held
the chain.

And he had lost it.

His first emotion was envy so violent that his sight
darkened. He wanted her to fail, to forget a line,
to trip over the gown, to force the audience to
laugh. When the first sob came from the stalls, he
clenched his hands.

"What nonsense," he muttered. "Sentimental rubbish."

No one answered.

Yureva approached the end of the scene. Her face turned
toward the wings, and for one instant her eyes met
Kostromsky's. She saw him watching. The remembered insult entered her
expression: love shrinking before cruelty, the disciple before the fallen
idol, Ophelia before Hamlet. Without intending it, he completed her
art.

She spoke her last words and went out as though
drifting after something invisible.

For a second there was silence.

Then the theatre erupted.

Applause broke from nine hundred breasts at once, violent, joyous,
almost savage. People rose. The gallery stamped. Handkerchiefs waved from
the boxes. Voices called:

"Yureva! Yureva!"

She stopped in the wings, terrified by the noise.

"Go!" cried Babanov, pushing her forward. "Bow!"

She went before the curtain, bowed awkwardly, and returned.

The applause did not cease.

"Again!"

She went again. Someone threw roses. One struck her shoulder
and fell at her feet.

Kostromsky watched from the passage. The sound was familiar—too familiar.
It was the very storm that had once beaten around
him. He recognised its degrees, its rising waves, the separate
cry which provokes a renewed outbreak, the stamping by which
the gallery expresses love when hands are insufficient. The public
had not changed. It could still adore. It was he
whom it no longer adored.

Yureva returned for the third time, flushed, trembling, with tears
upon her cheeks. As she passed, her sleeve touched him.

He seized her shoulder.

"That is enough!"

She started.

"The public is calling," Babanov protested.

"The public is a fool."

Again the cries arose.

"Yureva!"

Babanov attempted to lead her back. Kostromsky pushed her aside
with such violence that she struck the wall.

"You little fool!" he hissed. "Do you think those cattle
love you? You grimace, whine, scatter your hair, and they
call it art! Go—take your triumph! Feed upon it while
it lasts!"

Her face became white. She looked at him with an
expression in which pain gradually extinguished reverence.

That look was unbearable.

"Do not stare at me!"

"I am sorry," she whispered.

"Sorry? For whom?"

"For you, Alexander Petrovitch."

He raised his hand as though to strike her. Yakovlef
stepped between them.

"Enough."

The two men looked at each other.

Kostromsky's hand fell.

From before the curtain came continued applause, then murmurs of
surprise. The orchestra leader, uncertain what to do, lifted his
baton and began the entr'acte. The musicians played too loudly.

"I shall not continue," Kostromsky said.

Babanov laughed nervously.

"What do you mean?"

"I have finished."

"But there is another act! The graveyard—the duel—the death! Alexander
Petrovitch, my dear benefactor, they will smash the seats!"

"Let them."

"You cannot abandon Hamlet before he dies!"

"He is dead."

Kostromsky turned toward his dressing-room.

Babanov followed, wringing his hands.

"Think of the public! Think of my position! Think of
the governor! We can shorten the act. Ten minutes—five! You
may omit the fencing. Only die, Alexander Petrovitch! For the
love of God, come out and die!"

The actor shut the door in his face.

## VII

For several minutes Kostromsky stood without moving.

Outside, Babanov pleaded, threatened, groaned, and finally ran away in
search of Yakovlef. The orchestra continued to play. Its cheerful
strains, intended to soothe the public, penetrated the thin door
with an air of monstrous frivolity.

Kostromsky approached the mirror.

Hamlet confronted him.

The face was magnificent at a distance. White forehead, dark
eyes, finely marked brows, a mouth compressed by thought—there was
the portrait reproduced in albums and shop windows. But when
he leaned nearer he saw the sweat eating channels through
the paint, the sagging flesh under the chin, the inflamed
veins beside the nose. The prince was melting. The old
drunkard emerged.

He tore off the wig.

Grey hair, flattened and damp, fell about his skull. He
rubbed his cheek with a towel. Flesh-coloured paint, black pencil
and rouge smeared together. He rubbed harder, almost fiercely, as
if the false face were a stain of which he
must purify himself. The towel became filthy; the skin beneath
burned red.

The mirror now showed neither Hamlet nor Kostromsky the genius,
but Alexander Petrovitch, sixty-two years old, exhausted, frightened, alone.

He sat down.

On the wall hung his visiting costume, upon the table
lay the skull used in the graveyard scene, its plaster
forehead chipped above one eye. He picked it up.

"How many times?" he murmured.

How many times had he held that skull and spoken
of Yorick? A thousand? Two thousand? He had made audiences
meditate upon mortality while living as though no reckoning awaited
him. He had preached the vanity of ambition in cities
where he demanded larger letters on the posters. He had
lamented the frailty of flesh and afterwards drunk until morning.
Shakspeare had placed truth upon his tongue; he had converted
it into effects.

The plaster skull grinned.

Kostromsky hurled it across the room. It struck the wall
and broke.

Then he wept.

At first the tears came slowly, surprising him. He brushed
them away with annoyance. But grief, once permitted entrance, rushed
upon him with illimitable force. He bent over the table,
his shoulders shaking. Paint mingled with tears and stained his
hands.

He wept for his lost art, but also for his
youth, and it was impossible to separate the two. He
saw himself as he had been in that theatre: hungry,
thin, arrogant, rehearsing beneath a single gas jet after midnight;
believing that suffering ennobled him and every sacrifice was light
because the future blazed ahead. What would that young man
say if shown this ending? He would turn away in
disgust. Or perhaps he would not believe it. Youth never
believes in the old age it prepares for itself.

Kostromsky remembered chances rejected, friends insulted, women abandoned, parts neglected
because supper had seemed more urgent than rehearsal. He remembered
directors who warned him, physicians who begged him to stop
drinking, an old actress who had said, "Guard your gift;
it is not your property." He had laughed at them
all. The gift was inexhaustible! Applause would always arise! If
one company dismissed him, ten managers would kneel at his
hotel door!

Arrogance, selfishness, drink—three patient executioners. They had not killed him
at once. They had taken a tone from his voice,
a thought from his memory, a degree of truth from
each gesture. Because the theft was gradual he had not
perceived it. When at last he looked, the treasury was
empty.

What remained to him? Touring small towns upon the credit
of his name, deceiving audiences too timid to hiss? Playing
kings who could not remember their kingdoms? Becoming an anecdote
told in greenrooms: "I saw Kostromsky when he was great"?
Waiting until managers ceased to answer his telegrams?

On the table lay a small razor used for trimming
the wig. His gaze rested upon it.

To be, or not to be.

The irony was so cruel that he laughed through his
tears. A real blade, a real question—and no audience! What
admirable staging! He imagined the door forced open, Babanov shrieking,
the newspapers: TRAGIC DEATH OF THE GREAT KOSTROMSKY. Flowers would
come again. Obituaries would restore everything. Critics would forget the
failed soliloquy and write of his immortal genius. Death might
accomplish the effect denied to art.

He took up the razor.

The handle was light. The blade, poorly sharpened, reflected the
gas flame.

But the impulse passed almost at once. Not courage restrained
him, nor hope. He felt only weariness. Even death appeared
another performance, and he had no strength left to play
it.

He put down the razor.

The orchestra stopped. Beyond the door came confused noises: footsteps,
angry voices, seats closing, the dull roar of a disappointed
public. Babanov had evidently announced that the performance could not
continue owing to sudden illness. Some spectators demanded their money;
others lingered, hoping Yureva might appear once more.

The theatre gradually emptied.

Kostromsky sat in darkness after the gas burner began to
fail. No one disturbed him. Babanov, having exhausted entreaties, had
turned to financial disaster; the actors changed their clothes in
silence. Stagehands commenced dismantling Elsinore before Hamlet's death had taken
place.

Presently, through the wall, Kostromsky heard someone crying.

The sound was soft and restrained. It came from the
adjoining dressing-room.

Yureva.

He raised his head.

At first her tears irritated him. Why should she cry?
She had triumphed. The future stood before her with flowers
in its hands. To-night she had received in one hour
what others begged for through years of humiliation.

Then he remembered the look in her eyes after he
pushed her. He remembered her words: I am sorry for
you.

He had struck at the only person in the theatre
who had approached him with love. She had worshipped him;
for that very reason he had desired to wound her.
Her success had been innocent, yet he had treated it
as treachery. What had she understood of his terror? She
was a child suddenly dragged from the gallery, thrust into
a white gown, and ordered to act beside a famous
man. If she possessed talent, was that a crime?

The jealousy ebbed, leaving shame.

Compassion, long stifled by vanity but not wholly dead, stirred
within him. Perhaps this was the final spark of the
gift he had squandered: the power to enter, however late,
into another's suffering.

He rose and went to the door.

## VIII

The corridors were nearly dark. A solitary lamp burned beside
the staircase; shadows of ropes and hanging costumes wavered upon
the walls. Through the open door to the stage one
could see workmen carrying away the painted walls of the
palace. The footlights had been extinguished. Beyond them, the empty
auditorium yawned like a cavern, its rows of red seats
covered in grey gloom.

Yureva sat upon a trunk outside her dressing-room. She had
changed into her brown gown, but her hair still hung
loosely over her shoulders. The wreath lay crushed beside her.
She covered her face with both hands.

Kostromsky stopped before her.

She heard him and rose quickly. Fear passed across her
face.

"Do not be afraid," he said.

His voice no longer possessed Hamlet's resonance. It was the
tired voice of an old man.

"I came to ask you to forgive me."

She looked at him, uncertain whether this too might conceal
cruelty.

"I behaved vilely," he continued. "No—do not deny it out
of politeness. I know the names of things now. For
once I know them."

"You were ill, Alexander Petrovitch."

"I was envious."

She lowered her eyes.

"Yes, envious of you. It sounds ridiculous, does it not?
Kostromsky, whose name covers the walls, envious of a pupil
without a pair of theatrical shoes. But the ridiculous is
sometimes more painful than the sublime."

He sat upon the trunk and passed a hand over
his face. Without wig or paint, in the poor corridor
light, he appeared suddenly ancient.

"You must understand," he said. "When you played, the public
listened to you as it once listened to me. I
felt the current leave me and go toward you. An
actor knows it. He may pretend, he may accuse the
public, the author, the company, the weather—but he knows. To-night
I knew."

Yureva remained silent.

"I wanted to hurt you because you had shown me
the truth. Not intentionally. You did not even look at
me. That made it worse. If you had plotted to
defeat me, I might have hated you honestly. But you
merely felt. You opened your heart, and they followed you."

"I could not have played without you," she said. "When
you looked at me—"

"Ah, yes. I looked at you with hatred. It was
useful to Ophelia."

"No. Before that. You made everything real."

For a moment his old vanity raised its head. He
felt the sweetness of her tribute and nearly accepted it
as proof that his judgment had been mistaken. But enlightenment,
once suffered, could not be entirely dismissed.

"I helped you," he said. "Perhaps. A dying fire may
light another candle. But do not confuse that with what
I was. Nor with what I am."

He looked toward the dismantled stage. A carpenter dropped a
plank; the crash reverberated through the empty house.

"You have talent," he said.

Yureva drew a breath.

"Yakovlef told me you had instinct. I laughed at him.
He was right. You have more than instinct. You have
sincerity, and sincerity upon the stage is rarer than beauty,
intelligence, or voice. Guard it. Technique will come. Fame may
come. Money, flowers, flatterers, fools with articles, women who envy
you, men who swear they understand you—all may come. They
will tell you that you are sacred because they desire
to possess a little of your triumph. Do not believe
them."

She sat down at the opposite end of the trunk.

"Is fame so evil?"

"No. It is delightful. Poison is often delightful, otherwise no
one would take it. Applause enters the blood more swiftly
than vodka. One begins by needing it after a great
performance; afterwards one needs it for entering, for speaking, for
merely showing one's face. At last one cannot live without
the sound, though one has ceased to deserve it."

He smiled bitterly.

"I was not always what you saw to-night. Remember that,
if you can. I worked. I suffered. There were evenings
when I gave them all I possessed and more—when something
passed through me that did not belong to me, and
I was afraid of it even while I rejoiced. That
is why the ruin is so complete. If I had
always been a charlatan, there would be nothing to mourn."

"I know you were great," Yureva whispered.

"Were. A small word! An abyss contained in four letters."

She began to weep again, but now her tears were
for him.

Kostromsky looked at her with tenderness. There was no desire
in it, no vanity, no theatrical paternalism; only the grave
compassion of one condemned traveller for another who has just
set out upon the road.

"Listen to me, Anna Sergeyevna."

She was startled that he knew her Christian name and
patronymic.

"If your gift grows, serve it, not yourself. Do not
imagine it is immortal because it burns brightly. Everything living
can die. Work while it lives. Keep your mind clear.
Spare your body. Let no manager persuade you that exhaustion
is devotion to art, and no admirer that debauchery is
freedom. They lied to me, but I invited the lie."

He paused.

"And when the day comes—if it comes—when you feel the
current weaken, when a phrase which once opened heaven becomes
only a phrase, when the audience waits and you have
nothing to give—go."

She gazed at him.

"Leave the stage?"

"At once."

"But if one still hopes?"

"Hope is then the cruellest prompter. It whispers lines belonging
to yesterday."

He leaned closer, speaking with sudden intensity.

"Go before they teach you. Do not wait for polite
applause. Do not travel from town to town selling the
memory of your face. Do not hate the young because
they possess what has left you. Bow while they are
still asking you to return. Then close the door yourself."

The girl shivered.

"I cannot imagine it."

"Of course not. Spring cannot imagine snow. But remember."

Footsteps approached. Babanov emerged from the darkness carrying a ledger,
his waistcoat unbuttoned and his face purple with indignation.

"Alexander Petrovitch! At last! I have searched everywhere. A fine
business! We must discuss the repayment. The governor's secretary threatens—"

He noticed Yureva and checked himself.

Kostromsky stood.

"How much do you owe me?"

"Your honorarium? After this catastrophe? Well, by the contract—though, strictly
speaking, failure to complete—still, out of respect—"

"Keep it."

Babanov blinked.

"Keep what?"

"All of it."

"Seven hundred and fifty roubles?"

"Yes."

The manager's expression passed rapidly through suspicion, relief, and renewed
alarm.

"But to-morrow—The Robbers. We must announce whether—"

"There will be no performance."

"You need rest. By noon—"

"I shall be gone by noon."

"But the tickets are sold!"

"Return the money."

"With what?"

"With my seven hundred and fifty roubles. For the balance
sell Hamlet's crown."

Babanov stared at him as one might stare at a
madman whose madness has become inconveniently lucid.

"You cannot end your engagement in this fashion."

"I have ended more than the engagement."

"What shall I print?"

"Print the truth."

"The truth? On a theatrical bill?"

Kostromsky almost laughed.

"Then print sudden illness. That is near enough."

Babanov began another protest, but Kostromsky passed him. Yureva rose.

"Alexander Petrovitch!"

He turned.

She wished to say something worthy of the moment—something about
gratitude, forgiveness, his greatness, her sorrow—but words failed her. She
took his hand and kissed it.

He looked down at her bent head.

Once such homage would have seemed his right. Now it
humbled him.

"Do not kiss that hand," he said softly. "It struck
at you."

"It also showed me Hamlet."

He withdrew his hand and touched her hair in blessing.

"Good-bye, Ophelia."

Then he walked toward the stage.

No one stopped him. Babanov had begun calculating under his
breath; Yureva remained beside the trunk; from a distant room
came the laughter of two chorus girls already speaking of
supper.

Kostromsky crossed between the heaps of discarded scenery. Denmark had
vanished. The painted battlements leaned face downward; the arras lay
rolled beside a broken throne; the graveyard spade rested upon
a chair. Stagehands moved like indifferent executioners among the remains.

He paused at the line of the extinguished footlights.

The auditorium was empty.

How small it appeared without people! Nine hundred red seats,
a tarnished chandelier, gilded boxes, the dark mouth of the
gallery. Yet from that ordinary space had arisen the force
which governed his life. He had feared it, seduced it,
despised it, depended upon it. To-night it had judged him,
not cruelly, not even consciously. The public had merely turned
toward life where it found life.

There lay its innocence and its mercilessness.

Kostromsky bowed.

No applause answered.

He remained bent for an instant, then straightened and descended
by the side passage. At the outer door the porter,
half asleep, hurried to fetch his coat. Kostromsky put it
on without assistance. The sable collar no longer seemed magnificent;
under the weak lamp it showed its bald patches plainly.

Outside, snow had begun to fall.

The square before the theatre was deserted except for a
few sledges moving toward the distant avenue. Posters bearing his
enormous name fluttered on the walls. One had torn loose
at the corner and snapped in the wind. Beneath KOSTROMSKY,
in letters so small that they were scarcely visible, stood
the name YUREVA.

He read it and smiled.

The cold air entered his lungs painfully, but it cleared
his head. He did not know where he would go.
The hotel, the railway station, another town—these had ceased to
matter. For the first time in forty years there was
no next performance awaiting him, no manager, no call-boy, no
mirror, no audience breathing behind a curtain.

The emptiness was terrible.

It was also honest.

Behind him the theatre doors opened, and a workman threw
out a bucket of dirty water. For a moment voices
and hammering escaped into the night; then the doors closed,
and silence returned.

Kostromsky walked across the square. His steps were slow but
steady. At the corner he looked back once at the
dim façade, at the posters, at the high window behind
which Yureva perhaps still sat beneath the wavering gaslight.

"I have finished," he said.

Snow settled upon his hat and shoulders, erasing his footprints
almost as soon as he made them.

"I have finished."

## MARY FOULKES'S FAMILY

I first knew Mary Foulkes when she was nine years
old, though, as her father's wagon had been grinding over
the Sierra road for six years before that period, it
is probable that she had already known me in some
earlier and less responsible condition. She had no mother, except
the great brown Mother who spread pine-needles for her feet,
shook snow from her skirts upon the summit, and alternately
washed and scorched the road according to the season. Her
father, Bill Foulkes, was a teamster; a silent, broad-backed man,
who spoke to his mules with profane intimacy, and to
his daughter with a shyness that was almost reverence.

Mary traveled with him whenever weather, freight, and the uncertain
temper of six mules permitted. She sat upon the high
box among buffalo robes, flour dust, chains, and such miscellaneous
articles as are supposed by masculine housekeeping to be equally
necessary; and from this elevation she governed a family of
five children.

I use the word "children" deliberately. The most ordinary student
of anatomy might have raised objections to their structure, while
a person of narrow religious principles could have denied them
souls; but along the road, from Red Chief to Eagle's
Gap, their humanity was never publicly disputed. We had too
much respect for Mary, and perhaps too little confidence in
our own definitions.

The eldest was Angelica, a female of uncertain age, composed
principally of a flour sack. Her complexion had been painted
upon cotton by Pete Mallory, who kept the Halfway House,
and whose artistic practice had previously been confined to lettering
whisky barrels. Angelica's left eye looked permanently toward heaven, while
the right watched the earth with a suspicion justified by
experience. Her hair was unravelled rope, her body sawdust, and
her legs terminated in two blue wooden pegs. Mary considered
her delicate.

The twins, Ebenezer and Emmeline, had enjoyed a previous state
of existence as ninepins in the Red Chief saloon. They
were presented to Mary after their premature retirement from sport,
Ebenezer having been split by a ball thrown in anger,
and Emmeline having lost nearly all her paint in what
Pete described as "a heap too active a youth." Their
faces were supplied by charcoal; their arms, fastened with nails,
maintained an attitude of perpetual astonishment. Mary distinguished their sexes
by tying a strip of red flannel round Emmeline's waist.
Ebenezer, possessing no such adornment, was understood to be a
boy.

The fourth child was Baby Silas, whose canvas body was
stuffed with beans, except where the beans had escaped through
infirm seams and been replaced by buckshot. This mixed constitution
gave him a moral weight beyond his years. When shaken,
he emitted a grave internal warning, like a statesman considering
taxation. His head was a smooth white stone from Rattlesnake
Creek, and his features, being frequently renewed in pencil, varied
with his health and Mary's disposition.

The youngest was Misery.

Misery had begun, I believe, as a china doll, although
nothing of the original creature remained below the neck after
Jim Hoskins's dog had mistaken her for something edible. Nor
did the neck itself survive the subsequent operation performed by
Doc Briggs with a cold chisel. In reconstructing her, Hank
Larrabee, the blacksmith, made an artistic suggestion. He forged a
small globular head of cast iron, furnished it with two
punctures for eyes, attached three prongs beneath, and drove these
firmly into a body compounded of blanket, leather, and wool.

The result was durable rather than beautiful.

Mary accepted it without criticism. She wrapped the iron countenance
in lace, kissed it, and named it Misery because, she
said, "she's had such a deal of it already."

From that day Hank was commonly regarded as Misery's godfather.

It would be an error to suppose Mary's maternity was
either idle or sentimental. It was practical, vigilant, and admirably
impartial. Angelica's delicacy required wraps in August; Ebenezer's alleged wickedness
demanded correction; Emmeline's nerves prohibited travel behind the gray leaders;
Silas was perpetually teething; and Misery, although constitutionally incapable of
fever, suffered it every Thursday. Mary fed them from an
empty mustard spoon, washed their faces at springs, and laid
them in a long starch box at night. If rain
penetrated the wagon cover she sat upright holding that box
under her dress, while Bill Foulkes slept in the weather
and never knew it.

We knew it.

Rough men, whose expressed opinions of infancy were neither numerous
nor favorable, became strangely informed regarding Mary's household. Pete kept
a fragment of candle for Angelica's birthdays. Doc Briggs prescribed
for Silas and once listened solemnly to his stony chest.

"Respiration's weak," he said, after a prolonged examination.

"He don't breathe very loud," Mary admitted anxiously.

"Families differ," returned Doc. "My uncle on my mother's side
scarcely breathed at all for upward of thirty year, an'
lived to be eighty-seven."

This relieved her.

Jake Sweeney, who had driven freight since the road was
a deer trail and possessed manners calculated to make a
bear self-conscious, carried sugar in his pocket for Emmeline. He
pretended that the child ate it when Mary was absent,
though we had reason to believe he ate it himself.
Hank Larrabee polished Misery's head with axle grease, a process
which gave her an evanescent lustre and blackened Mary's kisses;
and Pete Mallory, after being informed that Ebenezer had learned
to swear, took the ninepin behind the stable and addressed
him for twenty minutes upon the degradation of profanity.

"I told him," said Pete afterward, "that no gent could
expect to rise in this country if he give way
to low language."

Pete's own account of the interview contained expressions which, had
Ebenezer possessed ordinary imitative faculties, would have destroyed its moral
purpose.

The children were not exempt from accident. On one winter
journey, when the road beyond Dead Mule Grade was a
white wall and the wagon moved within a whirl of
snow, Emmeline fell from beneath Mary's arm and disappeared. The
loss was not discovered until the team had gone nearly
half a mile.

Bill stopped.

"Road's choked," said Jake, who was traveling behind us. "Couldn't
find a mule in it."

"Em'line ain't a mule," Mary replied.

There was no accusation in this statement. That made it
worse.

Bill climbed down without a word. Jake, Pete, and I
went back with him, probing the drifts with whip-stocks. It
was dusk before Pete found a red thread above the
snow. Beneath it lay Emmeline, rigid, damp, and deprived of
both arms.

Mary received the remains with composure, but her chin trembled.

"She's froze," said Jake, avoiding our eyes.

Doc Briggs, who happened upon us at the Halfway House,
ordered immediate stimulants. He placed Emmeline beside the stove, poured
whisky over her wooden head, and fitted two new arms
cut from cigar-box cedar.

"Will she get well?" Mary whispered.

"She'll pull through," said Doc. "But"—he glanced severely at the
ninepin—"she's acquired dissipated habits young."

For several months afterward Emmeline smelled strongly of spirits, a
circumstance Mary attempted to conceal from Ebenezer.

At another time Angelica was run over by the hind
wheel of her grandfather's wagon. I say "grandfather's" because Bill,
having accepted Mary's relationship to the doll, could not logically
evade his own. The accident flattened Angelica to an attenuation
painful to behold and distributed her sawdust over six feet
of road. Bill sat on a rock with the empty
skin in his hands.

"Reckon I've killed her," he said.

Mary knelt and gathered the sawdust, including pine bark, gravel,
and two beetles which had become mingled with the remains.
Then she carried the whole to Mrs. Ricketts at the
Toll Gate. That excellent woman, who professed no patience with
"fool notions," spent her afternoon restoring Angelica with bran from
her own flour bin and made her a new dress
afterward.

"You've stuffed her too fat," Mary observed.

"Growing child," said Mrs. Ricketts shortly.

Thus Mary's family flourished, and I think we flourished with
it. There are affections which civilized men announce in drawing
rooms and print upon cards; ours, being less educated, went
about disguised as repairs. We carved arms, stitched bodies, saved
ribbon, and postponed wagons in snowstorms. No one kissed the
children except Mary. But no man on the route would
have left one lying in the road.

Then the railroad came.

For two years we had heard of it as a
metallic threat beyond the mountains. Surveyors appeared, driving stakes through
familiar pastures with the calm brutality of science. Chinese laborers
crept along the cliffs; blasting shook the pines; grades widened;
camps sprang up where deer had drunk; and at last
the locomotive entered Eagle's Gap, breathing smoke, ringing a bell,
and making in an afternoon a journey which had formerly
demanded two days, three quarrels, and the loss of a
horseshoe.

We stood beside it with our hands in our pockets.

"Snivelization," said Jake Sweeney. "That's what she is."

Yet he went into the railway office the next week
and asked for work.

Bill's hauling dwindled. Families came into the Gap—people with clocks,
carpets, books, and opinions concerning children. A schoolhouse was built
near the station, and presently a committee informed Bill that
Mary must attend.

Bill received this command as he received most injuries: silently.
Mary heard him and asked whether the children might go
too.

"Only one doll," said Mrs. Baines, the teacher, with a
smile suggesting that even one was a concession to barbarism.

"They ain't dolls," Mary answered.

Mrs. Baines smiled again. It was an educated smile and
consequently final.

The domestic consultation that followed at Pete's house was among
the gravest I remember. Mary sat at the table, with
all five before her. We stood about awkwardly. Separation had
entered that prolific household for the first time.

"Angelica needs me most," Mary said, "but she can't study
on account of her eyes. Ebenezer'd act bad. Emmeline's afeared
of strangers. Silas makes a noise when he's jounced, and
Misery"—she lifted the iron infant—"Misery don't care what happens."

Misery was selected.

The others must be provided for, and here the road
displayed a generosity that would have embarrassed it if mentioned
directly. Mrs. Ricketts adopted Angelica, though insisting that she was
merely "keeping the rag thing outer the dust." Pete took
Ebenezer, on the ground that the boy required a firm
masculine influence. Jake accepted Emmeline after stipulating that her previous
taste for whisky should not be charged against him. Doc
Briggs took Baby Silas because, as he explained, the child's
internal condition ought to be watched professionally. Hank claimed a
continuing interest in Misery, but agreed that she should accompany
Mary to school.

Each Friday afternoon the scattered family was reunited at the
Halfway House. Jake brought Emmeline in a saddlebag, her red
flannel showing from the top. Doc carried Silas in his
medicine case among lancets and pills. Pete generally arrived with
Ebenezer projecting from his breast pocket and explained that he
had taken the boy to the saloon only to show
him what to avoid. Mrs. Ricketts conveyed Angelica in a
basket under a clean napkin.

Mary examined each child minutely.

"Emmeline's hair's comin' off," she would tell Jake.

"Climate," Jake replied. "Dry weather's hard on females."

"Ebenezer's got a bruise."

"Fell outer bed," said Pete.

"He don't sleep in a bed."

"That's likely why he fell outer it."

Then there were mending, feeding, instruction, and family prayers. We
sat on boxes and watched her, while outside the new
trains called sharply through the pines. The sound troubled us,
though none said why.

School changed Mary slowly at first. She washed her hands
more often and corrected Pete's grammar once, an act which
nearly ended their friendship. She learned that beans were not
suitable organs for a child, that iron did not take
fever, and that ninepins were inanimate. These facts she repeated
when required, but on Friday she asked Emmeline whether she
had been lonesome. Education had cracked the door; imagination still
kept its small foot against it.

The final opening came when Mary was twelve. An aunt
in Sacramento, awakened after long indifference to the impropriety of
her brother's arrangements, determined that the child should be sent
to a convent school. Bill protested by not answering three
letters. The aunt then arrived in person, dressed in black
silk, and inspected the wagon, the starch-box cradle, Mary's sunburned
face, and Misery's iron head.

The decision was immediate.

Mary was to go for three years.

Her departure brought the whole road to the station. The
children, however, were forbidden. Mary herself agreed they had better
remain, since, as she privately informed us, convents were places
where "ladies was awful particular, and babies might holler in
church."

She distributed final instructions. Angelica was not to sleep near
a draft. Ebenezer was to be kept from cards. Emmeline
must never be reminded of the snowdrift. Silas required shaking
every morning to prevent the buckshot from settling. Misery might
be polished with grease, but not too much, because it
tasted bad.

Then she kissed them all and left them with us.

When the train moved away, she leaned from the window,
her old hat trembling in the wind, and called, "Be
good to 'em!"

We promised. There were handkerchiefs waved from the cars and
hats waved from the platform. Jake Sweeney did neither. He
stood with Emmeline hidden under his coat and swore at
the engine until it was gone.

Mary wrote. Her first letter, addressed collectively to "My Dear
Childern and Their Keepers," was carried for a week in
Pete's pocket before being read aloud at the Halfway House:

> Dere All, I am wel and studyin jography which
is countries. Sister Agnes says I improve but my n's
look like u's. Tell Angeliker I seen a little girl
with yello hare like her only not so much. Tell
Ebeneser not to go in Petes salune. Mr Sweeny must
rap Emmelines throat in red flannel when it snose. Baby
Silas shud be shuck gentle. Dear Misery I dreem of
you most becos you never had a pretty face and
might think I forgot. I have not forgot no one.
Your loving Mother, Mary Foulkes.

Jake blew his nose loudly and said the room was
smoky.

For the first year letters came every month. In the
second they came at Christmas, Easter, and Bill's birthday. Their
spelling improved. "Childern" became "children"; "Angeliker" became "Angelica"; geography ceased
to be "countries" and became a subject in which Mary
had received distinction. The directions for care grew briefer. Misery
was no longer directly addressed.

The third year brought two letters, both affectionate, correct, and
almost wholly concerned with Bill. One contained a pressed flower
and the information that Mary had begun French. Pete examined
the flower suspiciously.

"Don't say which youngster it's for," he observed.

We divided its petals among five.

Nevertheless, we maintained the household. Angelica received a new face,
painted by the station agent, Pete's hand having grown uncertain.
Ebenezer's split was bound in brass. Jake fashioned Emmeline a
traveling cradle from a cigar box. Doc replaced Silas's escaping
buckshot with dried peas, declaring that age demanded a lighter
diet. Hank polished Misery until her blank iron features reflected
the forge fire.

When word came that Mary would return in June, our
preparations acquired the character of a public festival. Bill bought
a new coat and wore it three days beforehand to
break its stiffness. Mrs. Ricketts baked a cake. Pete washed
Ebenezer with soap, thereby removing most of his face and
necessitating a hurried restoration. Emmeline received fresh flannel; Angelica, a
bonnet; Silas, a blue sash. Hank tied a bit of
white ribbon around Misery's iron neck.

We met at the station an hour early. The five
children were arranged upon a baggage truck, and I have
a very vivid recollection of their appearance in the mountain
sunlight—absurd, battered, expectant; a flour-sack lady, two mutilated ninepins, a
rattling stone-headed infant, and an iron-faced monster shining beneath bridal
ribbon. Behind them stood the men who had guarded them
for three years, each affecting an interest in the rails,
the timetable, or the weather.

The train came around the curve.

Bill moved forward. We followed with the truck.

A young lady descended.

She was tall—or seemed tall to us—slender, gloved, and dressed
in gray. Her hair was arranged smoothly beneath a fashionable
hat. Her face, though finer and paler, was Mary's; yet
it held an acquired composure which checked us more surely
than unrecognition. A nun handed down her valise. Mary kissed
Bill, first upon one cheek and then the other, and
laughed at his new coat.

Then she saw us.

"How kind of you all to come!" she said.

Her voice was sweet, distinct, and strangely without the road
in it.

Pete stepped aside. The baggage truck was directly behind him.
For one dangerous instant Angelica's wandering eye, Ebenezer's fresh charcoal
features, and Misery's polished head confronted the young lady across
the platform.

Mary looked puzzled.

"What have you there?" she asked.

It was Jake who saved us. With a movement unexpectedly
quick in so old a man, he flung his horse
blanket over the truck.

"Freight," he said.

"Fragile freight," added Doc Briggs.

Mary smiled politely. "How very careful you are!"

That settled it.

While Bill led her toward the wagon, talking in a
low, proud voice, we wheeled the covered family behind the
station. No agreement was spoken. Mrs. Ricketts took Angelica into
her basket; Pete put Ebenezer inside his coat; Jake thrust
Emmeline into the saddlebag; Doc closed Silas among his instruments.
Hank held Misery last.

The white ribbon had slipped, and he retied it with
his great burned fingers.

"Reckon she'd laugh at this un," he said.

"No," I answered, though I was less certain than I
pretended. "She wouldn't laugh."

Hank looked toward the road, where the gray figure walked
beside Bill under the tall pines.

"Mebbe not," he said. "That's worse."

We did not take the children to the welcome supper.
They never again assembled on Fridays at the Halfway House.
Angelica remained in Mrs. Ricketts's attic; Ebenezer stood for years
behind Pete's bar, discreetly screened by bottles; Emmeline rode in
Jake's saddlebag until he died; Silas occupied a drawer in
Doc's office; and Misery sat above Hank's forge, her iron
face glowing whenever the fire was stirred.

Mary stayed with Bill through the summer. She was gentle,
accomplished, and attentive. She sang hymns at the new church,
read French beneath the pines, and once spoke tenderly of
her "odd little childhood." Then she went to Sacramento and
afterward married, I believe, a lawyer.

We had welcomed Mary Foulkes home. But the little mother
for whom five impossible children had lived—and through whom we
had briefly become better men—never came back.

Sergeant Morton had been angry since sunrise. The road was
bad; the heat had arrived early; the lieutenant had given
the order with the mild voice of a man who
would not have to obey it.

"Take Flagler and fourteen men," the lieutenant had said. "Occupy
the house beyond the orchards. Hold until relieved."

Until relieved! It was one of those expressions by which
the army concealed its ignorance. Morton had saluted and marched
away, thinking of headquarters under the poplars, of coffee boiling
there, and of the colonel's clean boots.

Now the detachment moved along a chalky lane between stone
walls. The men's packs bobbed in dreary rhythm. Dust whitened
their trousers. Behind Morton, Corporal Flagler marched with his eyes
half shut, as if sleep were a curtain he could
draw between himself and military duty.

Morton turned suddenly.

"Davis! Cooney! What have you got under your shirts?"

The two privates stopped. Their blouses bulged with green and
yellow knobs. Davis looked afflicted by a monstrous disease.

"Nothing, Sergeant."

"Nothing? You've both grown tumors since breakfast, have you?"

Three pears escaped Cooney's shirt and struck the road. One
rolled to Morton's boot. The column watched it with profound
interest.

"Stealing from civilians," said Morton. "That's what the army's come
to. Give a man a rifle and he thinks every
hen, pear, bed sheet, and brass candlestick belongs to him."

"We was hungry," Davis said.

"You were hungry yesterday. You'll be hungry tomorrow. Must the
whole country be eaten because your belly can't understand discipline?"

Cooney began removing fruit from beneath his blouse. The bumps
diminished one by one. He placed eleven pears on the
wall with an injured air.

"Throw them back."

"There ain't nobody there, Sergeant."

"Throw them back anyway."

The pears sailed into the orchard. One struck a branch
and burst in a pale splash. The men grinned. Morton
saw them and grew angrier.

"Fine soldiers. Fourteen thieves, one sleeping corporal, and me. If
the enemy sees us, he'll die laughing."

"Fifteen thieves," murmured someone.

"What?"

"Nothing, Sergeant."

Flagler opened his eyes. "Best detail in the regiment, Sergeant,"
he said loyally.

Morton stared at him. Flagler had agreed with every complaint
for six months, sometimes before hearing it.

"You'd say so if they gave me fourteen goats."

"Yes, Sergeant. Fine animals, goats."

The house stood alone on a low rise beyond the
orchard. It was square, built of grey stone, with a
red roof and green shutters. White flowers leaned against one
wall, and bees travelled among them in sober employment. A
narrow road passed the front door before dropping toward a
bridge. Beyond the bridge stretched fields, yellow and green, then
a dark wooded ridge.

Morton inspected the place with the grave displeasure of an
engineer discovering that civilians had built it without consulting him.
There were four rooms below, four above, a cellar, a
well behind the kitchen, and windows commanding the road and
fields.

"Not bad," he admitted.

The men entered and released themselves from their packs. Belts,
blankets, and cartridge boxes fell in heaps. Flagler selected a
bare room upstairs, put his pack beneath his head, and
went immediately to sleep in a rectangle of sunshine.

Morton found him ten minutes later.

"Corporal!"

Flagler sat up. A cartridge belt had wound itself around
his ankle.

"Sergeant?"

"I suppose you expect the enemy to knock before entering?"

"No, Sergeant."

"Then post two men on the roof, two at the
back, four below. Loop the shutters. Block the doors with
furniture. Move!"

The house awakened to banging and complaint. A wardrobe came
down the stairs like a drunk and crushed a chair.
Men dragged a table against the front entrance. Johnston discovered
a pipe and smoked while carrying mattresses. Patterson broke holes
in the plaster beside the upper windows, each aperture becoming
a pale, ragged eye.

Morton passed from room to room. His temper improved as
the house grew ugly. He ordered ammunition stacked in corners,
water carried from the well, blankets hung where sunlight might
betray movement. By noon the peaceful dwelling had acquired the
hostile expression of a clenched fist.

"There," he said to Flagler. "A company couldn't take it."

"No, Sergeant."

"A battalion would have trouble."

"Yes, Sergeant."

Morton gazed across the fields. Far away, on the wooded
ridge, something moved and vanished.

"With proper support," he said, "this position is nearly impregnable."

Below, Davis had found another pear in his pocket. He
cut it into slices with his bayonet and shared it
among six men.

For an hour nothing happened. The bees continued. Heat lay
on the roof. From the upper room came Flagler's breathing,
regular and offensive. A soldier began telling how his brother
had married a woman who could lift a flour barrel.
No one believed him, but the details grew elaborate.

Patterson, watching through a loophole, saw a tiny figure cross
a gap near the ridge. At that distance it resembled
a needle walking upright across a ballroom floor.

"Sergeant."

Morton came beside him.

"Movement yonder."

The figure was gone. Morton watched until his eye watered.

"Probably a farmer."

"Carrying a rifle?"

"Farmers carry things."

The first bullet struck the shutters between their faces. It
made a flat crack and threw splinters into Patterson's mouth.

Morton dropped. Patterson remained standing for a moment, astonished, then
folded himself beneath the window.

From the ridge came a faint, scattered popping. Bullets arrived
at once, humming over the roof, spitting stone from the
walls, whining through rooms. One entered the house, struck a
kettle in the kitchen, and sent it ringing across the
floor.

"Posts!" Morton shouted. "Flagler, get those men below! Johnston, back
window! Nobody fires without a target!"

The soldiers scrambled. Their equipment seized them. A sling caught
on a nail; two men collided in the passage; Davis
crawled behind the wardrobe and discovered Cooney already there.

"Out!" Morton roared. "You can hide after you're dead."

Men reached windows and loopholes. Rifles thrust into daylight. The
firing outside thickened, a swarm of metallic insects searching the
house.

"See them now," Patterson said.

Little figures slipped from the trees into the fields.

"Four hundred yards," said Morton. "Independent fire. Take your time."

The house answered. Smoke jumped from its cracks and windows.
The first volley had a ragged sound, but the second
was steadier. A distant figure turned halfway around and fell.
This proof produced a moment of silence among the defenders.

"You see?" Morton said. "They're men. Nothing else."

The enemy advanced in small groups, kneeling, running, disappearing into
folds of ground. Their bullets sang against the stone. The
room filled with the smell of burnt paper, hot oil,
and holiday firecrackers.

Johnston fired rapidly from the rear window.

"Slow down," Flagler told him.

"They're coming through the orchard."

"They're not."

"They are. I saw them."

"You saw leaves."

Johnston looked again. A bullet entered above his left eye.
His head snapped back, and his body hung across the
sill with both arms outside, as if he had become
tired while climbing in.

Flagler pulled him down.

"When was Johnston hit?" Morton demanded from the doorway.

"Just now."

"When?"

"Just now, Sergeant."

"I know it was just now. Get him out of
that window."

"He's dead."

"Then he can't help us there."

They dragged Johnston aside. Three more bullets passed through his
coat afterward by chance. Each made the cloth twitch.

A cry came from below. Morton ran down. Davis sat
against the wall, holding his neck. Blood flowed between his
fingers and vanished beneath his collar.

"I'm sorry, Sergeant," he said.

"What for?"

"Getting hit."

"Don't be a fool."

"I wasn't leaning out much."

"You leaned enough."

Morton tore open the collar. The wound was bad but
not immediately fatal. He packed it with cloth and called
for Cooney.

"Stay with him."

"But my post—"

"I know your post. When he stops bleeding, go back
to it. If he stops breathing, go back sooner."

The words sounded brutal, and they comforted Cooney more than
tenderness would have done.

The firing continued. Plaster dust floated through the rooms. It
settled on faces and made the men look old. Somewhere
a clock, unnoticed until then, ticked with enormous composure.

An hour passed, though to the men it seemed either
ten minutes or a day. The enemy reached the orchard
wall. Their heads appeared above it and vanished under the
defenders' fire. Morton moved continually, correcting aim, distributing cartridges, striking
rifles aside when men fired blindly.

"Make every shot useful! You're not throwing gravel at dogs."

Patterson left his loophole and crouched in the passage.

"I can't go back in there," he said.

Morton stopped.

Patterson pointed toward the front room. "It's death in there."

The room beyond was full of yellow smoke. A dead
soldier lay beside the window. Blood from his shoulder had
spread under him, and someone's boot had crossed it, leaving
a greasy red streak toward the door.

"It's death everywhere," Morton said.

"I can't."

Morton seized Patterson's collar and pushed him into the room.
For one instant Patterson hated the sergeant more intensely than
he feared the bullets. That hatred carried him to the
loophole. He fired, worked the bolt, and fired again.

"Good," Morton said, and moved on.

No message came from the regiment. No gun sounded behind
them. Morton twice sent a man to the rear attic
to look for signals. The second time the man returned
with a bullet through his forearm.

"Nothing, Sergeant. Road's empty."

"They'll come."

Flagler glanced at him but did not agree. His automatic
loyalty had at last encountered a statement too large for
it.

By midafternoon nine men could still fire. The house no
longer seemed large. Bodies occupied the useful spaces; wounded men
crawled into corners and made small private noises. Empty cartridges
rolled underfoot. The walls sweated smoke.

Outside, the fields remained brilliant. Clouds moved over them with
white, slow dignity. A bird settled on the orchard wall,
considered the battle, and flew elsewhere.

The enemy rushed from the trees.

"Now!" Morton cried.

Every rifle in the house spoke. Men fired from windows,
from broken shutters, from holes near the floor. Flagler stood
on the stairs directing two positions at once. Cooney laughed
each time his rifle kicked, not because he was amused
but because some valve in him had failed.

The charge broke. Figures ran back or lay still among
the grass.

"We can hold this place," Morton said.

No one answered.

"We can hold it!"

"Yes, Sergeant," Flagler said faintly.

A bullet struck Flagler in the stomach. He sat down
on the stair with great care.

Morton looked at him. "Bad?"

"I think so."

"Can you shoot?"

"From here."

"Then cover the door."

Flagler rested his rifle across his knees. "Fine position, Sergeant."

Morton almost smiled.

The next attack came from three sides. Bullets beat the
walls like thrown grain. Stones chipped inward. A grenade burst
near the kitchen, blowing the barricade apart and filling the
lower floor with black smoke. Two soldiers ran for the
rear.

Morton met them in the passage.

"Back!"

"They're in the yard!"

"Then you won't have far to shoot."

One man obeyed. The other pressed himself against the wall,
staring. Morton struck him once across the face. The man
blinked, raised his rifle, and returned to the kitchen.

Morton felt a savage pride. These were the thieves, sleepers,
liars, and bellyachers he had cursed on the road. Now
they moved when he shouted. They fired though their hands
shook. They stepped over friends without looking down. He had
believed discipline was something ruined by officers and evaded by
men. Here, in this wrecked house, it seemed to be
merely the distance between a command and obedience, and sometimes
that distance was as thin as a breath.

Still no regiment appeared.

Near sunset only five defenders remained unwounded. Patterson was killed
at his loophole, falling backward with an expression of mild
surprise. Cooney, trying to drag ammunition upstairs, was shot through
the thigh. Davis had ceased breathing long before, but no
one had noticed the moment.

Morton took a rifle and fired from the front room.
Enemy soldiers were crossing the road. They seemed very close.
He could distinguish belts, faces, and one man's red beard.

"We've got them!" he shouted.

A bullet entered beneath his ribs.

He stepped back, annoyed, as if someone had interrupted him.
His rifle slipped. He put both hands against the wound,
then looked at the blood with disbelief. All day he
had considered bullets as objects to be directed, avoided, counted,
and answered. This one had behaved without discipline.

He sank beside the wall.

The remaining men gathered near him, except Flagler, who was
dead on the stair with his rifle still across his
knees.

"Hold the windows," Morton said.

"Sergeant—"

"Hold them."

The room darkened. The firing sounded far away, then close
enough to touch. Morton thought of the regiment on the
road, ranks advancing, flags above the dust. Perhaps they were
coming. Perhaps they had always been coming. The army, having
misplaced him, would surely discover him at the proper dramatic
instant.

He lifted his head.

"Forward, regiment," he said. "Come on, then."

His head dropped.

The last shots from the house were fired after dusk.
Then there was silence.

Enemy troops approached cautiously, spreading through the orchard and along
the walls. They threw two grenades through the broken windows
before entering. The explosions disturbed only the dead and caused
one wounded man to groan.

A young enemy officer stepped through the kitchen. He covered
his mouth against the smoke. His boots slid among cartridge
cases. In each room he found bodies, blood, shattered furniture,
and rifles so hot that some still smelled of scorched
wood.

"How many?" he asked.

A soldier counted twice.

"Sixteen altogether, sir. Including the sergeant."

The officer looked around the ruined house. He had watched
its fire command the road, the orchard, and the fields
through the whole afternoon. He had seen two assaults collapse
before it and had requested another company for the final
attack.

"Sixteen?"

"Yes, sir."

The officer stared at Morton's body beneath the window.

"We thought," he said, "there were at least a hundred."

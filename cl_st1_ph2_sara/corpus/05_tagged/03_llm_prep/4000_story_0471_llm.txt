### McTurkle and the Crisis

The year we discovered McTurkle was the year Harvard's football
team came apart in sections.

I don't mean morally. There were no scandals worth mentioning,
and nobody was expelled except a substitute tackle who had
been intending to leave anyway. I mean that the team
came apart physically. Bixby twisted a knee against Brown, Potter
dislocated a shoulder in practice, and somebody stepped on Peabody's
hand when he was picking himself up after a perfectly
friendly scrimmage. Then our fullback caught a cold which worked
downward and became bronchitis, and our best end was struck
by a bicycle in the Yard. By the middle of
November the training table looked like a hospital banquet, and
the men who could still walk were being preserved under
glass for Yale.

The newspapers tried to make the best of it. They
said the spirit of the team was unconquerable. This generally
means that the team itself has been conquered three times
and is expected to make it four.

Yale, meanwhile, was eating raw meat and practising in secret.
At least that was the report. Their line averaged somewhere
between two hundred pounds and a freight locomotive, and they
had a halfback named Doolittle who had run through Princeton
as if Princeton were painted on a curtain. One Cambridge
paper printed a diagram showing how Harvard might win. It
involved a northwest gale, two Yale fumbles, and divine intervention.

Naturally, enthusiasm became a matter of Duty and Patriotism.

There were notices in all the dormitories commanding every Harvard
man to attend a monster pep meeting in the Union
on Thursday night. The captains of former teams were to
speak, and a celebrated graduate was coming from New York
to tell us that eleven men fighting for the Crimson
could not be beaten. He had told us the same
thing the year before, just before they were.

We were ready, though. We had been holding enthusiasm in
reserve, and by Thursday evening it was a drug on
the market. Men came pouring across the Yard with red
flags, megaphones, canes, bells, and one brass cuspidor which had
been borrowed from an eating club and was never adequately
explained. There must have been two thousand of us. Maybe
there were five thousand. When you are in the middle
of a crowd, every man counts as three, especially if
he is standing on your foot.

Bud Hanley and I arrived at the Union at seven-forty,
full of college spirit and pie. The doors were locked.

On the steps stood a committee man in a derby
hat. He raised both hands.

"Fellows!" he cried.

We cheered.

"Fellows, I have an announcement."

We cheered the announcement.

"Owing to circumstances beyond the control of the committee—"

That sounded like Yale influence, and somebody hissed.

"—the speakers have been unable to reach Cambridge, and the
meeting has therefore been postponed."

There was a silence. It rolled backward through the crowd
like a cold wave. We had brought all that enthusiasm
out in the open, and now there was nowhere to
check it. You can't tell two thousand Harvard men at
eight o'clock at night to take their patriotism home and
put it to bed.

A sophomore shouted, "To the Yard!"

So we went to the Yard.

I never knew who led us. Probably nobody. A crowd
can grow legs of its own when it gets excited.
We swung through the gate, trampled three flower beds that
weren't doing much at that season, and began to sing.
We sang "Fair Harvard" until it became unfair to Harvard,
and then "Up the Street," and then a song about
Eli which had never received faculty approval. Windows went up
all along the dormitories. Men leaned out in pajamas and
asked what had happened.

"Yale!" we yelled.

That was enough. They came down.

The procession took on men at every entry. It gathered
freshmen, seniors, law students, a graduate student in slippers, and
a young instructor who joined us to restore order and
was last seen waving a wastebasket over his head. We
marched around the Yard twice and through Harvard Square once,
tying up the streetcars and terrifying a horse belonging to
the Adams House stable. The horse entered into the spirit
of the occasion and took a milk wagon halfway to
Watertown.

But something was wrong.

"We need a band," Bud said.

That was it. A procession without a band is merely
a large number of people going in the same direction.
We had voices, bells, the cuspidor, and a fellow who
could whistle through his fingers like escaping steam; but we
lacked music in the formal sense.

"Where do you get a band at eight-thirty Thursday night?"
I asked.

Bud stopped so suddenly that six men climbed over him.

"McTurkle," he said.

I knew McTurkle slightly. Everybody knew him slightly. He lived
on the top floor of Hollis and had been there,
so far as we could discover, since the administration of
President Eliot. He was thin and long-legged, with pale hair,
enormous spectacles, and a way of blinking as if the
world were written in very small print. He was a
prodigious grind. He studied Romance languages, comparative literature, medieval philosophy,
and a few other subjects which had been discontinued on
account of difficulty.

Also he played the French horn.

Why a French horn is called French I don't know.
Possibly because no American would admit responsibility. It is a
circular brass instrument into which the performer blows a note
at one end, after which the note travels around and
around, gets balled up and confused, and comes out somewhere
else. It resembles a French verb. McTurkle practised every evening,
and men passing beneath his window used to remove their
hats because they thought something was dying.

"He's our band," Bud said.

"McTurkle is one man."

"So was Napoleon."

This seemed to settle it.

We scooted ahead by a side route while the procession
circled Memorial Hall. There were five of us by then—Bud,
Pete Henderson, Little Jones, a man called Sibley whom nobody
remembered inviting, and I. We dashed up the stairs in
Hollis and pounded on McTurkle's door.

After a while it opened four inches, and McTurkle looked
out.

He wore a green-and-purple plaid lounging coat, and his spectacles
magnified his eyes until he looked like a startled owl.
Behind him were books—books on the table, books on the
chairs, books in heaps on the floor—and in the centre
of the room, shining beneath the gas, lay the French
horn.

"Ah," he said. "Good evening."

"McTurkle," Bud said solemnly, "Harvard needs you."

McTurkle blinked.

"I beg your pardon?"

Bud stepped inside. We followed, although McTurkle hadn't exactly asked
us.

"Outside that window," Bud said, pointing toward the noise in
the Yard, "the university is assembling."

A cheer rose below and shook dust from the ceiling.

"I had observed," McTurkle said, "a certain unusual animation."

"It is more than animation. It is a crisis."

"A crisis?"

"The eve of battle has arrived. Tomorrow our representatives go
forth to meet Yale upon the scene of conflict. The
hour is dark. The odds are desperate. Our crippled warriors
need to know that Harvard stands behind them as one
man."

McTurkle looked at the five of us.

"As—ah—one man?"

"Figuratively," Bud said. "At present Harvard stands behind them as
about three thousand men, and more are coming out of
Thayer every minute. But they have no music."

McTurkle glanced at his horn.

Bud saw it and lowered his voice.

"You possess a gift, McTurkle. Perhaps you have sometimes wondered
why Providence placed that gift in your keeping."

McTurkle admitted that he had not.

"Tonight," Bud continued, "you know. There are moments in the
history of great institutions when scholarship must clasp hands with
action. When Art must leave her cloistered chamber, buckle on
her sword, and march beneath flaming pinions."

Pete Henderson made a choking sound, but turned it into
a cough.

"I had intended," McTurkle said, "to finish a monograph on
irregular Provençal participles."

"Will Provençal participles beat Yale?"

McTurkle considered this seriously.

"Not in any direct sense, I suppose."

"Exactly. We aren't asking this for ourselves. We ask it
for Harvard. Come out and lead us."

"Lead you?"

"With your horn."

At that instant the procession came beneath the window.

"We want a band!" someone shouted.

The cry spread.

"A band! A band! We want a band!"

Bud threw open the window and leaned out.

"Fellows!" he roared. "Harvard has a band!"

The answer hit us like a cannon.

McTurkle turned pink, then red, then a noble purple which
matched his lounging coat. For four years men had passed
him without seeing him, unless they wanted his notes from
a lecture. Now thousands were demanding him. He picked up
the horn with both hands.

"If you believe," he said, swallowing, "that my participation would
be of material benefit—"

Bud seized his hand.

"McTurkle, future generations will bless you."

"I should prefer," McTurkle said, "that they refrain from any
exaggerated estimate."

We got him downstairs.

When he appeared in the doorway the cheering was terrific.
It ran from the front of the procession to the
rear, and then ran back to make certain it hadn't
missed anybody. Men who had never heard of McTurkle shouted
his name as if he had just scored against Yale.

"Mc-Tur-kle! Mc-Tur-kle! Mc-Tur-kle!"

He stood on the steps, blinking at the multitude, his
long legs protruding below the plaid coat and the horn
clasped to his bosom. He looked frightened and happy and
about nine feet tall.

McTurkle was transformed.

Bud raised an arm for silence.

"Fellows, this is McTurkle—Harvard's band!"

We cheered.

"Play!" came a voice.

"What shall I render?" McTurkle asked Bud.

"Anything. Something stirring."

McTurkle thought. Then he put the horn to his lips
and played "Annie Laurie."

At least he said afterward that it was "Annie Laurie."
The first note was low and thoughtful, the second climbed
two flights of stairs, and the third went off toward
Somerville by itself. McTurkle pursued it bravely. He achieved notes
which had not been included in the original composition and
several which had not previously existed. By the time he
reached the refrain, Annie Laurie had received her final death
blow.

The crowd loved it.

We sang. Not "Annie Laurie," because we couldn't locate it,
but a football song in approximately the same key. Then
we started marching.

McTurkle went first, with Bud beside him to indicate the
route. Behind them came the cuspidor, beaten with a cane,
then the finger-whistler, then the entire university. We crossed the
Square, wheeled down toward the river, and headed for the
Stadium, though I don't believe anybody had proposed this. McTurkle
played continuously. He played a German student song, "Home, Sweet
Home," a portion of something by Wagner, and a scale
which lasted from Quincy Street to the bridge.

The French horn is not adapted to marching. McTurkle had
to look over it, around it, and occasionally through it.
His glasses clouded, his coat flapped, and his long legs
moved like a pair of compasses; but he never faltered.
Whenever he paused for breath the crowd yelled, "More!" and
he gave us more. He must have covered fourteen octaves
that night, several of them twice.

At the bridge the police tried to stop us.

"Where are you going?" an officer demanded.

"To the scene of the conflict!" Bud replied.

"What conflict?"

Bud pointed grandly toward the Stadium.

The officer looked at McTurkle, who was playing what may
have been "The Marseillaise," and then at the three thousand
men behind him.

"Go on," he said. "Only go on."

We entered the Stadium through a gate somebody had neglected
to lock. The great concrete curves were black against the
sky, and our footsteps and shouting rolled around the empty
seats like artillery. We marched once about the field. McTurkle
led us over the goal line, down the side, and
directly across a patch of fresh turf which the athletic
management had been nursing for two weeks. Nobody noticed until
afterward.

Then we grouped in the stands and sang every song
we knew. We cheered the team, the captain, the coach,
the injured substitutes, the college, the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, and
McTurkle. Particularly McTurkle.

"Speech!" someone cried.

"Speech! Speech! McTurkle!"

He tried to hide behind his horn, but there was
not enough horn in the right places.

Bud conducted him to the middle of the field and
stood him on a trainer's box. The crowd quieted by
degrees.

McTurkle removed his spectacles, polished them, replaced them, and looked
at us. His expression was solemn.

"Gentlemen," he began, "this manifestation is—ah—unexpected."

"A-a-aye!" yelled the crowd.

"I had not been aware until this evening that my
musical studies possessed a practical relation to the collective life
of the university."

Terrific applause.

"I am deeply sensible of the confidence which you have
reposed in me, although I am perhaps insufficiently informed concerning
the precise nature of tomorrow's undertaking."

"Yale!" we shouted.

"Yes," said McTurkle. "Yale. I understand that a contest is
to occur between representative groups of the two institutions."

"Right!"

"And I am informed that Harvard's prospects are regarded as—ah—not
wholly satisfactory."

Groans, cries of "We'll lick 'em!" and a prolonged blast
from the cuspidor.

McTurkle raised one hand.

"Nevertheless, history teaches us that the probable is not invariably
the actual. Hannibal crossed the Alps under circumstances of considerable
inconvenience. The Greeks at Marathon were numerically inferior. Roland at
Roncesvalles—though defeated—established a tradition of enduring literary value."

We cheered Roland.

"Therefore, gentlemen, I venture to express the hope that our
players will acquit themselves with resolution, whether at the bat
or in the field—"

There was a pause.

"At the what?" somebody called.

McTurkle blinked.

"Is it not baseball?"

The Stadium exploded.

Men fell over benches. Bud sat down on the turf.
The cuspidor rolled unattended to the thirty-yard line. McTurkle looked
from face to face, distressed but dignified.

"I perceive," he said, when the noise had subsided a
little, "that I have made an error of classification."

"It's football!" we roared.

"Ah. Football. Naturally." He adjusted his spectacles. "In that event,
certain portions of my analogy may require modification. The fundamental
principle, however, remains intact. Harvard should endeavor to secure a
larger total of points than Yale."

That brought us up standing. It was the clearest statement
of policy we had heard all season.

McTurkle lifted the horn.

"Gentlemen," he said, "I thank you."

Then he played "Annie Laurie" again, and we sang "Fair
Harvard" to it, and this time the two tunes met
briefly in the middle before separating forever.

We marched back at eleven. Or midnight. I forget which.
By then there were rumors that the team had heard
us from its training quarters and been inspired beyond measure.
There were also rumors that the team had called the
police, but we rejected those as Yale propaganda.

At the Yard gate we hoisted McTurkle onto our shoulders.
This was difficult because of his legs and dangerous because
of the horn, but Duty carried us through. We bore
him to Hollis while everybody chanted his name. He bowed
from the doorway, plaid coat, spectacles, horn and all.

"Good night, gentlemen," he called. "I trust that the athletic
event will terminate favorably."

Then he went upstairs to his participles.

This is where the story ought to have a climax.
McTurkle should have appeared at the game in a crimson
cloak, played the team to victory, and been elected president
of the class. But life around Harvard was not arranged
by dramatists that year.

The next morning I met him in the Square carrying
six books.

"See you at the game," I said.

"I fear not," he replied.

"Not going?"

"I have lost an evening's work, and there is also
a difficult passage for the horn which I must master
before Monday."

"But McTurkle, Harvard needs you!"

He looked pleased, though he shook his head.

"I believe my function was preliminary," he said. "The actual
contest, as I now comprehend it, is conducted without musical
accompaniment."

Then he moseyed toward the Library.

Yale beat us twenty-eight to nothing. Doolittle ran through our
line like Sam Hill, Potter's shoulder came loose again, and
Peabody was stepped on in the other hand. Our spirit
remained unconquerable, but that's about all that remained.

Still, I've heard men blame the coaches, the weather, the
injuries, the signals, and the northwest gale which failed to
arrive. I have even heard it said that the demonstration
kept the team awake.

But nobody can blame McTurkle.

He never attended the game.

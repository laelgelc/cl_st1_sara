At five o'clock, when the sun descends toward the Esterel
and the shadows of the palms lengthen across the promenade,
Madame de Parville comes down to the sea.

She walks slowly, with the careful grace of persons whose
strength must be counted and distributed among the hours. A
white shawl covers her shoulders; one hand gathers it against
her breast, while the other trails along the stone balustrade.
Her face has the transparent pallor of wax, and upon
each cheek there is a little fixed colour, too brilliant
to be natural. Yet she smiles.

Before her, the Mediterranean stretches immense and blue, a blue
so pure that it seems not a colour but a
happiness. The sails far out are motionless. Along the shore,
villas gleam among dark pines like patches of snow that
have somehow become warm; orange trees lift their glossy leaves
above garden walls; the air carries the mingled perfume of
salt, flowers, dust, and sun-warmed foliage. Everywhere there is light—on
the windows, on the pale façades, on the ripples, on
the wings of the gulls. Even the mountains, violet in
the distance, appear soft and hospitable.

Madame de Parville sits on a bench and looks.

She looks at the fishermen drawing in a net. She
looks at two English children rolling hoops beneath the palms.
She looks at the red disk of the sun and
at the long golden road it lays upon the water.
Her breathing is short, and now and then a dry
cough shakes her. But she is content. The physician told
her that morning, with lowered eyes and phrases made vague
by compassion, that the left lung was almost destroyed and
that the right was becoming affected.

She had understood.

She will be no more.

Not after many years, not after the slow passage from
youth to age, but soon—perhaps before the orange blossom falls,
perhaps before the first great heat drives the strangers away.
This thought, which should have terrified her, has given her
peace.

For if she dies here, she will not return to
Normandy.

She had been born in Paris, in a small apartment
looking upon a noisy street near the Madeleine. Her father,
a clerk in a ministry, possessed few resources and many
respectable connections. Her mother died when she was seventeen, leaving
her no fortune except some linen, two bracelets, and a
timid conviction that marriage was the only event by which
a woman's life might be secured.

At twenty-two she married Henry de Parville.

He was thirty-eight, broad-shouldered, red-cheeked, and wealthy enough to be
called an excellent match. His manners in Paris had seemed
almost amusing in their provincial bluntness. He laughed loudly, ate
heartily, and spoke with affection of his lands, his tenants,
his horses, his dogs, and a château in Normandy which
had belonged to the Parvilles since the reign of Louis
XIII. He was not coarse; he was merely certain. He
knew what was good, what was proper, what was sufficient.
He had never doubted himself, because nothing within him had
ever contradicted anything else.

She did not love him. But she had been taught
that affection came after marriage, as furniture comes after the
lease is signed. He appeared honest and kind. He brought
flowers, though he forgot whether she preferred roses or violets,
and when he spoke of the château he called it
"our home" with a warm emphasis that touched her.

They arrived there in June.

It was summer.

The road wound between high hedges heavy with leaves. Apple
trees covered the slopes; cattle stood knee-deep in grass; red
poppies burned in the fields. The château appeared at last
above a sheet of green water, its gray walls half
hidden by elms. Swallows flew beneath the eaves, and the
tall roof rose against the sky like a knife blade.

The house was enormous. It contained galleries that led to
locked rooms, stairways worn into hollows by vanished feet, portraits
blackened by smoke, and furniture which no one had moved
for a hundred years. Madame de Parville was at first
delighted by its age. She opened cabinets, inspected tapestries, laughed
at the stiff ladies in faded satin, and leaned from
the mullioned windows to watch Henry return from the fields.

He was pleased by her pleasure.

"You see," he said, "one breathes here. Paris is fit
only for invalids and foreigners."

During those first weeks she walked in the garden, read
beneath the lime trees, and arranged flowers in the cold
drawing-room without yet noticing that it was cold. Henry left
after breakfast to examine a roof, judge a horse, visit
a tenant, or shoot some creature whose destruction appeared necessary
to the proper ordering of the estate. He returned hungry,
mud on his boots, followed by dogs.

He loved his dogs with an open tenderness. He rubbed
their ears, called them fools and beauties, forgave their wet
paws upon the carpet, and worried if one refused its
food. His wife watched him sometimes with a faint astonishment.
She did not envy the animals precisely. Still, she wondered
whether he would ever observe, without being told, that she
too was silent, cold, or weary.

The autumn came.

Rain fell for days together. The garden darkened; leaves decayed
in the paths; the pond became the colour of lead.
Damp spread over the walls in irregular stains. The corridors
smelled of stone, old cloth, and extinguished fires. All along
the façade the wind searched the windows, found their cracks,
and entered in thin, crafty currents of air.

Madame de Parville began to shiver.

At dinner she kept her shawl around her shoulders. In
the drawing-room she sat so close to the hearth that
the heat burned one side of her face while her
back remained cold. The fire was large, but the room
was larger. Its warmth rose uselessly toward the carved ceiling
and disappeared.

"Henry," she said one evening, "could we not have a
furnace?"

He looked up from a hunting journal.

"A furnace?"

"For the house. A proper one. The air is so
damp."

He laughed, not unkindly.

"My dear child, this house has stood three centuries without
one."

She waited, supposing this to be the beginning of his
answer. It was the whole of it.

"But I am cold," she said.

"You sit too much. Walk. Ride. The blood must circulate."

"I do walk."

"Not enough."

He returned to his journal.

She tried walking. She crossed the fields in boots which
the mud seized at every step. Rain gathered on her
veil and ran beneath her collar. Crows rose from the
ploughed land in a black disturbance, wheeled above her, and
settled again behind her as though closing a door. She
came home wet, exhausted, and colder than before.

The winter came.

Snow covered the park, erased the paths, buried the shrubs,
and lay upon the roof in a white mass beneath
which the château seemed to crouch. At night the timbers
cracked. Doors stirred in their frames. The cold did not
remain outside; it inhabited the rooms. It hid under carpets,
descended the chimneys, slipped between the bed curtains, waited in
the sheets. The walls themselves gave off a frozen hatred
accumulated through centuries of Norman winters.

She asked to go to Paris.

"For a month," she said. "Only a month, Henry. We
might return in February."

"Paris in January! Fog, dirt, receptions, expenses. What would we
do there?"

"I should see my friends."

"What friends? Those little women who chatter until midnight? You
have everything here."

She looked around the dining-room: the ancestral silver, the dark
portraits, the high windows shuddering under the wind.

"Yes," she said. "Everything."

He believed her.

He did not understand. When she explained that her feet
remained cold for hours, he offered thicker stockings. When she
complained of the draughts, he ordered a servant to place
a rolled rug beneath the door. When she wept one
morning because ice had formed inside her window, he kissed
her forehead and told her not to be childish. He
was neither angry nor malicious. He simply could not imagine
that what was endurable to Henry de Parville might be
torture to another.

Again she spoke of the furnace.

"It would spoil the walls," he said. "Besides, the expense
would be absurd. We have twelve fireplaces."

"Twelve fires cannot warm twelve great rooms."

"Then remain in one."

She did remain in one. She sat beside the drawing-room
hearth with a blanket over her knees, listening to the
dogs bark in the courtyard and the rain strike the
panes. Hours passed. Nothing happened. The next day resembled the
day before; the next week resembled the week before. She
was twenty-three.

Was this her life? These stones, these crows, these dripping
branches? Henry entering with ruddy cheeks, saying, "Cold? Nonsense! It
is splendid weather"? The endless winters, each waiting behind the
brief green summers? Nothing else?

Nothing.

Her timidity, which prevented argument, did not prevent thought. On
the contrary, her unspoken wishes turned slowly into fixed ideas.
The furnace became more than a machine of pipes and
iron. It became proof that she had been heard. If
Henry installed it, then her suffering would exist; if he
refused, she herself was somehow unreal.

One night, after dinner, he announced that the thermometer had
fallen twelve degrees.

"We shall have excellent shooting tomorrow," he said.

She was standing before the fire, warming her hands.

"Would you put in the furnace if I became ill?"

He laughed.

"You will not become ill. You have the constitution of
a kitten."

"But if I did?"

"My dear, one does not rebuild a château because a
young woman has a cold."

A young woman has a cold.

The phrase accompanied her upstairs. Her room was so chill
that her breath showed before her. The maid undressed her
quickly, placed heated bricks beneath the blankets, and left. Madame
de Parville lay listening to the silence. Henry slept in
the adjoining room. Beyond the windows, moonlight spread over the
snow.

Then the idea came to her, simple and complete.

If he needed an illness, she would give him one.

She rose. Her bare feet touched the boards and recoiled.
For a moment she hesitated, trembling already. Then she removed
her nightdress and put on only a thin chemise. She
opened the door with great care, passed along the corridor,
descended the servants' stair, and entered the unused breakfast room,
whose French window gave upon the terrace.

The bolts resisted. Her fingers, clumsy with cold and fear,
made a noise against the iron. She paused, hearing her
own heart. No one came.

The door opened.

Air struck her body like water. She gasped. The terrace,
the lawn, the trees, the entire world were white beneath
the moon. She stepped outside.

At the first touch of snow her feet seemed cut.
It was not merely cold; it was painful as a
wound. She nearly ran back. But she thought of the
drawing-room, the rolled rug beneath the door, Henry's laugh.

She went forward.

One step. Another. Another.

The snow reached her ankles. It adhered to her skin,
melted, and froze again. Her teeth knocked together. She crossed
the terrace and descended toward the lawn, holding her arms
away from her sides as though submitting to some grotesque
medical examination. Her chemise fluttered in the wind. She began
to cough.

"That is enough," she whispered.

Yet she stayed.

She stood beside a stone vase until she could no
longer feel her toes. She walked around the frozen basin.
She picked up handfuls of snow and pressed them against
her throat and breast. The act was childish, methodical, terrible.
She had no desire to die. She wanted only to
be believed.

When she returned, she could scarcely close the window. She
climbed the stairs on hands and knees, leaving small wet
marks behind her. In bed she shook so violently that
the wooden frame creaked.

By morning she had a fever.

Henry sent for the physician. During the first day he
remained cheerful.

"A chill," he said. "I told you to walk more."

On the third day the physician ceased answering him directly.
On the fifth, another physician arrived from Rouen. They spoke
in the corridor in low voices, and Henry, who feared
nothing he understood, grew terrified before their guarded looks.

It was pneumonia.

For three weeks she lay between fire and fever, sometimes
begging to be covered, sometimes tearing away the sheets. She
saw the black crows flying around the bed; she believed
snow was falling from the canopy; she cried that someone
must shut the windows although they were already sealed.

Henry sat near her, massive and helpless. He stroked the
dogs no longer. Once, when she became conscious, she heard
him speaking with the architect.

"A furnace," he said. "Yes, yes, in every room if
necessary. Begin at once."

She smiled.

That smile puzzled the nurse, who supposed it gratitude.

Madame de Parville recovered, if recovery may be said of
a body which rises from danger permanently diminished. Her fever
fell, but the cough remained. She could walk only a
few steps without breathlessness; one lung had been gravely injured.
The physicians ordered Cannes.

Henry accompanied her south and complained during the journey of
the railway food. At Cannes he rented a villa for
the season, chose the brightest rooms, and displayed a remorse
which expressed itself through expenditure. He bought cushions, shawls, medicines,
flowers. He questioned physicians, distrusted servants, and required the windows
to be closed whenever a breeze arose.

She forgave him. Perhaps she had never accused him. He
had installed the furnace.

Then, after six weeks, he returned to Normandy because a
steward had mismanaged a sale of timber. He left her
with a companion and promised to come in the spring.

At first her health improved.

How could it not? The sun entered her room each
morning like a friend. She breakfasted on the terrace. She
drove beside the sea, saw mimosas flowering in January, heard
fountains in gardens, and breathed air in which no ancient
dampness lurked. Her cheeks regained a little colour. Her appetite
returned. The physician praised her progress.

"You will be quite yourself again," he said.

That sentence filled her with horror.

Quite herself meant well. Well meant Normandy. Normandy meant the
low sky, the wet fields, the army of crows, the
snow pressing against the windows. It meant Henry saying, "The
house is warm now," as if warmth created after ruin
could restore what it had destroyed.

A letter came from him.

The furnace worked splendidly. The gallery was almost too warm.
He expected her home by October.

By October.

All day she thought of the words. At night she
dreamed of the château. She saw its roof cutting the
gray sky; she heard the draughts whisper behind the tapestries.
She woke with her hands at her throat.

The next morning she rose before her companion and went
into the garden in her nightclothes. The dawn was chilly.
Dew darkened her slippers. She remained beneath the trees until
she began to cough.

After that she became cunning.

Whenever the physician declared her stronger, she found some small
means of undoing his work. She left a window open
after sunset. She removed her shawl during a cold drive.
She sat in damp clothes. Once, during a week of
rain, she walked alone beside the harbour until her shoes
filled with water.

She must cough.

Without the cough they would send her back. Without the
pallor, the fever, the blood upon the handkerchief, there would
be no reason to let her remain beneath this blue
sky. Health was not deliverance. Health was the sentence.

The companion scolded her; the physician frowned; Henry wrote that
she must obey them both. None suspected the quiet determination
with which she preserved her illness. They believed her careless,
capricious, perhaps foolish. And she, who had never been able
to impose one wish upon another human being, imposed her
will upon her own flesh.

Month by month it yielded.

Now she sits beside the Mediterranean and knows she has
succeeded too well.

The sun touches the horizon. The golden road upon the
water narrows, reddens, and breaks among the waves. A warmth
remains in the stones under her hand. She thinks that
death here cannot be cold. It will come softly, amid
the scent of orange blossom, with the sea breathing below
the windows.

She will be no more.

And there will be no winter.

Her companion brings the evening newspaper. Madame de Parville opens
it without interest. Halfway down a column she sees a
familiar word: Paris. Heavy snow has fallen there since morning.
Traffic is impeded; the boulevards are white.

She shivers despite the warm air.

"We must go home," the companion says.

They walk slowly to the villa. A letter lies on
the table, broad and square, addressed in Henry's hand. She
recognizes at once the thick Norman paper. Before opening it,
she coughs. The cough bends her double; it tears at
her chest with obstinate, regular blows. When it ceases, a
red stain marks the handkerchief.

She reads.

Henry writes of rain, a sick mare, damaged trees, and
the remarkable success of the heating apparatus. Even in the
north bedrooms, he says, one can now remain without a
coat. Had they known, they ought to have installed it
years ago.

Her hand falls upon her knee.

Outside, the last light fades over the sea. The villas
whiten in the dusk; a bell sounds from the harbour;
warm darkness rises among the orange trees.

Madame de Parville smiles faintly.

She has had her furnace put in.

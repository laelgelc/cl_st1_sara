It is not my custom to trouble the public with
every uncommon relation that is carried from one tea-table to
another, for most of these perish upon the first sober
inquiry; but the following matter hath been attested by a
person whom I have known above thirteen years, and whose
temper, conduct, and circumstances I have examined with more care
than perhaps those who have made the greatest noise against
her. I mean Mrs. Elizabeth Bargrave, of Canterbury, a woman
neither melancholy nor fanciful, but of a cheerful discourse, a
sound understanding, and so patient under very hard usage in
her own family, that those who know the particulars wonder
at her constancy more than at the event I am
about to relate.

I visited Mrs. Bargrave three several times concerning it, and
once in the company of a gentleman of undoubted credit,
who desired that every circumstance might be repeated from the
beginning, without our directing her by questions. Her account agreed
in all material points; and though she sometimes remembered afterward
a small circumstance which she had omitted before, as naturally
happens in a conversation of near two hours, she never
contradicted herself. I can likewise avouch that she hath made
no profit by the report, but, on the contrary, hath
suffered disturbance from strangers, resentment from certain relations of the
deceased, and much idle censure from persons who think it
wit to deny whatever they cannot explain.

Mrs. Bargrave and Mrs. Margaret Veal had been acquainted from
childhood. Their fathers lived for some years in the same
parish, and the young women, being both under affliction, though
of different kinds, contracted that intimacy which is often stronger
than friendships made in prosperity. Mrs. Bargrave had a severe
mother-in-law, and Mrs. Veal was charged, before she had strength
for it, with the care of a sick brother and
an imprudent household. They read together such books as came
within their reach, chiefly discourses concerning resignation, death, and future
happiness; and when one was denied liberty or money, the
other supplied her with company, which was then the better
charity of the two.

After Mrs. Bargrave's marriage, her condition was not amended as
her friends had hoped. Her husband, though capable of behaving
civilly abroad, was subject at home to violent passions, and
had more than once struck her, besides withholding those common
necessaries which a wife might reasonably expect. I mention this
not to expose him, but because some have pretended that
grief had disordered her understanding. Yet no one who hath
conversed with her can observe the least confusion in her
speech or business. She kept her house with prudence upon
very little, instructed her children, preserved a decent appearance, and,
when provoked, spoke rather of Providence than revenge.

Mrs. Veal, meanwhile, went to live with her brother, Mr.
Thomas Veal, whose affairs carried him from one town to
another. By degrees the correspondence between the women grew cold.
Mrs. Bargrave wrote twice and received no answer. Once, meeting
Mrs. Veal by chance, she found her civil but reserved,
as persons sometimes become when advanced a little in fortune
and desirous to forget the witnesses of their former want.
This neglect grieved Mrs. Bargrave, yet she neither reproached her
nor spoke ill of her; and above two years passed
without their seeing one another.

On Saturday, the eighth day of September, in the year
1705, Mrs. Bargrave was sitting alone in the front room
of her house, after having put her work aside. It
was about twelve at noon, the day clear, and the
street full of ordinary business. Her husband was absent; one
child was gone on an errand; and the maid, who
had been washing in the back part of the house,
was at that time above stairs. Mrs. Bargrave had been
reflecting upon her troubles, and saying within herself that, since
no earthly alteration appeared likely, she must endeavour to make
affliction useful and depend upon that goodness which can turn
even cruelty to our advantage.

As she sat thus, there came a knocking at the
door, not loud, but distinct. Mrs. Bargrave opened it herself
and saw Mrs. Veal standing without, in a riding habit
of scoured silk, of a sober striped pattern, newly made
up, with the sleeves drawn close at the wrist. She
had a hood upon her head, though the weather did
not require it, and carried no bundle, servant, nor riding
whip.

"Madam," says Mrs. Bargrave, being surprised, "I am glad to
see you, for it is a great while since I
had that satisfaction."

Mrs. Veal stepped in and answered, "You may well be
surprised, for I have used you very unkindly; but I
could not go my journey without first seeing you."

Mrs. Bargrave led her into the room and offered her
the best chair. Mrs. Veal sat down upright, with one
hand upon her knee, and looked earnestly at her friend.

"My dear Mrs. Bargrave," says she, "forgive me that I
have neglected you. We loved one another when neither of
us had many to love us, and I have not
answered that obligation as I ought."

Mrs. Bargrave told her she had long forgiven it, and
desired she would not afflict herself upon an old matter.

"But I must speak of it," says Mrs. Veal, clapping
her hand once upon her knee, "because friendships are not
so light a thing as the world accounts them. We
forget those who helped us in adversity, and afterward wonder
that Heaven suffers us to be forgotten."

Mrs. Bargrave observed that her friend spoke with greater solemnity
than formerly, but supposed that sickness, or the prospect of
a journey, had put serious thoughts into her mind. She
asked whether she had been ill. Upon this Mrs. Veal
drew her hand slowly across her eyes and said, "I
have been indisposed, but am better now than I was."

She would not explain further, and immediately began to speak
of Mrs. Bargrave's condition, concerning which she knew more than
Mrs. Bargrave thought had come to her knowledge. She named
several instances of her husband's unkindness, yet did so without
railing against him.

"These are sharp trials," says Mrs. Veal, "but they are
not endless. There is a time when the oppressed shall
not need to petition for quiet, nor the injured defend
their innocence. If we could see how short our sufferings
are, and how lasting the advantage of bearing them well,
we should be ashamed of our impatience."

Mrs. Bargrave said that such reflections were easy in a
book, but difficult when a harsh word, a blow, or
the want of bread came upon one unexpectedly.

"They are difficult," says Mrs. Veal, "and therefore valuable. God
doth not crown us for what costs us nothing. But
do not think that submission requires you to call evil
good, or to cease from lawful endeavours for your safety.
Only preserve your soul from hatred; for hatred gives your
persecutor a lodging in your breast, even when he is
absent."

These words, as Mrs. Bargrave declared to me, affected her
more than any sermon she had lately heard, because they
came from one who had known her before her misfortunes
and could speak to the particular wound. Mrs. Bargrave wept.
Mrs. Veal did not weep, but passed her hand again
before her eyes, keeping her head very straight, and said,
"There, there; you must not sink. Your deliverance may be
nearer than you suppose, though perhaps it will not come
in the form you expect."

They then discoursed of the books they had read when
young. Mrs. Veal asked whether Mrs. Bargrave still possessed a
small volume concerning the consolations of death, bound in brown
calf, with several passages marked in the margin. Mrs. Bargrave
fetched it from a shelf and laid it upon the
table. Mrs. Veal turned over some leaves, or seemed to
do so, though Mrs. Bargrave afterward could not be certain
that she heard the paper move.

"This author," says Mrs. Veal, "speaks well where he shows
that death is terrible chiefly at a distance, as a
dark gate seen before the traveller knows what country lies
beyond it. But here he is too curious."

She pointed to a place treating of the manner in
which departed souls may know the affairs of the living.

"We should be content," she continued, "to know that they
live unto God and that virtue is not lost. Too
much disputing about the employment of spirits commonly proceeds from
pride, for we would measure another state by the rules
of this. Yet be assured that there is a future
happiness, and that the separation which appears entire to us
may not be so in itself."

Mrs. Bargrave answered, smiling through her tears, "You speak, Madam,
as if you had been better informed than the author."

At these words Mrs. Veal looked fixedly upon her, but
with no anger. "Some truths," says she, "are known at
once when the hindrance is taken away."

Mrs. Bargrave thought the expression uncommon, yet before she could
ask what was meant, Mrs. Veal inquired whether her daughter
was at home. Mrs. Bargrave said she was gone into
the neighbourhood but might soon return.

"I should be glad to see the child," says Mrs.
Veal. "She was little when I saw her last. But
if she comes not before I go, give her my
love, and teach her that no condition is contemptible where
the conscience is at peace."

Mrs. Bargrave then proposed sending for her daughter. Mrs. Veal
opposed it at first, saying the child should not be
hurried; but Mrs. Bargrave, desiring another person to see her
old friend, called to the maid above stairs and bade
her go to a neighbour's house for the girl. The
maid afterward declared that she plainly heard her mistress talking
with a woman before she was called, and distinguished two
voices, though she did not enter the room nor know
whose the second was. This testimony is material, because it
confutes the pretence that Mrs. Bargrave had fallen asleep and
dreamed the whole conversation.

While the maid was gone, Mrs. Veal admired a piece
of needlework upon the table, and Mrs. Bargrave, looking more
carefully at her dress, said, "This is a handsome habit,
and one I have not seen before."

"No," says Mrs. Veal; "it was lately made. The silk
was scoured, and my sister thought it would not bear
making up again, but it hath done very well."

She lifted the edge of the sleeve as she spoke,
and Mrs. Bargrave observed a small repaired place near the
seam. The habit was of a striped scoured silk, not
new from the mercer, but newly fashioned; and this description
was afterward owned by the woman who helped make it,
though she could not imagine how Mrs. Bargrave came to
know it.

Mrs. Veal next said, with some abruptness, "I desire you
to write to my brother."

Mrs. Bargrave asked upon what subject.

"Tell him," says Mrs. Veal, "that I would have the
two broad gold pieces in the little black purse given
to my cousin Watson's daughters, one to each; and there
are three rings in the upper drawer of the cabinet,
wrapped in a paper on which is written 'For remembrance.'
Let my sister have the plain ring, and the other
two be disposed of as I formerly said."

Mrs. Bargrave was uneasy at this commission. "Madam," says she,
"your brother may think it a great freedom in me
to meddle with your money and jewels. It would be
better that you should write to him yourself."

"I cannot write now," replied Mrs. Veal.

"Then stay till after your journey."

"No," says she, "it must be done soon."

Mrs. Bargrave asked where the journey was to be, and
whether Mr. Veal went with her. Mrs. Veal waived the
question, saying only, "I am going where I have business
of consequence, and perhaps shall not return to his house."
She added that Mr. Bretton had for several years paid
her ten pounds at Lady Day, a matter which, she
believed, her brother did not fully understand; and she desired
Mrs. Bargrave to mention it, not as a debt then
demanded, but as a circumstance by which her message might
be known to proceed from herself.

Mrs. Bargrave again objected that such private affairs ought not
to pass through her hands. Upon this Mrs. Veal leaned
a little forward, but would not bow her head, and
said very earnestly, "My dear friend, do this for me.
You will not be injured by an act of kindness,
though you may be blamed for it."

Mrs. Bargrave promised at last that she would report her
words exactly, provided Mrs. Veal's relations did not take offence.
Mrs. Veal seemed satisfied and resumed her discourse concerning Providence.
She said that the greatest error of afflicted persons was
to conclude themselves forsaken because they were not immediately relieved;
whereas a trial might itself be a form of instruction,
and the delay of help might prepare the soul to
receive a better help than it first desired.

I should have told you before that Mrs. Bargrave offered
her some cake and a glass of cordial. Mrs. Veal
refused both. Mrs. Bargrave pressed her to take at least
a little water after her journey into town.

"I have no need of it," says Mrs. Veal.

"You used to complain of thirst," answered Mrs. Bargrave.

"That trouble is over," says she, and then turned the
discourse so quickly that Mrs. Bargrave did not reflect upon
the oddness of the reply until afterward.

The daughter not returning, Mrs. Veal rose at about one
quarter past one, as Mrs. Bargrave judged by the clock.
Yet she did not immediately leave, but stood near the
window and looked into the street. Several persons passed: a
butcher's boy, two market women, and an elderly gentleman whom
Mrs. Bargrave knew by sight. There was nothing dark, solitary,
or alarming in the day; and this ought to be
remembered by those who would account for every apparition by
shadows, vapours, or the terror of midnight.

Mrs. Bargrave asked her friend to remain for dinner.

"I cannot," says Mrs. Veal. "My time is shorter than
yours."

She then embraced Mrs. Bargrave, though the latter afterward said
the embrace seemed light and gave her neither warmth nor
cold. At the door Mrs. Veal repeated, "Remember the rings,
the purse, and Mr. Bretton's allowance; but above all remember
that our friendship is renewed, and that no honest sorrow
is forgotten."

Mrs. Bargrave walked with her as far as the little
gate. Mrs. Veal proceeded toward the market, keeping an even
pace and holding her riding habit close about her. Mrs.
Bargrave watched until a cart passed between them; and when
it had gone by, she could no longer distinguish her
friend among the people. She supposed she had turned into
a shop or taken the narrow lane toward the inn.

The maid returned without the daughter, who had gone farther
than was expected. Mrs. Bargrave expressed disappointment and said, "You
have missed Mrs. Veal, who hath been here this hour
and more."

The maid answered that she had heard a gentlewoman's voice
but was sorry not to have seen her. That evening
Mrs. Bargrave related the visit to a neighbour, particularly mentioning
the scoured-silk habit and the intended journey. The neighbour remembers
this well, because she asked whether Mrs. Veal had come
on horseback, and Mrs. Bargrave replied that she had seen
no horse.

On Monday morning, the tenth of September, Mrs. Bargrave sent
to inquire where Mrs. Veal lodged, intending to deliver the
message before the journey. The person returned with news that
Mrs. Veal had died at her brother's house on Friday,
the seventh, at twelve o'clock at noon, which was exactly
twenty-four hours before she knocked at Mrs. Bargrave's door.

Mrs. Bargrave at first denied the report and said there
must be some mistake of names. She declared that Mrs.
Veal had sat with her on Saturday from noon until
after one, had spoken of her gown, her money, her
rings, and had gone toward the market in full day.
But the death was immediately confirmed by two relations who
had been present; and a physician's servant, who carried a
message to the house, remembered the hour, because the cathedral
clock struck twelve while he stood at the threshold and
was told she had just expired.

This discovery greatly disordered Mrs. Bargrave, though not with such
terror as some imagine. She was chiefly amazed that she
had felt no fear during the visit and had not
understood her friend's expressions. Upon recollection, many of them appeared
plain: that she was better than she had been; that
her former thirst was over; that her time was shorter;
and that certain truths were known when the hindrance was
removed. Yet Mrs. Bargrave did not, for this reason, embellish
her first account; for the neighbour to whom she spoke
on Saturday confirmed those parts related before the death was
known.

When Mr. Veal heard of the story, he treated it
with contempt and afterward with anger. He denied at first
that there were any such rings or purse, and said
Mrs. Bargrave sought notice by abusing a mourning family. But
when the upper drawer of the cabinet was opened, three
rings were found wrapped in a paper with words to
the effect Mrs. Bargrave had reported; and in a small
black purse, placed behind folded linen, lay two broad pieces
of gold. It was then pretended that Mrs. Bargrave might
have learned these particulars during the former friendship. This is
improbable, because the purse had been put there within the
last twelvemonth, and the paper about the rings was written
after the women ceased to correspond.

The matter of Mr. Bretton's ten pounds was likewise true.
I asked Mr. Bretton's clerk, a sober man who had
no interest in the dispute, and he affirmed that such
a payment had been made annually to Mrs. Veal, not
through her brother's accounts, but privately upon an old family
agreement. Mr. Veal himself seemed surprised when it was mentioned.
If Mrs. Bargrave invented this knowledge, let those who accuse
her show by what means she obtained it.

As to the gown, Mrs. Veal's sister acknowledged that it
was a scoured silk and newly made into a riding
habit, but complained that the description had been gathered from
a servant. The servant, however, declared she had never spoken
with Mrs. Bargrave before the report spread through the town.
Moreover, Mrs. Bargrave had named the repaired place at the
sleeve, which was so small that it could hardly be
observed unless the wearer lifted the edge, as the apparition
had done.

There are those who say Mrs. Bargrave saw another woman
and mistook her. But what other woman knew the private
friendship, the marked book, the rings, the black purse, the
broad pieces, and Mr. Bretton's allowance? What stranger would come
at noon, beg pardon for an old neglect, discourse for
near two hours in Mrs. Veal's known manner, wear Mrs.
Veal's particular habit, and then vanish into the business of
the market without asking charity, lodging, or any worldly advantage?
Such an explanation is more marvellous than the fact it
is designed to avoid.

Others allege that Mrs. Bargrave, being oppressed by her husband,
formed the whole in a distracted imagination. To this I
answer that affliction is not madness; and if every ill-used
wife be denied credit, much injustice may be committed safely.
Her household affairs were orderly that day and afterward. She
remembered the clock, sent for her daughter, called to the
maid, produced the book requested, and repeated the visit before
she knew of Mrs. Veal's death. A delusion might deceive
one sense, but here are words overheard, facts confirmed, objects
discovered, and a chronology fixed by independent persons.

One material thing more: Mrs. Bargrave never said she saw
her friend disappear. She saw her walk away in the
common street and lost sight of her when a cart
intervened. This plainness is, in my opinion, a strong mark
of truth. An inventor would likely have made the figure
sink through the earth, dissolve into flame, or foretell some
public calamity. Instead, the visitor spoke of forgiveness, books, a
child, a mended sleeve, a few rings, and two pieces
of gold. The very littleness of several circumstances gives authority
to the greater.

Nor did Mrs. Bargrave desire the commission concerning the property.
She twice refused it and undertook it only upon earnest
entreaty. She claimed no ring, no money, nor any favor
from Mr. Veal; and his hostility hath brought her more
uneasiness than reputation can compensate. Persons of quality who came
to hear her found her unwilling to repeat the matter
merely for curiosity. To those who asked soberly she answered;
to those who laughed she commonly said, "You may believe
as you please, but I must not deny what I
saw and heard."

I once asked her whether, if she had known Mrs.
Veal to be dead, she would have admitted her.

"I cannot tell," says Mrs. Bargrave. "Perhaps fear would have
deprived me of the comfort intended. She came as my
friend, and I received her as such. God was pleased
to hide the alarming part until the profitable part was
delivered."

"And what," said I, "do you judge to have been
the chief purpose of the visit?"

"To repair an unkindness," she replied, "to direct a small
act of justice, and to assure one afflicted woman that
death doth not destroy either knowledge or charity."

This answer she gave without enthusiasm and with such composure
that the gentleman present afterward told me he was more
moved by her manner than by the strangeness of the
narrative.

I do not affirm that every tale of spirits deserves
belief, nor that Providence is bound to satisfy our curiosity
by such appearances. Many reports are false, some are magnified,
and others proceed from disease or fraud. But it is
no part of reason to reject an event merely because
its cause is hidden. We believe many effects in nature
while disputing the manner of them; and it would be
odd philosophy to receive nothing upon testimony except what we
could have discovered without testimony.

Here we have a woman of established sobriety; a relation
given before she knew the decisive circumstance of death; a
conversation overheard in part; a garment privately altered and exactly
described; money and rings found where she said; an allowance
unknown to her in the ordinary course of things; and
an account maintained without gain, variation, or vain display. Against
these stand chiefly the displeasure of a brother and the
general unwillingness of men to own that the invisible world
may sometimes approach the visible.

For my own part, after hearing Mrs. Bargrave, questioning the
maid and neighbour, examining the dates, and receiving confirmation concerning
the habit, the gold, and Mr. Bretton's payment, I am
as well satisfied of the substance of this matter as
I can reasonably be of any private occurrence at which
I was not myself present. Let others assign the mode
of it if they are able. I relate only what
is avouched.

Mrs. Bargrave continues in the same modest condition and hath
gained no release from ordinary afflictions by being favoured with
an extraordinary consolation. Yet she says the remembrance of that
Saturday strengthens her when her troubles are greatest. She considers
not the apparition as a prodigy sent to distinguish her,
but as an act of mercy suited to her need;
and she often repeats the final words of her departed
friend, that no honest sorrow is forgotten.

Thus a friendship neglected in life was renewed after death;
a poor woman, whom the world had little regarded, received
comfort when she most required it; and several minute truths
were left behind as seals to a greater truth, namely,
that our present sight is not the measure of all
existence, nor the grave an argument against future happiness. If
this account promotes patience, reconciliation, and a serious regard to
eternity, it hath answered a better end than wonder; and
if any reject it, let them at least confute the
evidence before they ridicule the fact.

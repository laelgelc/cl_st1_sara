There are ball games a man remembers because he won
them, and ball games he remembers because he lost them,
and then there are games which remain bright in his
mind because somebody else took the medicine.

The most memorable game I ever played belonged to the
third class.

It happened when I was covering second base for Rochester,
in a season when we had a club good enough
to lick anything in our league and vain enough to
believe we could lick almost anything outside it. Gillinger was
captain and first baseman, a six-foot-three monument with hands like
smoked hams. "Deerfoot" Dugan played center and could run down
a telegram. Old Hathaway did most of the twirling when
brains counted for more than steam. We had youth, age,
speed, safe hitting, in-shoots, out-drops, tobacco, confidence, and an assortment
of bad tempers. What we did not have was much
affection for our manager, Horace Merritt.

Merritt was the kind of manager who could look at
a double play and calculate the wear on the ball.
He never saw a crowd; he saw so many paid
admissions. If a thunderstorm washed out a game, he did
not blame Providence for the rain. He blamed Providence for
neglecting to send it before the gates opened.

Toward the middle of July we had a scheduled day
off. A day off in professional baseball is not twenty-four
hours on a calendar. It is a cool lake, a
fishing pole, a clean shirt, a long sleep, and the
right not to hear an umpire's voice. We had laid
plans for that day with the care ordinarily given to
military campaigns.

Then Merritt walked into the clubhouse after a hot extra-inning
game and said:

"Pack your grips, fellars. We're goin' to Canada in the
mornin'."

For a moment nobody answered. We had just beaten Syracuse
in eleven innings, and the clubhouse resembled a hospital after
a boiler explosion. Spike Nolan had one shoe off and
was examining a blister. Hathaway's arm rested in a tub
of hot water. Deerfoot lay flat on a bench with
a wet towel across his face. Gillinger was chewing tobacco
with the slow solemnity of an ox considering murder.

"What part of Canada?" I asked.

"Guelph."

"What for?"

"Ball game."

That brought every corpse in the room to life.

"On our day off?" said Nolan.

Merritt nodded.

Gillinger removed his tobacco and looked at it, as if
hoping to discover legal advice there.

"My grandmother's dead," he said.

"Your grandmother died in Buffalo last month," said Merritt.

"That was my mother's mother. This is the other league."

"I got a lame ankle," said Deerfoot, leaping up to
demonstrate it.

"My shoulder's gone," said our catcher, Pete Coogan, rotating the
wrong arm.

"I'm comin' down with malaria," said I.

"Can't git malaria here," Merritt snapped.

"I caught it in advance."

Within thirty seconds every man had developed some affliction, obligation,
or bereavement. We had sprains, chills, weak hearts, sick uncles,
church engagements, poisoned thumbs, and one threatened marriage. Hathaway, who
was thirty-eight and had been in baseball since the invention
of foul lines, merely lifted his gray head and said:

"If I pitch on my day off, my arm'll fall
off."

"Then pitch till it does," said Merritt. "They guarantee us
a good gate. Each player gets a share."

That softened a few of the younger fellows, but not
many. Money is a fine medicine, yet a day off
in July is finer. Merritt, however, had signed papers, accepted
an advance, and probably spent our share in his imagination.

"You're goin'," he said. "They got a local club up
there that thinks it can play ball. Farmers, shopkeepers, lumber
handlers—stiffs. We'll give 'em five innings of regular ball, make
it look close, then cinch the game and come home.
Easy money."

"Who's pitching?" asked Hathaway.

"You are."

Hathaway removed his arm from the hot water and gazed
sadly at it.

"Good-by, old friend," he said.

We rode north the next morning in a humor black
enough to smoke the car windows. Merritt traveled in the
forward coach and counted tickets, receipts, and prospective dollars. We
stayed together and insulted Canada.

None of us had seen Guelph, but that did not
interfere with our opinions. According to Nolan, every Canadian wore
snowshoes in July. Coogan expected the bases to be slabs
of ice. Deerfoot had heard that Canadian baseball allowed the
batter to carry the bat around the sacks in self-defense.
Gillinger said nothing for an hour, then observed that any
country which required a man to work on a day
off was unfit for civilized sport.

At the border a customs officer asked Coogan if he
had anything to declare.

"Only my arm," Coogan said. "Merritt's exporting it without permission."

The officer looked at him, looked at Merritt, and waved
us through. I have always believed he understood the situation.

Guelph surprised us. Instead of a frozen wilderness, it was
green and hot, with broad fields, brick buildings, shade trees,
and half the population waiting around the station. Boys climbed
posts to see us. Men in suspenders discussed our batting
averages. Girls waved handkerchiefs. A brass band attacked a tune
and followed us toward the grounds as if we were
prisoners being marched to execution.

The ball park stood beyond the town, with a good
infield, a rough but honest outfield, and temporary stands packed
before we arrived. Wagons ringed the fences. People sat on
roofs, rails, boxes, barrels, and each other. There were farmers
with red necks, factory men with scarred hands, merchants in
straw hats, children sticky with candy, and women who seemed
to know as much baseball as anybody present. The crowd
made a low, steady sound, like buffalo moving beyond a
hill.

"That's a gate," Merritt said, and his face opened like
a cash drawer.

Our opponents were already warming up.

At first sight they confirmed our worst prejudices. Their uniforms
did not match. One man wore trousers belonging to a
larger relative; another had stockings of two different colors. Their
captain, a tremendous fellow named McCreery, had shoulders broad enough
to hang an advertisement on. Their shortstop was a narrow,
freckled youth who looked as if he had left a
plow in the next field. Their pitcher, Baird, was long,
loose, and sunburned, with wrists like rope knots.

"Rubes," Nolan said.

Then Baird threw one.

The ball crossed the plate like a pea shot from
an air gun and struck the catcher's mitt with a
report that made Coogan blink.

"Lively rube," I said.

The freckled shortstop took grounders. He moved neither gracefully nor
according to any method we recognized. He simply vanished from
one spot and appeared at another. A ball hit ten
feet to his left was in his hand before the
batboy had time to duck. He threw across the diamond
without setting himself, and McCreery caught the ball chest-high with
a smack.

Deerfoot stopped laughing.

Their outfielders practiced throws to the plate. The left fielder,
a black-haired woodsman called Angus, sent the ball in on
a line from deep territory. It did not arch. It
traveled like a bullet seeking a grievance.

"They ain't polished," Gillinger admitted, "but they're built terrible."

We still expected to win. A professional club does not
surrender its conceit during warm-up. We had seen minor-league wonders
before—men who could perform miracles with no one on base
and drop their own caps in a game. Besides, these
Canadians were local heroes facing trained players. We intended to
let them enjoy themselves, then close the door.

We batted first. Baird struck out Deerfoot on three pitched
balls.

Deerfoot returned to the bench wearing the expression of a
man who has misplaced a staircase in the dark.

"Couldn't you see it?" I asked.

"Sure. I saw three of 'em. Never saw the same
one twice."

I went up next. Baird's first pitch rose under my
hands. The second broke away. On the third I got
enough wood for a hard grasscutter between short and third.
I was already thinking of second base when the freckled
youth shot sideways, scooped the ball behind his ankle, turned
in the air, and threw me out by two steps.

"That hayseed's got springs," I said.

Gillinger slammed a liner toward right. Their second baseman jumped,
stretched until he looked eight feet tall, and speared it
with one hand.

Three men up; three men extinguished.

The crowd roared with delight. Merritt frowned, not yet from
anxiety but because we were making the amateurs look too
good without charging extra.

In Guelph's half Hathaway walked to the box with the
melancholy courage of an old soldier. He had no blazing
speed left, but he could make a baseball approach the
plate from six directions and arrive by none of them.
He fed Baird a slow curve, a fast one inside,
and a drop. Baird missed all three.

"Now," Hathaway said when he returned to the bench, "if
the rest of 'em are pitchers, we're safe."

They were not.

The next batter, the freckled shortstop, beat out a slow
roller that Nolan fielded perfectly. The boy ran like a
cat escaping a kettle. McCreery followed with a drive that
nearly amputated Hathaway at the knee. Hathaway lifted one foot
by instinct, the ball passed beneath it, and Deerfoot held
the runners to first and third with a splendid throw.

Angus came up. Hathaway coaxed him into a high fly,
but it went deep enough to score the runner. Guelph
led one to nothing.

"That'll keep the crowd interested," Merritt said.

Gillinger turned upon him.

"If we get 'em any more interested, they'll eat us."

During the next three innings we learned that the Canadians
could field anything which remained within the limits of the
province. Nolan punched a safe-looking hit over second; Angus sprinted
in from left, crossed behind short, and caught it at
his shoe tops. Coogan drove one against the right-field wind;
their fielder ran back without looking, turned at the last
instant, and took it over his shoulder. Hathaway laid down
a bunt fine enough to roll along the chalk, but
Baird pounced on it like a tiger, threw from his
knees, and got him.

We hit hard, soft, high, low, fair, and nearly foul.
They caught everything. When they could not catch it, they
knocked it down. When they could not knock it down,
somebody backed them up. From amusement we passed to irritation,
from irritation to determination, and from determination to that passionate
state in which nine professionals try to murder a ball
because a village club has wounded their dignity.

The harder we tried, the more remarkable those Canadians became.

Meanwhile they gathered another run. McCreery doubled off the center-field
fence in the fourth. The blow sounded like a pistol
shot and left a dent in the boards. A sacrifice
moved him over, and a long fly brought him home.

Two to nothing.

The crowd was no longer merely interested. It had become
a weather system. Hats flew, bells rang, children shrieked, and
an elderly woman behind our bench informed Gillinger that her
sister could bat better than he could.

"Bring her down," Gillinger called. "We need her."

At the end of the fifth Merritt met us beside
the water bucket. He had lost the prosperous glow he
wore at the gate. His mouth looked as if it
had been fastened with wire.

"What's the matter with you loafers?" he demanded.

"They're good," I said.

"They're stiffs!"

"They're two runs better than these stiffs," said Gillinger.

"You fellars are throwin' the game."

That was dangerous language. Gillinger rose slowly. When Gillinger rose
slowly, sensible men moved quickly.

Hathaway pushed him back.

"Save it," the old twirler said. "If we kill him
here, we got to carry him home."

Merritt glanced toward the grandstand, then toward the Guelph bench.
He motioned Coogan and me close.

"I got somethin'," he muttered.

"So has Pete," I said. "His is a strained shoulder."

"Shut up. Look."

From inside his coat Merritt drew a baseball. It appeared
ordinary—white leather, red seams, regulation size—but he held it gingerly,
as if it might bite.

I knew what it was. Every old ballplayer had heard
of the rabbit, though few admitted using one. A rabbit
was built with a rubber heart. Where an honest baseball
contained tightly wound yarn around a modest center, this outlaw
had enough spring in it to furnish a hotel mattress.
Off a bat it traveled farther and faster. On hard
earth it did not bounce according to geometry, common sense,
or Christian teaching. It struck a pebble and changed its
mind. It crouched, leaped, kicked, doubled back, or climbed a
man. And the more it was hit, the warmer and
livelier its rubber center became.

"That ain't legal," I said.

Merritt showed his teeth.

"You want your share of the gate?"

"I want my face."

"Gimmee the game ball when we go in. I'll make
the switch."

Gillinger had come close enough to hear. His code of
ethics was flexible where victories were concerned, but he did
not like Merritt's tone.

"Suppose the ump spots it?"

"He won't."

"Suppose Guelph gets to hit it?"

"They won't. Soon as we're ahead, foul it over the
stand and put the regular ball back."

It was a mean scheme, a cheap scheme, and a
scheme unworthy of trained athletes.

Naturally, after looking at the score, we agreed to it.

The umpire was a square-built local named Wetherby, with pale
eyes and a voice that could halt logging machinery. He
had handled the game well and given neither side favors.
That made the trick more risky. But when we came
in for the sixth, Merritt lounged near home plate complaining
about a scuff on the ball. Coogan tossed it to
him. Merritt rubbed it, turned his back for no more
than a second, and pitched the rabbit to Baird.

I was first to bat.

The rabbit came in high. I swung late and chopped
it straight down. It struck in front of the plate,
bounded ten feet over Baird's head, landed behind second, shot
sideways away from the center fielder, and was still hopping
indignantly when I reached third.

The park became silent except for Merritt.

"Run, you fool!" he screamed.

I scored standing up while Angus chased the ball toward
left field. He finally trapped it beneath both knees and
looked at it with personal suspicion.

The crowd laughed. So did we. At that stage the
rabbit was funny because it hated Canadians.

Deerfoot came next and hit a little bounder toward third.
The third baseman charged. The ball dipped under his glove,
struck his shin, caromed toward short, hit the freckled youth's
foot, and bounded into the visitors' dugout. Deerfoot reached second.

Nolan slapped one through the box. Baird got both hands
on it. The rabbit climbed his wrists, struck him under
the chin, and fell behind the mound. Deerfoot scored, tying
the game.

Then Gillinger came up.

"Don't kill it," I told him.

He swung.

Crack!

The rabbit went between the left and center fielders with
a noise like torn canvas. It struck the ground once
and sprang against the fence eight feet high. Angus caught
it on the rebound, but it kicked from his hands
and rolled under a wagon outside the grounds. By the
time a boy retrieved it, Gillinger had lumbered around all
four bases, puffing like a locomotive pulling a courthouse.

After that there was no baseball. There was a riot
conducted with scorekeeping.

Coogan hit a grasscutter that rose over the second baseman's
shoulder. Hathaway bounced one off third base; it flew into
the grandstand. Baird threw carefully, recklessly, inside, outside, and finally
nowhere near the plate, but the rabbit would not permit
order. If we missed it, we walked. If we touched
it, it performed.

The Guelph fielders were superb, brave, quick, and doomed. They
flung themselves upon the rabbit and came away empty-handed or
bruised. McCreery blocked a hard bounder with his chest; it
dropped at his feet, then bounced between his legs. The
shortstop made a wonderful stop deep in the hole, but
his throw struck the ground before first and vaulted thirty
feet over the baseman's head. Their right fielder caught a
fly cleanly, only to have the ball spring from his
glove as though insulted by confinement.

Around and around we went. We batted through the order
twice and started a third journey. We scored fourteen runs.

The crowd's laughter turned to astonishment, then anger. They knew
baseball. They had seen bad innings, strange hops, and clubs
go up in the air. Nobody had seen the earth
itself join the visiting team.

Merritt was wild with happiness.

"Foul it out!" he kept calling. "Lose it! Knock it
over!"

We tried. That was the first warning that the rabbit
had begun managing the game.

Nolan aimed foul and hit fair. Coogan tried to lift
it over the roof and popped to the catcher. I
deliberately sliced a pitch toward the right-field stand, but the
rabbit curved back and landed inside the line. Finally Gillinger
swung at a ball near his eyes and sent a
towering foul beyond third.

The rabbit rose above the grandstand. Every Rochester player breathed
out.

Then a boy on the roof caught it in his
cap.

"Throw it here!" shouted Wetherby.

Merritt ran toward the stand waving both arms.

"Keep it, son! Souvenir!"

The umpire fixed him with those pale eyes.

"Throw—that—ball—here."

The boy obeyed. Wetherby examined the rabbit, weighed it, squeezed
it, and looked at Merritt. Merritt smiled with the innocence
of a burglar found under a bed.

"Lively ball," the umpire said.

"Hot weather," Merritt replied.

"Same ball stays in play."

"We always change after a big inning."

"You'll change after the game."

Merritt's smile disappeared.

"But—"

"Your club hit it," said Wetherby. "Their club hits it.
Take the field."

We went out leading fourteen to two, and never have
twelve runs felt so small.

Hathaway stood on the mound holding the rabbit at arm's
length.

"I won't pitch this thing," he said.

"You got to," Merritt called.

"It's swelled."

It had. The seams appeared tight, and the ball seemed
warm even from where I stood. Every blow awakened the
rubber center further. It was no longer a ball. It
was a jumping jackrabbit with fever.

Baird, the pitcher, led off for Guelph. Hathaway tried a
slow curve. The rabbit refused to curve. It went straight
over the plate, and Baird met it.

Bing!

The ball passed Hathaway's ear, struck the center-field ground, bounded
over Deerfoot's head, and smacked the fence. Deerfoot wheeled. On
the second bounce it cleared him coming back. Baird made
third.

"Keep it down!" Merritt yelled.

Hathaway looked toward our bench.

"Come out here and sit on it!"

The freckled shortstop hit the next pitch at Nolan. Spike
set himself beautifully. The ball struck six feet in front
of him, shot to his right, then appeared to reconsider
and knocked his cap off on the left. One run
scored.

McCreery came up.

I had watched that giant hit an honest baseball against
the fence. I now considered the rabbit in his hands
and moved backward until I could smell the outfield grass.

Hathaway delivered an in-shoot. McCreery stepped and lambasted it.

Crack!

The ball came toward me three feet off the ground.
There are moments when a man's training conquers his intelligence.
I could have ducked. I should have ducked. Instead I
put up my glove.

The rabbit struck the heel of it, folded my wrist
against my chest, and carried glove, hand, and myself backward.
I performed half a somersault and landed with both legs
pointing toward Canada.

When I sat up, the ball was rolling in right
field. McCreery was on second, and Gillinger stood over me.

"How many fingers?" he asked.

"On who?"

"You're all right."

Angus hit one at Gillinger. It struck first base, bounded
into his stomach, and dropped him like a felled tree.
Another run scored. The next batter drove a liner off
Hathaway's thigh. The old twirler spun twice, sat down, and
remained there.

Merritt ran out.

"You hurt?"

"No," Hathaway said. "I always pitch sittin' down."

"Can you keep goin'?"

"My leg's numb."

"Then it can't hurt."

Hathaway stared up at him. "Horace, if I could stand,
I'd kill you."

The crowd cheered this sentiment.

Merritt had to replace him. Our relief pitcher, Lefty Grove—not
the famous Lefty, but a smaller, more nervous article—came in
without warming up. Merritt met him near the foul line
and tried to slip him another ball.

Wetherby saw it.

"Hold on!"

Merritt jerked his hand behind him.

"What?"

"Gimmee that."

"This is our warm-up ball."

"Gimmee it."

Merritt surrendered an honest baseball. Wetherby put it in his
pocket, then pointed to the rabbit lying near second.

"That one."

"You can't make us use a dangerous ball!"

"You introduced it."

"I never!"

The umpire stepped close enough to inspect Merritt's tonsils.

"Take your medicine."

That phrase traveled through the crowd. Ten thousand Canadians repeated
it with pleasure.

"Take yer medicine, Merritt!"

"Give him a spoon!"

"Open wide, Horace!"

Merritt shrank visibly. At the beginning of the day he
had seemed tall enough to count gate receipts over everybody's
shoulder. Now he looked no larger than a batboy caught
stealing apples.

Lefty took the rabbit and pitched.

The Canadians fell upon him like woodsmen attacking a pine.
They did not try to place their hits. They swung
from the roots. Every contact was a pistol crack. Balls
scooted between us, over us, under us, and occasionally through
us. Deerfoot chased one to the fence, had it bounce
back past him, caught it barehanded on the next rebound,
and threw toward the plate. The rabbit hit a clod
halfway in, turned north, and rolled to the Guelph bench.

Three runs scored.

Coogan attempted to block a low pitch with his chest
protector. It climbed beneath his mask and rang it like
a dinner bell. He tore off the mask, threw it
down, and announced he had retired from baseball. Wetherby told
him to put it on.

Nolan stopped a grounder with his knee and began hopping
in a circle. Gillinger took a throw at first; the
rabbit sprang from his mitt and struck an innocent coach.
I dived for a liner, missed it by a yard,
and congratulated myself on still possessing teeth.

Run after run crossed the plate.

Our fourteen-run carnival had been comic because it happened to
strangers. The Canadians' reply contained what Hathaway afterward called an
element of tragedy. We were battered, scraped, stretched, trampled, and
jeered. Yet beneath our suffering ran a disgraceful current of
delight, because every bruise landed upon Merritt's scheme.

At fourteen to eleven he ordered us to bear down.

"At what?" Gillinger shouted. "The ball ain't there long enough!"

At fourteen to twelve Merritt threatened fines.

"Fine the rabbit!" said Coogan.

At fourteen to thirteen Lefty walked two men rather than
pitch across the plate. Wetherby called him over.

"If you keep throwin' away, I'll order you to pitch
underhand."

Lefty looked at Merritt. Merritt looked at the gate, as
if hoping his money might rescue him.

McCreery came up with two out and the tying run
on third. Lefty floated one shoulder-high. The giant chopped it.
For once the rabbit behaved like a decent baseball. It
rolled straight toward Merritt, who had stationed himself just outside
our bench, too nervous to sit.

"Foul!" Merritt cried before it reached the line.

It was fair by two feet.

The ball struck a stone.

Bing!

It leaped sideways and hit Merritt just above the ankle.
He gave a howl, seized his leg, and danced into
fair territory while the runner scored. McCreery reached first. The
game was tied.

No Rochester player hurried to assist our manager. Gillinger leaned
on his knees, helpless with laughter. Hathaway, reclining beside the
bench with his leg bandaged, beat the ground with his
cap. Even Lefty smiled on the mound, though he was
facing a powerful Canadian with the winning run aboard.

Merritt limped behind the bench and ordered us to finish
them.

Angus stepped in.

Lefty's first pitch nearly struck him. The second bounced over
Coogan's head. McCreery reached second. With first base open, common
strategy called for a walk, but strategy had packed its
grip and left Guelph several innings earlier.

"Pitch to him!" Merritt screamed. "They're amateurs!"

Angus's face showed no opinion. He merely tightened his blackened
hands on the bat.

Lefty threw.

Crack!

The line drive went past the mound at about the
height of a man's belt. I moved one step toward
it and then stopped, because Merritt, limping back along the
edge of the field to continue his advice, had wandered
directly into its path.

The rabbit struck him squarely below the ribs.

There was a sound between a drumbeat and a grunt.
Merritt folded around the ball. For one astonishing instant he
seemed to sit upon nothing. Then he rolled over, clutched
his middle, and began writhing in the dust.

The rabbit caromed into foul territory.

McCreery crossed the plate with the winning run.

The stands erupted. Men threw hats; women waved parasols; boys
came over the fence like a tribe of young cannibals.
Angus was carried toward the clubhouse. Baird disappeared beneath a
heap of teammates. The band played three tunes at once.
Somewhere a bell rang without stopping.

We lost fifteen to fourteen.

We were professionals, and pride required us to appear dejected.
We tried. Gillinger covered his mouth with his glove. Coogan
bent double as if in grief. Nolan buried his face
against my shoulder. But when Merritt rose at last, pale
and furious, with dust in his hair and both arms
wrapped around his stomach, our discipline broke.

We cheered him.

Not loudly at first. A few handclaps. Then Hathaway called,
"Take your medicine, Horace!" and the whole Rochester club let
go.

Merritt cursed us individually and collectively. He fined Gillinger, released
Hathaway, suspended Coogan, and threatened to leave the rest of
us in Canada. Since he could scarcely walk and had
to ride to the station beside the team physician, none
of these punishments possessed much authority.

Wetherby found the rabbit near the foul line and handed
it to him.

"Your property," the umpire said.

Merritt snatched it.

On the road from the grounds we passed a green
swamp edged with reeds. The crowd still followed us at
a festive distance. Merritt halted, drew back his arm, and
hurled the rabbit far out over the brown water.

It struck with a splash.

Then it bounced.

I swear it bounced twice upon the surface—once like a
skipped stone, once like a live thing making for shore.
Merritt stared at it in horror. Gillinger removed his cap.
Hathaway, leaning on two of us, offered a solemn farewell.

The rabbit vanished among the reeds, carrying with it fourteen
runs, a manager's dignity, and what remained of our day
off.

We collected our shares of the gate a week later.
Merritt deducted for damaged uniforms, lost equipment, medical treatment, and
what he called insubordination. My portion scarcely paid for the
wrapping on my wrist. Hathaway was not released, Coogan was
not suspended, and Gillinger's fine disappeared after the captain explained
that Merritt might otherwise have to find a new first
baseman, second baseman, catcher, center fielder, and pitching staff.

Years afterward, whenever Rochester players gathered, somebody would mention Guelph.
We never spoke first of the Canadians' speed, though it
was marvelous; nor of Baird's pitching, though he had shut
us out with an honest ball; nor of McCreery's hitting,
which could have frightened artillery. We spoke of Nolan's cap,
Gillinger's stomach, Coogan's ringing mask, my backward somersault, and Hathaway
sitting on the mound.

Last of all, we remembered Merritt folding around that line
drive while the winning run came home.

We lost the game, our pride, and most of the
gate money. Yet when I reckon up the victories of
my career, I count Guelph among the sweetest.

For a man may forget a pennant, but he never
forgets the afternoon a rabbit made his manager take his
medicine.

# THE STEEL IN ARNOLD'S HEART

No man who knew Arnold Vane in his twenty-sixth year
would have accused him of fear. He had crossed the
African littoral with six porters and returned with two; he
had slept beneath a burning roof rather than descend by
a ladder which he believed unsafe; and once, in the
Vosges, when a wounded boar ripped open his horse, he
killed the beast with a hunting-knife before permitting us to
attend to his broken ankle. Courage was not with him
an exertion, but a condition of the blood.

Yet I saw that man die of terror.

The circumstances were so extraordinary, and the medical facts attending
them so often misunderstood, that I have determined to set
down the whole case precisely as I witnessed it. If
my account appear improbable, I can only reply that Nature
is less scrupulous than fiction, and that Dr. Entrefort, who
conducted the treatment, still preserves his notes.

It was a little after midnight when Hippolyte, Arnold's French
servant, burst into my lodging without hat or overcoat.

"Monsieur Leigh!" he gasped. "Come! It is my master. He
has been murdered!"

I caught up my coat.

"By whom?"

"A woman—an angel—a devil—I do not know! But the knife
is in him."

"Have you sent for a surgeon?"

"For three. One is there. He will not touch it.
He says Monsieur Arnold is already dead, although Monsieur Arnold
curses him with great strength."

This inconsistency reassured me more than any assurance could have
done. We drove at once to Arnold's house in Belgrave
Square.

The library presented a scene which remains painted upon my
memory. Two lamps burned beneath green shades. A fire smouldered
low upon the hearth. One chair was overturned; a crystal
decanter lay broken on the carpet; and Arnold stood upright
beside his writing-table, one hand resting upon it, his face
singularly composed.

From the left side of his chest projected the hilt
of a weapon.

It was no common knife. The grip, scarcely four inches
long, was of dark silver, chased with vines and serpents,
and terminated in a cabochon ruby. A little guard, crusted
with seed-pearls, pressed against the cloth of Arnold's evening coat.
Save for one narrow line of blood, black against the
white waistcoat, there was surprisingly little evidence of violence.

Dr. Rowell, a respectable practitioner of our quarter, stood before
him with both hands raised.

"You must lie down," he was saying.

"I shall do nothing of the kind," replied Arnold. "You
told me movement might kill me."

"Violent movement—yes. But standing cannot continue."

"It has continued admirably for twenty minutes."

"Arnold!" said I.

He turned his head and even smiled.

"My dear Leigh, forgive the inconvenience. I sent for you
because Rowell appears determined to let me die professionally."

Dr. Rowell flushed.

"I have said only that the weapon cannot be withdrawn
here."

"Then withdraw it elsewhere."

"Were the blade removed," said Rowell, "the resulting haemorrhage might
be instantaneous. I cannot determine what vessel has been injured.
I must have assistance."

"Where is Entrefort?" asked Arnold.

That name produced an unpleasant silence.

Dr. Etienne Entrefort had lately astonished London by three operations
which more cautious surgeons had declared impossible. He possessed, in
equal degree, daring, dexterity, and a contempt for received opinion.
Some called him a genius; others, whose patients he had
saved after they themselves had despaired, called him worse. Arnold
had known him in Paris and trusted him absolutely.

"I have sent to his rooms," said I.

"So have I," replied Rowell. "He is not there."

Hippolyte uttered a moan from the doorway.

"He went to Croydon this afternoon. A child—an injury to
the skull—"

"Then fetch him," said Arnold.

"A carriage has gone," replied Rowell.

"Good. We shall wait."

"Wait!" cried Hippolyte. "With that thing in the heart?"

Arnold looked down at the jewelled hilt.

"Not in the heart, I think. A little too high."

Dr. Rowell stepped forward.

"Do not touch it!"

"I had no intention of doing so."

At that instant the ruby in the pommel trembled. Whether
from some muscular contraction or from the faint vibration of
the floor, I cannot say. Arnold's lips whitened. Dr. Rowell
caught his arm, and I placed a chair behind him.

"Sit," said I.

He obeyed, very slowly.

We cut away the coat and waistcoat, but left the
shirt undisturbed about the wound. The weapon had entered between
the third and fourth ribs, somewhat to the left of
the sternum, and with a direction downward and inward. Each
respiration caused a scarcely perceptible movement of the hilt. To
watch that tiny oscillation was horrible. It seemed a pendulum
measuring off the seconds of his life.

"Who did this?" I asked.

Arnold's eyes hardened.

"That does not concern the case."

"It concerns the police."

"The police shall not hear of it."

"A robber?" said Rowell.

"No."

"A quarrel?"

"No."

"Then an accident?"

Arnold looked at him.

"You are a poor liar, Doctor. Do not invite others
to the profession."

For nearly an hour we remained in that oppressive room.
Arnold neither complained nor fainted. His pulse was swift but
surprisingly full. He spoke little and breathed with care, as
if every inspiration had first to be weighed and approved.
Rowell twice approached the hilt and twice withdrew without touching
it.

At last wheels sounded in the square. The bell rang
violently; rapid steps crossed the hall; and Dr. Entrefort entered.

He was a small, narrow man, not yet forty, with
a colourless face and black eyes of uncommon brilliancy. Rain
shone upon his hair. He threw his gloves upon the
table, glanced once at Arnold, and said:

"Who has interfered with it?"

"No one," replied Rowell.

"Excellent. How long?"

"An hour and forty minutes."

"Pulse?"

"One hundred and twelve."

"Cough?"

"None."

"Haemoptysis?"

"None."

"Loss of consciousness?"

"None," said Arnold. "And I object to being discussed like
a corpse."

Entrefort bent close to the weapon without touching it. His
keen eyes travelled from the hilt to the stain upon
the shirt, then to Arnold's face.

"Pain?"

"Little."

"Behind the breastbone?"

"Yes."

"Between the shoulders?"

"A dull pressure."

Entrefort placed two fingers upon Arnold's wrist and another hand
lightly at the side of his throat. His expression changed—not
to alarm, but to an intense and almost delighted attention.

"Well?" asked Arnold.

"Well," said Entrefort, "you have contrived something new."

"Is it fatal?"

"Everything is fatal if continued long enough."

Rowell made an impatient sound.

"For Heaven's sake, Entrefort!"

The surgeon ignored him. He examined the minute bloodstain through
a lens; measured the exposed portion of the weapon; and
applied his ear, first to Arnold's back and then to
his chest. Finally he stood upright.

"This is not a knife," said he.

"It has performed the office," observed Arnold.

"It is a stiletto—Italian, perhaps seventeenth century. The blade will
be long, slender, rigid, and either triangular or round in
section. From the absence of external haemorrhage I incline to
the round."

"You cannot know that," said Rowell.

"Then why is he alive?"

Rowell stared at him.

Entrefort pointed to the wound.

"A flat blade divides tissues and leaves an aperture corresponding
roughly to its width. A triangular blade tears three radiating
channels. Either injury, if carried to the depth indicated here,
should have filled the thoracic cavity with blood before your
carriage reached the square. But a polished rod of small
circumference may penetrate an elastic vessel and remain as a
plug within the opening it has made. There is no
bleeding because the instrument itself prevents bleeding."

"Then it has struck a vessel?" said I.

"A vessel of consequence."

"The pulmonary artery?" suggested Rowell.

Entrefort shook his head.

"Observe the direction. Downward, inward, and slightly posterior. The lung
may be grazed, but there is neither cough nor air
at the wound. The pericardium is probably untouched. No, gentlemen.
Unless the assassin's hand changed course upon impact, the point
lies in, or immediately against, the ascending aorta."

Hippolyte collapsed into a chair.

"The aorta!" I exclaimed.

Arnold alone remained composed.

"Can you remove it?"

"No."

"Can you leave it?"

"For the present."

"For how long?"

"That is the question."

He turned suddenly toward the servant.

"Hippolyte, bring me a basin, warm water, towels, a fine
saw, two files, a small vice, and the pliers from
the tool cupboard."

Hippolyte gaped at him.

"A saw?"

"Quickly."

Then Entrefort pointed to the ruby pommel.

"This weapon is valuable, I suppose?"

Arnold gave a faint smile.

"Very. That seems an odd consideration."

"I may be obliged to destroy the handle."

"Proceed with your work."

While the instruments were collected, Entrefort questioned Arnold in an
apparently careless manner.

"The blow was delivered at close quarters?"

"Yes."

"Suddenly?"

"Yes."

"Your assailant stood facing you?"

"Yes."

"Right-handed?"

Arnold hesitated.

"I think so."

"Not a strong person."

No answer.

"The blade entered between the ribs but did not strike
one. That was chance. Yet it passed through coat, waistcoat,
linen, and muscle without being deflected. The weapon is extremely
sharp. It was driven by anger rather than strength. Your
assailant held it beneath the hand, so—"

He demonstrated briefly.

Arnold's face became sterner.

"Enough."

"A woman," said Entrefort.

Hippolyte crossed himself. Rowell looked from one to the other,
while I confess that a suspicion already forming in my
mind became certainty.

Only three weeks before, Arnold had broken his engagement to
Mademoiselle Celestine D'Arcy, a young widow of French birth and
obscure family. I had met her twice. She was beautiful,
proud, and possessed of those large dark eyes which seem
soft only until they are opposed. Arnold had never spoken
to me of the rupture. Rumour said that she had
demanded an immediate marriage and that he, discovering some deception
concerning her past, had refused.

Arnold now said coldly:

"You are here as my surgeon, Entrefort, not as a
detective."

"I am both whenever the wound has a history. Did
she withdraw the stiletto?"

"No. I seized her wrist."

"Did the weapon move after entering?"

"No."

"That may have saved you. Where is she?"

"Gone."

Entrefort's eyes glittered.

"Then let us hope she does not return to complete
an imperfect operation."

The necessary articles arrived. Entrefort washed his hands and the
skin surrounding the wound, then cut away the shirt. He
fixed the ornate handle in the padded jaws of the
little vice, supporting its weight so that no strain should
be transmitted to the blade.

"What are you doing?" asked Rowell.

"Removing what we cannot preserve."

"But if the sawing jars the weapon—"

"It must not."

"And afterward?"

"Afterward we shall know whether he dies now or later."

Arnold looked at him steadily.

"You have a soothing manner."

"I am not employed to soothe."

The first touch of the saw upon the silver made
a thin, dreadful sound. Entrefort stopped at once.

"Hippolyte, hold the vice. Leigh, stand behind the chair. Rowell,
watch the carotid pulse and speak if its character alters.
Arnold, do not breathe deeply."

"I do breathe from time to time."

"You may continue, but without enthusiasm."

Again the saw whispered across the metal.

It was the most agonizing quarter-hour I have ever endured.
The operation itself produced almost no blood. Its horror lay
in our knowledge that a fraction of an inch, a
tremor of the hand, a cough, a sneeze, even one
involuntary start, might convert that quiet room into a place
of death. Sweat collected upon Arnold's forehead, yet he uttered
no sound. Entrefort's fingers never wavered. He cut through silver,
then through an inner sleeve of brass, filing each rough
edge before proceeding.

At last the jewelled hilt came free in his hand.

From Arnold's breast projected less than half an inch of
dark steel.

Entrefort examined the severed handle.

"Round," said he. "As I thought."

Rowell leaned closer. The socket revealed the base of a
narrow cylindrical blade, scarcely thicker than a knitting needle.

"You cannot leave that fragment exposed," he said.

"I shall not."

"Nor can you push it farther in."

"Certainly not."

"Then what—?"

Entrefort requested his smallest cutting forceps. He steadied the blade
at the skin with a slotted shield and clipped it
off so close that the remaining end disappeared beneath the
tissues. A single crimson bead rose from the wound. He
covered it with a compress and bandage.

Hippolyte stared in horror.

"You have lost it inside him!"

"I know precisely where it is."

"You leave the knife in Monsieur?"

"The knife, no. The blade, yes."

"But it will travel! To the heart—to the brain—"

"If it travels, the distance required to kill him is
very short."

Hippolyte made a choking sound.

Arnold said, "Can I stand?"

"No."

"Can I live?"

Entrefort took a chair opposite him.

"Listen carefully. The blade has almost certainly pierced the wall
of the ascending aorta and may have traversed part of
its diameter. The arterial wall, being elastic, has closed tightly
around the steel. If I withdraw it, you bleed into
the thoracic cavity and die before I can expose the
vessel. If I attempt to reach it surgically, I must
divide the sternum or resect several ribs, displace the lung,
open the pericardial region, and control a vessel in which
the blood passes under sufficient pressure to project several feet.
No operation presently known gives a reasonable hope."

"Then there is none?"

"I did not say so. The blade is sterile enough
for the moment, having passed through clean cloth and carrying
little gross contamination. It may remain fixed. Fibrin will gather
about the puncture. The tissues may form a sheath. In
time the vessel itself may strengthen around it."

"And meanwhile?" asked I.

"Meanwhile he must avoid fever, suppuration, traumatic aneurysm, embolism, secondary
haemorrhage, tetanus, violent coughing, falls, blows, sudden muscular effort, and
any emotion capable of sharply increasing arterial pressure."

Arnold raised his eyebrows.

"Is that all?"

"No. There is also rust."

Even Rowell stared.

"Oxidation?" said he.

"Steel within the body alters. If the blade corrodes unevenly,
a fragment may break loose, enter the circulation, and be
carried into the carotid, producing paralysis; into a coronary branch,
producing immediate death; or into some lesser artery, where it
may establish gangrene. If it remains rigid, the constant pulsation
of the aorta against it may enlarge the puncture. If
it becomes loose—"

"Yes?" said Arnold.

"You die."

"How soon?"

"In one second or in fifty years."

Arnold considered this.

"Then what do you propose?"

Entrefort requested pen and paper. He wrote a prescription in
his fine, angular hand and gave it to Rowell.

"Five minims of diluted muriatic acid in a full tumbler
of water, after food, three times daily. Increase by one
minim at the end of each month until twelve are
reached, unless gastric irritation appears. Continue for five years."

Rowell read the paper twice.

"Muriatic acid! What possible—"

Entrefort looked at him so sharply that he stopped.

Arnold held out his hand for the prescription.

"Will it save me?"

"It is an experiment."

"On what authority?"

"My own."

"You are the devil," said Arnold.

Entrefort smiled without warmth.

"You do me too great honor."

We moved Arnold to his bed on a rigid board.
Entrefort remained until dawn, watching each change in pulse and
respiration. No internal haemorrhage declared itself. By noon the following
day Arnold's condition was unchanged; after forty-eight hours he had
a slight fever, which subsided; and by the tenth day
the external wound had closed.

Entrefort visited daily for a month. He never explained the
acid. When Rowell argued that so diluted a quantity could
have no influence upon a piece of metal buried in
the chest, Entrefort replied:

"Then it can do no harm."

When I suggested that Arnold had a right to understand
his treatment, he said:

"Knowledge is not always medicine."

Within six weeks he left England upon a long professional
journey through India and the Levant, having accepted an appointment
connected with military hospitals. Before his departure he gave Arnold
minute instructions.

"You may walk," he said. "You may ride in a
quiet carriage. You must not run, fence, box, hunt, row,
climb, quarrel, dance, or make love."

"The last prohibition," said Arnold, "is unnecessary."

Entrefort watched him.

"See that it remains so. Above all, avoid shocks. A
violent start, a fall, even a fit of anger, may
disturb the blade. Your physical courage is of no use
against an involuntary movement."

Arnold laughed.

"I have never yet been frightened."

"That," replied Entrefort, "is why you do not know how
dangerous fear can be."

---

For the first year Arnold behaved with admirable judgment. He
resumed his reading, managed his estates, and received a few
intimate friends. He walked daily in the square. His colour
returned; his appetite was excellent; and Dr. Rowell, who examined
him each week, could detect neither murmur nor irregularity of
the heart.

Nevertheless, Arnold never forgot the steel.

How could he? Each morning the acid stood beside his
plate. Each night he placed his fingers against his chest,
searching for some vibration intelligible only to himself. If he
coughed, he grew silent. If a carriage wheel struck a
stone, his hand flew to the old scar. He dismissed
a footman for dropping a tray and ceased to use
the staircase after stumbling upon its lowest step.

The second year altered him more visibly. He sold his
horses. He had the heavy doors padded so they could
not slam. The clocks were stopped, for one struck unexpectedly
during sleep and caused him such agitation that Rowell was
summoned at three in the morning. Children were forbidden the
house. Visitors surrendered their walking-sticks in the hall lest one
should fall upon the floor.

"You treat yourself as a vase," I told him.

"A vase does not contain its own hammer," he replied.

"That blade may be perfectly fixed."

"May be."

"Entrefort believed you would live."

"Entrefort believed I might live. There is a difference large
enough to die in."

By the third year I scarcely recognized my friend. The
fearless traveller had become a pale recluse who crossed a
carpet as though it concealed precipices. He would not read
political news because anger accelerated the pulse. He abandoned wine,
then coffee, then meat, upon theories of his own concerning
the pressure of the blood. Medical treatises filled his library.
He could discourse for hours upon aneurysm, embolism, inflammation of
the mediastinum, and secondary circulation. Every sensation became a symptom;
every symptom, a verdict.

Once I found him sitting rigidly in his chair, his
face grey.

"What has happened?"

"The blade moved."

"Did you feel pain?"

"No."

"Faintness?"

"No."

"Then why do you suppose it moved?"

"I did not feel it."

I confess I lost patience.

"My dear Arnold, you have not felt it for three
years!"

"Exactly. Why should I cease to feel what I never
felt unless some change has occurred?"

There was no reasoning with such ingenuity. Rowell prescribed travel,
occupation, companionship. Arnold rejected all three. Travel involved jolting; occupation
involved excitement; companionship involved unforeseen conduct.

The muriatic acid alone he took with religious exactness.

Four years had passed when Entrefort returned to London.

I learned of his arrival from Rowell and hastened to
the surgeon's new house in Cavendish Square. Entrefort looked older,
though his eyes had lost none of their penetrating brightness.
He received me with genuine pleasure.

"And Vane?" was his first question.

"Alive."

"I expected it."

"But wretchedly altered."

"Pain?"

"None."

"Fever?"

"None."

"Cardiac disturbance?"

"None that Rowell can discover."

"Then in what is he altered?"

"In soul," said I. "You saved a lion and left
us a mouse."

Entrefort's expression became grave.

"Does he still take the acid?"

"Without omission."

"Four years?"

"To the day."

"Then I must examine him."

I was about to reply when the door opened and
a woman entered.

"My dear," said Entrefort, rising, "permit me to present an
old friend, Mr. James Leigh. Leigh, my wife."

For a moment I did not move.

She had changed. The hard brilliance of youth had softened;
her dress was sober; a streak of silver appeared in
the dark hair above one temple. But the eyes were
the same—large, deep, and capable of passing in an instant
from warmth to defiance.

It was Celestine D'Arcy.

She recognized me. Her hand, extended in courtesy, stopped midway.
All colour left her face.

"You!" she whispered.

Entrefort looked from her to me.

"You have met?"

"Some years ago," said I.

"Where?" he asked.

Neither of us answered.

His keen gaze rested upon his wife's face. Then, slowly,
it passed to mine. I saw the whole chain of
inference assemble behind those glittering eyes: my confusion, her terror,
my friendship with Arnold, the woman's hand that had driven
the stiletto.

"Ah," said he softly. "I perceive."

Celestine seized his sleeve.

"Etienne, I must speak with you."

"In a moment."

"Now!"

Before he could follow her, a servant entered with a
card.

"Mr. Arnold Vane, sir."

I uttered an exclamation.

Entrefort wheeled toward me.

"You said he would not leave his house."

"He has not done so for months."

The servant explained that Mr. Vane had come in a
closed carriage and insisted upon seeing Dr. Entrefort immediately. Doubtless
news of the surgeon's return had overcome even his terror
of the streets.

"Take my wife to the morning-room," said Entrefort. "Then admit
him here."

Celestine drew back.

"No. I will go upstairs."

The servant opened the door for her at the very
instant Arnold entered from the hall.

Thus they met upon the threshold.

Arnold had become thin and slightly stooped, but at first
sight of Entrefort something of his old animation returned. He
advanced quickly—too quickly, I thought—with both hands extended.

"My dear doctor, at last—"

Then he saw the woman.

He stopped.

Celestine's fingers closed upon the edge of the door. Her
lips parted, but no sound came. The two regarded each
other with a fixed and dreadful recognition.

"You," said Arnold.

It was not spoken loudly. Indeed, the single word scarcely
reached me. Yet his whole body recoiled as though she
had struck him again. One heel caught upon the carpet.
He recovered himself, clutched his breast, and turned an ashen
face toward Entrefort.

"The blade!"

"Stand still!" commanded the surgeon.

"It has moved."

"No."

"I felt it—here—"

Entrefort sprang forward and seized his wrist.

"Your pulse is strong. Look at me, Vane. Nothing has
happened."

"She—she has come back—"

Celestine gave a low cry.

"I did not know! Arnold, I swear—"

"Silence!" thundered Entrefort.

Arnold's eyes remained upon her. Terror, naked and immeasurable, transformed
them. This was not the old man mastering pain, but
a hunted creature already persuaded of its death.

"I am bleeding within," he whispered.

"You are not," said Entrefort. "There is no sign of
it."

"I can feel the warmth."

"Imagination."

"The point has torn free."

"No!"

Arnold staggered. I caught one arm while Entrefort supported the
other. His knees gave way, and we lowered him to
the carpet.

"Rowell!" said I.

"Send for him. And brandy—quickly!"

Entrefort tore open Arnold's coat and shirt. The old scar
was a small white point between the ribs. There was
no swelling, no discoloration, no external blood. He placed his
ear against the chest, then felt the carotid.

"Arnold," he said sharply, "hear me. The artery is not
ruptured."

Arnold's gaze wandered.

"You told me—a shock—one second—or fifty years—"

"I was wrong. Do you understand? Wrong!"

That confession from Entrefort might have recalled a dead man,
but Arnold appeared not to hear it.

Celestine had sunk against the wall. Her face was hidden
in her hands.

"I killed him," she said. "Again—I killed him again."

"No one has killed him," replied Entrefort, though his own
face had become terribly pale.

Arnold's breathing came in shallow sighs. His pulse, which I
could see beating wildly at the throat, became irregular. Entrefort
administered brandy, then a stimulant from his case. He applied
heat, loosened every garment, and worked with concentrated fury. For
one instant Arnold's eyes cleared.

"Leigh," he murmured.

"I am here."

"Do not move me. The steel—"

Those were his last words.

Within three minutes he was dead.

Dr. Rowell arrived in time to find Entrefort still kneeling
beside the body. Celestine had been led away unconscious. I
stood at the window, incapable of speech.

Rowell examined Arnold and looked gravely at Entrefort.

"Internal haemorrhage?"

"It must be," said I. "The shock loosened the blade
precisely as you feared."

Entrefort did not answer. His eyes were fixed upon the
white scar.

At length he said:

"There will be an examination."

The autopsy took place the next morning, Rowell, Entrefort, and
I being present. I shall not burden the reader with
every detail. No blood had entered the thoracic cavity. The
lung was sound; the pericardium contained no abnormal fluid; and
the heart, though arrested, showed no structural lesion sufficient to
explain death.

Then Entrefort exposed the ascending aorta.

Upon its outer wall lay a small, fibrous nodule, scarcely
larger than a pea. He opened the vessel longitudinally. The
inner surface beneath that nodule was smooth and perfectly healed.

There was no blade.

Rowell stared at the tissues.

"It has migrated."

"Impossible," said Entrefort.

"Then where is it?"

Entrefort dissected the fibrous nodule with exquisite care. From its
centre he removed a black speck of metal no larger
than a grain of sand. He placed it upon a
porcelain dish.

"That," said he, "is all that remains."

I looked at him in bewilderment.

"The acid?"

"Yes."

Rowell shook his head.

"You cannot mean that diluted muriatic acid passed unchanged through
the blood and attacked the steel."

"Not unchanged, and not directly. The prescription altered the chemical
conditions of digestion and absorption only slightly; but slightly, continued
over years, was enough. The tissues surrounding a foreign body
are not inert. Fluids pass through them; chlorides are present;
oxidation proceeds. I intended to encourage the slow corrosion of
the blade while the artery strengthened about the puncture. Had
the metal disappeared too quickly, haemorrhage might have followed. Had
it remained entire, its mechanical danger would have continued. Therefore
the doses were increased by degrees."

"And the vessel healed behind it?" asked I.

"About it first, then across its track as the point
diminished. Observe the cicatricial tissue. The blade must have ceased
to enter the lumen more than a year ago. For
several months, perhaps longer, Vane was in no physical danger
whatever."

Rowell glanced toward the room in which the body lay.

"Then what killed him?"

Entrefort washed his hands before replying.

"Fear."

The word seemed more dreadful than any anatomical explanation.

"The sight of my wife," he continued, "produced sudden terror.
He believed the blade displaced. That belief accelerated the heart,
disordered respiration, and overwhelmed the nervous centres. There are deaths
from inhibition of the heart through the vagus; deaths from
shock without visible lesion; deaths because the mind announces catastrophe
and the body obeys. He survived a puncture of the
aorta, yet died of an injury which did not exist."

"And Celestine?" I asked.

Entrefort's face became unreadable.

"She knew nothing of his condition. She believed him long
recovered—or dead. When I met her abroad she confessed that,
in a moment of madness, she had wounded a man
who refused to marry her. She did not name him.
I did not ask. There are questions which a husband
omits and a surgeon should not."

He looked down at the infinitesimal relic upon the dish.

"She desired revenge four years ago," said I bitterly.

"Yes."

"And achieved it yesterday without lifting her hand."

Entrefort covered the fragment with a glass.

"Not she," he said. "I warned him that fear might
kill him. He remembered the warning after the danger had
passed. Had I told him what the acid was intended
to do, he might have taken hope—or refused the experiment,
or poisoned himself by increasing the dose. I chose secrecy.
It saved his artery and destroyed his peace."

For once there was no sardonic light in that extraordinary
man's eyes.

Arnold was buried three days later. The official certificate attributed
death to cardiac failure following nervous shock, a phrase at
once accurate and wholly inadequate. Celestine did not attend the
funeral. Within the year she and Entrefort left England, and
I never saw her again.

The jewelled hilt of the stiletto came into my possession
with Arnold's papers. I keep it locked away. Its ruby
still glows with a sombre fire, and the little silver
serpents still twist about the empty socket from which the
blade once sprang. Visitors who see it admire the workmanship
and ask why so beautiful an object was mutilated.

I seldom answer.

For to explain it is to confess a truth more
disturbing than any tale of murder: that steel may rust,

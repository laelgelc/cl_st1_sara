### The Wood Beyond the Hayfield

It was in the latter part of June, in a
year which I need not particularise, that I walked from
Minehead towards a small village lying somewhere between the Quantock
country and the eastern verge of Exmoor. I had no
business there, nor, so far as I knew, had anyone
in the village the least business with me. I was
making one of those leisurely journeys which a man, if
he be fortunate, undertakes in youth and remembers in age—not
because anything of consequence appeared likely to happen upon the
road, but because the road itself, winding where it pleased
through orchards, over combes, and beside the murmuring hedges, seemed
sufficient occupation for the day.

My knapsack was light, my map tolerably accurate, and the
weather of that ripe and spacious kind which makes even
distance amiable. During the morning I had passed through a
region so saturated with summer that every living thing appeared
to be yielding up its own peculiar essence to the
sun. Honeysuckle hung in warm clusters over the banks; foxgloves
burned like little towers of rose-coloured flame under the hazels;
and here and there, where the road rose above the
hedgerows, I caught a pale glimmer of the Bristol Channel,
laid as smoothly in the distance as a strip of
beaten metal. There was sea-salt in the air, though the
water itself was many miles away, and beneath that wandering
savour lay the richer scents of hay, warm earth, elder
blossom and the innumerable green things crushed underfoot.

By one o'clock I was hungry. I had breakfasted early,
and my confidence in the provision of village inns had
not induced me to carry more than a crust and
an apple. The map showed a hamlet a mile farther
on, and there, after descending a lane of such depth
and narrowness that the branches almost met above my head,
I came upon a low, whitewashed house bearing the sign
of the Golden Hart.

The inn stood withdrawn from the road behind a small
orchard. A stone path, bordered by thyme and pinks, led
between two lines of apple-trees to the porch; and beyond
the house, seen through an open gate, green meadows dropped
towards a brook and rose again in long, sunlit slopes.
The place had an air of secluded sufficiency, as though
the whole summer had entered there and, finding no reason
to depart, had remained asleep among the leaves.

I knocked with my stick upon the open door. Presently
a young woman appeared from the passage, drying her hands
upon her apron.

She was, I thought, about twenty years of age, though
country youth, having fewer artificial gradations than ours, is not
always easy to determine. She was tall and strongly made,
with brown hair, grey eyes, and a colour which the
heat had deepened in her cheeks. There was nothing consciously
graceful in her manner; yet the ease with which she
stood, one hand still holding the corner of her apron,
the other resting against the door, belonged to that natural
harmony which painters labour to recover after civilisation has lost
it.

"Can I have something to eat?" I asked.

"Yes, sir. There's cold ham, if that'll do, and eggs
can be got directly."

It would do excellently. She led me through the passage
to a small parlour at the side of the house,
apologising because the larger room was being cleaned. The parlour
contained a round table, several horsehair chairs, a faded engraving
of the Queen, two framed samplers, and a glass cupboard
crowded with china shepherdesses, mugs, shells and funeral cards. Its
chief attraction, however, was a bow-window which looked across the
orchard towards the hill.

"You won't mind the room being a bit close?" she
said. "I'll open the window."

She did so, and at once the room was filled
with light, birdsong and the drowsy labouring of bees. Then
she went away to prepare my meal.

It was only after I had removed my knapsack and
sat down that I perceived I was not alone.

An old man occupied a chair in the farthest corner,
where the curtain, stirred now and then by the air,
cast moving shadows over him. His presence surprised me, for
the girl had not addressed him, nor had I noticed
him upon entering; but there was no reason why another
guest should not have been placed in the same parlour.
I bowed, and he inclined his head gravely in return.

He was dressed as an elderly labourer or small farmer
might have dressed twenty years before. His corduroy breeches were
worn almost white at the knees; his stockings were of
grey wool; and a faded bottle-green coat, too large for
his thin body, hung loosely from his shoulders. Between his
hands he held an ash stick, polished by long use.
His boots were dusty, and there were fragments of dry
grass caught upon them, as though he had crossed fields
to reach the inn.

Yet these rustic particulars, exact as I remember them, do
not convey the impression he made upon me. His body
was bent and spare, but the head rising from it
had a nobility which no poverty of dress could disguise.
The hair, very thick and perfectly silver, swept back from
a broad forehead; the nose was aquiline, the mouth finely
formed, and the eyes—at first I supposed them pale blue—seemed
to retain and return whatever light entered the room. They
did not glitter; rather they possessed a steady interior radiance,
such as one sees in deep water when the sky
shines through it.

"You have walked far," he said.

His voice was a second surprise. It was gentle, cultivated
and singularly resonant, without any trace of the local accent.
I answered that I had come about eleven miles, and
hoped to do another eight before evening.

"Eight," he repeated, as if the number had some interest
for him. "Then you are going northward?"

I showed him my map and indicated the road I
intended to follow. He leaned forward, though not, I thought,
to inspect the paper.

"That way is longer than it appears," he said. "The
hills deceive us. But all journeys do that."

"They generally seem longer to the feet than to the
eye," I replied.

A smile moved over his face.

"Sometimes they are shorter. Sometimes a whole life is but
a step from one field into another."

I took this for the reflection of an old man
and smiled in my turn. The conversation might have ended
there had not the innkeeper's daughter entered with bread, cheese
and a jug of cider.

"Will the gentleman have the ham now?" she asked.

I said I would, and glanced towards my companion, expecting
her to ask whether he also required anything. She neither
looked at him nor made the slightest alteration in her
movements to avoid him. Indeed, when she drew back the
curtain, her arm seemed almost to pass across his shoulder.

"Is your friend dining?" I asked.

"My friend, sir?"

"This gentleman."

She turned towards the corner. The old man looked steadily
at me.

"There's no one there, sir," she said.

Her colour had changed—not dramatically, but enough for me to
observe it.

I rose. The chair was occupied; the ash stick stood
between the old man's knees; one of his dusty boots
rested upon the red pattern of the carpet. I could
have described every crease of his coat. Nevertheless, the girl
continued to look through him at the wall.

"The old gentleman beside the window," I said.

She put down the plate she carried.

"Are you feeling poorly, sir? It's powerful hot outside."

I confess that my first conclusion was not supernatural. I
believed either that she was playing some rustic joke, though
her face made that unlikely, or that the heat and
long walk had disturbed my sight. I looked away, pressed
my fingers against my eyelids, and looked again. He had
not moved.

"Never mind," I said. "Perhaps the sun has been too
much for me."

At the word "sun" the old man lowered his eyes.

The girl remained for a moment, regarding me with an
uneasiness she could not conceal; then, after asking whether I
wanted mustard, she left the room.

"You have frightened her," I said.

"It is not I who have frightened her."

"Can she not see you?"

"She saw me once."

His tone discouraged a trivial answer. Outside, the orchard appeared
to have grown unnaturally bright. Every apple-leaf was edged with
fire; the grass beneath lay in a green so profound
that the shadows seemed woven into it, and the blackbird's
song, repeated from tree to tree, came upon the air
like waves of lucid sound.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"A man who lived here."

"And you do not live here now?"

"I moved."

He said it simply. Yet the word, which in ordinary
usage might have meant no more than a change of
parish, seemed to open beneath us like a depth.

"Where did you move?"

"Not far. No one moves far."

I had ceased to feel hungry, though the ham, brown
and fragrant, stood before me. The old man watched the
orchard, and his face had assumed an expression of impersonal
benevolence mingled, I thought, with weariness. It was not the
weariness of disease or age, but of one who had
travelled through many protean appearances and found in each the
same sorrow under another name.

"Why have you come back?" I asked.

"For someone."

The room appeared suddenly cooler.

"For someone here?"

"For one who is needed."

"Needed where?"

"Elsewhere."

These answers, which might have sounded absurd in another mouth,
neither provoked nor satisfied me. They carried authority, but not
menace. If I feared him, it was with that higher
fear which sometimes accompanies great music or the sight of
mountains at evening—a sense, not that harm approaches, but that
the familiar boundaries of one's being have grown frail.

He rose. I had supposed him infirm, yet he stood
with ease; and as he did so, his faded coat
and humble clothes seemed for an instant merely accidental, like
garments adopted for a purpose and soon to be laid
aside.

"You are willing to see," he said.

"I do not know what you mean."

"No. But you are willing."

He moved towards the door. At the threshold he turned.

"Come to the Wood of the Dead at midnight."

I knew no wood of that name. I said as
much.

"It lies beyond the third meadow, under the northward hill.
There is a path from the churchyard."

"And if I come?"

"You will see whom I have come for."

The prospect, thus baldly expressed, was sufficiently dreadful; yet his
face retained so tender and serene an expression that the
dread was dissolved in wonder.

"Why should I be shown?"

"Because you saw me."

He passed through the doorway.

I followed at once into the passage. It was empty.
The outer door stood open, displaying the orchard, the path
and the sun-struck road beyond; but no figure crossed the
brightness. I went outside and looked behind the house. There
was nowhere a man could have concealed himself in those
few seconds. The grass at the edge of the path,
long enough to have bent beneath a foot, stood upright
and motionless.

When I returned to the parlour, the old man's chair
had been pushed back from the wall. Upon the carpet,
where his boots had rested, lay three or four dry
blades of grass.

I ate some bread and ham, drank the cider, and
endeavoured to consider the affair rationally. Fatigue, heat and hunger
may produce strange disturbances; and it was not impossible that
some peculiarity of light and curtain had provided the crude
material from which imagination constructed a figure. But hallucinations do
not generally converse with consistency, cast shadows, disturb chairs, or
leave grass upon carpets. Moreover, I knew, even then, that
I had not imagined him. Whatever he had been, I
had seen him as plainly as I saw the table.

The girl came presently to remove the dishes. She entered
reluctantly and did not approach the corner.

"Are you better now, sir?"

"Perfectly, thank you. And I must apologise if I alarmed
you."

She gathered the plates, but her eyes remained fixed upon
me.

"What was he like?"

The question was almost whispered.

I described the old man—his silver hair, green coat, corduroys
and ash stick. Before I had finished, she set the
plate down again, lest, I suppose, she should drop it.

"Did he speak?"

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

"That he had come for someone."

At this she turned so pale that I sprang up
and offered her the chair. She refused it, gripping the
table instead.

"Come for someone," she repeated. "Oh, sir—are you sure those
were his words?"

"As sure as I can be of anything that has
happened in this room. Who is he?"

She crossed to the door, called for her mother, then
reconsidered and shut it.

"That was Master Henslow," she said. "It must have been.
And if he's been seen—"

She broke off.

"Tell me about him."

At first she would say little. Country people, though often
rich in legend, are reluctant to expose it to the
educated stranger, whose disbelief they anticipate and secretly despise. But
I assured her that I had no intention of laughing;
and after I had paid for my meal—an action which,
by restoring the ordinary relation between guest and inn, seemed
to reassure her—she sat beside the window and told me
the history.

Matthew Henslow had been born, she said, at Ashbrook Farm,
a little above the village. His family had occupied it
for generations and might once have been gentlefolk, though in
later days they were reckoned substantial yeomen. He inherited the
farm, married a woman from near Dulverton, and lived to
a great age. He had no child of his own;
but he stood godfather to half the parish, settled quarrels,
lent money without interest, and paid secretly for the schooling
of boys who would otherwise have gone at ten years
old into the fields. When a labourer was injured, Henslow
saw that the household did not starve; when fever came,
his wife moved from cottage to cottage carrying broth and
linen. There was, the girl said, scarcely a family within
six miles that did not owe him gratitude.

"He was a wonderful kind gentleman," she added. "Though he'd
look straight through a lie, and there was no deceiving
him."

In middle age he began to speak of the wood.

It was a small plantation of Scotch firs beyond the
churchyard, planted, according to one account, upon the site of
an older grove. Its true name was Blackthorn Copse, but
no one in the village called it that. From a
time earlier than living memory it had been known as
the Wood of the Dead. There was nothing sinister in
its appearance by day. Children gathered cones there; lovers used
the path beneath the hill; and the rector himself had
once publicly rebuked the villagers for attaching heathen fancies to
half an acre of perfectly ordinary trees.

Henslow claimed, nevertheless, that on certain evenings he saw people
enter the wood who had no earthly reason for doing
so. They did not always appear as they were at
the time. An old woman might pass him with the
figure she had possessed at seventeen; a child might go
by with the grave face of an adult. Some wore
wedding clothes, some working clothes, some garments of a fashion
long outmoded. They never returned by the path.

At first he told only his wife. Later, when certain
of those he had seen died soon afterwards, the story
spread. He disliked the gossip and refused to be treated
as a prophet. He did not know, he said, when
death would come, nor by what outward cause; he saw
only that the person had been called and was already,
in some fashion, upon the journey.

"Did he always see them before they died?" I asked.

"So they say. My grandmother's sister was one. He saw
her go in carrying a basket of white roses, though
there wasn't a rose blooming then, it being February. She
died at Easter, and they put white flowers on her
grave."

The girl's own mother had been left an orphan when
young, and Henslow had taken a special interest in her.
He had paid the premium for her apprenticeship to a
dressmaker in Taunton; later, when she married the present innkeeper,
he gave them money towards the lease of the Golden
Hart. The daughter had therefore known him during the first
six years of her life.

"I remember sitting on his knee," she said. "He had
a silver watch, and he'd let me listen to it.
He used to call me his little maid. When he
died, he left me a sovereign to buy a Bible."

Henslow's wife had died first. He was said to have
seen her enter the wood many months before her illness,
wearing the blue gown in which she had been married.
After her death he became more solitary. One evening, returning
from church, he met a neighbour near the gate and
remarked, with perfect composure, that he had just watched himself
go among the trees.

He died three days later in his sleep.

Since that time his apparition had occasionally been reported in
the parish. A shepherd saw him standing beside the wood
before the death of the rector; a child spoke with
him upon the bridge the week before her infant brother
died; and once, during an autumn storm, he had been
observed crossing the churchyard while the bell rang of its
own accord—or was believed to have done so. The tales
differed in detail, but upon one point all agreed.

"He comes before a death," said the girl.

The blackbird in the orchard had ceased singing. I was
conscious instead of the faint clink of glasses in another
room and of a fly beating against the windowpane.

"What is your name?" I asked, though I hardly know
why.

"Margaret Vellacott, sir. They call me Meg."

"And has anyone seen him lately?"

"Not these nine years. Not since little Tom Prowse was
drowned."

She looked again at the vacant chair.

"But he spoke to you. That's worse."

"Why worse?"

"Because he's never spoke since he died—not that I've heard.
If he says he's come for someone, then someone must
go."

She rose and took up the plates. Her distress was
evident; but youth, even when alarmed, cannot long remain entirely
abstracted from its own concerns. A sound of whistling came
from the yard, and a robust young man passed the
window carrying a pitchfork over his shoulder. He had a
brown, handsome face, his shirt was open at the throat,
and fragments of hay clung to his hair.

He saw Meg and grinned. Her gravity broke for an
instant into a smile.

"That's George Rattenbury," she said, with a blush which told
me more than the name. "He works at Highercombe."

The young man tapped the window with the handle of
his fork.

"Coming up to the long field, Meg?" he called. "We'll
have it turned before supper."

"If Father can spare me."

"You promised."

"I promised no such thing."

But she smiled again, and he went on whistling.

This innocent exchange restored proportion to the room. Whatever Henslow's
appearance signified, it seemed monstrous to connect it with the
sun-browned young fellow outside or the vigorous girl beside me.
Death, though continually active, is difficult to associate with those
in whom life speaks loudly. My reason therefore resumed its
accustomed office and advised me that village traditions, a hot
road, and the half-conscious perception of some passing wayfarer had
combined to deceive me.

I asked whether there was accommodation for the night. Meg
said they had one spare bedroom, though a commercial traveller
sometimes used it on Thursdays. This was Tuesday. I engaged
the room, partly because the day had grown oppressively warm
and partly—indeed chiefly—because the invitation to the wood had already
taken possession of my mind.

The afternoon passed with a slowness almost ceremonial. I walked
through the village, visited the church, examined Henslow's monument and
traced the path towards the wood. His stone was set
in the north wall of the nave. The inscription, after
recording the dates of his birth and death, praised "a
life of humility, charity and steadfast hope." Beneath the text
someone had placed fresh daisies in a jar.

From the churchyard gate I could see the plantation under
the hill. It lay beyond three meadows, precisely as he
had said: a dark, narrow congregation of pines, standing so
compactly together that, although the afternoon sun poured over the
fields around it, little light appeared to enter among the
trunks. There were taller and more extensive woods upon the
surrounding slopes, yet the eye returned continually to this one.
Its darkness did not seem due merely to shade. It
had the positive quality of a presence.

I approached within a hundred yards, then stopped. That I
should hesitate in broad daylight annoyed me; but the reluctance
was bodily rather than rational, as if the air itself
had thickened. Presently a jay flew out with a harsh
cry, and the commonplace movement released me. I returned to
the inn by a different path.

The Golden Hart became lively towards evening. Labourers came in
for cider; a carrier stopped with news from neighbouring farms;
and the innkeeper, a stout, cheerful man, returned from market.
I ate bread, cold beef and pickled onions in the
kitchen, listening while the hay harvest was discussed with a
gravity more commonly given to affairs of state. The weather,
all agreed, must hold another four days. Highercombe's long field
would be ready to carry on Thursday. George Rattenbury, seated
opposite Meg, declared that he could smell rain when it
was fifty miles away, and there was none tonight.

Meg reproved him for boasting. He answered that she would
believe him after they were married.

The kitchen erupted in laughter. She threw a crust at
him, scarlet with embarrassment, but not displeased. The sight affected
me strangely. Their future appeared so natural and complete—the wedding,
the tenancy of a small farm, children, increasing substance, middle
age beneath familiar trees—that to doubt it seemed an offence
against the visible order of things.

No mention was made of Henslow. Meg had evidently kept
our conversation to herself.

At ten o'clock the household retired. I was shown to
a low bedroom beneath the eaves, where the sheets smelled
of lavender and the small window looked towards the church
tower. I lay down fully clothed, intending to rest until
half past eleven.

I did not sleep. The day's images moved through my
mind with extraordinary vividness: the sea glimmering beyond the hills,
Henslow's luminous eyes, the dry grass on the carpet, Meg
smiling at George through the open window. At times I
thought I heard footsteps in the road, but each sound
proved to be the movement of leaves or the creaking
timbers of the inn. The village sank into a silence
so profound that the ticking watch beside me grew intolerably
loud.

At eleven-thirty I rose, put on my boots, and went
downstairs.

The moon had not yet risen. Outside, the warmth of
the day lingered among the walls, but in the meadows
a cool breath moved over the grass. No light showed
in the inn. I crossed the orchard, unlatched the gate
and took the lane towards the church.

It is curious how different a known place becomes at
night. The cottages, harmless in sunshine, withdrew behind their gardens
and appeared to listen. The church tower rose without detail
against a sky crowded with stars. Even the road had
lost the character of a road; it was a pale
suggestion passing between masses of darkness, and my own footsteps
seemed not to advance along it but to disturb something
sleeping beneath it.

The churchyard gate stood open.

I entered. The gravestones leaned at various angles in the
starlight, some white and distinct, others scarcely separable from the
earth. There was no wind here. The yews did not
stir; the grass, silvered with dew, bent silently under my
boots. As I passed the porch, the church clock gave
a preliminary click, then struck twelve.

Each note seemed to travel far beyond the capacity of
the little bell. The first sounded above me; the second
answered from the hill; the third trembled among the stars.
By the twelfth, time itself appeared to have been beaten
thin, until past and future lay almost transparent upon the
immense Present.

At the farther gate he waited.

I could not say that I saw Henslow arrive. One
moment the gate showed empty against the field; the next
his bent figure was there, both hands resting upon the
ash stick. His clothes were the same, but his face
had altered—or perhaps night revealed what daylight had concealed. It
was paler, more august, and very sorrowful.

"You came," he said.

"I said I would."

"No. You did not say it. But you came."

He turned into the meadow.

I followed him. Dew soaked my boots at once, though
he seemed to leave no track in the grass. The
first meadow rose gently from the churchyard; the second dipped
towards a brook, which we crossed by stepping-stones; the third
lay broad and flat beneath the hill. Beyond it stood
the Wood of the Dead.

As we approached, the ordinary sounds of night diminished. I
had heard an owl near the church, the rustle of
some small animal in the hedge, and water moving under
the stones. One by one these ceased, until even the
noise of my own passage through the grass seemed detached
from me and very far away.

Henslow did not look back.

At the edge of the wood he paused. The pines
rose black and columnar before us, their upper branches woven
into a roof through which only fragments of starlight entered.
A narrow path ran between them. It was not wholly
dark; the fallen needles possessed a faint grey lustre, and
the trunks, repeating themselves into the distance, resembled the pillars
of an immeasurable ruin.

"What am I to see?" I asked.

"What is already so."

"Has someone died?"

"That word belongs to clocks."

I wanted to question him further, but he lifted one
hand. The gesture was neither a command nor warning; it
was more like that of a musician asking silence before
the first note.

We entered.

The resinous scent of the pines deepened. It mingled with
another fragrance, ineffable and elusive—roses, perhaps, or new-mown hay, or
the breath of orchards after rain. At each step the
darkness seemed to unfold rather than recede. I lost all
sense of the meadow behind us. The path curved, though
I knew the wood could not be extensive enough to
contain so long a way; and once, looking between the
trunks, I fancied I saw not the adjoining field but
a remote and luminous country under unfamiliar stars.

Then the old man stopped.

"Look," he said.

At first there was nothing before us but a wall
of shadow. Gradually a point of gold appeared in it,
no larger than a candle-flame. It widened, brightened and spread
noiselessly from trunk to trunk, until the darkness thinned like
smoke.

I saw a hayfield at noon.

The transformation was so complete that I cried out. A
moment before I had stood in midnight beneath the pines;
now dazzling sunshine lay upon a broad field enclosed by
flowering hedges. The sky was of an almost intolerable blue.
Hay, newly cut and partly gathered into rows, covered the
slope with a pale golden roughness; every stalk seemed individually
lit, and above it quivered that transparent haze born of
great heat.

Men and women were at work. Their shirts and dresses
shone in the sun; forks flashed; the air was full
of voices, laughter, the rasp of rakes and the soft
collapsing sound of hay being turned. Yet all these noises
reached me with a peculiar fullness, as though each were
not merely heard but seen, felt and tasted. Laughter broke
like cool water; the metallic ring of a tool sent
a silver brightness across the field; the scent of hay
moved visibly in warm amber waves.

I looked behind me. Henslow stood at my side, but
the wood was still there—dark trunks at his back, night
above his head. In front of us burned the summer
noon. We occupied the border of two incompatible worlds.

"What place is this?"

"You know it."

I did. It was Highercombe's long field, which I had
seen from the lane that afternoon.

"And when?"

He did not answer.

The labourers came nearer. Among them I recognised George Rattenbury,
bareheaded and brown-armed, pitching hay upon a wagon. He appeared
not to see us. Presently he leapt down, wiped his
forehead with his sleeve and called to someone beyond my
line of vision.

Meg came running over the slope.

She wore a light print dress with the sleeves rolled
above her elbows, and a broad straw hat hung by
its ribbons against her back. Her hair had loosened in
the heat; her face was bright with exertion and amusement.
I had thought her beautiful at the inn, but in
the field she seemed the very embodied spirit of that
golden day—young, ardent, wholly at one with sun, earth and
the fragrant abundance around her.

George held up a cup.

"You'll want this," he said.

"That's mine," she answered. "You've no call to steal it."

"I kept it safe."

"You drank half."

"To see it wasn't poisoned."

She tried to take the cup; he lifted it beyond
her reach. She sprang for it, laughing, and he caught
her round the waist with his free arm. For a
moment she struggled, though not seriously. The other haymakers shouted
encouragement and advice.

"Let me go, George!"

"Say when you'll marry me."

"When the moon turns green."

"That'll be tonight, then."

"You great fool!"

"Michaelmas?"

"No."

"Christmas?"

"No."

"Before next haymaking?"

At this she ceased struggling. Their laughter softened; and though
I could not hear what she said, he lowered the
cup and bent his face towards hers. The whole future
which I had imagined in the kitchen seemed again to
gather around them, richer now and more inevitable. The field
itself appeared to consent. Daisies shone at their feet; the
hedges leaned inward under the burden of roses; high above,
a lark poured down such ecstasy that joy and pain
became indistinguishable.

I turned to Henslow.

"Why do you show me this?"

His gaze rested upon Meg.

"Because this also is true."

He stepped forward to the margin of the sunlight.

"Margaret," he called.

He did not raise his voice. Nevertheless, the name crossed
the field with a depth and sweetness greater than any
human sound I have heard. It passed through the laughter
without silencing it, through the lark's song without breaking it,
and through my own body as though every bone had
become a resonant string.

No one moved except Meg.

She released George's hand and looked towards us.

Her face changed. The mischief and brightness remained, but beneath
them appeared a recognition so profound that it seemed older
than her life. George said something and tried to draw
her back. She did not hear him.

Again Henslow called.

"Margaret."

She came.

At first she walked; then, as one who sees home
after long absence, she hurried. The haymakers continued their labour.
George turned away, smiling as if she still stood beside
him. None saw her cross the field.

"Stop her," I said.

Henslow's eyes were filled with compassion.

"From what?"

"From coming here—from leaving them."

"She has heard."

I ran forward—or attempted to do so. At the boundary
between wood and field an invisible resistance met me. It
was not a wall; rather it was the weight of
the whole natural order, gentle but immovable. I could no
more cross it than a reflection can step out of
glass.

Meg approached. As she did, the sunlight around her became
increasingly intense, until her figure seemed woven of brightness. Yet
I saw every familiar detail: the flush in her cheeks,
the strand of hair stuck to her temple, the small
tear in the cuff of her dress, and a spray
of clover caught at her waist.

She entered the wood.

The moment she passed the first pine, the straw hat
fell from her back and vanished; the heat left her
face; her limbs appeared suddenly heavy. She was still the
young woman from the hayfield, but a great fatigue had
descended upon her.

Henslow opened his arms.

She rested against him with the simplicity of a child.
Her head sank upon his breast; one hand clung loosely
to the lapel of his faded green coat.

"Father," she said.

The word startled me, for I knew he was not
her father; but it seemed to name, not a relation
of blood, but the refuge she found in him.

"My little maid," he answered.

"I am so tired."

"I know."

"It was hot in the field."

"Yes."

"I wanted to stay. George was there, and everything was
so happy."

"I know."

Her eyes lifted to his.

"But you called me."

"I called."

"And I have come willingly."

At that, the old man bent his noble head and
kissed her forehead.

The hayfield vanished.

There was no fading, no gradual return of darkness. Sun,
sky, labourers, flowers and laughter were extinguished together, as a
picture may be withdrawn from a lantern. I found myself
among the pines at midnight, with my hands outstretched and
my heart beating violently.

Henslow and Meg had disappeared.

For some moments I remained where I was, unable either
to advance or retreat. The ordinary night slowly reassembled itself.
Wind moved high in the branches. Somewhere beyond the wood
a sheep coughed. The resinous odour of the pines returned,
but beneath it lingered the warm sweetness of hay.

I called Henslow's name. There was no reply.

I called "Margaret."

At once, far within the wood—or far beyond it—a girl
laughed. The sound was neither mocking nor sorrowful. It was
the same laughter I had heard in the sunny field,
but purified of distance and desire, and it left behind
a silence of such vastness that I fell upon my
knees.

What occurred in my mind during the next minutes, if
minutes they were, I cannot adequately record. I had a
momentary glimpse of a mightier universe, not remote from ours
but interpenetrating it at every point—a universe in which our
divisions of before and after, presence and absence, possession and
loss, were the poor conventions of creatures who see only
one surface of an immeasurable design. I understood nothing; yet
I seemed to stand on the verge of understanding all.
My highest joy contained all possible pain, and my dread
was filled with a tenderness too great to endure.

Then the glimpse closed. I was a tired traveller kneeling
on damp needles in a little Somerset wood.

I made my way back with difficulty. The path, which
had seemed interminable behind Henslow, brought me to the meadow
in scarcely a dozen steps. The moon had risen above
the hill, turning the wet grass white. As I crossed
the brook, I looked into the water and scarcely recognised
my own face.

The inn was silent. I entered by the door I
had left unlatched and climbed the stairs. Before reaching my
room I heard a movement below; Meg stood in the
passage in her nightdress, holding a candle.

"Is that you, sir?"

"Yes."

"You've been out."

"I could not sleep."

She lifted the candle. Its flame made a little golden
cave in the darkness, and within it her living face
looked grave but untroubled.

"Did you go to the wood?"

I hesitated.

"Yes."

"And did you see him again?"

"Yes."

Her fingers tightened upon the candlestick.

"Who had he come for?"

There are moments when truth, though demanded, would be a
form of cruelty. Moreover, what truth could I have told?
The vision might represent a death already decreed, a symbolic
warning, an hallucination born of the legend, or some possibility
which knowledge itself might alter. I had no right to
place its burden upon her.

"I could not tell," I said. "There was no one
I knew."

She searched my face. Whether she believed me I cannot
say.

"I'm glad of that," she whispered. "I've been thinking of
it all evening."

Then she smiled with an embarrassed return to the ordinary.

"George says old stories can't hurt anyone. He says we'll
laugh about it at Christmas."

"I hope you will."

"So do I. Good night, sir."

"Good night, Meg."

She went back to her room. I watched the candlelight
diminish along the passage and disappear under the door.

I left early next morning. A mist lay over the
valley, luminous and faintly blue, but above it the hilltops
were already gold. Meg prepared bread and coffee for me.
She seemed pale, though she said she had slept well.
George came into the orchard as I fastened my knapsack;
he was carrying two rakes and called impatiently that the
hay would not turn itself.

She laughed and ran to join him.

At the gate she looked back and waved.

I walked northward.

For several weeks I remained disturbed by what I had
seen. I wrote a full account while the particulars were
fresh, including the dialogue as nearly as memory allowed. Later
I attempted explanations. The old man might have been a
living person whom Meg, for some reason, pretended not to
see; the midnight vision might have been a dream or
trance induced by expectation; the sunny field might have been
no prophecy at all, but an imaginative construction assembled from
George, Meg and the village talk of haymaking.

Yet such explanations, however necessary to the reason, altered no
whit the quality of the experience. In dreams we usually
accept incongruities while they occur and discover them upon waking.
In the wood I had been conscious at every instant
of the impossibility before me. I had tested it, questioned
it, resisted it. And above all there remained the voice
which had called her by her own name.

HE had called her by her own name.

During the following months I heard nothing from the village.
I had no acquaintances there, and though I sometimes considered
writing to the inn, I could invent no pretext that
would not seem peculiar. By autumn the practical concerns of
life reasserted themselves. I went abroad that winter, returned in
spring, and might perhaps never have visited the place again
had not chance—or that agency to which we give the
name of chance when we cannot discern its intention—brought me
within twenty miles of it almost exactly a year later.

It was again June. The resemblance of the season awakened
memories I had not lost so much as buried. On
an impulse I altered my route and walked towards the
Golden Hart.

The day was overcast and cooler than the former one.
Rain had fallen during the night; drops clung to the
dog-roses, and the lanes smelled of wet earth. When I
reached the village, the church tower appeared smaller than I
remembered. The Wood of the Dead, seen beneath the hill,
was merely a patch of dark trees.

The inn stood unchanged behind its orchard. Yet before I
entered I knew that something was absent. It was not
a sound, for bees worked among the clover and a
cart rattled in the yard. Rather the place lacked that
inward illumination by which, on my first visit, every leaf
and stone had seemed to possess a secret life.

The innkeeper met me in the porch. He recognised me
after a moment and welcomed me with the professional warmth
accorded to a returning guest.

"You stayed the night last haymaking, didn't you, sir? Walked
up from Minehead?"

I said that I had.

"You'll be wanting dinner. We've a nice bit of mutton."

He led me to the same small parlour. The chair
in the corner had been removed; otherwise the room was
unchanged.

I asked, as casually as I could, whether his daughter
was at home.

His face became still.

"You hadn't heard, then?"

"No."

"Our Meg died last summer."

Though I had crossed many miles expecting those words, their
utterance deprived me of speech.

"It was the heat," he continued. "That terrible hot spell
we had just after you were here. She went up
to Highercombe to help with the long field. They were
turning hay all afternoon, and there wasn't a breath of
air. Towards four o'clock she complained of her head, then
dropped down insensible."

He looked through the window into the orchard.

"They carried her home. Doctor said it was sunstroke. She
came round once or twice, but fever set in. She
lasted six days."

"Was she conscious?"

"Not properly. She talked a deal, but we couldn't follow
it. Kept saying someone had called her and she mustn't
keep him waiting. Once she asked us to open the
door because Father had come."

"Her father?"

"I was beside her. She didn't mean me."

He shook his head, then added in the restrained tone
of one who has repeated the facts until they have
become a wall against grief:

"The doctor did all he could."

"And George Rattenbury?"

"They were to have been married at Michaelmas. He took
it hard. Went to Bristol after Christmas. Couldn't bear the
place."

The innkeeper left me to order the meal. I sat
by the open window, listening to the bees and the
faint voices from the yard. Upon the sill stood a
small Bible with a cracked leather cover. Inside it, written
in a childish hand, was Margaret Vellacott's name; beneath that,
in a firm old script, the words: "From her loving
godfather, Matthew Henslow."

After dinner I went to the churchyard.

Meg's grave lay near the north gate. The stone was
new and painfully white among the older monuments. It recorded
her age as twenty-one. Someone had planted daisies over her,
and at the head of the grave, though roses were
abundant everywhere, stood a little jar containing fresh-cut hay.

Beyond the gate the three meadows shone under a clearing
sky. Men were working in the farther field. Their voices
rose and fell upon the wind, too distant for words;
once a girl laughed, and my heart answered before reason
could forbid it.

I did not enter the wood. There was no need.

As I turned away, sunlight broke through the clouds and
travelled across the valley. It touched the church roof, the
wet gravestones, the meadow and, last of all, the dark
pines beneath the hill. For one instant the wood opened
into a depth of golden green, innocent and full of
peace. Then the clouds closed, and it was only a
little country plantation, whispering in the rain-washed air.

Whether Matthew Henslow came to foretell Margaret's death, to cause
it, or merely to reveal a journey already accomplished in
that immense Present of which our hours are scattered shadows,
I shall not presume to decide. The physician's account was
plain: the day had been hot, the labour severe, and
sunstroke sufficient to explain all that followed. I accept it.

Yet I remember the midnight wood; the field burning beneath
a sun not then risen; the young man's arm about
the laughing girl; and the old rustic waiting in his
faded coat, not as an executioner, but as one who
had travelled back to meet a tired child upon a
difficult road.

And sometimes, when summer is at its height and the
scent of hay comes through an open window, I hear
again that gentle voice crossing the bright field—not loud, but
irresistible—calling the beloved by her own name.

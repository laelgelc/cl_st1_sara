# THE CONDITIONS OF TERROR

At a little before midnight a boy named Ezra Venn,
having lost the wood-road which led from Dacey's mill to
his father's farm, came upon a light in the forest.

The discovery was not altogether reassuring. A light in a
forest may signify shelter, but it may also signify persons
from whom shelter is desirable. Ezra was a stout-hearted lad
of sixteen, descended from men who had disputed the wilderness
with bears, Indians, tax collectors, and other bodiless malevolences; nevertheless,
he approached with caution.

Between the black trunks of the pines he saw, first,
a faint yellow point, which appeared and disappeared as the
branches stirred. Advancing, he distinguished the form of a house—a
low, long building with a collapsed porch and a roof
that had sunk in the middle, as if beneath the
weight of some invisible occupant. One chimney remained; the other
had fallen through the roof. No fence enclosed the place,
though here and there among the weeds a grey post
leaned from the earth like a rotten tooth.

The house had been deserted longer than Ezra had lived.
It was known as the Breede place, and was said
to be haunted. In a thinly inhabited rural district, a
deserted house not said to be haunted would be regarded
as an architectural curiosity.

The light came from an unglazed window. Ezra crept toward
it, grateful for the silence of his own feet upon
the damp mould, and stopped beneath the sill. His eyes
were level with the lower edge of the opening.

Within, at a small table, sat a man reading.

The table was covered with dust except where the man's
sleeve had swept it clean. Upon it stood a single
candle in a brass socket, a watch, and a thick
manuscript tied with black thread. The room behind him was
wholly dark. The flame illuminated his hands, his shirt-front, and
his face, but did not seem to penetrate beyond these
things; he appeared less like a man seated in a
room than like certain detached portions of a man exhibited
against vacancy.

Ezra knew him. He was Willard Marsh, who wrote essays
for the city journals and had once addressed the schoolhouse
audience on "The Decline of Superstition." The decline had then
seemed both rapid and meritorious. It appeared now to have
been arrested.

Marsh's eyes moved across the pages, but at every few
lines they lifted toward a door at the farther side
of the room. That door was open. Beyond it there
was nothing visible.

Presently he took up his watch and held it near
the candle. His lips moved.

Ezra could not hear the words, yet he understood them
to be a computation of the minutes before midnight. The
boy's own heart, encouraged by the stillness to make itself
conspicuous, began beating with an audible insistence. He wondered if
the reader heard it. Marsh suddenly turned his face toward
the window.

It was dead-yellow and wet with perspiration.

The boy sank below the sill and remained there, ashamed
of his terror and therefore unable to overcome it. After
a little time he raised his head.

Marsh had resumed reading. One hand rested upon the manuscript;
the other gripped the edge of the table. The candle
flame stood perfectly upright. Nothing moved in the room—nothing but
the reader's eyes.

Then, from a tree immediately above the house, a screech-owl
uttered its cry.

Marsh sprang to his feet. The table overturned; the candle
went out.

The boy took to his heels.

He ran against trees, fell twice, tore his coat upon
a thorn-bush and, after wandering for nearly two hours, found
the road not fifty yards from the place where he
had lost it. His repentance for having looked through another
man's window was profound, passionate, and proportionate to its uselessness.

## THE DAY BEFORE

"Your error," said Colston, "is not that you disbelieve in
ghosts. Your error is that you do not know how
to read about them."

He said this in Marsh's library, at four o'clock of
a bright October afternoon. Sunlight filled the windows; a coal
fire burned with social rather than thermal intention; and on
a tray between the two men stood the materials of
a confidence which is commonly distilled from hot water.

Colston was a writer of tales. His admirers called him
original, his enemies called him diseased, and the booksellers called
him irregular. There were rumours that he used opium, but
rumours of vice are the revenge which dull minds take
upon intelligences whose operations they cannot follow.

Marsh smiled.

"I read from left to right," he said. "In exceptional
cases I turn the page."

"You read a tale of terror," Colston continued, ignoring him,
"at breakfast, with a spoon in your cup and a
tradesman at the door. Your wife asks whether the chimney
has been swept; the butcher's cart rattles in the street;
a dog contests a bone beneath the window. You arrive
at the apparition—and complain that you are not afraid."

"A competent apparition should survive a butcher's cart."

"No. Art is not required to overcome deliberate resistance. Music
demands silence; painting demands light; devotion demands solitude. Why should
terror alone be expected to work its effect amid every
circumstance hostile to it?"

"Because," replied Marsh, "terror pretends to deal with realities. A
murderer does not cease to be formidable because one meets
him after luncheon."

"But a written murderer is not a murderer. He is
a combination of marks upon paper, helpless until the reader
consents to become his accomplice. The author can open the
door, but the reader must enter. He must surrender the
cheerful room, the familiar furniture, the reassuring noises. He must
make himself accessible."

"To what?"

"To whatever waits."

Colston leaned forward. He was a thin man, with colourless
eyes and a pallor which in another might have suggested
ill health. In him it seemed rather the natural complexion
of thought.

"Give me," he said, "an abandoned house, a midnight hour,
a solitary candle. Put the reader beyond call, beyond interruption,
beyond those petty assurances by which the mind protects itself
from its own knowledge. Then give me a page—one page!—and
if I cannot make him afraid, I will burn everything
I have written."

"You ask the surroundings to do the work."

"I ask them only to silence contradiction. The forest says,
'You are alone.' The darkness says, 'Your senses are useless.'
The old house says, 'Men have suffered here, and may
suffer again.' My page says merely, 'Attend.'"

Marsh poured himself another glass.

"And if the reader remains unafraid?"

"Then he is either without imagination or without honesty."

"A convenient doctrine. Failure proves the critic a fool or
a liar."

"It may prove him dead."

Marsh laughed. Colston did not.

For a few seconds the only sound was the small
settling of coal in the grate. Outside, wheels passed over
the dry street, and the afternoon retained its innocent and
commercial appearance.

"You have something new, then?" Marsh asked.

"A manuscript."

"About a ghost?"

"That is an imperfect description."

"Is it good?"

"It is effective."

"On whom has it been tried?"

"On no one."

"Then your confidence is theological."

Colston rose and walked to the window. The glass reflected
his face against the sunlit street, giving him for an
instant the appearance of standing outside in a world less
substantial than the room.

"You know the Breede house?" he asked.

"The ruin beyond Dacey's wood?"

"It is not a ruin. One room is sound. There
is a table, a chair, and a door which will
not remain shut."

"How admirably furnished."

"The elder Breede died there forty years ago."

"Most old men die somewhere."

"He was found beside that table."

"That detail improves neither his death nor the table."

"His daughter declared that for three nights afterward she heard
him crossing the upper floor."

"There is no upper floor."

"There was then."

Marsh looked at his friend with amused attention.

"You have selected your theatre."

"I have. Go there tonight. Take this manuscript. Begin reading
at eleven. You are not to carry a lantern into
the room—only a candle. You are not to bring a
weapon, a dog, or a companion. You are not to
leave until you have finished."

"And then?"

"Then you may return and tell me whether the butcher's
cart was essential to your courage."

Marsh's smile faded slightly—not from fear, but from that irritation
which a courageous man feels when he perceives that his
courage has been privately assessed.

"You have arranged the matter prettily," he said. "If I
refuse, I lack honesty or imagination; if I consent, a
draught under the door will become evidence of genius."

"I claim no evidence. I offer an experiment."

"And if I fall asleep?"

"You will not."

Colston removed from inside his coat a packet of closely
written sheets, folded once and bound with black thread. He
placed it on the table. Marsh reached for it, but
Colston laid two fingers upon the packet.

"Do not open it before eleven."

"Another condition?"

"The last."

"Why?"

"Because knowledge, like wine, loses something by being left uncorked
in daylight."

Marsh took the packet.

"Very well. At eleven tonight, in the Breede house, alone,
by one candle. I shall read your dreadful production and
return in time to report that its punctuation is alarming."

Colston held out his hand. His own was cold.

"At midnight," he said, "you may revise that opinion."

"Why midnight?"

"That is explained in the manuscript."

As Marsh turned away, Colston added:

"I shall not see you again before then."

The words were ordinary enough, yet something in their arrangement
caused Marsh to look back. Colston was standing by the
fire, watching him.

"You speak as if one of us were going on
a journey."

"One of us is," said Colston.

## THE DAY AFTER

Ezra told his story at breakfast. His father first accused
him of drunkenness, then of cowardice, and finally of having
visited the Breede place in company with venturesome boys—three explanations
which, though mutually injurious, satisfied the paternal instinct for completeness.

The boy persisted. By nine o'clock his torn coat and
a smear of candle-grease upon one sleeve had obtained him
a hearing. At ten he set out for the house
with his father, two neighbours, the constable, and Silas Rook,
whose distinction was that he had once seen a corpse
before.

They found the outer door ajar. The passage smelled of
damp timber, mice, and something older than either. Ezra led
the way to the room, pausing upon the threshold with
a solemnity which the others mistook for fear. It was
pride. He had brought scepticism to the place where it
must apologize to Truth.

Here was the dead body of a man.

Willard Marsh lay upon his side beside the overturned table.
One arm was doubled beneath him; the other extended toward
the open doorway. His features were drawn backward in an
expression not easily referable to pain, and his open eyes
appeared fixed upon something at the height of a standing
man. Beneath his mouth was a small pool of saliva
in which a black beetle struggled with roaring wings.

Silas Rook, after a brief examination conducted chiefly with his
eyes, said that Marsh had died of fright. The constable
observed that "fright" was not a disease known to the
county records. Ezra's father suggested apoplexy. A neighbour discovered a
dark bruise at the dead man's temple and proposed that
he had struck it in falling. Thus science advanced by
the democratic method.

The candle had been crushed beneath the table. Marsh's watch
lay nearby, its crystal broken, its hands stopped at twelve
minutes after midnight.

The manuscript was found partly under the body.

At the inquest two days later, a physician testified that
Marsh's heart had been unsound and that violent emotion might
have caused death. Asked what had produced the violent emotion,
he replied that this was not a medical question. The
distinction was proper and gave universal dissatisfaction.

Colston could not be found.

He had left his lodging on the previous evening, shortly
after ten, wearing a dark coat and carrying no baggage.
The landlady reported that he had seemed unusually cheerful. As
she had never before seen him cheerful, the circumstance was
considered ominous.

The coroner read aloud the final page of the manuscript.
The earlier pages, he said, contained a tale of obscure
import concerning a man who could enter another's thoughts at
a fixed hour; but as no two listeners afterward agreed
upon its events, that summary must suffice.

The final page was not part of the tale. It
was addressed directly to Marsh.

## THE MANUSCRIPT

You have said that fear is a defect of evidence.
Before the candle expires you shall possess evidence, though I
think you will find the defect remains.

The story you have read was written to prepare not
your belief, but your attention. You are now listening. You
have listened for several minutes to the door behind you,
though nothing has crossed its threshold. That is as I
intended.

At midnight I shall come to you.

You will not hear my step in the passage, for
I shall not use the passage. You will not see
the door move, for I shall not use the door.
Nevertheless, when your watch marks the hour, look up.

If I have correctly judged both the conditions and the
reader, these pages will explain your death.

They may also explain mine.

C.

## FROM THE COUNTY TIMES, OCTOBER 19—

Yesterday afternoon Mr. Abel Breede, son-in-law of the late owner
of the deserted Breede property, obtained possession of the manuscript
found beside Mr. Marsh's body. Declaring that it contained "an
insult to the family and an encouragement to fools," he
burned it in the courthouse stove before a legal order
could prevent him.

Mr. Breede was censured for destroying possible evidence. He replied
that evidence which required a haunted house, a solitary reader,
and a candle was too particular in its habits to
be of public service.

No trace of Colston has been discovered.

On the night following the burning, Ezra Venn passed the
Breede place at a prudent distance. He afterward denied having
seen a light in the unglazed window. This denial might
have been more persuasive had anyone accused him of seeing
one.

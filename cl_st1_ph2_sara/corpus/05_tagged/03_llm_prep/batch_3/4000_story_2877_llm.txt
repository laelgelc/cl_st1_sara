## THE LAST VOYAGE OF LORD NELSON

For three days the Lochleven had lain upon the Pacific
as motionless as a painted ship upon a painted sea.

She was a full-rigged iron vessel of nearly two thousand
tons register, homeward-built and deep-water tried, though upon this voyage
she was bound outward from Cardiff to San Francisco with
four thousand tons of coal beneath her hatches. Her black
hull, newly painted before departure, shone in the strong equatorial
light; her yards were squared with mathematical exactness; and the
white canvas, hanging loose from the lifts, stirred only when
the heavy folds rolled with the long breathing of the
ocean.

There is no silence so complete as that of a
becalmed sailing ship. Even in a light air there are
the whisper of water along the bends, the occasional slap
of a rope against a mast, and the low complaint
of the rudder-chains. But when wind and current alike seem
to sleep, every little sound becomes separate and important. A
block creaks aloft; a sailor coughs under the forecastle-head; somewhere
in the galley a saucepan-lid falls with a clatter astonishing
enough to draw every eye.

The sea about the Lochleven resembled a vast mirror of
blue steel. Looking over the side, one could see to
a depth which appeared incredible. Pale lemon-coloured fish moved under
the counter; others, of deep orange and violet, flashed like
jewels through the clear water. A troop of pilot-fish had
attached themselves to the ship and attended her with the
solemnity of an escort.

These pretty creatures, striped with blue, are often supposed by
sailors to guide the shark, from which belief they have
obtained their name. Naturalists are less willing to credit them
with so charitable or so wicked an occupation. They probably
follow large moving bodies for the fragments of food that
may be disturbed or thrown overboard. Yet on the third
morning there could be no doubt that their customary companion
was present, for a long grey shadow glided from beneath
the bows, turned with scarcely a movement of its tail,
and showed a triangular fin above the polished surface.

Seven-year-old Howard Stafford watched it from the poop.

"Would he eat a man whole, mother?"

"He would not have the opportunity if the man remained
aboard," replied Mrs. Stafford.

This answer did not satisfy Howard, whose questions were seldom
diminished by answers.

"Would he eat Lord Nelson?"

Lord Nelson was a wooden sailor, nine inches high, dressed
in a blue coat painted with much gold braid. One
arm had been lost during a gale off the Cape,
but Howard maintained that this made the figure more faithful
to history. At present the little admiral stood upon the
rail, supported by a turn of spun-yarn round his waist,
and stared with an expression of varnished resolution at the
shark below.

"I think," said Mrs. Stafford, "that Lord Nelson had better
come away from the side."

Howard obeyed, though reluctantly. An hour later he was racing
along the main-deck upon the back of a turtle which
had been caught during the morning watch. The animal, a
great green creature weighing more than a hundredweight, had been
turned loose for exercise before being placed in the long-boat,
and Howard, with the cook's assistance, had mounted its broad
shell. The turtle advanced in patient misery, lifting its head
at each step, while its rider waved the one-armed admiral
and shouted orders to an imaginary fleet.

The sailors laughed. The men of a deep-water ship are
commonly kind to a child, though they may pretend otherwise;
and Howard, having been born at sea and passed more
of his short life upon planked decks than upon solid
ground, was accepted almost as one of the company. He
knew the names of the sails, understood why wet swabs
must not be left against ironwork, and had once helped
the steward count spoons. He was therefore a person of
consequence.

Captain Stafford watched the game from the break of the
poop. He was a grave, lean man of forty-five, with
a close brown beard already touched with grey and eyes
accustomed to search horizons. He smiled when the turtle turned
unexpectedly and Howard slid into the scupper. Then, still smiling,
he raised his head.

Again that slight smell of gas.

It was so faint that another man might have disregarded
it. There was no smoke, no unusual warmth, nothing visible
to excite alarm. The captain stood quite still, drew a
slow breath through his nostrils, and looked forward.

Coal, especially certain varieties of Welsh coal, may heat when
closely packed in a ship's hold. The smaller fragments expose
a large surface to the air; oxidation begins; heat is
produced faster than it can escape; and at last, unless
the process is checked, fire appears deep within the cargo.
Cardiff coal was famous for its power in steam furnaces,
but seamen knew that under unfavourable conditions it might carry
its own furnace across the world.

Captain Stafford called the chief mate.

"Mr. Rennick, bring the testing-rod."

The mate's cheerful face altered slightly, but he asked no
question. A long iron rod was fetched and driven down
through the coal wherever access could be obtained. When withdrawn,
it was passed rapidly from hand to hand. Most portions
felt cool. Near the after-end of the main hatch, however,
the lower part was distinctly warm.

"Open her up," said the captain.

The order went forward. The awning was furled, the hatch-cover
lifted, and the tarpaulins rolled aside. At once there issued
from the hold a breath of air warmer than that
upon the deck and carrying the faint, sickly taint the
captain had detected.

The men began removing coal. They worked in watches, stripped
to shirts and trousers, blackened within minutes by dust that
clung to their sweating bodies. Baskets were filled, hauled up,
and emptied upon the deck. Trenches were cut downward and
across the cargo in the hope of reaching the heated
portion and separating it from the rest.

By noon the tranquil character of the day had vanished,
although sea and sky remained unchanged. The decks, holystoned to
snowy cleanness at dawn, were covered with black heaps. Picks
rang below. Blocks squealed; baskets rose and fell; and the
officers moved continually between hatch and sounding-pipe.

Mrs. Stafford remained on the poop with Howard. She had
not been told the full extent of the captain's fear,
but a sailor's wife does not need to be told
everything.

"Is the coal on fire?" she asked when her husband
came aft.

"We have found heating."

"That was not what I asked."

"No flame has been found," he replied. "We may cut
it out before it develops."

She looked into his face and saw that this was
hope, not belief.

"What shall I do?"

"Keep the boy with you. Pack one small bag. Warm
clothing, water-bottles, biscuits—nothing else."

She glanced toward the companionway. In the cabin below were
letters from her dead father, a silver tea service presented
upon her marriage, Howard's baby clothes, books read during many
voyages, and the little possessions which make a moving house
a home. The Lochleven had been her home for six
years.

"One bag?" she repeated.

"One."

She inclined her head. There was no protest. Discipline at
sea cannot stop at the cabin door.

All that afternoon the crew laboured. A pump was rigged,
and water poured into the trench; but coal stows closely,
and water does not readily penetrate to a hidden seat
of fire. It may cool the upper layers while the
heat continues below. Worse still, the admission of air through
newly cut passages sometimes feeds the very combustion it is
intended to expose.

Toward evening a seaman working in the trench became dizzy.
He was hauled on deck, where he lay gasping until
the clean air restored him. Soon afterward another man complained
of violent pain behind the eyes.

"Out of it, all hands!" called Mr. Rennick.

The men emerged, stumbling and black-faced. A blue-grey vapour hovered
in the lower opening.

The captain ordered the hatch closed and covered. Oakum was
driven into every crevice; wet sails were spread over the
hatchway; and the edges were weighted with chain. The forward
ventilators were turned down and sealed. If the fire could
not be reached, it must be starved.

At sunset the ocean displayed a magnificence which seemed almost
insulting. The western sky passed from pale green to gold,
from gold to orange, and then to a crimson so
deep that the black spars stood against it like bars
of iron. Pink clouds lay along the horizon in terraces;
above them floated one solitary island of purple vapour, fringed
with flame. The colours were reflected under the ship until
she seemed suspended between two heavens.

Howard ate bread and jam beside the skylight.

"Will there be Sunday pudding to-morrow?" he asked.

Mrs. Stafford had forgotten that the next day was the
Sabbath.

"I expect the cook will do his best."

"Father says people mustn't do unnecessary work on Sunday."

"Your father also says that necessary work must be done
whenever it presents itself."

Howard considered this loophole in the Fourth Commandment.

"Is putting out a fire necessary work?"

"Very necessary."

"Then God won't mind?"

"No, dear."

He appeared comforted, and fed a crumb to Lord Nelson.

During the night the temperature beneath the hatch continued to
rise. The testing-rod, inserted through a small aperture which was
plugged immediately afterward, came up hot enough to be uncomfortable
in the hand. Yet there was still no visible smoke.
The ship retained her handsome and orderly appearance; the brass
of the binnacle gleamed; the boats hung in their davits;
the masts rose in perfect security against the stars. Only
those who walked her deck knew that a demon of
destruction was eating into her heart.

At eight bells Captain Stafford altered the watch arrangements. Two
men were stationed by each boat, provisions were brought from
the lazarette, and breakers of water were lashed beneath the
thwarts. Compasses, lanterns, signal rockets, charts, a sextant, fishing-lines, oilskins,
and medicine were divided among the three boats. The plugs
were examined; the falls cleared; and every grummet, rowlock, and
steering-oar was tested.

The long-boat would take the greater number of the crew.
The starboard lifeboat was assigned to Mr. Rennick, and the
port boat to the second mate. Captain Stafford intended to
remain with the long-boat, but not, as every man understood,
until the last possible moment.

No captain willingly abandons his ship. To a landsman, a
vessel is property, valuable perhaps, but replaceable. To the master
who has guided her through darkness, storm, and narrow water,
she is responsibility made visible. Her timbers have answered his
judgment; her canvas has carried his decisions; and every mile
of her track has been laid upon his conscience. So
long as a reasonable chance remained, Captain Stafford would not
leave the Lochleven.

Dawn put an end to suspense, but not to danger.
A little air came from the south-east, rippling the surface
and filling the upper sails. The courses were loosed; the
braces hauled; and the yards trimmed. Slowly the ship gathered
way.

A murmur of relief passed among the men. To be
moving was in itself a comfort.

The captain went to the chart-room. The Galapagos Islands lay
to the north-west, a lonely group of volcanic lands upon
the equator. They were still far away, and the wind
was uncertain, but they offered the nearest hope. He marked
the position, calculated the drift, and gave the helmsman a
course.

Mrs. Stafford joined him at the chart-table.

"Can we reach them?"

"If this air holds."

"And if it does not?"

He folded the dividers.

"Then we shall have to reach them in the boats."

The Sabbath service was held at ten o'clock, though the
congregation remained divided between stations of duty. The captain read
from the Prayer Book upon the poop; blackened sailors stood
bareheaded by the pin-rails; and the sea, now lightly ruffled,
shone around them under a white sun.

Howard listened for some minutes, then whispered to his mother,
"Has father preached all the sermons he knows?"

"Hush."

"He preached that one near Cape Horn."

"The words may be the same when the need is
the same."

The captain's voice did not falter. He prayed for those
in peril on the sea, but when the short service
ended, he closed the book and said, "Now, lads, back
to work. Providence does not excuse an uncleared fall."

This sentiment was understood by all hands.

Throughout the day the wind strengthened by degrees. The Lochleven
moved at four knots, then five. Every rag of serviceable
canvas was set. Royals rose above the topgallant-sails; staysails drew
between the masts; and the ship, heeling gently, appeared to
flee from the hidden enemy within her.

There was hope. Men spoke more freely. The cook produced
the promised pudding, though it contained more flour than fruit,
and Howard ate two portions. Even Mrs. Stafford allowed herself
to reopen the small bag and add a miniature of
her mother.

At four o'clock the testing-rod came up almost too hot
to touch.

An hour later smoke appeared near the fore-end of the
main hatch. It was no more than a thread at
first, thin and wavering, so like a shadow that the
apprentice who saw it doubted his eyes. Then another thread
joined it. The oakum at the seam became brown.

"Water!"

Canvas hoses were dragged across the deck and coupled to
the pumps. The men worked until the sweat cut white
channels through the coal-dust on their faces. Water flooded the
covered hatch and poured away through the scuppers in black
streams. Steam rose along the coaming.

The smoke ceased.

That cessation was more alarming than reassuring. Fire could withdraw
into the cargo as an animal withdraws into its den.

The apprentice, a red-haired youth named Bell, went below to
fetch his sea-chest. Mr. Rennick found him kneeling beside it.

"What are you about? One bag only."

"My mother packed it, sir."

The chest was a plain deal box, painted green, with
his initials picked out rather uncertainly in white. His widowed
mother had put into it two woollen jerseys, a Bible,
six pairs of stockings, and a small cake which had
not survived the tropics.

"You cannot take it in the boat."

Bell rubbed his sleeve across his eyes, leaving a black
streak upon his forehead.

"No, sir."

He removed the Bible and one jersey. Then he shut
the lid carefully, as though his mother might later inspect
the way he had left it.

Night brought a violent squall. The stars disappeared; the wind
struck from the east with a scream in the rigging;
and the sea, so lately a mirror, leapt into short,
angry ridges. Sail was shortened in haste. Men ran aloft,
lay out upon the yards, and gathered canvas while rain
swept the decks in silver sheets.

For twenty minutes the Lochleven drove north-westward at nearly ten
knots.

Lightning opened the whole sky. In its glare the wet
sails appeared white as bone; the ocean was steel-blue, streaked
with foam; and the officers upon the poop looked like
figures cut from black paper. Then darkness shut down again
with doubled force.

Howard had been dressed in a cork life-belt and placed
in his mother's berth. He objected to its stiffness.

"I can't sleep in this."

"You need not sleep."

"Am I to be drowned?"

"No."

"Then why must I wear it?"

Mrs. Stafford tightened the strap. "Because your father ordered it."

To Howard this was more conclusive than any discussion of
probabilities.

The squall passed as suddenly as it had come, leaving
the ship rolling heavily under reduced sail. The pumps had
gained upon the water in the hold, but a poisonous
smell now issued around the mast partners. Caulking was driven
down with marline-spikes; wet blankets were wrapped about the openings;
everything that might burn readily—spare spars, coils of rope, paint,
lamp-oil, loose timber—was moved as far as possible from the
main hatch or thrown overboard.

The turtle went over the side. It struck the water
with a heavy splash, floated bewildered for a moment, and
then disappeared.

Howard watched it go.

"We've saved him," he said.

Mrs. Stafford looked toward the smoke creeping along the deck.

"Yes. We have saved him."

During the second day intermittent squalls came from the east,
each one carrying the ship nearer the islands. Between them
the wind failed, the sails collapsed, and the rudder-chains clanked
reproachfully. The alternation was almost more difficult to endure than
a steady gale. Hope rose with every darkening cloud and
fell when the canvas began to flap.

Anxiety and suspense destroy appetite more surely than hard labour.
Food was set out, but much of it returned untouched.
The men drank heavily from the water-breaker, for the fumes
dried the mouth and left a metallic taste upon the
tongue.

At noon smoke began to rise through the seams of
the deck itself. It was pale at first, then yellowish,
and finally dark enough to cast a stain across the
sunlight. When the ship rolled, little puffs were forced out
around the bolts.

The deck remained cool in some places and grew painfully
warm in others. Captain Stafford walked it foot by foot,
stooping to lay his palm upon the planks. Near the
mainmast he snatched his hand away.

Below, invisible beneath several yards of coal, the fire had
widened. It was no longer a heated pocket which might
be drowned or cut out. The cargo was becoming a
furnace.

"We lower at sunset," said Mr. Rennick.

"If conditions permit, we remain until dawn," replied the captain.

The mate looked toward the boats. "It will be ugly
work in darkness if she opens."

"I know."

The captain's decision was not stubbornness. The islands, though nearer,
had not yet been sighted; navigation in open boats by
night was difficult; and the gathering sea made lowering dangerous.
Every hour gained under sail might save a day at
the oars. Yet an hour too many would destroy them
all. Such balances are not found in books. They are
weighed within one man, and other lives depend upon the
steadiness of the scale.

Shortly before sunset a lookout aloft shouted that he saw
land.

All eyes turned north-west. For several minutes there was nothing
but a bank of cloud. Then, as the light shifted,
a dark cone appeared beneath it—sharp, barren, unmistakable.

The Galapagos.

A cheer broke from the crew. Howard waved Lord Nelson.
Mrs. Stafford clasped her hands, though she did not speak.

The islands looked close in that transparent air, but the
captain knew them to be many miles distant. Their volcanic
summits rose high above the horizon and deceived the eye.
Still, land was there.

Another squall followed the sunset, and the ship ran before
it with squared yards. The men stood by the braces.
Sparks now mingled with the smoke, tiny red points which
appeared and vanished near the hatch. Water hissed upon the
deck, but no quantity that the pumps could deliver made
any difference.

At midnight the long-boat was swung out. The lifeboats were
lowered to the level of the rail. Women and children
are ordinarily sent away first in a disaster, but Mrs.
Stafford was the only woman and Howard the only child,
and neither would leave while the boats could not safely
remain alongside.

The sky cleared. Starlight lay coldly upon the sea. Ahead,
the black summit of the nearest island rose larger, but
a broad space of water still intervened.

At two bells a dull report sounded below.

The deck lifted under the men's feet and settled again.

No one spoke.

A crack appeared beside the main hatch. Smoke rushed through
it, thick, hot, and suffocating. The pitch in the seams
softened and bubbled. Somewhere beneath them a timber split with
the sound of a pistol-shot.

Captain Stafford went to the rail and judged the sea.
Then he looked once along his ship—from the carved scroll
at the bow to the mizzen rigging, from the neat
white boats to the smoke pouring out under the mainstay.

Then came the order:

"Abandon ship!"

There was no confusion. Discipline, enforced through months of ordinary
duties, now justified every scrubbed deck and every drill. The
boats were lowered; the water-breakers passed down; provisions, instruments, blankets,
and signal gear followed. Men descended by the falls in
their assigned order.

Mrs. Stafford stood by the port rail with Howard's hand
in hers. The boy carried Lord Nelson under one arm.

"Into the boat, Mary," said the captain.

She looked at him.

"You are coming?"

"When the men are clear."

She kissed him once and descended. Howard was passed down
by a sailor and settled beneath the after-thwart. The boat
rose and fell, striking the ship's side until fenders were
thrust between.

A gust of smoke swept over them. Howard coughed and
covered his face. In doing so, he set Lord Nelson
upon the thwart.

Above, the remaining men came down. Captain Stafford was last.
Before leaving, he went to the chart-room, took the ship's
papers and chronometer, and stopped for one moment at the
companionway.

Through the open door he could see the cabin table,
the swinging lamp, the red-covered chair where Howard did his
lessons, and a vase fastened to the bulkhead. Everything was
in its place. The familiar room seemed merely to await
breakfast.

He closed the door.

As he descended, flame showed for the first time—a narrow
blue tongue that licked around the hatch-coaming and vanished.

"Cast off!"

The boats pulled clear.

In that instant Howard remembered his wooden sailor.

"Lord Nelson!" he cried, scrambling up.

Mrs. Stafford caught him around the waist.

"He's there! He's on the ship!"

The little figure stood upon the lifeboat's vacated thwart no
longer. The violent bumping alongside had knocked it over the
gunwale, but whether into the sea or back upon the
Lochleven's deck no one could tell.

"Sit down, Howard," ordered his father from the long-boat.

"But he has only one arm! He can't swim!"

The oars dipped. Every stroke carried them farther from the
ship.

For some minutes the Lochleven showed no outward change beyond
the issuing smoke. She moved slowly on under topsails, deserted
yet stately, her helm lashed, her dark hull shouldering the
waves. It was as though she had refused to acknowledge
that human hands had relinquished her.

Then the main deck burst open.

A column of sparks, smoke, and flame shot upward with
the force of a volcanic eruption. Planks spun into the
air. The main hatch-cover rose entire, turned once, and fell
burning across the rail. Flame poured from the opening in
deep orange masses, seized the pin-rails, and ran with eager
speed along tarred ropes and dry wood.

The boats rested on their oars.

No spectacle could have been more terrible or more magnificent.
Fire climbed the mainmast in twisting ribbons; the lower rigging
glowed; sails caught one after another, becoming immense sheets of
pale gold edged with crimson. The royal fluttered for a
moment like a flag of fire and disappeared. Burning fragments
fell hissing into the sea.

The coal below was now one enormous furnace. Each opening
in the hull drew air to it; each ventilator became
a chimney. The iron sides grew red near the waterline.
Flames streamed from the cabin skylight and wrapped themselves about
the mizzenmast. The bell upon the forecastle gave one unexpected
stroke as its support twisted.

The sea reflected the conflagration until the boats floated upon
a sea of blood. Faces shone red; oar-blades flashed; every
rope and thwart threw an ink-black shadow. Above the ship
hung a mountain of smoke, its under-surface crimson and its
summit lost among the stars.

Mrs. Stafford watched six years of her life burn without
a cry. The silver, the letters, the little clothes, the
books, the chair in which she had nursed Howard through
fever—all were within that furnace. Bell stared at the place
where his green sea-chest lay, and thought of his mother
painting the unsteady white initials. Mr. Rennick removed his cap.

Captain Stafford alone kept his eyes moving from ship to
boats, from boats to compass, from compass to the dark
land ahead. Grief could be given its hour later. At
present twenty-nine persons were afloat in frail cockle-shells upon an
open sea.

"We must pull away," he said.

The oars entered the glowing water.

Behind them the foremast fell, sending up a storm of
sparks. The mainmast followed with a noise like prolonged thunder.
For another hour the burning hull remained visible, lower and
fiercer, until distance and the lifting swell hid it. A
red stain lingered upon the sky long after the ship
herself was gone.

The passage to the islands lasted three days.

The wind which had befriended them failed at dawn, and
the men rowed in watches beneath a pitiless sun. Water
was issued by measure. Biscuits, softened for Howard and for
two sick seamen, formed their chief food. At night they
steered by the stars and slept where they sat, heads
bowed upon their knees.

The Galapagos appeared first as blue mountains, then as brown
cliffs streaked with black lava. Sea-birds circled the boats. A
seal rose near the long-boat, stared with round mild eyes,
and vanished. On the third morning they found a narrow
landing where dark rocks broke the surf.

One by one the survivors stepped ashore.

The land was harsh and volcanic, but it did not
move beneath their feet, and that alone made it beautiful.
Men knelt to drink from a spring discovered beyond the
rocks. Others lay down under thorny bushes and slept. Captain
Stafford counted every person twice before permitting himself to rest.

Howard sat apart upon a block of lava. His face
was sunburned, his lips cracked, and his small life-belt hung
loosely about him. Mrs. Stafford brought him water.

"We are safe now," she said.

He nodded.

"You were very brave."

Another nod.

Then his mouth trembled.

"Lord Nelson wasn't brave," he whispered. "He couldn't be, with
nobody to hold him."

Mrs. Stafford gathered him into her arms. Until then he
had not wept—not when the smoke blinded him, not when
he entered the boat, not during the long nights upon
the sea. Now the tears came in a flood.

The men who had lost their clothes, their wages, their
sea-chests, and almost their lives turned away from the child's
grief. Captain Stafford stood looking toward the empty horizon where
his ship had burned. He understood that to Howard the
destruction of four thousand tons of coal, a fine vessel,
and years of labour was too vast a thing to
comprehend. But a one-armed wooden sailor was small enough to
love and therefore small enough to mourn.

Long afterward, when Howard was an old man and the
age of sail itself had nearly vanished from the seas,
he would still remember the Lochleven as she became a
volcano beneath the equatorial stars. Yet even at four score,
the brightest flame in that dreadful picture would not burn
so sharply in his memory as the tears he shed
upon the black Galapagos lava for poor Lord Nelson, who
had been left alone aboard the burning ship.

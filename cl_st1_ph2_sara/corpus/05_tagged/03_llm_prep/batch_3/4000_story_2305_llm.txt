That mug there with the blue windmill on it? That
belonged to Jim Kendall. I keep it settin' there, though
there ain't no likelihood anybody'll claim it now. His initials
is scratched on the bottom, J.K., only he done it
himself with my shears one Saturday night and made the
K backwards. He certainly was a card, Jim was.

No, you wouldn't of known him. You said you was
from over by Danton, didn't you? Jim never got much
farther than Bell City unless you count the time the
sheriff took him to the county seat, and naturally he
didn't see much of the scenery on that trip. Tip
your head a little. That's right. Your hair grows contrary
on this side.

Jim used to set in that chair by the window,
when we only had the two chairs and Lem Porter
was workin' for me. Lem's in Arizona now on account
of his lungs, though according to the postcards he sends,
the lungs is about the only part of him that
ain't improved. Jim would come in about nine in the
mornin', if he'd been fired again, and stay till dinner,
tellin' stories and borrowin' tobacco. If he had a job,
he'd come in at noon and stay till his boss
sent somebody after him.

He was a good barber-shop man. Some fellows come in
and just set, and you feel like they're watchin' you
waste time. Jim could keep everybody lively. He knew every
joke that'd been told in three counties, and when he
didn't know one he made it up, or changed the
names in an old one so's it sounded like it
happened here.

He worked at the box factory off and on. Mostly
off. Old Man Tuttle would discharge him Monday and take
him back Wednesday because Jim could do two men's work
when he felt like it. The trouble was he generally
felt like doin' no man's work. He'd start the saw,
pile up enough slats to make a noise, and then
slip out the back to Henny's poolroom. If Tuttle come
huntin' him, Jim would imitate Mrs. Tuttle over the telephone
and tell the old man the baby had swallowed a
button.

They didn't have no baby. Their youngest was seventeen.

That was Jim all over. He could imitate anybody. If
you'd heard him shut your eyes and do Preacher Baines
askin' for a contribution, you'd of reached for your pocketbook.
He could do Mayor Rice and the depot caller and
old Miss Peebles that taught fifth grade. He used to
telephone Miss Peebles in her own voice and ask her
landlady if she'd please put a hot brick in the
bed because her feet was cold. The landlady thought Miss
Peebles was losin' her mind.

I guess it wasn't exactly fair to Miss Peebles, but
she took things too serious anyhow. Jim certainly was a
card.

He married young. His wife's name was Cora Mellen before
she married him, and her folks had the little grocery
out on Mill Street. Cora was a nice-lookin' girl then,
kind of light-haired and quiet, with a way of lookin'
at the floor when men spoke to her. Her mother
wanted her to marry Frank Dobbins, the plumber, which goes
to show mothers don't always know everything, because Frank's been
married three times and don't live with none of 'em.

Jim courted Cora by comin' to the grocery every night
and eatin' raisins out of the barrel. He'd buy a
nickel's worth of cheese and stay two hours. Old Mellen
said afterward Jim cost him near six dollars in raisins
before the weddin'. Jim said that was cheaper than an
engagement ring.

They had two kids, a boy and a girl. The
boy, Benny, was about eleven when Jim died, and little
Pearl was eight, or maybe nine. You never seen children
act so still when their father come into a room.
Jim said he had 'em trained. He'd whistle through his
teeth and they'd stop whatever they was doin', just like
dogs.

Once Benny tracked mud over the kitchen after Cora'd scrubbed
it, and Jim made him stand out by the pump
in his stocking feet while it was sleetin'. He said
that would teach the boy not to bring outdoors indoors.
Cora tried to let him in, and Jim locked the
door and put the key in his pocket. Benny took
a fever after that and missed school two weeks.

But Jim didn't mean him no permanent harm. He was
rough, is all, and believed boys ought to be hardened.
He'd been raised that way himself, he said, though Jim's
mother told me his father never struck him except once,
and that was after Jim tied a tin pan to
a blind horse.

Cora done dressmakin' to help out, especially during the periods
Jim was between positions. That's what he called bein' fired—between
positions. There'd be a card in their front window, DRESSMAKING
AND ALTERATIONS, and Cora would set up half the night
turnin' somebody's old Sunday dress into a newer old Sunday
dress. Then Jim might come in with four or five
fellows and tell her to fry eggs.

If there wasn't no eggs, he'd laugh and say, "What
kind of hotel you runnin' here?"

Cora'd say, "Jim, I ain't got money till Mrs. Felton
pays me."

And he'd say, "There's always money if a person knows
where to look."

Then he'd look in her sewing box.

He drank pretty steady. Not all day every day, you
understand, because a man can't afford that in a town
this size unless he owns the drugstore. But whenever he
had money, or knew somebody that did, he'd get hold
of gin. Cheap gin, too, that smelled like furniture polish.
You could always tell when Jim had been drinkin', not
because he staggered, but because he got polite. He'd call
me "Professor" and offer to buy the shop.

He owed me for nineteen haircuts when he died. I
ain't complainin'. A man can't collect off a widow with
two children, and anyway he brought trade in here. Folks
would come to hear him. There's some value in that.

Hold still a second. I got a little place behind
your ear.

There. Didn't draw hardly no blood.

Jim's best jokes was the ones he played on people,
not just things he said. At least that's what the
fellows thought. There was the time he put axle grease
on Constable Wendt's horse saddle just before the Memorial Day
parade. Wendt's white pants was spoiled, and he had to
ride the whole length of Main Street settin' crooked so
nobody'd see the mark, which made everybody look at it.

And once Jim sent three undertakers to old Mr. Clabe's
house the same afternoon. Mr. Clabe was ninety-one and had
been expectin' to die since he was sixty. Every time
a wagon stopped out front, he'd get into bed and
fold his hands. By the third undertaker he got mad
enough to come downtown and chase Jim clear around the
courthouse with his cane.

Jim could run when he had to.

But his favorite one, I guess, was Paul Dickson. You
probably seen Paul when you come from the depot—the tall,
loose-jointed fellow sweepin' in front of the feed store. His
head kind of leans to one side and his mouth
stays open like he's listenin' for somethin'. That's Paul.

He ain't what you'd call crazy. He knows people and
can count change if you don't hurry him. But he
never got much beyond twelve years old inside, though he
was twenty-seven when Jim died. His mother had scarlet fever
before he was born, or maybe it was Paul had
it after; I've heard both. Anyhow, everybody here knows Paul
ain't responsible the same as other folks.

He works wherever they'll let him. Sweeps the feed store,
carries ashes out of the hotel, fills coal bins. If
you tell him a thing plain, he'll do it till
somebody tells him to stop. One winter Mr. Lacy told
him to shovel the walk every time it snowed, and
Paul kept shovelin' through April whenever a white feather come
down from the poultry house.

Jim could get him to do most anything.

He'd say, "Paul, the marshal's lookin' for you."

Paul would go pale and ask, "What'd I done, Jim?"

Jim would whisper, "Never mind. Hide in the coal shed
and don't come out till dark."

Then Paul would stay in there five hours while Jim
sat here tellin' everybody how the marshal nearly had him.

Or Jim would give Paul a dime and say the
dime was counterfeit and federal men had seen him take
it. Paul would carry it around wrapped in paper, scared
to spend it and scared to throw it away. Once
he cried half the afternoon because Jim told him counterfeiters
got hung.

We explained to him they didn't, but Paul kept askin',
"Jim wouldn't lie about the government, would he?"

Well, what can you say?

I used to tell Jim to let him alone. I
did, plenty of times. "Jim," I'd say, "you better pick
on somebody your own size."

And Jim would say, "There ain't nobody my size."

He meant mentally, you see. That was the joke.

The boys would laugh, and Jim would grin that sideways
grin of his. He had a broken tooth right here.
Got it from Cora's brother after Cora come to the
grocery with a black eye. Jim said she walked into
the cupboard door. Cora's brother said in that case the
cupboard door could fight better than Jim, and maybe it
could, because Jim lost the tooth.

Still, him and Cora's brother made it up. Men generally
does. Cora didn't have much to say about it.

Now, Julie Gregg—that's another part of it. You notice the
Gregg house when you come in? Big gray place across
from the Methodist church, with iron deer on the lawn.
Julie was their only child. Her father owned Gregg's Implements
and had money enough to worry about who she spoke
to.

She was a good-lookin' girl, though not the baby-doll kind.
Tall and dark, and she looked straight at you. Some
men don't care for a woman lookin' straight at 'em.
They prefer one that looks impressed. Jim said Julie thought
she was too good for this town, but she never
acted that way with me. She'd bring her father in
for a haircut when his rheumatism was bad and set
there readin' the farm paper without pretendin' she enjoyed it.

Jim took a notion to her about a year before
he died.

He was married, of course, but Jim didn't regard bein'
married as a serious obstacle. He said marriage was like
havin' a season ticket—you could still watch an exhibition game.
He'd say that right in front of Cora, too. She'd
go on mendin' his shirt like she hadn't heard.

Julie wouldn't have nothin' to do with him. He'd wait
outside the picture show and offer to walk her home,
and she'd cross the street. He sent her flowers once—asters
he cut out of the cemetery—and she sent 'em back.
Jim told everybody she was mad for him but cautious
on account of her father.

Nobody believed that except maybe Paul.

Paul thought Jim knew everything about women, guns, law, baseball,
and weather. If Jim said it would rain, Paul carried
an umbrella under a clear sky. Jim liked that. He
liked havin' one person that never caught on.

One time he told Paul that Julie had promised to
kiss whichever man could stand barefoot on the courthouse steps
longest in January. Paul stood there near twenty minutes before
Marshal Wendt found him. His toes turned purple. When Julie
heard about it, she went to Paul's mother with medicine
and cried.

Jim said she cried because Paul quit too soon.

I didn't laugh at that one. Well, maybe I smiled
when he first told it, because of the way he
told things, but I said it was kind of a
raw thing to do. Jim said Paul's feet was too
big anyhow and a little frost might shrink 'em.

That was Jim.

Then Doctor Ralph Stair come to town.

Old Doctor Bell had died of pneumonia, which didn't give
people much confidence in his methods, and Ralph bought the
practice from the estate. He wasn't over thirty, tall, clean-built,
with black hair he wore brushed back. He'd studied in
Chicago and had one of them roadsters that looked like
it was goin' fast while it stood still.

He come in here the second day and asked for
a shave, though he didn't hardly need one. Fine beard.
You doctors and lawyers generally has fine beards. I don't
know if it's education or indoor work.

Ralph didn't talk much. That set him apart around here.
If you asked what he thought about the weather, he'd
say, "It may clear," and let it go at that.
Jim said the doctor kept quiet so people would suppose
he was thinkin', but I guess he really was.

All the girls noticed him. There wasn't hardly an unmarried
woman in town that didn't develop a stitch in her
side during his first month. Mrs. Peebles had palpitations three
times a week till he sent her a bill. Julie
didn't chase him like some did, but they got acquainted
through church, and pretty soon he was callin' at the
Gregg house.

Jim didn't like that.

He'd set in here and imitate Ralph sayin', "Open your
mouth and show me your bank account." Or he'd claim
Ralph used perfume. It wasn't perfume; it was some clean-smellin'
hair tonic from Chicago. I ordered a bottle after I
asked him, but nobody else bought any. Around here, if
a tonic don't smell strong enough to stun flies, men
don't trust it.

Jim tried to make out Ralph was afraid of him.
One afternoon the doctor was in that chair and Jim
come in feelin' sociable from gin. He slapped Ralph on
the shoulder and said, "Doc, they tell me you're stealin'
my girl."

Ralph looked at him in the mirror and said, "I
didn't know your daughter was old enough to court."

That tickled everybody but Jim. Jim's girl was eight, like
I said. He laughed after a second, but it wasn't
his laughin' laugh.

He said, "No harm done, Doc. There's plenty to go
around."

Ralph said, "I don't believe Miss Gregg would like bein'
spoken of that way."

And Jim said, "You ask her what she likes. I
know."

Then Ralph turned in the chair. He didn't raise his
voice. "You keep her name out of your talk."

Jim said he was only kiddin' and bought cigars for
the shop. Cheap cigars. Ralph left his layin' on the
shelf.

After that Jim worked on a scheme. You could always
tell when he had one because he'd set with his
eyes narrowed and rub the broken place in his tooth
with his tongue. He asked me questions about Ralph—what time
he come in, what kind of telephone he had, when
his office girl went home. I didn't know what he
was up to. If I had, I'd of told somebody.

Ralph's office was over Tenny's Drugstore, at the head of
the side stairs. The office girl, Miss Kane, left at
five-thirty, and Ralph generally made calls from six till eight.
One Thursday he had to go out to the Clark
farm, eleven miles east, where their hired man got kicked
by a mule.

Jim knew it. Everybody knew it, because Mrs. Clark called
the drugstore and said what happened loud enough for Tenny
to hear without the telephone.

Julie was rehearsin' for the church concert that night. She
was to sing a solo, and Jim knew she'd be
home by eight. He got Pike Lawrence and Eddie Moss
and two others together at Henny's. I ain't sayin' their
names because they got families and one of 'em is
on the school board now. They had a bottle and
went up the back stairs to Ralph's office.

The door wasn't locked. Folks didn't lock offices much then.

Jim telephoned Julie from Ralph's desk.

Like I said, he could imitate anybody, and Ralph's voice
was easy because it was low and kind of flat.
Jim told Julie he'd got back from the Clark place
and needed her to come to the office right away.
Said it concerned her father and he couldn't explain over
the phone.

Julie asked if her father was hurt.

Jim, talkin' like Ralph, said, "No, but please come alone."

So she come.

It was rainin' a little, and she wore a light
coat over her choir dress. The fellows had turned out
the front-office lamp and left only the one in the
examination room. Jim sat behind Ralph's desk with the lamp
at his back. He'd put on Ralph's white coat.

When Julie stepped inside, he said in the doctor's voice,
"Close the door."

She closed it.

Then Jim switched to his own voice and said, "That's
right, sweetheart. The doctor'll see you now."

The fellows come out of the dark laughin'.

Now, they claimed afterward they only meant to scare her.
That's what men always say when a joke don't land
right. Julie backed against the door and tried to open
it, but Pike had turned the key. Jim stood up
in the white coat and told her they'd called a
consultation on her case.

He had a ruler and pretended it was a thermometer.

He said, "Patient suffers from a swollen opinion of herself."

Eddie said, "Dangerous?"

Jim said, "Fatal unless reduced by laughin' gas."

They all laughed.

Julie told 'em to let her out. Jim said not
till she admitted she'd rather have him than Ralph. She
wouldn't say it. He put his hand under her chin
and tried to make her look at him. She struck
him across the mouth.

That made the broken tooth cut his lip.

He wasn't jokin' for a minute then.

He caught both her wrists and pushed her against the
desk. The others quit laughin', or most of 'em did.
Julie kicked him in the shin and got one hand
loose. She knocked over Ralph's tray of instruments, and the
noise scared the fellows more than anything, because they knew
somebody downstairs might hear.

Pike unlocked the door.

Julie ran out, and Jim ran after her, still wearin'
the white coat. The others followed because people will follow
anything once they've started.

She went down the stairs and through Tenny's store. Tenny
was in back mixin' cough syrup, but his soda boy
seen her. She ran into the rain without her hat.
Jim stopped at the store door and called after her
in Ralph's voice, "Don't forget your prescription!"

Then he bowed to the soda boy.

He certainly could think quick.

The story was all over town by noon next day,
but different every place you heard it. According to Jim,
Julie had come because she knew it was him and
then lost her nerve. According to Pike, nobody touched her.
According to Eddie, he hadn't even been there. The soda
boy said there was twenty men chasin' her and Jim
had a butcher knife.

I suppose the truth was somewhere in between, like usual.

Julie stayed in the house two days. Her father wanted
arrests made, but she wouldn't go to the marshal. Maybe
she didn't want to stand in court while a roomful
of men asked what Jim done and how close he
stood and where he put his hands. You know how
them hearings go. The one that gets hurt has to
furnish all the entertainment.

Ralph come in here Saturday mornin'. He looked like he
hadn't slept. Jim was in the back room playin' cards
with Lem and didn't come out. Ralph asked me what
I'd heard.

I said, "You know how stories grow."

He said, "I asked what you heard."

So I told him the mild version. I said Jim
and the boys played a little trick with the telephone
and Julie got frightened.

Ralph said, "They used my name and my office."

I said, "Jim's sorry, I guess."

From the back Jim called, "Tell Doc I'll pay for
the broken instruments."

Ralph walked to the back-room door. Jim was holdin' five
cards and grinnin'.

Ralph said, "Come out."

Jim said, "Deal's not finished."

Ralph said, "Come out now."

Jim come out, because there was somethin' in the way
Ralph said it. Ralph shut the back-room door so the
others couldn't see, but they could hear. We could all
hear.

He asked Jim exactly what happened.

Jim said, "Ask the lady."

Ralph hit him once.

It wasn't a wild swing. He hit him straight in
the face and knocked him into the hat rack. Jim
come up with the rack around his shoulders and tried
to laugh. Blood was runnin' from his nose.

He said, "Doctors shouldn't lose their patients."

Ralph hit him again, and I got between 'em. I
had to. A barber can't have a fight in the
front shop on Saturday mornin'; it gives the place a
bad name.

Jim kept makin' remarks while I wiped his nose. Ralph
stood by the door, white as a sheet. Finally he
said, "If you ever go near her again, I'll kill
you."

Jim said, "That's doctor talk."

Then Ralph left.

Jim told everybody Ralph had surprised him or he'd of
tore him apart. He said he let the doctor hit
him because professional men had soft hands and needed exercise.
But he didn't go near Julie again, not where anybody
could see.

That might of ended it, except for Paul.

Paul knew Julie. Like I said, she'd been kind to
him. She gave him an old overcoat of her father's
and helped his mother get coal from the church fund.
He called her Miss Julie even though she told him
not to.

The night of the trick, Paul was sweepin' the drugstore
sidewalk under the awning. He seen Julie run out cryin'
and seen Jim stand in the doorway with the white
coat on. He didn't understand it then. Next day Jim
told Paul she'd come to get married and changed her
mind.

Paul believed him for a while.

Then the soda boy told Paul some of it, and
Paul went to Julie's house. Her father chased him off
because Mr. Gregg was chasin' nearly everybody off that week.
Paul stood by the iron deer till Julie come to
the upstairs window. He hollered, "Did Jim hurt you, Miss
Julie?"

She pulled the curtain shut.

Paul set down on the curb and cried.

Later he went to Ralph's office. Ralph told me about
that part, though not till after everything else. Paul kept
twistin' his cap and askin' why Jim wore the doctor's
coat.

Ralph said, "Because he's a coward."

Paul asked, "Did he hurt Miss Julie?"

Ralph said, "Yes."

Paul asked, "Is he goin' to jail?"

Ralph said he didn't know.

Paul thought about it and said, "Jim says nobody can
jail him."

Ralph was still mad—anybody would of been—and he said something
he likely forgot as soon as it was out. He
said, "A man who can do a thing like that
ought not to be allowed to live."

Paul asked, "You mean Jim?"

Ralph said, "Yes, Jim."

Then he told Paul to go home.

That's all. Just words. You and me would know a
man says things when he's mad that he don't mean
as instructions. Paul wasn't like you and me, though. He
remembered exact. He could repeat a sentence six months later
if you said it serious enough.

For the next week he kept away from Jim. Jim
noticed and didn't like it. A fellow don't like losin'
his best audience.

He'd call, "Hey, Paul, the circus wants its giraffe back!"

Paul would keep walkin'.

Jim threw a potato at him outside the grocery. Hit
him behind the ear. Paul picked it up and set
it on a barrel, careful, like the potato hadn't done
nothing.

Then duck season opened.

Jim was a good shot when he was sober and
not too bad when he wasn't. He had an old
twelve-gauge with a stock wrapped in copper wire. Every fall
he hunted down by Sago Marsh. Mostly he brought back
one duck and six stories.

The Saturday he died, he come in here before daylight.
I was open early because the factory run a special
shift. Jim had on hip boots and a brown coat,
and he'd been drinkin'. Not drunk, exactly, but warmed up.
He asked if I'd seen Paul.

I said no.

He said, "I'm takin' him huntin'. It's time the boy
learned a man's sport."

I told him Paul had never handled a gun.

Jim said, "Then he ain't had no bad habits taught
him."

I said it didn't sound smart.

He said, "You jealous because I didn't invite you?"

Well, I wasn't. Sago Marsh is cold as a landlord's
heart, and the mud'll pull a boot right off. Besides,
Jim probably wanted Paul along to carry the decoys.

Paul come by about six. He had bread and two
hard-boiled eggs wrapped in newspaper. His mother told him not
to go, he said, but Jim told him ducks wouldn't
wait.

Jim clapped him on the back and said, "Paul's my
pal again."

Paul looked at him a long time and said, "Am
I?"

Jim laughed. "Sure. Ain't I takin' you where the men
go?"

They left in McNulty's old Ford. Jim had borrowed it
without explainin' exactly where he was takin' it.

Around ten, three farmers heard a shot out by the
marsh. They didn't think nothin' of it. It was duck
season. About eleven-thirty Paul walked into the Clark farmyard without
a hat. His pants was wet clear to the waist
and there was mud on his face.

Mrs. Clark asked where Jim was.

Paul said, "Jim's dead."

She screamed, and that brought the men from the barn.

Paul led 'em to the blind. Jim was layin' half
in the reeds, shot in the back of the head.
The gun was on the ground ten feet away. One
barrel had been fired.

Paul told 'em Jim handed him the gun while he
went to set a decoy. Paul said he stumbled on
a root and the gun went off.

There ain't many roots in Sago Marsh, but there's some.
Willow roots, maybe.

Paul said the gun kicked out of his hands. Then
he said he dropped it before it fired. Later he
said he didn't remember. That ain't unusual when a person's
frightened. Even smart people mix up details.

Ralph was coroner as well as doctor. Small towns don't
hire a separate man just to pronounce folks dead; there
ain't enough business. He went out with Marshal Wendt and
looked at Jim and the blind and the gun.

The muzzle had been about six feet from Jim, maybe
less. Ralph said the shot pattern showed that. Paul was
taller than Jim by a head, so if he held
the gun careless, it could of happened just that way.

Marshal Wendt asked Paul to show him how he held
it.

Paul picked up a broom at the Clark place and
held it level against his shoulder, like a man that
had practiced.

Then he looked at Ralph and let the broom sag.

"Not that way," he said. "I held it wrong."

Ralph watched him.

Paul said, "Jim told me put my finger here."

He touched the place where the trigger would be.

Wendt asked, "Was Jim standin' or bendin'?"

Paul said, "He was laughin'."

That wasn't an answer, but it was Paul.

Ralph decided it was accidental. He could of called an
inquest, but he said there wasn't no purpose makin' Paul
repeat it before twelve men. Jim had knowingly handed a
loaded gun to somebody unfamiliar with firearms. That was negligence,
Ralph said, and the result was plain.

Wendt agreed. Wendt generally agreed with a doctor when the
alternative meant paperwork.

They brought Jim back on a door laid across the
wagon. Cora identified him. She didn't faint. She looked at
him a while and asked whether his factory wages was
paid through Friday.

Some people thought that sounded cold, but she had two
children and six dollars in the house.

The funeral was Monday. Biggest one we'd had since Mayor
Rice's first wife died. The box factory closed for two
hours, and Henny shut the poolroom, which was more respect
than he showed his own brother.

Everybody had a Jim story. Men stood outside the church
laughin' about the axle grease and the undertakers and the
time he put a snake in the collection basket. They
said the town wouldn't be the same.

Cora sat in front with Benny and Pearl. Benny kept
lookin' toward the door like he expected Jim to come
in and say it was all another joke. Pearl cried
because other people was cryin'. Julie didn't attend. Ralph did,
in back.

Paul come late. He wore the overcoat Julie gave him,
though the day was warm. He stood across the street
by the iron fence and watched them carry the casket
out.

Somebody told him he ought to go home.

He said, "Doctor says it was an accident."

And the fellow said, "That's right, Paul."

Paul said it again: "Doctor says it was an accident."

He smiled a little then. Not a happy smile. More
like when a child has remembered the answer he was
taught.

Afterward, folks took food to Cora. She got more groceries
that week than Jim had bought in three years. Old
Man Tuttle gave Benny a job sweepin' at the factory
when he got old enough, and Mrs. Gregg paid Cora
to make choir robes. She and the children moved to
her mother's place last spring.

Ralph married Julie in June. Quiet weddin'. They live in
the white house near the water tower. Paul cuts their
grass. He won't take money from Julie, but Ralph slips
it to his mother.

I seen Paul and Ralph together once, right outside this
window. Paul was pushin' the mower and Ralph come home
in his car. Paul stopped him and asked, "Bad men
oughtn't be let live, do they, Doctor?"

Ralph looked around to see if anybody heard.

Then he said, "You mustn't talk like that, Paul."

Paul said, "I don't."

Ralph went inside without answerin'.

Maybe there wasn't nothin' in it. Paul asks queer questions.
Last week he asked me whether fish get thirsty.

Still, I sometimes wonder why Jim took him huntin' right
then, when Paul had been avoidin' him. Maybe Jim wanted
to make up. Maybe he wanted to scare him with
the gun or send him wadin' into deep water. You
couldn't ever tell where Jim's jokes was headed till somebody
got there.

And I wonder about Ralph, too. Not that he'd do
anything crooked. He's a fine doctor. Saved Mrs. Tuttle when
her appendix busted, and never pressed Cora for the funeral
examination fee. But he knew what he'd said to Paul.
He knew after Paul told him.

An inquest wouldn't of brought Jim back. It might of
sent Paul someplace, and what good would that do? Paul
works, minds his mother, and don't bother nobody. Jim, on
the other hand—well, Jim was Jim.

There was talk for a while, naturally. A town'll talk
if a dog looks cross-eyed. Henny said Paul couldn't plan
a murder, and Pike said anybody could pull a trigger.
Miss Peebles said Providence had used a weak vessel. Preacher
Baines told her not to speak blasphemous, though I noticed
his sermon next Sunday was about the wicked fallin' into
their own pit.

Cora never asked questions. When somebody said it must of
been terrible for Jim, she said, "It was quick, wasn't
it?" Ralph told her yes. She said, "Then he had
that much kindness."

That was a peculiar remark, but grief affects folks different.

Me, I prefer to remember Jim when he was in
this shop makin' everybody laugh. Like the day he put
soap in Lem's coffee and Lem drank half the cup
before he noticed. Or the time he answered my telephone
in a woman's voice and told a drummer he'd reached
the maternity home. You ought to have heard him. Nobody
could imitate voices like Jim.

He could be mischievous, sure. And the drink was bad
for him. And he ought to of treated Cora and
the kids better. That affair with Julie was a kind
of a raw thing, too, though the boys was all
excited and likely didn't realize how it looked to her.

But I don't hold with speakin' ill of the dead.
Whatever else you say, Jim kept this town from bein'
dull.

That mug's been there two years now. I washed it
once after the funeral, but it looked too new, so
I let the dust gather back. Sometimes Paul comes in
and stares at it. He don't set in Jim's old
chair. If it's the only one open, he waits.

Last month he asked me whether a dead man could
play a joke.

I said, "What kind of question is that?"

He said, "One where nobody laughs."

Then he went out.

## THE GANDER OF BALLYCLOUGH

It was on an evening in the latter end of
October, when the year, like a penitent monarch weary of
magnificence, had put aside its ornaments, that I found myself
travelling through a remote district of Tipperary. My road wound
slowly between low hills, whose summits were veiled in vapour,
while beneath them extended a various prospect of bog, pasture,
and neglected tillage. Here a ruined castle, blackened by the
rains of centuries, rose above the thorn; there the shattered
gable of a chapel retained, in its empty window, the
last amber light of sunset. A river, too inconsiderable to
be named upon any map, stole among the meadows, glimmering
at intervals through reeds and alder-boughs, as though ashamed to
display its poverty.

Yet the scene was beautiful—beautiful with that peculiar and unutterably
mournful loveliness which belongs to Ireland. The soil was rich,
and they who cultivated it were hungry; the valleys smiled,
and the cabins within them seemed to mourn. Nature had
scattered abundance with a prodigal hand, while man, by some
cruel inversion of justice, had been compelled to reap only
dependence and decay. O Ireland! fairest in sorrow, most fertile
in famine, how long must thy sweetness be made bitter,
thy courage suspected, and thy children driven from the bosom
that nourished them?

My horse, an aged grey whose meditative pace was admirably
suited to my humour, had nearly brought me to the
summit of a long ascent when I perceived a man
sitting upon the roadside. He was broad-shouldered and weather-beaten, with
a frieze coat folded beneath him and a blackthorn stick
laid across his knees. Beside him stood a small cart
laden with turf, one wheel of which had sunk deeply
into the soft margin of the road.

I offered him assistance, but he declined it with civility.

"Much obliged to your raverance," said he, touching his hat,
for my dress sufficiently declared my calling; "but the baste
is restin', and I'm restin', and the wheel itself has
as good a right to rest as either iv us.
We'll all three be the better for it."

There was philosophy in the answer, and good humour in
the speaker. I dismounted, partly to relieve my horse and
partly because the declining light rendered haste useless. From where
we stood, a substantial farmhouse was visible beyond the river,
surrounded by ricks, orchards, and numerous low outbuildings.

"That," said my companion, observing the direction of my eye,
"is Ballyclough, your raverance—the very place where Terence Mooney lived,
that had the knowledgeable gandher."

"A knowledgeable gander?"

"The same, sir. Sure, the fame iv him went from
this to Cashel, and farther, only the people beyant Cashel
is mighty jealous about admittin' anything wonderful that didn't happen
among themselves."

I asked him to relate the history; and, after first
inquiring whether I had leisure enough to hear the truth
properly, for, as he observed, "a lie may be tould
in a hurry, but the truth requires elbow-room," he settled
himself more conveniently upon his coat and began.

"Terence Mooney, your raverance, was a snug man, as times
went, and held twelve acres, besides the mountain grazin', and
had a lease that would have been good enough if
the agent had let it alone. He was a hard-workin'
man, too, when there wasn't a fair, a weddin', a
pattern, a station, a funeral, or any other lawful prevention
in the way; and he had cows, pigs, hens, ducks,
a mare wid a white face, and a wife by
the name iv Biddy, that could hear a whisper through
two stone walls and a thunderstorm.

"Now, among all the stock about the place there was
an ould white gandher, a grand, stately bird, wid a
neck on him like a bishop's crozier and an eye
as solemn as a process-server's. He'd walk about the yard
considherin' everything, and if a pig got into the praties,
or a child upset the milk, or the mare broke
the gate, he'd be there lookin' on as much as
to say he knew beforehand it would come to that.

"Terence had an admiration for the bird beyond all exposition.
'Biddy,' says he, 'there's sinse in that gandher.'

"'There's more sinse in him than in some that wears
breeches,' says Biddy, lookin' at Terence.

"'I know who you mane,' says Terence.

"'Then you've more sinse than I gave you credit for,'
says she.

"But it wasn't only Terence that the bird was fond
of. The gandher followed him everywhere—through the haggard, round the
field, down to the river, and wanst, begorra, half a
mile along the road to mass, till Terence turned him
back for fear Father Crotty would consider it disrespectful. When
Terence sat down, the gandher sat by him; when Terence
spoke, the bird stretched his neck and listened; and if
any man contradicted Terence, the gandher hissed at him immediate,
which was an argument Terence valued highly.

"'Look at that now,' he'd say. 'The crathur knows justice
from oppression.'

"The neighbours began to remark it, for there's nothin' can
happen in a parish, from a hen losin' a feather
to a landlord losin' his life, but the neighbours will
remark it. Some said the gandher was only grateful because
Terence fed him; but others said gratitude wasn't natural to
any dumb baste except a dog, and even a dog
would forget it at dinner-time. Ould Judy Ryan declared she
had seen the gandher standin' in the moonlight wid his
wings crossed behind his back, meditatin' like a Christian. Mick
the Joiner swore it knocked three times at the kitchen
door on the anniversary of Terence's father's death. And little
Nelly Doolan, that was forever seein' what nobody else could,
said it winked at her during the rosary.

"'There's somethin' in it,' says the neighbours.

"'There's feathers in it,' says Biddy Mooney, who had no
patience wid mysteries unless they could be boiled for supper.

"But Terence grew uneasy in his mind. His father, rest
his soul, had been a quiet, respectable man, barrin' a
weakness for disputation and another for poteen; and he'd died
sudden one night after arguin' for five hours whether Saint
Patrick had first landed in Wicklow or Waterford. Terence began
to think maybe the gandher's behaviour had some connection wid
him.

"So at last he went to Jer Garvan, the fairy
doctor.

"Jer lived over by Knocknasheega, in a cabin that had
more herbs hangin' from the rafters than there was thatch
on the roof. He could cure cows from overlookin', children
from the elf-shot, women from bad dreams, and men from
work, though the last cure was generally accomplished without his
assistance. He had book-larnin', too, or said he had, and
spoke so grand that no wan could understand him, which
proved it beyond contradiction.

"Terence found him sittin' by the fire, readin' a torn
almanac upside down.

"'God save all here,' says Terence.

"'And you likewise, in a proportional degree,' says Jer.

"'I came for advice,' says Terence.

"'That's seven and sixpence,' says Jer.

"'I didn't get it yet,' says Terence.

"'You'll value it the more when it's paid for,' says
Jer.

"Well, Terence laid down two shillings, a copper, and a
twist of tobacco, and promised the remainder after harvest, providin'
the advice proved Christian and serviceable. Then he tould him
all about the gandher—how it followed him, listened to him,
hissed at his enemies, and conducted itself altogether wid a
solemnity unbecomin' poultry.

"Jer shut one eye and looked into the fire.

"'Does it ate?' says he.

"'It does,' says Terence.

"'That's suspicious,' says Jer.

"'Why so?'

"'Because,' says Jer.

"'Because what?'

"'Because eatin' is a remarkable symptom in them that's alive,
and sometimes in them that's otherwise.'

"Terence crossed himself.

"'Does it drink?' says Jer.

"'Water,' says Terence.

"'Worse again,' says Jer. 'Water is an aquatical element, and
spirits is greatly affected by elements.'

"'Do you think it's a fairy?' says Terence.

"'I do not,' says Jer.

"'A divil?'

"'Not exactly.'

"'What then?'

"Jer leaned forward till his nose was near singein' in
the turf, and says he, 'Terence Mooney, the gandher contains
a transmigrated responsibility.'

"'A what?' says Terence.

"'A wanderin' soul,' says Jer, 'descended through the lenial and
legitimate postariors of your own family.'

"'Whose soul?' says Terence, turnin' pale.

"'Your father's,' says Jer.

"Terence gave a groan that lifted the ashes.

"'My father in the gandher!' says he.

"'As snug as a tenant under a long lease,' says
Jer.

"'But why wouldn't he speak to me?'

"'Pride,' says Jer.

"'He wasn't proud.'

"'Then caution.'

"'He was never cautious.'

"'That confirms it,' says Jer. 'A spirit commonly acquires after
death whatever quality was deficient in life.'

"'And how'll we make him declare himself?' says Terence.

"Jer considered again, and at last says he, 'There's but
wan constitutional operation. You must frighten him out iv his
anonymous silence. Let on you're goin' to sell him at
Tipperary market to a poulterer, for immediate assassination and cookery.
If your father is there, filial indignation will compel a
discovery.'

"Terence didn't like the plan, but Jer explained that it
was founded upon gasthronomy, pneumatics, and the ancient regulations of
the Milesians, and consequently couldn't fail. So they arranged that
on the following Thursday the gandher should be put into
a hamper and carried to market by two of Terence's
boys, Larry and Shawn, while Jer and Terence would watch
what came of it.

"Now, Biddy Mooney heard a part of this conversation, and
she was neither pleased nor pusilanimous in her mind.

"'Your father, indeed!' says she. 'A decent man he was,
whatever faults he had, and is it likely he'd come
back wid webbed feet to be peckin' at cabbage-stalks?'

"'Jer knows the sciences,' says Terence.

"'Jer knows how to get two shillings and tobacco from
a fool,' says Biddy.

"'Woman,' says Terence, 'have respect for the dead.'

"'I have,' says she; 'and that's why I won't have
their characters destroyed by every long-necked bird in the parish.'

"But Terence was determined. The hamper was brought from the
loft—a great strong basket, wide in the belly and narrow
at the mouth—and a bed of hay was put in
it to keep the gandher comfortable on the road. Then
Larry and Shawn were ordered to be ready before sunrise.

"That night Jer came to the house to perform some
private observations, and Terence brought out a bottle to assist
him. Jer said spirits was dangerous in cases of transmigration,
but a moderate quantity steadied the intellect; and, as neither
of them could agree what quantity was moderate, they continued
the investigation till the bottle was empty.

"After Jer went home, or in the direction he believed
to be home, Terence remained in the yard, talkin' to
the gandher.

"'Father,' says he, 'if you're in there, don't be alarmed.
It's only a deception for your own benefit.'

"The gandher gave a sleepy hiss.

"'I understand,' says Terence. 'You're bound by supernatural regulations. Well,
good night, father.'

"He took two steps toward the house, but the earth
made a turn under him, and he sat down sudden.
Then he remembered the hamper and thought the hay inside
it would make an elegant bed till his head settled.
So into it he climbed, feet first, and stretched himself
under the hay; and there he fell asleep, wid a
serenity that neither conscience nor Biddy could have afforded him
indoors.

"Before dawn Larry and Shawn came to the yard. They
were stout lads, but fearful, especially Shawn, whose mother had
warned him never to trust a white bird, a red-haired
woman, or any man that asked the time after midnight.

"'Where's the gandher?' says Larry.

"'There,' says Shawn.

"The bird was standin' beside the hamper, lookin' as innocent
as milk.

"'Catch him,' says Larry.

"'Catch him yourself,' says Shawn.

"'I'm holdin' the hamper.'

"'It's on the ground.'

"'I'm morally holdin' it,' says Larry.

"At last, after racin' the bird twice round the haggard
and fallin' wan over another in the dung-heap, they caught
him and put him into the hamper. The gandher settled
on top of the hay, and Terence, bein' buried underneath
and beyond all immediate information, never stirred.

"They tied down the lid, lifted the hamper into the
cart, and started for Tipperary, wid the mare trottin' brisk
and the dawn comin' grey over the hills.

"For the first mile all went peaceable. The gandher shifted
now and then, and the boys tried not to hear
it. But presently they came to a part of the
road where the stones were plentiful and the road itself
was absent. The cart struck a rut deep enough to
drown a curate, gave a leap, and came down wid
a crack that woke Terence.

"He didn't know where he was. He felt feathers on
his face, hay in his mouth, and the whole world
jumpin' under him.

"'Murdher!' says he, muffled-like. 'Where are ye takin' me?'

"Shawn dropped the reins.

"'Did you hear that?' says he.

"'Hear what?' says Larry, though his hair was standin' up
under his hat.

"'The gandher spoke.'

"'It did not.'

"From the hamper came another voice: 'Larry! Shawn! Stop the
cart, ye born robbers!'

"The mare stopped of her own accord, havin' no desire
to be involved in family revelations.

"'It knows our names,' whispers Shawn.

"'Plenty knows our names,' says Larry. 'There's no witchcraft in
that.'

"'Who's plenty?'

"'People.'

"'Is the gandher people?'

"Before Larry could answer, the cart-wheel sank in another hole,
and Terence was skelped against the side of the basket.

"'I'm Terence Mooney!' he roared. 'Let me out, or I'll
pulverise every bone in the two of ye!'

"At that, Shawn gave a screech.

"'It says it's Terence!'

"'It can't be Terence,' says Larry. 'We left Terence at
home.'

"'Then it's his father, and he's confused in the generations.'

"'Drive on,' says Larry.

"'Where?'

"'Anywhere there's a priest.'

"So they turned from the market road and drove for
Father Crotty's house, the mare gallopin', the cart rattlin', the
hamper jumpin', the gandher squawkin', and Terence cursin' from underneath
in such a mixed and terrible manner that the boys
were certain all the lost souls in Ireland had taken
lodgings in the basket.

"Father Crotty was at breakfast when they arrived. He was
a round, comfortable man, strict upon fasting in others, but
charitable in his interpretation of it for himself. Larry rushed
into the kitchen, white as flour.

"'Your raverance,' says he, 'the gandher is talkin'.'

"'Then let him finish,' says Father Crotty, butterin' his bread.

"'He says he's Terence Mooney.'

"The priest put down the knife.

"'That,' says he, 'is a different theological complexion.'

"He came outside, where the hamper was heavin' and shakin'
on the cart.

"'Who is within?' says Father Crotty.

"'It's me, your raverance!' bawled Terence. 'Open this murderin' basket!'

"Father Crotty stepped back.

"'A most accurate imitation,' says he.

"'Accurate!' says Terence. 'I'll give ye accuracy if I get
out!'

"'Do not answer it irreverently,' says the priest to the
boys. 'Evil powers are remarkable mimics.'

"'I'm no evil power!' cries Terence.

"'Naturally it would say so,' says Father Crotty.

"Now, there was a little bridge near the house, where
the river ran quick between two banks. Father Crotty remembered
that spirits, fairies, witches, and troublesome influences in general had
a dislike to runnin' water; so he ordered the hamper
to be carried there and hung from the rail by
a rope.

"Larry and Shawn didn't like touchin' it, but a priest's
order is a priest's order, especially when the priest himself
stands at a safe distance. They got a rope, passed
it round the hamper, and hoisted it over the stream.

"'What are ye at?' shouted Terence, feelin' himself swung into
the air.

"'Be silent!' says Father Crotty, puttin' on his stole.

"'I will not!'

"'You hear the obstinacy of it,' says the priest.

"Then he began the prayers, first in Latin and afterward
in English, lest the spirit might be deficient in education.
At every response Terence answered wid a threat; at every
threat the gandher flapped its wings; and at every flap
Larry and Shawn let the rope slip a little.

"'Depart!' cried Father Crotty.

"'Open the lid!' cried Terence.

"'Declare thy name!'

"'I declared it twenty times!'

"'If thou art Terence Mooney—'

"'If!'

"'—give a proper sign.'

"'I'll give ye a sign on the side of the
head!'

"The priest lifted the holy water. At that instant the
mare, which had followed them to the bridge, put her
nose against his shoulder. Father Crotty, thinkin' he was seized
from behind by an infernal agency, made a tremendous start
and emptied the whole vessel over Larry.

"Larry let go the rope.

"The hamper fell two feet, stopped wid a jerk, and
the bottom, which had been weakened by Terence's struggles, gave
way altogether. Down came Terence, the gandher, the hay, and
half the basket—splash dash into the river.

"Shawn, who still held one end of the rope, was
pulled over the rail after them. Larry caught at Shawn
and went too. Father Crotty stepped forward to save Larry,
the mare stepped forward to save Father Crotty, or perhaps
to smell his pocket, and in another instant priest, boys,
bird, basket, hay, and Terence were all rollin' together in
the water, gasp-in', puffin', kickin', prayin', cursin', and quackin'—though the
quackin' came from two ducks that had no concern in
the matter except curiosity.

"The river wasn't deep, glory be to God, but it
was wet beyond description. Terence rose first, wid the remains
of the hamper round his waist and the gandher perched
on his shoulder. Father Crotty came up next, holdin' his
hat in one hand and Shawn by the hair wid
the other. Larry crawled to the bank on all fours,
and the mare stood in the middle of the stream,
lookin' at them wid the compassion of a Christian and
the judgment of a horse.

"'Now!' says Terence, shakin' himself. 'Will some one tell me
the manin' of this assassination?'

"'Terence!' says Father Crotty.

"'Who else?'

"'How came you into the hamper?'

"Terence opened his mouth, but before he could answer Biddy
Mooney appeared on the road. Jer Garvan was behind her,
for the news had travelled home by a short road,
as bad news always does, and Biddy had come at
a pace that left Jer's science half a mile in
the rear.

"'How did he get into it?' says she. 'That's what
I'd like to know.'

"Terence looked at Biddy, then at the priest, then at
the boys, and then at the gandher.

"'I was put there,' says he.

"'By whom?' says Father Crotty.

"'By—by supernatural circumventions.'

"'You climbed into it drunk,' says Biddy.

"'I deny the intoxication,' says Terence. 'I may have been
fatigued in the intellect.'

"'He was transmigrated accidental,' says Jer, comin' up breathless. 'It's
a case of double occupancy.'

"'Hold your tongue, Jer Garvan,' says Father Crotty.

"'Wid respect to your raverance,' says Jer, 'the ecclesiastical experiment
was conducted too near the water.'

"'Be off,' says the priest.

"'That itself proves the obscurity,' says Jer, and off he
went.

"Now, you'd think the matter was plain enough, your raverance;
but it wasn't. Larry and Shawn swore to their dyin'
day that the gandher had spoken in Terence's own voice,
for hadn't they heard it before ever they knew Terence
was in the basket? Biddy maintained that Terence had crawled
in drunk, but she would never say so outside the
family, because a woman may abuse her husband herself and
still break the head of any neighbour that agrees wid
her. Terence declared he had no remembrance of enterin' the
hamper, and therefore it could not have occurred in a
regular or legal manner. And Father Crotty, when asked for
his opinion, said the affair involved questions upon which it
would be rash to pronounce without authority from a bishop.

"As for the gandher, he said nothin', and that was
considered the strongest evidence of all.

"From that day Terence treated the bird better than ever.
It had the best oats, the first cabbage-leaves, and a
dry bed near the hearth in winter. If Biddy complained,
Terence would say, 'After what the poor crathur suffered in
the river, small blame to him if he requires comfort.'

"The gandher lived to a mighty age, though not so
mighty as Terence, who survived till he was ninety-three, or
ninety-seven, accord-in' to which of his grandchildren was tellin' it.
He died in his bed, respectable and surrounded by his
family, leavin' seven sons, five daughters, forty-one grandchildren, and postariors
beyond computation.

"And the farm beyant there," concluded my informant, pointing through
the deepening twilight, "belongs this minute to Tim Mooney, that's
descended from Terence in the direct lenial line. The gandher's
descendants is there too, though no wan can identify them
particular, on account of intermarriage."

He rose as he spoke, shook out his frieze coat,
and called to the horse. The wheel came free of
the mire with scarcely an effort, as if the cart
also had waited only for the conclusion of the tale.

Night had gathered over Ballyclough. One pale star trembled above
the broken chapel, and from the unseen river there came
a continuous murmur, now resembling laughter, now lamentation. My companion
drove away, saluting me with his stick; and I remained
for some moments upon the road, listening until the creak
of his wheels had faded among the hills.

Whether the gander of Ballyclough ever contained a human spirit
I shall not presume to determine. There are mysteries which
survive, not because they cannot be explained, but because explanation
would require men to surrender their dignity, women their discretion,
priests their authority, and neighbours their delight in wonder. Such
sacrifices are beyond ordinary nature. Thus error becomes tradition, tradition
ripens into history, and history, repeated beside an autumn road,
acquires a truth more enduring than fact.

I mounted and rode onward through the darkness; and Ireland,
beautiful, wounded, credulous, mirthful Ireland, seemed to move beside me—her
sorrow in the wind, her laughter in the water, and
in both an immortality no conqueror had ever understood.

# The Variable Eyes of Mr. Sampey

Before he became Hoolagaloo, the Wild Man of the Calabrian
Wilderness, Battista Belladonna was an honest woodcutter, and possessed no
more wilderness than was ordinarily collected beneath his fingernails.

He was a prodigious man. His shoulders appeared to have
been designed for supporting municipal buildings; his hands resembled two
discontented hams; and when he struck a tree with his
axe, the tree commonly experienced a brief period of meditation
and then fell down. Battista had black hair in abundance,
black eyebrows in profusion, and a black mustache of such
aggressive luxuriance that sparrows sometimes reconsidered their migration upon seeing
it.

His nose, however, was once quite ordinary.

This feature was diminished during a disagreement with another woodcutter
concerning a girl, a mule, and the ownership of a
green umbrella. The precise merits of the quarrel were never
determined, because the girl married a tinsmith, the mule died
without making a statement, and the umbrella was afterward discovered
to belong to the village priest. But in the course
of the controversy Battista's adversary bit—or cut, or otherwise detached—the
southern portion of Battista's nose.

A man may endure poverty, disappointment, and the triumph of
a rival; but he dislikes to endure them without a
complete nose.

Battista therefore visited a surgeon in Naples, a small, polished
gentleman who had read several German books and was consequently
prepared to perform anything. This physician considered Battista's injury with
scientific ardor.

"I shall restore it," he said.

"With what?" demanded Battista.

"With yourself."

The operation was explained. A strip of skin would be
taken from Battista's scalp, where Nature had supplied more covering
than was needed, and attached to the mutilated organ. The
proposal possessed the grandeur of finance: a surplus in one
department was to correct a deficit in another.

Battista consented.

The surgeon performed his task with skill, ceremony, and an
assistant who fainted only twice. The wound healed. The nose
flourished. Unfortunately, so did the hair.

At first the new growth was a modest down, barely
perceptible except in sunlight or conversation at close quarters. Then
it thickened. Within six months Battista's reconstructed nose was surrounded
by a glossy black tuft, vigorous, curling, and apparently determined
to rejoin his mustache by the shortest route. He shaved
it. It returned. He singed it. It returned with increased
confidence. He applied oils recommended by a barber, acids sold
by an apothecary, and the prayer of an aunt who
enjoyed an extensive reputation in cases of warts. Nothing discouraged
it.

People stared. Children followed him. Dogs barked from motives of
professional jealousy.

Thus afflicted by science, Battista abandoned Naples and went to
America, where grotesqueness, if sufficiently advertised, has never been obliged
to remain a private misfortune.

At that time Signor Aloysius Castellani conducted Castellani's Universal Museum
of Marvels, Instruction, Innocent Amusement, and Living Curiosities on Fourteenth
Street. The institution contained a tattooed admiral who had never
seen the sea; a Circassian beauty born in Hoboken; the
skeleton of a mermaid composed with great ingenuity from a
codfish and a monkey; and Professor Peregrine, who swallowed swords
except on Sundays, when he swallowed fire for religious reasons.

Castellani saw Battista and became emotional.

"My friend," he said, "Providence has made you hideous."

Battista scowled.

"Do not misunderstand me. I speak as a businessman. Hideousness
without management is calamity; hideousness properly exhibited is capital. What
wages do you earn?"

Battista named the sum paid him for unloading fruit.

Castellani offered four times as much and invented a history
before luncheon.

According to the enormous red bills that presently covered walls,
fences, and the dignified flank of a streetcar, Battista was
no longer an Italian woodcutter injured in an undistinguished quarrel.
He was Hoolagaloo, Last Prince of the Hair-Nosed Tribe, captured
after desperate combat in the volcanic forests of an island
not shown on prejudiced maps. He ate bark, conversed with
leopards, and could smell human fear at a distance of
seven miles. His costume consisted of goatskins, brass bracelets, and
an expression of insurrectionary ferocity. Three times daily he rattled
the bars of a gilded cage and attempted to devour
an attendant.

Visitors adored him. Boys offered him peanuts. Ladies shrieked when
he thrust his tufted nose between the bars. Bold gentlemen
pulled the tuft to test its authenticity, an indignity for
which Castellani paid Battista an additional dollar and a half
per week.

Prosperity reconciles a man to much. Battista accepted Hoolagaloo, and
Hoolagaloo accepted the public.

Then he fell in love.

Zoe Castellani, the proprietor's daughter, was twenty, rosy, plump, and
romantically dissatisfied. Every afternoon and evening she appeared upon the
stage as the Severed Lady. Her head, surrounded by gilt
moldings and red velvet, rested apparently upon a silver platter
while her body was presumed to be enjoying an independent
existence in some remote cupboard. The illusion was accomplished by
mirrors, trapdoors, black cloth, and two assistant carpenters; but Zoe's
smile supplied the poetry.

Between performances she read romances.

These books had persuaded her that the world contained one
man intended especially for her: pale, courageous, sorrowful, and endowed
with eyes of soft, limpid amber. She did not insist
that he own a castle, though she preferred battlements. She
would overlook the absence of a white horse. But upon
the amber eyes she was adamant.

"My hero will have them," she told her father.

"Then let him have a salary too," said Castellani. "Eyes
don't pay rent unless they're in the business."

Battista's eyes were black—deep, hot, and efficient, like two holes
burned through iron. He nevertheless worshiped Zoe with the devotion
of a volcano toward a neighboring village. He brought her
flowers, boxes of sugared fruit, and once a bracelet which
had disappeared from a visitor during the crush around his
cage. Zoe accepted the flowers, returned the bracelet, and regarded
his nose with kind but unconquerable alarm.

The other lover was Mr. Horace Sampey.

Sampey wrote paragraphs for newspapers, jokes for comic weeklies, speeches
for aldermen, and letters for persons whose emotions exceeded their
grammar. He was thin, pale, neat, and poor. His clothes
were brushed beyond the reasonable endurance of cloth. He had
a high forehead, a restrained manner, and gray eyes of
no distinction whatever.

He came frequently to the museum under the pretext of
preparing an article called "Strange Souls Behind the Sideshow Curtain."
The article was never prepared. Sampey secretly loved Zoe.

He loved her rosy cheeks, her gilt frame, her belief
in destiny, and even the small red mark made upon
her chin by the silver platter. But love, when combined
with poverty and observation, does not immediately speak. It calculates.

Sampey perceived three difficulties. Castellani admired money. Battista possessed money.
Zoe required amber eyes.

The first difficulty was financial, the second muscular, and the
third optical. Of these, Sampey attacked the optical one first,
because it seemed least likely to break his neck.

Near the museum lived Dr. Ezekiel Prinn, an optician, inventor,
and unsuccessful philosopher. Dr. Prinn manufactured tiny shells of colored
glass, curved with exquisite accuracy, which could be placed over
the visible portion of the eyeball. He had designed them
for an actor who wished to play Othello with blue
eyes on Monday and Richard the Third with green eyes
on Tuesday. The actor had fled without paying; science is
frequently embarrassed by art.

Sampey purchased two pairs: one amber and one scarlet.

On a Thursday evening, while Zoe sat behind the stage
reading of a baron who had rescued eleven maidens from
a tower before breakfast, Sampey entered quietly.

"Miss Castellani," he said, "I must bid you farewell."

She lowered the book.

"Farewell? Are you going away?"

"I am going where a man goes when he has
discovered that his presence brings danger to one whom he
esteems."

This sentence was meaningless, but it had architecture.

"What danger?"

Sampey turned his face toward the green-shaded lamp. Over his
gray eyes he had placed the amber shells.

Zoe stared.

There were the eyes—soft, pale, limpid amber! They glowed with
melancholy effulgence. They were the eyes of the imprisoned prince,
the forest knight, the wounded captain, and every other excellent
person who had ever occupied a final chapter.

"You!" she whispered.

Sampey sighed.

"I had hoped you would never see them."

"Why?"

"They betray what I conceal."

"What do they betray?"

"A certain capacity for heroism," said Sampey modestly.

He explained that his eyes altered under the pressure of
profound emotion. Ordinarily gray, because he lived quietly and suppressed
himself, they became amber when tenderness awakened the nobler forces
of his nature. In moments of righteous anger they assumed
another color, which he declined to mention.

The charm of faith is its absurdity. A probable statement
must fight for acceptance; an impossible one enters the mind
dressed as royalty.

Zoe believed him.

"Then you are the one," she said.

"I feared as much."

There are men who announce virtue with trumpets, and men
who permit a pair of glass shells to announce it
for them. Sampey belonged to the quieter school.

Zoe extended her hand. He kissed it with ceremonious restraint.
At that instant Battista, who had approached with a paper
sack of roasted chestnuts, saw them through the half-open door.

The sack burst in his hand.

Chestnuts rolled down the corridor like an escaping congregation.

Battista entered. In the costume of Hoolagaloo he was always
formidable; in love he became geological.

"You!" he cried, pointing at Sampey. "You come here again,
I break you!"

"Battista!" said Zoe.

"I break him little—so little they sweep him away!"

Sampey's heart attempted to leave by way of his collar.
He was not physically brave. Indeed, he had always held
that courage was an admirable quality to recommend to other
people. But a timid man who has prepared his illusion
possesses an advantage over a strong man who has not
prepared his nerves.

Sampey closed his eyes, slipped a handkerchief to his face,
and in one practiced movement exchanged the amber shells for
the scarlet ones.

Then he looked at Battista.

The effect was prodigious.

Where mild gray eyes had been, two disks of violent
scarlet burned beneath Sampey's pale brow. They did not resemble
human eyes. They resembled warning lamps hung at the entrance
to the infernal regions.

Battista stopped.

Sampey advanced one step.

"You have awakened," he said softly, "the color I hoped
never to show."

Battista backed against the wall.

"You know what amber means," continued Sampey. "You do not
know what scarlet means."

Battista did not, and ignorance supplied details more terrible than
information could have furnished.

"What does it mean?" asked Zoe breathlessly.

"Justice," said Sampey.

This answer was so simple that it appalled everybody.

Battista crossed himself, stumbled over the chestnuts, and fled. He
did not stop until he reached his cage, where he
locked himself in and refused to emerge for the final
performance. Castellani was obliged to announce that Hoolagaloo was observing
a sacred tribal fast.

Sampey replaced the gray shells—which were no shells at all,
but merely his natural eyes—and went home trembling. A victory
of intellect is exhausting when intellect has expected to be
killed throughout it.

The next morning Battista told Castellani that Sampey possessed diabolical
eyes.

Castellani became interested.

"Different colors?"

"Red! Like blood, fire, tomato—everything bad!"

"And he can do this when he wishes?"

"He looks—bah!—a man's bones become cold."

Castellani summoned Sampey.

The young writer arrived in his shiniest coat and found
the proprietor seated beneath a poster advertising Madame Zuleika, the
Egyptian Prophetess, who had been born Mary Flannigan and could
foresee everything except dismissal.

"Mr. Sampey," said Castellani, "I am informed that you have
remarkable eyes."

"They are a burden."

"Can you make them amber?"

"When the proper emotion commands me."

"Scarlet?"

"Under provocation."

"Purple?"

"Not at present."

Castellani leaned forward.

"How much?"

Sampey looked injured.

"You mistake me, sir."

"I never mistake talent. How much to exhibit them?"

"My emotional nature is not for sale."

"Everything in this building is for sale except the fire
exits. Twenty-five dollars a week."

Sampey shook his head.

"Thirty."

"My dignity—"

"Forty, and your name above the Fat Boy."

Sampey rose as if offended.

Castellani seized his sleeve.

"Wait. What do you want?"

Here Sampey displayed that heroism which truly belonged to him:
the heroism of negotiation. A poor man asking for wages
is timid because he needs them; a poor man asking
for ownership becomes sublime because refusal leaves him no poorer.

"Half the museum," said Sampey.

Castellani laughed until the tattooed admiral came from the adjoining
room to see whether medical assistance was required.

"Half!"

"In exchange," said Sampey calmly, "for the exclusive exhibition rights
to the only man alive who alters the color of
his eyes by moral force."

"You're a newspaper scribbler!"

"Precisely. I can also write the advertisements."

This consideration sobered Castellani.

Sampey produced a proposed handbill.

> THE HUMAN PRISM!
> PROFESSOR HORACE SAMPEY,
> WHOSE EYES OBEY HIS SOUL!
> GRAY IN REPOSE! AMBER IN LOVE! SCARLET IN WRATH!
> PHYSICIANS BAFFLED—CLERGYMEN IMPRESSED—LADIES WARNED!

Castellani read it twice. He saw crowds pressing at the
doors, extra performances, illustrated interviews, and an increase in the
price of reserved chairs. He saw money, which is the
most convincing color known to man.

"Not half the property," he said. "Half the profits."

"Half-interest in the business."

"One-third."

"Half."

"Two-fifths."

"Half, and I write all notices without additional compensation."

Castellani groaned. He walked about. He examined Sampey's commonplace gray
eyes with resentment. Finally he extended his hand.

"Half—and may posterity condemn you."

"Posterity seldom audits successful men," said Sampey.

The contract was signed that afternoon.

Within a week New York encountered Professor Sampey, the Chromatic
Wonder of the Human Soul.

His exhibition was conducted with dignity. Sampey sat upon a
velvet chair in a darkened cabinet while Castellani lectured upon
the ocular system. Gray represented intellectual calm. At the entrance
of Zoe, bearing a white rose, Sampey lowered his lids;
an attendant adjusted the lamps; and when he looked up,
his eyes shone amber. Ladies sighed. One elderly gentleman fainted,
though afterward he admitted that the room had been close.

Then Hoolagaloo was introduced in chains and instructed to threaten
Zoe. Sampey's eyes became scarlet. Hoolagaloo recoiled with such sincerity
that the audience applauded for seven minutes.

Battista hated the act, but Castellani increased his pay. Hatred,
like other luxuries, becomes supportable on a good salary.

The newspapers wrote columns. Doctors disputed. A professor from Boston
declared the phenomenon impossible, which brought three hundred additional visitors
on Saturday. Ministers referred to Sampey in sermons. A manufacturer
offered him five hundred dollars to make his eyes blue
and endorse a washing powder. Sampey declined, having discovered that
restraint increases value.

He moved into better rooms. He bought a new coat.
He paid his debts, including the small account of Dr.
Prinn, who promised professional silence and received free admission for
life. Castellani began calling him "my dear partner." Zoe called
him Horace.

Battista watched these developments with a jealousy so dense that
it seemed to increase his weight.

He no longer believed wholly in the supernatural explanation. Terror,
after repeated performances and regular wages, had softened into suspicion.
He noticed that Sampey always covered his eyes with a
handkerchief before the transformation. He noticed Dr. Prinn entering the
dressing room. He noticed, finally, a tiny amber curve of
glass fall from Sampey's pocket.

Battista picked it up.

He held it before the light. He placed it against
his own eye and saw the world transformed into a
landscape of yellow smoke. His fear vanished. In its place
came triumph, immense and hairy.

"The scoundrel!" he exclaimed. "The little paper man! I expose
him!"

He did not expose Sampey immediately. Vengeance, to be satisfactory,
requires an audience.

The opportunity arrived at a supper given by Castellani to
celebrate the museum's most profitable month. A long table was
set upon the stage beneath garlands, paper lanterns, and the
suspended lower half of the Severed Lady apparatus. The company
included performers, reporters, two aldermen, Dr. Prinn, and a theatrical
manager who had eaten free meals in every celebrated establishment
in Manhattan.

Zoe sat beside Sampey. She wore a pale blue dress
and a white rose. Sampey's eyes were amber, for romance
was officially in progress. Castellani rose with champagne in his
hand.

"To my daughter," he began, "and to my partner, Professor
Sampey, whose extraordinary gift—"

"Gift!" roared Battista.

He leaped upon the stage in evening clothes of heroic
tightness. The guests turned. Professor Peregrine accidentally swallowed a dessert
fork and was obliged to retire.

Battista pointed at Sampey.

"Fraud!"

Every museum performer looked offended. The word, used in that
company, had an almost personal breadth.

Castellani frowned.

"What is this?"

"This!" Battista held up the amber glass shell. "His wonderful
eye! Glass! He puts it on. He takes it off.
No soul, no miracle, no hero—glass!"

A silence fell.

Dr. Prinn attempted to disappear behind the Fat Boy, an
ambitious but partially successful operation.

Battista strode to Sampey and, before anyone could prevent him,
pressed a thumb beneath the young man's eyelid. The amber
shell slipped out and landed in the butter.

A reporter sprang upon his chair.

"Magnificent!" he cried, and began taking notes.

Zoe stared at Sampey. One of his eyes was amber;
the other, exposed, was gray. Destiny had rarely appeared more
inconvenienced.

"You deceived me," she said.

Sampey removed the remaining shell and placed it beside his
plate. His face was pale, but his manner remained composed.

"I improved the evidence," he replied.

Battista laughed with the terrible joy of a man who
sees his enemy crushed beneath fact.

"No hero! No magic! No marriage! I marry Zoe now!"

"Your chronology is defective," said Sampey.

"What?"

"You cannot marry her now."

"Why not?"

"Because I married her thirty hours ago."

This announcement produced a second silence, superior in quality to
the first.

Zoe blushed. Castellani opened his mouth. Battista appeared to have
been struck upon the head by invisible furniture.

Sampey drew a folded certificate from his breast pocket and
handed it to Castellani.

The proprietor read it.

"Married?" he said.

"Yesterday morning," said Zoe. "At City Hall."

"But why didn't you tell me?"

"Horace said it would be commercially unwise before the supper."

Castellani looked at Sampey. Then he looked at the reporters.
Then he looked at the contract by which Sampey owned
half the business. His paternal emotion and his commercial perspicacity
engaged in a brief struggle. Commercial perspicacity won by two
falls.

He embraced his daughter.

"My child!"

He embraced Sampey less tenderly but more strategically.

"My son and partner!"

Battista uttered a noise from the Calabrian wilderness.

"He is cheat!"

Castellani turned upon him.

"And what are you? Did you ever converse with a
leopard? Were you ever prince of anything? You were cutting
wood when I rescued you from honesty! This whole institution
stands upon deception, presentation, upholstery, and rent. The question is
not whether a marvel is false. The question is whether
it draws."

He plucked the glass shell from the butter and polished
it with a napkin.

"This draws."

The reporters applauded. They already perceived the next day's headlines:
**HUMAN PRISM EXPOSED—SECRET WEDDING REVEALED—WILD MAN FOILED AT FEAST.** Truth
is valuable when it can be printed large.

Battista looked at Zoe.

"You knew?"

"Not at first," she said. "Horace confessed before we married."

"And you marry him after?"

Zoe glanced lovingly at Sampey's gray eyes.

"He wished so much to be my hero that he
manufactured the necessary color. I considered that very devoted."

Faith does not perish when confronted with fraud. It merely
revises the miracle.

Battista looked about the table for sympathy and found only
professional admiration. The Circassian Beauty asked Dr. Prinn whether he
could make her eyes violet. The tattooed admiral proposed an
act in which his tattoos changed expression. Castellani was already
whispering to a reporter that the exposure itself had been
rehearsed at enormous expense.

The defeated Wild Man tore the ornamental tuft from a
paper lantern, cast it upon the floor, and departed.

He left show business the next morning.

In later years travelers in a certain northern lumber camp
sometimes encountered an immense Italian chopping wood with a ferocity
that caused forests to recede before official surveys required them
to do so. He wore a broad hat over his
black hair and kept his tufted nose concealed by a
red handkerchief. If anyone asked whether he had once been
Hoolagaloo, Last Prince of the Hair-Nosed Tribe, he raised his
axe in a manner discouraging historical research.

Castellani's museum prospered.

The exposure of Professor Sampey did not injure attendance. On
the contrary, the public came in multitudes to see how
the deception had been accomplished. Sampey added a lecture entitled
"The Ethics of Optical Emotion," during which he demonstrated the
amber and scarlet shells, discussed self-command, and sold souvenir glass
eyes in the lobby. He later invented emerald jealousy, sapphire
grief, and a purple state described as transcendental finance.

He became wealthy. He wrote no more speeches for aldermen.

Zoe continued for a time as the Severed Lady, though
marriage made the title seem unnecessarily ominous. Eventually she retired
from the platter and supervised costumes. She remained convinced that
Sampey was heroic. Perhaps she was right. He had faced
poverty, Hoolagaloo, Castellani, exposure, and matrimony, and had profited by
all four.

As for Sampey, he loved his wife, honored his contract,
advertised with genius, and never again deceived Zoe except in
matters involving the price of presents and the condition of
his health after supper.

His eyes remained naturally gray.

But whenever Zoe recalled their courtship and asked to see
them once more as soft, pale, limpid amber, he put
in the glass shells, turned toward the lamp, and regarded
her with the effulgent tenderness of a manufactured destiny.

She believed in him.

And belief, when it is happy, solvent, and properly exhibited
twice daily, is perhaps as genuine a marvel as the
world can afford.

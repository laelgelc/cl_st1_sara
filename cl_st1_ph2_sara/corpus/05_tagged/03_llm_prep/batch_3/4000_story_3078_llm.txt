In the days of the Khaleefeh Hároon Er-Rasheed, when Baghdad
was the abode of peace and the meeting-place of merchants
and learned men, it happened that the Khaleefeh was troubled
with wakefulness; and he sent for Jaạfar the Barmekee, his
Wezeer, and said to him,—O Jaạfar, my bosom is contracted,
and sleep hath fled from mine eyes; therefore I desire
to descend into the city, that I may behold the
condition of my subjects.

So Jaạfar replied,—I hear and obey, O Prince of the
Faithful. And the Khaleefeh disguised himself as a merchant, and
Jaạfar did likewise, and Mesroor the executioner went with them
as their servant; and they passed forth from the palace
by a private door, and traversed the streets and markets
until they came to the bank of the Tigris.

There they beheld an old fisherman carrying upon his head
a net and a basket, and walking with bent back
and sorrowful countenance. The Khaleefeh said to him,—O sheykh, what
is thine occupation? He answered,—I am a fisherman, and I
have a wife and children, and from morning until this
hour I have cast my net and caught nothing; and
now I return to them without bread, and know not
how I shall endure their crying. Then he recited these
verses:—

The poor man walketh amid mankind in fear,
Though no offence be written to his name;
The wealthy speak, and every ear inclineth,
The poor complain, and straight they bear the blame.
How many judges smile upon oppression,
And call the weak man guilty of his shame!

The Khaleefeh, hearing this, was moved with compassion, and said,—Wilt
thou return to the river and cast thy net once
for my sake? Whatever thou drawest forth I will buy
of thee for a hundred pieces of gold.

The fisherman rejoiced and replied,—By Allah, O my lord, I
will cast it, though I have cast it a hundred
times. So he went down to the water and threw
in the net, and waited awhile, and then drew it
toward the bank. He found it heavy, and called upon
the three to aid him; and they pulled together until
there appeared a chest of palm-wood, bound with iron bands
and secured with a lock. The Khaleefeh gave the fisherman
the promised gold, whereupon the man kissed his hands, prayed
for the prolongation of his life, and departed wondering at
the bounty of fate.

Then Mesroor lifted the chest upon his shoulders, and they
returned with it to the palace. When they had entered
a private chamber, the Khaleefeh ordered that lights should be
brought, and he commanded Mesroor to break the lock. Mesroor
struck it with a hammer, and the lock flew apart.
They raised the lid and found within it a basket
sewn in red wool. This they cut open, and beneath
it was an izár wrapped many times about a bundle.
They removed fold after fold, and lo, there appeared the
body of a young woman, cut in pieces, and her
beauty remained visible notwithstanding the violence that had been done
unto her.

At this sight the Khaleefeh wept, and afterward his face
became red with anger, and he turned to Jaạfar and
cried,—O dog of a Wezeer, shall a woman be slain
in my city, and shall her body be cast into
my river, while I sit upon the throne and know
nothing? By Allah, I will take vengeance for this oppressed
one, or I will hang thee and forty of the
sons of thine uncle at the gate of my palace!

Jaạfar said,—O Prince of the Faithful, grant me time in
which to seek the murderer.

He answered,—I grant thee three days; and if thou bringest
him not before me when they are ended, thy blood
and the blood of thy kindred shall answer for hers.

Jaạfar went forth with a sorrowful heart, saying within himself,—How
shall I discover the murderer of an unknown woman whose
body came from the Tigris? If I seize one upon
suspicion, I may destroy the innocent; and if I seize
none, the Khaleefeh will destroy me and my family. There
is no strength nor power but in Allah, the High,
the Great.

During the first day he caused proclamation to be made
throughout Baghdad, and questioned the keepers of the gates, the
ferrymen, the water-carriers, the boatmen, and the watchmen of the
quarters; but he learned nothing. On the second day he
summoned physicians, washerwomen, brokers, and sellers of women's garments, yet
no person knew the murdered damsel. On the third day
he renewed his inquiries from dawn until sunset, but the
hidden thing remained hidden.

Then the Khaleefeh commanded that Jaạfar and forty of his
relatives should be brought forth and crucified before the palace.
The beams were set up, and ropes were prepared, and
a crier went through Baghdad proclaiming,—Whoso desireth to behold the
execution of Jaạfar the Barmekee and forty of his kindred,
let him come unto the place before the palace!

The people assembled in crowds, weeping for Jaạfar because of
his generosity and justice. He took leave of his household
and submitted himself to the decree, and the executioners came
near to bind him. But while they were thus engaged,
a young man, comely of form and clad in clean
garments, pressed through the people, crying,—Make way for me, O
people! Bring me before the Wezeer!

When he stood before Jaạfar he said,—O Wezeer, release thyself
and thy family, for I am he who slew the
young woman found in the chest. I killed her with
mine own hand, cut her body in pieces, placed it
in the basket and the chest, and cast it into
the Tigris.

Jaạfar rejoiced at his words, yet he grieved for the
youth, and was about to question him when an aged
sheykh pushed through the crowd and cried,—Believe not this young
man, O Wezeer! He hath spoken falsely to save another.
I am the murderer, and upon me alone must the
punishment fall.

The young man said,—O sheykh, return to thy house, for
thou art guiltless, and none but I committed the deed.

But the old man answered,—Nay, my son. Thou art young,
and the world is before thee; but I am old
and have taken my portion of its joys and griefs.
Let my life redeem thine.

So Jaạfar took them both before the Khaleefeh, and related
what had passed. The Khaleefeh looked upon the two and
said,—Which of you killed the woman?

The young man replied,—I killed her, O Prince of the
Faithful. I can describe the chest, the cords, the red
wool, the izár, and the scar upon her right hand;
and if thou command me, I will relate the cause
of the deed.

The old man bowed his head, for he knew that
the proof was complete. Then the Khaleefeh said,—Tell me thy
story, and speak truth; for falsehood before kings is destruction.

The young man replied,—Know, O Prince of the Faithful, that
this sheykh is the father of the murdered woman, and
she was my cousin and my wife. I married her
when she was yet young, and during eleven years she
dwelt with me, and I saw in her no evil.
She bore me three sons, and she guarded my house
and substance, and obeyed me in all that was lawful.

Some months ago she fell sick, and her weakness increased.
I brought physicians and medicines, and sat beside her by
night, and denied her nothing that she desired. One day
she opened her eyes and said,—O son of mine uncle,
I long for an apple; if I smell one and
taste a little of it, perhaps my heart will be
refreshed.

I arose immediately and went through all the markets of
Baghdad, but found no apples, for it was not their
season. I asked the fruit-sellers, and one of them said,—Such
apples as thou seekest are nowhere save in the garden
of the Prince of the Faithful at Basra, and the
keeper will not part with them cheaply.

So, O Commander of the Faithful, I journeyed from Baghdad
to Basra for the sake of my wife. I endured
heat by day and cold by night; I ate little,
slept upon the ground, and ceased not to travel until
I reached the garden belonging to thee. I besought the
keeper and told him of my wife's illness, and he
sold me three apples for three pieces of gold. Each
apple bore upon its rind the seal of thy garden.

Then I returned to Baghdad, and the journey going and
returning occupied fifteen days. I entered my house and gave
the three apples to my wife. But she was exceedingly
weak, and she placed them beside her and took no
pleasure in them. After this I went to my shop.

While I sat there, a tall black slave passed before
me, tossing an apple in his hand. I beheld the
seal upon it, and knew it to be of the
three for which I had suffered so greatly. I called
to him and said,—O good slave, whence hadst thou this
apple?

He laughed and answered,—My mistress gave it me. Her husband
is a foolish merchant who loveth her beyond measure. She
was sick and desired apples, so he traveled to Basra
and bought three from the Khaleefeh's garden for three pieces
of gold. While the poor fool was absent, I visited
her; and she gave me this one to eat.

When I heard his words, the world became black before
my face, and the fire of jealousy consumed my reason.
I closed my shop and hastened home. I went to
the place where the apples had been laid and found
but two. Then I said to my wife,—Where is the
third apple?

She turned her face toward me and answered faintly,—I know
not, O son of mine uncle.

Then I believed the slave's lie, and Satan mastered me.
I drew a knife, slew her, and cut her body
in pieces. I wrapped the pieces in her izár, sewed
them within the basket, placed the basket in the chest,
and when night came I carried it to the Tigris
and cast it into the water.

After I returned, my eldest son sat weeping at the
door. I asked him,—Why dost thou weep? He replied,—O my
father, I took one of the apples that were beside
my mother, and went forth to play. A black slave
snatched it from my hand. I ran after him and
told him that it belonged to my sick mother, and
that thou hadst journeyed to Basra and paid three pieces
of gold for three apples; but he struck me and
went away with it. I feared thee, and therefore returned
not until now. I pray thee, tell my mother not
to punish me.

When I heard this, O Prince of the Faithful, I
knew that the slave had lied, and that my wife
was innocent and faithful. I uttered a great cry and
fell senseless. This old man, her father, came and found
me thus; and I told him all that had happened.
We have mourned together from that hour, and my tears
have not ceased to flow. Yet tears cannot restore the
slain, nor repentance join together that which the knife hath
divided.

The young man then bowed himself to the ground and
said,—Now take my life for hers, O Prince of the
Faithful. I have confessed without compulsion, and death is lighter
to me than the remembrance of my deed.

The Khaleefeh marveled at the story, and the old sheykh
wept, and Jaạfar and all who were present wept with
him. Then Hároon Er-Rasheed said,—By Allah, this is a strange
tale, and were it written with needles upon the corners
of the eyes, it would be a warning to him
who taketh warning. The young man was deceived by the
speech of the slave, and rage deprived him of understanding.
I pardon him because of his repentance and because he
came freely to death. But the lying slave is the
cause of this calamity, and justice demandeth that he be
found.

Then he turned to Jaạfar and said,—I command thee to
bring before me within three days the slave who stole
the apple. If thou bring him not, I will hang
thee in his place.

Jaạfar answered,—O Prince of the Faithful, shall I twice be
threatened with death for one chest?

The Khaleefeh said,—Dispute not my command. Thou hast three days.

So Jaạfar departed, saying,—The first search was for a murderer
who came to me of his own will; but how
shall I find among the slaves of Baghdad one who
stole an apple from a child? By Allah, I will
remain in my house, and the ready Dispeller of trouble
will do what He willeth.

The first day passed, and no tidings came. The second
day passed likewise. On the third, Jaạfar summoned the Ḳáḍee,
made his testament, paid his debts, and divided gifts among
his servants. He then bade farewell to his sons and
daughters, and all the house was filled with lamentation.

Among his children was a little daughter whom he loved
more tenderly than the rest. He drew her to him
and embraced her, and while he pressed her to his
breast he felt within her pocket something round and hard.
He put in his hand and drew forth an apple,
upon which was stamped the seal of the Khaleefeh's garden.

At the sight of it his colour changed, and he
cried,—O my daughter, whence came this apple?

She answered,—My father, our slave Reyhan brought it four days
ago, and I bought it of him for two pieces
of silver.

Jaạfar sent immediately for Reyhan, who was a tall black
slave. When the man came, Jaạfar held up the apple
and said,—Whence hadst thou this?

Reyhan trembled and replied,—O my master, I did not steal
it from thy house, nor from the palace.

Jaạfar cried,—Then from whom didst thou steal it? Speak, or
I will cause thee to taste the torment of the
guilty!

The slave said,—Know, O my master, that five days ago
I was walking in one of the streets, and I
saw some boys at play. One of them had this
apple. I snatched it from him, and he followed me,
weeping, and said that his mother was sick and had
desired an apple, and that his father had journeyed to
Basra and brought her three from the garden of the
Khaleefeh. I struck the boy and came away. Then thy
daughter saw the apple and purchased it from me for
two pieces of silver.

When Jaạfar heard this, he exclaimed,—There is no deity but
Allah! From the theft of an apple hath arisen murder,
dismemberment, the grief of children, and the threatened death of
forty innocent men! He ordered Reyhan to be bound, and
took him before the Khaleefeh before the appointed hour had
ended.

Then Jaạfar related how the apple had been found in
his daughter's pocket and repeated the confession of the slave.
The young man's son was brought, and he recognized Reyhan,
saying,—This is he who took the apple and struck me.

The Khaleefeh was astonished, and he laughed until he fell
backward; then he became grave and said,—Praise be to Allah,
who bringeth forth the hidden thing and delivereth His servants
when hope is cut off. O Jaạfar, thy deliverance was
concealed in thine own house, and thou knewest it not.

He ordered that Reyhan should be punished for his theft
and falsehood; but Jaạfar interceded, saying,—O Prince of the Faithful,
if thou hast pardoned the free man who slew his
faithful wife in anger, pardon also this slave whose foolish
lie brought destruction farther than he intended.

So the Khaleefeh pardoned Reyhan for Jaạfar's sake, and bestowed
money upon the old sheykh and the children of the
murdered woman, and commanded that the young man should care
for them and never forget the innocent blood that had
been shed.

Afterward he summoned the writers of the court and said,—Record
this history from its beginning to its end: the fisherman
and his net, the chest and the murdered woman, the
journey to Basra, the three apples, the lie of the
slave, the confession of the young man, and the deliverance
of Jaạfar. Let it be preserved in the treasury and
proclaimed among the people, that the hasty may learn patience,
the jealous may seek proof, and the powerful may know
that from the smallest injustice there may spring a calamity
that reacheth many lives.

And the scribes wrote it in letters of gold; and
Allah is more knowing of that which is hidden, and
more just in His decrees.

## ERMENGARDE; OR, THE MORTGAGE, THE MAIDEN, AND THE MINERAL
RIGHTS

### I. A CHILD OF SIXTEEN

Ermengarde Stubbs stood beneath the old apple tree, looking exceedingly
innocent.

She was sixteen—according to her mother—and had been sixteen for
a considerable period. Her hair was the pale gold of
sunrise, assisted by a preparation costing forty-nine cents per bottle;
her eyes were blue, her cheeks pink, and her nose
so delicately Roman that it might have conquered Gaul had
it been given an army.

Behind her stretched the Stubbs farm: forty acres of reluctant
corn, three cows, a barn leaning toward the southeast, and
a brook which babbled continually without communicating anything of commercial
value.

Hiram Stubbs, Ermengarde's father, was a poor but honest farmer
whenever the revenue officers were near. At other times he
manufactured a cordial locally known as Old Stubbs's Mountain Persuader,
though there were no mountains within eighty miles.

"Ermengarde!" cried a rich young voice.

It was Jack Manly, her childhood sweetheart. He strode toward
her with the fearless tread of a man who possessed
twelve dollars, a clean collar, and no information whatever regarding
compound interest.

"Light of my life!" he exclaimed.

"Jack!"

"Ermengarde!"

They clasped hands. This was more sanitary than embracing and
much cheaper than marriage.

"My angel," said Jack, "we have loved each other since
childhood."

"Yes," she replied. "You used to pull my braids."

"It was because I adored you."

"You also put a frog in my lunch pail."

"Ah, beloved! A boy's heart must express itself somehow."

He sank upon one knee, crushing a dandelion which had
never harmed him.

"Ermengarde, be mine!"

"I am already engaged to you," she reminded him.

"True. Then continue to be mine!"

She consented. Jack produced a ring purchased at Dibble's Hardware,
Harness, Coffins, and Fancy Goods Emporium. It contained a large
imitation diamond guaranteed to resemble the genuine article at a
distance of twenty feet in poor light.

"Oh, Jack!"

"Wear it always."

"I shall, unless it turns green."

At that moment there came the crack of a riding-crop
and the crunch of expensive boots upon inexpensive soil.

'Squire Septimus Hardman approached.

He was tall, black-eyed, black-coated, and black-hearted, though the last
item was not visible without surgery. His moustache curled upward
at both ends like two mortgage hooks. He owned the
bank, the mill, the general store, six houses, and most
of the opinions expressed within the county.

"Ha!" he said, for he was a villain and had
kept himself in practice.

Jack rose.

"What seek you here, Hardman?"

"The same thing you seek, young Manly."

"Her love?"

"No. The farm."

Ermengarde gasped.

Hardman smiled slowly. He knew what they did not: beneath
the Stubbs farm lay gold—GOLD!!—in quantities estimated by Professor Gneiss
of the State Geological Institute at approximately $700,000, less extraction
expenses and freight.

Hardman had discovered this by reading a geological report, an
underhanded method seldom employed by heroes.

"Your father owes me five thousand dollars," he told Ermengarde.
"The mortgage falls due in thirty days."

"He has not five thousand dollars!"

"I had rather counted on that."

"You crool man!"

"I prefer 'efficient.' Marry me, Ermengarde, and the mortgage shall
disappear."

Jack struck a posture.

"Never! I will go to the city, wrest fortune from
the marts of trade, and return before the fatal day!"

Hardman laughed.

"You?"

"Yes, I!"

"And how will you obtain five thousand dollars?"

Jack had not reached that chapter.

"By honest toil," he said finally.

And the poor dub meant it.

### II. THE CITY CALLS

That evening the Stubbs family gathered in the kitchen.

Hiram sat with his boots upon the stove and sorrow
in his whiskers. Hannah Stubbs, his wife, kneaded bread with
the muscular resignation of a woman who had kneaded both
bread and facts for many years.

"Foreclose!" groaned Hiram. "He can't do it!"

"He owns the mortgage," said Hannah.

"That makes it look worse."

Jack entered carrying a carpetbag containing two shirts, one sandwich,
and ambition.

"Farewell," he cried. "I go to the great city!"

"Bring back money," said Hiram.

"Bring back honor," said Ermengarde.

"Bring back the carpetbag," added Hannah. "It's borrowed."

Jack kissed Ermengarde beneath the lamp.

"Wait for me, precious."

"Until the thirtieth day."

"Longer!"

"We shall see."

He departed into the darkness, taking the south road because
it led to the railway and because the north road
ended in a duck pond.

On the following morning Hardman came again.

"Have you reconsidered?" he asked.

"No!"

He twirled his moustache clockwise.

"Then I must employ sterner measures."

"Hark!" cried Ermengarde. "What are they?"

But he did not tell her. Villains must preserve some
mystery or become merely businessmen.

That night, as Ermengarde passed the lilac bush, a sack
descended over her head. Powerful arms lifted her into a
carriage.

She screamed.

The horses galloped away.

The Stubbs dog barked bravely after them for eleven yards,
then remembered a bone.

---

### III. ABDUCTED!

Ermengarde awoke in a ruined hunting lodge three miles from
home. Its windows were barred, its door locked, and its
wallpaper distressing.

A hideous old hag brought her bread and water.

"You shall never escape, pretty chee-ild," croaked the hag.

"Why are you talking like that?"

"Mr. Hardman pays extra for atmosphere."

At midnight Hardman entered, cloaked to the chin.

"Mine at last!" he cried.

"Unhand me!"

"I am not handing you."

"Then unkidnap me!"

"Consent to become muh wife, and you are free."

"Never! My heart belongs to Jack."

"Your father's land belongs almost to me."

Ermengarde folded her arms. "I defy you."

Hardman paced the chamber. Then, quite suddenly, he stopped. An
idea had struck him—an experience less painful than it sounds.

Why, he reflected, should he marry a penniless farm girl
in order to obtain property he could acquire by foreclosure?
Marriage involved ceremonies, relatives, and breakfast conversation. Foreclosure required only
a clerk and a red stamp.

"You may go," he said.

Ermengarde stared. "Go?"

"Yes."

"But you abducted me."

"A temporary error in business strategy."

"You villain!"

"Madam, there is no need to become personal."

He drove her home himself, because the hag had retired
and Ermengarde did not know the road.

Hiram was overjoyed to see his daughter, though he said
he would have advertised for her tomorrow if notices in
the county paper had not cost twenty cents a line.

Hardman tipped his hat and drove away.

Ermengarde watched him thoughtfully.

She had learned something valuable: Hardman had committed kidnapping and
could be made uncomfortable by a jury.

She placed this knowledge carefully in her heart, beside Jack's
love and several other negotiable assets.

---

### IV. THE HUNTER

Three days later Algernon Reginald Jones came across the Stubbs
farm carrying a rifle, field glasses, a butterfly net, and
a city manner.

He was hunting woodcock, though he could not identify one.

Near the brook he stumbled over a yellow rock.

"Gadzooks!" he murmured, having been educated.

He chipped it with his knife, examined it through a
lens, applied nitric acid from the pocket laboratory every gentleman
carries when trespassing, and turned pale.

"Gold!"

Ermengarde appeared, bearing a milk pail.

"Who are you?"

"Algernon Reginald Jones, sportsman, traveller, and member of three clubs
which would not admit your father."

"How nice."

He looked at her. She looked at him. He looked
again, for his first glance had been merely preliminary.

"Beauteous rustic!" he breathed. "Do you know what lies beneath
your feet?"

"Shoes."

"Gold! A magnificent vein of it!"

Ermengarde dropped the pail. The milk ran white over the
earth like the tears of an underpaid angel.

"Gold?"

"Yes. But Hardman will seize it when he forecloses."

"Can we prevent him?"

Algernon considered. "Not immediately. However, we can elope."

The logical connection was imperfect, but his eyes were dark
and his motorcar waited beyond the fence.

"What of Jack?"

"Who?"

"My childhood sweetheart."

"Childhood," said Algernon solemnly, "is a beautiful dream from which
wealth awakens us."

This sounded profound. Ermengarde packed her imitation diamond, two dresses,
and the better half of a chicken.

At dusk she climbed into Algernon's motorcar.

"To the city!" he cried.

"To fortune!" she answered.

The car would not start.

They walked to the station.

---

### V. LOVE'S EXPRESS

On the night train Algernon spoke fervently of devotion.

"Thou art the moon of my heavens, the cream in
my coffee, the preferred stock of my emotional corporation."

"Oh, Algy!"

"When we reach the city, we shall marry."

"And claim the gold?"

"In due course."

He went into the smoking car. Ermengarde settled back upon
the velvet seat and discovered an envelope beneath it.

The letter was addressed to Algernon in a feminine hand:

> Darling Algy—
> Come back to your own Violet. I forgive the
chorus girl, the widow, and the girl in Albany. Remember,
you promised to marry me Tuesday.
> Your faithful
> Violet

Ermengarde read it twice. Tuesday was tomorrow.

Algernon returned.

"My angel—"

"Who is Violet?"

His face changed, though not enough to improve it.

"A cousin."

"Do you marry your cousins on Tuesdays?"

"Only in the South."

"And the chorus girl?"

"A misunderstanding."

"The widow?"

"A misunderstanding with property."

Ermengarde rose. The train thundered through the darkness at forty-two
miles per hour.

"Open that window," she said.

"Why?"

"The air is close."

He opened it.

Ermengarde pushed him out.

It was not a ladylike act, but circumstances alter etiquette.

She watched him vanish into the night, then pulled down
the shade and slept.

Algernon landed in a haystack. He had survived—this much became
evident later.

---

### VI. VIRTUE AT THREE DOLLARS A WEEK

The city received Ermengarde with open arms, searched her pockets,
and charged her rent.

Within a fortnight her money was gone. She answered advertisements
seeking refined young ladies for pleasant work and found that
"pleasant" meant twelve hours standing, while "refined" meant willing to
accept three dollars a week.

She sold artificial flowers, addressed envelopes, and once considered a
position in a cabaret.

"Is it immoral?" she asked the manager.

"Certainly."

"What does it pay?"

"Four dollars."

"I shall retain my innocence."

At last she became a maid in the home of
Mrs. Aurelia Van Itty, a widow whose wealth was so
great that she had ceased counting it personally.

One afternoon Ermengarde found a diamond bracelet beneath a sofa.
It contained seventeen stones, all genuine, even at close range.

She carried it to Mrs. Van Itty.

"Madam, you dropped this."

The widow gazed at her in amazement.

"You might have stolen it!"

"Yes, madam."

"Why did you not?"

Ermengarde calculated quickly. "Because honesty is its own reward."

Mrs. Van Itty burst into tears.

"My child! In all my acquaintance, no one has ever
said that without trying to sell me something."

She adopted Ermengarde the following Thursday.

Thus Virtue triumphed, receiving a town house, a motorcar, an
allowance of ten thousand dollars a year, and breakfast in
bed.

The wages of honesty had improved.

---

### VII. THE CHAUFFEUR'S SECRET

Months passed. Ermengarde acquired gowns, jewels, and the ability to
look through persons who earned less than five thousand annually.

One morning Mrs. Van Itty hired a new chauffeur.

He appeared in cap and goggles.

"Where shall I drive you, miss?"

Ermengarde stared.

"Algernon!"

"The same."

"You are alive!"

"Do not sound disappointed."

"How did you survive?"

"Hay. Providence often employs agricultural materials."

He explained that Violet had married a dentist on Monday,
the chorus girl had joined a convent for publicity, and
the widow's property had been mortgaged. Reduced circumstances had forced
him into useful labor.

"Ermengarde," he whispered, "do you remember the gold?"

She did.

"Hardman's foreclosure is set for Friday. Once he gains the
farm, the gold is his."

"Then drive me home!"

Mrs. Van Itty overheard them.

"My darling daughter, we shall all go. I have never
seen your humble birthplace."

"It is very humble," said Ermengarde. "Take smelling salts."

They departed at once in a touring car packed with
trunks, servants, luncheon, and ready money.

---

### VIII. THE FATAL FRIDAY

At the Stubbs farm, Hardman stood upon the porch holding
the mortgage.

Hiram, Hannah, and the three cows regarded him with despair.
The cows' despair was chiefly digestive.

"The hour has come!" announced Hardman.

"Mercy!" cried Hiram.

"Never!"

"Another week!"

"No!"

"A day?"

"No!"

"Until after lunch?"

Hardman hesitated, but duty hardened him.

At that instant a motor horn sounded.

Ermengarde swept into the yard wearing white satin, pearls, and
a hat requiring a separate postal district. Mrs. Van Itty
followed. Algernon remained at the wheel, where former fiancés are
most useful.

"Stop!" cried Ermengarde.

She drew out a cheque.

"How much is owed?"

"With interest, fees, penalties, document charges, and one mysterious item
listed as 'handling,' six thousand four hundred and twelve dollars."

She wrote the amount.

Hardman's moustache drooped.

"Foiled!"

"Temporarily," said Ermengarde.

A cheer rose from the household.

Then another figure appeared upon the road.

It was Jack Manly.

He wore a new suit and carried prosperity in one
hand. With the other he led a plump young woman
in a travelling cloak.

"Ermengarde!" he cried.

"Jack! You returned!"

"Yes. I sought my fortune. I found employment in a
button factory, became assistant manager, and now earn eighteen dollars
a week."

"My hero!"

"There is more. This is my wife, Clarissa."

Ermengarde blinked.

"Your wife?"

"I feared I should not return in thirty days. Clarissa's
father owns the factory."

Clarissa smiled.

"I've heard so much about you."

Ermengarde removed Jack's imitation diamond from her finger.

"Then you release me from our engagement?"

"Entirely."

"Oh, thank heaven!"

Jack looked slightly hurt, though not enough to endanger his
marriage.

---

### IX. THE WOMAN FROM THE PAST

Mrs. Van Itty had been staring at Hannah Stubbs.

"You!" she whispered.

Hannah dropped a pie.

"You!" she answered, which saved time.

"Twenty-eight years ago," cried Mrs. Van Itty, "you were my
nurse!"

Hannah trembled.

"You remember?"

"Can a mother forget the woman who stole her baby?"

Everyone gasped, including the cows, though one may merely have
coughed.

Mrs. Van Itty turned to Ermengarde.

"My lost child had a crescent-shaped birthmark upon her left
shoulder."

Ermengarde drew aside the satin.

There it was—a crescent, slightly obscured by powder.

"My baby!" shrieked Mrs. Van Itty.

"My mother!" cried Ermengarde.

"Then I ain't?" said Hannah.

"No," said Mrs. Van Itty.

Hannah seemed relieved. Sixteen years of motherhood—more precisely twenty-eight—had been
tiring.

"You told me I was sixteen," Ermengarde accused her.

"You were small for your age."

The truth emerged. Hannah, then a nurse, had stolen the
infant Maude Van Itty because the child possessed a gold
rattle and because Hannah misunderstood the legal distinction between borrowing
and abduction. She had renamed her Ermengarde and, finding arithmetic
distasteful, stopped increasing her age at sixteen.

"So I am Maude," said Ermengarde, "and twenty-eight years old."

"Yes, darling."

"And as Mrs. Van Itty's daughter, I have no blood
claim to the Stubbs farm."

"None," said the family lawyer, who had arrived by coincidence
in the second motorcar.

Ermengarde grew pale.

The gold! The magnificent, glittering, chemically authenticated gold!

Hiram, as owner, could sell the land. Jack's factory father-in-law
might finance extraction. Hardman could purchase another mortgage. Everyone might
profit except Ermengarde.

In that solemn hour her innocence departed, or perhaps merely
ceased using cosmetics.

---

### X. THE TRIUMPH OF TRUE LOVE

That night Ermengarde found Hardman beneath the apple tree. He
was examining mineral samples with a lantern and muttering figures.

"Septimus," she said softly.

He started.

"Ermengarde!"

"Maude, legally. But let us not be formal."

"What do you want?"

"You."

Hardman nearly swallowed his moustache.

"This afternoon you called me a serpent."

"I have always admired reptiles. They know where the warm
stones are."

He narrowed his black eyes.

"What is your scheme?"

"My dear 'Squire, you once kidnapped me."

"I released you."

"Kidnapping remains kidnapping even when followed by courteous transportation."

"You would prosecute?"

"Unless you marry me."

"Marry you!"

"Yes. Then you will foreclose upon the Stubbs farm."

"But you paid the mortgage."

Ermengarde smiled and produced the cheque.

Hardman examined it.

She had forgotten to sign it.

"By thunder!"

"After foreclosure," she continued, "you will possess the gold. As
your wife, I shall assist you in enjoying it. Fifty-fifty."

"Sixty-forty."

"Fifty-fifty, or the district attorney hears of the lodge, the
barred window, and the hideous hag."

"Fifty-fifty," he said.

He dropped upon one knee, crushing the same unfortunate family
of dandelions.

"Ermengarde—Maude—beauteous blonde, will you be muh wife?"

"I will, Septimus."

"Do you love me?"

She looked into his cruel black eyes, his calculated heart,
and his estimated seven hundred thousand dollars less extraction expenses.

"With all my interests," she whispered.

They were married the next morning.

At noon Hardman foreclosed upon the farm. Hiram protested until
informed that he might remain as superintendent at thirty dollars
a month and continue manufacturing Mountain Persuader in a concealed
cellar.

Mrs. Van Itty wept over her recovered daughter. Jack shook
Hardman's hand. Clarissa ordered new buttons for the wedding dress.
Algernon asked whether the mine required a chauffeur and was
struck with a geological hammer—not fatally.

The first gold was taken from the earth three weeks
later.

Ermengarde stood beside her husband as the yellow metal glittered
in the sun.

"Mine!" cried Hardman.

"Mine," corrected Ermengarde.

And thus the maiden was saved, the villain rewarded, the
hero comfortably married elsewhere, the mortgage successfully foreclosed, and Virtue
crowned at last with mineral rights.

Which is generally the sort of crown Virtue prefers.

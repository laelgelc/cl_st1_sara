> Between them hangs the rainbow's stair."

When, after twelve years of civic pavements, quarterly rents, door-bells,
dinner-bells, and all the brazen alarums whereby men in towns
are reminded that time is money and money a very
nervous master, I withdrew to an old farmhouse among the
northern uplands, I congratulated myself that at last I had
escaped architecture.

The house, indeed, was architecture's apology. It stood broad-backed and
weather-browed upon a gradual swell of land, with a chimney
like a grave senator planted in its midst, and with
small windows peering from under the eaves as if ashamed
of having glass in them. No pediment proclaimed it; no
marble lion guarded it; no bronze Triton, condemned to perpetual
expectoration, embellished its approach. It had been builded, so the
county chronicle said, in the reign of one of the
Georges; which George no man knew, since all the Georges,
viewed at our distance, resemble successive rainy Mondays. Yet the
oak beams were sound; the well was sweet; the garret
capacious; and the hearthstone—black, wide, immovable—became to me a domestic
Kaaba, round which in winter my household affections performed their
warm pilgrimages.

But the house had no piazza.

A defect? My practical neighbor, Squire Talcutt, denied it.

"A piazza," said he, chewing the conclusion as well as
the tobacco from which it seemed extracted, "is a contrivance
for collecting snow in winter, rain in spring, mosquitoes in
summer, and rheumatism in autumn."

Now Talcutt had outlived two wives, three barns, and every
enthusiasm except distrust. His counsel was therefore venerable, but not
alluring.

A piazza must be had.

The question was—where?

On the east? There dawn came prying too early, peeped
into one's dressing-gown, turned the breakfast cream to pearl, and
exposed, with republican impartiality, both the cobweb in the cornice
and the philosophy in one's face. Besides, eastward the land
descended to a marsh, over which the morning mists drilled
in white battalions until routed by the sun.

On the south? Too torrid. There July would establish an
African protectorate, and every board become a gridiron. One might
as well lounge upon the brazen shield of Achilles, after
battle.

On the west? There stood the orchard, agreeable enough in
May, when each apple tree carried a bridal veil, but
melancholy in November, when the twisted limbs seemed a parliament
of witches voting the death of summer. Moreover, sunset, though
splendid, is a bankrupt's magnificence—gold everywhere, and not a coin
to keep.

But to the north were the mountains.

To the north was Charlemagne.

There, across leagues of meadow, pasture, river-flat, and ascending forest,
the great range sat enthroned: the emperor in the center,
crowned at times with cloud; on his right Roland, scarred
with ravines; on his left Oliver, grave in blue steel;
and around them all the peers and paladins, lesser peaks
and purple captains, keeping their endless court above the world.
Nearer hills, green and civil, bent before those solemn presences
like provincial nobles before an iron throne.

"North?" said Talcutt. "You'll never get sun."

"So much the better."

"You'll get weather."

"That is what mountains are for."

"You'll freeze."

"I shall contemplate."

He looked at me with compassion. A countryman pardons madness
when it can pay the carpenter.

So the piazza was builded upon the north side: long,
low, and wide enough for a chair, a table, a
book, a spyglass, a spaniel, and the several postures of
a gentleman avoiding useful employment. Its pillars were plain; its
roof was shingled; its floor, at first raw and glaring,
soon took the gray baptism of storms. From it I
commanded my chosen kingdom.

Immediately below lay the kitchen garden, where cabbages, those green
turbans of the soil, sat in rows; beyond, the mowing
fields rolled off in long dun waves; beyond these ran
a silver brook, continually escaping and continually returning through alder
and willow; farther still, farms appeared—white walls, red barns, steeples
sharp as compass needles—and then woods, woods, woods, climbing until
all particular trees were merged in one vast garment flung
over the knees of the mountains.

I called the piazza my quarterdeck. The fields were my
inland ocean; the cattle, slow whales grazing under green water;
hayricks, merchantmen with cargoes of summer; crows, black privateers forever
boarding the corn. Yet unlike other captains, I neither sailed
nor desired a port. My voyage consisted in remaining still.
With pipe unlit—for I enjoyed the ceremony more than the
smoke—and book unopened—for the prospect had written a better—I kept
watch over the north.

In spring the mountains were austere abbots, hooded in rain.
In summer they became enormous sleeping animals, their flanks dark
with fir, their breath the thundercloud. Winter restored them to
primeval marble, as if some Scandinavian sculptor had hewn them
during the night. But autumn—autumn was their coronation.

Then the maples kindled, not here and there, but by
nations. Scarlet republics revolted against the evergreen kings; gold ran
through the valleys like news of victory; the beeches held
pale candles; sumacs lifted bloody spears. In the clear declining
sun the whole range seemed less a tract of earth
than the burning western wall of heaven mistakenly set in
the north.

It was during such an afternoon that I first observed
the Spot.

I capitalize it because ignorance is the mother of majesty.
A known place is merely a place; an unknown place
is Atlantis.

High upon the breast of the farther mountain, where a
ravine opened between two wooded ridges, there shone a small,
singular brightness. Not constant; no. It came and vanished as
thin clouds went over the sun. At one moment there
was only blue distance; at the next, a square spark,
minute but intense, as though a spirit had struck flint
against the mountain.

I fetched the spyglass.

Through its round, wavering world appeared a clearing; within the
clearing, something that might be a cottage; and in the
cottage, if cottage it were, a window receiving the sun.
Yet magnified distance is often but enlarged uncertainty. The glass
brought the object nearer to doubt, not knowledge.

"A shingle roof," said Talcutt, when on another day I
compelled him to look.

"A silver shield," said I.

"Old boards."

"Enchanted battlements."

"A window."

"The eye of the fairy queen."

"It needs putty."

Thus ended the symposium.

But the Spot would not end. At morning, when the
rest of that high hollow lay in violet shadow, the
window sometimes flashed like a star detained after dawn. At
noon it disappeared, modest as a hermit. Toward evening it
burned again, a tiny golden furnace. Once, following a mountain
shower, a rainbow planted one foot near the clearing; and
though reason, that surveyor with a chain, informed me that
the bright arch stood leagues before it, fancy—who carries no
instruments and therefore measures the universe—set the colored gate exactly
there.

Beyond the gate, what then?

Fairyland, surely.

A poor fairyland, perhaps, according to the assessor; rich enough
according to the heart. I pictured a cottage made venerable,
not wretched, by age; moss softening its roof; a clear
spring laughing under fern; wood-smoke rising blue and domestic among
yellow birches. At the window sat a maiden—not idle, for
even fairies in our republic must work—but winding silk, or
illuminating some ancient book, or sewing stars upon the cloak
of night. Her cheek held the mountain rose; her eyes,
the mountain twilight. Birds brought her berries; deer ate salt
from her hand; the moon, passing late, paused to silver
her needle.

Why a maiden? Ask the rainbow why it is curved.
Imagination has its geometry, though Euclid omitted it.

At times I supposed her lonely. Then, to amend the
matter, I gave her a grandmother, very old, very wise,
and conveniently asleep except when counsel was needed. At other
times I bestowed upon her seven small brothers, all woodcutters,
who returned at sunset singing. But the brothers made too
much noise, and the grandmother, by some malign domestic evolution,
threatened to become an aunt. I dismissed them. The maiden
remained.

So does a speck become a world. So does distance,
that most industrious of painters, lay gold upon rot, purple
upon poverty, and heavenly blue over the common smoke of
mortal chimneys.

For several weeks I enjoyed my invention with no wish
to test it. A mystery examined is a tax paid
upon delight. Yet desire, like a foxhound, grows restless at
the threshold. The repeated flash seemed at last no reflection,
but a signal.

Come.

One evening it shone, vanished, shone again.

Come, come.

Then the rainbow returned, paler than before, and leaned its
dissolving ladder upon the mountain.

A third invitation.

"Fairyland," said I, "shall be visited."

My mare, Dido, dissented when saddled before sunrise. She looked
north, looked at me, and sighed with that equine profundity
which rebukes man's belief that he alone foresees disaster. Nevertheless,
with provisions in the saddlebag, a cloak behind me, and
the spyglass slung like a small brass oracle at my
side, I set forth.

At first the expedition was a holiday. The road went
down among farms whose chimneys smoked breakfast; children waved from
doorways; geese disputed our passage with Roman discipline; and roosters,
posted upon stone walls, announced us to the countryside. Dido's
hoofs rang briskly. The mountains, though apparently close enough to
receive a tossed apple, retreated with every mile, those blue
creditors who promise settlement but postpone the appointment.

On, then.

Past the millpond, where the inverted sky lay caught under
yellow leaves; past the meetinghouse, white and severe as a
doctrine; past fields in which pumpkins, round and orange, seemed
fallen planets; past a graveyard where slate stones leaned east
and west, weary dominoes abandoned by Death.

On.

The road narrowed. Farms grew fewer. Stone walls lost their
straight civic intention and strayed into thickets. Goldenrods stood at
every fork like torch-bearing guides, but all pointed in several
directions. Once I asked a man cutting brush whether the
upper mountain road led to the high clearing.

"Which clearing?"

"The one below the northern notch, where the sun strikes
a house."

He rested upon his axe.

"Sun strikes most houses, given time."

"A lonely cottage—perhaps silver-roofed."

"Plenty lonely. None silver."

"Near the rainbow."

At that he turned back to his brush.

I rode on.

By noon the country had become a broken sea. The
road mounted long green swells, plunged into hollows, twisted around
ledges, and appeared continually on the point of foundering. Dido
was no longer a mare but a stout yawl laboring
under too much captain. Jays flew ahead, blue pilots whose
advice consisted of alarm. Squirrels scolded from trees. A hawk
revolved above us, tracing with one wing the cold circumference
of solitude.

At a deserted farm I found a gate hanging by
one hinge, the bars green with mould. Behind it stood
a shed containing the skeleton of a wagon and a
rusted threshing wheel, its iron teeth fixed in the grin
of obsolete hunger. Nettles possessed the doorstep. In the orchard,
fallen apples lay brown and sweetly corrupt, each with its
congregation of wasps.

Fairyland not yet.

I ate beneath an elm, while Dido cropped the sparse
grass and regarded me as one might regard an author
halfway through a tedious allegory. Then we resumed.

The ascent sharpened. Ruts exposed stones like the vertebrae of
some buried beast. Branches struck my hat; roots clutched Dido's
shoes. The lower woods, warm with maple and chestnut, gave
way to firs. Their trunks rose black and close, a
Gothic chapter-house in which no monks had prayed for a
thousand years. The sun, entering by narrow clerestories, laid cold
bars upon the moss. Here sound was altered. A snapped
twig traveled far; a hidden stream muttered under rocks; the
mare's breathing seemed an intruder's confession.

On, on.

I began to understand why chivalric knights required enchanted horses.
An ordinary mare, though excellent in moral character, objects to
becoming Pegasus after luncheon.

At last the path became too steep. I tethered Dido
in a small grassy interval where water trickled from a
stone trough, commended her to Providence and her own good
sense—the latter being the surer keeper—and continued on foot.

The goldenrods had deserted me. Ferns succeeded them.

Ferns, ferns, ferns: green feathers laid upon the earth; green
hands raised from the ravine; green waves arrested before breaking.
They brushed my knees, concealed stones, and filled every opening
not claimed by brier. Above them the woods climbed without
end.

Occasionally I saw the high sky, hard and blue, and
fancied the ridge near. But a mountain, approached, builds secret
mountains from its own substance. Each summit proved a porch
to another height; each turn revealed a new stair; every
upward step was answered by two more waiting under leaves.

My fairy maiden, meanwhile, underwent revisions. Her mountain rose faded;
her silken labor became homespun; the obedient deer developed ticks.
Still, the bright window remained. Comfort has strange stamina when
carried by a weary man.

Toward midafternoon I crossed a charcoal track: black earth, wheel
marks, hacked stumps, and, among the trees, several low mounds
like buried furnaces. The air bore a faint burnt odor,
bitter and old. Presently came the dull stroke of an
axe—pause—stroke—pause—each blow separated by a silence larger than itself.

I called. No answer.

The axe ceased.

After a time it began again, farther off, or seeming
farther because the woods had swallowed the direction.

Then I emerged upon the clearing.

No rainbow guarded it.

The place lay in a shallow fold below the ridge,
open to the western sun and every wind that had
learned cruelty upon the summits. Grass grew thin among stones.
Several trees, dead at the top, leaned away from the
weather. Near a blackened pit stood cords of cut wood;
beyond them, a small garden enclosed by poles, where a
few cabbages endured under torn leaves.

And the cottage?

There it stood.

Silver-roofed, yes—if weather-eaten shingles, bleached and curled, may be silver.
Moss-grown, yes—but moss, seen closely, was not romance's velvet; it
was damp, decay, the soft green handwriting of ruin. One
corner had sunk. The chimney listed. The door, patched with
unmatched boards, resembled a beggar's coat. A broken chair stood
beside it, and over the chair crawled a vine which
had produced neither flower nor fruit, only a multitude of
dusty leaves.

The enchanted window faced the sinking sun.

Its panes were small, bubbled, smeared, and fly-specked. One was
cracked diagonally and stuffed at the edge with rag. Yet
the light striking it made the familiar golden flash—the trumpet
note that had crossed miles to summon me.

Thus Aladdin's palace, brought near, asks for putty.

I knocked.

No answer.

I knocked again; then heard within a chair scrape, a
light step, and the drawing of a bolt. The door
opened narrowly.

A young woman looked out.

Not the maiden of my piazza; and yet young. Her
hair, brown and abundant, was drawn tightly back, as if
even one loose strand would be an extravagance. Her face
had the pallor not of lilies but of long indoor
labor. The eyes were gray, large, and wakeful—too wakeful; eyes
that seemed to have forgotten the descent of lids. She
wore a plain dress, clean but much mended, and held
in one hand a length of coarse sewing.

"Good afternoon," said I, uncovering with a ceremony somewhat disproportioned
to the door. "I fear I have mistaken my road."

"There is only one road," she replied, glancing beyond me.
"You must have come by it."

Plain truth, like cold water, checks rhetoric.

"I wished to reach the northern valley," I said. "But
the mountain, like a jealous giant, has detained me."

"This is the northern side."

"Then perhaps I have not mistaken it after all. Might
I ask a cup of water?"

She opened the door.

The room was low and dim, though the window blazed.
A table, two stools, a narrow bed curtained by a
faded quilt, a cupboard with three plates, a small stove,
a spinning wheel without its band—these made the inventory. Bunches
of herbs hung from a beam. In a corner stood
wood chips and a basket of charcoal. The floor sloped
perceptibly toward the sunken wall.

She brought water in a tin cup. It was cold
and tasted of iron.

"Excellent," I said. "A draught from Hippocrene."

"The spring runs by the ash tree," she answered. "It
gets leaves in it after rain."

I drank again, less classically.

Her name, I learned, was Marianna. She lived there with
her brother, Eli, younger by two years. Their parents were
dead. The cottage had belonged to an uncle, also dead.
Eli cut wood on the upper slopes and burned charcoal
in the pits, sometimes remaining out all night to tend
them lest the slow fires fail or flame.

"And you are much alone?"

"Mostly."

"Yet in such scenery," I said, turning toward the window,
"solitude may have noble company."

She followed my look, not admiringly.

The pane admitted the sun directly. Its western glare fell
across the table, whitening the worn wood, exposing each scratch,
each crumb, each dead fly imprisoned in the sill. Marianna
moved the sewing from the light.

"It is hot there," she said.

"Hot, perhaps; but wonderfully gilded."

"It fades the cloth."

"Still, when that glass shines across the valley, it is
like a lamp set in the mountain."

"It shines in my eyes from noon till sundown."

"Can you not curtain it?"

"The cloth makes the room too dark. And the roof
leaks on the other side, so I cannot put the
table there."

Each phrase closed a painted door.

I examined the sewing. It was piecework—seams upon coarse gray
garments, each like the last, bundled by dozens and carried
down monthly to a village merchant. The rate she named
seemed less a wage than an insult divided into pennies.

"You sew all day?"

"When there is light."

"And when there is not?"

"Sometimes by the lamp."

"Surely not late?"

"When the bundles are due."

"And sleep?"

At this she made a small motion, neither shrug nor
shiver.

"I do not sleep much."

The statement was quiet. Quiet things enter deepest.

Outside, the axe sounded again: stroke—pause—stroke. Marianna counted three blows
before speaking.

"When Eli is at the pits," she said, "I hear
everything."

"What things?"

"The things there are."

Her words rebuked, without intending it, all my elves.

She pointed toward the window. On the floor the frame
cast a long cross of shadow, divided by the warped
panes. The branches of a nearby ash moved across it.

"That one comes first," she said. "The tall one. It
reaches the cupboard near three. Then the crooked one follows.
When the wind rises, they quarrel."

"You have named the shadows?"

"I know them."

"There is a difference?"

"They are never exactly alike."

I looked. One shadow, thin and forked, climbed the wall;
another trembled over the table; a third, broad and shapeless,
waited near the bed. What from my piazza had been
a point of gold was here a little kingdom of
recurring darkness, measured hour by hour by an unsleeping girl.

"And at night?" I asked.

"The moon shadows are different. Fir shadows are blacker than
ash. Snow shadows are light. Sometimes the smoke lies down
and there are none."

"You make companions of them."

"No," she said. "Companions go away and come back because
they choose."

Her needle resumed its passage. In, out; in, out. Not
Penelope delaying destiny, but destiny itself stitching without end.

I attempted cheerfulness, that bright medicine commonly prescribed by the
comfortable.

"Yet spring must be beautiful here. The first violets, the
returning birds, the brooks unbound—surely the mountain then puts off
its iron and wears a bridal garment."

"The path is mud. The snow comes through the roof.
Blackflies begin before the ground is dry."

"Summer, then?"

"Hot. The flies get in the meat. The spring runs
low."

"But autumn! This very season—look how the woods are royal
with color."

"That is when Eli works hardest. The wind blows sparks
from the pits. Dead leaves catch."

"Winter?"

She looked at me.

I surrendered the calendar.

For some moments only the needle spoke, a tiny steel
logic. The sun descended another degree and struck the cracked
pane. Fire leaped there; across many miles, had I been
sitting at home, I might then have raised my spyglass
and saluted fairyland.

"How came you here today?" she asked.

I explained, though not entirely. I spoke of the lower
road, the deserted farm, the ascent, the fern-covered path. I
omitted the maiden who embroidered stars. Certain lies are too
innocent to confess and too shameful to repeat.

"You came from far?"

"From beyond the river flats."

"Do you know the white house?"

"What white house?"

She put down the sewing and went to the window.
Shielding her eyes from the glare, she indicated a point
far southward, where the lands unrolled in descending bands of
purple, russet, and smoky gold.

"There," she said. "Near the long meadow. Above the dark
trees."

I saw nothing at first. Then, in a notch of
nearer woodland, immeasurably diminished by distance, a pale speck shone.

My house.

Its northern wall received the afternoon brightness. The new piazza
made a slender gray line. One upper window reflected the
sky so purely that it looked blue, not glass. Smoke
rose from my grave senatorial chimney and drifted level across
the fields, gentle as incense.

"Do you see it?" Marianna asked.

"Yes."

"Is it a great house?"

"No. That is—distance may enlarge importance while diminishing size."

"It looks large to me."

She leaned nearer the pane. For the first time her
tired face altered. A little warmth, not supplied by the
oppressive sun, entered it.

"I watch it when the air is clear. In the
morning it is white; before rain it turns gray; at
sunset the windows are gold. There must be many rooms."

"A few."

"And people?"

"People, certainly."

"Happy people?"

The question was not eager. Had it been eager, I
might have answered lightly. It was spoken with the grave
simplicity of one asking whether a distant country had summer.

"Why do you think them happy?"

"The grass is smooth all around. The roof is whole.
There are tall chimneys, so there must be fires in
winter. Sometimes I see something moving along the front—perhaps someone
walking there."

Myself, upon the piazza; myself, pacing with book unopened, lord
of the quarterdeck, admiral of hayfields, discoverer of fairylands.

"There may be a lady," Marianna continued. "I think she
sits where the front is shaded and looks toward the
mountains. She would have time to look. Perhaps she plays
music. Perhaps people come to see her."

Each word furnished my rooms with an inhabitant no less
imaginary than the maiden I had brought to hers.

"And does she never weary?" I asked.

"Not there."

"Does rain never enter?"

"The roof is bright."

"Do insects not trouble her?"

"There are screens, perhaps."

"Does she not lie awake?"

Marianna turned from the window, mildly surprised.

"Why should she?"

Why, indeed? Insomnia could not cross those miles. Rheumatism stopped
at the river. Debt foundered in the meadow. Disappointment, climbing
toward the white house, saw its golden panes and mistook
them for heaven.

I felt the name of that house rise to my
lips. I might have said, "It is mine. I am
the happy inhabitant. The shaded front is my piazza; the
moving figure, myself." Truth stood ready, plain and serviceable, like
a chair. Yet I did not offer it.

Was it cowardice? Perhaps. Vanity? Certainly some drop of that
old poison colored the blood. But more than these, I
feared to destroy the one fair country her window possessed.
Had I declared myself, what then? She would have looked
upon my travel-stained coat, my scratched boots, my commonplace face,
and fairyland would have suffered an eviction. My sound roof,
warm hearth, books, spare rooms—all remained advantages; but Happiness, that
august resident whom she had seen moving along the front,
would vanish, leaving only a gentleman who complained when his
tea cooled.

No. I could not confess.

"That house," I said slowly, "may contain its cares."

"All houses do."

"Then why choose that one?"

"Because it is far."

The answer passed through ornament as wind passes through a
ruined chapel.

She returned to her stool and took up the garment.
The thread had knotted. She bent to undo it, but
her fingers trembled with fatigue.

"Have you never gone down?" I asked.

"Once, when I was little. Not so far as the
river. Mother was living then."

"You might go again."

"Eli needs me here."

"For a day?"

"The pits do not keep days. Nor the sewing."

"Could no neighbor—?"

"The nearest is five miles."

"Friends?"

She drew the knot tighter by mistake.

"People do not climb without business."

I had climbed without business and called it adventure.

Outside, a wind came down from the ridge. The cottage
creaked in several places, like an old ship remembering storms.
Ash leaves scraped the roof. In the chimney something loose
began a faint metallic tapping—tap, pause, tap—the counterpart of Eli's
axe.

Marianna listened.

"That keeps me awake when the wind is west," she
said.

"It might be fastened."

"Eli says so."

"Has he no time?"

"He says he will after the next burning."

"And after that?"

"There is another."

The room had grown colder though the sun still blazed.
I noticed now what the golden pane had concealed: damp
stains near the ceiling; a widening crack behind the stove;
stuffing pushed between logs; one green shoot of moss inside
the sill. Beauty, viewed broadly, had been composed of particulars,
and each particular was distress.

I rose. Evening approached, and the mountain path would be
difficult in darkness.

"You will not stay till Eli comes?" she asked.

Her tone was courteous, not urging; but the very absence
of appeal accused me.

"I must reach my horse before night."

"Yes. The path is bad."

I placed money on the table, then, ashamed of its
nakedness, asked whether she would sell me the tin cup.

"It is not worth that."

"A souvenir of the spring."

"We need the cup."

Of course.

I withdrew my hand and left only a smaller coin,
naming it payment for the water. She looked at it,
then at me.

"Water is not sold here."

There are palaces in which hospitality is cheaper.

I pocketed the coin. At the door I paused.

"When you see the white house," I said, "do not
be too certain that happiness lives there."

"When you see this one," she replied, "do not be
too certain of anything."

The sun struck the window behind her. For an instant
she stood within the very blaze that had enchanted me,
but the light made no halo. It showed the hollows
beneath her eyes.

I descended.

No birds piloted me. The goldenrods, where found, were weeds.
Ferns clutched rather than saluted. The Gothic firs lost their
architecture and became only trees among which a man might
break his neck. The charcoal mounds smoked through their coverings,
low, breathing monsters fed by a boy who sacrificed his
youth to keep them darkly alive.

Near one pit I encountered Eli.

He was scarcely more than seventeen, thin, blackened with soot,
and carrying an axe. His hair and eyebrows were dusted
gray. Weariness had bent him without yet making him strong.
He told me the lower path was safer; asked if
I had seen Marianna; nodded when I said yes; and
hurried on, because a pit had opened and might flame.

"Do you sleep there?" I called after him.

"When I can."

"When is that?"

But the woods took him.

Dido greeted me with reproach softened by relief. We went
down slowly, she choosing the road and I accepting, for
once, guidance without allegory. Night gathered first under the trees,
then rose among them. The last sunlight withdrew from the
upper branches as a king retreats from successive provinces. When
at length we reached open country, one star burned over
Charlemagne's shoulder.

Across the valley, invisible in the dusk, stood the cottage.
No golden signal came. Its dirty pane, deprived of sun,
had ceased to deceive.

Hours later my own house appeared. Lamps glowed at two
windows. The chimney smoked. The piazza stretched under the eaves,
dry and sheltered. A servant opened the door before I
dismounted, exclaiming at my lateness; supper waited; the hearth had
been lighted against the chill.

Happy palace.

I entered like an impostor.

Enough.

I made no second expedition to fairyland. Dido, observing this
reform, recovered some respect for human judgment. Talcutt, hearing that
the roof of the upper cottage needed repair, sent shingles—anonymously,
because benevolence embarrassed him more than praise—and I found means
for the village merchant to increase the rate paid for
certain seams, though merchants, like mountains, are moved only by
forces patiently applied. Whether the shingles reached the roof before
winter, whether the pennies purchased sleep, whether Marianna ever descended
to the river flats, I cannot surely tell. Charity itself,
viewed from afar, may shine more brightly than it shelters.

The piazza remained.

How could it not? Its posts were sound; the carpenter
had been paid; philosophy does not refund construction costs. I
still sat there through the seasons. Spring sent its white
mists drilling over the marsh. Summer launched cloud fleets above
the peaks. Autumn again unfurled its scarlet republics and golden
kingdoms. Snow restored Charlemagne and his peers to marble.

Yet the prospect had suffered a change not marked upon
the land.

Whenever the high clearing brightened, I no longer saw a
fairy at her starry needle. I saw coarse cloth, in,
out; in, out. When the window flashed, I saw flies
caught in the sill, a crack stuffed with rag, glare
beating upon weary eyes. When the rainbow leaned toward the
mountain, its colors no longer formed a gate; they became
beautiful distances through which no foot might pass.

And sometimes, looking north, I knew that from the mountain
another gaze might be traveling south. Across woods, woods, woods;
across farms diminished into toys; across the silver thread of
river and the long meadow-sea; toward my white house, my
sound roof, my shaded piazza. Perhaps in that gaze my
dwelling still shone as a palace. Perhaps a gracious lady
sat within, forever unwearied, forever visited by music. Perhaps a
happy man paced there, master of his hours.

At such moments I would rise from my chair and
enter the house, unwilling to play the phantom.

Daylight is a master scene-painter. He hangs purple behind poverty,
pours gold upon common glass, gives ruined roofs a silver
dignity, and sets Hope, in bright costume, at every inaccessible
window. But night is the honest stagehand. At a pull
he lowers the colored cloths, extinguishes the footlights, removes palace,
mountain, rainbow, and enchanted pane; then, in the dark, one
hears what the spectacle concealed—the axe in the wood, the
needle through cloth, the sleeper who cannot sleep.

Still, morning comes; still the curtain rises; still the mountains
are beautiful.

And still, though I know the trick, I look.

The morning was cold and cheerful, and the town had
only just begun to uncover its smells.

To a man it was a row of grey streets,
closed shutters, pale chimneys, and gutters shining after the night's
rain; but to Jack, who was a young white-and-brown pointer
of good family and an excellent opinion of himself, it
was a map far more exact. Here the baker's cellar
breathed warm yeast through an iron grating; there a soap-boiler
sent out a bitter, clean vapour; farther on were cabbage
leaves, damp leather, horse-sweat, lamp oil, perfume, ashes, and, best
of all, the magnificent odour of sausages hanging in a
shop whose owner had more hooks than generosity.

Jack trotted behind the cook, now pressing his nose to
the pavement, now raising it toward some delicious window. The
cook carried a market basket and walked with the offended
haste of a woman who has risen before others and
considers this a moral achievement.

"Keep close, you good-for-nothing!" she called.

Jack waved his tail. He understood the tone better than
the words and knew that it contained no real danger.
The cook often called him a thief, a vagabond, and
an abomination, yet she saved him burnt crusts and sometimes
let gravy remain upon a plate longer than strict housekeeping
required. Human speech, in Jack's experience, was chiefly a means
of disguising affection.

At the corner of Fishmongers' Lane, an old spaniel had
left a message upon a stone post. Jack stopped to
examine it. The message was complicated, containing news of illness,
a challenge to a red setter, and an absurd claim
concerning ownership of the entire lane. Jack replied briefly but
firmly. When he looked up, the cook had disappeared.

He was not alarmed. A dog of intelligence, he thought,
could not be lost in a town containing so many
smells. He pursued first the scent of onions, which belonged
to the cook's basket, but it crossed that of a
cartload of onions and became as difficult to follow as
one whisper in a crowd. He tried again near a
doorway, found only a charwoman, a cat, and three generations
of rats, and decided that the market must lie in
the direction of roasted fat.

This conclusion, though agreeable, was incorrect.

He had reached a little square when he saw two
men standing beside a black cart. One held a pole
with a hoop at its end; the other wore thick
gloves and had a face marked by that tranquil severity
which comes to officials who are permitted to hurt creatures
smaller than themselves.

Jack paused.

"Come here, little gentleman," said the man with the pole.

Jack, who liked being called a gentleman, advanced one step.
The hoop flew through the air, grew suddenly enormous, and
closed about his neck. The earth jerked backward. He kicked,
twisted, yelped, and attempted to bite the invisible power that
had seized him; but it lifted him by the collar,
dragged him across the stones, and flung him through a
grated door into darkness.

The door slammed.

For several moments Jack could believe only that he had
been captured by some supernatural apparatus invented particularly for the
punishment of curiosity.*

*The instrument, though miraculous to Jack, was merely the municipal
dog-catcher's noose, manufactured under contract at a cost of eleven
francs apiece and described in the official accounts as "humane
equipment."

The cart moved. Its wheels struck a stone, and Jack
rolled against a collection of legs, ribs, tails, and indignant
noses.

"Monsieur!" cried a thin voice. "Kindly remove your foot from
my ear."

"I beg your pardon," said Jack, finding himself upon the
trembling body of a small greyhound bitch clothed in a
blue wool jacket. She had a narrow head, yellow eyes,
and legs so delicate that they appeared intended less for
walking than for drawing admiration. A silver bell hung from
her collar, although it no longer rang; fear had pressed
it into the folds of her jacket.

Beside her sat a dachshund with the body of a
crocodile, the feet of a doll, and an expression of
hereditary grievance. His tail, bent upward like the figure nine,
remained stiff even when the cart jolted. He smelled of
cushions, furniture polish, and an elderly lady's lap.

Opposite him crouched a mouse-coloured dog of no recognizable breed.
His coat was rubbed bare at the elbows; his head
hung low; and his eyes seemed to have spent years
watching doors that did not open.

Near the back of the cage stood Bouton, a fat
black-and-white house dog whose collar was ornamented with brass studs.
He panted continually and glanced toward the men on the
driver's bench with a hopeful air, as if expecting to
be praised for having been arrested.

"I am sure this is a mistake," he said. "The
authorities are excellent fellows. I bark at beggars for them."

In the most dignified corner sat Arto, an old poodle,
shaved after the circus fashion. A rough mane surrounded his
head; bracelets of wool covered his ankles; and the tuft
upon his tail trembled like a small plume over a
soldier's helmet. His eyes were cloudy but intelligent. Even seated
in a prison cart, he held one forepaw slightly raised,
as he had once done when waiting for applause.

Last of all, half concealed beneath the bench, lay a
stray so strange in colour that Jack stared at him.
The creature had perhaps originally been yellow, but neglect, disease,
soot, and some violet dye had combined to produce a
hue never intended by nature. One ear was torn; hair
had fallen from his flanks; and his long muzzle bore
several old scars. He smelled of alleys, tannery water, hunger,
and independence.

"Where are we going?" Jack asked when he had recovered
his breath.

No one answered at once. The greyhound shivered. The mouse-coloured
dog sighed. Bouton smiled uneasily.

Arto inclined his head.

"Young sir," he said, "you have the misfortune to be
travelling in municipal custody."

"But I belong to someone."

"Most of us have belonged to someone."

"My master will come for me."

"That," said Arto, "is possible. Hope is a privilege enjoyed
especially by the young and the recently captured."

Jack disliked the old dog's manner, though he could not
deny its distinction.

"My name is Jack," he said. "I live in the
house with the green gate, near a garden where there
are ducks. My master is a clerk in the Ministry,
and the cook was taking me to market."

"Then why," asked the dachshund, "did she not keep you
on a lead?"

"A gentleman is not led."

"A gentleman," said the violet dog from beneath the bench,
"is led by anything that smells stronger than his sense."

The greyhound gave a little gasp.

"What a coarse person!"

"He is correct, mademoiselle," said Arto. "Truth commonly enters society
with muddy paws."

The cart rattled through a busier street. From outside came
the cries of vendors, the cracking of whips, the ringing
of a tram, and the happy barking of a dog
who ran free beside a butcher's wagon. All the prisoners
lifted their heads. Through the bars Jack glimpsed boots passing,
wheels turning, and a child carrying a hoop. No one
looked into the cage.

Jack threw himself against the door.

"Cook!" he barked. "Cook! I am here!"

His voice was swallowed by the town. The man on
the driver's bench struck the grating with his pole.

"Quiet, vermin!"

The dogs drew back. Bouton wagged his tail submissively.

"You see," he whispered, "he wishes for order."

Arto smiled without amusement.

"Our friend Bouton," he said, "has lived all his life
among respectable people. He therefore supposes that whatever is done
by an official must be reasonable."

"It usually is," Bouton replied. "There are regulations."

"There are also ropes."

The mouse-coloured dog raised his head.

"Will they beat us?"

"Not unnecessarily," said Arto.

The greyhound sighed with relief.

"They will do nothing unnecessarily," Arto continued, "except perhaps what
is convenient."

Jack turned upon him.

"You speak as though you know this place."

"I have visited it."

"And escaped?"

"I was reclaimed. There is a difference."

Arto settled himself while the others watched. He had once
belonged to Professor Bartelli's Grand Equestrian and Zoological Theatre, where
he had danced on two legs, counted to twelve, chosen
flags by colour, and pretended to die for his country
amid the applause of persons who would not have given
him a bone afterward. He had travelled in wagons, slept
under painted scenery, crossed rivers on ferries, and known both
artists and creditors. In his youth he had believed intelligence
a shield. Age had taught him that intelligence in a
dog is valued only when it amuses a man.

"My professor reclaimed me the first time," he said. "He
needed me for the evening performance. Later, when I could
no longer jump through three hoops without coughing, he sold
me to a tavern keeper. The tavern keeper lost his
license, and I lost my home. Since then I have
observed public institutions from below, which is the only position
from which their foundations may be properly studied."

"But what happens at the pound?" asked the greyhound.

Arto examined her blue jacket with gentle pity.

"For several days they keep us behind wire. Those with
owners may be bought out."

"Bought!" cried the dachshund. "I have already been bought. At
a very serious price."

"Governments," Arto replied, "do not object to selling the same
thing twice."

"And if no one comes?" asked Jack.

The old poodle was silent.

Outside, the cart passed the slaughter quarter. Jack smelled blood
before he saw the low brick buildings. It was an
immense, warm smell, mingled with wet straw, dung, steam, and
iron. Every dog in the cage became still.

"If no one comes?" Jack repeated.

"They kill us," said the violet dog.

The greyhound uttered a sharp cry. Bouton licked his lips.

"That cannot be legal."

"It is especially legal," said the stray. "That is why
it is done in daylight."

Arto looked reproachfully at him.

"There is no need to alarm the ladies with blunt
expressions."

"Does a polite word make a noose softer?"

"No, but dignity is the last possession of the unfortunate."

"Then keep yours. Fleas don't eat dignity."

Jack pressed close to Arto.

"How do they kill us?"

"In various ways," said the poodle. "Economy encourages invention. Sometimes
poison, sometimes gas, sometimes a blow. When too many dogs
are confined together without food, the problem may solve itself.
Hunger abolishes social distinctions more thoroughly than any philosopher."

The dachshund drew away from the others.

"I warn you," he said, "that my family is connected
with a judge."

The mouse-coloured dog laughed once, without pleasure.

"And afterward?" whispered the greyhound.

"Afterward," said Arto, "skins may be sold. Fat has uses.
Nothing is wasted except suffering."

Bouton's round face had lost its hopeful expression.

"My master will certainly come. He calls me his faithful
one."

"Did he put your name upon your collar?" asked the
violet dog.

Bouton looked down. The brass-studded collar bore no plate.

"He knows my face."

"All men know the face of faithfulness," said the stray.
"They just don't always know where they left it."

For a while no one spoke. The cart jolted onward,
and each prisoner followed a private trail of memory. The
greyhound remembered a scented bedroom and hands that had dressed
her in winter; the dachshund remembered a hearthrug beneath a
portrait of his owner's dead husband; Bouton remembered scraps tossed
from a hotel kitchen and the boots he had guarded
so conscientiously. The mouse-coloured dog remembered several masters, none clearly
enough to expect.

Jack thought of the green gate, the ducks, the cook's
noisy skirts, and his master's slippers, which he was forbidden
to chew and therefore loved intensely. Until that morning, he
had believed these things to be parts of the world,
like rain and earth. Now he understood that they could
continue without him.

"We must escape," he said.

The dachshund glanced at the locked grating.

"A brilliant proposal."

"There are seven of us. Only two men."

"Seven dogs," said Arto, "do not equal two men."

"Why not?"

"Because men have arranged the arithmetic."

Jack began pacing as far as the cramped cage allowed.

"We can rush the door when they open it. I
shall seize one by the leg. Monsieur Long-Body can go
beneath the other. Mademoiselle can run for help."

"I cannot run in this jacket," said the greyhound. "It
catches at the shoulder."

"I can bark," offered Bouton.

"You always can," muttered the mouse-coloured dog.

Arto shook his mane.

"My dear Jack, courage is charming, particularly at your age.
But men possess clubs, walls, locks, carts, laws, and the
agreement of other men. We possess teeth, and they remove
even those when it suits them. Their strength is organized;
ours quarrels over bones."

"Then dogs should organize."

"For what purpose? To appoint a chairman while the catcher
tightens the noose?"

"To be free!"

"Free to starve? Free to freeze? Free to fight every
larger dog and flee every smaller man? Freedom is a
fine smell carried from a distant kitchen. Up close, one
often discovers there is nothing in the pot."

The violet dog opened one yellow eye.

"You've eaten so long from human bowls," he said, "you
think the bowl grows the meat."

"And you," replied Arto, "have lived so long in gutters
that you mistake misery for independence."

"I've never called misery anything but misery."

"Then what do you call happiness?"

"A full belly when I earn it, dry ground when
I find it, and no hand on my neck."

"A modest paradise."

"Better a narrow door I choose than a palace where
I'm kicked."

Arto turned to the others with the patient air of
a lecturer interrupted by an uneducated man.

"Let us speak plainly. A dog's fate rests with his
master. Under a kind master, he is fed, sheltered, protected,
and loved. Under a cruel one, he suffers. Without a
master, he belongs to every hunger and every stick. Thus
the wisest thing a dog can do is to seek
a good human being and serve him faithfully."

"And if the good human loses him?" Jack asked.

"Then fate has been unkind."

"If he grows tired of him?" asked the mouse-coloured dog.

"Then the man was not good."

"If all the good ones are already owned?" said the
stray.

Arto frowned.

"One must not destroy a sound principle by multiplying accidents."

The violet dog rose. He was taller than Jack had
supposed, though his back sagged and one hind leg trembled.

"Your principle says everything depends on them," he said. "That
suits them. It also suits dogs too frightened to try."

"I have tried more things than you have imagined."

"In a circus?"

"In life, sir."

"Same thing, perhaps. A hoop, a whip, applause, and supper
if the trick pleases."

Arto's eyes flashed, but before he could answer, the cart
turned sharply. The prisoners were thrown together. The greyhound cried
out; the dachshund's little feet paddled uselessly; Jack struck his
shoulder against the bars. Then the wheels slowed.

A heavy gate creaked open.

The smell of the pound entered the cart: lime, wet
rope, old straw, disinfectant, excrement, fear, and beneath all these
the faded odour of many dogs who were no longer
there.

The cart passed into a yard surrounded by a high
board fence. Nails projected along the top, some bent outward,
others rusted into hooks. Beyond it stood bare poplars and
a strip of colourless sky.

The catchers climbed down. One unlocked the rear door while
the other waited with the pole.

"Out!"

The dachshund was dragged first. His small claws scraped furiously
across the floor.

"You will regret this!" he screamed. "My lady knows magistrates!"

The greyhound followed, then Bouton, who wagged his tail until
the noose tightened and turned his wagging into a cough.
The mouse-coloured dog submitted without protest.

When Jack's turn came, he sprang for the open yard.
He had taken three strides when the pole caught him
again. He was pulled sideways and thrust toward a pen.

"Still think seven dogs beat two men?" Arto asked quietly
as he passed.

Jack had no answer.

The prisoners were driven across the yard. Behind the wire
of other pens, dogs pressed forward: mastiffs reduced to great
skulls and shoulders, terriers with bloodshot eyes, puppies whose round
stomachs showed through sparse fur, old hounds too tired to
rise. Some begged. Some cursed. Some merely stared. One licked
the wire over and over, as though patience might wear
through iron.

The violet dog came last. The catcher disliked touching him
and attempted to guide him with the pole from a
distance.

"Move, you purple devil."

The stray crouched. His nose worked. He looked not at
the men, nor at the open pen, but at the
fence.

Jack followed his gaze. From the packed earth to the
lowest safe edge was twice the height any reasonable dog
would jump. Above that waited the nails.

"Don't," Jack whispered.

The stray's muscles tightened under his ruined coat.

Arto saw it too.

"Impossible," he said. "Even in my best years—"

The violet dog ran.

At first his lame hind leg made his course uneven,
and the catcher laughed. Then the dog gathered speed. He
passed the pen, crossed the yard, and struck the ground
before the fence with such force that dirt flew from
beneath his paws.

He rose.

For an instant he seemed to hang upright against the
boards, forelegs reaching, ribs exposed, torn ear flattened by the
wind. His paws found the top. A nail entered his
side. The skin stretched; his body swung; and a strip
of flesh opened red beneath the violet stain.

Every dog cried out.

The catcher rushed forward with the pole.

The stray kicked once, slipped, and nearly fell back. The
hook tore farther through him. Yet instead of surrendering to
the pain, he twisted his body upon it, used the
very nail that wounded him as a point of support,
and flung one hind leg over the fence.

The pole struck the boards below his tail.

Then he was gone.

There came a thud on the other side, a silence,
and afterward the quick, uneven beat of paws retreating through
dead leaves.

The men shouted. One ran for the gate; the other
climbed onto a barrel and peered over the fence. But
the violet dog had already passed beyond sight. Only drops
of blood remained upon the topmost board, bright against the
grey wood.

In the yard no dog moved.

Jack stood with his neck bruised by the noose and
his heart beating as though it too had cleared the
fence. The greyhound had forgotten her jacket. The dachshund's tail
no longer resembled the proud figure nine. Bouton stared at
the empty sky above the boards. Even the dogs in
the distant pens were silent, listening to the diminishing footsteps
of one of their kind.

Arto remained where the catcher had released him. His old
circus paw was lifted, but no audience applauded, no master
commanded, and no piece of sugar waited as a reward.

He looked at the torn flesh on the nail, then
at the fence, and finally toward the place where the
stray had disappeared.

The violet dog was wounded, hungry, homeless, and alone; nevertheless,
every stride now carried him in a direction chosen by
himself.

Arto lowered his paw.

Without speaking, the learned poodle understood that he had been
mistaken.

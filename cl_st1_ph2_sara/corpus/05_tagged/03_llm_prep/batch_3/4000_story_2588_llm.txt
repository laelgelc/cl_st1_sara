### THE ROOMS BETWEEN

**JULY 3.**—I HAVE come to Bellwether in obedience to Dr.
Ralston, who assures me that my lungs require country air,
idleness, and freedom from intellectual agitation. The prescription is ingenious.
Country air is available without fee; idleness is my ordinary
condition during the summer recess; and intellectual agitation has never,
according to the chairman of my department, been among my
professional dangers.

I am assistant professor of comparative philology at Wycliffe College,
a title contrived to suggest both attainment and expectation while
guaranteeing neither. After twelve years I remain "promising." A man
may live a long while upon promise, though the diet
is not strengthening.

The Bellwether Hotel stands above the village, a wooden monument
to departed prosperity. Its veranda sags, its carpets exhale the
dust of administrations long fallen, and its electric bulbs possess
the conscientious dimness of candles in a crypt. Nevertheless, my
room is large, the tariff moderate, and the proprietor has
promised that the nights are cool.

He has lied regarding the nights.

My room, Number 2, is at the front of the
second-floor corridor. Opposite is Number 1, unoccupied. Beyond mine, in
order, are 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12. The rooms
across the corridor bear the odd numbers. Most are empty.
At the far end, in Number 12, there is a
pale man.

I noticed him this evening while returning from supper. He
stood in his doorway, one long white hand upon the
knob, observing me.

He is tall, broad-shouldered, and apparently in excellent health, though
his face has the color of old ivory. His hair
is black, his eyes blacker. He smiled when I bowed,
but did not return the salutation.

I wonder who he is.

---

**JULY 4.**—THE COUNTRY air has produced a cough.

At breakfast I inquired of the venerable bell-boy concerning the
occupant of Number 12. I call him a bell-boy because
that is his office; in years he might have been
a schoolmate of President Buchanan.

"Tall gentleman?" I asked. "Very pale?"

He contemplated the coffee-pot.

"Folks comes and goes," he said.

This is the sort of gnomic utterance for which rustic
persons are admired by writers who do not have to
obtain information from them.

The pale man did not appear in the dining room.
I remained over my toast for forty minutes, pretending to
read the county newspaper. It contained an account of a
calf with two tails and an editorial against Bolshevism. Neither
sustained me.

This afternoon I walked to the end of the corridor.
Number 12 was closed. I considered knocking, but one does
not force an acquaintance merely because a hotel offers no
better diversion. Besides, I have never been adept at introductions.
If solitude were an academic qualification, I should long ago
have obtained a full professorship.

At nine I saw him again. He was standing outside
Number 12, smiling.

There is something odd in the smile. It is not
friendliness. Nor is it mockery. It resembles recognition.

Yet I am certain we have never met.

---

**JULY 6.**—HE HAS moved to Number 10.

I discovered this at noon. The door of 12 stood
open while the chambermaid aired the room. Number 10, formerly
vacant, was shut; and as I passed it, the pale
man emerged.

"You find the hotel comfortable?" I asked.

He looked directly into my face. His eyes are not
merely dark; they seem without reflection, as though light enters
them and is detained.

He smiled and went down the stairs.

I confess I was irritated. A civil question deserves a
civil reply. The faculty may ignore my recommendations, students may
mispronounce every vowel entrusted to them, and publishers may return
my monograph unread; but I have not yet become invisible.

Why did he change rooms? Number 10 can possess no
advantage over 12. Both overlook the kitchen roof, and Number
10 is nearer the bathroom, whose pipes trumpet at uncertain
intervals. Perhaps he objects to the elms outside 12. Perhaps
he is one of those restless travelers who imagine happiness
to reside perpetually in the next apartment.

I shall make his acquaintance at supper, if he appears.

He did not.

---

**JULY 8.**—MY TEMPERATURE is 100.2, which Dr. Ralston's country air
has not had the courtesy to reduce. There is also
a pain below my left shoulder. I have written him,
though I know the answer: rest, milk, and a tonic
whose principal virtue is its price.

The only other permanent guest upon this floor is an
elderly woman in Number 8. Her name is Mrs. Gable,
and she knits purple garments on the veranda. Whether they
are intended for an infant, a spaniel, or an unusually
modest tea-pot I can not determine.

She addressed me today.

"You don't look hearty," she said.

Neither, in candor, does Mrs. Gable. She is small, gray,
and folded inward like a letter that has been carried
too long in a pocket.

I asked whether she knew the gentleman in Number 10.

"There's no gentleman there," she replied.

I attributed this to deafness. When I repeated the question,
she frowned and said that she had heard me perfectly
the first time.

Yet tonight the pale man stood before Number 10. I
saw him. Mrs. Gable may reserve the noun "gentleman" for
those who acknowledge introductions. In that case her distinction is
just.

He seems interested in me. As I climbed the stairs,
pausing twice for breath, his gaze followed every step.

I wonder whether he is a physician.

---

**JULY 10.**—AN EXTRAORDINARY thing: he has taken Number 8.

Mrs. Gable was removed from it shortly before dawn.

I was awakened at three by hurried footsteps, a woman's
whisper, and the venerable bell-boy descending the stairs with a
speed perilous at his age. Later came Dr. Fenwick, the
village practitioner—a moist, red person whose waistcoat suggests professional success
more persuasively than does his conversation.

At breakfast I learned that Mrs. Gable had suffered an
attack of the heart. She is now in a smaller
chamber downstairs, where Fenwick attends her.

At ten I saw the pale man unlock Number 8
and enter.

His advance is becoming systematic: 12, 10, 8. It resembles
a school exercise in subtraction. I can not imagine why
the management indulges these migrations. Perhaps he receives a reduced
rate by occupying rooms immediately after they have been vacated.
Hotels possess economics as mysterious as theology.

He is now only three doors from my own.

I attempted a walk, but the heat pressed upon the
road like a hand. The villagers stared. A thin man
in a black coat is apparently more remarkable here than
a two-tailed calf, though less worthy of newspaper notice.

On returning I found the pale man at his threshold.
For one absurd instant I thought he had been waiting.

His smile widened as I passed.

---

**JULY 11.**—MRS. GABLE is dead.

The announcement was made over breakfast by the clerk, a
narrow young man whose clipped mustache appears to have been
pasted beneath his nose as an afterthought. He said "passed
away," that genteel phrase by which death is invited to
leave through the servants' entrance.

She died at four yesterday afternoon. At approximately that hour—I
remember because the courthouse clock was striking—the pale man entered
her room.

The coincidence troubles me.

I have remained in bed most of today. My cough
is worse, and there was blood upon the handkerchief this
morning. Not much. A professor's blood, like his salary, is
issued sparingly.

Dr. Fenwick examined me and declared that I possess a
"delicate constitution." For this revelation he charged three dollars. He
advises me not to excite myself.

I asked whether he knew the guest in Number 8.

"What guest?"

"The tall, pale fellow."

Fenwick said he had seen no one. He then listened
to my chest with renewed attention, as though the pale
man might be concealed there.

After he departed I heard a door open. The pale
man crossed from Number 8 to Number 6.

He has skipped no room.

Two doors remain between us.

---

**JULY 12.**—I SLEPT badly. Each time the building creaked I
imagined footsteps in the corridor.

This is childish. A sequence is not necessarily a design.
The human intellect imposes patterns upon accidents; I have said
so often enough in lectures, usually when unable to explain
an irregular verb.

Still: 12 to 10, 10 to 8 upon Mrs. Gable's
illness, 8 to 6 upon her death.

Why toward the front? Why toward me?

There may be another object. Number 2 is not the
only destination. He might intend to descend by the front
staircase after sampling every chamber. He may be an inspector.
He may be the proprietor in disguise. He may be
insane.

At noon I dragged a chair into the corridor and
sat reading within sight of Number 6. My book was
a German treatise upon consonantal decay, formerly an unfailing narcotic.
Today I comprehended not a sentence.

At two the door opened. The pale man looked out.

I determined to hold his gaze. It is an elementary
contest of will, and I have stared down sophomores suspected
of consulting notes during examinations.

I lost.

His eyes seemed old beyond calculation, patient without kindness. Before
them my indignation became small and foolish. I returned to
my room, nearly falling as I crossed the threshold.

I heard no laughter.

I should have preferred laughter.

---

**JULY 13.**—HE IS in Number 4.

I KNOW this, although I did not see him move.

At dawn I woke upon the floor. I remember rising
during the night for water; beyond that, nothing. The pitcher
was broken, and one fragment had cut my palm. I
must have lain there several hours.

When I had gained the bed, I heard the knob
of Number 4 turn. The pale man came out, immaculate,
composed, and strong. He glanced toward my open door.

The smile was no longer ambiguous.

It was welcome.

I summoned the bell-boy and asked to be transferred downstairs.
He scratched his white head and said every lower room
was occupied by a commercial party selling agricultural machinery. I
offered an additional dollar. This produced regret but no room.

"Then put me in Number 1."

"Number 1 ain't fit, sir. Ceiling fell in last winter."

Thus civilization fails: not from war or pestilence, but from
deferred repairs.

I instructed him to bring the clerk.

---

**JULY 13, LATER.**—THE CLERK came at five. Our conversation must
be recorded exactly, for either he is a liar or
I am losing my reason.

"I wish to know the name of the gentleman in
Number 4," I said.

"There isn't anyone in Number 4."

"I saw him leave it this morning."

The clerk looked at me with the careful neutrality commonly
reserved for drunkards and deans.

"That room's empty."

"He occupied Number 6 yesterday, Number 8 before that, and
previously Numbers 10 and 12."

"All those rooms are empty, sir. Except Number 8 was
Mrs. Gable's."

"You gave him the keys."

"No, sir."

"A tall man, black hair, extraordinarily pale."

"No one answering that description is registered here."

"Then he enters without registering."

The clerk shook his head. "Those doors are kept locked."

"Bring me the key to Number 4."

He hesitated, but obeyed. We crossed the corridor together. The
lock turned stiffly. Dust lay upon the furniture. The bed
was covered; the wash-basin dry. No luggage, no clothing, no
sign of habitation.

Yet the room was cold. In this suffocating weather it
was cold.

On the pillow lay the purple knitting Mrs. Gable had
carried on the veranda.

The clerk did not see it. Or claimed not to.

I understand now.

---

**JULY 14.**—DEATH is a tall pale man who observes the
decencies of hotel architecture.

How simple it appears after recognition! He began at the
farthest chamber and has approached in order. Mrs. Gable detained
him. That was why he entered Number 8 when she
fell ill, why he remained until she died, why he
then resumed his progress.

And I, with the analytical gifts for which Wycliffe pays
me eighteen hundred dollars yearly, speculated about plumbing and reduced
tariffs.

There is humor in this, if there be humor anywhere.
All my life I have waited for advancement. At last
something advances toward me.

Number 4 adjoins my own.

Today the pale man has not appeared, but I hear
him. A measured step crosses the floor, pauses beside the
wall, and returns. Sometimes there is a faint scraping, as
of fingernails—or perhaps a key—testing the partition.

Dr. Fenwick came again. He says pneumonia. His expression supplied
what his vocabulary omitted. He wished to send me to
the county hospital, but there is no ambulance until morning,
and he doubts I should endure the journey.

I did not mention my neighbor. One resents being thought
delirious when one has at last become accurate.

Fenwick has gone for a nurse.

I do not think she will arrive in time.

---

**JULY 14, MIDNIGHT.**—THE CORRIDOR is silent.

I have bolted my door and pushed the bureau against
it. Such precautions are contemptible, but dignity is a garment
readily discarded when one hears a knob turn in the
next room.

My fever is high. The lamp flame wears a halo.
The musty roses upon the wallpaper seem to open and
close as I watch them.

A little while ago the footsteps in Number 4 ceased.

Then came one footfall in the corridor.

Another.

They stopped outside my room.

No knock followed. This courtesy troubles me more than violence
would have done. A visitor who knocks may be refused.
This one has no need to ask.

I think of my classroom, the chalk dust in the
afternoon light, the rows of incurious faces. I think of
the book I did not finish, the promotion not granted,
the woman I once intended to ask to marry me
and did not. A life may be lost through no
single catastrophe, but by a thousand postponed gestures.

The knob is moving.

The bolt strains, though no hand is visible beneath the
door.

I had supposed that at the end I should argue.
I find I have nothing to say.

The bureau slides softly across the carpet.

The door begins to open.

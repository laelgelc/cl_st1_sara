The stamps went on at eleven.

For ten minutes beforehand the mill stood over the black
shoulder of the mountain as quiet as a stranded ship,
every window yellow in the damp, and the long ore
chutes dripping steadily after the evening rain. Then the belt
took hold. Somewhere below us a wheel coughed, revolved twice,
and stopped; a second wheel answered with a hollow bump;
chains slapped, timbers trembled, and the five stamps rose uncertainly,
like horses trying to keep their legs on a cattle
deck. Presently they found the gait. The separate blows melted
into one tremendous roaring gallop that shook the quartz dust
from the rafters and made the lamp flame quiver in
its cage.

It was the middle of our night shift at the
Little Ophir, up in the Sierra foothills. The rain had
washed the heat out of the air, and when Bunt
and I stepped from the car tunnel for our half-hour
spell, the wet pines smelled sharp enough to taste. Far
below, the creek whitened over the stones. Beyond it the
hills went down fold after fold into darkness, and over
the last fold hung a thin red smear where some
rancher was burning brush.

Bunt sat on an overturned powder box, pushed his hat
back, and began cutting tobacco from a black plug. He
was a narrow old fellow, leather-coloured and loose-jointed, with a
grey moustache that drooped at the corners as though discouraged
by what it had heard him say. He worked the
tobacco between his palms and watched young Rufe Daley come
out of the tunnel.

Rufe was car-boy that month. He had a chest too
narrow for his shoulders, and the mine damp had given
him a cough that barked once, waited, and barked again.
He leaned against the waste dump until the fit was
over.

Bunt followed him with his eyes.

"Say," he observed, "that there cough puts me in mind
o' Clarence."

"Who was Clarence?"

"Well, sir," said Bunt, "Clarence was a friend o' mine
down to Yuma, an' the hottest man I ever see."

"Hot-tempered?"

"No. Temperature. Clarence was consumptive."

He packed the tobacco into his pipe, lighted it with
a splinter from the powder box, and drew until the
bowl glowed. The stamps filled the pause, pounding under us
with the measured fury of surf in a cavern.

"Clarence," Bunt resumed, "was what you mout call an educated
consumptive. He understood his complaint thorough, an' knew the name
of every little noise it made inside him. He could
tell by the set of his collar whether his left
lung was behavin' scandalous, an' he carried three kinds o'
cough mixture, two thermometers, an' a printed circular from a
doctor in Philadelphia tellin' him not to worry.

"That circular worried Clarence worse'n the disease.

"He'd come out to Arizona because somebody told him the
air was dry. It was dry, too. I've saw wagon
tires shrink off their wheels whilst the teamster was eatin'
dinner. But the heat done Clarence no permanent good, unless
y'counted the interest he took in it.

"There was five or six of 'em stoppin' at Mrs.
Castigan's boardin' house, all consumptives, an' all watchin' one another
like poker players. Each one was certain the others wasn't
so sick as they claimed. Clarence, he said there was
men in that house who'd cough three times before breakfast
just to create an impression.

"So they got to comparin' symptoms. One would say he'd
had a night sweat, an' another would claim two; then
a third would come in with a hemorrhage, an' that
would close the conversation. Bime-by they took to temperatures.

"Now, a thermometer is a harmless-lookin' little arrangement, but you
set six sick men to gamblin' with it an' it
gets as exciting as stud poker with the rent money
in the pot. Every afternoon at four they'd gather in
the back parlour, put the instruments under their tongues, an'
wait. Mrs. Castigan held the stakes.

"They played for small money—two bits admission, winner take all—because
the doctor had warned 'em against excitement.

"The rules was strict. No hot coffee inside of thirty
minutes. No settin' in the sun. No rubbin' the bulb
on the pants. One fellow name of Jessup got barred
for holdin' his thermometer against the lamp chimney, though he
maintained he was only tryin' to read it.

"Clarence had a natural gift. Most days he registered a
hundred an' two without strainin', an' when he'd been coughin'
active he could touch a hundred an' three an' a
fraction. There was a Denver man could run him close,
but Clarence had the endurance. He said fever was like
a race horse: blood told in the stretch.

"One Tuesday I was there watchin' the sport. The room
was quiet, except for six men breathin' through their noses
an' Mrs. Castigan rattlin' the chips. The sun was comin'
through the blind in thin yellow stripes. Clarence sat opposite
me with his eyes bulged, guardin' that thermometer under his
tongue like it was the last cartridge in camp.

"Then in come Peg-leg Smith.

"Did you ever hear tell o' Peg-leg?"

I said I had not.

Bunt regarded me with compassion.

"You been denied advantages," he said. "Peg-leg Smith was famous
from Tucson clean across to the Colorado, though not for
anything a man's widow would put on his stone. He
was a cow-rustlin', claim-jumpin', hair-liftin' maverick, with one sound leg,
one wooden leg, an' no settled convictions except about profanity.

"He hated profanity.

"I don't mean he disapproved of it mild, like a
schoolma'am. It stirred him deep. You could cheat at cards,
steal a horse, or shoot a man from behind a
water barrel, an' Peg-leg would allow there was two sides
to the question; but if you used a cuss word
in his hearin', he considered you'd attacked the foundations of
society.

"How he come by that notion I never learned. Some
said his mother raised him strict. Some said a parson
had saved his life. My belief is he just wanted
one virtue he could carry without inconvenience.

"He didn't drink much, neither, because liquor interfered with his
balance. That wooden leg was made of mesquite, heavy at
the bottom, with an iron shoe onto it, an' when
he was sober he could manage it wonderful. He didn't
walk, precisely. He come along in a system of hops
an' swings, like a kangaroo workin' a churn. But give
him three drinks an' his navigation got uncertain.

"Well, Peg-leg comes into Mrs. Castigan's parlour without knockin'. He'd
been lookin' for a man from Bisbee, so he said,
though the Bisbee man had took the precaution to be
elsewhere.

"He looks around.

"'What's this?' says he.

"Nobody answered, because everybody had thermometers in their mouths.

"Peg-leg sees the money on the table. 'A game?' says
he.

"Clarence nodded.

"Peg-leg says, 'Deal me in.'

"Clarence took out his thermometer long enough to explain that
it wasn't cards; that it was a tournament for invalids,
the party showin' the highest bodily heat collectin' the purse.

"Peg-leg thought this over. Then he says, 'I ain't no
invalid.'

"'You got one artificial extremity,' says Clarence. 'That ought to
qualify you.'

"Peg-leg didn't like that. He looked at Clarence, an' Clarence
put the thermometer back under his tongue.

"Now, Clarence was the gentlest man ever breathed. He'd apologise
to a mosquito for slappin' at it. But disease had
shortened his patience, an' the contest had got him keyed
up. When the Denver man's timepiece clicked four, they all
pulled their thermometers an' began readin'.

"Jessup had a hundred an' one.

"The Denver man had a hundred an' two an' four
tenths.

"Clarence held his to the light. His hand commenced to
shake. 'Hundred an' three!' says he. 'By—'

"An' then he spoke one profane word.

"It wasn't what I'd call a large word. I've heard
stronger expressions used over a boiled egg. But Peg-leg heard
it.

"He turned white around the mouth.

"'Who said that?' says he.

"Everybody pointed at Clarence. Sick men are like that when
firearms appear; they don't waste time protectin' the guilty.

"Peg-leg shut the door. Then he took out a long
revolver and laid it across his forearm.

"'Young man,' says he, 'I've made it my duty to
discourage vile language in this territory.'

"Clarence swallowed hard. It's lucky he'd removed the thermometer.

"'I beg your pardon,' says he. 'I was excited.'

"'Excitement,' says Peg-leg, 'ain't no defence.'

"'It won't happen again.'

"'That's correct,' says Peg-leg, cockin' the gun.

"Well, sir, I'm not a brave man. Never was. Some
men has courage stored in 'em like water in a
tank, ready whenever the valve is pulled. Mine comes and
goes according to circumstances, an' mostly it goes. But Clarence
was my friend, an' he was too weak to run,
an' besides, Mrs. Castigan had gone under the table with
the stakes.

"So I moved.

"I didn't formulate no plan. Plans are fine if the
other man will wait. I just kicked a chair into
Peg-leg's good knee, grabbed him around the belt, an' upset
him.

"A man with two natural legs can fall in several
graceful ways, but Peg-leg had only one choice. He went
over sideways like a rickety table.

"The gun exploded. The bullet hit Mrs. Castigan's china shepherdess
on the mantel an' removed the shepherdess from useful life.
All six consumptives hollered, an' four of 'em got under
the furniture. The other two was already there.

"Peg-leg rolled onto his back, cussin'—which shows how a reformer
will relax his principles when pressed—and tried to bring the
revolver around.

"I stamped on his wrist. Then I took hold of
the wooden leg.

"I didn't know it come off.

"It did.

"There was straps an' a socket to the contraption, but
in the confusion something give way, an' all at once
I was standin' there with about four foot of polished
mesquite in my hands, while Peg-leg lay on the carpet
lookin' shorter than he had any right to.

"He was surprised. So was I.

"He grabbed for the gun with his other hand. I
reached it first, shoved the revolver into my belt, an'
backed toward the window, still carryin' the leg.

"'Gimme that!' he bellows.

"'Which?' says I.

"'Both!'

"'You stay there,' says I.

"'I'll skin you! I'll cut your heart out! I'll—'

"Here he delivered himself of language that would've caused him
to shoot Clarence six times over.

"Mrs. Castigan's back parlour was on the second floor, but
outside the window there was a porch roof, an' from
the roof you could get onto the stable shed. I
climbed out.

"Clarence came after me.

"He wasn't built for speed, but fear improved him. The
rest followed, each man bringin' his thermometer, because there was
money unsettled.

"We got onto the porch roof. Behind us Peg-leg dragged
hisself to the window. Without that leg he couldn't stand,
but he come astonishing fast on his hands an' one
knee, makin' a noise like a bear pullin' down a
stove.

"'Bunt!' he roars. 'Bunt, I know you!'

"That was disagreeable information, because I had hoped he didn't.

"'I'll follow you to the end o' the earth!'

"'Don't exert yourself,' says I, an' jumped to the shed.

"Clarence landed beside me, coughed twice, an' says, 'What'll we
do?'

"'Run.'

"'I can't run.'

"'Then run slow.'

"We crossed the stable roof an' dropped into the alley.
There was a buckboard standin' there with the lines wrapped
around the brake. I piled Clarence in, jumped after him,
whipped up, an' we went out of Yuma with the
six consumptives strung behind like the tail of a comet.

"I still had Peg-leg's revolver in my belt an' Peg-leg's
leg crossways over my knees.

"Now, south of town the road ran near a dry
wash, an' beyond the wash there was a little canyon,
not deep enough to be celebrated but deep enough to
lose property in. When we reached it, I throwed the
revolver as far as I could. It went down among
the rocks.

"Then I throwed the leg.

"I intended to throw it into the canyon, too, but
the buckboard hit a chuck-hole just as I let go.
The leg sailed off crossways, hit a mesquite bush, turned
end over end, an' disappeared down the bank.

"'You mout ought to keep that,' Clarence says.

"'What for?'

"'Evidence.'

"'It's evidence against us.'

"So we kept drivin'. Clarence won the temperature money, by
the way. After that run he registered a hundred an'
five, an' nobody ever disputed the title."

Bunt paused to relight his pipe. Rufe had returned to
the tunnel, and a string of empty cars came rattling
out, pushed by two men whose lamps swung in the
dark like low stars. The hoist bell rang once; then
twice. From the shaft house came the swift winding whine
of the cable.

"What happened to the leg?" I asked.

"Well," said Bunt, "that's where the business got delicate.

"The next morning a boy found it caught in the
mesquite. He knowed whose it was. There wasn't two one-legged
men in Yuma, an' if there had been, the other
would likely have owned a more peaceable article.

"But the boy brought it to me.

"He says Peg-leg was offerin' fifty dollars for its return
an' a hundred dollars for information concernin' me, the difference
bein' accounted for by sentiment.

"I paid the boy two dollars an' stored the leg
in a feed bin.

"Peg-leg had borrowed a crutch from the doctor an' was
conductin' inquiries. The first day he promised to kill me.
The second day he promised to kill me slow. By
the third he had worked out details.

"I'm skeered, I tell you. I don't want no foolish
misunderstandin' about that. I was skeered from my hat clear
down to my boot nails. Peg-leg had done for nine
men that was known, an' maybe others that was too
bashful to report. He was patient, too. He once waited
eleven months outside a jail to shoot a fellow when
his sentence expired. That kind o' perseverance is discouragin' to
the party waited for.

"So I studied.

"I could leave Arizona, but Peg-leg said he'd follow. I
could return the leg, but then he'd be mobile. I
could keep it, but a man can't spend his life
guardin' a feed bin. I considered shootin' him, only he
had friends, or at least associates who might mistake revenge
for friendship.

"Then I remembered his stand against profanity.

"I sent word I'd meet him at the old freight
corral, provided he come alone an' without weapons. He sent
back word he'd come as he pleased. We compromised: he
come with a derringer, an' I stood behind a water
trough with a shotgun.

"He arrived on the doctor's crutch, swingin' along savage, his
coat tails flyin'. When he got within thirty feet I
told him to halt.

"'Where's my leg?' he says.

"'Safe.'

"'Fetch it.'

"'We'll talk.'

"'There ain't nothin' to talk about.'

"'There is a moral issue,' says I.

"That stopped him. A moral issue was his weakness.

"'What issue?'

"'Profanity.'

"He rested against the crutch an' looked suspicious.

"'You,' says I, 'are opposed to profanity on grounds of
conscience, public order, private decency, an' general civilization.'

"'I am.'

"'You enforce them views with firearms.'

"'I do.'

"'I myself,' says I, 'have been careless in speech.'

"Peg-leg says he'd heard me use language that would blister
paint.

"'That's true,' says I. 'But I'm prepared to reform.'

"'Reform, then. After I kill you.'

"'No,' says I. 'That don't encourage reform. What you want
is results.'

"He scowled. Peg-leg was a quick man with a gun,
but prolonged reasoning wearied him.

"So I laid out the contract.

"'I, Bunt,' says I, 'party of the first part, do
hereby agree an' covenant to renounce, abandon, discontinue, an' forever
quit all profanity, cussin', swearin', blasphemin', an' careless reference to
sacred subjects, for an' durin' the natural life of you,
Peg-leg Smith, party of the second part.'

"'Where do I come in?' he says.

"'You come in very handsome. In consideration of said reform,
you agree not to shoot, stab, poison, strangle, beat, burn,
drown, maim, molest, annoy, pursue, persecute, or otherwise injure the
party of the first part.'

"'An' my leg?'

"'Returned upon execution of the agreement.'

"'S'pose you swear?'

"'Then the agreement terminates, an' you can kill me.'

"'S'pose I kill you anyway?'

"'Then you don't get the leg.'

"He reflected.

"'I could find it.'

"'Maybe. But Arizona's broad, an' feed bins is numerous.'

"That bothered him. He had a personal attachment to the
leg. It had belonged, he told me later, to a
gambler in El Paso before it belonged to him, an'
was seasoned mesquite with a silver dollar buried in the
heel for luck. I never asked how the gambler happened
to part with it.

"Peg-leg says, 'How long is this bindin'?'

"'As long as you live.'

"'An' no longer?'

"'No longer.'

"'Put it in writin'.'

"So we went to a notary. I had the document
prepared in elegant language, mentionin' heirs, assigns, acts of Providence,
an' all parties in sound mind, though that last clause
was strainin' matters. Peg-leg signed with a cross because writin'
interfered with his gun hand. I signed full name. The
notary witnessed it from behind a roll-top desk.

"Then I returned the leg.

"Peg-leg strapped it on, stamped twice to test the fit,
walked around the room, an' says, 'Bunt, I hope to
hear you swear soon.'

"'You won't,' says I.

"An' he didn't.

"That agreement made a changed man of me. Not better,
maybe, but cautious. I quit profanity complete. I wouldn't even
say 'darn' to a mule. Once, when a blasting cap
went off in my pocket an' took part of my
pants with it, I described the occurrence as unfortunate.

"Peg-leg used to test me.

"He'd meet me outside a saloon an' step on my
foot. He'd spill coffee in my lap. One time he
put a scorpion in my bedroll an' sat nearby with
his revolver ready. I removed the animal an' called it
an insect.

"For three years we lived under that covenant, each of
us honourable according to his nature.

"He never harmed me.

"He harmed a large number of other people, you understand.
The bargain didn't improve him general. If anything, it give
him confidence. He shot a faro dealer in Prescott for
sayin' something objectionable about the queen of hearts. He chased
a teamster half across Pinal County because the teamster addressed
his mules in customary terms. He broke up a wedding
in Globe when the bride's father dropped the ring an'
expressed hisself.

"But with me he was exact. He wouldn't let another
man interfere, neither. Once at a mining camp a drunk
pulled a knife on me, an' Peg-leg shot the knife
out of his hand.

"'Nobody's to damage Bunt while our contract's active,' he says.
'It mout tempt him into language.'

"That was Peg-leg's idea of ethics. He protected my morals
by keepin' me from provocation."

"You kept your promise all that time?" I asked.

"Every syllable," said Bunt. "A bargain is a bargain. Besides,
he carried the duplicate in his shirt pocket an' read
it to people. Or got people to read it, him
not bein' literary.

"But restraint tells on a man. There's occasions when ordinary
English ain't sufficient. A loose saddle, a crooked card game,
a burro that eats your blankets—such matters demand expression. I
got so loaded with unspoken profanity that I dreamed in
it. I'd wake up sweatin', with both hands clamped over
my mouth.

"I began followin' reports of Peg-leg's health.

"Every time I heard he'd been in a gun fight,
my spirits rose. Every time I learned he'd survived it,
they sunk. He was shot twice, stabbed once, bit by
a rattler, throwed from a horse, an' caught in a
flash flood, an' none of it done more than annoy
him. He seemed preserved special for my discipline.

"Then, three years after the Yuma turn-up, I went to
Red Basin.

"Red Basin wasn't much of a town, but it possessed
a courthouse, seven saloons, a Chinese laundry, an' a hotel
called the Grand Continental, which was neither. It stood four
stories high, built mostly of dry boards, wallpaper, an' optimism.
The fire escapes had been ordered but not received.

"I arrived toward evening, took a room on the second
floor, an' went across the street to eat.

"About midnight somebody yelled fire.

"The whole Grand Continental was alight at the back. Flame
come through the kitchen windows, ran up the stairs, an'
commenced eatin' them galleries like grass. Men formed a bucket
line from the horse trough, but the buckets leaked, an'
half the men stopped to give advice.

"I helped carry out trunks. Folks will risk their lives
for baggage they wouldn't buy at auction. We saved three
sample cases of suspenders, a cage with no bird in
it, two mattresses, an' the hotel register. Then the roof
over the kitchen fell, an' we moved back into the
street.

"Windows was bustin'. Smoke rolled out black, turned red, an'
went up full of sparks. Women hollered from the lower
rooms until ladders reached 'em. A drummer slid down a
rain pipe in his nightshirt. Another man jumped from the
second floor, landed in the suspenders, an' lived.

"Then somebody yelled, 'There's a man on the fourth!'

"We looked up.

"There was Peg-leg Smith.

"He stood at the top window with smoke behind him,
wearin' his trousers, one boot, an' the wooden leg. He'd
tied a wet towel around his head. I recognised him
by the method of locomotion when he climbed onto the
sill.

"'Get a ladder!' he hollers.

"The longest ladder reached only to the third floor.

"'Jump!' somebody shouted.

"'I'll break my leg!'

"That struck the crowd funny, though it wasn't intended so.

"The flames come through the room behind him. Peg-leg fired
his revolver into the street to promote activity.

"'Do somethin', you loafers!'

"A fellow near the horse trough yelled, 'We'll hold a
blanket!'

"At that the men below spread out, raised their arms,
an' shouted for him to jump. Smoke covered Peg-leg, then
cleared. He looked down four stories. He looked back at
the fire.

"I stood on the edge of the crowd.

"He saw me.

"'Bunt!' he hollers. 'Is that blanket ready?'

"I had not used a profane word for three years,
two months, an' eleven days. I had endured mules, gamblers,
mine owners, bad coffee, wet powder, Mexican saddles, an' Peg-leg's
scorpion. I knew the agreement remained bindin' an' obligatory while
the party of the second part was alive.

"So I answered careful.

"'Everything's ready, Peg-leg.'

"He swung both feet over the sill. The wooden one
struck the wall with a knock we could hear above
the fire.

"'You're sure?'

"'Come ahead.'

"He pushed off.

"For a second he was spread against the light, arms
wide, towel streamin', that iron-shod leg pointin' straight down. Then
he turned halfway over and come through the smoke like
a busted windmill.

"He hit the street.

"The wooden leg broke at the socket. Peg-leg never said
anything. He was dead before I could determine the exact
instant the covenant expired.

"I walked to him, took off my hat, an' then
relieved my mind for about five minutes."

The stamps below us checked suddenly. Their gallop broke into
separate blows, then into bumps, then silence. In the great
quiet that followed, water could be heard dropping from the
ore chute, and far down the hill a night bird
called once.

I asked Bunt why Peg-leg had missed the blanket.

Bunt knocked the ash from his pipe against the powder
box.

"They hadn't any blanket."

### THE SHANGHAI CHICKEN WHO WOULD NOT EAT GRAVEL

The Shanghai Hen had twelve Chickens, and she thought them
the finest brood in the poultry-yard. This was not at
all surprising, for most mothers think their own children unusually
handsome. Still, it must be confessed that the young Shanghais
were a sight worth seeing when they walked through the
short grass behind her, lifting their long legs very carefully
and peeping to one another about everything they found.

Their father, the Shanghai Cock, was proud of them too.
He had a broad breast, a fine red comb, and
dark green tail-feathers which shone in the sunshine. His legs
were long and yellow, and he considered short legs quite
beneath notice.

"Look at them," he would say. "Every one a true
Shanghai!"

Then he would stretch his neck and crow, and the
Dorking Cock would crow because he had crowed, and the
Bantam Cock would crow because the Dorking had crowed, and
soon every Cock in the neighbourhood would be calling as
loudly as possible without knowing why. You can imagine what
a noise they made.

Yet there was one thing which troubled the Shanghai Hen.
Eleven of her Chickens grew round and sturdy, but the
twelfth remained thin.

At first she told herself that he would improve. He
had been a small Chick from the day when he
chipped his shell, and perhaps he needed more time. She
scratched up the soft earth for him and called whenever
she found a tender root, a seed, or a wriggling
insect.

"Here, my child," she would cluck. "This is especially for
you."

The little fellow came running, for he liked special attentions.
He ate the seed and swallowed the insect, but somehow
he did not grow as the others did. His wings
looked too large for him, his breast was narrow, and
his long legs seemed longer every morning.

His brothers began to notice it.

"If you grow any thinner," said one, "the wind will
carry you over the fence."

"He may not be a Shanghai at all," said another.
"Perhaps he is a Bantam who has been pulled out
lengthwise."

This was foolish talk, for a Bantam is a small
Chicken and does not become a Shanghai merely by being
stretched. But the thin Chicken felt hurt, and he walked
away as though he had not heard.

The Dorking Chickens also whispered about him. Dorkings have five
toes on each foot, and they were quite as proud
of their extra toes as the Shanghais were of their
long legs.

"He will never make a fine Cock," said a young
Dorking Hen. "He could not lead a flock. A Grasshopper
might frighten him."

The thin Chicken pretended to chase a fly. Secretly, he
looked at his shadow upon the ground. It was a
very narrow shadow indeed.

His mother became more anxious each day. She watched him
eat grain, and she saw him drink from the shallow
pan. She looked beneath his wings for troublesome insects and
examined his feet. She warned him not to remain in
the hot sun, and at night she tucked him close
beside her on the roost.

That roosting-time was always a busy affair. One Chicken wanted
the highest place, another wanted the warmest, and a third
pushed between two sisters who did not want him there.
They fluttered, lurched, complained, changed places, and nearly knocked the
Bantam Hen from her perch below. At last all settled
down, although nobody had obtained exactly the place he wished.

When the others slept, the Shanghai Hen sometimes remained awake.
Through a crack in the hen-house she could see the
moon above the barn, and she wondered what was wrong
with her child.

She had worked hard for him. First she had laid
the egg from which he came. Then she had sat
through weary days and nights, leaving the nest only long
enough to eat and drink. She had covered him when
he was cold, called him from danger, and shown him
what to eat. If a Hawk had swooped into the
yard, she would have spread her wings and fought for
him, though the Hawk was larger and stronger than she.

Mothers do a great many things which their children do
not remember.

One afternoon, while the brood fed beside the garden path,
the Shanghai Hen noticed something curious. Whenever she called the
Chickens to pick up tiny stones, eleven obeyed, but the
thin one turned aside.

"Come back," she said.

"I am looking for a Beetle," he answered.

"There is no Beetle there."

"There might be."

The Shanghai Hen watched more closely. She scratched among the
dry earth and uncovered several small pieces of gravel.

"Children," she called, "take some of this."

The eleven plump Chickens came at once. They selected small
stones and swallowed them. The thin Chicken picked one up,
made a face, and dropped it.

"Why did you do that?" asked his mother.

"It tastes bad."

"Gravel is not eaten for its taste."

"Then I do not see why anybody should eat it."

"Have you been leaving it all this time?"

The young Chicken tossed his head.

"I eat grain. I eat worms. Yesterday I ate part
of a cabbage-leaf, and this morning I caught a Fly.
Those are proper foods. Stones are not food."

His mother drew him away from the others.

"My child," she said, "Chickens have no teeth."

"I know that," he replied. "I am not a baby."

"Then because you are not a baby, you should listen.
We swallow grain whole, and it passes into a strong
part inside us called the gizzard. The little stones remain
there and help grind the grain, much as teeth grind
food in the mouths of other creatures. Without gravel, you
cannot make proper use of what you eat. That is
why you are thin."

The Chicken looked at the stones.

"They are hard."

"They must be hard to do their work."

"They are dusty."

"A little dust will not injure you."

"I do not like them."

"You need not like a thing in order to need
it."

This was excellent sense, but the Chicken had already decided
that he knew best.

"I am old enough to choose for myself," he said.

"You are old enough to obey for a reason," answered
his mother.

"So!" said the Chicken, and he stalked away.

Now "So!" is a very small word, but it may
contain a great deal of naughtiness when spoken in the
proper manner.

The Shanghai Hen did not run after him. She knew
that chasing a stubborn Chicken often makes him run farther.
Yet she was sad, and the Shanghai Cock found her
standing alone near the water-pan.

"He must learn," said the Cock when she told him.

"He is growing weaker while he learns."

"He has my legs," replied the Cock proudly. "He will
be all right."

But fine legs cannot perform the work of a gizzard,
and the young Chicken was not all right. He tired
when the brood ran across the yard. He could not
scratch deeply in the stubble. When the others fluttered onto
a low rail, he missed it and tumbled backward, which
caused the Guinea Fowl to laugh in their harsh, rattling
voices.

"Perhaps he is becoming smaller," said one of his brothers.

"Perhaps he will disappear entirely," said another.

Even the young Dorking Hen laughed when she saw him
chased from a crumb by the Bantam Chick.

That evening he looked again at his shadow. The setting
sun made it long, but it could not make it
plump. His neck appeared like a bent twig, and his
legs like two straws.

"I do not care what they think," he muttered.

But he did care. Worse than their teasing was the
knowledge that his mother had been right. Each day his
body felt more tired, and each day he remembered the
gravel.

The next morning the Shanghai Hen led her brood to
the orchard. Near an old apple tree she found a
patch of excellent small stones.

"Take care to choose pieces you can swallow," she told
the younger Chickens.

The thin Chicken wandered away as usual, but this time
he peeped over his shoulder. When he was certain nobody
watched, he picked up a tiny stone.

It tasted precisely as bad as he remembered.

He nearly dropped it. Then a Dorking Chick came around
the tree, and in his hurry not to be seen,
he swallowed it.

Nothing dreadful happened.

He took another, and then a third. After that he
scratched leaves over the place and walked off with a
careless air, as though he had merely been admiring the
apple tree.

For several days he ate gravel in secret. He rose
early and searched beside the path before the other fowls
left the hen-house. Sometimes he pretended to hunt seeds while
choosing stones. He did not tell his mother because he
was ashamed to admit his mistake.

Soon he began to feel stronger. The grain he swallowed
was properly ground in his gizzard, and his body could
use it to build flesh, feathers, and strength. He ran
without gasping. He scratched so vigorously that the earth flew
behind him. One afternoon he leaped upon the rail at
the first attempt.

"Well!" cried his brothers.

The Shanghai Hen said nothing, but she noticed. Mothers usually
do.

Week by week his breast grew broader. His feathers became
smooth, and his comb showed a brighter red. When he
stood in the sunshine, his shadow no longer looked like
two straws carrying a bundle of loose feathers. It looked
like the shadow of a healthy young Shanghai.

The Dorking Chick who had laughed at him now found
reasons to feed nearby.

"You scratch very well," she said one day.

"I have been practising."

"You are taller than your brothers."

"We Shanghais generally are."

This was a rather vain reply, but he was not
quite cured of every fault at once.

Although he was healthy again, he was not entirely happy.
His mother had never asked how he improved, and that
made him more uncomfortable than questions would have done. When
she called the brood to gravel, he now came with
the others, but if he saw her looking at him,
he turned his head.

Then one morning his youngest brother refused to swallow a
stone.

"It tastes bad," said the little Chick. "I am old
enough to decide for myself."

The older Chicken started. Those were almost the very words
he had used, and they sounded remarkably foolish when spoken
by somebody else.

"You should do as Mother says," he told his brother.

"Why? You did not."

The young Shanghai was silent. He could have walked away.
He could have pretended not to hear. Instead, he saw
his mother patiently scratching among the gravel, and he remembered
her sleepless nights, her warnings, and the warm shelter of
her wings.

"You are right," he said. "I did not, and I
suffered for it."

All the Chickens turned to listen.

"I thought gravel was unpleasant and therefore unnecessary," he continued.
"I thought being old enough to have an opinion meant
being wise enough to reject advice. But I grew thin
because my gizzard could not grind my food properly. I
began eating stones secretly, and that is why I became
strong."

He faced his mother.

"I was foolish and naughty. You told me the truth,
and I was too proud to admit it. I am
sorry."

The Shanghai Hen stepped close and gently arranged a feather
on his neck.

"I knew you had begun eating gravel," she said.

"You knew?"

"Certainly. Do you suppose a mother with twelve Chickens does
not know who is scratching beside the path before sunrise?"

The poultry-yard laughed, and the young Shanghai laughed too.

His little brother swallowed the stone.

"It is still unpleasant," he complained.

"Many necessary things are," said the older Chicken. "But avoiding
a small unpleasant duty often brings a much greater trouble.
It is easier to swallow a little gravel than to
carry weakness and shame for weeks."

The Shanghai Cock, who had heard the speech, lifted his
head.

"A true Shanghai!" he declared, and crowed.

The Dorking Cock crowed because the Shanghai Cock had crowed.
The Bantam Cock answered the Dorking, the Cock beyond the
fence answered the Bantam, and soon the whole neighbourhood rang
again.

The young Dorking Hen came to stand beside the Shanghai
Chicken.

"I never truly believed a Grasshopper could frighten you," she
said.

"I believe you did."

"Well," she replied, "that was before you became wise."

From that day, the young Shanghai was admired not only
for his long legs and shining feathers, but for his
good sense. Whenever a younger Chick complained about gravel, early
roosting, keeping near the Hen when a Hawk was overhead,
or any other necessary rule, he told his own story
plainly.

And his mother, watching him lead the younger ones safely
through the stubble, was prouder of his honest confession than
she had ever been of his height.

"He has grown at last," she said.

She did not mean only that he had grown plump.
You know there is more than one way of growing,
and the best growth is sometimes the kind that cannot
be measured by looking at a shadow.

## The Santa Maria Skylights

There were twenty-six children in the Santa Maria Flats, and
not one of them had ever seen Santa Claus come
through the front door.

This was not because they had failed to watch. Oh,
me, no! Dorothy Bell had once remained behind a palm
in the entrance hall for forty-seven minutes, although the palm
was dusty, the tub leaked, and the hall boy twice
ordered her upstairs. Ernest Willett had watched from the second
landing until bedtime. Roderick May had even offered the elevator
boy five cents to report any stout old gentleman carrying
a sack.

But no Santa Claus appeared.

You must understand that the Santa Maria was a Very
Fine Building. Those capitals belong to the agent, who used
them whenever he advertised a vacant apartment. It had polished
brass doors, polished marble floors, polished banisters, and a uniformed
man whose chief business was to prevent persons who had
not been polished from entering at all.

Above everything and everybody reigned the Lease.

The Lease said that tenants must not keep dogs—not even
little dogs; must not beat rugs from windows; must not
hang washing upon fire escapes; must not play in halls;
must not roll hoops in the courtyard; must not whistle
in elevators; must not scratch matches upon walls; and must
not admit peddlers, organ-grinders, suspicious strangers, or persons bearing parcels
unless those persons could satisfactorily explain themselves to the hall
man.

The children had not read the Lease, but they knew
it. Their fathers quoted it at breakfast, their mothers quoted
it at luncheon, the janitor quoted it at all hours,
and the hall man appeared to have swallowed a copy,
for whenever he opened his mouth some clause came out.

Thus the children of the Santa Maria did not run—not
jump—not yell—not slide down banisters—not build forts behind the potted
palms—not do any of the comfortable things which make childhood
endurable. At least they did not do them when adults
were looking.

Now, early in December, Ernest Willett was lying upon the
red Bokara rug in his mother's drawing-room, studying the gas
fireplace.

The rug had cost a great deal of money and
was said to be Oriental. It possessed twelve colors that
no sensible rainbow would have admitted and enough germs to
establish an independent republic. Ernest's mother permitted nobody to walk
upon it in outdoor shoes, though Ernest lay upon it
with his nose two inches from its surface and remained,
by the mercy of Providence, alive.

The fireplace was narrow. It had brass bars, three imitation
logs, and a row of blue gas jets. Above it
stood a green vase shaped like a discouraged cucumber, which
Mrs. Willett called Art.

Ernest measured the opening with his arm.

Then he sat up very suddenly.

"Mamma," he said, "how does Santa Claus get into flats?"

Mrs. Willett was writing dinner invitations and thinking about whether
Mrs. Pomeroy's new gown was French or merely hopeful.

"Through the chimney, dear."

"There isn't any chimney."

"Of course there is."

"Where?"

"In the fireplace."

Ernest looked again.

"He couldn't get through this. Not even if he took
off his coat."

Mrs. Willett wrote, "We shall be delighted," though at that
moment she would not have been delighted by anything except
the discovery that Mrs. Pomeroy's gown had been made in
Hoboken.

"Santa Claus can go anywhere," she said.

"Can he pass the hall man?"

"Certainly."

"Without being announced?"

Mrs. Willett's pen stopped.

"Well—really, Ernest! Go and play."

There are questions which adults dismiss because they are foolish,
and there are questions which adults call foolish because they
cannot answer them. Ernest understood this, as children generally do.
He went away without another word, but apprehension, cold-footed and
unpleasant, crept around his heart.

That afternoon he summoned a council in the basement bicycle-room.

All twenty-six children attended, from Roderick May, aged eleven, to
little Lottie Phelps, aged four, who brought a broken doll
and contributed nothing to the discussion except three peppermints and
an occasional "Why?"

Ernest stated the case.

"Santa Claus comes down chimneys. We haven't got chimneys."

"We've got fireplaces," said Roderick cheerfully.

"Gas ones. I measured ours."

"He might squeeze."

"He is fat."

"He might make himself thin."

"How?"

Roderick had no useful suggestion. Dorothy Bell, who was of
a practical turn, proposed an inspection.

So they inspected.

They went first to the Willetts' apartment, where Ernest demonstrated
the impossibility of passage by placing his head between the
brass bars and becoming stuck. His mother rang for the
maid, the maid rang for the janitor, and the janitor,
arriving with a screwdriver, asked why a boy with ordinary
intelligence should insert his ears into a gas grate.

Ernest did not answer. Martyrs seldom explain themselves.

The next fireplace was no better. Nor the next. Nor
the next.

They inspected nineteen fireplaces that day and seven the next.
Some were white, some black, some tiled in pink flowers,
and one belonged to an Esthetic Lady and was filled
with silver paper because she said flames were vulgar. But
all were narrow. All had grates. All ended in pipes
through which a cat could not have passed without reducing
its whiskers.

At last the twenty-six children assembled on the fourth-floor landing.

"Could he come through a window?" asked Dorothy.

"They're locked in winter," said Ernest.

"The fire escape?"

"The Lease says nobody can use it except in case
of fire."

"Santa Claus might say Christmas was a fire."

"He wouldn't tell a lie."

"Maybe the elevator shaft," said Roderick.

This appeared promising until they remembered the elevator ran all
night on Christmas Eve for the convenience of late diners,
theatre parties, and gentlemen who had forgotten their latchkeys. Even
Santa Claus could scarcely descend a shaft while an elevator
car was rising through him.

"The front door?" whispered Lottie.

They all looked toward the entrance.

At that very moment a messenger boy attempted to cross
the marble floor without presenting his package for inspection. The
hall man stopped him with one white-gloved hand. There followed
questions, counter-questions, references, suspicions, and a demand that the recipient
be summoned by speaking tube. The messenger boy waited seven
minutes and used language which caused Dorothy to cover Lottie's
ears.

The children turned away.

Hope lay strangled!

There remained one authority to consult: the Telephone Boy.

The Telephone Boy lived somewhere beneath the basement, among boilers,
batteries, coils of wire, discarded trunks, and odors of cabbage
proceeding from the servants' dining-room. He was thirteen, very thin,
and knew everything. He knew which cook was leaving, which
father came home latest, which tenants owed the butcher, and
what the janitor said after accidentally stepping into the elevator
shaft when the car was on the sixth floor.

The janitor had fallen only as far as the basement
net, but he had expressed himself during the journey.

The Telephone Boy also knew which wires to connect when
a lady wished to speak to the grocer and which
to connect when she wished to speak to her husband's
office. These were not always the same wires, but no
one could have done more with the information available.

They found him on a stool before the switchboard, eating
a dry roll.

"Telephone Boy," said Ernest solemnly, "can Santa Claus get into
the Santa Maria?"

The boy stopped chewing.

"Why wouldn't he?"

"There are no chimneys."

"He could come through the doors."

"The hall man won't admit strangers."

"He might know him."

"The hall man says he don't know nobody without a
card."

The Telephone Boy considered. Though he lived near the coal
bins and wore trousers too short for him, he possessed
the judicial manner of a Supreme Court.

"What about packages?"

"They have to be examined."

"Windows?"

"Locked."

"Fire escape?"

"Against the Lease."

"Elevator shaft?"

"Elevator runs all night."

The Telephone Boy tightened his belt by one hole. He
did this often when his supper had been small.

"Then," he said, "I guess he can't."

Dear, dear! It is one thing to fear misfortune in
the private recesses of your own heart, where it may
perhaps be chased away by cake or a new picture
book. It is another thing to hear that misfortune confirmed
by a Person Who Knows.

The twenty-six children stared at him.

"You mean," said Dorothy, "there won't be any Christmas?"

"Not unless he finds a way."

"But Santa Claus always finds a way," said Roderick, rather
faintly.

"Maybe," replied the Telephone Boy.

That "maybe" was the blackest word ever spoken in the
basement of the Santa Maria.

From that hour the children altered.

They ceased racing to the elevator. They did not quarrel
over the window seat. They did not drop marbles down
the speaking tubes or tie the Pomeroys' doorbell to the
brass umbrella stand. They walked softly, spoke gently, and looked
with mournful eyes at every fireplace they passed.

Their parents were delighted.

"The building is really becoming civilized," said Mrs. Bell.

"Ernest has developed self-control," said Mrs. Willett.

"Roderick has not broken anything for a week," said Mr.
May, lowering his newspaper in astonishment.

The janitor alone was suspicious. He had known children for
twenty years and understood that silence in a building containing
twenty-six of them is not peace but preparation. He examined
the water pipes, the elevator cables, and the coal chutes.
Finding no dynamite, he grew gloomier than ever.

By the twentieth of December the children were pale.

Mrs. Phelps decided that Lottie had sewer gas and sent
for the doctor. The doctor looked at Lottie's tongue, listened
to her chest, prescribed a medicine of astonishing bitterness, and
charged three dollars. Lottie drank one spoonful, wept, and continued
to fear for Santa Claus with every organ pronounced perfectly
sound.

The grown people discussed the matter in drawing-rooms.

"It may be the weather."

"It may be too much schooling."

"It may be the rugs. Foreign rugs are full of
microbes."

"Perhaps children are becoming serious at an earlier age."

Nobody asked the children.

This is the advantage of being grown up: when a
thing is mysterious, you may explain it without knowing anything
about it.

On the afternoon before Christmas, Ernest, Dorothy, Roderick, and the
others held their final council.

The Telephone Boy attended by invitation. He had no family
in the building and expected no presents. He said he
did not care. Then he looked at the bright shop
windows across the street and tightened his belt again.

"We have tried every way," said Ernest. "Doors, windows, fireplaces,
elevator, fire escape, speaking tubes—"

"Santa couldn't come through a speaking tube," objected Roderick.

"Not with his pack," admitted Ernest. "So it is settled."

No one asked what was settled. They knew.

That evening the Santa Maria shone with electric lights. Carriages
stopped at the door. Ladies rustled through the vestibule carrying
flowers; gentlemen arrived with parcels which the hall man examined
as if each contained a revolution. From certain apartments came
the smells of roasting turkey, hot mince pies, and rich
sauces which travelled under doors with no respect whatever for
the Lease.

But the children went quietly to bed.

They hung stockings, because one must observe the forms of
hope even when hope has packed its trunk. Ernest hung
his upon the brass bar of the gas grate. Dorothy
pinned hers to a chair. Roderick, with determined optimism, hung
two.

The Telephone Boy hung nothing. He returned to his little
room beneath the telephone shaft, wrapped himself in an old
blanket, and said aloud that Christmas was mostly for babies.

Above the building the sky darkened.

The wind had been sulking all day behind the western
warehouses, muttering down alleys, flipping newspapers into gutters, and snatching
hats from respectable heads. At ten o'clock it began to
roar. At eleven it struck the Santa Maria with both
fists.

What a wind!

It whistled around the cornices with a hundred tongues. It
rushed down the avenue like a hungry wolf. It shook
windows, banged shutters, overturned ash barrels, and sent a tin
sign galloping past the entrance in a manner wholly inconsistent
with municipal regulations.

The mothers woke and thought of draughts.

The fathers woke and thought of insurance.

The janitor woke and thought of everything at once.

Then came a crash upon the roof.

The great skylight above the main stairway lifted, shuddered, and
sailed into the darkness like a glass-winged bird. A second
skylight tore from its frame. A third broke inward with
a splendid clatter, showering harmless fragments upon the top landing
and causing the Esthetic Lady to appear in a violet
dressing gown demanding to know whether the building was falling.

"Not yet, ma'am," said the janitor, and hurried upward with
a hammer, three boards, and no cheerful expectations.

All night the storm labored. It ripped and pulled, howled
and hammered. It rolled chimney pots along the roofs and
tossed clouds across the moon. The children heard it dimly,
but one by one they placed their hands in the
shadowy clasp of the Sandman and passed into dreams where
Santa Claus stood forever upon the pavement, arguing with the
hall man.

Near dawn the wind stopped.

The city lay under a soft white snow. The air
was still. The Santa Maria, deprived of its skylights and
possessing several new leaks, looked battered but dignified—like a duchess
who has been caught in a railway accident and does
not intend to discuss it.

At six o'clock Ernest opened his eyes.

Something glittered in the drawing-room.

He sat up. He listened. Then he ran—not walked, mind
you, though the Lease expressly forbade running—and burst through the
door.

The gas grate had disappeared behind holly. From its brass
bar hung a stocking so fat that the seams complained.
Beside it stood a little tree covered with silver stars,
red apples, gilded nuts, candles, and strings of popcorn. Beneath
the tree were boxes. Large boxes. Small boxes. Round boxes.
Boxes tied with blue ribbon, boxes tied with red ribbon,
boxes that rattled, boxes that squeaked, and one magnificent box
from which came the unmistakable breathing of a puppy.

At the same moment twenty-five other doors flew open.

The Santa Maria exploded.

Children shouted from every floor. Elevator bells rang. Fathers came
forth in dressing gowns; mothers appeared with their hair in
states that would have ruined them socially had any other
mothers not looked precisely the same.

"He came!"

"Santa came!"

"Look at my train!"

"A doll!"

"Skates!"

"Candy!"

"A drum!"

"Two drums!"

The Lease was forgotten. The children ran—not only ran, but
jumped, yelled, slid, pounded, dragged carts through halls, and blew
horns directly beside the Esthetic Lady's door. Universal brotherhood prevailed
from cellar to roof.

Ernest, carrying a trumpet in one hand and a wooden
sword in the other, met Dorothy beneath the broken skylight.

They looked up.

Through the empty frame shone the pale Christmas sky. A
scrap of red wool clung to one jagged corner.

Dorothy pointed.

"The skylight!"

Ernest's eyes widened.

"Of course! The wind opened it for him!"

Roderick arrived pulling a sled.

"I told you he'd find a way."

"You said maybe he could make himself thin."

"Well, perhaps he did that too."

The twenty-six children gathered under the opening and gazed upward
in triumph. The problem was solved. Modern architecture, guarded doors,
gas grates, elevator regulations, polished brass, and the Herodian pronunciamento
of the Lease had all been defeated by one old
gentleman, eight reindeer, and a windstorm.

But what of the Telephone Boy?

Ah, you thought he had been forgotten!

You are mistaken.

At half past six he awoke because something tickled his
nose. It was the tassel of a red stocking hanging
from the pipe above his cot. The stocking contained oranges,
nuts, a knife with four blades, a pair of warm
gloves, a silver watch, and enough candy to make him
distrust food for several hours.

At the foot of the cot stood new boots.

Upon the stool lay a coat.

And beside the switchboard was a basket containing turkey, rolls,
mince pies, jam, sausages, and a card upon which twenty-six
names had been written in twenty-six different manners.

The boy stared at these things. Then he looked up
the long telephone shaft.

Far above him a narrow skylight stood open to the
morning.

"I'll be switched!" he said softly.

He did not tighten his belt once that day.

By noon the Santa Maria had surrendered completely to Christmas.
The hall man wore a paper crown. The elevator boy
carried children up and down without asking which floor. The
Esthetic Lady allowed a tin locomotive to pass over her
Persian runner. Mrs. Willett forgot Mrs. Pomeroy's gown. Mr. May
crawled under a table to repair a mechanical bear and
remained there through luncheon.

Even the Lease lay silent in the agent's office, which
was the best thing it had ever done.

Only the janitor remained gloomy.

He had been awake all night. Three skylights were gone;
two water pipes had frozen; the top-floor carpet was soaked;
the elevator had developed a cough; and somebody had rolled
a new red wagon over his foot. He moved through
the rejoicing with a ladder on his shoulder and an
expression fit for a funeral.

Then Kara Johnson stopped him.

Kara was seven, small and serious, with two brown braids.
She carried a parcel wrapped in newspaper and tied with
green yarn.

"This is for you," she said.

"For me?" asked the janitor.

"Nobody else."

He set down the ladder and opened it.

Inside was a necktie Kara had made herself from a
strip of purple cloth. It was crooked. It was too
wide at one end and too narrow at the other.
Across it she had embroidered, in yellow thread, a row
of objects intended to be stars but resembling fried eggs.

The janitor held it up.

"I made it," said Kara. "Because you take care of
the building. And because you fell down the elevator shaft."

The janitor looked at the tie. He looked at Kara.
He looked at the twenty-five other children crowded behind her,
all waiting.

Then an extraordinary thing happened.

The janitor smiled.

It began doubtfully at one corner of his mouth, as
if unused to the journey. It travelled across his face,
pushed the wrinkles aside, and shone out so broadly that
the hall man dropped a parcel in astonishment.

The janitor put on the tie at once.

It hung sideways. The embroidered stars blazed upon his shirtfront.
No duke wearing a decoration from his sovereign ever bore
himself more proudly.

That evening the children composed a poem. It had six
verses, though only the final one need trouble us:

> When doors are shut and chimneys small,
> And watchmen guard the marble hall,
> The Christmas wind blows fierce and bright
> And opens up the roof at night.

The meter was not perfect, but neither was the Santa
Maria, especially after the storm.

And if you should ever live in a Very Fine
Building where the rules are many, the fireplaces narrow, and
the hall man suspicious, do not despair. Hang up your
stocking. Go to sleep. Trust Santa Claus.

For there are more ways into a house than an
agent can put into a Lease, and Christmas—bless it!—has never
yet been stopped by polished brass.

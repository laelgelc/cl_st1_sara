## The Court Beyond the Barricade

It was in the autumn of that memorable year when
the bells of London had grown hoarse with tolling, and
when every respectable citizen who possessed either a horse, a
country cousin, or a sound pair of heels had prudently
removed his person from the metropolis, that two mariners belonging
to the *Saucy Wench*, a merchant brig then lying below
the bridge, found themselves at midnight in the tap-room of
the Crooked Bishop.

The taller of the pair—who, from the prodigious extension of
his lower members, had acquired among his companions the concise
and appropriate appellation of Legs—was an object which no reasonable
observer could have contemplated without some astonishment. He stood six
feet seven inches in his stockings; yet Nature, having expended
herself upon his altitude, had neglected almost every other dimension.
His knees were knots upon ropes; his elbows appeared contrivances
for hanging garments; his shoulders projected like the broken yards
of a dismantled vessel; and his head, which was narrow,
lantern-jawed, and furnished with a melancholy quantity of reddish hair,
nodded perpetually at the summit of his person, as a
signal-lamp nods in a gale.

Hugh Tarpaulin, on the contrary, had been built upon principles
of economy in height and prodigality in circumference. He was
four feet nine, perhaps four feet ten when indignation elevated
him; but across the beam he possessed the dimensions of
a small beer-cask. His cheeks, naturally round, had been improved
by liquor into globes of a rich Tyrian purple; his
eyes rolled in their sockets with the lively independence of
two peas upon a trencher; and his mouth, which extended
nearly from ear to ear, seemed expressly constructed for drinking,
singing, laughing, swearing, and the consumption of salt beef in
unequal proportions. If Legs resembled a mast, Hugh resembled its
buoy; if one was a compass-needle, the other was the
compass-box; and if either had fallen overboard, the first must
inevitably have gone down by the head, while the second
could hardly have been persuaded to sink at all.

These worthy persons had, during the earlier part of the
evening, applied themselves to the serious extermination of several measures
of ale, three black-jacks of spiced wine, a flask which
the hostess affirmed to be Spanish, and a smaller bottle
which no one affirmed to be anything. Having reached that
agreeable elevation of spirit at which a man considers his
purse an impertinence and his creditor a personal enemy, they
called for a final quart, drank it with a gravity
becoming the ceremony, and proceeded toward the door.

"Hold!" cried Mistress Gudgeon, proprietress of the Crooked Bishop, placing
before the exit a person as broad, if not quite
so spherical, as Tarpaulin's own. "Hold, you bilge-fed sons of
a rotten galley! Who pays?"

Legs gazed down upon her from his eminence.

"Madam," said he, with a mournful dignity, "the question is
improperly timed."

"Improperly timed?"

"Entirely. Payment is a morning operation. We are now engaged
in midnight."

"You are engaged in robbery!" screamed Mistress Gudgeon.

Hugh put his thumb to his nose and expanded his
fingers in the manner of a fan.

"Put it in the ship's account," said he. "Charge it
to wind, weather, and His Majesty's fishes."

"I'll charge it to your hides!"

She snatched up a pewter measure and launched it with
such precision that it passed through the interval between Legs's
knees and inflicted a considerable insult upon a gentleman sleeping
beneath the table. The gentleman arose with a howl. The
hostess shouted "Thieves!" Legs overturned a bench. Hugh seized a
cold pudding for ballast. Two drawers, a fiddler, the insulted
sleeper, and a dog with no apparent interest in finance
joined the pursuit; and our heroes, issuing into the night
with that suddenness peculiar to men who discover that an
account has been kept, ran eastward through a confusion of
lanes.

Now, to say the truth, neither sailor could have explained
why he ran eastward rather than westward, northward, upward, or
directly into the Thames. Legs maintained afterward that he steered
by a certain star; but the star in question was
a swinging lantern, and his course described so many triangles
that no geometer would have admitted it as navigation. Hugh
followed because Hugh's legs, once excited, were incapable of immediate
deliberation.

Behind them came Mistress Gudgeon, crying murder; behind Mistress Gudgeon
came the drawers; behind the drawers came the fiddler; and
behind all came the dog, who had now decided that
the whole business was political.

Onward went the mariners—down Fishbone Alley, across Saint Mortimer's Close,
through a court in which seven cats held parliament, and
into a street so narrow that Legs, swaying from side
to side, polished both walls with his shoulders. At the
farther end rose a barricade of timber, paving stones, broken
carts, and household furniture, crowned at intervals by spikes. Upon
it hung a board bearing, in letters legible even to
sobriety:

**PESTILENCE WITHIN.
BY ORDER OF THE LORD MAYOR,
NO MAN SHALL ENTER OR DEPART
ON PAIN OF DEATH.**

A red cross, newly painted, sprawled beneath the proclamation.

Hugh stopped.

"Legs," said he, "there's a lee shore."

"There's an elevation," replied Legs, contemplating the obstacle with one
eye. "We ascend."

"Did you read the cargo manifest?"

"I never read when pursued. It confuses the judgment."

Mistress Gudgeon appeared at the mouth of the street, brandishing
a ladle. Her reinforcement clattered behind her.

The barricade, viewed under these circumstances, lost much of its
objectionable character. Legs mounted it in three long motions, resembling
a spider climbing a cupboard. Tarpaulin scrambled after him, assisted
from below by the point of the landlady's ladle and
from above by a grip upon his collar. The timbers
groaned; a chair collapsed; Hugh rolled over the summit; Legs
toppled after; and both descended upon the forbidden side amid
a cataract of stones, crockery, and one wooden image of
Saint Giles.

Their pursuers came no farther. Mistress Gudgeon, though courageous in
matters of credit, entertained orthodox opinions respecting contagion. She stood
beyond the barrier, poured forth a final broadside of maledictions,
and retired with her fleet, leaving the dog to bark
twice at the proclamation and then follow more sensible company.

The sailors rose.

Before them lay a district upon which silence had settled,
not as snow settles, lightly and with purity, but as
a damp cloth is thrown over the face of a
corpse. No lamp burned in any casement. The moon, half
strangled in a yellow vapour, revealed rows of leaning houses
whose upper stories approached one another like conspirators. Doors hung
open; shutters beat intermittently against walls; weeds pierced the paving;
and from the gutters arose an odour compounded of wet
straw, extinguished fires, spoiled victuals, old medicines, and that final
corruption which even drunkenness recognizes, though it may decline to
name it.

This part of London had been closed for fourteen days.

The sickness, which began modestly in a cellar and afterward
displayed all the ambition of a conqueror, had passed from
house to house with such celerity that men who breakfasted
in tolerable health were measured for winding-sheets before supper. Physicians
disputed concerning vapours, conjunctions, divine displeasure, subterranean exhalations, and the
excessive consumption of onions; but the plague, unmoved by argument,
continued its rounds. Nurses fled from patients, priests from parishes,
heirs from benefactors. Whole families disappeared behind doors marked with
the red cross. Carts went groaning through the streets at
dawn, collecting their melancholy freight. When drivers themselves fell dead,
the horses wandered unattended until they overturned among the pits.

At length the authorities, finding every measure ineffectual except the
measure which placed danger at a convenient distance, enclosed the
most afflicted quarter with barricades. The healthy were kept out,
the sick were kept in, and civic wisdom congratulated itself
upon having separated misery from observation.

Into this region, then, Legs and Hugh Tarpaulin had entered;
and I think it no injustice to their understanding to
state that several minutes passed before they perceived any alteration
in the general character of the night.

Hugh recovered the pudding, which had survived the descent in
a flattened condition, and tucked it beneath his arm.

"Quiet anchorage," he observed.

"Too quiet," replied Legs.

"Every berth shut."

"Maybe the watch is asleep."

"Then we'll not wake him."

They proceeded.

At the first crossing they encountered an abandoned handcart containing
three covered shapes. Tarpaulin lifted a corner of the cloth,
looked beneath it, replaced it, and touched his cap.

"Passengers," said he. "All bound one way."

Farther on, a corpse sat upright against a pump, as
though waiting for someone to bring a cup. A bundle
lay in a doorway and began to cough; but when
Legs bent over it, the coughing ceased. From an upper
window came a low laugh—whether human, mechanical, or merely the
wind contending with a hinge, our heroes did not determine.
They increased their pace, though each afterward denied having done
so.

The vapour thickened. It crawled around their ankles, entered cellars,
issued from drains, and collected in the hollows of the
street. Once Hugh stepped upon something soft and round. It
burst with a melancholy sigh. He swore that it was
a bladder; Legs, who had seen the tuft of hair
attached to it, preserved a charitable silence.

"Legs," said Hugh presently, "this cruise lacks society."

"Our late society was too attentive."

"A man wants moderation. I dislike a street where everybody
asks for money, but I dislike one where nobody asks
for anything. It has an uncommercial air."

They turned into Bell-and-Bone Lane, where the houses had suffered
grievously from neglect. Beams projected like broken ribs; roofs sagged;
plaster had fallen away in leprous patches. A sign representing
an angel with a shovel swung overhead, and beneath it
stood an undertaker's establishment. The windows were boarded; the door
appeared shut; yet from within came a glow, a murmur
of voices, and—most persuasive of all to the nautical intellect—the
clinking of vessels.

Hugh raised a finger.

"Cargo shifting."

"Glass cargo," said Legs.

"Liquid cargo."

"Contraband, perhaps."

"Then it needs honest inspection."

They approached. The door, when pushed, yielded with a long
complaint; and our friends entered a narrow passage lined on
either side by coffins standing upright. Some were plain, some
richly nailed, some no larger than fiddle cases. A black
cloth brushed Hugh's face. Legs struck his forehead against the
crossed bones of a funeral hatchment and brought down a
shower of dust, two wooden cherubs, and a dead rat
of venerable appearance.

At the passage end was a second door, from behind
which issued a voice of great magnificence:

"—and therefore, beloved sister, the purple vintage of the southern
province must be reserved for our anatomical experiments, while the
brown ale of the northern ward may properly be surrendered
to the household."

"Hear!" cried several voices.

"Anatomy," whispered Hugh. "That's drinking with long words."

He opened the door.

The apartment beyond had once served for the display and
preparation of funerary merchandise, but it had lately been arranged
with an originality which merits particular description. In the centre
stood a long table composed of coffin lids laid across
trestles. Upon it smoked an enormous bowl of punch, flanked
by bottles, stone jars, flagons, onions, candles, dice, cold meats,
and two human skulls employed as drinking cups. Coffins, placed
horizontally, served as seats; others, turned upright, formed cupboards. A
funeral pall had been stretched against one wall as a
tapestry, while wreaths, hatchments, shrouds, and little leaden plates bearing
the names of deceased persons constituted the remaining decorations.

From a beam depended a complete skeleton. A candle had
been fixed within its skull, and the flame, shining through
the eye-holes and chattering teeth, bestowed upon the entire room
that fitful and ghastly illumination which persons of taste prefer
at an entertainment devoted to mortality.

Around the table sat six individuals.

This company I will endeavour to delineate one by one;
for it is in the accurate inventory of wonders that
the conscientious historian differs from the ordinary liar.

At the head sat a man whose stature, though inferior
to that of Legs, was rendered imposing by the prodigious
elevation of his forehead. This forehead rose above his eyebrows
in successive polished terraces, until it vanished beneath a crown
manufactured from a brass warming-pan. His face below was narrow
and blue; his eyes were sunk deeply; and his mouth,
being both thin and habitually pursed, suggested that he had
spent his life whistling at funerals. He wore a black
velvet actor's doublet, a scarlet petticoat over one shoulder by
way of mantle, and about his neck a chain composed
of coffin handles. In his right hand he carried a
sceptre fashioned from a thigh-bone, at the top of which
had been attached a pewter knob.

At his side reclined a lady of uncommon amplitude. Her
body, inflated by either dropsy, beer, upholstery, or a coalition
of the three, spread across two coffins and threatened a
third. She wore a widow's veil, a stomacher embroidered with
silver skulls, and a necklace of small apothecaries' bottles. Her
cheeks were dead white; her lips were vermilion; and her
single visible eye regarded the world with the moist severity
of an oyster. Upon her head sat a coronet of
horses' teeth. This was the queen.

Opposite her was a gentleman composed chiefly of nose. That
organ descended, purple and mountainous, over his mouth, forcing his
words to emerge from either side in separate currents. He
was clad from chin to heel in a child's coffin,
through which holes had been cut for his head, arms,
and legs; and he sat so stiffly that whenever he
desired drink he was obliged to topple forward until his
nose entered the bowl. His title, as we later learned,
was His Grace the Archduke Pest-Ulent.

Beside the archduke perched a thin young woman with a
neck like a heron and ears of such dimensions that
they seemed less parts of her head than handles by
which the head might be carried away. Her gown was
made of stitched winding-sheets. A string of little bells encircled
her waist, tinkling at every breath; and a pair of
spectacles without glass rested on the point of her chin.
She was Princess Ana-Pest, patroness of fevers, fainting-fits, and devotional
music.

The fifth personage was a small old man, yellow, wrinkled,
and bent nearly double by gout. His feet were wrapped
in funeral cushions, his hands trembled, and his lower jaw
moved without interruption, though no sound proceeded from it except
at irregular intervals, when he barked like a fox. A
mitre constructed from folded death notices adorned his head. This
ecclesiastic was the Grand Pestifer, spiritual adviser to the crown.

The sixth and last was a youth—or perhaps a very
old woman; the matter remains in controversy—whose face was red
on one side and green on the other. One eye
stared upward, the second inward. His or her limbs performed
small independent dances beneath a coat of hearse-plumes, and from
the shoulders projected two black boards inscribed "AGED 19" and
"DIED SUDDENLY." This creature was Tem-Pest, royal infant and presumptive
heir.

When Legs and Tarpaulin entered, the six turned toward them
with one motion, save Tem-Pest, whose head turned first, whose
eyes followed separately, and whose body completed the manoeuvre after
an interval.

There ensued a silence.

Hugh removed the crushed pudding from beneath his arm, placed
it politely upon the table, and looked into the punch
bowl.

"Evening," said he.

The man in the warming-pan crown rose. His height increased
by reason of the forehead until the crown scraped the
skeleton's toes.

"Profane and inexplicable intruders!" he began. "By what negligence of
our sentinels, what audacity of your own degraded natures, or
what especial interposition of the universal monarch whom we serve,
have you penetrated the sacred privacy of our court?"

Legs glanced over his shoulder at the empty passage.

"Sentinels asleep," he said.

"Treason!" cried the queen.

"Treason!" squeaked Ana-Pest.

"Treason!" barked the Grand Pestifer.

"Treason!" shouted the archduke, one half of the word issuing
from each side of his nose.

Tem-Pest clapped and fell beneath the table.

"Peace!" commanded the crowned man, striking the thigh-bone upon a
skull. "We are King Pest the First—sovereign of vapours, prince
of pustules, lord of the lazar-house, hereditary duke of decomposition,
and by the especial favour of Death, emperor of this
abandoned ward. The majestic lady at our right is our
serene consort, Queen Pest-Iferous. The remainder, as their physiognomies should
have informed even minds pickled in salt water, constitute our
august family."

Tarpaulin bowed to the punch.

"Very august," said he. "Especially the old bottle with the
nose."

The archduke attempted to rise. His coffin caught against the
table, and he fell backward with his feet in the
air.

"Treason!" cried the family once more.

Legs placed one long hand upon Hugh's shoulder.

"Mind your tongue," he said. "We are in foreign waters."

"We are in an undertaker's back room."

"Same thing, often."

King Pest resumed his seat, arranging his petticoat-mantle with dignity.

"Since it is evident," said he, "that ignorance rather than
diplomacy has brought you before our throne, we shall require
your names, conditions, nationalities, purposes, opinions respecting mortality, and the
precise cubic capacity of your stomachs."

"Legs," said Legs.

"Hugh Tarpaulin," said Hugh. "Able-bodied seaman, except in the morning."

"Your purpose?"

"Seeking the ship."

"Your opinions respecting mortality?"

"Bad for business."

"And the cubic capacity of your stomach?"

Hugh considered.

"That depends on the liquor. Ale rides light. Wine goes
lower. Brandy can be stowed in odd corners. Water I
never take aboard unless the hold is afire."

The royal family nodded gravely, as though this answer displayed
considerable science.

King Pest then turned toward Legs.

"And you?"

"My hold," replied the tall sailor, "is deep but narrow.
I take cargo by the glass, not the flood."

The queen whispered in her husband's ear, though, owing to
the amplitude of her person, the operation resembled a feather-bed
rolling against a poker. The king listened; the Grand Pestifer
barked; Ana-Pest jingled; the archduke recovered his vertical position; and
Tem-Pest crawled from beneath the table with a marrow-bone in
its mouth.

"Mariners," said King Pest at length, "your arrival, though irregular,
may not be wholly unpropitious. Know that this court is
assembled for purposes at once philosophical, medicinal, sacramental, and fiscal.
When the timid inhabitants deserted these streets, they abandoned cellars
whose contents, if neglected, would inevitably fall into decay. We,
who despise vulgar fear, have dedicated ourselves to a solemn
examination of those alcoholic treasures. Each cask is tasted, classified,
debated, and, where necessary, consumed, in honour of Death, our
overlord."

"Public service," murmured Hugh.

"Of the loftiest description. We have investigated seven taverns, four
monasteries, two private collections, the buttery of a bishop, and
the medicine chest of a deceased surgeon. The sacrifices have
been severe. His Grace the archduke lost consciousness in a
barrel of malmsey. Our royal infant has not spoken intelligibly
since Tuesday. Her Majesty has expanded three inches in the
service of science."

The queen laid a hand upon her bosom and shed
a tear into a skull.

"Nevertheless," continued the king, "our work proceeds. Authority, however, must
be maintained. You have burst without announcement into the imperial
presence; you have failed to kneel; you have introduced a
pudding of republican appearance; and you have referred to our
blood relation as an old bottle."

"The nose misled me," said Hugh.

"Silence! For these offences the law prescribes death."

"Harsh anchorage," observed Legs.

"Yet our clemency is commensurate with our power. Each offender
shall be permitted a choice. Before the great clock in
Saint Gaspard's tower strikes one, he shall drink, without rest
or spillage, one gallon of our green usquebaugh. If he
accomplish the feat, he shall be pardoned, ennobled, and appointed
Inspector of Cellars. If he fails, he shall be immersed
head downward in that barrel of brown ale until the
obstinacy departs from his body."

The king pointed his sceptre toward an immense cask standing
near the wall. Its top had been removed. The liquid
within, black beneath the candlelight, trembled gently whenever the bells
on Ana-Pest's gown stirred.

Hugh's eyes brightened.

"One gallon each?"

"One."

"Is there more?"

The queen uttered a cachinnatory squeal. The archduke thumped his
coffin. Even the Grand Pestifer ceased chewing his own jaw
long enough to grin.

Legs, however, regarded the green liquor, which had been brought
forward in two funeral jars, with profound dissatisfaction. It smoked
faintly and had already begun to remove the varnish from
the table.

"No," said he.

The court became still.

King Pest leaned forward. "No?"

"No gallon."

"Do you reject our mercy?"

"I reject the cargo. That stuff would eat the copper
off a ship's bottom."

"It is the royal usquebaugh."

"It is bilge-water with ambition."

The queen gasped so violently that one of her apothecary
bottles entered her mouth. Ana-Pest beat her ears with both
hands. The archduke's nose deepened from purple to black.

Hugh, meanwhile, had been studying the king. He moved to
the left. The king turned away. Hugh moved to the
right. The king drew his mantle over his face. Tarpaulin
placed both fists upon his hips.

"Sink me," he said slowly, "if I don't know that
forehead."

King Pest struck the table.

"Guards!"

No guards appeared.

"I've seen it over greasepaint," continued Hugh. "I've seen it
stabbed by a Moor, poisoned by a duke, crowned by
a ghost, and chased round a stage by a man
dressed as a bear."

"Treason!" said Ana-Pest, but without confidence.

"You're Tim Hurlygurly," cried Hugh, "the playhouse fellow from the
Red Cockerel! You played a dead king last winter and
sneezed in the coffin. I threw an onion."

The effect of this disclosure was immediate and remarkable.

The king's face collapsed beneath his forehead. The queen looked
at him; the archduke looked at the queen; Ana-Pest looked
at everybody; the Grand Pestifer barked with what sounded suspiciously
like laughter. Tem-Pest climbed onto a coffin and began chanting,
"Tim! Tim! Tim!" while striking a skull with the marrow-bone.

"Seize the slanderer!" roared the monarch.

"Actor!" shouted Hugh. "Rogue! Cellar-thief! His Majesty of the Warming-Pan!"

"Treason!" screamed the entire court.

The queen, being nearest to Tarpaulin and endowed with an
enthusiasm disproportionate even to her bulk, launched herself from her
coffins. For one majestic instant she sailed through the ghastly
light, veil streaming, bottles rattling, coronet flashing. She struck Hugh
amidships. The two revolved together, demolished a trestle, rebounded from
the wall, and rolled toward the open ale barrel.

Hugh caught the rim.

The queen sat upon his fingers.

He vanished into the cask with a sound resembling a
church organ swallowing a boot.

Legs made no speech. His countenance, ordinarily mournful, assumed that
complete vacancy which in certain seamen precedes tempestuous action. He
reached across the table, took King Pest by the coffin-handle
chain, lifted him bodily, and deposited him headfirst through an
open trapdoor leading to the cellar.

The king's descent was accompanied by three crashes, an oath
of theatrical excellence, and the remote shattering of bottles.

The court rose.

The archduke advanced rigidly, using his nose as a ram.
Legs stepped aside. His Grace passed between the tall sailor's
knees and entered the passage, where his coffin stuck crosswise
between the upright coffins. Ana-Pest rushed forward, bells jangling, and
attempted to bite Legs in the thigh; but his thigh
was at a height inconvenient to her, and she secured
only a portion of his coat. The Grand Pestifer crawled
beneath the table and attacked Legs's ankles with the thigh-bone
sceptre. Tem-Pest climbed the suspended skeleton and shrieked encouragement from
its ribs.

Queen Pest-Iferous, having disposed of Hugh, returned with her veil
wrapped around one eye and a carving knife in either
hand.

Legs looked upward.

The skeleton looked downward.

"Lend us a hand, mate," said the sailor.

With one pull he tore the skeleton from its hook.

There are weapons whose use is dignified by antiquity—the sword,
the pike, the bow, the battle-axe. There are others whose
novelty supplies whatever grandeur tradition has denied them. Legs's employment
of a complete human skeleton partook of both descriptions. Grasping
it by the spinal column, he swept it around his
head with a whistling of bones. The feet struck Ana-Pest
and sent her jingling into a stack of child coffins.
One arm detached itself, crossed the room, and extinguished a
candle. The skull collided with the Grand Pestifer's mitre, driving
the old man backward into the punch bowl. A second
revolution removed the queen's knives and a quantity of her
veil.

Tem-Pest, deprived of its perch, clung to the skeleton's pelvis
and described wide circles through the air, crying, "Majesty! Majesty!"
at every turn.

The room became a theatre of comprehensive disorder.

The archduke, still wedged in the passage, kicked his legs
with great energy and caused the upright coffins to fall
one after another, each knocking down its neighbour, until the
entire corridor collapsed with a solemn succession of booms. Ana-Pest
emerged from the little coffins wearing three of them upon
her arms and head. The Grand Pestifer lifted his face
from the punch, barked bubbles, and disappeared again. Queen Pest-Iferous
seized the funeral pall and cast it over Legs, who
for several seconds continued to swing the skeleton beneath a
black canopy, looking less like a combatant than an unusually
violent tent.

From the barrel came a muffled pounding.

"Hugh!" cried Legs.

The pounding became furious.

He threw aside the pall, released the skeleton, and sprang
toward the cask. The queen seized his coat-tails. Ana-Pest seized
the queen. Tem-Pest seized Ana-Pest's winding-sheet. The Grand Pestifer, rising
blind and dripping, seized Tem-Pest. Thus connected in a chain
of nobility, they were dragged across the floor behind Legs
until he reached the barrel.

The cask was immensely heavy. Legs bent his knees, braced
his back, and pulled.

Nothing happened.

The queen bit him.

He pulled again.

The barrel tilted an inch.

A deep and aqueous voice from within said, "Heave!"

Legs heaved.

The barrel rose upon its rim, balanced there with deliberation,
and overturned.

No ordinary account can adequately represent the consequences. The brown
ale came forth not as a beverage but as a
liberation. It struck the table, lifted the coffin lids from
their trestles, swept bottles, skulls, onions, dice, meat, candles, and
royal paraphernalia into one advancing wave, and spread to every
quarter of the room. Hugh emerged in the van, seated
upright, purple-faced, and blowing foam from his nostrils like a
marine deity of low reputation.

The queen, still attached to Legs's coat, received the first
force of the inundation and was floated backward upon a
coffin lid. Ana-Pest climbed upon her and rang violently. Tem-Pest
rode past in the punch bowl. The Grand Pestifer disappeared
beneath a wreath inscribed "Our Loss Is Heaven's Gain." The
archduke, freed at last from the passage by the pressure
of the liquid, shot forward in his coffin garment with
the velocity of a launched boat and struck the opposite
wall nose first.

Meanwhile the flood poured through the open trapdoor.

From below arose the voice of King Pest.

"Help! Assistance! The monarchy is drowning!"

A succession of empty casks, disturbed by the cataract, rolled
in the cellar. The king cried out once, twice, and
then emerged through the trapdoor borne upon a rising fermentation
of ale, wine, broken bottles, and deceased rats. His warming-pan
crown was gone; his petticoat mantle covered his head; and
his thigh-bone sceptre, floating beside him, struck him repeatedly upon
the forehead as if performing a constitutional rebuke.

"Treason!" he spluttered.

"Treason!" answered the court from various points of wreckage.

"Treason!" echoed Hugh, who had found the word attractive.

The remaining candles hissed out. Only the flame in the
skeleton's skull continued to burn, the skull having lodged upon
a shelf above the flood. Its illumination now discovered a
scene which, for grandeur of catastrophe and meanness of materials,
has perhaps never been surpassed.

The table had collapsed. Coffins sailed in squadrons. The queen
revolved slowly upon her lid, using a carving knife as
an oar. Ana-Pest clung to the queen's coronet by her
teeth. The archduke lay upon his back, his coffin full
of ale, his great nose projecting above the surface like
a promontory at high tide. The Grand Pestifer barked from
inside a cupboard. Tem-Pest, entirely delighted, navigated the punch bowl
around the room by paddling with two dead men's nameplates.
King Pest attempted to mount the trestles, but each time
he rose, Legs pushed him down with the skeleton's detached
arm.

Hugh climbed to his feet.

His clothes streamed. His cheeks had lost none of their
colour. He opened his mouth, exhaled a quantity of ale,
and said:

"Wasteful."

"Can you walk?" asked Legs.

"Can I walk? I've taken a gallon outside and half
a barrel inside. I'm ballasted to the eyebrows."

The queen's coffin lid collided with him. She caught him
by the neck.

"Assassin!" she cried.

Hugh contemplated her single visible eye, her horses' teeth, her
dripping necklace, and the amplitude with which Providence had equipped
her.

"Widow?" he inquired.

"Queen!"

"Near enough."

He put one arm around her waist—or around that portion
of it which could be located—and dragged her toward the
passage.

Legs, not to be outdone in gallantry, lifted Ana-Pest from
the wreckage. The princess shrieked, jingled, kicked him in the
ribs, and called upon plague, heaven, hell, and the city
constables.

"Quiet," said Legs. "You're rescued."

"From what?"

He looked around the room.

"From society."

King Pest perceived their intention and rose waist-deep in the
flood.

"Villains! Pirates! You dare abduct the sacred women of our
house?"

Hugh adjusted the queen beneath his arm as a porter
adjusts a parcel.

"Salvage," said he. "Law of the sea."

"This is not the sea!"

Hugh pointed to the floating coffins.

"It's improving."

The sailors made for the door. The passage was blocked
by fallen merchandise, but Legs, using his princess as a
counterweight, kicked a path through it. Hugh followed with the
queen, who alternately demanded release and instructed him to mind
her bottles. Behind them the court attempted pursuit. The archduke
could not bend. The Grand Pestifer could not see. Tem-Pest
refused to leave the punch bowl. King Pest mounted a
coffin, proclaimed himself admiral, and immediately capsized.

Our heroes emerged into Bell-and-Bone Lane.

The night air, foul as it was, seemed wholesome after
the vapours of the royal chamber. Dawn had begun to
dilute the eastern darkness with a pale green light. Somewhere
beyond the barricades a cart bell rang. Somewhere nearer, a
window opened and a voice prayed to be taken. The
sailors paused only long enough to determine the direction of
the river.

"This way," said Legs.

"That's north."

"It feels wet."

"Everything feels wet."

They set off, Legs striding with Ana-Pest over one shoulder,
Hugh rolling forward beneath Queen Pest-Iferous like a beetle transporting
a duchess. Behind them the undertaker's shop continued to emit
cries, splashes, proclamations, barks, and the recurrent chorus of "Treason!"

The plague streets offered no opposition. Death, who had guarded
them efficiently against sober men, had made no provision for
drunken sailors carrying counterfeit royalty. They passed the coughing bundle,
now silent; the corpse beside the pump; the handcart with
its covered passengers. Queen Pest-Iferous, sobered by motion, began to
give directions.

"Left at the saint without a head," she said. "Then
right by the burned apothecary. There is a breach near
the old bakehouse."

"You know the channel," said Hugh.

"I have used it before."

"To enter?"

"To bring in wine."

"Public service?"

"Of the loftiest description."

Ana-Pest, dangling down Legs's back, ceased struggling.

"Where are you taking us?" she demanded.

"To the *Saucy Wench*."

"What is that?"

"A brig."

"Is there drink?"

"Rum."

"Then walk faster."

They found the breach as the queen had promised—a gap
between a cartwheel and two beams. Legs passed through with
little difficulty, though Ana-Pest's ears caught momentarily on either side.
Hugh's passage presented a more serious geometrical problem. He pushed;
the queen complained; the barricade creaked. At last a rotten
bureau gave way, and sailor, sovereign, woodwork, and several stones
rolled together into the healthy portion of London.

A watchman standing nearby raised his lantern.

"Who goes there?"

Legs rose to his full altitude. The lantern illuminated his
gaunt, dripping figure, the princess's enormous ears, Hugh's purple face,
and the queen's white immensity crowned with horses' teeth.

The watchman dropped the lantern and fled.

"Proper respect," said Hugh.

The four continued toward the river through streets just beginning
to stir. Bakers' boys, apprentices, scavengers, early market women, and
two minor clerics witnessed their passage. Each received a different
explanation. To one they were ambassadors. To another, shipwrecked nobility.
To a third, a wedding party from Limehouse. Hugh told
a fishwife that the queen was an imported mermaid; Legs
informed a constable that Ana-Pest was a navigational instrument. The
constable, observing her ears, admitted that she might be.

At the wharf the tide was turning.

The *Saucy Wench* lay in the grey water, her spars
and rigging black against the whitening sky. A sleepy mate
leaned over the rail. When he saw Legs and Hugh
approaching with their acquisitions, he removed his cap, scratched his
head, and shouted:

"What cargo's that?"

"Royal freight!" called Hugh.

"Dutiable?"

"Not if we stow it quick."

A boat was lowered. The queen entered it with considerable
dignity and displacement; Ana-Pest followed willingly upon learning that the
rum was aboard. Legs took the oars. Hugh sat in
the stern, still holding the flattened pudding, which by some
miracle of devotion had remained in his possession throughout the
battle.

As they pulled away, the bells of London began their
morning tolling. The sound rolled over roofs and chimneys, over
barricades and burial pits, over the undertaker's ruined shop where
King Pest and his family still disputed the succession amid
floating coffins. It was a solemn sound, and the river
mist received it mournfully.

Hugh listened.

"Funeral?" he asked.

"Likely."

"Whose?"

Legs considered the great city, pallid beneath dawn, and dipped
his oars.

"Everybody's, sooner or later."

Hugh accepted this philosophy with a nod, opened the rescued
pudding, and offered a portion to the queen.

Thus did Legs and Hugh Tarpaulin escape the dominions of
Pest—owing their preservation not to prudence, valour, piety, medical wisdom,
or the protection of lawful authority, but to unpaid ale,
defective barricades, an actor's forehead, and the circumstance that a
skeleton, though ill-fitted for conversation, may prove an admirable weapon
when grasped by the spine.

### ALF THINKS ALOUD

We were fencing out past Southern Cross when Alfred O'Briar
came along with a swag that looked too heavy for
him and asked if there was any chance of a
job. We were mostly T'othersiders ourselves, and had knocked about
together from Bourke to the West, so we reckoned we
were a close and respectable camp—which meant that we borrowed
one another's tobacco without asking, fought over the frying-pan, and
distrusted every stranger until he had shouted.

Alf got the job because the boss was short of
hands, not because any of us took to him.

He was a little, stooping chap, with sandy whiskers, pale
eyes, and a vacant sort of grin that annoyed you
when there was nothing in particular to grin at. He
never told a yarn unless he was asked, and then
he spoiled it by stopping when he had finished. If
he sat quiet, we said he was sulky; if he
joined in, we said he was forcing himself on us.
If he knocked off with the rest, he was a
loafer; if he kept at it a few minutes longer,
we reckoned he was trying to crawl round the boss.

That was the kind of fair-minded crowd we were.

Still, Alf could graft. Also, he had two gifts that
should have made him popular, but somehow didn't. He could
mimic any man after hearing him speak half a dozen
words, and he could throw his voice anywhere—from inside a
flour-bag, under a bunk, or halfway up a gimlet gum.

The first time he tried it on us, we were
at tea. A voice came from the scrub behind the
cook's tent:

"Bill! Bill! Here's the boss!"

Bill dropped a pannikin, kicked the cards under a blanket,
and stood up looking innocent.

Then Alf gave his silly grin.

Bill said a man who played tricks like that would
get his head punched some day. But next night he
got Alf to imitate the boss for the amusement of
a teamster, and was offended because Alf wouldn't do it
twice.

There was an old hand we called the Oracle, on
account of his never giving an opinion till everybody else
had finished. He listened to Alf once, spat thoughtfully into
the fire, and said:

"I've heerd of such cases before."

That settled nothing, but it sounded as if it did.

Alf pitched his tent a little away from ours, behind
a patch of mulga. He kept it neat, and generally
went there after tea. We thought this pride. Then one
night Long Mitchell, who slept nearest, heard voices in Alf's
tent.

There was Alf's voice, and another man's, and, according to
Mitchell, a woman's.

Next morning Mitchell told us while Alf was at the
water-hole.

"A woman!" said Bill.

"So it sounded."

"In his tent?"

"So it sounded."

Bill looked towards the mulga with the expression of a
man whose family name had been brought into disgrace.

We questioned Mitchell, but he would not swear to the
woman. He said he had been half asleep. That night
we listened and heard nothing. The night after, Ned heard
a girl laughing there. On Sunday, while the rest of
us were playing euchre, two men heard an old woman
talking fast and an old man coughing.

By Monday the camp had a Case.

We could not agree whether Alf had one visitor or
several, how they reached the place without tracks, nor where
they went before daylight; but we agreed it was scandalous.
We had no married men with us, and no minister
within a hundred miles, yet we were very particular about
the reputation of the camp.

A mounted constable called for dinner that week. He was
a big, red Irishman named Cassidy, and Bill, who could
no more keep a secret than a sieve could hold
tea, hinted at mysterious goings-on.

Cassidy listened gravely.

"Ye want to be careful," he said. "A dacent camp's
a dacent camp, an' if there's anny female person concealed
upon the premises—"

"It's canvas," said Mitchell.

"Canvas or castle," said Cassidy, "the law makes no distinction
in morals. Speak to the man quietly. There may be
an innocent explanation, though I'm dashed if I can see
it."

The Oracle had been smoking beside the fire. Cassidy turned
to him.

"You've travelled. What d'ye make of it?"

The Oracle looked straight through the constable.

"Eh?"

"The woman in O'Briar's tent."

"What woman?"

Cassidy repeated it louder.

The Oracle cupped his ear.

"I'm gettin' deaf," he said.

After Cassidy rode away, Bill reckoned we ought to hold
a meeting. Mitchell reckoned we ought to mind our own
business. We held the meeting.

We decided that two men should watch Alf's tent, merely
in the interests of respectability. Mitchell was chosen because he
had first heard the voices, and I was chosen because
I objected.

It was a warm, still night. Alf had gone into
his tent early, carrying a candle and a bundle of
old letters. About nine, Mitchell and I crept through the
mulga and lay behind a log. I felt a fool,
and told Mitchell so.

"You look one," he whispered.

Presently Alf began muttering. Then there was silence. After that
we heard footsteps—or what sounded like footsteps—approaching from a long
way off.

A dog barked near the tent.

There wasn't a dog in camp.

A girl's voice cried, clear and eager:

"Mother! Mother! Here's Alfred coming up the track!"

Mitchell nudged me so hard I nearly called out.

An older woman answered from inside—or from beyond the tent;
it was impossible to tell.

"Alfred? Sure, Mary, don't be makin' game av me."

"It is him! I'd know the walk of him anywhere."

Then Alf's voice came, faint at first, like a man
calling from the road:

"Mary!"

"Alfred!"

There followed such a flutter of welcome that Mitchell raised
himself to look. I dragged him down.

"We've got him," I whispered.

"For what?" said Mitchell.

A man's voice, deep and wheezy, spoke next.

"Come inside, lad. Come in out av the cold."

It was a hot night, but I supposed people said
those things at home from habit.

The voices shifted into the tent. We heard a chair
scrape, a kettle-lid rattle, and a fire being poked, though
Alf owned neither chair, kettle, nor fireplace.

The old woman said:

"Ye've been away a terrible while, Alfred."

"Three years and seven months, Mrs O'Connor."

"Call me Mother," she said softly.

There was a pause.

"Mother," said Alf.

I remember that word made me uncomfortable, though I couldn't
have told why. Mitchell stopped fidgeting.

Mary asked him about the country.

"Hard enough," said Alf. "But I got work most places.
Fencing, sinking, loading wool—anything that came."

"And did ye save?"

"A bit. Not what I meant to. There was sickness
at one camp, and I helped a mate home. Then
work went slack. But I've enough for the passage back,
and a few pounds over."

The old man coughed.

"Money isn't all, lad."

"No, Mr O'Connor."

"Father," corrected the old woman.

"Not yet, Bridget," said the old man, but there was
laughter in his voice.

Mary said, "Alfred, have ye forgotten what ye promised me?"

"No."

"Have ye changed?"

"No, Mary."

"Nor have I."

I kicked Mitchell gently, meaning that this settled the guilt
of the whole business; but he caught my ankle and
held it still.

The old man spoke after a while.

"I was against ye once, Alfred. Ye were quiet, an'
I took quietness for want of spirit. There's men who
make a great noise and do little, and men who
say nothing because they've work in mind. I know the
difference now."

Alf gave a short laugh, his ordinary foolish laugh, but
it did not sound foolish from outside in the dark.

"I never could talk proper," he said.

"Ye can say yes or no, can't ye?" said Mary.

"Yes."

"Then say yes when Father asks ye."

"Mary!" said the mother.

"Let the girl alone," said the old man. Then, very
solemnly: "Alfred O'Briar, d'ye still wish to marry our Mary?"

"If she'll have me."

"Answer him, Mary," said the mother.

"As if he didn't know!"

"Answer dacent."

"Yes, Alfred."

Then there was crying, laughing, handshaking, chairs moving, the dog
barking, and the old woman declaring she would make tea.
It was as complete a family scene as you could
hear through any slab wall in the country.

I whispered, "There's five of 'em in there, counting Alf."

"Six with the dog," said Mitchell.

We crawled round to the entrance. The candle threw one
shadow against the canvas.

Mitchell looked at me, then pulled the flap aside.

Alf lay on his bunk, fully dressed, with his hands
under his head. The old letters were scattered beside him.
His eyes were shut, and there was a smile on
his face—not the vacant grin we knew, but the quiet
look of a man who has reached home after a
long journey.

There was nobody else in the tent.

I stared under the bunk, though a cat could scarcely
have hidden there. Mitchell let the flap fall, and we
backed away without waking Alf.

At the log I said:

"But the girl—the old people—the dog—"

"Alf," said Mitchell.

"All of them?"

"All of them."

"What was he doing?"

"Thinking aloud."

That was Mitchell. He could hit the right words and
leave them there.

We told the Oracle, because somehow we knew he was
safe. He smoked for a while, looking towards Alf's tent.

"I've heerd of such cases before," he said.

But this time it did not sound like boasting.

Nothing was said to the others. Next morning Bill started
on the Case again, and the Oracle told him there
were fences enough in Western Australia without a man poking
his nose through another. Mitchell asked Alf to take first
cut at the bacon. I lent him a clean shirt
and pretended it was too small for me. The rest
followed our lead without knowing why, for camp opinion turns
as easily as smoke.

Alf seemed puzzled by our friendliness. Once or twice he
gave that grin, and nobody called it vacant.

A fortnight later he drew his money and rolled his
swag. He said he was going east and, after that,
perhaps farther. He did not say Ireland, nor Mary, nor
home, and we did not ask.

We walked with him to the track. Bill shouted tobacco.
Ned gave him a better strap for his swag. Mitchell
shook his hand as if he meant it.

"Good luck, Alf," he said.

"Thanks," said Alf. "Same to you fellows."

He went down the white road, small under the great
sky, and never looked back.

The Oracle watched until the mulga hid him.

"I've heerd of such cases before," he said quietly.

And, for once, none of us asked him what he
meant.

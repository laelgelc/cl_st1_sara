## The Pointing of Providence

There were three things upon which everybody in Meadowby agreed:
that Josephine Elliott made the best plum preserves in the
county, that David Hartley had never possessed the least notion
of managing a house, and that he had been in
love with Josephine for eighteen years.

Upon most subjects Meadowby permitted differences of opinion. Mrs. Tom
Sentner maintained that hens laid better if their nests faced
east, while old Mr. Pike declared such a belief contrary
to nature and Scripture. The congregation divided every spring over
the proper colour for the church fence. Even the weather
was a matter for controversy. But no one disputed the
plum preserves, David's helplessness, or his love.

Josephine herself disputed the last whenever anyone was rash enough
to mention it.

"Nonsense," she would say, whisking a spoon through cake batter
or pounding bread dough as if the offending notion lay
beneath her fists. "David's got into a habit, that's all.
A habit ain't love."

As David had proposed to her every year—sometimes twice a
year—for nearly two decades, it must be admitted that the
habit was firmly rooted.

The Elliott and Hartley farms adjoined each other along a
crooked line marked by a cedar fence, three gray boulders,
and a dispute concerning six feet of pasture which had
outlived both sets of parents. From Josephine's kitchen window one
could see David's red barns beyond a dip of snowy
field. From David's eastern bedroom one could see the amber
lamp in Josephine's pantry at dusk. David had privately regarded
that light as the friendliest star in the heavens. Josephine
regarded it as an indication that she had forgotten to
lower the wick.

On a blue February afternoon David came across the fields
to take tea with her. He had been invited, certainly;
Josephine was particular on that point. She invited him to
tea once a month because they had been neighbours since
childhood, because David was lonely, and because no Christian woman
could eat hot biscuits with quince jelly while a man
next door lived chiefly on fried pork and melancholy.

There was no other reason.

Josephine had set the dining-room table with the blue-flowered china
bought long ago for a wedding that had never taken
place. She used it freely and without sentiment. China was
meant to be used, she said, and if Meadowby chose
to make a romance out of plates, Meadowby must bear
the burden of its own foolishness.

Nevertheless, when David saw the blue cups, the ruby preserve
in its glass bowl, the plum cake shining with sugar,
the yellow butter curled like little roses, and Josephine at
the head of the table in her brown homespun dress,
he felt that the room presented a vision of domestic
happiness so complete that a less sentimental man might have
lost his head.

David had never been a less sentimental man.

Outside, the snow fields lay smooth beneath the fading sun,
their hollows filled with violet shadows. Frost had drawn silver
palms upon the windows. The grandfather clock ticked comfortably in
the corner, and the stove purred. Josephine poured tea. Her
hands were plump, capable hands, with dimples at the knuckles.
David had often wondered how a wedding ring would look
half lost among those dimples.

"You take two lumps," she said.

"Yes, Josephine."

"You always have."

"Yes, Josephine."

"Then why are you staring at me as if I'd
asked you?"

"I wasn't exactly staring."

"You were exactly staring."

David put a biscuit on his plate and buttered it
with guilty attention. At forty-six, he was still able to
look like a boy caught stealing apples. His hair had
gone thin at the temples, but his face was gentle
and smooth-shaven, and his blue eyes had a patience in
them which Josephine sometimes found provoking.

"How's Zillah?" she asked.

"Pretty well. Complaining of her back."

"Zillah always did complain of her back."

"She says it's the damp."

"It's laziness."

David stirred his tea, although the sugar was long dissolved.
His sister Zillah had been his housekeeper since their mother
died. Josephine and Zillah had not spoken for six years,
owing to a disagreement at the church sewing circle concerning
the proper way to cut a child's nightdress. Both women
had forgotten the details. Neither had forgotten the disagreement.

"The cake is real good," David ventured.

"It ought to be. I made it."

"And the preserves."

"You've eaten them for twenty years."

"They seem better every time."

Josephine glanced at him sharply. Compliments from David were dangerous
chiefly because they led somewhere. He never approached a proposal
directly. He circled it with praise, weather, theology, and poetry,
as a timid general might surround a fortified town.

To discourage military operations, Josephine picked up her knitting.

She knitted aggressively. The gray sock snapped and twisted between
her needles while David ate a second helping of cake
and watched the fire. Presently he cleared his throat.

Josephine's needles clicked faster.

"There's a verse," David began, "that says—"

"No."

"I haven't said it yet."

"You're going to quote poetry."

"It just came into my mind."

"Send it out again."

David looked injured. "It's about winter and the warmth of
the hearth."

"I don't care if it's about King Solomon and all
his wives. I won't listen."

"I wasn't thinking of King Solomon."

"You'd better not. He's no example for a Christian man."

David sighed. The clock ticked. A stick settled in the
stove with a soft shower of sparks.

Josephine knew what was coming. David knew she knew. The
clock, if clocks know anything after observing human beings for
eighty years, knew too.

David got up.

"Sit down," said Josephine.

"I think better standing."

"That's never been proved."

He stood nevertheless, one hand on the back of his
chair. His face was very earnest.

"Josephine, I've cared for you a long while."

"Eighteen years. You've told me."

"And I s'pose I'll go on caring for you."

"I've never asked you to."

"No. But I can't seem to help it."

Josephine knitted around a corner so sharply that the needle
almost squeaked.

"I have a good farm," David continued. "It's clear of
debt except for that little note on the north meadow,
and I could pay that come fall. There's room in
the house, and I'd try to make you comfortable. I
know I ain't much at talking—"

"You talk enough when you get started."

"—but I'd be kind to you, Josephine. I wish you'd
marry me. I do wish you would."

There it was, for perhaps the twenty-third time.

Josephine laid down the sock. Her cheeks had grown pink,
whether with anger or some less manageable emotion she did
not inquire. She believed in dealing with emotions as she
dealt with mice in the pantry—quickly, firmly, and without examining
them too closely.

"David Hartley, I've told you no until the word is
worn thin. I'm comfortable as I am. My farm pays.
My house suits me. I can go to bed when
I please and get up when I please, though as
a matter of fact I always get up at five.
I answer to nobody. Why should I give up peace
and independence to marry any man?"

"You wouldn't have to give up peace."

"I'd have to give up independence."

"You could manage things."

"I manage things now."

"You could manage me," David said hopefully.

"That," said Josephine, "is exactly what I don't intend to
do."

David's hand tightened on the chair. "You don't care for
me, then?"

"I care for you as a neighbour."

"You make more kinds of cake for me than you
make for other neighbours."

"That's because you look half starved."

"You use the blue china."

"I use it for the minister too."

"You mended my coat last November."

"The sleeve was hanging off."

"You remembered I liked pickled beets."

"You've told everyone in Meadowby you like pickled beets."

Each cherished evidence of affection became common humanity under Josephine's
brisk handling. David's hope faded, but slowly. It had survived
a great deal.

"Perhaps next year—"

"No!"

Her voice rang against the blue dishes.

David blinked.

"I mean it," Josephine said. "There'll be no next year,
nor next month, nor next Tuesday. If you ever ask
me again, David Hartley, I'll stop speaking to you. Entirely.
I won't so much as say good morning over the
fence."

David turned pale. Josephine nearly softened, but hardened again in
time.

"You couldn't mean that," he said.

"I do mean it."

"Not speak at all?"

"Not a word."

David looked toward the frosted window, beyond which his farm
lay under the darkening sky. Then he looked at Josephine.
"I couldn't stand that."

"Then don't propose again."

"All right," he said quietly. "I won't."

He took his coat from the peg. Josephine followed him
to the porch, although she could not have said why.
The cold swept in, fresh and stinging. Above the western
woods, the sky was striped with greenish gold.

"You'd better wrap your muffler closer," she said.

David obeyed.

"And mind the drift by the cedar fence."

"Yes, Josephine."

He went down the path, tall, stooped a little at
the shoulders, his dark figure moving over the snow. At
the gate he turned as if he might speak. Josephine
stood with one hand on the door.

He remembered his promise and went on.

Josephine shut the door crossly.

For the next week she was more industrious than usual.
She baked six loaves of bread, sorted the seed catalogue,
cleaned the pantry shelves, made a batch of candles, and
finished three socks, of which only two matched. She told
herself she was glad David had finally understood. No sensible
woman wanted a man forever hanging about with poetry in
his pocket and proposals in his eyes.

Yet the month had a queer emptiness in it. David
did not come over. She saw him once at the
distant barn, carrying a forkful of hay, and once on
the road, where he lifted his cap. Josephine nodded. He
had not spoken, so technically her threat did not apply;
but the nod troubled her afterward. It seemed a very
small return for eighteen years.

Then, late one night, someone pounded on her kitchen door.

Josephine had gone upstairs, but she was not asleep. The
wind was shaking snow against the windows, and frost glittered
in the moonlight. She put on her flannel wrapper, lit
a lamp, and descended.

David stood outside.

Snow clung to his cap and shoulders. His face was
gray with fatigue and fear.

"Josephine," he said, "Zillah's awful sick."

That was enough. Josephine forgot the sewing circle, the nightdress,
and six years of dignified estrangement.

"What's wrong with her?"

"Fever. She can't breathe proper. Doctor Benson says pneumonia. He's
been, but he had to go to the McCraes'. Their
youngest boy broke his leg. I don't know what to
do."

"Did the doctor leave directions?"

"Yes, but—"

"But you've lost them, I s'pose."

"No. They're on the table. I can read them. Only
she's worse, and I—Josephine, would you come?"

There are women who hesitate attractively in a crisis. Josephine
was not one of them.

"Go hitch the mare," she ordered. "I'll dress and pack
a bag. Put hot stones in the sleigh. And don't
stand there letting the cold in."

Within twenty minutes they were driving across the road, the
mare's bells muffled by the storm. The moon appeared and
vanished among racing clouds. Snow smoked over the fields, and
the bare maples creaked along the lane.

David held the reins in mittened hands. "I knew you'd
come."

"You didn't know any such thing."

"I hoped."

"Well, hoping and knowing are different. Did you give Zillah
the medicine at ten?"

"Yes."

"How much?"

"A tablespoon."

"Was it meant to be a tablespoon?"

David's silence was terrible.

Josephine snatched the doctor's directions from his coat pocket and
held them near the lantern. "A teaspoon! David Hartley!"

"I thought the spoon on the table was the one
he meant."

"Has she had more than one dose?"

"No."

"Providence takes care of fools, but it must get tired."

Zillah had suffered no harm from David's error, though Josephine
declared this more owing to the weakness of the medicine
than to any heavenly intervention. She found the patient feverish,
coughing, frightened, and disagreeable—the last being, in Josephine's opinion, a
hopeful sign.

She also found the Hartley house in a condition which
caused her to doubt whether civilization had ever reached that
side of the road.

The kitchen stove was choked with ashes. Dirty cups crowded
the sink. A saucepan containing something black stood on the
back of the range, and no one could have said
whether it had once been porridge, gravy, or harness dressing.
Boots blocked the doorway. Newspapers covered two chairs, three plates,
and most of the floor. The pantry smelled of onions,
damp wool, and defeat.

Josephine's first duty was to Zillah. Her second, she decided
grimly, was to rescue the house from itself.

For four nights Zillah hovered dangerously near death. Josephine slept
in short spells on a cot beside her bed, measured
medicine, mixed mustard plasters, warmed broth, changed linen, and listened
to the harsh struggle of her breathing. David moved about
on tiptoe, carrying wood and water, doing exactly as he
was told and frequently doing it wrong.

Mrs. Tom Sentner came during the afternoons to relieve Josephine.

"I never expected to see you nursing Zillah Hartley," she
whispered on the first day, glancing at the bed.

"This ain't the time to expect or not expect things,"
Josephine said. "Hold that basin."

Mrs. Sentner held it, but her mind was busy. By
evening, all Meadowby knew that Josephine Elliott had gone to
David Hartley's in the middle of the night and was
staying there. By the next morning, details had been added.
She had crossed the fields through a blizzard, though in
fact she had gone by road. She had taken charge
of the house, which was true. She had forgiven Zillah,
which was uncertain. She and David were as good as
engaged, which was nonsense.

Josephine said so when Mrs. Sentner hinted at it.

"You know how folks talk," said Mrs. Sentner.

"I know how you talk."

"Well, I only repeat what comes to me."

"You generally improve it on the journey."

Mrs. Sentner was not offended. She had known Josephine for
thirty years and considered bluntness merely her way of being
sociable.

On the fifth morning, Doctor Benson pronounced Zillah improved.

"She'll pull through," he said, buttoning his fur coat. "Good
nursing did it."

"Good medicine helped," Josephine allowed.

"Not that tablespoon."

David, standing by the stove, turned red.

When the doctor had gone, Josephine made oatmeal and fried
ham. She had cleaned the kitchen in sections during Zillah's
illness, beginning with whatever she needed most urgently and spreading
outward like an army of occupation. The stove shone black
and solid. The floor had been scrubbed to its boards.
Clean curtains hung at the windows. Jars stood in ranks
on the pantry shelves. Even the clock had been dusted
and wound, and its ticking filled the room with orderly
contentment.

David came in after feeding the cattle. His hair was
sprinkled with snow, his face pinched from cold.

"Sit down," Josephine said.

"I ought to split kindling."

"You ought to eat."

"I can do it after—"

"Sit."

David sat.

She put oatmeal before him, then ham, potatoes, brown bread,
and preserved pears. He looked at the table with reverence.
His usual breakfast consisted of scorched cornmeal eaten standing up,
unless Zillah felt energetic, in which case there might be
cold biscuits.

"You've made enough for threshing day," he said.

"You need feeding."

"I didn't know how hungry I was."

"That's because you're never fed enough to remember what hunger's
for."

David tasted the pears. A look of peace came over
his face. Josephine, watching from the stove, felt a sudden
ache of pity so sharp that she nearly dropped the
coffeepot.

The man had worked faithfully all his life. His fences
were sound, his stock well kept, his accounts honest. Yet
no one darned his socks until his toes came through;
no one saw that he had warm soup when he
came in wet; no one put a lamp in the
window because he was expected home. Zillah's housekeeping had declined
by slow degrees, and David, being David, had accepted every
discomfort as if it were weather.

Josephine poured his coffee rather more violently than necessary.

"How long has that cuff been torn?"

David examined his sleeve. "A while."

"A while meaning since Christmas?"

"Maybe before."

"Take the shirt off after breakfast."

"Here?"

"Not at the table! Go upstairs and put on another.
If you've got another fit to be seen."

He smiled into his coffee.

"What are you grinning at?"

"It's pleasant being ordered around."

"Then you've had a deprived life."

That afternoon, while Zillah slept, Josephine investigated David's bureau. She
found four shirts missing buttons, two undershirts in need of
patches, and fourteen socks, each with at least one hole.
She sat by the kitchen window with her workbasket and
began mending.

The late sun lay pale gold across the floor. Beyond
the glass, snow rested on the orchard branches, blue-white and
pure. A kettle hummed. David, repairing a harness strap near
the stove, glanced at Josephine whenever he believed she would
not notice.

She always noticed.

"Keep your eyes on that buckle," she said.

"Yes, Josephine."

A little later he looked again. Candlelight had begun to
gather in the room, amber along the wall and soft
around Josephine's brown dress. Her head was bent over his
sock. The sight was so dear and so entirely like
the life he had wanted that his throat tightened.

He could not ask for it. He had promised.

Josephine bit off a thread. "What's the matter now?"

"Nothing."

"You sighed."

"I was only thinking."

"Don't overdo it."

He bent to the harness, smiling faintly. Josephine saw the
smile and felt unreasonably irritated.

The days settled into a pattern. Josephine rose early, lit
the kitchen fire, prepared breakfast, and carried a tray to
Zillah. She swept, cooked, washed, aired rooms, brewed flaxseed tea,
and ruled the establishment from cellar to attic. David brought
in wood without being asked after the second day and
wiped his boots after the fourth. On the seventh, he
hung his coat on its peg of his own accord.

Josephine observed these improvements with satisfaction. A man could be
trained, apparently, if one began before hope was entirely gone.

Zillah recovered slowly and without gratitude.

"You've moved my blue jar," she complained when she was
strong enough to sit up.

"It was full of grease."

"I kept things in it."

"You kept mouse droppings in it."

"You had no call to turn my pantry upside down."

"I turned it right side up."

Zillah glared from among the pillows. Illness had made her
thin and yellow, but not mild. "You always thought you
knew better than everybody."

"I generally do."

David, caught between them, looked anxiously at the window.

Yet even Zillah could not quarrel continuously while being fed
chicken broth, baked apples, custard, toast, and Josephine's rice pudding.
She ate each dish with criticism and finished it to
the last spoonful.

"You put too much nutmeg in this," she said.

"I'll take it away."

"I didn't say I couldn't eat it."

Josephine left the bowl.

By the second week, danger had passed. Mrs. Sentner came
less often, disappointed to find the household so peaceful. She
would have preferred a scene of reconciliation, rivalry, or open
warfare. Instead, Zillah dozed upstairs, David worked in the barn,
and Josephine rolled pastry in the spotless kitchen.

"What'll you do when Zillah's up?" Mrs. Sentner asked.

"Go home, naturally."

Mrs. Sentner glanced toward the window. "David'll miss your cooking."

"He got along before."

"Did he?"

Josephine's rolling pin stopped.

Mrs. Sentner selected a raisin from the bowl. "Tom says
David's put on five pounds."

"Tom can't tell a pound from a bushel."

"He looks different, anyhow. More spry."

"He's eating proper food."

"And he whistles in the barn."

"Men whistle. It's one of their foolish habits."

"Tom don't."

"No one would ask him to."

Mrs. Sentner ate another raisin and departed well pleased.

Josephine rolled the crust too thin and had to begin
again.

She had intended to return home as soon as Zillah
could sit up. Then she decided to wait until Zillah
could walk across the room. After that it seemed unreasonable
to leave before the patient managed the stairs. When Zillah
finally came down to the kitchen, wrapped in two shawls
and full of complaints, Josephine said she would stay only
until Saturday.

Saturday arrived clear and bitter. The snow sparkled so brightly
that the sky seemed faded beside it. Josephine packed her
bag after breakfast.

David watched from the doorway.

"You're going today, then?"

"I said I would."

"Yes."

"Zillah can manage."

"Yes."

"I've filled the wood box, baked six loaves, and left
stew enough for two days. There are instructions for the
stove pasted beside it."

"Yes."

"The pantry's in order. Don't let Zillah mix the onions
and apples again."

"No."

"And if you come in wet, change your stockings."

"Yes, Josephine."

His obedience annoyed her. She folded her nightdress, unfolded it,
and folded it again.

"You needn't look as if you were going to a
funeral."

"I don't mean to."

"You've got your own house back."

"I never knew I'd lost it."

"Now you can do as you please."

David looked around the warm, shining kitchen. A pot of
soup simmered on the stove. Red geraniums, rescued from an
upstairs room, stood in the window. The table wore a
clean checked cloth, and the brass lamp shone as it
had not shone since his mother's time.

"I don't believe I care much for doing as I
please," he said.

Josephine put a comb into her bag with such force
that two teeth broke.

Zillah, established in the rocking chair, sniffed. She understood more
than either of them supposed, although she understood it in
a Zillah-like way.

"You're mighty eager to get home," she said.

"My house has been shut up near three weeks."

"Like as not, pipes have frozen."

"I drained them."

"Mice may have got in."

"My cat hasn't."

"Your hens won't know you."

"Hens ain't sentimental."

David carried Josephine's bag to the sleigh. The mare stamped
little hollows in the yard, and her breath rose in
silver clouds. He tucked the buffalo robe around Josephine with
careful hands.

"I'm obliged to you," he said.

"There's no need."

"There's every need. I don't know what we'd have done."

"You'd have managed."

"No," David said. "I don't think we would."

He stood beside the sleigh, his hand on its edge.
Josephine waited. If he had quoted poetry, she could have
scolded him. If he had proposed, she could have reminded
him of his promise. But David said nothing more.

At last she shook the reins and drove away.

Her own house was clean, quiet, and cold. The rooms
smelled faintly of apples and beeswax. Everything stood exactly where
she had left it. Her chair waited beside the stove;
her books lined the shelf; her clock ticked with independent
precision.

Josephine lit fires, drew water, fed the cat, inspected the
pantry, and found no frozen pipes, no mice, and no
disaster worth mentioning. By supper time the kitchen glowed. She
set one place at the table.

One place occupied remarkably little room.

She made tea, cut bread, and opened a jar of
pickles. There was soup enough in the pantry, but soup
reminded her of David bending over a steaming bowl as
if it were a gift from royalty. She ate bread
and pickles instead.

Afterward she knitted.

The clock ticked.

The stove purred.

The cat washed itself.

No one sighed, misplaced a spoon, praised the preserves, or
looked at Josephine when he thought she would not notice.

"This," Josephine told the cat, "is peace."

The cat yawned.

On Sunday, Meadowby church was crowded. News of Zillah's recovery
had spread, and people wished to see her, or at
least to see those associated with her. Zillah stayed home,
but David came alone and sat in the Hartley pew.
Josephine sat three rows ahead.

She did not turn around during the service. She was
aware nevertheless that David coughed once during the second hymn,
dropped his prayer book during the sermon, and did not
sing the final verse of "Abide with Me."

After church, Mrs. Sentner met Josephine by the gate.

"So you've gone back home."

"I have."

"David looks peaked."

"He looked perfectly well."

"You noticed, then."

"I have eyes."

Mrs. Sentner's own eyes brightened. "Folks wondered if maybe—"

"Folks had better wonder about their own affairs."

"Of course. Still, everybody says you made a fine couple
over there."

"Everybody wasn't over there."

"Tom says—"

"Tom knows less than everybody."

David approached, saving Mrs. Sentner from an answer. He removed
his cap.

"Good morning, Josephine."

"Good morning, David."

They had returned to neighbourly terms. That was exactly what
Josephine had demanded.

"How's Zillah?" she asked.

"Fair. She came downstairs yesterday."

"What did she eat?"

"Fried pork."

Josephine stared. "Fried pork?"

"She wanted it."

"She wanted to get sick again, did she?"

"I told her you wouldn't approve."

"And what did she say?"

David hesitated. "She said it was no concern of yours."

Josephine's mouth tightened. "She did, did she?"

Mrs. Sentner retreated a few steps in order to hear
better without appearing to listen.

"You tell her," Josephine said, "that if she eats fried
pork after pneumonia she's a bigger fool than I took
her for, and I've never underestimated her."

"Yes, Josephine."

"And what did you eat?"

"Some of the pork."

"David!"

"It was cooked."

"That's the worst thing you could say of it."

David looked meek. Josephine looked furious. Mrs. Sentner looked delighted.

"I left stew," Josephine said.

"Zillah gave it to the dog."

"The dog has more sense than either of you."

She marched away. Halfway home she realized that she had
behaved precisely as if David's meals were her concern.

They were not.

On Monday she churned butter with unusual violence. On Tuesday
she cleaned a chimney which did not need cleaning. On
Wednesday she saw smoke from the Hartley kitchen lying dark
against the sky and wondered whether the stove was drawing
badly. On Thursday David passed on the road and appeared
to have lost weight since Sunday, which was impossible but
plain.

On Friday morning Josephine baked a suet pudding.

It was David's favourite.

She stared at it when it came from the oven,
brown and fragrant, with raisins showing through its glossy surface.

"I made too much batter," she said aloud.

The cat, who had observed the measuring, declined to support
this statement.

Josephine wrapped the pudding in a cloth, put it in
a basket with brown bread, a jar of beef broth,
and six eggs, and set off across the fields. She
took the field path because it was shorter, not because
she wished to avoid being seen on the road.

At the cedar fence she found a drift over the
lower rails. David had cleared it when she stayed at
his house. Now snow lay deep and untrodden.

She climbed carefully, caught her skirt on a splinter, and
arrived at the other side in no charitable mood.

David was in the barn. Hearing her call, he came
out so quickly that he struck his shoulder against the
door.

"Josephine!"

"You needn't act astonished. I've been here before."

"Yes, but—well, I'm glad."

"I brought some things for Zillah."

"Of course."

The words were innocent, but Josephine suspected amusement in them.

"And for you," she added defiantly. "There's pudding."

David took the basket. "My favourite kind?"

"There's only one kind of suet pudding worth making."

His face lit with such simple pleasure that Josephine's irritation
turned, against her will, to tenderness.

"How are you getting on?" she asked.

"Fine."

His coat lacked a button. One glove had split at
the thumb. There was a streak of flour on his
sleeve, though how flour had reached the barn was a
mystery.

"Fine," Josephine repeated.

"Well enough."

"What did you have for breakfast?"

David looked toward the pasture.

"David."

"Toast."

"And?"

"Tea."

"And?"

"The toast got burned some."

"What did you have on it?"

"We're out of butter."

"You have two cows."

"Zillah hasn't felt like churning."

"And you've got no sense to do it yourself."

"I tried."

"What happened?"

"It didn't come."

Butter, Josephine reflected, did not generally respond to coaxing.

She went into the house.

The kitchen had declined in six days. It had not
reached its former depths, but it was travelling steadily. Ashes
dusted the stove. A basin of gray dishwater stood in
the sink. Crumbs covered the table. One of Josephine's carefully
labelled pantry jars now contained nails.

Zillah sat by the fire with her feet up, reading
an old newspaper.

"Well," she said, "I thought you'd be back."

"I brought broth."

"I ain't an invalid."

"You look like one."

"I'm stronger every day."

"Then why's the house in this condition?"

Zillah folded the paper. "It suits David and me."

Josephine turned. David had followed her in and stood clutching
the basket.

"Does it suit you?" she demanded.

David had faced runaway horses with greater confidence.

"I don't mind much."

"That wasn't my question."

"No," he admitted. "It doesn't suit me."

Zillah sniffed. "You were content enough before she came interfering."

"I didn't know any better before."

A silence fell.

Josephine looked at David. David looked at the floor. Zillah
looked from one to the other and understood everything at
once. Her mouth compressed.

"I see," she said.

"No, you don't," said Josephine.

"I see plenty."

"You generally see what ain't there."

Zillah rose, gathering her shawls about her. "I s'pose you
want me out."

"Nobody said so."

"Nobody has to. A body knows when she ain't wanted."

David set down the basket. "Now, Zillah—"

"I can go to Cousin Amelia's."

"You always said Amelia got on your nerves," he reminded
her.

"She does. But she ain't likely to poison me with
broth."

"My broth never poisoned anybody," Josephine said. "Though I can't
answer for fried pork."

Zillah went upstairs with dignity, pausing twice to cough for
effect.

David rubbed the back of his neck. "She's touchy."

"She's selfish."

"She's been sick."

"She was selfish before she was sick."

Josephine picked up a dishcloth and attacked the table. She
had not intended to clean, but the crumbs were intolerable.

David sat down slowly.

"Don't get comfortable," she said. "You can empty those ashes."

He did. Together they restored the kitchen. Neither spoke much.
There are conversations which may be avoided only by polishing
every object within reach, and Josephine polished the stove until
it reflected her stern face.

When the pudding was warmed, they ate some at the
table. Zillah refused to come down, which allowed David a
second helping.

"It's real good," he said.

"You always say that."

"I always mean it."

Josephine looked at his mended cuff, already coming loose because
Zillah had washed the shirt badly. She looked at the
clean table, the steaming pudding, the winter sunshine falling through
the geranium leaves. She felt, all at once, tired of
going home to one plate and a yawning cat. More
than that, she felt ashamed of leaving David to burned
toast, buttonless coats, and the slow return of disorder.

Pity, she told herself.

Duty.

Christian responsibility.

Anything but the truth.

"I'd better go," she said.

David's spoon stopped. "Yes."

"You might drive me. That basket's awkward."

"Yes."

They rode home beneath a sky clear as blue glass.
At Josephine's gate, David carried the empty basket to the
porch.

"Thank you for the pudding," he said.

"You're welcome."

"And everything."

"Yes."

He stood waiting. Josephine knew that if she invited him
in, she would give him supper. If she gave him
supper, he would sit by the stove. If he sat
by the stove, the house would no longer seem empty.
These were dangerous consequences.

"Good night," she said.

"Good night, Josephine."

He turned away.

"David."

He turned back instantly.

Josephine opened her mouth. Nothing practical came out. She saw
again his kitchen as it had been when she first
arrived: the dead stove, the black saucepan, the cold disorder.
She saw his face over a good breakfast, surprised by
comfort. She saw the torn gloves, the burned toast, the
patient loneliness of him.

"I'll have to do it," she said.

David came closer. "Do what?"

"Something sensible."

He waited.

Josephine had never been afraid of a task merely because
it was difficult. She could lift a flour barrel, doctor
a sick calf, bargain with a seed merchant, and tell
the minister when his sermon had gone on too long.
Yet now her heart beat so loudly that she was
sure David must hear it.

She remembered with annoyance that she had forbidden him ever
to propose again. At the time it had seemed an
excellent arrangement.

He had obeyed her.

Naturally, that left only one person in Meadowby capable of
correcting matters.

She would not quote poetry.

"Zillah ought to go somewhere for a spell," Josephine said.
"Cousin Amelia's would do. The air is milder there, and
Amelia's got no stairs."

David blinked. "Zillah wouldn't like it."

"Zillah doesn't like anything. That ain't an argument."

"No."

"She needs rest. Proper rest. And you need somebody here
who can keep a house fit for human beings."

David's expression changed, but he said nothing.

Josephine grew cross. "Don't stand there looking foolish."

"I'm trying not to."

"I could come over."

"For visits?"

"For good, David. I could keep house for you."

His eyes widened.

"But I won't do it with folks talking," Josephine continued
rapidly. "And they would talk. Meadowby can make a scandal
out of two cows standing under the same tree. There's
only one respectable, practical way to arrange it."

David's lips parted.

Josephine looked at the brass knocker, the snow bank, the
bare lilac bush—anywhere but at him.

"If you're still of the same mind," she said, "we
might as well get married."

For a second David did not move.

Then he said, very softly, "Josephine?"

"You heard me."

"You mean—you'd marry me?"

"I don't make statements I don't mean."

"Because of the housekeeping?"

"Well, somebody's got to see that you eat."

"And the buttons?"

"No man should go about shedding buttons like seed."

"And because Zillah needs a change?"

"That too."

David stepped onto the porch.

Josephine retreated until the door was behind her. "Mind, I
don't want any fuss. We can be married quiet. No
arches, no bells, and no one writing foolish poetry."

"I won't quote a line."

"And my farm stays mine."

"Of course."

"I'll keep my own account."

"Yes."

"And I won't have tobacco ashes on the hearth."

"I'll stop smoking."

"I didn't say that. You can smoke in the woodshed."

"Yes, Josephine."

His face had become so radiant that her own practical
arguments sounded thin even to herself.

"Well," she said, "is that settled?"

"No."

"No?"

David, who had obeyed Josephine for eighteen years and intended
to obey her for the rest of his life, became
masterful for perhaps the first time.

He put both arms around her.

"David Hartley!"

"I've waited eighteen years."

"That doesn't give you leave to crush my ribs."

"I'm going to kiss you."

"You'll do nothing of the—"

He kissed her.

Josephine had expected, if she had expected anything, that David
would ask permission, apologize, lose courage, or knock over the
umbrella stand. Instead, he kissed her with astonishing decision. The
winter porch, the blue shadows, the gold sunset, and every
sensible objection disappeared.

For one moment Josephine stood rigid.

Then her capable hands caught the lapels of his coat,
and she kissed him back.

When they separated, David looked younger by twenty years. Josephine's
cheeks were scarlet.

"You've got snow on my face," she said.

"I'm sorry."

"No, you ain't."

"No," David admitted happily. "I ain't."

She straightened his muffler, though it did not need straightening.
"We'd better tell Zillah."

"We'd better."

"She'll make a scene."

"Like as not."

"And Mrs. Sentner will tell the whole county before breakfast."

"She probably knows now."

Josephine glanced toward the road. A dark figure had just
vanished behind the Sentners' hedge.

"She does," she said grimly.

David laughed. Josephine tried not to, failed, and laughed with
him.

They stood together in the deepening dusk while the first
star brightened over the snowy fields. Behind them, Josephine's lamp
shone amber through the frosted window. Across the road, David's
chimney sent a straight plume of smoke into the calm
air.

"I always thought," David said, "that light of yours looked
like home."

"It is home."

"I mean my home."

Josephine looked up at him. "It will be," she said,
and because the words were dangerously tender, she added, "once
I've cleaned it properly."

David bent to kiss her again.

This time Josephine did not object at all.

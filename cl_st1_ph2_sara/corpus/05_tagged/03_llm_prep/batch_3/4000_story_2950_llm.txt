Kenelm Jerton entered the dining-room of the Grand Sybaris Hotel
with the obscure but persistent conviction that everybody was looking
at him. There was no particular reason why anybody should
do so. His clothes were correct without being memorable, his
face suggested an honourable neutrality on most public questions, and
he carried neither a Pekingese dog nor a political reputation.
Nevertheless, as he threaded his way among the crowded tables,
he felt himself to be the object of an interest
which was probably, he reflected, spuriously concealed.

The only vacant chair stood at a small table already
occupied by a young woman in green-grey velvet. She had
ordered nothing and was studying the menu with the concentration
of a Cabinet Minister confronted by an unfamiliar principle.

"Do sit down," she said. "You may be useful."

Jerton sat down because there are invitations which the timid
obey more rapidly than commands.

"I beg your pardon?" he said.

"I have lost my memory."

Jerton glanced round the room. Several hundred persons continued eating
with an indifference which seemed almost ostentatious.

"Entirely?" he asked.

"Not entirely. I know how to spell 'chrysanthemum,' and I
remember that the Ulwights are impossible after half-past eleven. What
I cannot remember is who I am."

"That is very inconvenient."

"Much more inconvenient than you suppose. I am almost certain
I am a titled woman."

She made the announcement without vanity, rather as one might
admit to having left an umbrella in a cab.

"Have you no letters, cards, or initials?"

"I arrived with nothing except six sovereigns and a belief
that I had travelled from somewhere important. There was a
heap of luggage in the hall, so I selected some
that looked as though it might belong to me."

"Was it labelled?"

"Kestrel-Smith."

"Then presumably—"

"Oh, I am not Kestrel-Smith. I knew that at once.
Nobody with my feeling for vowels could be called Kestrel-Smith."

Jerton was about to suggest that the luggage should have
been left to its owners, but the young woman had
the air of having disposed of that aspect of the
matter.

"The locks were troublesome," she continued. "The porter had to
force them. There are three evening gowns, two devotional books,
a photograph of a major in the Sappers, and enough
hairpins for a family of porcupines. None of them recalls
anything. The clothes fit rather badly, which is encouraging."

"How is that encouraging?"

"If they fitted perfectly, I might really be a Kestrel-Smith."

A waiter approached. The Fair Unknown looked again at the
menu.

"I shall have oysters," she announced.

"They are rather expensive here," said Jerton, who felt that
somebody ought to introduce economy into the inquiry.

"That is precisely why I must have them. Lady Braddleshrub
never eats oysters in months without an 'x,' because a
Bulgarian doctor once told her they encourage independence of thought.
If I eat them without hesitation, I am probably not
Lady Braddleshrub."

"The present month has an 'r,'" Jerton pointed out.

"Has it? Then the test is less valuable, but we
may as well proceed."

She ordered oysters, quails, asparagus, a peach Melba, and a
half-bottle of champagne. Jerton ordered cold beef, which seemed to
him morally defensible.

"You are wondering whether I am Lady Mousehilton," she said
when the champagne came.

"I was not, really."

"You should have been. She is exactly the sort of
person one forgets being. But Lady Mousehilton cannot bear men
with auburn moustaches."

"My moustache is brown."

"In this light it is auburn. If I flirt with
you, we may eliminate her."

Before Jerton could protest, she leaned across the table and
asked whether he believed that two people might meet for
the first time and yet recognise one another as victims
of the same destiny.

"I have never considered it," he said, turning the colour
of a threatened sunset.

"Then you are not encouraging enough for an accurate test.
Still, I felt no repulsion. Lady Mousehilton may be struck
off."

She ate two oysters with the calm of a scientist
who has sacrificed some unimportant creatures in the cause of
knowledge.

"Lady Befnal is another possibility," she resumed. "She tips outrageously.
Not generously—outrageously. She once gave a cloak-room attendant five pounds
because he had mistaken her for somebody else."

When the waiter replaced a fork, the young woman gave
him half a crown.

"There," she said. "Did that feel natural?"

"To the waiter, evidently."

"To me it felt excessive. I am not Lady Befnal."

Jerton's cold beef arrived and appeared to reproach him.

"You might inquire at the office," he suggested. "Perhaps they
know under what name you came."

"I did. They say I arrived alone, signed an unreadable
name, and asked if the Duke of Starping had left
any message."

"Does that suggest anything?"

"Only that I may be on terms with the Duke
of Starping. Unfortunately, half London asks whether he has left
messages, because he never has."

She considered the asparagus.

"There is also Lady Prunella Vex. She cannot pass a
page boy without sending him on an errand."

At that moment a hotel page came through the room
calling for Mr. Cuthbert Nollis.

"Boy," said the young woman, "find out which horse is
most fancied in the three-fifteen at Gatwick."

The page vanished.

"That came quite naturally," she observed. "I may be Lady
Prunella."

"Would she gamble on horses?"

"Never. That is the objection. If I back the horse
when he returns, I shall prove that I am not
she."

Jerton began to perceive that every avenue of investigation led
through some expensive district.

The page returned with the information that Amber Bishop was
considered unbeatable. The young woman sent five pounds to be
placed on it.

"Is that all the money you have?" asked Jerton.

"All but seventeen shillings and some French copper. Identity is
not recovered by half-measures."

Amber Bishop finished last but one, having delayed at the
starting gate to express a private disagreement with the proceedings.

"That settles Lady Prunella," said the young woman cheerfully. "Also
Lady Althea Crune, who always backs winners, though nobody knows
how. We are narrowing the field."

"You are narrowing other things as well," said Jerton, with
an unaccustomed approach to severity.

"My resources? Yes. I shall have to go to London."

"Why London?"

"The porter at the Megatherium Club knows everybody worth knowing
and a number of people who are merely unavoidable. I
once passed through the hall there disguised as a conservatory
palm, during the election of an unpopular bishop, and he
identified me immediately."

"You remember that?"

"I remember the porter. I do not remember whom he
identified."

The bill was placed before her. After examining it with
polite astonishment, she paid, leaving herself five shillings and sixpence.

"A third-class return to London costs more than that," she
said. "Would you lend me three pounds?"

Jerton had known her for forty-seven minutes. During that time
she had confessed to appropriating luggage, caused hotel property to
be burgled on her behalf, consumed a luncheon calculated to
embarrass a minor diplomat, and lost five pounds on an
animal which had shown no desire to participate in a
race. Yet the request for a loan seemed the first
incident which good breeding clearly required him to resist.

"I hardly think—" he began.

"I shall repay you the moment I discover who I
am. If I turn out to be the Duchess of
Ulwight, I shall probably send four pounds by mistake."

"That scarcely—"

"Of course, if you believe I am an adventuress—"

"I did not say that."

"You were careful not to. That is why I trust
you."

Jerton produced three pounds. He did so with the sensation
of watching another man commit an error from a position
too remote for interference.

"Thanks awfully," she said. "There is one other small matter."

There always was.

"The Kestrel-Smith luggage is in my room. It would be
awkward to take it to London, particularly as the labels
are so candid. Will you keep an eye on it
until I send instructions?"

"I cannot possibly take charge of stolen luggage."

"Appropriated luggage. Theft implies a settled identity and a definite
criminal intention. I possess neither."

"The owners may arrive."

"Then explain. You have a trustworthy face."

Jerton had often been told that he had a trustworthy
face; he had never before understood it as a threat.

The young woman rose.

"I must slip out by the side entrance. Hotel officials
become distressingly personal when one travels with forced locks and
no luggage. If the porter at the Megatherium fails, I
shall try the illustrated weeklies. They print photographs of titled
women at race meetings, generally looking as though they have
just remembered some private sorrow."

She held out her hand.

"You have been extremely helpful, Mr.—"

"Jerton."

"Of course. I knew it began with something uncompromising."

Then she disappeared among the palms and luncheon parties, leaving
Jerton with the bill for his cold beef and a
future populated by harassed railway officials, suspicious detectives, and distraught
Kestrel-Smiths.

He remained at the table, hoping that immobility might eventually
acquire the appearance of innocence. Presently a broad, sunburnt man
in golfing clothes paused beside him.

"Wasn't that Mrs. Stroope who was lunching with you?" he
asked.

"I don't know," said Jerton, with literal truth. "She does
not know either."

The golfer laughed.

"Lost her memory again, has she? Poor soul. It happens
every year or so. Usually after she has been overdoing
tournaments."

"You mean her story was true?"

"Oh, perfectly. She is Mrs. Algernon Stroope—the golfer. Won the
women's championship at St. Enodoc. Splendid player. When these attacks
come on, she forgets her name and invents something more
suitable."

"She believes she is a titled lady."

"She always does. Last time she thought she was the
Dowager Countess of Starping and dismissed three caddies for addressing
her as 'madam.' Her husband generally collects her before much
damage is done."

Jerton experienced a momentary relief. He had not been swindled
by an ordinary adventuress; he had merely financed the railway
journey of a champion golfer suffering from aristocratic delusions. The
distinction was delicate but comforting.

A violent bell rang in the hall. Voices followed—one authoritative,
one apologetic, and one belonging to a porter who wished
himself in another profession.

A hotel clerk hurried into the dining-room.

"Mr. Jerton, sir? A gentleman has arrived concerning some luggage."

Behind him appeared a tall, narrow man whose expression suggested
that generations of Kestrel-Smiths had regarded forced locks as a
personal insult.

"My trunks," he said, "have been broken open. I understand
they were placed in the room of a woman who
has left the hotel. I am told that you are
guarding them."

Jerton looked towards the main door. Two pages, a waiter,
and the manager had gathered there with the unobtrusive eagerness
of people determined not to miss anything. The side entrance
was already being watched by a porter.

There remained the corridor to the Turkish bath.

Jerton took it at a speed which, though socially conspicuous,
was tactically admirable. Ten minutes later, wrapped in a sheet
and seated amid clouds of steam, he reflected that public
attention was not always avoided by behaving correctly; sometimes it
was merely postponed until one had fewer clothes on.

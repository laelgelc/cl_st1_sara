# Little Joyce's Song

Everybody in the Marshall house was going to hear Madame
Laurin sing—everybody, that is, except Little Joyce and Denise.

Grandmother Marshall was going because it was proper that she
should be seen in the second row, wearing the gray
velvet gown and the Marshall diamonds. Aunt Edith was going
because Madame Laurin was famous, and Aunt Edith never neglected
a famous person if she could possibly help it. Chrissie
was going because she was sixteen, golden-haired, pink-cheeked and pretty,
and because a new white cloak had been made expressly
for the occasion.

Little Joyce was not going because she was only eleven,
and shy, and thin, and dark.

When you are shy and homely, and have elbows which
seem always to be in the wrong place, people seldom
think of taking you anywhere. They say, "The child would
not enjoy it," which is often a very comfortable way
of arranging that they themselves shall not be troubled.

Denise was not going because she was dying.

Nobody in the Marshall house used that dreadful word, of
course. Grandmother said Denise was "very poorly," and Aunt Edith
said the doctor feared she would not recover. But Joyce
knew. Denise had told her one evening when the sunset
made a red glory beyond the narrow attic window.

"I go soon, petite," Denise had whispered. "But I am
not afraid. No—de good God, He know Denise."

Denise had been in the Marshall family for thirty years.
She had rocked Aunt Edith in her cradle and dressed
Grandmother's hair in the days when Grandmother's hair was brown
instead of stately silver. Now she lay in a small
room over the kitchen, wasted and feverish, with her once-rosy
face no larger than a child's among the pillows.

Grandmother deserved a great deal of credit, she felt, for
allowing Denise to remain in the house. It would have
been much more convenient to send her to the hospital.
Grandmother also ordered broth and grapes and inquired every morning
how Denise had slept.

Little Joyce carried up the broth, washed the cups, smoothed
the bed, brushed Denise's gray hair, and sat beside her
through the long hours when pain made sleep impossible. But
this, naturally, was not considered remarkable. Joyce was merely a
child, and Denise had always been fond of her.

Indeed, Denise was the only person who had ever seemed
to find Little Joyce beautiful.

"Dose eyes!" she would say, holding Joyce's narrow face between
her trembling hands. "Dey are lak two stars on a
dark night. An' dose hands—ah, de beautiful leetle hands!"

Nobody else praised Joyce's hands. Grandmother generally noticed them only
when they were ink-stained, while Aunt Edith lamented that Joyce
had not inherited the Marshall complexion. Chrissie had inherited it
abundantly. She was a very pretty girl, and she knew
it in the innocent, unquestioning manner of one who has
been told so every day of her life.

On the afternoon of the concert Chrissie came flying into
Denise's room, smelling delightfully of violets and new fur.

"Only think, Denise! Madame Laurin is staying at the Royal
Shore Hotel. They say she has three rooms all full
of flowers, and a maid from Paris, and pearls as
large as cherries. I hope she sings the 'Song of
the River.' Everyone says it is wonderful."

Denise's hollow eyes shone.

"Madame Laurin! Ah, yes. I hear her once, long ago,
in Quebec. I was young den—young an' strong. She sing
de old songs my mother sing. If I could hear
her once more—just one leetle song—"

Her voice failed, and she turned her face toward the
window.

Chrissie looked uncomfortable. Sorrow was not becoming to an afternoon
of white cloaks and violets.

"How lovely it would be," she said vaguely. "But we
must go. Grandmother is calling. Good-by, Denise. I will tell
you all about it tomorrow."

Then she kissed the air above Denise's forehead and ran
away.

Joyce remained beside the bed, very still.

"Would you like it so much?" she asked.

Denise smiled apologetically.

"It is foolishness, petite. Madame Laurin, she sing for kings
an' great people. She cannot come sing for poor old
Denise. But when de heart is near heaven, it remember
many t'ings. I t'ink of my mother, an' de snow,
an' de bells across de St. Lawrence. Just one leetle
old song—ah, well!"

Joyce looked at the thin hands lying upon the quilt.
She knew that Denise would never see the St. Lawrence
again. She would never hear the sleigh bells ringing over
the snow, nor sit beside her mother's fire. But perhaps
she might hear one old song.

All that evening the thought followed Joyce.

Downstairs there was brightness and excitement. Carriages rolled away; silk
rustled; Grandmother's diamonds flashed. No one noticed the little dark
figure standing behind the library curtains as Chrissie departed like
a snow princess.

Afterward the house was quiet. Joyce went upstairs—not by the
broad front staircase, which curved impressively beneath the portraits of
departed Marshalls, but by the narrow stairs beyond the kitchen.
Joyce always took the shortest way. Grandmother considered this preference
most unMarshall-like.

In her room Joyce unlocked the bottom drawer of her
small bureau and lifted out her greatest treasure.

It was a little black doll, no longer than her
hand, carved from dark wood. Its face was grave and
strange; faint lines of gold still showed upon its stiff
headdress. An uncle who traveled in Egypt had sent it
to Joyce, saying that it had been found in an
ancient tomb and might once have belonged to a little
princess three thousand years ago.

Other people thought the doll ugly. Chrissie had laughed when
she saw it and asked why Joyce did not give
it to the museum.

But Joyce loved it.

The little black doll had slept under her pillow when
Grandmother scolded her, had listened when nobody else had time
to listen, and had understood all those griefs which are
too small for grown people but very large indeed for
lonely children. Joyce sometimes imagined the Egyptian princess had loved
it too. Perhaps that far-away child had also been shy.
Perhaps she had cried when they took her doll away.

"It is worth a great deal," Uncle Robert had said
carelessly.

Joyce remembered the words now.

A famous singer would expect to be paid a great
deal for singing. Joyce possessed no money except forty-three cents
in a china box. But she had the doll.

She held it tightly against her cheek.

"I love Denise more," she whispered.

That settled it.

The next morning was pale and windy. Madame Laurin was
to give a second concert that evening, and Joyce knew
the great singer would still be at the Royal Shore
Hotel. Immediately after breakfast she wrapped the doll in her
cleanest handkerchief and slipped out.

The hotel stood two miles away beyond the town, facing
the cold blue sea. Joyce had never entered it. In
summer its lawns bloomed with striped awnings and fashionable people;
in winter it seemed grander still, with tall windows glittering
above the lonely shore.

As Joyce approached, her courage began to fail. What if
Madame Laurin laughed? What if the servants refused to admit
her? What if the little black doll were not worth
enough?

She left the road and went down toward the beach
to think.

A woman was walking there alone, a short, splendid woman
wrapped in a dark crimson cloak. The wind tossed silver
threads through her black hair, and her eyes were bright
and kind. She did not look like the elegant ladies
who called on Grandmother Marshall. She looked as if she
might laugh loudly, weep freely and love people at once.

When she saw Joyce she stopped.

"Why, little one, you carry the troubles of the world
upon your forehead. Is the sea not beautiful enough to
make you smile?"

Joyce stared at her. Ordinarily she could not speak to
strangers. But there was something in the woman's voice like
a warm hand held out in darkness.

"I am trying to see Madame Laurin," Joyce said gravely.

"Ah! And why do you wish to see that terrible
person?"

"I want to ask her to sing one song."

The woman's eyebrows rose.

"One song? At the concert?"

"No. For Denise."

Then, because the woman waited without laughing, Joyce told her
everything—how Denise was dying, how she had once heard Madame
Laurin in Quebec, and how she longed for just one
little old French song before she went to heaven.

"And what will you give Madame Laurin for this private
concert?" asked the woman softly.

Joyce unwrapped the handkerchief.

The little black doll lay in her palm, dark and
solemn under the wintry sky.

The woman uttered a quick exclamation and took it with
careful fingers.

"But this is very old! It is Egyptian—perhaps of the
Eighteenth Dynasty. Where did you get such a treasure?"

"My uncle sent it to me. He said it belonged
to a princess."

"And you would give it away?"

Joyce's lips trembled. She had meant to be brave, but
the doll looked very small and dear in a stranger's
hand.

"Yes."

"Do you not love it?"

"Oh, yes! More than anything—except Denise."

The woman looked first at the doll and then at
Joyce. Her brilliant eyes suddenly filled with tears.

"And if Madame Laurin refuses?"

"I shall ask her to take the doll for half
a song."

At that the woman laughed—a beautiful, ringing laugh, though the
tears slipped down her cheeks.

"She will not refuse. I know Madame Laurin very well.
Indeed, little one, I am Madame Laurin."

Joyce became white with astonishment.

Madame Laurin bent and kissed her.

"Put away your treasure. I would not take it for
all the songs in the world. Lead me to Denise."

They went to the Marshall house by the kitchen door
because it was nearest. Madame Laurin appeared delighted with this
route, although Grandmother Marshall, had she seen the famous singer
passing the flour bin and the rows of shining saucepans,
might not have survived the shock.

The cook dropped a plate. The housemaid leaned against the
wall. Joyce led Madame Laurin straight up the narrow stairs.

Denise was awake, but very weak. She looked wonderingly at
the crimson-cloaked figure beside Joyce.

"Denise," said Joyce, breathless with triumph, "I have brought Madame
Laurin to sing for you."

For one moment Denise did not understand. Then recognition broke
over her face like sunrise.

"Madame—Laurin?"

"Yes, my dear," said the singer, kneeling beside the bed.
"And they tell me you and I both remember Quebec."

Denise began to cry soundlessly.

Madame Laurin took off her cloak. She asked no questions
about drawing rooms, pianos or audiences. She sat in the
plain wooden chair by the bed and held Denise's hand.

Then she sang.

She sang first a little cradle song, tender and low,
such as mothers have sung since the first baby found
the world too cold and strange. She sang of a
river broad beneath the moon, of pine forests bending under
snow, of bells traveling over frozen fields. She sang an
old hymn about the Virgin watching beside a sleeping child.

Denise's worn face changed. Pain went out of it. She
was no longer an old servant in a narrow room;
she was a young girl again, listening at dusk while
the St. Lawrence shone beyond her mother's door.

"Anodder," she pleaded. "Just one leetle anodder."

Madame Laurin sang another—and another.

By this time the household had discovered what was happening.
Aunt Edith stood in the passage with both hands raised
in amazement. Chrissie, still in her blue morning gown, peeped
over her shoulder. Grandmother Marshall arrived last, stately even in
haste, and deeply disturbed to find the greatest singer in
Canada seated in Denise's poor room without so much as
a formal introduction.

But even Grandmother did not interrupt.

Music filled the little room and flowed down the kitchen
stairs. It made the familiar walls seem to open upon
wide skies. For all true music is of God, whether
it is sung in a cathedral, in a concert hall,
or beside the bed of a dying servant.

When the last note faded, Denise lay smiling.

"Now I can go content," she whispered. "De good God
bless you, madame. An' my petite—my loving petite—"

Madame Laurin kissed her forehead. Then she turned to Joyce,
who had crouched beside the window with the little black
doll clasped against her heart.

"And now you must sing for me."

Joyce started.

"I cannot sing."

"Everyone can sing something."

"Chrissie sings," Joyce explained. "She has lessons. I only sing
to Denise when she cannot sleep."

"Then sing what you sing to Denise."

Joyce looked frightened. There were people in the doorway—Grandmother, Aunt
Edith, Chrissie, the maids. When everybody looks at you and
expects you to fail, it is very difficult to remember
that you have a voice at all.

But Denise said faintly, "Sing, petite."

So Joyce sang.

It was only the evening hymn Denise loved. Joyce began
so softly that Aunt Edith leaned forward to hear. Then
the voice strengthened.

Madame Laurin's face changed.

The voice was pure and full, with a strange golden
sweetness that seemed too great to have been hidden in
so small and awkward a child. It rose without effort,
bright as a bird mounting into morning, then deepened into
notes of such tenderness that Chrissie's blue eyes filled with
bewildered tears.

Joyce forgot the people. She forgot her shabby frock, her
dark face and troublesome elbows. She sang of rest, of
mercy, of a light beyond sorrow. And Denise listened, smiling.

When the hymn ended, no one spoke for a moment.

Then Madame Laurin seized Joyce by both hands.

"Child! Child! Who has trained you?"

"No one," said Joyce.

"Who has taught you to breathe so?"

"I don't know."

"Sing that phrase again."

Joyce sang it.

Madame Laurin turned upon the others with magnificent indignation.

"You have had this voice in your house—and no one
knew?"

Grandmother Marshall drew herself up.

"Joyce has never shown any particular musical inclination."

"Musical inclination! She has a gift—an extraordinary gift! Such quality,
such feeling, such natural placing! It must be trained. At
once. Do you hear me? At once!"

Grandmother heard. Everybody heard.

"She is very young," Aunt Edith murmured.

"Good! Then there is time to teach her properly. I
shall choose her master. If expense is a difficulty, I
will undertake it myself."

Expense had never been a difficulty to Grandmother Marshall, but
neglect, when named aloud, is an uncomfortable thing. She looked
at Joyce as if she had never seen her before.

Perhaps she had not.

She saw the thin little figure beside Denise's bed; the
delicate, beautiful hands Madame Laurin still held; the dark eyes
shining with courage and love. This was not merely the
plain child who spilled ink and preferred kitchen stairs. This
was her granddaughter—a granddaughter with a wonderful voice, certainly, but
also a child who had offered her dearest treasure to
comfort a dying friend.

"The expense will be mine," Grandmother said quietly. "Joyce shall
have every advantage."

Madame Laurin searched her handsome face.

"And affection also, I hope. Voices bloom best in warmth."

Grandmother's proud color rose. For once she had no polished
answer.

She crossed the room and stood before Joyce.

"Why did you not tell me Denise wished to hear
Madame Laurin?"

"I thought you might say it was impossible."

Grandmother winced.

"And were you truly going to give away your doll?"

"Yes, Grandmother."

"For Denise?"

Joyce nodded.

Then a very surprising thing happened. Grandmother Marshall knelt down
and put her arms around Little Joyce. Not carefully, not
ceremoniously, but closely—as if Joyce were something dear which she
had nearly lost.

"My child," she said, and her voice trembled, "can you
forgive me?"

Joyce did not know precisely what there was to forgive.
Children grow accustomed to loneliness as they grow accustomed to
an unbecoming dress; they suppose it belongs to them. But
she understood the arms around her and the new sound
in Grandmother's voice.

She dropped the little black doll, caught it again, and
then hid her face upon Grandmother's shoulder.

"Oh, Grandmother," she whispered, "do you think you could love
me—even if I never become a great singer?"

Grandmother held her more tightly.

"I love you now," she said.

Madame Laurin turned toward the window, perhaps because famous singers
must occasionally conceal tears like ordinary people. Aunt Edith wept
openly. Chrissie came forward and kissed Joyce with a humility
quite new to her pretty face.

Denise watched them all.

"Dat is de best song," she murmured.

She died peacefully a week later, with Joyce's hand in
hers and the old hymn trembling softly through the room.
There was sorrow, but no bitterness in it. Denise had
heard the songs of her childhood, and before she went
she had seen her petite gathered at last into love.

The years afterward brought masters, scales, difficult exercises and long
hours of faithful work. Madame Laurin kept watch over Joyce's
training, scolding, praising and prophesying magnificently. Grandmother kept her promise
too—not only the promise about lessons, which was easy, but
the promise of affection, which required her to change her
heart.

As for the little black doll, Joyce never gave it
away. It stood beside her bed through all the years
of study, grave and faithful as ever. And when at
last Joyce sang before a great audience, with Grandmother listening
proudly and Madame Laurin smiling from a box, the doll
waited in her dressing room.

For it had belonged to two lonely little girls, separated
by thousands of years, and perhaps it had comforted both.
Yet Joyce was lonely no longer.

Everybody knew then that she had a beautiful voice.

But the people who loved her best knew something more
wonderful still—that before anyone had discovered the beauty in her
singing, there had always been beauty in her heart.

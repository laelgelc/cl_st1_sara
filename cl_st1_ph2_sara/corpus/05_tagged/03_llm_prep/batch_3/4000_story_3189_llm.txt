# The Thirteenth Stroke of Vondervotteimittiss

It has been asserted by persons of no learning whatever—and
repeated, I regret to add, by Professor Knipperklatsch in the
third appendix to his *Chronometrical Geography of the Lesser Hills*—that
the borough of Vondervotteimittiss never existed. I shall not answer
so transparent an absurdity. The brass seal now before me,
bearing a cabbage rampant upon a clock-face proper, would alone
confound such skepticism; and if further testimony were required, I
might refer the reader to the worm-eaten municipal register of
Bürgermeister Hans Puffenstumpf, to the inscription discovered beneath the eastern
pig-trough, and to a memorandum in my own hand, written
upon the very afternoon of the events herein related.

I claim only rigid impartiality. If, during the progress of
this account, indignation should force from me such expressions as
"rascal," "scoundrel," or "long-legged nincompoop," the candid reader will attribute
them not to prejudice, but to that moral accuracy without
which history degenerates into mere fact.

Vondervotteimittiss lay, and perhaps still lies, in the exact middle
of a circular valley enclosed by twelve smooth hills. Why
twelve? Herr Doktor Doppelzinn maintains that each hill represented a
month; Father Schnapps, in his *De Montibus Rotundis*, insists that
the number arose from a shortage of hills; while the
oldest inhabitants, whose authority is naturally superior because none of
them could remember anything, declared that there had always been
twelve and therefore could never be thirteen.

No road crossed those hills. No traveler descended them. No
native climbed them, except once, in the reign of Bürgermeister
Von Glump, when a boy named Peter Peepchen pursued a
blue butterfly halfway up the southern slope. He was brought
back by the Council, washed in vinegar, deprived of cabbage
for two suppers, and compelled to apologize to the horizon.

Beyond the hills there was believed to be nothing worth
mentioning. Certain controversialists allowed the possible existence of fog, wolves,
foreigners, and bad time; but even these concessions were thought
dangerously liberal.

The borough itself was a model of arrangement. It consisted
of sixty houses—neither fifty-nine nor sixty-one—disposed in a perfect ring
about a paved market-place. Each house was built of red
brick, edged with black timber, roofed with blue slate, and
furnished with a green door, three white windows, one brass
knocker, and a chimney which leaned inward at precisely the
same angle. Before each house stood a garden; through each
garden ran a yellow path; beside each path rose a
sundial; and around each sundial flourished twenty-four cabbages.

The number twenty-four has been disputed. I have counted them.

In the first garden were twenty-four cabbages; in the second,
twenty-four; in the third, twenty-four; and so onward through the
entire circumference, making, by the ordinary rules of multiplication, one
thousand four hundred and forty cabbages. They were round cabbages,
sound cabbages, glossy cabbages, and—until the fatal day—punctual cabbages. They
opened their leaves at six in the morning, received water
at seven, were inspected at eight, dusted at nine, praised
at ten, and considered for dinner at eleven.

Every house contained a father, a mother, three children, a
pig, a cat, a canary, two feather beds, six chairs,
a cupboard, a copper kettle, and nine clocks. I except
the Bürgermeister's residence, which contained ten clocks, two pigs, and
an official umbrella; but this was privilege, not irregularity.

The fathers were small, broad men, with round heads, round
shoulders, round waistcoats, and stomachs of such civic magnificence that
the buttons upon them seemed less fastenings than medals. They
wore orange coats with tails reaching nearly to the ground,
green stockings, pink shoes, and silver buckles large enough to
serve, in an emergency, as dinner-plates. Their chins were prodigious.
Their eyes were pale and prominent. Their hair was combed
backward in seven ridges and tied with purple ribbon.

At sunrise each father wound his watch, opened his door,
looked at his cabbages, looked at the sky, distrusted the
sky, looked again at his watch, and said:

"Goot. It is morning."

The mothers were equally regular, though somewhat wider. They wore
scarlet petticoats, blue aprons, yellow caps, and wooden shoes painted
with minute representations of clocks and cabbages. At six they
stirred the porridge; at seven they polished the clocks; at
eight they combed the children; at nine they combed the
pig; at ten they cut cabbage; at eleven they put
cabbage into the pot; and at twelve they served it,
neither raw nor soft, but in that intermediate condition prescribed
by Municipal Ordinance XLIV, paragraph C.

The children differed from their parents only in size and
the absence of settled opinions concerning taxation. They marched three
abreast to the schoolhouse, where Master Ticktack taught them the
alphabet thus:

"A is for Alarm.
B is for Bell.
C is for Cabbage.
D is for Dial."

I suppress the remaining letters, partly because the reader can
infer them and partly because Master Ticktack never discovered anything
suitable for X.

As for the animals, they too were regulated. The cats
slept from one until four, scratched from four until five,
and watched the canaries from five until six. The canaries
sang only on the quarter-hour. The pigs ate according to
a printed table and were provided with little wooden watches
tied to their tails, not because they could read the
time, but because, as Councillor Grunzwig justly observed, "discipline begins
vere understanding ends."

All this order proceeded from the Great Clock.

The Great Clock occupied the tower in the centre of
the market-place. The tower was one hundred and one feet
high according to the town survey, ninety-eight according to its
shadow, and seven hundred according to the guidebook; but as
the guidebook included moral elevation, the discrepancy is easily reconciled.
Its walls were white, its roof was crimson, and upon
its summit revolved a gilded weathercock shaped like a cabbage
with wings.

The clock-face was enormous and blue. Its figures were gold;
its hands were black; its two eyes—if I may employ
a metaphor sanctioned by Archivist Blinkenbrood—looked down upon the borough
with an expression of grave disapproval. The pendulum, visible through
a glass panel, swung with a majestic clack—right, left; right,
left—like the legs of some enormous brass magistrate pacing forever
between two opinions.

At every hour the Great Clock struck. At every stroke
the nine clocks in every house replied; the fathers repeated
the number; the mothers nodded; the children counted upon their
fingers; the canaries chirped; the pigs wagged their watches; and
the cabbages, according to a manuscript too delicate for public
handling, inclined their outer leaves.

The care of this noble instrument belonged to the hereditary
Belfry-Man, old Dietrich Dunderkopf, the seventeenth of his line. He
lived in a room immediately beneath the bell, ate by
the pendulum, slept by the escapement, and had not descended
the tower in forty-two years. His beard was twisted into
two cords to prevent its entanglement in the wheels. His
left eye magnified and his right eye diminished, from long
use of lenses; thus, when he looked at a man,
he saw him simultaneously as a giant and a beetle,
which must have been invaluable in forming impartial judgments.

Every day at eleven minutes before noon, Dunderkopf oiled the
fourth wheel. At nine minutes before noon, he tested the
hammer. At seven minutes before noon, he looked from the
northern window. At five minutes before noon, he looked from
the southern window. At three minutes before noon, he took
off his cap. At one minute before noon, he placed
his hand upon his heart.

Then came twelve.

Then came dinner.

Then came contentment.

Such had been the custom from the remotest antiquity—from a
period, indeed, before which no records survive because, as the
Council wisely resolved, nothing had yet happened.

The fundamental statutes of Vondervotteimittiss were three:

First: That whatever had been was preferable to whatever might
be.

Second: That whatever lay beyond the hills was either impossible
or improper.

Third: That no respectable man required more than clocks and
cabbages.

These principles produced happiness of the most complete and motionless
description. No citizen was richer than his neighbor by more
than two spoons. No house was painted differently. No song
possessed an unexpected note. No child asked "Why?" twice. Even
dreams were reported each morning to the Clerk of Slumbers
and corrected when eccentric.

Yet upon the twenty-seventh day of Sauerkraut, in the year
18—, there occurred an event which I tremble to record.
I was present. I saw it. I heard it. I
have, furthermore, a dent in my hat caused by a
descending turnip, though turnips, I admit, are not otherwise material
to the narrative.

The morning began with perfect propriety. At six, the doors
opened. At seven, the cabbages were watered. At eight, the
pigs were combed. At nine, the Council met to debate
whether the western sundials cast shadows excessively to the east.
At ten, the debate was adjourned, all parties having agreed
that the sun was probably at fault. At eleven, six
hundred cooking-pots received six hundred cabbages.

At thirteen minutes before noon, however, little Greta Glumple—whose testimony
must be treated cautiously, since she was only eight and
had once imagined a cloud—pointed toward the eastern hill.

Something stood upon its summit.

The fathers lifted their spectacles. The mothers shaded their eyes.
The children opened their mouths. The pigs, being lower, saw
nothing.

At first the object resembled a black pin stuck into
the sky. Then it lengthened. Then it bent. Then it
sprang upward, flourished two arms, kicked one leg toward the
clouds, and began descending the hill in a manner so
contrary to gravity, dignity, and municipal precedent that several citizens
sat down without consulting their watches.

It was a man.

Or, to be exact, it was a thing dressed as
a man, though no proper man could have arranged himself
so maliciously. He was excessively tall and outrageously thin. His
coat was half black and half yellow, with one tail
short and the other dragging behind him like a disappointed
serpent. One stocking was red, the other striped. His shoes
turned up at the toes and bore tiny bells which
jingled at all the wrong moments. Upon his head perched
a sugarloaf hat decorated with a white feather, a brass
spoon, and what appeared to be the wing of a
bat.

His face was narrow; his nose was long; his chin
was longer. His eyes glittered beneath shaggy brows, and his
mouth stretched from cheek to cheek in a grin of
insufferable arithmetic—as though he had counted us and found one
too many.

Under his arm he carried a fiddle.

Now, the fiddle is not unknown to historical science. I
have inspected three. But this instrument was of a crooked,
reddish wood, with strings that seemed to twitch before they
were touched, and a bow so long that the rascal
used it alternately as cane, balancing-pole, sword, fishing-rod, and instrument
of personal insult.

Down the hill he came—hop, slide, twirl, tumble, recover; hop,
slide, twirl, tumble, recover—playing all the while a tune which
began nowhere, proceeded backward, and ended before it began. It
was not a march, for it marched sideways; not a
waltz, for it had five feet; not a gavotte, though
Madame Trippentrapp detected in it a corrupted *pas de chou*;
and certainly not music, unless cats falling through crockery may
be admitted to the conservatory.

At nine minutes before noon he entered the eastern gate.

"Stop!" cried the gatekeeper.

The stranger leaped over him.

"Name!" cried the registrar.

The stranger played three squeaks and a groan.

"Business!" cried the tax collector.

The stranger bowed, removed the collector's hat with his bow,
placed it upon a pig, and danced onward.

O villain! O unlicensed caterwauler! Yet I proceed calmly.

Through the market-place he zigzagged, though all civic movement had
always been circular. He skipped across gardens, crushed exactly one
cabbage in each, turned the sundials backward, opened doors that
should have been shut, shut doors that should have been
open, wound the tails of seventeen cats, and exchanged the
watches of Councillors Knopf and Knapp, who immediately accused each
other of being three minutes old.

The fathers shouted. The mothers shrieked. The children applauded until
corrected. The canaries sang between quarter-hours. The pigs pursued the
tax collector's hat.

At six minutes before noon the stranger reached the clock
tower.

The door was locked by seven bolts, three chains, and
an ordinance. He touched it with the tip of his
bow.

It flew open.

He entered. We heard his shoes upon the stair:

Jingle—scrape—jingle—thump!

Round and round he climbed, while above us old Dunderkopf,
having completed his inspection of the southern window, prepared himself
in innocence for the sacred stroke.

At four minutes before noon came a crash.

At three minutes, a yell.

At two minutes, the belfry window burst outward, and Dunderkopf's
cap floated down.

At one minute, we saw them.

There, behind the great blue face, struggled the hereditary guardian
and the fiddling scoundrel. Dunderkopf wielded his oil-can; the stranger
parried with his bow. Dunderkopf thrust with a winding-key; the
stranger ducked, spun, and struck the old man's bald crown—ping!—then
his left shoulder—pong!—then his right knee—piddle-paddle-ping! In this infamous manner
he converted the assault into a species of percussion accompaniment,
while his fiddle squealed above it like a fox disputing
theology.

The citizens stood frozen. No one had ever been beaten
in the belfry. There was no regulation for it.

Then the clock began to strike.

BONG!

"Von!" cried every father.

Ting! went every household clock.

BONG!

"Doo!" cried every mother.

Ting! ting! replied the watches.

BONG!

"Dree!" shouted the children.

BONG!

"Four!"

The stranger appeared at the highest window. He had tied
poor Dunderkopf to the pendulum by his beard. With every
swing the old man traveled right and left, right and
left, his boots describing arcs of melancholy precision.

BONG!

"Five!"

The cabbage-pots bubbled.

BONG!

"Six!"

The fathers drew their chairs toward the tables.

BONG!

"Seven!"

The mothers lifted their ladles.

BONG!

"Eight!"

The children tucked napkins beneath their chins.

BONG!

"Nine!"

The cats approached the scraps.

BONG!

"Ten!"

The canaries ceased singing.

BONG!

"Eleven!"

Every knife rose.

BONG!

"Dvelf!"

Every knife descended. Every lid came off. Every mouth opened.
Every citizen inhaled the lawful perfume of cabbage.

There should have been silence.

There was a pause—small, black, and dreadful.

The stranger grinned.

He seized the hammer-rope with both hands, kicked his heels
above his head, and dropped.

BONG!

"DIRTEEN!"

The word did not at first seem to belong to
any human language. It burst simultaneously from six hundred citizens,
sixty pigs, an uncertain number of children who counted twice,
and old Dunderkopf, who uttered it upside down while passing
the left extremity of the pendulum.

Thirteen!

Reader, imagine it if you can. I cannot, and I
was there.

Every clock in Vondervotteimittiss, from the Bürgermeister's ten-foot regulator to
the baby's tin repeater, struck again. Mantel clocks hopped from
mantelpieces. Cuckoos rushed from their doors and refused to return.
Pendulums twisted like snakes. Minute-hands chased hour-hands. Alarms exploded without
appointments. Watches tore loose from waistcoat pockets and spun upon
their chains; and the wooden watches attached to the pigs'
tails clattered so violently that the pigs, supposing themselves pursued
by carpenters, galloped through the market-place.

Dinner collapsed.

Some cabbages, interpreting the extra stroke as an instruction to
boil another hour, dissolved at once into green vapor. Others
hardened like cannonballs. One leaped from its pot, bounded through
a window, and struck my hat—the dent to which I
have already alluded. Soup ran uphill. Spoons rang against foreheads.
The fathers swallowed their pipes; the mothers put tobacco into
the sugar bowls; the children, released from arithmetic by the
failure of twelve, began counting everything beyond necessity.

"Dirteen, fourteen, fifteen—"

"Silence!" roared Master Ticktack. "Dere is no fifteen after dinner!"

But the disorder increased.

The cats barked at the canaries. The canaries crowed. The
pigs climbed garden walls. The sundials cast shadows in several
directions, although the sun, to its credit, remained comparatively sober.
Councillor Knopf discovered that Councillor Knapp's watch made him younger
and refused to return it. Madame Trippentrapp danced a funeral
polka. The tax collector chased his hat, which still rode
the largest pig and appeared to have assumed executive authority.

Above all sounded that diabolical fiddle.

The intruder sat astride the weathercock, sawing and scraping, jingling
and screeching, while with one foot he pulled the bell-rope
and with the other he kicked the minute-hand backward. The
tune multiplied itself. It came from chimneys, kettles, keyholes, cupboard
hinges, shoe buckles, and the hollow interiors of cabbages. The
tower swayed. The houses nodded. The sixty green doors opened
and shut in rhythm. The blue roofs lifted like hats.
Even the twelve hills seemed to bob, though Professor Knipperklatsch
later attributed this to collective dizziness.

"Stop him!" cried the Bürgermeister.

"How?" cried the Council.

"By precedent!"

"There is none!"

"Then appoint a committee!"

A committee was appointed, but before its chairman could state
the purpose of its formation, the Great Clock struck fourteen.

Or perhaps it struck one again. Authorities differ, and my
ink was shaken from the bottle.

At that instant every routine in the borough became entangled
with every other. Citizens went to bed while chewing dinner,
rose before lying down, wound their pigs, watered their watches,
polished their children, scolded their cabbages, and placed their clocks
in the oven. Three fathers departed for school. Nine canaries
were elected to the Council. The Clerk of Slumbers recorded
an awake dream and fined himself.

I confess—and history shall not blush for prudent retreat—that I
fled.

I passed the eastern gate while the villain's laughter rattled
the windows behind me. I crossed the forbidden slope. I
reached the summit and looked back once.

There lay Vondervotteimittiss, no longer circular but wriggling; its roofs
seesawing, its chimneys puffing backward, its inhabitants running clockwise and
counterclockwise at once. Over the tower flapped the bat-wing feather
of the fiddler's hat. Beneath him swung poor Dunderkopf. Around
him spun the hands of the Great Clock.

And again the bell sounded.

BONG!

How many strokes have followed, I do not know. Perhaps
it is striking still.

Therefore I make this appeal—not as partisan, not as citizen,
not as a man whose luncheon was ruined, but as
an impartial historian devoted solely to truth, order, punctual digestion,
and the ancient liberties of cabbage.

Let every friend of accurate time come forward! Let all
who wind their watches on Sunday, who respect the hour,
who distrust irregular music, who believe that twelve is sufficient
for any clock and that hills were intended to keep
strangers out—let them take up broom, umbrella, warming-pan, cabbage-knife, or
other constitutional weapon!

We shall return to Vondervotteimittiss.

We shall climb the tower.

We shall rescue Dunderkopf, restore the pendulum, boil the cabbages
anew, return the tax collector's hat, and drive that scraping,
jingling, grinning, thirteenth-stroking rascal beyond the twelve respectable hills.

Who is with me?

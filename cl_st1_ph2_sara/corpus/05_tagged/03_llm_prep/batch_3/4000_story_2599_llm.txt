# THE LAST FORMULA OF HERBERT WEST

## I. The Sound Beneath the Willows

Of Herbert West, whose disappearance from Boston remains an irritating
mystery to the police and an object of furtive speculation
among certain members of the medical profession, I can speak
with an authority which I profoundly wish were less complete.
The newspapers were wrong in nearly every particular, though I
cannot blame their editors; for the facts I gave the
authorities were of a sort which no sane man could
accept, and my own connexion with the ghastly business rendered
discretion imperative. They found blood, structural damage, and indications of
subterranean entrance, but no body. Since no body could be
produced, they inferred flight, murder by unknown persons, or some
criminal stratagem of West's own devising.

They did not believe that the dead had taken him.

Even now I dislike writing the sentence. There are combinations
of words which appear to carry with them a taint
deeper than their meaning, and this is one. Yet I
know what I saw in the old house beyond the
Fenway; I know what manner of shapes came soundlessly through
the opened wall; and I know whose waxen, still-living face
regarded us above a military collar whilst the others performed
their dreadful office. Before I can speak of that final
night, however, I must relate the earlier incidents—those progressive violations
which made the end not merely possible, but inevitable.

I first met West when we were students at the
medical school of Miskatonic University in Arkham. He was then,
as always afterward, slight of frame and almost deceptively youthful,
with fine yellow hair, pale-blue eyes, and spectacles whose lenses
caught the white glare of the laboratory lamps. His voice
was low and without emphasis. Even when proposing the most
abominable measures, he spoke as another might speak of altering
a diet or improving a surgical instrument.

His governing belief was simple. Life, he maintained, was not
an occult principle but a mechanical and chemical process. Death
was therefore not a metaphysical departure, but a cessation which
might be reversed if the tissues had not undergone decisive
decomposition. He had compounded a reagent which, when injected into
the principal vessels, would restore cellular activity and renew the
interrupted functions of the nervous system.

The faculty interdicted his work. Dean Allan Halsey, a physician
of venerable reputation and genuine kindness, declared that West's conclusions
had outrun his evidence, and that his methods were not
only dangerous but morally indefensible. West received this judgment with
his customary pallid calm.

"They object," he told me, "because they are still theologians
under their white coats."

I ought to have withdrawn from him then. Instead, fascinated
by his reasoning and flattered by his confidence, I became
his assistant.

Our first human experiment took place in a deserted farmhouse
two miles beyond Arkham, where tangled willows leaned over a
black, sluggish reach of the Miskatonic. We had fitted one
downstairs room with a table, a small furnace, electrical batteries,
surgical apparatus, and several bottles of West's cloudy, straw-coloured solution.
Heavy blankets covered the windows. We worked by dark lanterns,
since the least glimmer might have attracted the attention of
fishermen or of the vagrants who occasionally slept in the
barns.

The chief difficulty was obtaining what West called a sufficiently
fresh specimen.

For several weeks we watched the newspapers, visited hospital offices
under transparent pretexts, and made nocturnal surveys of the poorer
burying grounds. Then a labourer was drowned near Sefton's Mill.
The body, recovered within three hours, shewed no serious injury
and was buried the following afternoon in the village cemetery.
There were no relatives in the district.

That night, equipped with spades, lantern, ropes, and a canvas
sack containing our instruments, we went to the grave.

I shall not dwell upon the exhumation. Men imagine grave
robbery as an act of frantic blasphemy, but its actual
character is more hideous because it is so laborious. The
earth was wet and adhesive. Roots impeded the spades. Our
breath clouded before us, and once a dog barked from
a distant farm, causing me to remain crouched and motionless
for several minutes whilst West continued digging. When the lid
was exposed, he wiped his spectacles and examined his watch.

"Excellent," he whispered. "Interment has cooled him without freezing the
tissues."

The drowned man was broad-shouldered, coarse-featured, and of immense weight.
We carried him in a farm cart to the laboratory,
where West immediately began his preparations. The neck and arms
were cleaned; the vessels were exposed; and measured quantities of
reagent were introduced, first through the carotid artery and afterward
into the veins. West varied the concentration according to an
elaborate table derived from experiments upon animals.

For an hour we watched.

Nothing happened.

The face remained grey and swollen. The eyes did not
open. There was no contraction of the muscles, no alteration
in the temperature, and no perceptible cardiac response. At half
past three West admitted failure. He blamed the solution, saying
that the proportions had been calculated for smaller organisms and
that human tissue required a more powerful stimulus.

We left the body upon the table. Our nerves were
exhausted, and dawn was not far off. Before departing, West
poured several compounds into the furnace, fearing that the odour
might betray our activities should any wanderer enter the house.

We had crossed the meadow and reached the line of
willows when the sound arose behind us.

It was not a human cry, though it came from
a human throat. At first it was low and confused,
like the groaning of machinery beneath a great burden; then
it climbed with a rapidity that froze the blood, swelling
into a shriek of such daemoniac agony that the night
itself seemed to recoil from it. Birds burst from the
trees. A horse in a neighbouring pasture began to kick
against its fence. The shriek ceased, returned at a higher
pitch, and broke into a succession of raw, mindless howls.

West dropped the lantern.

We ran toward Arkham without speaking.

Behind us a red brightness appeared beyond the willows. One
of the compounds cast into the furnace must have ignited;
before sunrise the farmhouse was a roofless shell. We told
ourselves that the revived thing had perished in the fire,
and for a time even West seemed content to believe
it.

Three days later the village cemetery-keeper reported that the drowned
labourer's grave had been disturbed from beneath. The coffin was
empty. On the inner surface of the lid were splintered
furrows and dark fragments from ten torn fingernails.

After that discovery, I began to dread West's softest words.

## II. The Dean During the Fever

The first horrible incident should have ended our experiments, but
Herbert West possessed the peculiar courage of men to whom
conscience is merely an impediment in calculation. He admitted that
the drowned labourer had revived, and regarded the result not
as a warning but as an incomplete triumph. The screams,
convulsions, and apparent loss of reason he attributed to the
interval between death and injection.

"The brain deteriorates first," he said. "Restore circulation too late,
and one awakens only the lower centres. What we saw
was not the effect of my reagent, but of delay."

Thus the word *fresh* acquired for us a significance which
no wholesome mind could endure. West repeated it continually. A
fresh body; fresh cerebral tissue; a fresh cessation of the
heart. In lecture rooms and hospital wards he watched the
sick with a cold expectancy which I found increasingly difficult
to share. Nevertheless, I remained beside him. Habit, fear, intellectual
curiosity, and the knowledge of what we had already done
bound me more firmly than friendship.

Our second great opportunity came during the Arkham typhoid epidemic.

The disease spread first among factory families near the river,
then through boarding houses and student quarters, until every physician
in the county was occupied. Temporary wards were established in
college buildings. Nurses collapsed from exhaustion. Funeral carts travelled at
dawn to avoid frightening the living, and the bells of
three churches ceased tolling because their repetition had become intolerable.

West and I served throughout the crisis. In public he
was admirable—precise, indefatigable, and wholly free from panic. In private
he was transformed by opportunity. Death occurred everywhere, often only
a few doors from locked rooms in which his apparatus
might be concealed. Yet circumstances repeatedly thwarted him. Relatives remained
with the bodies; orderlies kept records; undertakers arrived too swiftly;
or the victims exhibited internal lesions which made them scientifically
undesirable.

Then Dean Halsey fell ill.

No man had laboured more courageously during the epidemic. Though
past middle age, he moved from ward to ward with
a strength that shamed younger men, and more than once
I saw him give up his own rest to comfort
some delirious stranger. When the fever seized him, the whole
medical school waited anxiously. He died shortly after midnight on
the fourth day.

West was beside me when the news came. Behind his
spectacles his eyes shewed no grief, only a sudden and
terrible concentration.

Halsey's body was placed temporarily in an unused dissecting-room, pending
removal in the morning. West had access to the building
and knew that the exhausted attendants would not return for
several hours. At two o'clock we carried the dean through
a connecting passage to a private laboratory.

I resisted then—not with the force decency required, but enough
to expose the wretched remnants of my moral sense. Halsey
had been our teacher. He had trusted us in the
wards and had opposed West not from jealousy, but from
humane conviction. To use his body seemed an outrage different
in kind from the violation of an unknown labourer's grave.

West listened and adjusted the flame beneath a steriliser.

"He is ideal," he said. "If his convictions were correct,
nothing will happen. If mine were correct, he will owe
me his return."

The injections were made within an hour of death. Because
the fever had weakened the circulation, West employed several points
of entry and added a stimulating preparation of his own
invention. A tremor appeared in the left hand. The lips
parted. Then the entire body bent upward with such violence
that the leather straps creaked against the table.

The eyes opened.

Nothing of Allan Halsey looked through them.

The creature snapped its teeth and emitted a hoarse coughing
bark. It tore one wrist free before we could increase
the restraints, and the hand which struck West possessed a
strength appallingly disproportionate to the wasted arm. He was flung
against a cabinet. Glass shattered; a bottle fell; and before
I could reach the door, the thing that had been
our dean rolled from the table and came toward me
upon hands and knees.

Its movements were neither wholly human nor recognisably animal. Fever
had drawn the cheeks tight, and the uncovered teeth shone
through blood from the bitten lips. There was no reason
in the face—only hunger, pain, and a malign animation far
more dreadful than immobility.

I seized an iron stool and struck it across the
shoulder. The blow would have disabled a living man. Halsey
turned, sprang upon me, and would certainly have torn out
my throat had West not driven a long needle into
the base of the skull. The creature convulsed and fell,
but before we could secure it, the drug cupboard—overturned during
the struggle—burst against the side door and forced it open.

Halsey escaped into the night.

Arkham remembers the crimes that followed, though no historian connects
them with the epidemic. Two men were found dead near
Water Street, their faces and hands mutilated by bites. A
child disappeared from a yard behind a laundry. Later, a
night watchman surprised a crouching shape in an alley and
was nearly killed before police subdued it with clubs and
nets. Because the captured being wore only a torn hospital
sheet and possessed no intelligible speech, it was assumed to
be a wandering lunatic from some foreign ship or charitable
institution.

The face was swollen, scarred, and befouled beyond easy recognition.
Only West and I knew that the howling prisoner taken
to Sefton Asylum had once been Dean Allan Halsey.

In later years, visitors occasionally remarked upon an extraordinarily violent
patient confined in the lowest ward—an ageing creature who beat
his head against the walls and reacted with inexplicable fury
to the odour of antiseptics. The asylum physicians never learned
his identity.

West expressed regret, but not for the murders.

"The fever altered the cerebral tissues," he said. "He wasn't
quite fresh enough."

## III. The Thing from the Potter's Field

After graduation we established a small practice in Bolton, a
manufacturing town whose eastern outskirts terminated in marshy fields and
a neglected burial ground used chiefly for paupers. The choice
was not accidental. Our house stood at the end of
a narrow road, screened by elms and separated from the
nearest neighbour by an abandoned orchard. Beneath it lay a
spacious cellar, which we divided into a surgery, a chemical
room, and a windowless chamber fitted with a steel table.

By then West had refined his reagent through innumerable trials
upon animals and upon fragments obtained from hospital waste. He
spoke less frequently of public recognition. His original ambition had
hardened into secrecy, and secrecy into obsession. I noticed also
that he had begun carrying a revolver.

The affair of Buck Robinson was one terrible and unforgettable
occasion, though at first it seemed no more than another
failure.

Robinson was a professional boxer of formidable size who had
fallen into disrepute through drinking and participation in unlicenced matches.
One winter night a contest was arranged in a barn
beyond Meadow Hill. West had treated several of the organisers
for minor injuries, and through these dubious patients we were
summoned when Robinson collapsed after a blow behind the ear.

We arrived to find the revellers fled and the dead
man stretched upon a blanket. His opponent insisted that Robinson
had remained conscious after the blow, then suddenly stiffened and
ceased breathing. There was no external wound beyond a bruise,
and death had occurred scarcely fifteen minutes before our arrival.

West's hand trembled as he examined the pupils.

He told the remaining men that the authorities would prosecute
everyone involved. If they allowed us to dispose privately of
the body, however, he would certify that Robinson had died
at our house from an unsuspected seizure. Fear persuaded them.
We carried the boxer away in our closed motor van
whilst snow began to cover the wheel tracks.

Never had we possessed a specimen so fresh, so muscular,
or so little injured. West performed the injections at once.
He used a stronger solution than before, reasoning that Robinson's
massive body demanded greater volume, and he supplemented it with
direct electrical stimulation of the heart.

There was no response.

For three hours we repeated the procedure. At last West
concluded that the blow had caused some obscure injury to
the brain stem. We could not report the death without
risking an inquiry, so before dawn we buried Robinson in
the potter's field behind our house. The grave was shallow
because the frozen ground resisted our tools, but we covered
it with boards, stones, and a mound of earth sufficient
to conceal our work.

The following week brought a thaw.

On the fifth night a woman living near the mills
reported the disappearance of her six-year-old son. Search parties examined
wells, sheds, rail yards, and the river. At noon a
bloodstained cap was found in a drainage ditch. West read
the report without comment, though I saw him glance toward
the burial ground.

That evening we inspected the grave.

The earth had collapsed inward. The boards were broken and
pushed aside.

What happened next returns to me whenever I hear heavy
footsteps in darkness. We were standing beside the empty pit
when something moved among the elms. There followed a thick,
wet breathing, and then the crack of a branch under
tremendous weight. West raised his lantern.

Buck Robinson stood at the edge of the light.

Decay had bloated rather than weakened him. Soil adhered to
his skin, and one eye hung closed beneath the bruised
temple. In his arms he carried something small, pale, and
dreadfully limp. When the lantern-beam touched his face, he dropped
his burden and came toward us.

No articulate sound issued from him. His lips drew back,
and a guttural vibration rolled in his chest like the
growl of some anthropomorphic beast dreamed by mediaeval monks. He
moved slowly for two steps, then rushed with hideous speed.

West fired six times.

The first bullets struck the torso without perceptible effect. The
fourth broke the jaw. The fifth entered the eye, and
the sixth shattered the upper skull. Robinson continued forward long
enough to seize the front of West's coat; then he
sagged, pulling West to his knees beneath the enormous corpse.

We burned what remained in the surgery furnace over a
period of two days. The smell penetrated the house despite
every chemical precaution.

The child's body was returned anonymously to the drainage ditch,
and public suspicion settled upon a gang of wandering criminals
who had recently passed through Bolton. No inquiry touched us.
Yet after Robinson, even West could no longer pretend that
his revived subjects were merely patients damaged by temporary delirium.
Something essential was absent when death had endured too long.
Movement returned, appetite returned, and fragments of instinct returned; but
memory, judgment, and human restraint were lost in the interval.

This conclusion did not deter him. It only increased his
hunger for immediacy.

We therefore ceased waiting for nature.

The man West selected was a travelling salesman named Edwin
Lyman, who called at our house with catalogues of surgical
equipment. He was about forty, in sound health, and had
told us casually that he was unmarried and expected in
Providence no earlier than the following week. I left West
speaking with him in the consultation-room and went to the
cellar to prepare a culture.

When I returned, Lyman was dead.

He sat upright in a chair, his head inclined as
though sleeping. A tiny puncture marked the skin behind the
ear. West stood beside him holding an empty syringe.

For a moment I could neither move nor speak.

"It is the only rigorous test," West said. "No trauma,
no disease, no deterioration, and no delay."

I accused him of murder. The word sounded feeble in
that curtained room. West did not deny it; he merely
observed that death would be of brief duration if his
theory were correct. His composure compelled me more surely than
any threat. Within minutes I was helping him carry Lyman
downstairs.

The reagent entered the veins eleven minutes after the heart
had stopped.

The response was almost immediate. Colour returned faintly to the
face. The chest shuddered and drew breath. One hand closed
upon the edge of the table. Then the eyes opened,
not with the vacant ferocity we had come to dread,
but with confusion, fear, and recognisable intelligence.

The man looked first at me, then at West.

His lips moved twice before sound emerged.

"You killed me," he whispered.

West stepped nearer, fascinated.

Lyman's expression changed. Memory appeared to gather in him with
the dreadful swiftness of a storm. He raised one arm
and pointed directly at West.

"You put the needle—"

A convulsion interrupted him. Blood welled from his mouth; the
revived heart, perhaps weakened by the poison West had used,
faltered under the stimulant. He died again before we could
intervene.

That accusation was the most important success of West's career.
It proved that prompt treatment could restore personality and recollection.
It also proved, though West refused to acknowledge it, that
the dead might remember their murderer.

We dissolved Edwin Lyman's remains in acid.

For months afterward, West woke at slight sounds and reached
for his revolver.

## IV. The Head That Gave Orders

The Great War furnished Herbert West with opportunities on a
scale which peace and civilisation had denied him. We entered
military service together and were eventually attached to a Canadian
surgical unit operating behind the lines in Flanders. The official
laboratories dealt with infection, transfusion, and the treatment of gas
injuries; but in a ruined stone building beyond the main
hospital tents, West established a second facility under the pretext
of pathological research.

There, whilst artillery muttered beyond the horizon and ambulances arrived
burdened with the fresh wreckage of battle, his experiments multiplied.

War had altered the meaning of horror. Actions which in
Bolton would have shaken a whole county were lost amid
the arithmetic of offensives. Bodies arrived without names; fragments were
gathered into blankets; men living at sunset were specimens before
dawn. West moved through this organised Tartarus with a serenity
almost cheerful. He altered his formula, devised portable injectors, and
attempted revivification upon soldiers dead from shock, haemorrhage, gas, and
concussion.

The results were uniformly ghastly.

One man recovered respiration but screamed without pause until we
destroyed him. Another opened his eyes and repeated the final
syllable of a prayer for six hours. Several exhibited muscular
activity without consciousness. A young infantryman sat upright, saluted an
absent officer, and then gnawed through his own tongue. We
learned to restrain every subject before treatment, and to keep
pistols near the operating table.

Yet West was satisfied that he approached perfection. In his
view, the battlefield failures resulted from injuries too extensive for
the restored machinery to function. What he required was a
man killed instantly, with the brain preserved and treatment administered
at once.

Major Eric Moreland Clapham-Lee appeared to fulfil these conditions in
all but one respect.

He had been a surgeon before the war and knew
something of West's theories. More than once he visited our
hidden laboratory, where his curiosity—though never so ruthless as West's—led
him to assist with preparations. He was a tall, dark-haired
man of uncommon self-command, and possessed a habit of looking
at West with an expression I could not interpret. Whether
it implied admiration, distrust, or an anticipation of eventual conflict,
I do not know.

During a German advance, Clapham-Lee's aeroplane was struck and crashed
near our position. Soldiers brought him to us within minutes.

His body was scarcely marked.

His head was missing.

It was found soon afterward in the wreckage of the
cockpit, severed cleanly by a twisted metal brace. I believed
even West would consider the case hopeless. Instead, his eyes
blazed behind his spectacles with that pale and unnatural ardour
which had first terrified me in Arkham.

"The brain is intact," he said. "The separation is irrelevant
if both systems are supplied independently."

We placed the body upon the steel table and the
head in a wide enamel basin cushioned with gauze. West
injected the torso through the great vessels of the neck,
sealing them around glass tubes connected to a mechanical pump.
For the head he prepared a separate circulation involving oxygenated
blood and a concentrated modification of the reagent.

The body responded first.

Its hands opened and closed. The chest rose. One arm
reached outward with deliberate motion, not the random spasm of
our earlier subjects, and tapped three times against the table.
At that moment the eyelids of the severed head fluttered.

Clapham-Lee looked at us.

There was intelligence in the gaze, but also pain so
absolute that I staggered back from the basin. The lips
formed words without audible breath. West bent over them, watching
intently, and after several efforts succeeded in attaching a tube
from the air pump to the divided trachea.

A bubbling whisper came.

"Body."

The hands upon the table clenched.

West asked whether Clapham-Lee understood him. The head blinked once.
Asked if he could command the separated body, it blinked
again; and immediately the right hand rose and pointed toward
the basin.

This was no bestial resurrection. Identity had survived. Consciousness existed
independently in the restored cerebral tissue, and some obscure connexion—electrical,
chemical, or of a nature which West would have raged
to hear called spiritual—remained between head and torso.

We had little time to study the phenomenon.

The bombardment resumed shortly after midnight. Shells fell near the
hospital, overturning lamps and showering the stone building with mortar.
Orderlies shouted outside, but West would not abandon his apparatus.
He was attempting to connect the severed head more completely
to the artificial circulation when a shell struck the adjoining
chamber.

I remember a white flash, an impact without sound, and
the sensation of falling through hot dust.

When I recovered consciousness, the laboratory was burning. Half the
roof had collapsed. West dragged me through an opening in
the wall seconds before the remaining masonry fell. Explosions from
stored oxygen and chemicals followed, reducing the place to a
crater of smoking stone.

We found no trace of Clapham-Lee's head or body. West
believed both had been destroyed.

It would have been better if we could have known
that the body had crawled from the wreckage before the
final collapse, carrying the enamel basin beneath one arm.

Years afterward a sentry confessed that he had seen such
a figure crossing a trench during the bombardment. He had
dismissed it as a hallucination caused by exhaustion: a headless
officer walking upright through the smoke, guided by a voice
which seemed to issue from something held against his breast.

## V. The Men Who Had No Graves

After the war we settled in Boston, where West's reputation
as a physician grew in curious proportion to the secret
degradation of his studies. He acquired a large old house
whose rear windows overlooked a disused branch of the city's
ancient subterranean drainage system. The respectable rooms above were furnished
with severity, but the cellar became a labyrinth of chemical
benches, cold chambers, cages, and locked cabinets.

By then West had lost much of his former delicacy.
He remained slight and pale, but fine lines had gathered
about his eyes, and his soft speech was punctuated by
abrupt silences in which he appeared to listen. He employed
multiple bolts on every door. Loaded firearms lay in desk
drawers, beneath cushions, and inside steriliser cabinets. He dismissed servants
after a few days and trusted no messenger who came
after dark.

His fear began with rumours.

From Sefton Asylum came a report that the unidentified violent
patient had escaped during a disturbance. An attendant was found
with a broken neck, and the lower ward door appeared
to have been forced outward by several persons, though only
one prisoner was missing.

Soon afterward, Bolton newspapers described the desecration of the potter's
field. Several graves had been opened. Among them was the
plot in which Buck Robinson had first been placed, though
West and I knew that nothing remained there. The significance
lay not in what had been taken, but in who
might have come looking.

Then a man visited our Boston house whilst West was
away.

He wore a long military coat despite the warmth, with
the collar turned high and the hat drawn low. His
posture was stiff. One gloved hand held a square black
case. He asked for West in a voice so faint
and metallic that I could not determine whether the sound
came from his throat or from some concealed instrument.

When I said Dr. West was absent, the visitor remained
motionless.

At last he placed the case upon the hall table.

"For the doctor," he murmured.

The hand that released the handle was grey and seamed
with old surgical scars.

I did not attempt to detain him. Through the window
I watched him cross the street and enter a closed
motor vehicle. Several figures sat within. One was extraordinarily large;
another bent forward with a bald, misshapen head; and beside
them crouched something whose face, glimpsed for only an instant,
recalled with soul-petrifying clarity the fever-wasted features of Dean Halsey.

West returned at dusk. When he saw the case, every
trace of colour left his face.

Inside lay a military identity disc belonging to Major Clapham-Lee,
a coil of rubber tubing, and a sheet of paper
bearing six words in block capitals:

**THE FRESH REMEMBER. THE OTHERS OBEY.**

West burned the message, though his hands shook so violently
that he dropped it twice.

For the first time he told me he regretted failing
to catalogue every subject. There had been more experiments than
even I knew—bodies treated in Arkham during the epidemic, anonymous
soldiers in Flanders, criminals obtained through dubious undertakers, and portions
of human tissue subjected to formulae in sealed containers. Most
had been destroyed, but West's standards of destruction were not
always exact. Fire, burial, acid, and dismemberment had seemed adequate
when we believed revived matter incapable of plan or co-operation.

Clapham-Lee had disproved that belief.

A brain separated from its body had retained intelligence. If
it also retained memory of West, it might seek those
lesser creatures whose nervous centres responded only to appetite and
command. Halsey could be found in Sefton. Robinson's remains, West
insisted, had been burned; but as he reviewed his notes
he became uncertain whether certain portions had been fully consumed.
There were others—nameless things buried in fields, locked in institutions,
or abandoned amid war.

The possibility of their assembling was inexpressibly dreadful. Yet West's
response was not repentance. It was preparation.

He reinforced the cellar doors, electrified the window grilles, and
mixed large quantities of an acid intended to dissolve organic
matter. He also developed a blue solution which, he claimed,
would arrest the revived tissues instantly by precipitating a vital
compound within the nerves. Syringes filled with this antidote were
placed in every room.

We slept little.

At night there were sounds under the house. Some were
undoubtedly the ordinary noises of pipes, rats, and settling masonry;
others had a deliberation no such causes could explain. Three
knocks would come from behind the cellar wall. After a
pause, there would be three more. Once I heard a
slow dragging movement beneath the floor of my bedroom, followed
by a faint bubbling murmur that pronounced my name.

West denied hearing it.

The police were of no assistance. We could not explain
our fears without confessing acts sufficient to ruin us, and
the vague complaint of suspicious strangers produced only a perfunctory
patrol. West considered leaving Boston, but some final remnant of
scientific pride held him. I think he wished to see
Clapham-Lee again. Terror and curiosity had become indistinguishable in him.

On the last evening, a heavy rain fell, flooding the
streets and gurgling through the ancient drains. West sat in
the library with three revolvers, two syringes, and a lamp
whose light made his face appear as colourless as the
dead he had awakened.

At half past midnight we heard stone break below us.

West rose.

"They are in the cellar," he said.

There was almost relief in his voice.

## VI. The Passage Under the House

Of the final catastrophe I can provide only the testimony
of my senses, and I know too well how little
that testimony is worth to men who have not heard
the dead moving in darkness.

We descended together. West carried a revolver in each hand,
whilst I bore the lantern and a metal case containing
the blue antidote. The electric lights had failed, though no
fuse was damaged. Water dripped somewhere behind the walls. With
each step the air grew colder and acquired an odour
compounded of damp earth, hospital chemicals, and opened graves.

The innermost laboratory appeared undisturbed. Steel instruments reflected the lantern.
Bottles stood in ordered ranks. Beyond the central table, however,
a line had appeared in the brick wall—a vertical fissure
where no doorway existed. Dust sifted from it. Then, with
ponderous slowness, a whole section of masonry leaned inward and
collapsed upon the floor.

Behind it yawned a black passage.

The tunnel was older than the house, perhaps part of
a colonial drain or forgotten conduit leading toward the river.
Its brickwork glistened with moisture. From the darkness came a
soft, multitudinous shuffling.

West raised his weapons.

The first figure entered the lantern-light upon all fours.

It had once been Allan Halsey.

Years in confinement and the chemical preservation of West's reagent
had altered him beyond ordinary decay. The face was hairless
and deeply scarred; the eyes rolled beneath milky films; and
the fingers terminated in thick, broken nails. Yet around one
wrist remained a corroded metal band from Sefton Asylum. He
sniffed the air, turned toward West, and uttered the same
coughing bark I had heard in Arkham.

Behind him came Buck Robinson.

I cannot explain his presence. Perhaps some portion overlooked in
our furnace had retained sufficient vitality to grow, or perhaps
West had lied when he claimed complete destruction. The colossal
body was patched with scars and burned tissue. One side
of the skull had been repaired with metal plates held
by wire, and the mouth hung crooked from the bullet
which had shattered the jaw. He moved upright, but with
the rolling heaviness of a bear.

Others followed: a soldier with a caved-in chest; a thin
figure whose neck bore the mark of a needle; a
thing wrapped in rotting bandages; and shapes I had never
seen, but which West recognised. With each arrival he retreated
until his back touched the steel table.

They did not attack.

They arranged themselves in two ragged lines and waited.

Last came Clapham-Lee.

His body wore the remnants of an officer's uniform under
a long black coat. The head was still absent. In
his left hand he carried a glass vessel fitted with
tubes and a small mechanical bellows. Within the vessel, supported
by dark fluid, rested the preserved head.

The eyes were open.

One tube entered the throat. With each compression of the
bellows, air passed through the ruined vocal apparatus and produced
a low, bubbling articulation. The dead officer's right hand worked
the device with grave regularity.

"Herbert West," said the head.

West fired.

The bullet struck the glass, but did not break it;
some metal shield, cunningly shaped around the vessel, deflected the
shot. At once Buck Robinson crossed the room and struck
the revolvers from West's hands. I opened the case of
antidote, but Halsey sprang upon me, hurling me against the
wall. The lantern fell without extinguishing, and in its sideways
glare I saw the assembled dead close around their maker.

West did not cry out then. He spoke rapidly, as
if addressing a hostile scientific society. He declared that Clapham-Lee's
survival vindicated the formula, that the degradation of the others
arose from post-mortem delay, and that all defects could be
corrected if they submitted to examination. Even at that ultimate
moment he sought experiment where another man would have sought
forgiveness.

The head watched him.

"You made us," it said.

West glanced toward me. His expression was neither pleading nor
remorseful. It was the look of a mathematician who has
discovered, one instant too late, an error hidden at the
foundation of his proof.

Clapham-Lee raised one hand.

The creatures seized West.

What followed was abrupt and almost methodical. Robinson held the
legs; the soldier gripped one arm; Halsey fastened his teeth
in the shoulder; and the others pulled with a concerted
strength no living bodies could have exerted. There was a
sound like wet cloth tearing. West screamed once—not loudly, but
with a thin incredulity more appalling than any prolonged shriek.

They tore him apart upon his own steel table.

I cannot set down the details. Certain memories refuse language,
not because they are vague, but because they are too
distinct. I remember the flash of his spectacles on the
floor. I remember one pale hand still contracting after it
had been separated from the arm. I remember Clapham-Lee's head
speaking directions whilst the headless body pointed to each piece
in turn.

The dead wrapped the fragments in rubberised cloth and carried
them into the tunnel.

Halsey remained until the last. He crouched beside me, his
ruined face so near that I felt the cold exhalation
from his nostrils. For an instant some remnant of the
old dean seemed to struggle behind the clouded eyes. He
touched my forehead with one clawed finger, as if bestowing
a grotesque benediction.

Then he followed the others.

Clapham-Lee departed last of all. At the entrance to the
passage, the head turned in its vessel and looked back
at me.

"You helped," it whispered.

The fallen masonry shifted after them, blocking the tunnel before
I recovered the use of my limbs.

Police arrived at dawn in response to reports of shots.
They found me wandering through the cellar, the rooms stained
with blood, the table overturned, and one section of wall
newly collapsed. Excavation revealed an old conduit extending for some
distance, but flooding and subsidence prevented pursuit. No human remains
were discovered. There were footprints, though the inspectors disagreed as
to their character; some were bare, some military, some abnormally
large, and some appeared to have been made by hands.

My account was naturally rejected.

The authorities concluded that West had been murdered by associates
engaged in illegal medical trafficking, and that I had suffered
a collapse brought on by shock. Later they suspected me,
but the absence of a body confounded prosecution. West's professional
colleagues preferred the theory that he had fled the country
after some private scandal. His chemical notes vanished from the
laboratory, along with every quantity of the revivifying reagent.

That disappearance troubles me more than the absence of his
corpse.

For I know the dead carried him away in pieces,
and I know that Clapham-Lee understood West's methods. Whether those
pieces were destroyed, preserved, or treated, I cannot say. On
certain nights, when rain fills the drains and the city's
buried passages awaken with furtive echoes, I hear a light,
familiar tapping beneath my floor.

Three knocks.

A pause.

Three more.

And sometimes, mingled with the subterranean gurgling, there comes a
soft voice—precise, patient, and almost apologetic—complaining from somewhere in the
dark that the specimens were not quite fresh enough.

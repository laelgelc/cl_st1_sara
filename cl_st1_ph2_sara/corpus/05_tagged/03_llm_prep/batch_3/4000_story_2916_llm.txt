### Jessamine of Cloverside

When the old Stacy place was sold, Jessamine Stacy felt
as though the very earth had been taken from beneath
her feet.

It was not a grand home. Its roof sagged over
the eastern chamber, the well-chain creaked, and the orchard yielded
more moss than apples. But sweet-brier roses climbed about the
kitchen window, robins nested in the maples, and beyond the
pasture the hills lay purple under the evening sky. Jessamine
had been born there, and there she had cared for
her father and mother until both were gone.

Her brother John had long since removed to the city.
When the farm was lost through debt, he wrote that
Jessamine must come to live with him.

"You will be comfortable here," he said. "And Clara will
be glad of your company."

Clara—Mrs. John Stacy—was stylish, brisk, and never glad of anything
that did not save her expense or increase her importance.

At first she told callers that dear Jessamine had come
to make her home with them. Before a month had
passed, Jessamine was rising before six to light the kitchen
fire, dressing the children, washing dishes, mending stockings, carrying coal,
answering the bell, and helping the hired girl, when there
was one. Presently Clara dismissed the girl altogether.

"It is foolish to keep a servant when there are
two women in the house," she said.

There were two women, certainly; but one made calls and
wore pale gloves, while the other baked, scrubbed, swept, and
went up and down three flights of stairs until her
feet ached at night.

John noticed little. He was worried about business and came
home tired.

"You know Clara has a great deal to manage," he
told Jessamine. "Be patient with her."

Jessamine was patient. She had always been patient. Yet patience
could not make the city streets green, nor turn the
smoke-darkened sky blue, nor give the hot, stifling kitchen the
fragrance of clover.

She grew thin. The roses faded from her cheeks, and
her brown hair, once curling and bright, was twisted back
without care. Sometimes, while paring potatoes beside the narrow window,
she remembered the old orchard after rain so vividly that
tears dropped upon her hands.

No one saw them.

One July morning the vegetable wagon stopped at the basement
door. Old Mr. Bell, who usually drove it, had broken
his leg, and his nephew Andrew was taking the route
for a spell.

Andrew Bell was a broad-shouldered young man with a sunburned
face, merry blue eyes, and a sprig of honeysuckle caught
in his buttonhole. He came down the steps carrying a
basket of peas.

"Fine morning," he said.

Jessamine looked beyond him at the strip of smoky sky.

"Is it?" she asked softly. "I had not noticed."

Andrew glanced at her more closely. She was pale enough
to have been ill, but her eyes were the clear
gray of a woodland brook.

"Fine out our way," he said. "The sun was coming
over the rye when I started, and there was mist
along the river. It'll be warm by noon, though."

"You live in the country?"

"At Cloverside Farm, twelve miles out. My uncle raises truck
for the city, and I help him when I'm not
busy with my own acres."

"Twelve miles," repeated Jessamine, as if he had said twelve
steps from paradise. "Are there woods near?"

"A beech wood back of the north meadow, and maples
by the house. There's a brook too, if you don't
mind getting your shoes muddy."

Jessamine smiled. It changed her face so wonderfully that Andrew
forgot the price of the peas.

"Clara!" called a sharp voice from above. "Jessamine, why are
you keeping that man?"

The smile vanished.

"I must go," she said. "How much are the peas?"

On the following morning Andrew brought beans, lettuce, and a
bunch of buttercups.

"These aren't in the bill," he said, laying the flowers
on the table.

Jessamine touched them as though they were gold.

"Did they grow at Cloverside?"

"Right by the lane. Common enough things."

"They are not common here."

The next day there were ferns, cool and green from
the woods. After that came sweet-brier roses, and then a
little sheaf of daisies mixed with red clover. Andrew discovered
that he could always find some reason for carrying the
basket down himself, though the Bell wagon had served the
Stacys for years without such ceremony.

Mrs. John saw the flowers and frowned.

"The vegetable man is becoming very familiar."

"He is kind," said Jessamine.

"Kind? I hope you do not encourage him. People in
our position must be careful."

Jessamine looked around the kitchen—the blackened stove, the cracked sink,
the heap of children's aprons waiting to be ironed—and wondered
what position Clara meant.

Andrew, meanwhile, was troubled by a misunderstanding.

One morning he said, "Does your husband like the city
as well as you do?"

"My husband?" Jessamine stared at him.

"I reckoned you were Mrs. Stacy."

"No. John is my brother."

Andrew's blue eyes brightened.

"Then you aren't married?"

"No."

"And this is your brother's house?"

"Yes."

"That explains it," he said.

"What does it explain?"

He reddened under his tan. "Why you didn't seem like
the mistress."

Jessamine laughed, though there was a little pain in it.

"I am useful here. That is almost the same thing,
perhaps."

It was not the same thing at all, and Andrew
knew it. As he drove away, he thought of her
in that dim kitchen.

"She's like a lark shut in a cupboard," he said
aloud. "No wonder she's forgotten how to sing."

A week later he asked whether she might go for
a drive on Saturday afternoon.

"To Cloverside," he added hastily. "Aunt Bell wants to meet
you. We'll be back before dark."

Jessamine's heart leaped; then it sank.

"Clara will never spare me."

But Clara was expecting guests and had no wish to
explain Jessamine's worn dress or uncertain place in the household.

"You may go if dinner is finished first," she said.
"And be sure you are back in time to put
the children to bed."

Jessamine worked with such happy speed that even Clara found
no fault. At two o'clock Andrew drove up in a
clean green wagon behind a stout bay mare. He had
brought a broad straw hat for Jessamine.

"The sun is friendly," he said, "but it can be
overfriendly."

As the streets fell behind them, Jessamine sat very still.
Warehouses gave way to scattered houses, houses to gardens, and
gardens to open fields. The air changed. It came fresh
across the grass, carrying the smell of warm earth and
pine woods.

"Oh," she breathed when the first wide meadow opened before
them.

Andrew pretended to attend to the mare, but he saw
the colour awakening in her cheeks.

At Cloverside, Aunt Bell came hurrying from the porch—a round,
motherly woman with silver spectacles and flour on one arm.

"So this is Jessamine! Andrew has talked of nobody else
these three weeks."

"Aunt!" protested Andrew.

"Well, you haven't. Come in, child. No—don't come in yet.
You look as if you had been starved for outdoors."

The farmhouse stood among maples, with honeysuckle over the porch
and hollyhocks beside the path. Chickens scratched under the lilacs.
Beyond the barn rolled fields of clover, corn, and yellowing
grain, and far away the hills rested beneath a blue
sky full of shining clouds.

Jessamine wandered from garden to orchard and from orchard to
brook. She knelt to dip her fingers in the clear
water. She gathered ferns in the beech wood and laughed
when Andrew shook a low branch and showered her with
leaves.

At supper she drank rich milk and ate Aunt Bell's
brown bread, honey, and raspberry preserves. Aunt Bell watched her
with satisfaction.

"You need feeding up," she declared. "A country girl has
no business looking like a white moth."

"I used to be strong," said Jessamine. "At home I
milked two cows and tended the garden."

"Did you, now?" Andrew asked. "Can you make butter?"

"Better than you can, I daresay."

"I can make it come, but Aunt says I pound
instead of churn."

"He does," said Aunt Bell. "The butter is frightened into
gathering."

Jessamine laughed until her eyes sparkled. Her hair had curled
in the country air, and the wind had blown a
wild rose colour into her face. Andrew looked at her
across the table and thought she belonged there as naturally
as the honeysuckle belonged to the porch.

Why should he take her back? Why could he not
see her at that table every evening, hear her step
in the dairy, and find her flowers in the blue
pitcher?

By the time he drove her home, he knew.

At the city door Jessamine's joy faded a little.

"It has been the happiest day I have had since
leaving home," she said. "Thank you, Andrew."

"I reckon Cloverside was glad to have you."

"Places cannot be glad."

"Can't they? The house looked brighter to me."

Before she could answer, Clara called impatiently from above.

Old Mr. Bell recovered soon afterward and resumed the route.
Andrew was needed at Cloverside, for harvest had begun, and
there were days when every sound hand must work from
dawn until the moon rose.

For several weeks Jessamine saw no more of him.

She missed his hearty voice, the sunshine of his blue
eyes, and the posies laid carelessly beside the vegetables. Old
Mr. Bell brought excellent cabbages but no buttercups. The kitchen
seemed darker than before because she had once escaped it.

Then misfortune came to John. His business, which had long
been failing, collapsed altogether. Bills appeared; furniture was sold; and
Clara alternated between reproaches and tears. John found a position
in a western town and announced that they must leave
within ten days.

"You will come, of course," he told Jessamine. "We shall
need you more than ever until we are settled."

The West! To Jessamine it sounded like another city kitchen,
only farther from every field she had ever loved.

"But John," she ventured, "perhaps I could find some other
place."

"What place?" demanded Clara. "You have no money. And after
all we have done for you!"

Jessamine said no more.

Old Mr. Bell heard of the failure when he delivered
the potatoes. That evening, as Andrew came in from binding
the last load of oats, his uncle said, "The Stacys
are going west."

Andrew dropped his hat.

"When?"

"Next Thursday."

"And Miss Jessamine?"

"Going with them, I suppose."

Andrew did not wait for supper. He washed at the
pump, changed his coat, harnessed the bay mare, and drove
toward the city while Aunt Bell stood on the porch
smiling to herself.

It was nearly dusk when Jessamine answered the basement bell.
At sight of Andrew, her whole tired face brightened.

"You have come back!"

"I came as soon as I heard. Are you going
west?"

"I must."

"No," said Andrew. "You mustn't."

Clara's voice sounded overhead, but Andrew stepped into the kitchen
and shut the door.

"Jessamine, I meant to wait until harvest was over and
I had time to say this properly. But there's no
time now, so I'll say it any way I can."

She stood with one hand against the table.

"I've got a good farm," he continued, "not grand, but
sound. There's the house under the maples, and Aunt Bell
will stay as long as we need her. There are
cows to milk, butter to churn, flowers to tend, and
a fellow who loves you more every day he lives."

Jessamine's lips parted, but no words came.

"I want you at Cloverside," Andrew said. "Not as somebody
useful. Not to wear yourself out for people who never
look to see if you're weary. I want you as
my wife, and I'll do my best to make you
happy. Will you come?"

No one had asked what she wanted since her mother
died. No one had thought her happiness worth planning for.
The wonder of it filled her eyes with tears.

"Oh, Andrew," she whispered, "do you truly care so much?"

"I reckon I do. More than all the clover fields
from here to the hills."

That was not a fine speech, perhaps, but Jessamine understood
it.

"Yes," she said.

Mrs. John was resentful. She spoke of ingratitude, haste, and
social differences; but John, ashamed at last, gave his consent.
They were married quietly three days before the family went
west.

When Andrew drove his bride to Cloverside, Aunt Bell waited
beneath the honeysuckle, and the late roses nodded along the
path. The maples were touched with gold, the barns stood
full, and cattle moved peacefully home through the fragrant dusk.

Jessamine lifted her face to the open sky.

"You will never be homesick here," Andrew told her.

And she never was.

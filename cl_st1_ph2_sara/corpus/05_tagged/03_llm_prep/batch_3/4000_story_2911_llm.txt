## Aunt Philippa's Wedding

When I was eighteen I believed that a journey of
seven hundred miles was enough to ruin any love affair.

I know better now. A journey of seven hundred miles
cannot ruin a sound love affair, while a journey of
seven yards may finish a weak one. But at eighteen
one does not reason from experience. One reasons from novels,
moonlight, and the expression in a young man's eyes when
he says good-bye.

My father sent me from Montreal to Prince Edward Island
in June, ostensibly because I looked pale and needed country
air. In reality, he wanted me taken out of Mark
Fenwick's way.

Father disliked the Fenwicks in a thorough, hereditary fashion. Old
Mr. Fenwick had once beaten him in a business matter,
and Father had never forgiven the whole family for it,
including several Fenwicks who were dead before the transaction occurred.
He said Mark was irresponsible, penniless, and too handsome to
be trusted. My mother said only that eighteen was very
young. As she had married at nineteen, this argument did
not impress me.

Unfortunately, Mark and I had quarrelled the evening before my
departure. It was a foolish quarrel, though I cannot remember
its exact beginning. Most quarrels at eighteen begin nowhere and
spread everywhere. I think Mark had laughed when I wanted
to be tragic, and I had accused him of not
caring. He then said that perhaps he did not; I
said I had long suspected it; and we separated with
all the dignity of two persons who were desperately hoping
the other would run after them.

Mark did not run after me.

Consequently, I left Montreal believing that my heart was broken
and took three hats, two white evening dresses, and a
silver photograph frame to Prince Edward Island.

Aunt Philippa met me at the little station in a
high, yellow-wheeled buggy drawn by a brown mare with an
injured opinion of railway trains. Aunt Philippa herself was visible
before anything else. She wore a purple print wrapper, a
green bonnet trimmed with red cherries, and black cotton gloves.
The colours quarrelled even more violently than Mark and I
had done, but Aunt Philippa appeared unconscious of it.

She was Father's eldest sister and possessed the family nose
in its most uncompromising form. Her mouth was determined, her
grey eyes piercing, and her square chin looked capable of
settling any question without assistance. She surveyed me from hat
to shoes.

"You're thin," she said. "But not so thin as your
father writ. He always did exaggerate anything he couldn't manage."

She took my valise from me before I could object
and put it under the buggy seat.

"I can manage that," I said.

"Mebbe. But I know I can."

This proved to be Aunt Philippa's general attitude toward life.

We drove eight miles along a grassy, elm-shaded road that
curved over low red hills and through small valleys opening
blue and bright toward the sea. Buttercups ran beside us
in a golden ribbon, and beyond the fences the fields
looked as green and smooth as folded silk. White farmhouses
stood among apple trees, each with its barns gathered respectfully
behind it. The salt wind came inland with a smell
of clover and warm earth mixed into it, and despite
my broken heart I thought the Island beautiful.

Aunt Philippa asked no questions about Mark until we were
halfway home. Then she said, without looking at me, "Your
father says you've got notions about a Fenwick."

"I am engaged to Mark," I replied.

"Does he know it?"

"Of course he knows it."

"Some men don't. I knew Matilda Peake to be engaged
six years to a man who thought he was merely
seeing her home from prayer meeting."

"Mark and I understand each other perfectly."

"Then what did you quarrel about?"

I started. "Father had no right to tell you."

"He didn't. You've been sighing every three minutes since you
got into the buggy, and every sigh has got a
quarrel at the bottom of it. Healthy girls don't sigh
over weather."

I looked away at the fields.

"It was not important," I said.

"Then you'll likely never get it settled. Big troubles can
be took hold of. Little ones slip through your fingers
and bite your ankles. That's the men for you."

This was my introduction to Aunt Philippa's favourite conclusion.

Her farm lay in a pretty, seaward-sloping valley three miles
from the village of Kildare. The house was painted a
green so vivid that it seemed to have been mixed
from June itself. Across the front gable AUNT PHILIPPA'S FARM
had been lettered in white capitals large enough to be
read from the opposing hill.

"Why have you put your name on the house?" I
asked.

"So folks will know whose it is."

"Couldn't they ask?"

"They do ask. The sign saves my breath."

There was a wide verandah with morning-glories climbing its posts,
a kitchen smelling of hot biscuits and lemon peel, and
a spare bedroom papered with pink roses. On my washstand
stood a china pitcher with a crack running through a
painted shepherdess. Aunt Philippa told me it had been cracked
since the day Uncle Andrew threw his boot at the
cat.

"I didn't know you had an Uncle Andrew," I said.

"You haven't. He died."

"Was he your husband?"

"No. He was my uncle. If I'd had a husband
who threw boots in the house, he wouldn't have lived
long enough to be an uncle to anybody."

A cat came in while we were having supper. It
was patched with black, white, yellow, and grey in such
irregular portions that it looked as if several cats had
been cut up and badly sewn together.

"That's Mosaic," said Aunt Philippa. "He's the best mouser in
Queen's County, though he ain't got enough beauty for one
cat and has too much for four."

Before my first week ended, I loved the farm. I
rose early, gathered eggs, learned to drive the brown mare,
picked strawberries, churned butter, and sat on the front steps
at twilight while the sea beyond the hills turned silver
under a young moon. I discovered that peas eaten in
the garden were entirely different vegetables from peas eaten in
Montreal. I learned that a cow can look meditative while
plotting murder and that hens have no gratitude.

Only one thing spoiled my happiness.

Mark did not write.

Every afternoon I walked to the green roadside box. Every
afternoon it contained Aunt Philippa's agricultural paper, a bill, a
circular praising patent medicine, or a letter for someone else
which the postmistress had inserted by mistake. For me there
was nothing.

"He knows I am here," I said on the seventh
empty afternoon. "I sent him my address."

"Did you send it before or after you quarrelled?"

"Before."

"Then mebbe he tore it up after."

"He would not do such a thing."

"Then mebbe he lost it. That's worse. A man that
keeps grudges is unpleasant, but a man that loses addresses
is useless."

"Aunt Philippa, do you suppose every man is either unpleasant
or useless?"

"No. Some are both."

She was shelling peas on the verandah as she said
it, snapping each green pod with a brisk finality that
suggested masculine necks.

"You are prejudiced," I told her.

"I've had opportunities."

Then she related the history of Mr. Abner Shaw, who
had married a pretty woman from Summerside and refused to
permit curtains because he said they shut out the light.
Mrs. Shaw endured an exposed existence for seventeen years, until
Abner developed a passion for photography and covered every window
with black cloth.

"He could live in the dark for a picture," Aunt
Philippa concluded, "but he couldn't abide a lace curtain for
his wife. That's the men for you."

Two days later, when no letter had come, she told
me about Simon Bell. Simon had made his wife walk
three miles to save the wear on the horse, but
after her death he drove six miles every Sunday to
put flowers on her grave.

"He was sorry then," I said.

"He enjoyed being sorry. It was cheaper than being kind."

"That is a dreadful thing to say."

"It was a dreadful thing to watch. That's the men
for you."

I tried not to listen to these accounts, but Aunt
Philippa had an inexhaustible supply of them. Her memory was
a graveyard in which every dead marriage had its own
labelled stone.

There was Henry Dobson, who complained for twenty years that
his wife's bread was heavy and, after she left him,
lived chiefly on crackers because he could not bake at
all. There was Caleb Morris, who gave his bride a
spinning wheel for her birthday and was offended when she
gave him a churn for his. There was Lemuel Pike,
who built a fine front parlour his wife was never
allowed to sit in, and then had his funeral there
because, as Aunt Philippa said, "Lem always got the best
of everything in the end."

After each story came the verdict.

"That's the men for you."

At first I laughed. Later I did not.

By July the roses had gone from the verandah, the
hay lay in yellow swaths on the hills, and still
Mark had not written. I began to imagine him walking
with other girls in Montreal. There were many girls there—pretty
girls, clever girls, girls with fathers who did not hate
the Fenwicks. I pictured Mark calling upon them, forgetting our
quarrel, forgetting my address, and finally forgetting me. I was
very efficient at making myself miserable.

Aunt Philippa watched me without sympathy.

"If you keep staring at that road, you'll wear a
hollow in it," she said.

"I am not staring at the road."

"No. You're merely facing it with both eyes open."

"Perhaps Mark is ill."

"Mebbe."

"Or perhaps he has had an accident."

"Mebbe."

"Or perhaps he wrote and the letter went astray."

"Mebbe."

"You might say something comforting."

"I might, but you wouldn't believe it. Comfort has to
fit a trouble. Yours is all shapes."

The letter came the next day.

I saw it in the box and knew Mark's handwriting
before I touched it. I ran all the way from
the road to the orchard, where I sat under an
apple tree because my knees had become unreliable. The envelope
was thick, and the first line made my heart leap.

"My dearest Ursula—"

I forgave him before I read another word.

Then I read the rest.

Mark had obtained a position with an engineering company in
South Africa. He was to sail from Halifax in three
weeks and expected to remain abroad for five years. He
had not written earlier because he had been trying to
see my father, hoping to secure his consent to our
marriage. Father had refused even to receive him.

Mark asked me to meet him in Halifax. We could
be married there and sail together. He had money enough
for the passage and believed his prospects excellent. He wrote
that he loved me, had always loved me, and could
not endure five years without me.

I read the letter three times, feeling by turns delighted,
terrified, proud, and sick.

Five years! At eighteen, five years seemed longer than the
recorded history of Europe.

I carried the letter into the house. Aunt Philippa was
rolling pastry at the kitchen table. She wore a blue
wrapper with large orange flowers, and there was flour on
the tip of the family nose.

"He has written," I said.

"So I judged. No tax notice ever made a girl
run like that."

"He is going to South Africa."

"Is he?"

"For five years."

"That's a good while. Though not so long once it's
over."

"He wants me to meet him in Halifax and marry
him."

Aunt Philippa cut a circle of pastry with the calmness
of an executioner.

"Do you want to?"

"Yes."

"Then why are you looking as if he'd asked you
to drown yourself?"

"Because I cannot go."

"Can't you afford the ticket?"

"You know what I mean. I cannot run away and
be married secretly. It would be shameful."

"Would it?"

"Yes."

She fitted the crust into a pie plate.

"Then don't."

Her answer was so simple that it irritated me.

"But if I do not, he will go without me."

"Likely."

"And I shall not see him for five years."

"Likely."

"He may forget me."

"Likely."

"Aunt Philippa!"

"Well, do you want me to lie while I'm making
lemon pie? Lies sour the filling."

I sat down and cried, which I had not intended
to do. Aunt Philippa went on rolling pastry. She did
not pat my shoulder or call me a poor child.
After a time she pushed a clean corner of her
apron toward me.

"Don't drip on the flour," she said.

"I love him," I sobbed.

"Then marry him."

"I cannot elope."

"Then don't elope."

"There is no other way."

"Mebbe there is."

I wiped my eyes and looked at her. "Father will
never consent."

"Your father ain't the Lord, though he has mistaken himself
for Him on occasion."

"What do you mean?"

Aunt Philippa pinched the edge of the pie into neat
flutes. "I mean, write to your young man and tell
him to come here."

"Here?"

"Here. He can marry you in my parlour and take
you to Halifax afterward."

I stared at her.

"You—you would let us?"

"Let you? It's my house. I'm proposing it."

"But you do not know Mark."

"I do."

"You have never met him."

"Meeting ain't the only way of knowing. I writ to
people."

"What people?"

"People who don't lie unless there's money in it. I
writ to his employer, his minister, the landlady where he
boards, and that cousin of his who married a McLean
girl in Charlottetown."

I could only repeat, "You wrote to his landlady?"

"Boarding-house women know a man better than his mother does.
Mothers see what they hope for. Landladies see muddy towels,
unpaid bills, and what time he comes home."

"And what did she say?"

"She says he pays regular, don't drink, don't swear where
ladies can hear him, and once sat up all night
with another boarder who had pneumonia. His employer says he's
got more energy than judgment, but judgment sometimes grows. The
minister says he don't attend church enough. Ministers always say
that. On the whole, he's better than most."

My amazement left me almost speechless, but not entirely.

"Why did you do all this?"

"Because I wanted to know whether you were a fool."

"And am I?"

"Certainly. But not about him."

I began to laugh and cry together.

Aunt Philippa put the pie in the oven.

"Write him," she ordered. "Tell him to come on the
Tuesday train. We'll have the wedding Thursday. That gives him
one day to change his mind and me one day
to prevent it."

I flung my arms around her. Aunt Philippa endured the
embrace stiffly.

"Mind my wrapper," she said. "Blue shows tears."

Mark's reply was delivered by telegram.

COMING TUESDAY STOP LOVE YOU STOP BLESS AUNT PHILIPPA STOP.

Aunt Philippa read it and sniffed.

"If he blesses me before he meets me, there'll be
nowhere for his opinion to go but down."

The next three weeks passed in such activity that I
had scarcely time to be frightened. Aunt Philippa decided that,
since I refused to elope because it was not respectable,
I should have enough respectability to satisfy the entire county.

She invited forty-six people.

"I do not know forty-six people," I protested.

"You will after they come."

She hired Mrs. McNab to help with the cooking, borrowed
chairs from the schoolhouse, ordered white ribbon from Charlottetown, and
had the parlour papered because the old pattern contained brown
roses she had always distrusted.

"Flowers have no business being brown before they're dead," she
said.

The wedding cake was made in three tiers. Aunt Philippa
refused to put silver cupids on it.

"Bare children shooting arrows ain't suitable for a Christian marriage."

She substituted sugar roses and a china dove which had
only one wing visible from the front.

My dress was white muslin. Aunt Philippa wanted silk, but
there was no time to have it made, and the
local dressmaker said that if she attempted silk in three
weeks she would either ruin the gown or her marriage.
Aunt Philippa considered the gown more important.

She gave me her mother's lace veil and a pearl
brooch I had never seen.

"I thought you disliked weddings," I said as she fastened
the brooch at my throat.

"I dislike most dinners, too, but when I give one
I see the potatoes are hot."

The question of ministers arose.

Aunt Philippa was a Presbyterian of the most fortified kind.
She had nothing against Methodists personally, she said, provided they
did not sing too loudly, tell her she was converted,
or expect to marry anybody in her house. The Reverend
Mr. McTavish, the Presbyterian minister at Kildare, promised to officiate.

"Be here at two sharp," Aunt Philippa told him. "Mark's
train for Halifax leaves at four-thirty Friday, and I won't
have any long prayers delaying their packing."

Mr. McTavish looked offended.

"A marriage service is not generally measured by the railway
timetable, Miss Fraser."

"It is when the bridegroom's sailing ship won't wait for
theology."

Aunt Philippa distrusted nearly everything about the wedding except her
own preparations. She warned me daily that Mark might not
come, might come late, might prove altered, or might lose
the ring.

"Men can lose anything," she said. "Ezra Biggs lost a
cow between his barn and pasture, and there weren't but
one road."

"How did he lose it?"

"He stopped to argue politics over a fence, and the
cow went home behind him. He hunted till dark and
blamed his wife because she hadn't told him it was
there. That's the men for you."

"Mark will come," I said.

"Mebbe. Don't lean your whole weight on hope. It's a
weak-backed thing."

He came.

I waited on the verandah Tuesday afternoon, hearing imaginary wheels
for an hour before the buggy appeared on the hill.
Aunt Philippa drove, erect and severe, and beside her sat
Mark.

The Island sunshine made him look browner than I remembered
and perhaps a little thinner. When he saw me, he
stood up in the moving buggy, struck his knee against
the dashboard, and nearly fell out.

Aunt Philippa caught the tail of his coat.

"Sit down," she said. "If you break your neck before
Thursday, Ursula will have to marry a corpse, and Mr.
McTavish won't approve."

Mark jumped down before the buggy had quite stopped. For
a few minutes Aunt Philippa, South Africa, Father, and the
forty-six invited guests ceased to exist.

Then Aunt Philippa tapped Mark's shoulder with the whip handle.

"You can kiss her after you carry in the valises.
Affection don't lighten luggage."

At supper she examined him without mercy.

"Can you support a wife?"

"I intend to."

"That ain't what I asked."

"I have a salary and the promise of promotion."

"Promises don't buy shoes."

"The company pays our passage and provides housing in Cape
Town."

"Have you ever kept a house?"

"No."

"Can you mend a stove?"

"I can try."

"Can you milk a cow?"

"I am going to South Africa, Miss Fraser, not into
the dairy business."

"You don't know what'll be there till you get there."

Mark looked at me, but I was of no assistance.
I knew Aunt Philippa had already decided in his favour.
This was merely her way of enjoying him.

"What will you do if Ursula gets homesick?" she asked.

"Bring her home."

"What if you can't afford to?"

"Make her less homesick."

"How?"

"I don't know yet."

Aunt Philippa nodded. "That's the first sensible answer you've give."

On Wednesday morning she took Mark around the farm. He
came back two hours later having repaired a broken gate,
caught an escaped pig, and promised to send her a
South African newspaper.

"She likes me," he whispered.

"Do not be vain. She made the insurance man catch
that pig last year."

The wedding day dawned clear, hot, and windy. The valley
shone under the August sun. Guests began arriving before noon,
although the ceremony was at two. They came in buggies
and wagons, bringing parcels, flowers, advice, and a powerful curiosity
about Mark.

Mrs. McNab commanded the kitchen. Aunt Philippa commanded everything else.
She wore a black silk dress with a crimson collar
and a bonnet trimmed with purple pansies. No two parts
of her costume agreed, but all obeyed.

At half past one I went upstairs to dress. My
hands trembled so badly that my cousin Flora had to
fasten my buttons. Below us the parlour filled with murmurs,
coughs, and the scraping of schoolhouse chairs. Mark waited by
the window with the ring safely in his waistcoat pocket;
Aunt Philippa had made him show it to her three
times.

At ten minutes to two, a boy galloped into the
yard on a lathered horse.

I heard Aunt Philippa's voice from the hall.

"What's afire?"

"Nothing's afire, Miss Fraser. It's Mr. McTavish. Old Mrs. Campbell's
dying, and they've sent for him. He says he can't
come."

For one moment the house became utterly quiet. Even the
guests seemed to stop breathing.

Aunt Philippa spoke first.

"Mrs. Campbell has been dying every August for twelve years."

"She says this time's different."

"She would."

The messenger shuffled his feet.

"Mr. McTavish says he's sorry."

"Sorry won't marry anybody."

"He said perhaps Mr. Tupper could do it."

Mr. Tupper was the Methodist minister from the neighbouring circuit.

There are crises in which long conviction struggles with immediate
necessity. I did not see Aunt Philippa's face, but I
knew that the Presbyterian faith, as she understood it, was
fighting for its life in the hall below.

"A Methodist," she said at last.

"He's just over at the Ellis place."

"A Methodist in my parlour."

Nobody answered.

"Fetch him," said Aunt Philippa. "And tell him not to
shout."

The messenger galloped away.

I sat down on the bed.

"This is an omen," I said.

"It is not," said Flora. "It is Mrs. Campbell."

"What if Mr. Tupper refuses?"

"A Methodist minister refuse a chance to marry two Presbyterians?
He'll run all the way."

He did not run, but he came in eleven minutes,
hot and cheerful, carrying a large black Bible. Mr. Tupper
was a round, pink man with a voice made for
open-air preaching. Aunt Philippa met him at the door.

"You know the Presbyterian service?" she demanded.

"I know the Christian service, Miss Fraser."

"That'll have to do. Keep it short."

"I shall perform the ceremony with proper solemnity."

"You can be solemn without being lengthy. Death proves that."

Mr. Tupper blinked, wiped his forehead, and entered the parlour.

At last I came downstairs on Mark's arm. The crowded
room blurred around me—white flowers, dark coats, coloured bonnets, the
china ornaments on the mantel, Aunt Philippa standing rigid beside
the door. Mark's hand was warm and steady over mine.

Mr. Tupper began in a low voice, evidently remembering Aunt
Philippa's warning. After the first sentence, however, habit overcame caution,
and his tones rose until he was marrying us with
enough force to include every unmarried person in the valley.

"DEARLY BELOVED—"

Aunt Philippa frowned.

The vows began. I repeated mine clearly, though I scarcely
heard my own voice. Mark put the ring on the
correct finger, which I considered a favourable sign.

Then I smelled smoke.

At first I thought it came from the kitchen stove.
A moment later a thin grey thread drifted past the
open parlour window. Aunt Philippa saw it too. Her eyes
moved from the window to Mr. Tupper, then to Mark
and me.

Outside, someone shouted.

"Fire!"

The guests turned their heads.

Aunt Philippa did not move.

Mr. Tupper faltered. "If there is some danger—"

"Finish," said Aunt Philippa.

"But, Miss Fraser—"

"You ain't pronounced them yet."

Smoke blew past the window in a thicker cloud. Mrs.
McNab appeared at the parlour door with a floury face.

"The kitchen roof's caught from the chimney!"

"Buckets," said Aunt Philippa. "Quietly."

"Quietly?"

"They're nearly married."

Mr. Tupper understood. He closed his eyes, raised one hand,
and spoke with tremendous speed.

"By the authority committed unto me as a minister of
the gospel I pronounce that they are husband and wife—"

"Now!" cried Aunt Philippa.

The wedding party poured out of the house.

There is no dignified way to fight a kitchen fire
in wedding clothes. Mark tore off his coat and climbed
onto the roof. Mr. Tupper formed a bucket line from
the well. Aunt Philippa stood on the back step directing
operations with such authority that the flames themselves appeared embarrassed.
I held up my white skirts and carried water until
Flora told me brides were not supposed to arrive at
their wedding breakfast wet to the knees.

The fire had started where a spark caught in dry
moss beneath a loose shingle. It looked alarming but was
not deep. In fifteen minutes it was out.

Mark came down blackened with soot, his hair singed at
one side. I kissed him in the yard before everybody.

"That," said Aunt Philippa, "was what you might call a
Methodist baptism."

Mr. Tupper laughed so heartily that she forgave him nearly
half his theology.

The wedding breakfast was served only twenty minutes late. The
lemon pies were unhurt, the cake had acquired a faint
smoky flavour, and one dish of cream had been overturned
by the pump. Everyone agreed it was the most exciting
wedding in the history of Kildare, though Mrs. Ellis maintained
that her sister's wedding had been equally memorable because the
groom's former fiancée had appeared during the blessing.

Aunt Philippa would not allow the fire to be discussed
while we ate.

"Roofs can be mended," she said. "Cake dries while people
talk."

The next morning Mark and I left for Halifax. Aunt
Philippa drove us to the station in the yellow-wheeled buggy.
My trunk was tied behind us; my bouquet, already wilting,
lay in my lap. The green farmhouse shone on the
hill when I looked back, and the white letters across
its gable seemed to wave good-bye.

For the first mile Aunt Philippa talked about practical matters—tickets,
luggage, seasickness, and the foolishness of wearing thin shoes on
shipboard. Then she became silent.

I had wanted all morning to ask her one question.

"Aunt Philippa, why did you help us?"

"I told you. The young man's better than most."

"But you do not believe in men."

"I believe in 'em. I just don't expect much."

"You arranged everything. You wrote to strangers, invited half the
county, gave me Grandmother's veil, and let a Methodist into
your parlour."

"The Methodist was necessity. Don't make romance out of that."

"Still, why did you do it?"

She flicked a fly from the mare's back with the
reins.

"Because once, when I was nineteen, a man asked me
to run away with him."

I turned to look at her.

She kept her eyes on the road.

"His name was Stephen Blair. He had red hair, no
money, and more plans than sense. My father wouldn't hear
of him. Stephen was going west and wanted me to
go. I said I couldn't do anything so disgraceful."

"What happened?"

"He went."

"And did he come back?"

"No."

"Did you love him?"

Aunt Philippa's determined mouth tightened.

"I thought I did."

That meant yes.

"What became of him?"

"He married a woman in Manitoba and had nine children.
Died of pneumonia at forty-seven. His widow wrote me afterward.
She said he was kind, careless, cheerful, and never remembered
to shut a door."

The buggy wheels murmured over the red road. Buttercups nodded
along the ditches just as they had on the day
of my arrival, but the fields were richer now, heavy
with late summer.

"Did you regret not going?" I asked softly.

"Every day for ten years. Every other day for the
next ten. After that I got busy."

"Oh, Aunt Philippa."

"Don't pity me. I've had my own farm, my own
money, and my own way. Few married women get all
three."

"But you helped me because you wished someone had helped
you."

"Mebbe."

She pulled up before the station. Mark jumped down to
see to the trunk, leaving us alone for a moment.

I put my hand over hers.

"Do you think you would have been happy with Stephen?"

Aunt Philippa considered.

"He'd likely have left every door open from here to
Manitoba. I hate a draught."

"Then perhaps you were right not to go."

"Mebbe."

"Yet you said you regretted it."

"I did."

"You cannot have it both ways."

"No," said Aunt Philippa. "But you can regret it both
ways. That's what keeps life even."

The train whistled in the distance. Mark called that it
was coming. Aunt Philippa kissed me once, briskly, and pushed
me toward him.

"Go on. You chose your trouble. Stand by it."

I climbed aboard with tears in my eyes and laughter
unsteadily behind them. Mark leaned from the carriage window to
call his thanks, but Aunt Philippa had already turned the
mare. She drove away without looking back, her purple pansies
trembling on her bonnet and the yellow wheels flashing in
the sun.

Mark proved neither perfect nor useless. He forgot addresses, left
doors open, lost one excellent umbrella, and once bought a
parrot without asking me. He was also kind, loyal, patient
with my homesickness, and remarkably good at mending stoves. We
were happy in South Africa, though not every day, which
is the only honest way to be happy for many
years.

As for Aunt Philippa, she wrote every fortnight. Her letters
contained weather, crop prices, Presbyterian complaints, and reports of local
husbands behaving badly. Nearly every account ended with the old
judgment:

"That's the men for you."

But every anniversary she sent Mark a gift, usually something
practical and always something he would never have thought to
buy for himself.

That was Aunt Philippa for you.

### The Mystery of Scarrick's

Mr. Scarrick's grocery establishment stood at the meeting-point of three
respectable roads and one social ambition. The roads led respectively
to the railway station, the parish church, and a district
where the houses had names instead of numbers; the ambition
belonged to Mrs. Scarrick, who hoped some day to be
asked to tea at one of them.

For twenty-one years the shop had supplied the neighbourhood with
coffee, preserved ginger, anchovy paste, household soap, and those small
hard biscuits which elderly ladies buy for imaginary children. It
had prospered without vulgar display. Lately, however, prosperity had developed
migratory instincts.

"The Imperial Stores have opened a tea-room," said Mr. Scarrick
bitterly, "with a string orchestra from four to six."

He was speaking to Lancelot Bream, an artist who occupied
the two rooms above the shop and was generally believed
to paint better than he paid. Lancelot's sister, Sylvia, sat
on a flour-bin and watched her brother draw Assyrian profiles
on the back of an invoice.

"And Lipping's have a cinematograph twice a week," continued the
grocer. "They give away balloons to children on Thursdays, and
Yorling and Sons have put in a palm-court."

"A palm-court?" said Sylvia.

"Three palms and a wicker chair," admitted Mr. Scarrick, "but
people speak of it as though the Caliph of Baghdad
received there. Shopping is no longer shopping. It is becoming
a variety entertainment with parcels."

"The public likes to be amused," observed Lancelot.

"I sell excellent groceries."

"That is your difficulty. Excellence satisfies an appetite, but it
does not occupy the imagination. A woman may require half
a pound of tea; she does not require to remain
forty minutes in the shop after buying it. Yet if
you give her something to wonder about, she will stay
until her domestic arrangements are in ruins."

Mr. Scarrick sighed.

"I cannot afford an orchestra."

"You do not need one."

"Nor a cinematograph."

"Still less."

Mr. Scarrick looked suspiciously at the Assyrian profiles.

"What do I need?"

"A mystery," said Lancelot.

There was a pause. A van from the Imperial Stores
passed the window, painted with golden cornucopias and driven by
a man in a green uniform suggestive of Balkan cavalry.

"A mystery?" repeated Mr. Scarrick.

"Mystery is the one form of entertainment for which the
audience provides most of the scenery and all of the
explanation. A dark face, an unnecessary purchase, a well-timed denial—and
your customers will do the rest. They will construct murders,
dynastic claims, secret marriages, and the dismemberment of at least
one empire before luncheon."

"I don't want the police in," said Mr. Scarrick.

"The police are never in when a mystery is interesting.
They arrive when it has become an offence."

Sylvia smiled at her brother.

"You have thought of something."

"I have thought," said Lancelot, "of pomegranates."

Mr. Scarrick waited for enlightenment; but enlightenment, like credit, was
not immediately forthcoming.

* * *

On the following Tuesday morning, when the shop contained Mrs.
Greyes, Miss Fritten, and a small boy engaged in selecting
a pennyworth of sweets by a process that threatened to
outlast the Ottoman Empire, a young man entered.

He was perhaps nineteen, slender, and dark enough to have
been born in any country that respectable suburbia discussed without
accuracy. His skin had the deep olive colouring of an
antique bronze; his hair was black and close-curled; and his
eyes moved about the shop with the bright watchfulness of
a creature hunted in an Eastern legend. He wore an
ordinary grey suit, which somehow increased rather than diminished his
foreignness.

"Two pomegranates," he said softly, placing a brass bowl on
the counter.

Mr. Scarrick weighed the fruit. Pomegranates were not an article
that the neighbourhood demanded with insistence; they remained in the
window chiefly because they looked scriptural.

"And quail seed," added the young man. "A handful only."

Mrs. Greyes stopped examining a pot of jam whose purity
had suddenly ceased to interest her.

"Quail seed?" she repeated.

The young man turned sharply. Then, gathering up the brass
bowl, he went behind a barrier of biscuit-tins and disappeared
through the door leading to the back premises.

Almost immediately a second figure entered the shop.

He was a large man in a long coat with
an astrachan collar. A black beard spread across the lower
part of his face with a magnificence rarely achieved outside
opera. His eyebrows appeared to have been selected on the
same principle. He looked at the customers, at the pomegranates,
and finally at Mr. Scarrick.

"A young person has been here," he said. "Dark. He
carries brass."

"No," said Mr. Scarrick.

It was the least convincing negative ever uttered in connection
with brass.

The bearded stranger placed both hands on the counter.

"You are sure?"

"Perfectly."

The stranger's gaze travelled to the half-open door behind the
biscuit-tins. For several seconds no one moved. Then he bowed
with a ceremoniousness that might have belonged to Persia, Portugal,
or a touring production of *The Arabian Nights*, and left
the shop.

The young man reappeared.

"He has gone?"

"Yes."

"You did not tell him?"

"No."

The young man pressed Mr. Scarrick's hand.

"Allah remember," he murmured, and departed by the rear entrance.

Mrs. Greyes bought the jam, two tins of sardines, a
packet of dates, and a pound of coffee. Miss Fritten,
who had entered for soda, purchased figs, rice, candied peel,
and a bottle of mushroom ketchup. They lingered for another
ten minutes, but the East had withdrawn behind the biscuit-tins
and showed no disposition to reappear.

By teatime the affair had reached seven drawing-rooms, two kitchens,
and the vicarage.

Mrs. Greyes inclined to the theory of an opium traffic
conducted through hollow pomegranates. Miss Fritten believed the young man
to be the illegitimate son of a Portuguese statesman, though
she could not name a Portuguese statesman without looking in
an atlas. Mrs. Lipping had heard that quail seed was
used in certain Oriental poisonings. Her sister-in-law knew positively that
brass bowls played a terrible part in Persian politics.

The next morning Scarrick's was unusually full.

Several ladies required coffee which they had forgotten to require
the day before. Others had come to ask whether pomegranates
should be boiled, stewed, or sent to the laundry. Mrs.
Yorling, whose cook regarded grocery shopping as a personal encroachment,
came herself and inquired about the home life of the
Bombay duck.

The dark young man entered at eleven-twenty.

He carried the brass bowl and wore an expression of
controlled desperation.

"Six oranges," he said. "The palest you possess."

Mr. Scarrick selected them with care.

"No pomegranates?" asked Mrs. Greyes, too artlessly.

The young man looked at her with a flash of
fear.

"They have failed us," he said.

No one knew who "they" were, but their failure was
immediately felt to have altered the balance of Central Asia.

A cab drew up outside. The bearded stranger descended.

This time the young man did not retreat through the
back door. He dropped behind a pyramid of flour sacks,
while Mr. Scarrick, with admirable presence of mind, placed a
display of breakfast cereal in front of him. The stranger
entered.

"The boy," he said. "He comes here."

"Never," said Mr. Scarrick.

"You sell him the red fruit."

"I sell red fruit to many people."

"And seed for the little birds."

"I supply seed to every bird that can pay for
it."

The stranger leaned forward.

"Beware, shopman. There are those who have vanished for less."

Mrs. Yorling bought twelve pounds of sugar. She did not
require twelve pounds of sugar, but it seemed impossible to
stand within reach of international violence without establishing some commercial
right to the position.

The stranger departed. After a cautious interval, the young man
rose from behind the flour.

"The moon is not yet eaten," he said obscurely.

Then he took his oranges and went.

That sentence gave the mystery a new and formidable direction.
Before evening there were eclipses, pagan ceremonies, a society of
Armenian astronomers, and an escaped prince whose execution had been
fixed for Christmas Eve. The local bridge club suspended play
for nine minutes while Miss Fritten explained that the pale
oranges undoubtedly represented severed heads. Mrs. Greyes rejected this as
fanciful and substituted stolen jewels.

Scarrick's became a place of pilgrimage.

Customers arrived early and remained late. They bought provisions with
the prodigality of besieged cities. One lady ordered three pots
of anchovy paste while watching the street; another acquired a
tin of quail seed, though she possessed neither quails nor
any reasonable prospect of them. Children were sent daily for
trivial commodities and instructed to notice everything. Husbands, returning from
town, were asked whether the Foreign Office had issued any
statement about pomegranates.

Mr. Scarrick's Christmas stock began to disappear. Figs went out
by the stone. Dates that had survived two previous winters
were carried away as though they contained dispatches from a
satrap. Even the crystallised apricots, which had hitherto resisted all
appeals except those of flies, found purchasers.

The performances varied sufficiently to prevent fatigue. Sometimes the young
man came in and left at once, having seen a
chalk mark on the door. Sometimes the bearded stranger arrived
first and waited beside the weighing machine, glaring at everyone
with the impartial menace of a rabid prophet. Once a
sealed envelope was discovered beneath a sack of lentils. It
contained only the railway timetable to Basingstoke, but Mrs. Lipping
pointed out that this was precisely what made it sinister.

Then came the veiled woman.

She entered on a Friday afternoon when the shop was
crowded beyond the dreams of ordinary trade. She wore black
from head to foot, except for a belt composed of
small metal plaques, and carried a purse of intricate silver
work. Her veil concealed her face, but not the fact
that she expected it to be watched.

"The pale fruit," she said to Mr. Scarrick.

"Oranges?" he asked.

She shuddered.

"Do not speak the name."

Mr. Scarrick placed six oranges on the counter.

The woman touched them one by one, then arranged them
in a crescent. Behind the biscuit-tin barrier the dark young
man crouched motionless. His position had been observed by every
person in the shop, chiefly because each supposed herself alone
in having observed it.

The door opened.

The bearded man stood on the threshold.

For once his assurance deserted him. He stared at the
veiled woman; she turned slowly, holding one of the oranges
in her gloved hand.

"You," he said.

"I," she answered.

The word fell on the shop with the weight of
an imperial decree.

"Where is he?"

She pointed—not toward the hiding-place, but toward the ceiling.

"He is where the seventh river passes under the throne."

The stranger recoiled.

"You have dared?"

"The sanhedrim has spoken."

This was geographically and historically confused, but no one in
the shop was in a condition to correct it.

The woman drew from her metal-work purse a small black
object and laid it upon the counter. It resembled a
date stone. The bearded man regarded it with terror.

"The Token!" he gasped.

The young man sprang from behind the biscuit-tins.

The stranger gave one violent cry, overturned a basket of
walnuts, and fled into the street. A moment later the
young man followed by the back way, while the veiled
woman swept out through the front with the six oranges.
The whole incident occupied less than forty seconds, but it
nourished the neighbourhood for a fortnight.

No one ever saw the three mysterious figures again.

Their disappearance admitted of many explanations. Mrs. Greyes believed they
had sailed from Southampton under assumed names. Miss Fritten maintained
that the young man had married the veiled woman in
a subterranean chapel near Samarkand. Mrs. Lipping had private information
that the bearded man's body had been found in the
Thames, though the newspapers, acting under Government instructions, had described
it as a barge.

Meanwhile, Scarrick's flourished. People who had come to witness murder,
diplomacy, or prohibited romance found themselves buying tea, soap, preserved
ginger, and excellent coffee. By Christmas Eve the shelves were
bare enough to suggest either commercial triumph or a visitation
of locusts.

* * *

On the last night before Christmas, Lancelot and Sylvia sat
with Mr. Scarrick in the little room behind the shop.
A bottle of tolerable port stood open among ledgers and
empty sample-boxes.

"I don't know how to thank you," said Mr. Scarrick.
"Everything sold. Even the citron peel."

"The citron peel was our greatest achievement," said Sylvia. "Any
imposture can dispose of dates."

"And the boy?" asked Mr. Scarrick. "He was very good."

"My model, Jimmy," said Lancelot. "He usually poses as Saint
Sebastian, so being pursued was not new to him."

"The veiled lady?"

"Sylvia."

Mr. Scarrick bowed to her with sincere admiration.

"And the dreadful Token?"

"A prune stone painted black."

Mr. Scarrick considered this.

"But the foreign gentleman? The beard alone must have cost
something."

"The foreign gentleman," said Lancelot, stroking his chin, "was myself."

Mr. Scarrick looked from the clean-shaven artist to the invoice
on which Assyrian kings still frowned majestically.

"Then I insist," he said, reaching for the cash-box, "on
paying for the black beard."

And on that point, being a man whose imagination had
strict commercial limits, he would accept no mystery.

In taking possession of my room at the Hôtel des
Sources, I found in the drawer of the writing-table, beneath
a railway timetable, a small notebook bound in green cloth.
The first pages had been torn out; the rest were
covered with a hurried but legible hand.

As the book bore neither name nor address, and as
the waiter, on being questioned, replied merely, "It must have
belonged to the gentleman before Monsieur," I read it.

Here it is, exactly as I found it.

---

**June 3.**

Arrived at Chatel-Guyon at five in the afternoon.

A watering place is a town where people who possess
money and have mislaid their health come to exchange the
former for the hope of recovering the latter. The arrangement
is generally satisfactory to the doctor.

The hotel stands opposite a square full of trees, nurses,
invalids, and those small, disconsolate dogs which seem bred expressly
for elderly ladies. Behind it rise the first wooded slopes
of the mountains. Before it, the valley opens toward a
plain infinite as the sea, pale beneath a bluish vapor.

My room is good. My bed is hard. The wallpaper
represents roses of a species unknown to botanists and evidently
poisonous.

At dinner forty-seven persons ate in silence. The knives made
a sound like rain on a zinc roof. One old
gentleman breathed heavily between mouthfuls, as if each spoonful of
soup were a hill he had to climb.

Afterward I walked to the casino. An orchestra was playing
beneath the trees. No one listened. A flute trilled desperately,
a foolish bird singing to a desert of wicker chairs.

I already regret Paris.

---

**June 4.**

First consultation with Doctor Baraduc.

He struck my chest, listened to my back, questioned my
stomach, doubted my liver, and condemned my habits. Doctors begin
by insulting one's manner of life and end by prescribing
another which no reasonable man would willingly endure.

I am to rise at six, drink three glasses of
the Saint-Mart spring, walk for an hour, breakfast lightly, rest,
bathe, rest again, dine without wine, walk once more, and
sleep at ten.

"What may I do for pleasure?" I asked.

"Get well," said he.

Physicians have no imagination.

At the spring, the patients advance in solemn procession, each
carrying a glass. They drink slowly and look into the
middle distance with the grave attention of priests receiving a
revelation. The water is warm, metallic, and tastes faintly of
an old key.

Weighed: eighty-six kilos.

---

**June 5.**

The treatment.

One meets the same faces at the same turns of
the same paths. We know one another by sight and
distrust one another by instinct. In ordinary life a man
may conceal his ailments, his age, and his appetite; at
a health resort these are announced publicly three times a
day.

A stout lady asked the spring attendant whether the water
would cure jealousy.

"Madame," he replied, "it cures what the doctor writes on
the card."

A prudent answer. It ought to be engraved above every
government office.

---

**June 6.**

Bath at seven. The attendant wrapped me in hot linen
until I resembled a boiled parcel.

Walked afterward to the hill above the town. The morning
was exquisite. The trees descended in green billows toward the
plain; villages shone white among fields of wheat; far away,
the mountains floated like clouds that had become tired of
traveling.

Nature here is admirable. Man alone has spoiled the place
by discovering mineral water in it.

At dinner the old gentleman opposite me said, "Salt, if
you please."

Those were the only words spoken at our table.

---

**June 7.**

Nothing.

Treatment.

---

**June 8.**

Ditto. Ditto.

I have lost one kilo and all desire to live.

---

**June 9.**

This afternoon the resort changed its character completely.

Two ladies arrived.

They are young—or young enough, which is the age that
matters—elegant, handsome, and dressed with that apparent simplicity which costs
more than magnificence. One is fair, with gray eyes and
an indolent smile; the other dark, quick, slender, and inclined
to laughter. They occupy rooms on the second floor and
call themselves Madame de Bréville and Madame Lormier.

Both are widows.

A widow is a woman to whom society allows a
past, permits a present, and watches in order to prevent
a future. She possesses the experience of marriage without the
inconvenience of a husband, and sorrow gives dignity to the
most becoming dresses. There is no social condition more complete.

They dined at the small table beside mine. The dark
one laughed twice. Every head in the room rose from
its plate.

Chatel-Guyon is delightful.

---

**June 10.**

I contrived an introduction.

Nothing is easier when three persons wish to know one
another and good breeding obliges each to pretend otherwise.

At the spring Madame de Bréville dropped her glass. I
picked it up. She thanked me. Madame Lormier observed that
mineral water was the only beverage which made one thirstier
after drinking it. I replied that its chief virtue lay
in providing strangers with a subject of conversation.

We walked together.

They have been here four days, though I had not
seen them because they had dined in their rooms. They
complain of the silence, the hours, the food, and the
doctor. Women become intimate through shared dislikes more quickly than
men through shared principles.

Madame Lormier asked what brought me here.

"My liver, according to the doctor."

"And according to you?"

"Curiosity."

She laughed. The cure is already producing excellent results.

At dinner they invited me to their table. The old
gentleman watched me desert ours with the expression of a
man whose last surviving son has joined a circus.

---

**June 11.**

Walked with my fair companions to the ruined tower above
the woods.

The path climbed beneath chestnut trees. Warm light fell in
round golden patches upon the moss; insects hummed invisibly; now
and then we saw, between the branches, the plain stretching
away beneath a veil of heat.

Madame de Bréville walks slowly and accepts an arm on
steep ground. Madame Lormier runs ahead and turns to mock
those who follow. Thus Nature, always economical, has divided feminine
charm into two methods: one delays you, the other draws
you onward.

From the tower we could see volcanic cones rising beyond
the fields, dark and regular, like extinguished furnaces. Madame de
Bréville declared the view melancholy.

"A beautiful landscape should have a memory," she said.

I replied that beauty has no need of memory; it
provides one for other people.

This seemed to please her.

Returned late for the bath. The attendant reproached me. I
told him exercise had detained me.

"With ladies?" he asked.

The lower classes know everything and respect nothing except exact
change.

---

**June 12.**

Excursion by carriage to Riom.

The entire hotel saw us depart: the two widows facing
forward, I opposite them, with their shawls and parasols entrusted
to my care. Faces appeared at windows. The stout lady
stopped on the steps. The old gentleman turned completely around
in his chair.

There are pleasures which one enjoys directly, and others which
acquire their flavor from witnesses. To be seated between two
pretty women is agreeable; to be seen seated between them
is happiness refined by civilization.

Riom possesses old houses, a church, a clock tower, narrow
streets, and inhabitants who examine foreigners without appearing to do
so. We lunched at an inn. Madame Lormier drank a
glass of white wine, though it is forbidden by her
physician, and made me swear secrecy.

There is no bond like a minor disobedience.

On the return journey Madame de Bréville grew tired and
closed her eyes. Her shoulder rested against mine for nearly
an hour. I remained motionless from consideration, although the road
was atrocious and my left leg went to sleep.

One must suffer for women, if only to have something
definite to remember about them.

---

**June 13.**

The treatment, embellished.

Drank the waters with the widows. Walked with the widows.
Breakfasted with the widows. Sat under the trees with the
widows. Dined with the widows.

Doctor Baraduc says my pulse is faster.

I told him the mountain air was stimulating.

---

**June 14.**

A scandal.

We went after dinner to hear the orchestra. Madame Lormier
wore a red rose at her waist; Madame de Bréville
had no ornament at all, which ornamented her still more.
We sat apart from the others and talked during the
music.

The respectable ladies disapproved. Respectability in a woman often consists
in being offended by the happiness of another.

A colonel's wife, seated behind us, whispered audibly that certain
people came to a health resort for purposes unrelated to
health.

Perfectly true. I came to lose weight, and now wish
only to lose time.

When the lamps were lighted among the trees, the paths
filled with moving shadows. Lanterns wandered under the leaves like
will-o'-the-wisps searching for respectable travelers to mislead. We strolled until
midnight.

Chatel-Guyon by night is enchanting.

---

**June 15.**

We drove to the crater lake at Tazenat.

The road passed between stone walls, orchards, and meadows where
cattle stood knee-deep in grass. Dust rose behind us, smelling
of warm earth, vanilla, and cows. The mountains drew closer,
their sides scarred with gray lava and clothed with broom.

The lake lies in a round, steep hollow, as if
a giant cannonball had struck the mountain and the wound
had filled with water. No house overlooks it. Woods descend
almost to the shore. The surface, dark at the edges
and gleaming in the middle, shone like a silver coin
dropped into a green bowl.

It was hot. There was no one near.

Madame Lormier proposed that we bathe.

Madame de Bréville protested for three minutes and consented in
the fourth. Women use objections as tollgates; once the proper
ceremony has been observed, one may pass.

They disappeared behind a screen of bushes. I retired in
the opposite direction. Presently Madame Lormier called:

"You are not looking?"

"No."

"Why not?"

There are questions which overturn the moral order.

We entered the water. It was cold enough to make
virtue seem easy. The dark widow swam boldly; the fair
one remained near the bank until I offered my hand.
Then she ventured farther, clinging first to my fingers, then
to my arm, then—when the bottom suddenly fell away—to my
neck.

The crater returned her cry in three diminishing echoes.

Above us the sky appeared perfectly round and blue. The
woods stood motionless. No sound reached us except the dripping
of water and Madame Lormier's laughter.

For ten minutes we were no longer civilized persons with
names, duties, doctors, mourning, reputations, and hotel bills. We were
three living creatures in a lonely lake, beneath the sun.

That is perhaps what people call innocence.

On shore, civilization resumed at once. We dressed separately and
discussed whether we might be seen returning with wet hair.

---

**June 16.**

Our expedition is known.

The colonel's wife no longer bows to the widows. The
stout lady bows more deeply to me. Men are generally
credited with adventures by women who condemn the women concerned.

At luncheon the old gentleman asked whether the lake was
cold.

So even he has heard.

I answered, "At first."

He looked at me for a long time and took
two helpings of pudding.

---

**June 17.**

A day of rain.

We remained in the salon. Madame de Bréville embroidered a
flower which, after six hours, resembled a purple spider. Madame
Lormier read a novel, turning the pages at intervals too
regular to suggest attention. I wrote letters and tore them
up.

Rain makes intimacy dangerous. A landscape disperses thought; four walls
concentrate it.

We spoke of marriage.

Madame Lormier declared she would never marry again.

Madame de Bréville said nothing.

I maintained that marriage is an admirable institution for people
who would otherwise be obliged to invent daily reasons for
remaining together. A husband may be dull without explanation; a
lover must constantly justify his presence.

"Then you recommend husbands?" asked the fair widow.

"For other women, certainly."

She looked down at her embroidery.

The purple spider acquired another leg.

---

**June 18.**

To Volvic.

The village is built of black stone and appears to
be in mourning for the volcano that supplied it. Even
the church seems cut from night. Yet gardens crowd against
the dark walls, bright with roses, and children play in
doorways as children will play at the entrance to a
tomb if given a hoop.

We visited a quarry, where men drew blocks from the
ancient lava. The guide spoke of eruptions with personal resentment,
as though the mountain had once competed with his family.

On the way back, we stopped beneath some walnut trees
to eat bread, cheese, and cherries. Madame Lormier pelted me
with cherry stones. Madame de Bréville fell silent.

When I asked whether she was tired, she said, "No.
I was thinking that pleasant days are sad because they
finish."

This is true, but it is a truth one should
conceal from a pleasant day. The knowledge of mortality has
spoiled more happiness than death itself.

I kissed her hand.

Madame Lormier, who had been looking the other way, remarked,
"The carriage is coming."

She sees even with her back.

---

**June 19.**

Two gentlemen arrived this morning.

One is tall, military, sunburned, and carries impatience like a
walking stick. The other is short, pale, carefully dressed, and
has the calm expression of a man who pays bills
only after examining every item.

They asked for Mesdames de Bréville and Lormier.

The widows received them upstairs.

At noon Madame Lormier sent word that she had a
headache. Madame de Bréville did not appear. At three, trunks
were brought down. At four, both ladies descended in traveling
dresses.

The tall gentleman offered his arm to Madame Lormier with
an authority unsuitable to a recent acquaintance. The pale one
spoke to Madame de Bréville in a low voice. She
answered without looking at him.

I approached.

"My dear friend," said Madame Lormier, "we are called away
unexpectedly."

"By relatives?"

"By circumstances."

Circumstances are the most extensive family in Europe.

Madame de Bréville gave me her hand.

"Do not forget the lake," she said.

The pale gentleman glanced at me. His expression suggested that
he knew the lake and disapproved of its management.

The carriage departed.

No explanation. No address. Nothing.

They were widows, doubtless. There are, however, husbands who possess
so little animation that their wives may be pardoned for
overlooking them.

At dinner I returned to my old table. The old
gentleman passed me the salt without being asked.

Chatel-Guyon is intolerable.

---

**June 20.**

Nothing.

Treatment.

The orchestra played in the evening. That idiotic flute again.

---

**June 21.**

Ditto.

Weighed: eighty-two kilos.

Doctor pleased. I am not.

---

**June 22.**

Walked alone to a village beyond the gorge. The road
wound beside a stream, crossed twice by stone bridges, then
climbed among vineyards and walnut trees.

At the inn I met the schoolmaster, a dry little
man with spectacles and a thirst for conversation. He told
me the story of the walnut grove.

Many years ago, he said, the village priest had been
troubled by the frequency of certain sins among the young
women of his parish. The usual penances—prayers, fasting, candles—produced no
visible improvement. Being a practical man, he resolved that invisible
faults should at least yield visible benefits.

Thereafter, whenever a woman confessed to misconduct, he ordered her
to plant a walnut tree beside the road.

"Why walnut trees?" I asked.

"Because, Monsieur, they live long."

The first year, eleven trees appeared. The second, nineteen. By
the fifth year the road was shaded for nearly two
kilometers.

The villagers praised the priest's interest in agriculture. Travelers admired
the avenue. The women, however, complained that each new tree
invited speculation.

One Sunday the priest preached against scandal. He declared that
no Christian had the right to guess by whom any
particular walnut had been planted. The following week someone tied
labels bearing women's names to the younger trees.

The priest removed them at dawn.

At last a severe winter killed half the grove. The
married women rejoiced, the unmarried ones said nothing, and the
men discussed the matter for months.

"And did the penance improve morals?" I asked.

The schoolmaster considered.

"No, Monsieur. But it improved the road."

Thus virtue, when wisely administered, may fail in its first
intention and succeed in another. Governments ought to study theology.

---

**June 23.**

Excursion to the plateau.

A splendid day. The valleys lay green and profound beneath
me; streams flashed among poplars; the distant plain dissolved into
luminous haze. Here and there a volcanic peak rose solitary
from the fields, solemn yet harmless, like an old criminal
who has become respectable.

I lunched on bread and cold chicken beside a shepherd's
hut. A dog barked for twenty minutes without approaching. Dogs
in the country resemble critics: their courage diminishes as the
distance closes.

For an hour I was happy. Then I remembered that
happiness would have been greater had I someone to whom
I could point out the view.

A landscape requires solitude for perfection and companionship for enjoyment.
This is contradictory, but so are most luxuries.

---

**June 24.**

Treatment.

Bath.

Rest.

A letter from Paris concerning business. How vulgar business appears
when one is ill! How important when one begins to
recover!

---

**June 25.**

The stout lady asked if I had news of "my
friends."

I said no.

She sighed with a satisfaction she attempted to disguise as
sympathy.

Public morality is often private curiosity wearing a black veil.

---

**June 26.**

Doctor Baraduc ordered an additional glass at the spring.

I protested that four glasses would drown me internally.

He said, "You have regained color."

There are compliments which amount to punishment.

In the afternoon I watched the new arrivals. A mother
with three daughters; a magistrate with gout; two merchants from
Lyon; an English couple who speak to each other only
when necessary and to foreigners not at all.

No widows.

---

**June 27.**

Weighed: eighty-three and a half kilos!

I demanded a second trial. Same result.

I have regained one kilo and a half in six
days. Doctor says fluctuations are natural. Nature is always invoked
when science has made a mistake.

What is the use of rising at six, drinking heated
iron, being kneaded, steamed, soaked, wrapped, starved, and sent marching
over mountains, if the flesh returns like a creditor?

Resolved to leave.

Packed after dinner. Packing is the one occupation in which
a man discovers how many useless things he considered indispensable
on arrival.

I found, in the pocket of my gray coat, a
cherry stone from Volvic.

I threw it out of the window.

Then I went downstairs, searched for it in the dark,
and could not find it.

---

**June 28.**

Departure.

Final weight: eighty-three kilos.

Net loss: three kilos.

Other losses uncertain.

Chatel-Guyon may be recommended for its air, its waters, its
woods, its crater lake, its roads, its old churches, its
black villages, its absurd patients, and its doctors, who are
no more dangerous than doctors elsewhere.

Without agreeable society, the cure is tedious.

With it, one neglects the cure.

The sensible man should therefore come accompanied by two charming
women in whom he has no emotional interest.

Such women do not exist.

Train at ten.

End.

---

There the manuscript stopped.

I remained at Chatel-Guyon twenty-five days myself. I drank the
same water, endured the same baths, walked upon the same
roads, admired the same plain, heard the same foolish flute,
and was weighed with equally disappointing results.

My experience differed from that of my predecessor in one
essential particular.

I never met the two widows.

# The Flowers on the Lake

Money, which is the blood-current of certain temperaments, had ceased
to circulate in the affairs of Lady Muriel Darnley.

There remained, it is true, the title; there remained also
an address in Half Moon Street, a maid whose fidelity
had survived three reductions in wages, and a wardrobe which,
by a succession of minor miracles, continued to produce effects
in Regent Street. But the title could not discharge an
account, the address belonged in substance to a mortgage company,
and the wardrobe had been turned, dyed, retrimmed and recombined
until the changing of its plumage resembled those theological transformations
by which the angels are said to maintain an eternal
freshness without possessing an annual dress allowance.

Lady Muriel's gloves presented the case more candidly. They had
been cleaned.

There are many degrees in the submerged estate of a
woman of fashion. She may cease to give dinners; she
may dismiss her second maid; she may visit the country
in August when every person whom she respects is over
the water; yet none of these privations is conclusive. But
cleaned gloves are indicatory. They bear witness, as a faded
ribbon does not, to the intervention of economy.

Lady Muriel regarded the pale grey pair upon her hands
as she crossed Regent Street one wet morning in October,
and disliked them. Along the inner hems, almost invisible unless
the gloves were reversed, were certain small black laundry signs—an
angular M, two dots, and the number 47. She had
noticed them when the parcel came home and had intended
to remonstrate. Then the charge for cleaning had seemed so
moderate that she had said nothing.

A fine rain silvered the roadway. Motor-cars glided by like
glossy beetles, and the shop windows exhibited furs, jewels, perfumes
and other necessities of existence to those who can pay
for them. Lady Muriel walked among these evidences of prosperity
with the composed face of one who had never been
denied anything. The empty purse in her muff made no
sound.

At the end of the street she turned beneath an
archway and entered a narrow office, furnished with good rugs,
bad bronzes, and the indefinable expensive ugliness by which international
business sometimes announces that it has no nationality. A boy
with hair brushed flat admitted her to the inner room.

Mr. Karl Hecklemeir rose from behind a broad writing-table.

He was a small, smooth person, precise in his clothes
and almost offensively clean. His oily black hair lay in
parallel curves upon his head; his pale eyes seemed to
alter colour according to the face upon which they rested;
and his pudgy hands, though soft, were capable of a
surprising stillness. In Berlin he might have been taken for
a Viennese, in Vienna for a Swiss, in Paris for
a Levantine, and in London for a foreigner of no
particular country. This chameleon quality had been useful to him.

"My dear Lady Muriel," he said. "You come through such
weather! It is devotion."

"I came for money."

"Ah!"

He resumed his chair. The exclamation was delicately sympathetic and
wholly discouraging.

During the later years of the war Lady Muriel had
been of considerable service to Hecklemeir. Men with important appointments
had talked to her because she was beautiful, because she
was titled, because her indifference stimulated vanity, and because it
did not occur to them that a daughter of the
Darnleys could require five hundred pounds. She had passed on
what she heard. Sometimes it had been trivial; sometimes it
had caused men at a distant desk to move ships,
delay trains or revise a list of names. She had
not inquired. Information was a commodity, like South African shares
or old lace, and only very vulgar persons ask what
becomes of a commodity after they have sold it.

But peace had ruined the trade.

"There is no money," said Hecklemeir, joining the tips of
his fingers. "I have explained this before. Everything is changed.
Your admirals no longer speak in interesting whispers. Your under-secretaries
know nothing worth repeating. The gentlemen who once paid me
now cultivate tobacco, sit on boards, or write memoirs to
prove that they were never connected with me."

"You have other gentlemen."

"Other gentlemen have other requirements."

"I can still obtain information."

He smiled.

"What information?"

"That depends upon what you want."

"That is the misfortune. At present I want nothing."

Lady Muriel opened her muff and took out a gold
cigarette case. It was a good case, though three cigarettes
lay within it in a poverty of silver paper. Hecklemeir
struck a match.

"You owe me something," she said.

"I have paid."

"You made a fortune."

"I preserved my expenses."

"You made a fortune out of people like me."

"Not out of people like you, dear lady. There are
no people like you."

She breathed smoke at him.

"I want a thousand pounds."

"My word!"

"Five hundred, then."

Hecklemeir shook his head. The movement did not disturb the
ordered curves of his hair.

"You are not listening. The old shop is closed. The
shutters are up. It was a wicked little shop, perhaps,
but profitable. Now—" He spread his hands. "Now there is
a League of Nations."

"Damn the League of Nations."

"Many people do. It does not make them solvent."

For one moment the weariness beneath Lady Muriel's composure became
visible. She had lived for five years on expedients, and
the supply of expedients appeared exhausted. The money from the
sale of the Darnley pearls had gone first; then the
settlement inherited from her mother; then the sums which Hecklemeir
paid in envelopes and never entered in books. Credit, that
courteous recognition of one's previous importance, had lingered longest. Now
even credit had developed memory.

"I have sixty pounds due on Friday," she said, "and
rather more on Monday."

"Then Monday is the prior and immediate peril."

"I could sell you the cigarette case."

"I do not smoke."

"You could lend me something on it."

"I am not a pawnbroker."

"No. A pawnbroker has principles."

He laughed at that, but he did not open a
drawer.

Lady Muriel rose.

"The wheel turns, Karl."

"Certainly. That is why prudent persons do not put their
fingers in it."

"One day I may have something you want."

"On that day," said Hecklemeir, "I shall be enchanted to
see you."

She descended again into the wet brilliance of Regent Street.

Gamblers, as I have written elsewhere, possess a peculiar relation
with the future. The ordinary person regards tomorrow as a
consequence; the gambler regards it as an accomplice. Lady Muriel
had never doubted that some event, some person, some forgotten
investment or revived devotion would arrive at the correct hour
and restore her affairs. Misfortune had pursued her, certainly, but
it had never quite overtaken her. There had always been
a door, a cheque, an envelope, a man who believed
her.

Now, walking westward beneath a borrowed umbrella, she considered her
relatives.

The Darnley family tree was extensive, but its branches were
remote, withered, or inhabited by persons who had already refused
her. An elderly cousin in Sussex had contributed fifty pounds
and a sermon. An aunt at Bath had offered a
room, on condition that Lady Muriel abandoned London, tobacco and
cosmetics. Her brother, the present Lord Darnley, communicated only through
solicitors. There remained one buried nut, one hive hitherto unplundered:
her mother's half-brother, Bramwell Winton.

She had not seen him for eleven years.

Bramwell was a biologist, a word which in the Darnley
vocabulary covered socialism, atheism and a regrettable indifference to dinner.
He had quarrelled with Muriel's mother over some question of
property and had attended neither her funeral nor Lady Muriel's
wedding. He was said to possess money, though no one
knew how much, and to spend it upon bones, beetles,
expeditions and other objects incapable of gratitude.

Lady Muriel turned into a bookseller's, consulted a directory, and
wrote down his address.

It was in a declining square beyond Bloomsbury, where the
houses stood shoulder to shoulder with the heavy patience of
old men. Two adjoining residences were shuttered; a third displayed
a physician's brass plate dulled to the colour of old
honey. Bramwell Winton's house had no plate, no bell-push and,
apparently, no servant.

Lady Muriel ascended the steps and pulled an iron handle
beside the door. Somewhere within the house there followed a
metallic concussion and the fall of a small object.

She waited.

Nothing happened.

She pulled again. The handle came farther from the wall,
dragging after it several inches of rusty wire. The bell-pull
had broken.

"Charming," she murmured.

The knocker was massive enough to awaken the dead. She
used it three times. There was still no reply.

The door, however, was not latched.

It opened under her hand into a narrow hall. The
air within was cold and smelled of dust, books and
extinguished fires. A furled umbrella leaned against a marble bust;
crates stood beneath the staircase; and on the tiled floor
lay the brass tongue of the bell, attached to its
severed wire.

"Uncle Bramwell!"

Her voice travelled upward and seemed to be absorbed by
the thick walls.

Then she heard, from the back of the house, the
confused murmur of men's voices.

The social laws governing entrance are less rigid in a
relative than in a stranger, and poverty has its own
ceremonial exemptions. Lady Muriel closed the door behind her and
proceeded along the passage. One room stood open, revealing shelves,
jars and a long table upon which the articulated skeleton
of a monkey appeared to be climbing out of a
dispatch box. Beyond it was another door, shut but imperfectly
fitted. The voices came through in fragments.

"—not a doubt of it," said one. "My word, Winton,
if Tony saw the thing—"

A second voice interrupted, deliberate and rather dry.

"Tony saw what Maartin permitted him to see."

"And Maartin?"

"Dead."

"Convenient for us, by gad."

"Godfrey!"

"Well, damn it, I don't mean convenient that the poor
devil died. But if he had lived he would have
got there first."

Lady Muriel halted.

She had recognised the first speaker. Sir Godfrey Kaye had
been at the Foreign Office during the war and had
once provided her, quite unconsciously, with the disposition of certain
naval stores. He was a lean, impulsive man, richer in
confidence than judgement. If Sir Godfrey had come privately to
Bramwell Winton, and if he spoke of getting somewhere first,
the colloquy might possess a value not immediately apparent.

She moved nearer.

"Let us proceed," said Winton. "Your nephew's diary was recovered
with the rest of his effects?"

"Not all of them. His gun was gone, his boots
were gone, and some rum lot had cut the brass
corners off his medicine chest. But the diary was sewn
inside his sleeping-bag. Tony always wrote everything down. A family
vice."

"And the map?"

"Among the last pages. Here."

There was a rustling of paper.

Lady Muriel looked about her. The passage was empty. On
a chair near the door lay Sir Godfrey's hat and
stick; beside them stood a leather dispatch box with foreign
labels upon it. The voices, though raised, had sounded faint
from the hall. The house was thick-walled and the neighbouring
buildings untenanted. These circumstances did not form a plan in
her mind; they merely registered themselves, as practical details register
in persons whose lives have trained them to estimate opportunity.

Inside the room Sir Godfrey resumed:

"Tony copied the route from Maartin's own map. See—railhead here,
river to Kwandu, then north-east along this range. He says
the natives would not go beyond the black marsh. Said
there were spirits in the water."

"Natives commonly attribute spiritual agency to an unfamiliar animal."

"Animal! Then you agree?"

"I agree that the evidence is not absurd."

"Not absurd? My dear fellow, it is prodigious!"

"You use those words indiscriminately."

"Look at the measurements. Look at the damned watercolour. Tony
saw them with his own eyes."

There was another pause.

Lady Muriel's interest sharpened.

"His own eyes may have been fevered," said Winton.

"He painted the thing the following morning."

"In an amber light, upon paper badly sized, with a
schoolboy's colour-box."

"Still, it is clear enough."

"To me, perhaps."

"My word, Bramwell, you are the coolest fish alive. If
this is what you say, it will set London by
the ears."

"It may interest the Zoological Society."

"Interest! It will make us famous."

"You are already famous."

"I am known in clubs. That is not the same
thing. I mean discovery. Headlines. Lectures. The Royal fellows nodding
as though they had believed it all along. And fortune
too, if we manage it properly."

Lady Muriel placed one gloved hand upon the door-frame.

Fame and fortune.

The words possess a vulgar sound when spoken together, yet
their conjunction is one of the oldest in human aspiration.
To Sir Godfrey fame came first because he had money;
to Lady Muriel fortune came first because she had none.

"How would you make a fortune?" asked Winton.

"There are museums, newspapers, American syndicates. Bring one back alive."

"You propose to bring it alive from the interior of
Africa?"

"Why not?"

"The difficulties would be considerable."

"All the better. Damn it, we shall go on our
own hook. You choose the scientific fellows; I'll finance the
expedition."

"That may cost twenty thousand pounds."

"Thirty, if need be."

Lady Muriel's fingers tightened upon the moulding.

Sir Godfrey could spend thirty thousand pounds to seek a
thing in an African lake, while she could not obtain
five hundred from Hecklemeir. The inequality was not new, but
at that moment it seemed personal.

Within the room Winton said, "The secrecy must be absolute."

"Naturally."

"Maartin's route was never published. Tony's diary has not been
examined by anyone?"

"Only myself."

"And the watercolour?"

"Was in the diary."

"Then we possess the sole record."

"The sole record of the biggest discovery since—well, since somebody
discovered something damned big. You are the man for comparisons."

"A discovery is not established by comparison."

"Oh, don't be a professor, Bramwell. What is it worth?"

"Scientifically?"

"Scientifically, commercially, any way you like."

There followed the creak of a chair.

"If the interpretation is correct," said Winton, "its scientific importance
can hardly be overstated. An isolated survival; a creature modified
through generations to an aquatic existence; perhaps an entire colony
protected by geography and native superstition. It might compel a
revision of several accepted views."

"And commercially?"

"I am not a showman."

"But worth money?"

"All marvels are worth money to somebody."

That sentence, innocently uttered, settled Lady Muriel's conduct.

The door opened suddenly.

She had time only to move two paces and bend
over Sir Godfrey's hat as if its appearance had caused
her surprise. He came out with his face flushed and
a portfolio beneath his arm.

"Lady Muriel!"

"Godfrey! Is this your hat?"

He stared at her, then at the hat.

"It has every appearance of it."

"I called on my uncle. The bell broke, and the
door was open. I thought the house deserted."

"My dear Muriel, you know Bramwell?"

"He is my uncle."

"Of course—by gad, I'd forgotten. These family ramifications defeat me."

Bramwell Winton appeared behind him.

He was a tall, stooping man of sixty, spare to
the point of severity, with iron-grey hair and a face
in which intellectual reserve had hardened into habit. His clothes
were old but not careless; his eyes, beneath straight brows,
were singularly attentive.

"Muriel," he said.

"Uncle Bramwell."

Neither attempted an embrace.

Sir Godfrey, conscious perhaps of an atmosphere but incapable of
enduring one, took up his stick.

"I must run. The committee at three. You'll lock those
papers up, Winton?"

"At once."

"And think over the personnel. My word, this is going
to be a business!"

He shook hands with Lady Muriel and departed along the
passage. The front door shut behind him.

Winton remained looking at his niece.

"You entered without announcement."

"The bell-pull came off."

"Yes. It has been defective."

"I knocked."

"I heard nothing."

"The walls are extraordinarily thick."

"They were built when quietness was considered a virtue."

He led her into the room.

It was a combination of study, laboratory and museum. Books
rose from the floor in unstable columns; the mantelpiece supported
skulls instead of ornaments; maps overlapped on the walls, and
a stuffed bird of melancholy aspect occupied a glass case
by the window. Yet the disorder was not dirty. Every
object, however strange, had been placed for use.

On the central table lay a folded map and a
small watercolour.

Lady Muriel saw it only for an instant before Winton
covered it with a sheet of blotting-paper. The glimpse disclosed
a lake of amber-brown, ringed by black-green reeds. Upon its
surface floated a cluster of curious reddish-purple flowers, each with
a thick, curving stem which disappeared into the water. Beyond
them, scarcely distinguishable from the haze, rose the blue wall
of a mountain.

The picture disappointed her. No temple, no mine, no golden
idol appeared; only the water, the mountain, and those improbable
flowers. But maps and watercolours do not excite wealthy men
to thirty-thousand-pound expeditions unless something lies behind them.

"You wished to see me," said Winton.

"Yes."

"After eleven years."

"I have often intended to come."

"Intention is the most economical form of action."

His tone was not hostile. Hostility might have been easier
to manage. It was merely exact.

Lady Muriel sat down.

"I am in a difficulty."

"So I supposed."

"A temporary difficulty."

"Financial?"

"Yes."

"How much?"

The directness encouraged her.

"A thousand pounds."

"No."

"Five hundred."

"No."

"You have not asked why I need it."

"The explanation would not create the money."

"But you have money."

"I have obligations."

"To dead beetles?"

"To work which I consider important."

"So did Mamma always say. You preferred your specimens to
your family."

"I preferred neither. Your mother made disagreement a test of
affection."

Lady Muriel smiled faintly.

"And you make agreement a condition of assistance?"

"No. I make prudence a condition."

"You think me imprudent."

"I think you have exhausted every easier creditor before remembering
our relationship."

This was so accurate that it seemed insolent.

"I did not come to be analysed."

"No. You came to borrow."

"Then lend me two hundred."

"What security can you offer?"

"My name."

"It has already secured more than it can support."

Her colour changed. Winton noticed it, as he noticed everything,
but did not soften.

"If you are without food or shelter," he said, "I
will provide both. I will also pay any necessary account
directly. I shall not give you money."

"I am not applying for parish relief."

"Then I can do nothing."

On the table, beneath the blotting-paper, lay fame and fortune.
Winton denied her two hundred pounds while Sir Godfrey promised
thirty thousand to pursue the thing represented there.

Lady Muriel rose.

"You have made your position plain."

"I intended to."

"And if I were being persecuted—if I were in danger?"

"Are you?"

She looked at him. A confession concerning Hecklemeir was impossible;
besides, she did not think of her wartime activities as
crimes. They had belonged to another moral climate, one in
which everybody deceived everybody and patriotic virtue was largely a
question of accounting.

"No," she said. "Not in danger."

"That is fortunate."

"Fortune," replied Lady Muriel, "has always behaved well to me
at the last moment."

"So people say who have survived the consequences of trusting
it."

He held the door open.

In the hall she observed, beneath the staircase, a narrow
iron safe set into the wall. It was of antediluvian
manufacture, painted black, with a brass escutcheon rubbed almost white
about the keyhole. Winton went to it carrying the map
and watercolour. Lady Muriel paused to fasten her glove.

From his waistcoat pocket he took a key, inserted it,
and turned it with a slight upward pressure. The lock
was plainly worn. He placed the papers inside, closed the
iron door and tested it twice. Then, instead of returning
the key to his pocket, he laid it upon the
marble shelf above the safe while he bent to move
a crate which obstructed the passage.

A small smooth key, lying carelessly beside the bust of
a Roman emperor.

Primitive instinct is older than ethics, but social instinct is
quicker. Lady Muriel did not look at the key again.
She thanked her uncle for his time, permitted him to
open the front door, and stepped into the square.

At the corner she discovered that she had left her
cigarette case in the study.

She stood beneath the dripping plane trees and considered.

To return openly would be natural. To retrieve the case
and depart would leave matters as they were. Yet inside
that house lay the sole record of a discovery for
which Sir Godfrey Kaye would spend thirty thousand pounds. All
marvels were worth money to somebody. Hecklemeir knew the somebody
for every marvel that could be sold, smuggled, suppressed or
transferred over the water.

Lady Muriel turned back.

The front door had not caught. The defective latch yielded
soundlessly.

The hall was empty.

From above came Winton's measured tread ascending the stairs. A
door closed. Water ran through a pipe in the wall.
Then there was silence.

Her cigarette case lay where she had left it, beside
the chair in the study. She picked it up. Her
hand did not tremble.

There are moments when intention, that economical form of action,
becomes action without the intermediate consent of character. Lady Muriel
returned to the hall.

The key remained upon the marble shelf.

She listened.

Nothing.

She tried to take it, but the smooth metal slipped
between her gloved fingers and clicked against the marble. The
sound seemed enormous. She caught it on the second attempt
and knelt before the safe.

The key entered readily enough, but would not turn.

She remembered Winton's hand lifting slightly. She pressed upward.

Still nothing.

The cleaned gloves, though thin, denied her the proper delicacy
of touch. She drew them off impatiently and placed them
on the floor beside her. The iron was cold beneath
her bare fingers. She raised the key, felt a worn
tooth engage, and turned.

The lock gave with a sigh.

Inside were three shelves. The upper held account books and
a canvas bag sealed with red wax; the lower was
crowded with metal boxes. Upon the middle shelf lay Sir
Godfrey's map and the watercolour.

Lady Muriel took both, then hesitated.

The map was large, difficult to conceal and covered with
an intricacy of lines which meant nothing to her. The
watercolour bore several pencilled words upon the reverse: "Lake Ngama.
Looking N.E. Dawn. Sept. 17. T.W.K. after H. Maartin."

She folded it once and slipped it inside her coat.

The map she replaced.

Then a thing happened.

A board creaked behind her.

Bramwell Winton stood at the foot of the stairs.

Neither spoke at first. His eyes moved from her face
to the open safe, to the key in her hand,
and lastly to the pale gloves upon the floor.

"Put it back," he said.

The command was quiet. Its very quietness abolished pretence.

Lady Muriel rose.

"You misunderstand."

"No."

"I came for my cigarette case."

"And opened my safe?"

"I saw the key. I wanted only to look."

"You have put something inside your coat."

She laughed, but the sound failed.

"Really, Uncle Bramwell—"

"Put it back."

He advanced.

Upon a small table near the safe lay a pistol.
It had been partly covered by old journals; perhaps Winton
had taken it from Sir Godfrey's dispatch box, or perhaps
he kept it there because the house contained valuable instruments.
Lady Muriel saw the dark grip. At the same instant
Winton saw that she had seen it.

He moved toward her.

What followed occupied less time than an explanation of it.
Lady Muriel snatched up the weapon. Winton seized her wrist.
She twisted away. The pistol lay between them, absurd, heavy
and cold.

"There is no need for melodrama," he said.

"Let go of me."

"Give me the pistol."

"Let go!"

There was a sharp report.

Winton stepped backward. His face expressed not pain but astonishment,
as if some proposition of elementary science had unexpectedly proved
false. One hand went to his breast. Then his knees
failed, and he fell against the safe, sliding sideways to
the tiled floor.

The concussion died within the thick walls.

Lady Muriel remained motionless, the pistol extended.

A thread of smoke lifted from the barrel.

"Uncle Bramwell?"

He did not answer.

She bent and touched his neck. She had no medical
knowledge, but death is less ambiguous than sentimental persons imagine.
Bramwell Winton had ceased to be a danger, a relative
or a creditor. He had become a body upon a
floor.

The woman was quick to act.

The pistol had fired by accident. Of that she was
certain. Yet certainty and proof are distant cousins. She wiped
the grip and trigger with her handkerchief, placed the weapon
near Winton's hand, and closed the safe. She removed the
key, rubbed it also, and returned it to the marble
shelf.

Then she listened.

No cry, knock or movement answered the shot. The adjoining
houses were shuttered; the physician's residence showed no face at
its windows. The thick walls which had concealed Sir Godfrey's
excited voice had contained the report.

Fortune had again behaved well to her at the last
moment.

She stepped over Winton's legs, opened the front door and
walked away. Only when she reached the next street did
she remember the watercolour inside her coat. Its presence reassured
her. The thing had not been done for nothing.

At a public telephone she called Hecklemeir.

"I have something," she said.

"What?"

"Something you will want."

"People who say that usually have something I do not
want."

"This concerns an undiscovered place in Africa."

There was a pause.

"Go on."

"A lake. A secret route. A discovery of immense scientific
importance. Sir Godfrey Kaye is prepared to spend thirty thousand
pounds on an expedition."

Hecklemeir's voice altered almost imperceptibly.

"Where are you?"

"Not far away."

"Come at five."

"I want cash."

"If the thing is worth cash."

"And a share."

"My dear Lady Muriel—"

"A share of everything. Newspapers, museums, syndicates, concessions—whatever it is."

"Bring the thing."

She replaced the receiver.

During the next hour she sat in a tea-shop, ordered
nothing but China tea, and studied the watercolour under the
shelter of a newspaper.

It remained incomprehensible.

The amber lake was rendered with some skill; the reeds
leaned in a morning wind; and the mountain beyond had
a cleft summit like a broken crown. The flowers upon
the water were the peculiar feature. There were perhaps twenty
of them, reddish-purple at the tips, each supported by a
thick grey-brown stem which curved upward out of the lake.
No leaves were visible. Beneath the surface the painter had
suggested vast, uncertain shadows.

On the reverse, beneath the inscription, was a rough line
of figures—latitude and longitude, presumably—and a tiny diagram of a
river fork.

She imagined orchids of a new species; medicinal plants; perhaps
some vegetable source of a drug. Yet Sir Godfrey and
Winton had spoken repeatedly of an animal. An isolated survival.
A creature modified to water. Bring one back alive.

The shadows beneath the lake might be animals feeding among
the flowers.

Whatever the thing was, it had killed Bramwell Winton.

Lady Muriel rejected the thought as soon as it presented
itself. Winton had been killed by an accident, and the
accident had no moral relation to the picture. Cause and
blame are often confused by persons who need both to
be simple.

At five precisely she returned to Hecklemeir's office.

He received her alone. The lamps had been lit, and
the rain-darkened windows reflected his polished head and the bronze
figure upon the cabinet behind him. A fire burned low
in the grate.

"Well?"

Lady Muriel remained standing.

"Before I show you, we arrange terms."

"We arrange value first."

"It is the sole record."

"Of what?"

"A discovery in the African interior. The man who found
it is dead. Another man who saw the record is—"
She stopped.

"Is what?"

"Unable to interfere."

Hecklemeir's pale eyes settled upon her face.

"Continue."

"Sir Godfrey Kaye intends to finance an expedition. My uncle,
Bramwell Winton, said the discovery might compel a revision of
accepted scientific views. Those were his words. He said its
importance could hardly be overstated."

"Bramwell Winton the biologist?"

"You know him?"

"I know of him."

"He also said every marvel is worth money to somebody."

"That is not invariably true."

"Sir Godfrey spoke of museums and American newspapers."

"Sir Godfrey speaks of many things."

"He will spend thirty thousand pounds."

"That proves only that he has thirty thousand pounds."

Lady Muriel drew the folded paper from her coat.

"Five hundred pounds now," she said, "and a quarter share
in any profit."

Hecklemeir held out his hand.

"Let me see."

"Your word first."

"My word depends upon the object."

She laid the watercolour upon his table but retained one
corner beneath her fingers.

At first he looked merely attentive. Then his brows lifted.
He drew the lamp nearer.

"Where did you get this?"

"That does not concern you."

He bent over the image.

The pudgy hands, ordinarily so quiet, moved rapidly now. He
turned the sheet, read the inscription, examined the pencilled figures,
and held the paper sideways to the light.

"You have the map?"

"This is the map."

"No. This is an image. There was another document."

"How do you know?"

"The route would require a larger map."

"I can get it."

The words escaped before prudence could revise them.

Hecklemeir looked up.

"Can you?"

"Yes."

"From Winton?"

"Possibly."

There was an interval in which the fire settled with
a small collapse of ash.

Then Hecklemeir laughed.

It was not the laugh of triumph she had expected,
nor even the cautious pleasure of a merchant who has
recognised a valuable article. It was a brief, incredulous sound.

"What is amusing?"

"You do not know what this is."

"I know enough."

"You know nothing."

"Then tell me."

He pointed to the reddish-purple flowers.

"You see those?"

"Obviously."

"They are not flowers."

"What are they?"

"The tips of trunks."

She stared at him.

"Trunks?"

"Elephants' trunks."

"That is absurd."

"Look at the shadows beneath the water. There—the forehead, the
ridge of a back, the ear folded close. These are
elephants, Lady Muriel. Aquatic elephants."

The phrase had an almost nursery foolishness.

She leaned over the picture. Once interpreted, the flowers altered
before her eyes. The thick stems were indeed trunks raised
for air; the reddish-purple tips were nostrils touched by the
dawn. Beneath each floated the drowned bulk of a creature,
enormous and tranquil.

Hecklemeir sat back.

"There have been rumours for fifty years," he said. "Maartin
searched for them. So did Van Huys and that Belgian
fellow—Perron. A race of small elephants living almost entirely in
water. They feed below the surface, travel by hidden channels
and come ashore only in the rains. Native stories, travellers'
nonsense. No specimen, no photograph. This—" He tapped the sheet.
"This may be the first evidence."

"Then it is priceless."

"To a zoologist, perhaps."

"You said there were rumours. Foreign governments—"

"Do not purchase elephants because they swim."

"Newspapers will."

"Newspapers purchase a verified story. Museums purchase a body. You
have a schoolboy's watercolour of purple objects on brown water."

"With the route."

"You do not have the route."

"I told you I can get it."

He folded his hands upon his waistcoat.

"My dear lady, what precisely do you expect me to
do? Sell aquatic elephants to the French? Blackmail the British
Empire with them? Inform Berlin that a herd of pachyderms
has learned to hold its breath?"

"Sir Godfrey sees money in it."

"Sir Godfrey sees headlines."

"You have contacts in America."

"I have contacts who buy oil concessions, political letters, industrial
processes and secrets capable of altering frontiers. This alters only
a paragraph in a book of natural history."

"You said it may be the first evidence."

"I am personally delighted. It is charming. A fairy tale
with tusks."

"You will not buy it?"

"No."

"Not even for a hundred pounds?"

"No."

"Fifty?"

Hecklemeir's expression became almost kind, which was worse than contempt.

"I would not buy it for five."

Lady Muriel snatched up the picture.

"You are lying."

"I lie when it is profitable. At present it is
easier to tell the truth."

"You mean to obtain the map yourself."

"To what purpose?"

"To sell it to Sir Godfrey."

"He already knows where it is."

"To a museum, then."

"The museum will write Winton a letter of congratulation and
ask him to donate the material."

At the name, Lady Muriel's face changed.

Hecklemeir noticed.

"Where is your uncle?" he asked.

"At home."

"And you obtained this from him?"

"That is my affair."

"You said the man who saw the record could not
interfere."

"I meant Maartin."

"No. Maartin has been dead for some time, and you
spoke in the present case."

"You are becoming tedious, Karl."

"Possibly. You are becoming interesting."

She put the watercolour inside her coat.

"I thought you had intelligence."

"I have. That is why I do not purchase zoology
from frightened ladies."

"I am not frightened."

"No? Then you are angry. I prefer you angry."

Lady Muriel went to the door.

Hecklemeir rose with the smooth politeness he used at the
conclusion of unprofitable interviews.

"One moment."

She turned.

"If you do obtain the map," he said, "do not
bring it here."

"You may regret that."

"I regret many things. They seldom include what I have
refused to buy."

In the outer office the flat-haired boy was putting papers
into a cabinet. Beside him stood a broad-shouldered man in
a dark overcoat, turning his hat between his hands. He
had a red, candid face, a clipped moustache and boots
too substantial for Hecklemeir's rugs.

When Lady Muriel appeared, the man looked at her with
the apologetic interest of a person who knows her name
but is uncertain whether to use it.

"Lady Muriel Darnley?"

"Yes?"

He took a small paper parcel from the cabinet.

"These belong to you, I believe."

She did not accept it.

"What is it?"

"A pair of ladies' gloves. Grey kid."

For the first time that day her composure failed openly.

"Where did you find them?"

"I did not find them, my lady. They were handed
in."

"By whom?"

"A gentleman who thought you might have left them behind."

"What gentleman?"

The man smiled in a plain, official manner.

"Perhaps you had better look first."

He untied the string and exposed the cleaned grey gloves.

They lay neatly together, one across the other, faintly creased
at the fingers. Along the inner hem of the upper
glove showed the angular M, the two dots, and the
number 47.

The passage in Bramwell Winton's house returned to her with
complete and merciless clarity: the smooth key slipping between her
gloved fingers; the click upon the marble; her impatience; the
gloves placed on the tiled floor beside the open safe.

She had not picked them up.

The man in the overcoat watched her. His manner remained
deferential, but deference is not always submission. Sometimes it is
merely the glove worn by authority.

"Are they yours, my lady?"

Lady Muriel made an effort to smile.

"They resemble mine."

"There is a laundry mark inside."

"I know nothing about laundry marks."

"No? Scotland Yard keeps a very useful register of them.
Most cleaning establishments have a private sign, you see, and
the customer is entered under the number. Quite convenient when
an article has been mislaid."

Behind her, in the inner office, Hecklemeir had become silent.

The detective—he was unmistakably that now—wrapped the gloves again with
patient fingers.

"You removed them recently, I think."

"I lose gloves constantly."

"Ladies often do."

"I may have left them in a cab."

"That is possible."

"Or a restaurant."

"Also possible."

"Then why bring them here?"

The detective glanced toward Hecklemeir's door.

"We have been making inquiries, my lady."

The blood-current of Lady Muriel's life seemed to stop.

Until that instant she had thought the principal disaster to
be the worthlessness of the watercolour. She had imagined Winton's
death behind her, contained by thick walls, shuttered houses and
fortunate silence. She had imagined herself floating, as she had
always floated, beyond the reach of consequence. The pistol had
been wiped. The key had been wiped. No servant had
seen her. The bell was broken. The door had stood
open.

But the gloves had lain beside the safe.

Cleaned gloves: witness of economy, instrument of theft, preservers of
the bare fingers she had exposed to iron, paper and
key. Cleaned gloves, with the small official hieroglyphics of poverty
stitched upon their hems.

Fortune, that courteous accomplice of gamblers, had not deserted her
at the last moment.

It had merely waited there before her, in a plain
dark overcoat, holding the parcel by its string.

"Accident!" the old man repeated, drawing the travelling rug more
closely over his knees. "You have only changed the name
of the mystery, my dear sir; you have not explained
it. If a tile falls from a roof and breaks
the head of Ivan instead of Piotr, you say accident—and
there is an end. A very convenient little word! It
is like putting a black curtain before a door and
declaring that there is no room beyond it."

The train swayed gently. Outside, in the darkness, wet telegraph
wires rose and fell against a paling strip of sky.
Our fellow-passengers were asleep: one in the corner with his
mouth open, another stretched upon the upper berth, his boots
protruding beneath the curtain. The old man and I had
been speaking, I forget how, of misfortune, and I had
made some ordinary remark about chance.

"No," he continued, lifting one thin finger, "I do not
maintain that everything is ordered. God preserve me from systems!
At my age one distrusts systems, doctors, and people who
know precisely what will happen next Thursday. But I have
observed—permit me to use that modest word—that in certain lives
there is a recurrence, a rhythm, a terrible fitness. You
look back and say: it could not have ended otherwise.
Yet, until the last moment, nothing compelled it to end
so. That is what I call absurd logicality."

He smiled apologetically.

"An ugly phrase, eh? I invented it twenty years ago
and have been ashamed of it ever since. Nevertheless, I
keep it. We retain our bad teeth because they have
served us too long.

"You think I am talking of Providence. Perhaps. Or Fate.
Or simply Something—capitalise it, if that assists you—which completes a
life as an artist completes a line. Only His art,
if He is an artist, is sometimes cruelly humorous. Consider
the great men. A conqueror climbs over nations, wears out
horses, soldiers, wives, ministers, maps; Europe shakes when he coughs—and
at the end he quarrels with a physician over medicine
and complains that the wind is damp. Another spends his
life praising liberty and dies begging a servant not to
leave him alone. A miser is buried in a costly
coffin; an atheist calls for a priest; a saint is
bitten by his own dog. You see? The conclusion seems
ridiculous, yet it gathers the whole life into one point.

"Of course these examples prove nothing. Historical examples are like
old coins: so many hands have rubbed them that the
face upon them is no longer visible. Besides, after dinner
every elderly gentleman becomes a philosopher. I am no philosopher.
I was a minor official, then an inspector, then an
unnecessary person with a pension. But I have lived alone
a great deal, and solitude, though it spoils the temper,
sometimes improves the eyesight.

"To see a human being—really to see him—you must forget
yourself for half an hour. This is difficult. We look
at another and ask: does he admire me? can he
help me? does he bore me? If you put those
questions aside, even the most ordinary fellow begins to shine,
not always pleasantly, but distinctly. I learned to do that
on railways. There is no better observatory. A carriage is
a little world compelled to confess itself. People eat, snore,
quarrel, tell lies, show photographs, remove their boots, and imagine
they remain mysterious.

"I shall tell you a case. Not to convince you—I
assure you; that is, I do not assure you of
anything—but because whenever I hear that word accident, I see
again a blue cap flying through the air.

"It was eighteen years ago, in early autumn. I was
travelling from Tomsk to Petersburg. The journey was then long
enough to make friendships, enemies, and, if one were young,
engagements. At Tomsk a young engineer entered our carriage. He
was perhaps thirty-two, broad in the shoulders, fresh-coloured, with a
small fair moustache and those bright, slightly protruding eyes one
sees in a sucking pig or a very good-natured boy.
Forgive the comparison; it is exact and not disrespectful. He
wore a blue cloth cap, a grey travelling blouse belted
at the waist, high boots, and across his body two
straps—one belonging to a dispatch case, the other to field-glasses.

"Behind him came a porter with luggage enough for a
diplomatic mission: two trunks, a basket, a rolled carpet, a
wooden box pierced with holes, three parcels tied in coloured
string, and an oblong case which he guarded as though
it contained the Crown jewels.

"'Careful, brother, careful!' he cried. 'That case is for my
wife. If you break it, she will forgive you, because
she forgives everybody; but I shall throw you under the
train.'

"He said this laughing, gave the porter twice the proper
fee, then turned to us and bowed.

"'Gentlemen, I beg your pardon in advance. I am bringing
home half Asia, and it will inconvenience your legs.'

"He began at once to arrange, rearrange, and explain his
possessions. The wooden box held a little dog—a shaggy, yellow,
asthmatic creature purchased for his daughter. The long case contained
a Chinese screen painted with cranes. There were silks for
his wife, a carved pipe for his brother-in-law, tea for
his mother, a Buriat knife for a friend, a fur
cap for somebody's child, stones, coins, photographs, toys, dried flowers,
two bottles of liqueur, and a white bearskin which, he
informed us, had been intended for a minister but which
his wife would probably put in the nursery.

"'Five years!' he said, holding up an open hand. 'Do
you understand? Five years in the Far East, with only
six months at home in all that time. When I
left, my little girl could say "papa," but she called
every bearded man papa, so it was not a great
distinction. Now she writes me letters. Look!'

"He showed us a sheet covered with enormous crooked characters:
COME SOON DEAR PAPASHA I AM BIG AND MAMASHA WAITS.

"He kissed the paper before returning it to his dispatch
case.

"There were four of us besides him: a timber merchant
who slept, ate, and scratched his beard; a student who
had lost money at cards and preserved a proud silence;
an old Siberian in a sheepskin coat; and myself. The
Siberian had a narrow brown face, a white beard divided
upon his chest, and eyes so still that he resembled
an ikon darkened by smoke. He rarely spoke. The engineer,
however, spoke enough for us all.

"Within an hour we knew his name—Alexei Mikhailovich Voronin—his salary,
the dimensions of his house near Petersburg, the colour of
his daughter's hair, his wife's habit of losing thimbles, and
the exact words in which she had accepted his proposal
eight years before.

"'I said, "Liza, I have debts, no prospects, and a
bad temper." She said, "Lyosha, your debts are small, your
prospects will improve, and your temper is not bad but
noisy." There! A woman who understands engineering as well as
marriage.'

"He laughed so heartily that the sleeping merchant awoke and
looked offended.

"Voronin apologised by offering him a cigar. He offered us
all cigars. When the student declined, he gave him an
orange. At the next station he bought cakes, pears, smoked
fish, newspapers, and little clay whistles in the form of
birds. He gave the fish to the Siberian, the newspapers
to me, the pears to the merchant, and the whistles
to nobody, because they were for his daughter.

"'She is nine. No, nearly ten. Imagine! I remember her
at four, with knees like buns and one tooth missing.
How can she be ten? It is fraudulent.'

"Then he grew thoughtful for perhaps half a minute, and
I saw beneath his cheerfulness something feverish, almost painful. He
had so long pictured his return that the real journey
seemed unable to move quickly enough. Every halt offended him.
Every mile already passed became worthless; only the miles remaining
possessed importance.

"At one station a telegram was brought to him. He
tore it open, read it, turned red, and read it
aloud without asking whether we wished to hear:

"'WE COUNT HOURS. ALL WELL. MASHA WEARS BLUE DRESS. LIZA.'

"'You see? They count the hours!'

"He wrote an answer upon his knee:

"'FLYING TO YOU. HEALTH EXCELLENT. EMBRACE BOTH. ALEXEI.'

"'Flying!' muttered the Siberian, looking out of the window. 'On
rails men do not fly.'

"Voronin laughed.

"'With this railway, grandfather, one does not even creep.'

"The Siberian did not answer at first. Then he said:

"'Do not pull the day toward you. It will come
by itself.'

"That was all. Do not imagine an omen in the
theatrical sense. The old fellow probably meant only that impatience
was foolish. But I remembered the words afterwards. We always
remember such words afterwards; before the event they are pebbles,
after it they become milestones.

"As we moved westward, the engineer's gaiety increased. He played
preference with the merchant and lost deliberately—or so he claimed.
He told stories of rivers in flood, of bridges carried
away, of a bear that had entered a surveyor's tent
and eaten soap, of a district chief who inspected a
road that did not exist. He mimicked officials, priests, Cossacks,
Chinese traders, even his own director. He was vain, yes;
noisy, indiscreet, sentimental, and a little exhausting. By the second
day the student hid behind a book whenever Voronin began,
'That reminds me—' But he was generous in the careless
way of happy men, and his happiness warmed the carriage
like a stove.

"At Omsk he received another telegram.

"'THREE DAYS ONLY. MASHA CANNOT SLEEP. I ALSO. LIZA.'

"He pressed it to his lips, then became embarrassed.

"'Gentlemen, laugh if you please. A married man kissing a
telegram is a comic object. But paper is better than
nothing.'

"He showed us photographs. Liza Sergeyevna was a slender woman
with serious dark eyes and hair parted smoothly in the
old fashion. In one photograph she stood beside a garden
chair; in another she held the hand of a solemn
little girl with two long plaits.

"'That was two years ago. Liza says she is thinner
now. Why thinner? Tell me, why should she be thinner?
She writes that she is perfectly well, but women conceal
everything to spare us. Perhaps the child has been ill.
Perhaps—'

"He stopped, alarmed by possibilities which had not existed a
moment before.

"'Nonsense,' I said. 'The telegram says all is well.'

"'Certainly! Of course. I am an idiot.'

"His spirits returned at once. He opened one of the
bottles of liqueur and insisted that we drink to wives,
daughters, roads, bridges, and the approach of Europe. Even the
student drank. The Siberian merely touched the glass to his
lips.

"That night I awoke and found Voronin sitting by the
window in his shirt, looking into the darkness. The carriage
lamp had been lowered. At intervals sparks flew past the
glass, and his face appeared and disappeared.

"'You cannot sleep?' I asked.

"'I have slept for five years,' he said. 'Why sleep
now?'

"Then, lowering his voice, he told me what he had
not told the others. In the first year of his
absence he and his wife had quarrelled by letter. A
foolish matter—money sent late, a sentence misunderstood, his jealousy of
a cousin who visited the house. For two months they
had written coldly. Then the child became ill, and fear
dissolved their pride. Since that time, he said, their letters
had grown more tender with every post.

"'Separation has taught us everything. When people live together, they
waste days. They quarrel over lamps, soup, mud in the
hall. At a distance you see the whole person. I
know now what she is. She knows me. We shall
begin again, but without stupidity. The best years are before
us.'

"He said this not boastfully but with such complete conviction
that I felt a chill. Why? I cannot tell you.
We commonly speak of next week, next year, old age;
without that harmless arrogance we could not plant a tree
or order a winter coat. Yet in his mouth the
future sounded too certain, too fully furnished. He had already
entered every room of it. He saw the Chinese screen
beside the piano, the bearskin in the nursery, his wife
pouring tea, his daughter blowing the clay whistle. Not one
chair had been left for misfortune.

"Do not misunderstand me. I did not foresee his death.
If I had foreseen it, I should have tied him
to the seat at Petersburg. I merely felt what one
feels in a theatre when the music becomes too sweet.

"In the morning he was himself again. At every considerable
station a telegram awaited him, and from every considerable station
he sent one. The words changed little:

"'TWO DAYS. WE SHALL MEET TRAIN. MASHA SENDS THOUSAND KISSES.'

"'ONE DAY. CANNOT BELIEVE. HAVE BOUGHT EVERYTHING. YOUR LYOSHA.'

"'TOMORROW TOMORROW TOMORROW. LIZA.'

"He showed us that last one with tears in his
protruding eyes.

"'Three tomorrows! That is my wife. She never wastes exclamation
marks, but she repeats words.'

"As the country grew more familiar to him, he pointed
out everything: a church cupola, a factory chimney, the pale
soil, the shape of the huts.

"'Russia!' he cried, though we had been in Russia all
along.

"He distributed the remaining oranges, gave his spare gloves to
a porter, and attempted to present the yellow dog to
the student because he suddenly feared Masha might dislike it.
Five minutes later he took it back, ashamed of having
doubted her.

"Near Moscow the merchant left us. Voronin embraced him. The
merchant, who had complained of him for three days, kissed
him twice and said:

"'May God grant you joy.'

"The Siberian left at the next junction. Before descending, he
looked for a long while at Voronin.

"'Well, grandfather?' the engineer said. 'Now may I pull the
day toward me?'

"'No,' replied the old man. 'Stand when the train moves.'

"'I always stand.'

"'Then sit.'

"He climbed down with his bundle and vanished among peasants,
soldiers, baskets, steam. Voronin laughed, but less loudly than usual.

"By the final day he could neither read nor converse.
He cleaned his boots, brushed his blouse, changed his collar,
opened and closed the dispatch case, counted the presents, misplaced
his ticket, found it in his cap, lost it again,
and accused the dog of eating it. Every quarter of
an hour he asked the conductor whether we were on
time.

"'Three minutes late.'

"'Why?'

"'A signal.'

"'Can we recover them?'

"'Perhaps.'

"'We must.'

"The conductor shrugged.

"Then the engineer took out his watch and began calculating
our speed from the posts beside the line. He knew
the distances and made figures upon the margin of a
newspaper.

"'Fifty-eight versts an hour. Now sixty-one. Good! If we maintain
sixty-one—no, the curve will reduce us. Curse the curves! Why
do railways curve?'

"An engineer asking why railways curve! The student smiled for
the first time in two days.

"Toward evening the light failed. Dirty clouds lay low over
the land. We passed wet fields, rusty pines, black pools,
settlements huddled round factories, sidings crowded with freight wagons. The
rails multiplied. Telegraph poles hurried backward in endless procession; signal
lamps appeared, red and green, then disappeared; locomotives stood breathing
beneath water towers like chained beasts. Everything announced the capital—the
smoke, the noise, the converging lines, the increasing exactness of
movement.

"Voronin no longer sat. He stood at the window, wiping
the glass with his sleeve.

"'How far?'

"'Thirty versts.'

"'And now?'

"'Twenty-two.'

"'We are slowing! Why are we slowing?'

"At a suburban platform he mistook a woman in black
for his wife and cried out. When she turned, old
and broad-faced, he laughed, but his hand trembled.

"'I shall not recognise Masha,' he said. 'That is my
punishment. My own daughter will stand before me and I
shall ask, "Whose little girl are you?"'

"'She will recognise you,' I told him.

"'From photographs? I have grown old.'

"He had not grown old; he looked younger than any
of us.

"Night had nearly fallen when the outer lamps of Petersburg
appeared. Rain streaked the windows. Passengers drew on coats and
reached for bundles. Porters could already be imagined before they
were seen; everyone became restless with the peculiar agitation of
arrival. The train moved slowly now between walls, warehouses, lines
of empty carriages. Wheels clattered over points. A whistle screamed
beneath an iron roof.

"Voronin had put on his blue cap and slung both
straps across his chest. He held the parcel of whistles
in one hand and the dog's box in the other.
I offered to carry something.

"'No, no! They must see me as I am—loaded with
tribute.'

"Then we entered the station.

"You know how a platform passes the windows at first
slowly, yet too quickly for the eye: pillars, lamps, advertisements,
benches, faces, porters in white aprons, soldiers, a child waving,
a woman running beside a carriage. Voronin pressed his forehead
to the glass.

"'There! No—there! Liza! Masha!'

"I saw them almost at the same instant. The photograph
had prepared me. Liza Sergeyevna wore a dark coat and
held herself very straight. The girl beside her was in
blue, exactly as the telegram had promised, and clutched a
bouquet wrapped in paper. They had not yet seen him.
They were searching the windows with strained, shining faces.

"Voronin flung open the door before the train had stopped.

"'Wait!' I said.

"He stood upon the step.

"'Liza! Mashenka! Here! Here I am!'

"They saw him.

"I cannot describe their faces. Or rather, I can describe
them too easily: recognition, disbelief, joy—ordinary words. But the thing
itself was extraordinary. The woman lifted both hands. The child
dropped the bouquet and ran.

"Voronin leaned outward. Perhaps the box struck the doorpost; perhaps
the strap of his dispatch case caught upon the handle;
perhaps his wet boot slipped on the iron step. I
saw his cap fly off. I saw his body turn,
not fall straight, but twist as though somebody beneath him
had seized his shoulder. For one fraction of a second
his face appeared below the level of the carriage floor—astonished,
almost offended.

"Then he disappeared.

"There was a shout. The train jolted. Something passed beneath
us.

"That is all I myself saw.

"Do you understand why I make the distinction? People decorate
horror, even unwillingly. Afterwards one man said Voronin had called
his wife's name while falling; another declared that he smiled;
a porter swore the child touched his hand. None of
that is true—or, at least, I cannot testify to it.
I heard only his first cry of greeting and then
the cry of the crowd.

"When the train stopped, I jumped down. Men were running
back along the platform. Liza Sergeyevna stood motionless, still with
her hands raised. The little girl had picked up neither
flowers nor father; she looked from her mother to the
wheels as though waiting for an explanation.

"They would not let us near at first. A railway
official pushed people away. Someone called for a doctor. The
dog, trapped in its box, barked with a thin, absurd
fury.

"At last I saw what remained. The wheels had passed
over the chest and shoulder. The cheerful body, the grey
blouse, the fair moustache, the arms that had carried gifts
across half a continent—all were reduced to a distorted red
lump upon the greasy stones. One boot had come off.
The dispatch case lay open, and among the rails were
telegrams, photographs, visiting cards, a child's letter, and two clay
whistles.

"Poor boy!

"The doctor knelt, rose at once, and covered him. There
was nothing to be done. We are fond of that
phrase also: nothing to be done. It is perhaps the
truest phrase in human language.

"I remained because somebody had to remain. The student went
for the police; I accompanied the widow to a room
at the station. She did not faint. People seldom behave
in great sorrow as novels instruct them. She sat beside
a table on which there was an inkstand chained to
its place and asked whether he had suffered. I lied.
Then she asked whether he had spoken of her during
the journey.

"'Continually,' I said.

"'And of Masha?'

"'Continually.'

"She nodded, as if receiving confirmation of a fact long
known.

"After some time she said:

"'We were too sure.'

"I thought she meant too sure of recognising him, or
of the hour of the train.

"'Too sure of what?' I asked.

"'Of being happy. For five years we said: afterwards. Afterwards
we shall live, afterwards we shall not quarrel, afterwards everything
will be repaid. In the last month we spoke as
if happiness were already ours. We should not have done
that.'

"'My dear lady,' I said, 'surely you do not blame
yourself for hoping?'

"She looked at me with a calmness more terrible than
tears.

"'When happiness hears its name called too loudly, it turns
away.'

"Later, of course, she wept. The child screamed when they
took away the dog's box, thinking perhaps that they were
stealing her last gift. Officials wrote reports. Porters swept the
platform. Another train arrived on the adjoining line; passengers descended,
embraced, complained about luggage, bargained with cabmen. Within an hour
the station had swallowed the whole event.

"I visited Liza Sergeyevna once, several months afterwards. The Chinese
screen had been repaired and stood in a corner. The
bearskin lay in the nursery. Masha showed me the yellow
dog, now fat and wheezing. On the mantelpiece, beneath Alexei
Mikhailovich's portrait, were the two clay whistles. The widow did
not repeat her remark, and I did not repeat mine.

"Was she right? Had they tempted Fate by counting the
hours? That is a savage notion. Must we whisper our
joy lest the heavens overhear? Must a mother, seeing her
child recover, pretend indifference? Must lovers conceal their love from
God like thieves concealing silver from the police? I refuse
such a doctrine.

"And yet—there is the blue cap.

"You say the boot slipped. Certainly. The step was wet,
the train was moving, the man was excited. Give me
a report, and I will sign it: accidental death caused
by imprudence. Every link is visible. Nothing supernatural occurred. Rails,
speed, rain, a strap, a foot placed badly—that is enough.

"But why does the conclusion seem so complete? Five years
of longing; thousands of versts; letters, calculations, signals, telegrams; two
figures waiting upon the platform; the train entering at the
appointed minute; his eyes finding them among hundreds—and then, at
the exact point where anticipation should become possession, the wheels
finish the sentence. Absurd! Monstrous! Yet with that same absurd
logicality of which I spoke.

"Perhaps we sit too near the woven cloth. We see
one dark thread crossing one bright thread and cry that
the pattern is spoiled. A prophet, a poet, a saint—someone
lifted high above himself—may glimpse the design. I do not.
I see only knots.

"Or perhaps there is no design at all. Perhaps existence
is not woven but tangled, and the pattern is invented
by old men in railway carriages because they cannot endure
a stain without imagining a hand that placed it there.
That also is possible. More than possible.

"Still, when supreme joy rises to its highest point, I
sometimes feel that Something waits beside it—not as punishment, no,
and not from envy, but as though completion demanded an
ending. Had Voronin lived, he might have quarrelled again with
Liza over lamps and soup. His daughter might have disappointed
him. He might have become bald, cautious, important, unfaithful; he
might have forgotten the five years that had made his
love so pure. Instead, for one second, he possessed everything:
wife, child, home, forgiveness, the future itself. Then the future
was taken away before it could become ordinary.

"Is that consolation? Certainly not. It is an abomination to
console the dead with metaphysics.

"I tell you only what I saw—and what, against my
reason, I felt."

The old man fell silent. Dawn had broadened beyond the
window. Fields emerged from the mist; the wet rails shone,
running side by side toward a curve whose end we
could not see.

After a while I said, perhaps merely to break the
silence:

"But you still call it accident?"

He looked at me, then at the waking passengers, and
smiled with great weariness.

"My dear sir," he said, "what else should we call
it?"

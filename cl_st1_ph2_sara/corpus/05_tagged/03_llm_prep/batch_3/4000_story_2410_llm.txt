# THE CANDLE OF WAULING

## I. Captain Walshawe

My uncle, Mr. Robert Watson, who died in the year
1848, at the respectable age of seventy-six, was neither a
visionary nor a liar. I mention these two negative virtues
at once, because the narrative I am about to relate
rests principally upon his testimony; and though he possessed many
positive excellences—being punctual in money matters, moderate at table, attentive
to sermons, and so exact in his recollection of dates
that he could tell you upon what day of the
week his grandfather's grey mare cast a shoe on the
road to Carlisle—still, for our present purpose, his freedom from
invention and illusion is the important consideration.

He was, I admit, a Methodist; and he had consequently
some notions concerning spiritual agency which my father, a Churchman
of the old port-and-parson school, thought unnecessarily particular. But my
uncle was no fool. He knew a curtain from a
ghost, and the creaking of a bedstead from the discourse
of a disembodied sinner. He was moreover a magistrate, had
examined witnesses in cases of sheep-stealing and assault, and understood,
beyond a cavil, the distinction between what a man supposes
and what he sees.

The extraordinary occurrence which he related to me took place
at Wauling, an old house in the west of England,
shortly after the death of Captain James Walshawe, his cousin
on the mother's side.

Captain Walshawe had been, in his youth, a remarkably handsome
vagabond. He entered the army with a commission purchased by
his father, a gentleman of some landed estate and very
little prudence, and distinguished himself less in the service of
his Majesty than in the service of tailors, vintners, horse-dealers,
actresses, and certain gentlemen who lived by the turn of
a card. Before he was five-and-twenty he had fought two
duels, broken three solemn promises of marriage, and contracted debts
enough to have sunk an alderman.

He used to speak of his misfortunes as though Providence
had selected him for peculiar persecution. If his horse lost
at Newmarket, the jockey was a villain; if he lost
at hazard, the dice were loaded; if a tradesman requested
payment, commerce was become intolerably grasping; and if a young
woman took his vows seriously, the sex had abandoned all
delicacy and moderation. He sowed his wild oats, in short,
with such industry that the crop came up thorns, docks,
nettles, and thistles, and left scarcely a wholesome inch of
ground in him.

At six-and-twenty, being pursued by a London attorney and two
bailiffs, he passed into Ireland with a brother officer. There
he met Margaret O'Neill, commonly called Peg, the only child
of a wealthy grazier near Clonmel.

Peg was not beautiful. My uncle, who had seen a
miniature of her, described her as a round, good-humoured, potato-faced
girl, with black eyes, a broad mouth, and a complexion
which came and went under the least agitation. She had,
however, forty thousand pounds, besides an estate charged only with
an annuity to an aunt; and Captain Walshawe, whose perception
in such matters was wonderfully acute, discovered within a fortnight
that her laugh was musical, her hand exquisite, her understanding
uncommon, and her person altogether superior to the insipid regularity
admired by ordinary men.

The O'Neills, who were Catholics and had heard something of
the Captain's reputation, opposed the match. Walshawe thereupon became romantic.
He wrote verses, watched beneath windows, bribed a maid, swore
that he should perish, and, having no intention of doing
so, persuaded Peg to elope with him.

They were married privately. Her father, after a tremendous outbreak—during
which he broke his cane across a table and called
his new son-in-law every name between Judas and a dancing-master—settled
an income upon Peg which the Captain could not directly
alienate. This legal precaution, though prudent, did little to protect
her happiness.

Walshawe brought her to Wauling, then a cheerful enough place,
I believe, with a well-kept garden and twenty servants. Before
three years had elapsed the garden was neglected, half the
servants dismissed, the best timber mortgaged, the plate reduced, and
every room in the house made acquainted with the Captain's
oaths.

He spent her income, frightened her out of her wits
with threats, and broke her heart.

There is no need to catalogue the smaller brutalities of
married life. A blow may be seen; but the perpetual
sneer, the door slammed in a timid face, the drunken
foot upon the stair at two in the morning, the
threat uttered and withdrawn, the money extorted by terror, and
the sudden civility practised only when money is expected—these leave
no mark fit for a coroner, yet kill as surely,
though by inches.

Peg endured all. She loved him for some years, feared
him for more, and at last, I suppose, pitied him;
which was an undeserved promotion.

She died before she had completed her thirty-second year.

## II. The Blessed Candle

Peg's wake was held in the great parlour at Wauling.
Some of her Irish relations had come over; there were
two priests, several servants from her father's country, and Molly
Doyle, an austere old woman who had attended Peg from
childhood and loved her with that fierce, jealous fidelity which
will forgive everything except an injury to its object.

The body lay in an open coffin. A crucifix was
placed upon the breast, and in the folded hands there
burned a long wax candle, blessed, as Molly afterwards declared,
upon Candlemas Day. Other candles stood about the room; and
the white face of the dead woman, framed in black
hair and linen, appeared by their uncertain light more peaceful
than it had looked for many years in life.

Captain Walshawe had been drinking since noon. Grief was not
among his infirmities, but self-pity was; and he resented the
presence of Peg's relations, the muttering of prayers, and, above
all, the tacit judgment of the servants. He entered the
room near midnight, flushed, disordered, and quarrelsome.

"What is all this mummery?" he demanded.

No one answered. One of the priests continued his prayer.

The Captain strode to the coffin and looked down upon
his wife. Whether some recollection pricked him, or whether the
stillness of her face was itself an accusation, I cannot
say. He muttered an oath and turned away. At that
moment a drop of wax from the candle in Peg's
hands fell upon his cuff.

He gave a violent start.

"D—n the thing!" he cried. "Must she burn my coat
after she is dead?"

Molly Doyle rose from her chair.

"Lave it where it is, Captain dear," said she; and
the words "Captain dear," as my uncle observed, were in
her mouth less an endearment than a warning. "The blessed
light is for her road."

"For her road!" said he. "Then she may travel in
the dark."

He snatched the candle from between the dead hands.

The women cried out. The priest nearest the coffin caught
Walshawe's arm, but the Captain flung him back and thrust
the candle toward Molly, wagging it within an inch of
her face.

"Here, you old croaking raven—take it, eat it, say your
spells over it, or light yourself to the devil with
it!"

Molly did not retreat. She was a small, meagre woman,
with heavy brows and eyes that seemed blacker than the
shadows about her; and while the wax streamed over his
fingers she raised one hand, not dramatically, I think, but
with the rigid deliberation of a person pronouncing something long
considered.

"Ye've quenched the light that was laid in the hands
of the innocent," she said. "And hear me now, James
Walshawe. Ye shall not die as other men die. Your
sowl shall go into that candle, bit by bit, and
lie cramped in the wick of it; and while one
inch is left unburned, ye shall cling to the world
ye've loved too well. But when the last spark goes,
out ye'll come—young, and strong, and proud first, and then
as God and sin have made ye. Out ye'll come,
and what ye've hidden ye'll show, and what ye've wronged
ye'll mend, and then—may the wind have ye!"

One of the priests rebuked her sharply.

Walshawe stared; then laughed so loudly that the windows seemed
to answer him.

"My soul in a farthing candle! Faith, Molly, you estimate
it handsomely."

He threw the candle upon the floor, set his heel
upon the flame, and ground it out. He would have
kicked it into the fire had not one of the
servants snatched it away. Seeing this, he seized it again,
carried it upstairs, and locked it, in the presence of
two witnesses, in a small iron box that stood in
his dressing-room.

The key he kept.

Molly Doyle left Wauling next morning. She returned to Ireland
and died there within the year. The Captain, though he
made a jest of the curse, would not destroy the
candle. A man may laugh at a loaded pistol and
yet prefer that it should not point at him. He
hid the iron box in a press, and whenever drink
made him communicative he related the affair with many profane
embellishments, always concluding that no priest, witch, or Irish serving-woman
had ever frightened James Walshawe.

Nevertheless he preserved the candle.

## III. Forty Years

Walshawe outlived Peg by more than forty years, and made
singularly bad use of every one of them.

His beauty coarsened early. The fine dark eyes became bloodshot,
the delicate mouth loose and purple, and the graceful figure
swollen about the middle while shrinking in the legs. Gout
arrived before fifty, an affection of the liver followed it,
and some disorder of the skin—treated privately by an apothecary
from Exeter—completed the outward chronicle of his habits.

He ceased to travel when credit failed him abroad, and
settled into the life of a provincial tyrant. He bullied
tenants, cheated dealers, tormented servants, and disputed bills with tradesmen
who had trusted him in their fathers' time. He would
sit in the dining-room beneath a portrait of himself as
a young officer—a handsome fellow in scarlet, with powdered hair,
a white hand upon his sword-hilt, and an expression of
insolent good-humour—and drink until the painted face seemed to mock
the living one.

He had no children. There were rumours, of course, but
rumours are neither heirs-at-law nor respectable subjects for genealogy. His
nearest relation was my uncle Watson, whom he disliked for
three reasons: Watson was sober, solvent, and likely to inherit.

During the Captain's last illness my uncle received no communication
from Wauling. The medical man attended him; an attorney named
Charke came frequently; and the housekeeper, Mrs. Dwyer, managed what
remained of the establishment. Charke had for many years conducted
the Captain's affairs, and was considered clever. This is a
word often applied in country places to a man whom
nobody trusts but everybody employs.

Certain leases affecting the most valuable farms on the Wauling
estate had been renewed at low rents during the Captain's
life, but with reversionary covenants beneficial to the estate. Without
the executed counterparts, considerable litigation was expected; and my uncle
knew that the papers had lately been in Walshawe's possession,
because the Captain had written to him, in a fit
of malignant hilarity, boasting that "Bobby Watson should find the
property a dry bone when he came to gnaw it."

Walshawe died in November, during a gale which tore six
great elms from the avenue. No will could be found.

The first letter announcing his death was followed by a
second from Charke, informing my uncle that, as heir-at-law, he
should attend without delay. The attorney added that several instruments
of importance were missing, but suggested that the deceased, whose
mind had become "reserved and contumacious," might have destroyed them.

My uncle set out for Wauling the next morning.

He arrived after dark, wet, chilled, and in no mood
to admire either the house or its inmates. The funeral
was fixed for the following day. Walshawe lay in a
closed coffin in the library, the condition of the body
having rendered exposure undesirable; and Charke, who had taken possession
of the escritoire and papers, received my uncle with a
long face and very bright eyes.

Their interview was not satisfactory.

"You are certain," said Watson, "that the counterparts were in
this house?"

"I am certain they were in the Captain's hands six
months ago."

"And since then?"

"Since then, my dear sir, who can say? Your lamented
cousin was not a man who invited confidence."

"He invited brandy," said my uncle; "and you commonly brought
it."

Charke bowed.

"If you imply—"

"I imply nothing. I ask where the leases are."

"If I knew, they should be produced."

"You searched his bureau?"

"In the presence of Mrs. Dwyer."

"His deed-box?"

"Likewise."

"The iron safe?"

"Every receptacle of a legal character."

This last phrase displeased my uncle, who suspected the attorney
of having selected it beforehand.

"We shall pray," said Watson, "that what is hidden may
be brought to light, and that the guilty person, if
there be one, may not prosper in his iniquity."

"I trust," said Charke stiffly, "that your devotions will not
be directed personally against me."

"They will be directed against Satan," replied my uncle, "and
each man may judge for himself how near he stands
to the target."

Charke withdrew shortly afterward. I believe him to have been
dishonest in a moderate, professional way, but I do not
think he had stolen the papers. Suspicion, however, is a
burr which adheres to a shabby coat more readily than
to a new one.

## IV. The Bed-Chamber

The bedroom prepared for my uncle was the best chamber
at Wauling, though "best," in that decayed house, signified only
that the rain had not yet entered through the ceiling.

It was a large, high room, panelled in dark oak.
The bed was a four-poster with faded crimson curtains; opposite
it stood a broad dressing-table containing three drawers on each
side, and above that a tall looking-glass, spotted and dim,
in which a man's reflection appeared to have suffered smallpox.
There were two presses, a washstand with a cracked basin,
six heavy chairs, a Turkey carpet worn white along the
common path, and a fireplace so deep that a child
might have stood upright behind the andirons.

Mrs. Dwyer apologized for the cold. The wind, she said,
came down the western chimney whenever it blew from the
sea, and no fire would remain steady.

My uncle asked for another room.

There was none ready; and as the servants had been
occupied with the funeral arrangements, he would not require one
to be prepared at so late an hour.

Upon the dressing-table he found a brass candlestick, an extinguisher,
a tinderbox, and a bundle of old household candles. One
of these differed from the rest. It was of yellowish
wax, thicker at the base, irregularly bent, and blackened at
the upper end, as though it had once been lighted
and rudely put out. A narrow ribbon, faded almost to
grey, clung to it under a crust of wax.

My uncle selected this candle because it was the longest.

He had no knowledge of the curse. The incident at
Peg's wake had occurred before his birth and was not
then a topic which Mrs. Dwyer thought proper for an
heir's first evening under the roof.

At half-past ten he dismissed the servant, locked his door,
and examined the room with his customary care. He looked
beneath the bed, behind the curtains, into both presses, and
even tried the panels near the chimney. This was not
cowardice. He carried papers and money; the house contained strangers;
and one of the lower windows had no fastening beyond
a wooden peg.

He then undressed, read a chapter of Job, prayed—remembering Mr.
Charke with perhaps more point than charity—and got into bed.

The candle burned upon the dressing-table, some twelve feet away.
Its flame was unusually small and blue at the root,
though the wick, which had split into two points, made
a bright red cap above. The wind cried and muttered
in the chimney; rain struck the glass by handfuls; and
now and then gravel from the roof rattled against the
window like dried peas thrown by an impatient boy.

My uncle lay awake for some time, thinking of the
funeral, the missing leases, and the unpleasant possibility of a
lawsuit. The crimson curtains swelled gently inward and collapsed again.
This movement he attributed to a draught.

Presently something gave a soft thump beneath the bed.

He raised himself upon his elbow and listened.

Nothing followed.

"A rat," he thought, and lay down again.

Within a minute came three faint knocks.

Tap—tap—tap.

They did not sound at the door. They seemed to
proceed from the dressing-table.

My uncle called, "Who is there?"

The candle flame lengthened, sank, and resumed its former size.

No one answered.

He got out of bed, took the poker, and searched
once more. The drawers were empty except for a broken
comb, two tarnished buttons, and a packet of lavender reduced
nearly to dust. There was no rat beneath the table
and no visible means by which the knocking could have
been produced.

He returned to bed, leaving the poker beside him.

As he drew the clothes over his shoulder, something touched
the sole of his foot.

It was not a draught. It was not the sheet.
It felt like the deliberate pressure of one cold finger.

My uncle kicked violently and sat upright.

The bed-curtains were still. The candle burned steadily. Beyond its
circle of light the panelling had the dull obscurity of
a wall seen underwater.

Then a whisper came from the direction of the dressing-table.

"Lower."

It was a dry, small voice, no louder than the
rubbing of paper against paper.

My uncle stared.

Again it said, "Lower."

He demanded who spoke. The storm answered with a long
ha-ha-hoo among the chimneys; a branch scraped the window; and
somewhere in the house a door banged, once, tremendously.

At this point, he afterward confessed, he considered calling the
servants. Pride prevented him. He had lectured Mrs. Dwyer at
supper upon "the gross superstitions of ignorant households," and did
not choose, within two hours, to present himself in his
nightshirt declaring that a drawer had whispered at him.

The candle had burned very low. Wax spread over the
brass socket and descended in pale folds upon the stem.
My uncle, who feared it might gutter and set fire
to the ribbon, rose, pinched off the loose end, and
placed the extinguisher over the flame.

The room became dark.

Almost instantly the extinguisher rang against the brass candlestick.

My uncle heard it distinctly: a light metallic tremor, followed
by a scrape.

He peered through the darkness. The fire had fallen low,
but a red fissure between two coals gave just enough
light to mark the square of the looking-glass and the
pallid window beyond.

The extinguisher rang again.

Then, very slowly, it rose.

## V. The Extinguisher Is Lifted

I asked my uncle repeatedly whether he might not have
overlooked some wire, spring, or other contrivance connected with the
candlestick. He replied, with some irritation, that he had handled
the article next morning, had caused it to be inspected
by a smith, and had found it an ordinary extinguisher
weighing a little less than three ounces.

He saw it rise perhaps half an inch, pause, and
rise again.

A thread of red light appeared beneath its rim.

The extinguisher tilted to one side and fell upon the
table.

The candle had re-lighted.

I do not mean that some lingering spark glowed in
the wick and gradually revived. The wick showed a clear
bead of flame at its extremity, although my uncle had
covered it for at least half a minute. The flame
was no larger than a grain of wheat, but it
burned with extraordinary brightness, casting the candlestick's shadow high upon
the wall.

Beneath the fallen extinguisher something moved.

At first he took it for a beetle. A small
black object came from under the rim, stopped, and appeared
to look about. It then rose upon two legs.

It was a man no taller than my uncle's thumb.

The figure wore a scarlet coat, white breeches, high black
boots, and a sword. Its face, though minute, was perfectly
distinct. It was the face of a handsome young officer,
dark-eyed, smiling, and insolent. One hand rested upon the sword-hilt;
the other adjusted a curl above the temple. The little
creature turned toward the looking-glass, admired itself, and laughed without
sound.

My uncle could neither move nor speak.

The figure stepped upon the base of the candlestick and
began to grow.

The process was not like the approach of an object
from a distance. It enlarged in its place, each feature
and garment preserving its proportion: first six inches high, then
a foot, then two feet. As it increased, it descended
from the dressing-table to the floor, yet continued rising until
its head stood level with the mirror.

The room altered about it. The scarlet coat glowed; the
white breeches seemed to collect the candlelight; the buckles flashed;
and the dark face, still young and smiling, became familiar
to my uncle.

He had seen it that evening in the portrait over
the dining-room mantelpiece.

It was Captain Walshawe at five-and-twenty.

The apparition bowed.

It then performed a curious and, under other circumstances, ludicrous
action. It closed one eye in a prodigiously deliberate wink,
inflated its right cheek, and laid one finger along its
nose. Having thus expressed the vulgar triumph of a man
who knows a secret, it pointed to the dressing-table.

"Lower," it whispered.

My uncle found his voice.

"What do you want?"

The figure pointed again, this time toward the bottom drawer
on the left.

Watson approached no nearer. "If there is something there, show
it."

The apparition's smile disappeared. It bent, seized the brass handle,
and pulled.

The drawer slid open.

The broken comb and tarnished buttons were visible within; nothing
more. The figure stamped its foot, thrust both hands into
the drawer, and drew it farther. It came out entirely
and fell upon the carpet with a crash.

Behind it was an empty wooden cavity.

The phantom knelt. Its arm extended—not merely in the natural
fashion, but lengthening from the shoulder like a reflection drawn
out in an ill-made mirror. The scarlet sleeve stretched to
twice its proper measure; the hand flattened and grew narrow;
and the fingers probed the rear of the opening.

A click followed.

A panel no broader than a book sprang inward.

Within the concealed recess lay a packet tied with green
tape.

My uncle saw upon the upper paper a red seal
and the words, "Wauling—Western Tenancies."

The apparition looked back at him and grinned.

"So!" said my uncle, though his teeth were beginning to
knock together. "You hid them."

The young officer placed a finger to his lips.

Then he changed.

## VI. The Visitation Culminates

The alteration began so gradually that my uncle could not
say at what instant youth ceased.

A line appeared across the smooth forehead. The cheeks lost
their clean contour. The powdered hair retreated from the temples,
while the body thickened beneath the uniform. The bright scarlet
faded to a soiled brick colour, the white waistcoat strained
across a swelling stomach, and the hand upon the floor
became heavier, redder, and more tremulous.

The phantom was Walshawe at forty.

Its expression had changed also. The gay insolence remained, but
suspicion had crept behind it; and the smile was now
the smile of a man who expects an advantage and
fears a reckoning. It gathered the packet to its breast,
looked furtively toward the door, and seemed to listen.

A distant sound passed through the house.

My uncle believed it to be the servants moving below;
but the phantom started as though it heard a bailiff's
knock.

It thrust the packet into the recess, closed the little
panel, replaced the drawer with great rapidity, and turned upon
Watson.

The face aged again.

The nose grew porous and purple. Pouches formed beneath the
eyes. The lips became thick; two of the teeth vanished;
the neck folded over a cravat that was no longer
white. The military coat melted, as it were, into a
greasy blue dressing-gown. One slipper appeared upon the right foot,
while the left was wrapped in flannel.

Walshawe stood at sixty: gouty, diseased, suspicious, and malicious.

He shook his fist at the bed.

No words issued at first, but the lips moved; and
from their movements my uncle understood that the dead Captain
was repeating some well-practised catalogue of obscenities. At length a
harsh whisper became audible.

"Mine! Mine while I breathe. Let Bobby starve. Let him
sue. Let him—"

The remainder was lost in a choking cough.

He stooped, picked up an imaginary glass, and drank. The
liquor seemed to run through him, for a dark stain
spread down the front of the dressing-gown and vanished at
the floor. He wiped his mouth, leered at the packet's
hiding-place, and snapped his fingers toward Watson.

Then his body expanded horribly.

The legs grew thin and bowed; the belly swelled; the
hands became knotted claws mottled with liver spots; and the
head sank between the shoulders. Sparse white hair hung in
damp wisps over a yellow scalp. One eyelid drooped. The
other eye, inflamed and bright, stared with the concentrated spite
of a creature whose powers have decayed before its appetite
for mischief.

This was Walshawe as he had been during his last
years.

My uncle had seen the dead man only twice in
life, but he recognized him beyond doubt. There was the
same puckered scar beside the mouth, the same flattened lobe
of the left ear, and the same ring upon the
little finger—a broad gold hoop set with a black stone.

The apparition waddled to the hidden drawer and tried to
open it. Its fingers slipped. It cursed, beat upon the
wood, and dragged with both hands. The drawer would not
stir.

"Burn them," it gasped. "Burn the lot. No heir—no rent—no—"

The candle flickered.

At that instant the phantom seemed to understand its condition.

It turned, not toward Watson, but toward the candle. The
swollen face collapsed with terror. It hobbled across the carpet,
snatched at the brass stem, and recoiled as if scorched.

The flame had sunk almost into the socket.

"No," whispered the figure.

It looked toward the chimney, then toward the door. The
wind outside fell suddenly silent. Every sound in the house
ceased—the rain, the shutters, the branches, the faint ticking of
a clock upon the landing. In that complete suspension my
uncle could hear the apparition's laboured breathing.

"No, Molly," it said.

The name was spoken not defiantly but in supplication.

The wick gave a soft crack.

The phantom's dressing-gown darkened into grave-clothes. Its cheeks sagged. The
lower jaw dropped, and the single staring eye filmed over.
A greenish shadow spread from the mouth to the temples.
The hands, which had been red and swollen, shrivelled and
blackened at the nails.

My uncle smelled damp earth.

The figure was now the dead Captain as he must
have appeared within his coffin in the library below.

A movement ran under the skin of the face.

At first it resembled the twitch of a muscle. Then
something pale emerged from one nostril and withdrew. Another white
thing worked between the lips. The cheek split soundlessly, and
from the fissure came a powder like grey flour, followed
by two small worms which fell upon the carpet and
disappeared before touching it.

The body leaned toward Watson.

He tried to pray. I believe he uttered only the
first three words, and those very indistinctly.

The candle flame shortened to a blue point.

The corpse changed with frightful rapidity. Flesh ran inward upon
the bones; the nose sank; the lips peeled back from
long yellow teeth. The garments rotted in strips. The breast
opened, showing—not ribs or organs—but a black hollow full of
red sparks, each revolving as though carried in a miniature
whirlwind.

The apparition extended both arms.

Whether it implored my uncle or meant to seize him,
he could not tell.

The last fragment of wick flared.

For one second the room was as bright as noon.

Walshawe stood before him in all his stages at once:
the handsome officer in scarlet, the sneering rake, the middle-aged
drunkard, the gouty tyrant, the bloated old sinner, the stiffened
corpse, and the collapsing skeleton. These forms did not stand
beside one another, but moved through the same space like
many portraits painted upon glass and rapidly shifted. Youth smiled
through age; the corpse grinned beneath the officer's lips; worms
twisted in the powdered curls; and the bright military eye
looked out from the empty socket of a skull.

Then the candle went out.

A cry broke from the apparition—not loud, but thin, prolonged,
and hateful, like the squeal of something caught behind a
wall.

The body fell to pieces.

It did not strike the floor. It crumbled inward, coat,
bones, face, and all, becoming a column of ash which
revolved above the carpet. The room burst suddenly into motion.
The bed-curtains flew straight out; the drawers opened and shut;
the door strained against its lock; ashes leaped from the
fireplace; and the mirror gave a loud crack from top
to bottom.

The grey column was swept toward the chimney.

As it passed the bed, a face formed within it—the
young face of Walshawe, distorted with rage. It winked once,
hideously, puffed its cheek, and was drawn backward into the
fireplace.

A red spark followed it up the flue.

From the roof came a furious roaring, as if the
storm had gathered itself into one blast; and above that
uproar my uncle heard, or believed he heard, a woman's
voice crying:

"Now the wind has ye!"

The chimney vomited soot.

The fire went out.

My uncle remembered nothing further until he found himself sitting
upon the floor in the passage, with three servants about
him and Mrs. Dwyer holding a lamp above his head.

The bedroom door was open, though the key remained turned
in the lock.

## VII. The Papers

My uncle did not relate the whole occurrence immediately. He
was carried to another room, given brandy—of which he ordinarily
disapproved but on that occasion took two glasses—and attended by
the local surgeon, who found no fever, no injury except
a bruise upon the knee, and no evidence of intoxication.

At nine o'clock, after the storm had passed, Watson returned
to the chamber in the presence of Mrs. Dwyer, Charke,
the surgeon, and a servant.

The candle was entirely consumed. A little yellow wax remained
in the socket, and beside the candlestick lay the extinguisher.

The mirror was cracked from top to bottom.

Soot covered the hearth and extended in a narrow trail
almost to the bed. The drawers, however, were closed.

My uncle directed Charke to remove the bottom drawer on
the left.

The attorney laughed nervously.

"If this is another search—"

"Remove it."

Charke pulled. The drawer stuck.

At my uncle's insistence the servant brought a screwdriver, and
after some labour the drawer was lifted from its runners.
Behind it appeared the plain wooden backing of the dressing-table.

"There," said Charke, with an air of relief. "Nothing."

"Press the lower corner."

"Which corner?"

"The right."

Charke did so. Nothing happened.

My uncle knelt and examined the wood. He then passed
his fingers along the lower edge and discovered a small
iron stud obscured by dirt. When this was pressed, a
panel sprang inward.

Within lay the packet tied with green tape.

The upper sheet bore a red seal and the words:

**WAULING—WESTERN TENANCIES.**

The packet contained the missing counterparts, together with a memorandum
in Walshawe's hand stating his intention to destroy them before
death. Why he had not done so is uncertain. Disease
may have prevented him, or malice may have prolonged the
pleasure of knowing that destruction remained possible. The instruments saved
my uncle a costly suit and increased the yearly value
of the estate by nearly four hundred pounds.

Mr. Charke, who had turned very pale, requested that Watson
should acknowledge in writing that the packet had not been
found in his custody. My uncle complied, though he added
that an innocent man ought not to wear guilt's complexion
merely because Providence had a dramatic manner.

Two curious circumstances remained.

The first concerned the candle.

When Mrs. Dwyer saw the wax in the candlestick, she
crossed herself and asked who had taken that particular candle
from the old iron box. My uncle replied that it
had been among the common candles upon the dressing-table.

She then related the history of Peg's wake and Molly
Doyle's curse.

The iron box, she said, had been found unlocked after
the Captain's death. Its key hung upon his watch-chain. She
had removed the candle, intending to show it to the
priest who would attend the funeral, but had been called
away before she could put it safely aside. A maid,
supposing it an ordinary household candle, carried it with the
others to the best chamber.

Mrs. Dwyer identified it by the faded ribbon and by
a deep impression near the base, said to have been
made by Captain Walshawe's signet when he crushed it in
his hand. A fragment of that impression remained in the
wax recovered from the socket.

The second circumstance concerned the apparition's successive forms.

My uncle described, without first seeing them, four portraits of
Walshawe preserved in different parts of the house. One represented
the Captain in uniform at five-and-twenty; another, painted about fifteen
years later, showed the first heaviness about the cheeks; a
miniature depicted him at sixty in a blue dressing-gown; and
a chalk drawing, executed by a visiting artist shortly before
his final illness, displayed the drooping eyelid, scar near the
mouth, flattened left ear, and black-stoned ring.

Watson's descriptions agreed with all four.

More strikingly, the surgeon—who had assisted in placing the body
in its coffin—declared that the last stage of the apparition,
before its decay, corresponded to the dead man's appearance in
articulo. This detail my uncle could not have known, for
the coffin had remained closed from before his arrival.

These facts were set down in a memorandum signed by
my uncle, Mrs. Dwyer, the surgeon, and two servants. Mr.
Charke declined to sign, on the ground that a legal
practitioner ought not to compromise himself by certifying preternatural occurrences.
He did, however, attest the finding of the leases.

The Captain was buried that afternoon.

There was a delay at the churchyard because a fallen
tree blocked the road, and the coffin had to be
carried the last half-mile by six men. As they passed
beneath the yews, a sudden gust tore the Captain's hat
from the coachman's head and sent it rolling among the
graves. Mrs. Dwyer regarded this as significant. My uncle did
not.

## VIII. What Followed

Watson inherited Wauling, but never made it his principal residence.
He repaired the roofs, discharged the mortgages, reduced the rents
of two poor tenants whom Walshawe had oppressed, and dismissed
Mr. Charke, though no charge respecting the missing leases could
be proved against him.

The best chamber was dismantled.

My uncle kept the dressing-table for several years, chiefly because
he considered it evidence. The secret recess was ingeniously contrived,
though not unique; a cabinetmaker in Bristol showed him two
similar mechanisms in old furniture. The mirror, with its crack,
remained above it until a housemaid refused to clean the
room after seeing, as she alleged, a little gentleman in
scarlet standing in the glass behind her.

That report my uncle treated with contempt. "Once a house
has acquired a ghost," he used to say, "every mouse
puts on a winding-sheet."

Yet he sold the dressing-table.

Wauling was partly demolished in 1862, after subsidence cracked the
western wall and rendered the old bedrooms unsafe. The wing
containing my uncle's chamber was the first to come down.
A workman afterward asserted that a quantity of grey ash
had fallen from a cavity beside the chimney, together with
a gold ring set with a black stone. I could
never verify this. No ring was produced, and the workman
had spent the morning in a public-house.

The central portion of the house remains, I believe, though
it has changed owners twice and is now occupied by
a family who know nothing of Captain Walshawe. Whether the
old place was ever reputed haunted before or after my
uncle's experience I cannot say with confidence. Local tradition is
a hospitable net: it catches every tale blown across the
parish and seldom distinguishes one dead gentleman from another.

As for the meaning of the visitation, each reader may
form his own judgment.

My uncle believed that Molly Doyle's imprecation had been literally
fulfilled; that the Captain's soul, imprisoned in the wick of
the candle he had torn from his wife's dead hands,
remained tethered to earthly existence until the last portion was
consumed; and that, upon its release, it was compelled—by mercy,
judgment, or some obscure economy of the spiritual world—to disclose
the papers it had wickedly concealed.

There are difficulties in this interpretation. Why should a curse
uttered in passion possess such authority? Why should a blessed
object become the gaol of a reprobate? Why did forty
years of the candle's preservation prolong the Captain's life, if
indeed it did? And why, when liberated, should the spirit
assist the very heir whom the living man intended to
injure?

I cannot answer.

It may be that punishment, in another state, begins not
with fire but with truth; and that the first office
required of a dishonest soul is to uncover what it
hid. It may be that Walshawe's revelation of the leases
was no voluntary act, but the final wrench of a
chain fastened long before by one faithful woman's grief. Or
it may be—as sensible persons will prefer—that my uncle dreamed
the apparition, discovered the recess by accident, and afterward connected
the exhausted candle with a family anecdote supplied by Mrs.
Dwyer.

But then there remain the knocks, the extinguisher, the broken
mirror, the concealed spring, the portraits he had not seen,
and the surgeon's testimony concerning the corpse.

My uncle's own conclusion was simpler.

"James Walshawe," he said, "was a scamp while young, a
brute while married, a rogue while old, and a nuisance
after death. But he showed me the leases, and I
will give even the devil his due."

He never afterward slept with a candle burning beside him.
If obliged to pass a night in a strange house,
he extinguished it himself, watched until the wick was cold,
and placed both candle and extinguisher in the washbasin at
the farther end of the room.

This precaution may appear excessive.

I only know that he practised it for the remaining
thirty-one years of his life.

The *Harvester* was slipping southward under easy canvas, with a
long Pacific swell lifting her stern and passing beneath her
in smooth, dark ridges. The sun had gone down behind
a bank of pearl-coloured cloud, but its light still lingered
upon the royals and turned the brass of the wheel
to fire. Forward, the men of the watch sat in
little companies about the booby hatch, smoking, mending clothes, or
listening to an old forecastle hand who coaxed a melancholy
tune from a concertina. The notes travelled aft through the
quiet evening, now clear, now lost in the creak of
blocks and the whisper along the bends.

It was the second dog-watch, that charitable hour when even
hard-worked sailors have leisure to remember that they are men.
There had been laughter a few minutes before—great, honest laughter
that made the helmsman grin at nothing while he held
the lubber's line steady—but a song about a sailor's daughter
had sobered them. Sailors are queerly fashioned in that respect.
A rough jest may be followed by a silence which
no man cares to explain.

Morgan, the first mate, stood by the weather mizzen rigging,
his pipe between his teeth and his eyes on the
fading west. He was a square, compact man of forty,
though the grey at his temples and a deep scar
running from the roots of his hair to his left
eyebrow made him appear older. The scar showed white whenever
he frowned, and that evening, as the last verse of
the song came floating aft, it shone plainly in the
dusk.

"Mr. Morgan," said the third mate, who was young enough
to ask questions which older men left alone, "was that
got at sea?"

Morgan took the pipe from his mouth.

"What?"

"That scar, sir."

He touched it with one finger, as though he had
forgotten it was there.

"Aye. At sea."

There was a pause. The third mate waited, and the
captain, who sat near the skylight with a chart upon
his knees, looked up but said nothing.

"It was when the *St. Lawrence* went down," Morgan added.

The name reached the men at the wheel, and one
of them turned his head. Every sailor on that coast
had heard of the *St. Lawrence*, posted missing with all
hands years before.

"I didn't know any one had been saved from her,"
said the third mate.

"No," Morgan answered slowly. "You wouldn't. For a long while
nobody did."

He leaned his shoulders against the rigging and looked forward,
where the concertina had ceased.

"I don't often speak of it," he said. "There ar'n't
much pleasure in telling how twenty-odd people went under, and
only two came up. But the night's quiet, and that
song put me in mind of Captain Fairley's girl. If
you care to hear it, I'll tell you just as
it happened."

No one spoke, which was answer enough.

"You may have seen the *St. Lawrence*," Morgan began. "She
was a full-rigged ship, eleven hundred tons register, built at
Sunderland, and as wholesome a craft as ever took salt
water. There was men said afterward that she must have
been rotten. That was false. Her timbers were sound, her
spars good, and she had made the passage from Liverpool
in a hundred and twelve days without starting so much
as a treenail. She was lost through bad stowage and
a drunken second mate—that, and nothing else.

"Captain Fairley was her master. A quiet man, but one
you'd obey before you knew he'd given an order. His
wife was with him, and his daughter Marion, seventeen years
old. The captain had taken them round the Horn because
he expected to lie some months upon the coast, and
Mrs. Fairley's health was delicate.

"Marion was not like the girls you generally saw visiting
a ship in harbour—laughing at the men's clothes and asking
whether the ropes were all necessary. She knew the braces
from the halyards, and could take the sun with her
father's sextant, though, of course, she could not work a
ship. At night she would stand by the break of
the poop and ask the names of the stars. I
remember showing her the Southern Cross before we lost it
astern, and she was grieved over it as though it
had been a country we should never see again.

"She embroidered a tobacco pouch for me on my birthday.
There was a ship worked upon it in white thread,
with more sails than any vessel ever carried, and my
initials beneath. I had it until the wreck. Such things
don't sound of much account—but afterward they do.

"We went into Nanaimo for coal, bound for San Francisco.
Captain Fairley had business ashore during the loading and left
Ike Summers, the second mate, in charge. Summers was a
smart seaman when sober. When he had liquor in him
he was worse than a green hand, because a green
hand knows he knows nothing.

"The coal came aboard fast. There was pressure to get
away, and the chutes were roaring from daylight until after
dark. Instead of keeping men below to trim it into
the wings and level the heaps, Summers let it pile
amidships. He was in and out of a saloon by
the wharf, and when I spoke to him his eyes
were red and his tongue thick.

"'You'll have that coal down out of the 'tween-decks before
we sail,' I said.

"'It'll settle,' says he.

"'Aye, it'll settle where it pleases when she takes a
list.'

"He laughed. 'You're always hunting trouble, Morgan.'

"Then I went below myself. You could see at once
what had been done. There were hollows beneath the beams
and ridges along the centre, with tons loose enough to
run at the first sharp heel. I sent men in
with shovels, but we were short-handed, and before we had
made a proper job of it the captain came aboard.
Summers met him with a tally sheet and reported all
cargo stowed.

"I took Captain Fairley aside.

"'Sir,' I said, 'she wants another day's trimming.'

"He looked toward the hatch. 'Summers reports her finished.'

"'Summers has been drinking.'

"The captain's face hardened. He called Summers aft and questioned
him.

"'Cargo's right enough, sir,' Summers said. 'The mate's nervous. She
may be a little by the head, but nothing to
speak of.'

"Fairley went below, and I went with him. The hatchways
had been cleared by then, and what showed appeared fair
enough, for Summers had set the men to flatten the
top. The danger lay deeper, in the spaces that had
not been filled.

"The captain did not like it. I could see that.
But our survey had been passed, the tug was ordered,
and owners do not thank a master for losing a
tide. He stood thinking a moment, then said, 'We'll proceed,
Mr. Morgan, and make all secure before we clear the
land.'

"That decision killed him, his wife, his daughter, and every
soul aboard but Simms and me. Yet I never blamed
Captain Fairley as I blamed Summers. The captain trusted an
officer's word. A ship must be run upon trust, or
she cannot be run at all.

"We towed out in the afternoon with a light air
from the north-west. Before the tug left us, the glass
had begun to fall. It was not a great fall,
mind you, nor was the sky ugly. There were streaks
of mare's-tail high up, and a thickness gathering seaward; nothing
that would have frightened a fisherman.

"Captain Fairley spoke to his wife and Marion and tried
to persuade them to return in the tug.

"'You'll be more comfortable ashore until I reach San Francisco,'
he told them.

"Mrs. Fairley smiled. 'We have rounded the Horn with you,
John. I think we can endure a little rough water.'

"Marion looked at me and said, 'Mr. Morgan does not
expect a storm, do you?'

"I answered that a sailor expects everything and promises nothing.
She laughed at that. I can hear her now.

"The tug cast off. We made sail, and by six
bells had tops'ils, courses, jib, and spanker drawing. The wind
freshened, but it was steady, and the ship walked southward
as prettily as you please. We had put the land
dim astern when a fog came down—not thick at first,
only a grey wall over the western water.

"At eight bells I gave the deck to Summers and
went below. I told him to call me if the
breeze increased or the ship developed a list. He was
sober then, or near it, and rather sulky.

"I had not been in my bunk twenty minutes when
I heard the fores'il slap once like a cannon. Then
came feet overhead and a sudden roar in the rigging.

"I was on deck before the man sent to call
me reached the companion.

"What a change! The fog had swallowed everything. The wind
came in savage puffs, boxing three points at a time,
and we were under nearly all plain sail. The *St.
Lawrence* lay over until the lee channels dipped, righted sluggishly,
and went down again.

"'Why in God's name didn't you shorten her?' I shouted.

"Summers was at the break of the poop, clinging to
a pinrail.

"'It struck all at once,' he said.

"Captain Fairley rushed up in his shirt and trousers.

"'All hands!' he cried. 'Stand by the tops'il halyards! Let
go tacks and sheets!'

"The watch below came tumbling out. Men ran forward, half
dressed and barefoot. We let go the main and fore
tacks; the courses thrashed like thunder. The ship rose a
little, and for perhaps ten seconds I thought we had
her.

"Then there came a sound from below—a low, muffled roar,
not loud compared with the sails, but every man who
heard it knew what it meant.

"The coal was moving.

"'It's gone to leeward!' Simms, the carpenter, yelled.

"The deck slanted under us. Blocks swung inward; loose gear
shot across to the rail. The ship heeled farther—twenty degrees,
thirty, more. The lee boats dipped into the sea, and
water burst over the bulwarks in green masses.

"'Cut away the mainmast!' the captain ordered.

"Axes were fetched, but before a blow could be struck
the main topmast went. There was a crack high above,
then a confusion of spars and rigging dropping through the
fog. The cro'-jack yard caught the mizzen stays. The mainmast,
strained sideways by the wreck aloft, buckled near the partners
and came down across the deck.

"It did not fall clean. The heel of it tore
through planking and beams as you might drive a bar
through the lid of a box. At once water began
pouring into the hull.

"Mrs. Fairley and Marion appeared at the companion. Mrs. Fairley
had thrown a cloak over her nightdress. Marion wore a
blue gown, and her hair was loose about her shoulders.
Her face was white, but she did not scream.

"'Go below!' the captain shouted.

"'No, John,' said Mrs. Fairley. That was all.

"The list increased until we were walking upon the bulwarks
instead of the deck. Men tried to clear the weather
lifeboat, but it hung almost over their heads, and the
falls had fouled in the wreckage.

"'The boats!' somebody cried.

"'All gone, sir!' answered another voice forward.

"I climbed along the weather rail toward the poop. Marion
was holding to the skylight, and I reached down to
her.

"'Take my hand!' I said.

"She caught it. I remember the feel of her fingers—cold,
though the night was warm. A sea came aboard and
swept waist-deep along the deck. For an instant she was
torn from her hold, but I kept her.

"Then something struck my shoulder. The spanker boom, or a
block from it, had swung across. I was thrown against
the corner of the house, and this scar was made
there. I did not lose my senses immediately. I saw
Captain Fairley with one arm around his wife and the
other reaching toward Marion. I heard men shouting in the
fog. I heard timber breaking below us.

"Marion's hand slipped out of mine.

"I got one glimpse of her blue dress below the
skylight, and then the ship rolled farther, and the sea
covered everything.

"There is not much more I can tell of the
sinking. It happened too quickly. The *St. Lawrence* did not
founder bows first or stern first; she lay down upon
her side like a wounded creature. The air came out
of her hatches with a great sob. Wreckage, coal dust,
and water were mixed together. I felt myself falling among
ropes. Something struck my forehead again, and after that there
was only a gurgling sound, farther and farther away.

"When I opened my eyes it was night and bitter
cold. I was lying with my cheek against wet planking.
A voice near me said, 'He ar'n't gone yet.'

"It was Simms.

"A section of the poop deck had broken free when
the ship sank. It was perhaps twelve feet across, cumbered
with splinters, bolts, and part of the skylight frame. Simms
had found me floating under one arm of the spanker
gaff and had dragged me upon it. How he did
it I never knew, for he was a small man
and had two fingers crushed.

"'Where's the ship?' I asked.

"'Down,' he said.

"'The boats?'

"'Down too.'

"'Any one else?'

"He listened before answering. The wind had passed, as suddenly
as it came. There was only the wash of the
swell among bits of wreck.

"'No one as I can hear.'

"We shouted until our throats were raw. Once we thought
some one answered, and Simms went into the water with
a line around him. He found an empty grating. After
that there was no sound but the sea.

"The fog cleared near midnight. A new moon showed between
the clouds, thin as a shaving from a carpenter's plane.
By its light we saw wreckage spread for half a
mile—hencoops, spars, cabin fittings, and black lumps of coal that
rose once beside us before sinking. Of the *St. Lawrence*
herself there was nothing.

"Simms bound my head with a strip torn from his
shirt. We had no water and no food. He kept
saying, 'We'll be picked up at daylight, sir. Them's busy
waters.' I knew we had been carried well off the
usual steamer track, but I let him talk.

"The night seemed a week long. Every swell washed over
our legs, and the planking worked beneath us. Toward morning
Simms began laughing at a little brass screw he had
found in the skylight frame. He said it was good
workmanship and ought not to be lost. I think he
was light-headed.

"At sunrise the sea was clear to the horizon. No
sail showed.

"About two hours later Simms touched my arm.

"'There's a blotch yonder,' he said.

"I could see nothing at first. Then, on the eastern
rim, there was a dark mark which rose and sank.
It became a sail, then three sails, then the upper
canvas of a ship standing toward us.

"We tore up a strip of board and fastened Simms's
red neckerchief to it. For an hour she seemed likely
to pass to the southward. We shouted, though she was
miles away. At last the sun flashed upon something on
her forecastle—glass or brass—and her yards began to swing.

"She was the English ship *Bramblemere*, from Tacoma for Hamburg.
Her boat took us off shortly before noon.

"'How many?' the officer asked as they lifted me in.

"'Two,' I said.

"'No—how many saved?'

"'Two. That's all.'

"They searched until evening. They found a lifebuoy, a broken
boat, and one of Marion's books, swollen with water. No
bodies were recovered.

"The *Bramblemere* could not put back. She had a fair
wind, a sick master, and orders to proceed. So Simms
and I went on in her—round the Horn again, through
calms, gales, and head winds—a hundred and ninety days from
the morning we were picked up until we reached Hamburg.

"You may think it strange that the world heard nothing.
The *Bramblemere* spoke no homeward-bound ship close enough to exchange
news, and we sighted no port. When we came into
Hamburg, Simms and I were supposed dead men. My wages
had been settled, my chest sold, and my name entered
with the rest.

"The first English paper I saw was in a shipping
office near the docks. There, under Casualties and Vessels Missing,
I read the last official word upon the ship we
had seen go down."

Morgan ceased. His pipe had gone out in his hand.
For a while nobody aboard the *Harvester* moved. Forward, the
concertina player began another tune, but softly now, as though
he too had heard.

The captain folded his chart.

"And Summers?" asked the third mate at last.

Morgan looked over the darkening sea.

"He went with the others," he said. "There was no
court to try him. Perhaps that was judgment enough."

In the files of the *Marine Register* for that year
appeared the following notice:

> **ST. LAWRENCE**—British ship, 1,107 tons register, Fairley, master; sailed
from Nanaimo with coal for San Francisco, and has not
since been reported. Portions of wreckage believed to belong to
the vessel have been observed off the coast. She is
therefore presumed to have foundered at sea, with her master,
his wife and daughter, officers, and crew. No survivors.

## The Cloth of the Dead Weaver

In the Icelandic settlement beyond the Mouse River, where the
North Dakota plain rolled white and unbroken to the edge
of heaven, there lived in former days an old woman
named Urda Bjarnason.

How old she was no man could say. Some declared
she had been a grown maiden when the great fire-mountain
burst through the snow in Iceland; others that she had
crossed the sea with the first company of settlers and
had sat upright through every storm, speaking verses from the
sagas while strong men lay a-sick in their bunks. Her
back was bent like a sickle, and her hands were
dark and small, yet her eyes remained clear and steelblue.
They were such eyes as seem to behold not only
the face before them, but the road behind and the
road yet to come.

Urda lived with her son Bjarni, who kept cattle and
had six children, all loud at table and silent when
their grandmother spoke. She sat near the cookstove through the
long winters, carding wool or mending mittens, and knew many
things which no newspaper had printed.

She knew when a blizzard would rise out of a
yellow sunset. She knew when a cow would calve before
morning. Once she bade a young man postpone his journey,
though the sky was clear; and before noon the wind
came down black with snow, and the young man thereafter
touched his cap whenever he passed her.

The older settlers did not wonder at this. They had
known such women in the old country, among the black
lava fields and the green farms under the mountains. But
the young, who had learned English and wore town shoes,
sometimes smiled.

Ingeborg Thorwald, who had attended the normal school for one
winter, said, "Grandmother Urda sees what she expects to see."

Urda looked at her over the rim of her coffee
cup.

"And thou, child," she answered, "having eyes, seest only what
the teacher has written on the board."

The men laughed, and Ingeborg tossed her yellow head; but
she came again the next Saturday evening, as Urda knew
she would.

That night the cold was bitter. Frost stood white upon
the nails in the walls, and the windowpanes were covered
with fern forests, silver rivers, and flowers that no summer
garden could grow. Outside, the snow shone pink beneath the
rising moon. The horses stamped in the stable, and from
far off came a sound like a gunshot when a
cottonwood split with the frost.

Within, the men sat around the stove with their coffee.
The women knitted. The children lay on the rag rug
or leaned against their mothers' knees. They had sung in
parts—old hymns carried over the northern sea—and now they asked
Urda for a tale.

"Tell the one of the seal woman," said a boy.

"Nay, the troll beneath Hecla," said another.

Urda's fingers rested upon the gray wool in her lap.

"I shall tell," she said, "of the cloth woven by
a dead woman. For ye complain of cold while wearing
two shirts, and there are quilts enough in this house
to bury a horse. Listen, then, and be thankful."

The room became still.

"This happened in Iceland," Urda began, "when my mother's mother
was young, and the mist lay so thick upon the
coast that boats entered the wrong fjords and men heard
bells ringing under the sea."

There lived then in a small turf house a fisherman
named Eirik, with his wife Sigrid and their two children,
Jon and Loa. Jon was nine years old and grave
beyond his age. Loa was seven, blueeyed and quick, and
she sang to herself while carrying water, while gathering driftwood,
and even while scraping scales from fish.

Their mother Sigrid was a weaver. She could set a
warp straighter than any woman in the district, and beneath
her hands common wool became warm cloth of good colour.
For Jon she had woven a brown coat with brass
buttons. For Loa she made a blue dress, deep as
the sea where it runs beneath shadow. In winter she
tucked wool about their feet and warmed their shirts by
the fire before waking them.

It stands to reason the children loved her.

Their father loved her also, though he was not a
man of many words. When he returned from the fishing,
wet and salt-stiff, he would stand in the doorway watching
Sigrid at the loom. The shuttle flew from hand to
hand, the beam creaked, and Loa sang under the table.

There are houses poor in coin that nevertheless possess abundance.
Such was their house.

But one day their mother died.

No warning came. In the morning she baked bread and
laughed because Loa had put two different stockings upon her
feet. At noon she placed her hand against her side.
Before midnight she lay quiet, and the wind combed the
roof grass while Eirik sat beside her, holding a hand
already cold.

After that, the loom stood silent.

For a time the father did what he could. He
burned porridge, mended clothes with sail thread, and tied Loa's
hair so poorly that one braid pointed north and the
other west. But he must fish, and children must be
fed. Before the year ended, he brought another wife into
the house.

Her name was Gudrun. She came from inland, bringing two
iron-bound chests, three baskets, a featherbed, and a purse of
coins which she counted every seventh night. She was broad
of shoulder and strong of arm, and none could say
she was lazy. She scrubbed the tables, salted the fish,
and kept the lamp glass bright.

Yet her heart was narrow.

She saw waste in every spoonful and idleness in every
breath. If Jon sat to rest after hauling peat, she
found another basket for him. If Loa sang while washing
wool, Gudrun said singing slowed the hands.

"Work first," she would say.

But work was never finished in that house.

Always they must work. Before dawn Jon carried water over
a path glazed with ice. Loa cleaned fish until the
scales clung silver to her hair. They gathered dung, turned
the drying racks, picked stones from the field, and searched
the shore for driftwood. Their fingers cracked and bled. Their
feet grew purple in wooden shoes too small for them.

The clothes Sigrid had woven grew thin. Jon's elbows broke
through his coat, and the hem of Loa's blue dress
climbed above her knees. Gudrun had cloth in one chest
and money in the other, but she would give them
none.

"Children grow," she said. "Why put good cloth upon backs
that lengthen like summer weeds?"

At night Jon and Loa slept beneath one ragged cover
in the little cupboard bed. Snow sifted through a crack
in the wall and melted upon their hair. Sometimes Jon
spread his coat over Loa. Sometimes Loa breathed upon his
hands to warm them. Neither complained to their father, for
Gudrun stood near whenever he returned, and Eirik, weary from
the sea, saw little except the food before him and
the dark bed beyond it.

So one winter evening, when the north wind had sharpened
every stone and the sea lay black between white shores,
Loa came in carrying peat. Her dress was wet to
the waist. One sleeve hung torn, and there was blood
upon her wrist.

Gudrun sat near the lamp counting coins.

"May I have a piece of wool for my sleeve?"
asked Loa.

"No."

"It lets in the cold."

"Then work faster and thou wilt be warm."

Loa stood without moving. She looked at the stacked coins
and then at the empty loom.

"Our mother would have woven cloth for us," she said.

Gudrun's hand closed over the coins.

"Thy mother is earth," she answered. "Let her weave there
if she can."

Jon heard and went pale. Loa began to weep, not
loudly, but with her face turned toward the wall.

That night the house slept.

The fisherman slept from weariness. The children slept with their
knees drawn close. The lamp went out. Only Gudrun remained
wakeful, for the wind made a loose board knock beneath
the eaves.

Knock, knock, knock.

She rose to fasten it, but as she put her
feet upon the floor, another sound came through the darkness.

Creak went the treadle.

Thump went the beam.

Swiftly whispered the shuttle.

Gudrun sat still.

The sound came from the loom.

At first she told herself a rat moved among the
cords. Then the moon broke through the clouds, and a
pale bar of light passed across the floor. There, seated
at the loom, was Sigrid.

She wore the linen in which she had been buried.
Her hair hung damp about her shoulders, and drops of
darkness fell from it, though the night was dry. Her
face was white and quiet. Only her hands moved, casting
the shuttle to and fro with great speed.

Creak went the treadle.

Thump went the beam.

Swiftly whispered the shuttle.

Upon the loom grew a cloth such as Gudrun had
never seen. Its warp was blue—now the blue of deep
ice, now the blue of twilight snow—and through it ran
a shining white woof like foam upon northern waves. It
was beautiful, and it was hateful. It gleamed though no
lamp burned.

Gudrun tried to call Eirik. Her tongue lay like stone
in her mouth. She tried to rise. Her limbs would
not obey.

Sigrid wove on.

When the cloth reached from beam to floor, the dead
woman cut it with a small silver knife. She stood.
She came across the room without sound, swaying as the
Northern Lights sway when no wind is felt below.

Then she cast the cloth about Gudrun.

It was cold as grave earth. It tightened over her
breast and pinned her arms against her sides. Gudrun smelled
wool, sea-salt, and the dried flowers that had been placed
in Sigrid's coffin.

The dead mother bent near.

"For the children," she said.

No more than that.

The moon went behind a cloud. The cloth vanished. The
loom was empty.

Gudrun fell upon the floor, and there Eirik found her
at daybreak, stiff with cold and unable for some time
to speak.

When speech returned, she said she had dreamed.

Men often call a thing a dream when they fear
to name it.

All that day Gudrun was quiet. She gave the children
porridge, but no butter. She looked at Loa's torn sleeve,
but did not mend it. At evening she took out
her purse, counted the coins twice, and put them back.

"Perhaps," thought she, "the dead appear but once."

That was a foolish thought.

The second night came colder than the first. Stars glittered
above the house like splinters of ice. Eirik slept. The
children slept. Gudrun lay with her eyes open, watching the
loom.

Near midnight the treadle moved.

Creak.

The beam answered.

Thump.

The shuttle passed from an unseen hand to another unseen
hand, and Sigrid was there again.

As before, she wore her burial linen. As before, her
dark hair dripped upon the floor. As before, she wove
the blue warp and the shining white woof, and the
cloth flowed from the loom like moonlight made solid.

Gudrun shut her eyes.

Still she heard the treadle.

She pulled the blanket over her face.

Still she heard the beam.

She pressed her hands against her ears.

Still the shuttle whispered through her skull.

When at last she looked, Sigrid stood beside the bed
holding the finished cloth. Her dead eyes were full of
neither rage nor mercy. That was more terrible than anger.

"For the children," she said.

The cloth fell.

It wound about Gudrun's feet, her waist, her throat. The
cold entered her bones. She saw black lava, open graves,
and the silver tips of waves beneath which drowned men
drifted with staring eyes. She would have promised anything, but
her tongue would not stir.

Sigrid leaned over her.

"Warmth," said the dead woman. "Work in measure. Bread enough.
Songs after dark."

Then all was gone.

In the early morning Gudrun rose before the others. She
opened the iron-bound chest, took out her purse, and did
not count the coins. She wrapped a shawl around herself
and walked three miles through blowing snow to the merchant's
house.

There she bought strong brown cloth for Jon, blue wool
for Loa, sheepskin for two pairs of shoes, and three
thick blankets. She paid also for needles, thread, and a
little red ribbon.

The merchant wondered at her haste.

"Is someone to be married?" he asked.

"Nay," said Gudrun. "Someone is not to be buried."

For three days she cut and stitched. Her fingers, though
strong, were clumsy, for fear had entered them. Yet she
worked until Jon had a coat lined with wool and
Loa a dress warm enough for the church road. She
made the shoes large, so their toes might grow. Upon
Loa's collar she sewed the red ribbon.

Afterward she gave them bread enough. She did not cease
to make them work, for all must work where winter
is long and food comes from sea and stone. But
she measured the baskets to their strength. She allowed Jon
to warm his hands. She allowed Loa to sing.

And in the evenings, when tasks were done, the children
sat beside the stove and told stories. Sometimes Eirik spoke
of storms and strange fish. Sometimes Jon read from the
sagas. Loa sang in a voice clear as water under
ice.

Gudrun sat near the loom and listened.

Never again did she say the dead were only earth.
Never again did she let a garment tear unmended or
a blanket grow too thin. If Loa's song ceased suddenly,
Gudrun would lift her head and glance toward the darkened
corner.

For she knew that love may lie beneath the ground,
but it does not always sleep.

Urda Bjarnason ended there.

For a little while no one in the North Dakota
farmhouse spoke. The stove settled with a red sigh. Beyond
the frosted glass the plain shone blue and white beneath
the moon, like a great cloth spread from earth to
heaven.

Then Ingeborg, who had studied at the normal school, asked,
"Did the dead woman truly weave, Grandmother Urda?"

Urda lifted the gray wool from her lap. Her old
fingers drew out the fibers and twisted them neatly together.

"The children were cold," she said. "Afterward they were warm.
What greater truth dost thou require?"

The men nodded, for they understood.

And before the company departed, every mother examined the mittens
of her children, every father drew the quilts higher over
the little beds, and Ingeborg herself laid another stick upon
the fire.

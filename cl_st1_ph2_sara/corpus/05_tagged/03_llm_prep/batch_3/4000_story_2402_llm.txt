# THE PROMISE OF PAT CONNELL

> *"Dreams are but interludes which Fancy makes;
> Yet sometimes Conscience speaks while Reason sleeps."*

There are few subjects upon which mankind have reasoned more
confidently, and concluded less satisfactorily, than the nature and uses
of dreams. The philosopher, resolving every thing into sensation, memory,
and the accidental motions of an overburthened brain, smiles at
the credulity which discovers admonition in a vision; the vulgar,
on the other hand, persuaded that futurity is continually betraying
itself through figures and shadows, convert every nocturnal terror into
a prophecy, and every trivial coincidence into its accomplishment. Between
these extremes the conscientious man may hesitate. He will not
surrender his judgment to superstition; neither will he rashly pronounce
that the Almighty, who has warned by dreams in former
ages, has imposed upon Himself an eternal silence in our
own.

Whether the occurrence which I am about to record ought
to be attributed to a natural impression, a disordered imagination,
a providential warning, or an agency more terrible than either,
I shall not presume to determine. I relate it because
I witnessed much of it; because those portions which I
did not witness were communicated to me by persons whose
veracity I had no cause to question; and because, after
the lapse of more than twenty years, every material circumstance
remains fixed in my recollection with a distinctness which ordinary
events seldom retain.

In the month of November, 18—, I was curate of
St. M——'s, in one of the poorest and most crowded
districts of Dublin. My chamber adjoined the vestry-room, and was
so situated that those who sought my assistance at unseasonable
hours might summon me without disturbing the clergyman under whose
direction I served. The arrangement afforded me abundant opportunities of
observing misery in forms which the prosperous can scarcely imagine.
I had seen hunger made patient by long familiarity, disease
endured without medicine, age without repose, childhood without innocence, and
death itself deprived of that decent quiet which even poverty
commonly endeavours to provide for it.

One morning, or rather at that dead interval when the
night has begun to decline, though no streak of day
is yet perceptible, I was awakened by a low but
continued knocking at the outer door. The wind had blown
violently during the evening, and a cold rain beat at
intervals against my casement; I therefore at first imagined that
some loosened shutter occasioned the sound. It ceased, returned, and
was followed by a child's voice, so timid that I
could scarcely distinguish the words.

"Plase, your reverence—plase, sir—get up, for the love of God!"

I descended immediately, and, having opened the door, found a
little girl, apparently not more than eleven years of age,
standing on the step. She had neither bonnet nor shoes;
a fragment of an old shawl was drawn over her
head, but afforded so little protection that her dark hair
clung wet upon her cheeks. Her lips were colourless with
cold, yet she neither complained nor wept.

"Who are you, my child?" said I.

"I'm Biddy Connell, sir."

"And what has brought you here at this hour?"

"It's my father. He's dyin', they say. Mother bid me
run for the priest; and I run every step, though
the watchman was afther frightenin' me at the corner. Oh,
plase, your reverence, don't delay!"

The name of Connell was well known to me. Patrick
Connell had once been an industrious carpenter, and, as I
had heard, a skilful one; but drunkenness, gradual in its
conquest and absolute in its dominion, had reduced him from
competence to want, from want to degradation, and from degradation
almost to brutality. His wife supported herself and three children
by washing; the eldest boy had been taken into employment
by an old tradesman who pitied him; and this little
creature, whose naked feet had traversed half a mile of
wet and frozen pavement, performed the offices which should have
belonged to a stronger messenger.

There is something peculiarly affecting in the premature fortitude of
a poor child. The offspring of affluence may be courageous
from instruction or from pride; but the child of misery
learns endurance before it understands hope. It suffers silently, not
because pain is light, but because complaint has never relieved
it. I wrapped the girl in my cloak while I
made the slight preparation necessary for my departure; and within
a few minutes we were proceeding through the streets.

The rain had ceased, but the air was raw, and
a low vapour hung between the houses. Our steps sounded
strangely in the deserted lanes. Here and there a lamp,
exhausted and yellow, revealed the dripping wall beneath it, while
the greater portion of our way lay in darkness. The
child went rapidly, sometimes running before me, then returning, as
though fearful that I might abandon her. At length she
stopped at the entrance of a narrow passage, from which
arose that mingled odour of damp straw, smoke, refuse, and
confined humanity which no person acquainted with the habitations of
the very poor can mistake.

"He's at the top, sir," she whispered.

The house had probably been respectable in the preceding century;
but neglect, subdivision, and the destructive tenancy of successive paupers
had reduced it to a condition scarcely compatible with safety.
The lower windows were stuffed with rags; the plaster had
fallen in broad patches; and the staircase, originally narrow, was
now so broken that we ascended partly by memory and
partly by touch. At the turn before the uppermost flight
there was a landing composed of loose and decayed boards.
One plank bent so alarmingly beneath me that I drew
back.

"Keep next the wall, your reverence," said Biddy. "There's a
bad bit there. Father said he'd mend it many a
time."

"And why has he not done so?"

The child made no answer. She had perhaps learned that
some truths, though understood by all, ought not to be
spoken by the children of the guilty.

We reached a garret, at the door of which several
persons were collected. On my approach they made way with
that reverence which, among the Irish poor, often survives every
other habit of order. Within, a single candle burned upon
the ledge of a broken cupboard. Its wavering light fell
upon a scene of extreme wretchedness. A heap of shavings
and straw served as a bed; beside it knelt Mrs.
Connell, a pale, worn woman, whose face bore those sharp
lines which sorrow cuts more rapidly than years. Two neighbours
stood near her; a third held a basin; and on
the bed lay Connell himself.

He was then about forty-five years of age, though dissipation
had made him look considerably older. His frame was large
and powerful; his hands, even in their present rigidity, showed
the strength acquired by labour; but his face was swollen
and of a dusky blue, his lips nearly black, and
his teeth fixed. The eyes were partly open, and the
white visible beneath the upper lids gave to his countenance
an expression so revolting that I involuntarily stopped.

A physician, whose attendance had been procured through some charitable
institution, was fastening his cloak at the foot of the
bed.

"You are too late, sir," he said quietly. "The man
is dead."

Mrs. Connell uttered a cry and caught my hand.

"No, no, doctor! Don't say it. He was speakin' not
an hour ago. Pat! Pat, jewel! Look up—the priest is
here!"

The physician shook his head. He informed me that Connell
had returned home intoxicated shortly before midnight, had eaten greedily,
and afterward fallen into a stupor. About two o'clock he
had been seized with violent convulsions, followed by complete insensibility.
Supposing the attack apoplectic, the physician had opened a vein
in the arm; but scarcely any blood appeared, and, after
the cessation of breathing and pulse, he had pronounced life
extinct.

"How long since?" I asked.

"Nearly ten minutes. There is no action of the heart;
the extremities are becoming cold. Nothing can be done."

He departed, promising, however, to send the proper certificate in
the course of the day.

It is not my intention to describe minutely the grief
which followed. Poverty does not blunt the affections, though it
often renders their expression violent. Mrs. Connell threw herself across
the body, calling upon her husband by every name which
tenderness, reproach, and despair could supply. One of the children
screamed; the youngest, awakened from sleep in a corner, began
to cry without knowing why; and the neighbours, after their
custom, mingled consolation with lamentation until the confusion became painful.

I succeeded at length in restoring some degree of quiet.
The wife was raised from the bed; the children knelt;
and I began those prayers which the Church has provided
for the afflicted. I had not proceeded far when one
of the women interrupted me.

"Merciful Heaven!" she exclaimed. "Look at him!"

All eyes were turned toward the bed.

Connell's right hand, which had lain clenched upon his breast,
had moved. Of this I am certain. It moved slowly,
opened, and grasped the coarse sheet. At the same instant
a gurgling sound issued from his throat.

Mrs. Connell rushed forward, but I held her back, believing
the motion to be one of those muscular contractions which
occasionally occur after death.

Again the hand moved. The body shuddered from head to
foot; the chest heaved; and from beneath the cloth which
had been wrapped about his arm a dark stream suddenly
flowed upon the bedding. The bandage placed over the incision
had slipped, and blood, at first obstructed, now escaped freely
from the vein.

What followed passed with such rapidity that I can communicate
only the principal impressions. Connell drew a tremendous breath, like
a man emerging after long submersion; his jaws separated; his
eyes opened to their full extent; and, as the women
had meanwhile thrown a white cloth over his head and
another beneath his chin, the whole arrangement of his livid
features, bloody arm, and struggling limbs presented an appearance so
ghastly that two persons fled from the room, declaring afterward
that they had seen the dead rise in the possession
of the Evil One.

"Hold him!" I cried. "He lives. Send for the physician—quickly!"

The supposed corpse sat upright. He tore the cloth from
his face, stared about him without recognition, and uttered three
times, with increasing force, the word—

"Fire!"

He then fell back, breathing heavily.

The physician, fortunately, had not proceeded far, and was brought
again to the garret. His surprise was manifest, though professional
pride induced him to conceal it under a grave composure.
He checked the bleeding, administered a stimulant, and remained until
the pulse became perceptible.

"The lancet has done its work somewhat late," he observed.
"The man was in a state resembling catalepsy, complicated, perhaps,
with apoplectic congestion. The obstruction in the vein has yielded,
and the circulation is restored."

"But you said he was dead," whispered one of the
neighbours.

"My good woman," replied he, with some severity, "if physicians
were infallible, they would be less useful than they are.
Keep him quiet, admit air, and give him nothing stronger
than water."

He then drew me aside.

"His recovery is extraordinary, but not miraculous. Cases of suspended
animation have occurred. I should not answer for his intellect,
however; the brain has suffered, and delirium may follow."

This explanation, though reasonable, did not altogether satisfy those present;
nor, I confess, did it remove the singular horror with
which I had seen the dead features resume expression. Still,
as there was no immediate occasion for my ministry, and
as rest was essential, I withdrew, promising to return later
in the day.

For nearly a week Connell remained feeble and incoherent. He
recognised his wife, but spoke little; and whenever he slept,
he started up, crying that the fire was rolling over
him. On the eighth day he sent Biddy to request
that I would visit him alone.

I found the room cleaner than before, though scarcely less
miserable. Connell sat near the small window, wrapped in a
blanket. His face had lost its swollen darkness, but was
so emaciated that the bones seemed unnaturally prominent. He rose
at my entrance, attempted to speak, and then, covering his
face, began to tremble.

"Sit down, Connell," said I. "You have been near death,
and ought to be thankful for your preservation."

"Near death!" he repeated. "Near it, is it? I was
dead, your reverence—dead, and down among them!"

"Among whom?"

"The damned."

He pronounced the word in so low a tone that
it seemed rather forced from him than spoken; then turned
his head toward the stair, as if apprehensive that some
listener might be outside.

I told him that the impressions of sickness are often
vivid, and that a man whose brain has been oppressed
by strong drink, fever, or interrupted circulation may behold images
which possess all the force of reality.

"Dreams, sir?" said he. "You think it was a dream?"

"Very probably."

"Then hear it. Hear it all, and tell me afther
if a dream could make a man feel the scorch
this minute upon his face."

He put both hands to his forehead, remained silent for
some time, and then began the following relation. I shall
preserve, as nearly as memory permits, his own expressions; for
the contrast between the homeliness of his language and the
monstrous grandeur of what he described produced an effect which
any refinement of mine would only diminish.

"When I came home that night," said he, "I was
bad with the drink. I knew it, and the wife
knew it; but I wasn't quarrelsome, praise be to God—not
that night. I lay down, and there was a weight
here"—he pressed his breast—"as if the big beam from Tim
Carty's yard was atop of me. I heerd the childer
talkin', and the wife cryin', and then the doctor's voice;
but they all went far off, far off, till they
were no louder than flies at a windy pane.

"Afther that I felt asy—too asy. I had no pain,
no breath, no body at all, as a man might
say; and yet I was sittin' on the side of
the bed, lookin' at myself stretched there. I saw the
doctor take my arm, and I saw Biddy come in
with you; but none of ye looked at me where
I was sittin'. Ye looked only at the thing on
the bed.

"I called out. 'Here I am!' says I. No one
minded. Then I went to the door. I didn't walk,
exactly, but was carried asy enough, and I came to
the stair. The house was dark, only there was a
red light comin' up through the bad plank on the
landin'.

"'That's queer,' says I; and I stooped to look.

"The minute I stooped, the board opened—not broke, mind you,
but opened like a mouth—and down I went. I tried
to catch the rail. My hand passed through it. I
tried to cry to the wife. The darkness choked me.
Down, down, down I went, faster than a stone in
a well, and the red light grew bigger underneath.

"At last I struck upon somethin' hard. It wasn't earth,
and it wasn't stone, but black and smooth like iron.
There was a roof miles over me, made of rock;
and under it was a place so big that Dublin,
and Ireland itself, might be put in a corner of
it and never noticed.

"There was a table in the middle—or what looked like
a table—stretchin' away till the end was lost. Thousands and
thousands sat round it; millions, maybe; men and women, kings
with crowns burnt black on them, soldiers, judges, beggars, fine
ladies, little starved crathurs, and people of every country under
heaven. None ate; none drank; none spoke above a whisper.
They only sat and waited.

"Over their heads rolled great balls of fire, red as
blood. They came slow at first, with a sound like
wind in a chimney; then they leaped and rushed, and
all the place shook. Whenever one passed, every face at
the table turned red, and I could see the mouths
open and the eyes strainin'; but the fire never gave
warmth. It scorched and froze together.

"I wanted to run, but there was no road back.
A little man beside me, no bigger than Biddy, made
room.

"'Sit down, Pat Connell,' says he.

"'How do you know me?' says I.

"He laughed, but there was no merriment in it. 'We
know all that are expected,' says he.

"I sat, because my knees failed. On the other side
was a woman with her hair over her face. She
kept sayin', 'One minute more—only one minute more'; and every
time the fire rolled past, a voice from under the
table answered, 'The minute is gone.'

"Then there came a noise from the far end. It
was like a thousand furnaces opened together. Every one bent
down, and I bent with them, though I didn't know
why. Somethin' was comin'.

"Your reverence, I can't tell what it was. Don't ask
me its shape, for it had no shape that would
stay the same. At one look it seemed a giant
taller than the roof; at another, it was a shadow
sittin' in a chair; then a face without a body,
and then a body with more faces than there are
days in the year. But the eyes—God preserve us!—the eyes
were always the same. They were dark, not fiery; and
every sin I ever done was alive in them.

"It sat at the head of the table, though that
was miles away; and still it was close enough to
touch me.

"'Patrick Connell,' says it.

"I couldn't answer.

"'Drunkard,' says it.

"Then all at the table whispered, 'Drunkard, drunkard, drunkard'; and
the roof sent it back—'Drunkard!'—till I thought the word would
split my skull.

"'You're not wanted yet,' says the great one. 'There has
been a mistake.'

"At that I found courage. 'Then let me go home,'
says I.

"'Will you come again?'

"'Never!' says I. 'Never, by the holy—'"

Connell stopped abruptly and wiped his brow.

"Go on," said I.

"I named the Blessed Name, sir. The fire stopped. Every
soul lifted its head, and the thing at the table
changed. It grew smaller, and darker, and angrier. The whole
cavern shook like a sheet in the wind.

"'You may go,' it says, 'but you must return.'

"'I'll not!' says I.

"'Promise, and go.'

"Then I thought of the wife and the childer, and
of Biddy runnin' barefoot, and of little Mick callin' me
father. I'd have promised any thing. 'I promise,' says I.
'Only let me out of this place.'

"It stretched an arm across the table. The arm came
on and on, passin' all the people, till the hand
was over me. I saw a mark in the palm,
black and deep like a hole burnt in timber.

"'Go, then,' says it, 'and when you stand again upon
the open board, remember.'

"The hand struck me. I was flyin' upward; the fires
went round me; I heerd voices roarin', 'He'll come back!
He'll come back!' Then there was a cut in my
arm, a rush through my heart, and I woke with
the cloths about my face."

He ceased. During the latter part of his narration his
voice had risen almost to a scream, and the perspiration
stood in large drops upon his forehead. I confess that
I was affected; not convinced, but affected beyond what I
considered reasonable. The minuteness of his recollection, the consistency of
its imagery, and, above all, the unstudied terror with which
he spoke, distinguished the account from the ordinary inventions of
one who desires to astonish.

Nevertheless, it was my duty to resist an interpretation which
might plunge him into despair.

"Connell," said I, "you suffered a seizure produced, in all
probability, by intemperance. While your faculties were suspended, fragments of
what passed around you—your wife's cries, the physician's operation, the
prayers, perhaps even the shaking of the house—were combined by
imagination into this fearful dream. You had often been warned
of the consequences of your life; conscience supplied the place
of punishment; and the dangerous landing, which you knew so
well, became naturally the entrance to your cavern."

"But the promise!" he cried. "I promised to go back."

"No promise made in delirium can bind the soul. Still
less can a promise extorted by terror give any right
to an evil being. Your life belongs to God, not
to the spectre of a disordered brain."

He stared at me eagerly.

"Then I'm not damned?"

"No living man has authority to call himself damned. Despair
is itself a temptation. If the vision has any spiritual
meaning, regard it not as a sentence but as a
warning. You have been restored when all believed you dead.
Use the life restored to you. Abandon drunkenness; repair, so
far as you can, the injuries you have committed; pray;
work; cherish your family. A soul truly turned toward God
need not fear all the powers of darkness."

He seized my hands and pressed them with painful force.

"I'll do it. Before Him that spared me, I'll never
taste the drink again. I'll work till the hands drop
from me. I'll pay every penny. And the board—I'll mend
the board this day."

"You are not yet strong enough for labour."

"Then to-morrow. There'll be no open place for it to
take me through."

This last observation showed that my reasoning had not wholly
removed his superstition; but as fear seemed, for the present,
allied with repentance, I did not press him further.

Connell's reformation, during the months which followed, was so complete
that even those who had laughed most loudly at his
vision were compelled to acknowledge its effects. He returned to
his trade under a master whom he had formerly offended;
he laboured steadily, received his wages on Saturday, and carried
them directly home. Debts, some so old that the creditors
had ceased to expect payment, were discharged by small instalments.
His children obtained shoes; the room was furnished with a
table, two chairs, and a proper bed; and Mrs. Connell,
whose appearance had formerly expressed the passive misery of one
who suffers without hope, recovered something of health and even
cheerfulness.

Nor was his amendment merely external. He attended divine service;
he avoided quarrels; he acknowledged injuries without seeking to excuse
them; and when mocked by his former associates, he endured
their ridicule with a humility which I should not previously
have thought possible in him.

These associates were not few. The drunkard, however wretched, seldom
lacks companions while he can provide a glass, a song,
or an example more degraded than themselves. They waited for
Connell at the corner; they called after him from public-house
doors; they assured him that a single measure could harm
no man, that his abstinence was pride, that his dream
was the doctor's lancet working upon an empty stomach. One
fellow, known as Red Larry, followed him for nearly a
street, imitating the groans of the damned and rolling an
imaginary globe of fire between his hands. Connell turned pale
but did not enter the house.

"Laugh away, Larry," he said. "Maybe the laugh'll be at
the other side of your mouth some night."

I reproved him afterward for the vindictive character of the
answer.

"I know, your reverence," he replied. "But when they talk
that way, the place comes back. I see the faces.
I hear the word. And sometimes—God forgive me—I wish another
had seen it, just to know I'm not mad."

He had not forgotten the landing. As soon as his
strength permitted, he removed the rotten plank and replaced it
with stout timber. Unsatisfied with this, he added a second
support beneath, drove in several enormous nails, and fixed a
bar along the wall. I happened to visit while he
was completing the work.

"There now," he said, striking the board with his heel;
"it would bear an elephant."

"It is well to prevent an accident," I observed, "but
you must not imagine that carpentry can secure a man
against spiritual danger."

"No, sir. But if ever I do go back—and I
won't, with God's help—it shan't be through that hole."

As winter passed into spring, his terrors appeared gradually to
diminish. He still dreamed at intervals, but less vividly; his
bodily health improved; and constant labour, which is often a
better physician to the imagination than argument, gave regularity to
his thoughts. I began to hope that the dreadful vision,
whether natural or preternatural, had accomplished a merciful purpose.

It may be asked why, if I believed the dream
capable of such salutary influence, I persisted in explaining it
by natural causes. The answer is simple. Religion cannot be
strengthened by falsehood, nor repentance safely founded upon terror alone.
Had Connell refrained from vice merely because he feared a
cavern, a table, and a monstrous ruler, his amendment would
have depended upon the continued vividness of those images; and
images fade. I wished him to hate drunkenness because it
degraded the faculties which God had given him, injured the
wife whom he had sworn to protect, starved his children,
and brought his body to the semblance of death. Fear
may awaken; it cannot always sustain. The flame which startles
the sleeper is not the lamp by which he must
afterward walk.

Yet there are appetites upon which reason, gratitude, shame, affection,
disease, and even the expectation of damnation seem to act
only as temporary restraints. Intemperance is not a single sin
standing apart, to be dismissed by one victorious resolution; it
is an enemy which leaves agents in the blood, spies
in the memory, and traitors in the imagination. The reformed
drunkard may pass the familiar door with composure to-day, with
difficulty to-morrow, and on the third day enter it almost
before he has consciously resolved to stop. Those who have
never known such bondage speak lightly of will; those who
have escaped it speak less confidently.

For nearly nine months Connell remained sober.

It was in August that the first change occurred. He
had been employed upon repairs at a warehouse near the
river, and one evening, returning later than usual, met an
old acquaintance named Denis H——, who had recently come from
Liverpool. Denis had known him in better days, before want
and drunkenness had completed their work, and greeted him with
sincere pleasure. They walked together; they spoke of former masters,
dead companions, youthful excursions, and the thousand harmless recollections by
which temptation sometimes disguises its approach.

At the door of a respectable public house Denis stopped.

"We'll take one glass for old times, Pat."

"I don't drink now."

"Not drink! Are you sick?"

"I was sick enough, Denis. Dead, near hand."

Denis laughed, supposing this an exaggeration.

"One glass won't kill ye twice. Come in out of
the rain."

Connell afterward declared—and I believe him—that he intended at first
to enter only for shelter. When the spirits were placed
upon the table, he refused. Denis drank to his health.
Connell raised the glass, set it down, and spoke of
his promise to his wife. Denis called for ale instead,
arguing that ale was not spirits; Connell tasted it. The
old warmth, the old companionship, the old deceitful ease returned
together. Ale led to whiskey; one glass demanded another; and
before ten o'clock the man who had withstood nine months
of ridicule and solicitation was once more intoxicated.

Two labourers carried him home.

Mrs. Connell, who had been waiting anxiously, heard their unsteady
feet upon the stair. She opened the door, saw her
husband hanging helpless between them, and uttered no reproach. That
silence, as she afterward told me, seemed to affect him
more than anger would have done. He struggled to stand,
looked at her, and began to sob.

"I've done it, Mary. I've gone and done it."

"Whisht, Pat. The childer are asleep."

"I promised him."

"Ye promised me too," she answered bitterly.

At these words he became suddenly sober in appearance, though
not in fact. He turned toward the landing, and such
terror passed over his face that the men supporting him
nearly lost their hold.

"Don't lave me there!" he cried. "For God's sake, boys,
carry me over it!"

They conveyed him into the room and placed him upon
the bed. For some time he muttered incoherently of the
table and the fires; afterward he fell into heavy sleep.

The next morning Biddy came for me. Connell was sitting
with his head bowed upon his hands. His first words
were—

"It's over, your reverence."

"What is over?"

"The chance I got. I broke it."

"You have fallen; you must rise."

"No. I was let out on a promise. I kept
it till last night, and then—Didn't I hear them when
I crossed the board? 'He'll come back,' says they."

I spoke to him with severity, for I feared that
indulgence might encourage despair, and despair another excess. I reminded
him that repentance is not proved by the absence of
temptation, but by resistance renewed after failure; that no infernal
covenant compelled him to drink; that he had entered the
house by his own will and must leave it by
the same moral agency. He listened, but without his former
eagerness.

At length he said, "I'll thry again."

He did try. For several weeks he resumed work and
abstained. Yet the serenity which had accompanied his first reformation
did not return. He frequently examined the landing; sometimes he
knelt upon it and prayed; sometimes, particularly after dusk, he
crossed it at a leap. He complained that a second
footstep followed his own upon the stair, and that, when
he woke at night, a dark figure stood near the
door.

These impressions might easily be attributed to a nervous system
disordered by his relapse, and I urged him to consult
the physician. He refused, saying that doctors could open a
vein but could not bleed out a promise.

I have little more to add; yet that little is
the part which has most frequently disturbed my own sleep,
and which, even now, I cannot write without an involuntary
pause.

On the evening of the 17 th of September, Connell
returned from work in his ordinary health. He ate supper
with his family, mended a broken stool, and listened while
Biddy read several verses from the New Testament. No liquor
had passed his lips. At about ten o'clock the family
retired. Connell and his wife occupied the bed; the younger
children slept behind a curtain; Biddy lay upon a small
mattress near the hearth.

Sometime after midnight Mrs. Connell awoke. The candle had been
extinguished, but moonlight entered through the uncovered portion of the
window, producing that uncertain illumination in which objects are visible
without being distinct. She saw her husband standing near the
door.

"Pat," she said, "where are ye goin'?"

He did not answer.

She then perceived that he was not alone.

A second figure stood beyond him, partly in the passage.
She could distinguish no features, no clothing, no outline sufficiently
exact to determine whether it was large or small; but
she believed it to be a man, and supposed, in
the first confusion of waking, that one of Connell's fellow-workmen
had called him.

"Who's there?" she demanded.

The figure moved backward. Connell followed.

Mrs. Connell rose upon her elbow.

"Pat, come back. Do ye hear me?"

He turned his head. The moonlight fell upon his face,
and she saw that his eyes were open, though they
had the fixed and unconscious expression of a sleeper's. His
lips moved.

"What did ye say?" she asked.

This time the words reached her.

"I promised."

He passed through the door beside the other figure.

Mrs. Connell sprang from the bed. Before her feet touched
the floor, she heard one step upon the stair, then
another—whether made by one person or two she could never
confidently state. There followed a heavy crash, a splintering sound,
and a cry so short that it seemed cut off
at its commencement.

The children awoke screaming. Biddy seized the candle and attempted
to light it; the mother ran into the passage without
waiting. At the head of the stair she stopped, for
the strengthened landing had given way, not in the centre
where the old plank had been replaced, but at its
outer edge, where the timber supporting the rail had decayed
within the wall.

Connell lay below, his head doubled beneath his body, his
feet resting upon the broken boards. Death must have been
instantaneous. His neck was completely dislocated.

No second person was found.

The door of the house was bolted from within. The
rooms below were searched; the roof was examined; every tenant
was questioned. One woman declared that she had heard rapid
footsteps descending after the fall; another had heard nothing until
Mrs. Connell screamed. A man sleeping in the lower room
affirmed that the outer door had not opened during the
night. Denis H—— was in Liverpool. Red Larry, upon whom
suspicion first fell, proved that he had been confined in
the watch-house since the preceding evening.

The physician, who examined the body, concluded that Connell had
risen in his sleep, walked into the passage, and fallen
through the weakened side of the landing. Somnambulism, he observed,
is frequently associated with strong mental impressions; the supposed companion
was probably a shadow cast by the door or an
illusion of the wife's half-awakened senses. Nothing, in his opinion,
could be more natural.

And perhaps he was right.

The landing, though repaired by Connell, had not been rebuilt
throughout. His weight near the rail might have displaced a
rotten support. His final words may have referred merely to
the dream which had so long possessed him. Mrs. Connell,
startled from sleep, may have mistaken a hanging coat, a
moving shadow, or the darkness itself for a human figure.
Terror, once awakened, completes its own evidence; memory unconsciously adjusts
uncertain particulars to the catastrophe which follows.

These explanations are rational, consistent, and, in all probability, true.

Yet why did Connell leave his bed at that hour?
Why did he appear to follow rather than precede the
figure? Why did the floor fail upon the very landing
through which he believed himself to have descended, and which,
with almost prophetic fear, he had laboured to strengthen? Above
all, what was the meaning of those last words—"I promised"—uttered
in sleep, if sleep it was, immediately before his death?

Was the vision merely the monstrous offspring of intemperance, guilt,
and suspended animation? Was it a warning mercifully granted, the
benefits of which he partly obtained, though human frailty at
length exposed him to a natural accident? Or was there
indeed, beneath the forms supplied by his disordered imagination, some
dreadful compact—invalid before Heaven, yet claimed by the Power that
deceived him—and did an unseen companion come, not to force,
but to invite; not to seize the penitent, but to
take advantage of one broken resolution?

I do not know.

I buried Patrick Connell in the churchyard of St. M——'s.
His wife and children followed the coffin; several workmen whom
his amendment had attached to him contributed to the expense;
and even some of his former drinking companions stood at
a distance, silent and abashed. I did not preach that
he was lost. God forbid that any servant of religion
should pronounce such a judgment upon a man whose latter
life, notwithstanding one grievous relapse, had displayed sincere contrition, honest
labour, restitution, and love. Neither did I tell those assembled
that his dream was meaningless. I warned them only that
vice, once admitted, may return after long exclusion; that no
man should sport with an appetite which has already made
him a slave; and that repentance, though ever open to
the living, ought not to be postponed to an hour
which may never be granted.

Mrs. Connell removed soon afterward to another part of the
city. Before leaving, she asked me to accompany her once
more to the deserted rooms. The furniture had been taken
away; the garret looked larger in its emptiness; and daylight,
entering freely, deprived it of much of its former horror.
We descended together.

At the landing she stopped.

The broken boards had been lifted aside. Beneath them was
only the narrow well of the staircase, filled with dust,
fragments of plaster, and the common refuse of an old
house. No red light glimmered below; no heat arose; no
cavern opened; no whisper of condemned multitudes disturbed the sober
noises of the street. Reason had every thing before it—rotten
timber, a steep fall, a sleeping man, a frightened woman—and
reason ought to have been satisfied.

Mrs. Connell pointed to the wall.

There, upon the plaster close to the place where her
husband's hand might naturally have struck in falling, were five
dark marks. They resembled the impressions of fingers, though larger
than those of an ordinary man, and appeared scorched rather
than stained.

"Were they there before?" I asked.

"No, your reverence."

I touched them. The plaster crumbled beneath my hand, blackened
by damp and age. Such marks may be produced in
many ways; smoke, moisture, rotten wood, or the extinguished candle
carried by Biddy might readily have occasioned them. I said
as much.

Mrs. Connell looked at me with an expression in which
grief had overcome fear.

"Maybe so, sir," she replied. "But Pat seen the mark
in the hand."

I made no answer.

As we reached the street, the church clock struck noon,
and the ordinary life of the city flowed around us—carts,
labourers, merchants, children, beggars, all moving beneath the clear September
sun. The open day rebuked the imagination of the night.
I parted from the widow, returned to my duties, and
resolved to think no more upon the matter.

But there are resolutions of the understanding which memory refuses
to obey. Even now, when the wind shakes an old
staircase, when a sleeper speaks an intelligible word, or when
I am summoned before dawn to some chamber where life
and death seem to contend in silence, I behold again
the blue face raised among the white cloths; I hear
the recovered man cry "Fire!"; I see the endless table
beneath the rolling globes; and, last of all, I distinguish
two figures passing through the moonlit door—one the unhappy Connell,
the other without name, feature, or certain substance.

Whether that companion was shadow, dream, providence, or tempter, the
reader must decide, if indeed decision be either possible or
wise. I affirm only the facts as they were witnessed
and reported to me. Patrick Connell dreamed that he descended
through the landing, promised to return, reformed his life, relapsed
once, and died upon that same landing while apparently following
another.

The boards were rotten; the night was dark; the human
mind is easily deceived.

And yet—he had promised.

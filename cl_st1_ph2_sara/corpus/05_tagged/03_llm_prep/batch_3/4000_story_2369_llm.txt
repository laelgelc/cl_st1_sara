It was over.

Ruin—complete, immediate, and practically payable on Tuesday—had descended upon Oxhead
Towers.

Lord Oxhead stood at the window of the Ancestral Breakfast-Room
and gazed across the park. The evening wind soughed (or
possibly sogged) among the cedars. Beyond them lay the ornamental
lake, now let to a fishmonger; beyond that, the west
meadow, mortgaged; and beyond that again the village of Lower
Oxhead, which had been sold in 1894, though the villagers,
from force of habit, continued to reside there.

The earl's face was pale and stricken.

For eight hundred years the Oxheads had inhabited Oxhead Towers.
One of them, Sir Marmaduke de Oxhead, had accompanied William
the Conqueror to England, though owing to a misunderstanding about
the boat he arrived in 1089. Another had distinguished himself
at Crécy by holding the horses at a safe distance.
A third, Lord Wilfred Oxhead, had been concealed in an
oak during the Wars of the Roses and had remained
there until the Restoration, which displayed unusual patience if not
exact chronology.

Their portraits lined the room: Oxheads in armour, Oxheads in
wigs, Oxheads in hunting clothes, one Oxhead in a bathing
costume, and the celebrated Lady Ermyntrude Oxhead dressed as Britannia
with an umbrella and a stuffed goose.

Above the fireplace hung the family arms: quarterly, first and
fourth, a dog intermittent upon a field of mustard; second,
three turnips passant; third, a plain parallelogram right centre, surmounted
by a boot. The supporters were a disappointed unicorn and
a chartered accountant regardant. Beneath appeared the ancient motto:

_Amo, amas, amat, amamus._

Its meaning had long been forgotten, but it was believed
to have something to do with courage.

Lord Oxhead turned from the window.

"All gone," he murmured. "The woods, the farms, the plate,
the mortgages—especially the mortgages. Nothing remains except the title, the
family pride, and six hundred and forty-seven pounds, eleven shillings
and fourpence demanded by Messrs. Hardup, Mixtup and Company before
noon tomorrow."

His hand trembled.

But poverty was not the only shadow upon him. There
was a secret in his life: a hideous, hereditary, or
at any rate extremely awkward secret. For thirty-seven years he
had guarded it. At times he had almost forgotten what
it was; but he knew that if it became known
the honour of the Oxheads would be shattered, the escutcheon
stained, and several persons in Milwaukee considerably surprised.

He had written the secret upon paper once, sealed it
in an envelope, placed the envelope in an iron box,
and hidden the box beneath the floor of the west
turret. Unfortunately, the west turret had afterward been sold for
building material. Since then he had carried the secret in
his bosom, where it caused indigestion.

The door opened.

Gwendoline entered.

She was a beautiful girl of thirty-three, with the fresh
innocence of one who has been carefully prevented from learning
anything. Her eyes were blue, her hair was golden, and
her thoughts—when she had them—were of the purest simplicity. She
wore white muslin, though it was November.

"Father," she said, in a low, flute-like voice.

"My daughter."

"I have something to tell you."

Lord Oxhead started. Persons in financial difficulties commonly start when
told this.

"Speak, child."

"I love."

The earl staggered toward a chair.

"Love!" he repeated. "Here? In this house?"

"Yes, Father. I have plighted my troth."

"Without consulting me?"

"I did consult you, Father. Yesterday at luncheon I asked
whether you objected to my marrying."

"And I said certainly not."

"Yes."

"I understood you to mean generally."

"So did I."

There was a silence, heavy with approaching explanation.

"Who is he?" demanded Lord Oxhead at last. "Is he
of noble blood? Is he a Courtenay, a Plantagenet, a
Bourbon, a Wisconsin?"

"He is an American."

"An American!"

The word fell upon the room like a tradesman's account.

Lord Oxhead covered his face.

"An American," he repeated. "One of those strange republican persons
without ancestors, who live in elevated railways and elect a
president every morning! Child, you cannot know what you say.
America is the land of Bolivar, Pocahontas, and the late
Mr. Buffalo Bill. True, Pocahontas was related to the Oxheads—she
may not actually have married your ancestor, but they undoubtedly
esteemed one another—but that does not reconcile me to the
country as a whole."

"He is very gentle," said Gwendoline.

"They frequently are before marriage."

"He speaks beautifully."

"In English?"

"Almost."

"What is his name?"

"Edwin Einstein."

Lord Oxhead recoiled.

"Einstein! I have never heard of the family."

"They are in business."

The earl uttered a hollow groan.

"Business! My child, no Oxhead has ever been in business."

"What of Uncle Algernon?"

"He sold patent braces, but anonymously."

"And Cousin Rupert?"

"He kept a public-house."

"A family public-house, Father."

"It had been in the family for generations," admitted the
earl, "which made it landed property."

He paced the chamber with courtly agitation.

"What business is this Einstein engaged in?"

"His father is interested in pork, copper, wheat, tramways, condensed
milk, rubber, newspapers, and peace."

"Peace?"

"He manufactures ammunition for it."

Lord Oxhead paused.

"Is the business extensive?"

"Edwin says his father controls nineteen companies, fourteen legislatures, three
railroads, and the price of breakfast."

The earl's manner softened almost imperceptibly.

"Indeed?"

"He is said to be worth eighty million dollars."

"Dollars," said Lord Oxhead cautiously. "What are they in pounds?"

"I do not know."

"Neither do I. But there are many of them, I
believe."

"Very many."

Lord Oxhead approached his daughter and took her hand with
the grave tenderness of an aristocrat reconsidering a principle.

"My child," he said, "birth is much. Blood is much.
The sacred traditions of an ancient house are much. But
eighty million dollars are also quite a good deal. I
should be unworthy of my ancestors if I permitted a
narrow prejudice to obstruct your happiness."

"Oh, Father!"

"Does the young man know of our position?"

"He has asked several questions."

"What questions?"

"He asked your age, whether you suffered from heart disease,
how much the estate was worth, whether the title descended
through the female line, and if the family jewels were
paste."

Lord Oxhead's eyes filled with tears.

"He is thoughtful."

"He also asked whether the creditors could disturb a marriage
settlement."

"Prudent as well! Gwendoline, I begin to love this young
man as a son."

"He is waiting in the library."

"In the library? Among the books?"

"We moved the books last month, Father."

"True. Bring him to me."

* * *

Edwin Einstein entered with the modest assurance of one who
had bought his assurance wholesale.

He was not tall, though when standing upon his fortune
he achieved a considerable elevation. His hair was black and
smooth; his moustache drooped pensively at both ends, as though
depressed by the exchange rate. One hand, strong, flexible, and
almost prehensile, held his hat. His eyes moved with romantic
ardour from Gwendoline to the silver inkstand, from the inkstand
to the miniature of Lady Ermyntrude, and from the miniature
to a small ruby pin in Lord Oxhead's cravat.

"Father," whispered Gwendoline, "this is Edwin."

"Mr. Einstein," said the earl, extending three fingers, "my daughter
has informed me of the honour you propose to confer
upon our house."

"Sure," said Edwin. "I am ready to put it through,
subject to written stipulations, searches, valuations, and the ordinary legal
formalities."

The earl bowed.

"Your language has the ring of sincerity."

"I love your daughter," continued Edwin. "When I first saw
her I says to myself, 'Edwin, there is the girl
for you.' She was standing under the portrait of the
old dame with the goose, and the whole proposition appealed
to me."

Gwendoline blushed.

"You are not, I understand, engaged in active labour?" asked
Lord Oxhead.

"Not if I can help it."

"Admirable. Idleness is the first condition of gentility."

"My father does the work. I watch him."

"Precisely what my family has done with its tenants for
centuries."

Edwin smiled, displaying a quantity of teeth that suggested both
health and investment.

"Then it is settled?" he asked.

"It is settled," replied Lord Oxhead. "Commerce and ancestry shall
clasp hands. The New World shall bring gold to the
Old; the Old World shall give in return those immaterial
distinctions which, being immaterial, are naturally the more expensive."

At that instant Edwin stepped fully into the light.

Lord Oxhead looked at him.

The earl's expression changed.

His face became grey. His eyes widened. One hand clutched
the family parallelogram embroidered upon his waistcoat.

"You!" he cried.

Edwin did not move.

"You—you are—"

Lord Oxhead pointed a shaking finger at him.

"The face! The moustache! After all these years—Oshkosh!"

He gave one terrible gasp and fell backward across a
chair.

Gwendoline screamed.

Edwin caught the silver inkstand before it reached the floor.

"Father!" cried Gwendoline.

But Lord Oxhead made no answer. The last Earl of
Oxhead was extinct. That is to say, he was dead.

* * *

The doctors disagreed with professional harmony.

One declared that death had resulted from failure of the
heart; another maintained that it had arisen from success of
the shock. A third, who had come from London and
therefore knew more, attributed it to an obscure hereditary condition
and charged two hundred guineas.

What Lord Oxhead had recognized in Edwin was never discovered.

Gwendoline questioned her lover.

"What did Father mean by 'Oshkosh'?"

"It is a city," said Edwin.

"Were you ever there?"

"I have been in many cities."

"Did you know Father before?"

"I have known many fathers."

This reply, though not exhaustive, satisfied her simplicity.

Search was made beneath the floors, behind the portraits, inside
the armour, and through the earl's papers. Nothing was found
except unpaid bills, a recipe for furniture polish, and a
memorandum reading, "Remember the awful secret," which proved that there
had been one but inconsiderately omitted its nature.

Perhaps Lord Oxhead had married under an assumed name; perhaps
Edwin was connected with the lost branch of the family;
perhaps the earl had once been a commercial traveller in
Wisconsin. It may even have concerned Pocahontas. The truth was
doubtless complicated, and therefore probably not interesting.

The wedding took place six weeks later.

It was deliberately quiet. There were no bridesmaids, because Edwin
considered them ornamental; no bells, because ringers required payment; no
flowers, because flowers died; no breakfast, because people had already
eaten one; and no honeymoon, because travel unsettled business. The
ceremony possessed all the austere beauty that can be obtained
by eliminating expenditure.

Gwendoline wore her mother's gown. Edwin wore Lord Oxhead's best
coat, altered at the shoulders and improved at the pockets.

After the marriage a change came over him. His English
grew rapidly more fluent. He ceased to say "I guess,"
abandoned the drooping moustache, and dressed with a quiet elegance
previously absent from his person. Within a year he could
pronounce "ancestral" without hesitation and "schedule" in two different ways.
He took to carrying a monocle, though neither eye required
it, and spoke of American visitors as "those extraordinary colonials."

Under his management, Oxhead Towers was restored. The lake was
bought back from the fishmonger, the west meadow redeemed, and
the villagers of Lower Oxhead purchased with the rest of
the property. The family arms were corrected by changing the
dog intermittent into a bull rampant with limited liability. Edwin
also extended the motto:

_Amo, amas, amat, amamus, amatis, amant._

Thus strengthened, it appeared above the door.

Gwendoline was happy. Edwin was rich. The creditors were paid
at ninepence in the pound and were therefore described as
satisfied.

As for the great secret which had clouded Lord Oxhead's
declining years, it had perished with him.

And considering the trouble it had already caused, this was
perhaps the first useful thing it had ever done.

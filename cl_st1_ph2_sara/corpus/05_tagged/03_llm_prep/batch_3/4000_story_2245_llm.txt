### THE TENANT ABOVE

*Now hear, if any care to hear,*
*What the old station records declare—*
*How the dumb dead, lacking breath,*
*Beat at a door from under death;*
*And how a hound, more wise than men,*
*Brought a lost master home again.*

Imray vanished on the seventeenth of May, between tea and
dinner, and the Indian Empire survived the circumstance.

This is not intended as a reproach to the Empire.
Imray was young, civil, industrious within reasonable limits, and possessed
of a clear handwriting which had caused him to be
employed upon revenue abstracts. Yet the machine, having mislaid one
of its smaller brass wheels, turned on with scarcely a
jar. His office-box was sealed; his pending papers were distributed;
a telegram went to the Secretary; and for three weeks
every mounted constable within forty miles looked into wells, ravines,
ruined temples, railway waiting-rooms, and such other places as a
young British official might reasonably or unreasonably choose for his
extinction.

The search was thorough. We dragged the canal below the
old bridge, though Imray could swim; questioned the hillmen, though
he spoke none of their dialects; and sent descriptions of
him to ports he had never visited. His horse was
found in its stable, his dogcart under the shed, and
his account at the Bank unaltered. There was money in
his writing-table, a half-written letter to his sister on the
blotting-pad, and a new mahseer-rod in the veranda which had
not yet been jointed. These things argued against flight. There
was no blood, no overturned furniture, and no native rumour—which
argued, according to the Police, against murder.

By June the rains came, beating the station flat beneath
them. The roads dissolved, the river took away two piers
of the new bridge, and the question of Imray shrank
to a paragraph in a monthly report.

By August even that had ceased.

His bungalow stood empty on the edge of the Civil
Lines, where the scrub dipped toward a dry watercourse. It
was an old house, broad-roofed and low, with deep verandas,
green shutters, and a white ceiling-cloth stretched under the rafters
to conceal the tiles, dust, scorpions, rats, snakes, and general
dishonour of the upper world. The compound had once been
well kept. After Imray's departure the roses ran wild, the
paths vanished under rank grass, and a creeper thrust one
inquisitive hand through the bathroom window. His servants remained for
a month, drawing wages from the Treasury under orders, and
then were absorbed into other households.

Bahadur Khan, Imray's khitmatgar, passed to Strickland.

This was natural, because Strickland took the bungalow when he
returned from the districts in September.

Strickland was then attached to the Police, though attachment is
a mild word for the relations between him and that
service. He knew more of the people among whom he
worked than was considered wholly respectable. He could sit for
half a night with a grain-dealer, a priest, a camel-thief,
or a village headman, and emerge with information which no
official memorandum had requested, but which generally proved useful six
months later. He went disguised where another man would have
sent an orderly; ate food that would have killed a
less prejudiced stomach; and kept, in addition to three rifles
and a cabinet of queer local poisons, a great brindled
bitch named Tietjens.

Tietjens was not beautiful. Her head was too broad, one
ear had been torn in a pig-hunt, and her tail
expressed contempt even when she slept. She loved Strickland, endured
me, and distrusted the greater portion of mankind upon evidence
which she was not prepared to communicate. She had broken
the wrist of a burglar at Umballa, discovered a missing
child in a millet field, and once prevented Strickland from
sitting upon a cobra. Her moral character was therefore better
established than that of most officials.

I went over to dine with Strickland on the third
evening after he had taken possession.

"You have chosen a melancholy kennel," I said, looking at
the long windows and the weeds silvering in the dusk.

"It is cheap," said Strickland.

"So are funerals, if one does not insist on horses."

"Imray did not object to the place."

"Imray's present opinions on house-property are unavailable."

Strickland called for a drink. Bahadur Khan brought it upon
a brass tray and stood in the doorway, tall, grey-bearded,
and composed. He had served Imray for nearly two years
and was said to be an admirable servant. Certainly he
moved without sound, remembered preferences, and had the gift—more valuable
in a servant than virtue—of appearing only when needed.

"Bahadur Khan," I said, "do you like returning to this
house?"

His eyes came to mine and dropped.

"Where the Presence commands, there is service."

"That means nothing," said Strickland.

"It means as much as most answers," I said.

The man withdrew. Tietjens, who had been lying beneath the
table, raised her head and followed him with her eyes.
When he passed out of sight she rose, went to
the threshold, smelled it carefully, and returned to Strickland's knee.

"She dislikes him," I said.

"She dislikes his boots. They were greased with camel-fat at
Peshawar."

Tietjens looked at Strickland as one who hears a friend
make an inadequate defence.

Dinner was indifferent, for Strickland had not yet arranged his
cook-room, and the rain had drowned the charcoal. While we
ate, a damp wind moved through the house. It smelled
of wet earth, old matting, and the peculiar sourness of
rooms shut through the hot weather. Once the curtain between
the dining-room and passage lifted slowly, though no wind touched
the lamp-flame.

I looked behind it. There was no one.

"Your curtain is badly hung," I said.

"It has been badly hung for fifteen years."

"Then it has developed ambition."

Strickland continued eating.

Presently there came a faint creak from the drawing-room: not
the complaint of timber contracting, but the sound a cane
chair makes when a man settles his weight in it.
Tietjens stiffened. The hair rose along her back in a
narrow ridge. She walked to the doorway and stood there,
peering into the unlighted room.

"Rat," said Strickland.

"No rat heavy enough to sit in a planter's chair
would need to hide from us."

Tietjens entered the drawing-room. We heard her nails tick upon
the floor, halt, and move again. Then she growled—a deep
and private growl, not intended as warning but as judgment.
A moment later she backed into the light, keeping her
head toward something that did not appear.

Strickland laid down his fork.

"Come here, old lady."

Tietjens obeyed, though unwillingly, and pushed her muzzle under his
hand.

"Has she done that before?" I asked.

"Each night."

"And you call it rats?"

"I call nothing by a name until I can arrest
it."

That was Strickland's fashion. He would not admit a mystery,
because mysteries cannot be entered in the charge-book; but he
never denied a fact, however inconvenient.

After dinner we smoked in the veranda. The rain had
stopped, and the world was black beyond the steps. Frogs
made a noise like cracked temple bells in the ditches;
water dropped from leaf to leaf; and far off a
jackal cried to the newly washed moon. The bungalow behind
us seemed to breathe through its open doors.

Tietjens lay at Strickland's feet until half-past ten. Then, without
warning, she lifted her head and stared down the length
of the veranda.

Nothing was there.

Her eyes moved steadily from the far corner toward us,
as though they followed the advance of a person who
walked very slowly. When they had travelled half the distance,
she rose. Her forelegs were rigid, her shoulders forward, and
every muscle of her body drew tight as a hawser
under strain.

"Hush," said Strickland.

Tietjens did not bark. She retreated one pace, then another.
Her eyes continued to follow that which we could not
see. At the drawing-room doorway she turned abruptly and ran
into the wet compound. There she stopped beneath a neem-tree
and refused all summons.

I confess that the performance disturbed me.

"A jackal crossed the grass," I said.

"No. She would have rushed it."

"Then a snake."

"She would have barked."

"Then your camel-fat theory."

Strickland made no answer. From within the house came the
soft click of a door-latch lifted and allowed to fall.
We went in together.

Every door stood open.

We searched the rooms with lamps. We looked under beds,
behind boxes, in the bath-room, the store-room, and the little
office where Imray's letter had been found. We examined the
shutters and the bolts. There was nobody; there was nothing;
there was only the smell of damp plaster and the
ticking of Strickland's travelling-clock.

When we returned to the dining-room, one chair stood a
foot from the table. I had pushed it in before
going out.

"Bahadur Khan!" Strickland called.

The servant appeared at once.

"Who has entered?"

"No man, Sahib."

"Who moved that chair?"

"I do not know."

"You know the house."

"I knew it when my former master gave commands."

"And after?"

Bahadur Khan's face was still.

"After, the house knew itself."

"Take the chair away," said Strickland.

For the first time, the man hesitated. It was no
more than the pause between two breaths, but we both
observed it. Then he carried the chair into the passage.

Tietjens slept under the neem-tree that night. Strickland himself dragged
her basket into his bedroom, but she crawled out through
the open window and returned to the wet earth. Thereafter
nothing could induce her to remain inside after sunset.

A dog may be whimsical once. Tietjens was whimsical for
six weeks.

During those weeks I visited Strickland often, partly because he
had tolerable tobacco and partly because fear, like curiosity, dislikes
being left alone. The haunting—if one must use so theatrical
a word—established a routine. It began at twilight, when forms
are uncertain and honest furniture assumes intentions. A curtain would
stir against the wind. A bolt would rattle in an
empty room. Steps crossed the veranda and ceased at the
old office. Once we heard fingers—very patient fingers—trying the locked
door of Strickland's bedroom. On another evening, while we played
cards, a long sigh travelled above us from one end
of the ceiling-cloth to the other.

Strickland looked up.

"Rat," he said.

"An asthmatic rat with boots."

"Possibly."

The white cloth overhead bellied down and slowly rose again.

I threw my cards upon the table.

"If this is your notion of cheap housing, I prefer
extravagance."

"There's nowhere else for you to stay. Your roof leaks."

"My roof admits only water."

"Water gives fever."

"Fever is recognised by medical science and behaves with comparative
honour."

Strickland smiled and dealt again.

The thing—whatever it was—did not attack. It entreated. That was
worse. It fluttered, waited, whispered, pressed at doors, crept through
passages, and moved chairs as a weak man might move
them to attract attention. More than once I woke in
the spare room with the conviction that someone stood beside
my bed, bending low and labouring to speak. I heard
breath, but no word. When I struck a match there
was only the mosquito-net and, beyond it, the pale square
of the door.

In the mornings all became absurd. Sunlight lay broadly on
the floors; crows quarrelled on the roof; servants shouted in
the cook-house; and Tietjens entered to eat breakfast with the
air of one who had no connection with last night's
folly. By ten o'clock Strickland was at his files, I
at my own work, and the bungalow a common, ill-built
bungalow like two hundred others.

But twilight returned punctually.

One evening in October, Strickland found Tietjens in the passage
before sunset. She stood beneath the place where the old
dining-room joined the drawing-room, looking upward. Her nose quivered. Every
few seconds she moved two paces to the left, then
back again, as if marking a scent above the ceiling.

Strickland brought a ladder and examined the cloth.

"There are stains," I said.

"Old rain-marks."

"One is new."

"The roof leaks."

"Everything in India leaks, lies, bites, or gives fever. You
must be more exact."

He touched the stain. It was no more than a
grey shadow, irregular and dry. Tietjens whined.

Strickland called Bahadur Khan.

The servant came in carrying a lamp, though there was
still light outside. At sight of the ladder he stopped.

"Has any man been above the ceiling?" Strickland asked.

"There is no floor above, Sahib."

"That was not my question."

"No man goes there."

"Could one go?"

"A child might crawl between the beams. A man would
break the cloth."

"Imray had repairs done before he disappeared."

"So it was told."

"You were here."

"I attended upon my master."

"Did workmen enter the roof?"

Bahadur Khan lifted both hands.

"Who remembers the feet of labourers after five months?"

Strickland descended.

"You do," he said. "Go."

The man bowed and withdrew. Tietjens watched him to the
door, then looked up again.

"Do you suspect him?" I asked.

"I suspect everyone who says he remembers nothing."

"That includes half the Service."

"The other half remembers what never happened."

He put away the ladder.

Nothing was done. There were inspections, a district tour, a
case concerning stolen cattle, and a dinner at the Club
where the Commissioner spoke for forty minutes on sanitation while
seven men drank unsafe water. Imray's bungalow continued its nightly
solicitations. The servants pretended deafness. Tietjens slept beneath the neem-tree.
Strickland made notes, and I made excuses not to visit
him after dark.

Then came the great storm of the twenty-third.

All afternoon the heat had lain upon the station like
a hand over the mouth. Toward evening a copper-coloured bank
rose in the east. The kites dropped from the sky;
cattle crowded against compound walls; and the leaves of the
pipal-trees turned their pale undersides outward. At seven the wind
came—first one hot breath, then a roar that bent the
bamboo and drove dust through the shutter cracks. Lightning burst
over us in white sheets, and the rain followed, hard
and straight, striking the roof like gravel flung from a
cart.

I was obliged to stay with Strickland, for the road
to my house became a brown torrent within ten minutes.
We dined in the inner room. Tietjens had been tied
beneath the veranda, but at the first thunderclap she snapped
her collar and disappeared into the cook-house. That she preferred
cooks, smoke, and wet ashes to the bungalow indicated the
depth of her conviction.

The storm drowned all smaller noises. The lamps guttered; water
ran under the doors; and portions of the compound travelled
toward the river. At nine there came a violent scraping
overhead.

Strickland looked up.

"That is not thunder," he said.

The ceiling-cloth sagged above the centre table. Something long and
dark showed against it, moving in curves.

I rose so quickly that my chair fell.

"Snake."

"Two," said Strickland.

A second shape slid along the first. The cloth dipped
under their weight and whitened again as they passed toward
the wall.

"I shall sleep at the Club."

"You cannot cross the culvert."

"Then I shall drown on the way. It is a
cleaner death."

Strickland took a hunting-knife, a lantern, and the short ladder.

"What are you doing?"

"Looking above."

"Two cobras have just announced possession."

"They may be rat-snakes."

"All snakes become rat-snakes when another man must handle them."

He placed the ladder beneath the stained portion of cloth.

At that instant a heavy body struck the ceiling from
above. The fabric tore half across the room, dropping dust,
dried leaves, rat-dung, and a shower of small insects upon
the table. One snake fell with it—a black, thick creature
nearly five feet long. It struck the floor, writhed, and
vanished beneath a cabinet.

The second remained among the rafters.

I moved into the veranda and declared that I could
observe perfectly from there.

Strickland enlarged the tear with his knife. The lantern showed
the underside of the roof: black beams, tile-battens, hanging cobwebs,
and accumulated rubbish. The air that descended was hot and
foul, with a sweet, sickly taint which had no business
in a roof.

Strickland's expression altered.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Bring the other lamp."

"I am guarding the veranda."

"Bring it."

I brought it, taking care not to approach the cabinet.

He climbed until his shoulders were through the opening. Dust
fell around him. For some moments he said nothing. Then
he shifted the lantern, leaned forward, and thrust one arm
between two beams.

"There is something wrapped here."

"Leave it wrapped."

"It is caught."

He pulled. The rafters gave a dry groan. A length
of decayed matting appeared, then a boot.

The boot hung directly above my head.

I moved again.

"Hold the ladder," Strickland said.

"You have found a boot. That is sufficient for one
evening."

"Hold it."

I held it. He pulled once more, and the arrangement
above came free all at once.

It broke through the remaining cloth with a soft, enormous
collapse and fell upon the dining-table, extinguishing one lamp, overturning
the brandy, and scattering our cards across the wet floor.

It had been a man.

The heat beneath the roof had dried rather than destroyed
him. He wore a mouldered evening suit. One arm was
drawn up across the chest; the other hung stiffly from
the table. The skin was dark and tightened over the
face; the teeth showed; and round the neck, immediately below
the jaw, ran a cut so deep and clean that
no medical opinion was necessary.

Strickland raised the lantern.

"It is Imray," he said.

There are moments when the mind refuses grandeur and occupies
itself with trifles. I noticed that one of Imray's shirt-studs
remained in place and that his left boot had been
freshly soled before his death. I also noticed a dead
beetle adhering to his sleeve. These observations seemed important at
the time.

The storm continued. Water beat upon the roof; the torn
ceiling-cloth flapped; and beneath the cabinet the fallen snake threshed
once and became still.

Strickland covered Imray's face with a napkin.

"His throat was cut," I said.

"Yes."

"He has been there since May."

"Yes."

"And we dined under him."

"Repeatedly."

"That is the most offensive part of the business."

"It would be."

He went to the veranda and called, "Bahadur Khan!"

No answer came.

He called again, not loudly.

After a minute Bahadur Khan entered from the servants' quarters.
His clothes were dry. Therefore he had not been outside.
He saw the torn ceiling, the dust, the shape upon
the table, and the blackened hand hanging down.

He did not cry out. He merely drew the corner
of his shawl across his mouth.

Strickland closed the outer doors.

"Come nearer," he said.

Bahadur Khan obeyed.

"Look."

"I have looked, Sahib."

"Who is it?"

"My former master."

"You knew he was there."

"Let the Presence judge."

"The Presence will judge later. I ask first."

There was a silence in which the rain seemed to
withdraw very far from us. Bahadur Khan's eyes moved once
toward the open passage. I stood there, though I cannot
claim bravery; the snake was behind me, and between murder
and venom I preferred murder, being able to see it.

Strickland spoke almost gently.

"You served Imray. You brought his dinner that night. You
told the cook he had gone out. You locked the
office. Later you said he had dismissed you until morning.
But he never left this house."

Bahadur Khan's lips moved.

"No, Sahib."

"You killed him."

The man straightened. He was taller than Strickland and, until
that moment, had seemed older.

"It was written."

"That may be. You killed him."

"Yes."

The word entered the room quietly.

"Why?"

Bahadur Khan looked at the covered face on the table.
When he answered, his voice had the formal cadence of
one giving testimony before a power beyond appeal.

"The Sahib had the eye."

"What eye?"

"The eye that dries the child in the cradle and
turns milk bitter in the mother. The eye that praises,
and by praise destroys."

Strickland waited.

"My son's son was brought to the veranda. A little
one—four years, no more. He wore the red cap from
Kabul, and silver upon the ankles. My former master saw
him and laughed because the child saluted as a soldier.
He said, 'A fine boy, Bahadur Khan,' and touched him
here."

The old man laid two fingers upon his own forehead.

"Three days later the fever came. The child burned. The
healer made smoke; the verses were read; a charm was
tied upon the arm. Nothing availed. Before the sixth sunrise
he died."

"Children die of fever," said Strickland.

"To the learned, all things have small names."

"And you believed Imray killed him by admiration?"

"I knew."

"So you cut your master's throat."

"The child's mother tore her hair at my feet. My
son sat without speech. The house was empty of the
boy's voice. Should I eat my master's salt and make
no answer?"

"You might have left his service."

"Would leaving call the child from the grave?"

"How did you do it?"

Bahadur Khan glanced at me, and perhaps my face told
him there was no need for concealment.

"After dinner he sat at the writing-table. I brought the
lamp behind him. He asked for soda-water. When he bent
to the glass, I drew the knife once. There was
little noise. I had prepared ropes and matting. The ladder
stood in the store-room because men had repaired the tiles.
I hid him above the cloth and cleaned the floor
before dawn."

"You did all this alone?"

"Alone."

"And the servants?"

"They knew nothing. They believed he had gone to the
Club."

"His horse remained."

"He sometimes walked."

"Why did you remain in the station?"

"Where should I go? A running man declares himself. I
waited."

"For what?"

"For this."

He indicated the body, Strickland, and the shut doors with
one movement.

Strickland crossed to the writing-table and took out his revolver.

"I shall send for the police guard."

Bahadur Khan smiled faintly.

"The Police are here."

"The regular guard."

"It is not needed."

Strickland looked at him.

"Why?"

Bahadur Khan leaned against the wall. For the first time
I saw moisture upon his forehead, though the storm had
cooled the room. His right foot twisted under him.

"When the roof broke," he said, "I came through the
cook-room. The black snake lay by the cabinet. It had
been cut by falling timber, and anger was in it.
I did not see."

He drew up the loose cloth of his trousers. Upon
the great toe were two small punctures, already darkening. The
foot had begun to swell.

Strickland knelt and examined it.

"How long?"

"Before you called the first time."

"Why did you not speak?"

"What gain?"

Strickland shouted for his medical-case and ordered a servant to
run for the doctor. Bahadur Khan laughed once—not insolently, but
with relief.

"The doctor lives beyond the culvert. The water is high."

"We can bind the leg and cut the wound."

"The snake has gone before your knife."

Strickland tightened a cord above the ankle and made two
swift incisions across the punctures. Bahadur Khan did not flinch.
I fetched permanganate, brandy, and the little black bottle whose
label Strickland trusted more than the Medical Department. We did
what could be done, which was not much and perhaps
nothing.

The swelling climbed.

Two constables arrived at last, soaked to the bone and
very unhappy at being summoned through the storm. They stood
guard in the passage while Bahadur Khan sat upon the
floor, his back against the wall, and watched the covered
figure of his former master.

"You will make a statement," said Strickland.

"I have spoken."

"It must be written."

"Write, then."

Strickland wrote in his neat official hand: the name, the
hour, the confession, the concealment, the motive. He read each
sentence aloud. Bahadur Khan corrected him once.

"Not my son," he said. "My son's son."

Strickland altered it.

"Do you sign?"

The old man pressed an inked thumb upon the paper.

Outside, the rain slackened. The house was very quiet now—quieter
than I had ever known it. No curtain stirred. No
latch rose. The torn ceiling-cloth hung motionless, showing the black
cavity where Imray had lain through five months of heat
and rain, over dinners, arguments, reports, and games of cards.

Bahadur Khan's breathing became slow.

"The rope can be removed," he said. "It pains me."

Strickland did not remove it.

"You think the snake is kinder than the gallows?"

"There are many deaths. This one does not shame my
sons."

"Murder shames them."

"To your law."

"To any law."

Bahadur Khan considered this.

"Perhaps. Yet the child was beautiful."

It was the only tender thing he said.

The doctor reached us after midnight, having crossed the flooded
culvert on a cart-rope. He carried injections, instruments, and the
temper of a man whose dinner has been interrupted for
insufficient reasons. One glance at Bahadur Khan silenced him. The
poison had travelled too far. He administered what he could
and asked questions which received no answer.

At twenty minutes past one, Bahadur Khan turned his head
toward the table.

"Now he is seen," he whispered.

Then he died.

Officially, the matter was simple. Imray had been murdered by
his servant, who confessed before witnesses and died from snakebite
before he could be brought to trial. The body was
identified. The knife was afterwards found wrapped in oilcloth beneath
a loose stone in the cook-room. The district authorities received
the report with satisfaction, because a solved murder improves statistics
more than a missing officer. Imray was buried with such
honours as the season and the roads allowed. Bahadur Khan's
family claimed his body before noon.

No reference was made to footsteps, curtains, sighs, moving chairs,
or the conduct of Tietjens. Government has no column for
such evidence.

Strickland repaired the ceiling. The snakes—there had been three in
the roof-space—were killed or driven out, the beams cleansed, and
the bungalow smoked until every living creature within it either
fled or became part of the establishment. The dining-table was
burned. I had urged this and was surprised when Strickland
agreed.

On the first evening after the funeral, I dined with
him again.

The air was cool. The storm had washed the dust
from the trees, and the moon shone upon the flooded
compound. We ate in the drawing-room, where no chair creaked
except under honest weight. The curtains hung still. No step
passed along the veranda. No fingers troubled the bolts.

At ten o'clock Tietjens came in from beneath the neem-tree.

She paused at the threshold, smelled the room, and looked
toward the new white ceiling. Then she walked through every
chamber of the bungalow, slowly and with official thoroughness. We
heard her inspect the office, the bath-room, and the passage.
Presently she returned, drank from her bowl, turned three times
upon the mat beside Strickland's bed, and lay down.

She slept there until morning.

Strickland filled his pipe.

"The house is quiet," I said.

"The rats have gone."

"Of course."

"And Tietjens?"

"She has changed her mind."

Tietjens opened one eye, regarded him gravely, and closed it
again.

I have never known whether Imray's ghost walked in that
bungalow, or whether heat, damp, vermin, memory, and a dog's
nose combined to accuse a murderer. Strickland, when asked, said
only that unburied facts are restless things.

That answer serves as well as another.

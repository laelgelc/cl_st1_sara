### THE BURIAL OF THE THANKSGIVING PUDDING

I have ate Thanksgiving dinners in places where a man
had to hold his plate with one hand and fend
off the furniture with the other; likewise I have gone
without any dinner at all, unless you count three raw
onions, a biscuit inhabited by more life than was wholesome,
and a swallow of rain-water caught in an old boot.
So I am not particular. A sailor that gets particular
about his victuals had better ship as steward on a
ferryboat, where the shore is always handy and the chickens
do not have time to go bad between ports.

But the Thanksgiving I am going to tell you about
was aboard the schooner *Ann Eliza*, bound from Pensacola to
Vera Cruz with yellow-pine lumber, two crates of agricultural machinery
that nobody could put together, and the Honorable Samuel T.
Warriner as cabin passenger.

Why he was honorable I never rightly found out. Some
said he had been in the Legislature of a Northern
State, and some said he had merely been invited to
run and had kept the title after declining. Cornwallis Jones,
our cook, held that an honorable man was one who
had fought a duel and killed both the other man
and his second; but Cornwallis's history was a bit uncertain.
So is mine, for that matter, except where it concerns
ropes, weather, wages, and the meanness of shipowners, which are
four branches of human knowledge I have studied practical.

I was mate of the *Ann Eliza*. Captain Silas Pratt
had command of her, if you could call it command
when the Gulf was smooth. In bad weather the schooner
commanded Pratt, and generally had him praying to three different
denominations at once. He was a good navigator when sober,
a fine seaman when half sober, and, when he was
neither, he could still find the cabin bottle in total
darkness with the ship half heeled over. He had a
red beard, a temper like a wet cat in a
flour barrel, and the habit of referring to every misfortune
as an outrage specially directed against him by the United
States Government.

Our bo's'un was Prince Jackson—a long, square-built fellow, black as
a stove-lid and twice as useful. Prince could read print,
which gave him an advantage over half the forecastle and
all of the afterguard except Warriner. He had once been
in the navy, and had acquired there a solemn manner,
a knowledge of regulations, and the ability to say "Very
good, sir" in such a way that it sounded like
"You'll be sorry for this." He could knot, splice, steer,
rig a jury topmast, doctor a sprained wrist, and make
a man ashamed of loafing without raising his voice.

Cornwallis Jones, on the other hand, could make coffee.

That is about as far as I care to go
in praising him.

He called himself Cornwallis because, as he often explained, he
was directly descended from Lord Cornwallis, who surrendered at Yorktown.
How this descent had been managed was never made clear,
and Prince said the only thing Cornwallis had inherited from
his lordship was a genius for surrendering when surrounded. The
cook was short and bow-legged, with a round face that
always appeared to have just thought of something profitable. He
wore a blue cotton shirt, checked trousers, and a white
apron that had long since surrendered its original color to
grease, soot, molasses, coffee, and the general drift of maritime
cookery.

He was superstitious even for a cook. He believed in
ghosts, signs, lucky bones, unlucky birds, the evil eye, drowned
men knocking under the counter, and little invisible devils that
lived in the flour barrel. If a knife fell point-first
into the deck, he said company was coming. If it
fell flat, he said bad weather was coming. If it
stuck in his foot, he said the devil had thrown
it; and once, when Prince asked whether the devil had
also left the knife greasy, Cornwallis would not speak to
him for two watches.

The *Ann Eliza* sailed out of Pensacola with a light
northerly wind and every prospect of a decent passage. Warriner
came aboard in a long black coat, a high collar,
and a hat that looked too educated for the Gulf
of Mexico. He was tall and spare, with whiskers alongside
his jaw and a mouth made for public speaking. His
baggage consisted of two trunks, six books, a silver-mounted cane,
and a leather case containing papers that he guarded as
if they were peace treaties.

He was going to Vera Cruz on business connected with
railroads, land, mines, politics, or all four mixed together. Whenever
anyone asked, he gave an answer about the inevitable development
of continental resources; and by the time he had finished,
the man that asked had forgotten the question.

He knew everything.

That was his chief weakness.

He knew navigation, though he had never been at sea;
medicine, though he fainted when Simmons cut his thumb; agriculture,
though he could not tell hay from tow; and the
habits of foreign nations, though he called our Spanish phrase
book a Portuguese dictionary. The first day out he informed
Captain Pratt that a schooner ought not to roll so
much.

"You don't say!" says Pratt.

"It is due, I presume, to improper stowage."

"No," says Pratt. "It's due to the sea being under
her."

Warriner considered this and said the captain's explanation had superficial
plausibility.

Now he was not a bad man. Pompous, yes; troublesome
without meaning to be, certainly; but liberal with his tobacco,
ready to laugh when the joke was not too plainly
against him, and capable of giving a sailor half a
dollar for carrying a trunk that the sailor had been
ordered to carry anyhow. The crew got used to him,
and he got used to us, though I do not
believe he ever got used to Cornwallis's cooking.

Nobody could.

The second morning Warriner lifted the cover off a dish,
stared into it, and asked, "What is this?"

"Hash," says Cornwallis.

"Of what?"

Cornwallis reflected. "Yesterday," he says.

That answer bothered the Honorable Samuel for the rest of
the day.

We made fair weather down the Gulf, keeping watch and
watch, with the *Ann Eliza* walking along under all plain
sail. The lumber was well stowed, the pumps sucked dry,
and the only trouble was Captain Pratt's conviction that the
barometer was insulting him. We had been out about five
days when Warriner came on deck after breakfast with one
of his books tucked under his arm and a serious
expression that meant he intended improving somebody.

"What date is Thursday?" he asks me.

"The twenty-ninth, by the log."

"It is Thanksgiving Day."

"Is it?" says I. "I thought Thanksgiving came according to
where you happened to live."

That was true in those days, or I thought it
was. One governor would declare it one week and another
governor the next, and a sailor outside soundings could miss
three Thanksgivings and strike Christmas twice, according to the newspapers
aboard.

Warriner said the day had been officially designated and was
consecrated by custom.

"Well," says I, "the weather may observe it, but I
doubt the cook will."

He gave me a reproachful look.

"Thanksgiving," says he, "is not merely a date. It is
an institution. It is the annual expression of a people's
gratitude for civil and religious liberty, domestic prosperity, agricultural abundance,
and the beneficence of Providence."

"Providence has not been liberal with the potatoes," says I.
"There's half a sack left."

He waved that aside. "Even at sea, among men temporarily
withdrawn from the settled influences of home, the occasion should
not pass wholly unnoticed."

Just then Simmons, who was aloft greasing the foretopmast, dropped
the swab, and it struck Warriner's hat with a noise
like a mud pie hitting a church door. That interfered
with the settled influences of home for a minute or
so; but after Simmons had apologized from above, Warriner picked
up his hat, inspected the grease, and went aft to
speak to the captain.

Pratt listened with one eye shut.

"Thanksgiving dinner?" says he. "All right. So long as it
don't cost me anything."

"I propose," says Warriner, "to provide the distinctive feature myself."

"If it's turkey, you'll have to provide the turkey. The
only live bird aboard is Cornwallis's rooster, and he says
that animal is descended from General Washington's eagle."

Cornwallis kept a lean red rooster in a slatted box
beside the galley. He had bought it in Pensacola, meaning,
so he said, to fatten it and sell it in
Vera Cruz. As the rooster got less fat every day,
we figured Cornwallis's mercantile scheme was proceeding in reverse.

"No," says Warriner. "A plum pudding."

Captain Pratt opened the other eye.

"What kind of pudding?"

"A traditional English plum pudding—rich, substantial, and emblematic of the
season."

"I never knew a pudding that stood for anything except
itself," says Pratt. "But go ahead. If Jones can make
it without blowing up the galley, he can make it."

That last remark ought to have warned us.

Warriner went directly to Cornwallis, who was peeling onions with
a knife in one hand and wiping his eyes with
the other. I was near enough to hear, having business
with a coffee cup.

"Jones," says Warriner, "are you competent to prepare a plum
pudding?"

Cornwallis drew himself up. "Mister Warriner, I is competent to
prepare anything eatable, and some things that ain't. I done
cooked puddin' for senators, governors, captains, colonels, bishops, and one
genuine Egyptian prince."

"Where was that?"

"Mobile."

Warriner accepted Mobile as explaining the Egyptian prince.

"This must be a proper pudding," says he. "Not a
common boiled composition, but one made in the English manner."

"I knows the English manner. My gran'father was English."

"Your grandfather?" I says. "I thought he was Lord Cornwallis."

"That's the one," says Cornwallis, not turning a hair. "He
was powerful English."

Warriner produced a folded paper. "Here is the recipe."

Cornwallis took it upside down and studied it.

"Eggs," says Warriner, "flour, breadcrumbs, suet, brown sugar, raisins, currants,
citron, candied peel, nutmeg, cinnamon, cloves, and other ingredients specified
there."

"All present," says Cornwallis.

"You haven't got currants," says I.

He looked injured. "Ain't raisins currants when they small?"

"You have no citron either."

"Citron is a manner of speaking."

"Nor suet."

At this he lost patience. "Mister Mate, if you aims
to cook this puddin', you better wear the apron. If
I cooks it, then suet is what I says suet
is."

I left him to it. A cook's galley is his
castle, and a sensible mate does not attack a fortified
position through the coffee steam.

Warriner had one more item. From beneath his coat he
drew a squat bottle wrapped in brown paper.

"The final preparation is essential," he says. "The pudding, when
brought to table, is to be decorated with holly, saturated
with brandy, and ignited."

Cornwallis's eyes fixed on the bottle. You could nearly hear
them click.

"Ignited?" says he.

"Set on fire."

"Yes, sir. I knows ignited. But why set good vittles
afire after they cooked?"

"For the effect."

Cornwallis looked doubtful. "Fire got a powerful effect on most
things."

"The spirit burns with a blue flame and imparts a
delicate flavor. You will use only a portion in the
pudding itself. The rest must be retained for the ceremonial
serving."

"Ceremonial," says Cornwallis reverently.

He took the bottle.

I ought to mention that this transfer happened on Tuesday,
two days before the dinner. Why Warriner trusted a quart
of brandy to Cornwallis forty-eight hours ahead of its proper
employment I cannot say. Some men can discuss continental resources
by the hour and yet cannot see a cook's hand
reaching for a cork.

Prince saw it, though. He was coiling down a halliard
just outside the galley.

"Better keep that bottle yourself, Mr. Warriner," says he.

"My good man," Warriner replies, "one must repose confidence in
those charged with responsibility."

Prince nodded. "Yes, sir. But you don't have to repose
the bottle with it."

Warriner smiled in a forgiving way and told Cornwallis to
guard it carefully.

"I guards it with my life," says Cornwallis, clasping it
to his apron.

"Guard it from yourself," says Prince.

Cornwallis snorted and disappeared into the galley.

For the next two days he talked of nothing but
that pudding. He hinted at secret ingredients. He chased Simmons
away from the galley door with a ladle. He borrowed
a canvas pudding bag from the steward's stores, though what
we had ever carried it for I do not know.
He chopped, grated, pounded, mixed, sampled, and sang hymns in
a voice like an anchor cable running through a rusty
hawse-pipe.

At intervals there came smells from the galley—some pleasant, some
doubtful, and one that drove the rooster into a fit
of crowing.

On Wednesday afternoon Prince says to me, "That brandy's gone."

"Maybe some is."

"All of it."

"How do you know?"

"I've seen Cornwallis sober, and I've seen him with religion.
Right now he's got both together, which means he's been
drinking."

I looked through the galley door. Cornwallis was leaning over
a pot, singing that he was bound for the Promised
Land, though judging by his steering he would not reach
it on the first tack.

"Warriner will skin him," says I.

"Mr. Warriner couldn't skin a boiled onion," says Prince. "But
the captain might."

Captain Pratt had already been making too free with his
own bottle, so we did not report our suspicions. There
is no benefit in telling one drinking man that another
drinking man has drunk a third man's drink. It only
creates thirsty discussion.

Thanksgiving morning came in fine and clear, with a moderate
breeze out of the northeast, long blue seas under us,
and flying-fish scattering from the schooner's bows like silver shavings.
Warriner appeared in his black coat and clean collar. Captain
Pratt shaved one side of his face, cut the other,
and decided symmetry was not worth bleeding for. Prince had
the decks washed down before eight bells, and even Cornwallis
was quiet.

Too quiet, as I now remember.

Warriner had planned the dinner for two o'clock, so both
watches might have some share in it. We were not
to have turkey, but Cornwallis roasted a leg of pork
and produced sweet potatoes, preserved apples, salt beef, pickles, biscuits,
and a gravy that would have caulked a deck seam.
The captain contributed two bottles of wine from his private
locker after first holding them to the light and grieving
over them. Warriner made a speech about gratitude. Simmons said
amen in the wrong place. The schooner gave an easy
roll, and a pickle slid off the table into Pratt's
lap.

Still, it was a good dinner.

The men of the watch below ate forward, but the
pudding ceremony was to include all hands. Warriner insisted on
it. He said a festival drew its significance from communal
participation; and as Cornwallis had locked the galley after dinner,
there was nothing else to participate in.

At last the cook appeared in the companionway carrying a
great oval dish.

He had arranged the performance with more ceremony than a
launch. He wore his cleanest apron, which looked dirty at
a distance but clean near the bottom, and he held
the dish high before him. Behind him came the cabin
boy with a box of matches.

The pudding was black, round, and heavy-looking. It had the
shape of a cannonball that had begun to melt. Something
stuck out all around its crown in a ragged circle.

Warriner stared.

"What is that decoration?"

Cornwallis beamed. "Holly, sir."

"That is not holly."

"No, sir?"

"It is feathers!"

They were feathers, beyond dispute—red and yellow tail feathers from
Cornwallis's rooster, set upright around the pudding so it resembled
an angry island chief or a Methodist hat after a
hurricane.

"Holly has green leaves and red berries," says Warriner.

"Ain't none aboard."

"Then why did you use feathers?"

"Feathers is festive."

"Festive!"

"Yes, sir. Look at a peacock."

Captain Pratt, who had already consumed his share of the
wine and was reaching into future shares, said the feathers
made no difference if the pudding was fit to eat.

Warriner shut his eyes for a moment, perhaps communing with
domestic prosperity. Then he rallied.

"The garnish is irregular," says he, "but the central tradition
remains. Place the dish here, Jones."

Cornwallis set it in the middle of the cabin table.
The men crowded in the doorway and along the companion
ladder. Prince stood aft with his arms folded. I noticed
that he had stationed himself nearest the open skylight.

Warriner examined the pudding again.

"Has it been properly saturated?"

"Yes, sir," says Cornwallis.

"With the brandy I gave you?"

There was the smallest pause.

"With the spirits provided, sir."

Prince caught my eye.

Warriner took up a long spill of paper and lit
it from the cabin lamp.

"Gentlemen," he says, holding the little flame aloft, "we are
separated by many leagues from our homes, but not from
the associations that render home sacred. This pudding, humble though
its surroundings may be—"

"Humph!" says Pratt, for the gravy had begun troubling him.

"—is an emblem of continuity. It links the wandering mariner
with the domestic hearth, the present with the past, and
appetite with gratitude."

"Set her afire before she founders," says Pratt.

Warriner touched the spill to the pudding.

Holy Moses!

That pudding did not burn with any delicate blue flame.
It went off like the eruption of a volcano that
had been saving up since Creation. There was a roar
and a great yellow sheet of fire leaped to the
cabin beams. The rooster feathers flashed, curled, and sailed blazing
through the air. One landed in Pratt's beard, another in
the gravy, and a third went up the sleeve of
the Honorable Samuel's black coat.

All hands parted our braces at once.

I went over backward across the settee. Simmons tried to
climb the companionway through two larger men and got wedged
like a bung in a barrel. Captain Pratt slapped his
beard, swore at the Government, and knocked the wine over.
Warriner spun around beating his sleeve while the cabin boy
threw a basin of water at him and hit the
captain instead.

The pudding kept burning.

It burned like a tar barrel. Flames ran over it,
under it, and around it. The dish cracked with a
sound like a pistol shot. A river of blazing liquid
crawled across the table toward the captain's papers.

Prince moved then. He seized the folded tablecloth, flung it
over the pudding, caught the whole smoking bundle in his
hands, and pitched it onto the deck. I got a
bucket from the scuttle-butt and poured water over it.

That made things worse for a second. The fire spread
over the water in bright, greasy tongues.

"Kerosene!" shouts Prince.

"Kerosene!" says I.

"Kerosene?" says Warriner, whose dignity had been badly singed but
not destroyed.

Prince smothered it with a second cloth, and this time
the flames died, leaving the cabin full of black smoke
and a smell fit to knock over a lighthouse with
a fish-hook. We opened the skylight and companion doors. Men
coughed, cursed, laughed, and stepped on each other. The rooster,
suddenly deprived of most of his ornament, crowed from the
galley as if the whole entertainment had been arranged in
his honor.

Captain Pratt stood dripping beside the table, half his beard
frizzled and a piece of raisin plastered to his forehead.

He looked at Cornwallis.

Cornwallis looked at the cabin boy.

The cabin boy looked at the pudding.

The pudding looked like h—ll with the fires out.

"What," says Captain Pratt very softly, "did you pour on
that thing?"

Cornwallis clasped his hands over his apron.

"Brandy, captain."

Prince bent down, touched a finger to the wet deck,
smelled it, and wiped his hand on Cornwallis's apron.

"Kerosene," says he.

There was a silence.

I have seen silences aboard ship that were calm and
pleasant, and silences before a squall, and silences when the
mate finds the man at the wheel has been steering
by the wrong star. This silence was the kind that
tells one party to begin running while he still owns
legs.

Cornwallis did not run. There was nowhere to go but
the foretop, and Prince could have pulled him down by
looking at him.

Captain Pratt's face went from red to purple.

"You infernal, lop-eared, galley-haunting son of a scorched frying-pan!" he
says. "Did you try to burn my ship?"

"No, sir!"

"Did you soak that pudding in kerosene?"

Cornwallis swallowed.

"It got kerosene tendencies."

"Tendencies!"

"Yes, sir. Some vittles naturally takes after lamp oil."

Warriner stepped forward. His right cuff was burned away, his
coat smelled like a refinery, and one whisker was shorter
than the other, but he spoke as if addressing the
Senate.

"Jones, I entrusted to you a bottle of high-quality French
brandy."

"You did, sir."

"Where is it?"

"In the pudding."

"The pudding contained kerosene."

Cornwallis looked at the black remains with surprise. "Then that
brandy must have altered powerful."

Captain Pratt made a noise deep in his throat.

Prince leaned toward me and says quietly, "If the captain
hits him now, we'll never get the truth. Let me
try him."

"How?"

"There's more than one way to sound a cook."

Pratt had begun rolling up his sleeves.

"Captain," says Prince, "before you discipline him, we'd better proceed
according to maritime law."

Pratt stopped. Captain Pratt had respect for maritime law whenever
it promised to hurt somebody else.

"What law?"

"The law concerning theft of ardent spirits at sea."

"There's a law for it?"

Prince gave him his navy look. "Very strict law, sir."

I knew Prince was inventing, because there is no sea
law that deals with theft of liquor separately. If there
were, no vessel could carry a full crew past Sandy
Hook. But Pratt swallowed the statement like an albacore does
a flying-fish.

"What's the penalty?"

Prince glanced at Cornwallis, who had gone gray around the
lips.

"We'll have to consult the Scripture."

"Scripture?" says Warriner.

"Old sea custom, sir. Captain keeps the ship's Bible in
his cabin."

Pratt did not own a Bible, and Prince knew it.
The nearest thing was Bowditch's *American Practical Navigator*, a thick
book with tables, rules, and enough figures to frighten any
honest man.

Pratt understood the wink I gave him, though he took
some time about it.

"Yes," says he. "The Bible. Bring it out."

I fetched Bowditch from the shelf and handed it to
Prince. The cover was worn, the pages yellow, and it
looked solemn enough to convict a bishop. Prince laid it
on the table, first sweeping away a burnt feather and
two currants.

Cornwallis watched him with his eyes bulging.

"Now," says Prince, opening the book near the section on
lunar distances, "this here offense falls under the Book of
Nebuchadnezzar, chapter eight, 'Concerning Cooks, Stewards, and Such Persons as
Misappropriate Strong Waters.'"

Warriner coughed into his hand.

Captain Pratt bit his lower lip.

I turned toward the skylight, because if Cornwallis saw my
face the trial was over.

Prince ran his finger down a column of logarithms.

"Here it is. 'If any man, being cook aboard a
vessel over ten tons burden and under three hundred, shall
drink, conceal, embezzle, convert, swallow, sip, taste, or otherwise remove
from lawful service any rum, brandy, gin, whisky, cordial, wine,
shrub, or spirituous medicine entrusted to him for culinary purpose—'"

Cornwallis lifted one hand.

"Does it say brandy particular?"

"It says brandy in capital letters," Prince replies.

"Oh, Lord!"

"'—then shall that man be examined before the master, mate,
bo's'un, passenger, and such other witnesses as have not themselves
drunk the evidence.'"

Captain Pratt cleared his throat and stepped a little farther
from the wine bottles.

Prince continued: "'And if the accused shall deny the offense,
a grindstone shall be made fast about his neck with
a new manila line, not less than three-inch circumference—'"

"New line?" I says, entering into it. "That's wasteful."

"Scripture is Scripture," says Prince.

"Go on," Pratt ordered.

"'—and he shall be cast over the lee bulwarks in
water exceeding six fathoms in depth. If innocent, he will
float, the same being the sign appointed for innocence. If
guilty, he will sink, the same being proof of guilt.'"

Cornwallis's knees parted company.

"A man with a grindstone ain't going to float!" he
cried.

Prince frowned at him. "You disputing the Bible?"

"No, sir, but I knows grindstones."

"Then you understand the seriousness."

Warriner turned away abruptly and appeared interested in the barometer.
Simmons made a choking sound at the companion door, and
I kicked him on the shin.

Prince read on: "'And after sinking, the offender shall remain
below until three bells of the next watch, unless he
sooner confesses.'"

"How he going to confess under water?" Cornwallis demanded.

"That," says Prince, "is his lookout."

Captain Pratt now entered into the spirit of it.

"Mate, what's the depth here?"

"Thirty-eight fathoms by the last cast."

"Plenty. Have a grindstone brought aft."

We had a grindstone lashed near the foremast. It was
not much of a grindstone, but Cornwallis had seen it
often enough to know its weight.

"Simmons," I called, "rouse it out."

Simmons vanished with unnecessary enthusiasm.

Cornwallis looked from Prince to Pratt, from Pratt to Warriner,
and from Warriner to me. He found no mercy, though
he might have found several men close to bursting.

"Mister Prince," he says, "read a little farther. Maybe there's
an amendment."

Prince turned the page. "Amendment says two grindstones in case
the accused has previously lied about his grandfather."

Cornwallis clapped both hands to his head.

"That ain't fair! A man's family history is private!"

"Bring him up," says Pratt.

At that moment Simmons and two others came aft rolling
the grindstone along the deck. It bumped over the seams
with a dreadful, judicial sound. They had already tied a
length of rope through the center. Simmons, who could be
quick when wickedness encouraged him, had even formed a noose
at the other end.

Cornwallis backed against the cabin bulkhead.

"Captain," says he, "I appeals."

"To who?"

"The Government."

"The Government has been after me for years," says Pratt.
"It won't help you."

"I appeals to Mister Warriner."

Warriner turned, composing his face.

"The law must take its course."

"I appeals to the rooster."

The rooster crowed once from the galley.

"Unfavorable," says Prince.

They rolled the grindstone nearer.

Cornwallis raised both hands.

"Hold on! I got a statement."

Prince shut Bowditch but kept one finger marking the place.
"Statement before or after immersion?"

"Before! Long before."

"Proceed."

Cornwallis drew a breath.

"That brandy was drank."

"By who?" says Pratt.

Cornwallis looked upward.

"The devil."

Nobody spoke.

"The devil," he repeated, gaining confidence, "come into the galley
Tuesday night, about two bells, and drank Mister Warriner's brandy."

Prince opened Bowditch again. "Devil ain't listed as a member
of the crew."

"He was a visitor."

"Did you invite him?"

"No, sir. Devil don't need inviting. That's his worst feature."

"What did he look like?" Warriner asked, for he was
beginning to enjoy the inquiry.

Cornwallis considered. "Short."

"How short?"

"About my height."

"Color?"

"It was dark."

"How was he dressed?"

"In my shirt."

Captain Pratt banged his fist on the table. "You drank
it!"

"No, sir. The devil drank it through me."

This distinction seemed important to Cornwallis.

He went on rapidly, with one eye on the grindstone.

"I had put that bottle behind the flour barrel, just
where Mister Warriner told me to preserve it—which he never
told me no such thing, but it was a natural
place. Then I heard a voice say, 'Cornwallis!' I turns
around, and ain't nobody there. Voice says, 'Cornwallis, take one
little taste, because you don't know if that brandy fit
for the Honorable Samuel T. Warriner.' I says, 'Get behind
me, Satan!' Then the voice says, 'I is behind you
already, and the corkscrew is in the second drawer.'"

Prince's shoulders began to shake, but his face stayed solemn.

"I took one little taste for safety," Cornwallis continued. "It
was fit. Then the devil says, 'Maybe it ain't fit
all the way down the bottle.' I says, 'Away with
you!' He says, 'Just one more.' After that he put
the bottle to my mouth."

"With your hand?" I asks.

"He used the hand convenient."

"Yours?"

"At that time he had possession of it."

"How long did this possession continue?" Warriner inquired.

"Till the bottle was empty."

"And then?"

"Then he departed."

"Leaving you drunk," says Pratt.

"Leaving me afflicted."

Captain Pratt picked up the rope end.

"And the kerosene?"

Cornwallis's expression became almost noble.

"That was where I tried to save Thanksgiving."

"Save it!"

"Yes, sir. Wednesday morning I wakes up and finds the
brandy gone by supernatural agency. I knows Mister Warriner expects
the pudding to ignite. I knows all hands expects the
blue flame. I says to myself, 'Cornwallis, you has been
visited by trouble, but you got responsibilities.' There wasn't no
more brandy. Captain had his cabin locked."

At this Pratt unconsciously felt for his key.

"So," Cornwallis went on, "I studies what else burns. Pork
grease burns, but smells common. Turpentine burns, but tastes sharp.
Kerosene burns beautiful. I only used enough for ceremony."

"You soaked it!" says Prince.

"The pudding was thirsty."

"And you expected us to eat it?"

"After the fire burned the kerosene off."

There it was. The complete case, confession, excuse, theology, and
culinary principle all in one. I have heard lawyers take
two days to make less sense.

For half a minute nobody moved. Then Simmons let out
a squeak at the doorway. I started laughing. Prince dropped
his head on Bowditch. Warriner's solemn face cracked, and even
Captain Pratt, with his burned beard and wet trousers, gave
a roar that shook the cabin lamp.

All hands followed.

They laughed on deck, in the companionway, and forward by
the windlass, though most of them had heard only pieces.
A sailor does not need the whole joke if everybody
else is laughing; he takes his bearings from the noise
and comes in under full sail. Simmons sat down on
the grindstone. The cabin boy leaned against the bulkhead and
wept. Prince tried to read the punishment again, but got
no farther than "three-inch circumference."

Cornwallis watched us suspiciously.

"So there ain't no such Scripture?" he says at last.

Prince held up Bowditch. "Every word printed right here."

Cornwallis peered at the columns. He could recognize figures, if
not much else.

"The Bible got an uncommon amount of arithmetic."

"That's the wages of sin," says I.

His suspicion increased, but the danger had passed. Captain Pratt
told him he was a condemned nuisance, docked him the
price of the brandy, and ordered him to clean the
cabin, scrape the pudding off the table, and never again
attempt to save a holiday.

Warriner rescued what remained of his coat and contemplated the
pudding.

It lay on the wet cloth, a dark, sodden, split
mass, studded with half-burned raisins and giving off a thin
smoke. One rooster feather still stood upright in the top.
No man could have looked at it and thought of
home, unless his home had lately burned down.

"I cannot permit it to end like this," Warriner says.

Captain Pratt wiped his eyes. "You thinking of eating it?"

"Certainly not."

"Then let it end."

"No," says Warriner. "It has played too memorable a part
in our observance. It deserves formal disposition."

He lifted the pudding, cloth and all, and carried it
on deck.

"What now?" I asks.

"A burial at sea."

That set the men laughing afresh, but Warriner remained grave.
He ordered all hands aft, and Pratt—who would have agreed
to a coronation by then—gave permission. Prince rigged a small
board as a bier. The ruined pudding was laid upon
it. Someone placed the scorched feather across the top. Cornwallis
was made to stand at the head as chief mourner,
though he said he had galley work.

The schooner moved steadily southward, her sails drawing pale against
the afternoon sky. The sea was deep blue, with white
caps here and there, and the sun laid a bright
road westward. It was foolish, of course, but there are
moments aboard ship when foolishness comes nearer to being necessary
than sense. We had been away from shore, we had
eaten too much, nearly burned the cabin, frightened a cook
with a navigation book, and laughed until our ribs hurt.
A ceremony was the natural next disaster.

Warriner stood beside the lee bulwarks. His black coat was
scorched at the cuff, his collar had wilted, and there
was soot across his cheek. Yet when he began speaking,
he might have been dedicating a monument.

"Shipmates," says he, "we are assembled to consign to the
keeping of the deep the mortal remains of what was
intended to be a plum pudding."

Captain Pratt bowed his head. Prince nudged Simmons, who removed
his cap.

"Conceived in generosity," Warriner continued, "compounded in ignorance, decorated in
misapprehension, and consumed by an element for which it possessed
an unnatural affinity, it has completed a brief but extraordinary
career."

Cornwallis muttered that the recipe was bad to begin with.

"Silence in the mourners," says Prince.

"It came among us," says Warriner, "as a symbol of
domestic fellowship. It leaves us as a warning against misplaced
confidence, unauthorized substitution, and the indiscriminate use of petroleum."

"That's Scripture," Pratt whispered.

"It united all ranks in expectation, separated them with remarkable
speed at the moment of ignition, and reunited them in
laughter. Though denied the honorable destiny of being eaten, it
has not existed in vain."

This was true. Few puddings have done more.

Warriner put one hand upon the board.

"Farewell, then, dark and sodden mass! Thou wert heavy in
the dish, dangerous in the flame, and impossible in the
mouth. No holly crowned thee, but the feather of a
rooster whose confidence survives the loss. No delicate blue radiance
attended thy final hour, but a conflagration visible, I have
no doubt, to vessels beyond the horizon. The ocean receives
thee. May no fish be injured."

"Amen," says Simmons.

The board was tilted.

The pudding hesitated at the edge, as if it had
reconsidered Vera Cruz, then slid over the lee bulwarks and
struck the water with a heavy splash. It floated for
perhaps three seconds, black and round amid the foam. Then
it turned once, showing the last red feather, and sank
stern first—or pudding first, there being no stern to it.

Warriner raised his hand.

Prince had fetched the hand foghorn from its locker. At
the signal he blew one long, mournful blast.

The note went out over the Gulf like a sick
bull calling through a drainpipe.

At once the rooster answered from the galley.

That finished what dignity remained. All hands broke up laughing,
Captain Pratt went below to dry his trousers, the watch
returned to the braces, and Cornwallis hurried forward to see
whether the devil had meddled with supper.

By sunset the cabin was scrubbed, though it smelled of
kerosene for the rest of the passage. Warriner and Prince
became good friends after that. The Honorable Samuel said Prince
possessed a remarkable gift for forensic invention, and Prince replied
that a navigation book was as useful as a Bible
if the other fellow could not read either one.

As for Cornwallis, he never admitted that Bowditch was not
Scripture. He only said some Bibles were written more nautical
than others. He also claimed the pudding would have been
excellent if we had let it burn another minute.

Perhaps it would.

I have eaten worse.

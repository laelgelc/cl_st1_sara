On 17 October 1914, at half past six in the
morning, Frau Ebermann discovered that she had influenza.

The diagnosis was her own, but it proved correct. There
were pains in the joints, a rawness beneath the breastbone,
alternating chills and heat, and an unpleasant enlargement of the
head. Her tongue, examined in the oval mirror above the
washstand, was heavily coated. By seven Anna had been sent
to the chemist for formamint, ammoniated quinine, eucalyptus pastilles, and
a fresh bottle of cologne, the old bottle being nearly
half empty and therefore unsuitable for an illness of uncertain
duration.

Before returning to bed, Frau Ebermann inspected the room.

The curtains were drawn equally, the tassels hanging at the
same height. The hairbrushes lay parallel on the imitation-marble washstand,
their ivory-coloured backs upward. The blue enamel basin had been
emptied. The framed portrait of the Kaiser stood between two
bronze-painted vases, and the morning newspaper, folded so that the
report of another victory could be read from the pillow,
rested at a right angle to the edge of the
bedside table.

Only the lace cover on the radiator was crooked.

It was not true lace, which would have scorched, but
a white machine-made article of an open-work pattern, bought at
Wertheim's and particularly admired by Frau Ebermann because it gave
the iron pipes a furnished appearance. One corner hung lower
than the other.

"Anna!"

Anna came in with the hot-water bottle beneath her arm.

"Straighten that cover."

"Yes, madam."

"No. The other way. Now you have pulled it too
far."

Anna adjusted it again.

"There. You see? Equal on both sides. It is not
difficult."

"No, madam."

"And put the bottle at my feet, not across them.
What use is a hot-water bottle across the feet?"

"None, madam."

Frau Ebermann got into bed. She was a stout woman
of fifty-three, with a capable forehead and small, pale eyes.
Her husband, a supplier of buckles and metal fittings to
the army, had gone to Leipzig on business. She was
not anxious. He had telegraphed that contracts were satisfactory, trains
overcrowded, spirits excellent. Everything, both private and national, was proceeding
as it ought.

She swallowed two quinine tablets and unfolded the newspaper.

Anna had neglected to shake the pillows evenly.

"Anna!"

But Anna was already in the passage, where, believing the
door shut, she said something which sounded like "old parade-ground."

"What did you say?"

"I said the sheets are aired, madam."

Frau Ebermann accepted this. It was important not to suspect
servants unjustly in wartime. They were excitable and often had
cousins in uniform.

By nine the fever had risen. The room acquired a
glassy brightness. The flowers on the wallpaper—maroon chrysanthemums joined by
grey-green stems—seemed to detach themselves and float a finger's breadth
from the wall. Her hands, lying on the counterpane, became
enormous, soft objects, without bones or skin, two pillows stuffed
with nothing. When she attempted to lift one, the radiator
moved away from her toward a pale, slanting horizon.

She closed her eyes.

When she opened them, a child was sitting on the
radiator.

He was perhaps seven years old. His legs were bare
below a pair of torn brown breeches, and his feet
were grey with dried mud. A scratch crossed his cheek.
He had removed the lace cover and folded it upon
his knees.

"You must not sit there," said Frau Ebermann.

The child looked at her.

"It is hot," she added. "And you have disturbed the
cover."

He slid down obediently. The radiator gave a small metallic
knock, as though relieved of his weight.

"How did you come in?"

"Through the door."

"That is impossible. Anna is in the kitchen."

The child considered this without embarrassment.

"She did not see me."

Frau Ebermann reached for the bell. Her hand travelled an
immense distance, grew small, and vanished among the folds of
the coverlet. At last she found the handle and rang.

Anna appeared almost immediately.

"There is a boy in the room."

Anna looked toward the radiator.

"Where, madam?"

"Beside you. Do not pretend stupidity. A dirty little boy
in brown breeches."

Anna's face assumed the careful vacancy of a servant hearing
something improper.

"There is nobody here, madam."

The boy stood so close to her that his sleeve
touched her apron.

"Send him away."

"Yes, madam."

Anna opened the door, made a vague shooing movement toward
the empty carpet, and closed it again.

"There, madam. He has gone."

"He has not. He is now by the wardrobe."

Anna glanced at the thermometer on the bedside table.

"I'll make some lime tea."

"Do so. And tell him not to touch anything."

When Anna had gone, the child put one finger upon
the polished wardrobe door. He drew it down through the
dustless shine, leaving no mark.

"You see," Frau Ebermann said, "that is exactly what I
mean."

"I wanted to see the horses."

"What horses?"

"The ones in Berlin."

"There are horses everywhere. You need not enter private apartments
to see them."

"Our horses are dead."

This was spoken simply, without complaint. Frau Ebermann disliked the
way children announced disagreeable facts, as if grown people had
no arrangements by which such facts might be corrected.

"Then you must go home."

"There isn't any home."

"Nonsense."

"It was knocked down. Then it burned."

He raised both hands, describing neither knocking down nor burning,
but merely showing how empty they were.

Frau Ebermann turned to the newspaper. The official report stated
that operations in the east continued favourably and that enemy
assertions concerning damage to civilian property were malicious inventions. This
was printed in heavy type. Heavy type was not employed
without reason.

"What is your village called?"

The child told her. The name was foreign and had
too many consonants.

"I have never heard of it."

"It was small."

"Then how can you expect the army to know it
was there? One must be reasonable."

The boy went to the door.

"Do not forget the cover," Frau Ebermann said.

He returned, placed the imitation lace on the radiator, and
pulled it almost, though not perfectly, straight.

Then he was gone.

At eleven Anna brought broth in a cup decorated with
pink roses. Frau Ebermann said nothing about the boy. Fever
was known to produce optical disturbances. She had read of
a gentleman who, after eating bad oysters, saw a green
dog in his umbrella stand. No one had concluded that
the dog existed.

She corrected the radiator cover herself and drank the broth.

At noon the street below filled with cheering. A column
of reserve troops was passing, their boots striking the pavement
in a rhythm which trembled through the water glass. Frau
Ebermann struggled upright. Through the gap in the curtains she
saw helmets, flags, red faces, and women throwing late flowers.
Splendid boys! Straight backs, polished buttons, every man in place.
Beyond them, above the roofs, the October sky was white
and clean.

"Another victory," she said.

Five children answered, "Yes."

They stood at the foot of the bed.

The boy in brown breeches had returned. With him were
two girls and two smaller boys. They were crowded close
together, though there was ample space. One girl had a
yellow ribbon tied to a few strands of hair; the
rest of her hair appeared to have been cut off
unevenly with a knife. The other wore a man's woollen
waistcoat over a scorched dress. One of the little boys
clutched a wooden wheel. The smallest had no coat and
kept his hands pressed beneath his armpits.

All five were dirty. Dirt lay not only upon their
clothes and faces but seemed to issue from them in
a faint, dry mist, settling invisibly upon the plush chair,
the flowered carpet, the lacquered table.

"You cannot remain here," said Frau Ebermann.

"We are waiting," said the girl with the ribbon.

"For whom?"

"Our people."

"What people?"

"Our mothers and fathers. And her grandmother. And his sisters."

She indicated the others in turn, like a pupil reciting
a lesson.

"They will come for us when they are finished being
dead," said the child with the wooden wheel.

"Hush," said the older girl.

"But that is what you said."

"You have misunderstood," Frau Ebermann told him. "Dead people do
not come for anybody."

The children looked at one another. This information appeared to
trouble them less than might have been expected.

"Then we shall wait longer," said the boy in brown
breeches.

"Not here."

"We have waited in other houses."

"In Berlin?"

"Yes."

"How many of you are there?"

The older girl began counting on her fingers. At ten
she stopped and looked toward the boy with the wheel.

"Hundreds," he suggested.

"Hundreds and hundreds," said the smallest.

"Thousands and thousands," said the girl with the ribbon.

Frau Ebermann laughed, but the laugh caught against the soreness
in her chest.

"That is absurd. If thousands of children had been killed,
it would have been reported. One cannot conceal thousands. Even
hundreds would be difficult."

"We are not concealed," said the boy in breeches. "We
are here."

"You are five."

"In this room."

"There! Precisely. Five. And I am ill."

The matter, once stated in figures, was much improved.

The girl in the scorched dress climbed onto the plush
chair.

"Get down at once. Your shoes—"

One shoe was missing. The foot beneath the dress was
wrapped in blackened cloth.

"Get down nevertheless. Furniture is not for standing on."

The girl descended. She did so politely, supporting herself with
one hand upon the chair back. Where her fingers had
rested, five dark marks appeared in the plush.

Frau Ebermann rang the bell.

Anna came with the lime tea.

"They have returned," Frau Ebermann said.

"Who, madam?"

"The children. There are five now."

Anna set down the tray and glanced about the room.
Her eyes passed over the group without pausing.

"Perhaps madam should sleep."

"Ask that girl why she is wearing a man's waistcoat."

Anna's mouth tightened.

"Which girl?"

"The one beside you."

The girl with the ribbon leaned toward Anna and whispered
something into her ear. Anna scratched the ear impatiently.

"Well?" Frau Ebermann demanded.

"The doctor is coming at three," said Anna.

"What did she tell you?"

"No one told me anything."

"She says it belonged to her father," Frau Ebermann said,
though the girl had made no sound that she could
hear. "They found it in a ditch. Really, these details
are unnecessary."

Anna pulled the counterpane up to her mistress's chin.

"You're burning, madam."

"And you are careless. There are children wandering through the
apartment."

"I'll lock the door."

"Then they will be locked in."

Anna went out muttering. This time Frau Ebermann distinctly heard
the word "crazy," but lacked the strength to call her
back.

The five children arranged themselves along the wall beneath the
chrysanthemums. Their stillness was objectionable. Ordinary children fidgeted, quarrelled, asked
for cake, and knocked over ornaments. These waited.

"Where did you come from?" Frau Ebermann asked at last.

They named villages one after another. The names meant nothing
to her.

"And what happened?"

"Soldiers came," said the older girl.

"Whose soldiers?"

"German soldiers."

"No."

"They had pointed helmets," said the child with the wheel,
delighted to remember. "And horses. Big ones."

"You must not accuse people merely because of their helmets."

"They put Grandfather by the well," said the girl with
the ribbon. "They said he knew where the other men
were. He did not know. Then the officer wiped his
spectacles before they shot him."

The fever contracted the room. The wardrobe and washstand leaned
inward; the ceiling sagged, warm and yellow, just above Frau
Ebermann's forehead.

"There are accidents in war," she said. "Children run beneath
horses. They play near guns after being warned. They pick
up cartridges. Afterwards the enemy makes stories."

"We did not make a story," said the boy in
breeches.

"You may not know that you made it."

The smallest child had begun shivering. Each shiver travelled through
the room and rattled the spoon in the saucer.

"We had ducks," he said.

"No one asked about ducks."

"Three white and one brown."

"Our roof fell on them," said the older girl.

"Hush."

"I had new breeches," said the first boy. "For Sunday."

"You are wearing breeches."

"Not the new ones."

"My ribbon was blue," said the girl. She touched the
yellow rag in her hair. "Mama said blue was better."

Frau Ebermann gripped the sheet. They were attempting to confuse
her with trifles. Ducks, ribbons, breeches—things too small to be
contradicted and therefore particularly dangerous. Official reports dealt properly with
divisions, positions, losses, and advances. They did not stoop to
poultry.

"You are untidy children," she said. "That is what you
are."

The boy with the wheel turned it slowly in his
hands.

"We were tidier before."

The doctor arrived at ten minutes past three. He was
a narrow young man with a reddish moustache and the
harried manner of one whose patients considered their illnesses acts
of patriotism. He listened to Frau Ebermann's lungs, counted her
pulse, and examined her tongue.

"Influenza," he said.

"I told Anna so at half past six."

"Rest, fluids, quinine if it agrees with you."

"There are five children in the room."

"Indeed?"

"Do not humour me. They are beneath the window."

He looked beneath the window.

"Refugees, they claim. From the east. They say our soldiers
destroyed their villages."

"Fever sometimes produces vivid images."

"I know that. A green dog, for example."

"Exactly."

"But why foreign children? I do not even like foreign
children."

The doctor closed his bag.

"Keep her quiet," he told Anna. "If the temperature rises
further, send for me."

When he had gone, Frau Ebermann experienced a brief and
satisfying clarity. The room resumed its proper dimensions. The children
faded until the pattern of the wallpaper could be seen
through their clothes. She smiled.

"You see? Images."

The girl in the scorched dress lifted the hem above
her knee.

The leg ended there.

There was no theatrical gush of blood, no cry. The
cloth was stuck to the rounded stump in stiff brown
folds. The child with the wheel opened his shirt and
showed a dark hole beneath his ribs. The smallest removed
his hands from his armpits; the fingers were blistered and
split. Behind the yellow ribbon, the side of the girl's
head was crushed inward. Last, the boy in breeches turned
around. Across his back ran the black, parallel marks of
wagon wheels.

Frau Ebermann stared.

The wounds seemed old and fresh at once. Blood began
to fall without movement: one drop from the chair, one
from the wallpaper, one from the brass bedstead. It struck
the carpet with quiet, deliberate taps.

"You should not show such things in a lady's bedroom,"
she whispered.

"We are sorry," said the older girl.

"We thought you did not believe us," said the boy
in breeches.

"There might have been an accident."

"Yes."

"Five accidents."

"Yes."

"Not thousands."

The children were silent.

"Say it. There were only five of you."

"There are only five of us here."

They joined hands. The smallest smiled at her with grave
courtesy.

"We will wait somewhere else," said the girl with the
ribbon.

"For your people?"

"Till they come."

"They cannot come. I explained that."

"Then till God comes."

One by one they passed through the closed door.

At dusk Anna entered with a lamp and found the
bed empty.

Frau Ebermann was on her knees beside the radiator. She
had torn away its lace cover and was scrubbing the
carpet with it. Her nightdress clung damply to her back;
her hair, usually pinned in a hard grey coil, hung
across her face.

"Madam!"

"Bring water."

"What are you doing?"

"It will not come out."

Anna saw no stain. The carpet's green and crimson medallions
were rubbed pale where Frau Ebermann had worked upon them.

"There's nothing there."

"Five places. Count them."

"You must get back to bed."

"Five," Frau Ebermann repeated. "Only five. The newspaper would have
said if there were more."

She scrubbed harder. The imitation lace tore between her hands.

"Look what you've done," Anna cried. "You'll ruin it."

"Already ruined. Everything touched. The chair, the wall, the floor.
Blood everywhere, and the room not ready."

"Ready for whom?"

Frau Ebermann raised her fever-bright face.

"For God, of course."

A cheer rose from the street. Another troop train was
leaving Berlin, and people ran beside it waving flags until
the platform ended.

Frau Ebermann bent again to the carpet.

"He must find everything in order," she said. "Everything straight.
Everything clean."

Anna tried to lift her, but she clutched the radiator
pipe with one hand and scrubbed with the other. The
torn lace darkened in her fist, though whether from dirt,
sweat, or something Anna could not see, Anna was unable
afterward to say.

"Five," Frau Ebermann whispered. "There could only have been five."

Then, listening toward the passage, she stopped.

Far beyond the locked door, beyond the swept stairs and
the polished vestibule, beyond the cheering streets, came the soft,
patient sound of children waiting.

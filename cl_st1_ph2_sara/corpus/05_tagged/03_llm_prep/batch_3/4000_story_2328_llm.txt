### THE SHARPER'S DIVIDEND

Steelman and Smith had been three days in the city,
in the dress and character of two bushmen with money
to blue and no experience in bluing it properly.

Their clothes were good enough for the country and bad
enough for town. Smith wore a new suit that appeared
to have been made for a larger man in a
different temper, and Steelman had a wide grey hat, a
red neckerchief, and boots whose squeak suggested prosperity painfully acquired.
They had talked loudly of sheds, cattle-camps, and long cheques;
had asked the waiter whether the theatre was respectable; and
had regarded the electric bells in the hotel with the
grave suspicion of men who expected machinery to go off.

On the fourth afternoon Steelman said, without moving his lips—

"He's behind the paper again."

Smith looked across the balcony. A smooth, dark gentleman sat
two tables off, reading the financial page. He had been
reading it for half an hour and had not once
turned it.

"Who?" asked Smith.

"You know."

"No."

Steelman sighed. It was a sigh of that injured description
commonly employed by a wife who knows perfectly well that
her husband understands, but wishes to make him say so
in order that she may contradict him.

"The bloke that's been tracking us since Tuesday."

"Oh, him."

"Don't look."

"I didn't."

"You did."

"I looked at the harbour."

"He's between you and the harbour."

Smith considered this.

"Well," he said, "I looked through him."

The sun was going down beyond the roofs, leaving streaks
of copper under the clouds and putting a cold shine
on the water. A steamer moved across the harbour, black
against the beautiful death of day. The smooth gentleman lowered
his paper half an inch.

Steelman stretched his legs and spoke in a louder tone.

"It's all very well for you to say so, Smith.
But I'm sick of graft. I don't intend to go
back and work like a bullock for every quid I
get."

Smith filled his pipe.

"You generally let the bullock do most of it."

"That's gratitude! After me bringing you to town and showing
you life."

"You showed me a tram."

"And wasn't that life? Nearly took yours."

Smith struck a match.

Steelman leaned nearer. "The thing is to put your money
into something solid—mines, shares, syndicates, and that sort of stuff.
Let capital work. That's what capital's for."

"I thought it was for keeping."

"That's why you'll never rise. You've got no enterprise. A
man might as well bury his cheque under a stump."

"Safer."

"There you go again—suspicious! You'd suspect your own mother if
she offered you change for a sovereign."

"She never had one."

The financial paper gave a slight rustle.

Steelman frowned at Smith with magnificent impatience.

"We've got close on a hundred and thirty pounds between
us, haven't we?"

"Have we?"

Steelman kicked him under the table.

"I mean—counting what we saved at Grinder's shed, and the
money from the horses."

"Oh. Yes."

"Nearer a hundred and forty, if anything."

Smith rubbed his shin.

"But," he said slowly, "we ain't putting it into anything."

"Why not?"

"Because town chaps are sharp."

"Rot! That's bush ignorance. A gentleman's a gentleman wherever you
meet him."

"Specially in a private bar," said Smith.

Steelman's face reddened. He was a good actor when he
had a responsive company, and a better one when the
company was mostly furniture.

"I'm ashamed of you. You think every man with a
clean collar wants to rob you."

"No. Some have already robbed someone else."

The paper rustled again.

Steelman rose suddenly.

"Come for a walk. I can't argue with you here.
Everybody can hear your low suspicions."

He strode inside. Smith followed at the pace of a
man who is not easily hurried and does not care
who knows it. As they passed the dark gentleman, Steelman
nodded with open-hearted country civility. The gentleman returned the nod,
smiled, and resumed reading the same paragraph.

They went down to the street, walked round the block,
and entered the hotel by the side door.

"You nearly overdid the hundred and forty," Smith said.

"You nearly spoilt the lot with that private-bar remark."

"Thought it helped."

"It did," admitted Steelman. "But don't think. It makes you
look unnatural."

"What next?"

"He'll speak to us to-night. If he doesn't, we'll make
him."

"And if he wants the hundred?"

"He won't get it."

"What will he get?"

"Ten."

Smith stopped.

"Ten quid?"

"Ten quid."

"Ours?"

"Whose else?"

"I was hoping it was his."

"It will be," said Steelman, "before we're finished."

Smith scratched his ear and followed.

The dark gentleman joined them after dinner. It happened in
the natural manner in which such things happen after they
have been carefully arranged by one party and anxiously awaited
by another. Steelman and Smith were sitting in the smoking-room
arguing as to whether a certain racehorse had four white
feet or only three, when the gentleman, passing their table,
observed that he had seen the horse run.

Steelman made room at once.

"That settles it," he said. "A man that's seen him
ought to know."

The gentleman laughed pleasantly. His name, he said, was Crawley.
He had interests in the country—mining interests, principally—and always liked
meeting practical men. Practical men were scarce in the city.

This compliment pleased Steelman so much that he ordered whisky.
Smith looked at Mr Crawley's hands.

"You haven't been mining lately," he said.

Crawley glanced down.

"No—my part is financial."

"Handle the gold after it's washed?"

"In a manner of speaking."

"Cleaner," said Smith.

Steelman kicked him.

Presently Crawley mentioned a reef. He did not force the
matter; your true sharper never forces anything until the victim
is running downhill. The reef was in the west, where
a new lead had been struck. A quiet concern. No
public floating, no market shouting, no crowd of little speculators.
A few gentlemen were taking it up privately. The assays
had been extraordinary.

"What's assays?" asked Smith.

"Tests," said Crawley.

"Why don't they say tests?"

"Scientific people prefer technical terms."

"Do they get more gold that way?"

Steelman broke in hastily.

"You must excuse my mate. He's got a suspicious nature.
He thinks an assay's something that might bite him."

Crawley smiled. He did not dislike Smith's suspicion. Suspicion, in
the countryman, was useful if properly managed: it gave the
sharper something to overcome, and thus made the countryman proud
when overcome.

"I admire caution," he said. "Indeed, I should advise you
not to touch any concern until you have satisfied yourself."

"There!" said Smith. "That's what I say."

"Yes," Steelman replied, with disgust; "and if Noah had listened
to you he'd have waited for a second opinion on
the weather."

Crawley described the mine. He described its position, its capital,
its crushing plant not yet erected, its reef already improving,
and its manager—an honest, rough fellow who cared nothing for
appearances and everything for results. The shares were ten pounds
each. An allotment, taken quietly before the next report, might
double within a week.

Steelman's eyes shone.

"A hundred would fetch two hundred?"

"It might."

"And it mightn't," said Smith.

"Certainly," Crawley said. "There is always risk."

Smith nodded. "That's the first straight thing I've heard about
it."

Steelman turned on him.

"Look here, Smith! We came to town to enjoy ourselves
and see if we couldn't make something. Here's a gentleman
practically putting us on to a good thing out of
friendship, and you sit chewing his lug."

"I ain't putting the hundred in."

"I'll put mine in."

"Then we divide now."

This brought a pause. Crawley studied the ash of his
cigar. Steelman drummed on the table. Somewhere outside, a cab
rattled over the stones.

At last Smith said—

"I'd risk a tenner."

Steelman stared.

"You would?"

"Just to test him."

Crawley lifted his head slightly.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Test the mine," said Smith. "Put ten in. If it
comes back with profit, maybe try more."

Crawley's smile was grave and almost paternal.

"The opportunity may not remain open."

"Then let it shut," said Smith.

Steelman paced the room twice, muttering about cowards, deadheads, and
men who would starve beside a bakery because they distrusted
the baker. Then he stopped.

"All right. Ten pounds. But if it turns out like
Mr Crawley says, we put in the hundred."

Smith hesitated.

Steelman thumped the table.

"Do we, or don't we?"

"We'll see."

"We put it in!"

"We'll see," Smith repeated.

Crawley intervened gently. "That is quite reasonable. Let your friend
be convinced by results."

The ten pounds were handed over next morning in the
private bar. Crawley gave them a receipt bearing the name
of the Golden Horizon Development Syndicate, and Steelman held the
paper upside down for some time before folding it carefully
into his pocketbook.

In that pocketbook, Smith noticed, there was the faded photograph
of a woman with a child on her knee. Steelman
shut the book quickly.

Crawley went out by the front entrance. Steelman and Smith
remained with their beer.

"Well?" said Smith.

"We wait."

"How long?"

"Till he thinks we've got the hundred ready."

"Have we?"

Steelman looked at him.

"No."

"Then he's thinking wrong."

"That," said Steelman, "is where our profit starts."

For two days Crawley was absent. Steelman played the impatient
investor to perfection. He asked three times for telegrams, went
down to the post-office, cursed the mine, defended it, and
told Smith that his miserable doubts had soured the whole
holiday. Smith sat on the balcony counting flies on the
ceiling.

On the third evening Crawley returned.

He looked tired, but triumphant. He carried a small leather
bag and suggested, with becoming caution, that they go upstairs.
In the bedroom he locked the door and drew the
blind, though the window looked only upon a brick wall.

"Gentlemen," he said, "I have good news."

"Knew it!" cried Steelman.

Smith remained seated on the edge of the bed.

"The original allotment," Crawley continued, "was made at a particularly
favourable moment. Certain interests have since come in. I was
able to dispose of your holding at rather more than
double."

He poured sovereigns upon the washstand. They made a rich,
decisive sound—quite unlike promises, prospectuses, and scientific terms.

"Twenty-one pounds," he said. "Your capital and eleven pounds profit."

Smith's eyes moved from the gold to Crawley.

"Quick work."

"In finance," said Crawley, "the opportunity is often brief."

Steelman seized the sovereigns and counted them twice. His hands
trembled very well; indeed, perhaps they trembled better than acting
required.

"Twenty-one! Smith, you old fool—twenty-one! And you wanted to keep
the ten in a sock!"

"Still got the sock," said Smith.

Crawley smiled.

"There is another allotment available. I can secure it until
noon to-morrow. A hundred pounds would—conservatively speaking—return two hundred, perhaps
two hundred and twenty."

Steelman swept the gold into his pocket.

"We'll do it."

Smith said nothing.

"We'll do it," Steelman repeated. "First thing in the morning."

Crawley buttoned his coat. "I shall call at ten."

When he had gone, Steelman listened at the door, opened
it suddenly, looked along the passage, and shut it again.
Then he took the twenty-one sovereigns from his pocket and
laid them in rows upon the table.

Smith watched him.

"You said we'd wait till it became his."

"It has."

"The ten was ours."

"And now the twenty-one is ours."

"Is it?"

Steelman stopped counting.

"What d'you mean?"

"He brought it."

"He brought it to rob us of a hundred."

"But he hasn't."

"No—and he won't."

Smith picked up a sovereign, tested it on the table,
and put it down.

"You're going to keep it?"

Steelman began walking the room. He walked from the bed
to the door, from the door to the washstand, and
back again, as if laying out a small claim and
expecting to strike a conscience at either end.

"Look here," he said. "That man makes his living robbing
mugs. Bushies mostly. Men come down after six months' graft,
with a cheque and a thirst, and he cleans them
out with mines that aren't there, horses that can't run,
or companies with more directors than shillings. Well, this time
he picked the wrong mugs."

"Still his eleven."

"It's stolen money."

"From someone else."

"Likely."

"So we're giving it back?"

Steelman glared.

"Who to?"

"Someone else."

"Name him."

"Don't know him."

"Address?"

"Don't know."

"There you are! You can't return money to a man
without his name and address. It isn't businesslike."

Smith nodded as if this were a point of law.

Steelman paced again. His face had lost the broad, foolish
good-humour it had worn for Crawley. He took out his
pocketbook and opened it. For a second the woman and
child looked up at him from the worn card.

"He meant to take the hundred," Steelman said, more quietly.
"He'd have taken our last bob if we'd had it.
He'd have left us to hump our swags back, and
laughed. We gave him his chance. He showed what he
was. Now he's paid for the test."

Smith gathered the sovereigns into two heaps.

"Ten back to us," he said. "Eleven for the test."

Steelman looked at the heaps. Then he gave Smith a
slow wink—without warmth, without amusement, and final as the click
of a lock.

"That's it," he said. "We charged him for testing."

At ten next morning Crawley came smiling. Steelman met him
on the balcony, where the harbour lay white under the
winter sun and the mist was lifting from the farther
hills.

Smith was eating an orange.

Crawley sat down.

"Well, gentlemen?"

Steelman sighed.

"Bad luck."

Crawley's smile remained, but required attention.

"What has happened?"

"Smith won't go on."

Crawley turned to Smith. "After yesterday's result?"

Smith separated another piece of orange.

"Too sudden."

"But the profit—"

"Might be bait."

Steelman threw up his hands.

"There! You hear him. I've argued till daylight. Called him
everything I could think of, and some things I had
to make up. He won't shift."

"Surely you control your own portion?"

"Partnership money. Can't touch it without him."

Crawley was silent.

It is an uncomfortable thing for a professional fisherman to
discover that the fish has eaten the bait, thanked him
for it, and declined the hook.

"The allotment closes at noon," he said.

"Let it," said Smith.

Crawley looked at Steelman. Steelman looked sorrowful, helpless, and nearly
idiotic. Then, for the briefest instant, he closed one eye.

The wink was not the wink of the bush fool
in the private bar.

Crawley understood.

His face altered very little. A good card-player does not
throw the pack at the wall merely because the other
man has four aces. He discussed the weather, remarked that
the city was unusually cold, and went away.

For a week Steelman and Smith saw nothing of him.

Then, on the night before they left, Crawley found Steelman
alone near the end of the balcony. The lights trembled
in the black water below, and a cold moon hung
above the roofs.

Crawley leaned on the rail.

"That was neatly done," he said.

Steelman did not answer.

"I mistook you."

"Most people do."

"I can use a man with your ability. There are
several sound lines—not the Horizon business; that was crude. With
your country manner and my connections we might do very
well."

Steelman turned his head.

"Partners?"

"Equal shares."

Steelman looked out across the harbour. For a moment he
seemed tired rather than triumphant.

Then he said—

"No."

Crawley waited.

Steelman put his hands in his pockets.

"I'm not taking pupils."

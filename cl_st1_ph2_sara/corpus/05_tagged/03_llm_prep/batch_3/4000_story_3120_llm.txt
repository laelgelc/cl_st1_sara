When Elizabeth Astrado came to Bellevale, which lies among the
mild swells of southern Iowa, where the corn grows tall
enough to hide a mounted man and the July heavens
have the lucent blue of a child's eyes, no one
knew by what road she had arrived.

She appeared one April morning at the little inn beside
the railway, attended by neither husband nor servant, and possessing,
so far as the town could ascertain, three enormous trunks,
six foreign boxes bound with brass, a black silk umbrella,
and a letter of credit which caused Mr. Bemis, the
banker, to polish his spectacles twice.

"Spanish," said Mrs. Bemis, before sunset.

"Italian," corrected Miss Green, the schoolmistress. "Astrado is Italian, or
near it."

"Likely a play-actress," observed Silas Crumb, who kept the feed
store and regarded the stage as a vestibule to perdition.

Yet Elizabeth was not dark, as the villagers had expected
a foreigner to be. Her hair was brown, with warm,
bronze glints in it; her face had the pale, grave
beauty of one who has suffered without acquiring bitterness; and
her eyes, though gray at first glance, became blue or
violet according to the sky, her dress, or the tenderness
of her thought. She spoke English perfectly, but with pauses
that seemed less an uncertainty of speech than a habit
of weighing words before she trusted them to the air.

Within a week she purchased the upland meadow beyond Deacon
Fry's orchard. It was a windy piece of earth, neglected
because it sloped inconveniently and because a spring, wandering capriciously
through its lower edge, made the ground too wet for
corn and too dry for profit. Elizabeth stood there one
morning while the April rain silvered the bare shrubs and
said:

"I will have this hill. It can breathe."

Old Fry stared at her.

"Land don't breathe, ma'am."

"Then it suffocates," she answered, smiling, "and that is worse."

She built upon the hill a cabin of four rooms,
with a broad kitchen, a stone chimney, and a porch
facing east. Behind it rose a barn, a dairy-house, and
a low shelter for hives. The carpenter, Amos Bell, declared
that she had made the plans herself; but what disturbed
Bellevale was the room at the northern end of the
cabin, for this room had one window of colored glass,
a lock of foreign manufacture, and no visible purpose.

"Fur closit," said Amos, when questioned.

"A closit with stained glass?" cried his wife. "Ridic'lous!"

The brass-bound trunks went into the cellar unopened. The foreign
boxes followed. Elizabeth retained the keys.

Then she planted clover — crimson, white, and purple —
and long beds of mignonette, whose humble blossoms seemed scarce
worthy of such devotion until summer came and their perfume
drifted down the road like an invisible procession. She bought
two Jersey cows, a mare with a silver mane, three
sheep, hens of no profitable breed, and twelve colonies of
bees. She learned to milk, though awkwardly; she churned butter;
she gathered eggs in a willow basket; and when her
first loaf emerged from the oven as heavy as masonry,
she laughed with so merry a humiliation that the carpenter's
little daughter, who had come on an errand, laughed also.

Thereafter, the child visited often.

Indeed, all children came presently. Elizabeth possessed that mysterious welcome
which infants discover sooner than their elders. They climbed upon
her porch, drank milk from blue bowls, pursued kittens through
the mignonette, and listened while she told them of seas
where the water was purple, cities where bells conversed from
tower to tower, and mountains whose snows turned rosy at
sunrise.

"Have you seen 'em?" asked little Ben Fry.

"Yes."

"All of 'em?"

"Too many of them."

"Why'd you come here?"

She looked across the clover, moving in green waves beneath
the wind.

"To see whether quietness was true."

This answer, carried home by Ben, afforded Bellevale material for
a fortnight.

The women visited her, singly at first, then in pairs.
They found the cabin extraordinarily bare. There were whitewashed walls,
rag rugs, plain crockery, an oaken table, a few rush-bottomed
chairs, and shelves of common books mingled with volumes in
languages Miss Green could not recognize. Elizabeth wore linen in
summer and wool in winter, always simply fashioned, though Mrs.
Bemis, touching a seam when its owner had turned away,
whispered that no Bellevale dressmaker could have made it. There
were no portraits, no silver ornaments, no frivolous cushions. Only
flowers abounded — lilies, violets, scarlet poppies, wheat in a
brown jar, apple boughs whitening before their season in the
warmth of the kitchen.

The northern room remained locked.

"Family skeleton," Mrs. Crumb decided.

"Could be treasure," said Amos's wife.

"Could be both," Miss Green suggested, and was admired for
the breadth of her imagination.

But curiosity, though it thrives vigorously in small towns, cannot
long remain wholly unsoftened by goodness. When Silas Crumb broke
his leg beneath a wagon, Elizabeth sat with his querulous
wife through two nights and made broth which even the
doctor praised. When the widow Lacey's cow died, another cow,
young and gentle, appeared in the widow's pasture; Elizabeth denied
knowledge of the matter so serenely that the lie seemed
almost a Christian virtue. She brought honey to the sick,
milk to the poor, books to Miss Green's clever pupils,
and once, when a wandering laborer was found dead beside
the railway, she paid for his coffin and placed above
his grave a small wooden cross.

"He warn't Catholic," objected Deacon Fry.

"He was tired," said Elizabeth. "The cross belongs to the
tired."

Her charities lacked the humiliating trumpet which often accompanies virtue.
She did not improve the poor, nor question the unfortunate
until their misery had been made respectable by explanation. She
gave as the apple tree gives shade, because abundance seeks
release. Yet she was no melancholy saint. She delighted in
the first calf staggering foolishly toward its mother; in the
smell of loam after rain; in warm bread broken by
hand; in the humming tumult of bees returning heavy with
golden theft. Sometimes she sang while working, and then those
who heard from the road paused involuntarily. Her voice had
been trained beyond any village accomplishment; it rose clear and
full, then sank to a tenderness which made the common
fields seem suddenly the remembered country of mankind.

Thus Bellevale, unable to understand her, began to love her.

John Hartington returned from college in June of Elizabeth's second
year.

He was the son of Dr. Hartington, dead these six
winters, and of a mother who had followed her husband
within the year. The old house in town belonged now
to John, together with a respectable acreage and sufficient money
to permit uncertainty concerning his profession. He was twenty-five, broad
of shoulder, straight of limb, brown from rowing and baseball,
and innocent without awkwardness. Books had furnished his mind but
had not yet persuaded him to distrust the evidence of
sun, soil, appetite, affection, and honest fatigue. He resembled a
young oak — not because he was stupid, as such
comparisons sometimes imply, but because strength in him seemed unconscious
of its strength.

He first saw Elizabeth beside the spring.

She was kneeling in the grass, holding a broken-winged thrush
while one of the village children fetched a basket. John,
walking over the hill with his coat upon his arm,
saw the white curve of her neck beneath her straw
hat, the little bird beating against her palm, and the
meadow behind her, lavish with clover.

"May I help?" he asked.

She raised her eyes. Whatever she had expected from life,
it was not precisely this sunburned young man standing against
the immense blue morning.

"You may keep him from being afraid."

"How?"

"By not being afraid yourself."

John knelt. Their hands met around the bird's trembling body.

"This wing is gone," he said gently.

"Yes. Yet the heart insists."

They contrived a nest in the basket and carried the
thrush to her porch. Elizabeth poured two cups of milk,
cool and fragrant from the dairy, and set before him
a plate of bread and honey. John ate with a
frank hunger which pleased her.

"You have come from far away," she said.

"Only from Massachusetts."

"That may be farther than Europe. Distance is not measured
by oceans."

He looked at her, puzzled and delighted.

"My father used to say you couldn't measure a thing
till you knew what it was."

"Your father was wise."

"He was a country doctor."

"Then he had stood at both doors."

"What doors?"

"The door by which we enter, and the door by
which we go out."

A shadow, like the shadow of a flying bird, crossed
her face. Then Ben Fry came shouting that a swarm
had left the upper hive, and Elizabeth sprang from her
chair with laughter. She placed a battered pan in John's
hands and bade him beat upon it while she followed
the dark, whirling cloud into the orchard.

So began their acquaintance.

The weeks went by; the clover ripened; the bees filled
their waxen chambers; and John's daily walk, however aimless at
departure, ended at the cabin. He repaired the pasture gate,
lifted sacks of grain, mended a wheel, and helped bring
in hay while Elizabeth drove the mare. They ate beneath
the trees, the bread wrapped in white linen, the butter
sweet with the scent of grass, the tea faintly perfumed
and foreign. Sometimes they spoke; sometimes the bees and leaves
spoke for them.

The village perceived the matter almost before they did.

"He's bewitched," said Mrs. Crumb.

"He's courtin'," said her husband.

"She's too old fur him."

"How old?"

"There's the wickedness of it. Nobody ken tell."

Elizabeth might have been thirty; she might have been thirty-eight.
Sorrow had laid upon her certain delicate marks which were
not age, and joy could erase them in an instant.
Beside John she sometimes appeared almost girlish; at other moments,
when her gaze passed beyond him toward countries unseen, she
seemed older than Bellevale, older than the prairie, old as
a queen in an ancient lament.

John did not care.

One evening, when heat lightning flickered behind distant clouds, he
found her at the little square piano she had at
last permitted Amos Bell to unpack. She was playing softly,
with the door open to the night. The music was
unlike the tunes of church or parlor; it moved with
proud sadness, broke into passion, then returned as though from
a great distance carrying forgiveness.

"Who wrote it?" John asked.

"A man who knew exile."

"Did you?"

She let her hands fall.

"I have never got used to anything," she said. "That
has been my misfortune. Also, perhaps, my salvation."

He stood beside her. The lamplight shone on her hair
and upon the plain black gown which could not conceal
the hauteur of her carriage.

"Elizabeth, I don't know who you were."

"No."

"I don't know why you came."

"No."

"I know who you are here."

Her lips trembled.

"Do you?"

"You are the truest thing I have ever seen."

Outside, the cattle moved softly through the dark; the leaves
rustled; the air smelled of mignonette and approaching rain.

"And you," she whispered, "are life before it has learned
to be ashamed."

He bent and kissed her.

Afterward she wept — not with maidenly confusion, but with
the terrible gratitude of one reprieved at the scaffold. John,
troubled yet unafraid, held her until the rain came down,
whispering through the orchard, cooling the earth, striking the roof
like innumerable gentle fingers.

They walked together in the mornings. They rode over the
open country at dusk. She taught him songs whose words
he could not understand; he taught her to whistle through
a blade of grass, which she never accomplished. He showed
her where wild strawberries reddened beneath their leaves. She showed
him how bees, drunk with mignonette, slept in the folded
petals of poppies.

Once they encountered a family of emigrants resting by the
road — weary people speaking a language John did not
know. Elizabeth spoke to them in their own tongue, gave
the mother money, kissed the infant, and watched their wagon
diminish westward.

"Were they your countrymen?" John asked.

"All people are my countrymen."

"That's a big country."

"No. The other countries are small. Men draw lines, set
guards beside them, and presently die defending an imagination. But
hunger has no nation; neither has a mother bending above
her child."

Her expression altered at the word. John saw in her
face a yearning so profound that it seemed a wound.

In October, beneath a sky pure as blue glass, he
asked her to marry him.

She was gathering seed from the mignonette. For a while
she did not answer. The dry pods rattled in her
hands.

"John, happiness is terrible."

"I've found it rather pleasant."

"You have not lost enough to fear it."

"Then teach me courage."

She searched his face.

"If I tell you nothing?"

"I'll know what you tell me by living."

"If there have been other names, duties, errors — "

"They're not here."

"If the world should come looking?"

"I'll stand in the road."

At that, she smiled, and the years fell from her.
She placed both hands upon his breast, as though listening
through them to the steady beat within.

"Yes," she said. "Before God, yes."

And they were married.

Bellevale attended in a body, partly from affection, partly to
observe whether any mysterious count, assassin, abandoned husband, or Papal
emissary would interrupt the ceremony. None did. Elizabeth wore gray
wool with a spray of white clover at her throat.
John looked magnificently solemn. Miss Green played the organ, the
Fry children scattered asters, and even Mrs. Crumb admitted afterward
that the bride conducted herself "proper, though foreign."

They lived at the cabin. John rented his land and
worked Elizabeth's meadow with his own hands, to the bewilderment
of men who considered labor admirable only when necessity made
it unavoidable. Winter enclosed them in whiteness. They read beside
the fire, played music, fed the animals, and walked beneath
stars that made the frozen fields glitter like the courts
of heaven. Spring loosened the brook; buds misted the trees
with green; lambs cried from the pasture; and Elizabeth moved
through the renewing world with a new radiance.

She was to have a child.

From that hour all things became votive. The wheat growing
under sun, the milk foaming in the pail, the hen
guarding her eggs, the ewe calling her lamb — each
common miracle seemed to Elizabeth an annunciation. She sewed small
garments of snowy linen. John made a cradle from walnut,
planing the wood until it shone beneath his hand. He
carved upon its head a bee among clover leaves.

"It is too beautiful," she told him.

"Nothing's too beautiful for our baby."

"Ah," she said, touching the empty cradle, "do not challenge
Providence."

But at night she would kneel beside it. Sometimes she
entered the locked northern room, and John heard no sound
except, once, the faint ringing of a little bell.

In the seventh month she brought him the keys.

"John, you must hear my story."

He looked at the ring in her palm. The keys
were small, intricate, blackened by years; they seemed to belong
to prisons, palaces, caskets, and old griefs.

"Is the story painful?"

"Yes."

"Will it make you happier to tell it?"

"No. But it is your right."

He closed her fingers over the keys.

"Then tell me after the child comes."

"To-morrow?"

"To-morrow."

She gazed at him long, with love and pity mingled
strangely in her eyes.

"To-morrow, and to-morrow, and to-morrow," she said. "How rich men
are who believe in to-morrow."

Summer deepened. The mignonette breathed sweetness day and night. Bees
labored in the clover; wheat bowed beneath its ripening burden;
heat gathered over the fields. Then, before her time, Elizabeth's
pains began.

The doctor came from the county seat. Mrs. Bemis and
the widow Lacey moved quietly through the house. John waited
beneath the apple trees, hearing footsteps, low voices, and once
a cry which seemed to tear the blue afternoon as
lightning tears a cloud.

The sun went down. Lamps were lit. The bees ceased
humming.

At midnight the doctor came to the porch.

"The child is dead."

John did not answer. He looked toward the eastern horizon
as though morning, arriving there, might contradict the words.

"And Elizabeth?"

The doctor removed his spectacles.

"She is very weak."

John entered the room. Elizabeth lay pale among white pillows,
her hair braided over one shoulder. The hapless little body
had been taken away. Her eyes opened when he knelt.

"I could not give you life," she whispered.

"You gave me mine."

A faint smile touched her mouth.

"Was it enough?"

"It is everything."

"You must not hate God."

"No."

"Nor seek the old story. It is dust. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Put me where the mignonette grows. Not in their cemetery
of names. There — where the bees will pass."

He bowed his head upon her hand.

"The cradle," she said after a while.

"What of it?"

"Break it. It must never rock another child."

Near dawn, when the first bird made one uncertain note
in the orchard, Elizabeth died.

They buried her upon the hill among the mignonette. Bellevale
came silently, ashamed of every curiosity it had cherished. Deacon
Fry read the Scriptures; Miss Green wept openly; the children
brought clover; and above them the bees went about their
appointed labor in the brilliant air. John placed no recital
of ancestry upon the stone. It bore only her name,
the years she had lived in Bellevale, and the words:
SHE FOUND PEACE.

That evening he destroyed the cradle.

Each blow of the axe sounded through the empty cabin.
The carved bee split; the polished rockers fell apart; the
little bed which had waited for life became kindling. Then
John sat until darkness concealed the pieces.

Days afterward, remembering the bell he had once heard, he
unlocked the northern room.

There was no treasure, no portrait of a secret lover,
no instrument of crime. Against the wall stood a small
Catholic altar, white and austere, bearing a silver crucifix, two
candlesticks, and an image of the Blessed Virgin with a
pierced heart. Dried lilies lay before it. Upon the kneeling
bench the wood was worn smooth.

John closed the door gently.

In the cellar he opened the trunks. Out of cedar
and camphor rose the habiliments of another existence: gowns heavy
with embroidery; velvet cloaks bordered in snowy fur; veils fine
as mist; slippers sewn with pearls; jewels burning in dark
cases — emerald, ruby, diamond, sapphire; rare books bearing armorial
plates; letters sealed by illustrious hands; parchments crowded with titles;
documents naming estates, palaces, revenues, and a family whose history
had mingled for centuries with courts and wars. There were
miniatures of a proud young woman unmistakably Elizabeth, though crowned
with gems and imprisoned in splendor. There were papers hinting
at a marriage, a death, political disaster, and some great
renunciation.

John read enough to understand that she had been wealthy,
titled, and unhappy.

Then he ceased.

Bellevale would have delighted in the tale. Newspapers would have
embroidered it; lawyers might have contested fortunes; distant persons, fragrant
with civilization and grievance, might have crossed the ocean to
reclaim her memory. John packed the letters away. The jewels
he gave, through a lawyer, to hospitals and orphanages, without
publishing their history. The gowns, those brilliant trumperies of a
vanished captivity, remained in cedar until time subdued their colors.

He kept only the silver crucifix.

Often, in the years afterward, he sat upon the porch
at twilight while the cattle moved through the meadow and
the bees returned, laden and harmonious, to their hives. The
mignonette spread beyond its beds and grew wild about Elizabeth's
grave. Children who had once drunk from her blue bowls
became men and women; strangers heard fragments of her story
and asked who she had really been.

John always answered:

"She was Elizabeth."

For he knew that the titles in the cellar had
not revealed her. They had merely named the walls from
which she escaped. Her truth had been in the bread
she baked, the milk she carried to the poor, the
songs she gave the night, the bird trembling between their
joined hands, the unborn child adored beneath her heart. She
had crossed seas not to find adventure but to lay
it down; had exchanged marble chambers for a cabin, servants
for labor, jewels for clover, homage for fellowship, and the
feverish magnificence of rank for the windy freedom of an
Iowa hill.

Her happiness had been brief. But it had been complete.

And when spring returned, whitening the orchard and waking the
brook, John could sometimes believe that no life is measured
by its length, nor any love by what it is
permitted to keep. The bees passed over the grave; the
fragrance rose; the earth, merciful and inexhaustible, renewed its green
covenant beneath the sun.

There, close to Nature's heart, Elizabeth slept.

# The Reindeer's Last Patrol

In the years when I first knew San Francisco Bay,
its waters supported more trades than an honest man could
count and more dishonest ones than the law could master.
From the wharves of the city to the tule-fringed sloughs
of San Pablo, men went out after salmon, striped bass,
sturgeon, smelt, and shrimp; and wherever fish ran in sufficient
number to pay for sailcloth and rice, there also appeared
the poacher with his hidden net, his false lights, and
his sharp eye for the patrol.

The worst mischief was done by the shrimp fleet. Its
fishermen worked fine-meshed nets over the mud-flats, gathering shrimp by
the basket and, with them, the fry of every useful
fish in the bay. A haul might contain a hundred
pounds of shrimp and twice that weight of young fish,
none longer than a finger. The shrimp went to market.
The fry, being worthless, were shoveled overboard in pale, glittering
masses. Thus one net destroyed what a thousand hooks might
afterward have taken.

Wherefore the fish patrol had been established; though it was
easier to establish such a patrol by statute than to
maintain it among five hundred miles of crooked shore.

I was sixteen that summer, but had already knocked about
the bay long enough to know its moods. I could
smell a change of tide in the mud, distinguish the
first dark line of a northwest breeze from the blue
of the water, and take a small boat through channels
where older men waited for daylight. These accomplishments, together with
a disposition to regard fear as a private inconvenience, procured
me a place in the patrol.

Our craft was the Reindeer, a broad, shallow sloop of
no great beauty and less value. Her spritsail had been
patched until the patches themselves required patching. She made water
in a dozen places, though ordinarily no faster than one
man could pump or bail. Her centreboard rattled, her rigging
hummed at all winds, and when close-hauled she sagged to
leeward like a tired crab. Yet I loved her. She
was quick in smooth water, answered the helm kindly, and
could nose over mud that held heavier vessels fast.

With me served George Haskell, a grown man of twenty-eight
who looked forty. He was narrow in the chest, yellow
about the eyes, and troubled perpetually by a cough which
he believed consumption and the doctor called nerves. He had
come to the patrol because he supposed the work consisted
of sailing in sunshine and warning boys away from forbidden
creeks. The first time a poacher showed fight, George discovered
his mistake.

Our chief, Captain Murchison, commanded a launch named the Curlew.
He was a square-built, iron-bearded man with no patience for
argument and a strong belief in surprise. It was from
him that we received word, late one September evening, that
the shrimp fleet had gathered in a creek below Point
San Pedro.

"They think the fog will hold," he said. "It will
not. We go in at slack water before dawn."

Besides the Curlew and the Reindeer, we had two borrowed
sloops, each carrying three patrolmen. Eight men in all were
to seize a fleet of perhaps twenty junks.

George counted them twice.

"They'll run," he said.

"They generally do," Murchison answered.

"And if they don't?"

"Then you'll earn your wages."

At midnight we slipped from Sausalito. The bay was black
and oily, with the fog lying close upon it. Our
lights were covered. We moved by compass and the suck
of the tide, the Curlew leading and the three sloops
following like blind dogs at heel. No man spoke above
a whisper. Once a steamer passed in the main channel,
her whistle rolling through the fog with such weight that
it seemed to shake the water under us. Then silence
shut down again.

Toward morning the air grew colder. The fog thinned overhead,
though it remained packed along the surface. We could see
the faint pallor of the sky and, now and again,
a dark patch of marsh sliding past. Somewhere ahead a
sweep creaked in its socket.

Captain Murchison lifted his hand.

The Curlew slowed. We luffed beside her, and for a
minute all four boats lay together, sails shaking gently. The
flood had nearly spent itself. The creek mouth, Murchison told
us, lay a quarter-mile ahead.

"No shooting unless they force it," he said. "Board, cut
away the nets, and put a man in charge of
each junk. Once six are taken, let the rest go.
We cannot handle more."

The wisdom of this was plain. A captured boat was
not only a prize but a burden. It required a
guard, and the guard had to watch prisoners who knew
every shallow and back channel of that coast.

We separated. The two borrowed sloops bore eastward, while the
Curlew and the Reindeer stole toward the mouth of the
creek.

Then the fog lifted as cleanly as a curtain.

There lay the fleet.

The junks rested over the gray water like a picture
painted in lampblack and brown. Their high sterns crowded one
upon another; their patched sails hung loose; men stooped over
baskets and coiled nets on the decks. Smoke went straight
up from little charcoal fires. Beyond them, the marsh shone
wet in the first light.

For several seconds no one moved. We had surprised them
so completely that surprise held both sides motionless.

Then a fisherman shouted.

The fleet sprang alive. Sails jerked upward. Sweeps splashed. Men
leaped from boat to boat. One junk, driving its long
bowsprit across another's deck, ripped away a length of rail
and carried it dangling. Two vessels collided bow on, crumpled
together, and drifted apart. The creek became a confusion of
brown sails, yelling crews, and churning mud.

"Stand by!" I cried.

I put the Reindeer before the faint breeze. She gathered
way, her spritsail filling, and we ploughed directly into the
fleet. George crouched in the bows with a revolver in
one hand and a boat-hook in the other. His face
was the colour of ashes.

A junk sheered across us. I bore down until our
bow struck her quarter with a crack that sent three
baskets overboard. George seized the rail and went across badly,
sprawling on hands and knees. I followed with the painter.

"Fish patrol!" I shouted. "You are seized!"

The crew did not wait to dispute it. Five of
them dived into a neighboring boat; the sixth tried to
follow, but George, finding courage now that the man's back
was turned, caught him by the blouse. I secured the
junk to the Reindeer, cut loose the illegal net, and
looked for our next prize.

The other patrolmen were at work. The Curlew had two
junks in tow. One borrowed sloop held another against the
bank. Captain Murchison stood erect in the launch, swinging an
oar and bellowing orders.

We captured our second junk without trouble. The third cost
us a split rail and a bruised shoulder when a
fisherman struck at me with a sweep. I got inside
the blow, drove him aft, and took the sweep away.
By then six junks had been stopped, the remainder scattering
over the flats where we could not pursue without abandoning
our prisoners.

The raid had required less than ten minutes.

Its consequences occupied the remainder of the day.

We gathered the prizes in open water. There were forty-three
prisoners, a number out of all proportion to our force.
Captain Murchison decided to take the six junks to San
Rafael, where their nets could be condemned and the men
brought before a magistrate. Since the Curlew was strongest, she
was to tow four prizes. The other two would be
sailed by patrolmen. The prisoners had to be divided between
the Curlew and the Reindeer.

"How many can you carry?" Murchison asked me.

I examined the Reindeer. She was already down by the
head from the collision, and water slushed above the floorboards.

"Twelve," I said.

He looked at the mass of prisoners. "You'll take twenty."

George's mouth fell open.

"The Curlew cannot carry the rest," Murchison continued. "The wind
will freshen. You should reach San Rafael in three hours."

I thought so too. That was my error.

The prisoners came aboard reluctantly, climbing over the side one
at a time. Most were tired men, soaked to the
waist and sullen over the loss of their nets. Among
them was a broad-shouldered fellow with a yellow silk handkerchief
tied about his head. During the raid I had seen
him leap across three boats, cast off a painter under
a patrolman's nose, and nearly free a captured junk. His
eyes missed nothing. When he stepped aboard the Reindeer, he
glanced first at the sail, then at the water under
the floorboards, then at George's revolver.

I marked him for the leader.

Twenty prisoners, George, and I made the Reindeer wallow like
a cockle-shell filled with stones. There was no room to
sit properly. Men crouched along both rails, crowded the tiny
cabin, and stood ankle-deep in the bilge. I set two
to bailing with kerosene tins while George cast off.

Captain Murchison raised a hand from the Curlew.

"Keep in company!"

"I will!"

But we did not.

For half an hour the breeze served us. The Reindeer
slipped northward, and I could see the Curlew with her
four junks strung astern, the whole procession moving slowly beyond
our lee quarter. Then the wind failed. The sails flapped
once and hung dead.

The last of the flood carried the Curlew toward the
main channel. It carried us eastward, into shoaler water. Murchison's
launch possessed an engine, but with four prizes in tow
he could not turn back without risking the whole capture.

I watched him diminish in the haze. He shouted something
through his hands, but the words did not reach us.

"There goes our company," George said.

"We shall meet them at San Rafael."

"When?"

"When the wind returns."

The wind did not return. The Reindeer drifted stern first,
her boom swaying from side to side. Worse, the leaking
increased. Whether the collision had started a butt or loosened
a plank I could not tell, but the water rose
above the floorboards and spread cold around our feet.

"Bail faster," I ordered.

The two men with the tins ceased work and looked
at Yellow Handkerchief. He said something quietly. They put down
the tins.

I pointed at the water. "Bail."

No one moved.

I repeated the order, first in English and then with
signs no man could mistake. One of the prisoners laughed.

George reached for his revolver. "Make them do it."

"They know the boat will sink."

"Then why won't they?"

"Because they think we'll put them ashore first."

Yellow Handkerchief confirmed this. He pointed toward the marsh, then
downward at the water in the boat. After that he
folded his arms.

I shook my head. "San Rafael."

He pointed shoreward again.

"San Rafael," I repeated.

The prisoners began talking among themselves. Their voices were low,
but the purpose in them was plain. They had discovered
that the Reindeer was weak, that the patrol fleet had
disappeared, and that twenty men stood against two.

Also, they had discovered something I had overlooked.

George wore a revolver openly. I had none.

Yellow Handkerchief's gaze settled on my empty belt. He smiled.

The tide swung. With slack water past, the ebb began
to draw us south and west, away from San Rafael.
I ordered four prisoners to man the sweeps. They ignored
me. When I moved toward the nearest, the whole crowded
mass shifted, and the Reindeer rolled until the lee rail
dipped within inches of the surface.

"Sit still!" I shouted.

They shifted back, laughing.

George leaned close to me. "We must put them ashore."

"And lose the arrest?"

"We've lost it already."

"Not yet."

"They can throw us overboard whenever they please."

"That would be murder. Refusing to bail is only stubbornness."

"Much good the distinction will do when you're drowned."

The water had reached halfway to my calves. I took
a tin and bailed. With the boat so crowded I
could not keep at it steadily, for each dip required
me to stoop among the prisoners, exposing my back. Yet
I threw out enough to slow the rise.

The prisoners watched for a while. Then two of them
opened the forward locker and brought out our provisions.

"Put those back," I said.

They continued. A loaf of bread was broken and passed
from hand to hand. Then came salt pork, cheese, and
a sack of dried apples. George protested, but no one
heeded him. Yellow Handkerchief accepted a wedge of cheese and
ate it deliberately while looking at me.

Hunger did not trouble me so much as the knowledge
that the locker also contained tools. A hatchet in twenty
hostile hands would change the situation.

I pushed through the press, snatched the hatchet from beneath
the bread sack, and tossed it into the little cabin
behind me. A prisoner seized my arm. I twisted free
and drove my shoulder into him. At once the boat
rocked violently. Men shouted. Water poured over the lee coaming
in a shining sheet.

For one dangerous second we hung there.

Then the sail emptied, and the Reindeer rolled upright.

The prisoners became very still. They understood that their own
numbers were a peril.

"Bail," I said.

Yellow Handkerchief shook his head.

The morning was advancing. Heat burned the last mist from
the marshes, and the broad upper bay lay bare around
us. Far to the northwest the water had darkened. A
wind-line ran down from San Pablo, wrinkling the surface as
it came.

I measured its progress and saw that it would reach
us in twenty minutes.

With a sound boat, the breeze would have been salvation.
With the Reindeer as she was, it might swamp us
before we covered half a mile.

George saw it also.

"We must reduce weight," he said. "Put them ashore."

"The nearest shore is mud-flat."

"Then make for the creek."

"Against the ebb?"

"Anywhere! Do something."

"I am doing something."

I began rigging the main-sheet so that I could control
it from the helm. Ordinarily it led aft through a
block and cleat. I cleared the kinks, saw that it
would run, and then inspected the rudder fittings. George watched
me with growing alarm.

"What are you planning?"

"To sail."

"We're half full of water."

"Then they had better bail."

He grasped my sleeve. "Listen to me. They'll rush us
when the wind comes."

"No. They will sit still if they wish to live."

"You don't know that."

"I know boats."

The dark line drew closer. Cat's-paws ran ahead of it,
touching the dead surface here and there. The sail stirred.
Our boom creaked outward.

Yellow Handkerchief spoke sharply. The prisoners shifted toward the weather
side, ready, I thought, to seize control when we approached
land.

George pulled me aft, crushing himself against the stern in
order to whisper.

"I won't allow it."

"What?"

"I won't let you drown us."

"Then bail."

"I mean it. Turn for shore and release them."

"No."

His hand went to the revolver.

At first I thought the movement another of his nervous
gestures. Then he drew the weapon and held it beneath
his coat, the muzzle against my ribs.

"Put the helm down," he said.

I looked at him. Sweat ran from his hairline, though
the morning was cool.

"You are a patrolman," I said.

"I'm not a fool."

"You're worse than a fool if you think these men
will spare either of us after you help them escape."

"They only want to get away."

"They want the revolver too."

His eyes flicked toward Yellow Handkerchief. That glance told the
leader everything. He rose slightly from his crouch. The men
around him tensed.

"George," I said, "put it back."

"Turn her."

"No."

He brought the revolver into the open. The prisoners saw
it pointed at me and broke into excited cries.

The first breath of the breeze struck us. The sail
filled with a hollow report, and the Reindeer leaned. Water
rolled across the bottom from lee to weather, then back
again.

"Turn her!" George cried.

I swung the tiller, not toward shore but so that
the boom crossed between us. George, expecting the boat to
answer the other way, lost his balance. I caught his
wrist with both hands.

The revolver fired.

The report, trapped among the crowded bodies, was deafening. The
ball passed through the sail and went whining over the
bay. George struck at me with his free hand. I
twisted his wrist outward, drove him against the stern, and
felt the weapon loosen.

Yellow Handkerchief lunged.

I tore the revolver free and fired past his head
into the water. The bullet struck with a flat smack
close alongside. He stopped.

"Sit down!"

No one obeyed at first.

I cocked the weapon and aimed directly at Yellow Handkerchief.
"Sit."

He sat. The others followed.

George crouched in the stern, nursing his wrist.

"You might have killed me," he said.

"You tried."

"I fired by accident."

"Be thankful I did not."

The wind strengthened. The Reindeer gathered way, but the water
within her gathered also, running to leeward and holding her
down. I kept the sheet eased. She moved sluggishly, dragging
her quarter, while little waves slapped over the low side.

"Bail!" I commanded.

No response.

I pointed the revolver at the tins. Two prisoners picked
them up, but Yellow Handkerchief spoke, and they dropped them
again.

That was the heart of the contest. They did not
believe I would shoot unarmed men for refusing to work,
and they were right. Nor could I force twenty men
individually. The revolver might stop a rush, but it could
not bail the Reindeer. We had gained no more than
a temporary stillness.

The breeze stiffened and the dark water marched past us
in short, hard ripples.

I saw then what must be done.

I hauled in the main-sheet.

George stared. "Let it go! She can't carry sail."

I hauled again until the boom came in and the
spritsail stood hard and rounded. The Reindeer heeled. Water climbed
the lee floorboards, covered the prisoners' ankles, reached their knees.

There were cries, but no one bailed.

I bore away a fraction, putting more pressure into the
sail.

The sloop leaned farther—over, over, and over. Her lee rail
touched the water. A thin stream poured aboard.

George clung to the stern. "You're mad!"

"Perhaps."

"You'll capsize us!"

"That depends on them."

The stream became a cascade. Bay water rushed across the
deck and mingled with the bilge. The men on the
lee side scrambled upward, but I swung the revolver toward
them.

"Stay where you are."

They froze in grotesque attitudes, gripping rail, mast, and one
another.

Yellow Handkerchief shouted at me. His face had lost its
smile. He pointed to the sheet and made a casting-off
motion.

I shook my head. "Bail."

He answered with a stream of angry words.

"Bail."

The wind grew stiffer and stiffer. The sprit bowed. The
rigging sang. The Reindeer drove faster, but she drove on
her side, half her hull buried. Each small wave slapped
aboard. The water inside rose higher and higher, and with
every gallon the boat became more sluggish, more helpless, and
more certain to turn turtle if struck by a gust.

George crawled toward the sheet.

I aimed at his hand. "Leave it."

"You won't shoot."

"Try me."

He drew back. The prisoners watched this exchange, and something
in their faces changed. They understood at last that there
would be no appeal from my decision. If they chose
the bottom, I would go there with them.

A gust struck.

The Reindeer buried her lee rail to the rowlocks. The
mast slanted over the bay. The prisoners cried out as
one man. Several began praying. The bread, the cheese, the
dried apples, and the empty provision sack floated together in
the bilge. A tin cup washed past my boot and
vanished overboard.

Yellow Handkerchief seized a kerosene tin.

He bailed once, flinging the water high across the lee
rail. He bailed again. Then he shouted to the others.

The change was immediate. Men snatched every vessel they could
find—tins, pots, kettles, wooden bowls, even their hats. Those who
had nothing used their hands. Water flew over both sides
in glittering sheets. They worked furiously, stumbling and colliding, yet
careful now not to shift their combined weight too suddenly.

I eased the sheet an inch.

"More," George pleaded.

"Not yet."

The men bailed faster. Yellow Handkerchief stood knee-deep at the
lowest point, filling two tins and passing them outward. He
roared orders with the authority of a mate on a
sinking ship. One man found the cook-pot beneath the cabin
bunk. Another ripped a board loose to reach the deepest
part of the bilge. Even George took up a dipper,
though he did more splashing than service.

Slowly the water fell.

First the lee floorboard appeared, then the edge of the
weather one. The Reindeer lifted under us. Her steering grew
livelier; her bow ceased ploughing and began to rise to
the chop.

I paid out the sheet and brought her upright.

A great sigh passed through the prisoners. Several collapsed where
they sat.

"Keep bailing," I said.

They kept bailing.

Thereafter we had no further question of authority. Yellow Handkerchief
chose four men for the sweeps. When I pointed toward
San Rafael, they bent to the work without complaint. Others
continued with the tins, while one crawled into the forward
space and stuffed cloth against the worst of the leak.
It was good work. I could not have done better
myself.

The wind, which had nearly destroyed us, now carried us
northward. We crossed the flats, rounded a low point of
marsh, and entered the long slough leading toward San Rafael.
There the breeze failed again, shut off by the banks.
The tide opposed us, and the Reindeer lost headway.

Without being told, Yellow Handkerchief gave an order. Six men
went overboard into one of the captured junk's small skiffs,
which we had been towing astern. They took its sweeps,
passed a line to our bow, and pulled. Four more
worked the Reindeer's own oars. Thus our prisoners towed us
through the slough.

George sat in the stern, silent and wrapped in his
coat.

Near noon we saw the Curlew moored by the town
landing, her four prizes ranged astern. Captain Murchison stood on
the pier with his beard thrust forward. He had expected
us two hours earlier and had begun arranging a search.

The prisoners in the skiff pulled us alongside as neatly
as a yacht's crew. Yellow Handkerchief coiled the line, set
it on deck, and waited.

Murchison looked from the prisoners to the bullet-hole in our
sail, then down at the water still slushing beneath the
floorboards.

"What happened?"

"The Reindeer leaked," I said.

"I can see that."

"The men declined to bail."

Murchison studied their wet clothes, the battered tins in their
hands, and the exhaustion on their faces.

"They appear to have reconsidered."

"They did."

George climbed to the pier without assistance. He avoided the
captain's eye.

The six junks were condemned, and their nets burned beyond
the marsh. The prisoners received fines, though most were released
within a few days upon promises that no sensible man
expected them to keep. I saw Yellow Handkerchief once more
outside the courthouse. He touched the knot of yellow silk
at his temple, looked at me gravely, and made a
slight bow. I returned it. There are contests in which
defeat brings no friendship, yet leaves respect behind.

As for George, he resigned from the patrol before the
week was out. He obtained a clerkship with a shipping
firm, where his principal dangers were ink, ledgers, and the
closing of a heavy safe door. Captain Murchison observed that
the position suited him admirably.

I agreed. Indeed, after seeing George with a revolver afloat,
I thought any occupation ashore suited him admirably.

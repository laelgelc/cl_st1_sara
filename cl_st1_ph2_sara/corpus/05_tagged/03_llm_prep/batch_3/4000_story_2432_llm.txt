## THE SEA-CIRCLE

PREPARATIONS unusually magnificent had been made for Christmas aboard the
*Wanderer*. They were magnificent, that is, when measured against eight
hundred miles of empty Pacific, a deck forty-seven feet from
bowsprit to taffrail, and a temperature which made roast turkey
an act of defiance against nature.

The turkey had come aboard at Papeete, alive and indignant,
and had spent twelve days tethered beneath the longboat, pecking
at bare brown ankles and expressing its opinion of sea
travel in a language understood by every member of the
crew. There were tins of English plum pudding; cranberries preserved
in glass; nuts from California; oranges, limes, bananas, and a
monstrous stalk of plantains; three bottles of champagne laid in
wet sacking under the cabin floor; and a box of
cigars which Boyd Duncan had been reserving since Valparaiso.

For the crew there were presents: tobacco, bright cotton shirts,
clasp knives, silver coins, and one phonograph with six records,
including a march, a sentimental ballad, and four dance tunes
so scratched by previous service that they sounded like rain
upon a tin roof.

For Minnie Duncan there was a small velvet case containing
an emerald, purchased in Sydney and concealed ever since among
engine-room spares. For Boyd there was a mystery wrapped in
sailcloth which Minnie guarded with such severity that even the
cook had been forbidden to enter her cabin.

Thus Christmas Eve found the *Wanderer* slipping westward through a
polished tropical sea, her auxiliary engine turning at an economical
beat, her mainsail and foresail winged out to drink the
faint following air. The sun had gone down in a
conflagration behind the Marquesan clouds. Overhead, the stars seemed not
remote points of fire but low-hanging lamps, and the Southern
Cross tilted above the black rim of the world.

Boyd stood aft in white trousers and a sleeveless undershirt,
his broad shoulders darkened by years of sun. He was
forty, though the sea and much laughter had conspired to
keep him younger. Minnie, perched on the cabin skylight, was
thirty-two, brown-haired, gray-eyed, and small enough to suggest fragility until
one remembered what she had done.

Together they had crossed the Kalahari in an ox wagon,
wintered among sealers on a subantarctic island, ridden out a
typhoon in a dismasted schooner off Formosa, and descended three
hundred miles of an unknown river in New Guinea with
one rifle, two tins of tea, and a canoe that
leaked at every seam. Minnie had once set Boyd's broken
collarbone while hostile villagers debated on the bank whether to
spear them. Boyd had once carried Minnie for two days
through a Sumatran swamp after fever had taken the strength
from her legs. They did not speak often of these
matters. Each privately considered the other responsible for their survival.

Minnie sniffed toward the galley.

"Something is burning."

"The cook's Christmas spirit," Boyd said.

"It smells like the pudding."

"Then it is our Christmas spirit."

She slid from the skylight and vanished below. A moment
later her voice rose through the companionway, crisp and commanding.

"Boyd! Have you been at the champagne?"

"I have not."

"One bottle is missing."

"Then Dettmar has been at it."

"No. He drinks whisky."

"An admirable distinction."

She reappeared with a bottle in her hand and triumph
in her face.

"It rolled behind the flour bin."

"Put it back. It may be frightened."

"You are a joy," she said. "A perpetual, exasperating joy."

Boyd bowed from the waist.

At eight bells Captain Paul Dettmar came aft to report.
He was a heavy man, thick through chest and neck,
with powerful hands and a blond mustache gone ragged at
the corners. There had been a time when his blue
eyes were clear and his skin held the clean red
of the North Sea. Now the eyes were watery, and
purple veins showed across his cheeks.

"The barometer has fallen two points," he said.

His voice was precise, but the precision had been assembled
piece by piece. Boyd smelled whisky.

"Any weather in it?" he asked.

"Squall to the south. Nothing more."

"Very well. Keep the canvas on unless it thickens."

Dettmar did not go. He stood with his legs spread
against a roll too slight to require balancing.

"The men have been given presents," he said.

"They have."

"And money."

"Two dollars apiece."

"Why?"

"It is Christmas."

Dettmar looked toward the forward deck, where the crew were
clustered about their packages. There were seven of them: two
Tahitians, three men from the Cook Islands, a Samoan engineer,
and a Portuguese cook from Honolulu who claimed kinship with
every Portuguese between Madeira and Macau. Their delight was noisy
and unfeigned. One had already put on his red shirt
over his blue one. Another was opening and closing his
clasp knife with reverence.

"You make them insolent," Dettmar said.

"I make them happy."

"Same thing, with natives."

Boyd's face did not alter, but Minnie, who had returned
to the skylight, ceased smiling.

"Go forward, Captain," Boyd said. "You're not fit for conversation."

Dettmar's heavy jaw moved.

"Not fit?"

"Not tonight."

"I have brought this vessel through every sea you chose."

"And I have paid you to do it."

"You think because you own the vessel—"

"I think because I have a nose. You've been drinking."

Dettmar came a pace nearer.

"You spy on me."

"No."

"You search my cabin."

"No."

"You set that engineer to watch me."

"The engineer has enough to do watching the engine."

Dettmar stared at him. Suspicion had lived so long in
the captain that it no longer needed evidence. It fed
upon glances, silences, missing bottles, closed doors, and laughter heard
from a distance. Whisky did not create the suspicion. Whisky
opened its cage.

"You know," he said.

"Yes," Boyd answered.

Minnie looked from one to the other. The lightness had
gone from the evening.

Dettmar's mouth twisted.

"You admit it."

"I admit nothing. But since you insist on making a
Christmas ceremony of the matter, come below."

The captain hesitated. Boyd indicated the companionway. Minnie entered first,
and the two men followed.

The saloon of the *Wanderer* was narrow, low-roofed, and crowded
with the souvenirs of five years' voyaging. A Maori paddle
crossed an Arab spear above the bookcase. Shell necklaces hung
from the lamp brackets. A barometer shared a bulkhead with
a Tibetan prayer wheel. Rifles stood in a rack beside
a cello which neither Duncan could play but which Minnie
had purchased from an impoverished musician in Manila because she
disliked the thought of its being pawned.

On the table, amid charts and brass dividers, stood a
little fir tree fashioned from green-painted rope. Minnie had made
it in two afternoons. Tiny flags served for ornaments.

Dettmar lurched against the table and set the flags trembling.

Boyd closed the door.

"Yes," he said. "I know."

"Know what?"

"That you have been stealing."

Minnie drew breath but did not speak.

Dettmar's face swelled. "That is a lie."

"You altered the fuel accounts at Suva. You charged me
for four hundred gallons we never received. You bought stores
through a friend at twice their value and divided the
difference. You sold two drums of lubricating oil in Papeete.
Before that, in Auckland, you arranged a repair which was
neither needed nor made."

"You cannot prove—"

"I have proved all of it."

The captain put one hand against the table. The other
had closed into a fist.

"How long?"

"Six months."

"And you said nothing?"

"No."

"Why?"

That was the question Boyd had hoped never to answer.
He had waited, watched, and given Dettmar opportunities to stop.
Each theft had been petty in comparison with the captain's
wages; each had seemed less greed than compulsion. Boyd knew
something of ruined men and the obscure pride which prevented
them from accepting charity while permitting them to steal it.

"Because I knew about Hamburg," he said.

Dettmar became still.

The engine throbbed beneath them. A spoon slid softly in
its rack and slid back again.

Minnie looked at Boyd. "Hamburg?"

"He was convicted of killing a man there. He served
eleven years. Later another man confessed, and Dettmar was pardoned."

"Pardoned," the captain repeated.

"I knew it before I hired you," Boyd continued. "You
had been refused by three shipping companies. Your certificate had
been restored, but no one trusted it. I thought a
man who had lost eleven years through no fault of
his own deserved room to stand upright."

Dettmar's eyes had cleared. It was not sobriety that cleared
them but something fiercer.

"You pitied me."

"I gave you command of my yacht."

"You pitied me!"

"I gave you a chance."

"A chance to be watched. A chance to eat your
food and take your orders while you waited for the
convict to show himself."

"If I'd waited for that, I'd have discharged you at
Suva."

"You knew I was innocent."

"I believed the pardon."

"The pardon." Dettmar began to laugh. It was a low,
thick sound. "The newspapers believed it. The minister believed it.
The shipping board believed it enough to return my papers.
Everyone believed it—except the dead man."

Minnie's hand moved toward the edge of the table.

Boyd said, "What do you mean?"

"I killed him."

The engine continued its regular beat. Outside, a block knocked
against a mast, slowly, patiently.

"He confessed," Boyd said. "The other man."

"He confessed for money. He was dying. What did prison
matter to him? My sister paid his family."

Dettmar's laugh ceased.

"I killed Kranz with an iron bar. I struck him
behind the ear. He had cheated me, and I struck
him. I told the court I had not. For eleven
years I said I had not. Then I walked out
innocent."

His voice had risen. Sweat gleamed on his forehead.

Minnie said quietly, "Paul, sit down."

He wheeled upon her.

"You call me Paul now."

"You are ill."

"I am drunk, Mrs. Duncan. Your husband has said so."

"And you are behaving like a fool."

For a second Dettmar stared at her. Boyd shifted his
weight. He had once seen a leopard make the same
small gathering of its muscles before it sprang.

But Dettmar turned back.

"You gave me a chance," he said. "What will you
give me now?"

"Your wages to the next port," Boyd answered. "At Attu-Attu
you leave the ship."

"Attu-Attu."

"Yes."

"There is no work there."

"That is not my affair."

"You will tell them."

"I'll state why I discharged you if I am asked.
I will not repeat what you said about Hamburg. But
the command ends at Attu-Attu."

Dettmar drew himself erect. For an instant some remnant of
the officer he had been asserted itself. Then the whisky
loosened him again.

"You cannot dismiss me at sea."

"I can. But I shall not. You remain captain until
we anchor."

"Captain," Dettmar muttered.

A shout came from forward.

At the same instant the engine-room telegraph rang twice. Feet
pounded the deck. The *Wanderer's* mainsail gave a heavy flap,
filled from the wrong side, and cracked like cannon fire.

Dettmar's head lifted. Drunk or sober, he was a sailor.

"Squall!" he cried, and plunged through the door.

They followed.

The southern sky had vanished behind a wall of black
vapor. It came low upon the sea, advancing with a
white line at its foot. The air, which had been
warm and idle, struck suddenly cold. Forward, the men were
already hauling at halyards.

"Let go the fore peak!" Dettmar roared. "Stand by the
throat! Down mainsail! Lively!"

The crew ran. Canvas thundered. A loose sheet whipped across
the deck and struck sparks from a ventilator. Boyd leaped
to the wheel while Dettmar bellowed engine orders down the
tube.

The squall struck.

Rain blotted out the stars. The *Wanderer* heeled until the
starboard rail went under and green water boiled along the
deck. Minnie, barefoot and bareheaded, crawled to a cleat and
freed a fouled line. A block swept past her temple.
Boyd spun the wheel, felt the rudder bite, and brought
the yacht's bow into the blast before the remains of
the mainsail could jibe.

For five minutes the world was wind, water, and shouted
commands. Then the blackness passed as swiftly as it had
come. The rain diminished. The yacht lifted out of the
smother, stripped to jib and engine, with water pouring through
her scuppers.

Stars returned in torn patches overhead.

Dettmar stood by the companionway, breathing heavily. His orders had
been correct. His seamanship had been swift and certain. It
was one of the bitter facts about the man that
the sea could trust him when men could not.

"Set the foresail when it clears," he said. "Course west
by north."

He did not look at Boyd.

"Captain," Boyd called.

Dettmar stopped.

"Get coffee. Then sleep. I'll take the deck until midnight."

"I am captain."

"Until Attu-Attu," Boyd said. "And you are going below."

For a moment it seemed the quarrel would begin again.
Then Dettmar gave a stiff nod and descended.

Minnie came aft wringing water from her hair.

"Well," she said, "that saved us from an awkward silence."

Boyd looked at her and laughed, not because anything was
amusing, but because she had given him permission.

"You are a joy," he said.

"So I've been told. What are you going to do
about him?"

"Put him ashore."

"Before he kills somebody else?"

The directness of it sobered him.

"Yes."

"Lock up the whisky."

"I'll do more than that."

In their cabin Boyd opened a drawer beneath the berth
and took out a blue-steel automatic pistol. He checked the
magazine, worked the slide, and placed the weapon in a
locker whose key he carried on his watch chain.

Minnie watched.

"Will you need it?"

"No."

"Then why lock it?"

"So he cannot."

Boyd went to Dettmar's cabin. The captain was not there.
In a sea chest under spare uniforms he found three
bottles of whisky, one empty, one half empty, and one
unopened. He carried them aft and poured their contents through
a scupper.

This was sensible, but it was late.

At midnight the sea was again mild. The squall had
left a steady following breeze, and the *Wanderer*, under foresail
and reefed main, slipped at seven knots through long black
undulations. The engine had been stopped. Only the hush of
water along the planking and the occasional creak of the
rigging disturbed the night.

Boyd remained on deck until one. He saw Dettmar nowhere.
At last, fatigued by the storm and the confrontation, he
yielded the watch to the mate, a grave Tahitian named
Ropati.

"Call me if the weather changes," Boyd said.

"Yes, sir."

"And if the captain comes on deck—call me then, too."

Ropati's eyes rested briefly on Boyd's face.

"Yes, sir."

Below, Minnie had not undressed. She lay atop the sheet
in a thin white dress, reading beneath the gimbaled lamp.

"Christmas morning," she said as Boyd entered.

"Is it?"

"Ten minutes of it already."

"I hope it improves."

He sat beside her, and for a while they discussed
Dettmar in low voices. Minnie wanted the captain confined. Boyd
objected that such action might divide the crew and endanger
the ship. Dettmar was still technically in command; he had
displayed no violence aboard; and Attu-Attu lay less than two
days ahead.

"He confessed to murder," Minnie said.

"Committed twenty years ago."

"He threatened you."

"He threatened my dignity."

"He looked at you as if he could see your
coffin."

"Most men look at me that way when I talk
too much."

She put aside the book.

"You feel responsible for him."

"I was responsible for hiring him."

"That is different."

"I thought I could put him back together."

"My dear, men are not engines. One cannot replace the
broken valve and polish the brass."

"I know."

"You know now."

He leaned down and kissed her.

"We'll have him ashore in forty hours. Until then we
keep clear, keep awake, and keep the pistol locked."

"And eat the turkey."

"Especially that."

The cabin fan stopped.

Neither noticed at first. The fan was a small electric
machine fixed above the doorway, and its ceaseless buzz had
become part of the yacht's breathing. With the engine idle,
the batteries supplied it. Now its blades slowed, turned once,
and hung motionless.

The lamp dimmed.

Boyd sat up.

"What's wrong?" Minnie asked.

"Battery switch, perhaps."

He opened the cabin door. The saloon was dark except
for moonlight entering through a port. Somewhere forward a board
creaked.

Then Minnie screamed.

Boyd turned.

Dettmar stood in the cabin doorway behind him.

How he had come there Boyd never understood. The captain
moved with startling speed. One arm swept around Minnie's waist.
The other seized her wrist. He dragged her from the
berth before Boyd could close with him.

"Let her go!"

Dettmar hurled Minnie toward the companionway. She caught the frame,
kicked backward, and struck his shin. He grunted. Boyd sprang.

The captain slammed the cabin door between them. The edge
struck Boyd's shoulder and threw him against the berth. By
the time he tore it open, Dettmar had Minnie on
deck.

Boyd reached the top of the companionway as Dettmar lifted
her bodily.

The deck was empty.

Ropati lay beside the wheel, motionless, a dark stain in
his hair.

Minnie struck at Dettmar's face. He carried her to the
rail.

"Paul!" Boyd shouted.

Dettmar looked back. In the moonlight his face had lost
its humanity. It was not rage alone, nor drink, nor
fear, but all three melted into a monstrosity that wore
the captain's features.

He threw Minnie overboard.

She vanished without a cry.

Boyd made a mistake.

He did not look at the wheel, the sails, the
unconscious mate, or the man by the rail. He did
not think. He ran two strides and dived after his
wife.

The sea closed over him with a warm shock. He
went deep, struck upward, and burst into moonlight amid silver
foam.

"Minnie!"

No answer.

The *Wanderer* passed twenty yards away, her black hull cutting
smoothly through the water. For one instant Boyd saw Dettmar
leaning over the rail. Then the captain disappeared.

"Minnie!"

"Here!"

Her voice came from behind him. He turned and swam.
A pale arm rose between swells. He reached her as
she went under and caught the back of her dress.

"I'm all right," she gasped. "I can swim."

"Save your breath."

Behind them a splash sounded. Something white bobbed on the
water.

"A buoy," Boyd said.

They swam to it. It was one of the *Wanderer's*
cork life rings, and a length of line trailed from
it. Dettmar had thrown it—or caused it to be thrown.
Boyd could not then guess why. Perhaps the last reflex
of seamanship had survived his crime. Perhaps he meant later
to claim that every effort had been made.

They thrust their arms through the ring.

The yacht was already two hundred yards away.

Boyd waited for her to round up. Surely Ropati would
recover. Surely the crew would hear the confusion. Surely Dettmar,
even Dettmar, could not intend—

The *Wanderer's* red port light swung away. Her green light
showed. The sails changed shape as she bore off before
the wind.

"She's turning," Minnie said.

"No."

The green light steadied.

The yacht was not turning back. She was setting a
course westward.

Boyd shouted until his chest hurt. Minnie shouted with him.
No answer came. The white stern light diminished, rose upon
a swell, disappeared, returned as a pinprick, and was gone.

They floated alone.

For a few minutes Boyd kicked after the vanished ship,
towing Minnie and the buoy. It was an instinctive effort,
devoid of reason. The *Wanderer* made seven knots. A strong
swimmer made two, and not for long.

"Stop," Minnie said.

He stopped.

The sea lifted and lowered them. The moon spread a
road of light across the water, but there was no
traveler upon it.

"Ropati?" Minnie asked.

"Dettmar struck him."

"Is he dead?"

"I don't know."

"He threw the buoy."

"Yes."

"That will be part of the story."

Boyd looked at her.

She was shivering despite the warmth of the sea. Her
wet hair lay across her face. One shoulder of her
dress had torn, and blood trickled from a scrape along
her arm.

"What story?"

"The one he tells when he reaches port. I fell
overboard. You dived after me. He threw the buoy and
searched all night."

Boyd saw it then, complete in every useful detail. Dettmar
had not acted in mere frenzy. The lights had failed
because he had opened the battery switch. He had disabled
Ropati and cleared the deck. He had thrown Minnie overboard
where Boyd must see her. He had known Boyd would
follow. And the buoy was not mercy but evidence.

"He'll circle," Boyd said. "He'll waste hours circling. He'll have
the log altered. The crew will swear they searched."

"Will they?"

"They may know nothing. By dawn he can tell them
we were lost during the squall."

Minnie considered this.

"Then we must survive to contradict him."

The matter-of-fact words struck Boyd harder than any lament could
have done.

"Yes," he said. "That is exactly what we must do."

They examined their resources. These consisted of the cork ring,
eleven feet of wet rope, Minnie's dress, Boyd's trousers, his
wristwatch—which had stopped—and a gold wedding ring apiece. There was
no water, food, knife, flare, sail, mirror, or container. Their
position was uncertain within fifteen miles. The nearest island lay
far beyond swimming distance.

The buoy would support them only if they remained partly
submerged. Boyd looped the trailing rope beneath Minnie's arms and
tied it so she could rest against the cork. He
kept one arm through the ring.

"You take it," she protested.

"We both take it."

"You're doing all the work."

"The buoy is."

"You're kicking."

"I have large, idle legs."

She laughed faintly. "And Christmas dinner?"

"Ruined."

"The turkey will be furious."

"I was promised plum pudding."

"You think only of yourself."

It was poor comedy, but comedy was a raft of
its own, and they climbed upon it.

Through the remaining hours of darkness they drifted westward. The
sea was not cold enough to kill quickly, yet continual
immersion stole warmth. Their teeth chattered. Small waves slapped their
faces. Salt entered eyes and mouths. Once something large moved
beneath them, trailing green fire, and Minnie's fingers tightened on
Boyd's arm. It passed without touching.

They talked at intervals to maintain wakefulness. They recalled Christmases
in other places: snow at his father's farm in Vermont;
rain against the windows of her childhood house in Devon;
a feast of goat meat in Patagonia; canned peaches shared
with prospectors above the Arctic Circle; a Christmas in Shanghai
when they had mistaken the date and celebrated on the
twenty-sixth.

"You gave me an emerald," Minnie said suddenly.

"I intended to."

"Where is it?"

"Under the flour."

"No, that was the champagne."

"Then under the engine-room gaskets."

"You hid my present in machinery?"

"Dettmar never looked at machinery unless it broke."

"What color?"

"Emerald color."

"Boyd."

"Green."

"How large?"

"Immoderate."

"You should have given it to me yesterday."

"I'm beginning to see that."

"What was my present to you?"

"I don't know."

"A sextant."

"I have a sextant."

"You have an abomination with a cracked shade glass and
a sentimental attachment to mildew. I bought you a proper
one."

"A German instrument?"

"English."

"Then we are reconciled."

Near dawn the breeze weakened. The sea flattened, and a
dim pallor spread behind them. The stars went out one
by one.

Day did not arrive gently. The sun lifted from the
horizon as a red edge, became a furnace, and commenced
its work.

At first the heat was welcome. Their shivering ceased. Their
clothes warmed. Boyd tied Minnie's torn skirt over her head
and shoulders. He attempted to make a covering for himself
from one trouser leg, but the effort nearly cost them
the garment, and they abandoned it.

By eight o'clock the sun was painful.

By ten it was an enemy.

Their skin, softened by hours in salt water, burned rapidly.
The glare off the sea stabbed beneath closed eyelids. There
was no shade except that made by bent necks and
the little width of the buoy. Their lips swelled. Thirst,
at first a dryness, became a presence with weight and
intention.

Boyd knew the principles of survival at sea. He had
read accounts, interviewed survivors, and once spent thirty-six hours in
a leaking whaleboat with six men and four ounces of
water. One did not drink seawater. One did not struggle
unnecessarily. One protected the head, conserved saliva, and resisted hallucination.

Knowledge was useful. Knowledge did not lessen thirst.

At noon a bank of clouds appeared to the north.
They watched it with desperate concentration. Rain hung beneath it
in gray threads, but the cloud passed miles away.

Minnie began to doze. Each time her head slipped forward,
Boyd woke her.

"Don't," she murmured.

"Don't what?"

"Talk."

"I wasn't talking."

"You were explaining the sextant."

He had not been. A warning moved through him.

"What did I say?"

"That an English sextant is loyal to the crown."

"A sound principle."

"Boyd."

"Yes?"

"If I become silly, don't be frightened."

"You have been silly for years."

"Good."

She closed her eyes again.

The horizon surrounded them in an unbroken circle. From wave
level it was terribly near, a blue wall only a
few miles distant. Nothing existed beyond it. The world had
contracted to a sea-circle, themselves at its center, while the
sun burned overhead like a white coin.

Boyd searched that circle. He searched until black motes swarmed
before his eyes. Each wave crest became a sail. Each
flash was metal. Once he saw the distinct smoke of
a steamer, dark against the northern sky, and shouted. Minnie
lifted her head. There was no smoke.

"You saw it?" he asked.

"No."

"It was there."

"No."

He lowered his face to the cork.

Hours passed without measure. His watch had filled with water;
its hands remained at seventeen minutes past one, the time
he had struck the sea. Christmas Day was advancing, but
clocks had ceased to concern them.

In the afternoon Minnie whispered, "You should let me go."

Boyd looked at her.

"It will support you better," she said.

"It supports us."

"Not for another night."

"We won't have another night."

"Because we'll be rescued?"

"Because I refuse to spend Christmas night in formal clothes."

She did not smile.

"Listen," he said. "There are ships on this ocean."

"Not here."

"Everywhere is here to a ship eventually."

"Very philosophical."

"Also, Dettmar made a mistake."

"What mistake?"

"He put us overboard in the shipping lane from Panama
to Australia."

"We are south of it."

"Near enough."

"How near?"

"A hundred miles."

This time she did smile, though the skin of her
cracked lips bled.

"You are a joy," she whispered.

Toward sunset a swell larger than the others rolled over
them. Minnie lost her grip. The rope held, but she
was dragged beneath the buoy. Boyd hauled her up. She
coughed and vomited salt water, then hung limp.

"Minnie!"

Her eyes opened.

"I'm here."

He retied the rope with fingers swollen and clumsy. The
knot required three attempts. He no longer trusted his hands.
They belonged to someone else—red, blistered instruments glimpsed at the
ends of his arms.

The sun descended. Its cruelty diminished, but with the first
shade came cold. Boyd feared the second night more than
he had feared the day. Minnie had lost strength. His
own legs cramped whenever he stopped moving them. The sea,
so empty under sunlight, would become inhabited by every terror
darkness could invent.

Then he heard a sound.

It was not wind. It was not surf or blood
beating in his ears. It was a low vibration, regular
and mechanical.

He raised his head.

"What?" Minnie asked.

"Listen."

They listened.

Nothing.

A wave lifted them. At its crest the sound returned:
a distant metallic pulse.

"Engine," Boyd said.

Minnie tried to turn within the rope.

"Where?"

He searched the horizon. The west was aflame with sunset.
To the east, already gray, he saw nothing. Southward, nothing.
Northward—

A masthead appeared above the sea-circle.

It vanished. Reappeared. Beneath it showed a second mast, then
a gray angular structure.

"A ship," he said.

Minnie made no answer.

"A ship!"

He shouted. The vessel was five or six miles distant,
perhaps more, moving east to west. At their level only
her upper works showed. She seemed enormous, yet she could
pass without seeing them. A life buoy and two human
heads were less than specks upon that bright, heaving waste.

Boyd tore the white strip from Minnie's shoulder covering and
waved it. His arm failed after a dozen strokes. He
shifted hands. He shouted until no voice came, then shouted
voicelessly.

The ship continued.

"She's passing," Minnie said.

"No."

"She's passing us."

Boyd released his grip on the buoy and stood in
the water as high as he could, waving both arms.
He sank, swallowed water, surfaced, and did it again.

On the ship, sunlight flashed.

A mirror, he thought. Then the flash came again, deliberate
and pointed.

The vessel altered shape.

Her bows were turning.

"She sees us," he croaked.

Minnie stared.

A tiny black object rose from the ship's deck. A
seaplane? No, a signal flag at a yard. Then came
the deep bellow of a siren, rolling across the water.

Minnie began to laugh. The laughter broke into sobbing. Boyd
put one arm around her, and together they watched the
gray ship grow.

She was an American cruiser, the *Concord*, bound from Samoa
to Honolulu after landing an astronomical party on a remote
island. The astronomers had delayed her sailing by four hours
because a professor had misplaced a case of photographic plates.
The captain, irritated by the delay, had afterward changed course
six miles south to test a suspected current. A junior
signalman, sweeping the sunset with glasses, had seen what he
first took to be a sea bird behaving oddly.

Thus accident piled upon accident until the chance in a
million became fact.

A launch came alongside them. Sailors lifted Minnie first. When
they attempted to take Boyd, he refused to release the
buoy.

"It's all right, sir," a petty officer said. "We have
you."

Boyd looked at the man without understanding.

"Let go, sir."

His fingers were opened one by one.

On the cruiser they were wrapped in blankets, given measured
water, treated for exposure, and placed in adjoining bunks in
the sick bay. Boyd slept twenty hours. When he woke,
Minnie sat beside him wearing an immense naval robe and
eating broth.

"You had mine," he accused.

"There is more."

"What day?"

"The twenty-sixth."

"We missed Christmas."

"We had it together."

"Without the pudding."

She put down the spoon.

"Boyd, the doctor says you shouted about plum pudding while
they carried you aboard."

"I hope they understand the seriousness of the loss."

"They think you were delirious."

"They are sailors. They should know better."

She touched his bandaged hand.

"You are a joy," she said.

"No. You."

And because neither possessed strength for more, the matter rested
there.

* * * * *

Attu-Attu was a steep green island lifting from the Pacific
like the roof of a drowned mountain. Its harbor, protected
by a broken reef, contained a government wharf, a wireless
mast, two trading stores, a church without a bell, and
the white bungalow of Mr. Horace Lingford, United States consul
by appointment and guardian of American interests by temperament.

Those interests were ordinarily modest. They involved certifying invoices, discouraging
drunken beachcombers from claiming citizenship, recording births on merchant ships,
and writing quarterly reports on copra which no one in
Washington appeared to read. Mr. Lingford performed these duties in
a high collar and cream linen suit, assisted by a
clerk who slept after lunch and a black cat that
slept at all hours.

The arrival of the *Concord's* launch bearing Boyd and Minnie
Duncan produced the greatest crisis of his official career.

"Attempted murder!" he exclaimed. "On an American-owned vessel? In waters
adjacent to my district? Dear me. Dear me indeed."

He polished his spectacles while Boyd gave the account.

"You are certain the man intends to call here?"

"He must," Boyd said. "The yacht requires water, and Attu-Attu
was our declared destination. If he avoids it after losing
us, he condemns himself."

"But if he has murdered the mate as well—"

"Ropati may be alive."

"And the other crew?"

"Dettmar will have told them an accident occurred."

Minnie sat in a cane chair beside the consul's desk.
Her face and arms were bandaged in places, and she
wore a loose dress borrowed from the wife of the
naval surgeon. Boyd's skin was dark red and peeling. His
hands remained swollen. Neither looked fit to stand, but Mr.
Lingford had already learned that appearance was a poor measure
of the Duncans.

"We must proceed lawfully," the consul declared. "Above all, lawfully.
There will be statements, depositions, perhaps a preliminary detention. Jurisdiction
may prove complex. The alleged acts occurred upon the high
seas; the accused is German-born, naturalized British, employed on an
American-owned yacht registered at Honolulu—"

"He threw my wife into the ocean," Boyd said.

"Yes. Quite. That does simplify the emotional aspect, though not
the jurisdictional one."

The *Concord* remained in harbor. Her captain placed six sailors
discreetly at the consul's disposal. Boyd requested that news of
their rescue be kept from shore. The island wireless operator
was sworn to silence. Minnie stayed at Lingford's bungalow. Boyd
occupied a room behind the consular office.

For two days no sail appeared.

On the third morning, shortly after nine, the lookout above
the signal station reported a white yacht approaching from the
east under engine and jib.

The *Wanderer* entered the harbor at eleven.

She came slowly through the reef passage with the American
ensign at half-mast.

Boyd watched through a shutter. He saw Ropati at the
wheel, a white bandage around his head. Relief passed through
him so sharply that he had to sit down. The
crew handled the lines with subdued efficiency. Dettmar stood on
the bridge in a clean uniform.

"He has shaved," Minnie observed from behind Boyd.

"He always shaves before lying."

"You cannot know that."

"I know it now."

The yacht anchored. A boat was lowered. Dettmar came ashore
carrying the ship's log under his arm.

Mr. Lingford stationed himself behind his desk. On the desk
stood an inkstand, an American flag, three trays of official
paper, a vase containing crimson hibiscus, and a silver-framed photograph
of the Secretary of State. The six sailors waited in
the back room with Boyd. Minnie remained at the bungalow.
This had been Boyd's condition. He did not want her
present when Dettmar learned that the sea had failed him.

The captain entered with his cap in hand.

"United States consul?"

"I am Horace Lingford."

"My name is Paul Dettmar, master of the American yacht
*Wanderer*."

His voice was weary and dignified. Grief had been carefully
added.

"I must report a tragedy at sea."

"Sit down, Captain."

Dettmar sat.

Lingford took up a pen. "Proceed."

"On Christmas morning, at approximately one-fifteen, Mrs. Duncan came on
deck. The vessel had passed through a squall, and the
decks remained wet. She slipped near the port rail and
fell overboard."

"I see."

"Mr. Duncan witnessed the accident. Before anyone could stop him,
he dived after her."

"How unfortunate."

"I immediately ordered the helm over. A life buoy was
thrown. We circled the position."

"For how long?"

"Until daylight, and for the whole of the following day."

"Exhaustive."

"No man could have done more." Dettmar's voice trembled. "Mr.
Duncan was my employer, but also my friend."

Behind the door, Boyd smiled without mirth.

Lingford consulted the blank paper before him.

"Were there witnesses?"

"The mate, Ropati, suffered a head injury during the earlier
squall and was below. The crew saw the search, naturally.
The accident itself happened very quickly."

"And the electric lights?"

Dettmar's eyes flickered.

"Lights?"

"I understood there had been difficulty with the batteries."

"Who told you that?"

The consul coughed. "An inference. You arrived in daylight. I
noticed nothing—"

"There was no difficulty."

"Indeed."

Dettmar placed the log upon the desk.

"The position and all actions are recorded. I have come
to request assistance in notifying the families and arranging disposition
of the vessel."

"You are satisfied the Duncans are dead?"

Dettmar stared at the vase of hibiscus.

"After so many hours in open sea, there can be
no hope."

"That depends," Lingford said. "Mr. Duncan, for example, possesses an
irritating amount of hope."

Silence followed.

Boyd opened the door and entered.

Dettmar did not rise. He did not speak. All the
manufactured grief fell from his face, leaving it naked.

"Good morning, Captain," Boyd said.

Dettmar's lips moved.

"You."

"Minnie sends her regards."

The captain surged to his feet, overturning the chair.

The sailors in the back room stepped forward. Boyd raised
a hand.

"Wait."

"My dear sir," Lingford began, "we agreed that the accused
would be taken into custody without—"

"We agreed that he would be taken into custody."

"Precisely."

"Afterward."

Boyd removed his coat. Beneath it he wore a soft
shirt, open at the neck. He folded the coat and
laid it on the corner of the consul's desk.

Dettmar backed toward the wall.

"You cannot," he said. "There are witnesses."

"There were no witnesses when you threw her overboard."

"It was the whisky."

"I poured out the whisky."

"You drove me to it. You knew. You watched me.
You meant to ruin me."

"I meant to put you ashore."

"You have no proof."

"Ropati is alive."

"He saw nothing."

"He saw you strike him."

"He lies!"

Boyd rolled up his sleeves.

Lingford hurried around the desk. "Mr. Duncan, as representative of
the United States Government, I expressly forbid violence in this
office."

"I told Dettmar what I would use if he ever
laid hands on Minnie."

"You told him?"

"Two bunches of naked knuckles."

"This is an official consulate!"

"It has good light."

Dettmar snatched at his hip. His hand found no pistol;
Boyd had locked the weapon aboard. The captain looked toward
the open window, then the door, but sailors filled one
and the consul obstructed the other.

He rushed Boyd.

The first blow was Dettmar's. It struck Boyd high on
the cheek and staggered him against the desk. The inkstand
jumped. The Secretary of State fell face downward.

Boyd came back with a straight left.

It landed on Dettmar's mouth. The captain's head snapped backward.
Before he recovered, Boyd drove a right into the body,
low beneath the ribs, and the big man folded with
a grunt.

"Stop!" cried Lingford.

Dettmar swung wildly. Boyd slipped inside the blow and struck
twice—left to the cheek, right to the jaw. Dettmar crashed
into the desk. Papers flew. The vase tipped, and water
spread across the green blotter.

The consul seized Boyd's arm.

"Sir! I protest!"

Boyd gently removed him and set him behind a filing
cabinet.

Dettmar seized the wooden flagstaff. He tore it from its
bracket and swung it like a club. Boyd ducked. The
gilt eagle at its point smashed through a glass-fronted bookcase.
Law reports tumbled to the floor.

"There!" Lingford wailed. "You see what comes of irregular procedure!"

Dettmar swung again. Boyd caught the staff, twisted, and drove
his forehead into the captain's nose. Blood spattered the white
wall. Dettmar released the staff.

Boyd hit him.

He hit him for the dark cabin and Minnie's scream.

He hit him for the pale arc of her body
passing over the rail.

He hit him for the receding green light and the
white stern light that vanished.

He hit him for the hours of cold, the burning
day, the thirst, the swollen lips, and the quiet voice
that had said he should let her go.

Dettmar was heavier and perhaps stronger, but he had drunk
away his speed and hated away his judgment. Boyd's fists
were blistered from the sea, his shoulders weakened by exposure,
yet each blow was directed by a will which had
survived the sea-circle and had been waiting ever since.

Dettmar reeled across the room. He struck a potted palm,
carried it down, rose on one knee, and charged again.

Boyd met him with an uppercut.

The captain's feet left the floor. He fell backward across
a cane chair, which collapsed beneath him with a report
like a pistol shot.

Crimson hibiscus blossoms, dislodged from the consul's desk, rained upon
his uniform.

He attempted to rise.

Boyd stood over him.

"Enough," said a woman's voice.

Minnie was in the doorway.

No one had heard her enter. She wore a broad
straw hat and carried a parasol. The six sailors made
way for her.

Boyd lowered his fists.

Dettmar looked up through blood and flowers. Whatever plea he
might have made died before Minnie's steady gray eyes.

She regarded him for a long moment.

"You searched all day, Captain?"

He said nothing.

"How conscientious."

She turned to the sailors.

"You may take him now."

They lifted Dettmar. He did not resist. With his arms
held on either side, he was led from the office
and down the white road toward the launch that would
carry him to the *Concord's* brig.

Mr. Lingford emerged from behind the cabinet.

His office was a ruin. Ink dripped from the desk.
The flagstaff was broken. The bookcase had lost its glass.
The potted palm lay uprooted amid legal volumes. The Secretary
of State stared upward from beneath Dettmar's crushed cap.

Lingford picked up a wet deposition form.

"I shall be required to report this," he said faintly.

"Put the repairs on my account," Boyd told him.

"It is not the expense. It is the principle. Assault
in a consular office! Destruction of federal property! Interference with
an official flag!"

"The flag interfered first."

Minnie examined Boyd's face. One eye was swelling. His knuckles
bled through newly broken skin.

"You look dreadful," she said.

"I feel magnificent."

"You promised bare knuckles."

"I kept the promise."

"You nearly struck him with the United States."

"He struck at me with it."

Mr. Lingford recovered the gilt eagle and cradled it mournfully.

"There will be inquiries."

"There are always inquiries," Boyd said.

"And affidavits."

"Send them to the yacht."

"And possibly charges against you."

"Send those too."

Minnie took Boyd's coat from the desk and held it
for him. He tried to put it on, but his
bruised shoulders protested. She helped him carefully.

Outside, Attu-Attu shone beneath the noon sun. Beyond the palms
lay the harbor, intensely blue, with the gray cruiser at
anchor and the *Wanderer* riding astern of her. Ropati waited
on the veranda, his head bandaged. When he saw the
Duncans, his solemn face opened into a smile.

"The turkey, sir," he said. "We did not eat him."

Boyd stopped.

"Alive?"

"In the longboat. He bit the captain twice."

Minnie laughed.

"Then there is justice in the world after all."

They descended the steps together. At the road Boyd paused,
looking from the yacht to Minnie.

"You were not supposed to come."

"I know."

"I told you to stay at the bungalow."

"I heard breaking glass."

"And naturally came toward it."

"Naturally."

"You stopped me too soon."

"I stopped you before Mr. Lingford declared war."

Behind them, the consul could be heard instructing his clerk
to inventory every damaged object in triplicate.

Boyd touched his swollen cheek.

"Did I make a fool of myself?"

"A comprehensive one."

"Are you ashamed?"

"Not particularly."

"You are a joy," he said.

Minnie slipped her arm through his. The cruiser's launch waited
at the wharf. Beyond it, upon the quiet harbor, the
*Wanderer's* white hull shone as innocent as a gull.

"No, my dear," Minnie answered. "You are."

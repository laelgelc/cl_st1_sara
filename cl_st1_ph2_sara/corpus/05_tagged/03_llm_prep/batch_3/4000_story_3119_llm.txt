### Miss Carew's Last Tenant

There was but one objection which the most fastidious person
could have urged against Miss Lydia Carew, and that was
the circumstance of her being dead.

This defect—for it is unnecessary to explain that death, in
Philadelphia, is regarded rather as a condition than as an
indiscretion—had persisted for twenty years. During life Miss Carew had
possessed every qualification for continued existence. She belonged to a
family whose silver had never been bought new, whose portraits
had never been flattering, and whose opinions had not altered
since the administration of President Monroe. She embroidered accurately, poured
tea without splashing the saucer, and had once caused a
young gentleman to leave the room merely by looking at
him after he had said "between you and I."

Her house, a tall, umbrageous residence in one of those
streets which improve as their pavements deteriorate, had descended through
several branches of the family. None of these branches flourished.
One married into trade; another removed to New York; a
third acquired money with a rapidity which made its antecedents
doubtful. The house was consequently rented, shut, rented again, and
finally inherited by two very, very distant cousins in Iowa.

That the cousins were named Boggs was unfortunate. That they
came from Iowa was worse. Philadelphia might have pardoned either
circumstance separately, but the conjunction seemed almost intentional.

Miss Amelia Boggs and Miss Prudence Boggs, however, were persons
of means, sobriety, and unassailable gloves. Their father had manufactured
agricultural implements, but had had the delicacy to die before
his daughters came East; and the implements, having been converted
into securities, no longer obtruded upon good society. The sisters
restored the Carew house at considerable expense, retaining what was
old, replacing what was ugly, and removing what was both—an
operation which greatly exercised the neighbors.

"They have taken down the rosewood cornices," said Mrs. Fallowdean.

"But they have kept the mantelpieces," replied Mrs. Prynne.

"They are from Iowa."

"Yes; but not recently."

This distinction saved them.

Within a year Amelia and Prudence had attained a Social
Position. The capitalization is proper, for the thing itself was
monumental, chilly, and not to be moved without the aid
of several families. They gave small dinners, declined large receptions,
and learned not to admire anybody until they had been
told whom everybody's grandmother was.

It was in the second winter that Miss Carew returned.

Prudence saw her first. She was sitting alone in the
drawing-room, engaged upon a square of embroidery which represented three
yellow poppies contending with a green dragonfly. The design was
modern, and therefore improbable. At half past nine Prudence became
conscious of lavender.

It was not the lavender of soap, sachets, or elderly
linen; it was a grave, pervasive perfume, compounded of summer
gardens, shut drawers, and moral superiority. Prudence raised her head.

A lady stood beside the cabinet of Dresden figures.

She was slender and indistinct, with pale curls arranged before
the Civil War and a gown of lilac silk so
faded that the flowers of the carpet could be seen
through it. Her hands were folded. Her expression was courteous,
sorrowful, and observant.

Prudence stared.

The lady looked at the embroidery, then at Prudence, then
once more at the embroidery. The look was not severe.
Severity may be resisted. It was merely compassionate.

"Good evening," said Prudence, because whatever happens in a drawing-room
must be met with civility.

The lady inclined her head.

"Have you called upon my sister?"

The apparition appeared to consider this question irrelevant. She moved
toward the door, not by walking—which would have involved a
vulgar disturbance of her skirts—but by becoming less visible near
the cabinet and more visible near the portière. At the
threshold she paused and glanced again at the dragonfly.

Then she disappeared.

Prudence rang the bell.

When Amelia came down, she found her sister standing before
the fire with the poker in one hand and the
embroidered square in the other.

"There has been a woman here," said Prudence.

"A caller?"

"A transparent one."

Amelia sat down. "You have been sewing too closely."

"I could see the cabinet through her."

"That was probably the cabinet's fault. It is excessively ornate."

"She smelled of lavender."

"Many respectable women do."

"She vanished."

Amelia's face assumed that calm which is the last refuge
of a person whose reason has received an affront.

"We do not believe in ghosts," she said.

"Certainly not," Prudence agreed. "But if we did, this one
disliked my poppies."

The next morning the yellow poppies were gone. In their
place, upon the same square of linen, appeared a wreath
of moss-roses surrounding the words, in minute and irreproachable stitches:

REMEMBER THY DUTIES.

Amelia said the servants had done it. Prudence observed that
the cook could not spell "potato," and that Clara, the
parlor-maid, considered a needle an instrument of punishment.

They put the embroidery away.

Thereafter Miss Carew manifested herself with increasing consideration. She never
appeared at meals, in bedrooms, or during the servants' afternoon
out. Her hours were from dusk until ten, and her
sphere the drawing-room, where her claim to precedence was perhaps
strongest.

At first she confined herself to inspection. She examined the
Wedgewood, regarded the curtains without enthusiasm, and read several pages
of a new French novel over Amelia's shoulder. On the
following evening the French novel was discovered behind a locked
bookcase, while a volume of Jane Austen lay upon the
table, open at a passage concerning the impropriety of hasty
judgments.

"She has a key," said Amelia.

"She has no substance," said Prudence.

"That makes a key less likely."

"It also makes the lock less useful."

A week later two stiff-backed chairs, banished by the sisters
to an upper passage, reappeared on either side of the
fireplace. The comfortable blue chair was removed to the coldest
corner. A brass Moorish lamp, of which Prudence was particularly
fond, vanished altogether, and in its place stood a painted
screen depicting a shepherdess conversing with a lamb under conditions
of remarkable intimacy.

The sisters questioned the servants. The servants wept, denied, accused
one another, and finally suggested rats.

"Rats do not hang watercolors," said Prudence.

For three small watercolors had appeared in the morning-room: Violets,
Heartsease, and A View of Fairmount Waterworks in 1841. Each
bore, in one corner, the initials L.C.

Miss Carew's taste spread through the house as gently and
relentlessly as mildew. Rose-patterned china displaced the sisters' plain white
service. Daguerreotypes multiplied upon tables. Crochet mats developed beneath objects
which had hitherto stood unprotected. A waxen bouquet, enclosed in
glass, appeared in the hall and could not be removed;
two footmen, a carpenter, and Prudence had tried.

Worse than these corporeal alterations was Miss Carew's silent approval.

She approved Amelia's refusal to attend a public lecture. She
approved Prudence's gray gown. She approved the sisters' unmarried state
with an enthusiasm which, though never expressed, was unmistakable. When
Mrs. Venn brought a divorced niece to tea, Miss Carew
appeared behind the sofa and remained blurred with indignation until
the niece departed. But when old Mr. Cresson spoke for
forty minutes on Family, she became almost solid.

The Boggs sisters began to feel that their lives were
being lived under the supervision of a superior chaperon who
had not even the courtesy to breathe.

"We do not believe in her," Amelia repeated.

"No," said Prudence, moving the French novel beneath a sofa
cushion. "But there is no reason to offend what does
not exist."

They made inquiries.

Such inquiries must be delicately conducted. One cannot ask at
luncheon whether a deceased gentlewoman habitually walks through the furniture
without risking an answer concerning one's own ancestry. Amelia therefore
approached Mrs. Fallowdean by degrees, beginning with the Carew silver,
passing to the Carew portraits, and arriving, as though by
accident, at the Carew dead.

Mrs. Fallowdean lowered her voice.

"Then you have seen Lydia."

"Prudence imagines she has perceived something."

"My dear Amelia, everybody perceives Lydia."

It appeared that Miss Carew had visited every family that
occupied the house. She had remained with the Bensons for
three nights, until Mr. Benson ordered the mahogany dining-table painted
white. She had left the Torpeys after their daughter sang
a comic song on Sunday. The Willses lost her when
they served champagne from tumblers; the Partridges, when they installed
plumbing of an advanced and ostentatious sort.

"She always goes," said Mrs. Fallowdean, "as soon as she
discovers something she cannot approve."

Amelia felt hope.

"And if she does approve?"

Mrs. Fallowdean looked at her with the solemn sympathy due
to an incurable malady.

"No one knows. She never has before."

The situation became grave.

The sisters first attempted aesthetic misconduct. They purchased scarlet cushions,
a Japanese gong, and a marble statuette whose want of
clothing was redeemed neither by allegory nor by climate. Miss
Carew removed the cushions, ignored the gong, and draped the
statue with a lace fichu.

Prudence whistled in the hall. Miss Carew merely looked pained.

Amelia read a newspaper on Sunday. The newspaper was found
folded beneath the coal scuttle, and a volume of sermons
substituted.

They invited persons of doubtful gentility. Miss Carew discriminated with
a subtlety that defeated them. She tolerated an actress because
the actress's grandfather had known the Carews; she rejected a
banker because he said "lovely" too often; she forgave a
Socialist after learning that he was a Cabot by birth.

"You see," said Prudence, "vice is useless without bad connections."

In March Miss Carew broke a cup.

It was one of Prudence's modern cups, angular in form
and decorated with silver squares. Miss Carew lifted it, regarded
it, and let it fall. The fragments lay upon the
hearth. The ghost then seated herself in the stiffest chair
and resumed Mansfield Park.

"That," said Amelia, very pale, "was exceedingly bad taste."

"On our part or hers?"

"On hers. The cup cost twelve dollars."

That pecuniary circumstance, which Miss Carew could not have been
expected to understand, transformed apprehension into resentment. The sisters resolved
to sell the house.

But no purchaser would take it. Philadelphia knew the Carew
residence and its incorporeal encumbrance. Persons who did not believe
in ghosts declined to acquire one; persons who did believe
in them preferred a ghost with less assured Social Position.

The deliverance came from Iowa.

Mr. Hiram B. Sloane arrived in April with a letter
from the sisters' lawyer and the intention of remaining two
days. He was large, cordial, prosperous, and Western to the
extent of calling Amelia "ma'am" before introduction. He had known
their father, admired agricultural machinery, and possessed that sublime indifference
to inherited consequence which in Philadelphia is sometimes mistaken for
deafness.

On his second evening they sat in the drawing-room. The
fire was low, the lamps were shaded, and lavender had
already announced Miss Carew. She occupied her chair beside the
mantel, faint but attentive, with Mansfield Park upon her knee.

Mr. Sloane did not observe her.

"I come East pretty near every spring," he said, "though
I don't get to stay only a couple days."

Miss Carew raised her eyes.

Prudence ceased embroidering.

"There is much to see," said Amelia faintly.

"Yes, ma'am. Me and my partner generally aim to look
the business over thorough. He wanted I should go to
Boston, but I told him there wasn't no use rushing."

Miss Carew's book closed.

Her outline sharpened. Amazement, indignation, and suffering passed successively over
her features. She looked toward Amelia as though expecting the
mistress of the house to summon assistance.

Amelia examined the fire.

Mr. Sloane continued happily.

"Traveling tires a man when he's not used to it
regular. The hotel I stopped to last night, the clerk
says to me, 'We haven't got no room unless you
want to sleep up on the sixth floor.'"

Miss Carew rose.

The lavender perfume became so strong that Prudence nearly sneezed.
The ghost extended one translucent hand, perhaps in protest, perhaps
in appeal.

Mr. Sloane leaned forward confidentially.

"So I says, 'Young man, I ain't never climbed six
flights for nobody, and I don't intend to start in
at my age.'"

There was a small sound like silk being torn a
great way off.

Miss Carew's face had become terrible—not with rage, for rage
is plebeian, but with grammatical despair.

"And between you and I," Mr. Sloane concluded, "I didn't
hardly get no sleep nowhere."

Miss Carew vanished.

She did not fade, withdraw, or become less discernible. She
departed with the abruptness of a candle extinguished by a
pistol-shot. Mansfield Park fell through the empty chair and struck
the floor.

The lavender went with her.

Amelia and Prudence sat motionless. Mr. Sloane looked from one
to the other.

"Something the matter?"

Prudence picked up the book.

"No," she said. "On the contrary, everything is quite correct."

Miss Carew returned no more. The stiff-backed chairs were sent
to auction, the wax bouquet was removed without difficulty, and
the Moorish lamp emerged from a cupboard where no one
had put it. Prudence restored her yellow poppies. Amelia read
the newspaper every Sunday.

As for Mr. Sloane, Philadelphia never accepted him; but the
Boggs sisters cherished his friendship with a gratitude which they
could not, without indelicacy, explain.

He had accomplished what impiety, plumbing, modern art, and twelve-dollar
china had failed to do.

He had offended Miss Carew beyond the grave—and done it
without no trouble at all.

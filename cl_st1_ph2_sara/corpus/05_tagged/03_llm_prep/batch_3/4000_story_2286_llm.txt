### The Lilac Bush

When Nikolai Yevgrafovich Almazof returned home, Vera knew at once
that some misfortune had occurred.

He did not ring twice, as was his custom, nor
call to her from the narrow entrance hall. He came
in heavily, still wearing his military overcoat, and passed through
to the sitting-room without removing either his cap or his
sword. His portfolio, swollen with papers, he threw upon the
floor. Then he dropped into a chair, thrust out his
legs, and stared into the darkest corner of the room.

Vera waited.

During the five years of their marriage she had learned
that questions asked too soon only irritated him. She sat
opposite him and watched his fingers. They moved restlessly over
his knee, snapped once, then began breaking into small pieces
the burnt matches lying in the ash-tray.

At last she said quietly:

"Well, Kolya? Was the plan rejected?"

He made no answer.

"Tell me. It is better to tell me."

"Rejected!" he cried, turning upon her. "If it were merely
rejected! No, there is an end of everything. Everything!"

He kicked the portfolio. A ruler and two pencils slipped
out and rolled beneath the table.

Vera bent down, picked them up, and placed them together.

"What happened?"

"What happened?" he repeated bitterly. "You may ask! Tomorrow I
shall be disgraced before the whole academy. I shall be
sent back to the regiment. Three years wasted—three years! And
all because of one filthy green blot."

"A blot?"

"Yes, a blot. Ink, paint—what does it matter? A blot
is stronger than a man's whole future."

He rose and began walking about the room, though there
was scarcely space between the table, the stove, and the
faded sofa. Their lodging was small, and its poverty could
not be hidden even by Vera's cleanliness. The curtains had
been washed until they were almost colourless; the carpet showed
the threads of its foundation; on the mantel stood two
candlesticks without candles.

Nikolai had twice failed to enter the Staff Academy. The
third time he had succeeded, and since then every examination
had been conquered only by painful labour. He was not
a brilliant man. He knew it himself, and the knowledge
tormented him. What others understood after one reading he mastered
after five sleepless nights. Vera copied his notes, questioned him
from textbooks, drew diagrams, economised on food and fuel, and
received every small success as though it were a victory
won by them both.

Now he stopped before her.

"You remember the survey I worked on until three this
morning?"

"Of course. I ruled the border for you."

"Well, when it was finished, I leaned across the table
and upset the little bottle of green colour. Only one
drop fell—but precisely where it must not fall. I tried
to wipe it away. That made it worse. A dirty
green patch, three millimetres long."

"And so?"

"And so!" He laughed without mirth. "I could not begin
the whole survey again. There was no time. I made
the patch into bushes. A clump of bushes beside the
road. Very neat bushes, too. You yourself would have admired
them."

Vera's eyes brightened for an instant, but she did not
smile.

"The professor noticed?"

"Immediately. He looked at the plan, looked at me, and
said: 'What bushes are these, Lieutenant Almazof?' I answered that
they were bushes at the bend of the road. Then
he frowned. He knows that ground as you know this
room. 'There are no bushes there,' he said. I insisted
there were."

"You insisted?"

"What else could I do? Confess that I had falsified
a survey? He would have thrown it in my face.
I said I had seen them myself."

Nikolai's voice fell. His anger had gone; only shame remained.

"The old pedant put on his spectacles and examined the
place again. 'No, Lieutenant,' he said, 'I have ridden over
that road for twenty years. Tomorrow we shall go there
together. If bushes are growing on that spot, I shall
apologise to you. If not—' He did not finish. He
did not need to."

Vera stood up.

"What time will you go?"

"At eight."

"And how far is it?"

"Five versts, perhaps six. Why?"

She crossed the room and took his arm.

"Kolya, there must be bushes there tomorrow."

He stared at her.

"What are you saying?"

"There must be bushes there."

"Oh, certainly! Perhaps you will order them to grow during
the night? Perhaps you will write to the Ministry of
Agriculture?"

"Do not be foolish. We shall plant them."

For several seconds he merely blinked. Then he gave a
short, angry laugh.

"At night? On land that does not belong to us?
In October?"

"Yes."

"Verochka, leave me alone. I have enough misery without jokes."

"I am not joking." She stamped her foot. "Get up.
Make haste!"

She went to the chest of drawers and pulled out
the top drawer. From beneath folded linen she took a
small wooden box. Nikolai watched as she opened it.

Inside lay her few valuables: a thin gold bracelet, a
ring set with a tiny stone, and a pair of
silver earrings. She placed them on the table. After a
moment's hesitation she removed her wedding ring as well.

"No," Nikolai said sharply. "Not that."

"Then your silver cigarette case."

"It was my father's."

"And the bracelet was my mother's. Come along."

He stood motionless, looking from the trinkets to his wife.

"It is madness."

"Perhaps. But it is useful madness."

Ten minutes later they were in the street.

The evening was cold, though the sky still held the
pale, pearly light of a Petersburg autumn night. A damp
wind moved scraps of paper along the pavement. Vera walked
rapidly, gathering her shawl beneath her chin. Nikolai followed with
the portfolio under his arm and the expression of a
man being led to his own execution.

The first pawnbroker they visited had already closed. At the
second, a sleepy clerk examined each object with insulting slowness.
He weighed the bracelet, tested the ring, breathed upon the
silver, and scratched the cigarette case near its hinge.

"For everything—twenty-three roubles."

"The bracelet alone cost thirty," Vera said.

"Then madam should sell it to the person who bought
it."

"The stones are genuine."

The clerk yawned.

"I do not lend on memories, madam. Twenty-three roubles."

Vera took the money.

From there they hired a droshky and drove to a
gardener whose establishment lay beyond the more crowded streets. It
was nearly ten o'clock when they reached the wooden gate.
The yard was dark, and a dog began barking furiously.

A window opened.

"Who is there?"

"We require a gardener," Vera called.

"At this hour?"

"At once."

The owner came out in boots and a sheepskin coat,
carrying a lantern. He was a broad, grey-bearded man with
suspicious eyes. When Vera explained that bushes must be planted
that very night beside a road six versts away, he
drew back.

"No. I want no business with such matters."

"What matters?"

"Secret matters. People do not plant bushes in darkness unless
they have stolen the land or wish to hide something
beneath it."

Nikolai groaned.

"There, you see? Let us go home."

But Vera caught the gardener by the sleeve.

"There is nothing criminal. My husband is an officer. Here
is the whole truth."

And she told him everything—the blot, the painted bushes, the
professor's certainty, and the inspection fixed for morning.

At first the gardener listened sternly. Then his moustache began
to twitch. At last he laughed so loudly that the
dog renewed its barking.

"Well, that is different! To plant a lie so that
it takes root—such work does not come every day."

"What bushes have you?" Vera asked.

He led them among rows of young trees and shrubs,
lifting his lantern over bundles of roots wrapped in sacking.

"Currants. Acacia. Honeysuckle. Young maples."

"No," Nikolai said. "The marks on the plan are broad.
Three bushes, perhaps four, close together."

The gardener raised the lantern.

"Lilacs."

Against the fence stood several young lilac bushes, their leaves
already darkened by autumn. They were not handsome, but they
were thick enough.

"Lilacs," Vera repeated. "We shall take them."

The gardener named a price. Vera counted the pawned roubles
upon an upturned barrel, bargaining only long enough to preserve
money for the labourers and cart. Before eleven, the lilacs,
spades, lanterns, and three sleepy workmen were loaded upon a
wagon.

They reached the place after midnight.

Nikolai found the bend in the road by comparing every
ditch and rise with his plan. The gardener measured the
distance twice, then thrust a peg into the earth.

"Here?"

"A little farther," Nikolai whispered. "No—back. Yes. Here precisely."

The men began to dig.

The soil was hard and stony. Spades struck roots; the
lanterns hissed in the damp wind; a horse stamped and
shook its harness. Vera stood beside the holes, directing everything
with such seriousness that the gardener obeyed her without argument.
She ordered the bushes placed closer together, had the loose
earth pressed down, and gathered fallen leaves to scatter over
the freshly disturbed ground.

Nikolai, ashamed of his earlier helplessness, worked beside the men
until his gloves split. Once he looked up and saw
Vera kneeling in the mud, arranging old leaves around the
lilac stems with her bare hands.

"Verochka," he murmured, "what am I making you do?"

She did not raise her head.

"Make haste. Dawn will not wait for your repentance."

By three o'clock it was finished. Four lilac bushes stood
beside the bend in the road as if they had
grown there for years. The gardener walked around them, narrowed
his eyes, shifted one broken branch, and declared that only
a botanist with a lantern would suspect anything.

On the journey home Nikolai held Vera's cold hands inside
his overcoat. Neither spoke. They were too tired even for
fear.

At seven he dressed for the inspection. His face was
pale, and he could not swallow his tea. Vera fastened
his collar, brushed dried earth from his sleeve, and accompanied
him to the door.

"Do not look guilty," she said.

"How does one look innocent?"

"As you looked yesterday, before you spilled the paint."

All day she waited.

The little clock ticked with unbearable distinctness. At noon its
alarm gave three hoarse, useless strokes. Vera tried to sew,
but put the needle through her finger. She listened to
every step upon the stairs.

It was nearly two when the door flew open.

Nikolai entered with no heaviness now. There was a spring
in his walk; his cap was pushed back; his face
shone. Before speaking, he seized Vera around the waist and
lifted her from the floor.

"Well?" she cried, laughing.

"He apologised!"

"The bushes?"

"He saw them! He rode straight to the place, dismounted,
and walked around them. 'What kind are they?' he asked.
I said, 'Lilacs, your Excellency.' He touched a branch and
said, 'Remarkable. I had never observed them.' Then he held
out his hand to me. Imagine it! 'Forgive me, Lieutenant,'
he said. 'At my age one should not trust one's
memory too confidently.'"

Nikolai imitated the professor's solemn voice, but without malice. The
terrible pedant of yesterday had already become an honourable and
generous old man.

"And the survey?" Vera asked.

"Accepted. He praised its accuracy."

They looked at one another. For a moment both remained
perfectly serious. Then Vera began to laugh; Nikolai joined her,
and they laughed until tears came, laughed at the green
paint, the sleepy pawnbroker, the suspicious gardener, the midnight wagon,
and the professor gravely examining bushes that had been lying
against a fence only a few hours before.

That evening their supper consisted of bread, tea, and the
last spoonful of jam. The bracelet, earrings, and cigarette case
were gone; debts still waited; new examinations would come. Yet
the poor room appeared warm and almost festive.

Nikolai broke a piece of bread and said:

"Lilacs are my favourite flower."

"Since when?"

"Since this morning."

Vera rested her hand upon his.

"Mine too," she said.

And from that day onward, whenever lilacs blossomed in Petersburg
and their heavy sweetness entered through an open window, they
remembered not the falsehood or the danger, but the night
when shame had been dug into the earth, covered with
leaves, and made to bloom.

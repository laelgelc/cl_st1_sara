At the end of our street the houses drew close
together as if for warmth, their sooty fronts turned inward
upon the narrow road. The last house stood apart behind
a wall furred with moss. It had belonged to a
priest who died there in winter, and although my aunt
had scrubbed the floors and burned rosemary in the grates,
something of his silence remained. In the back room we
found damp sermons, a cracked chalice-box, and novels whose curled
pages gave off the odour of cellars. A rusty bicycle-pump
lay beneath the pear tree, half buried in black leaves.

During the short days we were released from school before
the lamps were lit. Mangan and I flung our satchels
into the hall and ran out among the lanes behind
the houses, where the rain had softened the earth and
carts had made deep brown trenches. We chased one another
past gardens, ashpits and stables, past kitchen windows glowing yellow
in the dusk. Harness jingled inside dark sheds. Women called
children home. Above us, the sky narrowed to a strip
of violet, and the lamps lifted their feeble lanterns one
by one.

It was then that I began to watch Mangan's sister.

She came to the doorway each evening to call him
to his tea. I would pretend to tighten my bootlace
or examine the spokes of a broken wheel while she
waited beneath the fanlight. The light fell behind her and
passed through the loose edges of her hair, so that
her face remained dark while her outline burned softly. When
she moved, her dress made a small whisper. A silver
bracelet slipped along her wrist and caught the light.

Sometimes she called twice.

"Mangan! Are you deaf?"

He would answer from behind the wall, delaying on purpose,
while I stood hidden beside the empty shed and listened.
Her voice, though it carried only annoyance, entered me with
the solemnity of a bell heard across water.

In the mornings I arranged my departure so that I
might follow her. I waited at the parlour window, looking
through a slit in the blind until her door opened.
Then I seized my books and went out. I kept
always at a distance. Her brown figure moved before me
through the dimness, now hidden by a group of schoolgirls,
now appearing again beside the railings. At the corner where
our ways divided, I hurried forward until I had nearly
overtaken her. I never spoke. She would turn toward the
convent road, and I, carrying the secret splendour of having
been near her, continued to school.

Her name accompanied me everywhere. It sounded beneath the master's
questions and trembled in the scratching of pens. In the
crowded Saturday streets, where my aunt sent me to carry
parcels, it rose above the cries of fishmongers, the curses
of drivers, the clatter of hooves and the striking of
coins upon counters. I bore my aunt's basket through the
throng as though I carried a vessel in a procession.
The coarse hands, the sweating faces, the raw smell of
meat and wet wool seemed to press against me, but
her image stood between my soul and their confusion.

I did not understand what I desired. At night, kneeling
beside my bed, I tried to pray, but her name
came between the familiar words. My body seemed strung with
wires, and the least remembrance of her hand upon the
railing made them sing. Once, overcome by an emotion for
which I had no language, I went into the back
room where the priest's books lay swelling in the damp.
Rain struck the garden like handfuls of needles. I pressed
my palms together and whispered her name until it ceased
to be a name and became a prayer. Tears came
into my eyes, though I could not have said whether
I was joyful or miserable.

For many days I spoke to her only in imagination.
I rescued her from vague dangers; I travelled through deserts
and cities whose towers were red in the sunset; I
laid before her objects of gold, ivory and blue glass.
She accepted them gravely. In these visions she seldom spoke.
Her silence was necessary to the perfection I had made
of her.

One evening she addressed me.

The other boys had gone into the lane, but I
remained near the railings. She stood on the steps, turning
the silver bracelet around her wrist. The lamp over the
door shone upon the curve of her neck and upon
one white edge of her collar.

"Are you going to Araby?" she asked.

For an instant I believed that she had spoken some
word known only to us.

"Araby?"

"The bazaar. They say it will be grand."

I had seen the advertisements pasted on walls: a dark
palace, pointed arches, a moon above minarets. Until that moment
I had scarcely noticed them. Now the name opened before
me like a gate into the East. I saw coloured
lamps, carpets, spices, brass vessels and veiled merchants guarding jars
of precious stones.

"I am going," I said, though no permission had been
given.

She lowered her eyes.

"I cannot. There is a retreat at the convent."

The bracelet slid down her wrist as she rested one
hand upon the railing.

"If I go," I said, "I will bring you something."

She smiled. Mangan shouted from the lane, and she turned
away. The door closed behind her, leaving the steps empty
and common.

From that evening my life became a service ordered toward
Saturday. The hours between seemed maliciously invented. At school I
saw the word Araby written across the page of every
lesson. Numbers dissolved into lamps; the map of Europe became
a desert shore. The master, observing my idleness, struck the
desk with his ruler and asked whether I had taken
leave of my senses. The boys laughed. I lowered my
head, protecting my secret from them.

At home I searched my possessions for money. From a
tobacco tin beneath my shirts I took three pennies, a
sixpence blackened at the rim, and two foreign coins the
priest had left in a drawer. The foreign coins were
useless, but I polished them upon my sleeve and imagined
that they belonged to the country of the bazaar. I
considered what gift I might buy: a bracelet finer than
hers, a little bottle of perfume, a brooch shaped like
a star. Each object became radiant because her hand would
touch it.

Saturday arrived beneath a sky heavy with rain. At breakfast
I reminded my uncle that I wished to go to
the bazaar that evening.

"Yes, yes," he said, searching his pockets for a match.
"We'll see to it."

My aunt looked doubtful.

"He ought not to be out late."

"It won't be late," I said quickly.

My uncle laughed and asked whether I meant to purchase
the whole of Arabia. I felt the blood rise into
my face. Adults, I believed, could profane anything merely by
speaking of it.

The morning dragged past. I tried to read in the
front room, but the ticking clock divided time into tiny
cruelties. Outside, rain collected in the gutter and overflowed with
a hollow sound. Mrs Mercer came after tea, a broad
old woman who sold used stamps for charitable causes and
knew the illnesses of every family in three streets. She
sat by the fire discussing funerals, rents and the price
of coal. Her umbrella dripped steadily upon the tiles.

My uncle had not returned.

At six my aunt lit the lamp. The room shrank
around its yellow circle. Mrs Mercer said she could not
wait longer, for the night air was injurious to her
chest. She wrapped herself in a shawl, sighed several times,
and departed.

"Perhaps you had better give up going," my aunt said.

"He promised."

"He may have been kept."

I went to the upper room and pressed my forehead
against the cold pane. From there I could see the
street, wet and shining beneath the lamps. Figures passed and
vanished. Every approaching step caused my heart to rise; every
stranger's face cast it down again. Somewhere a clock struck
seven. Then another answered, farther away.

I descended and wandered from room to room. The house
seemed to conspire with the delay. Floorboards complained beneath me;
pipes muttered in the walls; the clock continued its dry,
deliberate speech. I thought of the bazaar already blazing with
light, while she perhaps sat at the convent chapel wondering
whether I had gone. The idea that she might be
thinking of me filled me with such pride that for
a little while I forgave the lateness of the hour.

My uncle returned after eight. I heard his key scrape
repeatedly at the lock before the door opened. He entered
humming, carrying the smell of rain and drink upon his
coat.

"You're late," my aunt said.

"Am I?"

"The boy has been waiting."

He looked at me without understanding. Then his face changed.

"Ah! The bazaar."

My anger was so sharp that I could not speak.
He removed his coat, searched one pocket, then another, and
began telling my aunt of a man he had met
near the bridge. She interrupted him.

"Give him the money, if he is to go at
all."

My uncle found a florin and held it above my
palm.

"When I was a lad," he said, "there was a
poem about an Arab bidding farewell to his horse."

I closed my fingers around the coin and fled before
he could remember the verses.

The streets leading to the station were almost empty. Rain
shivered beneath the lamps. At the booking window the clerk
glanced at the clock before giving me a third-class ticket.
I ran along the platform and entered a carriage where
only one man sat, asleep beneath a newspaper. The train
delayed, moved, stopped again outside the station, and then crawled
among rows of dark houses. Their rear windows passed like
extinguished eyes.

I sat upright, holding the florin in my fist. With
every minute the imagined bazaar grew brighter and more remote.
I pictured its entrance crowded with carriages, its roof hung
with flags, its aisles perfumed by sandalwood. I saw myself
returning through the night with a parcel hidden beneath my
coat. On Monday morning I would wait at the corner.
I would place the gift in her hand. She would
look at me then—not through me or past me, but
at me—and understand all that I had never said.

When the train reached the special platform, few passengers descended.
I followed painted signs across a muddy yard to a
large building whose upper windows were dark. A weary attendant
stood at the entrance.

"Nearly finished," he said.

I paid the admission reluctantly. The coin sounded loud upon
the little metal dish.

Inside, the great hall was darker than a church after
service. Most of the stalls were closed, their canvas coverings
drawn down like eyelids. Men carried boxes through the aisles.
Somewhere iron chains rattled, and from an upper gallery came
the brief laughter of people preparing to leave. The silence
between these sounds had nothing mysterious in it. It was
the silence of fatigue.

I walked toward the centre. A sign painted with the
name Araby hung above the darkness, but several bulbs in
its border had gone out. What remained of the word
trembled weakly in red and green.

At one open stall, tall porcelain jars stood on either
side of the counter. In my former dreams they would
have been guardians of an eastern treasury. Now I saw
dust on their painted shoulders and small white cards tied
to their necks with string. A young woman was speaking
to two men in English accents. They joked in low
voices. She laughed, not because she was amused, I thought,
but because the hour required some laughter from her.

I waited before the stall.

At last she turned.

"Do you wish to buy anything?"

Her tone was dutiful and indifferent. The two men looked
at me. On the counter lay saucers, vases, paper fans
and cheap necklaces. I touched the coins in my pocket
and understood that most of the objects were beyond my
means. Those I could afford appeared suddenly poor and foolish.
A painted thimble. A paper flower. A little glass animal
with one ear chipped.

"No, thank you," I said.

The woman moved away and resumed her conversation. One of
the men dropped a coin upon the counter and spun
it with his finger. They watched it circle, glitter, falter
and fall.

I remained there, pretending to examine the jars. In my
pocket I had a shilling, the sixpence, and the pennies
I had guarded through the week. Their weight seemed ridiculous.
The hall no longer opened upon deserts or distant kingdoms.
It was only a room hired for commerce, nearly empty,
where tired people counted money and wished to go home.

A voice called that the lights would be put out.

The gallery darkened first. Then one line of lamps above
the stalls vanished, and the shadows advanced across the floor.
I thought of Mangan's sister standing beneath the fanlight, of
her bracelet, her bowed head, the white edge of her
collar. But the image would no longer remain pure. I
saw that I had scarcely known her. I had taken
a few gestures, a voice at a doorway, and built
from them an altar to myself. The promise of the
gift had seemed noble only because I imagined myself transformed
by giving it.

Another light went out.

I stood alone in the narrowing dusk, my hand closed
around the useless coins. Shame rose in me, hot and
merciless. I had believed that desire made me chosen, that
a name repeated like a prayer must lead somewhere beyond
the brown streets, the ticking clocks, the smell of drink
and rain. Yet I had come only to this: shuttered
stalls, indifferent faces, and darkness purchased too late.

The last lamps failed above me.

I lifted my eyes into the black roof of the
hall. In that instant I saw myself as a creature
driven by vanity through a world that had never promised
to answer me. The vision wounded me because it was
mine and could not be blamed upon any other. Anguish
tightened my throat; anger burned behind my eyes.

I turned toward the exit, carrying nothing.

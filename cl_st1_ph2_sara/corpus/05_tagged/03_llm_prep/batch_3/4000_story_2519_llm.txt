Jacob Kent's ruling passion was cupidity. Other men in the
North had appetites—for whisky, for cards, for red meat after
famine, for the remembered warmth of women—but these were incidental
fevers, remittent and human. Kent's malady was constitutional. Gold had
entered his blood, yellowed his imagination, and established itself in
the throne-room of his soul, where, like some barbaric usurper,
it issued decrees to every lesser faculty.

He had come into the Klondike with a cracked iron
pot, two blankets, a muzzle-loading shot-gun, and a faith in
Providence which endured precisely until Providence required him to share
anything. Two years later he owned the travellers' cabin at
Crooked Elbow, where the winter trail quitted the Yukon, crossed
a wooded neck, and returned to the river eleven miles
farther on. He owned also three dogs, one lame mule,
fourteen hundred pounds of provisions, and a secret quantity of
gold dust which no man had seen and concerning which
all men speculated.

The cabin had not been built by Kent. It had
been raised by prospectors for the common salvation of the
trail, each contributing axe-work, moss-chinking, or profanity according to his
aptitude. Kent occupied it during a blizzard, remained after the
blizzard, nailed up a board announcing KENT'S HOTEL, and thereafter
charged two dollars for floor-space, one dollar for fire, fifty
cents for melted snow, and whatever his judgment could extort
for beans.

There were objections. Kent replied that civilization had arrived.

His procedure was admirable in its simplicity. When a traveller
came staggering from the steel-gray waste, beard stiff with ice
and lungs rasping from the cold, Kent waited until the
man had one mitten on the latch. Then he named
his terms. The wilderness behind, which was free, offered death;
the cabin before, which had once been free, offered life
for cash. It was a commercial situation of classical purity.

"Robbery," said men.

"Business," said Kent.

He sat in the centre of his web, a lean,
long-legged spider, and the flies came to him over the
frozen miles.

Yet Kent did not enjoy his gains. The true miser
differs from the spendthrift chiefly in that the one is
tormented before parting with his money and the other afterward.
Kent was tormented continuously. His hoard must be counted, concealed,
reconsidered, removed, re-concealed, and guarded against the population of the
earth, which he divided into thieves who knew about it
and thieves who would steal it if they knew.

Each night, after his guests slept, he drew from its
hiding-place a number of small buckskin sacks. He weighed them
on a pocket balance, pinched their throats, tested the knots,
and calculated the value of their contents by several methods,
distrusting every result and beginning anew. He knew the weight
to the quarter-ounce. He knew what it represented at sixteen
dollars to the ounce, at seventeen, and at the extravagant
rates reported from Dawson. He could estimate how many cabins,
dogs, mules, beans, blankets, and souls might be purchased with
it. He had even calculated the cost of taking it
Outside, though the thought of paying freight gave him pain.

The gold was coarse bench-dust, dark with black sand, but
to Kent it possessed an almost erotic radiance. Under the
candle it gleamed like the eyes of captive serpents. He
ran it through his fingers, slowly, voluptuously, and listened to
its soft gritty whisper upon the tin plate. Cleopatra's pearls
dissolved in wine were vulgar beside it; the golden calf
was a trinket; Mammon himself, had he beheld Kent crooning
over the dust, might have recognized an embarrassing disciple.

But gold attracts not merely thieves. It attracts the idea
of thieves, which is more industrious.

Kent dreamed of them. They came nightly through the door,
down the chimney, under the moss-chinking, and out of the
flour-barrel. They were large men, small men, Indians, sailors, priests,
policemen, and respectable merchants. Some carried knives; some produced receipts;
one claimed to be a government tax-collector, at which Kent
awoke screaming more violently than usual.

Most frequently there appeared a broad-shouldered man with a red
gash descending from the left temple to the corner of
the mouth. This phantom never spoke. He merely smiled, laid
one hand upon the gold, and with the other cut
Kent's throat. The sequence was invariable, though Kent, with the
illogical conservatism of dreamers, never profited by foreknowledge. The hand
descended, the knife flashed, and he awoke clutching his neck.

Thereafter he would light the candle, examine the window, inspect
the door, thrust a poker up the chimney, and verify
his treasure. Sometimes he moved it. He hid it beneath
the bunk, behind a log, in the flour, under the
hearth-stone, among the dog-harness, and once in the stove-pipe—a device
which nearly ruined him when he forgot and kindled the
morning fire.

There was one further complication. Kent walked in his sleep.

Somnambulism is a charitable contrivance by which the body escapes
for a few hours from the tyranny of the mind.
In Kent's case the liberated body displayed no gaiety. It
continued the business of concealment. Men had seen him rise
with closed eyes, shuffle about the cabin, test the latch,
mutter calculations, and return to bed. Once he saddled the
mule at three in the morning and attempted to put
it into a packing-case. Another time he buried twelve pounds
of bacon in the snow, then accused his guests of
eating it.

Kent denied these performances. He distrusted witnesses on principle, and
he held that a man asleep could not properly be
said to perform anything. This metaphysical distinction comforted him, though
it did not recover the bacon.

So matters stood when Jim Cardegee came up the Crooked
Elbow trail.

It was late in January, at that uncertain hour when
the day had ceased without the night having formally begun.
The Yukon lay beneath a white shroud, rough-breasted where pressure-ridges
thrust through the snow. Frost smoked above the open leads.
The spruce-trees, black and rigid, bore their burdens like funeral
mutes. Across this mortuary landscape moved five dogs, a low
sled, and a man whose voice arrived half a mile
before him.

"Gee, you lop-eared sons o' perdition! Gee, I says! Wot
d'you think this is—a bloomin' regatta? Lay into it, Noah!
Straighten that tow-line, Judas! 'Ere, you long-faced swab, I'll teach
you to foul your own rudder!"

The dogs, who had long since discovered that Cardegee's profanity
was atmospheric rather than punitive, trotted with resigned ears. He
strode behind the sled, short, immense in the shoulder, and
bow-legged from years at sea. A sealskin cap sat upon
a crop of grizzled hair; his cheeks were purple with
cold; frost hung in stalactites from his moustache. From his
left temple to the corner of his mouth ran a
red, puckered scar.

Kent saw the scar through the window.

For a moment he did not breathe. His recurring nightmare,
until then an inhabitant of the immaterial world, had put
on sea-boots and was abusing dogs within one hundred yards
of the cabin. Superstition, which is only fear decorated with
theory, sprang full-grown in Kent's mind. Here was proof—conclusive, mathematical
proof—that his dream had been a warning. The man had
come for the gold.

Cardegee kicked snow from his boots and entered without ceremony.

"Evenin', matey. Blisterin' little climate you got 'ere. I've sailed
the Roarin' Forties with the deck-load walkin' overboard, but this
'ere country freezes a man's thoughts before 'e can think
'em."

"Two dollars," Kent said.

Cardegee peered about. "Two dollars wot?"

"Shelter. One for the fire."

"Fire!" Cardegee approached the stove and held his mittens over
it. "You call that a fire? I've seen warmer glows
on a dead cod's conscience."

"Fifty cents for snow-water."

Cardegee turned slowly. The scar twisted when he grinned, and
Kent's scalp tightened.

"Wot's the matter with you?"

"Nothing."

"Then why're you sellin' the weather by the spoonful? Who
made you skipper o' this deck-house?"

"It's my cabin."

"Built it, did you?"

"I own it."

"Aye, an' I own the North Star, but I don't
charge sailors for steerage." Cardegee removed his coat. "Three dollars
an' a half, is it? I'll pay. I ain't goin'
outside to freeze merely to improve your morals."

He flung a small buckskin pouch upon the table. Kent
caught it before it ceased sliding and weighed it in
his palm.

"Light," he said.

"Light? Wot d'you mean, light?"

"Not three dollars and a half."

Cardegee snatched it back, opened it, and spilled several pinches
of dust into Kent's scale. "There! Weigh it, assay it,
marry it, an' raise a family by it. But don't
tell Jim Cardegee 'is reckoning's light."

"You're a sailor."

"Wot's that to you?"

"Where'd you get the scar?"

Cardegee's eyes narrowed. "Wot's that to you?"

"How'd you get it?"

"From a Portuguese second mate who mistook my countenance for
a chart an' tried to lay down a course on
it with a sheath-knife. I altered 'is latitude with a
marlin-spike. Any more family questions?"

Kent made no answer. Cardegee leaned over the table.

"See 'ere, Captain Undertaker, wot's the matter? Ever since I
come through that hatch you've looked at me as if
I was a sea-serpent in your wash-basin."

"I don't like your face."

"Then don't wear it."

Kent's right hand moved toward the shot-gun, which rested on
wooden pegs above his bunk.

Cardegee laughed. "Easy, now. Keep your batteries housed. I've paid
for the fire, an' I'll have no gun-play interruptin' my
enjoyment of it."

He cooked his supper from his own provisions, though Kent
offered beans at a price usually associated with fresh fruit
in mining camps. He talked as he ate. Cardegee had
wintered in whalers, deserted from merchant brigs, hunted seals off
Greenland, smuggled tobacco into unnamed ports, and spent seventeen months
on an island where the inhabitants wore nothing but shark's
teeth and moral disapproval. Whether these adventures were true mattered
little. They filled the cabin, shook the stove, and drove
silence into corners.

Kent watched him. Every movement confirmed guilt. When Cardegee glanced
at the bunk, he was locating the treasure. When he
examined the chimney, he was considering ingress. When he asked
where the trail led, he was planning escape. Innocence itself
would have convicted him, for Kent knew thieves to be
cunning.

At nine o'clock Cardegee rolled himself in his blankets beside
the stove.

"Call me at four bells," he said.

"What?"

"Daybreak, you land-lubbin' hotel-keeper. Me dogs have eighteen mile to
make."

Within five minutes he was asleep and snoring in three
distinct registers.

Kent waited.

He waited one hour, then another, seated rigidly upon his
bunk with the shot-gun across his knees. The candle guttered.
Frost thickened upon the panes. Outside, the wind explored the
eaves with long, thin fingers. Cardegee's snores rose and fell
like surf in a cavern.

At last Kent slipped from the bunk, crossed to the
wall, and removed a short section of moss-chinking behind a
suspended frying-pan. From the cavity he drew seven buckskin sacks
and one twist of oiled cloth. He counted them twice,
weighed three, and pressed the others lovingly between his palms.

Cardegee moved.

Kent extinguished the candle. For ten minutes he crouched in
darkness, the blood drumming in his ears. The sailor grunted,
muttered something concerning a mizzen-top, and resumed snoring.

The hiding-place was compromised. Cardegee had nearly awakened while it
stood open; therefore Cardegee might have seen it while asleep.
To Kent this was reasonable. He must move the treasure.

He considered the stove, rejected it; the flour-barrel, too obvious;
the mule's feed-box, too accessible; the roof, impossible without leaving
tracks. His eyes fell upon the shot-gun.

It was an ancient, heavy, double-barrelled weapon, bought from a
bankrupt trader who declared that it had killed moose. It
had certainly frightened them. The hammers were stiff, the right
nipple worn, and the Damascus barrels bore a suspicious swelling
near the breech. Kent kept both charges in place throughout
the winter—powder, felt wads, and buckshot—because danger, like opportunity, seldom
waited upon reloading.

He stared at the gun, then at Cardegee, then at
the treasure.

Afterward there would be a blank in his recollection.

The mind, overwrought by vigilance and deprived of sleep, withdrew
from its offices. Kent's eyes remained open, but their intelligence
departed. He rose. His movements became solemn, exact, somnambulic. He
took the shot-gun down, removed the ramrod, opened the sacks
one by one, and fed the gold into the muzzles.
Coarse dust, nuggets no larger than peas, and black sand
slid down upon the old charges. He tamped them with
the rod, added scraps of buckskin, tamped again, and poured
in more. The barrels swallowed his wealth until nearly every
ounce had vanished. What remained he wrapped in cloth and
forced after it.

Then he replaced the ramrod, hung the gun above the
bunk, restored the empty sacks to the wall, and lay
down.

At dawn, steel-gray fingers pressed against the window.

Cardegee awoke to an impression of discomfort not immediately traceable
to cold. His arms were drawn behind him. His ankles
were crossed and secured. A rawhide thong ran from wrists
to feet, so that any attempt to straighten one pair
tightened the other. The knots were clumsy but numerous, which
is the coward's equivalent of skill.

Kent sat at the table with the shot-gun across his
knees.

"Wot's this?" Cardegee demanded.

Kent said nothing.

"'Ere! Wot's the matter?"

"My gold."

"Wot gold?"

"You stole it."

Cardegee tested the rawhide. "You been drinkin' lamp-oil?"

"I know you took it."

"You know? You long-faced, frost-bitten son of a bilge-rat, I
never knew you 'ad any gold till this minute."

"I saw you in my dream."

There was a pause.

Cardegee rolled upon one hip and looked earnestly at him.
"Say that again."

"You've been coming for weeks. Scar and all."

"In your dream?"

"You cut my throat."

Cardegee whistled. "An' for this 'ere piece of private navigation
you've trussed me like Sunday beef?"

"You took it during the night."

"I slept all night."

"You pretended."

"You can ask my snorin'."

"I found the sacks empty."

"Then you hid it somewhere."

Kent's face altered. The possibility had occurred to him, but
he had rejected it because it deprived him of an
enemy.

"You stole it," he repeated.

Cardegee spent the next quarter-hour explaining Kent's ancestry, habits, probable
destination after death, and unsuitability for command at sea. His
vocabulary was technical, theological, anatomical, and international. He called upon
the practices of Malays, Portuguese, Barbary pirates, Liverpool boarding-house keepers,
and one female relative of a boatswain in Valparaiso. When
he had exhausted English, he introduced fragments of languages acquired
in ports where grammar was subordinate to knives.

Kent listened with the patience of a gravestone.

At length he said, "I'm going to hang you."

Cardegee ceased abruptly. "Wot?"

"Hang you."

"With wot?"

"There's rope."

"There ain't a beam high enough."

"I'll use the roof-tree."

"You'll pull your own bloomin' cabin down."

Kent considered this objection. He disliked damage to property.

"I'll shoot you," he said.

"That gun?"

Kent nodded.

Cardegee looked at the venerable weapon and laughed, though not
with complete spontaneity. "That old gas-pipe? Why, if you fire
that, it'll kill everybody in the room except the man
you point it at."

"I'll do it outside."

"When?"

"Noon."

"Why noon?"

Kent glanced toward the window. "So it's legal."

The association between noon and legality was obscure, but it
satisfied him. Murder committed at sunrise might be passion; at
midnight, treachery; but noon possessed an official nakedness. Courts adjourned
at noon, sheriffs hanged men at noon, and the sun
itself became a witness. Thus do ceremonial minds provide respectable
clothing for their appetites.

Kent dragged Cardegee outside.

The cold struck like an iron plate. The temperature stood
near fifty below, and the air was so still that
the smoke from the chimney rose in a blue column
before spreading beneath the pallid sky. Cardegee wore boots, shirt,
trousers, and undershirt. Kent allowed him his cap, not from
mercy, but because he feared a condemned man's ears might
freeze before noon and lend an untidy appearance to the
execution.

Beside the cabin was a shallow prospect-hole, eight feet across
and perhaps five deep, abandoned where the diggers had struck
frozen gravel without colour. Snow had drifted halfway over it,
leaving a dark crescent of exposed earth on the southern
side.

Kent propped Cardegee against a stump twelve paces from the
door. He secured the rawhide to a root and placed
a stick upright in the snow.

"When the shadow reaches that mark," he said, pointing to
a line scratched six inches away, "it'll be noon."

Cardegee examined the arrangement.

"Your chronometer's wrong."

"It's right."

"Sun's declination. Equation o' time. Variation of the compass. You
ain't allowed for none of 'em."

Kent stared.

"I was second mate in the Southern Cross," Cardegee continued
confidently, "an' navigated by sun, moon, stars, cook's clock, an'
the captain's temper. That shadow won't make noon for another
two hours."

Kent suspected deceit but did not understand astronomy sufficiently to
identify it. He went into the cabin and returned with
a watch.

"It's ten-fifteen."

"There, wot'd I tell you?"

"I already knew."

Cardegee began working at the rawhide.

He had discovered that one turn around his left wrist
crossed a small iron buckle on his suspender. By rotating
the wrist and sawing patiently, he could abrade the wet
hide. The labour demanded microscopic movement. Kent, seated on a
packing-case with the gun across his knees, watched his prisoner's
face, not his hands.

"Where's the gold?" Kent asked.

"Ask your dream."

"You'll tell before noon."

Cardegee looked at the gun. "Supposin' I don't?"

"I shoot."

"Supposin' I do?"

Kent thought. "I shoot afterward."

"Honest man, ain't you?"

"You know too much."

"I know you charge fifty cents for snow."

Kent's eyes returned to the cabin. The empty buckskin sacks
lay upon the table within. He had examined every hiding-place
he remembered: the wall, flour, stove, bunk, hearth, roof-space, feed-box,
wood-pile. He had searched Cardegee's clothes, blankets, pack, grub-sacks, dog-harness,
boots, and beard. The dust had vanished, and because matter
cannot vanish, Cardegee had taken it. That the sailor had
possessed neither opportunity nor place of concealment was evidence only
of exceptional criminal genius.

Time passed.

The sun, a bloodless disc, edged above the southern ridges.
The shadow crept toward the mark. Cold entered Cardegee by
layers. First his fingers burned, then numbed; next his toes
became separate centres of pain; presently his whole body shook
in spasms which made delicate work upon the rawhide almost
impossible.

He talked to keep his jaw from stiffening.

"You ever see a hangin' in 'Frisco, Jacob?"

Kent had not told him his first name and disliked
hearing it.

"No."

"I did. Man named Billy Pott. Stole seventeen dollars an'
a parrot. Parrot testified ag'in him."

"Birds can't testify."

"This one could. Said, 'Billy done it,' plain as a
bosun's whistle. Judge asked if he'd anything to say. Billy
says, 'Your Worship, the bird's prejudiced.' Then the parrot calls
the judge a red-nosed old thief, an' they hanged Billy
for contempt."

"You lie."

"Course I lie. Wot's it to you?"

The rawhide thinned.

At eleven a wind came whispering over the river. It
lifted loose crystals of snow and drove them low across
the ground, so that the surface seemed to smoke. The
dogs crawled from beneath the sled and whined for food.
Kent ignored them.

Cardegee saw something upon the western trail.

At first it was no more than a black point
wavering among the spruce. Then it emerged upon the open
neck: a dog-team, perhaps a mile distant, descending toward Crooked
Elbow. The driver walked beside the sled.

Cardegee's heart gave a leap, but his face retained its
frozen impudence.

"Company comin'," he said.

Kent turned. The sight disturbed him. An execution was a
private affair until it acquired a witness, whereupon it became
public and might involve explanation. Yet the traveller was distant.
At trail pace he would require forty minutes.

Kent consulted the watch. "Eleven twenty-three."

"Shadow says noon," Cardegee observed.

The tip had nearly touched the scratched line.

"Watch is right."

"Then your sun's wrong."

Kent shifted the stick to correspond with his watch.

Cardegee laughed until he coughed. "That's navigation for you! If
the world don't fit your figures, move the world."

The buckle had cut halfway through the rawhide. Blood from
his scraped wrist froze upon it and made the fibres
brittle. He sawed faster.

The traveller approached. Half a mile. The dogs were tired,
their heads low, tug-lines slack. Cardegee calculated speeds as he
once calculated the converging courses of vessels: the stranger at
two miles an hour, the shadow at its imperceptible rate,
the rawhide yielding fibre by fibre, Kent's suspicion rising with
every glance toward the trail.

At eleven forty Kent rose and went into the cabin.

Cardegee pulled desperately. The hide stretched. One strand parted with
a tiny report. He stopped before Kent returned.

Kent carried a powder-flask and a tin of percussion caps.
Though the shot-gun remained charged, he had no confidence in
the caps that had stood through weeks of damp and
frost. He picked the old ones from the nipples, fitted
fresh copper caps, and snapped the left hammer halfway down
to test its action.

Cardegee watched with professional distaste.

"You'll want to swab them bores," he said.

"They're loaded."

"Been loaded long?"

"Long enough."

"Powder draws damp. Might flash in the pan."

"Percussion guns don't have pans."

"Same family of disaster. Why don't you shoot yourself first,
just to see if she works?"

Kent sat down again.

Eleven forty-eight.

The traveller was now a quarter-mile away. His dogs had
caught the scent of Kent's team and quickened. The faint
jingle of harness came across the snow.

Cardegee twisted his wrists. The severed fibres parted one by
one, but the main turn held. His ankles were still
linked to his hands. Even free, he could not stand
quickly. The pit lay seven feet to his right. Between
him and its edge the snow was level, except for
one buried root.

Eleven fifty-three.

Kent paced twelve steps from the stump and stamped a
firing-place in the snow. He broke the gun at no
hinge, for it possessed none, but drew back both hammers
and set the butt against his shoulder experimentally. The weapon
was abnormally heavy. He attributed this to the dignity of
the occasion.

Cardegee glanced at the stranger. Two hundred yards.

"'Ere, Kent," he called. "Last chance."

"For you."

"No—for you. Cast me off, feed me dogs, an' I'll
forget this voyage."

"Where's my gold?"

"In your head, along with the rest of your vermin."

Kent raised the gun.

"Not yet," Cardegee said sharply. "You said noon."

Kent lowered it. His respect for forms was stronger than
his impatience.

Eleven fifty-seven.

The approaching traveller shouted a greeting. Kent did not answer.
The dogs came on, harness jingling, breath smoking.

Cardegee bent his wrists inward, then flung them apart with
all his strength. The cut rawhide elongated, held, and cut
deeper into his flesh.

Eleven fifty-eight.

Kent's face had become perfectly calm. In imagination he had
already recovered the gold. Cardegee would confess at the last
instant; criminals always confessed. Kent would dig where directed, find
the sacks, and perhaps charge the arriving traveller for witnessing
the execution.

Eleven fifty-nine.

The stranger was less than one hundred yards away. He
saw the bound man, saw Kent with the gun, and
halted in bewilderment. His lead-dog continued until the team bunched
against the sled.

"Hallo!" he called. "What's happening?"

"Stand off!" Kent shouted.

Cardegee felt the rawhide give a quarter-inch.

Kent looked at the watch.

The second hand travelled its small, imperturbable circle. There is
nothing so insolent as a clock in the presence of
human catastrophe. It divides grief, murder, love, and the roasting
of a potato into identical seconds, and ticks for a
dying man with the same economy it observes for a
sleeping one.

"Noon," said Kent.

The gun came up.

Cardegee tore his hands apart. The rawhide snapped. He flung
himself sidewise, but his bound ankles betrayed him. He fell,
rolled over the buried root, and went down the sloping
lip of the prospect-hole in a confusion of legs, rope,
snow, and curses.

Kent pulled both triggers.

The gun did not roar. It detonated.

For one white instant the ancient barrels contained powder-flame, wadding,
buckshot, compacted buckskin, and the concentrated substance of Jacob Kent's
worship. Then the weakened steel opened like a split reed.
The right barrel peeled outward; the left burst at the
swelling. The stock shattered beneath Kent's cheek. A jagged strip
of metal entered his throat, and another crossed his breast.

He remained upright for the fraction of time during which
the body refuses news from the brain. Then he folded
upon the snow.

The stranger ran forward.

Cardegee's head appeared above the rim of the pit.

"Wot's the matter?" he bellowed. "Wot in the seven salted
'ells is the matter now?"

The stranger, a long-faced man in a caribou parka, knelt
beside Kent and touched his neck. There was no need,
but humanity delights in belated formalities.

"He's dead."

"Dead?" Cardegee climbed out, dragging the remains of the rawhide.
"Serve 'im right. Tried to murder me with a condemned
boiler an' blowed 'is own upper works away. Cut these
lashings, matey, before I freeze solid."

The stranger drew a knife and freed him.

They stood over Kent. The snow around his body was
strewn with splintered wood, twisted iron, scraps of buckskin, black
powder-grains, and small dark particles which at first resembled sand.

Then the low sun found them.

A yellow spark shone near Kent's hand. Another winked from
the torn barrel. Cardegee stooped, pinched something between thumb and
finger, and stared.

Gold.

The stranger lifted the broken gun. From its ruptured muzzle
came a thin, glittering stream. Coarse dust pattered upon the
snow; flattened nuggets rolled into the red hollow beside Kent's
shoulder; black sand made delicate lines between them. More gold
lodged in the split barrel, impacted behind rags of buckskin
and wadding.

Cardegee watched it pour.

Slowly the scar from temple to mouth contorted into a
prodigious grin.

"Well, sink me," he said. "There's your cargo."

The stranger looked from the gold to the dead man.
"How did it get in the gun?"

Cardegee considered the empty cabin, the old weapon, the accusation,
and Kent's wild talk of dreams. He remembered hearing movement
in the night—movement which he had accepted as part of
his own sleep. Then he began to laugh.

He laughed until the dogs raised their heads; until the
traveller, comprehending nothing, smiled from contagion; until tears froze upon
his scarred cheeks. He slapped his thighs, stamped his feet,
and addressed the corpse between explosions.

"You thievin' old lunatic! You stole it from yourself! Hid
it in your own gun, then tried to shoot me
with it! Loaded with gold—by thunder, a regular robber baron's
broadside! Wot'd you reckon to do, Kent—send me to heaven
first-class an' pay my passage at the other end?"

The stranger gathered the spilled dust upon a square of
canvas. There were eleven pounds, three ounces, though some had
vanished into the snow and some had been driven, with
iron and shot, into Jacob Kent. The amount was later
entered upon an inventory at Dawson, together with the cabin,
provisions, mule, dogs, scales, and a schedule of debts owed
by deceased travellers who had never agreed that they owed
them.

Cardegee remained at Crooked Elbow two days, because his wrist
was injured and because he held that, having nearly been
murdered there, he was entitled to fire and snow-water without
charge. Before leaving, he removed KENT'S HOTEL from above the
door and split it for kindling.

As for Jacob Kent, he lay beneath a cairn on
the ridge, where the north wind searched eternally among the
stones. Men told the story in camps from Forty Mile
to Dawson, embellishing it according to thirst and distance. Some
said the gun discharged golden bullets; some that Kent's ghost
walked the trail counting sparks in the snow; some that
Cardegee inherited the treasure and squandered it in one magnificent
night. This last was false. The gold went through lawful
channels, which are merely sluice-boxes constructed by clerks.

Yet the essential fact remained untouched by exaggeration. Kent had
guarded his treasure from honest men, thieves, strangers, guests, dreams,
governments, and the casual depredations of Providence. He had hidden
it from the waking world so successfully that even he
could not find it. And in the end, with that
economy of means by which Fate sometimes proves herself a
humorist, he had employed his hoard as the instrument of
his own destruction.

Thus Mammon collected his final fee.

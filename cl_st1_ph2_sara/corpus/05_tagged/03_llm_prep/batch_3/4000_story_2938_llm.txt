### THE LAST THROW

Arthur Kimberlin had eaten nothing for three days, unless one
elects to dignify with the name of food a heel
of bread discovered upon a lodging-house stair and divided, after
grave consideration, between himself and a dog. The dog, being
deficient in honor but rich in teeth, obtained the larger
portion. Since then Arthur had walked the streets of San
Francisco in a rain that was neither violent enough to
excite sympathy nor gentle enough to be ignored; a cold,
patient rain, descending with the deliberate malice of a creditor
who knows that his debtor cannot escape.

He was twenty-seven years old, well born, moderately educated, and
wholly useless. His father had left him an honorable name
and four hundred dollars. The name remained. The money had
gone, first in decent lodgings, then in indecent lodgings, and
finally in those small expenditures by which a gentleman conceals
from the world that he is becoming a beggar. Arthur
could write a cultivated hand, quote Latin incorrectly but impressively,
and distinguish Burgundy from Bordeaux; yet none of these accomplishments
had persuaded the city to feed him.

He had sought employment until hope itself became humiliating. Men
had looked at his frayed cuffs, his clean speech, his
hollow cheeks, and promised to remember him. No man had
remembered. He had pawned his watch, his ring, his overcoat,
and at last the silver-mounted cane that had belonged to
his father. There remained upon his person one nickel, a
pair of gloves too worn to pawn, and a certain
stubborn remnant of pride which, though it possessed no market
value, weighed more heavily than all the rest.

That evening the rain found its way through his coat
and ran in cold threads down his back. He had
passed the same restaurant three times. In its window, beneath
two hissing gas-jets, reposed a roasted turkey, brown and magnificently
swollen, with a paper crown upon its breast; beside it
were a ham glazed like mahogany, a platter of oysters
embedded in green moss, scarlet lobsters, yellow cheeses, pyramids of
oranges, and a silver dish containing a pudding whose rich
sides appeared to tremble with benevolence.

Warm odors breathed through the opening door whenever a patron
entered. They took Arthur by the throat. They filled his
mouth with water and his brain with pictures. He saw
the blade pass through the turkey's crackling skin; saw the
pale steam rise; saw butter melt into floury potatoes; saw
gravy, thick and shining, descend over slices of tender meat.
Then the door shut, and he stood again in rain
and darkness.

He possessed a nickel. A nickel would purchase neither turkey
nor honor; it would purchase only a cup of coffee,
and he feared that the coffee, once swallowed, would awaken
the wolf in him to such ferocity that he must
either steal or scream. Hunger, when it has lasted one
day, is an appetite; on the second, a pain; on
the third, it becomes an intelligence. It argues, invents, commands.
It informs the starving man that every well-fed person has
robbed him personally.

Arthur turned from the window because he had begun to
hate the turkey.

A little farther down the street he found shelter in
the shallow embrasure of a closed tobacconist's shop. Another man
was already there.

"An abominable evening," said the man.

His voice was courteous and exact, but it had a
curious lack of warmth, as if every word had been
cooled before being uttered.

Arthur answered that it was indeed unpleasant and moved aside.
The shelter was scant; still, courtesy may divide what necessity
would monopolize.

The stranger was perhaps fifty years old. He wore a
black coat of excellent cloth, though its shoulders were wet,
and carried no umbrella. His face was newly shaved, with
a whiteness that did not seem natural to living skin.
About the corners of his mouth lay a faint bluish
stain. The nose was thin; the cheekbones were hard; and
the eyes, pale and fixed, regarded Arthur without blinking. One
veined hand rested against his breast. The other moved now
and then toward his chin, closing upon empty air, like
the hand of a man accustomed to stroking a beard
that was no longer there.

Arthur wondered whether the stranger were ill.

"You are cold," the pale man observed.

"A little."

"And hungry."

Arthur stiffened. "I have not complained, sir."

"No. Had you complained, I should not have spoken. Complaint
is the luxury of those who expect relief. Won't you
come in and take something?"

Across the way a red lamp shone above the entrance
to a basement saloon. Arthur hesitated. The invitation was humiliating,
but it was not alms. There are distinctions which remain
very clear to a gentleman until starvation makes them obscure.

"I would willingly accept a glass," he said.

The stranger's purple lip twitched, though whether in amusement or
pain Arthur could not tell.

They crossed the glistening street and descended six steps into
a long, low room yellow with gaslight and tobacco smoke.
A bar of dark wood occupied one side. Behind it
stood bottles of amber, green, and ruby liquor, all shining
with a jewel-like promise. Two clerks drank whiskey near the
stove. At the rear were several private booths, each enclosed
by stained wooden partitions and furnished with a table, two
benches, and a bell.

The bartender glanced at Arthur's clothes and then at his
companion.

"What'll it be?"

The pale man entered the last booth, sat with his
back to the wall, and took from his pocket a
banknote.

"Absinthe," he said. "Two glasses. My young friend will procure
it."

The note passed into Arthur's hand. It was a hundred-dollar
bill.

Its touch produced an extraordinary sensation. It seemed warm—warmer than
the stranger's fingers, warmer than the saloon, almost warmer than
food. A tingling moved from Arthur's palm along his wrist
and into his arm. For one foolish instant he thought
of running. A hundred dollars! Lodging, clothes, breakfasts, dinners, weeks
of human existence! Yet the thought expired beneath the pale
man's gaze.

"The bartender will change it," said the stranger.

Arthur carried it to the bar.

The bartender held it toward the light, looked at the
stranger, looked again at Arthur, and shrugged. He returned with
the absinthe, a small pitcher of water, two perforated spoons,
lumps of sugar, and a heap of change.

Arthur placed everything on the table, counting the money aloud.
The stranger listened with peculiar attention.

"You are honest," he said.

"I hope so."

"Never hope where you may know. Honesty is chiefly exact
arithmetic."

He pushed one glass toward Arthur. Water fell slowly upon
the sugar and descended in cloudy threads through the green
liquor. Arthur drank. The taste was bitter and aromatic; then
came a spreading heat, false but delicious, and for a
moment the terrible emptiness within him seemed clothed.

"You should have ordered food," the stranger said.

"I was invited to drink."

"Ah. You preserve the terms of the contract."

"I mean no offence."

"None is taken. A man who is hungry and yet
literal interests me."

From his coat the stranger produced a small leather cup
and threw three dice upon the table. They rolled softly
and came to rest beneath the gas-jet.

"Do you play?"

"No."

"Then you have not learned to cheat."

Arthur rose halfway. "Sir—"

"Sit down. I accuse you of nothing. Let us play
for amusement. The highest total wins. One dollar."

"I have only a nickel."

The stranger placed a silver dollar before him. "Borrow it."

Arthur looked at the coin.

"If I lose?"

"You owe me a dollar."

"And if I win?"

"You owe me nothing."

This was plainly absurd, but absinthe had made absurdity appear
merely unconventional. Arthur threw. The dice showed two sixes and
a five.

"Seventeen," said the stranger.

His own throw was eleven.

Arthur won again, then lost, then won three times. The
stranger paid without displeasure. His hands were narrow, heavily veined,
and almost transparent beneath the light. Each time he gathered
the dice, his fingers paused upon them as though receiving
some silent information.

"Ten dollars," he said.

"I ought not."

"Ought is a word invented by men afraid to discover
what they will do."

Arthur threw twelve. The stranger threw nine.

"Again."

The second glass of absinthe disappeared. Arthur no longer felt
wet. His coat might have belonged to some unfortunate person
standing miles away in the rain. The booth had contracted
to the dimensions of the green bottle, the white dice,
the pale face, and the money lying between them.

The stakes rose to fifty dollars, then one hundred. Arthur
protested, but weakly. He won with fourteen against thirteen; with
nine against eight; with sixteen against fifteen. When his throw
was poor, the stranger's was poorer. When Arthur cast three
sixes, the pale man's hand went suddenly to his breast.

"Are you ill?" Arthur asked.

"No."

"You are very white."

"I have always been accused of that."

"But your breathing—"

"Throw."

Arthur threw.

Money accumulated before him. Notes were drawn from an inner
pocket of the stranger's coat, always in neat packets, always
bound with narrow paper bands. The sight of them should
have sobered him; instead it intoxicated him more deeply than
the absinthe. Five hundred dollars became one thousand. One thousand
became five. At ten thousand Arthur attempted to stop.

"This is madness," he said.

"Madness is merely courage observed by a timid person."

"I cannot take so much from you."

"You have not taken it. You have won it."

"But I do not understand the game."

"There is nothing to understand. The cubes turn; the spots
appear; men call the result fortune because ignorance requires a
name."

The stranger poured him another glass. Arthur drank, though his
stomach revolted. A greenish mist seemed to lie between his
eyes and the world. The gas flame elongated; the wood
grain of the table writhed like worms; yet the dice
remained brilliantly distinct.

At twenty thousand dollars the stranger smiled. It was a
dreadful smile, involving no alteration of the eyes.

"At last," he murmured.

"At last what?"

"You begin to resemble a man who owns something."

Arthur looked at the notes. They meant meat, warmth, a
room, a coat, restored credit, restored dignity. They meant the
power to walk past the restaurant window and command that
the turkey be brought whole. Why should he stop? The
money had been risked freely. Honor permitted one to retain
that which another gentleman had honorably lost.

The pale man raised the stakes.

Arthur continued to win.

Now thirty thousand lay before him; now forty-two; now sixty.
He ceased counting accurately. The stranger arranged the notes himself,
tapping their edges into order with a rigid forefinger. His
breath came in shallow intervals. Once his head dropped forward,
but he raised it and stared at Arthur with such
concentrated hostility that the young man's elation turned to fear.

"Blink," Arthur said suddenly.

The stranger did not move.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You do not blink."

"Do you require me to?"

"No, certainly not."

"Then throw."

It is a curious fact that a man may sit
opposite death and concern himself with etiquette. He may perceive
the blue lip, the marble brow, the unnatural fixity of
the eyes, and yet refrain from crying out because a
cry would be ill-mannered. Decency has slain its thousands; embarrassment,
its tens of thousands.

Arthur threw fifteen.

The stranger's dice showed seven.

"Seventy-four thousand dollars," he said.

The words made no immediate impression. Seventy-four thousand was not
money; it was geography. It was streets of houses, years
of dinners, rooms blazing with coal fires. Arthur's mind tried
to contain it and failed.

"You have another seventy-four thousand," he said, looking at the
remaining packets.

"I have."

"Then you began with one hundred and forty-eight thousand."

The stranger's left hand rose toward the absent beard. "Did
I?"

A noise in the neighboring booth caused both men to
listen. Two persons had entered there. The partition did not
reach the ceiling, and their voices, though subdued, passed clearly
through the tobacco smoke.

"Waste of time," said one. "He'll be across the bay
by now."

"He cannot travel far," replied the other. "The doctor swears
his heart might stop any hour."

Arthur felt the pale man's gaze sharpen.

The first voice continued: "One hundred and forty-eight thousand dollars,
and not a bill recovered."

"He shaved the beard. That was clever."

"Not clever enough. I'd know those eyes. Never saw a
man stare like Corvin. And the hands—white, veiny hands, always
touching his chin where the beard used to be."

Arthur's blood seemed to withdraw from his skin.

The detectives—for detectives they plainly were—ordered drinks. Glasses struck the
neighboring table.

"I'm told he's unbeatable with dice," said the second man.
"Not lucky. Unbeatable. Has a trick of changing them in
the cup, or weighting them, or God knows what. Cleaned
out mining camps all through Nevada."

"A bank robber and a sharper. Admirable fellow."

"Dangerous, too, if his heart holds."

Arthur looked across the table.

The stranger was still watching him, but all expression had
gone from his face. For the first time Arthur understood
that the man's courtesy had not been kindness. He had
invited a starving fool out of the rain because he
required diversion—or a victim—or perhaps merely an audience before whom
to exercise that monstrous vanity which insists upon proving its
power even at the edge of death.

Corvin spoke softly.

"You have heard?"

"I could not avoid hearing."

"Then we need no longer play at introductions."

Arthur's hand moved toward the door.

The stranger drew the bolt.

"You will remain."

"Those men—"

"Will depart presently. You possess seventy-four thousand dollars of mine."

"I won it."

"Precisely. And I intend to win it back."

Arthur glanced at the partition. A cry would bring the
detectives. Yet Corvin's right hand had slipped beneath his coat,
and Arthur imagined a revolver there.

"One throw," said Corvin. "Your seventy-four thousand against mine. The
entire sum."

"No."

"You refuse?"

"I have won enough."

"Enough?" Corvin repeated, as if the word offended him. "There
is no enough. There is only all, and that which
is less than all."

His face had grown ghastly. Sweat stood upon the white
forehead. The hand beneath his coat came out empty and
pressed fiercely against his breast.

"You are ill," Arthur whispered.

"One throw."

"I will call those gentlemen."

"And explain that you willingly gambled with stolen money? That
you drank at my expense and attempted to depart with
half the proceeds of a robbery? How persuasive will hunger
look in a police court?"

Arthur could not reason; he could not understand. The absinthe
burned greenly in his brain. Seventy-four thousand dollars! He had
possessed it for less than a minute, yet already the
thought of surrendering it was like the thought of having
his flesh torn away.

"One throw," Corvin repeated. "You have won every time. Are
you afraid of fortune?"

Arthur seized the cup.

Corvin stopped him. "We use my dice."

"Why?"

"You heard them. I am unbeatable."

The hard smile returned.

Arthur ought to have shouted. He ought to have overturned
the table, unbolted the door, appealed to the detectives. Instead
he laughed. The laugh surprised him; it sounded thin and
reckless, the laugh of a stranger whose belly was full
and whose life had never known prudence.

"Very well," he said. "All."

They arranged the money in the middle of the table.
One hundred and forty-eight thousand dollars made a remarkably small
pile. Wealth, when reduced to paper, occupies less space than
a loaf of bread.

Arthur threw first.

The dice tumbled, struck the green bottle, and stopped.

Six. Six. Five.

"Seventeen," Arthur breathed.

Only one throw could defeat him.

Corvin took up the cup. His fingers had become clumsy.
One die slipped and rolled beneath the table. He bent
to recover it, remaining out of sight so long that
Arthur wondered if he had fallen. Then he rose, whiter
than before, and placed the die with the others.

From the next booth came the scrape of chairs.

"Let's try the waterfront," said one detective.

Their footsteps retreated. The outer door opened and closed.

Corvin shook the cup. The faint rattle seemed to continue
for an immeasurable time. Arthur watched the purple lip, the
fixed eyes, the hand at the breast. He thought: The
heart will fail before he throws. He thought it without
pity.

The dice fell.

Three sixes.

For several seconds neither man spoke.

Then Corvin said, "All."

The word was scarcely audible.

Arthur stared at the dice. Their black spots widened and
contracted. Three sixes—eighteen—the single throw that could defeat him. The
notorious cheat had done what he had promised. Arthur's fortune,
his dinners, clothes, rooms, and restored existence had crossed the
table and vanished.

"You cheated," he said.

Corvin did not answer.

"You cheated!"

The pale eyes stared directly into his. There was triumph
in them—or Arthur believed there was triumph. He waited for
Corvin to gather the notes. Corvin did not move.

Minutes may be measured by clocks, but terror employs a
different instrument. Arthur heard water dripping somewhere behind the bar;
heard the hiss of gas; heard his own heart and,
beneath it, the vast silence where another heart should have
been.

Corvin sat unnaturally erect.

Arthur reached across and touched his wrist.

It was cold.

He snatched his hand away. Corvin continued to stare, the
eyes wide, hard, victorious. Yet the victory belonged only to
their position. No breath disturbed the bluish lips. No pulse
moved beneath the transparent skin.

The robber was dead.

Arthur sat back and began to tremble.

Here was one hundred and forty-eight thousand dollars. Here was
a notorious criminal, dead by the act of God or
absinthe or his own vicious heart. The money had been
stolen. Therefore it belonged to the bank. Honor demanded that
Arthur summon the detectives.

Honor spoke clearly.

Hunger spoke louder.

He waited, as if the corpse might object. Corvin's gaze
seemed to know exactly what was occurring in him. It
accused before the theft; it despised before the surrender. Arthur
rose, sat down, and rose again.

"I won half," he whispered. "You cheated for the rest."

The dead man offered no contradiction.

Arthur gathered the packets. They were wonderfully substantial. He thrust
them into his coat, his trousers, beneath his waistcoat, wherever
a pocket or fold would receive them. Some he carried
in his hands. Then he unbolted the booth and ascended
from the saloon.

The rain had ceased.

San Francisco shone beneath him—lamps swimming in the wet streets,
windows burning gold, carriage wheels flashing, roofs descending toward the
black bay. The air struck his face with a freshness
so exquisite that he laughed aloud. His weakness had disappeared.
His legs were light, his lungs immense. He could have
run from the waterfront to the hills without drawing breath.

Food! He would eat immediately.

No—why immediately? A man with one hundred and forty-eight thousand
dollars need not hurry. Haste belonged to poverty. He would
walk first, deliberately, magnificently, permitting his appetite to ripen. He
would enter the restaurant whose turkey had insulted him and
buy not a slice, not a dinner, but the establishment.

He pictured the proprietor approaching.

"Your order, sir?"

"The restaurant."

"I beg pardon?"

"I will take the restaurant—walls, tables, stoves, waiters, turkey, and
that pudding which trembles so offensively. Name your price."

Then he would open the doors and admit every hungry
creature in San Francisco. Newsboys, sailors, seamstresses, Chinamen, widows, thieves,
drunkards, dogs—especially dogs, for he owed a debt to one—should
sit beneath the chandeliers while cooks rushed forth with smoking
platters.

There would be turkeys by the hundred, each brown and
bursting; hams thick as millstones; steaks tender enough to be
eaten with spoons; oysters in silver baths; geese swimming in
gravy; mountains of potatoes breaking open beneath yellow butter; soups
fragrant with onions; pies containing whole orchards; puddings as large
as church domes; coffee in shining urns; cream in buckets;
bread warm from the oven, white bread and brown bread,
rolls and biscuits and loaves so fresh that the crusts
sang when broken.

Arthur hurried toward the restaurant.

But no. It was insufficient. One restaurant could feed only
a few hundred. He would purchase all the restaurants. He
would abolish menus and prices. No man should again stand
in rain outside a window and hate a turkey.

He passed a bakery. The smell of hot bread struck
him like a hand. His knees weakened, and darkness fluttered
at the edges of his sight.

He could enter. He could buy everything there.

Yet he did not. To eat now, from a common
counter, would be grotesque. His first meal as a wealthy
man must be ceremonial. There should be music. There should
be flowers, golden plates, crystal glasses, waiters in white gloves.
Perhaps the mayor ought to attend. Perhaps the bells of
the city ought to be rung.

Moreover, he was no longer hungry. Hunger had gone beyond
pain into sublimity. It filled him without occupying him. It
was a pure white flame upon which his thoughts rose
like sparks.

He climbed a hill with impossible ease. The houses bowed
as he passed. Behind their windows families ate their little
suppers, unaware that Arthur Kimberlin had conquered want. He pitied
them. They possessed roast beef for one evening; he possessed
all future banquets. They swallowed; he imagined. Which was the
greater nourishment?

At the crest of the hill the city spread below,
jeweled and enormous. He raised both arms.

"Eat!" he commanded it.

The word rolled down the streets, entered alleys and cellars,
penetrated locked rooms. The poor awoke and found tables beside
their beds. Dead children sat up laughing, their mouths stained
with jam. Prisoners gnawed through bars made suddenly of sugar.
The bay itself thickened into soup—rich, dark, steaming soup—and ships
became silver spoons upon it.

Arthur laughed until tears ran down his face.

Corvin walked beside him now.

The dead gambler had recovered his beard, a long black
beard dripping rain. He carried the dice in one white
hand.

"You have not eaten," Corvin observed.

"I shall eat when all have eaten."

"They will never all have eaten."

"Then I shall purchase more."

"With what?"

Arthur struck his pockets. The packets were there, warm and
abundant.

"With this."

Corvin opened his hand. Three dice lay upon the palm,
each showing six.

"All," he said.

Arthur turned away. The street tilted sharply, becoming the floor
of the saloon booth. Lamp-posts narrowed into chair legs. The
moon changed to a gas flame enclosed in smoky glass.
Yet this was absurd. He was outside; he was rich;
he was on his way to a banquet.

A door appeared before him, tall as the gates of
heaven. Beyond it waited the turkey, the hams, the oysters,
the steaks, the golden soup, the mountainous puddings, all breathing
warmth. Arthur reached for the handle.

His hand would not rise.

He could not reason; he could not understand.

The door receded. The banquet lights diminished to three black
spots, then three more, then three more—eighteen in all, fixed
forever upon white bone.

"No," Arthur said. "I have the money."

Across from him Corvin stared.

Arthur attempted to stand, but the night folded softly about
his shoulders, warmer than any coat. He thought the restaurant
had opened at last. He smelled bread. He heard plates,
music, laughter, and a great multitude entering from the rain.

He smiled in welcome—

* * *

Near midnight the two detectives returned to the basement saloon.
Their search of the waterfront had yielded nothing, and one
of them wished for whiskey before reporting failure.

The bartender was wiping glasses.

"Two men still in the back booth?" asked the elder
detective.

The bartender glanced down the room. "Haven't heard a sound
from 'em. Door's bolted."

The detectives exchanged a look.

They called. There was no answer.

One climbed over the partition while the other forced the
bolt with a knife. When the door opened, the gas
was still burning above the table, though the green bottle
had fallen upon its side and a dark stain had
spread across the wood.

The money remained there in ordered packets: one hundred and
forty-eight thousand dollars.

The dice showed three sixes.

Corvin sat against the wall, his dead hand pressed to
his breast. Opposite him sat Arthur Kimberlin, equally still, his
face turned toward the fortune and wearing a faint, almost
hospitable smile.

The physician came and said that the older man's diseased
heart had ceased at the instant of triumph. Of the
younger, he spoke of prolonged starvation, exposure, and absinthe taken
upon an empty stomach. Such explanations satisfy the law and
are therefore useful.

But the elder detective, looking at the two rigid figures
staring across the money they could no longer win or
lose, remarked that both men appeared to have died rich.

The bartender shook his head.

"No," he said. "They died looking at it."

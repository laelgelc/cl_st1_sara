I

John Adderley Syson left the train at Wellingborough Halt with
a small leather bag, although he had no need of
luggage. He was to sleep in town at the King's
Head and return to London next afternoon. The bag, like
the silver-headed cane he carried, belonged to the ceremonious version
of himself which appeared whenever he went back among people
who had known him poor.

The April morning shone with a pale, uncommitted brightness. Beyond
the station hedge the country lifted in long green backs,
each hill carrying its dark wood like a bearskin garment
flung over one shoulder. Rain had fallen before dawn. The
road was black at the edges, and in every hoof-mark
a little sky trembled.

He stood listening. Somewhere behind the goods shed a chaffinch
poured out its hard, crystalline phrase. A cart moved invisibly
in the valley. Then, from the wet fields, came the
smell of young grass, clay, nettles, and the cold white
flowering of blackthorn.

Still the same, he thought.

The thought moved through him almost with physical sweetness. The
years in London—his marriage, his reviews and lectures, rooms blue
with tobacco smoke, dinners where clever persons glimmered and vanished
like fish in an aquarium—dropped away. He was twenty-two again,
lean and ardent, walking these roads with books under his
arm and Hilda Millership waiting at the farm.

He turned from the high road into a lane whose
banks rose above his shoulders. Hazel branches leaned out, their
first leaves opening like glad little hands. Beneath them grew
dog-mercury, dark and dull, and here and there woodruff made
a green star. Primroses watched from the roots, not merely
yellow as he remembered them, but cream, faint pink, even
one bruised into a pale purple at the heart. The
earth had been prodigal while he was away.

Yet he had not been away entirely. For nine years
he had written to Hilda: first every week, then every
month, then whenever some sudden remembrance obliged him. Books went
with the letters—Dante, French verse, a little volume of William
Morris, reproductions of Botticelli women who carried their remote, curved
mouths through worlds of sorrow. At Christmas he sent silk,
scent, once a small Florentine brooch. He had imagined these
things entering the farmhouse and making a secret chamber there:
an apartment of the mind where he and Hilda continued
unchanged.

Marian knew of the letters. He had never hidden them
from his wife, because concealment would have made them vulgar.
Marian had smiled in her delicate fashion and called Hilda
his woodland saint.

"A saint ought to have relics," Syson had answered.

He had said it brilliantly. He always knew when he
had said something brilliantly; the knowledge gave him a little
internal bow.

Now, nearing the first spinney, he wondered if Hilda wore
the brooch. He saw it at her throat, fastening the
plain brown stuff she preferred. She would have grown fuller,
perhaps, but not altered. Her nature had been finished early,
like the kernel inside a nut: close, pale, complete. She
had needed only warmth—his warmth—to ripen.

A pheasant rose almost beneath his feet, beating the air
with a furious noise. Syson started and laughed. The bird
sailed heavily across the young larches, its copper body burning
in the sunlight, and disappeared into the private wood.

A notice was nailed to an oak:

TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED.

The path he wanted ran directly beneath it.

He paused, amused by the coarse legal word. Trespass! As
though familiarity could become trespass merely because years had intervened.
He had walked there before the present owner bought the
estate; Hilda had shown him the path. On one June
evening they had found white butterflies asleep upon the grasses,
innumerable and frail, like petals that had forgotten to fall.

He climbed the bank and entered.

Immediately the world changed. The road, fields, and far farms
were extinguished. Pine trunks rose brick-red and purplish from mats
of brown needles. In the hollows, bluebells spread in azure
shallows, so dense that the eye seemed to wade through
colour. Here and there a late daffodil stood among them,
a yellow ruffled bird surprised into stillness. Sunlight came down
in separate columns, powdering the gnats.

Syson walked softly, almost reverently. His own footfall offended him.
This was the eternal country, the preserved country, where youth
remained stored beneath leaves. He felt that if he turned
sharply enough he might catch sight of his former self—a
narrow-shouldered young man in a worn coat—passing between the trees
ahead.

Instead he saw another man.

The fellow stood in the path twenty yards away, a
gun broken over one arm. He was young, perhaps twenty-five,
with a brown, narrow face and eyes of a peculiar
grey brightness. His keeper's coat fitted badly at the shoulders
because the shoulders themselves thrust too strongly. Even at rest
he seemed balanced for movement, the chest lifted, the legs
lightly apart. Syson had an impression not of strength exactly,
but of animal tension, life wound tight upon itself.

The keeper waited until Syson approached.

"This path's private."

His voice was quiet. That made the challenge more distinct.

"I am aware of the notice," Syson replied.

"Then you can read."

Syson glanced at him. "I congratulate us both."

The keeper's eyes did not alter.

"You'll have to go back."

"I am going to Millership's farm."

"Road's round the other side."

"I know the road. I also know this path."

"You've no leave to use it."

Syson felt the old country, which a moment before had
opened to receive him, contract with sudden hostility. He smiled.

"My name is Syson. John Adderley Syson."

He expected recognition, though he could not have said why.

It came. The keeper shifted the gun slightly and looked
at him from boots to hat.

"Oh," he said. "You."

The monosyllable struck more intimately than any greeting.

"You have the advantage of me."

"Arthur Pilbeam."

The name was familiar. Hilda had mentioned a Pilbeam family:
labourers at Langley End, people of no account except that
one of them bred good lurchers. Syson searched his letters
and found, two years back, a reference to "young Arthur,
who has taken the keepering." Nothing more.

"You know Miss Millership, I believe," Pilbeam said.

"I have known her since she was sixteen."

"That ain't what I asked."

"No?" Syson's laughter sounded in the wood, thin and brilliant.
A blackbird sprang from the bracken at the noise. "Then
perhaps you will improve the question."

Pilbeam's brown face reddened under the cheekbones.

"You've kept sending things."

"Have I?"

"You know you have. Letters. Books. A little gold pin."

"Silver-gilt, if the matter requires accuracy."

"She don't want 'em."

"She has not told me so."

"She wouldn't."

"Then I must decline to receive her wishes through a
gamekeeper."

For the first time Pilbeam moved toward him. It was
no more than half a pace, yet Syson felt his
own body become conspicuously slight, metropolitan, dressed. The young man's
hands were reddish and scratched, one thumb blackened at the
nail. They looked capable of taking hold.

"You leave her be," Pilbeam said. "You've got a wife."

The wood seemed to listen. Far overhead a pigeon beat
its wings and settled.

Syson's smile remained, but it belonged now to his face
alone.

"My domestic arrangements can hardly concern you."

"They concern her."

"On the contrary. Miss Millership and I have an understanding
that existed before you were old enough to carry that
gun."

Pilbeam's mouth hardened.

"Understanding! You've got a fine word for it. You went
off. She stayed. You married some town woman. Then you
send poetry back, as if words was a bit of
string you could tie round her."

Syson stared at him.

For one instant the young man appeared grotesque—an underkeeper presuming
to interpret relations whose refinement he could not conceive. Then
something in the phrase about string caused Syson an obscure
uneasiness. It had Hilda's severity in it. Had she spoken
so? Had these two discussed him in some low room,
at some hour of bodily confidence?

The thought gave him a little sickness.

"You seem well informed," he said.

"I know what I know."

"And what, precisely, is your claim?"

The keeper went still. In that stillness Syson recognised the
answer before it came.

"She loves me."

There was no triumph in the statement. Pilbeam spoke almost
angrily, as if love were a difficult animal entrusted to
him, apt to escape or bite.

Syson looked past him into the bluebells. Their colour had
become excessive, unreal, a painted phosphorescence lying over the dead
leaves.

"My dear fellow," he said, "that is a large assertion."

"You ask her."

"I intend to."

Pilbeam hesitated. Jealousy showed nakedly in his face, and because
it was naked Syson despised it; because it was powerful
he envied it.

"You going there now?"

"Yes."

"She knows?"

"No."

The keeper closed the gun with a small, decisive click.

"You'd best take the road."

Syson stepped aside from the path, as though examining a
spray of hyacinth.

"I shall take whichever way pleases me."

For several seconds neither man moved. Then Pilbeam's anger passed
into something like misery.

"Don't make trouble for her."

The appeal, sudden and unwilling, disarmed Syson more than threat
had done. He looked again at the young body, the
thrusting chest beneath its rough cloth, the anxious grey eyes.
This was not a rival fashioned from his own scale
of values. It was another order of being.

"I have never made trouble for Hilda," he said.

Pilbeam gave a short, incredulous breath.

"You don't know, do you?"

"Know what?"

But the keeper had turned away. He went between the
pines without looking back, moving so lightly that the wood
seemed to close behind him.

Syson remained among the bluebells. A linnet called from the
sunny edge; the sound fell bright and minute into the
silence.

He had come home, and already he had been accused
of trespass.

II

Millership's farm stood in a folded valley beyond the wood.
Its roofs appeared first, reddish-brown among orchards foamed with blossom;
then the long stone wall, a straw stack, and the
dark squares of windows. A brook went past the garden,
carrying the reflection of marsh-marigolds so that gold floated brokenly
over brown water.

Syson stopped at the gate.

Nothing was altered. The iron latch retained its bent neck.
A stone trough leaned beneath the yew. Even the elder
tree by the dairy had the same split trunk, though
one half was dead and diapered with grey fungus. The
place endured with an almost insulting fidelity.

He had expected emotion, perhaps tears. Instead he felt watched
by his own memory.

A woman crossed the yard carrying a basket of linen.
For a moment he thought it was Mrs Millership; then
she set down the basket and raised her face.

"You?—Addy!"

Hilda stood with both hands resting on the wicker rim.

She was not as he had pictured her, and yet
no single alteration could account for the difference. Her body
had become full without heaviness. The sleeves were rolled above
her elbows, showing firm arms coloured by sun; her throat
rose brown and smooth from an open collar. Her hair,
once soft and indefinite, was gathered in a thick crown
the colour of beech leaves after rain. The face was
broader. Only the eyes remained their old greenish hazel, though
even these seemed to look from deeper within herself.

"Hilda," he said.

She came to him, not running, but with a quickness
that shook the light across her face. She held out
both hands. He took them and kissed her cheek. Her
skin was warm; she smelled faintly of soap, linen, and
cows.

"Well!" she said. "You never told us."

"I wanted to find out whether surprise still existed in
England."

"You look very grand."

"And you look—"

He paused. The correct phrase failed him.

"Like a farm woman?" she supplied.

"Like yourself."

She studied him, and he had the uncomfortable sense that
her study was more exact than his own. He had
put on weight about the eyes; his hair was thinning
at the temples. In London these changes composed distinction. Here,
under Hilda's clear gaze, they might signify merely time.

"Come in," she said. "Mother's lying down. Her rheumatism's bad."

They entered the kitchen. The flagged floor shone from washing.
Copper pans gave back small distorted suns; bunches of thyme
and lavender hung from the beam; on the deep sill
a bowl held scarlet and pink primroses mixed with pale
purple ones. Syson recognised the blue bowl. Once Hilda had
filled it with cherries while he read aloud from Dante,
translating carelessly and stopping whenever she laughed.

He touched the rim.

"You kept it."

"Why shouldn't I?"

"Things disappear."

"Some do."

She set bread and cheese on the table, then brought
cider. There was no flutter in her service. She moved
calmly in the room, her skirt brushing her ankles, her
bare forearms crossing and recrossing before him. He watched them
with a curiosity that seemed almost disloyal to his memories.
He had always thought of her hands—her fine fingers holding
a page, a flower, a pencil. He had forgotten that
the hands belonged to arms, the arms to a living
and considerable body.

"You've been well?" she asked.

"Industriously well. Marian sends her regards."

The name entered the kitchen like a person dressed for
another climate.

"That was kind."

"You would like her."

"Would I?"

"She has great delicacy of perception."

Hilda cut the bread. "Then perhaps she wouldn't like me."

"Why not?"

"I've not much delicacy."

"My dear Hilda, you have the finest instinct of anyone
I know."

She smiled slightly. "That isn't what you used to say."

"What did I say?"

"That I must learn to distinguish. Everything was distinctions with
you. This love from that love, beauty from prettiness, feeling
from sensation. You were forever sorting me."

He laughed, but his hand tightened on the glass.

"At twenty-two one sorts the universe. Later one is grateful
if it remains in the cupboard."

"You still talk the same."

"Surely not."

"The words have grown better clothes."

He looked up sharply. Her expression was tranquil, almost affectionate.
This made the thrust worse.

Outside, hens scratched under the lilac. A bee blundered against
the window, fell upon its back, and revolved furiously.

"I met your gamekeeper," he said.

The knife stopped in Hilda's hand.

"Arthur?"

"Pilbeam. Yes."

"Where?"

"In Stainby Wood."

"You went through the private path?"

"I always went through it."

"It's preserved now."

"So Pilbeam explained with admirable feudal gravity."

Hilda freed the bee with the corner of a cloth
and opened the casement. It flew out into the white
blossom.

"What else did he explain?"

"Among other matters, that my correspondence causes distress."

She turned from the window. A faint colour had appeared
beneath her brown skin.

"He had no right."

"Then it is true?"

"What is true?"

"That my letters trouble him. That you—" He disliked the
bluntness of the words and yet had no finer ones.
"That you love him."

Hilda leaned against the sill. The orchard behind her was
a confusion of white flowers and blue air, making a
brightness around her head which some former Syson would have
called a halo. He saw the image arise, saw its
falsity, and could not prevent it.

"Yes," she said.

The simple answer seemed to pass through him without finding
a place to lodge.

"You love Arthur Pilbeam."

"Yes."

"Well." He lifted his glass and found it empty. "The
sentence acquires no greater plausibility through repetition."

"Why isn't it plausible?"

"My dear girl—"

"Don't call me that."

The refusal was quiet.

He set down the glass. "Very well. Why him?"

"Because he is himself."

"Most people suffer from that condition."

"He doesn't pretend it's something else."

"And I do?"

"You make yourself into words. Then you make everyone else
into words too."

Syson felt anger approach, cool and lucid. "This is unexpectedly
metaphysical."

"It's plain enough. Arthur knows what I am."

"And what are you?"

"A woman living here."

"Nothing more?"

"Everything more. But here."

He rose and walked to the hearth, where the old
black kettle breathed on the hob. Over the mantel hung
a coloured print he had given her, Botticelli's Primavera, faded
now to greenish ghosts. The pale women stepped forever through
their unreal grove, carrying flowers no season had grown.

"You once desired another life," he said.

"I desired what you made me see."

"That is a cruel way of describing awakening."

"Was I asleep?"

"You had never been given the means to express yourself."

"And you gave them?"

"I tried."

She folded the cloth, aligning its corners. "You wanted me
to become a person you could admire."

"I admired you already."

"No. You admired what you thought was hidden in me.
A nun, sometimes. A goddess sometimes. Your Beatrice in muddy
boots." Her mouth curved. "Never me."

His anger brightened into pain.

"That is unjust. I believed in your possibilities when no
one else did. I brought you books. I shared everything
I knew."

"Yes."

"You encouraged me too. Have you forgotten? When I had
the chance to go to London, it was you who
said I must. You said I would suffocate here."

"You would have."

"You glorified ambition. You told me my work mattered."

"It did matter."

"And now my leaving is recast as abandonment."

"I didn't say abandonment."

"Your keeper did."

"He's jealous."

"He is also, I suspect, your spokesman."

"No." Her voice hardened. "He speaks badly enough for himself.
You needn't put my words into his mouth, nor his
into mine."

There it was again: the refusal of his arrangement. He
felt as though the room, once so receptive to his
presence, had acquired edges against which he continually struck.

Hilda came to the table and sat opposite him.

"When you went away," she said, "I thought I should
go too. Not after you. Somewhere else. I read the
books and tried to think in the way you'd taught
me. Every feeling had to mean another feeling. Every tree
was a symbol. If I was lonely, I told myself
loneliness was freedom preparing itself."

"Was that phrase mine?"

"It might have been."

"It is rather good."

She looked at him, and unwilling amusement passed between them.
For a moment they were restored: conspirators against solemnity, sitting
in the old kitchen while rain shut out the world.
The moment was so complete that he leaned toward her.

"Hilda—"

But she had already withdrawn.

"Then Father was ill," she continued. "There was work. Mother
couldn't manage. I stayed. For years I thought staying meant
I had failed."

"Did it not?"

"No."

The word dropped with the density of a stone.

She looked down at her hands. "One spring I was
cutting osiers by the brook. It had flooded, and I
stepped too far and went in up to my waist.
Arthur pulled me out. We sat under the alders while
my clothes dried. He didn't say I was a river
nymph or that the flood was the dark water of
the soul. He laughed at me. Then he gave me
his coat."

"How irresistible."

"Not then. I thought him stupid."

"And now?"

"Now I know stupidity has many kinds."

Syson bowed.

"I walked into that."

"You always walk where there's a notice."

He turned to the window. Beyond the orchard, the hill
climbed toward Stainby Wood. The pines stood black-green under a
sky now deepening into blue. A yellowhammer repeated its little
descending song from the hedge, monotonous and exact.

"Do you intend to marry him?"

Hilda was silent.

He looked back. Her face had changed. The assurance remained,
but beneath it lay some thought she would not easily
offer him.

"No," she said at last. "Not yet."

"Does he know?"

"Yes."

"And he accepts this pastoral uncertainty?"

"He doesn't like it."

"Then what is it? Prudence? Your father's will? A recognition,
perhaps, that instinct has social limitations?"

"It is freedom."

He laughed. The sound was brilliant again, scattering from the
copper and glass.

"Freedom! Forgive me. I had not expected to find our
old abstractions domesticated so near the pigsties."

"You see? If I use a word, it is because
you once lent it me."

"I do not claim copyright in the English language."

"You claim more than that."

He was suddenly tired. He sat down.

"What do you mean by freedom?"

Hilda answered slowly. "I mean that Arthur comes because he
wants me, and I go because I want him. Not
because the farm needs a man, or Mother needs someone
in the house, or because people say it is time.
If I marry him now, everything will close round us—fields,
wages, babies, his temper, my duty. Perhaps I shall marry
him later. But what we have now is ours."

The candour of it disturbed him more profoundly than a
confession of secret marriage could have done. He saw not
innocence deceived by appetite, nor a rustic engagement awaiting respectability,
but a woman deliberately holding open a space for desire.
This did not accord with his Hilda. His Hilda had
possessed a severe inward chastity, a white flame beyond touch.

"You are his mistress," he said.

The ugliness was intentional.

Hilda flushed, but did not lower her eyes.

"If that is the name you need."

"And this is fulfilment?"

"Sometimes."

He got up abruptly. The kitchen seemed too warm, dense
with bread, herbs, Hilda's living body. The Botticelli women on
the wall had become absurdly bloodless.

"I should like to see the garden," he said.

They went outside.

Cloud-shadows travelled over the fields. Along the brook, kingcups burned
among reeds, while above them two martins crossed and recrossed
the water, stitching the air. Hilda walked without a hat.
At the lower gate she put her hand upon the
top bar and looked over the meadow.

"This was where you read me the poem about Proserpine,"
she said.

"You remember."

"I remember nearly everything."

The admission stirred hope against his will.

"Then it was not all illusion."

"No. It happened."

"You speak as though that were less than meaning."

"Things happen first. Meaning comes after and changes them."

He looked at her profile—the firm chin, the sunlit down
upon her cheek, the thick hair pinned above the nape.
How had he once seen only a spiritual pallor in
this woman? There was nothing pale about her. She was
brown, green, warm-coloured, a plant rooted heavily in its own
earth.

"You might have come with me," he said.

"No."

"You said you wanted to."

"I wanted to want it."

"With education, with companionship—"

"I should have died."

"Not literally, I assume."

"Perhaps not. But I am like a plant. Move me
into a finer pot if you please, give me a
conservatory and a Latin name—I grow in this soil."

"And Pilbeam is the soil?"

"No." She turned on him. "That is what you always
do. If I belong to myself, you must immediately discover
whom I belong to instead."

He had no answer.

From the farmyard came the clang of a bucket, then
a man's voice speaking to the dog. Hilda stiffened almost
imperceptibly.

Arthur Pilbeam came through the gate.

He had left his gun and wore no cap. Without
the signs of his office he looked younger, but no
less taut. His gaze went first to Hilda, then to
Syson. A whole conversation of anxiety and reproach passed across
his face before he spoke.

"Mr Syson."

"Mr Pilbeam."

Hilda remained between them, one hand still on the gate.

"You've been to the house, then," Arthur said.

"As you see."

"I see."

"Arthur," Hilda said, "don't."

"I'm not doing anything."

"No. You're only filling the field with it."

Syson smiled. "Miss Millership has been explaining the advantages of
unlegalised affection."

Arthur's face darkened.

"What's that to you?"

"Very little, apparently."

"It ought to be nothing."

"Arthur!" Hilda's voice cut sharply.

The young man looked at her. At once his anger
became hurt, which he attempted unsuccessfully to conceal.

"He comes back married, walks where he likes, talks how
he likes—"

"I have told him," she said.

"What?"

"Everything that matters."

Arthur's eyes searched hers. "And the rest?"

"There is no rest."

Syson watched them. He had never seen himself look at
another human being with Arthur's naked need, and he felt
both superior and impoverished.

"I should go," he said.

Neither protested.

He bowed to Hilda with a formality that was almost
parody. "Thank you for the cider. And the metaphysics."

"Addy—"

But whatever she might have said was prevented by Arthur's
presence, or by her own unwillingness. Syson waited one second,
two. Then he turned up the path beside the brook.

Behind him the silence of the two lovers remained more
audible than speech.

III

He did not take the road to town.

At the top of the meadow Syson entered the wood
again, though not by the marked path. He moved through
young birches and hazel, pushing aside branches wet from the
morning rain. The sun had strengthened. Its warmth drew odours
from bark, moss, crushed sorrel. Small white flowers of milkwort
showed beside the purple lousewort; a bumblebee lowered itself heavily
into each bloom like a velvet king.

He told himself he wanted solitude. More exactly, he wanted
a conclusion superior to the one Hilda had given him.
The conversation at the gate could not be the final
form of nine years. There must be some further truth,
some refinement in which his pain became intelligible and therefore
partly beautiful.

Yet every phrase he composed sounded false.

You wanted me to become a person you could admire.

He struck at a hazel switch with his cane. Its
new leaves trembled violently.

Had he done that? Certainly he had urged her to
read, to think, to reject the numb fate of a
farmer's daughter. Was influence now a crime? Every love created
an image of the beloved; without imagination, desire was merely
appetite. He had offered sublimation. She had chosen a gamekeeper's
hut.

He stopped.

Somewhere ahead, Arthur's voice sounded.

Syson moved cautiously toward it. Through the trees he saw
a narrow clearing and, under three pines, a low hut
built of boards blackened by weather. A heap of cut
bracken lay beside the wall; rabbit wires hung from a
nail; above the door a jay's blue feather had been
wedged into a crack.

The keeper stood outside. Hilda faced him.

Syson's first impulse was to step out and announce himself.
But Arthur spoke, and the misery in his tone held
Syson motionless.

"You'll let him write again."

"I can't stop a letter coming."

"You can burn it."

"I may."

"May!"

"Don't begin."

"You had him there, eating with you."

"He arrived."

"You looked glad."

"I was glad."

Arthur turned away and kicked at a piece of bark.

Behind a screen of young spruce, Syson felt humiliation rise
like heat into his face. He ought not to listen.
He knew this with perfect clarity. Yet to leave would
be to surrender the only unguarded truth he might ever
receive. He remained.

Hilda went nearer to Arthur, but did not touch him.

"I was glad because he belonged to my life once."

"Once?"

"Yes."

"He don't think it's once. The way he looks at
you—like he's got some secret claim."

"That is his trouble."

"Yours too, maybe."

"No."

Arthur gave a harsh little laugh. "You use his words."

"I use any words I please."

"You talk about freedom and self and all that. You
never talked so before his books."

"I never talked much at all before his books. Do
you want me dumb?"

"I want you mine."

The sentence seemed to frighten him after he had spoken
it. His shoulders lifted, and the hands at his sides
closed.

Hilda looked at him steadily.

"I am not yours."

Arthur's face went grey beneath the brown.

Syson experienced a quick, contemptible pleasure. Here, then, was the
limitation of the young man's confidence. Hilda refused everyone. Her
autonomy was merely a polished name for coldness.

Then she raised both hands and took Arthur's face between
them.

"I am not yours," she repeated, "but I love you."

He seized her wrists, not roughly, but as if the
words had struck him off balance.

"Then marry me."

"Not now."

"Why not? You come here. You stay with me. Everyone'll
know sooner or later."

"Let them."

"It's easy for you to say."

"No, it isn't."

"My mother says—"

"I am not marrying your mother."

"She says you think yourself above us."

Hilda's mouth tightened. "And do you?"

"No."

"You do when he comes. You look at his coat
and hear his talk, and then you think I must
be comparing you."

"Aren't you?"

"Yes," she said.

Arthur released her wrists.

The cruelty of the answer made even Syson draw breath.
But Hilda followed Arthur as he turned toward the hut.

"I compare everyone," she said. "You compare too. He knows
poems. You know where the vixen crosses after rain. He
can make a room listen. You can wait three hours
without moving. He sees meanings in everything. You see what
is there."

"And which do you want?"

"You."

Arthur looked at her over his shoulder.

"All of me?"

"All that you are. Not what you might become if
I worked at you."

The echo of her accusation against Syson was unmistakable. He
felt it enter him with a slow, surgical precision.

Arthur sat on the step of the hut. Hilda remained
standing. For some moments there was no sound but the
small, liquid ticking of a wren among the roots.

"He's passed out, then?" Arthur asked.

"Yes."

"Clean out?"

"Clean out of my life."

Syson closed his eyes.

He had thought pain might come as violence, a tearing
or collapse. Instead the statement produced an enormous stillness. In
that stillness he saw his letters arriving year after year,
their envelopes placed on the kitchen mantel; the books opened,
perhaps read, perhaps not; the Florentine brooch lying in a
drawer among buttons. He had inhabited no secret chamber. He
had been paper entering a house.

Arthur said, "You won't write back?"

"No."

"Nor meet him?"

"He may come. It makes no difference."

"What if he says he loves you?"

"He doesn't."

"He does."

"No. He loves the light he used to see round
me. He made it himself."

The precision was intolerable. Syson wanted to step forward and
deny her, to explain the serious and incorruptible quality of
what he had felt. Yet the young spruce pressed softly
against his sleeve, and he remained hidden like a thief.

Arthur looked toward the trees. Syson froze. But the keeper
saw nothing.

"What was he to you?" Arthur asked.

Hilda sat beside him.

"He opened a window," she said. "Before Addy, I thought
the farm was the whole world because it was all
I knew. Then he showed me there were cities, pictures,
languages, people who lived by the mind. For a while
I thought the window was a door, and that I
must go through."

"Why didn't you?"

"I looked long enough."

"And shut it?"

"No. I know the world is there. That is different
from having to leave this one."

Arthur picked up her hand and examined the palm, tracing
a line in it with his blackened thumb.

"You'll tire of me."

"Perhaps."

"You say things like that to hurt."

"I say them because promises can be another kind of
lie."

"I could promise."

"I know."

"I'd never look at another woman."

"You would."

"I wouldn't!"

Hilda smiled, not mockingly. "You're alive, Arthur."

"What's that mean?"

"It means I don't want your goodness if it has
to strangle you."

He stared at her in bewilderment. Syson almost smiled. There
were his ideas again, altered by her, brought down from
their philosophical heights and made to walk barefoot in the
dirt. He had taught her freedom as a noble abstraction;
she used it to refuse marriage while sitting beside her
lover's bed.

For the hut had a bed. Through the open doorway
Syson could see the dim interior: a rough table, a
chair, a lamp, the corner of a narrow mattress covered
by a brown blanket. On a shelf stood one of
the books he had sent Hilda, its green spine unmistakable.
Morris's poems.

The discovery shocked him more than the bed.

The book had crossed over. His gift, intended as a
bridge between Hilda and himself, stood in the hidden room
she shared with Arthur. It belonged to their intimacy now.
Perhaps she had read aloud there. Perhaps the keeper had
listened, his head on her lap, understanding little and possessing
everything.

Hilda rose.

"I must go back."

Arthur caught her hand. "Stay a bit."

"Mother will wake."

"Five minutes."

"You said that yesterday."

"Stay."

His plea was boyish, but his body as he stood
before her was not. He drew her close. At first
she held herself separate, head lifted, the crown of her
hair bright in the light beyond the pines. Then something
in her gave way—not submission, Syson saw, but consent. Her
arms went round Arthur's neck.

The young man bent his face into the warm hollow
below her ear. Hilda closed her eyes. Her expression lost
its thoughtfulness, its defensive intelligence. It became deep, grave, almost
blind. She pressed him against her with the strength of
her whole body.

Syson could not look away.

No painted Primavera, no Beatrice in a crimson robe, no
white nun behind a grille could survive that embrace. Hilda
was not translated by it into spirit. She was brought
more densely into herself: her brown arms, her throat, her
weight upon the earth. Arthur's hand spread across her back.
They stood rooted together, two forms of the same urgent
life, yet even now not merged. Syson saw Hilda's fingers
loosen in the keeper's hair, then close again by choice.

She whispered something.

Arthur lifted his head. "What?"

"I said, don't be afraid of him."

"I'm not."

"You are."

"He's clever."

"So are foxes."

"He's got the world."

"No one has the world."

"You think he's finer than me."

"In some ways."

Arthur flinched.

"In others," she continued, "he is less real than that
feather."

She touched the blue jay's feather above the hut door.

Syson felt the judgment land upon him with curious finality.
Less real than a feather: he who had interpreted, distinguished,
illuminated. He had made the world into language so completely
that language now stood between him and existence like glass.

Arthur kissed her. This time there was no hesitation. Hilda
received the kiss, then ended it, placing both palms upon
his chest.

"I am going."

"Tomorrow?"

"If I can."

"Tonight?"

"No."

He looked toward the bed.

She laughed softly. "Greedy."

"You like me greedy."

"Sometimes."

"Marry me at midsummer."

"We'll see."

"That means no."

"It means the year hasn't reached May."

She passed her hand down his cheek, and the tenderness
of the gesture was more painful to Syson than their
kiss.

Then Hilda went away between the trees.

She came so near his hiding place that he could
see a loose strand of hair against her throat. If
she had turned her head, she must have discovered him.
But she walked on, looking ahead. Whether some instinct told
her he was there, he never knew.

Arthur remained outside the hut. His whole body listened after
her. At last he took the jay's feather from the
crack and turned it between finger and thumb. Then he
went inside and shut the door.

Syson waited.

The wood resumed itself by degrees. A beetle moved over
the wet bark beside his hand, black with a violet
lustre. Midges revolved in a beam of sunshine. From the
valley came the faint calling of cattle, and farther off
the church clock struck four, each note entering the trees
round and slow.

Still the same. Unaltered.

But the phrase no longer comforted him. The wood had
not preserved his youth; it had merely continued without it.
Bluebells spread where they had always spread, and each spring
was wholly itself, not a repetition designed for his return.
He had mistaken recurrence for fidelity.

He emerged from the spruces and stood before the hut.

He could knock. He imagined Arthur opening the door and
finding him there, pale, smiling, terrible in his knowledge. There
might be a scene: accusation, irony, perhaps even a blow.
Such drama would at least restore him to the centre.

He did not knock.

On the ground lay a page torn from some old
sporting paper. Rain had blurred the print until horses, advertisements,
and results flowed together in grey streams. Syson prodded it
with his cane, then turned away.

The shortest route to Wellingborough descended by the quarry and
crossed the high fields. He took it slowly.

Toward evening the sky cleared to an immaculate blue. Clouds
withdrawn to the east stood in rose-coloured towers. The wet
hedges shone; hawthorn buds showed green-white among black twigs; on
the ploughed land rooks walked with purple shoulders, solemn as
minor priests. Every ditch, tree, bird, and stone appeared distinct,
marvellous, for ever new.

The beauty oppressed him.

He had always believed that perception redeemed pain. To see
clearly was to transform suffering into form; to name a
colour, a movement, a quality of light was already to
rise above it. But now the world's loveliness repeated itself
without relief. Blue after blue, gold after gold, blossom after
blossom—the inexhaustible radiance became a monotone hell.

At the crest of the hill he looked back.

Millership's farm lay small in the valley, smoke ascending straight
from one chimney. The brook caught the sunset in three
red fragments. Stainby Wood had become a single dark mass,
concealing the hut, Arthur, Hilda, all that bodily and local
life from which Syson felt himself expelled.

Yet expelled was not the true word. One could only
be expelled from a place one had entered. He had
never entered Hilda's life. He had stood outside it, describing
the windows.

The recognition wounded his vanity first. He saw the letters
he had written, the subtle confidences, the quotations chosen with
exquisite relevance. How generous, how faithful he had considered himself!
In truth he had maintained Hilda as one keeps a
lamp burning before an image: not for the image's sake,
but to illuminate one's own devotion.

Then, beneath vanity, another pain opened—quieter, almost tender.

He had loved her. Not as she was; he could
not claim that now. But something in him had reached
toward something in her, and if the reaching had failed,
it had not been wholly false. Youth itself had spoken
through them, lavish and mistaken. Hilda had taken from him
a window. He had taken from her a figure of
permanence. Neither gift had remained what the giver intended.

He thought of Marian in London, arranging flowers on the
dining table with her fine, undecided hands. Had he seen
her more clearly? Or had he made of her another
necessary form—wife, companion, intelligence—while some actual woman lived behind the
names?

The question frightened him.

At the station road darkness had begun to collect beneath
the elms. A thrush sang from the upper branches, repeating
its phrases with obstinate sweetness. Near the ditch, the first
stitchwort flowers shone white. Syson bent and picked one, then
immediately regretted it. Severed, it looked meagre in his hand.

He let it fall.

A cart overtook him. The driver, an old man folded
into a green coat, raised his whip.

"Goin' town way, sir?"

"Yes."

"Get up, then. Road's none too dry."

Syson climbed beside him. The cart smelled of sacks, horse,
and turnips. Its wheels entered every rut with a jolt
that defeated thought. The old man spoke of rain, lambing,
the price of feed. Syson answered when required.

At the edge of town, lamps appeared one by one
in the dusk. Windows glowed behind curtains. A woman called
a child indoors. Somewhere a piano stumbled through a hymn.

"You lodging at the King's Head?" the driver asked.

"For tonight."

"London tomorrow?"

"Yes."

"Ah. London." The old man considered the word without admiration.
"Couldn't abide it myself."

"No," Syson said. "Perhaps one must be transplanted young."

The driver glanced at him, found nothing practical in the
remark, and clicked to the horse.

At the inn Syson went up to his room. It
was narrow and smelled of coal dust and lavender furniture
polish. He set his bag upon the bed, removed his
gloves, and stood before the spotted mirror.

The face within it looked older than the one that
had left the train that morning. He knew this was
sentiment; faces did not age in a day. Nevertheless, some
expression had been stripped from him. The eyes appeared less
protected.

On the table lay writing paper stamped with the inn's
crest. He sat down.

Dear Hilda,

He stopped.

He might write nobly: forgive the intrusion; be happy; our
friendship has passed into another phase. He could quote something—Dante's
final vision, perhaps, or the line from the French poem
about love surviving its own object. He could fashion the
day into a last gift, beautifully phrased and therefore still
partly his.

He tore the sheet across.

On another he wrote:

Dear Marian,
I shall return by the noon train tomorrow.

After a pause he added:

I have much to tell you, though I do not
yet know what it is.

That sentence seemed dangerously close to effect. He considered striking
it out, but let it remain.

He opened the window. The town roofs descended toward the
unseen country. Above them the night was dark blue, and
one star burned with steady, impersonal brightness.

Somewhere beyond the hills Hilda would be moving through the
farmhouse, setting the supper, tending her mother, perhaps thinking of
Arthur, perhaps not thinking of either man. The freedom she
claimed did not need Syson's understanding. This was the final
injury and the first truth.

He stood a long while at the window.

Then he folded Marian's letter, extinguished the lamp, and allowed
the countryside to disappear into its own darkness.

Van Cheele had spent the better part of an afternoon
explaining the habits of the water-vole to a retired colonel
who had mistaken it throughout for the water-rat and was
not perceptibly improved by the correction. He was therefore in
that agreeable state of mind which follows the discharge of
useful information upon an unwilling listener.

It was on his way home, through the woods that
formed the most respectable portion of his property, that he
became aware of an unfamiliar figure stretched on a flat
stone beside a dark pool.

The figure was that of a boy of perhaps sixteen,
brown-limbed, narrow-flanked, and entirely without clothing. His wet hair lay
in black points across his forehead, and his eyes, yellow-brown
and watchful, followed Van Cheele's approach without embarrassment. He looked
less like a swimmer resting after exertion than some woodland
creature which had found the stone warm and saw no
reason to surrender it.

Van Cheele was annoyed. A naked stranger in one's woods
suggested trespass, indecency, and possible correspondence with the local newspaper.

"What are you doing here?" he demanded.

"Lying in the sun," said the boy.

There was little sun to speak of. The afternoon had
already begun to lose colour, and a thin brightness slid
between the branches without warming anything.

"You have been bathing, I suppose."

"Yes."

"Where are your clothes?"

"I have none."

This answer, though concise, struck Van Cheele as intentionally unhelpful.

"Where do you live?"

"Here."

"You cannot live in a wood."

"I do."

Van Cheele drew himself up. He was accustomed to being
considered an authority on rural matters by people who lived
in towns, and an authority on most other matters by
himself.

"You mean that you camp here?"

"I sleep here when I sleep."

"And what do you eat?"

"Flesh."

The boy smiled as he said it. The smile was
beautiful in the way that the shine on a knife
is beautiful.

"Rabbits, no doubt," said Van Cheele, seizing upon a recognisable
offence. "You have been poaching."

"Rabbits sometimes. Hares. Birds when I can get them. Lambs
are better."

Van Cheele remembered that two lambs had vanished from a
neighbouring farm during the past fortnight. Their disappearance had been
attributed to gypsies, dogs, or agricultural depression, according to the
prejudices of the person discussing it.

"You speak as if you hunted them."

"I do."

"With what? Traps?"

The boy turned his head and looked into the thickening
undergrowth.

"I hunt on four feet at night," he said.

It was not the kind of statement which ought to
have been made calmly. Van Cheele decided that the boy
was either mad or trying to be offensive. He preferred
the second explanation; madness carries responsibilities for the householder who
discovers it.

"What is your name?"

"I have had several."

"What do people call you?"

"People do not call me."

"Have you parents?"

"Not now."

There was a pause. Somewhere among the hazels a bird
gave a sharp alarm-note and was silent.

Van Cheele recalled, with an unwillingness that made the recollection
unusually exact, the disappearance of the miller's youngest child two
months earlier. The little boy had gone to gather flowers
near the stream and had never returned. Search parties had
beaten the woods, the mill-pond had been dragged, and a
charitable collection had been opened for the parents, though what
precise benefit money could confer in the circumstances had not
been specified. No trace had been found except a small
shoe in a muddy ditch.

"Do you ever eat children?" Van Cheele asked, attempting the
tone of a magistrate testing a foolish witness.

The boy's eyes came back to him.

"Sometimes."

A low laugh followed the word. It contained no amusement
that Van Cheele could recognise.

"This nonsense has gone far enough," he said. "You will
leave my woods at once. I will not have vagrants
living here, clothed or otherwise."

The boy rose with a smooth, unhurried movement. Standing, he
seemed taller than Van Cheele had expected, though there was
scarcely any weight on him beyond muscle and bone. For
a moment the last pale light touched his shoulders and
made him look like a figure from an old pagan
carving, designed before morality had become a local concern.

"I shall leave the woods," he said.

Then he slipped between the trees. He did not run,
yet within seconds he had disappeared. Van Cheele listened for
the crack of twigs or the brush of leaves and
heard nothing.

He went home by the longer path.

At dinner he mentioned the encounter to his aunt, Miss
Agatha Van Cheele, who managed his household and a considerable
part of the parish conscience. She had organised blankets for
the poor, hymns for the young, and improving lectures for
everyone; the beneficiaries had shown varying degrees of gratitude.

"A homeless boy?" she said. "In this weather? And you
sent him away?"

"He was not merely homeless," said Van Cheele. "He was
entirely unclothed."

"That makes it worse."

"It made it conspicuous."

"You should have brought him here."

Van Cheele applied himself to his soup. His aunt's charity
was of the military kind: rapid in movement, firm in
occupation, and indifferent to civilian objections.

"He claimed to live on raw flesh," he said.

"Starvation often produces strange fancies."

"He said he ate children."

"Boys say shocking things when they wish to attract notice.
You probably questioned him unsympathetically."

It was difficult to continue a discussion in which cannibalism
was treated as the result of an unfortunate interviewing manner.

The next morning, while Van Cheele was dictating a letter
to the parish council concerning the destruction of rare fungi
by Sunday picnickers, the housemaid entered to say that there
was a young person in the morning-room.

"What young person?"

"He doesn't say, sir."

"Is he clothed?"

The maid considered the question improper but answered it.

"Not at present, sir."

Van Cheele found the boy lying on the deep window-seat,
studying a green fly that moved across the glass. Mud
streaked his legs; his hair was wet with dew. He
looked round with the composed recognition of one host greeting
another.

"You told me to leave the woods," he observed.

"I did not tell you to enter my house."

"No."

Van Cheele had scarcely prepared a suitable reply when his
aunt came in. She stopped, gave a small exclamation, and
rang the bell.

The boy watched her.

"You poor child," she said. "How long have you been
wandering about like this?"

"I do not wander."

"Of course not," said Miss Van Cheele, who accepted contradiction
as a symptom of distress. "We must find you something
to wear. And something hot to eat."

"I should like meat."

"You shall have breakfast."

"Raw meat."

"Cook will see what can be done," said Miss Van
Cheele, with the serene inaccuracy of a person who has
never consulted cook on an unusual request.

Within an hour the boy had been washed, combed, and
placed inside a suit of clothes formerly belonging to a
curate's nephew. The suit was dark, durable, and calculated to
discourage every natural movement. The boy submitted with a stillness
more menacing than resistance. Shoes caused greater difficulty. He stared
at them as though they were traps of an elementary
but insulting design.

"He must have a name," Miss Van Cheele declared.

"He says he has had several," said Van Cheele.

"That is obviously impossible. I think Gabriel would suit him.
There is something rather angelic about his face."

"Not all angels were considered safe," Van Cheele remarked.

"And Ernest," continued his aunt, ignoring him, "after my poor
brother. Gabriel-Ernest. It sounds trustworthy."

The boy repeated the name softly, as if testing an
unfamiliar taste.

Thus Gabriel-Ernest entered the household.

He adjusted to domestic life with disconcerting ease. He slept
little, ate enormously, and showed a marked preference for meat
so undercooked that the kitchen staff regarded it as a
personal criticism. Dogs shivered or snarled when he passed. The
stable cat disappeared for three days and returned with a
permanent objection to the west lawn. Gabriel-Ernest himself was courteous
when courtesy cost him nothing and silent when questioned.

Miss Van Cheele found his silence touching.

"He has suffered," she told visitors. "One can see it
in the reserve of his manner."

Visitors generally saw his eyes and did not prolong their
inspection.

Van Cheele tried more than once to learn where he
had come from. The answers remained unprofitable.

"Were you born near here?"

"I do not remember being born."

"Someone must have looked after you."

"For a time."

"Who?"

"A woman in a hut beyond the hills."

"What became of her?"

"She grew old."

"And then?"

"She was afraid."

This was followed by the same low laugh Van Cheele
had heard beside the pool.

During the following days, more livestock vanished. A farmer lost
a lamb from a field enclosed by a stone wall.
Three hens disappeared from a locked shed, leaving feathers in
the yard and a splintered board at the back. Gabriel-Ernest
listened to these reports with his head slightly inclined. Once
Van Cheele noticed a dark stain at the cuff of
the curate's nephew's suit.

"It is blackberry juice," said Miss Van Cheele before he
could ask.

"There are no blackberries yet."

"Then it is something else. One should not pry into
every stain a boy acquires."

Van Cheele began to feel that his own house had
become hostile to reasonable inquiry. He was not frightened, precisely.
He merely found himself locking his bedroom window at night,
an action he attributed to the unseasonable damp.

A week after the boy's arrival, Van Cheele drove into
the market town and encountered Cunningham, an artist who had
once stayed at the house to paint woodland studies. Cunningham's
pictures did not sell, which gave him a reputation for
sincerity among people who did not buy them.

Over luncheon Van Cheele described his discovery, omitting the more
ridiculous parts and thereby leaving little to tell.

Cunningham laid down his glass.

"A dark-haired boy? Tawny eyes? Moves as if he never
quite touches the ground?"

"You have seen him?"

"In your woods. Last spring."

"Why did you say nothing?"

"I was not certain that what I saw would improve
in the telling."

Van Cheele disliked statements of this kind from artists. They
suggested that imagination was being used where ordinary observation would
have been sufficient.

"What did you see?"

Cunningham looked towards the bright windows of the restaurant.

"I was sketching near the pool at sunset. The boy
was standing on the far bank. Naked, as you describe
him. The sky behind him was orange and rose-coloured, and
for a moment his whole body seemed cut out of
the light."

"Yes, yes," said Van Cheele. "What happened?"

"The colour went suddenly. You know how it does at
sunset—the warmth drains away, and everything becomes grey before one
has noticed the change. The boy dropped to his hands."

"Perhaps he slipped."

"He did not slip. His back lengthened. His shoulders altered.
There was hair where the skin had been."

Cunningham paused.

"A wolf came out of the shadow and ran past
me."

Van Cheele gave a short laugh which failed to sound
social.

"You are saying that the boy became a wolf."

"I am saying what I saw."

"Such things do not happen."

"Then you need not be troubled."

The answer was irritatingly complete.

Van Cheele remembered the naked figure on the stone, the
missing lambs, the miller's child, the boy's literal claim to
hunt on four feet. His mind, which had rejected each
fact separately, now received them together with an unpleasant efficiency.

"What time did this occur?"

"At sunset."

Van Cheele looked at the clock.

The afternoon was already late.

He left Cunningham without the customary argument over the bill
and drove home at a speed which endangered two cyclists
and a flock of geese. The western sky was still
bright, but the light had acquired that exhausted brilliance which
precedes extinction.

At the house he found his aunt arranging flowers for
the church.

"Where is Gabriel-Ernest?"

"What a way to come in. He has gone out."

"Where?"

Miss Van Cheele selected a white chrysanthemum and removed an
imperfect petal.

"I sent him to escort little Michael home."

For a moment Van Cheele could not attach meaning to
the sentence.

"Michael?"

"The schoolmistress's youngest. He stayed after the Sunday-school practice. The
path by the mill-stream is lonely, and Gabriel-Ernest offered to
take him."

"Offered?"

"Well, I suggested it. He agreed most sweetly."

Van Cheele was already at the door.

The path to the stream ran westward through meadow and
alder wood. The sun hung low above the fields, flattened
and red, while long shadows reached across the grass like
bars. Van Cheele ran with a disregard for dignity which
would have astonished the parish council.

He called the boy's adopted name. The woods returned it
in fragments.

At the top of the slope he saw the mill-stream
below him, silver between the reeds. On the farther bank
two small figures moved along the path: Michael in a
blue jersey and, several paces behind, Gabriel-Ernest.

The sun touched the horizon.

"Gabriel!" Van Cheele shouted.

The taller figure stopped and turned. Even at that distance
Van Cheele saw the face lift towards the dying light.

Then the sun went down.

Orange and pink vanished from the water. Cold grey spread
over the bank. The alders became a single wall of
darkness.

There was a child's scream.

It was brief, high, and final.

Van Cheele stumbled down the slope, tore through nettles, and
splashed into the shallows. He called until his throat hurt.
Men came from the mill with lanterns; later, villagers arrived
with poles and ropes. They searched the banks, the sluice,
and the deep pool below the wheel.

Neither Michael nor Gabriel-Ernest was found.

On a patch of weed-grown ground beside the stream lay
the dark suit, the shirt, and the hated shoes, neatly
abandoned except where the evening wind had disturbed them.

By morning the village had formed its conclusion. Gabriel-Ernest, seeing
the child fall into the water, had thrown off his
clothes and plunged in to save him. Both had been
swept beneath the mill-wheel. The absence of bodies was explained
by currents, weeds, and Providence, none of which could be
cross-examined.

Miss Van Cheele was prostrate with grief, though not too
prostrate to correct the local newspaper's spelling of Gabriel-Ernest. The
paper called him "a brave orphan lad" and devoted an
editorial to the moral value of rescuing destitute children. A
subscription was opened for a memorial.

The parish selected a brass tablet for the church wall.
It bore the figure of an angel above two clasped
hands and recorded that Gabriel-Ernest Van Cheele had given his
life in an heroic effort to save a little child.

Miss Van Cheele contributed generously. The parish council contributed publicly.
The Sunday school collected coins in a china bowl while
being instructed on self-sacrifice.

Van Cheele alone refused.

His aunt thought the refusal heartless, the parish thought it
eccentric, and the newspaper thought it unworthy of a gentleman.

Van Cheele thought the memorial quite expensive enough without asking
him to help pay for the glorification of a successful
appetite.

### The Name Given

On Saturday afternoon Hilda Rowbotham came out of school by
the front gate instead of slipping, as she ordinarily did,
through the alley behind the infants' yard. The change was
small, yet it made her conspicuous. She was dressed in
black, though the day was warm, and her narrow shoulders
tilted beneath a jacket whose velvet collar had gone green
at the edges. Under one arm she carried a cardboard
box; in her gloved hand she held a purse with
a steel clasp.

The children who still lingered at the railings watched her
pass.

"Good afternoon, Miss Rowbotham."

"Good afternoon, Elsie. Good afternoon, Clara. Run along now."

Her smile came rapidly and disappeared before it had completed
itself. She walked with an upright, floating carefulness, as if
the jar of each step might crack something within her.
The doctor had told her that her heart was enlarged.
She imagined it sometimes as a dark, tender fruit pressing
against the cage of her ribs.

She was going to Parson's, the baker's, for the christening
cakes.

The shop stood between the pawnbroker's and a warehouse where
flour was unloaded in soft white sacks. There was a
sweet, scorched smell in the doorway. Inside, loaves rose in
ranks upon the shelves, brown and complacent, and glass dishes
displayed buns glazed yellow, currant squares, macaroons and little pink
cakes with a knob of sugar on each.

Mr Parson came from the bakehouse wiping his hands. He
was a thick man with pale eyelashes. Flour lay in
the creases of his arms like dust upon old plaster.

"Well?" he said.

Hilda's fingers fluttered over the clasp of her purse.

"I ordered a box yesterday—Miss Rowbotham—though of course you may
not remember, there being so many customers. For Sunday. A
family occasion, merely. Nothing elaborate."

"Aye."

"The child is to be christened."

"Aye."

He did not ask whose child. This silence, more than
a question, filled the shop.

"My younger sister's," Hilda added. "Emma's little boy. We are
not making a great affair of it, naturally. Still, one
must mark such things. A child cannot be blamed for
circumstances preceding its arrival, can it?"

Mr Parson turned away. He lifted down a square cardboard
box tied with blue string.

"Two-and-six."

"Quite. I thought there might be perhaps six of the
almond and six of the—these pink ones are very pretty.
Rather festive, without being showy."

"Two-and-six."

She paid. He counted the coins with his floury finger.
Behind him, through the half-open bakehouse door, a young man
in a vest bent over a trough of dough. Hilda
saw only the nape of his neck, reddish above the
white cloth, and the heavy forearms plunged to the wrist.
He did not turn round. Something in her stopped so
sharply that her heart gave a thick recoil.

Mr Parson pushed the box towards her.

"The bag?" she asked. "I think it may rain."

He fetched a large paper bag bearing the name PARSON
& SON in black letters. He forced the box into
it, crumpling the corners.

"There," he said.

She wished to make some light remark about the weather,
the school fête, or the extraordinary appetite of children after
sacred ceremonies. Words crowded at her lips and frightened one
another back. Mr Parson looked past her to the street.

"Good afternoon, then," she said.

"Aye."

As she left, the young man in the bakehouse lifted
his head. She felt, without seeing, that he had done
so. The paper bag swung from her hand, broad and
public as a placard.

At home the kitchen was full of steam and temper.
Bertha was slicing bread at the table, pressing the knife
down with both hands. Their mother knelt before the range,
raking ashes into a pan. Emma sat by the window
with the baby at her breast. The child sucked with
closed eyes, one red fist lodged against the white slope
of her flesh.

"Shut that door," Bertha said. "There's enough draught."

"I have shut it."

"You haven't. It's on the latch."

Hilda put down the cakes, returned, and shut the door
more firmly. The little exertion made her breathless. She removed
her hat and pressed a hand to her side, hoping
no one had noticed.

Their father noticed. From the horsehair chair near the fire
he watched her with his large grey eyes.

"Tha's bin down town?" he asked.

"Yes, Father."

"What's in th' bag?"

"The cakes."

"Ah. Cakes for th' little lad."

His delight was immediate and unguarded. It made Emma's mouth
harden over the baby's head.

Matthew Rowbotham had been a hewer until a fall of
stone damaged his spine. Afterwards came the trembling, the uncertain
feet, the slurred outbreaks of speech. He was only sixty-one,
but his beard had whitened, and his body, once powerful
enough to carry a prop timber alone, now leaned sideways
as though an invisible weight hung from one shoulder. His
hands remained immense. When they closed upon the arms of
the chair, the house seemed still to belong to them.

"Let's see 'em, Hilda."

"They are for tomorrow."

"I can look, canna?"

Bertha drove the knife through the loaf.

"You'll have them stale afore they're wanted."

"Nay, our Bertha, I'll do nöwt to 'em."

Hilda drew out the box and untied it. The sweet
smell entered the kitchen and mingled with bacon fat, damp
washing, coal smoke and the sour ointment rubbed into their
father's legs. He gazed at the pink cakes with a
child's reverence.

"Bonny," he said. "Eh, they're bonny."

Emma shifted the infant from one breast to the other.
For an instant her breast was bare, heavy and veined,
the nipple wet. Hilda looked away. Bertha did not. Their
mother rose from the hearth with the ash pan and
stared at the floor.

The baby made a small ravenous sound.

"Tha should name him Matthew," the old man said.

"He's being called Joseph William," Emma replied.

"Matthew Joseph."

"No."

He seemed not to hear the refusal, or else he
folded it into the indulgence he now practised. Since his
illness he had become tender at inconvenient moments. His tenderness
had something greedy in it. In earlier years he had
commanded: no daughter was to walk out after nine, no
son to answer back, no book to be read at
table, no friend to cross the threshold unless examined and
approved. Now he blessed them, praised their hands, called them
his lambs. The blessing fastened them as securely as the
command.

"He's got my brow," he said.

"He has not."

"Aye, he has. Look at him."

"He's got nöwt of yours."

"Emma," their mother murmured.

Emma bent over the child. She could hate him when
they spoke of him—the nameless father's child, the village's morsel,
the proof of her stupidity wrapped in a flannel square.
But when his mouth searched her skin, when his hot
body lay along her arm, love went through her so
fiercely that it was indistinguishable from pain. She wanted to
seize him and run where no one knew her. She
wanted to thrust him into their judging hands and be
free.

The old man smiled.

"Joseph William," he repeated. "A grand name."

No one answered.

On Sunday the front room was opened. It had the
solemn chill of a room preserved for deaths, visits and
religion. Hilda dusted the china ornaments and laid the table
with the best cloth. There were sandwiches of tinned salmon,
bread and butter in thin overlapping scales, a cold tongue,
a pot of mustard and the cakes arranged on two
plates. In the centre stood a blue china bowl filled
with late daisies from the strip of garden behind the
ashpit.

Their mother objected to the daisies.

"They're common," she said. "You could have fetched Michaelmas."

"They looked fresh," Hilda replied. "And white is appropriate."

"White for purity," Bertha said, and gave a short laugh.

Emma, upstairs, heard it.

She stood before the looking-glass fastening her blouse. The baby
lay on the bed in the long christening robe lent
by a cousin. The robe was too magnificent for him:
fluted lace, satin ribbons, a skirt extending beyond his feet
like the train of some miniature prince. His face, above
it, was red and cross.

"Don't you start," she told him.

He started. His cry was outraged, without shame, and she
envied it.

Laurie had not yet come from the pit. He had
been put on Sunday maintenance and was expected at three.
The clergyman was due at half-past. Their father waited in
the front room wearing a black suit which hung from
him in broad, exhausted folds. Hilda had combed his beard
and fastened his collar. The collar troubled him; he inserted
one finger beneath it repeatedly, rubbing his neck.

"Am I right, lass?" he asked.

"You look very nice, Father."

"Like old times?"

"Quite."

He searched her face. Her obedience disturbed him more than
resentment would have done. She had always been the clever
one, quick at lessons, delicate, too proud for the village
and too frightened to leave it. He had made her
remain until she obtained the school post. He had called
it protection. Now, seeing the fine bones of her face
and the blue shadow about her mouth, he wondered whether
he had protected her life out of her.

"Hilda—"

"Yes?"

"I dunna want thee upset."

"I am not upset."

"Nay."

He put out a hand, but she moved to straighten
the cakes. The paper bag from the baker's lay folded
on the sideboard. PARSON & SON showed above the crease.
She turned it over.

The Reverend Mr Ellison arrived punctually, bringing with him cold
air and a faint smell of wet wool. He was
young to be so bald. His voice was low and
careful, formed for bedsides and embarrassed parlours.

"A happy occasion," he said, taking Hilda's hand.

"Yes. Indeed. Though quiet, as you see."

"Quiet occasions are often the happier."

"That has not invariably been our experience," Bertha said.

Hilda gave her a warning glance.

Mr Ellison smiled as if something pleasant had been said.
He greeted Mrs Rowbotham, then Emma, who had descended with
the child. When he turned to the old man, his
pastoral ease faltered. Matthew's right hand swallowed his.

"Tha's come to put th' lad among God's folk," Matthew
said.

"Yes. To receive him into the fellowship of Christ."

"Aye, that's it."

The clergyman understood the fervour better than the words. He
drew nearer, inclining his head.

"And you are the grandfather."

"I am. More's th' pity, happen."

There was a silence.

The baby was laid in Emma's arms. Hilda and Bertha
stood beside her as sponsors. Their mother remained behind them,
mouth pinched, while the old man sat forward in his
chair. Rain ticked lightly against the window. From the kitchen
came the settling clink of the range.

Mr Ellison opened his book.

"Dearly beloved, forasmuch as all men are conceived and born
in sin—"

Emma felt the blood rise from her chest to her
throat. There it was at once: sin, announced before her
sisters, her mother, the old man, though everyone had known
it for nine months and long before the swelling could
be hidden. She looked at the clergyman's soft white hand
resting upon the book. How clean he was. Even his
nails seemed baptised.

The baby slept. His lashes were scarcely visible.

Hilda listened to the phrases and tried to fit herself
inside them. Renunciation. Faith. Obedience. Her heart beat irregularly, then
paused with such decision that she thought, This is it.
But it stumbled on. No one saw. They were all
watching Emma.

"Name this child."

"Joseph William," Emma said.

Mr Ellison took him. The child woke as the clergyman's
arm displaced his warmth. His face tightened.

"Joseph William, I baptise thee in the name of the
Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost."

Water shone on the baby's forehead. He cried once, violently.
The old man uttered a sound like a laugh.

"Aye," he said. "Give tongue, little un."

"Father, hush," Bertha whispered.

But Matthew had begun to tremble. It travelled from his
knees upward, a rough mechanical shudder. His great hands worked
on the chair arms. Mr Ellison concluded the prayer and
returned the child to Emma.

Then the old man tried to rise.

"Stay where you are," Hilda said.

"I mun kneel."

"You cannot."

"I mun."

He pushed himself upright. For a moment he stood, enormous
and swaying, his face lifted. Then one foot dragged, his
knee folded, and Hilda sprang forward. She was too slight
to hold him. Bertha caught his arm, Mr Ellison the
other, and between them they lowered him not to the
floor but back into the chair.

He resisted.

"Let me pray," he said. "Let me pray for 'em."

"Of course," Mr Ellison replied, shaken. "We may all pray
seated."

Matthew bowed his head. The others did not. They watched
him.

"Lord," he began, "Lord Almighty, tha knows me."

The clergyman's eyelids flickered.

"Tha knows what I wor. I wor hard. I wor
hard as th' face underground, and I called it righteousness.
I held 'em, Lord. I held all on 'em because
I were feared they'd go from me. I said it
wor for their good. Nay, it wor for mine."

"Father," Bertha said.

He lifted one hand.

"Let me speak. Lord, I've been laid on 'em like
a roof wi' no window. Our Hilda—look at her, Lord,
poor thin lass, heart knocking wrong in her. She had
wings in her once. I gripped 'em shut. Our Bertha,
all sharp because I'd have her bend, and our Emma—"

"Don't," Emma said.

"—our Emma, wi' her child and no man beside her.
If she sinned, Lord, I taught her hiding. I taught
'em all fear. And Laurie—our lad goes down pit black
and comes home blacker in his soul because he hates
me, and I made him hate."

Mrs Rowbotham sat down suddenly. Her face showed neither grief
nor surprise, only the fatigue of a woman hearing aloud
what she had spent thirty years keeping quiet.

"Father in heaven," Matthew continued, his voice swelling, "take my
hand off 'em. I canna do it. It's dead, but
it grips. Break it, Lord. Break what I made. Let
Hilda breathe. Let Bertha soften. Let Emma stand up wi'
her lad and no shame on her. Let Laurie be
a man beyond me. I kept 'em close till they
grew crooked. Set 'em straight."

The baby began to whimper. Emma rocked him without taking
her eyes from her father.

"And this little un, Lord—Joseph William—let him grow where there's
light. Let him bend and not be broke. Let him
be like a young tree by running water, wi' room
for every leaf. Dunna let our darkness fall on him.
Wash it off. Wash me off him."

His voice failed. Saliva gleamed at the corner of his
mouth. Hilda, moved and humiliated, longed to wipe it away,
but she could not cross the space.

Mr Ellison said softly, "Amen."

Matthew looked up.

"I've not done."

No one moved.

"Lord, because of me they're what they are. Because of
me there's no love spoken here without it hurting. Because
of me—"

"Stop it!" Emma cried.

The child woke fully and screamed.

The old man stopped. His mouth remained open. In the
little silence beneath the screaming, his confession appeared suddenly not
noble but indecent, another act of mastery performed before them.
He had named their wounds and made himself the centre
of each. Even repentance must fill the room with him.

Mr Ellison put a hand on Matthew's shoulder.

"God's mercy is greater than our wrong."

"Aye," Matthew whispered. "But will it reach 'em?"

Laurie entered from the kitchen.

He was still in pit clothes, his face black except
where sweat had drawn pale channels beside his nose. His
cap sat at the back of his head. He smelled
of coal, leather and underground water.

"Well," he said. "Is it done?"

"Take your boots off," Bertha answered.

"I asked if it's done."

"You are late," Hilda said. "And Mr Ellison is here."

"I can see parson."

Mr Ellison greeted him. Laurie nodded, amused by the room:
the flowers, the white robe, Emma's angry face, his father
collapsed in black broadcloth. He had heard the last of
the prayer through the kitchen door. The words had touched
him and therefore enraged him.

"So we've washed the babby clean, have we?" he said.

"Be quiet," Emma told him.

"What's his name?"

"You know his name."

Laurie saw the folded paper bag on the sideboard. He
turned it, read the print and smiled.

"Parson's cakes from Parson's shop. That's proper."

Hilda's lips whitened.

"Leave them alone."

But Laurie had caught the danger in her voice. He
glanced at Emma. Beneath the pit dirt, his expression sharpened.

"Pat-a-cake, pat-a-cake, baker's man," he sang.

Emma went still.

Bertha dropped the bread knife on the table.

"Shut thy mouth," she said.

"Bake me a cake as fast as tha can—"

Emma crossed the room before anyone understood. Holding the baby
in one arm, she struck Laurie with the other hand.
Her palm left a clean grey smear upon his cheek.

He laughed.

It was enough. In his laugh the concealed young man
in the bakehouse appeared: the reddish neck, the arms buried
in dough, the head that did not turn. Mr Ellison
looked from Emma to the bag. He understood, and wished
not to understand.

Matthew tried to rise again.

"Who is he?" he demanded. "Which one?"

"Nobody," Emma said.

"Is it Parson's lad?"

"Nobody!"

"Did he promise thee owt?"

"Sit down."

"I'll fetch him. I'll have him here."

"You'll do nöwt!" Her voice rang through the house. "You'll
not fetch him, nor speak for me, nor pray for
me. He wouldn't have me. That's all. And I wouldn't
have him now."

The baby screamed against her breast, his face purple.

Matthew sagged back. For one moment his authority had returned
entire—the demand, the threat, the readiness to seize another life
and force it into shape. He had confessed nothing away.

Hilda moved first.

"Tea," she said quickly. "Everything is ready. Mr Ellison, you
must have tea before you go. The weather is so
unpleasant, and you have perhaps another service, but one cannot
conduct a baptism without some refreshment afterwards. Bertha, the kettle."

Her speech ran on, frail and feverish, throwing napkins and
teaspoons over the nakedness of the scene.

They sat. They ate tinned salmon, bread and butter, cold
tongue and cakes. Laurie washed his hands but not his
face. Emma fed the baby beneath a shawl. Matthew took
one pink cake and held it without biting. Mr Ellison
praised the daisies. Mrs Rowbotham poured tea from the brown
pot until the cups stood filled in front of people
who did not want them.

The ceremony had altered nothing. Yet each was conscious of
having been seen.

After Mr Ellison left, the family divided along its old
cracks. Bertha accused Laurie of filthiness. Laurie said truth was
not filth merely because women disliked it. Hilda wrapped the
remaining cakes in the baker's bag, then tore the printed
name through and put the pieces in the fire. Emma
went upstairs with Joseph William. Their mother washed the cups.

Matthew remained in the front room.

He called once, "Emma."

No answer came.

He called, "Hilda, lass."

She stood in the kitchen, one hand against her heart,
listening. She could have gone to him. She knew he
was alone, frightened by his failing body and by the
things he had at last perceived. Pity rose in her,
thin but genuine. With it came the old obedience. More
terrible than anger, it moved her foot towards the door.

Bertha looked at her.

"Don't."

Hilda stopped.

Through the wall they heard their father weeping. His weeping
was heavy and difficult, almost a labour. No one went
in.

The next morning was clear. The rain had washed the
soot from the leaves and left the garden shining beneath
a pale sky. Hilda came downstairs late, having slept badly.
Bertha was cutting bacon. Emma sat by the range with
the child bundled against her. Their mother shook the hearthrug
at the back door.

Matthew had somehow reached the window. He stood supported by
a stick and the sill, his body pitched dangerously forward.
Sunlight lay upon his beard.

"Look," he said.

No one replied.

"Lasses, look at them daisies."

The flowers in the garden had opened after the rain,
white about their yellow hearts. Against the black fence and
the coal-dusted soil they had a startling brightness, as if
scraps of daylight had taken root there.

"Eh," the old man said, laughing softly. "Hundreds on 'em.
Little suns. There's glory for thee."

His face was joyful. The joy asked nothing; perhaps for
that instant it was pure. He looked from the flowers
to his daughters, wanting them to share it.

Hilda lowered her eyes to the table. Bertha turned the
bacon in the pan. Emma drew the shawl higher over
the baby's face.

The old man kept smiling at the daisies. Behind him
the room seemed to lean towards his damaged body, as
iron filings incline towards a magnet. His children were grown,
yet none had travelled beyond the reach of that pull.
They had hardened in resistance, twisted in obedience, and hidden
in shame; even now, when his legs failed and his
thoughts broke apart, his love descended on them with the
weight of command.

"Look," he said again.

But his daughters sat close together in the warm kitchen,
mute and sullen, while beyond the glass the small white
flowers burned innocently in the morning.

# THE CLOISTER AND THE MILL

## I. The Cloister of Bachelors

Turn out of the clangorous river of Fleet Street—where omnibus,
cab, dray, errand-boy, barrister, hawker, and bewildered foreigner are perpetually
borne eastward or westward, like miscellaneous wreckage on a tide
which has forgotten the sea—and pass beneath a low, blackened
arch, so modest in aperture that a bishop's coach might
overlook it, though a debtor's eye would assuredly discover it;
then, after a few paces, pause.

Behind you London roars. Before you London dreams.

There are courts which seem less built than secreted, as
the pearl is secreted in the oyster; courts quiet as
chapels after the congregation has departed; courts whose brick fronts,
softened by soot and time, look down upon flagged walks
with the meditative complacency of old judges who have survived
both their passions and their clients. Here no butcher's cart
invades with gory axle, no nursery-maid wheels a perambulator freighted
with hereditary lamentation, no tradesman bawls his wares beneath a
domestic window. The place belongs to Law—not to justice, perhaps,
which is a winged and homeless angel—but to Law settled,
wigged, chambered, docketed, and decently provided with coal.

This was the Temple; and into it I, an American
merchant in seeds, labels, envelopes, twine, and those minor instruments
whereby Nature is persuaded to transact business, first entered upon
a mild October afternoon.

I had imagined London in masses: Parliament, cathedral, bridge, fog,
palace, prison. But the Temple was an exception inserted in
the very grammar of the town. The city, ordinarily declamatory,
became parenthetical. Ivy softened a wall which had resisted three
dynasties and innumerable suits in chancery. A pump stood in
a court with the grave uselessness of an abdicated monarch.
Through a window I saw a young clerk copying a
document so enormous that it seemed less a deed than
a continent. Beyond him, upon a shelf, files rose in
leathern ranks, as if all the dead quarrels of England
had been embalmed there, each with a red tape wound
solemnly about its ribs.

"Do you seek Mr. Alverton's chambers, sir?" asked a porter.

Now Mr. Francis Alverton, counsellor-at-law and bachelor-at-ease, had been my
fellow passenger on the Atlantic packet. On shipboard, while less
disciplined stomachs yielded their allegiance to Neptune, he alone dined
regularly and discussed Roman tenure through weather which made the
soup climb the cabin wall. Arriving before me in London,
he had sent an invitation engraved in a hand so
stately that the supper it announced seemed likely to be
attended by Magna Charta herself.

I showed the porter the card.

"Second court, third stair, first floor; oak on the left,"
said he, and vanished into an entry whose darkness swallowed
him with professional discretion.

Oak! Stair! Court! Every word had the odor of centuries.
In America we number offices, polish brass plates, and announce
attorneys in letters visible across a prairie; but here mystery
was half the fee. I crossed a garden where a
few leaves, yellow as old parchment, descended without wind. Upon
one bench sat a ruddy gentleman reading Tacitus; upon another,
a lean one sleeping over The Times. Neither had the
appearance of being expected at home.

At the appointed oak I knocked.

The door opened, not upon one man's lodging, but upon
the glowing interior of some secular abbey dedicated to appetite,
argument, and celibacy. Lamps shone upon dark panels. A fire,
broad-shouldered and hospitable, wrestled cheerfully with two logs. Silver caught
the light in quick, confidential flashes. Over the chimney hung
a portrait of a knight whose iron gauntlet rested upon
a sword; immediately beneath it lay a barrister's horsehair wig
beside three sharpened quills. Thus had chivalry declined, or advanced:
the sword into the pen, the helmet into the wig,
the tournament into a suit concerning drains.

"Welcome!" cried Alverton. "You are the tenth, and therefore save
us from the barbarism of an uneven table."

Nine bachelors advanced or inclined toward me. Nine! Not lads
hiding from matrimony, nor gray misers whom matrimony had repudiated,
but prosperous, educated, well-preserved wights, each inhabiting his own snug
island in the archipelago of Law. Their ages ranged from
thirty to that venerable uncertainty at which a man ceases
to grow older and merely grows more judicial. One had
argued before the Lords; one had excavated a Roman bath
in Kent; one collected Venetian glass; one had been twice
to Damascus but never, he proudly informed me, to a
christening. There was a military barrister who knew all campaigns
except those conducted in nurseries; a musical barrister who played
the viol but had never heard a baby's rattle after
midnight; and a pale antiquary who could describe the marriage
customs of the Visigoths while maintaining, upon practical marriage, an
ignorance pure as Adam's before the operation upon his rib.

The tenth bachelor—I speak of myself only ceremonially—was placed near
the fire.

Presently the waiter entered.

He was a bald, grave, spherical man, with the brow
of Socrates, the circumference of Falstaff, and the noiseless tread
of a priest approaching an altar. Behind him came lesser
ministers bearing covered dishes. He surveyed us, the table, the
decanters, the clock, and the fire; then, making one imperceptible
adjustment to a fork, he assumed command.

Had Caesar possessed that waiter, Gaul would have surrendered before
dinner.

The first advance consisted of soup, amber and profound, in
which floated islands of bread. Then came fish, white-fleshed and
delicate, attended by sauces whose genealogy no republican tongue could
trace. A saddle of mutton followed, fortified with potatoes, flanked
by spinach, and supported in reserve by a ham of
such polished magnitude that it seemed the red moon of
some more nourishing planet. Pies appeared; birds arrived in battalions;
jellies trembled but did not retreat. All the while the
Socratic commander directed his forces with napkin signals and murmured
orders, bringing up Madeira when the argument languished, Burgundy when
the mutton resisted, and Champagne at the precise instant when
sobriety threatened to become organized.

No wife asked whether the window were open. No child
upset the salt. No pale domestic messenger whispered that the
cook had quarreled with the housemaid, that the baby had
swallowed a button, that the chimney smoked, that the school
bill had come, or that Aunt Judith intended a visit
of six weeks. Pain! Trouble! The two great duns of
mortal life were denied entrance by the oak.

Outside, London labored, married, bore children, buried them, kept shop,
lost causes, missed trains, and paid interest. Inside, ten men
debated whether Falernian wine had truly deserved Horace.

"Depend upon it," said the antiquary, raising his glass, "Horace
praised the amphora because Maecenas paid for it."

"Then," replied Alverton, "his testimony is the more valuable. Gratitude
is the cellar of criticism."

We drank to criticism.

A gentleman opposite me, whose face had ripened into that
amiable crimson which long residence near good port imparts, asked
whether the forests of my country were indeed as boundless
as reported.

"Nearly so," I said.

"And do your citizens cut them down?"

"With patriotic rapidity."

"A remarkable people. You destroy in fifty years what would
furnish us litigation for five hundred."

We drank to American expedition.

The talk flowed—not in one stream, which might have flooded
a listener, but in sparkling channels crossing and recrossing the
table. Constantinople, cricket, canon law, the superiority of English beef,
the inferiority of English weather, the probability of Homer's blindness,
and the certainty of every man's own palate were successively
considered. Anecdote relieved argument; contradiction seasoned assent. One bachelor had
seen a Spanish duke eat an orange with a knife
and fork; another had known a judge who quoted Virgil
when drunk, but only legal precedents when sober; a third
maintained that no civilized man should marry before forty, while
a fourth amended the proposition by striking out the last
two words.

The decanters circulated. Red, golden, brown, they pursued one another
round the mahogany like horses upon a convivial course. Whenever
one flagged, the waiter, vigilant field-marshal of our felicity, replaced
it with a fresher charger. It would have been discourteous,
indeed almost cruel, not to inquire continually after one another's
health; and since health, like a doubtful witness, requires repeated
examination, we interrogated it in Madeira, cross-examined it in claret,
and finally dismissed it with honors in port.

"Another glass?" asked my host.

"Merely as testimony to the climate," said I.

"Which climate?"

"The interior climate of these chambers."

He bowed. "Always temperate; occasional showers of wit toward midnight."

By degrees the knight above the chimney seemed to relax
his iron posture. The carved lions upon the mantel grinned.
The volumes in the bookcases—equity, torts, ecclesiastical pleas, feudal incidents—lost
something of their threat and became a learned congregation listening
indulgently to our wassailing. Time himself, that lean process-server who
elsewhere thrusts summonses beneath every door, here took off his
shoes and slept upon the hearth.

What women inhabited this region? Washerwomen, perhaps, on certain mornings;
an elderly housekeeper ascending with keys; a widow who sold
sealing wax near the gate. But wives? Daughters? Young mothers
with care upon the brow and arithmetic in the purse?
None. The inhabitants were sons without parents, husbands without wives,
fathers without children—men severed from consequence and preserved in comfort
as fruit in spirits.

Do not suppose they were hard-hearted. Far from it! They
subscribed to hospitals, deplored distress in the newspapers, and knew
several moving cases at second hand. One had defended a
governess without a fee. Another annually sent five guineas to
a distressed-clergymen's fund. Their benevolence was genuine, regular, receipted, and
never permitted to disturb dinner.

The fire sank; dessert ascended. Nuts, figs, pale grapes, ruddy
apples, and a cheese of formidable fragrance occupied the field
abandoned by the birds. The waiter set before us a
bowl of hot punch. Steam curled upward like incense from
a pagan font.

At that hour the Temple seemed not an institution of
London, but an enchanted province floating beyond ordinary obligation. Here
were education without pedantry, wealth without ostentation, age without decay,
companionship without sacrifice. No apron tugged a sleeve; no fever
burned behind a wall; no feminine eye, bright with hope
or dulled by fatigue, asked any man there what he
had done with his portion of the world.

"What a life!" I exclaimed.

"What else should life be?" said Alverton.

Indeed—what else?

Near midnight I departed. The porter had closed one gate,
but a smaller door admitted me into Fleet Street. The
transition was violent. A cold drizzle had begun. A woman,
stooping beneath a basket of unsold flowers, hurried past. Two
ragged girls slept under an awning with their heads upon
a bundle of newspapers. A cab splashed them. Neither stirred.

Yet the port glowed within me; the laughter of the
bachelors rang pleasantly behind; and I confess—yes, write it against
me if you must—that I looked back toward the hidden
courts as toward Eden, and thought the flaming sword had
been set not to expel those fortunate Adams, but to
keep every troublesome Eve outside.

## II. The Road to Devil's Loom

My business in America was seeds.

Not poetry's seeds, reader; not those airy germs which philosophers
scatter in lectures and reap, after many seasons, in footnotes;
but palpable seed—beet, carrot, cabbage, cucumber, squash, onion, turnip—those diminutive
brown and yellow promises from which the kitchen garden takes
its republic of leaves.

To distribute them, I required envelopes: millions of little paper
sepulchres wherein vegetable life might sleep until summoned by April.
The paper must be thin, tough, absorbent of ink, uniform
in weight, and cheap. Especially cheap. Commerce, whatever its advertisements
may declare, adores cheapness with a devotion more constant than
love, more exacting than religion.

For several years I purchased from agents. Then, hearing that
a remote New England mill manufactured a peculiarly fine white
paper at a rate which would diminish my expenses by
three-eighths of a cent per thousand envelopes, I resolved—being then
as zealous for fractions as a crusader for relics—to visit
the place myself.

It was February.

The mill lay beyond Black Notch, a mountain cleft of
evil reputation, in a district so sparsely inhabited that maps
indicated it chiefly by leaving it blank. I traveled first
by rail, then by sleigh, and finally, when the innkeeper
at North Sable refused both horse and driver, by a
narrow cutter hired from a farmer whose expression implied that
he sold me not transportation, but a funeral.

"You'll want the gray mare," he said.

"Is she strong?"

"She knows the road."

"That was not my question."

"It's the answer you need."

The mare was harnessed. The sky, already low, had the
color of unwritten paper. Snow covered road, field, wall, and
frozen stream, abolishing distinctions with tyrannical impartiality. A stump resembled
a crouching man; a man at sufficient distance might have
passed for a stump. Chimneys smoked without visible houses. The
sun hung faintly in the west like a coin worn
smooth by too many hands.

At first the road rose gently between farms. Then the
farms ceased. Stone walls, half drowned in drifts, wandered into
the woods and ended there, as if the vanished husbandmen
had attempted to fence wilderness and been devoured in the
act. The firs drew closer. Their boughs, loaded with snow,
bent like mourners above a procession. Now and again a
great trunk cracked in the frost—sharp, sudden, musket-like—and the mare
started.

"Steady, gray philosopher," I said. "The shot is not for
us."

She turned one dark eye backward, withholding agreement.

Toward dusk the wind awoke. It did not begin; it
arrived—an invisible multitude rushing down from the peaks, shrieking through
the pines, snatching the loose snow from the ground and
flinging it against my face. The road narrowed beside a
ravine. Far below, some unfrozen current boiled blackly under shelves
of ice. Its voice ascended in broken growls.

At a fork stood a signboard, its letters nearly erased:

LEFT HAND—BLACK NOTCH MILL
RIGHT HAND—SABLE VILLAGE

Below, carved by some cheerful rustic, appeared the words:

NO ROAD FOR WOMEN.

I laughed, though not heartily. "A masculine road, then! Come,
Madam Mare; let us trespass."

The left-hand track plunged downward. The snow, driven level across
it, concealed every rut. On one side rose a wall
of rock ribbed with icicles; on the other the ravine
opened its black throat. The mare proceeded not by sight,
for sight had become useless, but by that inherited wisdom
which men call instinct whenever an animal exhibits intelligence superior
to their own.

The pass had local names. I learned them afterward, though
the places seemed to announce themselves as I came: Hangman's
Turn, where the road doubled beneath an arm of dead
pine; Coffin Shelf, a ledge scarcely wider than the cutter;
Widow's Breath, where wind issued continually from a fissure in
the rock; and, deepest of all, Devil's Loom, where the
red river, compressed between black cliffs, wove itself into foam.

Red river? So it appeared. Whether iron stained the water,
or sunset, filtered through the storm, imparted that sanguinary hue,
I could not determine. But there it ran beneath the
white world—a long, torn vein in the body of winter.

The Temple returned to me.

Why then? Why should London's green courts, warm fire, and
circular decanters arise in a mountain pass where my eyelashes
froze and my hands ached within fur gloves? Yet arise
they did. I saw Alverton lifting the port. I heard
the antiquary dispute about Horace. The shriek of the wind
became laughter; the red river, claret; the ice-clad trees, grave
barristers in wigs.

Then the cutter struck a buried stone.

The vision shattered. I was flung sideways; the mare staggered;
one runner hung over the ravine. For an instant we
balanced between commerce and eternity. I scrambled out, seized the
bridle, and pulled while the mare, with a heave more
rational than any action of mine, regained the track.

"Venerable lady!" I cried, embracing her icy neck. "You have
preserved a customer!"

She sneezed upon me.

Darkness thickened. I began to fear that the mill, like
a fraudulent prospectus, existed only on paper. But after another
mile the cliffs withdrew, and I saw, through the storm,
a long white structure crouched beside the river.

No lamps shone in its windows. Yet from within came
a steady vibration—whirring, humming, beating—too regular for water, too vast
for any single engine. The building seemed an avalanche arrested
before its fall, a mass of pallid matter pinned to
the valley by a tall black chimney.

This was the mill.

A bell sounded once.

The doors opened, and women came out.

## III. The White Procession

At first I mistook them for children because of their
slightness and because the storm erased the lower parts of
their figures. Then they approached: young women, perhaps twenty, perhaps
thirty—it was difficult to count faces so similar in pallor,
bonnets so alike, shawls so thin. They issued in two
files without conversation. Snow struck them, clung to them, whitened
them further. One coughed into her hand. Another tied a
rag about her throat. A third carried a tin dinner
pail upon which, in faded blue, a bird had been
painted.

None looked toward me except one girl at the end.

Her face was narrow, almost translucent; the brows lightly marked;
the lips without color. Yet the eyes were dark and
living. For one second they met mine—not imploringly, not resentfully,
but with the startled reserve of a creature which has
forgotten that it may be observed. Then she lowered them
and followed the rest toward a row of boardinghouses half
buried beyond the mill.

A man in a tarred coat appeared at the door.

"You from North Sable?" he called.

"Yes."

"Mr. Brindle?"

"Yes."

"Drive round. Stable's behind the rag house."

The gray mare required no second invitation. Within a low
shed I rubbed her down while a stable boy supplied
oats. That boy, blue-nosed and silent, might have been fourteen
or forty; labor had rubbed the dates from him. I
then followed the man in the tarred coat to the
office.

He introduced himself as Mr. Voss, superintendent.

Voss was spare, black-haired, and dry, as if all the
moisture denied the paper had collected nowhere in him. His
clothes fitted closely; his fingers were long; his eyes quick,
dark, and incapable of rest. He apologized for the weather,
not because he held himself responsible for it, but because
it had delayed my arrival and therefore disturbed a schedule.

"You'll stay the night," he said. "Road's shut by now."

"I had expected an inn."

"There's no inn."

"A private house, then?"

"There's the company house."

"Comfortable?"

"It has beds."

His answer possessed the dreadful sufficiency of a legal verdict.

Our business began at once. Samples were produced, held to
the light, torn, weighed, dampened, folded, and written upon. Voss
spoke of fiber, finish, sizing, loss, speed, and freight. The
paper was admirable. It received ink as a desert receives
rain. It folded without cracking. It was white—white beyond common
whiteness, not creamy, not warm, but blankly, absolutely white.

"You bleach heavily," I observed.

"We bleach thoroughly."

"And the cost?"

He named it.

Ah, that fraction! My commercial heart leaped. Three-eighths of a
cent per thousand, and perhaps one-half if I ordered above
a certain quantity. Envelopes multiplied before me; profits unfolded their
green leaves. I saw catalogues, warehouses, western agencies. Beet, carrot,
cabbage, cucumber! The republic of vegetables marched prosperously across my
mind.

Yet beneath our words, beneath the scratch of Voss's pencil
and the tapping of sleet against the panes, the mill
continued its enormous murmur.

"How many hands do you employ?" I asked.

"Eighty-three at present."

"Men and women?"

"Fourteen men. The rest girls."

"Girls?"

"They suit the work."

He drew a line beneath a figure.

"Why?"

"Nimbler fingers. Less wages. Fewer disturbances."

"Fewer?"

"They board together. No families in the valley."

No families. The phrase opened, for one flashing instant, upon
the Temple: no wives, no children, no domestic disturbances. But
there the absence had been a luxury; here it was
a condition of hire.

"You arrived at change of shift," Voss continued. "Night hands
going in now. Day hands out. We run continuously except
Sunday maintenance."

"Continuously?"

"Paper won't pay if machinery prays."

He smiled. It was not an agreeable smile, yet I
suspect he intended no cruelty. He worshiped continuity as other
men worship saints.

I signed a preliminary memorandum. It remained only to inspect
the mill before approving the full contract.

"Now?" I asked, glancing toward the black windows.

"Better now. You'll see everything running."

He took a lantern from a peg, though much of
the mill, he said, had gas. As we entered the
corridor, heat breathed upon us—wet, chemical, stale. My frozen cheeks
stung. The floor trembled. Somewhere a bell rang twice, and
from a room ahead came the iron rhythm:

Whir—hiss—thump.
Whir—hiss—thump.
Whir—hiss—thump.

"Mind your coat near the shafts," said Voss.

## IV. The Rag Room

We first ascended to the rag chamber, where the ancestry
of paper lay heaped in mountainous disgrace.

Rags! White rags, gray rags, black rags; linen, cotton, canvas;
sleeves severed from forgotten arms, collars escaped from forgotten throats,
curtains which had concealed births and deaths, tablecloths stained by
extinct feasts, hospital sheets, soldiers' bandages, children's frocks, widows' caps,
sailors' trousers, petticoats, flour sacks, shrouds perhaps—for what rag confesses
its final office?

They rose in bales to the beams. Cut cords bristled
like nerves. Dust swam beneath the lamps.

Women sorted them at long tables. Each held a curved
knife fixed upright in the board and drew the cloth
against its edge, slicing away seams, buttons, hooks, and impurities.
Snip, rip, cast. Snip, rip, cast. Their hands moved rapidly;
their faces scarcely moved at all.

"Imported and domestic stock," said Voss. "We separate by grade."

One girl shook out a small crimson dress, no larger
than an infant might wear. For an instant the garment
opened in her hands as if inhabited. Then she cut
it across the breast and threw the halves into separate
bins.

The military barrister of the Temple had carved a bird
with just such precision.

"Dust troublesome?" I asked.

"At first."

"At first?"

"They get used to it."

A girl nearby coughed. She bent double, pressing her wrist
against her mouth, but her other hand continued sorting.

"Is there ventilation?"

Voss pointed toward a round aperture in the wall. It
was clogged with snow.

"The storm interferes."

The girl straightened. A streak of blood, narrow as a
red thread, marked her wrist before she wiped it on
her apron.

I moved on.

At the far end, three women fed the rags into
a revolving cylinder armed with steel teeth. The machine seized
the cloth, tore it, and discharged a pale, fibrous cloud.
It did not resemble an instrument fashioned by men; rather
some armored appetite from beneath the earth, blind but exact,
eternally chewing the cast-off garments of mankind.

"Devil," said Voss.

I started.

"What?"

"That's what the hands call the cutter. Rag devil."

An apt baptism! The creature opened no mouth, yet devoured;
possessed no stomach, yet was never filled. A loose strip
of lace fluttered from the heap, delicate as sea foam.
The iron teeth caught it. Gone.

As the women leaned forward, their hair concealed beneath caps,
their skirts drawn close from the shafts, I noticed a
terrible accord between body and mechanism. The machine pulled; the
arms supplied. The wheel revolved; the torsos bent. No command
was spoken. Long practice had made every girl a cog
of flesh engaging a cog of iron.

"Do accidents occur?" I asked.

"Not often."

That phrase—not often—has comforted more prosperous consciences than all the
sermons ever preached.

Voss showed me a wooden box containing buttons, buckles, hooks,
coins, thimbles, and other objects recovered from the rags. He
stirred them with his finger.

"Found a wedding ring last month."

"What became of it?"

"Sold."

"To whom?"

He shrugged. "A peddler."

A wedding ring traveling from finger to rag, from rag
to iron box, from iron box to peddler—was it on
its way to another finger, another promise, another worn garment?
The cycle seemed less mechanical than human.

Near the door sat the dark-eyed girl I had seen
outside. She was replacing the knife blade in her table.

"What is your name?" I asked.

She looked toward Voss before answering.

"Miriam Hale."

"How long have you worked here, Miriam?"

"Four years."

"You are from this district?"

"No, sir."

"Where is your home?"

She hesitated. "East."

East! As if the rising of the sun were a
county.

"Do you find the work difficult?"

Her fingers tightened upon the blade.

"No, sir."

The machine roared. Dust curled between us. I wished to
ask whether she was happy, but the question, suitable enough
beneath a chandelier after three glasses of port, appeared monstrous
there.

Voss tapped his lantern.

"Bleaching vats next."

Miriam returned to her rags. A sleeve passed beneath her
knife. A collar. A child's stocking. Snip, rip, cast.

## V. The Whitening

We descended by an iron stair into vapor.

The bleaching room was a cavern of tubs and pipes.
Great vats, sunk to their rims in the floor, received
the shredded fiber. Steam wandered in dense processions, revealing and
concealing the workers. The air bit the throat with chlorine.
Lamps floated like moons in fog.

Here men tended valves while women pushed wheeled bins along
wet planks. Their shoes slipped; their skirts clung damply to
their ankles. One girl's bare forearms were mottled red and
white.

"The bleach extracts color," Voss explained. "Ink, dye, stain—everything."

"Everything?"

"Everything that can be extracted."

He spoke with professional pride.

A vat revolved slowly. Within, the rags churned in a
pale liquor. Crimson, blue, brown, the accumulated colors of innumerable
lives weakened, bled, and vanished. The soldier's bandage surrendered battle;
the child's dress surrendered play; the widow's cap surrendered grief.
All distinction dissolved into fiber. All history was whitened.

Blankness, then, was not original purity. It was the result
of violence.

I leaned over the rail. Steam rose into my face,
and in the turning pulp I imagined the bachelor table
stripped of its colors. Burgundy faded; gold plate paled; green
ivy grayed; ruddy cheeks whitened. The ten of us sat
around a blank board lifting empty glasses with blank hands.

I drew back.

"Strong fumes," said Voss.

"Yes. Fumes."

At one vat a young woman had paused, gripping the
handle of her bin.

"Move along, Ellen," called the foreman.

She did not move.

He crossed to her, touched her shoulder, and she folded—not
dramatically, not with a cry, but softly, as wet paper
folds. Two girls caught her before she struck the planks.

"Take her to the washroom," said Voss.

They obeyed. The line of bins closed around the vacancy.

"Is she ill?" I asked.

"Fainted."

"From the fumes?"

"Could be breakfast."

"Lack of it?"

"Could be."

He checked his watch.

The room did not stop. No wheel moderated; no belt
loosened; no steam diminished. A human body had failed, but
production, being of sterner constitution, proceeded.

"How long are the shifts?"

"Twelve hours."

"Twelve!"

"Half an hour for meals."

"And at night?"

"Twelve."

"Do they alternate?"

"Monthly."

The waiter in the Temple had watched our health with
almost maternal vigilance. Here a girl fainted, and the clock
alone received attention.

"Are wages sufficient?"

"They are the county rate."

This was an answer shaped like an answer, colored like
an answer, stamped and circulated as an answer, yet containing
no answer—paper currency of the conscience.

Beyond the vats stood washing engines in which the fiber
was cleansed. Their paddles revolved with a heavy, watery beating,
like giant hearts condemned to pump forever. The floor shook
beneath them. Warm drops fell from the rafters.

A woman passed carrying a bucket. She was perhaps fifty—the
oldest female worker I saw—and her face, unlike the young
faces, retained expression: a fixed, bitter compression around the mouth.
As she went by, she looked at Voss, then at
me.

"Buying?" she asked.

"Yes," I said.

"Buy plenty."

Her tone puzzled me.

"Why do you say that?"

"So they'll run Sundays too."

Voss turned sharply. "Mrs. Rane."

She continued without haste.

"Pay her no mind," he told me. "Her daughter left
last winter."

"Left the mill?"

"Left everything."

We walked on.

What did he mean? Married? Fled? Died? In such places
the verbs wear masks.

## VI. The Iron Animals

From bleaching we entered the beating room, and there speech
became almost impossible.

Enormous engines occupied the floor, each filled with pulp and
armed with revolving bars which crushed the fibers finer and
finer. Wheels spun overhead; belts traveled diagonally through the gloom;
pistons thrust; rods flashed. The whole building appeared less to
contain machinery than to have been consumed by it, ribs,
veins, and muscles of iron growing through brick and timber
until the mill itself became one animal composed of many.

Whirring! Humming! Hammering! A ceaseless industrial weather.

Voss shouted explanations into my ear. I heard fragments.

"FIBER LENGTH—"

"—SIZING—"

"—CONSISTENCY—"

His mouth moved like that of a man speaking beyond
aquarium glass.

Girls stood at troughs testing the pulp with their fingers.
Others watched gauges. One crossed between two belts so rapidly
that my breath stopped. Her shawl's loose end fluttered toward
a shaft; she caught it without looking, tucked it in,
and proceeded.

The engines fascinated despite horror. There was grandeur in their
precision, seduction in their certainty. No hesitation, no melancholy, no
memory. Iron does not awaken unwillingly; iron does not count
the years; iron never asks whether life elsewhere is warm.
Feed it, oil it, drive it, and it fulfills its
purpose with the innocence of damnation.

Men admire this innocence. We call it efficiency.

At a narrow platform above the beaters, I again saw
Miriam. She leaned over a trough, lifting pulp in a
wooden scoop, letting the milky liquid stream back.

Voss led me toward her.

"Show Mr. Brindle the stock."

She handed me the scoop. The pulp within resembled thin
porridge, but when I pressed it between finger and thumb,
minute fibers clung to the skin.

"This becomes paper?"

"Yes, sir."

"In how long?"

"Seven minutes from the chest to the reel."

"Only seven?"

"If nothing catches."

"What catches?"

She looked at the moving belts.

"Anything."

Her eyes returned to mine. In them I read—not accusation,
which I might have resisted; not appeal, which I might
have answered with money—but knowledge. She knew the machine could
take anything. Rag. Hair. Finger. Hour. Youth. Name. She knew;
Voss knew; perhaps I alone had required instruction.

"Do you work at several stations?" I asked.

"Where they put me."

"And when does your shift end?"

"Six."

It was then near eight at night.

"Ten hours more?"

She nodded.

"What do you do afterward?"

"Sleep."

"And before?"

"Sleep."

A perfect circle: work preparing for sleep, sleep preparing for
work. No court, no garden, no books, no talk flowing
from Homer to weather; only one blank interval feeding another.

"Have you no recreation?"

The word sounded absurdly ornamental, a silk tassel hung upon
an iron door.

"Sunday, sometimes."

"What then?"

"Wash."

Voss beckoned impatiently. I returned the scoop.

As we descended, I glanced back. Miriam had resumed testing
the pulp. Her face, seen through steam and machinery, seemed
already a watermark embedded in the future sheet—visible only when
held against the light.

## VII. The Endless Web

At last we came to the paper machine.

Imagine a hall long as a cathedral nave, but instead
of saints in colored glass, windows blind with frost; instead
of an organ, shafts and rollers; instead of worshipers, lines
of pale girls; instead of an altar, a breast-high apparatus
from which issued, continuously and miraculously, a broad white web.

The liquid pulp flowed first upon a traveling screen. Water
drained away. Shaking frames knitted the fibers together. The tender
film passed between presses, then around heated cylinders, each stage
making it drier, smoother, stronger, whiter. At the farther end
it wound upon a reel in a vast, immaculate roll.

Wet nothing entered. Blank paper emerged.

It was beautiful.

There lies the shame and mystery: it was beautiful. The
web traveled with such unbroken grace, such snowy breadth and
silent inevitability, that I could not deny admiration. Had I
seen only the product and not the hands, only the
white river and not the pallid banks of girls, I
might have called it purity materialized.

Along one side stood the tenders. Each watched a portion
of the process. Their task appeared light. A touch here,
a lifted edge there, a quick tying of broken web,
a glance at the gauge. Yet their attention could never
cease. The machine demanded eyes as tribute. Minute followed minute,
hour hour, while the blank sheet streamed before them, hypnotic,
interminable.

One girl's brow was lined horizontally by concentration, as if
the paper had ruled her face.

Another moved her lips. Praying? Counting? Repeating a song inaudible
beneath the mechanism?

A third had pinned a dried flower to the wall.
The vibration shook it continually, though there was no breeze.
Its color had faded to the same neutral hue as
the paper.

"What speed?" I asked.

Voss indicated a dial.

"Two hundred feet per minute."

Two hundred feet! The machine devoured distance while remaining in
place. In one hour it traveled more road than the
gray mare had crossed all day; in one shift, leagues
upon leagues of whiteness—yet no girl beside it advanced an
inch.

A tear appeared in the web.

Instantly the paper snapped upward, whipping loose. Bells clattered. The
girls sprang together. One cut the ragged end; another drew
the new edge through rollers; a third adjusted tension. Their
movements were swift, concerted, almost military.

The bachelor dinner returned again: the waiter deploying dishes, decanters
circling, each guest performing his flourish in the campaign against
hunger. There abundance had been playfully martial. Here labor was
war without banners, wounds without dispatches, discipline without victory.

The machine resumed.

"And so it goes," said Voss.

"And so it goes," I repeated.

He mistook my tone for approval.

"You can see why we guarantee uniform stock."

Uniform! Yes. Uniform fiber, uniform thickness, uniform women; blank paper,
blank hours, blank faces. Difference entered as rag and emerged
abolished.

At the reel, two girls lifted finished paper to a
cutting table. One was very young. Her hair, where it
escaped her cap, had the pale gold of straw. She
coughed repeatedly but suppressed the sound in her sleeve.

I approached her during a pause.

"How old are you?" I asked.

"Sixteen."

"Sixteen?"

"Next May."

"Where are your parents?"

"Father's in Maine. Mother's dead."

"Do you send money home?"

"To the little ones."

"How many?"

"Four."

Thus even here, where families were said not to exist,
invisible children fed upon the girl's labor. She was sister,
daughter, wage, remittance; yet before the machine she was merely
a pair of hands.

"Does the cough trouble you?"

"No, sir."

Again that answer. No, sir. The catechism of necessity.

Voss drew me away to show the calendars—great polished rollers
imparting finish to the sheet. Paper passed between them and
came forth smooth enough for the finest printing.

"Feel that," he said.

I laid my hand upon a sample.

It was cool, firm, exquisite.

What letters would be written upon it? Love letters, perhaps.
Invitations to dinners. Legal opinions. Sermons on charity. Bills for
wine. Poems celebrating snow. Advertisements promising health. Marriage contracts. Death
notices. Seed envelopes.

Every civilized sentiment required a surface, and here was the
surface—white, silent, affordable.

## VIII. The Folding Room

The final chamber was quieter, though quiet there meant only
that one could hear the machinery distinctly instead of being
stunned by it.

Cut sheets came along a belt to rows of women
who inspected, counted, folded, and packed them. Tap, lift, fold.
Tap, lift, fold. Their fingers moved with astonishing speed. Some
tied reams; some stamped wrappers; some fed smaller machines which
clipped and sorted.

Gas jets burned overhead, staining the air yellow. The windows
were black mirrors. In them the room repeated itself: girl
behind girl, machine behind machine, an illimitable colony of reflections
laboring in a night without exterior.

Upon a stool sat the girl who had fainted. Ellen.
A cup stood beside her.

"Recovered?" Voss asked.

"Yes."

"Then back at six?"

She looked at him.

"I'm night shift."

"So you are."

She rose.

"But surely," I said, "she should rest."

Both turned toward me. My interference embarrassed Ellen more than
it annoyed Voss.

"She says she's able," he replied.

"I'm able," she whispered.

Able! The word had been beaten thin, bleached of protest,
pressed smooth.

A stack of sheets slid crookedly. Ellen straightened it and
resumed counting.

I walked down the row. Twenty-five, fifty, one hundred, fold.
Twenty-five, fifty, one hundred, fold. Paper clicked against paper. Fingers
reddened at the tips. Faces bent beneath the gaslight.

At the last station Miriam wrapped reams.

Our eyes met once more.

"Do you read?" I asked, holding up a sheet.

"When I can."

"What?"

"Whatever comes."

"Books?"

"Sometimes spoiled print from the city. Old papers."

"What do you like?"

She considered, as though no one had asked before.

"Travels."

The answer pierced me more keenly than complaint.

"Travels where?"

"Anywhere."

Anywhere! For a girl stationed beside two hundred feet per
minute of motion, anywhere was the fabulous country.

"I have traveled," I said foolishly.

"Yes, sir."

"I was in London last autumn."

She waited.

"In a place called the Temple. Very old courts, gardens,
libraries. Lawyers live there."

"Are there women?"

"Few."

"Do they make paper?"

The question was asked without irony, yet irony stood immense
behind it.

"No."

"What do they make?"

I could have said arguments, judgments, deeds, delays. I could
have said comfort. I could have said nothing.

"Documents," I answered.

She folded the wrapper around a ream.

"This may go there," said Voss. "We ship considerable writing
stock to Boston, and Boston exports."

The paper beneath Miriam's hand might indeed cross the ocean,
enter the Temple, receive some learned barrister's opinion concerning estates
or railways, and lie upon his desk beside the port.
Her labor might visit those green courts though she never
would.

A bell rang.

Every girl at once stepped back.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Meal period," said Voss.

From beneath benches they brought tin pails. No dining room
awaited them. They sat where they had stood, among paper
dust and oil smells. Bread, cold potatoes, an apple divided,
something gray in a jar. Miriam opened her pail and
withdrew two slices of bread with molasses between.

Half an hour for meals.

I remembered the saddle of mutton, the birds, the pies,
the steaming punch; the waiter's tactical deployments; the solemn concern
with which we had examined one another's health in successive
wines. Here no decanter ran its genial circuit. A water
bucket stood at the end of the room with one
tin dipper for all.

One girl laughed.

It was the first laugh I had heard within the
mill. A companion had balanced a crust upon her nose.
The laugh was brief, surprised, girlish—so girlish that the whole
terrible abstraction of "hands" broke apart. These were not pallid
attendants born beside machinery. They had childhoods, vanities, jokes, dislikes,
secret songs. One might dance. One might imitate the foreman.
One might love scarlet ribbon. One might dread thunderstorms. One
might remember a pear tree at home.

Then Voss looked over.

The crust came down. The laugh ceased.

Humanity had appeared by accident and was corrected.

## IX. The Company House

My inspection ended near ten. Voss led me through the
storm to the company house, a wooden building behind the
mill where visitors, foremen, and certain unmarried men lodged apart
from the women's boarding row.

Supper had been left for us: salt pork, beans, brown
bread, and coffee boiled to a judicial bitterness. The food
was plentiful, though not paradisiacal. Voss ate quickly, folding business
into mastication.

"You've seen the process," he said. "Any objection to the
stock?"

"None to the stock."

"The terms?"

"They are favorable."

"Then we can sign tonight."

The contract lay beside my plate. White paper. Black lines.
Blank spaces patiently awaiting quantities, dates, signatures.

"How long do the girls remain?" I asked.

"At the mill?"

"Yes."

"Varies."

"Years?"

"Some."

"And afterward?"

"Marry, go home, take sick, find another mill."

"Take sick."

He added beans to his plate.

"You're concerned about labor," he said.

"Should I not be?"

"Concern is easy in a visitor."

The sentence stopped me.

Voss drank his coffee.

"You come once," he continued. "Storm outside, steam inside, girls
look pale. You compare it to whatever comfortable thing you
know. Then you leave. We remain. Orders remain. Freight remains.
Wages Saturday. Coal bill monthly. Banks quarterly. If I cut
hours, another mill takes the contract. If I raise wages,
buyers go elsewhere."

"Buyers such as I."

"Precisely."

He did not accuse me. Accusation might have allowed defense.
He merely inserted me into the machine.

"What would you have me do?" I asked.

"Sign or don't sign."

"You make morality sound like an order form."

"In business it generally arrives as one."

Outside, the wind struck the house. From beyond the wall
came the mill's vibration, subdued but constant. Cups trembled faintly
in their saucers.

"I could pay more," I said.

"You could."

"If the increase went to wages."

"Perhaps."

"Perhaps?"

"Owners decide. And if your envelopes cost more?"

"I could charge more."

"Customers might buy from another seedsman."

The iron animals extended far beyond the valley. Their belts
ran invisibly through countinghouse, shop, household, purse. Voss was caught;
the girls were caught; I too was caught, though in
a warmer portion of the mechanism.

Yet there are degrees of captivity. I could leave.

The girls could not—not without hunger following close behind.

Voss pushed the contract toward me.

I signed.

Let no artful narrator conceal the ink at this point.
I signed. The paper was excellent; the price advantageous; my
competitors active. I obtained a promise, not quite explicit, that
ventilation would be improved and fainting workers permitted rest. I
added a small allowance per ton, designated for the boardinghouses.
Perhaps it reached them. Perhaps it repaired a roof. Perhaps
it vanished into figures. Good intentions are light cargo; they
seldom delay a commercial vehicle.

After Voss retired, I went to my room.

The bed was clean. A white counterpane covered it. White
curtains hung at the window. White frost flowered upon the
glass. I extinguished the lamp, but whiteness remained, faintly luminous
in the darkness.

Sleep did not come.

The machine's pulse traveled through the ground:

Whir—hiss—thump.
Whir—hiss—thump.

I imagined the paper passing over rollers beneath my bed,
broad and endless, carrying me upon it like a corpse
on a winding sheet. I saw the Temple bachelors seated
around their table, but the cloth expanded, flowed through the
window, crossed the Atlantic, climbed the mountains, and entered the
mill. It wrapped the mutton, covered the books, stopped the
mouths of the laughing men. The decanters whitened. The wine
within turned clear as bleach.

Then the girls entered the chambers, not bodily, but as
pale impressions in every sheet. Miriam stood behind Alverton's chair.
Ellen leaned over the antiquary. The sixteen-year-old with the cough
waited beside the Socratic waiter. No bachelor saw them. He
wrote upon the paper; beneath his words, their faces remained.

At midnight the shifts changed again, or some portion changed.
I heard footsteps outside. Looking through the frost, I discerned
women crossing the yard in the storm. The lantern by
the gate shone upon them one by one, then released
them into darkness.

A file of white figures entering a white sepulchre.

I returned to bed, but the bed had become another
blank sheet, and I feared to lie upon it.

## X. Departure

Morning brought no sunrise, only a gradual disclosure of snow.

The storm had weakened. Voss announced that the road might
be passable if I departed before noon. The gray mare
greeted me with the composed reproach of one who has
rescued a fool and finds him still foolish.

As the cutter was brought round, I walked once more
toward the mill.

Daylight did not improve it. The building's boards were stained;
icicles hung from the eaves like organ pipes; black smoke
descended instead of rising. The river ran red-brown beneath cakes
of ice. On its opposite bank the pines stood immovable,
witnesses sworn to silence.

Miriam was crossing from the boardinghouse.

"Your shift is over?" I asked.

"Yes, sir."

Her face in daylight looked more tired than by gas.

"I am leaving."

"Yes, sir."

"I signed the order."

She glanced at the mill.

"That's good."

"Is it?"

"For work."

She began to pass.

"Miriam."

She stopped.

I took out my purse. Here was the ancient refuge
of discomfort: money privately transferred, conscience privately soothed. Yet to
offer a coin seemed both necessary and insulting.

"Could I send you books?" I asked.

A faint change touched her face.

"Travels?"

"Yes. Travels."

She gave me the name of the boardinghouse keeper. I
wrote it down upon one of Voss's sample sheets.

"Where would you go," I asked, "if you could go
anywhere?"

The mill's bell rang. Though her labor was finished, she
turned toward the sound by habit.

"Somewhere green," she said.

Then she walked away.

Somewhere green.

The words accompanied me through Black Notch. The mountains remained
white; the cliffs black; the river red. Yet in my
sample case lay paper intended for seed envelopes—white chambers soon
to contain tiny brown possibilities of green.

Was that consolation? Beware of consolation too neatly symbolic. A
cabbage seed does not redeem a factory. A flower printed
on a tin pail does not make spring. Still, the
mind, desperate for bridges, builds them from whatever lies at
hand.

At Coffin Shelf I looked back. Smoke marked the mill's
position long after the building vanished. At Widow's Breath the
wind rose once, scattering snow like torn paper. At Hangman's
Turn the mare quickened, eager for the open country.

By dusk we reached the farms. Lamps shone. Through one
window I saw a family at supper: a man, a
woman, three children. The youngest pounded a spoon. The mother
rose twice. The father wiped spilled milk with his sleeve.
Confusion! Expense! Trouble! Yet warmth gathered around them in a
manner no bachelor fire had achieved.

I thought of the Temple and felt its charm still.
Let me not falsify memory. The dinner had been delightful.
The talk was brilliant. Fellowship is no crime; comfort no
sin; wine does not become blood merely because misery exists
elsewhere. But insulation, when mistaken for innocence—there begins the darker
arithmetic.

The bachelors had seemed free because the costs of life
were kept outside their gate. The mill girls seemed bound
because costs collected around them. Yet between gate and mill
ran roads, ships, contracts, ledgers, purchases, appetites—an enormous web finer
than paper and stronger than iron.

I had traveled it both ways.

## XI. The First Shipment

Spring came.

One morning, while rain tapped pleasantly upon my warehouse roof,
the first shipment from Black Notch arrived: twenty crates of
fine white stock. My clerks opened them. The sheets were
flawless.

"Remarkable finish," said my foreman.

"Yes."

"And cheap."

"Yes."

We printed seed envelopes.

Across each blank surface the press stamped bright vegetables: red
tomatoes, green cucumbers, purple cabbages, golden squash. Color returned to
the bleached paper in cheerful commercial emblems. Beneath the pictures
appeared promises:

EARLY ABUNDANCE.
HARDY.
PROLIFIC.
THRIVES IN POOR SOIL.
SURE CROP.

The phrases, ordinarily innocent, disturbed me. Hardy! Prolific! Thrives in
poor soil! What cruel horticulturist had devised mankind?

I held an envelope to the light. For an instant
the thin sheet showed a faint irregularity, a wavering thread
in the fiber. It resembled a vein. Then the light
shifted and it was gone.

I sent books to Miriam: voyages in warm seas, travels
among Italian gardens, an account of tropical forests, and a
volume describing the green courts of old London. I enclosed
no letter beyond my name, fearing alike sentiment and condescension.

Months passed without reply.

In October I returned to London. Alverton summoned me again
to the Temple. The same oak opened; the same fire
welcomed; several of the same bachelors advanced with hands extended.
The Socratic waiter, perhaps rounder, perhaps merely nearer, commanded another
magnificent dinner.

The decanters began their circuit.

"Your health," said Alverton.

"And yours."

"Your American enterprise prospers?"

"Exceedingly."

"Still conquering the wilderness with turnips?"

"With turnips, beets, and paper."

"Paper!" cried the antiquary. "The true skin of civilization."

The phrase struck the table like a dropped knife, though
no one else heard it fall.

A legal opinion lay beside my host's elbow, written upon
white paper of extraordinary finish. I lifted one corner.

"Excellent stock," he said. "Our stationer imports it."

"From America?"

"I believe so."

I held it against the lamp.

There, within the sheet, scarcely perceptible, ran a faint wandering
fiber.

Miriam's face arose—not as I had first seen it in
the storm, but as she might see herself in one
of the books: walking under green trees in a country
without bells.

"Why so solemn?" asked Alverton. "You look as if the
paper had delivered judgment."

"Perhaps it has."

He laughed and filled my glass.

Dinner advanced. Fish, mutton, birds, pies. Talk climbed and glittered.
The bachelors were learned, generous, charming. No wife interrupted. No
child cried. No machine sounded.

Yet under the laughter I heard it.

Whir—hiss—thump.

The waiter brought dessert.

Whir—hiss—thump.

Port darkened the crystal.

Whir—hiss—thump.

And when the decanter came to me, red as the
river beneath the ice, I saw beyond it—not dimly, not
fantastically, but with the terrible plainness by which distant truths
sometimes enter a comfortable room—the long frozen valley, the white
mill, the endless web, and the rows of unmarried women
bending beside the iron animals while the unmarried men drank
tenderly to one another's health.

I passed the decanter without filling my glass.

"Abstinence?" cried the antiquary. "In the Temple?"

"Only tonight."

But it was not only that night.

For afterward, whenever I unfolded a letter, opened a book,
signed a contract, wrapped a parcel, or poured seeds into
their little white envelopes, the paper was no longer blank.
No paper has been blank to me since.

Hold it to the light, reader.

Hold it carefully.

There are faces in it.

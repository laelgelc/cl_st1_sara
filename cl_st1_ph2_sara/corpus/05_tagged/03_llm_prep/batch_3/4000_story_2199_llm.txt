## The Burning of the *Sarah Sands*

In 1857, when regiments were being hurried eastward to meet
the Indian Mutiny, the 54 th Regiment sailed from Portsmouth
aboard the steamship *Sarah Sands*. She was not the vessel
which a prudent man would have selected for carrying soldiers
halfway round the world. She was overcrowded, imperfectly found, and,
what was worse, possessed a crew of whom the kindest
surviving description is that they were bad, lazy and insubordinate.

This must not be taken to include every sailor aboard
her. Some did their duty handsomely; a few did more
than their duty. But the crew as a body had
given trouble almost from the beginning, and the officers of
the regiment soon discovered that the military passengers might have
to supply not only their own discipline but a considerable
part of the ship's.

The voyage from Portsmouth was slow and vexatious. Steamers of
that period were not the obedient greyhounds we know today.
They combined sails with engines, consumed coal at an alarming
rate, and sometimes seemed to inherit the disadvantages of both
systems without enjoying the full benefit of either. The *Sarah
Sands* laboured southward, losing time, temper and stores. Then, in
a squall, she lost her foremast.

A foremast, you understand, is not an umbrella-stick which can
be dispensed with because the weather has turned fine. Its
fall leaves wreckage, ropes and spars dragging or thrashing about;
it weakens the ship's sailing power; and, unless the wreckage
is cleared quickly, it may hammer through the hull. The
soldiers turned to with the sailors who remained useful, cut
away what must be cut away, and continued toward India
under diminished canvas.

They were in seas near Mauritius when the real calamity
came.

The first warning was smoke rising from the afterpart of
the ship—that is, from the stern. It may have seemed
no great thing at the beginning. Ships then carried fire
in their galleys, lamps in their cabins and furnaces beneath
their boilers; smoke was not in itself a summons to
Judgment. But this smoke thickened. Men sent below came back
coughing. Then came the cry which no ship at sea
can hear without terror:

"Fire!"

The danger lay not merely in burning timber, heated iron
and choking vapour. Near the seat of the fire were
the ammunition magazines.

There are moments when a number of separate facts become
one fact, and that fact is death. The ship was
far from land. The seas were known to be full
of sharks. The boats could not safely carry everyone. The
foremast was gone. The stern was burning. And the fire
was creeping toward powder and cartridges enough to tear the
vessel open.

Some of the crew lowered boats and kept away from
the ship. One record uses language of official moderation concerning
this proceeding. There is no need for us to do
so. It was infamous. They had not been ordered to
abandon the vessel; they had merely discovered that the boats
were cooler than the deck and that burning powder was
unpleasant company.

The soldiers remained.

The first concern of the regiment was its Colours. To
a civilian, a pair of embroidered flags may appear less
valuable than a loaf of bread or a sound plank.
To a regiment the Colours represent its history, its honour,
and, in a very real sense, the very soul of
the Battalion. They bear the names of former battles; men
rally to them, die beneath them and salute them as
they would salute the regiment itself.

The Colours were brought from danger and secured. This was
not sentimentality before business. It was business of the military
kind, and it was done promptly.

Then the men attacked the ammunition.

Boxes of cartridges were dragged from smoke-filled compartments, passed from
hand to hand and thrown into the sea. Barrels and
cases followed. Men stumbled over one another in darkness, scorched
their clothes, blistered their hands and came out gasping; after
a little air they went back. There was, according to
the accounts, a fair amount of fainting that evening, but
fainting was treated as an interruption rather than a conclusion.

Richard Richmond and W. Wiles are among the names attached
to these dangerous journeys below. Others went unnamed. One man,
armed with a hatchet, worked where burning wreckage had to
be severed from the ship. His name is uncertain, but
his hatchet is remembered—which is perhaps as good a monument
as many men obtain.

Nearly all the ammunition was removed. Nearly all is an
important phrase. In ordinary affairs the distinction between all and
nearly all may be no more than a clerk's annoyance.
Beside a fire it may be the difference between a
loud evening and no morning at all.

Meanwhile, officers and men were making provision for the worst.
Spars, doors, gratings and lengths of timber were lashed into
rafts. Water-casks and anything else that might float were added.
A provident lieutenant directed this work, though it must have
been clear to everyone that a raft in those waters
offered only a choice between drowning slowly and being eaten
with greater leisure.

The sharks had gathered.

The burning stern shone red upon the sea, and dark
fins moved through the reflected light. The water looked black
beyond the glare and blood-coloured beneath it. Men working at
the rail could see the creatures turning below, patient as
tax collectors. This is not emphasized in every official record,
official records having a dislike of sharks unless one can
enter them under a heading; but the sharks were there.

Efforts were made to cut away blazing portions of the
stern and to prevent the flames from travelling forward. Buckets
passed in lines. Pumps were manned. Hoses were dragged across
the deck. Men hauled, chopped, sluiced and smothered. Orders had
to be shouted through smoke and the crackling of timber.
The deck grew hot beneath their feet.

The captain, who appears to have possessed both courage and
a cheerful temper, moved among the workers. The soldiers' answer
to suggestions that the ship might have to be abandoned
was simple:

"We'll fight till we're driven overboard."

That sentence contains the whole business. They did not say
that they were unafraid. Sensible men are afraid of a
burning powder magazine. They said that they would continue working
until there was no deck left on which to work.

Then the remaining powder exploded.

There was a flash, a concussion and a roar as
if the whole stern had been struck by artillery. Decking
leapt; partitions vanished; iron twisted; burning fragments flew into the
night. The afterpart of the *Sarah Sands* was gutted. For
an instant she resembled a theatre after the scenery and
roof have fallen together, except that the stage was afloat
and the audience had no wish to leave by the
exits provided.

The blast threw men down. Some believed that the ship
had broken in two. One voice cried something to the
effect that the game was up.

"Lights out, then," another answered.

This is not in any official record, and perhaps the
words were polished in repetition; but they are exactly the
sort of barrack-room joke which men make when death has
come too close to be treated ceremoniously.

The ship did not sink. She settled by the stern,
shuddered, and remained afloat.

The explosion had torn an opening in her and destroyed
much of the after accommodation. Water poured in. Burning cartridges
spluttered among wreckage like gigantic crackers. The steering arrangements were
damaged, and the heat threatened the coal bunkers. Had the
fire passed forward through the bunkers, it might have reached
the engines and the remaining stores; had the sea entered
faster, the vessel would have gone down stern first.

There was now some deviation from the usual military evolutions.

Men who had been trained to form line, counter-march, load
and fire found themselves handling pumps, crowbars, axes and coal-shovels.
Sergeants counted gangs instead of cartridges. Officers stationed men at
hoses and hatchways as if placing companies in battle. The
discipline was the same; only the enemy had altered.

Mr. Frazer, the engineer, now produced his plan. Being a
Scotch engineer, he may be presumed to have kept his
trump-card in reserve until others were sufficiently distressed to appreciate
it.

A watertight bulkhead stood between the fire and a part
of the ship which could still be flooded under control.
A bulkhead is an upright partition across the hull—not merely
a wall, but a barrier designed to keep water in
one section from entering another. Usually one does not cut
holes in a watertight bulkhead. The name itself discourages the
practice. Mr. Frazer proposed that they clear the burning coal
from the bunkers, cut through the bulkhead, and direct water
upon the hidden seat of the fire.

It was desperate, but desperation should not be confused with
folly. If they did nothing, the fire would spread. If
they flooded without judgment, the ship might lose stability and
roll over. Frazer's scheme gave them a chance to drown
the fire without drowning themselves.

The men turned to again.

Coal had to be dragged and shovelled from spaces already
hot enough to blister skin through clothing. The bunkers were
black caverns filled with smoke, steam and bluish vapour. A
man could enter, work for a minute, stagger out blind
and retching, and be replaced by the next. Water striking
red-hot iron hissed back as scalding steam. Boots slipped on
wet coal. Shirts burned through at the shoulders. Hands became
raw.

The bulkhead was attacked with chisels, hammers and whatever tools
could be found. Iron resisted, as iron generally does at
inconvenient moments. Each stroke was made in suffocating heat; each
small opening admitted smoke before it admitted relief. At last
they cut through, enlarged the gap and forced water where
it was needed.

All night the labour continued—pumping, bucketing, sluicing and damping; clearing
coal, dragging wreckage, strengthening lashings and watching the wounded stern.
There was no single heroic gesture after which everyone could
cheer and go to bed. There were only hundreds of
unpleasant tasks, each of which had to be done correctly
and then done again.

The quartermaster-sergeant kept account of stores and barrels with an
almost offensive serenity. If the ship had sunk, one imagines
he would have entered the fact in the proper column
before getting wet. Such men are invaluable in a disaster.
Courage may rush into flames, but method remembers where the
axes were put.

Toward morning the heat began to lessen. Smoke still poured
from the openings, but it no longer came with the
same force. The iron ceased glowing. Men could approach places
which, hours earlier, had been furnaces. Buckets were emptied into
blackened cavities; hoses searched corners; smoking timber was chopped out
and thrown overboard.

At daylight the stern of the *Sarah Sands* presented an
astonishing spectacle. Cabins, partitions and fittings were gone. Beams stood
naked and charred. Plates had buckled. Water sloshed where men
had slept. The ship looked less like a vessel than
the skeleton of one, and a badly scorched skeleton at
that.

But the fire was subdued.

Not one life had been lost.

That sentence deserves to stand alone. Men had entered burning
compartments, handled ammunition beside flames, endured an explosion which gutted
the stern, worked above shark-filled water and fought fire through
the night—and every soul remained alive.

They were not yet safe. The old woman, as someone
called the ship, had survived the fire and must now
survive the sea.

The hull had flooded. The pumps were kept going until
arms and backs ached beyond complaint. A large hole had
to be plugged with timber, canvas, bedding and such packing
as could be forced into it. Loose water tanks, torn
from their fastenings by the explosion, thundered against the inside
of the hull whenever the ship rolled. Men crawled among
them, secured ropes and chains, and lashed the tanks down.
A single tank breaking free at the wrong moment might
have smashed the weakened plates.

The rudder-head and steering gear were damaged, so an improvised
arrangement was contrived with ropes, tackles and spare spars. Steering
a sound ship in open water is one thing. Steering
a half-burned steamer, down by the stern and carrying one
serviceable mast, is rather like guiding a wounded bull with
pack-thread.

Nevertheless, they set a course for Mauritius.

For twelve days the *Sarah Sands* crept toward Port Louis.
Her remaining mast carried what canvas could be trusted; her
engines and pumps were watched without rest; men sounded the
wells to learn whether the water was rising. Every change
of wind threatened her. Every heavy sea struck the patched
stern like a hammer. She could not be allowed to
fall helplessly before the waves, for if they entered the
gutted afterpart the game truly would have been up.

The soldiers maintained watches, pumped, tightened lashings, inspected the plug
and worked the makeshift steering gear. They slept where they
could. Their clothes were scorched, soaked and stiff with salt.
Their provisions had suffered, and nearly every ordinary comfort had
disappeared.

It is to be presumed that they complained. British soldiers
have never regarded silence as part of discipline. But complaint
is not mutiny, and grumbling men may work exceedingly well.
The important fact is that every order was obeyed.

At last Mauritius rose from the sea.

The entry into Port Louis was a public triumph, though
the *Sarah Sands* herself can hardly have looked triumphant. She
came in with one mast, a ruined stern, blackened sides
and improvised steering, like a veteran arriving on parade in
a torn coat. Boats crowded round her. News of the
fire had preceded or spread from her, and the people
of the port received the regiment with admiration. Speeches were
made, honours offered, and much ceremonious politeness displayed.

The troops still had to reach India.

They were transferred in due course to another vessel, which
also proved inadequate. Their hardships continued: crowding, discomfort, poor arrangements
and the weary delays which seem to attend military transport
whenever speed is most required. Having survived fire, explosion, flooding
and sharks, they encountered another severe deprivation.

They ran short of tobacco.

There are soldiers who may have considered this almost as
serious as the burning of the *Sarah Sands*. Tobacco then
was not an occasional luxury but the companion of watches,
marches and damp nights. A regiment without tobacco becomes thoughtful,
and thoughtful regiments are rarely cheerful.

Near Calcutta they met an American captain who heard of
their condition. He supplied the men with tobacco and cigars,
generously and without delay. When payment was offered, he refused
it. The story says he would take nothing from men
who had behaved as they had aboard the burning ship.

This small episode is worth preserving. International friendship is commonly
discussed by ministers over large tables; it is more pleasantly
practised by one seaman giving tobacco to tired soldiers.

The 54 th went on to its duty in India.
Their adventure might have remained only a famous regimental tale,
improved in messes and barrack-rooms, had it not been recognized
in a general order. That order praised their conduct and
drew from it a lesson entirely characteristic of the age,
but no less true for that reason.

Their preservation, it declared, had shown the value of subordination
and strict obedience.

The language is formal, almost cold. It says nothing of
sharks in the red water, of men fainting in smoke,
of hot coal dragged from bunkers, of the hatchet rising
and falling in the glare, of the joke after the
explosion, or of the twelve days during which a burned
ship limped toward Mauritius. Official language rarely smells of smoke.

Yet the order was right.

The *Sarah Sands* was not saved by one man's splendid
impulse. She was saved because hundreds of men, frightened, exhausted
and often in pain, obeyed; because they passed boxes when
told to pass them, pumped when told to pump, entered
the smoke when their turn came, and returned after being
carried out; because officers commanded, sergeants organized, engineers calculated and
rank and file worked.

Courage was there in abundance, but discipline gave courage hands.

And so the old ship, blazing from within and ringed
by sharks without, did not become a tomb. At morning
she still floated; twelve days later she entered harbour; and
not one man had been lost.

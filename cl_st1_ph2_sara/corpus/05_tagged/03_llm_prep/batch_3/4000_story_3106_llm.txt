### THE CATTLE BUSINESS

There are fortunes in cattle. They are generally the fortunes
put into them.

A man comes out from town with a letter to
a bank manager, a new pair of leggings, and calculations
showing that if eight hundred cows each produce one calf
every year, and the calves in turn produce other calves—with
proper allowance for bulls, compound interest, and Providence—he will be
a wealthy man in seven seasons and a member of
Parliament in ten.

The figures are unanswerable. The cattle answer them.

You get your country first. It is fine country, carrying
one beast to ten acres in an ordinary season, one
beast to five acres in a good season, and no
beasts whatever when you have borrowed enough money to stock
it properly. You buy cows at a high price because
everybody wants cows. Then the market falls because everybody has
got them. Next the rain keeps away. The creeks become
strings of green puddles, the grass turns to wire, the
scrub crackles, and the cattle walk about with their ribs
showing like hoops on a wrecked barrel.

The owner says, "It must rain soon."

It does. It rains in Sydney, where he is trying
to renew the overdraft.

At last the drought breaks. Clouds pile up from the
west, thunder goes rolling over the ranges, and the owner
rides out in a waterproof coat to watch prosperity descend.
Six inches fall in a night. The dry creek becomes
a brown wall full of logs, dead trees, fencing wire,
and astonished cattle. Half the calves are drowned. The remainder
get bogged round the new waterholes, while the cows, after
surviving eleven months without grass, die from eating too much
of it.

Then comes pleuro. Or redwater. Or ticks. Or a Government
inspector who says there may be ticks, which is financially
much the same thing.

There is no arguing with cattle-country. It has made up
its mind against capital.

The stockman, however, does fairly well. He draws wages, eats
beef, wears out the station horses, and observes the owner's
misfortunes with respectful interest. If the season is good, he
is paid. If it is bad, he is paid until
the cheque bounces, after which he shifts to another station.
The owner remains to explain matters to the bank.

Thus the hired man has the better investment.

Cattle themselves are not wicked in the usual sense. A
bullock does not rise in the morning determined to ruin
you. He merely knows that you wish him to do
something, and this awakens his intelligence.

Left alone, he will stand all day under a gidyea
tree, chewing with the solemnity of a magistrate. Attempt to
move him north, and he remembers urgent business in the
south. Head him off, and he goes east. Put three
good riders round him, and he lies down. Dismount to
twist his tail, and he gets up with extraordinary suddenness
and puts a horn through the horse's saddle-flap.

A mob of travelling bullocks will follow the leaders for
two hundred miles with hardly a protest. Then one old
red steer discovers Individual Liberty. He turns aside at a
patch of scrub. The cattle behind him turn also; the
wing swings out; the tail doubles back; and in thirty
seconds eight hundred bullocks are trotting towards yesterday.

That red steer is the agitator. Every mob has one.
Were he in politics, he would have a newspaper.

Cows are more gifted. A cow with a young calf
can hide it in grass six inches high, return to
the camp wearing an expression of injured innocence, and lead
a stock-rider past the spot fourteen times. If the rider
finds the calf, she charges him. If he does not
find it, she goes back after dark and complains loudly
that he has stolen it.

A scrub cow, when pressed, will dive beneath a leaning
log which clears her horns by the thickness of a
postage stamp. The horse following her will either refuse, strike
the timber with his knees, or pass underneath and remove
the rider. The cow then waits on the other side
to see which occurred.

Calves have only two ideas: to get on the wrong
side of every fence and to regard any cow except
their own mother as a source of milk. At branding
time they bawl, run backwards, fall over the rails, and
tie themselves into knots with other calves. Yet the moment
the gate is opened they become racehorses and cannot be
caught by anything under twelve stone carrying a stock-whip.

Bulls are different. A quiet bull is an animal that
has not yet found the right occasion.

It is commonly supposed that cattle are stupid. This is
unjust. They remember every broken panel in a hundred miles
of fence, every patch of poison bush, every bog hole,
and every stockman who ever struck them. What they do
not remember is the gate through which they passed five
minutes ago. This is not stupidity. It is policy.

They see things, too.

No man who has watched cattle camped at midnight doubts
that there are ghosts in the bush. The mob will
be lying quietly, the horses half asleep, the night so
still that you can hear a bullock tear grass fifty
yards away. Then one beast lifts his head and stares.
Another rises. A ripple goes through the camp. Horns knock;
chains jingle; the mob crowds together, all looking at the
same patch of darkness.

There is nothing there.

That is what makes it serious.

An old drover once told me that cattle can see
every man who has died on the track.

"They don't mind the dead men," he said. "It's the
dead cattle behind 'em that start the rush."

He may have been joking, but he shifted his night-horse
closer to the mob while he said it.

Sheep do not see ghosts. Sheep have no need. Give
a sheep good grass, clean water, shade, and protection from
dogs, and it will search the paddock until it finds
a rusty nail to die beside. In flood-time sheep drown
in heaps because each considers the lowest place safest. In
drought they walk into dams, get stuck, and use one
another as stepping-stones. Sheep-men, from long association with them, come
to believe that all stock can be controlled by noise.

I once saw three such men help to draft cattle
at Wombat Flat.

They arrived in spotless moleskins with whips long enough to
signal ships. The manager wanted forty fat bullocks taken from
a quiet mob of three hundred. Two Queensland drovers were
there—Ned Rourke and a little iron-grey man called Billy Shaw—sitting
loose in their saddles while their camp-horses dozed.

The chief sheep-man looked at the cattle.

"Easy," he said. "We'll rattle 'em through."

He placed his companions at opposite ends of the yard,
where they could shout at each other, and rode into
the mob cracking his whip. One hundred cattle went up
the fence. One hundred came down. The remainder revolved in
the middle. Dust covered the yard, bullocks climbed over bullocks,
rails sprang out, and a brindled cow put her head
under the sheep-man's horse and lifted it bodily.

"Block 'em!" he roared.

His friends blocked everything, particularly the gateways.

A blue bullock jumped the drafting fence. Twenty-seven followed him.
The sheep-men galloped after them, shouting, "Way there!" to open
country. Their whips cracked like rifle fire. The mob, concluding
that murder had begun, spread over Wombat Flat in six
directions.

Ned and Billy watched them go.

The chief came back with his hat crushed and his
horse white-eyed.

"Hard cattle," he said.

Billy removed a grass stalk from his mouth.

"You've had a lot of practice not drafting 'em," he
said.

After dinner the two drovers brought the cattle back. They
did it without a whip-crack. Ned rode wide on the
left, Billy went quietly round the leaders, and the mob
folded in upon itself as water closes behind a stone.
In twenty minutes the cattle were in the yard. In
another fifteen the forty fats stood outside, and the rest
were grazing as if nothing had happened.

That is stockmanship. It looks like laziness to the man
who cannot do it.

The best work is seen at a cattle-camp, where the
ground is open and the mob has room to make
a fool of you. As I write, I can see
again a morning on the Barcoo: a pale sky, heat
already trembling above the plain, flies busy round the horses'
eyes, and seven hundred cattle packed on a bare flat
under a haze of dust.

We were cutting fats for the southern road. The mob
shifted continually—not travelling, but boiling. Black backs, red backs, horns,
humps, tossing heads; cows calling calves, calves answering other cows;
bullocks shouldering through gaps that closed before them. The whole
camp moved like a muddy river turning in a flood.

Near the centre stood the beast we wanted, a white
steer with red ears and horns wide as a gate.
He knew he was wanted. There could be no doubt
of that. Whenever the cut-out man came near, the steer
slid behind a wall of cattle and looked innocent.

The rider was young Jack Lacey on a bay camp-horse
called Comet. Jack sat with his reins low and his
whip hanging. Comet watched the steer. His ears went forward
when the white back showed, and flattened when it vanished.
He knew the work as well as the man, perhaps
better, and was paid less.

Jack entered the mob at a walk.

The cattle opened grudgingly. A horn brushed his knee; a
cow swung across him; two bullocks jammed together and backed
apart. Comet edged through sideways, placing his feet delicately among
heels and horns. The white steer drifted towards the far
wing.

Jack did not hurry. That was the point.

He let the steer believe he had not been noticed.
He passed him, turned behind a red cow, and came
back on the other side. Comet's head bent; his quarters
gathered beneath him.

The steer saw the gap.

He dashed.

Comet went with him in one bound. The mob split
before them. Dust flew. The steer swerved left; Comet wheeled
left. He doubled right; the horse was already there, low
to the ground, Jack motionless above him except for one
lifted hand.

For an instant the steer faced the mob. Behind him
was safety—horns, dust, confusion, the restless sea. He plunged for
it.

"Come away!"

Comet sprang across his nose. The steer checked so sharply
that his forefeet furrowed the earth. He spun, charged down
the line, and found horse and rider beside him. Jack
gave him room—not much, just enough to keep him moving
outward.

The mob sucked at him. Every beast seemed to call,
"Back here! Back into trouble!"

The white steer hesitated.

Comet crept forward, neck stretched, ears pinned, daring him to
turn. Jack's whip rose—not cracking, merely visible. Man, horse, and
steer stood fixed in the dust while the camp rolled
and muttered behind them.

Then the steer yielded.

He trotted towards the cut, pretending that this had been
his intention all morning. Comet followed at an easy canter.
At the edge of the holding mob another rider opened
a space, the white steer passed through, and the gap
shut behind him.

Jack turned back, brushing dust from his sleeve.

The town man beside me had been holding his breath.

"Good horse," he said.

Jack looked down at Comet's wet neck.

"Fair," he answered. "He'll learn."

That is the bush judgment. Praise a man and he
distrusts you; praise his horse and he reduces the compliment
by half. Yet horse and rider had done in forty
seconds what six noisy men might not do before dinner.

Beyond them the white steer stood among the fats, already
planning his escape. The cattle in the main camp surged
again, horns shining through the dust. Somewhere inside that moving
multitude another bullock was discovering Liberty, another cow was concealing
a calf, and another quiet bull was awaiting his occasion.

And far away, in an office, some hopeful gentleman was
multiplying eight hundred cows by seven prosperous years.

On paper, they were behaving beautifully.

### THE HEAD OF TOBY DAMMIT
*A Tale with a Moral, Though Not One to Be
Used Indoors*

There are critics—and I regret to say that several of
them dine well—who maintain that a tale without a moral
is no tale at all, but merely an anecdote in
its night-shirt. These gentlemen discover design where ordinary men perceive
only disorder, and, like the celebrated Persian philosopher Al-Khumbug, who
proved that rain descended in order to wet umbrellas, they
will not admit that any event can occur without having
first obtained a certificate of significance. If a poet introduces
a blue curtain, they demand to know why it is
not green; if a heroine sneezes, they infer a revolution;
if a dog crosses the road, they pronounce the animal
an emblem of Eternity, the road an allegory of Doubt,
and the crossing itself an attack upon the established church.

For my own part, I detest such extravagance. I am
a plain man. I relate facts. Should instruction happen to
follow them—as a bailiff follows a debtor—it is no affair
of mine. Yet the history of my lamented friend Tobias
Dammit is so obviously furnished with a moral that even
the dullest reviewer could not mislay it without assistance. The
moral is this: never discipline a child upward when he
ought to be disciplined downward. Or perhaps it is: never
bet what you cannot afford to lose. There may be
two morals. I have known stories to contain fewer facts
and a dozen morals, so the surplus need alarm nobody.

Toby Dammit was born in a respectable quarter of a
disreputable town, at half past five on a Sunday morning.
The day and hour were unfortunate. Had he arrived at
six, the bells might have improved him; had he arrived
on Monday, commerce might have sobered him; but at half
past five on Sunday the bells were not yet awake,
commerce was asleep, and morality itself had not breakfasted.

His parents were persons of theory. His father believed in
firmness, his mother in tenderness, and both agreed that these
virtues should be exercised with implements. The father employed a
rod of ash; the mother preferred a broad slipper, lined
with rabbit fur, because, as she observed, punishment ought never
to be separated from affection. Their principal error was anatomical.
Whenever Toby committed an offence with the lower part of
his person, they corrected the upper; whenever the upper part
offended, they addressed the lower. Thus, if he kicked the
nurse, he was struck upon the crown; if he bit
the physician, he was beaten upon the heels. The moral
fluids, being naturally delicate, were in consequence driven in the
wrong direction.

This is not speculation. It is hydraulics.

At six weeks Toby lied. At two months he swore,
though his vocabulary was necessarily limited. At three months he
drank gin from a medicine spoon and demanded another dose
on scientific grounds. At four months he cheated his grandmother
at cards; at five he had not only acquired the
cards but mortgaged the cradle. Before his first birthday he
had learned to pinch, pilfer, spit, contradict, calculate compound interest,
and feign religious enthusiasm when elderly ladies were present. He
screamed in C major, sulked in E flat, and bit
in every key. His appetite was that of an army
contractor; his temper, that of a Turkish tax collector; his
conscience, an article so microscopic that the family physician declared
it imaginary.

Do not suppose I exaggerate. Exaggeration is the refuge of
persons deficient in detail. I possess detail.

The parents, perceiving these tendencies, increased the remedy. They beat
him longitudinally, transversely, morally, alphabetically, and according to a schedule
recommended by Professor Krummholz in his *De Pueris Improbandis*, quarto
edition, Leipzig, with diagrams. The professor's doctrine was that the
human character resembles an olive tree: the more it is
struck, the more oil it yields. Toby yielded no oil,
though he produced upon occasion a fluid of another description.
They then attempted kindness. Kindness made him suspicious. They attempted
neglect. Neglect made him enterprising. They locked him in a
cupboard with a Testament, and he emerged with three silver
spoons. They sent him to school, where he learned nothing
except the weaknesses of the schoolmaster. They sent him to
sea, but the captain returned him by parcel post.

At fourteen he had already been expelled from four academies,
two debating societies, a temperance lodge, and the Society for
the Prevention of Unnecessary Expulsion. His offences were various, but
his defence was uniform: he had not done it; if
he had done it, he had been compelled; if he
had not been compelled, the act had been misunderstood; and
if it had not been misunderstood, the injured party richly
deserved it.

This method of reasoning afterward became popular in politics.

I met Toby when we were both young men, though
I was the younger in years and the older in
sobriety, prudence, judgment, moral elevation, personal dignity, and such other
qualities as are generally admitted by those who know me
only slightly. The introduction occurred in a cellar devoted to
the sale of vinous refreshments. Toby was standing upon a
table, explaining to a magistrate why magistracy was a form
of organized impertinence. The magistrate, who possessed no taste for
philosophy, ordered him down. Toby declined. The table collapsed, and
our friendship began beneath it.

His appearance at that period was striking—frequently by design. He
was short, compact, and constructed as if intended to be
thrown. His hair projected in five directions, none of them
reputable. One eye was gray, the other more gray; his
nose turned up at the world, while his mouth turned
down at particular portions of it. He dressed with a
magnificence which creditors called fraud and Toby called confidence. His
waistcoat was yellow, his coat blue, his boots red-brown, and
his linen an uncertain color expressive of experience.

His principal accomplishment was profanity. Yet I must defend him
from the charge of habitual swearing, since he rarely invoked
Heaven and never, so far as I know, addressed any
infernal personage except conditionally. His favorite expression—his punctuation, his signature,
the amen to every creed and the cork to every
bottle—was:

"I'll bet the Devil my head."

If the weather seemed fair, he would bet the Devil
his head that it would rain. If it rained, he
would bet the Devil his head that it would clear.
He bet his head upon horses with four legs, horses
with three, lawyers with two faces, actors with none, soups,
sermons, elections, eclipses, toothaches, foreign policy, and the precise time
required to boil an egg. He never laid money, because
money was scarce; he never offered his coat, because winter
was long; but his head, being attached and apparently useless,
was always at hazard.

I reasoned with him.

"My dear Toby," I said, "language is not merely wind
ornamented by teeth. Words have weight. A figure of speech,
often repeated, may acquire substance. Consider the Latin: *Verbum sapienti
sat est*—a word is enough for a wise man."

"Then save your words," said Toby, "for I'll bet the
Devil my head you never find one."

I remonstrated—but to no purpose.

I demonstrated—in vain.

I expostulated—with a similar economy of result.

I pointed out that the Devil, if he existed, was
notoriously litigious; that an oral compact, when repeated before witnesses,
might satisfy the infernal statutes; that one should not risk
the seat of reason, memory, perception, and hair without first
determining its pecuniary value. Toby listened with the expression of
a sheep remembering a dream. Then he winked, placed his
thumb against his nose, extended his fingers in a fanlike
configuration much admired among statesmen, and said:

"I'll bet the Devil my head you're afraid."

Afraid! The accusation was absurd. I have never feared anything
which could be avoided by prudence, distance, or the intervention
of another person. Nevertheless, friendship imposed duties. I continued my
efforts.

I introduced Toby to a clergyman. Toby asked whether eternal
punishment was payable by installments.

I introduced him to a physician. Toby stole the physician's
watch and returned it when informed that it kept poor
time.

I introduced him to a transcendental philosopher, who gazed for
seventeen minutes at Toby's left ear and pronounced it a
symbol of the Absolute yearning toward the Not-Itself. Toby knocked
him down, maintaining that the Not-Itself had begun it.

I procured books: Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius, a manual of
bookkeeping, and a pamphlet entitled *Sixteen Methods of Becoming Respectable
Without Capital*. He used Seneca to prop a window, Epictetus
to light a cigar, Aurelius to level a table, the
bookkeeping manual to conceal his accounts, and the pamphlet to
wrap fish.

He was not reformed.

Years passed, as years will when no officer is stationed
to prevent them. Toby's vices matured. His youthful insolence became
adult principle; his idleness developed into a system; his debts
acquired genealogies. He drank, gambled, quarreled, borrowed, promised, forgot, remembered
selectively, and expressed contempt for every institution from marriage to
municipal drainage. Poverty, that most unbecoming of moral defects, clung
to him with the pertinacity of a creditor. Still, he
retained his head.

This last circumstance encouraged him.

It was in the autumn of a year which I
could name, but will not, because dates encourage pedantry, that
Toby and I undertook a journey on foot to a
neighboring city. We had intended to travel by coach. The
proprietor, however, held obsolete opinions regarding payment in advance, while
Toby held progressive opinions regarding payment not at all. Between
obsolete and progressive ideas there can be no compromise. We
walked.

Toward evening the road entered a low, marshy region where
the reeds whispered like creditors outside a bedroom door. The
sun descended behind a range of smoky hills; a damp
vapor crawled from the ditches; the trees, stripped by the
season, gesticulated against the sky with the melancholy vehemence of
unpaid tragedians. Toby was cheerful. Gloom always improved him, perhaps
by contrast.

Presently we came to a covered bridge of considerable length
and very little architectural conscience. Its roof sagged; its sides
leaned inward; its planks complained underfoot. At the entrance appeared
a board bearing the inscription:

**WALK YOUR HORSES.**

Toby read it carefully.

"We have no horses," I observed.

"I'll bet the Devil my head," said he, "the notice
is intended for us."

I shuddered—not from fear, but from the evening chill, which
happened at that instant to resemble fear in every physical
respect.

Inside, the bridge was darker than the road. A few
narrow openings admitted strips of greenish twilight, dividing the gloom
into compartments. Beneath us the river moved unseen, with a
thick sucking sound suggestive of a giant drinking soup. Our
footsteps multiplied in the rafters, so that two men entered
and twenty-six appeared to follow.

Halfway across we encountered a turnstile.

Why a turnstile had been placed in a covered bridge,
I cannot say. There was no field to protect, no
toll to collect, no cattle to regulate. It stood there
upon a raised plank platform, narrow, angular, and officious, like
a minor official in an empty department. Its horizontal arms
revolved stiffly. Above it, the darkness was complicated by crossbeams,
braces, hooks, and other architectural parentheses.

Toby halted.

"A contemptible obstacle," he said.

"Quite," I replied. "We will pass through it."

"Through it? I shall leap over it."

"My dear Dammit," said I, "the distinction between valor and
inconvenience is one which civilization has labored centuries to establish."

"I'll jump it."

"You will turn it."

"I'll clear it by three feet."

"You are scarcely five feet yourself."

"Four feet eleven and a half," said Toby, with dignity.
"But genius is not measured from the floor."

He stepped backward and surveyed the apparatus. He flexed his
knees, rolled his shoulders, spat upon each palm, adjusted his
neckcloth, and gave the turnstile a look of concentrated animosity.

"I'll bet the Devil my head I clear it."

The echo returned, faintly:

"Head—head—head."

At that moment a voice behind us said:

"Ahem."

We turned.

From the shadows at the side of the bridge emerged
a little old gentleman whom I had not previously observed.
This failure of observation has been criticized, but unjustly. A
man cannot be expected to observe what he has not
seen, and I had not seen him because he was
in the shadows. The logic is complete.

He was dressed entirely in black: black coat, black waistcoat,
black breeches, black stockings, and a hat whose brim concealed
the upper portion of his face. His complexion, so far
as visible, was of that pale yellow sometimes produced by
age, illness, candlelight, or an unwholesome conscience. He limped upon
the right leg. Over one arm he carried a folded
apron of black leather, such as might be worn by
a butcher, an engraver, a chemist, or a gentleman with
private expectations.

He bowed.

Toby stared.

I bowed in return, civility being cheapest when danger is
uncertain.

"Ahem," said the old gentleman again.

"Ahem," said I.

Toby said something less ceremonial.

"Sir," the stranger began, addressing Toby, "unless my hearing—which has
been injured by long attention to human promises—deceives me, you
have proposed a wager."

"No concern of yours," said Toby.

"On the contrary, sir, it may become very much my
concern. You offered, I believe, to bet the Devil your
head that you could leap over this turnstile."

Toby's mouth opened. His mouth shut. His left eye became
smaller than his right. For the first time in our
acquaintance, language approached him and passed by.

I intervened.

"The phrase," I explained, "is idiomatic, figurative, colloquial, jocular, hyperbolical,
and, in a strict commercial sense, insolvent."

The old gentleman inclined his head.

"Quite so. Yet many valuable contracts begin in jest. Marriage,
for example. I am authorized to accept."

"Authorized by whom?" I asked.

He smiled with only one side of his mouth.

"By the interested party."

Toby recovered himself. Pride is a powerful restorative, particularly in
fools.

"Done!" he cried. "I'll jump it. And if I don't,
he may have my head and welcome. It has brought
me little profit."

"That," said the old gentleman, "is often the owner's valuation
and never the purchaser's."

He unfolded the black apron and tied it carefully around
his waist. The action was performed with such neatness, such
businesslike composure, that I experienced a profound uneasiness on Toby's
behalf. I took my friend by the elbow.

"Reflect," I whispered. "There are braces above. The light is
defective. Your boots are worn. Your judgment is worse. Withdraw."

"Never."

"Apologize."

"Never."

"Pretend intoxication."

"I am intoxicated."

"Then pretend sobriety. It will alarm him."

Toby shook me off.

The old gentleman inspected the ground. He tested the turnstile,
examined its arms, paced the distance, and wiped a spot
from his apron. Then, with the air of an umpire
at an athletic festival in ancient Greece—or, what is nearly
the same thing, a clerk supervising the removal of furniture—he
stationed himself beyond the barrier.

"The conditions," he said, "are simple. The gentleman will take
his run from that plank, spring without touching the stile,
and alight upon this side. If he succeeds, the claim
is void. If he fails, the pledged article becomes immediately
payable."

"Immediately?" I said.

"Delivery is preferred."

Toby laughed. The laugh rose among the beams and came
down thinner.

He retreated twelve paces. He removed his coat and handed
it to me. I examined the pockets from a motive
of friendship and found nothing. He removed his hat, tightened
his belt, rubbed his palms once more, and bent forward.

The stranger lifted one finger.

"Ahem."

Toby stamped his right foot.

"Ahem," he replied.

The stranger lifted a second finger.

Toby rocked backward.

I endeavored to speak, but my tongue, which is ordinarily
of a practical and energetic disposition, remained unemployed.

The stranger raised a third finger.

"One," he said.

Toby lowered his head.

"Two."

He drew breath.

"Three."

Toby ran.

His first step was short, his second longer, his third
magnificent. He flew along the planks with the velocity of
scandal through a small town. At the edge of the
platform he compressed himself like a spring, flung both arms
upward, and rose.

I have witnessed celebrated leaps. I saw the Neapolitan acrobat
Belladonna clear six chairs, a cook, and a bassoon. I
once observed a bankrupt cross a dining room when the
waiter presented the bill. Neither performance equaled Toby's.

He passed above the turnstile.

He rose one foot—two feet—three. His knees touched his chest.
His arms described a noble arc. For an instant he
hung in the greenish gloom like Mercury, if Mercury had
worn red-brown boots and owed money.

Then there came a sharp metallic sound.

It was not loud. A carving knife drawn across a
plate might produce something similar; so might the snapping of
a large pair of shears.

Toby's body completed the leap.

His head did not.

The concealed edge of an iron brace, sharpened perhaps by
rust or Providence, had encountered his neck at the highest
point of his flight. The body landed upright upon the
far platform, took two quick steps, and stood wavering with
an air of uncertainty that would have been comic under
less anatomical conditions.

The old gentleman was ready.

He advanced half a pace, spread the black apron, and
caught Toby's head as expertly as a nurse receives an
infant—or as a waiter receives a pudding, though the comparison
is deficient in sentiment. The head rolled once in the
leather and settled with the face upward. Its expression was
one of extreme surprise. I confess I had never seen
Toby look so intelligent.

"A clean decision," said the old man.

He bowed to me, tucked the apron and its contents
beneath his arm, and limped rapidly toward the end of
the bridge.

"Stop!" I cried. "Sir! The matter is irregular! There must
be witnesses, papers, an inventory!"

He did not stop.

I pursued him for several yards, but darkness intervened. There
was a smell of singed wool, a flash like the
striking of a match, and a brief burst of laughter
from beneath the bridge. When I reached the opening, nobody
was visible upon the road.

I returned to Toby.

His body had fallen against the turnstile, which revolved slowly
and pushed him first one way, then another, with a
deliberation that seemed indecent. I disengaged him. The injury was
serious. Indeed, I may say without medical exaggeration that it
was among the gravest losses a man can sustain in
the region of the neck.

Still, I did not abandon hope. Hope costs nothing.

I conveyed him to the nearest village in a cart
borrowed from a farmer who objected until I explained that
the patient was unable to remember his name and therefore
could not be charged. We placed Toby in bed at
an inn and summoned three physicians: Dr. Scalpel, Dr. Bolus,
and Dr. Moonshine, the last being a practitioner of the
transcendental school.

Dr. Scalpel examined the wound and recommended amputation.

"Of what?" I asked.

He considered.

Dr. Bolus prescribed twelve pills, one every hour, to be
taken orally.

I pointed out a practical difficulty.

He said that medicine must not yield to circumstance.

Dr. Moonshine sat beside the bed, closed his eyes, and
declared that the head was merely the phenomenal manifestation of
a higher cranial principle; that true consciousness did not reside
in matter; that Toby, viewed from the Absolute, possessed more
head than before, because the absence of the visible organ
liberated the invisible one.

"Can you restore him?" I asked.

"Restoration," he replied, "implies separateness, and separateness is illusion."

"Can he eat?"

"Appetite is the soul's nostalgia for unity."

I threw him downstairs. I did so without anger, but
with philosophical precision.

For several days Toby lingered. The physicians disagreed as to
whether he was alive, dead, or passing through an interesting
intermediate state suitable for publication. His pulse continued, though without
enthusiasm. He kicked when pinched. Once, when I mentioned the
Devil, his right hand rose feebly and formed the old
insulting gesture with thumb and fingers. This demonstration convinced me
that personality survives extensive physical reduction.

We nourished him through a funnel. We applied bandages, poultices,
magnets, electricity, mustard, prayer, brandy, and a patent neck-renovator. Nothing
succeeded. The physicians held a consultation and concluded that the
missing head had retarded recovery.

On the seventh morning Toby died.

I grieved deeply. I had lost a friend, a companion,
a moral responsibility, and twelve shillings advanced for medical attendance.
I ordered a modest coffin of unusual shape, since the
undertaker insisted upon charging by length and Toby no longer
required the customary allowance at one end. Even economy, however,
could not rescue the funeral from debt.

I applied first to Toby's relatives. They denied the relationship.

I applied to his creditors. They said they had buried
enough money in him already.

I applied to the Society for Moral Improvement, arguing that
Toby's fate supplied an example of uncommon educational value. The
secretary replied that the society accepted examples but not expenses.

Finally I approached the Transcendental Brotherhood, of which Dr. Moonshine
was a luminary. Surely, I said, men who regarded matter
as illusion could not object to an illusory bill.

They objected immediately.

They maintained that burial was a material ceremony; that Toby's
essential self had entered the Universal Over-Soul; that the coffin-maker
existed only in my perception; and that payment would confirm
him in a gross attachment to phenomena. I observed that
the coffin-maker's attachment to phenomena was already gross and that
he threatened proceedings. They recommended meditation.

Meditation failed to satisfy the undertaker.

Thus, after six weeks, no subscription having been obtained and
the graveyard account continuing to accrue interest, I was compelled
to adopt a practical measure. With the assistance of the
sexton—whose metaphysics were simple but whose fee was moderate—I had
Toby exhumed.

The body was in excellent condition, considering its disadvantages.

I sold it to a dealer who supplied meat to
the city kennels. He complained of the missing head and
reduced the price accordingly. I protested that the head had
never formed part of the offer, being, in the strict
legal sense, the property of another party. We compromised.

The proceeds paid the sexton, half the undertaker, and for
my supper.

Such was the end of Toby Dammit. If the reader
has failed to discover the moral, I cannot help him.
A moral, like a head, is an excellent thing to
have; but after it has once been lost through foolish
wagering, even the most conscientious friend may find the remainder
difficult to dispose of.

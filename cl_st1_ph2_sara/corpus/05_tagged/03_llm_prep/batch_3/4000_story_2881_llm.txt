# THE MARINE PUZZLE

I have never pretended to understand dreams, nor, until the
extraordinary events I am about to relate, had I supposed
that any sensible man could be guided by one beyond
the breakfast-table. Yet there hangs to-day in my parlor, between
an engraving of the Battle of New Orleans and a
portrait of my late wife, a little panel upon which
my daughter Alice has worked, in gold thread upon black
velvet, the following figures:

**3° 51' South Latitude
32° 25' West Longitude**

Visitors commonly take it for some nautical motto belonging to
my nephew, Captain Ralph Spencer; and when they ask its
meaning Alice replies, with a smile, that it is merely
a marine puzzle. That answer has served us for years.
The full explanation, however, is another matter.

In the autumn of 1892 I was a widower of
forty-eight, living in New Orleans and conducting a business in
ship chandlery which had descended to me from my father.
I had prospered, but I had also worked with a
persistence which my physician declared to be foolish, and which
Alice, then a young lady of nineteen, described more plainly
as wicked.

"You are growing yellow, papa," she said one morning, regarding
me across the coffee urn. "And yesterday you asked Mr.
Jenkins three times whether the invoice from Mobile had arrived."

"Mr. Jenkins is slow," I answered.

"The invoice was lying beneath your hand each time."

This was true, and consequently irritating. I took refuge behind
the newspaper.

That afternoon Doctor Mandeville called, listened to my heart, looked
at my tongue, and asked more questions than I thought
necessary.

"You have no disease that I can discover," he said
at last.

"Then I am glad to hear it."

"But if you continue at this pace you will shortly
acquire one. You need rest, change, sea air, and freedom
from ledgers."

"I cannot abandon the business."

"You can abandon it for three months, or it may
abandon you permanently."

This sort of language, I have observed, gives physicians a
satisfaction out of proportion to its value. Nevertheless, I promised
to consider the matter, and that night, after an unusually
wearisome day, I went early to bed.

Toward morning I dreamed.

I stood upon the shore of a small islet. The
hour was night, yet the place was lighted by a
moon of such whiteness that every grain of sand appeared
distinct. A low mist moved above the water, and the
surf, as it broke upon a reef, shone with a
pale green fire. Behind me rose a single palm, twisted
as though by constant winds; before me lay a black
rock, flat upon one side and marked by lines which
I could not read.

Two men stood near the rock.

One wore a dark military cloak, though the air was
tropical, and the other had the dress of a common
sailor. Their faces remained in shadow. Neither spoke. The soldier
pointed to the rock; the sailor pointed to the palm.
Then both turned toward the sea, where, beyond the islet,
a great peak towered against the moon.

All at once the scene vanished. I found myself in
a room resembling neither my own chamber nor any place
I knew. Before me was an enormous blackboard, and upon
it, in characters of dazzling white, appeared:

**3° 51' S.
32° 25' W.**

The figures increased until they filled the room. They seemed
to lean over me like falling walls.

I awoke with a cry.

For several minutes I lay listening to the clock and
to the soft rattle of a carriage in the street.
The impression of the dream was so strong that I
lit my lamp, took a pencil, and wrote the figures
upon the back of an envelope. Having done this, I
felt rather ashamed of myself, extinguished the light, and presently
slept again.

At breakfast I told Alice the circumstances.

"A buried treasure!" she exclaimed. "Of course the men were
pirates."

"Pirates do not commonly provide latitude and longitude."

"Then they were scientific pirates."

"It was only a dream."

"Yet you wrote down the figures."

"That proves merely that I was not fully awake."

She asked to see the envelope, and I handed it
to her. She read the numbers aloud, then set the
paper beside my plate.

"You should look them up."

"I have better employment."

But after breakfast, instead of going directly to my office,
I entered the library and took down an atlas. The
spot indicated lay in the South Atlantic, some two hundred
miles from the Brazilian coast, in the vicinity of a
group of islands bearing the name Fernando de Noronha.

I confess that the discovery gave me a disagreeable sensation.
Had the figures pointed into empty ocean, I should have
laughed and forgotten them; but the existence of land near
that precise position lent the dream an appearance of intention.
Still, I closed the atlas, went downtown, and by noon
had become absorbed in business.

Two nights later the dream returned.

There was the moonlit islet, the phosphorescent surf, the bent
palm, the marked rock, and the two silent men. Again
the soldier pointed to the stone and the sailor to
the tree. This time I walked toward them, though every
step required an effort. When I had nearly reached the
rock, the military figure drew a sword and traced upon
its flat surface two words:

**Mas distante occidente.**

The letters burned red for an instant. Then came the
blackboard and the gigantic figures:

**3° 51' S.—32° 25' W.**

I awoke trembling, and, prosaic businessman though I was, I
did not sleep again.

At breakfast Alice observed my condition.

"You have dreamed of the island."

"How do you know?"

"You are staring at the sugar bowl as if coordinates
were engraved upon it."

When I repeated the new words, she fetched a Portuguese
dictionary left by an old clerk of mine who had
once traded at Bahia. With some difficulty we discovered their
meaning: "Farther west," or, more literally, "more distant westward."

"Papa," Alice said gravely, "you must go there."

"To a Brazilian convict island on the authority of a
nightmare?"

"It might be very good for your health."

This argument was so characteristic that I laughed in spite
of myself. The matter might have ended there but for
a letter received that afternoon from my nephew Ralph.

Ralph Spencer was the son of my elder brother and,
at thirty-two, master and part owner of the brig *Sea
Witch*. He had followed the sea from boyhood and possessed
that calm competence which makes landsmen willing to forgive a
sailor for knowing so much more than themselves. His letter
announced that he would shortly sail from New York for
Pernambuco with machinery and general cargo, touching at New Orleans
to take on stores. Having heard from Alice that I
was unwell, he invited me to accompany him.

"You can return with us," he wrote, "unless the mate
makes a sailor of you and ships you before the
mast. We shall not promise luxury, but you will get
salt air enough, and there is no ledger aboard except
my own."

Pernambuco! The name seemed another finger pointing toward the dream.
It lay upon the Brazilian coast, and the *Sea Witch*,
returning northward, might pass within reach of Fernando de Noronha.

I showed the letter to Alice.

"It is settled," she said.

"Nothing is settled. Ralph may refuse to approach the island."

"Then you must persuade him."

"He will think me mad."

"He has always thought business men slightly mad. This will
make no difference."

Within a week I had arranged my affairs, committed the
chandlery to Mr. Jenkins, packed clothing altogether unsuitable for the
sea, and secured from a bookseller such maps and notices
of Fernando de Noronha as he could obtain. These informed
me that the principal island was used by Brazil as
a penal settlement; that a lofty peak made it visible
at a great distance; and that several small islets and
rocks lay near it. One old chart placed the group
so close to my dreamed position that the difference was
scarcely measurable by the scale before me.

Ralph listened to my recital in the cabin of the
*Sea Witch*, his elbows upon the table and a half-concealed
smile at the corners of his mouth.

"And you want me to put the brig among unknown
currents and ugly reefs," he said, "because two dumb gentlemen
came to you in your sleep?"

"I ask only that, if weather and duty permit, we
pass near enough to examine the place."

"And what then?"

"I shall go ashore."

"On a convict island?"

"On one of the neighboring islets, if possible."

"To inspect a palm tree."

"And a rock."

He whistled softly.

"Uncle George, I have carried strange cargoes, but never before
a treasure hunter."

"I am not a treasure hunter. I do not know
that there is treasure."

"What do you expect beneath the palm?"

"I have no expectation whatever."

"Then why dig?"

"Because I dreamed that I should."

At this he laughed outright, but when he saw that
I was offended, he became serious.

"Well," he said, "you need the voyage, at any rate.
Come with us to Pernambuco. If on the homeward run
we can safely make the island, I will consider it.
That is all I promise."

We sailed down the Mississippi on a gray December morning,
with a tug puffing ahead and the wet levees slipping
past us. The *Sea Witch* was a trim brig of
four hundred tons, with a black hull, white boats, and
a gilt scroll beneath her bowsprit. I had often supplied
vessels, but had never before made an ocean voyage, and
I soon discovered that familiarity with invoices for cordage and
paint does not enable a man to keep his feet
upon a sloping deck.

The pilot had scarcely left us when a fresh breeze
sprang up from the northeast. The brig heeled, the rigging
hummed, and loose objects in my cabin began a violent
migration from starboard to port. I went on deck with
as much dignity as I could command, but the dignity
shortly departed, together with my breakfast.

Seth Hawkins, the mate, found me clinging to the weather
rail. He was a lean, brown man from Maine, with
gray whiskers, keen eyes, and a manner of addressing everyone
as though he had shipped with him for twenty years.

"Don't crow yet, Mr. Spencer," said he. "The Gulf's only
shaking hands with you."

"If this is a handshake," I replied, "I hope it
never embraces me."

He grinned.

"You'll have your sea legs before we strike the trades.
Then you'll eat enough to frighten the cook."

His prophecy was fulfilled. For three miserable days I cared
nothing for dreams, islands, treasure, business, or even life; but
on the fourth morning I came on deck beneath a
blue sky, saw the Gulf rolling in long splendor to
the horizon, and felt suddenly hungry. From that hour my
recovery was rapid.

The days assumed an agreeable order. At seven bells I
drank coffee; at eight I breakfasted with Ralph; afterward I
walked the deck, read, talked with the mate, or watched
the men at their work. I learned the distinction between
braces and halyards, buntlines and clewlines; I ceased to duck
when a block swung over my head; and, after disgracing
myself at the wheel under Seth's instruction, I gained some
notion of keeping a course.

"Not bad for a landsman," he said one afternoon. "You
only swing her four points each side of the compass
now."

We ran southeastward through the Bahamas and into the open
Atlantic. The weather grew soft and brilliant; flying fish scattered
before the bows; at night the wake streamed with phosphorescence,
as if the *Sea Witch* dragged a green comet behind
her. My appetite became prodigious, my sleep sound, and the
yellow look which had disturbed Alice vanished beneath an honest
coat of sunburn.

Ralph watched this improvement with satisfaction.

"If the dream does nothing else," he said, "it has
saved Doctor Mandeville's reputation."

For several days we lay in the Doldrums, that belt
of uncertain airs near the equator where a sailing vessel
may slap her canvas to pieces while advancing scarcely a
mile. Wet-looking clouds piled themselves in the heavens, rain descended
in silver sheets, and an hour later the sea burned
beneath a pitiless sun. The sails gave idle reports against
the masts; the blocks creaked; even the most industrious sailor
found leisure to contemplate eternity.

During one dog-watch I showed Seth my chart and told
him the story. He heard me without interruption, chewing a
fragment of tobacco and glancing now and then toward the
compass.

"I've known men dream true," he said when I had
finished.

"You believe there is something buried there?"

"I didn't say that. I knew a cook once who
dreamed the foretopmast would go. Next day it went."

"Was that remarkable?"

"Not very. It had been sprung for a month, and
the cook had eyes. Still, the dream was true."

"That is no encouragement."

"Belay encouragement. If there's an island, a palm, and a
marked rock where you saw 'em, I'll help you dig.
If there ain't, we'll gather shells and say nothing to
the captain."

The trades returned at last, and we swept southward under
crowded canvas. One rosy dawn the Brazilian coast rose from
the sea, low and hazy; later the white buildings and
red roofs of Pernambuco appeared, with palms beyond and boats
swarming through the harbor.

Our stay there occupied nearly three weeks. Ralph was engaged
with consignees and customs officials, while I made discreet inquiries
regarding Fernando de Noronha. Everyone knew the island, but few
desired to visit it. It was a military station and
penal colony, governed with strictness, and strangers could not land
without permission. Small islets near the principal settlement were sometimes
visited for fishing or fruit, though even this depended upon
the governor's humor.

One elderly Portuguese merchant examined my coordinates.

"You have the position nearly," he said. "The charts differ.
There are currents, and the old observations were not exact.
Why does the senhor ask?"

"I have an interest in geography."

He gave me a look which showed that geography was
not, in his opinion, a sufficient explanation for any man's
conduct.

When the *Sea Witch* had discharged her cargo and taken
aboard coffee, hides, and other freight, I renewed my request
to Ralph. He frowned, walked to the stern windows, and
stood looking out.

"The island is off our direct course," he said.

"Not greatly."

"There are reefs."

"You need not bring the brig close."

"There may be trouble with the authorities."

"We shall ask permission."

"And when they ask why an American merchant has come
with picks and shovels to a convict settlement?"

"We shall say we are collecting melons."

Ralph turned.

"Melons!"

"I am told they grow upon one of the islets."

Seth, who had entered unnoticed, gave a cough which imperfectly
concealed a laugh.

"Melons is healthy, Captain," said he.

"You two have arranged this."

"We have discussed possibilities," I admitted.

Ralph looked from one to the other and threw up
his hands.

"Very well! If the weather holds, I will make the
island. But I remain aboard, and at the first sign
of official objection you return without argument. I will not
lose brig, license, and reputation for all the dream-treasure in
the Atlantic."

We sailed at noon, and two days later, shortly after
sunrise, the lookout hailed the deck.

"Land ho!"

I sprang from my cabin half dressed and found Ralph
already examining a dark point through his glass. The morning
was clear, the sea lively beneath a fresh easterly wind.
At first I could distinguish nothing; then a jagged shape
detached itself from the horizon, and above it rose a
peak of extraordinary steepness.

My heart began to beat heavily.

"The peak!" I cried. "It is the peak of my
dream!"

Ralph lowered the glass.

"It is the Peak of Fernando, which thousands have seen
without dreaming of it. Keep cool, Uncle George."

But as we approached, detail after detail became visible: the
main island with its rugged heights; smaller rocks scattered along
the shore; white surf breaking over reefs; and, westward of
the settlement, a low islet green with vines and crowned
by several palms.

One of those palms leaned southward under the pressure of
the trade wind.

I could not speak. Ralph looked at me, then again
at the islet, and his skepticism lost some of its
assurance.

A Brazilian launch came out to us, carrying an officer
and four soldiers. Ralph received them with courtesy, displayed his
papers, and explained that we had been delayed in the
Doldrums and desired fresh fruit. The officer, a stout, mustached
gentleman whose white uniform fitted him badly, appeared suspicious until
Seth produced two bottles of excellent Kentucky whisky from Ralph's
private stores. After a conference conducted in mixed Portuguese, Spanish,
French, and signs, he permitted a boat to visit the
western islet for water-melons, provided we carried no firearms and
returned before sunset.

"You must first report to the governor," he added. "His
Excellenza gives the final permission."

Accordingly Ralph stood the brig off and on while four
of us went ashore at the settlement: Seth, myself, and
two sailors named Briggs and Martin. We had placed in
the boat several sacks, a small spade, a pick, and
a short iron bar, all concealed beneath baskets and a
tarpaulin. The tools, I reflected, were natural enough for gathering
melons if no one considered the matter closely.

The settlement consisted of whitewashed buildings climbing a dusty slope
beneath the great peak. Soldiers lounged near a guardhouse, and
parties of prisoners worked under supervision along the road. The
governor received us in a shaded veranda. He was a
grave man with silver spectacles and spoke some English.

"You desire fruit?" he asked.

"Fresh fruit and a little exercise, Excellenza," I replied.

He examined me in a manner which made me conscious
of every wrinkle in my clothing.

"You are merchant?"

"In New Orleans."

"And you voyage for health?"

"Yes."

"It is far to come for melons."

"We were passing the island."

"So." He tapped the table with a pencil. "You may
go. The western islet is not prison ground, but you
will remain there only. No specimens of stone, no birds,
no cutting of trees."

This last prohibition disturbed me.

"We shall do no injury," I said.

He signed a paper and handed it to a corporal,
who accompanied us to the boat but did not embark.
As we shoved off I noticed a clerk speaking rapidly
to the officer who had boarded the *Sea Witch*. Both
men stared at our covered baskets.

"Someone smells our melons before we've picked 'em," Seth muttered.
"Best bear a hand."

The islet lay less than a mile from the main
shore. We pulled through a narrow passage where the water
changed from dark blue to a transparent green, and landed
upon a crescent of white sand. The sailors hauled the
boat above the wash, while I stood overwhelmed by recognition.

It was the place.

There lay the reef where the surf broke in phosphorescent
fire in my dream; there, beyond the strait, towered the
peak; there stood the twisted palm. Even the arrangement of
rocks upon the beach was familiar. Daylight had robbed the
scene of terror, but not of wonder. I felt as
if I had stepped into a chamber visited long ago
and never forgotten.

Seth touched my arm.

"Which way?"

I pointed to a black rock twenty yards inland.

We approached it through creeping vines and coarse grass. Its
seaward face was rough, but the western side formed a
nearly level surface. Sand, lichen, and weather stains covered it.
I knelt and brushed vigorously with my handkerchief.

At first I saw only scratches. Then a straight line
appeared, followed by a curve, and beneath it the faint
outline of letters.

"I saw it—the rock of my dream at last!"

Seth dropped to his knees beside me. With his knife
he scraped away the lichen, taking care not to cut
the stone. Presently the inscription stood plain enough:

**M. D. O.
V PALMAS**

"Portuguese," I said. "The words from my dream—*Mas distante occidente*.
Farther west."

"And five palms," said Seth. "Or a V that means
something else."

We looked westward. The bent tree stood near the center
of the islet, but beyond it, partly hidden by brush,
were other palms. From the rock to the last of
these I counted approximately five equal intervals.

"Five palm-lengths?" I suggested.

"Maybe five palms west," said Seth. "Let's try the farthest
tree."

Briggs and Martin, meanwhile, had discovered an excellent patch of
water-melons and were filling the sacks with enthusiasm.

"Easy there!" Seth called. "Leave room for anything more valuable."

"A melon's valuable in this heat," Briggs answered.

"Pile in six, then come lend a hand."

We carried the tools beyond the first palm, but the
ground showed no sign. At the second we found coral
rock scarcely six inches beneath the soil. The third had
fallen long ago. The fourth stood upright and healthy. The
fifth—the farthest west—was blasted down the center, as if lightning
had struck it years before. Only a ragged trunk and
one living plume remained.

My excitement returned.

"The blasted palm!" I said. "In the dream the sailor
pointed to it."

"No time for speeches," Seth replied. "Where do we dig?"

A shallow depression lay on the western side of the
trunk. We thrust in the spade. Beneath a foot of
sand it struck something hard, but this proved to be
a root. We shifted nearer the tree, encountered stones, and
shifted again.

For twenty minutes we found nothing. Sweat ran into my
eyes; my hands blistered; doubt began its sober work upon
me. Perhaps the dream had brought me only to an
island resembling itself. Perhaps the letters upon the rock were
the idle work of some sailor. Perhaps we should return
to the brig carrying nothing but melons and ridicule.

Then Seth drove the iron bar deep into the soil.

It rang.

Not the dull knock of stone, but a clear metallic
sound!

All four of us fell to work. The hole widened
rapidly, exposing a gray surface three feet below ground. It
was zinc, greenish at the seams and dented by the
bar. We uncovered the top of a rectangular box, perhaps
thirty inches long and eighteen broad. Iron handles were fixed
at either end.

"Treasure or stove parts," said Seth, "it's heavy."

The box had been placed within a rough casing of
timber, much decayed, and covered with sheets of zinc soldered
together against dampness. We passed a rope beneath it and,
with great labor, raised it to the surface.

At that instant Martin, who had gone to the beach,
shouted:

"Boat coming from the settlement!"

I ran to the shore. A long, narrow craft, half
canoe and half raft, was approaching under sail and paddles.
Six or eight men were aboard, among them a uniformed
officer.

"How far?" called Seth.

"Half a mile, and coming fast."

"Then we've got to choose between melons and the stove."

"The box!"

"That's my vote. Briggs, dump those sacks!"

The sailors protested, but obeyed. We wrapped the box in
the tarpaulin, lashed two oars to the handles, and staggered
with it toward the boat. Its weight was astonishing. Twice
we set it down; once the rotten outer wood gave
way and nearly trapped my foot. The approaching men shouted.
Though I could not understand their words, the tone was
unmistakable.

"Hurry, you sons of sea-cooks!" cried Seth. "They're not inviting
us to dinner."

We shoved the box beneath the after thwart, launched the
boat, and tumbled in. Briggs and Martin bent to the
oars; Seth took the tiller; I seized a second pair.
My blisters broke at the first stroke, but I pulled
with a strength born of alarm.

The *Sea Witch* lay nearly two miles away, standing toward
us under easy canvas. Ralph had evidently observed the pursuing
craft and guessed trouble, for men were already at the
braces, and the jibs shook as the brig prepared to
come round.

The Brazilian boat gained steadily. Its paddlers kept a rapid
beat, while we were burdened by the box and by
those few melons which Briggs had managed, in defiance of
reason, to preserve.

"Throw them over!" Seth ordered.

Briggs hurled out two. Martin retained one between his feet.

"For supper," he gasped.

A shot sounded behind us.

The ball struck the water twenty yards to starboard. Whether
it was intended as a warning or missed us by
accident, I do not know.

"Santa Maria!" Seth exclaimed. "They're in earnest."

The brig swung into the wind. Her sails thundered and
went aback; men hauled upon the braces; then the yards
came round and she gathered way on the starboard tack,
passing so close ahead that I could see Ralph leaning
over the taffrail.

"Pull!" he shouted. "Pull, all of you!"

A line flew from the quarter and fell short. Seth
put the tiller over. Our boat rose upon a swell,
Briggs caught the line, and twenty hands hauled us alongside.
The box was slung aboard first; we scrambled after it;
and the boat came up dripping beneath the davits.

"Main topsail! Let fall! Sheet home!" Ralph roared.

The yards were alive with men. Canvas dropped and filled.
The *Sea Witch* leaned to the freshening wind and drove
northward, white water foaming beneath her lee rail.

The pursuers came within shouting distance. Their officer waved the
governor's paper and demanded that we return. Ralph, standing upon
the quarter-deck, answered that we had been fired upon without
cause and would report the affair to the Brazilian government.
This declaration was more spirited than practical, but it sounded
excellent across the water.

For ten minutes the catamaran followed; then the brig's speed
told. The gap widened. The officer shouted one last threat,
and the smaller craft turned back toward the island.

Ralph faced me.

"Well, Uncle George?"

"I am sorry to have caused difficulty."

"You are sorry! I am carrying stolen Brazilian property, I
have fled from an armed officer, and you are sorry."

"We do not yet know it is property."

"What is in that box?"

"I cannot say."

Seth wiped his face with his sleeve.

"If it's stove parts, Captain, I recommend we throw Mr.
Spencer over with 'em."

Even Ralph laughed at that. His anger passed quickly, though
he insisted that we stand well away from Fernando de
Noronha before examining our prize.

At sunset the island was a purple shape astern. The
great peak caught the last light, then faded into haze.
After supper we gathered in the cabin: Ralph, Seth, the
two sailors, and I. The box rested upon the table,
leaving scarcely room for the lamp. Its zinc covering resisted
our efforts until Seth attacked the solder with a cold
chisel. Beneath lay blackened hardwood, bound with copper straps.

The lock had rusted, but the hasp yielded to the
iron bar. Ralph raised the lid.

Within were rows of canvas packets, each sealed with wax.

For a moment no one spoke.

Seth lifted one packet, cut the cord, and poured its
contents upon the table. Gold and silver coins rolled beneath
the lamp; some bore the profile of Emperor Dom Pedro,
others the arms of Brazil. Martin gave a yell that
brought the steward running.

"Belay!" Seth said. "You'll have the whole watch in here."

Beneath the packets lay bundles of paper currency in an
oiled silk wrapping, and under these a flat tin case.
In the case we found a document written in Portuguese,
with a shorter statement in French. Ralph knew enough of
the latter language to translate, and I afterward obtained a
careful English rendering. It read substantially as follows:

> I, Leon da Costa, colonel and commander in the
service of His Imperial Majesty Dom Pedro the Second, place
here the sum of thirty-four thousand milreis, being my lawful
fortune and that of my household. Political dissensions having overthrown
the Empire, and my loyalty being known, I fear confiscation
and imprisonment. I therefore conceal this property until order shall
return, or until I may safely remove it. The mark
upon the eastern rock points westward to the fifth palm.
Whoever finds this deposit is entreated, upon his honor and
before God, to restore it to me or to my
lawful heirs.
>
> Fernando de Noronha, December 2, 1889.
>
> **LEON DA COSTA**

The silence following this declaration was very different from the
excited silence with which we had opened the box.

"Then it is not pirate gold," Ralph said.

"No."

"And the owner may be alive."

"Yes."

Seth slowly pushed the coins back into their packet.

"Thirty-four thousand milreis," he observed. "How much is that in
honest money?"

The value of the Brazilian milreis varied, but by the
rates then current the hoard represented a large fortune—considerably more
than sixty thousand dollars, though an exact calculation was difficult
because of the mixture of coin and notes.

Briggs stared at the box mournfully.

"We got shot at for another man's money."

"We shall keep it safe for him," I said.

"And if he's dead?"

"Then we shall seek his heirs."

Martin looked at his surviving melon.

"This voyage," he declared, "has been badly managed."

Ralph regarded me across the table.

"You agree the money must not be touched?"

"Certainly. We shall inventory and seal it in the presence
of witnesses. On reaching home I will institute inquiries through
the Brazilian consul, banks, and military offices. Until ownership is
determined, the fund will remain intact."

He held out his hand.

"I knew you would say that."

The inventory occupied much of the next day. The total
corresponded almost exactly with Da Costa's statement. We replaced everything,
wrapped the damaged box in new canvas, and secured it
in Ralph's cabin. The men were warned to speak of
it to no one, a caution which ensured that every
sailor on the *Sea Witch* knew the story before nightfall.

Our homeward passage proved uneventful, which was perhaps fortunate, for
I had received enough excitement to last an ordinary lifetime.
We crossed the equator with a fair wind, weathered a
heavy squall in which the foretopmast staysail split from head
to foot, and passed once more through rosy mornings, blue
noons, and nights when the stars appeared to hang among
the royal yards. I had gained weight, as Seth frequently
informed me.

"You'll have to pay freight on yourself when we reach
New Orleans," he said.

I came to love the ordered activity of the brig:
the bells marking the watches, the cry of the leadsman
in shoal water, the sudden thunder of canvas when she
missed stays, and the quiet talk by the taffrail during
the dog-watch. Business anxieties which had seemed overwhelming now appeared
small and manageable. Whether the dream brought me fortune or
not, it had already restored something more necessary.

Alice met us at the levee. She looked first at
my sunburned face, then at the mysterious canvas-covered box being
carried ashore.

"You found it!" she cried.

"Hush," I said. "For the present we have found only

The responsibility lasted nearly a year.

I deposited the treasure in a bank vault and wrote
to the Brazilian consul, to the authorities at Pernambuco, to
merchants in Rio de Janeiro, and to an officer whose
name had been supplied by my Portuguese acquaintance. Replies came
slowly and were often contradictory. One Leon da Costa had
served under the Empire; another had been a customs official;
a third was said to have emigrated to Portugal. For
some months I feared that the rightful claimant might never
be identified.

At last, through a former military secretary, we obtained definite
information. Colonel Leon Manuel da Costa, once a commander loyal
to Dom Pedro, had been arrested after the establishment of
the Republic, released through lack of evidence, and sent under
supervision to Fernando de Noronha. He had afterward returned to
the mainland, but died of yellow fever at Recife in
August of 1890. His wife had died before him; their
only child had not survived infancy; and no lawful heirs
could be discovered. His effects had been administered as negligible,
for he was believed to have lost his fortune during
the political upheaval.

I submitted the facts to counsel and communicated again with
the Brazilian representatives. Since the money had been intentionally hidden,
never claimed, and recovered outside the prison settlement, no government
title was asserted. After proper notices and a further delay,
I was advised that I might lawfully retain it.

Even then I experienced a certain reluctance. I could not
forget the final words of the document—"upon his honor and
before God." Yet Da Costa was dead; there were no
heirs; and all reasonable inquiries had been made. I therefore
accepted the treasure, though not with the careless joy I
had imagined during our voyage.

I gave five thousand dollars to Ralph, who protested until
I reminded him that without the *Sea Witch* the dream
would have remained a scribble upon an envelope. Seth Hawkins
received a substantial present, as did Briggs and Martin; the
latter declared that he had always known the melon would
bring luck. A portion was placed securely for Alice, and
another portion went to the widows' fund of a seamen's
charity. The remainder entered my business and prospered there in
a manner less romantic but more useful than burial beneath
a palm.

As for the explanation of the dream, I have none.

I had never heard of Leon da Costa, never visited
Brazil, and never seen an accurate picture of Fernando de
Noronha. The coordinates had not, to my knowledge, crossed my
eye in any ledger, newspaper, or letter. A philosopher may
say that forgotten impressions combined themselves in sleep; a clergyman
may speak of Providence; a sailor will commonly nod and
declare that stranger things happen at sea.

Seth's conclusion was characteristic.

"The colonel wanted his box found," he said. "You were
the nearest man foolish enough to fetch it."

Ralph continues to command the *Sea Witch*, though he has
since exchanged her old lower masts and complains that steamships
are driving honest canvas from the oceans. My health remains
excellent. I no longer permit business to consume every hour,
and once each year I make a voyage, though I
have never again sailed near the convict island.

The blasted palm may still lean above the empty pit;
the black rock may still bear its weathered letters; and
on moonlit nights the surf doubtless burns green upon the
reef as it did in my dream. Whether two shadowy
figures ever stand there now, pointing in silence toward what
is no longer hidden, I cannot say.

Alice, some years after our return, made the parlor panel
from the original figures which I had written upon the
envelope. She chose black velvet to represent the board of
my dream and worked the numerals in white and gold.
Beneath them, almost too small to be noticed, she stitched
a palm tree, a rock, and a tiny brig under
full sail.

Guests frequently pause before it.

"What is the significance of those coordinates, Mr. Spencer?" they
ask. "Do they mark the scene of a wreck?"

"Not exactly."

"A naval battle?"

"No."

"Then perhaps Captain Ralph discovered some new island?"

At this point Alice and I exchange a glance.

"It is only a marine puzzle," she says.

And, after all, I know of no better answer.

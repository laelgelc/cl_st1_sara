### The Thirty-Nine Stones

We were fishing off Waihee in an outrigger which appeared
to have been designed expressly to test the Christian virtues.
Its bottom was hard, its gunwale sharp, its shade nonexistent,
and its motion compounded the qualities of a cradle, a
cork and a drunken horse. The sun stood overhead with
the stern attention of an overseer; the wind had gone
somewhere else; and the sea, smooth as hammered blue metal,
lifted us slowly toward heaven and then, with a private
malice, lowered us into the glittering hollow between two swells.

Kohokumu sat in the stern, brown, lean and indestructible. He
was more than seventy years old, though he refused arithmetic
upon the subject.

"I have lived," he said, "since before the great wind
that took the church roof at Wailuku. Also before the
sickness when Pua's three wives died, one after another, though
not so quickly as he wished."

This information did not help me calculate his age, but
it explained something of his philosophy.

He wore a faded cotton shirt, a red loincloth, and
an immense wreath of tobacco smoke. His bare feet, twisted
by reefs and hardened by lava, grasped the outrigger as
naturally as roots grip a cliff. A dozen bamboo poles
projected from the canoe. Their lines descended forty feet through
a lucid green world where I could see pale sand,
antlered coral, and dark fish slipping to and fro like
thoughts at the bottom of a dream.

We had been there since dawn. I was burned, cramped,
thirsty and intermittently sick. Kohokumu was in devilish fine form.
He had eaten two raw sea eggs, half a dried
flying fish, and a quantity of poi; he had laughed
at my hat; and now, because the fish were not
biting, he had begun to sing.

His chant concerned Maui, the Promethean demi-god who hauled islands
from the deep, lifted the sky, snared the sun, obtained
fire from an ancestress, and performed other public services of
a magnitude which would nowadays require several governments, an international
loan and a commission of engineers.

Kohokumu sang in a high, quavering voice. Each line seemed
to have three beginnings and no end. It mounted, circled,
broke upon some sonorous name, and began again. I endured
perhaps twenty minutes of it. Then the canoe dipped, my
stomach rose, and philosophy deserted me.

"Kohokumu," I said, "how long is Maui going to continue
this performance?"

The old man stopped, his mouth still open upon a
vowel.

"O Lakana, he has not yet begun."

"He has hauled up three islands, beaten the sun, stolen
fire, raised the heavens, and tangled the ocean with his
fishhook. If that is not a beginning, I tremble to
think what the fellow did afterward."

Kohokumu considered me with mild black eyes.

"Afterward he tried to overcome death."

"And failed, I hope. Otherwise he must be a great
bore by this time."

"He failed," said Kohokumu gravely. "Therefore you may be content."

He resumed the chant.

I seized one of the bamboo poles as if it
were a weapon.

"Listen," I protested. "You are an intelligent man. You know
perfectly well that no person fished up an island. Islands
are made by volcanic action, coral growth, subsidence, upheaval and
a number of other respectable processes."

"Yes," he said.

"And no one snared the sun with a rope."

"No common rope."

"Nor persuaded it to go slowly for half the year."

"Why not?"

"Because the sun does not go."

Kohokumu smiled. "Then Maui was wiser than the song. He
caused that which does not go to go more slowly."

"That is nonsense."

"Perhaps. Yet you say the days are longer at one
season and shorter at another. The old people said Maui
bargained with the sun. You say the earth inclines itself
and travels around. Both ways, the fishermen gain more daylight
in summer. Is the fish concerned with the name of
the hook?"

I informed him that an explanation must correspond with observed
fact.

He nodded courteously. "And Maui's bargain corresponds. Before, the sun
ran too fast. After, it traveled more gently. The work
of men could be finished."

"The appearance is explained, but the cause is false."

"Ah," he said. "You know the cause?"

"Certainly."

"You have gone outside the sky and watched the earth
go around?"

"No."

"Your father watched?"

"No."

"His father?"

"Of course not."

"Then perhaps a man wrote it in a book."

"A great many men proved it."

"Men you do not know?"

"Men of science."

Kohokumu sighed with satisfaction, as though I had confirmed his
argument.

"Your ancestors tell you through books. Mine tell me through
mouths. Therefore your ancestors speak truth, while mine tell whoppers."

I had used that word earlier in the morning, and
regretted it now.

"There is evidence," I insisted. "Measurements, calculations, instruments."

"Our evidence is the length of the day, the time
of planting, the season of fish, and the old chant
which does not forget."

"But Maui cannot have been large enough to seize the
sun."

"Your God made the sun."

"That is different."

"How?"

Here was the trap, and I stepped into it with
the dignity of an educated man.

"The biblical account," I said, "is not to be taken
literally in every particular."

"Then it is a chant?"

"A spiritual allegory."

Kohokumu's face brightened. "Good! Maui also is spiritual allegory."

"That is not what you were claiming five minutes ago."

"Five minutes ago you called him a liar. Now you
have promoted him."

I shifted on the hard board, which had by then
made an enemy of every bone in my body.

"You cannot compare a primitive legend with the profound symbolism
of Genesis."

"Why not? In your story, light comes before the sun.
In Maui's story, the sun already exists but behaves badly.
To me, a badly behaved sun is easier."

"It depends upon interpretation."

"All great truths," Kohokumu said, "depend upon interpretation. That is
why priests and lawyers eat well."

The sea rolled under us. Far inland the mountains of
Maui rose through white vapors, their folds deep green and
violet. Along the shore, palms shimmered in the glare, and
beyond them the cane fields lay in orderly squares, civilized,
profitable and quite irrelevant. A tropic bird hung over the
water, as still as a white mark painted on the
sky.

Kohokumu began another chant.

This one told how, in the first ages, the sky
lay so low that men crawled beneath it, unable to
stand. Maui braced his shoulders against the blue roof and
raised it. The tale, repeated line by line, seemed likely
to occupy the afternoon.

"Stop!" I cried. "If Maui lifted the sky, on what
did he stand?"

"On the earth."

"And what supported the earth?"

"The sea."

"And what supported the sea?"

Kohokumu pointed downward. "Perhaps you should dive and ask."

At that instant one of the lines twitched. We both
became attentive. The bamboo bent, sprang straight, then bent again.

"A fish?" I whispered.

"No. Tako."

The line jerked toward a patch of coral. Kohokumu hauled
it carefully. Something clung below, not hooked, but unwilling to
surrender the bait. Twenty feet from the surface a gray
shape opened like a foul flower and then contracted. I
saw arms uncurl, pale underneath and dark above, with rows
of suckers flashing.

The old man looked at it and chuckled.

"Big one."

"Bring it up."

"He will release before the canoe."

"Then hook him."

"No hook."

Before I understood his intention, Kohokumu put down the line,
stripped off his shirt, and wound a cord around his
white hair. The body revealed was narrow and knotted, with
ribs like the hoops of a basket, but there was
no softness in it. Age had dried him without diminishing
him.

"You are not going down there," I said.

He slipped a short wooden knife into his loincloth.

"Forty feet," I continued. "And that creature is the size
of a washtub."

"Tako is good eating."

"It may eat you first."

"He has no poi."

Then Kohokumu filled his lungs and went over the side.

He entered without a splash. The surface closed, and through
it I watched him descend, brown back diminishing in the
green. He did not swim as I swam, with much
labor and little result. He folded himself and fell headfirst,
giving an occasional movement of his feet. The octopus had
let go of the bait and flowed into a coral
crevice.

At the bottom Kohokumu settled on his knees.

For a moment he was hidden by a burst of
sand. Then an arm whipped out and wound around his
wrist. Another caught his shoulder. The old man thrust both
hands into the hole. More arms emerged—gray, muscular and horribly
intimate. They crawled over his chest, looped his neck and
clung to his face. The water darkened with ink.

I gripped the outrigger. My heart performed all the activity
which Kohokumu's heart, at that depth, wisely avoided. Twenty seconds
passed. Thirty. Forty.

The black cloud thinned. Inside it there was a confusion
of legs, arms and tentacles. Kohokumu's heels braced against the
reef. He pulled. The octopus spread itself across the coral,
suckers clinging with such force that I could see the
flesh draw taut between them. Kohokumu twisted, released one arm,
tore another loose, and suddenly came backward with the whole
formidable creature upon him.

He kicked toward the surface.

The octopus covered his head. Its mantle bulged above his
brow like a monstrous cap; two arms streamed behind; the
others tightened around his throat and body. Halfway up he
seized the mantle, dragged it to his mouth, and bit
between the eyes.

I saw the bite distinctly.

He bit again.

The arms loosened. Kohokumu rose beside the canoe and exploded
into air, spitting seawater and laughter. I hauled at his
shoulder while he pushed the octopus before him. Together we
dropped it into the bottom.

Even dead, it seemed reluctant to accept the change in
circumstances. Its arms writhed over our feet, the suckers fastening
and releasing with obscene little kisses. One coiled around my
ankle. I shouted and kicked, nearly capsizing us. Kohokumu fell
against the gunwale, laughing until tears ran down his cheeks.

"O Young Wise One! Behold! He desires your scientific explanation!"

I freed myself with the fish knife and retired as
far as one may retire in a twelve-foot canoe.

Kohokumu sat down, breathing easily. A thin line of blood
ran from his cheek where a sucker had torn the
skin. He wiped it with his shirt, recovered the bait,
and reset the line.

"You are over seventy," I said.

"Perhaps."

"You stayed below nearly a minute."

"Perhaps."

"That animal might have drowned you."

"Not today."

"And you killed it with your teeth."

"My teeth are old, but the tako did not know."

He patted the dead creature affectionately.

"What keeps you alive?" I asked. "You smoke, you have
never consulted a doctor, you eat things which civilized digestion
refuses even to name, and you perform feats that would
rupture a man of thirty."

"The sea keeps me."

"The sea kills men."

"So does bread, when there is none."

He leaned over, scooped water in his hands, and poured
it over his face. In that gesture there was no
theatrical reverence. It was intimate and careless, like a child
rubbing his cheek against his mother's skirt.

"She made us," he said. "Before men walked, they swam.
Before the bird flew, its grandfather moved in water. Before
your thoughts learned to speak, they floated in darkness."

I stared at him.

"That," I admitted, "is surprisingly close to certain scientific theories."

Kohokumu showed his worn teeth. "Then your scientists are learning
the old chant."

"You cannot possibly know the doctrine of evolution."

"I know that the crab does not grow on the
mountain. I know the child rests in water inside the
wahine—woman—before he sees the sun. I know the turtle comes
from the sea, lays its eggs on land, and returns.
A man needs no spectacles to see that life travels."

"That is observation, not proof."

"Proof is an observation wearing shoes."

He pointed toward the blue depths.

"My mother is below. Not the woman who bore me;
she was Kalua, and she had a loud voice and
a heavy hand. The older mother is below. Someday I
shall go into her. She will close over me. The
little fish will take my flesh, the coral my bones,
and perhaps I shall come again."

"As what?"

"A shark, if I remain greedy. A turtle, if I
become wise. A small silver fish, if I learn obedience."

"And if you learn none of these?"

"Then I shall return as a storyteller and trouble another
Lakana."

The sun had moved westward, though by Kohokumu's account this
was doubtless the result of an ancient labor dispute between
Maui and the solar body. A faint breeze roughened the
sea. The ridges inland became dark, and one long cloud
unfolded above them like smoke from a sacrificial fire.

"Do you literally believe you will be born again?" I
asked.

"Do you literally believe you are awake?"

I laughed. "At present my bruises are convincing."

"In a dream, pain is also convincing."

"But I awaken."

"Perhaps death is awakening."

"That is merely a metaphor."

"Perhaps this is merely a metaphor."

He waved one hand, including the canoe, the mountains, the
dead octopus and my aching person.

I told him that if reality were a dream, it
was a singularly uncomfortable one.

"Whose dream?" he asked.

"Yours, apparently."

"No. In my dream you are not so argumentative."

"Then mine."

"In your dream I would be younger, so that my
strength should not shame you."

There was no safe route through this discussion.

Kohokumu drew up one line, inspected the untouched hook, and
dropped it again. "Perhaps we are dreamed by Maui. Perhaps
Maui sits beyond the horizon, sleeping after his labors. He
dreams islands, suns, fish, old men and strangers with books
inside their heads. When he turns in sleep, the earth
shakes. When he sighs, the trade wind blows. When he
awakens—puff!—where then is Lakana?"

"Where, for that matter, is Kohokumu?"

The old man shrugged. "I am in the story. That
is enough."

"You confuse truth with imagination."

"No. You separate them too sharply. A net with holes
too small catches water and loses fish."

"I prefer facts."

"Then I shall tell you a fact."

"Not another song."

"No song."

"Nor a legend."

"No legend."

"Nor anything concerning Maui."

"Maui will not enter."

"Nor a man who turns into a shark, a pig
who marries a princess, a stone that gives birth, a
lizard longer than Lahaina, or an ancestor who climbs a
rainbow."

"None of these."

I settled myself suspiciously. "Proceed."

"This happened at Honokohau when my mother's grandfather's elder sister
was young."

"That is not a promising beginning."

"It happened at the place where the black cliff makes
a half-moon and the freshwater comes through the stones beneath
the sea. The hole is there. The stones are there.
The family is known. Therefore it is fact."

"I reserve judgment."

"You reserve it as a miser reserves money. Listen.

"In that time there lived a boy called Keikiwai—the Water
Baby. Eleven years old he was: small in the shoulders,
quick in the hands, with hair burned red by sun
and skin that shone when wet. He slept beside the
surf. He could remain underwater longer than a priest can
remain at a feast, and he understood the speech of
fish.

"You do not believe fish speak."

"I promised only to listen."

"Good. Then your disbelief may sit behind you and be
quiet.

"Keikiwai knew the warning cry of the small reef fish
when the ulua hunted. He knew the gossip of parrotfish,
who complain while eating and eat while complaining. He knew
the lobster's prayer when the moon is dark. He knew
the speech of sharks, which is simple and concerns chiefly
hunger.

"At this time a king visited Honokohau. He arrived with
paddlers, warriors, priests, drummers, three fat counselors and a wife
whose appetite was more powerful than any of them. The
people made a luau—a feast—with pig, poi, mullet, seaweed, bananas,
breadfruit and sweet potatoes. But the king's wife looked upon
these things and said, 'Where are the lobsters?'

"The headman trembled, because when a king's wife asks where
something is, a wise man does not answer that it
is somewhere else.

"Now, in the lagoon were many lobsters, red and thick
and excellent. But forty sharks had entered through the reef
passage. Forty! They lay in the green water from the
freshwater spring to the black cliff, and for three days
no man had dared dive.

"The headman called the fishermen.

"'Bring lobsters.'

"The fishermen looked at the sharks.

"'Our eyes are weak,' they said. 'We see no lobsters.'

"The headman called the young men.

"'Bring lobsters.'

"The young men rubbed their stomachs and said they had
eaten something harmful.

"Then Keikiwai stepped forward.

"'I shall bring them.'

"His mother struck him on the head. His aunt struck
his mother for raising a foolish son. His grandmother struck
them both and said the boy had spoken before the
king and must now either dive or be killed for
mockery. Thus the family settled the matter.

"Keikiwai took a basket and walked to the cliff. The
forty sharks saw him.

"'Here comes food,' said the shark with the longest tail.

"'Small food,' said the next.

"'Tender food,' said the third.

"'Mine,' said all forty.

"Keikiwai heard them. At the edge of the lagoon he
bent and picked up a piece of black lava. Then
he called:

"'Eat me if you wish, O sharks! Yet I warn
you: the shark with the shortest tail is my protector.'

"At this the sharks became uneasy.

"'Which has the shortest tail?' asked the first.

"'Not I,' said the second.

"'Certainly not I,' said the third.

"Every shark turned to inspect his neighbor's tail, but a
shark cannot measure well while swimming, and no shark considers
his own tail short.

"Keikiwai threw the lava stone far to the right.

"Splash!

"All forty sharks rushed toward the sound. Keikiwai dived to
the left, went down to the ledge, placed one large
lobster in his basket, and returned to shore.

"The king's wife ate it.

"'Another,' she said.

"The next day Keikiwai came again. The sharks had spent
the night discussing him.

"'He escaped,' said the longest-tailed shark.

"'Because his protector warned him,' said another.

"'Who is the protector?'

"'The one with the shortest tail.'

"They tried to compare their tails. They swam side by
side. They swam in circles. They lined themselves against the
cliff, but the tide moved them. At last one cried:

"'There! That fellow's tail is shorter than mine!'

"The others looked. Since they were hungry and tired of
measuring, they agreed.

"They ate him.

"Thus there remained thirty-nine sharks.

"Keikiwai came to the edge with his basket.

"'Eat me if you wish, O sharks! Yet the shark
with the shortest tail is my protector.'

"The thirty-nine sharks looked at one another.

"Keikiwai threw a stone.

"Splash!

"They rushed to the right; he dived to the left;
he took a lobster and returned.

"The king's wife ate it.

"'Another,' she said."

Kohokumu paused to examine his lines.

"You intend to repeat this thirty-nine times," I said.

"The thing happened thirty-nine times."

"The tale will not."

"O Lakana, truth cannot be hurried."

"Summarize."

He looked injured, but complied.

"Each night the sharks measured tails. Each night they found
the shortest. Each night they ate him. Each morning Keikiwai
cried, 'The shortest-tailed shark is my protector!' Each morning he
threw a stone—splash!—and while they hunted the sound, he dived
for a lobster.

"After the fifth day, there were thirty-five sharks.

"After the tenth, thirty.

"After the twentieth, twenty.

"They became fat. They became suspicious. They also became poor
at counting, for the shark who had counted heads was
eaten on the seventh night, and the shark who replaced
him could count only as high as the number of
fins he could see without turning.

"When twelve remained, they held a council.

"'This boy deceives us,' said one.

"'Impossible,' said another. 'He tells us plainly that the shortest-tailed
shark protects him.'

"'Then why does the protector allow us to eat him?'

"'Because we always eat the wrong shark.'

"This seemed reasonable.

"They measured again.

"The next morning there were eleven.

"Keikiwai came.

"'Eat me if you wish—'

"'We know!' cried the sharks. 'The shortest tail!'

"He threw the stone.

"Splash!

"They rushed to the right. He dived left, and the
king's wife received her twenty-ninth lobster.

"When only four sharks remained, each was enormous, having eaten
so many of his companions. Their bellies dragged near the
sand, and they could no longer turn quickly. Nevertheless, hunger
is a hole that grows wider when filled.

"The four placed their tails together.

"'Yours is shortest,' said the first to the second.

"'No, yours,' said the second to the third.

"'The fourth has hidden part of his tail under a
rock,' said the third.

"The fourth denied it.

"They quarreled until moonrise and then ate the first, because
he had begun the argument.

"Three remained.

"On the following day Keikiwai threw his thirty-eighth stone—"

"Thirty-eighth?" I interrupted. "By my count it should not be."

"Your count is foreign. Mine is family count."

"Arithmetic has no nationality."

"Then why do merchants and fishermen never agree?"

He went on.

"The three rushed to the splash. Keikiwai brought up a
lobster.

"That night the three sharks agreed there could be no
safety while any tail remained unmeasured. They bit one another's
tails to make them equal. But the first bit too
much from the second, and the third laughed. So the
second and third ate the first.

"Two remained, each so swollen that he resembled a canoe
house with fins.

"'Your tail is shorter,' said one.

"'Your eyes are crooked,' said the other.

"They placed their noses against the cliff and stretched their
tails toward the sea. A wave moved the first forward.
The second declared this cheating. They fought, and the stronger
ate the weaker, though with difficulty, because there was very
little room remaining inside him.

"Now there was one shark.

"He was the longest shark, the shortest shark, the fattest
shark and the thinnest shark in the lagoon. He was
also, by necessity, Keikiwai's protector.

"On the next morning the boy came with his basket.
The final shark waited beneath the cliff.

"Keikiwai called, 'Eat me if you wish, O shark! Yet
the shark with the shortest tail is my protector.'

"The shark looked behind him. There was no other tail.

"'I am the protector,' he said.

"'So you are,' said Keikiwai.

"'Then you have lied. I shall eat you.'

"'Before you eat me, measure your tail.'

"'Against what?'

"'Against the place where the tails of the other sharks
ended.'

"The shark swam to the rocks where the measuring had
occurred. Because he was swollen with thirty-nine sharks—"

"Thirty-nine?" I said. "If there were forty originally, and he
is the last, he could have eaten, directly or indirectly—"

"O Divider of Fish Bones, be silent. He was swollen
with thirty-nine sharks. Keikiwai lifted the thirty-ninth lava stone.

"'Your tail is indeed the shortest,' the boy said.

"The shark twisted to see. But his belly was too
great. He twisted harder. Inside him were sharks which had
eaten sharks which had eaten sharks. Their fins and teeth
pressed one upon another like angry people in a small
house.

"Keikiwai threw the stone.

"Splash!

"The shark lunged toward it from long habit.

"His belly struck a pinnacle of coral.

"Pop!"

Kohokumu clapped his hands so sharply that I started.

"He burst. Out came tails, fins, teeth, bones, greed, argument
and bad arithmetic. The water boiled. Crabs hid. The king's
counselors fled uphill. Keikiwai dived quietly, selected the finest lobster
in the lagoon, and carried it to the king's wife.

"She looked at it and said, 'I am tired of
lobster. Have you no chicken?'

"Therefore," Kohokumu concluded, "the people of Honokohau remember Keikiwai, who
saved the divers by feeding sharks to themselves."

For a little while there was only the wash of
water against the hull and the faint ticking of suckers
as the dead octopus relaxed at our feet.

"That," I said at last, "is the factual account you
promised?"

"Yes."

"A boy understood sharks."

"Yes."

"Forty sharks successively ate one another because he announced that
the shortest-tailed one protected him."

"Exactly."

"The last consumed thirty-nine and burst upon coral."

"You listened well."

"And there are thirty-nine stones?"

"Below the cliff."

"Why not forty?"

"The first day he did not need a stone."

"You said he picked one up."

"He picked it up, but perhaps he did not throw
it."

"You said he threw it."

"Then one stone was carried away by the current."

"You have an answer for everything."

"No. Only for questions."

I demanded his evidence.

Kohokumu raised one finger. "My mother's grandfather's elder sister heard
the tale from Keikiwai's daughter."

"Not from Keikiwai?"

"He was dead."

"Convenient."

"Death often is."

A second finger rose. "The diving hole remains. You may
see it."

"A lagoon proves no talking fish."

A third finger. "The black cliff is there."

"Cliffs are notoriously careless about corroboration."

A fourth. "The freshwater rises beneath the salt water, as
the tale says."

"That could be observed by anyone."

A fifth. "The thirty-nine stones lie below."

"Lava stones lie everywhere in these islands."

"Not those stones."

"What distinguishes them?"

"They are the stones Keikiwai threw."

I looked at him helplessly.

He smiled, benevolent in victory. "Also, when I was a
boy, an old woman of Honokohau had a lobster shell.
It came from the last lobster."

"You saw this shell?"

"I saw a shell."

"How did you know it was the shell?"

"She told me."

"And how did she know?"

"Her grandmother told her."

Thus did the fortress of evidence close its gates.

"You call that knowledge?"

"Yes."

"It is hearsay, tradition, coincidence and invention."

"It is family, place, memory and stones."

"The stones prove nothing."

"Then dive and count them."

"And if there are thirty-nine?"

"You will say someone placed them there."

"Naturally."

"And if there are thirty-eight, you will say I lied."

"Naturally."

"And if there are forty?"

"I shall say you counted badly."

Kohokumu laughed. "Behold the open mind of the man of
science! It is a door with three locks."

Before I could frame a reply, the bamboo poles sprang
alive.

One bent almost double. Another rattled across the gunwale. A
third jerked from its notch and would have vanished had
Kohokumu not caught it with his toes. Instantly the philosopher
disappeared and the fisherman took his place.

"Pull, Lakana! Pull!"

I seized the nearest pole. The line cut sideways, humming.
Far below, silver bodies flashed over the coral. Kohokumu hauled
hand over hand, shouting instructions, abuse and encouragement in two
languages. A fish broke the surface beside the canoe, scarlet
as fire, thrashing spray into our faces. My own line
came up with two shining akule twisting upon it. The
outrigger rocked; the dead octopus slid against my feet; hooks
glittered; fish beat the boards like sudden rain.

For ten minutes there was no room in the world
for mythology. There were only lines to clear, hooks to
free, bait to save and the splendid, practical fact of
fish.

At last the school passed. The poles straightened. Sixteen fish
lay in the bottom, bright scales dimming beside the monstrous
tako. Kohokumu rebaited his hooks with swift brown fingers. His
breath remained untroubled.

I looked toward the shore, where evening had begun to
gather purple in the valleys. Somewhere beyond those ridges lay
Honokohau, its black cliff, its spring beneath the sea, and,
conceivably, thirty-nine ordinary stones upon which an extraordinary certainty had
been built.

"Kohokumu," I began.

He held up a warning hand.

"No more questions. The fish are returning."

"But about Keikiwai—"

"I know it for a fact."

"You cannot know—"

"For a fact, O Lakana."

The tip of his pole dipped. He leaned forward, smiling
at the water.

"For a fact," he repeated, and struck.

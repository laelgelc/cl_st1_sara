# The Paradise of Ellison

There are few propositions upon which mankind is so eagerly
unanimous, and so perpetually at variance, as that which affirms
happiness to be the proper object of existence. The miser
discovers it in accumulation; the prodigal, in dispersion; the statesman,
in that troubled pre-eminence which is dignified by the name
of power; the poet, perhaps, in those brief moments when
the unsubstantial creation of his intellect assumes, to his own
vision, a reality more persuasive than the world. Yet it
may be questioned whether these several seekers pursue happiness itself,
or merely some glittering symbol which custom has substituted for
it. We kneel before the casket, and forget to inquire
if the jewel be within.

My friend Ellison—whose life, because it was exceptionally fortunate, has
been considered by the unimaginative as exceptionally uninteresting—had meditated upon
this subject with a patience at once philosophical and practical.
His conclusions were not many. Indeed, he maintained that felicity,
in the widest and noblest sense, depended upon four conditions,
neither one of which could be omitted without impairing the
harmony of the whole.

The first was continual exercise in the open air—not violent
labour, which exhausts the frame and brutalises the sensibility, but
that temperate and varied employment which keeps the body in
health while leaving the intellect unoppressed. The second was the
love of a woman whose beauty should awaken, and whose
character should sustain, the ideal passion. The third was contempt
for ambition; or, more properly, an entire exemption from that
necessity of comparison by which one man measures his dignity
against the abasement of another. The fourth—and Ellison regarded it
as comprehending the other three—was devotion to some spiritual purpose:
some undertaking so elevated above pecuniary appetite, so inexhaustible in
its demands upon taste, and so intimately allied with beauty,
that the pursuit of it might continue through life without
degenerating into routine.

"Give me these," he once said, "and I will surrender
without reluctance the applause of cities, the authority of princes,
and the discoveries of sages. Health, love, freedom, and a
purpose which looks beyond the grave—what residue is there in
happiness which these do not contain?"

I objected that health was uncertain, love perishable, freedom largely
fictitious, and every purpose vulnerable to satiety.

"You speak," he replied, "of accidents as if they were
essences. Health may be lost; therefore let it be cherished.
Love may be interrupted by death; therefore let it be
made worthy of immortality. Freedom may be limited; therefore let
us refuse its voluntary surrender. As for satiety, it attacks
only those occupations whose objects can be counted. He who
spends his life in making money may at last possess
enough; he who devotes himself to beauty can never possess
too much."

It would have been easy to dismiss such doctrines as
the graceful extravagance of youth, had not an event occurred
which permitted him to translate speculation into existence. The event
was one which, in any ordinary history, would have formed
the catastrophe rather than the commencement. Ellison inherited a fortune.

Fortunes are comparative. That which constitutes opulence in a village
becomes competence in a provincial capital, and poverty in the
neighbourhood of a court. But the wealth which descended upon
my friend had acquired, even before it reached him, an
almost fabulous celebrity. A remote ancestor—an austere, silent, mathematically constituted
person, who appears to have regarded mankind chiefly as an
agency for increasing compound interest—had placed a moderate sum in
securities, with the injunction that principal and revenue should remain
untouched for a hundred years. The directions were observed with
a fidelity rare in testamentary affairs, partly through the ingenuity
of the instrument, and partly, I suspect, because no possible
claimant could comprehend the ultimate dimensions of the bequest.

When the century expired, the accumulated capital was announced to
Ellison in figures which I shall not reproduce, lest the
reader accuse either my memory of derangement or arithmetic of
sorcery. It will suffice to say that the annual revenue
equalled the entire property of several noble families; that the
monthly income might have purchased a palace; that the daily
increase exceeded what prudent men call independence; that the hourly
increment would have supported a scholar in elegance; and that,
during the minute occupied by signing the papers, Ellison became
richer by a sum for which a tradesman might have
laboured during half his life.

The announcement produced in him neither ecstasy nor incredulity. He
examined the documents carefully, asked several questions respecting obligations attached
to the estate, and then walked with me for two
hours through a wood in which the first green of
spring was unfolding.

"What shall you do?" I asked at last.

"That," he answered, "which I should have done with less
means, but upon a smaller field."

He would not build hospitals merely that his name might
be carved above their portals; yet he provided anonymously and
with admirable judgment for suffering which could be substantially relieved.
He would not collect pictures because others had pronounced them
precious; nor maintain houses in twenty cities, to remain perpetually
absent from nineteen; nor convert gold into horses, retinues, offices,
elections, wines, or the costly ennui called fashion. He held
luxury in no ascetic abhorrence. A beautiful object, however expensive,
delighted him; but expense alone was, in his estimation, the
least beautiful property an object could possess.

His marriage, which occurred within the year, fulfilled the second
of his conditions. Mary was not merely lovely—loveliness, unsupported by
intelligence, may fascinate the senses while fatiguing the soul—but possessed
that serenity which gives to beauty the permanence of expression.
Her features were classical without coldness; her dark eyes, although
usually tranquil, had the peculiar luminosity of deep water when
the sun strikes far beneath its surface; and her manner
united tenderness with a self-command which never hardened into reserve.
She understood Ellison without pretending always to agree with him,
and admired his genius without becoming its flatterer. This distinction
is rarer than love.

The project which was henceforth to absorb them had already
occupied his dreams. He proposed to create—not a garden, for
the term was contemptibly inadequate; not a park, which suggested
avenues, lawns, deer, and an English peerage; not even a
region of ornamental landscape, for ornament implies something superadded—but a
vast composition in earth, water, foliage, light, architecture, and atmosphere:
a work whose material should be Nature, whose method should
be Art, and whose final impression should transcend both.

I remember the evening upon which he first explained his
conception. We sat in a library opening upon a terrace.
Rain had ceased, but its fragrance remained among the shrubs;
beyond them a declining moon hung amid clouds of pale
bronze.

"Of all the arts," said Ellison, "that which men call
landscape gardening is the most comprehensive and the least understood.
The painter disposes colours upon a surface; the sculptor gives
rhythm to arrested substance; the musician arranges sound in time;
the architect commands masses, proportions, and intervals. But the creator
of landscape employs colour, form, sound, fragrance, motion, distance, and
time together. His canvas is the earth; his pigments are
forests and flowers; his lights are the sun, moon, and
stars; his shadows are clouds and seasons. The years become
his assistants. Winds modify his harmonies. Rivers carry his design
through space. He composes not for the eye alone, but
for the entire bodily and spiritual susceptibility of man."

"You would make the gardener superior to the poet?" I
asked.

"I would make the perfect gardener a poet. Consider the
distinction. Poetry is not verse, nor painting, nor music; it
is that faculty which combines familiar elements into relations capable
of awakening an exalted emotion. A natural scene may be
beautiful, but Nature does not, so far as human vision
can determine, compose exclusively for our contemplation. She permits accident;
she tolerates repetition; she overwhelms effect by boundless detail. The
artist selects. He does not improve Nature as one repairs
a defective machine—such a phrase would be absurd—but disengages from
her infinity an intelligible expression."

I urged that the charm of wild scenery depended largely
upon its independence of intention.

"Assuredly," he replied, "and therefore intention must conceal itself. The
vulgar artist insists upon being recognised. He cuts a straight
road through a forest that every visitor may admire his
authority; he compels a stream to leap because he has
built a wall; he places a marble nymph where no
nymph, being sane, would either bathe or recline. The true
artist effaces his hand. He creates arrangement, but preserves apparent
liberty; he excludes deformity, but leaves irregularity; he preconcerts surprise,
yet allows the spectator to believe that surprise has discovered
him."

Mary, who had been listening beside the window, smiled.

"And where," she asked, "will you find a country patient
enough to endure all this improvement without confessing it?"

"That," said Ellison, "is the first difficulty."

It occupied four years.

During this period I accompanied them through districts which, to
ordinary judgment, would have appeared abundantly fitted for terrestrial paradise.
We examined valleys in which ancient woods descended to lakes
of an almost Alpine purity; plains bounded by purple hills;
islands whose shores were enamelled with perpetual verdure; ruined monastic
estates where streams murmured beneath ivy and broken arches. One
region was rejected because the mountains oppressed rather than enclosed
it; another because its atmosphere was too frequently colourless; another
because neighbouring manufactures sent a narrow but intolerable stain across
the western horizon; another because the river, although magnificent in
volume, was deficient in variety of motion. Ellison demanded seclusion
without remoteness, magnitude without monotony, wildness without sterility, and sufficient
proximity to civilisation that all materials, instruments, and skilled labour
might be obtained without delay.

At times I accused him of seeking a geography which
existed only in his imagination.

"All geography," he answered, "exists first in imagination. We recognise
in the world what the mind has prepared itself to
desire."

In the fourth autumn we entered a province which had
been little frequented, not because it lay far from populous
districts, but because roads, commerce, and fashionable curiosity had passed
on either side of it. A broad river wound through
the country, navigable for many miles, yet broken into such
curves that no extensive prospect revealed the direction of its
course. Low hills, clothed with oak, ash, and chestnut, rose
from its margins; behind these, at irregular distances, appeared loftier
ridges, blue with vapour. Villages were numerous enough for convenience,
but so placed in folds of the land that neither
smoke nor steeple intruded upon the principal vistas.

We had travelled since morning in a small boat, landing
wherever some variation of bank or woodland attracted Ellison's scrutiny.
Near sunset the river narrowed between two elevations of singular
form. That on the left was a steep hill, feathered
to the water with dark trees; the opposite bank consisted
of pale rock, fractured into shallow recesses and hung with
vines already crimsoned by the season. The channel curved sharply,
so that what lay beyond was invisible. Yet through the
opening came a cool current of air burdened with the
odour of cedar and some late-flowering shrub whose perfume was
unknown to me.

Ellison raised his head.

"Do you perceive it?" he asked.

"The fragrance?"

"The enclosure."

We passed the turn. Before us the river expanded into
a long, quiet basin, perhaps two miles in extent, terminated
by another apparently impenetrable bend. On either side the ground
rose in terraces not distinctly marked as such, but indicated
by alternating belts of meadow and wood. Far away, through
a cleft between the hills, one snowy summit caught the
last radiance of day. The remainder of the scene lay
in violet shadow, except where the water, retaining the sky,
resembled a path of luminous metal.

Mary spoke no word. Ellison looked from the river to
the heights, from the heights to the distant mountain, and
then backward toward the narrow gateway by which we had
entered. I saw in his face the stillness of conviction.

Before midnight he had learned that the basin formed the
lowest portion of an extensive domain, divided among several proprietors
but capable of purchase. Within six months the boundaries were
his. Within a year all roads crossing the estate had
been diverted—without public inconvenience and with liberal compensation—while a village
whose presence disturbed one essential prospect was removed bodily to
a more advantageous position beyond the eastern ridge. Its inhabitants,
far from complaining, discovered themselves possessed of better houses, richer
soil, a school, and an annuity derived from lands which
Ellison transferred to their common use.

Only then did his true labour commence.

It is impossible, within any reasonable compass, to record the
operations of the succeeding twenty years. They involved engineers, botanists,
architects, painters, geologists, hydraulic mechanicians, and gardeners collected from many
countries; yet no one among these, nor any combination of
them, comprehended the design in its totality. Each received a
fragment. Ellison alone retained the whole.

He rose before dawn, passed ten or twelve hours in
the open air, and returned with an elasticity which mere
leisure could never have bestowed. One day found him directing
the excavation of a lake; another, determining the precise inclination
at which a slope should descend toward a grove; another,
experimenting upon the distance from which a certain tree displayed
most nobly the architecture of its branches. He imported cedars
from Lebanon, cypresses from the Mediterranean, palms from islands whose
names seemed themselves perfumes, and flowering plants gathered from the
high valleys of continents then scarcely explored. But he never
assembled rarities as a collector arranges specimens. Whatever refused citizenship
in the general effect was excluded, however costly, ancient, or
celebrated.

"Curiosity," he said, "is not beauty. A garden may astonish
by containing a thousand foreign things, and still resemble a
vocabulary without a poem."

The water system alone demanded seven years. Existing rivers were
preserved in their apparent courses, but their volume was secretly
regulated through reservoirs concealed among the northern hills. Subterranean conduits,
descending by imperceptible gradients, supplied springs where springs appeared inevitable;
maintained cascades through the driest summer; and fed innumerable channels
which, though apparently capricious, obeyed a single hydraulic design. No
wheel was seen, no pipe detected, no mechanical pulse heard.
The streams seemed born from moss and rock; the fountains
rose as if persuaded upward by delight.

Nor was architecture neglected; but architecture, in Ellison's domain, never
asserted an independent existence. Buildings appeared as conclusions toward which
the landscape had silently reasoned. A pavilion of white stone
stood where a crescent of dark firs demanded a luminous
centre; a bridge of one shallow arch crossed a stream
precisely at the point where the eye desired continuity between
two lawns; a tower, half veiled in ivy, lifted itself
above an oak wood, not as a monument imposed upon
the trees, but as though the trees, aspiring through centuries,
had finally flowered into masonry.

Mary shared these labours with a devotion in which there
was nothing submissive. Certain regions of softer character were entrusted
almost entirely to her judgment; and Ellison, who would dispute
an inch with an engineer, accepted her verdict in matters
of colour with affectionate humility. She understood the emotional value
of concealment. Where he inclined toward grandeur, she introduced repose;
where his imagination multiplied splendours, she reserved a meadow, a
solitary birch, a reach of untroubled water. Their minds moved
in counterpoint—not opposition, but harmonious independence.

I visited them frequently, and at every visit some new
portion of the design emerged from confusion. Yet the process
itself was often destructive. Hills were reduced, ravines deepened, forests
thinned, rocks transported at enormous labour, and mature trees removed
with masses of soil sufficient to preserve their life. To
a spectator ignorant of the purpose, the estate must at
times have resembled a country devastated by giants. Ellison was
distressed by this temporary deformity, but never deterred.

"Creation on our scale," he observed, "is merely judicious disturbance.
Even the planting of a rose wounds the earth."

The expenditure was commensurate with the design; still, his income
exceeded it. This circumstance amused him.

"My ancestor," he said, "spent a century accumulating permission for
me to move a mountain. The mountain proves more obstinate
than the money."

At length, although no landscape is completed while growth, decay,
weather, and light continue their transformations, the principal composition attained
maturity. Ellison was then no longer young. His hair had
whitened; his figure retained its vigour, but his movements possessed
the measured economy of age. He had achieved no public
office, founded no dynasty, dictated no fashion, and engraved his
name upon none of the clamorous institutions through which vanity
negotiates with oblivion. Yet I have known no man whose
days appeared so thoroughly inhabited. The four conditions had remained
his: health through activity; love through Mary; freedom through indifference
to ambition; purpose through the creation of beauty.

Did this completeness constitute happiness? I believe it did. Not
uninterrupted pleasure—for pain visited him as it visits all organisms
endowed with nerves and memory; not security—for he knew too
well the fragility of those he loved; not self-satisfaction—for no
artist is wholly satisfied whose ideal advances as rapidly as
his work. His happiness consisted rather in unity. He did
not desire in the morning what he despised at night.
His means did not contradict his ends. His body, affections,
intellect, and conscience moved, with such discord as humanity cannot
escape, toward one centre.

He died in his seventy-second year, beneath a group of
plane trees overlooking the western water. His last afternoon had
been spent in correcting the direction of a narrow path,
because, as he told me, it revealed the reflection of
a distant portico one instant too soon. Mary survived him
by little more than a year. The estate then passed,
under conditions which he had carefully established, to trustees charged
with its preservation. Admission was granted only upon particular days
and to a restricted number of visitors; for crowds, he
maintained, destroy landscape not always by physical injury, but by
interposing between the individual and solitude.

It was after both had gone that I approached, for
the first time, by the ceremonial route which Ellison had
designed for strangers. During his life I had entered by
practical roads, crossed unfinished grounds, consulted plans, and viewed effects
from behind their machinery. Now the machinery was concealed, the
plans withdrawn, the labourers vanished. I was to encounter the
work not as an accomplice, but as a pilgrim.

Our party consisted of six. We embarked shortly after sunrise
at a town some twelve miles below the estate. The
boat, constructed after an antique model, was broad enough for
ease but low in the water, and moved beneath the
strokes of two silent rowers. No badge, inscription, or ornamental
device announced our destination. Indeed, for the first hour, nothing
distinguished the voyage from an excursion through a region of
cultivated and tranquil beauty.

The river was wide and mildly animated. Farms descended to
its banks; cattle stood knee-deep among the reeds; orchards, heavy
with fruit, surrounded houses whose blue smoke ascended vertically into
the lucid morning. Children called from a ferry. A mill-wheel
revolved with that solemn and monotonous sound which, being associated
with industry remote from haste, tranquillises rather than disturbs the
imagination.

Gradually the evidences of habitation diminished. The farms became larger
and less frequent; hedges yielded to thickets; the hills approached
the water. Yet the transition was so measured that no
moment could be named at which cultivation ceased and solitude
began. A cottage would appear where we believed ourselves already
remote, and then, for a mile, no roof, fence, road,
or grazing animal would be seen.

The river, meanwhile, grew more sinuous. Its curves no longer
permitted a prospect exceeding a few hundred yards. At every
turn the channel seemed terminated by foliage, only to disclose,
as we advanced, another reach differing subtly from the last.
Here the banks were low and brilliant with meadow flowers;
there they rose abruptly in masses of laurel and hemlock;
farther on, grey rocks leaned above a depth so transparent
that submerged branches appeared suspended in amber air.

I began to perceive the governance of an invisible intelligence.
No object proclaimed design, yet every object participated in sequence.
A pale grove followed a dark one; agitation of water
succeeded repose; near detail prepared distant magnitude. The impressions were
distinct, but their boundaries melted into one another like phrases
in music.

At noon the sky became overcast. The change, which elsewhere
might have dulled the landscape, introduced a graver beauty. We
entered a narrow channel overshadowed by cliffs of black stone.
Enormous cedars stretched from either side, interlacing their limbs high
above us, so that the river flowed beneath a natural
vault. Moss descended from the branches in long, funereal draperies.
The air was cool, aromatic, and strangely still.

Not a bird crossed the opening—not an insect troubled the
surface—not a dead branch, not a withered leaf, not a
stray pebble broke the profound propriety of the scene. Yet
there was no appearance of neatness, that petty virtue of
suburban grounds. The rocks were irregular; roots contorted themselves over
the banks; vines wandered in apparent lawlessness. It was wilderness
purified of accident—savagery translated into style.

The water darkened until it reflected nothing. Our oars, entering
it, awakened low reverberations from the cliffs; and these echoes,
prolonged and softened by distance, resembled the notes of some
melancholy instrument played underground. Once I heard, or fancied that
I heard, a sustained chord beneath the ripple. It faded
when I attended to it, returned when I ceased to
listen, and accompanied us thereafter like a memory.

The passage contracted. Ahead, the cliffs seemed to meet. No
outlet was visible; no landing offered itself. One of our
companions turned toward the rowers, but they continued without hesitation.
At the last instant, where a curtain of hanging ivy
appeared to touch the opposite rock, the boat swerved sharply.
We passed through a cleft so narrow that I might
have extended my hands to either wall.

Darkness enveloped us for perhaps twenty seconds. The roof was
natural stone, though its surface, faintly luminous with mineral moisture,
suggested the interior of an immense shell. Then a point
of radiance shone ahead, enlarged, and burst upon us.

I despair of conveying the revelation.

We emerged into a circular lake, enclosed by rising ground
whose entire circumference glowed beneath the restored sunlight. The cloud
had divided precisely above the basin. From the dark aperture
through which we came, the surrounding splendour possessed an almost
supernatural violence.

The lake was of the purest blue—not the blue of
pigment, nor even of ordinary sky, but a depth of
colour seeming to contain light within itself. Across its surface
moved broad reflections of gold, emerald, and rose. The banks
ascended in sweeping curves, clothed from water to summit with
flowering vegetation arranged in masses so vast that the eye
abandoned enumeration. One slope burned with crimson azaleas; another was
pale with lilies and silver-leaved shrubs; a third, partly shadowed,
displayed violets so intense that the earth appeared covered with
fragments of evening.

Above these lower luxuriances rose groves of trees selected no
less for hue than form. Copper beeches spread canopies of
sombre purple; birches lifted white stems amid translucent green; cedars
accumulated shadow; acacias trembled with feathery brightness. Through openings between
them, remote lawns ascended toward terraces, and terraces toward higher
woods, until the entire amphitheatre terminated in a ring of
blue hills.

Yet abundance alone cannot explain the impression. There was abundance,
even prodigality; but it had the discipline of celestial geometry.
Every colour intensified its neighbour or subdued it; every line
conducted the eye toward another line; every mass was balanced,
not by symmetry, but by emotional equivalence. The flowers resembled
jewels only because jewels are the nearest dead approximation to
living colour—rubies melted into petals, sapphires dissolved in shadow, opals
expanded into dew. The lawns had the softness of velvet
and the lustre of satin; the white boles of distant
trees shone like ivory columns; and the lake beneath all
was an inverted heaven in which the terrestrial pageant repeated
itself, tremulous and spiritualised.

At the centre of the basin floated an island, though
at first its shores were indistinguishable beneath pendulous foliage. Upon
it stood a structure which might have been called a
temple, had it possessed any recognisable allegiance to a historical
order. Its foundations appeared to rise from the water; its
slender pillars, neither wholly Grecian nor Oriental, supported a roof
of pale green metal that curved like an opening leaf.
Between the columns there were no walls, only shadow. A
flight of broad steps descended into the lake, and along
these steps water ran in a transparent sheet so thin
that the stone beneath seemed covered by moving crystal.

No person was visible. No smoke indicated habitation. The entire
domain presented itself as the work of beings who had
completed their purpose and withdrawn.

Our boat moved slowly along the southern margin. The concealed
music of the water became more distinct, though still no
instrument could be discovered. Small streams entered the lake through
channels lined with coloured stone; each produced its own cadence,
and their mingled sounds formed a harmony too irregular to
be mechanical, too coherent to be accidental. Ellison had tuned
the descents, as I afterwards learned, by varying the width,
depth, and substance of their beds.

We landed in a recess where white flowers grew down
to the water. The path before us was scarcely a
path; it declared itself only through the easier yielding of
turf and the occasional exposure of a flat stone veiled
in moss. We ascended.

At every interval the lake disappeared, then returned under a
new aspect. From one point it lay far beneath us,
an oval mirror enclosed in flowers; from another only a
fragment glittered between tree trunks; from a third the island
temple stood isolated against the opposite hillside, its pillars magnified
by reflection. These concealments and revelations were not caprices. Ellison
understood that a beautiful object continuously displayed may become merely
present, whereas an object recovered after deprivation acquires the emotion
of return.

The path entered a grove of immense trees. Their trunks
stood at distances apparently fortuitous, yet sufficient to permit long,
cathedral perspectives among them. The ground was covered, not with
grass, but with a minute plant of deep green upon
which our steps left no mark. Ferns occupied the hollows;
scarlet fungi gleamed near roots; high overhead, leaves divided the
sun into innumerable golden fragments.

In the centre of the grove a spring welled silently
from a basin of dark rock. No figure presided over
it, no verse instructed us how to feel. Beside the
basin lay a single block of stone, broad enough for
two persons. Here, I knew, Mary had often sat. The
knowledge altered the scene. Until then the domain had appeared
exempt from mortality; now affection entered it, and with affection,
grief.

Beyond the grove the character of the landscape changed. The
ground rose more abruptly; vegetation became sparse; broken masses of
granite emerged from heather and low pine. A wind, unfelt
below, moved across the ridge. The softness of the lake
region gave place to severity, but the transition was accomplished
through intermediate degrees—flowering meadow, open woodland, shrub, fern, heath, rock—so
that we seemed to have travelled many leagues and ascended
into another climate.

At the summit stood no building. The reward was space.

The estate extended beneath us in an immensity impossible to
reconcile with the narrowness of its approach. River, lake, forest,
ravine, garden, and distant architecture appeared not as separate possessions,
but as movements within one composition. To the east, a
chain of pools caught the sun in descending flashes. To
the west, a broad woodland darkened toward a gorge from
which rose the white breath of a cataract. Northward, beyond
folds of cultivated greenery, the remote snowy mountain seen on
our first exploration lifted itself with extraordinary distinctness. Its position
had determined innumerable alignments throughout the domain; it was the
final accent in vistas miles apart, the natural pinnacle to
which Ellison's art had subordinated everything.

Nearer, but still far below, I perceived the roofs of
a residence almost hidden among trees. It was Mary's house—low,
extensive, built of warm grey stone, with courts opening toward
the south. Its modesty in so magnificent a setting was
itself magnificent. A palace would have contended with the landscape;
this dwelling completed it by suggesting human felicity without human
dominion.

We descended by another route into the western forest. Here
Ellison had permitted gloom its fullest expression. The trees grew
densely, their leaves of a dark and polished green. Ravines
crossed the ground; water whispered beneath stones without appearing. The
path wound among precipices clothed in moss of such thickness
that the rock seemed draped in antique velvet. Occasionally a
shaft of light descended from some unseen aperture overhead and
illuminated a single object—a pale flower, a pool, the twisted
root of a yew—while all around remained obscure.

At the deepest point of the forest we reached the
cataract. It did not reveal itself suddenly as spectacle; first
came vibration underfoot, then coolness, then a murmur increasing to
solemn thunder. The path turned around a wall of rock,
and we stood in a cavernous recess facing a fall
of water perhaps eighty feet in height.

The stream issued from darkness above, divided upon a projection,
and descended in two unequal veils. Behind the thinner veil
the rock gleamed with mineral colours—green, bronze, violet, and black.
Spray filled the recess, but through some management of currents
it did not reach the ledge on which we stood.
Ferns of prodigious size bordered the pool; beyond them the
outflow vanished immediately beneath an arch of roots.

The scene was sublime, yet not gigantic; fearful, yet without
danger. Its terror had been purified, as the wilderness had
been purified, of all that might compel the body to
resist the imagination. We could surrender to awe because no
stone would fall, no flood rise, no path fail. Art
had prepared security and concealed the preparation.

From the cataract we emerged, after a long ascent, into
evening.

The western region differed from all that preceded it. Here
the lines were extended and serene. A broad lawn, descending
almost imperceptibly, terminated in a river whose farther bank was
hidden by mist. Groups of trees stood upon the grass
with an amplitude denied to those in the forest. Their
branches, freed from competition, expressed the complete genius of each
species. A cedar spread like a dark green cloud; an
elm lifted innumerable delicate divisions into the amber sky; a
cluster of poplars rose like flames arrested in silver.

Along the horizon, low terraces of flowers received the sunset.
Their colours, subdued by distance, no longer resembled gems, but
vapours—rose, amethyst, saffron, and faint blue. As the sun declined,
concealed mechanisms released a quantity of water along the upper
terrace. It descended over the flowering slopes in threads and
films, catching the light, until the entire hillside seemed transformed
into a silent cataract of jewels.

Then the sun vanished.

For a few moments the earth lost colour, and every
form became solemnly distinct. The first star appeared above the
cedar. Lights, placed no one could determine where, awakened gradually
among the paths and waters—not lamps, for no flame or
fixture was visible, but diffused radiances resembling imprisoned moonlight. Some
shone beneath the surface of pools; some touched the undersides
of leaves; some revealed distant architecture while leaving the intervening
ground obscure. The effect was not illumination, but enchantment.

We reached the plane trees beneath which Ellison had died.
Their pale trunks stood around a circular platform overlooking the
river. Upon the stone balustrade there was an inscription; not
his name, nor a catalogue of virtues, but a sentence
drawn, I think, from no author:

**WHAT IS BEAUTIFUL IS NOT THEREFORE ETERNAL; YET WHILE IT
IS BEAUTIFUL, ETERNITY LOOKS THROUGH IT.**

I remained after the others had moved away.

Below me the river travelled through darkness, receiving here and
there the reflection of a star. From the distant lake
came the almost inaudible harmony of falling water. Fragrance rose
from unseen flowers. The whole immense composition—its mountains and mosses,
its architecture and shadows, its streams controlled beneath an appearance
of liberty—seemed less a possession than a thought made habitable.

It might be objected that Ellison had accomplished no useful
work. He had fed no nation, discovered no law of
matter, conquered no disease. The objection proceeds from that melancholy
philosophy which recognises necessity as sacred and delight as frivolous.
But man does not live by preservation alone. The body
asks bread; the spirit asks significance. When every hunger has
been appeased, when every wound has been bandaged, when justice
has established its most exact proportion—what then? Shall existence justify
itself merely by continuing?

Ellison believed that beauty was not an ornament upon life,
but one of the evidences by which life becomes tolerable
to consciousness. He had used wealth, not to multiply his
appetites, but to liberate a purpose. He had accepted Nature,
not as a slave accepts authority, nor as a tyrant
imposes alteration, but as one intelligence addresses another through an
intermedium of forms. His art did not rival creation. It
confessed, by every selection, its dependence upon creation; yet it
added the distinctly human act of love.

And was he happy?

I return to the question because all biography, however embellished
by incident, is ultimately an inquiry into the relation between
desire and destiny. Ellison desired what his circumstances permitted; but
this alone would not have saved him, for circumstance often
enlarges desire until fulfilment becomes impossible. His greater fortune was
inward: he knew what to refuse. He refused ambition, display,
rivalry, dissipation, and the sterile worship of accumulation. He refused
to let the opinions of those without vision determine the
employment of vision. Above all, he refused that division of
the self by which men labour for what they do
not esteem, acquire what they cannot enjoy, and reach at
last a summit from which their own lives appear contemptible.

He loved one woman; he served one idea; he worked
in the air and sunlight; he remained master of his
hours. If happiness be not here, the word has no
meaning.

As I departed, our boat passed once more through the
narrow cleft. Behind us the circular lake shone beneath the
rising moon. The island temple appeared suspended between water and
sky; its pillars repeated downward until the reflection met them,
and no line divided substance from image. Then the ivy-curtained
rock intervened. Radiance contracted to a crescent, to a thread,
to nothing.

We returned through the gloomy channel. The concealed music followed
us faintly; the cedar vault opened; ordinary stars appeared. By
midnight we had reached the pastoral country, where lamps burned
in cottages and dogs barked at our passage. The world
resumed its familiar imperfection—roads, fences, smoke, accidental trees, neglected banks,
and houses erected without relation to earth or heaven.

Yet for many days I could not wholly believe in
this inferior reality. The colours of Ellison's domain persisted behind
visible colours; its harmonies haunted common waters; its order made
disorder seem temporary. I understood then the ultimate generosity of
his design. He had not merely constructed a retreat for
himself, nor even a monument for posterity. He had created
a standard within the imagination. Whoever entered that paradise carried
away a fragment of it—not a flower, not a stone,
not any perishable possession, but an expanded conception of what
the earth, interpreted by reverence and genius, might become.

Thus his garden survives beyond its boundaries. It survives wherever
a tree is spared because its curve completes a prospect;
wherever a stream is freed from ugliness; wherever architecture consents
to belong to the ground from which it rises; wherever
wealth, weary at last of counting itself, submits to beauty.
More profoundly, it survives in the possibility that a human
life may be composed as he composed his landscape—not by
suppressing every irregularity, nor by forcing all things into symmetry,
but by selecting a purpose, arranging desire around it, concealing
the machinery of sacrifice, and reserving, amid magnificence, those quiet
spaces in which love may sit beside a spring.

I have sometimes dreamed of Arnheim—for so Ellison called the
domain—as existing no longer upon any map. In the dream
I approach by water. The banks grow dark; the channel
closes; the oars awaken music from the rock. Then comes
the cleft, the momentary night, the widening point of fire.
I pass through—and before me, untouched by age, lies the
blue lake, the flowering amphitheatre, the temple poised upon its
own reflection, and the far mountain burning with an unearthly
snow.

Mary stands beneath the plane trees. Ellison is beside her,
no longer old. They do not beckon; they have no
need. The landscape itself is their gesture of welcome.

And while I gaze, uncertain whether I behold a work
of man transfigured into Nature, or Nature perfected through the
dream of man, the waters deepen into an inverted heaven,
the hills ascend in cataracts of colour, and the whole
enchanted realm seems to breathe with one immeasurable, tranquil life.

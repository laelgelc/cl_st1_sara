I was going to tell you about a wedding. It
took place on a wet November morning, in a church
so crowded that the steam of damp overcoats clouded the
windows and the candles seemed to burn underwater. But no—if
I begin with the wedding, you will not understand why
I laughed when I first saw the bridegroom, nor why,
a moment later, I felt ashamed of having laughed.

Let me begin instead with a New Year's Eve party,
exactly five years earlier.

I had been invited by a man who was not
quite my friend. He was what people call a connection:
a prosperous contractor, hospitable from calculation, genial from habit, and
anxious to gather in his drawing room everyone who might
someday be useful. He had invited several respectable families, two
officials, a celebrated lady who spoke through her nose, one
impoverished provincial with extraordinary whiskers, and Julian Mastakovich.

That name, I assure you, was pronounced with reverence.

As for me, I was of no use to anyone.
This gave me liberty. A useful man must talk; a
useless man may observe. I like observing, particularly at gatherings
where everyone is determined to appear natural. Nothing is more
instructive.

The party was nominally for children. A fir tree stood
in the drawing room, blazing with candles, gilded nuts and
sugared fruits. Beneath it lay presents in bright paper. The
host's own children—three remarkably well-fed little beings—were already flushed with
anticipation. Other children stood about in ribbons, lace collars and
velvet jackets, their eyes fixed upon the parcels with that
solemn greed which adults consider charming because it resembles their
own.

The gifts were distributed according to a principle so exact
that one might have supposed the host had consulted a
table of ranks.

The son of a wealthy merchant received a wooden cavalry
regiment, with cannon. The daughter of an influential official received
a musical box. A banker's child was given an elaborate
game involving ships, islands and real brass coins. The lesser
children received lesser wonders: paint boxes, drums, hoops, toy kitchens.

At the bottom of this descending scale stood a thin,
freckled boy of perhaps ten. He was the son of
the governess. His jacket was too short in the sleeves
and had been carefully brushed until the cloth shone. He
waited without pushing, his hands behind his back. At last
he was given a little book of improving stories, without
pictures and bound in gray paper.

He thanked the host.

That was the worst of it: he thanked him beautifully.

The finest gift was reserved for the host's daughter, an
eleven-year-old girl with large dark eyes and pale, serious cheeks.
A splendid doll was placed in her arms. It wore
a white satin gown and possessed, as someone loudly announced,
real hair. The girl did not cry out like the
others. She looked at the doll, then at the people
watching her, and hugged it from embarrassment rather than delight.

"There is our little princess!" said the mother.

"Our future beauty!" cried the nasal lady.

"And our future happiness," added the father, in a tone
which suggested that happiness had already been deposited at interest.

I did not understand this last remark until I overheard,
near the dining-room door, two gentlemen discussing the girl's prospects.

"Three hundred thousand rubles," whispered one.

"Already?"

"Already set aside. Land besides, perhaps. By the time she
is sixteen—"

Here they noticed me and began talking about the weather.

Now Julian Mastakovich had heard them too.

He was a round, imposing gentleman, with a shining forehead,
heavy cheeks, little folds at the back of his neck,
and the air of a man accustomed to see doors
open before he had quite reached them. He perspired even
when standing still. When he laughed, he threw back his
head but kept his eyes watchful, as though checking whether
everyone else had begun laughing first.

Until that moment he had treated the children as a
species of decorative furniture. He had patted one bald little
head, called another "a fine young citizen," and retreated whenever
sticky fingers approached his coat. But after hearing those words—three
hundred thousand—he became thoughtful.

He went to a window and drummed upon the glass.

"Three hundred," I heard him murmur. "At four per cent…
twelve annually… five years… sixty. No, with accumulation… and securities
may rise… four and a half, perhaps… Three hundred—hm!—at least
three hundred and eighty. With the land… four hundred. Possibly
five."

He stopped, frowned, and calculated again upon his fingers.

I confess I moved nearer.

"Five years," he whispered. "She will be sixteen. Exactly."

Then he turned and looked at the child.

There are glances which undress, glances which threaten, glances which
beg. His appraised. He looked from the girl to her
father, from the father to the mother, and then back
to the doll clasped against the little satin bodice. His
face flushed still more. He produced a handkerchief and wiped
his forehead.

At that instant our host hurried up, beaming.

"My dear Julian Mastakovich! You are neglecting us. You must
have some champagne."

"Presently, presently," said the worthy man, without taking his eyes
from the girl. "Your daughter is enchanting."

"A good child. Modest. Remarkably affectionate."

"Eleven, did you say?"

"Eleven last September."

"Indeed? She appears—she has such understanding in her face."

The girl, meanwhile, escaped into the adjoining sitting room. She
carried the doll by one arm, its satin feet dragging
over the carpet.

A few minutes later I followed. I don't know why.
Curiosity, perhaps; or that instinct which leads an idle man
into places where respectable people would rather he did not
go.

The sitting room was dimmer than the drawing room. Only
two lamps burned there, and the noise of the party
came through the doorway in gusts. Behind a large stand
of plants I discovered the little girl and the governess's
son seated on the floor. The magnificent doll lay between
them, stripped of bonnet and shoes.

The boy had made a bed for it from a
sofa cushion.

"It is ill," the girl said.

"No, only tired," he answered.

"Then you must be the doctor."

"I don't know how."

"You can pretend."

"All right. It has a fever."

"Will it die?"

"No," he said quickly. "I won't let it."

They bent together over the doll with such grave concern
that I almost withdrew. Their alliance, I later learned, had
begun in the drawing room. The richer children had refused
to admit the boy into their game. He had offered
them his apple; they took it, but still would not
let him command the cavalry. Then one stout little tyrant
had ordered him to be a horse. The boy had
gone on all fours and carried the tyrant halfway across
the room, hoping by this humiliation to purchase admission. Instead,
he had been kicked for moving too slowly.

The girl had seen it. She had taken his hand
and led him away.

Children sometimes understand justice before adults teach them its disadvantages.

As I watched, a shadow crossed the doorway. Julian Mastakovich
entered.

He did not see me behind the plants.

He advanced on tiptoe—not an easy movement for a man
of his circumference—and paused. His face had assumed an expression
of sugary tenderness, but the effect was spoiled by his
breathing. He puffed softly, like a kettle attempting discretion.

"What are we doing here, my little angel?" he asked.

The girl started. The boy rose at once.

"We're playing," she said.

"Playing! Delightful. And with your beautiful doll? Come, tell me,
my precious child, do you know what dolls are made
of?"

She shook her head and held the doll tighter.

"Rags," said Julian Mastakovich, smiling. "Little scraps and rags, cleverly
arranged. But you—what are you made of, eh?"

The girl looked alarmed.

"Sugar," he suggested. "Cream? Rose petals?"

She backed toward the sofa.

He bent over her and, glancing at the door, pinched
her cheek. She winced. He attempted to kiss her forehead,
but she turned away and put the doll between them.

The poor boy stepped forward.

"She doesn't like it," he said.

Julian Mastakovich straightened.

"What?"

"She doesn't like being pinched."

The great man's face changed. It was remarkable how quickly
the rose petals disappeared from his own cheeks, leaving only
meat and anger.

"And who are you?" he asked.

The boy said nothing.

"Go back to the other children."

"He's playing with me," said the girl.

"Yes, yes, but he must go. Go, boy."

"No," said the girl, catching her companion's sleeve.

Julian Mastakovich looked again toward the door. Then he leaned
close to the boy.

"Off with you."

"We made the doll's bed," the boy whispered.

"I said off!"

The little girl began to tremble.

"Don't send him away."

"My sweet child, he must not disturb you. You have
your lovely doll. I shall bring you something much finer
when I visit. Would you like that? A little horse?
A house? A necklace?"

"I want him to stay."

Julian smiled with his lips, though his eyes had become
vicious.

"He is a naughty boy."

"He isn't!"

"A very naughty boy. Look, he has spoiled the cushion.
Go away, sir!"

The boy held his ground. Thin as he was, he
planted his shabby boots firmly on the carpet.

This resistance infuriated Julian Mastakovich more than open insult from
an equal could have done. He seized the handkerchief from
his pocket and flicked it at the boy's face.

"Shoo! Shoo, I tell you!"

The boy ducked behind an armchair.

Julian pursued him.

I wish you could have seen our honored personage circling
that chair: flushed, wheezing, his coat tails flying, striking with
his handkerchief while the child dodged silently from side to
side. There was something so absurd in it that I
laughed aloud.

Julian froze.

He peered behind the plants and discovered me.

For several seconds we stared at one another. His embarrassment
was so great that it almost deserved pity. Almost.

"Ah!" he cried. "You are here?"

"As you see."

"I was amusing the children."

"Very successfully."

The boy took advantage of the pause and ran from
the room. The girl followed, clutching her doll by its
naked waist.

Julian Mastakovich folded his handkerchief with great care.

"A nervous little fellow," he said. "Badly brought up."

"On the contrary, I thought him courageous."

"Courageous? Insolent! Children of that class must be taught discipline
early. Otherwise they grow up with expectations."

"Expectations are dangerous."

He looked sharply at me. Then, deciding that a man
of no consequence could safely be despised, he turned away.

In the drawing room the host had cornered the governess's
son beside the tree. The child was trying not to
cry. One of the host's well-fed cherubs accused him of
breaking a toy trumpet, though the trumpet lay intact beneath
the table.

"You mustn't quarrel with your betters," the host told him.

The boy glanced at his mother. The governess stood near
the wall, pale and helpless.

"He didn't quarrel," said the host's daughter. "They were cruel
to him."

"Hush, darling. You don't understand."

"I do understand!"

Her mother hurried over, laughed, and kissed her.

"What spirit! She will rule us all someday."

Julian Mastakovich appeared behind them.

"What a noble heart," he said warmly. "Such sympathy! Such
rare feeling in one so young!"

The girl recoiled when she saw him. No one noticed,
or no one wished to notice.

The host drew Julian aside.

"My dear friend," he began, lowering his voice while arranging
his face into an expression of paternal concern, "since we
are speaking of that poor boy—his mother is an excellent
woman, thoroughly dependable—I wonder if you might do something for
him?"

"For whom?"

"The governess's son. A place in one of your offices,
perhaps. He writes a fine hand."

Julian Mastakovich raised his eyebrows.

"A place? My dear sir, you know how matters stand.
There are no vacancies. None whatever."

"Not immediately, of course. But with a recommendation—"

"Impossible. Positions are requested every day by persons of family,
persons with service, persons with claims. If one gave places
merely from compassion, where would it end?"

The host nodded gravely, as if contemplating an abyss filled
with employed poor boys.

"Quite so, quite so."

"Besides," Julian continued, "I have observed the child. A difficult
nature. Proud. Disobedient."

"He is shy."

"Pride often resembles shyness."

At this point the girl's father approached with champagne. Julian's
whole body softened.

"My esteemed friend!" he exclaimed. "I have just been speaking
of your daughter. What purity! What intelligence! A child, and
yet already such dignity. Believe me, I meet many families
in my position, but rarely—very rarely—"

The father glowed.

"You are too kind."

"Not at all. I am sincere to a fault. Her
education, I suppose, will be continued at home?"

"Certainly. We want to preserve her innocence."

"Admirable. The world is so corrupt."

Here Julian sighed as though corruption were a personal affront
he had spent his life resisting.

The mother joined them. He bowed over her hand, praised
the party, the tree, the children, the arrangement of the
lamps, the sweetness of the cakes and, finally, the household's
entire moral atmosphere. Within ten minutes he had accepted an
invitation to dine the following week. Within twenty he was
speaking of the family as though Providence had only just
introduced them to one another after long intending the match.

I stood nearby and watched him.

I also asked, loudly and with every appearance of innocence,
whether Julian Mastakovich was married.

He turned crimson.

The host coughed. The mother looked down. The provincial gentleman
stroked his magnificent whiskers as though they had asked the
question.

"No," Julian said at last. "I have not had that
happiness."

"Ah," I replied. "Then perhaps it is still being calculated."

He understood me. I saw it in his eyes. But
I was nobody, and a nobody's insolence is best punished
by pretending not to hear. He turned his back and
began praising the little girl's skill at the piano.

The evening ended with embraces, carriage bells and servants searching
for lost mittens. The poor boy left beside his mother,
carrying the gray book under his arm. The wealthy girl
watched him from the staircase. Her doll hung forgotten at
her side.

Julian Mastakovich was the last guest to depart. At the
door he pressed the host's hands and promised to visit
very soon.

That was the New Year's party.

Five years passed.

I heard Julian's name from time to time. He advanced
in rank; he acquired an additional decoration; he was spoken
of as a man whose influence could open any door—except,
naturally, the door of an office for a governess's son.
I also heard that he had become intimate with a
certain wealthy household and was paying particular attention to its
young heiress.

Then came that rainy November morning with which I almost
began.

I had gone into the church merely to escape the
downpour. A wedding was about to take place. The nave
was crowded with curious people, and along the walls stood
women whispering beneath damp shawls.

"Five hundred thousand," one said.

"Not counting property."

"She is only sixteen."

"But an excellent marriage. He is a distinguished man."

"Old enough to be her father."

"What does that matter, with such a position?"

The choir stirred. Heads turned.

The bridegroom entered first, splendidly dressed, broad, flushed and solemn.
Time had enlarged him without altering him. The same folds
rested at his neck; the same watchful eyes measured the
faces around him. It was Julian Mastakovich.

For an instant I remembered the handkerchief, the armchair and
the great man puffing after a frightened child. I nearly
laughed.

Then the bride entered.

She was pale beneath her veil. Her large dark eyes
appeared larger than I remembered, and there were tears in
them—not the ceremonial tears admired by aunts, but heavy, unwilling
tears which she tried repeatedly to blink away. Her hand
trembled on her father's arm. She looked so young that
the orange blossoms seemed not bridal ornaments but flowers laid
upon a child's grave.

Good God, I thought, it is she.

The priest began. Julian stood upright, dignified, triumphant. People around
me murmured about his rank, her beauty, the brilliant future
before them. No one mentioned a dim sitting room, a
doll without its shoes, or a thin boy in a
shiny jacket who had once refused to abandon her.

"Five hundred thousand," the woman beside me repeated. "They say
it has grown to that."

Five years: three hundred thousand, four per cent, accumulation, securities,
land.

I looked at the bridegroom.

His calculations had been correct.

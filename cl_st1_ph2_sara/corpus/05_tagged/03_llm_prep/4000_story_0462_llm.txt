## Jane Acts for the Best

I never did hold with romantic love, and I do
not now, in spite of what Uncle Peter and Mattie
and half the village may say. Love is very well
in songs, where nobody has to buy bacon or pay
rent, and in story-books, where a young man's coat never
wears at the elbows unless he is a duke in
disguise. But put love into a cottage with a smoky
chimney, six shillings owing for flour, and twins expected before
Michaelmas, and you will soon see what it is worth.

I put it to you: can kisses be boiled for
broth? Will sighs mend a broken window? Does a girl's
heart keep her feet dry in November?

No. Yet because I have always possessed a little common
sense, people call me hard.

My cousin Mattie was quite different. Mattie could look at
a dead rose for twenty minutes and remember who had
given it her. She kept bits of ribbon, old notes,
and one cracked button in a box lined with blue
paper, though none of them was worth a penny. When
I told her so, she said they was precious to
her.

"Precious!" says I. "That button come off Jack Halibut's working
waistcoat."

"That is why," says she.

And there you have Mattie all over.

We both lived with her father, my Uncle Peter Bell,
at High Ash Farm. My own father and mother being
dead, Uncle Peter had taken me in when I was
twelve, and I worked for my keep from that day
onward. I made no grievance of it. I skimmed the
milk, fed the hens, helped with the cheeses, took butter
into market, and could get more jam from a basket
of gooseberries than Mattie could, because she was forever letting
the pan boil over while she stared out of the
window.

It was through one of these fits of staring that
Jack Halibut first got a hold on her.

He was assistant to old Davy Crumb, the blacksmith, and
he had strong shoulders, white teeth, and nothing else worth
mentioning. He earned eighteen shillings a week when work was
good, which it often wasn't, and he had an old
mother in a damp cottage beyond the mill. But Mattie
said he had an honest face.

So has a cow, if you come to that.

Jack used to pass the farm of evenings, and Mattie
would find business by the gate. If there was no
business, she made some. She carried grain to hens that
had gone to roost, fetched water when the pail was
full, and once took a letter to the post that
had no stamp and no address because, if you'll believe
me, it was a list of things she meant to
buy in town.

I warned her.

"Mattie," says I, "he may be handsome by candlelight, but
candlelight don't show holes in stockings."

"He has no holes in his stockings," says she.

"How do you know?"

She went as pink as boiled ham, which answered that
question without answering it.

Uncle Peter found them by the orchard wall one Sunday
after chapel. Jack was holding Mattie's hand, or she was
holding his; I won't be unjust, though in my opinion
the distinction is no larger than the difference between a
sixpence and two threepences.

Uncle Peter put his foot down.

"You're a good lad, Jack," says he, "but a good
lad without savings is a cart without wheels. It may
be sound timber, but it won't carry my daughter."

"I can work," says Jack.

"So can a horse," says Uncle Peter, "and no sensible
father marries his girl to one."

Mattie began crying. She could cry as easy as another
person sneezes.

Jack stood very straight and says, "What must I have
before you'll consent?"

"Enough to take a cottage, furnish it decent, and keep
a wife through a bad winter."

"Name the sum."

Uncle Peter named two hundred pounds, which seemed to me
generous, for I should have asked three. Jack's face fell
at first, as well it might, for he had then
saved seven pounds, fourteen shillings, and a foreign coin that
old Davy had taken for a farthing. But he looked
at Mattie, and Mattie looked at him, and they went
on looking till I began to feel like a stool
left in the rain.

"I'll get it," says Jack.

"Not from Davy's forge," says Uncle Peter.

"Then I'll go where I can."

A month later he sailed for America.

Mattie made a great tragedy of it. She stood on
the lane till the carrier's cart was out of sight,
and then she came indoors with her face as long
as a fiddle and sat down beside the flour bin.

"Do get up," says I. "You'll mark your dress."

"He is gone," says she.

"That is generally what happens when a person goes."

"He may never come back."

"Then it is fortunate you did not marry him first."

She looked at me as if I had said something
cruel, when I was only pointing out the bright side.
It is a curious thing that people who boast of
tender hearts cannot bear plain truth.

Jack wrote from Liverpool, and then from New York, and
then from a place called Pittsburgh, where there was iron
works and smoke enough, by his account, to make our
blacksmith's chimney seem like a lady's perfume bottle. His letters
come regularly at first. Mattie read them until the folds
split. She would not show them to me, which I
considered unkind after all the times I had fetched them
from the post.

I did see pieces, naturally. A letter left open on
a kitchen table is no more private than a notice
nailed to the church gate.

He wrote of long hours, hard labour, and wages four
times what Davy paid. Later he had some share in
a machine business, though what sort of machines Mattie could
not explain. She only knew Jack was doing well and
loved her. There might have been twenty pages about boilers
and one line saying, "My own dear girl," and that
was the line she remembered.

For three years she waited.

Now, I do not deny that she had chances. Young
Mr. Pottle, whose father kept the Red Lion and owned
two cottages, asked her twice to walk out with him.
Ned Swan, the miller's nephew, brought her a china brooch
from Birmingham. Even Mr. Rudge, a widower with a comfortable
coal business and only four children, made inquiries.

I advised Mr. Rudge. Four children is a consideration, but
so is a horse and trap.

"I love Jack," says Mattie.

"You loved him three years ago," says I. "That is
not the same thing. A preserve may keep three years,
but it won't taste fresh."

"He loves me too."

"How do you know what a man is doing across
the ocean? He may have married an American."

"He would tell me."

I laughed at that. Men do not always tell. If
they did, where would lawyers get their dinner?

The truth was, though there is no use making too
much of it, I had sometimes wondered about Jack myself.
Not when he was poor, of course. A woman should
never encourage a young man in habits he cannot afford.
But as his letters spoke of contracts, partners, and money
put away in a bank, he seemed to improve wonderfully.

It was not his fortune I cared for. I should
like that understood. Money alone never attracted me. It was
the steadiness that money represented, and the house it might
furnish, and the servants it might pay, and the respect
it might purchase. Any sensible girl would think the same.

When the important letter come, Mattie and I was making
blackberry jam.

She had on a blue apron, and her hair was
twisted up anyhow because of the heat. I was lifting
jars from the boiler. The postman put the letter through
the open window, and it fell beside the sugar scales.

Mattie knew Jack's writing at once. Her hand shook so
that she upset a saucer.

"You have broke the good one," says I.

"He is coming home!"

She had not even opened the letter. That is the
sort of foolishness I mean.

When she did open it, she read with both hands
pressed to her cheeks. Then she laughed, then cried, then
kissed the paper, which had travelled through several dirty post
offices.

"Well?" says I.

"He is home already. He landed four days ago. He
has been in London on business, and he comes here
Thursday."

"Does he?"

"He wants to see me before he sees Father. He
says there is something he must tell me alone."

I thought that sounded suspicious.

Mattie read on. Jack had arranged to meet her at
eight on Thursday evening, by the old yew beside St.
Bartholomew's Church. He enclosed a little note meant to be
put in her prayer-book, in case Uncle Peter should see
the larger letter. There was no sense in that arrangement,
for Uncle Peter never touched anybody's prayer-book except in church,
and then only his own, but lovers enjoy complication as
cats enjoy string.

"What does he say of his circumstances?" I asked.

She looked blank. "He says he has succeeded beyond his
hopes."

"How far beyond?"

"I don't know."

"Does he mention figures?"

"No."

"Property?"

"No."

"Bank interest?"

"No, Jane."

"Then what has filled three sheets?"

She held the letter against her breast. "Things you would
not understand."

That was gratitude for you.

On Thursday the house was in a flutter, though Uncle
Peter knew nothing. Mattie changed her dress three times. First
she put on the green muslin bought at Easter, then
the grey with the pearl buttons, and then—what do you
suppose?—she chose the old brown dress she had worn the
day Jack left.

"It is faded at the shoulders," says I.

"He liked it."

"He liked you in it when you was eighteen and
had a waist two inches smaller."

She did not attend.

She put on no jewellery except the little brass locket
Jack had given her, though she owned the china brooch
from Ned Swan and a fine jet pin that had
belonged to her mother. I offered to lend her my
garnet earrings, but she refused.

"I want to look as I did when he went,"
she says.

"You cannot. Three years has passed."

"It has not changed how I feel."

There are remarks so foolish that common sense has no
tool to get a hold on them.

At half past seven Uncle Peter went to look at
a sick calf in the far pasture. Mattie took her
prayer-book, though it was not Sunday, and slipped from the
kitchen.

I watched her from the window.

Now, I had not meant to follow. I had a
batch of jam to cover, two pans to scour, and
my best bonnet wanted new ribbon. But as the poet
says, Satan finds some mischief still for idle hands to
do, and I was not idle, so there was no
reason Satan should be blamed. I followed because Mattie was
innocent and impractical, Jack had been abroad among strangers, and
somebody ought to see that all was proper.

Besides, I wanted to know how rich he was.

I kept far enough behind that Mattie did not hear
me. She went down the lane, crossed the green, and
entered the churchyard by the little side gate. The sun
had gone behind the ridge, but the sky was still
pale, and the church windows showed black under the ivy.

Jack was not yet there.

Mattie walked around the church twice, went beneath the yew,
came out again, and then tried the church door. Old
Mr. Page, the sexton, often left it unlocked when he
had been sweeping, and that evening he had. Mattie went
inside.

No doubt she wished to pray. Or perhaps she wished
to look at herself in the brass tablet near the
vestry, which gave a fair reflection if you stood sideways.
I am not judging.

The key was in the outside of the lock.

I stood looking at it.

Then I looked toward the road.

Then, quite without forming any serious plan, I turned the
key.

It was a sort of joke.

At least, I think so. Mattie had played jokes herself.
Once she put salt in my tea, though she said
it was an accident, and another time she hid my
thimble. I only intended to keep her there a minute,
so I might speak privately to Jack and discover his
intentions. If his intentions was honourable, no harm would be
done; and if they was not, Mattie would thank me.

There come a little knock from inside.

"Jane?" says Mattie.

I was surprised she knew it was me.

"Is that you, Jane?"

I made no answer. There is no use beginning an
explanation before a person is calm enough to hear it.

Then I saw Jack coming up through the ash copse.

I knew him at once, though he was changed. He
had grown broader and carried himself like a man accustomed
to giving orders. His coat was dark blue and fitted
him across the shoulders; his boots shone; there was a
gold chain over his waistcoat, a diamond ring on his
little finger, and a travelling bag in his hand that
looked real leather, not pressed pasteboard.

You may say I noticed these things quickly. Some people
notice sunsets. I notice what is useful.

He came straight toward me, then stopped.

"Jane Bell?"

"Jane Marsh," says I. "But you are near enough."

"Where is Mattie?"

No "How do you do?" No remark on my appearance,
though I had put on a clean collar that afternoon.
Travel may improve a man's coat without improving his manners.

"Mattie?" says I.

"Yes, Mattie. She received my letter?"

"Oh, she received it."

"Then why isn't she here?"

I glanced toward the church. There was another knock, louder,
but Jack took it for a branch tapping the door,
or else he did not hear.

"Poor Jack," says I.

His face altered.

"What do you mean?"

I had not decided what I meant, but once a
conversation has started, it must be carried through, like washing
on a windy day.

"I told her you would be disappointed," says I.

"Told her? She isn't coming?"

"She went out this evening," says I, which was perfectly
true. "But not to meet you."

He frowned like a July thunderstorm. "Who did she go
to meet?"

"I ought not say."

"Jane, tell me."

"You have been away a long time."

"Three years, two months."

"A girl can change in three years."

"Mattie has written every month."

"A woman may write from habit. I write down butter
sales every Saturday, but that don't mean I love butter."

He took a step toward me. "Has she promised herself
to somebody else?"

I lowered my eyes. I have always known how to
make my face match an occasion, as one matches ribbon
to cloth.

"You had better ask her."

"I am asking you."

"It is not for me to betray Mattie's confidence."

This was true too, because she had given me none.

He gripped the handle of his travelling bag. "Is it
Pottle?"

I said nothing.

"Or Swan? I heard of that miller fellow."

"I shall mention no names."

"There is a man, then."

"I did not say so."

"You meant it."

If a person insists on drawing conclusions, whose fault is
that?

From inside the church came three sharp blows.

Jack looked round. "What is that?"

"Wood settling," says I. "The church is old."

He stared at the door, but then turned back. Hurt
pride is a strong bridle.

"Why didn't she tell me?" he says.

"She wished to spare you."

"Spare me! By letting me travel four thousand miles?"

"Mattie never thinks of distances. You know how she is."

He paced under the yew, and I could see anger
and misery wrestling in him like two dogs in a
sack. I felt sorry for him. I truly did. No
woman likes to see a well-dressed man suffer.

"I made it," says he. "I brought home more than
Peter Bell asked. Ten times more. I own half the
works now. I have bought the old Mercer house outside
the village. I was going to tell her—"

"The Mercer house?" says I.

It had twelve rooms, a walled garden, stables, and a
glasshouse, though three panes was cracked.

"Yes. I had it put in order. New carpets. A
piano. She said once she wanted a room looking west."

Mattie had said that when she was sixteen, after seeing
a sunset. She had no practical use for a west
room.

"She might have remembered," says I.

He laughed, but there was no pleasure in it. "I
remembered everything."

That was when I began to perceive how deeply Mattie
had wronged him—or would have wronged him, had what I
implied been factual.

"You must not let it spoil your life," says I
gently.

"What do you care?"

"More than you know."

He looked at me then. Properly looked, I mean. I
had worn my plum-coloured dress, not from design but because
the other was hanging to air, and my hair was
smooth. I am not vain, but I knew I looked
capable. Some men prefer a pretty fool, but a man
who owns a machine business requires a wife who can
reckon.

"You?" says he.

"There was more than one person who waited for news
from America."

I did not say I had waited for him. I
had waited for the letters, which is different, but he
was in no condition for fine distinctions.

"You never cared for me before."

"A girl is not always free to show what she
feels."

Behind us, Mattie began rattling the latch.

Jack did not seem to hear. "You used to laugh
at me."

"I laughed to hide my tears."

This come into my head from a song, and it
served very well.

He walked away three paces, turned, and says, "If she
has thrown me over, I will not crawl after her."

"Naturally."

"She shall see I can do without her."

"Quite right."

"One wife is as good as another, if she is
faithful."

I should not have put it so myself, but I
understood his meaning.

He came close. His face was pale and set, and
I knew he was acting from anger. But many a
sound bargain is made because somebody is angry. If we
waited for perfect temper, no pigs would ever be sold
at market.

"Jane," says he, "will you marry me?"

Now, some might say I ought to have asked for
time. But delay can lose an opportunity, and I had
long believed quick decisions to be the mark of a
strong mind.

"Yes," says I.

At that exact moment there came a thump from the
church that made a pigeon fly out of the tower.

Jack glanced up.

"Those timbers want examining," says I. "You being prosperous now,
perhaps you could subscribe."

He scarcely listened. "Then it is settled?"

"I do not go back on my word."

Neither did Mattie, as it turned out, but there is
no use looking ahead.

Jack took my hand. He did not kiss me, which
I considered decent in a churchyard, though we was technically
engaged. The diamond on his finger flashed. I wondered whether
the ring was an American fashion or whether he might
give it to me after having it made smaller.

"Your uncle will be astonished," says he.

"He respects practical arrangements."

"He'll ask about Mattie."

"She has made her choice, and we have made ours."

Another bang come from inside. Then Mattie called my name,
plain enough.

"Jane! Let me out!"

Jack's hand dropped from mine.

"What was that?"

Before I could answer, the church bell gave one enormous
clang.

Mattie had found the rope.

The sound struck over our heads and rolled across the
green. A flock of rooks rose from the elm trees,
Uncle Peter's dogs began barking half a mile away, and
old Mrs. Tapp came running from her cottage with a
saucepan in her hand.

Clang!

Jack stared at the tower.

Clang! Clang!

"It appears," says I, "that someone has been accidentally locked
in."

"Who?"

There are moments when no answer will improve a situation.

Jack seized the church-door handle and shook it. "It's locked."

"So it is."

"The key's here."

He looked at the key, then at me.

I have never liked suspicious expressions.

"Open it," says he.

"I was about to."

"Open it!"

There was no need to shout. I turned the key.

By then people was hurrying from every direction. Mr. Page
the sexton came puffing through the gate, followed by the
baker, two boys, Mrs. Tapp with her saucepan, Ned Swan,
and old Davy Crumb, who happened to be drinking at
the Red Lion. Uncle Peter appeared last, running as fast
as a man of sixty can run in boots clogged
with pasture mud.

Jack pulled the door wide.

Mattie stood inside with the bell rope in both hands.
Her old brown dress was dusty, her hair had fallen
down, and there was a smear on her cheek where
she had tried the little vestry window. She looked first
at me, then at Jack.

"Jack!"

That was all she said.

She flew at him.

Not angrily. Not doubtful. Not like a girl who had
changed her mind or promised herself to Mr. Pottle. She
threw both arms around his neck before the whole village,
and Jack dropped his travelling bag and held her as
if she was the only living creature left after a
flood.

It was excessively revealing.

"Mattie," says he, "why was you in there?"

"She locked me in!"

Every face turned toward me.

"Do not make it sound worse than it was," says
I. "The key happened to turn."

"You turned it," says Mattie.

"I was keeping you from making a foolish secret meeting."

"With Jack?"

"At the time I could not be certain who he
was."

"You called him Jack before he reached the gate," says
she.

It is surprising what people remember when they mean to
be disagreeable.

Jack held Mattie by the shoulders and searched her face.
"Jane told me you loved another man."

"I did not," says I quickly. "I let you infer
it."

Mattie's eyes grew round. "Another man?"

"She said you had gone to meet him."

"I said you had gone out, but not to meet
Jack. You was inside the church, and he was outside,
so strictly speaking—"

"Jane!" roared Uncle Peter.

He has always been impatient with exact language.

Jack's face darkened. "You told me she had changed."

"I said a girl can change. Everybody knows that."

"You said she wrote from habit."

"I offered butter sales as an example."

"And you said you had waited faithful for me!"

"No. I said there was more than one person who
waited for news from America. The whole village waited for
news. Your mother waited. Davy waited. I waited. That makes
considerably more than one."

Mrs. Tapp gave a squeak and covered it with her
saucepan. Several others began coughing. There was damp in the
churchyard, but not enough to account for it.

Mattie still stared at Jack. "Did you believe her?"

"For five minutes," says he miserably.

"What did you do?"

Here I thought Jack might show discretion. A gentleman should
not repeat private words, especially words spoken under emotional strain.

But Jack says, "I asked Jane to marry me."

Mattie let go of him.

The whole churchyard went silent.

Then Davy Crumb made a sound like a forge bellows
bursting, and turned his back. The two boys sat down
among the gravestones and stuffed their fists into their mouths.
Even Uncle Peter's eyebrows climbed.

"And I accepted," says I, for if a fact must
come out, it should at least come out accurately.

Mattie's face went white.

Jack seized her hands. "I was hurt and furious. I
thought you'd made a fool of me."

"And so you asked Jane?"

"One wife is as good as another, he said," I
added, because I saw no reason I should carry all
the blame.

Mattie pulled her hands away.

For one moment I thought my engagement might stand after
all. A man cannot marry a woman who refuses to
forgive him, and a promise is a promise.

Then Jack says, "No. That was a wicked, stupid thing
to say, and if you never speak to me again,
I'll have earned it. But I crossed the ocean for
you. I bought Mercer House for you. I remembered the
west room. I've loved you every day, Mattie, even when
I was too tired to eat. I believed Jane because
I was afraid—afraid you had found someone better."

"I could not," says Mattie.

"Well," says I, "you could, but you chose not to."

Nobody attended.

Mattie began crying, naturally. Jack wiped the dusty smear from
her cheek with his handkerchief. Then she touched his face
as if she had to prove he was solid, and
says, "I wore the brown dress."

"I see that."

"You always liked it."

"I liked you in anything."

That is the sort of remark men make before marriage.

Then they embraced again, and the villagers cheered, though what
business it was of theirs I cannot say.

Uncle Peter came forward. "Have you got the two hundred
pounds?"

Jack laughed. "I have two thousand and more, sir, besides
my share in the works."

"Then you have my blessing."

"You said I needed enough to keep a wife through
a bad winter."

"With two thousand, you may keep her through several. Though,"
Uncle Peter added, looking at Mattie's dusty dress, "she never
was expensive."

I considered this unfair. Mattie might not care for expense,
but she did not know how to prevent it. Only
the week before, she had left cream where the cat
could reach it.

"What about my engagement?" says I.

The cheering stopped.

Jack turned to me. "There is no engagement."

"You asked. I accepted. There was witnesses near enough, if
the church walls don't count."

"I asked under a false belief you created."

"A belief is a belief."

"You lied to me."

"I did nothing of the kind. I was careful not
to."

"That makes it worse," says Uncle Peter.

Now, I put it to you: how can avoiding a
lie be worse than telling one? There is no satisfying
some people.

Mattie came toward me. Her face was wet, and for
a moment I thought she meant to strike me, though
she had never struck anything larger than a moth.

"Why, Jane?"

I could have answered in several ways. I might have
said I wished to test Jack's constancy, which clearly needed
testing. I might have said Mattie's secret meeting was improper.
I might have mentioned Mercer House, the bank money, the
gold chain, or the west room, though those details would
have been misunderstood.

So I says, "I acted for the best."

"For whose best?" says she.

Some questions are mere traps.

Uncle Peter sent Mattie and Jack ahead to the farm,
where he said they should have supper and discuss wedding
dates. The villagers followed without invitation, because news travels best
on its own feet. Mrs. Tapp went to fetch a
pudding; the baker promised buns; Davy said there ought to
be ale.

I started after them.

Uncle Peter blocked the gate.

"Not you."

"I live at High Ash."

"You may remain here till you have considered what you
done."

"I have considered it already."

"That," says he, "is what worries me."

He took the church key from my hand and gave
it to Mr. Page. Then he went off, leaving me
among the graves while everybody else hurried toward lights, food,
and celebration.

It was chilly by then. I sat on the low
wall for a while and listened to the bell's last
trembling die away. Through the trees I could see Mercer
House on the ridge, with sunset still caught in its
upper windows. The west room, no doubt.

Mattie married Jack six weeks later.

I was not asked to be bridesmaid. Uncle Peter said
he could not trust me near a church door. That
was a poor joke, but everybody laughed.

Mattie wore white, though cream would have suited her colouring
better, and Jack gave her a pearl necklace that must
have cost thirty pounds. She scarcely looked at the pearls.
She looked at him. Such waste is painful to witness.

They live at Mercer House now. Jack's mother has the
south cottage, warm and dry, and Uncle Peter goes to
dinner every Sunday. Mattie has two maids, yet still makes
Jack's favourite blackberry jam herself, which proves that money cannot
give a woman sense.

As for me, Uncle Peter did not turn me out,
though he keeps the church key hung where I cannot
reach it without a chair. He no longer lets me
take the butter money to market, which is unjust, for
I never stole a farthing. Jack speaks civil when we
meet. Mattie has forgiven me, she says, but people who
forgive you generally remember the matter at every convenient opportunity.

And what did I gain by it? Nothing. That ought
to show I was not self-seeking.

They say all's well that ends well. Mattie got Jack,
Jack got Mattie, Uncle Peter got a rich son-in-law, and
the village got enough talk to last through winter. Even
Mr. Page got a new lock for the church, paid
for by Jack.

Only I was excluded from supper that night.

But I had had all the supper I wanted.

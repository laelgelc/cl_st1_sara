### A Christmas Eve in the Old House

It is now more than forty years since the Christmas
of which I am about to speak; yet, whenever the
first snow lies blue in the twilight, and the bells
of a passing sledge are heard beyond the windows, I
see again the narrow streets of old Christiania, the black
gables, the lamps swinging in the wind, and one illuminated
pane upon which I looked with exceedingly little Christian resignation.

I was then a newly commissioned lieutenant, and possessed, besides
my sword, a pair of epaulettes of which I was
secretly vain, a moustache scarcely visible except in strong sunshine,
and that cheerful confidence in my own consequence which belongs
to the age of twenty-one. Unfortunately, I had also possessed
a fever. It had left me pale, weak in the
knees, and subject to such sudden changes of spirits that
in the morning I regarded life as a glorious campaign,
while by four o'clock I was prepared to believe myself
abandoned by mankind.

My father's house lay nearly seven Norwegian miles from the
town. I had intended to go home three days before
Christmas, but the regimental surgeon, who had a heart of
iron where young officers were concerned, forbade the journey.

"You may eat," said he, "but not too much; drink,
but very little; walk, but not far; sleep, if you
can; and, above all, remain where you are."

He then went away to dine with a family at
Aker, leaving me to practise these negative virtues alone.

I lodged in an ancient house which stood in a
street now so altered that I should hesitate to point
out the spot. The lower storey was of stone, with
small arched windows; the upper was built of dark timber,
bulging outward as though it had grown weary of standing
upright. A gateway, strong enough for a fortress, opened upon
a paved yard, where an old pump, two wood-sheds, and
a pear-tree of fabulous age maintained a melancholy existence. There
were deep rooms, crooked passages, unexpected steps, presses built into
the walls, and a garret extending over the whole house,
full of broken spinning-wheels, worm-eaten chests, portraits without names, and
names without portraits.

My landladies, Miss Mette and Miss Cicely Hagerup, were sisters,
both advanced in years, though each spoke of the other
as "the elder one." They had caps like small snowdrifts,
black silk mittens, and countenances which united benevolence with a
certain ceremonious dignity. Miss Mette was tall and moved slowly;
Miss Cicely was round, quick, and perpetually losing her spectacles.
They had inhabited the house since the reign of King
Christian VII, and seemed to me scarcely less a part
of it than the carved chimney-piece or the Dutch tiles
around the stove.

Christmas Eve came with a north-west wind. Snow swept along
the street in white smoke; the sky darkened before three;
doors banged, bells rang, servants hurried past with baskets, and
every window appeared to promise the happiness denied to me.
I had received no letter from home. The post had
been delayed, the roads were said to be heavy, and
I pictured my father, mother, sisters, brothers, dogs, horses, servants,
and even the old red cow, assembled without me.

I sat at my window in a military cloak, though
the stove was hot, and indulged in those black reflections
which are most impressive when one is young, convalescent, and
certain of an audience in one's own breast.

At half-past five there came a gentle knock.

"Enter!" I cried, in a tone intended to indicate that
sorrow had made me stern.

Miss Cicely put her cap around the door.

"Lieutenant," said she, "my sister and I cannot permit you
to remain alone upon Christmas Eve."

"You are very kind, Miss Cicely; but I fear I
should be poor company."

"Then you will suit us perfectly, for we are all
poor company until supper."

She disappeared before I could frame another objection; and presently
Miss Mette entered, carrying a wax candle.

"You must come," she said. "The children are here, and
Mother Skau, and my cousin Madame Lange. We have porridge,
cakes, a little wine, and a pork roast which Cicely
declares is too large, though I consider it scarcely respectable.
Put on your better coat, Lieutenant. Christmas Eve should not
find a man sulking in his chamber."

There was no resisting this. Ten minutes later I followed
her through the passage to the best parlour.

The room made a picture which has remained in my
memory when many scenes of greater magnificence have vanished. A
fire burned upon the hearth; candles stood before the mirror
and upon the old square piano; red apples, nuts, and
small gilt baskets hung upon a fir-tree in the corner;
and over the polished table was spread a cloth white
enough, as Miss Cicely afterward observed, to put the snow
to shame. The walls bore portraits of deceased Hagerups—pastors, magistrates,
merchants, and one captain who had gone down with his
vessel off Jutland, though, to judge from his portrait, no
storm should have had courage to approach him.

Three children belonged to Madame Lange: Elise, aged eleven; Henrik,
aged nine; and little Andreas, who had reached six, but
denied it whenever the statement appeared likely to deprive him
of a privilege. Madame Lange herself was a mild widow
with smooth hair and a voice which made even reproof
sound apologetic.

Mother Skau, who was neither our mother nor, so far
as I know, any Skau's mother, sat close to the
stove. She had once been nurse in the Hagerup family,
and was now a stout, red-cheeked old woman, wrapped in
so many shawls that she resembled a parcel sent from
the country. On the table beside her stood a snuff-box,
a glass of currant wine, and a saucer containing sugared
almonds, all three of which she visited in regular order.

"Here is the Lieutenant!" cried the children.

"Not so loud," said Madame Lange.

"Let them shout," said Mother Skau. "Noise keeps away what
ought not to come. Sit here, Lieutenant, where I can
see whether the fever has left your eyes. H'm! Pale
as candle-grease, but that may be the uniform."

I sat down among them; and before many minutes had
passed, the children, who possessed the blessed art of regarding
every grown man under thirty as a possible conspirator, had
persuaded me to cut paper soldiers, mend a wooden horse,
and hide three nuts in my pockets for purposes which
were never explained.

Outside, the wind cried in the chimney. The old house
responded with creaks, taps, and long sighs from distant passages.
There was no sound which Miss Mette could not attribute
to shrinking timber, nor any which Mother Skau could not
attribute to something less natural.

"That was the garret door," said Elise, when a dull
blow shook the ceiling.

"The garret door is bolted," replied Miss Mette.

"Then," said Mother Skau, taking snuff, "it is some one
who does not require doors."

The children drew closer.

"Tell us a story," said Henrik.

"No," said Madame Lange.

"Yes," said Andreas.

"Not a frightening one," Madame Lange added.

"A very frightening one," said Elise.

"And one that is true," Henrik insisted.

"All good stories are true in one place or another,"
observed Mother Skau.

Miss Cicely, who had found her spectacles upon her own
forehead, folded her hands.

"Perhaps," said she, "we might begin with the house-brownie. There
is nothing alarming in a brownie if he is properly
treated."

"There is," said Mother Skau, "if he is not."

The children turned at once to Miss Cicely, and she
began.

"My grandmother's father—I believe it was her father, though it
may have been her uncle—owned a farm in Gudbrandsdal. The
farm was called Lower Hov, unless it was Upper Hov;
but that does not signify, for the brownie knew the
difference even if I do not. He lived in the
barn, behind the great beam, and was no higher than
Andreas there."

"I am high," said Andreas.

"So was the brownie, for his kind. He wore a
grey jacket, red cap, leather breeches, and wooden shoes. No
one saw him often, but every one saw his work.
If a cow loosened herself in the night, he tied
her again; if the horses lacked hay, he pulled it
from the loft; and if a lazy servant left the
stable dirty, the brownie flung the broom after him in
the morning."

"Did it hurt?" asked Henrik.

"If the servant deserved it."

"That is not an answer," said Henrik.

"It is the best answer you will get in this
world," said Mother Skau.

Miss Cicely continued. "On Christmas Eve a bowl of porridge,
with a large piece of butter in the middle, was
always set for him in the barn. One year the
dairymaid, who was a careless girl—Karen, or Maren, or perhaps
Anne—"

"She has begun badly," Mother Skau remarked.

"—put the butter at the bottom of the bowl, thinking
it made no difference. The brownie came after midnight, tasted
the porridge, and found no butter. He was so angry
that he went into the cowshed and struck the best
cow dead."

"Oh!" cried Elise.

"He then returned, ate deeper, and found the butter. At
once he was filled with remorse. 'I have been hasty,'
said he. 'The gude-wife did not forget me after all.'
So he went to the neighbouring farm, took their best
cow, and brought it back to replace the one he
had killed."

"That was stealing," said Henrik.

"Brownies," said Miss Cicely, "have an imperfect understanding of property."

"And did the neighbour come for his cow?" asked Andreas.

"He did; but the brownie seized him by his belt
and hung him upon the weathercock until morning."

Madame Lange looked doubtful. "Cicely, you said there was nothing
alarming in it."

"Nothing at all, my dear. The man was taken down
and lived for years."

"Crooked," added Mother Skau. "He was crooked ever afterward, if
it is the same story."

"It may not be."

"It is always the same story," said Mother Skau, "only
the farm changes."

The children laughed, and even Madame Lange consented to smile.
A gust of wind scattered snow against the panes like
a handful of dry peas.

"Tell another brownie story," said Elise.

"One which you know yourself," said Henrik.

"Nobody knows a brownie story himself," said Miss Mette. "That
would require being older than is convenient."

Mother Skau inhaled so heroic a pinch of snuff that
speech became impossible for nearly a minute. She coughed, waved
away assistance, drank currant wine, coughed again, and at last
announced that she knew a tale upon the word of
a woman incapable of falsehood.

"She was a cook at a place near Drammen," Mother
Skau began, "and the brownie there was a little black
fellow, hairy about the arms, with eyes like two sparks
under a kettle. He was civil to those who were
civil to him; but he could not abide quarrelling.

"Now, the farmer and his wife quarrelled from breakfast until
bedtime. If he said the sun shone, she fetched an
umbrella; if she said the porridge was hot, he put
on his mittens. The brownie bore it for seven years.
Then, on the eighth Christmas Eve, while they were shouting
over a dish of sausages, he came down from the
loft with his red cap over one ear.

"'I am leaving,' said he.

"'Good riddance!' cried the farmer.

"'Take the cat with you!' cried the wife.

"The brownie made no reply, but carried his little chest
to a farm two miles distant. The servant who drove
the cart had gone scarcely half-way when he heard a
voice from inside the chest:

"'How far have we come?'

"'As far as the mill bridge,' said the servant.

"Then a tiny voice under the cart answered:

"'I am coming too.'

"It was the other brownie—the brownie of the new farm—hanging
underneath by his fingers.

"'Lor' bless and preserve us!' cried the servant. 'Then there
will be two brownies where there should be one.'

"'No,' said the first. 'You take my place, and I
shall take yours.'

"So they exchanged farms. But the brownie who went to
the quarrelsome house lasted only three days. On the fourth
morning both brownies met upon the bridge, each carrying his
chest.

"'Can you endure them?' asked one.

"'No,' said the other. 'Can you?'

"'No.'

"They sat upon the railing and considered. At last they
agreed to return together and cure the farmer and his
wife. That night, when the quarrel began, one brownie took
the husband by the hair, the other took the wife
by the heels, and they beat them together like two
rugs until neither had breath to speak.

"After that there was peace in the house."

"Were they killed?" asked Andreas.

"Not at all. They had eleven children afterward."

"That," Madame Lange said, "is not a story I should
have chosen."

"But it contains a moral," replied Miss Mette.

"All beatings contain morals when other people receive them," said
Mother Skau.

At this point Miss Cicely brought round a plate of
thin cakes flavoured with aniseed. We ate, and the old
clock in the corner struck seven, pausing so long between
the strokes that Andreas counted nine. The fire settled with
a soft crash; shadows moved behind the high-backed chairs, and
the painted captain over the piano appeared to advance one
polished boot from his frame.

"Now trolls," said Henrik. "Brownies are too small."

"Yes, trolls!" cried Elise. "A great one with three heads."

"Seven," said Andreas.

"Three is more probable," observed Miss Mette.

"Nothing about trolls is probable," said Madame Lange.

"That is why they are worth hearing about," I ventured;
and, having now forgotten both my loneliness and my fever,
I offered to relate a story told to me by
Thor, my father's old servant.

The children composed themselves at once.

"Once upon a time," I began, "there was a charcoal-burner
named Halvor, who lived at the edge of a forest
with his wife, a white goat, and a yellow dog.
Whether he had children I cannot say; Thor put in
two when he told the story on Sunday and took
them out again on Monday. Halvor was poor, but he
owned a gun, a sledge, and an old brown bear
which he had captured as a cub."

"People do not own bears," said Henrik.

"This man did. He had named it Bishop."

"Why?"

"Because it sat solemnly and disliked being disturbed at dinner.

"One Christmas Eve Halvor set out to carry the bear
to a gentleman who wished to buy it. Snow came
on heavily, and when darkness fell he saw a light
at a farmhouse. The farmer stood at the door wringing
his hands.

"'Can I lodge here?' asked Halvor.

"'You may,' replied the farmer, 'but you will get neither
food nor rest. Every Christmas Eve a company of trolls
comes down from the mountain. They eat our supper, drink
our ale, break the furniture, pinch the servants, and dance
on the roof. We are leaving before they arrive.'

"'I shall remain,' said Halvor, 'if I may put Bishop
under the stove.'

"The farmer agreed, and fled with his household. Halvor chained
the bear in the kitchen, lay down behind the woodpile,
and waited.

"At midnight the door burst open. In came the trolls:
tall trolls, short trolls, trolls with noses like ladles, trolls
with tails like ropes, and one old troll-wife whose three
heads contradicted one another. They filled the room and demanded
Christmas fare. One emptied the porridge-pot; another swallowed the ham
whole; a third drank ale from the butter churn."

Andreas sighed with admiration.

"Presently the smallest troll saw the bear's nose sticking from
under the stove.

"'Oh, what a pretty cat!' he cried. 'May I feed
it sausage?'

"Before any one could stop him, he thrust a forkful
against Bishop's nose. The bear sprang out, broke its chain,
and seized the troll by the breeches. The troll screamed;
the three-headed wife screamed in three different keys; and the
entire company rushed for the door. Bishop followed them across
the yard, scattering trolls into snowdrifts, trees, and the frozen
duck-pond.

"The next Christmas Eve, as the farmer stood in his
doorway, he heard a voice call from the mountain:

"'Does your great cat still live?'

"'Yes!' shouted the farmer. 'And she has seven kittens, each
twice as large as herself!'

"'Then we shall not come!' cried the troll; and none
ever did."

Andreas clapped. Elise declared that she had heard the same
story, but with a polar bear; Henrik maintained that the
farmer's assertion about the kittens was a lie and ought
not to have succeeded.

"It was a military stratagem," I told him.

Miss Mette nodded graciously. "That makes it quite respectable."

Mother Skau, however, sniffed. "Trolls are not so easily cheated."

"You have known some?" I asked.

"I have known people with worse manners."

She then related, without waiting to be pressed, how a
fiddler had once been carried into a mountain and compelled
to play for a troll wedding. The bride was as
long as a church aisle, with a silver crown and
a cow's tail hidden beneath her green gown; the bridegroom
wore a red waistcoat and had moss growing from both
ears. Their guests drank molten gold, ate roasted stones, and
danced until sparks flew from their heels.

"The fiddler played," said Mother Skau, "because what else could
he do? He played a spring dance, a halling, and
a tune which his grandfather had learned from a waterfall.
At last an old troll offered him a drinking horn.

"'Drink, little man.'

"But the fiddler suspected it was no wholesome brew. He
threw the contents over his shoulder, and wherever the drops
fell, the rock smoked.

"'You have spilt my ale,' said the troll.

"'My hand slipped,' said the fiddler.

"'Then your head shall slip next.'

"Just at that moment the church bells rang for Christmas
morning. The mountain split open; all the trolls shrieked and
vanished; and the fiddler found himself sitting in the snow
with the drinking horn in his hand. He carried it
to the king's treasury, where it remains."

"Have you seen it?" asked Henrik.

"I have seen a horn."

"Was it that one?"

"They said it was."

"Who said?"

"A man at the door."

Henrik shook his head with the severity of a judge.
Mother Skau rewarded his scepticism by pinching his ear.

Miss Mette now suggested a song, perhaps because Madame Lange
had begun to look anxiously at the children. We sang
two verses of a Christmas hymn. Miss Cicely played the
piano, missing certain notes with such confidence that they appeared
to be at fault; and for a little while the
room was filled with living voices instead of trolls. Yet,
when the last chord died, the wind returned more loudly
than before, descending through the chimney with a long, hollow
murmur.

"What about Peter Gynt?" Elise asked.

"Which Peter Gynt?" said Miss Cicely.

"The hunter," replied the child. "The one who saw the
invisible beasts."

"There have been many Peter Gynts," Mother Skau declared. "Too
many, if half their tales are true."

Miss Mette knew one. She settled more upright in her
chair and spoke with the grave precision of a person
reading from an invisible family chronicle.

"Peter Gynt was a hunter who feared neither man nor
beast, because, as he said, men made too much noise
and beasts knew better than to attack him. One autumn
evening he was crossing the mountains above Vågå. The mist
lay so thick that he could not see the point
of his gun. He had lost the track, his food
was gone, and the night promised frost.

"As he stumbled among the stones, his foot struck something
soft.

"'Take care!' said a voice.

"Peter stopped. He saw no one.

"'Who are you?'

"'I am a crooked horned buck,' answered the voice, 'and
you have trodden upon my ear.'

"Peter felt around and found nothing. He walked on. Soon
he struck another invisible body.

"'Take care!' cried a second voice. 'You have kicked my
grandmother.'

"'How many of you are here?' Peter asked.

"'As many as the hills have stones.'

"Then the mist lifted a little, and he perceived—not with
his eyes, perhaps, but with that other sight which sometimes
comes to lonely travellers—that the whole mountain was covered with
beasts. There were bucks as large as barns, birds with
iron feathers, cats whose whiskers swept the valleys, and a
serpent curled three times around the highest peak. None could
be seen plainly; but the air bent around them, and
their breathing rolled through the fog.

"'Which way leads to Vågå?' Peter called.

"A voice behind him said, 'Straight through my left eye.'

"Another said, 'Under my tongue.'

"A third said, 'Between the teeth of my youngest child.'

"Peter considered. Then he took a silver button from his
coat, loaded his gun with it, and fired into the
air. At the shot, all the invisible beasts leaped. The
mountain trembled; stones rolled down the slopes; the mist tore
apart like a curtain. Peter saw the true path immediately
below him.

"But one enormous creature—whether buck, bird, or serpent he never
knew—followed him as far as the churchyard. He could hear
its claws upon the frozen ground: click, click, click. At
the gate he turned and made the sign of the
cross. The sound ceased, and in the morning they found
three marks upon the gatepost, burned so deep that no
plane could remove them."

"Are the marks there still?" whispered Elise.

"They were there in my grandmother's time."

"And now?"

"I have not lately examined the gate."

Mother Skau nodded. "That is the way of it. Young
people ask whether marks remain, but never trouble to go
and look."

I confess that Miss Mette's quiet voice, and especially her
account of the clicking claws, affected me more than the
trolls. I looked toward the dark window, where my own
pale reflection seemed to stand outside in the snow. My
recent illness had left the nerves too ready to take
alarm, though pride prevented me from admitting it.

The children, however, were delighted.

"Another!" they cried. "Tell us another! A ghost this time!"

"No ghosts," said Madame Lange firmly.

"A little ghost," pleaded Andreas.

"There are no little ghosts."

"How do you know?"

Madame Lange found no immediate reply.

Miss Cicely busied herself with the candles. "There is the
story of the dead congregation," she said, apparently to no
one.

Mother Skau put down her glass.

"That is no tale for children."

"Then it must be a very good one," said Henrik.

Madame Lange objected, but softly; the children promised not to
be frightened, which is the promise children most readily make
and least frequently keep; and I, who should have supported
the mother, asked Miss Cicely whether the occurrence had taken
place in Christiania.

"Not in the town," she replied, "but at a little
church north of here. My father knew the pastor who
heard it from the sexton's widow, and she was a
woman of unimpeachable character."

Mother Skau muttered that a good character was no defence
against the dead.

Miss Cicely lowered her voice.

"Many years ago there lived near that church an old
woman named Ingeborg. She was poor, but pious, and earned
her bread by weaving. On Christmas Eve she sat at
her loom later than was customary, for she had promised
a piece of linen to the pastor's wife. The moon
shone brightly upon the snow, and the church bell struck
eleven.

"Ingeborg thought it was the morning bell. The clock she
possessed had stopped; she was old and tired; and, seeing
light in the church windows, she supposed she had slept
through the night and that the Christmas service had begun.

"She put on her cloak, took her hymn-book, and hurried
across the fields. The church door stood open. Within, every
candle burned, and the pews were crowded.

"At first Ingeborg was ashamed to be late. She slipped
into the last pew and bowed her head. Yet something
seemed strange. Though the church was full, no one coughed,
moved a foot, turned a page, or whispered. The air
was cold enough to show every breath, but no breath
appeared.

"The pastor stood in the pulpit with his back toward
her. He wore an old-fashioned ruff and a black gown
powdered with dust. When he turned, Ingeborg recognised Pastor Rasmus,
who had been dead twenty years.

"She looked about her. In the nearest pew sat her
former mistress, dead before Ingeborg's marriage. Beside her was the
miller drowned in the spring flood; beyond him, a child
buried the previous summer. All the congregation were dead.

"Ingeborg could neither rise nor cry out.

"Then a woman sitting before her turned slowly. It was
Ingeborg's godmother, who had been dead since Ingeborg was a
girl.

"'Go,' the dead woman whispered. 'This service is not for
you.'

"Ingeborg's hands shook so that she dropped her hymn-book.

"'Go at once,' said the godmother. 'They have begun to
know you.'

"Now faces were turning throughout the church—not quickly, but one
after another, row after row. Their countenances were pale, their
eyes fixed, and earth clung to some of their garments.
The dead pastor lifted one hand.

"Ingeborg sprang up and ran.

"As she reached the porch, many cold fingers caught the
back of her cloak. She heard no footsteps behind her,
only the rustle of grave-clothes and the turning of hundreds
of heads. The cloak held. She tore herself free, crossed
the churchyard, and fell senseless at her own door.

"In the morning they found her there. When the sexton
unlocked the church for the true service, a strip of
her cloak lay inside the door; and every shred had
been torn as if by many hands."

No one spoke when Miss Cicely finished. The room had
become very still. Even the fire appeared to listen.

Little Andreas had drawn both feet up upon his chair.
Suddenly he gave a sharp scream.

"Something touched my leg!"

Madame Lange caught him; Elise shrieked because he had shrieked;
Henrik overturned a stool. Miss Mette raised the candle, and
Mother Skau, after peering beneath the table, discovered that Andreas's
right foot, numbed by its position, had descended upon his
left ankle.

"You are your own ghost," she told him.

This brought such general laughter that the dead congregation withdrew
at once to its proper distance. Andreas laughed loudest, though
with tears still shining on his cheeks.

The parlour door opened, and the maid announced supper.

Never has a procession from fear to appetite been more
rapid. We entered the dining-room, where a great bowl of
rice porridge steamed upon the table. There were pork, sausages,
cabbage, apples, preserved cherries, cakes of several shapes, and a
jelly which trembled at every movement as though it retained
the terrors we had escaped. Miss Mette gave us each
a spoonful of porridge; Miss Cicely watched to see who
found the hidden almond; Mother Skau declared the pork underdone
and ate three slices to prove it.

The almond came to me.

"Then the Lieutenant will be married before next Christmas!" cried
Elise.

"Impossible," said I. "I am under the surgeon's orders."

"Surgeons have nothing to do with it," Mother Skau answered.

We drank a modest glass of wine to Christmas, to
absent friends, and to the hope that all travellers might
find open doors. At this toast I thought of home.
My melancholy returned, but no longer bitterly. The kindness around
me had softened it; and when Miss Mette wished health
to my father and Miss Cicely health to my mother,
I could only bow, for my voice failed.

At ten the children were wrapped in cloaks and comforters.
Henrik carried a lantern, Elise held her mother's hand, and
Andreas, already half asleep, was borne away by the maid.
Mother Skau departed in a hired sledge, shouting from beneath
her shawls that we must leave porridge in the garret.
The bells receded along the street.

My landladies accompanied me to my door.

"You have not passed so sad a Christmas Eve after
all," said Miss Mette.

"No," I replied. "I shall remember it with gratitude."

"Do not remember the church story," said Miss Cicely. "I
ought not to have told it."

"Dream of the pork," advised Miss Mette. "It was more
substantial."

I wished them good night and went to bed.

The advice was excellent, but impossible to follow. Whether the
fever still lurked in me, whether the wine had been
stronger than Miss Mette believed, or whether porridge, pork, cakes,
jelly, trolls, brownies, and dead congregations should never be crowded
into one invalid upon the same evening, I cannot say.
I slept, but my sleep was a theatre in which
every tale returned wearing the garments of the others.

First I dreamed that I sat upon the brown bear,
Bishop, and rode through the streets of Christiania. He wore
my epaulettes, while I had only a red brownie's cap.
At every window stood a troll eating rice porridge. Each
troll had the face of the regimental surgeon and called
after me, "You may ride, but not far; eat, but
not much; wake, if you can!"

Then the bear carried me into a mountain where Mother
Skau, with three heads and a silver crown, presided over
a wedding. Miss Cicely played the fiddle; the children danced
with invisible beasts; and Miss Mette walked among them asking
whether it was Upper Hov or Lower Hov and finding
neither answer satisfactory.

The scene changed. I stood outside the old church. Snow
covered the graves, but beneath it I heard innumerable fingers
scratching upward. The door opened of itself. Within sat my
ancestors, dressed according to their portraits. My great-grandfather wore a
wig; an aunt who had died in infancy appeared as
a severe old lady; and the painted sea-captain from the
Hagerups' parlour occupied the pulpit.

He turned—and became my grandfather, who in life had been
the gravest of men.

"Too late!" he cried. "The Lieutenant is too late for
Christmas!"

At once every dead face turned toward me.

I tried to retreat, but cold fingers seized my cloak.
The congregation rose. My grandfather leaped from the pulpit, performed
a somersault with astonishing agility, and called for a dance.
The organ began a wild halling. Skeletons stamped upon the
pews; the drowned miller spun the troll-bride; the brownie beat
time with a dead cow; and Peter Gynt flew overhead
upon an invisible buck, shouting that the way home lay

In the midst of this uproar a peasant in a
sheepskin coat pushed toward me. His beard was covered with
frost; his hands were enormous; and he repeated, with dreadful
persistence:

"Are you well enough? Are you well enough? Are you
well enough?"

I struggled to wake, but the peasant shook me.

"Lieutenant! Lieutenant, sir! Are you well enough to travel?"

I opened my eyes.

Grey Christmas morning filled the room. The fire had died;
the candle had burned into its socket; and beside my
bed stood—not a dead peasant, troll, or invisible beast, but
Thor, my father's old servant, in his broad sheepskin coat.

For several seconds I stared at him.

Thor stared back, his weather-beaten countenance divided by a grin.

"Well, sir," he said, "the master said I was to
ask if you were well enough; and if you were
well enough, I was to bring you; but if you
were not well enough, I was not to bring you,
though I was to ask the doctor; and if the
doctor said—"

"Thor!"

"Yes, Lieutenant?"

"Are you real?"

He scratched his head. "I have been so until now."

He held out a letter. Upon the chair lay my
father's great fur travelling-coat, and from the street below came
the impatient ringing of sledge-bells.

The letter was brief. My father wrote that the roads
had improved, that he had sent Thor with our sure-footed
mare, Freya, and that, if I had no fever and
felt equal to the journey, I was to come home
without delay. My mother added three lines in the margin,
one of my sisters had drawn six exclamation marks beneath
them, and my youngest brother had written, "BRING HIM," in
capitals across the seal.

I believe I laughed like a child. I got out
of bed, discovered myself far stronger than I had been
the day before, and dressed while Thor repeated my father's
instructions in every possible arrangement.

Miss Mette and Miss Cicely came hurrying from their rooms,
each wearing a shawl and carrying a candle.

"What has happened?" cried Miss Cicely.

"I am going home!"

They comprehended at once. There followed coffee, bread, cold pork,
questions about the road, warnings against the wind, and such
active rejoicing that the whole house appeared to awaken. Miss
Mette wrapped a comforter around my neck; Miss Cicely slipped
cakes into my pocket; both insisted that I wear my
father's fur coat over everything else.

As Thor carried down my portmanteau, Miss Cicely said, "You
see, Lieutenant, the strange man who woke you had a
natural explanation."

"Entirely natural," I replied.

"But perhaps," Miss Mette added, "you had better not tell
Mother Skau. She prefers explanations of another kind."

The morning was pale and clear when we drove from
the gateway. Snow lay over the roofs and softened the
black façades; smoke rose straight from the chimneys; church bells
sounded across the town, solemn yet joyful. Here and there
a door opened upon children in their Christmas clothes, servants
carrying baskets, or old gentlemen saluting one another with elaborate
bows.

Freya trotted briskly. The runners sang upon the packed snow;
Thor cracked his whip without touching the mare; and the
city fell behind us.

Beyond the last houses the country opened white and shining.
Pines stood laden with snow; frozen rivers wound between the
hills; and every farm we passed had a little curl
of smoke above it and straw set out for the
birds. The air bit my cheeks, but beneath the fur
coat I was warm, and with each mile I seemed
to leave weakness behind.

Thor talked nearly all the way. He told me how
my mother had looked from the window each time bells
were heard; how my father had twice declared that no
sensible man would send a convalescent over winter roads and
had then ordered the sledge before daylight; how my sisters
had dressed the tree but refused to light it; and
how even the old dog, knowing that something was expected,
slept in the entrance hall.

At noon we climbed the last ridge. There, across the
white fields, stood home: the long yellow house, the dark
firs behind it, the barns and stable roofs, and smoke
rising from the kitchen chimney.

A figure at an upper window waved a handkerchief. Another
appeared at the door. Then the door flew open, and
the entire household poured out as though the house itself
could no longer contain them.

My father came first, walking with dignity for perhaps six
paces and then forgetting it. My mother followed without cloak
or bonnet. My sisters ran past them both; my brothers
shouted; the old dog bounded through the snow, barking so
violently that he rolled over.

Before Freya had stopped, I was pulled from the sledge
and embraced, questioned, scolded for being pale, praised for coming,
and carried almost bodily into the hall. The tree was
lighted that evening; the Christmas dinner was reheated with additions;
every one spoke at once; and I found, beneath my
plate, a paper star upon which my youngest sister had
written, "For our Lieutenant, who came after all."

Thus ended the most melancholy Christmas Eve of my youth
and began, I think, the happiest Christmas Day of my
life.

I have since travelled farther in worse weather, passed festivals
in foreign cities, and sat at grander tables than either
Miss Mette's or my father's. Yet no splendour has ever
effaced that old parlour, with its portraits and candles, the
children's eager countenances, Mother Skau's snuff-box, the wind in the
chimney, and the kindly voices saying, "Tell us another."

Nor, when I hear sledge-bells on a winter morning, can
I altogether prevent myself from expecting the door to open
upon Thor in his sheepskin coat, bearing a letter and
repeating, as though the fate of kingdoms depended upon the
answer:

"Are you well enough, Lieutenant? The master says, if you
are well enough, you are to come home."

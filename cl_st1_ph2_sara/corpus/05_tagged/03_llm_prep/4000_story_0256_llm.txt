# The Man Who Lost His Shadow

In the hot lands the sun has another manner than
with us; there it does not merely shine, it commands.
It sits over the white houses like a great fiery
magistrate, and every creature that can creep into a crack
does so without waiting to be summoned. The dogs lie
under the carts with their tongues out; the orange-sellers cover
their baskets with damp cloths; the water-carriers ring their little
brass bells, though no one has strength enough to call
to them; and the streets, which before sunrise are full
of asses, merchants, children, soldiers, beggars, priests, funerals, flower-girls, and
gentlemen who think themselves too important to perspire, become at
noon as empty as a church on a week-day.

Into one of those countries there came a learned young
man from the cold lands. He had read much about
the true, and the good, and the beautiful; he had
written a little concerning them also, though no one had
paid him for it, and scarcely anyone had read it.
Yet he was young, and when one is young one
can live upon hope almost as comfortably as upon roast
meat—at least for a time.

He lodged in a tall house where the rooms had
marble floors and green shutters. His chamber looked across a
narrow street toward another house, old and silent, with a
balcony of wrought iron and flowers which no hand appeared
to water. By day its shutters were closed; by night,
when all else was dark, a pale radiance shone from
within.

The learned man could not sleep on account of the
heat. He sat upon his balcony in his dressing-gown, with
a wet cloth round his head, and watched the opposite
house.

"What can be there?" said he. "No one enters, and
no one comes forth; yet the flowers blossom, and there
is light behind the curtains. Perhaps some old lady resides
there, who fears the sun. Perhaps a poet! Perhaps Poesy
herself!"

At that moment music sounded from the house—not music played
upon violin or flute, but something like the whisper of
a forest heard in a dream. The flowers upon the
balcony lifted their heads. The learned man also lifted his;
but then the music ceased, and a young maiden, so
bright that he could not distinguish her face, passed through
the illuminated saloon and disappeared.

"Poesy!" cried he. "In truth, it must be she!"

Now a lamp burned behind the learned man, and therefore
his shadow was cast upon the wall opposite. It stretched
all the way across the street; its head touched the
strange balcony, and one of its long black arms lay
directly upon the half-open door.

The learned man looked at it and laughed.

"Thou art nearer to Poesy than I," said he. "Go
in, then! Make thyself useful for once. Look about thee,
and return to tell me what thou hast seen."

He nodded to his shadow, and the shadow nodded also.
He bent his body, and it bent its body. He
waved his hand; it waved its hand. Then the curtain
in the opposite house stirred, the door opened a little
wider, and the shadow slipped through.

At least that was what the learned man thought he
saw.

"Do not remain until morning!" he called.

The door closed. The music sounded once more, exceedingly faint
and far away.

The learned man waited. He went from the balcony into
the chamber, and from the chamber to the balcony. He
coughed; he stamped; he stood before the lamp and moved
his arms. No shadow answered him.

"That is a fine jest!" said he. "I sent it
over in play, and now it takes the matter seriously."

He went to bed, but he slept badly. In the
morning the sun stood behind him as he walked, and
he looked before him upon the pavement.

There was no shadow.

He turned, jumped, bowed, and walked sideways. It was no
use. His shadow had truly departed.

This vexed him greatly, for a man may lose an
umbrella in the hot lands and be thought absent-minded; but
if he loses his shadow, people suppose there is something
wrong with his substance.

He therefore remained indoors during the brightest hours. At dusk
he went out, keeping close to walls and awnings. Once
a cake-seller looked at his feet with such astonishment that
the learned man hid behind a basket of honey-cakes until
the fellow had passed. Another time two children followed him,
crying, "The thin gentleman has forgotten something!" and he was
obliged to give them a coin to forget it also.

After some weeks a faint grey border appeared at his
heels. It was no larger than the shadow of a
sparrow. Day by day it increased; it learned, as it
were, the shape of his boots, his coat, his arms,
and lastly his head. Before he left the country he
possessed a new shadow, though it was at first rather
delicate and did not venture far from him.

"That is well," said the learned man. "One must not
grieve forever over an unfaithful servant."

He returned to the cold lands, where fogs hung over
the roofs and the sun was modest enough to appear
only when invited. There he wrote books concerning the true,
and the good, and the beautiful. They were very serious
books. One professor praised the footnotes but doubted the conclusions;
another praised the conclusions but objected to the footnotes; a
newspaper declared that the work deserved attention, which meant that
no one on the newspaper had read it. The books
were placed in libraries, and dust—which is an impartial critic—settled
upon them.

Years and days passed away.

The learned man grew older, but not richer. His coat
became shiny at the elbows, his boots became philosophical concerning
rain, and his new shadow grew strong and respectable. It
followed him faithfully; but perhaps because it had been born
in later years, it was never quite so long or
so lively as the first.

One evening, when the learned man sat writing an essay
to prove that goodness is its own reward, there came
a gentle knock at the door.

"Come in!" said he.

A stranger entered.

He was remarkably well dressed. His black coat fitted him
without a wrinkle; his linen was fine enough for a
bishop; a heavy gold chain crossed his waistcoat, and rings
sparkled upon his fingers. He carried a silver-headed cane and
wore polished boots which made the learned man instinctively draw
his own beneath the chair.

The stranger was very thin; but thinness, when clothed by
an excellent tailor, is often mistaken for distinction. His face
was pale and smooth, his hair black, and his eyes
had a peculiar habit of looking not at a person,
but around him—as though searching the wall for some keyhole
that had been forgotten.

"Whom have I the honor of speaking?" asked the learned
man.

The stranger smiled.

"I expected thou wouldst not know me."

The learned man started. No stranger of quality had addressed
him with the familiar "thou" since he was a boy.

"Have we met?"

"We have been nearer than brothers."

"Nearer?"

"We have lain upon the same floor, climbed the same
staircase, and stretched ourselves beneath the same sun."

The stranger turned about, and the learned man saw—yes, sure
enough!—that he cast no shadow.

"Good heavens!" cried the learned man. "Thou art—"

"Thy old shadow," said the visitor. "The first one. I
have come to pay my respects."

The learned man sprang up, knocking over the inkstand.

"This is marvellous! Sit down—nay, forgive me! Does a shadow
sit? But thou art no longer—thou hast flesh, clothes, jewels!"

"And money," said the shadow, seating himself. "Do not omit
money. Clothes may deceive, jewels may be borrowed, but money
convinces everyone."

"Tell me everything!" cried the learned man. "What happened when
thou enteredst the house opposite?"

"Everything happened."

"And what didst thou see?"

"Everything."

"And who dwelt there?"

"Poesy."

"I knew it!" said the learned man, and his eyes
shone. "Speak! Was she beautiful? Was the place filled with
divine forms? Did the eternal reveal itself in earthly symbols?
Didst thou behold the sources of art?"

The shadow leaned back and crossed one elegant leg over
the other.

"I stood in the outer chamber," said he.

"Only the outer chamber?"

"Only! Thou speakest like a man who has never been
admitted even to the courtyard. From that outer chamber I
saw enough. The light nearly destroyed me. Had I entered
the innermost saloon, I should have become transparent, and transparency
is fatal to one who intends to prosper."

"But what didst thou learn?"

"I learned my value. Until then I had supposed myself
merely thy shadow. In the presence of Poesy I discovered
that even a shadow may contain possibilities. I remained behind
the curtain by day; by moonlight I ventured forth. At
first I was as thin as smoke. I feared that
a chambermaid might sweep me away. But I watched, listened,
and waited."

"And Poesy?"

"She passed near me. That was enough. She did not
speak to me; persons of the highest rank often bestow
favor in that manner. Her radiance gave me substance—not all
at once, for such things must be managed with circumspection.
First I could bend without thy bending. Then I could
move from one wall to another. Next I learned to
curl myself beneath doors. Finally I acquired feet."

"Feet!"

"Feet are indispensable in society. After that came a body,
though a body is less important than a well-made coat.
I procured both. I allowed a beard to grow; no
one trusts a man whose chin suggests that he was
born yesterday. Then I went out into the world."

The learned man clasped his hands.

"And thou hast become wealthy by poetry?"

"By knowledge," said the shadow.

"Ah! By teaching?"

"By knowing what ought not to be known."

The shadow tapped the side of his nose.

"In every house," he continued, "there is a room where
the lamp burns late and the curtains are closely drawn.
I visited such rooms. I lay behind sofas; I lengthened
myself along passages; I stood between husband and wife, minister
and secretary, creditor and debtor. I heard the pious man
count dishonest money, the patriot sell a letter, the critic
praise in private what he condemned in print, and the
lady with the marble virtue whisper to a gentleman by
the conservatory door."

"That was shameful!" cried the learned man.

"Yes," said the shadow, "very shameful indeed; and therefore extremely
valuable."

"Didst thou expose them?"

"Sometimes. But exposure is a coin which may be spent
only once; silence pays interest. I never said, 'Give me
money, or I shall speak.' That would have been coarse.
I merely called upon them, looked thoughtful, and said, 'What
an agreeable evening we had on Thursday!' They understood. A
statesman found me employment; a banker advised me in investments;
a duchess introduced me at court. Soon people said I
was discreet."

"Discreet!"

"That is what society calls a man who knows its
secrets and has not yet published them."

The learned man walked to and fro.

"This knowledge must have made thee unhappy."

"On the contrary. It made others unhappy, and that is
often a source of influence. I grew rich. Men bowed
to me because they feared I might remember them; women
smiled because they hoped I had forgotten them. I made
myself little before the great, and they called me modest;
I made myself great before the little, and they called
me powerful. Now I travel. I keep servants. I have
been invited to dine with persons whose ancestors would not
have permitted thee to stand in their entrance hall."

"That may be," said the learned man sadly, "yet I
possess truth."

The shadow looked around the poor room: at the patched
curtain, the cold stove, the stack of unread books, and
the supper consisting of bread and an apple.

"Does truth always dine so plainly?"

"The world is ill," said the learned man. "But I
write against its sickness. I write of the true, and
the good, and the beautiful."

"Those are excellent things," said the shadow. "I have seen
them mentioned in funeral speeches."

He rose.

"I must go. Here is my card. If thou shouldst
require anything—within reason—send to me. But one matter: thou must
address me as 'you.' I called thee 'thou' because of
our former intimacy; that was sentiment, and sentiment should never
be made a precedent."

The learned man flushed.

"Thou wert my shadow."

"Formerly. Circumstances alter grammar."

"Then you," said the learned man with effort, "will perhaps
call me 'you' also."

The shadow smiled.

"You are sensitive. Very well—when we are alone."

He put on his hat, bowed, and departed. The learned
man watched from the window. A magnificent carriage waited below.
The shadow entered it, and two servants sprang up behind.

The learned man's new shadow lay long across the floor
in the candlelight. It trembled a little, though there was
no wind.

Years and days passed away, and the learned man continued
to write. He wrote that beauty without goodness is an
empty mask, that knowledge without truth is a lantern in
the hands of a thief, and that a man must
never surrender the inward for the outward. His words were
noble, clear, and entirely unprofitable.

People said, "He is a good man, but impractical." That
is a sentence by which the world forgives itself for
neglecting a good man.

He became thin; then thinner. His cough remained after winter
had gone. The physician felt his pulse, asked whether he
had means, and, learning that he had none, prescribed fresh
air in a tone which suggested that air also ought
to be paid for.

One morning a carriage stopped before the house, and the
former shadow entered. He looked younger than before, though perhaps
that was the work of his barber.

"You appear ill," said he.

"I am ill."

"You require travel."

"I cannot afford it."

"I can. I am going to a celebrated watering-place. There
are mineral springs, music, mountains, gaming tables, and many sick
persons who have money enough to make illness fashionable. You
shall accompany me."

The learned man hesitated.

"I will accept no payment for silence."

"Silence? My dear sir, who spoke of silence? You shall
be my companion."

"On equal terms?"

The shadow examined his gloves.

"In private, yes. But society is precise. It knows how
a prince should look, how a servant should stand, how
a widow should sigh, and how an author should be
ignored. It would be inconvenient to explain our history at
every inn."

"What do you propose?"

"Only that when others are present, you shall conduct yourself
as my shadow."

The learned man drew back.

"Never!"

"Do not be dramatic. You may walk behind me, sit
somewhat lower at table, carry no gold-headed cane, and answer
when addressed. In return you shall travel without expense, eat
well, and recover your health. At night, when the doors
are shut, we may converse as equals. You may even
call me 'thou,' if it comforts you."

"That is monstrous."

"It is economical."

"I was once thy master."

"And I was once flat. We have both improved."

The learned man refused. But fever came, and with fever
the room lengthened and tilted; the books on the shelves
whispered that principles are splendid bedfellows but poor nurses. Three
days later the shadow returned, and the learned man consented.

"Only for the journey," said he.

"Certainly," replied the shadow. "Everything permanent begins in that manner."

They travelled southward.

The shadow occupied the best seat in the carriage; the
learned man sat opposite, wrapped in a plain cloak. At
the inns the landlord bowed to the gold chain and
scarcely looked at the pale face. When they crossed frontiers,
officials examined the learned man's papers closely but saluted the
shadow without opening his passport. Money has a likeness which
is recognized in every kingdom.

The shadow called him "my good fellow" before servants and
"my learned friend" when alone. Yet even in private the
equality gradually diminished. At first they dined at the same
table; later the shadow said that appearances must be preserved
for practice. At first the learned man had a chamber
beside his; later it was above the stables, because the
shadow slept lightly and the learned man coughed.

"Thou promisedst—" the learned man began one evening.

"'You promised,' if you please," said the shadow. "The waiter
may be at the door."

"No one is there."

"One should cultivate correctness even without witnesses; witnesses have a
way of appearing."

The learned man looked at him long.

"Thou hast learned much from mankind."

"Everything," said the shadow. "I saw everything, and I know
everything."

At last they came to the watering-place.

It lay among blue mountains, with white hotels, green walks,
pavilions, fountains, flags, orchestras, bathhouses, doctors, confectioners, dancing-masters, flower-women, fortune-tellers,
invalid chairs, and healthy persons who complained more loudly than
the sick. At six in the morning a bell rang,
and everyone drank mineral water with faces of heroic disgust;
at noon they discussed their diseases; in the evening they
dressed magnificently to prove that death, if it came, would
find them in proper clothes.

Kings travelled there without crowns, bankers without ledgers, ladies without
husbands, and husbands without questions. It was said to be
a place for health, which was true if one possessed
enough money to be ill comfortably.

Among the visitors was a princess who ruled a small
but prosperous kingdom. She was learned after the royal fashion:
that is, many professors had told her what they knew,
and none had dared discover what she did not understand.
Yet she was not foolish. Her eyes were exceedingly sharp;
she could notice a false jewel at twenty paces, a
missing title in an introduction, or a wrinkle in a
courtier's smile. Her one misfortune was that she saw too
well, and therefore the physicians had advised the watering-place.

"Your Highness must encounter novelty," said the chief physician. "The
eye fatigues itself when it sees the same falsehoods daily."

The princess walked each evening upon the terrace. There she
noticed the shadow, who appeared in black cloth, with diamonds
at his throat and three decorations upon his breast. He
had purchased two of the decorations; the third had been
bestowed by a grateful minister whose letters he had not
published.

Behind him walked the learned man, quiet, pale, and plainly
dressed.

The princess looked once, twice, and a third time.

"That gentleman casts no shadow," said she to her lady-in-waiting.

The lady-in-waiting looked toward the ground.

"Perhaps it is the fashion in his country, Your Highness."

"Fashions alter sleeves, not sunlight."

The next evening the princess arranged to meet the stranger
near the fountain. She approached him with royal graciousness.

"You are newly arrived, sir."

"I arrived only that I might regret not having come
sooner," said the shadow, bowing.

That answer pleased her; it said nothing and sounded polished,
which is much esteemed at courts.

"You come from the cold lands?"

"I have come from all lands."

"And you travel alone?"

The shadow indicated the learned man.

"I have my shadow with me."

"Your shadow?"

"A singular creature, is he not? I have permitted him
to assume almost human form. It amuses me, and he
is useful. I have given him clothes, education, and a
certain independence, though one must not give a shadow too
much independence; it becomes melancholy."

The princess gazed at the learned man.

"He resembles a man extraordinarily."

"I spared no expense."

"But you yourself cast no shadow."

"Because mine walks behind me."

The princess was silent. She had never heard of such
a thing; but princesses encounter many wonders, and etiquette forbids
astonishment until rank has been established.

"Can your shadow speak?"

"Upon learned subjects, incessantly."

"Then I should like to question it."

The shadow bowed.

"You will oblige me by doing so. His answers may
entertain Your Highness; and if a shadow knows so much,
you may judge what the master knows."

That was wisely thought—at least the princess considered it so.

The following morning she sent for the learned man. He
entered her private saloon, where books stood behind gilded wire
and maps hung upon the walls. The princess sat beside
a window, and two ladies embroidered flowers which no botanist
would have recognized.

"I am told," said the princess, "that you are a
shadow."

"I am told so also," replied the learned man.

"Do you deny it?"

He looked toward the doors. A chamberlain stood there; beyond
him were guards. The shadow had warned him to preserve
appearances.

"I have had another condition," said he.

"Indeed? Your master claims to have made you humanlike."

"He has certainly made my condition strange."

The princess interpreted this as the modesty of a well-trained
shadow.

"What do you know?"

"A little concerning the laws by which men ought to
live, though very little concerning those by which they succeed."

"Explain the difference."

"The first ask what is true; the second, what will
be believed. The first ask what is good; the second,
what will be praised. The first seek beauty; the second
purchase its costume."

The princess leaned forward.

"That is cleverly said. What is truth?"

"That which remains when advantage has ceased speaking."

"And goodness?"

"To refuse profit from another's degradation."

"And beauty?"

"The light by which truth and goodness become lovable."

The princess reflected. No court philosopher had spoken so plainly,
for court philosophers know that definitions can be dangerous when
a sovereign recognizes herself in them.

"You are a remarkable shadow," she said.

"I was once a man of learning."

The ladies tittered; they supposed this a fanciful way of
speaking.

"And your master?" asked the princess. "What does he know?"

The learned man's face became troubled.

"He knows what men conceal."

"Only that?"

"In the world, Your Highness, that is often accounted everything."

The princess dismissed him, but she remained thoughtful.

"If the shadow speaks thus," said she, "the master must
indeed be extraordinary. It would be unreasonable for the lesser
to possess more wisdom than the greater."

Royal reason frequently begins with rank and works backward.

That evening she conversed with the shadow. She asked him
difficult questions concerning government, art, human nature, and the stars.

The shadow answered with great fluency.

Concerning government he said, "A wise ruler rewards loyalty before
merit, because merit may stand alone, while loyalty must be
fed."

Concerning art he said, "The best picture is that which
critics fear to condemn."

Concerning human nature he said, "Every man has a door,
though some keep the handle inside."

Concerning the stars he said, "They are exceedingly distant, and
therefore enjoy excellent reputations."

The princess smiled at every answer. When he did not
know a matter, he spoke ambiguously; when ambiguity failed, he
praised the question; and when praise was insufficient, he hinted
that the subject involved secrets of state. Thus he passed
for profound.

They danced together at the grand ball. The shadow moved
lightly, for dancing had always belonged to his nature. He
bent, lengthened, glided, and turned; he seemed everywhere and nowhere.
The princess had never known a partner who followed light
so perfectly.

The learned man stood by a pillar.

"If anyone had paid particular attention," the narrator may say,
"he would have observed that the learned man cast a
shadow, while the gentleman dancing with the princess cast none."
But no one paid particular attention. There were uniforms, jewels,
bare shoulders, medals, ostrich feathers, wax lights, mirrors, and music;
and when there is enough brilliance in a saloon, people
seldom look upon the floor.

The princess fell in love.

She did so circumspectly, for she was a reigning princess
and love must be examined by ministers. She asked the
shadow about his fortune.

"Considerable."

"His birth?" asked the chamberlain afterward.

"Obscurely illustrious," replied the shadow.

"His political opinions?"

"Adaptable."

"His reputation?"

"Men speak of him carefully."

"That indicates importance," said the chamberlain.

The ministers made inquiries. A banker declared the stranger's credit
excellent. A duke remembered dining with him. Three officials became
pale at the mention of his name and recommended him
warmly. A newspaper wrote that he possessed "those rare qualities
which silence envy and command universal respect," though the editor
could not have named a single quality. Thus the inquiry
proved entirely satisfactory.

The princess said to the shadow:

"I believe you understand me."

"I understand everyone," he replied.

"And perhaps you—esteem me?"

"I esteem Your Highness beyond expression."

She wished for love, but accepted esteem beyond expression; among
royal persons that often serves.

Their marriage was discussed.

That night the shadow entered the learned man's room. The
man sat by the open window, looking at the moon.

"Well," said the shadow, "fortune has exceeded even my expectations.
I am to marry the princess."

"I know."

"You do not congratulate me."

"I pity her."

"That is unnecessary. She will possess a husband whom everyone
respects."

"She will possess a lie."

"Most thrones possess several; one more will not burden hers."

The learned man rose.

"This must end. I have endured humiliation because I was
ill and weak. I have walked behind thee; I have
allowed strangers to mistake substance for shade. But I shall
not permit a kingdom to be deceived."

The shadow seated himself and examined a diamond upon his
hand.

"My good fellow, let us speak practically. When I become
king, you shall have a house, a pension, a library,
and the title of chief philosopher. You may write as
many books as you please concerning the true, and the
good, and the beautiful. They shall be printed at the
public expense in an edition so splendid that no one
will dare read them."

"I refuse."

"You shall dine from silver."

"I refuse."

"You shall have servants."

"I refuse."

"You may address me as 'thou' once every Sunday in
private."

"I tell thee, I refuse!"

The shadow's eyes narrowed.

"What do you desire?"

"To be acknowledged as the man, and thee as the
shadow."

"That cannot be arranged."

"It is the truth."

"My dear sir, truth is not a chair that may
be placed wherever there is room. At court, every object
has its appointed position. I am the royal bridegroom; you
are my shadow. The arrangement has been announced, discussed, investigated,
and approved. It is now more solid than fact."

"I shall go to the princess."

"And say what?"

"That thou wert born at my feet. That I sent
thee into the house of Poesy. That thou returnedst enriched
by secrets and made thyself master by fraud."

The shadow laughed, but there was no mirth in the
sound.

"She will think you mad."

"She has spoken with me. She knows I possess reason."

"Precisely. She believes my shadow possesses reason. That proves my
greatness. If the shadow is learned, what must the master
be? Every word you have spoken in wisdom has increased
her admiration for me."

The learned man became pale.

"Then wisdom itself is made thy accomplice."

"Wisdom is like a seal; it belongs to whoever holds
the document."

"I will speak nevertheless."

The shadow stood.

"Consider. I offer comfort, honor, leisure, and long life. All
I require is that you remain what society already believes
you to be."

"A shadow?"

"My shadow."

"I would rather die as a man."

"That," said the shadow softly, "may be arranged."

He rang the bell.

Two officers entered.

"This unfortunate creature is unwell," said the shadow. "He has
suffered from a fixed delusion. He imagines himself human and
believes me to be his shadow. Take him gently. He
must not be permitted to speak with Her Highness; agitation
would be fatal."

The learned man cried out, but the officers had received
orders. One pitied him, the other found him troublesome, and
together they removed him with perfect correctness.

The shadow went immediately to the princess. He appeared grave,
and a little moisture shone in his eyes; he had
placed it there with his handkerchief in the passage.

"A sorrow has befallen me," said he.

"My beloved, what is it?"

"My shadow has lost his reason."

"The learned one?"

"Alas, yes. Too much humanity has been dangerous for him.
He now believes that he is a real man and
that I am his shadow."

"How dreadful!"

"I blame myself. I clothed him too well; I educated
him beyond his station; I allowed him to speak freely.
Benevolence, when carried too far, becomes cruelty."

The princess pressed his hand.

"Can he be cured?"

The shadow sighed.

"The physicians fear not. Worse still, his madness has taken
an ambitious form. He declares that he will expose me,
claim my place, and prevent our marriage."

"That is treason."

"It is sickness," said the shadow generously. "We must not
call it treason."

"How noble you are!"

"I would forgive him. But if the people heard his
ravings, they might misunderstand. You know how readily the ignorant
confuse an assertion with a fact."

The princess walked to the window. Below, workmen were hanging
lamps for the wedding festivities.

"What must be done?"

The shadow bowed his head.

"I cannot advise Your Highness. My affection makes me weak."

"Then I must be strong."

She rang for the chief minister. He came quickly; chief
ministers sleep lightly when marriages and successions are in question.

"There is a madman in custody," said the princess. "He
believes himself to be a human being and the royal
bridegroom to be his shadow."

The minister blinked.

"A very dangerous delusion, Your Highness."

"He must not disturb the people."

"Certainly not."

"He must not suffer," said the princess.

"Certainly not."

"He must be dealt with quietly."

The minister bowed.

"That is the most merciful way."

And thus it was settled.

The learned man sat in a stone room with a
small barred window. Moonlight lay upon the floor, and beside
him lay his new shadow, thin and faithful. He spoke
to it.

"Thou hast remained with me," said he.

The shadow moved when he moved.

"I lost the first because I sent it where I
dared not go. I thought Poesy would make it noble.
Perhaps Poesy only gives power; the use of power comes
from what one already is."

The shadow lengthened toward the wall.

"I wrote of the true, and no one listened. I
wrote of the good, and they called it impractical. I
wrote of the beautiful, and they bound it in handsome
covers. Yet I thought that if I spoke clearly enough,
truth must triumph. I did not know that falsehood might
wear gold chains, dine with ministers, and arrive in a
carriage."

Footsteps sounded outside.

The learned man stood.

"Still, a man must speak it."

The door opened.

What occurred afterward was entered in no public record. The
minister reported that the unfortunate creature had died suddenly during
the night. The physician signed a paper. The gaoler received
a coin and remembered nothing. That is how a learned
man may be deprived of life with less commotion than
is required to cancel a banquet.

The wedding took place the next day.

Cannons fired from the walls; bells rang from every tower;
white horses drew the royal carriage; tradesmen hung carpets from
their windows; children scattered flowers; soldiers presented arms; poets composed
verses upon the union of Wisdom and Beauty; professors delivered
addresses; newspapers printed supplements bordered in gold. At night the
entire city was illuminated.

The shadow stood beside the princess upon the palace balcony.
He wore a crown, and the jewels upon his breast
flashed in the torchlight. The people shouted until they were
hoarse.

"Long live the king!"

He bowed.

Behind him rose a thousand lights, and before him lay
the crowded square. Yet even amid so much brightness he
cast no shadow, for he had none.

No one noticed.

The princess looked upon her husband with pride. The ministers
looked upon one another with relief. The poets looked toward
the treasury. The newspapers looked toward the public appetite. And
beneath a little earth outside the city lay the man
who had loved the true, and the good, and the
beautiful.

He had no monument. His books remained unsold. In time
their pages were used by shopkeepers to wrap spices and
soap, which at least gave them circulation.

As for the shadow, he governed long and prosperously. He
knew what everyone feared, and therefore everyone called him wise.
He made himself little before danger and great before weakness;
he praised virtue in public and purchased silence in private;
he rewarded appearances, decorated obedience, and established an academy for
the study of truth, whose members were forbidden to mention
inconvenient facts.

And whenever a young scholar spoke too earnestly of goodness,
the courtiers smiled and said:

"Excellent! His Majesty has a shadow who once spoke in
precisely that manner."

Then all laughed, for that was considered exceedingly witty.

Yes, the city was brightly illuminated, the bells rang, and
the people rejoiced. They had gained a king who understood
the world, and they had lost a man who understood
what the world ought to be.

That exchange pleased them very well.

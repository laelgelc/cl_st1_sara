The six married men had dined too well to speak
respectfully of marriage.

Coffee had been served, the brandy was passing from hand
to hand, and blue spirals of smoke ascended toward the
chandelier. Their host, who was to be married on the
following Thursday, listened with the uneasy smile of a traveller
receiving advice from men who had survived a dangerous country.

They had all been bachelors together ten years before. Now
they compared their captivity, praised their children, complained of household
expenses, and recalled with tenderness those vanished days when a
man could dine at midnight, sleep at noon, and account
to nobody for the condition of his waistcoat.

Only Pierre Letoile said nothing.

He sat smiling, a broad, well-fed man of forty, whose
wife was generally admitted to be the prettiest and most
agreeable woman of their circle.

At last the bridegroom pointed a cigar at him.

"You have no right to be silent, Pierre. You were
the fiercest enemy of marriage among us, and you are
the happiest husband."

"The two facts are connected," said Pierre.

"How?"

"I married against my will."

They laughed.

"You were carried to the church by gendarmes?"

"Very nearly."

"Then tell us."

Pierre filled his glass, stretched his legs toward the fire,
and began:

"You remember what I was at twenty-nine. I had three
principles: never borrow money, never fight before breakfast, and never
marry. The first two I sometimes neglected; the third I
considered sacred.

"I did not dislike women. On the contrary! I liked
them too much to attach myself to one. A wife
seemed to me a charming person transformed by law into
a tax collector, a magistrate and a customs officer. I
intended to remain free, grow fat, and die peacefully in
the arms of a devoted housekeeper.

"It was in that condition of wisdom that I went
to Normandy for the wedding of my cousin Henri.

"The whole countryside had been invited. There were farmers in
blue blouses, old women in great white caps, village girls
with red cheeks, municipal officers, two priests, a notary, a
colonel on half-pay, and enough cousins to populate a canton.
We ate beneath a tent erected in the courtyard because
no room in the house could contain us.

"I can still see that table. There were towers of
bread, yellow mountains of butter, hams shining beneath their jelly,
black sausages, cheeses as large as carriage wheels, ducks, chickens,
rabbits and enormous dishes whose contents nobody could identify but
which everybody ate. At each end stood a cask: one
gave red wine and the other golden cider.

"The dinner began at two in the afternoon. At six
we were still eating.

"Now, in Paris, one knows when one has drunk too
much, because the chairs grow uncertain and the faces become
affectionate. In Normandy there are no such warnings. The wine
persuades you to taste the cider, the cider sends you
back to the wine, and between the two you preserve
an appearance of perfect reason while losing reason itself.

"After dinner the dancing began. Fiddlers scraped upon their instruments
in a barn illuminated by torches. The flames smoked, the
girls laughed, the men stamped, and the floor trembled as
though a regiment were crossing a bridge.

"I danced with everybody—the bride, the bride's mother, the cook,
three farmers' daughters, and a widow who weighed, I am
certain, more than my horse. Each dance made me thirsty.
Each thirst required a glass. Each glass restored my strength
for another dance.

"Among the guests was Mademoiselle Claire Dumoulin, the colonel's daughter.
I had noticed her during dinner, principally because she contradicted
me.

"She was fair, straight, and rather tall, with clear gray
eyes and the frank manner of a young officer. She
rode, hunted, and spoke without lowering her voice. At that
time I preferred women who appeared to have no opinions
until I had supplied them. Mademoiselle Dumoulin had enough opinions
for a ministry.

"We danced twice.

"'You dance too violently,' she said.

"'Mademoiselle, everything should be done violently at a wedding.'

"'Even drinking?'

"'Drinking above all.'

"'Then you will be ill.'

"'Never.'

"She shrugged her shoulders, and I decided she was overbearing.
That was my whole judgment upon the woman with whom
I have since passed five years.

"At about two in the morning, my legs ceased to
obey the music. The dancers still bounded about me, red,
steaming and breathless; the torches sputtered; the fiddle had become
a shriek divided into little pieces; and I felt an
immense desire for bed.

"My room was on the second floor. I knew this
because my aunt had explained it to me three times.

"'You go up the large staircase,' she had said, 'cross
the landing, enter the left passage, and take the fourth
door on the right.'

"Nothing could be simpler.

"I ascended the staircase with care, holding the banister in
both hands. It was a long ascent. At moments the
steps rose to meet me; at others they withdrew suddenly
and left me suspended over an abyss. Nevertheless, I reached
the landing and congratulated myself.

"There the difficulties began.

"The lamps had gone out. Before me lay two passages,
one to the right and one to the left. I
remembered clearly that my room was in the left passage,
but I no longer knew whether the passage itself was
to the left or whether only my door was on
that side.

"I chose the right passage after mature reflection, because it
appeared on my left when I turned around.

"Then I began counting the doors.

"One.

"I touched it with my hand.

"Two.

"I embraced the wall.

"Three.

"I stopped to recover my balance.

"Four.

"The door was locked.

"This seemed an insult. I tried the next. It opened
silently.

"The room was dark. I could distinguish the pale square
of a window and the vague outline of a bed.
I advanced toward it, but halfway across the room my
knee encountered an armchair. The chair received me. I sat
down to consider whether the bed was worth the remainder
of the journey.

"It stood perhaps six feet away. Six feet! At sea
that is nothing; in a dark bedroom after a Norman
wedding it is an ocean.

"I removed one shoe. The effort exhausted me. I placed
the other foot upon a stool, folded my arms and
said, 'I shall rest for one minute.'

"I awoke to the explosion of a pistol.

"At least I believed it was a pistol. In fact,
it was the voice of Colonel Dumoulin shouting:

"'Scoundrel!'

"The shutters had been opened. Daylight filled the room. Before
me stood the colonel in his shirt sleeves, purple in
the face, with one fist raised. In the bed, her
hair spread over the pillow and the blankets drawn to
her chin, lay Mademoiselle Claire.

"I looked at her. I looked at him. I looked
at my stockinged foot upon the stool.

"Then I understood.

"I had slept beside his daughter's bed!

"'Colonel,' I said, 'there is a mistake.'

"His fist descended. I avoided it by falling backward with
the armchair. The noise brought the household. Doors opened, women
cried out, servants ran in, and within thirty seconds the
room contained my uncle, my aunt, the bridegroom, two cousins,
a maid, the colonel and myself. Mademoiselle Dumoulin had hidden
her face beneath the sheet.

"'He has dishonoured her!' roared the colonel.

"'I have done nothing of the kind!' I replied from
the carpet.

"'What are you doing in her bedroom?'

"'Sleeping.'

"'Why here?'

"'Because I thought it was my room.'

"There was a silence.

"You understand the perfection of my explanation. It was true,
reasonable and absolutely unbelievable.

"My uncle sent everyone away except the colonel, and dragged
me into his room. There he put on his spectacles
and assumed the dignity of a judge.

"'Tell me exactly what happened.'

"I told him.

"'You drank wine?'

"'Yes.'

"'And cider?'

"'Yes.'

"'How much?'

"'I cannot remember.'

"'Naturally. You danced with the young lady?'

"'Twice.'

"'You spoke to her?'

"'Certainly. One does not dance in silence.'

"He walked to the window.

"'Pierre, you must ask for her hand.'

"I jumped up.

"'Never! Never! I entered the wrong room. That is all.'

"'That is not all. You were found there at seven
in the morning.'

"'In an armchair!'

"'Without one shoe.'

"'Does a shoe constitute marriage?'

"'In these circumstances it may.'

"I declared that I would explain publicly. I would call
the servants, draw a plan of the passages, reconstruct my
journey, exhibit the armchair and, if necessary, repeat the experiment
under official supervision.

"My uncle listened patiently.

"'And who will believe you?'

"'Every sensible person.'

"'There is no sensible person where a young woman's reputation
is concerned.'

"The colonel entered at that moment. He had dressed, and
now looked even more dangerous because his coat was buttoned
to the chin.

"'Will he marry her?' he demanded.

"'No!' I said.

"'Then I shall kill him.'

"'You see,' said my uncle, 'the situation is becoming simple.'

"The remainder of the day was passed in negotiations. The
men argued in one room, the women wept in another,
and I was conducted between them like a prisoner whose
sentence had not yet been decided.

"My aunt embraced me.

"'She is charming, Pierre.'

"'Then let someone else marry her.'

"'After this?'

"'Nothing happened!'

"'My poor boy, that makes it worse. If something had
happened, people might understand it.'

"Toward evening the two mothers formed a kind of jury.
They represented to me that Claire could no longer appear
in society, that the story would travel through the district
before nightfall, that the colonel would certainly challenge me, that
one of us would certainly be killed, and that my
death would break my aunt's heart while the colonel's death
would leave Claire an orphan.

"'Therefore,' concluded my aunt, 'you alone can prevent four tragedies.'

"'By committing a fifth.'

"She began to weep again.

"I attempted flight that night. My luggage had disappeared. My
horse had been sent, by a singular coincidence, to a
pasture six miles away. At breakfast the colonel sat opposite
me. At luncheon my uncle showed me a draft of
the marriage contract. By dinner the entire household addressed me
with the grave kindness shown to a condemned man.

"On the third day I surrendered.

"'Very well,' I said. 'I will marry her. But understand
that I do so under protest.'

"'You may protest after the wedding,' said my uncle. 'It
will be safer.'

"The banns were arranged, the families hurried everything, and before
I had fully recovered from my headache I found myself
at the church beside Mademoiselle Dumoulin.

"She had scarcely spoken to me since that morning. She
was pale and calm. I mistook her silence for pride
and her calmness for hostility. During the ceremony I promised
fidelity in the tone of a man pleading not guilty.

"That evening, when at last we were alone, I walked
up and down the room. I had determined to behave
honourably, coldly and with perfect clarity.

"Claire sat beside the bed, still wearing her white dress.
Her hands trembled in her lap.

"'Madame,' I began, 'you know that this marriage was forced
upon us.'

"She raised her eyes.

"'Yes.'

"'I do not accuse you.'

"'Thank you.'

"'We shall preserve appearances. You will have your liberty, and
I shall retain mine.'

"She became even paler, but bowed her head.

"'As you wish.'

"'I shall never demand anything from you.'

"'As you wish.'

"Her voice was so faint that I stopped walking.

"Then she stood and faced me with a courage that
seemed almost military, though tears were running down her cheeks.

"'You saved my honour,' she said. 'I know that you
dislike me, and I know I have ruined your life.
I will obey you in everything. If you wish me
to live apart from you, I shall do so. If
you wish me to enter a convent, I shall go.
If my death would give you back your freedom—'

"'What nonsense!'

"'I mean it. You need only tell me.'

"And suddenly I saw her, not as the overbearing girl
who had criticized my dancing, not as the colonel's daughter,
not as the cause of my captivity, but as a
beautiful young woman who was frightened, humiliated and ready to
sacrifice herself for an accident that was entirely mine.

"I approached her.

"'Claire,' I said, 'there will be no convent.'

"She waited.

"'And no death.'

"Then, as I am not eloquent, I kissed her.

"That is how our marriage began. Within a month I
discovered she laughed at my jokes; within six months I
discovered she improved them; and within a year I could
no longer understand how I had lived without her. She
still contradicts me, but usually when I am wrong, which
is an intolerable habit and an extremely useful one.

"We have been married five years. I have never regretted
it for five minutes."

Pierre emptied his glass.

The bridegroom had listened with increasing satisfaction.

"So chance chose your wife?"

"Chance, cider, and a badly lighted passage."

"And you advise me to trust marriage?"

Pierre considered.

"My dear fellow, marriage is a lottery in which prudent
men study the tickets, fools select the prettiest number, and
Providence occasionally puts the prize into the pocket of a
drunkard."

One of the husbands raised his glass.

"To Providence!"

"No," said Pierre. "To the wrong door."

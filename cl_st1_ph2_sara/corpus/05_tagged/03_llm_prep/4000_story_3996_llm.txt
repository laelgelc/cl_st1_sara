# The Education of Mary Hill

New York received Mary Hill at eight o'clock on a
wet October morning, examined her hat, her suitcase, and her
ambitions, and appeared to say, "You will do—for the present."

Mary did not hear this verdict. She heard whistles, bells,
hoofs, motors, newsboys, porters, elevated trains, and several million citizens
demanding the immediate removal of several million other citizens from
their path. She had come from Brantford's Crossing, Ontario, where
a person could stand in the middle of Main Street
long enough to reconsider his whole life without being struck
by anything more dangerous than public opinion. Here the pavement
itself seemed impatient with her.

She stood outside the station holding a neat brown bag
and the address of a boarding-house in West Thirty-Eighth Street.
The rain fell with businesslike severity. Taxis rushed past her,
bearing men who already had appointments. Umbrellas met and fenced.
The crowd poured round her as water pours round a
stone, except that water does not mutter when delayed.

Mary took a deep breath.

"I shall like it," she told herself.

This was courage. Courage often begins by telling a lie
in a firm voice.

She was twenty-three, dark-haired, grey-eyed, and sufficiently pretty to have
been forgiven many small errors of judgment in Brantford's Crossing.
Her father kept the drug-store opposite the Methodist church. Her
mother possessed that peculiar Canadian talent for making affection sound
like a warning against draughts. Mary had been trained in
stenography at the commercial college in Hamilton and had come
to New York because Hamilton, though admirable, had declined to
become the centre of the universe.

There was another reason. There nearly always is.

Eddy Moore had written to her.

Eddy had been born in Brantford's Crossing, but he had
never really belonged there. Even at seventeen he had worn
his clothes as if expecting a photographer. He could play
the mandolin, quote poetry, and borrow five dollars without causing
the lender to feel that money had entered into the
matter. Two years earlier he had gone to New York.
His letters, written irregularly and from changing addresses, spoke of
"deals," "people downtown," "a little thing in copper," and "dinner
with men who matter." They suggested that New York had
recognized him instantly as one of its own.

His last letter had contained the sentence: "There ought to
be no difficulty in placing a girl of your ability."

Mary had read that sentence eleven times.

It is astonishing what a romantic glow can be produced
by the word "placing" when written by the proper hand.

Her first week in New York was one of those
instructive periods which improve the character and ruin the shoes.
Each morning she put on her best suit, pinned hope
beneath her collar, and set out to find employment. Each
evening she returned with the hope slightly bent.

At employment agencies she met fifty other young women who
could take dictation faster, spell "miscellaneous" with greater confidence, and
answer the question "What salary do you expect?" without looking
as though accused of theft. Office managers admired her references
and regretted that they required experience. Mary pointed out, first
tactfully and then with increasing heat, that experience could not
be obtained without employment. Office managers agreed that this was
most unfortunate and rang for the next applicant.

On Thursday a stout gentleman dictated a letter about pig-iron
at such speed that pig-iron emerged from Mary's notes as
a complaint concerning a man named Piggins. On Friday she
arrived at an interview three minutes late because she had
taken an express train in the wrong direction. The manager
informed her that punctuality was the soul of business. Mary,
who had spent forty minutes proving this by travelling to
Harlem, did not argue.

By the second Monday New York had ceased to be
a glittering magician and had become a very large person
who knew she was waiting in the hall but had
no intention of coming down.

Then Eddy called.

He entered the boarding-house parlour at six, bringing with him
a broad-brimmed hat, a pearl-grey overcoat, and an atmosphere of
having just left a conference where nations had been rearranged.
Mary, who had spent the afternoon copying addresses for an
agency at one cent each, rose with a cry of
relief.

"Eddy!"

"My dear Mary!"

He held both her hands and looked into her face.
No man in Brantford's Crossing had ever looked at a
woman quite as Eddy did. The local method had been
to inspect some point over her left shoulder and ask
whether she thought it might rain. Eddy's gaze conveyed welcome,
admiration, recollection, and a faint suggestion that violins had started
playing in an adjoining room.

"You look wonderful," he said.

"I look tired."

"Wonderful—and tired. New York has begun its work."

"What work?"

"Changing you."

Mary smiled. "It hasn't had time."

"It takes no time. That is the remarkable thing about
this city."

This was an excellent observation, and Eddy knew it. He
sat beside her on the horsehair sofa and listened while
she described her failures. At least, he appeared to listen.
His eyes were sympathetic; his voice supplied soft encouragement at
the proper places; only once did he answer "Yes, yes,
exactly" to a question about whether she ought to go
home.

"You mustn't dream of it," he said when the misunderstanding
had been cleared away. "I've been making inquiries."

"Have you really?"

"My dear girl, of course. I think I have found
the very thing."

Mary's hand tightened on the sofa cushion.

"What is it?"

"A broker's office. Excellent concern. Sound, expanding, plenty of opportunity.
The man needs a private secretary."

"Who is he?"

Eddy hesitated. It was a graceful hesitation, carefully turned at
the edges.

"You remember Joe Rendal?"

Mary stared.

"Joe Rendal! Not Joe?"

"The same."

The Joe Rendal whom Mary remembered had been nineteen, long
in the wrist, sudden in the ankle, and incapable of
entering a room without apologizing to some article of furniture.
He had adored Mary for three years with a fidelity
which would have been touching if it had not been
so inconvenient. At dances he had stood near the wall,
watching her dance with Eddy. At picnics he had carried
her coat, her basket, and, figuratively, her contempt. Once, beside
Mill Pond, he had tried to tell her that her
eyes reminded him of stars. Unfortunately, he had trodden on
a rake and finished the declaration while holding his nose.

Mary had been kind to him. At least, she had
practiced upon him that pleasant form of tyranny which pretty
girls call kindness.

"He's a broker?" she said.

"Doing remarkably well."

"Joe?"

"Wall Street has odd tastes."

She laughed, then felt disloyal and stopped.

"I suppose he still wears collars too big for him."

"Not noticeably."

"And does he still turn red when he speaks?"

"Only when the market goes against him, I imagine. He
has changed."

The statement was disturbing. Mary had no objection to Joe
Rendal's becoming prosperous; she wished every happiness to those who
had admired her. But she had unconsciously preserved him in
her mind as one preserves an old photograph: a little
faded, a little foolish, and always facing in her direction.

"Would he give me the position?"

"Leave that to me."

"How did you know he needed someone?"

"I hear things," said Eddy.

This, too, was one of his best effects.

* * *

Rendal & Company occupied six rooms on the twelfth floor
of a building in Broad Street. The outer office contained
four clerks, three telephones, two ticker machines, one office-boy, and
enough noise to conduct a small but spirited war.

Mary arrived at ten precisely. The office-boy, whose name was
Albert, looked at her with the grave reserve of one
who might at any moment be obliged to lunch with
a duke. He accepted her card, vanished, and returned almost
instantly.

"Mr. Rendal says go in."

"Mr. Rendal says I may go in," corrected Mary.

Albert considered this.

"He didn't say it that way."

She entered.

A man stood at the window with a telephone receiver
in one hand. He was broad-shouldered, well dressed, and alert.
His dark hair was brushed back from a forehead which
seemed to have acquired ideas since leaving Brantford's Crossing. He
spoke into the telephone with quick authority.

"No. Sell at eighty-three. If Martin says eighty-four, tell him
I admire his optimism but cannot finance it. Yes. Now."

He hung up and turned.

For a moment Mary looked for Joe behind him.

Then the man smiled.

"Well, Mary."

It was Joe's smile. That had not changed. It remained
slightly crooked and unexpectedly boyish. Everything else had been revised.

"Joe!"

"Eddy said you might come."

There was no blush. There was no collision with the
desk. He came forward, shook her hand, and placed a
chair for her without overturning it. New York, having taken
the raw material of Joe Rendal, had apparently hired an
expert tailor, a fencing-master, and a drill sergeant.

Mary sat down.

"So you're a broker," she said.

"I have been accused of it."

"And successful?"

"My creditors speak well of me."

She glanced round the private office. Its heavy desk, deep
carpet, maps, telephones, and polished cabinets indicated that the creditors
possessed some reason for enthusiasm.

"You've changed," she said.

"So Eddy tells everyone. It saves him from discussing the
weather."

The remark annoyed her, chiefly because Eddy had used almost
the same phrase to her.

"Eddy has been very kind."

"He enjoys kindness when it involves no capital."

"What do you mean?"

"Nothing. He told me you were looking for a position."

"Yes."

Joe sat behind the desk. The change in geography altered
the whole interview. Mary had expected, vaguely, that he would
beg her to work for him. Instead, he opened a
drawer and took out a typewritten sheet.

"My secretary left on Saturday," he said. "Can you take
one hundred and twenty words a minute?"

"On ordinary matter."

"Nothing here is ordinary. Can you type accurately?"

"Yes."

"Spell?"

"Certainly."

"Keep your head when five men speak at once, the
telephone rings, the market falls six points, Albert announces a
lunatic, and I ask for a letter I have accidentally
put in my pocket?"

"I don't know."

"Good. Anyone who said yes would be dangerous."

He gave her the sheet.

"Copy that, please. Machine in the next room."

Mary rose with dignity. She had not come from Canada
to be examined by Joe Rendal, who had once waited
two hours outside a church social because she had forgotten
promising him the last dance. The revolt of the serf
was all very well in history, but there are limits.

She copied the sheet. Anger improved her speed, as it
improves almost everything except judgment. When she returned, Joe compared
the copy with the original.

"Two errors."

"One is in your original."

"So it is."

"And the other is because the 'e' sticks."

"So it does."

He leaned back and regarded her. Mary prepared to be
humble if absolutely necessary.

"Salary thirty dollars a week to start," he said. "Forty
after two months if we both survive."

She had hoped for twenty-five.

"Thank you," she said coolly.

"Can you begin now?"

"Now?"

"The office has a prejudice in favour of work during
office hours."

Mary took off her gloves.

By five o'clock she understood why Joe had inquired whether
she could keep her head. The day had included a
music-hall collision scene, a railway panic, a political convention, and
the closing minutes of a cup final, all compressed into
six rooms and supplied with telephones.

Men rushed in carrying slips of paper as though the
slips were wounded comrades. Telephones rang with the moral insistence
of creditors. The tickers chattered like two mechanical aunts discussing
a scandal. Where ordinary men walked, Joe bounded; where ordinary
men requested, he commanded; where ordinary men frowned, he produced
an expression which caused clerks to discover forgotten duties in
distant rooms.

At half-past twelve a red-faced man named Simms burst in
shouting that Bellamy Central had broken seventy.

"Then pick up the pieces," said Joe. "Buy five hundred."

"It may go to sixty!"

"Then we'll have saved ten points of falling."

At two, a messenger brought news which made three clerks
speak at once and Albert drop his dignity into the
waste-paper basket. Joe stood in the centre of it all,
calm and swift, issuing instructions, changing them, remembering figures, dictating
two letters between telephone calls, and somehow finding time to
ask Mary if she had eaten.

She had not.

"Neither have I," he said. "That proves the democratic nature
of finance."

At four the storm passed. Men stopped running. The telephones,
defeated but vindictive, rang only at intervals. Joe came into
Mary's little office, loosened his collar, and sat on the
edge of the desk.

"Well?"

Mary was tired enough to be truthful.

"I never imagined you could do it."

"Neither did my first partner."

"I mean—you were always so—"

"Yes?"

She searched for a charitable word and failed.

"Awkward."

"I know. I used to have to count my arms
every morning to make sure no new ones had grown
in the night."

Mary laughed in spite of herself.

"You aren't a bit like that now."

"The city changed me."

There was a pause. Eddy had said it; now Joe
said it. New York seemed to be running a general
alteration business.

Joe's expression softened.

"You haven't changed much."

Something in his voice brought back the old days: the
pond, the summer evenings, the young man by the wall
at dances watching her as if she were the only
person who had been supplied with music. Mary felt suddenly
uncomfortable.

"I hope I've improved."

"You couldn't."

This was dangerously near the old Joe. She rose and
began arranging papers.

"Where shall I put these?"

His face became businesslike at once.

"Blue file. And, Mary—"

"Yes?"

"I'm glad you're here."

She looked up, but he had gone.

* * *

For three weeks Mary disliked Joe each morning, respected him
by noon, and nearly liked him by five. It was
an unsettling programme.

He was an exacting employer. He expected letters to be
found where they had been filed, which is a rigid
and almost Oriental demand. He objected to mistakes in figures
merely because figures, as he put it, were narrow-minded things
that refused to understand intention. When Mary arrived seven minutes
late, he looked at the clock and then at her.

"The clock is fast," she said.

"So is the market. You must catch both."

"Streetcar delay."

"Start earlier."

"You have no sympathy."

"Plenty. Sympathy begins at nine-thirty."

She called him a pig under her breath.

"Spell it with one 'g,'" he said without turning.

Yet he was never petty. When she made an error,
he corrected it and forgot it. When she did well,
he noticed. If work kept her late, a cab appeared.
If a caller became insolent, Joe's voice acquired a smoothness
which generally removed the caller within thirty seconds. And on
the afternoon when Mary's mother's letter made Brantford's Crossing seem
unbearably far away, he found Mary crying over the copying
machine and behaved almost tactfully.

He did not ask what was wrong. He placed a
clean handkerchief beside her, said, "The machine has always had
a depressing personality," and left.

Ten minutes later Albert brought tea and a plate of
cakes.

"Mr. Rendal says eat 'em."

"Mr. Rendal says—"

"He said it just like that."

Mary ate them.

Eddy, meanwhile, came often to see her. Sometimes he met
her for luncheon; sometimes he waited downstairs after work. He
seemed to know every restaurant in which the lights were
flattering and the portions small. He introduced her to men
with shining hair and women who spoke of Europe as
if it were a hotel they had recently rejected.

With Eddy, New York became again the city she had
imagined before arriving: theatres, gold lettering, music behind glass doors,
taxis sliding through rain-polished streets. He interpreted it for her.
This building contained a famous lawyer; that window belonged to
an actress; the stout man at the corner had made
two million in rubber. Eddy knew the price of everything,
the private history of everybody, and the waiter at no
restaurant.

He spoke frequently of Joe.

"Old Joe is coming along," he said one evening.

"Coming along? He's very successful."

"Yes, yes, of course. In his way."

"What way?"

"The solid way. He has force."

The concession sounded faintly patronizing.

"He's cleverer than people thought at home."

"My dear Mary, everyone is clever in a bull market."

Mary did not know what a bull market was, but
she resented it on Joe's behalf.

"He works very hard."

"Does he? Poor fellow."

"And he's generous."

Eddy's eyebrows moved.

"With money?"

"With everything."

"Ah."

"What does 'ah' mean?"

"Nothing. It is merely the shortest comment in the language."

She studied him. He looked as handsome as ever, but
the light was above him and showed two fine lines
at the corners of his mouth. Mary had never noticed
them before. It was probably the light.

"Do you and Joe do business together?" she asked.

"Occasionally."

"He doesn't talk about it."

"Joe is secretive."

"He says you're unreliable."

The words slipped out. Eddy's fingers tightened for an instant
around his coffee cup; then he smiled.

"Does he?"

"He said you enjoyed kindness when it cost nothing."

"Typical Joe."

"Why?"

"Jealousy."

Mary flushed. "Don't be absurd."

"My dear girl, surely you know."

"Know what?"

"That he is still in love with you."

The restaurant, which had been noisy a moment before, appeared
to withdraw tactfully to a distance.

"That was years ago."

"Years do not cure everyone."

Mary looked down at the tablecloth.

"How would you know?"

"Joe and I had dinner last week. He spoke of
you."

"What did he say?"

Eddy smiled into his cup.

"I don't betray confidences."

There is no device more effective for betraying a confidence
than to announce that one possesses it.

Mary went home disturbed. It was not displeasing to hear
that Joe loved her. She would have denied that it
pleased her, but denial is the ceremonial costume of many
agreeable emotions. What disturbed her was the thought that his
brisk commands, his jokes, and his cool employer's manner might
conceal the old devotion.

The next morning she watched him covertly.

Joe dictated letters, opened telegrams, and argued with Boston. He
showed no sign of a broken heart. At eleven he
asked why she had typed "fifteen thousand" instead of "fifty
thousand."

"I was thinking," she said.

"Try to overcome it during business hours."

She looked at him coldly.

"Eddy says you're jealous of him."

Joe stopped.

"Does he?"

"Yes."

"He would."

"Are you?"

Joe pushed a paperweight three inches to the left.

"Mary, if Eddy Moore tells you the sun is shining,
take an umbrella."

"That's hateful."

"It is also waterproof."

"You've never liked him."

"I liked him when we were twelve. He had a
red sled."

"And now?"

"Now he has other methods of getting downhill."

Mary rose.

"You're horrid."

"Sit down."

"I won't be ordered about."

"You are holding the letter I need."

She flung it onto his desk.

He picked it up and glanced at it.

"Thank you. Also, you have put Mr. Paterson's letter in
the Pearl Street file. Nothing belonging to Eddy should go
into the Pearl Street file."

"I didn't know it belonged to Eddy."

"It doesn't. That is why it shouldn't go there."

She stared at him.

"Do you always talk in riddles?"

"Only when plain language would make you throw more stationery."

He smiled, but his eyes were serious.

"Listen to me, Mary. Eddy is amusing, attractive, and as
safe as a loose stair at midnight. Don't discuss this
office with him."

"I don't."

"Don't discuss clients, letters, transactions, or the Pearl Street property."

"I've never even heard of it."

"Excellent. Continue."

"You have no right to warn me against my friends."

"I have every right to warn my secretary against mine."

"Is Eddy your friend?"

"He eats my dinners."

"That isn't an answer."

"It is the New York definition."

She walked out with her head high. Joe called after
her.

"And bring back the Paterson letter!"

She did, because rebellion is one thing and thirty dollars
a week another.

* * *

There came a Thursday when the market, bored with ordinary
misconduct, went mad.

From ten o'clock the office ceased to resemble a place
where business was performed and became a giant fowl-run into
which someone had introduced a fox with a megaphone. Stocks
plunged, recovered, turned somersaults, and plunged again. Customers filled the
outer room. Simms lost his voice before noon and continued
shouting with the expression of a man betrayed by his
own throat.

A large client named Mr. Kessler arrived from Chicago, demanding
to know why Joe had sold his holdings. At the
same moment the bank called about a loan, a reporter
asked for a statement, and Albert announced that the wiring
in the records room smelled hot.

Mary expected Joe to explode.

Instead, he became quieter.

He sent Simms to the floor with two orders, soothed
the bank, refused the reporter, told Albert to fetch the
building engineer, and invited Mr. Kessler into the private office.
There he listened while the Chicago man roared for four
minutes.

When Mr. Kessler paused for breath, Joe placed a telegram
before him.

"You instructed us to sell if the stock reached sixty-eight."

"I did nothing of the kind."

"Your telegram."

"I meant sixty-eight after dividends."

"You omitted the final three words."

"You should have understood."

"A broker who understands what a customer means instead of
what he says is soon selling pencils. We obeyed the
telegram."

Kessler struck the desk.

"You've cost me twenty thousand dollars!"

"I have saved you thirty. The stock is now fifty-one."

As if summoned for the punch line, the ticker in
the outer office confirmed it.

Mr. Kessler looked at the tape. His anger collapsed so
suddenly that it seemed to have been standing on a
trapdoor.

"Why did it break?"

"Because men who knew nothing bought from men who knew
something."

"What should I do?"

"Nothing until tomorrow."

"I want to buy it back."

"No."

"It may recover."

"It may learn to sing. Go to your hotel."

Mr. Kessler went.

At three, smoke began curling from the records room. Albert
emerged carrying a waste-paper basket and the solemn conviction that
heroism had at last justified his employment. The engineer dealt
with the wires. At four, Joe completed a transaction which
earned the firm more money than Mary's father made in
a year.

At half-past four he sat down, rubbed his eyes, and
asked for coffee.

Mary brought it herself.

"You were wonderful," she said.

Joe looked behind him.

"I'm speaking to you."

"Then repeat it slowly. I may never hear it again."

She laughed.

"I mean it. You knew exactly what to do."

"That is why I have the large office."

"I used to think you were—"

"All hands, feet, and apologies?"

"Something like that."

"I was. One can't remain nineteen forever. The clothes wouldn't
fit."

She leaned against the desk.

"Were you frightened today?"

"Thoroughly."

"You didn't look it."

"Looking frightened makes other people industrious in the wrong direction."

He drank his coffee.

"Why did your last secretary leave?"

The question came without planning. Joe's cup paused.

"Did Eddy ask you that?"

"No."

"Has he spoken to you about office business?"

"No."

Joe set down the cup.

"My last secretary was named Miss Vale. She was efficient
and ambitious. Eddy persuaded her that friendship required her to
show him certain letters."

Mary stiffened.

"I don't believe it."

"I didn't expect you to."

"What happened?"

"I discovered it."

"And dismissed her?"

"She left before I had the pleasure."

"What did Eddy say?"

"That she had misunderstood him."

"Perhaps she had."

"Perhaps the wiring in the records room was smoking because
it had taken up cigars."

Mary's admiration vanished beneath anger.

"You think everyone is dishonest except yourself."

"No. I think Eddy is dishonest. It saves time to
be specific."

"You're jealous."

Joe's expression changed. For once no quip came.

"Yes," he said. "I am jealous."

Mary had not expected admission. She looked at him.

"But that doesn't make me wrong," he added.

The outer-office telephone rang. Simms shouted through the door that
Boston wanted him.

Joe stood.

"Don't let Eddy use you, Mary."

"He wouldn't."

"I hope not."

He went out, leaving her with the empty coffee cup
and a curious beating of the heart.

* * *

Eddy did not mention business for ten days.

This strengthened Mary's faith in him. Innocence, when watched, has
a way of becoming impressively well behaved.

He took her to a musical comedy, sent violets to
the office, and spoke tenderly of Brantford's Crossing. He remembered
the willow near Mill Pond, the church picnics, Mary's blue
dress at the winter dance. His recollections were not always
accurate—the dress had been white, and the willow was a
maple—but sentiment is judged by warmth rather than botany.

One evening they dined at a quiet restaurant near Washington
Square. Eddy seemed preoccupied. He broke his bread into small
pieces, looked several times toward the door, and answered "Yes,
yes" when Mary said the orchestra was too loud, although
there was no orchestra.

"What's wrong?" she asked.

"Wrong?"

"You're worried."

He smiled.

"I cannot hide anything from you."

This was not true, but it was beautifully spoken.

"It is merely business."

"Can I help?"

"No, no."

He sighed in a manner suggesting that he would continue
to refuse help until she had offered it three times.

"Tell me," said Mary.

"It concerns Joe."

"What about him?"

Eddy lowered his voice.

"There is a property in Pearl Street."

Mary remembered Joe's warning.

"What property?"

"A group of buildings. Nothing very exciting to look at—old
brick, warehouses, a few shops. But a railway company is
considering a terminal nearby. If it does, the value will
triple."

"How do you know?"

"I have sources."

"And Joe?"

"He has been negotiating to buy it quietly."

Mary pushed back her chair a little.

"You shouldn't tell me this."

"My dear Mary, it is hardly a secret between us."

"It's Joe's business."

"Joe has no moral claim to a city block."

"What do you want?"

Eddy looked hurt.

"Want?"

"Yes."

He was silent. The waiter arrived and asked whether they
desired dessert. Eddy dismissed him with a glance of refined
suffering.

"At times," he said, "one is forced to decide whether
loyalty belongs to a man or to what is right."

This sentence had a noble sound and no immediate meaning.

"What is right?"

"The property is owned by a widow and her son.
Joe means to buy before they learn of the railway
plan."

"Joe wouldn't cheat them."

"Not legally."

"That isn't what I said."

Eddy leaned toward her.

"I am trying to protect them. But I must know
the price Joe intends to offer and when he will
act. A letter was sent to him today from Cartwright
& Bell. It contains the terms."

Mary felt suddenly cold.

"How do you know a letter was sent?"

"One hears things."

"You want me to read it?"

"Not read it. Merely make a copy."

"No."

The answer came so quickly that Eddy blinked.

"Mary, listen—"

"No."

"It would harm no one."

"It would betray Joe."

"It would prevent Joe from exploiting people who trust him."

"Then tell the people what you know."

"I cannot reveal my source."

"Tell Joe."

"He would deny everything."

"Then you have no proof."

"The letter is proof."

He reached across the table and touched her hand.

"You are the only person I can trust."

Once, that phrase from Eddy would have opened every door
in Mary's judgment and invited reason to wait outside. Now
it seemed to her oddly rehearsed.

She withdrew her hand.

"Did you ask Miss Vale to copy letters?"

His face altered. Only slightly; a tightening round the mouth,
a flicker in the eyes. But charm is a delicate
mechanism, and one loose spring can reveal the machinery.

"So Joe told you that."

"Did you?"

"She misunderstood."

"What did you ask her?"

"Nothing improper."

"What?"

Eddy tore another piece of bread.

"There was a market report. Public information, really."

"Then why did you need her to get it?"

"I didn't need her. She volunteered."

"Joe says you persuaded her."

"Joe would."

"Did she volunteer?"

"Mary, this is absurd. You're cross-examining me like a lawyer."

"Answer."

He smiled, but the smile had become work.

"My dear girl, business isn't as simple as Brantford's Crossing.
Here one must sometimes move indirectly."

"Crookedly?"

"That is an ugly word."

"It describes an ugly thing."

He leaned back.

"I see Joe has been busy."

"Joe warned me. You have just proved him right."

"By asking you to prevent a fraud?"

"By asking me to steal a letter."

"Copy a letter."

"Steal what is in it."

His voice hardened.

"You owe me something. I found you the position."

"Did you find it so I could spy for you?"

For a second he had no answer. In that second
Mary saw the entire structure: the helpful letter, the inquiries,
the position discovered at precisely the office where he wanted
information. What had seemed romantic became calculated. What had appeared
generous became cheap. The lights of the restaurant did not
change, but Eddy did.

Or perhaps he had not changed at all. This is
more common.

He recovered.

"You're being theatrical."

"No. I'm being slow. I should have understood sooner."

She reached for her gloves.

"Mary, sit down."

"I won't be ordered about."

"You don't mind when Joe does it."

"That's office business."

"This is childish."

She stood.

The waiter appeared again, drawn by the instinct which tells
waiters when a private catastrophe is nearing its bill.

"Coffee, madam?"

"No, thank you."

Eddy rose too.

"You'll regret this."

"Perhaps."

"Joe is using you."

"No. You tried to."

She walked out.

The rain had stopped. Washington Square shone under the lamps,
black branches crossing the pale sky. Mary walked quickly toward
Fifth Avenue, angry with Eddy, angrier with herself, and most
angry of all with Joe for having been right in
such an irritatingly complete manner.

Halfway to the avenue, she heard footsteps behind her.

"Mary!"

It was not Eddy. Joe came up beside her, hat
tilted back, breath visible in the cold air.

"What are you doing here?" she demanded.

"Walking with unusual speed."

"Were you following me?"

"Yes."

"You eavesdropper!"

"Technically, an eavesdropper listens under eaves. The restaurant has an
awning."

"You had no right."

"I had every interest."

"Did you hear what we said?"

"Enough."

"You arranged it!"

"No. Eddy arranged it. I merely gave him scenery."

She stopped.

"What do you mean?"

Joe took her arm and moved her away from the
curb as a taxi swept past.

"The Cartwright & Bell letter was bait."

Mary stared.

"There is no railway terminal?"

"There may be some century, but none is planned."

"No widow?"

"The Pearl Street property belongs to three brothers from Hoboken,
all with moustaches."

"And the letter?"

"Contained figures designed to tempt Eddy."

Her anger found a new direction.

"You used me as a trap."

"I used the letter as a trap."

"You knew he would ask me!"

"I thought he might."

"Because Miss Vale—"

"Yes."

"You let me go to dinner with him knowing he
might ask me to betray you?"

"I could hardly lock you in the filing cabinet."

"You could have told me the truth."

"You wouldn't believe the truth when I told it plainly."

"So you tricked me."

"I tricked Eddy."

"You spied on me."

"I followed because if you agreed, I intended to stop
you before you took the letter."

Mary's face burned.

"You thought I might agree."

Joe did not answer quickly enough.

"You did!"

"I was afraid you might."

"You have a very poor opinion of me."

"I have a very poor opinion of Eddy, and he
has spent years practicing on people."

"That isn't the same."

"No," said Joe quietly. "It isn't."

They stood facing one another while the city streamed round
them. A bus growled at the corner. Two men argued
beside a newsstand. Somewhere a policeman blew his whistle with
the despairing persistence of a man trying to discipline the
Atlantic Ocean.

Mary's anger weakened.

"I said no," she murmured.

"I heard."

"At once."

"I heard that too."

"He said I owed him because he found me the
job."

Joe gave a short laugh.

"What?"

"He didn't find it. I asked him to write to
you."

"You did?"

"My secretary had gone. I knew you had trained as
a stenographer. I didn't know your address."

Mary looked at him.

"Why didn't you write to my father?"

"Pride."

"Whose?"

"Mine. I thought if Eddy suggested it, you might come.
If I asked, you might remember the rake at Mill
Pond."

She wanted to laugh and cry simultaneously, a combination at
which the human face is not expert.

"Then Eddy didn't do anything."

"He delivered a message and took the commission in gratitude."

"Oh!"

"Exactly."

At that moment Eddy came out of the restaurant and
looked up and down the street. He saw them. For
an instant the three stood fixed in an arrangement which
even a dull playwright would have recognized as useful.

Eddy crossed toward them.

"So," he said, "this was planned."

Joe nodded. "Not your part. That was spontaneous villainy."

"You planted the letter."

"Yes."

"There is no deal?"

"There is a deal. You and I are through."

Eddy's composure cracked.

"You can't make accusations based on—on a private conversation."

"I can choose my dinner companions based on one."

"You've been trying to turn Mary against me."

"You managed that without assistance."

Eddy looked at Mary.

"You believe him?"

She met his eyes. Once they had seemed deep. Now
they appeared merely well lit.

"Yes."

"I see."

He put on his hat with care.

"You will both be sorry."

"Possibly," said Joe. "But not at breakfast."

Eddy walked away.

Joe watched him reach the corner.

"He hasn't paid for dinner," Mary said suddenly.

"No."

"You'll have to."

"Yes."

She began to laugh. Joe looked offended.

"It is not funny. He ordered the sole."

That finished her. She laughed until tears came, while passers-by
gave them the brief, resentful attention New Yorkers reserve for
strangers enjoying themselves in public.

* * *

The next morning Mary arrived at nine-twenty.

Joe was already at his desk. He glanced at the
clock.

"You're early."

"I wished to speak to you."

"That sounds expensive. Sit down."

She sat. The office had not yet gathered speed. The
tickers murmured privately; Albert was removing his overcoat with the
solemnity of a cabinet minister assuming office.

Mary folded her gloves.

"You were right about Eddy."

Joe looked through a stack of letters.

"Yes."

"You needn't look so pleased."

"I'm not pleased. I'm checking whether the date is correct.
I am rarely right before noon."

"I misjudged him."

"Yes."

"And I misjudged you."

Joe stopped turning the papers.

"That is more interesting."

"I thought because Eddy was polished and amusing, he must
be good."

"A natural mistake. Poison labels are often beautifully printed."

"And because you were—"

"An employer?"

"Bossy."

"Ah."

"And sarcastic."

"True."

"And suspicious and interfering and conceited—"

"This apology is developing unusual features."

"I thought you had become hard."

Joe leaned back.

"Have I?"

"No."

"Pity. It is useful in business."

"You're kind, though you hide it."

"I don't hide it. I put it in the blue
file under K."

"You helped me come to New York."

"Yes."

"You gave me the position."

"You earned it after the first morning."

"And you tried to protect me."

"I made a mess of that."

Mary looked down at her gloves.

"No. I made the mess."

Silence entered the office. It was not accustomed to the
place and stood awkwardly between them.

Joe rose and closed the door to the outer room.

"Mary."

She looked up.

He stood with one hand on the back of a
chair. For the first time since she had come to
New York, something of the old Joe returned: not the
awkwardness exactly, but the uncertainty beneath it, the young man
at the edge of a dance hoping for the final
waltz.

"You know what Eddy told you," he said.

"That you were jealous?"

"Yes."

"You admitted that."

"I admitted the least embarrassing part."

Mary's heart began behaving like a telephone in a panic.

Joe walked to the window and back.

"This is absurd. I can negotiate a loan with three
banks before lunch, but whenever I try to say one
particular thing to you, I feel nineteen and see that
rake rising from the grass."

Mary smiled.

"You held your nose."

"It was bleeding."

"You said my eyes were like stars through your handkerchief."

"I had prepared the sentence."

"It was a nice sentence."

"It did not receive a fair performance."

He came nearer.

"I loved you then."

She did not answer.

"I thought I would get over it. People do. They
recover from fever, bad investments, and amateur theatricals. I came
here, worked, made money, learned to buy collars, and discovered
that I could enter a room without knocking over the
room. But I didn't get over you."

"Joe—"

"Let me finish. I have been finishing this speech for
six years."

She nodded.

"When Eddy told me you might come, I thought it
would be easy. I would employ you, act calmly, and
allow you to observe what an extraordinary fellow I had
become. Within five minutes you made me feel as if
I ought to apologize to the desk."

"I didn't mean to."

"You never did. That was part of your power."

He smiled, but only briefly.

"I love you now. Not because you refused Eddy last
night, though I shall treasure that performance. Not because you
are beautiful, though the fact has caused considerable inconvenience. I
love you because when you are wrong you are wrong
magnificently, and when you see the truth you go straight
to it even if it bites. I love your courage,
your temper, your homesickness, your ridiculous pride, and the way
you correct Albert's grammar when he is bringing food."

From beyond the door came Albert's voice: "I can hear
you."

Joe opened the door.

"Then stop."

"Yes, sir."

Joe closed it again.

"Where was I?"

"My ridiculous pride."

"Ah, yes. It is one of your finest qualities, except
when pointed at me."

Mary's eyes filled. Joe saw and became alarmed.

"Don't cry. I have no defense against that."

"I'm not crying."

"Your eyes appear to have sprung a leak."

She laughed shakily.

"You said New York changed you," she said.

"It did."

"It made you confident."

"Outwardly."

"And successful."

"At intervals."

"It made me think you were a stranger."

"I thought so too."

She stood.

"When I came here, I believed Eddy was everything you
weren't. Easy, polished, exciting. And I believed you were everything
you had been."

"A collection of spare limbs?"

"Yes."

"Severe, but historically sound."

"But New York didn't make Eddy selfish. It only gave
him more chances to use it. And it didn't make
you loyal. You were always loyal."

Joe said nothing.

Mary came round the desk.

"The city changed me too."

His face was very still.

"How?"

"At home, I liked having you love me."

He winced.

"I know. I'm ashamed."

"Continue. This is painful but educational."

"I thought your devotion belonged to me, like my blue
coat or the piano stool. I never considered what it
cost you. When I arrived here and saw you had
become someone important—someone who didn't wait for me to notice
him—I resented it."

"The revolt of the property."

"Yes. Then I watched you work. I saw how people
trusted you. I saw that you were brave when everyone
else was frightened and kind when no one was looking.
And I began to—"

She stopped.

Joe waited. Outside, a telephone rang. Simms answered it in
a roar. A ticker machine clicked out the financial history
of the minute. New York, which respected no confession, continued
doing business.

"I began to love you," Mary said.

Joe's hand tightened on the chair.

"Say that again."

"I won't."

"Why not?"

"You heard me."

"Albert heard me, but I didn't hear you properly."

She smiled.

"New York has changed me too."

Joe crossed the space between them.

At this point the private telephone rang.

He ignored it.

It rang again, louder, with the outraged manner of a
machine accustomed to immediate obedience.

"Answer it," Mary whispered.

"Never."

"It may be important."

"Nothing is."

The door opened three inches and Albert's face appeared.

"Mr. Rendal, Boston says—"

"Albert."

"Yes, sir?"

"Go away."

Albert withdrew. The door closed.

Joe took Mary's hands.

"Are you certain?"

"No," she said. "But I'm brave."

"That will do."

He kissed her.

The telephone continued ringing. The tickers continued ticking. Simms shouted
that Bellamy Central had risen four points. Somewhere below, Broad
Street surged and clattered, buying, selling, hurrying, deceiving, trusting, winning,
losing, and changing everybody it could lay its hands upon.

After some time Mary drew back.

"What about Boston?"

"Boston can wait."

"What about Bellamy Central?"

"It has survived worse."

"What about the Pearl Street letters?"

Joe looked thoughtful.

"We should file them."

"In the blue file?"

"No. Frame them."

The door opened again. Albert entered bearing a tray with
two cups of coffee and the expression of a man
whose relations with dukes had accustomed him to delicate scenes.

"I thought you might want these," he said.

"Albert," said Mary, "Mr. Rendal says you may put them
down."

Albert looked at Joe.

"He didn't say it that way."

Joe laughed.

"Give the boy a raise."

"How much?"

"Two dollars a week."

Albert set down the tray.

"I heard that too," he said.

Then he retired, closing the door with dignity.

Mary looked at Joe.

"Do you suppose he heard everything?"

"Certainly."

"How awful."

"Not at all. By noon the whole office will know,
and by three they'll have the date of the wedding."

"We haven't chosen one."

"New York works quickly."

She moved toward the window. Twelve floors below, the streets
crossed in dark channels between the buildings, full of tiny
urgent figures. When she had first arrived, the crowd had
frightened her. It had seemed a vast machine built to
grind down hesitation. Now she saw something else: not one
crowd, but thousands of solitary hopes moving side by side,
each carrying its private letter, its unpaid bill, its concealed
fear, its ridiculous dream.

Joe stood beside her.

"Do you miss home?" he asked.

"Yes."

"So do I."

"Would you ever go back?"

"To live?"

"Perhaps."

He considered.

"I don't know. Brantford's Crossing may not be ready for
us."

"It survived Eddy."

"Barely."

She slipped her arm through his.

Below them, New York lifted its steel shoulders, raised one
stone eyebrow, and seemed to say, "Huh! Another pair."

But who cares what cities think? They are notorious cynics
in public and sentimentalists after dark.

The telephone rang once more.

Joe sighed and reached for it.

"Rendal speaking."

Mary watched his face become alert. He listened, asked two
swift questions, and covered the mouthpiece.

"Bellamy Central is rising."

"What will you do?"

"Buy."

"I thought Mr. Kessler owned it."

"He does not yet know that he wishes to own
more."

"You're impossible."

"You love me."

"I may recover."

"You won't. I have experience."

He turned back to the telephone and gave the order.

Mary sat at her machine, inserted a fresh sheet of
paper, and waited for dictation. The day rushed in upon
them: calls, telegrams, clients, figures, decisions, alarms. It came like
weather, like battle, like a brass band falling downstairs. Where
others trotted, Joe sprang. Where others protested, he smiled. Where
others lost their heads, he remembered where he had filed
them.

At eleven he passed Mary's desk and placed a folded
note beside her hand.

She opened it.

It contained seven words:

**Dinner tonight. No Eddy. I pay. Love, Joe.**

Mary looked toward the private office. Joe was speaking to
three men at once, but he caught her eye.

She nodded.

And New York, which had changed them both and revealed
them both and very nearly made a scandal of the
whole affair, gave a whistle, rang a bell, pocketed its
commission, and went on to the next customer.

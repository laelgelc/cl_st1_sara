### The House in Spring

At six o'clock Mr. Neave left the office.

For several minutes he stood beneath the stone archway, one
gloved hand resting on the brass rail, while the city
hurried past as though it had received astonishing news. The
trams clanged; wheels flashed; young men sprang on and off
the steps of omnibuses; girls in pale dresses streamed from
the shops with parcels balanced against their hips. Above the
roofs the sky was still golden, and every upper window
held a square of fire.

Spring! It had come in a single afternoon, warm, scented,
importunate. It pressed against him like an eager young creature
crying, "Come along! Quickly! You mustn't miss it!"

But Mr. Neave did not want to be touched by
spring.

He was cold. Cold and tired—absurdly tired. His knees trembled
beneath his weight, and his fingers, gripping the rail, seemed
hardly to belong to him.

H'm, h'm! This was unusual.

He had walked home every fine evening for thirty-seven years.
It was one of his rules. A man who sat
at a desk all day needed exercise. He had said
so a thousand times, especially to Harold, who believed exercise
meant driving to the tennis club and strolling twice round
the lawns.

Yet the long shining street before him appeared impossible. The
air had thickened. It was no longer air at all,
but warm transparent water through which he must push his
old body while the young darted past like bright fish.

Old body? Nonsense.

He descended the steps carefully.

At the corner a flower seller held out bunches of
violets. Their sweetness reached him with almost painful sharpness. For
one moment he thought of buying some for Charlotte. But
Charlotte's rooms were already full of flowers. There would be
bowls in the drawing room, jars on the stairs, perhaps
lilies at dinner. Flowers arrived constantly: from the girls' admirers,
from hostesses, from Harold when he remembered he had forgotten
something.

Harold.

The name seemed to weigh more heavily than his coat.

That morning Mr. Neave had again found the correspondence unopened
on Harold's desk. Three letters from Melbourne, two telegrams, and
the revised contract lying where it had been put yesterday.
Harold had appeared at noon, beautifully dressed and faintly apologetic.

"My dear governor, don't look like that. I was kept
up horribly late."

Kept up! At the Bellmans' supper, no doubt, or playing
cards with those fellows at the club. Enjoying himself.

And when Mr. Neave had spoken—quietly, reasonably—of responsibility, Harold had
smiled and said—

Well, what had he said?

Something about not letting business become a disease. Something light,
clever, intolerable.

"He'll settle down," Charlotte always declared. "You never give the
boy time."

Time! Harold was thirty-one.

Mr. Neave moved with the crowd, though more than once
a shoulder struck his arm and someone turned impatiently. He
wished they would not hurry so. What could be waiting
for them? Dinners, theatres, women at lighted windows. Young life
calling to young life.

The thought came oddly: there was no call for him.

At home, however, they were always begging him to retire.

"You've earned your rest, Father," the girls cried.

Charlotte said it tenderly, almost proudly. "We can't have you
wearing yourself out. Harold will manage splendidly."

Splendidly! H'm, h'm!

They knew nothing of the business. They saw the car
at the door, the summer house, the servants, the accounts
paid without question. They had never watched a doubtful investment
through sleepless months or sat opposite a ruined man who
still smiled while signing his name. To them the business
was a deep, dependable well. Harold need only lean over
and lower the bucket.

But suppose the rope slipped?

At the next crossing Mr. Neave paused so long that
a policeman touched his sleeve.

"Now then, sir."

"Yes, yes. Quite."

He reached the quieter streets at last. The gardens there
were overflowing. Branches of blossom hung above white walls; lawns
shone with new grass; curtains breathed from open windows. The
houses looked freshly awakened, their bright faces turned toward evening.

His own house stood at the end of the crescent,
wide and creamy, with green shutters and a balcony tangled
in young vine. All the windows were open. A long
curtain floated outward and fell back, floated and fell, as
though the house itself were breathing.

On the steps stood two tennis racquets, a silver-headed cane
and a basket of pink roses.

How full it was. Full of flowers, clothes, visitors, music,
laughter. Full of everything he had laboured to give them.

And yet, approaching it, Mr. Neave felt like a tradesman
arriving at the wrong door.

The maid opened before he rang.

"Mrs. Neave and the young ladies are in the morning
room, sir."

From somewhere within came Marion's voice, high and tragic.

"But, Mother, if she wears yellow I shall simply refuse
to go!"

Then laughter—Charlotte's soft peal, Ethel's sharper one—and the faint clatter
of teacups.

Mr. Neave removed his hat. The hall, crowded with palms
and polished tables, swayed slightly.

"There he is!" cried Ethel.

They came at once, all three of them, wonderfully fresh
and finished. Charlotte wore grey silk with a fall of
lace; Ethel was in white; Marion had a green ribbon
about her dark hair. They surrounded him in a cloud
of perfume.

"Father, how dreadful you look!"

"My poor old darling!"

"Sit down instantly."

Charlotte took his hand between hers. "You're frozen."

"No, no. Merely a little tired."

"A little!" Marion repeated, as if before an audience. "He
admits to being a little tired. Mother, ring for brandy.
Ring for a doctor. Ring for the archbishop!"

They guided him into the morning room and lowered him
into the largest chair. Cushions were pushed behind his head;
a footstool appeared; someone loosened his collar.

This was kindness, certainly. An ideal family, people called them.
The Neaves—so united, so affectionate. He ought to feel grateful.

But they had already turned away.

"Where were we?" Ethel asked.

"Clara's dress."

"Yellow," Marion said darkly.

Charlotte poured tea while the girls discussed Clara, who had
become engaged to a man none of them admired but
all of them intended to dine with on Thursday. There
were ices to be ordered for Saturday, a garden party
on Monday, and the question of whether Mrs. Bellman had
deliberately failed to invite the Ashfords.

Their voices flew over Mr. Neave like ribbons in a
wind.

He looked at the laden table: little cakes glazed in
pink and green, strawberries under silver, thin bread, cream, honey,
two kinds of jam. Too much. The room itself was
too much—too soft, too bright, too sweet. Roses leaned from
painted bowls; the carpet glowed beneath his shoes; tiny gilt
chairs stood about waiting for tiny gilt people.

It was all too rich for him.

"Where is Harold?" he asked.

Nobody heard.

Marion was imitating Mrs. Bellman: one hand upon her heart,
her eyes lifted.

"My dear, happiness has made you positively plain!"

They screamed with laughter.

"Where is Harold?" Mr. Neave repeated.

Charlotte glanced round. "Harold? He telephoned, darling. He may be
late."

"Late for dinner?"

"He said—what did he say, Ethel?"

"Oh, something about Tom Fraser and a horse."

"No, it was the motor."

"He said," Charlotte began, smiling vaguely, "he said not to
wait if—"

The three of them looked at one another.

"Well, it doesn't matter," she concluded. "He is enjoying himself."

Enjoying himself.

Mr. Neave closed his eyes. Instantly the colours of the
room burned brighter behind his lids.

"Father's falling asleep," Ethel whispered.

"Let him. He looks worn out."

"Poor treasure."

Their voices softened for perhaps half a minute. Then Marion
remembered the blue sash, and the talk sprang up again.

After a while Charlotte rang. William came to help Mr.
Neave upstairs.

"I can manage," he said.

Of course he could manage. He mounted slowly, refusing the
arm held near his elbow. At each landing he paused
as if examining the pictures. Family portraits, landscapes, horses—the things
one put upon stairs because nobody looked at them.

His room was cool and shadowy. The sun had withdrawn
from that side of the house, and the open window
admitted a smell of damp earth. William laid out his
dinner clothes and departed.

Mr. Neave sat before the dressing table.

There he was in the glass: a heavy old face,
grey moustache, pale folds beneath the eyes. Not ill. No.
But changed. He leaned closer.

When had that happened?

He remembered his father at sixty-eight, stern and broad in
a black coat, already an old man. But Mr. Neave
himself was sixty-eight. Curious. All his life old age had
remained his father's possession, an ugly coat hanging in another
man's cupboard. Now, somehow, he had put it on.

Well, well!

He began to undress. One boot came off. The other
resisted, and the effort left him breathless. He rested his
head against the chair.

Only for a moment.

From far below came a doorbell, quick footsteps, a burst
of greeting. The house trembled with arrivals. Or perhaps it
was not the house. Perhaps it was the street, the
whole city, vibrating with youth and spring.

Mr. Neave saw a staircase.

It rose steeply through darkness, step upon step, without banister
or landing. A little ancient fellow was climbing it. His
coat hung from his shoulders; his thin legs bent like
wire; one hand clutched a bundle of papers to his
chest.

Painfully he lifted one foot, then the other.

Who was the fellow? Some clerk kept too long? Someone
Mr. Neave ought to pension?

"Stop him," Mr. Neave tried to say. "That's enough. Tell
him to come down."

But the climber went on.

At the top was the dining room, brilliant with candles.
Charlotte and the girls sat among silver and flowers, laughing.
A chair waited at the end of the table.

The old fellow climbed toward it.

No—he had turned. Now the staircase led to the office.
Desks floated above him; ledgers opened their great black wings;
telephones rang from step to step.

He must get back. There was no one else. Harold
had not come. Harold had said—

What had Harold said?

The ancient fellow stumbled, caught himself, climbed again.

"Stop him, somebody!"

But the family only laughed. Their mouths opened and closed
soundlessly. Charlotte raised a glass. Marion wore yellow now, or
was it flame? Ethel's pearls ran like drops of water
down her dress.

The little fellow was Mr. Neave.

He knew this without surprise. He was watching himself climb
and was also inside that trembling body, feeling each step
strike upward through his bones. The papers beneath his arm
grew heavier. Contracts, mortgages, bills, invitations, dressmakers' accounts, school fees,
jewellery, journeys, flowers—everything the house had eaten, everything it still
demanded.

At the top Harold lounged in Mr. Neave's chair, young,
handsome, smiling.

"My dear governor," he said, "why do you take things
so seriously?"

Then the chair was empty.

The stairs continued.

Mr. Neave woke.

The room was dark.

For a time he did not move. He could hear
music downstairs—a piano touched lightly, then laughter. They had not
called him. Or they had called and he had not
answered. His shirt lay across his knees, and one foot
was still booted.

Outside, the last light had gone from the sky. The
window was a black square in which one pale curtain
stirred.

He did not know where he was.

That was his bed, surely; those were his brushes, his
watch, Charlotte's photograph in the silver frame. Yet each object
appeared to belong to a room prepared for him by
strangers. Even the photograph was strange. Charlotte smiled beneath a
broad hat, charming and composed. His wife. Yes.

But what did that mean?

Downstairs were his daughters, women with smooth arms and bright
voices. Harold, wherever he might be, was his son. They
possessed his name, his house, the years of his labour.
Still, he could not reach them. There was a clear
hard wall between them and him, like glass in the
dark.

Had he loved them? Certainly. He had provided, protected, arranged.
He had hurried from breakfast to office, office to dinner,
winter to summer, one year into the next. He had
signed cheques, approved schools, bought horses, taken houses by the
sea. Love had been included in all that. Naturally.

But where had his life gone while he was arranging
theirs?

A woman's face appeared before him.

Not Charlotte's.

A pale young face under a garden hat; dark eyes
wet with grief. Behind her, leaves of a passion-vine clung
to a veranda post, and purple flowers hung like little
wounded hearts. She held both his hands.

"You will forget," she whispered.

He could almost hear the insects humming in the hot
garden. He could smell dust and crushed mint beneath their
feet. He had been young—astonishingly young—and she had loved him.
Or he had loved her. There had been a farewell,
a journey, a promise he had not kept.

What was her name?

He bent toward her in memory, but the years rushed
between them: the wedding, the first office, Charlotte's parties, babies,
illnesses, accounts, Harold's school reports, larger premises, a larger house.
The pale girl withdrew behind it all, still watching him.

My true wife, he thought wildly. My lost life.

Then which life had been the dream? This house, this
family, this endless prosperous day? Had he fallen asleep in
that far garden and imagined thirty-seven years of work? Or
had he dreamed the girl because the darkness required one
person who had once seen him—not the provider, not the
governor, not poor old Father, but himself?

He lifted his hands. They were old hands.

From below came a tremendous burst of laughter. The chandelier
must be lighted now. Charlotte would be at the head
of the table, Ethel beside some young man, Marion performing
her despair. Harold's chair might still be empty.

An ideal family.

Mr. Neave smiled, though his mouth trembled.

A knock sounded, and William entered with quiet disapproval.

"Dinner is served, sir. Mrs. Neave asked me to say
they are waiting."

Waiting. At last they were waiting for him.

The servant switched on the light. The pale girl vanished.
The staircase became only the familiar carpeted passage beyond the
door.

Mr. Neave looked at his unfinished dressing, at the stubborn
boot, at his face startled in the mirror. For one
instant he wanted to cry out—to tell William that there
had been some mistake, that the years were wrong, that
another life was hidden just behind this one.

Instead he straightened his shoulders.

"Yes, yes," he said. "I'm coming."

William withdrew.

Mr. Neave rose carefully. From downstairs came the bright clink
of silver, and Charlotte's voice floated upward, affectionate, impatient:

"Where is he?"

"I'm coming," he called again.

And, gripping the banister, he began to descend.

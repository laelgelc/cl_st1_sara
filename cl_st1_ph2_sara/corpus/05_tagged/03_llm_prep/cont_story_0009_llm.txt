What makes a man fall in love with a house?

Loneliness, probably. Money helps.

I had both.

The agent telephoned on a wet Tuesday in January and
said she had found something unusual, by which she generally
meant something with bad wiring. But Harrowgate was unusual. It
sat above the river on two hundred acres of folded
pasture and exhausted orchard, a gray stone house built in
1791, with chimneys like raised fingers and thirteen windows across
the front. Thirteen exactly. I counted them before I got
out of the car.

Mel Hays was waiting under the portico. A narrow old
fellow in a green cardigan, no coat despite the rain.
He had white hair combed straight back and a face
so thoroughly lined it looked engraved.

You'll be Mr. Vale, he said.

I said I hoped so.

He laughed. We liked each other immediately.

Or I thought we did. Perhaps that is the first
vanity in this account—that I can identify the moment another
person comes to like me. I have always considered myself
perceptive. Often what I perceived was merely what I wanted.

Mel's wife was upstairs. Cancer, he told me quietly, as
the agent shook rain from her umbrella in the hall.
The medical expenses had been considerable. He did not dwell
on this. He touched the banister with two fingers and
said the walnut had come from the property, probably the
north woods, though the papers were unclear.

That was how we began: not with money, but walnut.

He took me through every room. The agent followed for
a while, then gave up and sat in the library
tapping at her telephone. Mel showed me musket scoring beside
the pantry door, an iron trivet made by a local
blacksmith, a cupboard where traveling clergy were said to have
hidden communion silver during the troubles. Which troubles? There are
always troubles if a house stands long enough.

Outside were two limestone hitching posts, a smokehouse leaning gallantly
toward collapse, and the execution oak.

Only one hanging, Mel said. People call it the execution
oak because it sounds better than the tree where a
horse thief was hanged once.

I admired him for that.

We climbed the back stairs, which wound so tightly I
had to turn my shoulders, and entered the servants' rooms
beneath the eaves. There were children's initials cut into one
sill, a wooden soldier without an arm, three dead flies
in a blue glass bottle. The roof sighed in the
wind. I could hear rain ticking over us, and I
felt—how can I say it without sounding like a fool?—received.

Yes. Received by the house.

Already I saw myself there. I would repair the orchard,
catalog the letters in the library, restore the smokehouse but
not too much. Restoration can be murder conducted with excellent
taste. I would lead guests through the passage behind the
dining room and casually mention that a governor had slept
there in 1814. I would become, not the owner exactly,
but the steward.

The distinction pleased me.

It was also nonsense. I wanted to own it.

At lunch Mel and I sat at a scrubbed pine
table while the agent discussed acreage and septic fields. Mel's
wife did not come down. Once we heard a bell
upstairs, and he left us. He returned ten minutes later
looking older, apologized, and poured coffee.

The price was low. Embarrassingly low. I could have paid
twice as much without selling anything I cared about. This
fact gave me the warm sensation of virtue.

I told the agent I was ready to proceed.

Mel held out his hand. His was cool and surprisingly
strong.

Then, still holding mine, he said there was one small
matter. His wife might not live to see the transfer
completed. Afterward, if I had no objection, would I permit
him to visit once in a while? Perhaps stay overnight.
Not often. A night in the small room over the
kitchen, maybe on the anniversary of their marriage.

I did not refuse.

This matters to me, apparently, so let me repeat it.
I did not refuse.

I hesitated.

Two seconds? Three? I looked toward the agent. I thought
of liability, boundaries, future guests, an old man appearing with
a suitcase whenever memory pinched him. I thought, too, though
I would not have admitted it then: When I buy
a thing, I want it to become mine.

Then I smiled and said, Of course. We can arrange
that.

Mel released my hand.

No, he said.

I thought he meant no need to arrange it formally.

He said, I won't sell.

The agent laughed—a nervous little bark. I told him he
had misunderstood. He said he had not. His face had
closed, not angrily but completely, like shutters before weather.

I raised my offer by fifty thousand dollars.

No.

By a hundred.

No.

I said his request was perfectly reasonable. I had only
been thinking.

Yes, he said. You were.

Meaning: I had been weighed, measured, and found to have
paused.

I drove home furious.

Not at first. At first I was wounded. Fury came
later, after I had explained the event to myself several
times and made him the villain. What sort of man
endangered his sick wife's security because another man took a
breath before answering? What sort of childish, brittle old tyrant
demanded instant tenderness as a condition of sale?

An odd duck, I told friends.

A tragic case.

Proud.

I did not mention the look I gave the agent.
I did not mention imagining his suitcase in my hall.

Mrs. Hays died in March.

I learned this from the obituary, which described Harrowgate as
the family home for five generations. I sent flowers. No
card. Or rather, there was the florist's card with my
printed name, but no message. At the time I considered
restraint respectful.

In April I wrote Mel.

Dear Mr. Hays, I began, though we had been Mel
and Daniel during the tour. I expressed sorrow. I spoke
of misunderstandings and renewed opportunities. I said my offer remained
open and that I would be honored to preserve Harrowgate
in the manner it deserved.

Not in the manner he deserved. The house.

He did not answer.

I began driving past. There was no practical reason. Harrowgate
lay eighteen miles beyond the turn for my office. Still,
on bright mornings I found myself taking River Road.

In spring, dandelions covered the south lawn. In June, one
shutter hung loose from the west wing. By August the
gutters spilled black weeds. I sometimes saw Mel's truck beside
the kitchen door, sometimes a light in the upstairs room,
never Mel himself.

I wrote again.

This letter was less graceful. I reminded him that old
houses require capital, that sentiment is not maintenance, that his
refusal harmed the very property he claimed to love. I
used the phrase mutual responsibility, though there was nothing mutual
between us. I offered still more money.

No answer.

October brought a storm. Part of the smokehouse roof collapsed,
and a blue tarp appeared over it, badly tied. The
orchard grass rose waist-high. Wild turkeys wandered across the drive,
huge stupid creatures, stepping through the ruins of cultivation like
miniature prehistoric kings.

I laughed when I saw them.

Then I pulled over and cried.

Just for a minute. Because of the house, I said.

Well. Not only the house.

My third letter accused him openly. Pride, I wrote, was
not principle. Obstinacy was not love. If Harrowgate fell because
he preferred decay to compromise, history would know whom to
blame.

History. As if committees would convene.

I mailed that one.

Years passed. Not many, though decay is industrious. The porch
sank at its eastern corner. Frost opened a crack in
one column. A sycamore branch broke a bedroom window, and
for an entire winter a square of plywood stared toward
the road like a blind brown eye.

I made offers through intermediaries. Mel rejected them. Once, at
a charity dinner, a banker told me Mel had asked
about selling timber. I said, What a pity. The banker
looked at me oddly.

My fourth letter was cruel.

I told him his wife would have been ashamed. I
said he had turned her home into a monument to
his spite. I described the cracked columns, the rotten gutter
packed with leaves and bird filth, the roofline sagging over
the servants' rooms. I imagined myself saving all of it
and him watching from some cramped apartment while workmen restored
what he had failed to protect.

To my credit, I did not send it.

There. You hear that? To my credit. I still want
a coin for declining to strike an old man with
his dead wife.

The letter remains in my desk.

Now I am dying.

It is not dramatic. No blood on handkerchiefs, no physician
lowering his eyes at the bedside. Pancreas. Liver. Words like
metastasis and palliative, words engineered to move smoothly past terror.
I have months, perhaps weeks. My body has become a
badly managed estate.

Last Sunday I drove to Harrowgate.

The agent—long retired—had told me Mel was still there. Ninety-one
now. Nearly deaf. The house looked smaller than I remembered,
which was unfair. The central roof had fallen inward. Virginia
creeper covered the portico, and one hitching post lay on
its side. Turkeys occupied the lawn. Naturally.

I parked at the road.

I could have gone to the door.

I did not.

I sat with the engine running and imagined a final
letter.

Mel,

We were fools.

No. Too easy, that we. I was a fool. You
asked whether you might remain attached to the place where
you had loved your wife, and I treated your grief
like a clause to be reviewed. I agreed, yes, but
you heard the pause before it, and the pause was
true.

You were proud afterward. So was I. Your pride kept
the house; mine kept the wound. Between us we preserved
nothing.

I forgive you.

No. That is wrong too, or only half right. Forgiveness
is not a medal I pin upon myself. Forgive me.

The house will go. If not this winter, another. The
oak will go, despite its famous dead horse thief. The
walnut rail will darken, split, burn, or be carried off.
Those children's initials will vanish. You will go. I am
almost gone already.

And whatever judgment I have held against you will go
with me unless I put it down first.

Friend who might have been. Friend I should have been.

Pray for me, if you still pray. I will pray
for you.

That is the letter I compose at night while the
medicines fail and the walls make small settling sounds.

I have not written it.

Not yet.

Because I am tired.

Well, that is not true. I am not too tired.
Because the hand shakes. Also not true; I can dictate.
Because he may not understand, may tear it up, may
die before it arrives.

Excuses.

The truth is that some hard little property in me
still insists I was wronged first.

So I wait, while he waits in his collapsing house
and I wait in my collapsing body, each of us
guarding what cannot be kept.

Tomorrow I will write.

I will.

Only I must not wait too long.

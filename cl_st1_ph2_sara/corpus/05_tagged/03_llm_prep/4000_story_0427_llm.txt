### The Sculptor's Light

In the reign of King Charles the Eighth, when France
was full of Italian songs, Italian poisons, Italian brocades, and
Italian men of genius who possessed everything except money, there
came to the court at Amboise a young Florentine sculptor
named Angelo Cappara. He was then in the April of
his life, having scarcely counted twenty-three years; handsome in the
grave Tuscan manner, with black curls, long hands, and eyes
which seemed able to discover a Venus within the roughest
block of marble, though they could not look for ten
seconds into the eyes of a living woman.

He lodged under a tiler's roof near the river, ate
onions more often than capons, and owned one velvet doublet
whose elbows he concealed with such skill that many believed
it to be new. Yet the King praised his figures,
the courtiers disputed for his medals, and the high-born ladies
came into his workshop on the pretence of observing how
a chisel was held. They admired his naked gods very
much, and the naked sculptor—at least in their imaginations—still more.

Angelo, however, was painfully timid. When a duchess asked whether
he loved beauty, he answered, "Yes." When she inquired whether
beauty had ever loved him, he answered, "No." When she
asked if he desired that this unhappy condition should be
amended, he became red to the ears, struck the nose
from a clay Apollo, and said nothing at all.

Now shyness is a lock to which women are always
trying keys, particularly when there is nothing worth guarding behind
it. I am wrong: there was much behind Angelo's silence—whole
gardens, palaces, moonlit chambers, and beds hung with crimson damask.
In solitude he was more eloquent than Cicero. He imagined
how he would worship a mistress, honour her, obey her,
write verses upon her slipper, carve her shoulders in alabaster,
defend her reputation with his sword, and amuse her during
rain. But when any lady came near enough to be
worshipped, honoured, obeyed, carved, defended, or amused, his heart jumped
like a goat and his tongue lay dead in his
mouth.

This condition made him melancholy. He walked beside the Loire
like a dove left solitary in its nest; he sighed
over marble breasts; and his statues acquired such warmth in
the hips and such tenderness about the lips that certain
churchmen complained of them, though they returned repeatedly to inspect
the offence. At the same time, his distraction caused him
to spoil three noses in one fortnight. Thus love, even
before it arrives, improves the flesh and damages the face.

Among the ladies who visited his workshop was Madame de
Vaurennes, wife to a gentleman attached to the King's household.
She was beautiful enough to make virtue inconvenient. Her hair
had the colour of ripe wheat, her neck that of
new cream, and her mouth was one of those small
red gates through which folly enters a man and wisdom
goes out. Her husband was rich, jealous, and frequently employed
at court, three circumstances which furnish the beginning of many
histories and the end of more reputations.

She watched Angelo work upon a little figure of Diana.

"You make women very fair, Master Angelo."

"I copy what I see, madame."

This was his first complete sentence to her, and she
valued it as a captain values the first breach in
a fortress.

"Then you must see me often," she replied.

He struck the chisel upon his thumb.

Some days later, a page brought him a message commanding—inviting,
I should say—him to visit Madame de Vaurennes at her
lodging after dusk, when her husband would be occupied with
the King. Angelo read the note twelve times. He perfumed
himself with a costly essence purchased upon credit, brushed his
unfortunate doublet, borrowed a cloak, and went through the streets
believing every passer-by knew where he was going and why.

The lady received him in a small chamber lighted by
two candles. There were cushions, sweetmeats, wine, a fire, and
a bed whose curtains appeared to Angelo more magnificent than
the gates of Paradise.

"You are frightened," she said.

"No."

"You tremble."

"Yes."

"Do you love me?"

At this he looked upon her, and all his imprisoned
eloquence rushed to the bars.

"With my soul, madame; with my blood; with all that
I am and all that my art might make me.
I have loved you since before I knew you, for
every beautiful thing I ever dreamed was but a herald
announcing your face."

This was well said, especially by a man who had
lately been unable to name a stool in female company.
The lady rewarded him with her hand, then her cheek,
and at last her mouth. Angelo, who had entered the
chamber a sculptor, became fire, hunger, trumpet, siege engine, and
victorious army within the space of one kiss.

He knelt. She raised him. He embraced her. She permitted
his hand to travel some distance through the provinces of
love, then arrested it at the frontier.

"My husband!" she whispered.

There was a step upon the stair.

Angelo was thrust behind a curtain, removed through a servant's
passage, and sent home in a state resembling that of
a thirsty prisoner who, having glimpsed green fields and a
fountain through the bars, is given a cup of salt.

The next day she sent him a rose.

Thereafter he visited her whenever Monsieur de Vaurennes was believed
to be absent. Each visit followed the same campaign. The
lady allowed a skirmish of the lips, an advance of
the hands, perhaps even the capture of some outwork of
the skirt; but just as Angelo prepared to enter the
garden of love, there came a noise, a bell, a
servant's cough, or the dreadful cry:

"My husband!"

Sometimes the husband was indeed below. Sometimes he was merely
expected. Sometimes, as everyone knows happens in such affairs, he
existed only in the lady's prudence. Yet Angelo accepted every
retreat because she restored his courage with tears, kisses, and
declarations that she loved him beyond honour, heaven, and life
itself.

A month passed in this fashion. He grew thin. His
statues grew voluptuous. He carved a Magdalen whose expression caused
the Queen's ladies to laugh, though they would not say
why. He began a Saint Sebastian and gave the saint
Madame de Vaurennes's mouth; then, in jealousy of the arrows
that touched the figure, he broke it to pieces.

"My life is being eaten by teaspoons," he told her.
"You give me promises as misers give alms, one little
coin at a time, while keeping the treasure locked."

"Ungrateful man! Do I not risk everything?"

"You risk a kiss. I risk my reason."

"I am a woman of honour."

"Then why have you taught me dishonour?"

She wept, and Angelo immediately begged pardon. Tears are the
artillery of beautiful women: they cost little powder and make
great breaches.

At last she swore that, if only her husband could
be kept away until the house slept, she would deny
Angelo nothing. She named a certain Thursday upon which Monsieur
de Vaurennes was to dine with officers of the King.
Angelo, whose desire had now studied strategy, bribed two merry
companions to detain the husband with dice, wine, quarrels, and,
if necessary, a feigned injury. When all was quiet, the
lady was to place a lamp in the high window
overlooking the lane. Angelo would see it and enter by
the garden door.

Thursday came. His friends met Monsieur de Vaurennes and carried
him to a tavern. They drank to Lombardy, to France,
to the King, to every saint who tolerated gambling, and
to several ladies whom no saint could have tolerated. The
husband settled himself over the dice with the obstinacy of
a man who intended not to go home before dawn.

Angelo hurried to the lane.

The evening bell had scarcely ceased when a light appeared
in Madame de Vaurennes's window.

He stared at it.

No household could yet be sleeping. The servants had barely
cleared supper. Her husband had not been absent an hour.
According to their agreement, the lamp meant that all was
safe; but being shown so soon, it meant plainly: Do
not come. Danger exists, and I prefer my skin to
thy heart.

The little flame burned above him, yellow, calm, and treacherous.
In that instant Angelo understood—or believed he understood, which in
love amounts to the same thing—that all her vows had
been ornaments, her tears warm water, her kisses bait upon
a hook. She desired him enough to excite herself, not
enough to incur peril; enough to keep him jumping outside
the hedge, not enough to open the gate.

He waited until midnight. The lamp remained.

Then he went away, carrying hell beneath his borrowed cloak.

For two days he neither ate nor worked. On the
third he placed a narrow sculptor's knife in his sleeve
and sent word that he must see her, if only
to say farewell. Madame de Vaurennes, perhaps curious, perhaps remorseful,
perhaps merely bored—for these three sisters often wear one another's
gowns—admitted him.

She sat before a mirror while a servant arranged her
hair. At Angelo's entrance she dismissed the girl and turned,
smiling.

"My poor Florentine! Why did you not come?"

"The light was already in the window."

"Yes. I feared my husband might return."

"He could not. My friends held him at dice."

Her smile changed its dress but did not depart.

"How could I know that?"

"You knew my signal. You knew my life hung upon
that lamp."

"Do not speak like a tragedy. You are here now."

She offered her hand. He did not take it.

"You told me thou wouldst hazard heaven, honour, and life."

"Must lovers weigh every foolish word spoken between kisses?"

"To thee they were words. To me they were sacraments."

"Oh, Angelo!"

She rose and touched his cheek. At that accustomed kindness
his body betrayed him; desire came flooding back, hot and
humiliated. She saw it and smiled with renewed confidence.

"Come," she murmured. "My husband is at the palace. We
have time."

But Angelo stepped away.

"No. When I begged, thou gavest me fear. Now that
I accuse, thou wouldst give me pity. I will have
neither."

"What do you want?"

"That thou remember me."

She laughed uneasily. "Have I not said I shall?"

"Words again."

He drew the little knife.

Her face went white.

"Silence, madame. I have carved beauty all my life, and
therefore I know where beauty lives. It lives not in
the soul, as poets lie, but here—in the smooth cheek,
in the proud line from eye to mouth, in that
soft kingdom for which men kneel and women bargain. Your
have made my life a dungeon. I shall make thy
mirror a witness. When others look away, thou wilt remember
the lover at whom thou smiled. When thy husband asks
where the scar came from, thou mayst tell him it
was the signature of a fool."

"You wretch!"

She fled toward the door. He caught her wrist.

Then, seeing that his purpose was not a lover's theatrical
threat but something colder, she flung her arms about him.

"Take me! I am yours. See, I refuse nothing. Lock
the door. Stay until morning. Only put away the knife."

For one terrible moment the garden stood open. Every gate
had fallen; every fruit was offered. Angelo looked upon the
bed, upon her loosened hair, upon the mouth that had
tormented his sleep. His heart leaped toward her.

Then he laughed.

"Now I know. Thou lovest thy face more than thou
lovest me."

Before she could cry out, he drew the blade once
across her left cheek.

It was not a deep wound, but it was long
enough. A red line appeared upon the whiteness as sudden
as sunset upon snow. She screamed. Angelo released her, stared
at what he had done with the astonishment common to
men who mistake imagination for resolve until resolve becomes an
act, and fled down the stair.

I shall not excuse him. A lover may be deceived
without becoming a butcher; many are so deceived every day
and continue to dine well. Yet there was in his
crime something of that fierce folly which courts admire from
a safe distance.

Monsieur de Vaurennes demanded justice. Angelo was captured before he
reached Tours, brought back in irons, and condemned to hang.
The lady's wound healed, though it left a slender mark
which powders could soften but not abolish.

At court the affair made a prodigious noise. Some condemned
the sculptor, others the lady, and most condemned both while
longing to know every particular. One noblewoman, the widowed Countess
de Brézé, had long admired Angelo's beauty, genius, and silence.
Now she admired his passion also, since women sometimes mistake
violence committed for another woman as proof of devotion available
to themselves.

She knelt before the King.

"Sire, France has enough ropes and too few artists. Give
me this mad Florentine, and I shall answer for his
conduct."

Charles, who loved art, romance, and pardons that cost him
nothing, granted her petition. Angelo was brought from prison, washed,
dressed, and delivered into the Countess's household as though he
were a dangerous statue newly purchased from Italy.

She offered him rooms, money, protection, and, without using vulgar
terms, that especial thing for which he had nearly lost
his neck.

Angelo bowed.

"Madame, I owe you my life."

"Then let your life be mine."

"It cannot."

"Do you still love that woman?"

"With all my misery."

"She betrayed you."

"Yes."

"She caused your ruin."

"Yes."

"She would have seen you hanged."

"Perhaps."

"And yet you refuse me for her?"

"Madame, the heart is not honest merchandise. It cannot repay
a debt merely because payment would be convenient."

This reply displeased the Countess, as noble generosity commonly expects
lucre. Nevertheless, she did not return him to the gallows,
which proves her better than many.

Angelo abandoned sculpture and entered the Church. Whether he sought
penitence, safety, or a robe beneath which the old fire
might burn unseen, no man can say. He studied theology,
Greek, canon law, and the ancient philosophers; he became a
bishop, then a cardinal, and was renowned throughout Christendom for
his learning. His enemies said he understood marble better than
mercy. His friends declared the scar upon Madame de Vaurennes's
cheek had cut wisdom into his own heart.

In old age, when he possessed palaces, servants, books, and
purple silk enough to clothe all the poverty of his
youth, he kept upon his table a small lamp of
Florentine workmanship. Sometimes, after supper, he ordered it lighted and
dismissed everyone. Then the great cardinal sat alone, watching the
flame, remembering a high window at Amboise, a woman's mouth,
a garden never entered, and the cruel sweetness of being
young and over head and ears in love.

One account says that Madame de Vaurennes forgave him. Another
says she cursed his name until death. A third declares
that, being still beautiful, she made the scar fashionable, so
that ladies drew crimson lines upon their cheeks at masques.
I believe this last version, because vanity is the only
wound that can turn a wound into ornament.

Thus Angelo gained honour, wisdom, and holy office, but never
again that brief torment which had made him both more
alive and less human. This teaches us nothing worth knowing,
except perhaps that a lamp may lie, a kiss may
lend money at ruinous interest, and a man who can
shape a perfect face in stone may still destroy one
in flesh.

And the tale, notwithstanding its absurdities, is in every way
true.

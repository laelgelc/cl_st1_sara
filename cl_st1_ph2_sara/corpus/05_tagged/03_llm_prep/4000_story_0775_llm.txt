The River's Promise

On the last evening before I went away to teach
the winter term at Willow Creek, five miles beyond the
last railway siding and nearly forty from Sandtown, I camped
with the boys on an island which had not existed
in April. The river had made it during the June
freshet, first laying down a pale hook of sand behind
a stranded cottonwood, then spreading the hook into a bar,
and finally clothing its borders with willow seedlings and little
yellow-green weeds that stood no higher than our knees. We
had watched it grow all summer. Because it was new,
and because no farmer had yet measured or claimed it,
we felt that it belonged to us.

The Platte was always making and unmaking things in those
days. It took a corner from one man's cornfield and
gave another man half an acre of white sand; it
opened a channel where cattle had crossed in May, and
by September abandoned an old current to mud, frogs, and
blackbirds. Seen at noon, when the low water slipped over
its many beds of gravel, the river seemed shallow and
complaisant, chuckling at the blue herons that stepped through it.
At night it changed. The separate currents gathered one voice,
and that voice was deep and doleful, as though all
the water that had ever gone down to the Missouri
regretted the country it was leaving.

We crossed at sundown in the Hasslers' flat-bottomed fishing boat,
taking our blankets, a coffee pot, two loaves of bread,
a slab of bacon, six roasting ears stolen with the
farmer's tacit permission, and Otto Krause's little hatchet. The channel
nearest town was already blue with evening, but the western
water held the sunlight and tossed it among the sand
bars in broken sheets of brass. From the high bank
came the smell of ripe corn, dust, and rank ironweed.
Somewhere in the fields a man was calling cows, and
his cries travelled farther than he could have intended, becoming
lonely and important in the dusk.

There were six of us, counting me. Tip Smith sat
in the bow, holding his bundle between his bony knees
and looking down into the water. He was a narrow,
brown boy with straight hair that never stayed parted and
wrists which showed too far beyond his sleeves. His father
painted houses when there were houses to paint, and in
slack seasons sharpened tools and mended clocks. Tip collected curious
things: tobacco tags, bird eggs, foreign stamps, blue bottles from
missionaries, bits of ore, and pictures cut from railway folders.
He never talked much until some idea had taken entire
possession of him; then his shyness fell away, and he
spoke with a stubborn ardour that made us listen.

Otto Krause, who rowed, was broad in the shoulders and
already had a man's red hands. He knew the price
of hogs and binder twine, could guess the weight of
a steer within twenty pounds, and considered every distant country
chiefly with reference to what might be raised or dug
there. Percy Bell wore spectacles which were always slipping down
his small nose. He read detective papers behind the counter
of his mother's grocery and accepted everything printed in them,
including secret brotherhoods in Borneo and white queens in Central
Africa. Joe Hassler, owner by inheritance of the boat, was
long-legged, deliberate, and smelled agreeably of river mud and fish
scales. Arthur Lane, the oldest of us by some months,
lay in the stern with his hands under his head.
He had a pale, good-humoured face and a pleasant voice,
and could read poetry without making us ashamed of him.

As for me, I was seventeen and considered myself nearly
gone from boyhood because the county superintendent had given me
a certificate and the trustees at Willow Creek had agreed
to pay me thirty-five dollars a month. Yet when the
boat grated upon the island and Joe leaped out with
the chain, I forgot the bleak schoolhouse, the room I
was to occupy over a widow's kitchen, and the twenty-seven
children whose names I had been studying. I was only
one of the six again, running barefoot over cool sand
while Arthur and Percy gathered driftwood.

We built our fire in a hollow left by the
spring water, where three old logs had lodged together. Behind
us the young willows whispered with the least movement of
air; before us the river divided, one current flowing dark
and smooth beneath the town bank, the other breaking over
gravel in a strip of golden turbulence. We fried the
bacon in Joe's black pan, roasted the corn in its
husks, and made coffee so strong that Otto said it
would float a horseshoe. The bread got sandy, as food
always did on the river, but no supper I have
eaten since has tasted better.

Beyond the cottonwoods on the mainland, the town lamps came
out one by one. We could distinguish the red lantern
at the railway switch and, much later, hear the westbound
freight labouring up the grade. Its whistle seemed to open
a door through the night. We stopped talking and listened
until the last faint concussion of the wheels had passed
beyond the grain elevators.

"Tomorrow night you'll be clear out there," Percy said to
me.

"Willow Creek ain't clear out anywhere," Otto objected. "It's only
forty miles."

"It's forty miles from us," Tip said.

That gave the distance dignity. We lay around the fire,
feeding it willow branches that flamed quickly and died into
feathery ash. Arthur asked me whether I meant to stay
in Willow Creek all winter. I said I supposed I
did, unless the children killed me. Joe advised me to
keep the bigger boys after school and thrash them separately.
Percy said country schools were sometimes haunted, especially if built
near old emigrant trails. Otto wanted to know what I
would save out of my salary.

"Enough to go somewhere next summer," I told him.

"Where?"

"Any place. Colorado, maybe. Or Mexico."

"Mexico's full of silver," Otto said. "My uncle says there
are mines down there nobody's worked since the Spaniards."

"There are ruins, too," Arthur added. "Cities older than anything
in Europe."

Percy pushed his spectacles up. "They sacrifice people yet in
some parts. I read about a traveller they caught and
tied on a stone."

"Maybe he was a liar," Joe said.

Arthur laughed softly. "Then he was probably a traveller."

The stars had multiplied while we talked. They hung low
over the plains, not scattered upon the sky so much
as burning through it, and the Milky Way made a
pale road from the northeastern bluffs to the open prairie
beyond the river. We tried to find the constellations Arthur
knew, but soon abandoned them and began inventing our own:
Otto's Plough, Percy's Revolver, the Great Catfish, the Schoolmaster's Switch.
A red star near the horizon became a campfire in
Arizona, and a dark interval between two cloudy reaches of
the Milky Way was the canyon where ancient people had
hidden their gold.

It was Tip who first spoke of the old nations.
He had been lying on his stomach, drawing lines in
the sand with a charred stick.

"Do you suppose," he asked, "there are places nobody's ever
got to yet?"

"Sure," said Joe. "Up toward the North Pole."

"I don't mean ice. I mean places right in the
country, with grass and trees, that nobody can get onto."

Otto considered this. "If there's grass, cattle can get there.
And if cattle can, men can."

"Not if it's straight up all around."

Percy rolled nearer the fire. "You mean a mountain?"

"No. A kind of table. Uncle Bill saw it when
he was hauling freight in New Mexico. He saw it
three times, but he never got close."

We knew Tip's Uncle Bill chiefly as a disreputable figure
who appeared at long intervals in a sun-faded coat, slept
two days in the Smiths' shed, and departed before anyone
could ask him to work. He had prospected, punched cattle,
carried mail, and once, according to Tip, eaten Christmas dinner
with a Mexican governor. The elders of Sandtown placed little
reliance upon his reports; we placed a great deal.

"What's on it?" Percy demanded.

Tip sat up. Firelight struck one side of his thin
face and left the other black.

"Nobody knows. That's the point."

Then, because we laughed, he grew offended and would say
no more until Arthur passed him the coffee pot and
remarked that any place Uncle Bill had failed to reach
must be interesting. Tip took a swallow, wiped his mouth
on his sleeve, and began again.

It lay, he said, in a waste country west of
the Pecos, beyond a range of low black hills where
nothing grew but thorn and bunch grass. For two days
a traveller crossed red flats, the earth opening here and
there into dry gulches, and on the third morning he
saw the bluff. It stood alone, miles from the broken
hills, rising out of the plain in one tremendous block.
Its sides were red stone, darker than brick and brighter
than blood when the sun struck them, and so steep
that even the eagles nested below the summit. The top
was level and green. After rain, small waterfalls spilled over
the rim and vanished in the sand before they reached
the bottom.

"How high?" Otto interrupted.

"Uncle Bill said near a thousand feet."

"Then he lied by half," Joe observed.

Tip ignored him. Long ago, before the Spaniards came and
before the tribes carried guns, a peaceful people had lived
upon that summit. They were farmers and potters, small dark
men who planted maize and beans in the deep soil
above, while their women wove bright blankets and made jars
painted with birds. There was a spring in the centre
of their village and cedar trees along the northern edge.
From their housetops they could see enemies crossing the plain
two days before they arrived.

"But how did they get up?" Percy asked.

"There was a stairway."

It had been cut into a narrow seam in the
eastern wall, Tip explained, so cunningly that from below one
saw only shadow. The first builders had driven pegs into
the rock, laid poles from peg to peg, and carried
baskets of broken stone until they made steps broad enough
for two men to climb together. At the bottom stood
a gate between boulders; at the top another gate opened
directly into the village. Every night the people drew up
the lowest ladders, and no enemy could follow.

For generations they lived securely. In spring the fields upon
the summit became green before the desert below; in summer
the corn stood higher than a man's head; at harvest
the people lighted fires along the rim, and from far
out on the plain the bluff looked like a great
altar with stars burning upon it. They traded turquoise and
painted bowls with friendly tribes, and sent their hunters into
the hills for deer.

One autumn nearly all the able men descended for a
long hunt. Only old men, boys, women, and small children
remained above. The hunters had been gone four days when
clouds came out of the southwest. All afternoon the people
watched them piling against the sky—black underneath, copper at the
edges—and by sunset the air smelled of wet stone though
no rain had fallen. Then the storm broke.

Tip's voice grew lower. We forgot the cheerful fire before
us and watched instead that invisible storm sweep over the
red country. Rain fell for a night and a day.
Water poured down the hidden seam, loosening the poles and
tearing out the stonework; lightning split the upper gate; and
toward dawn an entire shelf of the bluff gave way.
The stairway went down in a roaring slide that buried
the foot of the wall beneath hundreds of tons of
rock.

When the sky cleared, the people above came to the
rim. Their stair was gone. They called into the empty
plain, but the hunters were many miles away.

"They could build another," Otto said.

"How?" Tip demanded. "All their strong men were gone, and
they couldn't get to the bottom to start."

"They could let ropes down."

"They had ropes, but none long enough. And the wall
bellied outward under the edge, so a rope only hung
in the air."

Arthur, who had been smiling earlier, no longer smiled. "What
happened to the hunters?"

Their enemies had seen them depart. A war party followed,
waited until the hunt was finished and the men were
burdened with meat, then attacked in a narrow canyon. Not
one hunter escaped. Weeks passed before the people upon the
bluff understood. Each morning they went to the eastern rim
expecting to see their men returning across the plain. Each
evening they went home silently.

They had corn stored for winter, Tip said, and at
first they were not afraid. But the storm had choked
the spring with fallen rock. It continued to give water,
though less every day. They killed their dogs and goats.
They ground seed corn, then roots, then the bark of
cedar trees. The old people refused food so the children
might live. Signals were built upon the rim, but no
friendly tribe came near enough to see them, and the
hostile people who did come only camped beyond bowshot and
watched.

By spring there was no smoke above the bluff.

Many years later, wandering shepherds found fragments of painted pottery
at the base, washed down by the rains. They called
the place Mesa de los Encantados, because they believed the
village remained under enchantment. Even now, Tip said, a man
standing far away at sunset could sometimes see walls along
the summit and what looked like a white robe moving
between the cedars. But no one had climbed it since
the stairway fell. Prospectors had circled it, soldiers had fired
ropes over projections, and one Mexican had tried to ascend
in a basket carried by a balloon. The winds beat
them all. The top remained green and silent, keeping whatever
the vanished people had left there.

For a while none of us spoke. The river went
on gurgling over the gravel, but its familiar voice seemed
to come from a great distance. Beyond the willows a
mourning dove called once, was silent, and called again.

"There'd be bones," Percy said at last.

"And turquoise," Otto added. "Likely jars full of it."

"Maybe records," Arthur said. "Pictures telling who they were."

Joe turned a coal over with a stick. "Likely there's
nothing but rattlesnakes."

Tip leaned toward us. "I know how to get up."

That was the declaration for which his whole story had
been preparing us. He took from his pocket a dirty
scrap of paper and spread it on the sand. By
the light of two matches we saw a drawing of
the bluff, square as a trunk, with a black scratch
hanging over one edge.

"A kite," Tip said.

Otto laughed. "You can't ride a kite a thousand feet."

"Not ride it. Listen. You make a big box kite,
bigger than Joe's boat, and fly it from the windward
side. It carries fishing line over the top. When it
drops beyond the other edge, somebody goes around and catches
the line. Then you use that to pull over twine,
and the twine pulls a rope. You drag the rope
back until it catches around a cedar or a rock."

"It might not catch," I said.

"Then you pull over another with an iron hook. When
one holds, you climb with a sling around your waist.
After the first fellow gets up, he fastens a rope
ladder."

Joe scratched his head. "Who catches the kite line if
it falls on top?"

Tip frowned. "You don't let it. You fly it clear
across."

"Suppose the wind changes?"

"You wait for the right wind."

This answer satisfied us more than it should have. All
adventures, we believed, required only the right wind, a length
of stout rope, and the resolution to begin. We bent
over Tip's diagram, correcting and enlarging it. Otto proposed a
windlass; Joe insisted upon tested knots; Percy wanted rifles in
case the hostile tribe still guarded the mesa. Arthur said
we should take cameras, notebooks, and plaster for making casts
of inscriptions. I undertook to secure leave from whichever school
employed me and to meet the others in Sandtown on
the first of June.

"Which June?" Arthur asked.

"First one we're all free."

"That might be a long time."

"Then whoever gets there first goes up," Tip said. "And
he's got to come back and tell the rest."

We made the pledge formally, our hands joined above the
dying fire. If any one of us reached the red
bluff, he was to send word to the others before
he climbed, if possible; but whether he went alone or
with strangers, whether he found treasure, bones, painted jars, inscriptions,
or nothing at all, he must return and tell us
exactly what lay upon the summit. Percy suggested we sign
in blood. Joe said sand in the cut would poison
us, and Arthur observed that six solemn handshakes ought to
bind honest men. So we shook hands all around, each
boy looking more serious than the one before him.

The moon rose after midnight, enormous and red, lifting over
the cornfields like a barbaric ship under full sail. Its
light silvered one channel and turned the other black. Our
fire sank to a basin of crimson embers. Wrapped in
our blankets, we continued to talk at intervals—of routes through
New Mexico, provisions, money, pack animals, and the disposition of
the treasure Otto was certain we should find. Gradually the
voices ceased. Joe and Percy slept first; then Otto's breathing
became heavy. Tip lay awake beside me, staring at the
moonlit willows.

"You think my way would work?" he whispered.

"I think one of them would."

"I'm going there," he said. "Even if I have to
go alone."

At seventeen, I could not imagine a chain strong enough
to hold him.

Near dawn a cool wind came upstream. The moon had
whitened and diminished; the stars withdrew into the azure of
morning, and mist formed over the shallow channels in long,
level bands. We rose stiff and sandy, cooked the remaining
bacon, and watched three pelicans descend upon an upstream bar.
They came out of the grey air with solemn wings,
their bodies catching the first rose-coloured light. For one instant
the whole river—sand, mist, birds, and running water—seemed to move
westward instead of east, toward the mountains and the red
tableland beyond them.

Then the sun appeared, the town windows flashed, and someone
on the bank called that the boat was wanted.

At Christmas, when I came home from Willow Creek, we
renewed the pledge in the back room of Mrs. Bell's
grocery. Snow was packed against the windows, and barrels of
apples and kerosene gave the room a close, domestic smell.
We had changed a little in four months. I wore
a stiff collar; Otto had begun working full-time at the
stockyards; Arthur was coughing from a cold that never quite
left him. Yet Tip brought out the same creased drawing,
and for an hour the red bluff rose among the
pickle jars and flour sacks, more real to us than
the frozen streets outside.

That was the last time all six of us were
together.

Twenty years are easily written and variously lived. I taught
three winters, studied law, and eventually settled in a city
where the river was confined between stone embankments and crossed
by so many bridges that one scarcely noticed the water.
Each year I meant to travel west. Each year there
were examinations, a partnership, a sick parent, a marriage, children,
debts, and the innumerable small claims by which a life,
without seeming to choose, chooses its direction.

Otto prospered. He owned an implement business, then two grain
elevators, and travelled as far as Omaha and Kansas City,
always returning on the night train because no clerk could
be trusted with the books. He continued to believe there
was wealth upon the bluff, but considered the expense of
seeking it unsound.

Joe Hassler lost a leg when a cable snapped at
the sand works. Afterward he kept fishing boats for hire
and knew every channel the river made, though he seldom
crossed the county line.

Percy bought one of the first automobiles in Sandtown. He
drove it over all the roads within a hundred miles
and spoke of these excursions as expeditions, but he disliked
trains, hotels, unfamiliar food, and any highway on which a
mechanic could not be found before dark.

Arthur Lane died at twenty-three. His family buried him on
the hill above the river, beneath a cottonwood whose leaves
are never still, even when the air below is motionless.
Among his few books I found, years later, a railway
map of New Mexico with a red circle drawn in
the western emptiness.

Tip came nearest to going and yet was held most
tightly. His father died, leaving debts and three younger children.
Tip painted houses, repaired clocks, and married a girl who
had been delicate from childhood. There were seasons when he
had money saved for the journey, but then a baby
came, or his wife required a doctor, or work failed.
The kite plan remained in a cigar box with foreign
stamps, tobacco tags, and a shard of painted pottery Uncle
Bill had once sent from Santa Fe.

When I saw Tip last, his hair was thinning and
there were white streaks above his ears. He sat on
his back steps at dusk, winding copper wire around the
armature of a small motor. His son, a sharp-faced boy
of nine, was building a kite from laths and newspaper.

"Pa says there's a place in New Mexico nobody's ever
climbed," the child told me. "We're going when I'm big
enough."

Tip looked up, embarrassed, but the old light had returned
to his eyes.

"He knows the way," he said.

Beyond the houses, hidden by warehouses and the railway embankment,
the river moved through its new channels. We could not
see it, but presently we heard it—a low, restless sound
under the evening noises of Sandtown, full of distance and
inconsolable regret. The boy lifted his unfinished kite against the
sky to test its shape, and Tip watched him with
grave attention, as though already feeling for the right wind.

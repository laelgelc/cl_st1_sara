# The Barlow Decoration

On the Saturday evening before Decoration Day, John Stover and
Henry Merrill sat upon the broad step of Asa Brown's
store and looked across the village toward the west. There
was not much of Barlow to look across. The road
came down from the north between gray stone walls and
fields lately ploughed, widened itself before the store and meeting-house,
and then went on again, as if somewhat ashamed of
having made so much account of the village. Four elms
stood about the open square; the meeting-house belfry rose above
them, with a gilt weather vane that had long ago
ceased to glitter except at sunset; and beyond the last
roofs the country fell away to a low interval, green
with young grass, and afterward climbed through budding woods to
the blue western mountains.

It had been a backward spring. Snow had lain in
the north hollows until the first week of May, and
the alders along Barlow Brook were only now showing their
leaves. Yet the air was mild, and had that soft,
earthy sweetness which comes when the ground has at last
consented to another year. The farmers were behind with their
planting. Every man in Barlow who had a piece of
land was tired, anxious, and unwilling to lose an hour.

John Stover leaned forward, his heavy hands hanging between his
knees. He was a square, silent man, with a gray
beard and one shoulder higher than the other. Henry Merrill,
who had come down from the Ridge for a half-pound
of tea and a paper of saleratus, sat beside him
with his hat pushed back. Henry's face was narrow and
kindly, and his eyes had kept their youthful look in
spite of weather and grief. Asa Brown stood in the
store doorway, where he could watch the road and still
hear the occasional customer who moved among his flour barrels
and calico shelves.

"Most eight o'clock," said Asa. "Sun keeps up late now."

"Has to," said John Stover. "There's enough for it to
see to."

Henry smiled, but his gaze remained on the western brightness.
He had been harrowing all day, and the fine dust
lay in the seams of his coat. "We shall git
the corn in," he said. "We always do, somehow."

"Somehow ain't a method," Asa returned. "I told Almiry this
noon I didn't know when I'd seen folks so drove.
There's Enoch Trafton ain't finished ploughin' his home lot, an'
he sets out a hundred an' fifty bushel o' potatoes."

"He'll set 'em," said John. "Enoch can work when he's
cornered."

A wagon rattled down the north road, carrying two boys
and a load of empty milk cans. The boys called
good evening, and Asa answered them. Then the square was
quiet again. From an open window behind the meeting-house came
the thin, repeated notes of a child practicing upon a
melodeon. A robin flew down to the watering trough, found
the place too public, and went away.

Asa folded his arms. "Decoration Day's Tuesday," he observed.

Neither man answered at once.

"We never done nothin' here," Asa continued. "Other places do.
Over to East Wilby they have speakin', an' the school
children, an' a brass band if they can git it.
North Barlow's put up a soldiers' monument, though there ain't
twelve names on it. Here we be, with forty-odd went
out from this town, an' we never done so much
as stick a flag onto ary grave."

John Stover raised his head. "There ain't forty-odd left."

"No," said Asa. "That's just it."

The melodeon ceased. Its last note seemed to wait uncertainly
in the air and then disappear.

Henry Merrill counted slowly upon his fingers, not because he
needed to, but because the gesture helped him bring the
absent men before him. "There's us three," he said. "Luther
Pike, if his rheumatiz ain't laid him up; Ben Dole;
Silas Cook; Joseph Staples; Calvin Brackett; an' Martin Tighe."

"Martin can't march," said John.

"He can ride," Asa replied. "That makes nine, all told."

"Cal Brackett's gone to his daughter's in Lowell," said Henry.
"He wan't expectin' home till June."

"Eight, then."

They looked at one another with an uneasiness that was
not due to the smallness of the number alone. In
their common talk they rarely mentioned the war. They spoke
of pensions, sometimes, and of old wounds when one of
them was kept from work; they disputed the merits of
generals at caucus time, and laughed over incidents which had
once seemed terrible. But the war itself lay behind them
like a tract of wild country crossed in youth. Each
knew that the others had been there. There was no
need to describe it.

Asa came out upon the step. "Seems to me we've
waited long enough."

John rubbed one palm against the other. "Plantin's late."

"Decoration Day comes late, too, this year."

"Not late enough for corn."

Henry looked toward the meeting-house and the burying ground behind
it, where the slate stones made dark, uneven lines among
the grass. "Who's buried there?" he asked.

"Soldiers? Abner Holt, George Lunt, the two Marston boys, Captain
Elbridge, young Sam Ames—"

"Sam's over in the family ground."

"Well, that ain't far. There's Hiram Cole up to the
Ridge yard, an' Eben Munson an' John Tighe at the
poor-farm ground."

Asa's list ended less confidently. The paupers' graves lay at
the farther edge of town, in a rough acre behind
the poor farm, where burials were made cheaply and remembered
for a season or two. John Tighe had been Martin's
elder brother. Eben Munson had once had a good farm,
a young wife promised to him, and a reputation for
courage. Rum had afterward clinched with him and thrown him
hard. Barlow had been willing to remember the rum longer
than the courage.

John Stover turned toward Asa. "You count Eb?"

"I do for this."

"He died on the town."

"He near about died for the country first," Asa answered.
"I guess one thing don't wipe out the other."

There was a long pause.

Henry Merrill spoke at last. "Eb carried me half a
mile, after I got hit at Cedar Creek."

Asa knew the story, but John looked around.

"I never told much about it," Henry continued. "We was
mixed up, an' I couldn't step. Eb got hold of
me an' said, 'Come, Hen, 'tain't a proper place to
set.' He had blood all down one sleeve himself. He
hauled me back behind a wall. I don't know how.
He wan't sober a good many times afterward, but he
was sober enough that day."

John gave a slow nod. "Then we'll count him."

The decision, once made, began at once to gather practical
difficulties. They must find flags, flowers, music, wagons, and some
order of marching. They must notify the school and selectmen,
ask the minister for a prayer, and discover whether Martin
Tighe could bear the long ride to the two graveyards.
Asa maintained that they ought to have a printed notice.
John said there was no time to print one, and
no necessity either, since Barlow could spread news faster from
kitchen to kitchen than any newspaper. Henry suggested that the
church bell be rung at one o'clock.

"If we start at one, we'll be to the poor
farm by three," said Asa. "We ought to march slow.
Makes more of us."

"We'll look like eight men whether we go fast or
slow," John observed.

"Not if folks join in behind."

"Who's goin' to?"

"Everybody, if they've got public sperit."

John looked across at the silent houses. "That's what we
don't know."

Asa was not discouraged. He went inside and brought out
an old account book, on whose blank leaves he began
to write names and errands. His handwriting was large and
angular, and he dipped his pen with unnecessary frequency.

"Luther Pike's got his fife," Henry said.

"Can he play it?"

"He could at Yorktown."

"That's been some time."

"His grandson plays the drum in school exhibitions," said John.
"They can practice together."

"What'll we wear?" Asa asked.

"Clothes," said John.

"I mean uniforms."

John's expression altered. "Mine's in the attic. Coat wouldn't meet
across me now."

"Mine would meet twice," said Henry, with a faint laugh.
"Almiry took it in for Timothy once, when they had
a war tableau at the Grange."

Asa had kept his uniform brushed and folded in a
chest because his wife believed it protected woollens from moths
better than any other garment in the house. He proposed
that every man should wear what remained of his army
clothes, even if it were only a cap or belt.

"Folks ought to know who we be," he said.

"They know us," John replied.

"They know us to trade horses with, an' to ask
about hay weather. That ain't quite the same."

This was so nearly a statement of their whole purpose
that the other men were silent. They had grown old
in Barlow among people who knew their faults, debts, boundaries,
and yields of potatoes. It seemed almost presumptuous to ask
the town to look at them in another light.

Before they separated, they agreed that John should see the
minister and selectmen; Henry should go to Luther Pike and
Martin Tighe; and Asa should provide flags, send word to
the school, and persuade the women to gather flowers. They
would meet Monday evening and settle the route.

"An' no speechifyin' from us," John said as he rose.

"I never offered," Asa protested.

"You was comin' to it."

"I only thought somebody ought to state what it's for."

"They'll know," said Henry.

John Stover walked home along the southern road, past fields
lying black beneath the pale sky. At the turn he
could see the upper windows of his house among the
maples and, beyond them, the little orchard his father had
planted. His brother Daniel had gone to war from that
house and had never returned. Somewhere in Virginia there was
a place where Daniel had been put into the earth,
but no one had ever been able to tell the
family exactly where.

For thirty years John had thought of that unknown grave
only at intervals, and usually while doing something practical: mending
a wall, cutting seed potatoes, searching through old papers. That
evening it seemed nearer than the Barlow burying ground. He
wondered whether any stranger had ever laid a flower there.

Sunday carried the plan through the town. It went up
the Ridge in the minister's chaise, crossed the interval with
the doctor, entered kitchens by way of children sent upon
errands, and reached the farthest farms before night. There was
some criticism. Mrs. Amos Trafton said the veterans ought to
have begun years before, when there were enough of them
to make a proper appearance. Old Captain Elbridge's widow thought
the procession should be confined to officers and their families
until someone reminded her that there were no living officers.
Several farmers declared they could not spare the time, but
their wives found means to answer that Decoration Day lasted
no longer than an afternoon.

The schoolmistress, Miss Julia Wren, received the news as if
a national responsibility had been placed in her hands. She
dismissed arithmetic early on Monday and set her pupils to
making wreaths of evergreen and tissue-paper roses. Real flowers were
scarce. The lilacs were only beginning, the apple blossoms uncertain,
and the gardens had little beyond daffodils, violets, and bleeding-heart.
Yet by Tuesday morning every household seemed to have found
something. Children appeared at Asa Brown's store carrying baskets of
ground pine, wild cherry, white violets, and sprays of flowering
currant. Mrs. Merrill sacrificed the first tulips from the south
side of her house. Mrs. Stover sent great branches of
plum bloom, though John said they would shed before the
march was half done.

Monday evening found the three organizers at the store again.
They had accomplished more than they had expected. The minister
would pray; the church bell would ring; two wagons had
been offered for elderly people; the school would attend; and
Luther Pike believed that, given a careful choice of tunes,
his breath would last.

"Martin says he'll go," Henry reported. "His wife was against
it. She says the joltin' gives him spasms. But Martin
took on so she give in."

Martin Tighe had lost a leg above the knee and
the use of one arm. A bullet lodged near his
spine had never been removed, and age had increased the
old injury until he spent most days in a chair
by the kitchen stove, inconvenient to himself and to those
who cared for him. His son Daniel, a sober young
carpenter, would drive him.

"He got any uniform?" Asa asked.

"Cap. That's all. Dan says he can wrap him in
a blue blanket."

"John Tighe's grave must have a flag," said Asa.

"All of 'em will."

"What about Eb Munson?"

"All of 'em," John repeated.

Asa cleared his throat and looked down at his account
book. "Martha Down's in town."

John's face hardened. "What's that got to do with us?"

"She come from Providence to see her sister. Almiry says
she's been askin' about the doin's."

"She can ask."

Henry Merrill turned the tea package in his hands. Martha
Down had been the handsome daughter of a prosperous Barlow
farmer. She and Eb Munson had expected to marry when
he came home. While his regiment was still in Virginia,
she had married a traveling agent from Rhode Island and
gone away. Eben returned wounded and bitter, took first to
hard labor and afterward to hard drinking, and lost one
thing after another until the poor farm received him. Barlow
had always found the sequence simple.

"I never knew just how 'twas," Henry said.

"Plain enough," John answered.

"Mebbe. Things look plain at a distance."

Asa shut the book. "Well, she ain't asked to take
part. I only mentioned she was here."

They settled the route. The procession would form at the
store, pass Grandmother Dexter's house, stop at the meeting-house yard
and the Ames family ground, then go by wagon and
on foot to the Ridge cemetery and poor farm. This
made a journey of nearly six miles, but Asa held
that if they omitted any grave there would be talk.

"There'll be talk anyway," said John.

"Yes," Asa agreed cheerfully, "but it needn't have cause."

Decoration Day dawned bright and still. The eastern sky grew
yellow behind a range of low hills, and mist lay
over the interval like folded muslin. By seven o'clock there
were sounds in every dooryard: pails set down, hens called,
harness buckled, pump handles creaking. Men hurried through their morning
work with the suspicious energy of those who intend to
take an afternoon away from it. The air warmed early.
By noon the apple trees were white and pink against
the dark barns, and the young leaves of the elms
cast small, trembling shadows.

John Stover ate little dinner. Afterward he went upstairs to
the spare room, where his wife had laid out the
old blue coat. It smelled of cedar and time. One
cuff was frayed, and the brass buttons had darkened. He
put it on with difficulty. The cloth pulled beneath his
arms, and he could not fasten the lower buttons.

"You look real well," his wife said from the doorway.

"I look fat."

"You look as if you belonged to it."

He adjusted the belt, which had been lengthened with a
piece of brown leather. On the bed lay his cap
and a small packet of letters tied with string. His
wife had found them in the same chest but had
not opened them.

John touched the packet. "Dan's," he said.

"I know."

He stood a moment, then put one letter inside his
coat.

At half past twelve, people began to come into the
village. Some walked, some drove, and some arrived in farm
wagons with boards laid across for seats. They tied horses
behind the meeting-house and along Asa's back fence. Children ran
everywhere, carrying flowers and flags. The church bell struck one,
paused, and then continued in slow, solemn strokes which went
out over the fields.

The eight veterans assembled behind the store.

They presented no uniform appearance. Asa Brown's coat was carefully
preserved but much too tight at the neck. Henry Merrill
wore his army trousers, altered and patched, with an ordinary
black coat. Ben Dole had only a cap, Luther Pike
a belt, Joseph Staples a faded blue overcoat despite the
warm day, and Silas Cook an empty sleeve pinned across
his breast. John Stover stood at the right of the
line and tried not to notice how old they all
appeared.

Martin Tighe's wagon waited beside them. Daniel Tighe had cushioned
the seat with quilts and propped his father upright. Martin's
face was white and narrow under the army cap. The
blue blanket about his shoulders hid the shape of his
body, so that his head seemed to rise alone from
the old color.

"You able?" John asked him.

Martin's mouth twisted in a smile. "Able enough to set,
if Dan's able enough to drive."

Luther Pike and his grandson, Freddy, stood at the front.
Luther's fife was yellowed ivory, with one silver key. Freddy
had borrowed a drum from the Sons of Temperance band
in East Wilby; it was almost as broad as he
was.

"What tune?" Asa whispered.

"Marchin' Through Georgia," said Luther.

"We wan't all in Georgia."

"Tune was."

John told them to begin.

Luther lifted the fife. The first notes were thin and
wandering, and Freddy entered too soon with a violent roll.
A horse at the hitching rail shied, several children laughed,
and Luther stopped.

"Start ag'in," he said.

They did. This time the tune held together, though with
gaps where Luther took breath. John gave the word, and
the veterans moved into the road.

At first they were painfully conscious of themselves. Their steps
disagreed; their knees were stiff; Asa swung his arms too
energetically, while Joseph Staples fell behind and then hurried to
regain the line. The old coats, drawn from chests and
attics, made them look less like a company than like
survivors of several forgotten theatricals. They passed Asa's store and
the church, and a gust of wind lifted the large
flag carried by Henry Merrill.

Then the crowd followed.

The schoolchildren came first, two by two under Miss Wren's
severe direction, bearing small flags and wreaths. Behind them walked
the women, the selectmen, the minister, men in their Sunday
coats, young fellows who had intended only to watch, and
mothers carrying babies. Wagons rolled at the rear. Nearly the
whole town of Barlow entered the road.

Asa glanced backward, stumbled, and caught himself.

"Don't gawk," said John.

"There's a sight of 'em."

"Keep step."

"I be keepin' it, all I can."

The procession went north beneath the elms. Windows stood open,
and flags appeared from places where no one had known
flags were kept. One small boy had fastened a toy
banner to a bean pole; an old woman waved a
red handkerchief from an upstairs room. Familiar houses took on
an altered look, not because they were decorated grandly, but
because each offered some sign that the day had been
understood.

At Grandmother Dexter's gate, John raised his hand, and the
line stopped.

She was ninety-three and had been waiting in a straight-backed
chair just inside the dooryard. Two sons and three grandsons
had gone from her house to the war. Only one
son came home, and he had died ten years later
of a fever believed to have begun in a Southern
prison. Her granddaughter helped her stand.

Grandmother Dexter was small and bent, dressed in black silk
turned brown at the seams. She held a flag no
larger than her hand.

John faced the veterans. "Salute," he said.

Eight hands rose. Martin Tighe, in the wagon, made an
effort with his good arm and brought his fingers unsteadily
to the brim of his cap.

The old woman looked from one face to another. Her
lips moved, but no one heard what she said. Then
she lifted the little flag.

The crowd behind became entirely still. There was no plan
for this stop beyond the salute, yet it was one
of the great moments of the day. The veterans had
thought they were honoring an old woman. They suddenly felt
that she stood for all those who had watched the
road and waited.

Luther began the fife again. The procession moved on.

At the meeting-house burying ground, the grass had been newly
cut, and the odor of it mingled with lilac from
the minister's yard. The soldiers' graves were scattered among family
lots. Some had tall marble stones; others had slate markers
with hands pointing heavenward. Captain Elbridge's monument was the largest,
and his widow had arranged chairs beside it for her
household.

The minister prayed briefly. He thanked God for sacrifice, asked
mercy for the nation's errors, and prayed that peace might
make men worthy of what war had cost. Then Miss
Wren called the names.

At each name two children went forward with a wreath,
and Henry Merrill planted a small flag. The veterans stood
uncovered in the sun. A bee moved heavily among the
plum blossoms John's wife had sent. Somewhere outside the wall
a cow bawled to be let into pasture.

When Miss Wren called Samuel Ames, the procession crossed the
road to the little family ground among the pines. Sam
had been seventeen. His mother had lied about his age
because he was determined to go and she feared he
would run away if prevented. She had lived long enough
to repent her consent every day. Now her granddaughter placed
violets beneath his stone.

From there they passed out of the village toward the
Ridge.

The road climbed steadily, bordered first by farms and then
by young birch and maple. Dust rose under the marching
feet. Luther's fife had grown uncertain, and Freddy's drumming became
gentler from fatigue. Men who had begun at the rear
came forward to relieve the older veterans of wreaths and
flags, but no one offered to carry Henry Merrill's banner.
He held it in both hands and watched its shadow
move along the road.

The procession lengthened over a quarter mile. Those at the
front could hear the wagons behind them but could not
see the last. Children picked dandelions as they walked. Women
exchanged news in low voices. A few men discussed whether
rain would come by Thursday. Yet whenever the fife recovered
a martial phrase, conversation stopped and the march found its
step again.

Henry Merrill began to remember another road.

It had been night, and the mud was deep. Men
moved on both sides of him, their faces invisible, their
canteens and bayonets making small sounds. He had been hungry
beyond complaint and so tired that he slept while walking.
Somewhere ahead a soldier sang two lines of a hymn
and was cursed into silence. That army had seemed endless
then. Regiments filled the road, and more were always coming
out of the dark.

Now there were eight.

Such processions would grow smaller and more pathetic year by
year. One man would fail to appear, and then another;
the uniforms would be folded away for the last time;
boys who had watched from roadsides would become old men
themselves. Yet on that bright Barlow afternoon the eight did
not think of themselves as the remnant of a generation.
They thought chiefly of keeping their feet from dragging and
of reaching the Ridge with dignity.

At the Ridge cemetery, they decorated three graves. Luther Pike
sat on the stone wall and breathed through his mouth
while Miss Wren's pupils sang a verse of "America." Their
voices were high and not wholly together, but they went
sweetly across the open fields.

From the Ridge there was a broad view of Barlow.
The village lay below, its meeting-house white among the elms,
its roofs half hidden by fruit trees. Beyond it ran
the brook, flashing here and there, and beyond the brook
rose the blue mountains. The veterans had looked upon that
landscape all their lives, but from the graveyard it seemed
unusually complete and dear.

Asa Brown wiped his forehead. "If this don't bring on
rain, nothin' will," he said.

John gave him a reproving look.

"I meant the singin'. Almiry says singin' outdoors always brings
showers."

Henry smiled. "You're scairt of feelin' solemn too long."

"Mebbe I be. It don't come natural."

They resumed the road to the poor farm.

The town farm stood in a hollow near the eastern
boundary, a long, old-fashioned house with a sagging ell and
three great chimneys. Those who lived there—aged men, two simple-minded
sisters, a crippled boy, and a deaf woman who had
once taught school—were gathered by the gate in their best
clothes. The keeper had raised a flag from the hayfork
pole, and it hung almost motionless in the still air.

Behind the barn, up a cart track bordered by juniper,
lay the paupers' ground.

There was no wall around it. Brambles had entered from
the pasture, and young poplars grew among the graves. Some
mounds had sunk until they were scarcely distinguishable. A few
were marked with fieldstones; more had nothing. John Tighe's grave
could be found because Martin remembered the white birch near
it. Eben Munson's lay three places beyond.

The procession crowded into the rough ground and became quiet
in a different way than it had at the village
cemetery. Here there were no family lots, no carved names,
no iron fences. Poverty had made the dead anonymous.

Daniel Tighe brought the wagon as near as the track
allowed. He and John Stover lifted Martin into a chair.
The effort caused Martin pain, and his face took on
a gray color, but he shook his head when they
suggested he remain in the wagon.

"I come for John," he said.

They carried him to his brother's grave.

The minister read the burial service. A child set down
a wreath. Martin looked at the small flag which Henry
pushed into the earth, and his good hand closed upon
the blue blanket.

"John was the better soldier," he said suddenly. "Folks don't
know it, account of me bein' the one that got
smashed. He never shirked ary time. He died of lung
fever after he come home. There wan't no pension for
him."

No one answered. Daniel laid a hand on his father's
shoulder.

At Eben Munson's grave, they discovered fresh flowers.

They were not the school wreaths nor any of the
baskets brought from the village. A cluster of white lilacs,
dark red tulips, and sprays of myrtle had been laid
upon the sunken mound. The stems were wrapped in a
fine linen handkerchief.

Asa Brown noticed them first. He stooped but did not
touch them.

"Somebody's been here," he said.

"Looks so," John replied.

No one in the crowd claimed the offering. The women
glanced at one another. Henry Merrill saw, at the far
edge of the gathering, a veiled woman in gray standing
beside Mrs. Down of the north road. Before he could
be certain of her face, she moved behind the others.

Miss Wren called Eben's name.

For a moment no child stepped forward. The two little
girls assigned to the grave had become confused among the
unmarked mounds. Then Henry took their wreath and laid it
beside the fresh flowers.

He remained there, looking down.

"I guess I ought to say somethin'," he began.

John lifted his head. Asa became very still.

"Eb Munson wan't accounted much of, after the war. I
ain't goin' to claim he was. Rum got him, an'
got him bad. But there was a day when I
couldn't walk, an' the firing was close, an' Eb carried
me out. He'd been hit himself. I never thanked him
proper. Mebbe he wouldn't have listened if I had."

Henry stopped. His voice was steady, but he could not
continue.

John Stover set the flag at the head of the
grave. "We thank him now," he said.

The words went no farther than the people near him,
yet their simplicity was enough. Men who had spoken harshly
of Eben while he lived now looked at the neglected
earth and remembered stories that had nearly disappeared beneath the
later one. Courage did not excuse all weakness, but weakness
could not abolish courage.

Daniel Tighe had brought a bugle. He had never served
in the army, but as a boy he had learned
the calls from his father. At John's request he went
a little distance up the slope.

The crowd turned toward him.

The first note of taps came uncertainly. Daniel's lips were
dry, and the tone broke. He began again.

This time the call rose clear above the rough ground,
crossed the poor farm roofs and pasture, and went into
the budding woods. The familiar intervals opened a door in
the minds of the veterans. For an instant the spring
afternoon was gone. There were campfires burning low, rows of
dark tents, wet blankets, distant horses, and men lying down
in their clothes. There were voices that had not been
heard for thirty years and young faces which death had
kept from changing.

John Stover looked toward the mountains. Henry bowed his head.
Asa fixed his attention upon a juniper bush as if
its smallest branch required study. None of them could look
at another.

Martin Tighe began to weep without sound.

The last note weakened and disappeared. A thrush answered from
the woods.

There was a long pause.

Then the minister said, "Amen," though there had been no
prayer.

They returned to the village late in the afternoon. The
march had lost its order. Children rode in wagons; Luther
Pike carried his fife in his pocket; Freddy's drum was
slung upon the back of a young farmer. The veterans
walked as they could, except Martin, who lay against the
cushions with his eyes closed.

Yet when the meeting-house came into view, John called the
men into line once more. Luther protested that he had
no breath left, but John asked only for the first
tune. The old man took out his fife. Freddy reclaimed
the drum.

They entered Barlow to music.

People who had stayed behind came to their gates. The
western sun lay along the road and shone through the
flags, making their red stripes bright. The eight veterans passed
beneath the elms with the whole town behind them. Their
line was uneven, their uniforms faded, and their music poor;
but no procession in Barlow had ever been received with
greater seriousness.

At the store, they halted. The minister gave a benediction.
Then, without arrangement, the schoolchildren cheered.

The sound startled the horses and embarrassed the veterans. Asa
Brown removed his cap and waved it. Henry laughed. John
Stover stood sternly for a moment, then raised his hand
to the crowd.

Supper waited in farm kitchens, cows were calling from barnyards,
and evening work could no longer be delayed. The gathering
broke apart quickly. Wagons went north and south; families climbed
the roads toward home; children departed with wilted flowers and
small flags. Within half an hour the square was almost
empty.

The veterans helped Martin Tighe into his son's wagon.

"Hard day?" Henry asked him.

"Best I've had," Martin replied. "I can hurt tomorrow."

Daniel drove away slowly.

John, Henry, and Asa remained before the store. They had
eaten nothing since noon except gingerbread distributed by the school
committee, and Asa found cheese, crackers, and a bottle of
ginger beer in his stock. They took these to the
step where they had sat three evenings before.

The village settled into its ordinary sounds. Pails rang against
barn floors. A woman called a child. From the interval
came the lowing of cattle. The flags on the meeting-house
and store drooped in the quiet air.

"Well," Asa said, after they had eaten. "We done it."

"We did," said Henry.

John cut another piece of cheese. "Could improve the marchin'."

"You was the one crowded me at Grandmother Dexter's."

"You was out of step."

"I had a stone in my boot."

"You had it all the way to the poor farm,
then."

Asa laughed, and Henry joined him. Their laughter had fatigue
in it, and relief.

"Folks turned out," Asa said presently.

"More'n I expected," John admitted.

"Near the whole town. I told you there was public
sperit here, if anybody could git hold of it."

"You said you didn't know."

"I knew more'n I let on."

Henry leaned against the post. His face had a tired
peace. "They looked different," he said.

"Who?"

"Everybody. Us, too."

Asa nodded. "Silas Cook stood up straighter than I ever
saw him. Even old Joe Staples. He generally looks as
if he was apologizin' for bein' anywheres."

John folded the paper that had held the cheese. "People
forget."

"So do we," Henry said.

They considered this. It was easy to charge forgetfulness against
the young, who knew the war from schoolbooks and political
speeches. Yet the veterans themselves had allowed names, graves, and
stories to pass out of common knowledge. They had been
busy. They had come home to mortgages, old parents, worn
farms, children, sickness, and all the countless demands by which
years conceal their passage. Memory had seemed private, and often
burdensome. Now they understood that what was not told would
vanish.

"We ought to make a record," John said.

"A printed one?" Asa asked at once.

"Mebbe. First off, write things down. Every Barlow man that
went, what rigiment, where he was, when he come home
or where he died."

"Captain Elbridge's widow's got papers."

"Luther knows about the Wilby company," Henry added. "Martin can
tell more'n folks think, if somebody'll set patient."

"We ought to git the school children in," Asa said.
"Have 'em write accounts. Prize for the best."

"Don't make a show of it," John cautioned.

"There you be. If anybody proposes anythin' that might be
noticed, you call it a show."

"We ain't sellin' patent medicine."

"No, but history that stays shut in a chest ain't
much better'n no history."

Henry lifted a hand between them. "We can do both.
Write it plain an' read some of it next year."

"Next year," Asa repeated, pleased by the sound.

The phrase assumed that all three would be present, though
none of them could be certain. John's shoulder troubled him
more each winter; Henry had a weakness of the heart
which the doctor called manageable; Asa, apparently the strongest, had
once collapsed behind his counter and afterward dismissed it as
indigestion. They did not speak of these matters.

The store door opened behind them. Asa's wife, Almira, came
out with a shawl over her head.

"Asa," she said, "Martha Down's waitin' in the back room."

Asa turned. "For me?"

"She wants to speak to you three."

John's expression closed. Henry looked toward the road.

Almira lowered her voice. "She's been waitin' near half an
hour. She didn't want to come out while folks was
round."

Asa rose. "Tell her to step here."

Martha Down appeared in the doorway.

She was near sixty, though at first glance she seemed
older. Her dress was fine gray wool, made in a
city fashion but without ornament, and the veil she had
worn at the poor-farm ground was folded over one arm.
She had once been famous for her color and dark
hair. The color was gone, and her hair was almost
white, but her face retained something proud and direct.

The three men stood awkwardly.

"I won't keep you," she said. Her voice had lost
much of the Barlow accent, though not all. "I want
to ask about Eben Munson's grave."

No one replied immediately.

Martha looked at Henry. "I heard what you said there."

"I didn't say much."

"You said more than anybody has said for him in
a great while."

John's gaze fell to the fine linen handkerchief she carried.
One corner bore a dark green stain from flower stems.

"You put them flowers there," he said.

"Yes."

Asa shifted his weight. "They was handsome."

"I went early. I didn't want anyone to know."

"Folks'll likely know," Asa said, with honesty rather than malice.
"Things git known here."

"I suppose they do."

She turned the handkerchief between her fingers. The men waited.
Serious feelings had always been difficult in Barlow when there
was no work to be done while they were expressed.

"I have been told there is no stone," Martha said.
"I knew he was buried at the town farm, but
I thought— I don't know what I thought. I should
like to pay for one. Nothing large. His name, his
regiment, and the dates, if they can be found."

Asa looked at John. John still regarded Martha with a
reserve that had hardened over many years.

"You can do it," he said. "No law against it."

"I want your help with the wording."

"Why ours?"

Martha met his eyes. "Because you were his comrades."

The word altered the little group. John's severity did not
disappear, but it lost its certainty.

Henry drew forward one of the store chairs. "You better
set down."

"No, thank you."

"You come all the way to Barlow on account of
this?" Asa asked.

"I came to see my sister."

"But you knew Decoration Day was comin'?"

Martha's face changed. "Yes."

Another silence followed. A moth struck once against the store
window and dropped onto the sill.

John spoke with some effort. "Folks always laid Eb's goin'
down to what happened betwixt you an' him."

"I know."

"Maybe they had reason."

"Yes."

Henry looked at John, but Martha went on before either
man could interrupt.

"I was twenty-one. Eben had been gone nearly three years.
His letters stopped for seven months. We heard his regiment
had been in a battle and that many were missing.
My father was ill, the farm was mortgaged, and Mr.
Down offered a way out for all of us. Then
a letter came from Eben two days before the wedding."

She paused.

"I married Mr. Down."

Asa cleared his throat. "Was you happy?"

"That has nothing to do with Eben."

"Mebbe it has, some."

Martha looked past them toward the darkening road. "My husband
was a decent man. He knew nothing until afterward. I
would not punish him for what I had done, so
I never came back. Eben wrote once when he returned.
I answered. He sent my letter back unopened."

"He was proud," Henry said.

"He had a right."

John Stover's hands had closed upon the edge of his
coat. He remembered Eben as a young man, loud in
laughter, quick to volunteer for any dangerous duty, and afterward
as a gaunt figure seen unsteadily crossing the village road.
It had been convenient to place all blame upon a
single act by a woman who had gone away. Yet
wars and ruined hopes were not so simple. Men had
come home carrying injuries no doctor named; some endured, some
failed, and often the world judged the result without understanding
the contest.

"Eb made his own choices, too," Henry said gently.

"I know that," Martha replied. "Knowing it has never helped."

The men made no answer.

She took a small envelope from her reticule and held
it toward Asa. "There is money here. If it is
not enough, write to me. If there is anything left,
use it for John Tighe's grave."

Asa accepted the envelope but did not open it. "We
was talkin' of markin' both, some way."

"I'm glad."

John lifted his head. "The stone ought to say he
served faithful."

Tears came suddenly into Martha's eyes, but she did not
turn away. "He did."

"I mean that ought to be on it. Folks know
the rest. Let the stone tell what they've forgot."

Martha pressed the stained handkerchief to her lips. After a
moment she thanked them and went back through the store.

They heard Almira speak softly to her, the rear door
open, and then the light sound of a carriage moving
away along the north road.

Asa sat down again. The envelope remained in his hand.

"Well," he said, "there's a thing."

Henry looked toward the bend where Martha had disappeared. "We
didn't view it all round."

"We generally don't," said John.

Asa turned the envelope over. "I used to think she
never cared."

"So did I," Henry admitted.

"She cared," John said. "Mebbe too late ain't the same
as not at all."

The church bell struck eight. Its voice went over the
roofs and fields, slower now than at noon, and the
mountains had become a dark, level mass beneath the afterglow.

John took the old letter from inside his coat. "This
is one Dan wrote," he said. "My wife found it
with the uniform. He tells about a night they slept
in a cornfield, an' how the frost come. He says
Eb Munson give him half a blanket."

Henry leaned nearer. "I never heard that."

"Neither'd I, or I'd forgot."

"Put it in the record," Asa said.

John nodded. "I mean to."

They sat together until the square was dark. Now and
then someone passed on the road and called good night.
The little flags remained at the graves, stirring when no
wind could be felt below, and in many houses children
were telling over what they had seen: the old blue
coats, Grandmother Dexter's salute, Martin Tighe in his wagon, the
rough ground behind the poor farm, and Daniel's bugle calling
across the fields.

Barlow had not become a different town in one afternoon.
Its people would return to their planting, their grievances, their
economies, and their habit of judging one another by narrow
evidence. Martin Tighe would again be burdensome when the honor
of the day had passed. The poor would still be
poor, the forgotten in danger of being forgotten, and public
spirit would sometimes need to be coaxed from behind locked
doors.

Yet something had been gained.

The familiar men who marched badly through the dust had
been seen, for a few hours, as members of a
great and vanishing army. The paupers' graves had become part
of the town's history. An old woman's losses had been
saluted. A child's toy flag, a borrowed drum, faded cloth,
and spring flowers had carried meanings larger than themselves. Even
Eben Munson, defeated in peace after proving brave in war,
had been given back his whole human story; and Martha
Down, long fixed in Barlow's judgment as heartless, had stepped
for one moment from that hard outline and been recognized
as another bearer of the past.

At last Henry Merrill rose.

"Long day," he said.

"Good one," Asa answered.

John fastened the top button of his old coat. "We'll
do better next year."

They parted beneath the elms. Henry took the Ridge road,
Asa went in to close his store, and John walked
south toward home, carrying his brother's letter close against his
breast.

Behind the meeting-house the new flags were barely visible among
the stones. Above them the belfry and weather vane stood
black against the remaining light, and farther west the mountains
kept their old, watchful place. The village was quiet again,
but it was not quite the quietness of forgetfulness.

Barlow had remembered.

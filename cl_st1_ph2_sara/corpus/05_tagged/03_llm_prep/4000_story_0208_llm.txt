# ROSES AND FORGET-ME-NOTS

## I. ROSES

It was one of those bitter December days when the
wind seems to have lost its way, and goes rushing
angrily round every corner, shaking doors, rattling windows, snatching at
umbrellas, and driving the sleet into the faces of all
unfortunate travellers. The clouds hung low over the city; the
pavements were glazed with ice and spotted with mud; even
the sparrows looked forlorn as they hopped under the dripping
eaves, while the pigeons strutted about in their little red
boots as if much offended by the weather.

Through this dreary scene hurried a small girl carrying a
large bandbox.

She wore a faded cotton dress, a shawl too thin
for winter, and a bonnet which had plainly belonged to
somebody older and larger than herself. Her boots were broken;
the wet came through them at every step, and her
fingers, clasped about the string of the box, were red
and numb with cold.

This was Lizzie Green, an orphan of fourteen, though hunger
and hard work made her look scarcely twelve. She was
errand girl to Madame Vernet, a fashionable milliner, who dressed
rich ladies in silks and feathers while her youngest workwoman
often lacked bread enough for dinner.

"Make haste, child!" Madame had said, giving her the bandbox.
"This bonnet is for Miss Belle Hamilton, and if one
bow is crushed, or one feather spotted, you shall pay
for it."

Poor Lizzie knew very well that she could not pay
for it if she worked till she was a hundred;
so she tucked the box beneath her shawl as well
as she could and fought her way onward.

At last she reached a handsome house in a quiet
square. The brass knocker shone like gold, lights glimmered behind
crimson curtains, and through a basement window came a warm,
delicious smell of roasting meat.

Lizzie sighed as she mounted the steps.

A servant admitted her and led her into a dressing-room,
where she was told to wait. To the tired child
it seemed almost like a little fairy palace. A bright
fire danced upon the hearth; a soft carpet covered the
floor; lace curtains fell about the windows; and pretty trifles
of ivory, silver, ribbon, and china stood upon every table.

But what delighted Lizzie most was the window garden.

There were ferns, geraniums, heliotropes, and roses, all fresh and
blooming in spite of the storm. One delicate pink rose
leaned from among the glossy leaves, so fair and fragrant
that it looked like a rosy face smiling out upon
the shabby little stranger.

Lizzie forgot her cold feet. She forgot the wet shawl
clinging to her shoulders and the severe face of Madame
Vernet awaiting her return. She drew near the plants as
naturally as a butterfly or bee.

"Oh, you beauty!" she whispered. "If I only knew how
to make one like you!"

This was Lizzie's secret ambition. She had watched the flower-makers
who sometimes came to the shop, and she longed to
learn their art. A petal dropped in the street, a
leaf blown from a lady's bonnet, even a bit of
colored paper shaped by chance beneath her scissors—such things filled
her head with plans. But materials cost money, lessons cost
more, and Madame Vernet only laughed when the errand girl
ventured to ask questions.

"You make flowers!" the woman had exclaimed. "Learn first to
carry a box without stumbling over your own feet."

Yet Lizzie persisted in dreaming. She believed that if she
could take that rose home, pull it carefully apart, trace
each velvet petal, and discover how the green leaves folded
about the stem, she might copy it in muslin. The
thought came suddenly; it stayed; it grew.

She looked toward the door.

No one was there.

She touched the rose. Its cool leaves brushed her wrist,
and its fragrance seemed to invite her. One little pull—only
one—and it would be hidden beneath her shawl.

"I'd never take anything else," she murmured. "And they've got
so many. They wouldn't miss just one."

That is the dangerous little sentence by which many a
wrong act first enters the heart. They have so much;
I have so little; they will not miss it. But
another person's abundance cannot make a dishonest deed honest, any
more than darkness can turn a thorn into a flower.

Lizzie hesitated. Then footsteps sounded in the passage. Frightened, ashamed,
and desperate all at once, she broke off the rose
and thrust it beneath her shawl.

The door opened.

A young girl came in, followed by a maid carrying
a heap of white muslin. Belle Hamilton was about Lizzie's
age, but there the likeness ended. She had bright eyes,
soft curls, warm cheeks, and the assured, happy air of
one who had always been loved and cared for. Her
blue cashmere dress fitted neatly; a little gold chain shone
about her neck; and her slippers, peeping from under her
skirt, were trimmed with swansdown.

"Oh, is that my bonnet?" she cried. "Marie, open it
at once! I do hope Madame has not put those
horrid yellow roses on it."

Marie lifted the cover with a flourish.

"Behold, mademoiselle! It is ravishing—superb! The roses, they are not
yellow; they are of the palest cream."

Belle examined the bonnet, first on one side, then on
the other.

"The feather is crooked."

"But no, mademoiselle!"

"It is. And the ribbon is too broad. I told
Madame I wanted it narrow."

Lizzie stood dripping upon the rich carpet, listening in silence.
She thought of Madame's warning and trembled. If Miss Belle
sent the bonnet back, there would be scolding at least,
perhaps no supper, perhaps dismissal.

Belle presently noticed her.

"Why, Marie, look at that poor child! She is wet
through."

Lizzie colored and tried to hide her broken boots beneath
her skirt.

"You came all the way from Madame Vernet's in this
storm?" Belle asked.

"Yes, miss."

"Didn't you ride?"

"No, miss."

"Not even in the omnibus?"

"I haven't got any money for that."

Belle's face changed. A moment before, a crooked feather had
seemed a real calamity; now, looking at Lizzie's blue lips
and shivering figure, she felt ashamed of her complaint.

"Come near the fire," she said. "You must be half
frozen."

"I ought to go back, miss."

"Not until you are dry. Marie, take off her wet
shawl."

Lizzie drew back quickly, pressing one hand over the hidden
rose.

"No, please!"

"Do not be frightened," said Belle. "We won't hurt you."

The gentle tone hurt her more than harshness could have
done. Yet she dared not uncover the flower, and clung
to her shawl so tightly that Belle supposed her shy.

"Then keep it on, if you like; but sit here."

She pushed forward a cushioned chair. Lizzie perched upon its
edge, feeling that her muddy shoes disgraced the golden lilies
woven into the carpet.

Belle looked at them too.

"Those boots are quite worn out."

"They'll last a bit longer, miss."

"They won't keep out snow."

"I haven't got any others."

Belle stood thoughtful for a moment, then ran to a
closet and returned with a pair of stout buttoned boots.

"Mamma bought these for me, but they pinch. I wore
them only twice. Would they fit you?"

Lizzie stared.

"For me?"

"Yes, if you want them."

Want them! Her own toes were wet and aching; the
worn soles flapped when she walked. Still she hesitated, unused
to gifts and fearful of kindness.

"I'd like 'em ever so much," she confessed, "but I
haven't anything to pay."

"Nonsense! They are a present."

Belle knelt upon the carpet and held one boot beside
Lizzie's foot. Marie protested that mademoiselle would soil her dress,
but Belle laughed.

"They are just the size. Put them on."

The shabby boots were removed, the dry ones buttoned, and
poor Lizzie's feet felt so warm and comfortable that she
could scarcely believe they belonged to her.

"There!" said Belle triumphantly. "Now for something to eat. Marie,
bring some cake and milk."

"Oh, no, miss; I mustn't."

"You must. I am sure you are hungry."

Lizzie was very hungry, and the cake looked wonderfully good.
She ate slowly at first, ashamed of her appetite; but
Belle talked pleasantly, asking where she lived, what work she
did, and whether she liked flowers.

At that question Lizzie's hand flew again to her shawl.

"Yes, miss," she faltered. "Better than anything."

"So do I. Only I always kill them when I
try to take care of them. Mamma says I love
them selfishly because I like to gather them, but forget
to water the roots."

Belle laughed, yet the words struck Lizzie like a reproof
meant for her alone.

"Would you like some?" Belle continued. "You may take all
the fading ones from that basket. They will be thrown
away tomorrow."

The basket was full of roses, violets, and sprays of
fragrant heliotrope. Some were bent, but many were fresher than
any flowers Lizzie had ever owned.

"All those?" she whispered.

"Yes. And I will give you two geranium leaves to
copy, if that is what you wish to do."

"How did you know?"

"You were looking at them as if you wanted to
learn their shapes. Perhaps you can make artificial flowers."

"Not yet. But I mean to—some day."

"Then you shall," Belle said confidently. "People who mean to
do things often do them."

This simple faith completed what the boots, the fire, and
the food had begun. Lizzie could endure the stolen rose
no longer. It seemed to burn against her heart. She
rose so suddenly that the cup rattled in its saucer.

"I'm bad!" she cried. "You oughtn't to be kind to
me. I took it—I stole it!"

Belle and Marie looked at her in astonishment.

With trembling fingers Lizzie drew out the rose. Crushed beneath
the shawl, it hung bruised and broken from its stem.

"I wanted to copy it," she sobbed. "I thought you
had plenty and wouldn't care. But I knew it was
wrong. I knew all the time. You may send for
the police, miss. Only—only don't take the boots till I've
put mine on again."

"The police!" exclaimed Belle. "For one rose?"

"It wasn't mine."

"No, but you have told me."

"Because you were good. If you'd scolded, maybe I'd have
kept it. But you gave me all those things, and
spoke as if I was honest."

Belle was young, yet she understood something important at that
moment. The child before her had not been won by
fear, but by trust. She put an arm about Lizzie's
shaking shoulders.

"Don't cry. I forgive you."

"Truly?"

"Truly. You must never steal again, even when you want
something very much."

"I won't! I'd sooner go without forever."

"I believe you. And you may keep the rose—though it
is sadly crushed now."

"No," Lizzie said, laying it upon the table. "I don't
want that one anymore."

Belle chose the loveliest fresh rose from the basket and
gave it to her instead.

"Take this honestly, then. Copy it if you can; and
when you become a great flower-maker, you shall make roses
for me."

Lizzie's tearful face brightened.

"I will, miss. I'll make you the prettiest flowers I
know how."

She went away carrying the old boots in a parcel,
the basket under her arm, and the fresh rose safely
pinned inside her dress. The storm had not ceased, the
streets were still bleak, and Madame Vernet would certainly complain
that she had lingered; but there was a warmth in
poor Lizzie's heart which no wind could chill.

For several days she treasured the rose, studying every curve
and fold. Before it faded, she pulled it carefully apart,
copied the petals upon scraps of paper, and pressed the
leaves between the pages of an old book.

Then came the accident which altered all her life.

Madame sent her out with a bonnet far more costly
than Belle's, covered with lace, satin, and delicate white plumes.

"If harm comes to it, never show your face here
again," she said.

The morning had been warmer, but toward evening the frost
returned. Water upon the stone steps of a great house
had frozen into a clear, treacherous sheet. Lizzie, unable to
see it in the dusk, set her foot down, slipped,
and fell heavily.

The bandbox rolled into the street.

A carriage wheel passed over it.

Lizzie tried to rise, but a sharp pain shot through
her side and leg. The world turned dark; voices sounded
far away; then she knew nothing more.

When she woke, she was in a narrow bed in
a hospital ward. Her head was bandaged, her body bruised,
and one leg lay heavy and helpless beneath the covers.

The bonnet was ruined, of course. Madame Vernet dismissed her
without wages and declared that such a clumsy girl deserved
no recommendation. It was useless to explain that the steps
had been icy. Expensive lace weighed more with Madame than
an errand girl's pain.

For many weeks Lizzie lay ill. There was no mother
to sit beside her, no father to ask the doctors
hopeful questions. A busy nurse brought food and medicine; a
charitable woman occasionally read aloud; but the long evenings were
very lonely.

Sometimes temptation returned in another form.

"What use was it to be honest?" whispered a bitter
thought. "You confessed, you tried to do right, and see
what came of it."

But then Lizzie remembered Belle's face, the warm boots, the
cake, the flowers, and the arm laid kindly around her
shoulders.

"She believed me," Lizzie would say. "I won't make her
belief a lie."

When spring came, she left the hospital upon crutches. Her
injured leg had mended badly, and though she might one
day walk without support, she would always be lame.

For a while it seemed that every door was shut.
She could no longer hurry with bandboxes; employers did not
want a slow errand girl; and the little savings she
had made were soon gone. Yet sorrow, though a stern
teacher, sometimes discovers lessons which ease leaves unread. Lizzie's fingers
were still quick, her eyes were true, and her love
of flowers had survived pain and poverty.

An elderly flower-maker who lodged in the same dilapidated house
saw the paper patterns Lizzie had copied from Belle's rose.

"You have patience," she said, "and you have an eye
for the turn of a petal. I can teach you
a little."

So the old ambition began to take useful shape. Lizzie
learned to stiffen muslin, to shade it with delicate colors,
to curl petals upon heated tools, to bind stems, and
to cut leaves so faithfully that one almost expected them
to stir in a summer breeze. Her first roses were
clumsy, her violets stiff, and her daisies much too large;
but she tried again and again.

By degrees she earned a few pennies, then a few
shillings. She laid aside her crutch for a cane. She
moved from the darkest attic to a small room where
sunlight reached the window. And there, among wires, silks, dyes,
scissors, and boxes, she made flowers while real ones nodded
in cracked pots upon the sill like friendly household spirits.

The pink rose Belle had given her faded long ago,
but its pattern remained.

More precious still, the memory remained.

## II. FORGET-ME-NOTS

Five years passed, and one bright winter evening the Hamilton
house was in a delightful confusion. Fires blazed, servants hurried
along the passages, bouquets arrived from the florist, and upstairs
Belle stood before a mirror in her first ball gown.

She had grown into a lovely young woman. Her dress
was white, soft as a cloud, with a blue sash
and little silver stars upon the sleeves. Marie, more excitable
than ever, fluttered about her with pins between her lips.

"It is perfection!" she declared. "Mademoiselle resembles the moon, the
snow, the morning—everything most exquisite!"

"But the wreath?" cried Belle. "Where is my wreath of
forget-me-nots?"

Marie clasped her hands.

"Ah, that terrible wreath!"

Belle had set her heart upon wearing blue forget-me-nots in
her hair. She had imagined them for weeks, had described
them to her dressmaker, and had rejected roses, pearls, and
silver leaves in their favor. But when the box was
opened that afternoon, the wreath proved to be purple, heavy,
and ugly.

"This will never do," Belle had said.

Since then, servants had searched the fashionable shops without success.
One had wreaths of roses, another lilies, another blue flowers
which looked like neither forget-me-nots nor anything else that grew
in a sensible garden.

Now the carriage would arrive in two hours.

"If I cannot have the right wreath, I shall not
go," Belle announced.

Her mother, seated calmly by the fire, smiled.

"That would be a great sacrifice to make for a
few artificial flowers."

"They are not a few flowers, Mamma. They are the
only thing which will make the dress complete."

"Five years ago a crooked feather could spoil your happiness.
I hoped you had grown wiser."

Belle laughed and kissed her.

"I am wiser in important matters. But this is my
first ball."

Anyone who has ever wanted a particular thing in a
great hurry can understand how every poor substitute appears ten
times worse than it otherwise would. Belle tried a spray
of white roses and took it off; she tried pearls
and declared herself ancient; she tried a blue ribbon and
said she looked like a schoolgirl.

At last a young seamstress who had come to alter
the sash spoke timidly.

"There is a flower-maker in Willow Lane who may do
it, miss. She makes beautiful work."

"In Willow Lane?" said Marie doubtfully. "That is not a
fashionable address."

"I don't care whether she lives in a palace or
a cellar," cried Belle. "Can she make forget-me-nots?"

"I have seen some of hers, miss. They looked almost
alive."

Belle put on a cloak over her ball dress.

"Mamma, I shall go myself. A servant will misunderstand. Willow
Lane cannot be far."

Her mother protested, Marie exclaimed, and the seamstress stared; but
when Belle had determined upon anything, she generally acted before
other people had finished explaining why she should not.

Willow Lane lay far beyond the handsome square and its
rows of lighted windows. The carriage entered narrower streets, passed
dim shops and crowded dwellings, and stopped at last before
a modest building with a painted sign:

**ELIZABETH GREEN
ARTIFICIAL FLOWERS
ORDERS EXECUTED WITH CARE**

Belle climbed the stairs, gathering up her white skirt beneath
the cloak. She found a half-open door and knocked.

"Come in," said a pleasant voice.

The room was small but wonderfully neat. A bright lamp
stood upon the worktable, illuminating baskets of leaves, trays of
tinted petals, reels of silk, and delicate sprays hung in
bunches along the wall. A canary slept in a cage
near the stove, and three plants filled the window. Their
blossoms were humble ones, yet the room possessed that cosy,
homelike air which cleanliness and content can give even where
money is scarce.

A young woman sat at the table. She was pale
and simply dressed, and a cane leaned beside her chair.
Her brown hair was smooth, her hands slender and busy,
and her face, though not beautiful, had a sweet, thoughtful
expression.

Belle did not recognize her.

"I am told you make forget-me-nots," she began. "I need
a wreath tonight—within two hours, if it is possible. I
know it is terribly late."

The flower-maker looked up.

For one moment she said nothing. Her hand trembled, and
the blue petal she held fluttered to the floor.

"Miss Belle?" she whispered.

Belle started.

"Do you know me?"

The young woman smiled; then tears filled her eyes.

"I should know you anywhere."

Belle searched the face before her, puzzled by some faint
memory. The plain room vanished for an instant; she saw
a wintry dressing-room, a soaked shawl, broken boots, and a
frightened child holding out a stolen rose.

"Lizzie!" she cried. "Little Lizzie from Madame Vernet's!"

"Yes, miss."

Belle took both her hands.

"Why, you are grown! And you make flowers after all.
I am so glad. But your cane—are you ill?"

Lizzie told her briefly of the icy steps, the crushed
bonnet, the hospital, and the lameness that had followed. She
spoke without complaint, but Belle's bright face grew sorrowful.

"And I never knew," she said. "I never asked what
became of you."

"You had no way to know."

"I might have found one."

"You had already done more than you guessed."

Lizzie opened a little drawer and took out several paper
shapes, yellowed and worn with age. They were the patterns
of a rose.

"Do you remember these?"

"The pink rose I gave you?"

"Yes. It was my first lesson. I copied it badly
enough, but it made me begin. And when I was
in the hospital, I remembered what you said—that people who
mean to do things often do them. I repeated it
when the pain was bad and when no one would
employ me."

Belle looked at the faded patterns with new respect.

"But surely those few words did not do all that."

"No," Lizzie replied gently. "The boots warmed my feet, and
the food warmed me too; but your kind look warmed
something deeper. You see, miss, I had stolen your rose.
If you had called me a thief and sent me
away, I might have thought there was no use trying
to be anything else. Instead, you trusted me. Afterward, when
I was hungry and could have taken money from a
workroom table, I remembered you. When another girl asked me
to help sell some ribbon she had stolen, I remembered
you. I used to think, 'Miss Belle believed I could
be honest, and I WON'T disappoint her.'"

Belle's eyes overflowed.

"I had forgotten the whole matter for years."

"I never forgot."

There are moments when the light of truth falls suddenly
upon a familiar act and shows us consequences stretching far
beyond our sight. Belle had considered the boots, food, and
flowers a small impulse of generosity, hardly worth remembering. Now
she saw that a gentle word, carelessly spoken and soon
forgotten by the giver, might remain in another heart like
a lamp through years of darkness.

"And now," said Lizzie, wiping her eyes and smiling, "you
need forget-me-nots."

"I do—but I cannot ask you to make them in
so little time."

"You need not ask. I shall do it."

"Can you?"

"I will."

Lizzie drew forward a box of blue muslin. Her manner
changed at once from tender recollection to practical energy. She
selected the right shade, cut tiny petals with astonishing speed,
and showed Belle several finished blossoms.

"They are perfect!" Belle exclaimed. "Each has the little yellow
eye."

"The leaves must be small and soft. The wreath should
be light, since your dress is delicate."

Belle opened her cloak, and the shining gown appeared.

"How lovely!" said Lizzie with an involuntary sigh.

Belle heard no envy in it, only the innocent delight
in beauty which she remembered from long ago.

"You must come to the house when you finish," she
said. "I want Mamma to know you."

"I shall bring the wreath before the carriage leaves."

"And tomorrow I will come back, and we will talk
of everything."

Belle departed, but Lizzie did not watch the carriage drive
away. She had only an hour and a half, and
each flower required five little petals, a yellow centre, a
fine stem, and two tender leaves.

The hands upon the clock moved mercilessly. The canary stirred
beneath its cover; the stove sank low; shadows crowded outside
the lamplight. Lizzie bent over her work, cutting, shaping, binding,
and arranging.

Her lame leg ached. She had worked since dawn, and
her eyes smarted. Still she would not hurry carelessly. This
flower was too large; she cast it aside. That blue
was too cold; she warmed it with a touch of
color. Every blossom must be worthy of the girl who
had given her the first honest rose.

A neighbor came in to borrow a match.

"Working yet, Lizzie?"

"For an old friend."

"You look ready to faint."

"I can rest tomorrow."

When the last spray was fastened, Lizzie held up the
wreath.

It was charming—light, graceful, and fresh as a chain of
blossoms gathered beside a spring brook. The flowers curved naturally;
here and there a bud half opened among the leaves;
and not a thread or wire could be seen.

Lizzie wrapped it in tissue paper, put on her bonnet
and cloak, and took up her cane.

The storm of five years before seemed to have returned.
Snow was falling thickly, and the pavements were slippery. A
cab would have cost more than she could spare, and
none passed Willow Lane; so she set out on foot.

Every step hurt. She moved slowly, guarding the box beneath
her cloak. Once she slipped and caught herself against a
railing, trembling at the thought of another fall. Yet she
went on through the snow, past the dark shops and
crowded crossings, toward the distant square.

At the Hamilton house all was anxiety.

"The carriage is here," said Belle's mother.

"She has not come," Belle answered, looking from the window.

"Wear the pearls, darling."

"I cannot. She promised."

Marie flew up and down the stairs.

"Perhaps the poor artiste has expired from exertion! Perhaps the
wreath is destroyed! Perhaps—"

The bell rang.

Belle ran into the hall before the servant could reach
the door.

Lizzie stood upon the threshold, white with snow, one hand
gripping her cane and the other holding out the box.

"It is done," she said.

Belle carried her into the warm drawing-room and opened the
parcel. Even Marie became silent.

"Ah!" she breathed at last. "That is not a wreath.
It is a poem!"

Placed among Belle's dark curls, the blue flowers looked so
natural that her mother bent near to smell them.

"They are exquisite," she said. "But, my dear, the maker
has paid too dearly for them. Look how pale she
is."

Lizzie tried to say she was only tired, but the
room swam before her. Belle made her sit by the
fire, brought wine and cake, and knelt to remove the
wet snow from her shoes.

The scene was so like the one long ago that
Lizzie laughed softly.

"You are warming my feet again."

"And scolding myself for letting you come through a storm."

"I wanted to bring them."

"What do I owe you?"

Lizzie named a modest price.

"That is not enough."

"It is my usual charge."

"But this was not a usual order."

Belle's hand closed over hers.

"Let me help you properly. I have friends who would
buy your flowers. You shall make all mine, and I
will send customers to you. Perhaps we can find you
a better room, with more light, and someone to carry
orders so that you need not walk."

Lizzie's face flushed.

"I don't want charity, miss."

"Nor do I offer it. I offer work, friendship, and
a little repayment for this beautiful wreath."

"Friendship?" Lizzie repeated.

"If you will have mine."

Poor Lizzie, who had known so much loneliness, could not
answer at once. She looked at Belle in the white
gown and blue garland, shining with youth, wealth, and happiness;
then she looked down at her own worn hands. Yet
Belle's clasp did not loosen.

"At least tell me what you want most," Belle urged.
"There must be something I can do."

Lizzie thought a moment.

"There is one thing."

"What is it?"

"When you are married, may I make the flowers for
your wedding?"

Belle blushed and laughed, for no wedding had yet been
announced, though perhaps one was not impossible.

"Yes," she said. "Every flower. Roses, if you like."

"Roses and forget-me-nots," Lizzie answered.

From that evening, her life grew steadily brighter. Belle kept
her promise. Orders came from ladies who admired the famous
blue wreath, and Lizzie soon employed two girls poorer than
she had once been. She taught them patiently, paid them
fairly, and never forgot that tired workers need kind words
as well as wages.

Belle visited often. At first she went from gratitude; afterward
she went from affection. She learned that a little room
in Willow Lane might contain courage, skill, humor, and contentment,
while Lizzie learned to receive kindness without fear and prosperity
without pride.

In due time there was indeed a wedding, and Lizzie
made the flowers. White roses crowned the bride, while among
them hid a few tiny forget-me-nots. Belle would have no
other wreath.

Years later the garland, faded and fragile, still lay in
a cedar box. To strangers it was only an old
ornament, but to the two friends it told a long
story: of a storm, a stolen rose, a confession, an
icy fall, patient labor, and kindness returned after many years.

Thus friendship bloomed out of the dead roses; and the
little blue flowers seemed always to whisper the lesson their
name expresses so sweetly:

Forget not the poor, the tempted, or the sorrowful. A
gift may warm the body for a day, but a
gentle word and a trusting look may save a heart
for a lifetime.

### THE OLD FIRM

There are few things in this world more impersonal than
a name painted in letters six feet high upon a
hoarding; and there are few things, when you come to
ponder the matter, more intensely personal than the men who
quarrel beneath it.

A name may be placarded from Charing Cross to York;
it may stare from railway arches, blush upon theatre curtains,
and lie in innocent blue type beside the births and
deaths in a provincial newspaper. It may be recommended by
bishops who never heard of it, actors who never used
it, and physicians whose signatures have been reproduced with a
freedom unknown to the laws of penmanship. The public comes
in time to regard such a name not as the
property of mortal men, but as one of the fixed
institutions of the country, like the Bank, the fog, or
the income tax.

Yet behind those serene capital letters there are commonly two
gentlemen in frock-coats disputing over the petty cash.

The famous firm of Danby and Strong was of this
order. For eight years its name was printed, posted, enamelled,
illuminated and, upon one memorable occasion, projected by a magic
lantern upon the side of St. Paul's Cathedral until a
policeman interfered. Danby and Strong manufactured paper collars.

The modern reader, accustomed to less removable forms of respectability,
may smile at the paper collar; but it was a
serious article of commerce. It presented to the clerk, the
curate, the travelling salesman and the thrifty bridegroom the appearance
of linen without the tyranny of the laundry. A man
might wear purity at breakfast, soil it at luncheon and
put on a fresh reputation before dinner. Danby and Strong
did not invent this convenience, though their advertisements strongly suggested
that civilisation had waited for them; but they improved it,
named it, and sold it by the million.

Their chief triumph was the "Imperishable Albion." It was neither
imperishable nor especially English, the paper being Swedish and the
gum American; but these defects did not prevent it from
becoming popular. A row of statesmen, explorers and eminent divines
appeared in the advertisements wearing the Albion with expressions of
grave gratitude. Underneath ran the legend:

> DANBY AND STRONG'S IMPERISHABLE ALBION.
> IT ENCIRCLES THE EMPIRE.

There are men who will forgive an insult to their
wives sooner than an alteration in their trade-mark. William Strong
belonged to this numerous, though insufficiently classified, division of mankind.

Strong was large, florid, energetic and noisy. He had the
useful commercial faculty of believing his own statements five minutes
before he made them. John Danby was smaller, pale, exact
and silent. He could add a column of figures while
Strong was dictating a testimonial from a duke, and could
discover a halfpenny error in a ledger at a distance
which would have prevented him from recognising his own mother.
It was said in the office that Strong made the
business and Danby kept it; which, being repeated to both
partners, naturally pleased neither.

At first they hit it off admirably. Strong invented campaigns;
Danby found the money for them. Strong proposed that every
schoolboy in England should receive an Albion collar on the
Queen's birthday; Danby reduced the proposal to six schools in
Lambeth and obtained the collars back when the headmasters objected.
Strong desired to sue a rival manufacturer, three newspapers, a
music-hall comedian and the Archbishop of Canterbury; Danby, after examining
counsel's opinion, persuaded him to sue nobody. In this manner
the firm prospered.

Prosperity, however, is not always favourable to friendship. Adversity compels
men to stand shoulder to shoulder, if only because the
bailiff is in front of them; success permits them to
turn and observe one another.

Their first serious difference concerned the celebrated "Naval Collar," which
Strong wished to advertise by floating fifty thousand specimens down
the Thames. Danby remarked that paper sank when wet. Strong
replied that this was precisely why Danby would never understand
advertising. Danby retorted that Strong would never understand paper. From
that moment the matter ceased to concern collars.

It is astonishing how rapidly an argument can enlarge its
boundaries. The Naval Collar led to the travelling expenses; the
travelling expenses to Mrs. Strong's use of the firm's brougham;
the brougham to the employment of Danby's nephew; the nephew
to a dinner given in 1889; and the dinner to
an observation made by Strong before either man had entered
the partnership. Within a month they had reviewed, revised and
embittered the whole of their acquaintance.

They ceased to speak except by memorandum. Since their desks
stood in the same room, this gave employment to two
office boys, who carried notes across seven feet of carpet.

"Mr. Strong presents his compliments to Mr. Danby and begs
to inquire by whose authority the warehouse cat is being
fed from the firm's account."

"Mr. Danby presents his compliments to Mr. Strong and encloses
the fishmonger's receipt for threepence."

"Mr. Strong declines to recognise a receipt in pencil."

"Mr. Danby refers Mr. Strong to the Partnership Act."

The cat, being a creature of sense, removed herself to
a neighbouring printer.

Solicitors presently appeared. Solicitors are excellent gentlemen, and it is
unfair to reproach them because a quarrel which enters their
office thin and hungry commonly emerges fat enough to fill
a courtroom. There were injunctions, counter-injunctions, applications for accounts, threats
of mandamuses, a writ of ejectment concerning a cupboard, and
prolonged argument over the ownership of a brass model of
the human neck. Each partner charged the other with destroying
the business, and each proved his sincerity by helping him
to do it.

Orders went unanswered while letters passed between counsel. Customers who
asked for collars received affidavits. A German competitor copied the
Albion, improved the fastening and captured the export trade. The
bank grew nervous; the creditors grew bold; the workmen grew
idle. Finally, after a magnificent expenditure upon law, Danby and
Strong were declared bankrupt.

At the last meeting of creditors, Strong sat on one
side of the table and Danby on the other. Between
them lay the remains of a concern which, eighteen months
earlier, had yielded each man four thousand pounds a year.
The trustee observed that a friendly arrangement might still preserve
something.

"I will agree to anything," said Danby quietly, "which does
not require me to meet Mr. Strong."

Strong rose so violently that he upset an inkstand.

"If I meet him again," he said, "it will not
be in a solicitor's office."

This remark was afterwards remembered. Remarks of that sort nearly
always are—though seldom by the people whose business it would
be to remember them in time.

Strong emigrated to Texas.

Now an Englishman who has never crossed the Atlantic conceives
Texas to be a plain of indefinite extent, occupied by
buffaloes, revolvers and gentlemen in leather trousers who spend the
intervals between murders in galloping after cattle. He believes that
every Texan sleeps in his boots, breakfasts upon raw beef,
and goes out after luncheon to pot some favourite enemy.
The truth, as usual, is less convenient. In Texas there
are banks, land agents, dry-goods stores, school boards, taxes, bad
debts and men who speak for half an hour without
firing a shot. There are even solicitors, though they are
not called by that name.

Strong was at first disappointed.

He had gone west under the impression that civilisation ended
abruptly at the quay in Liverpool. Instead, he found an
elderly merchant in Galveston who wore spectacles, discussed freight rates
and offered him employment selling cotton cloth. Strong rejected the
proposal with contempt. He had not crossed an ocean to
measure calico.

"What do you expect to do?" asked the merchant.

"Ride," said Strong. "Shoot. Deal with cattle."

"Ever rode?"

"In the Row."

"Ever shot?"

"At Bisley."

"Ever dealt with cattle?"

"I was in business with one for twelve years."

The merchant laughed, though Strong had not intended humour, and
gave him a letter to a ranch owner near the
Brazos.

Strong learned. Hatred is a poor virtue, but it can
sustain a man through considerable inconvenience. He learned to sit
a Western saddle, to throw a rope, to endure heat,
dust, bad coffee and the intimate society of men who
washed infrequently but judged character well. He acquired a broad
hat, high boots and two Colts, the latter being less
necessary to his occupation than he supposed, but more necessary
to his conception of it. He grew lean. The London
colour left his cheeks; the softness went from his hands;
and his eyes, always bold, became steady.

The change appeared remarkable to those who imagine that a
black coat contains a civilised man. Clothes restrain nothing. The
savage who had disputed over the warehouse cat remained the
savage who now rode beneath the Texan sun; only the
correspondence had ceased, and he carried his final argument upon
his hip.

For nearly three years he neither saw nor heard of
Danby. He told himself that he had forgotten him. A
man commonly repeats this assertion concerning an enemy with an
earnestness he never wastes upon a friend.

Then, one afternoon, Strong entered a dry-goods store in a
small prairie town to purchase tobacco. While the proprietor searched
among his boxes, Strong's eyes travelled idly over the shelves.
Suddenly they became fixed.

There, between a packet of pins and a tower of
shaving soap, stood a familiar blue carton. Upon it, somewhat
faded by the voyage, were printed the words:

> DANBY AND STRONG'S IMPERISHABLE ALBION.
> IT ENCIRCLES THE EMPIRE.

Strong took down the box.

"Where did this come from?"

The proprietor, a good-natured man with a grey beard, looked
over his spectacles.

"Those collars? State agent sent 'em from Houston. Pretty fair
trade in 'em among hotel clerks and undertakers."

"Who manufactures them?"

"Name's on the box, I reckon."

"Danby and Strong no longer exists."

"That so? Then the party who sends the bills hasn't
heard of it."

Strong's thumb pressed into the cardboard.

The name had survived. The partnership had been wrecked, the
machinery sold, the creditors compromised at sixpence in the pound;
yet here was the old blue carton, travelling prosperously across
the world without him. There is no ghost so offensive
as the ghost that makes money.

He questioned the merchant with an appearance of carelessness. The
merchant, having nothing to conceal and liking conversation, explained that
an Englishman named Danby had revived the brand, arranged for
American distribution and was at that moment travelling through Texas
to appoint agencies. He was expected within the week.

"Travels alone, mostly," said the merchant. "Quiet little fellow. Don't
look like a man who could encircle much of anything."

Strong bought the box and carried it to his lodging.
That night he placed it upon the table and sat
opposite it.

Perhaps Danby had purchased the trade-mark legally from the trustee.
Perhaps he had risked new capital, worked patiently and rebuilt
what their folly had destroyed. Perhaps he was entitled to
use a name which had been as much his as
Strong's. The law might have decided all these questions.

The law, however, is a great restrainer; and Strong was
in a country where, upon certain empty roads, it seemed
very far away.

He learned Danby's intended route. The collar merchant would leave
on Thursday for a settlement some thirty miles north, crossing
a lonely stretch of prairie and spending one night at
a stage station. Strong knew the country well. He also
knew a shallow ravine, hidden by mesquite, where the trail
dipped before rising towards a line of low hills.

On Thursday morning he saddled a fresh horse.

The day was white with heat. No cloud moved above
the prairie, and the grass lay pale and endless beneath
a sky which seemed not to bend over the earth,
but to withdraw from it. Strong reached the ravine before
noon, tethered his horse behind the scrub and waited.

Waiting for a man whom one intends to murder must
be a curious employment. We are apt to suppose that
great passions sustain themselves at an heroic temperature; but the
body has vulgar claims. Strong grew thirsty. An ant entered
his sleeve. His left foot became numb. Twice he imagined
that he heard hoofs, and twice the sound was only
the dry whisper of grasshoppers. Yet through these small discomforts
his purpose remained hard and complete.

At last a rider appeared upon the trail.

Danby rode badly, with the patient stiffness of a man
who regards a horse as an objectionable form of railway
carriage. He wore a light coat and a narrow-brimmed hat
unsuited to the sun. A leather sample case was strapped
behind his saddle. Even at a distance Strong recognised the
slight stoop of the shoulders and the forward inclination of
the head—the posture of a man eternally examining figures.

Strong mounted and rode out.

Danby reined in. For a moment he stared without understanding;
then the colour left his face.

"You," he said.

"Yes."

"What are you doing here?"

"Representing the old firm."

Danby glanced towards the ravine and then across the empty
prairie. There was no house, tree or moving creature in
sight.

"I have no wish to speak with you," he said.

"You had no objection to using my name."

"I bought the trade-mark from the trustee."

"With money hidden from the creditors?"

"That is a lie."

"We need not reopen the accounts," said Strong. "I have
found a quicker way of staying the proceedings."

His right hand dropped to his revolver.

Danby made a sudden movement towards the inside of his
coat.

"Take your hand out," said Strong.

Danby obeyed.

"Two fingers only. Now draw it."

Slowly Danby brought out a small nickel-plated revolver.

"Drop it."

The weapon fell into the dust.

"Get off your horse."

Danby dismounted. His knees nearly failed as his boots touched
the ground, but he steadied himself against the saddle.

Strong remained mounted, looking down upon him. In his London
office he had often imagined this meeting, though the setting
had varied according to his mood: sometimes a deserted street,
sometimes a foreign quay, sometimes the steps of a court.
None of those visions had possessed the immense light, the
silent grass or this small, pale man standing beside a
sweating horse.

"What do you want?" asked Danby.

Strong smiled.

"I am going to murder you."

The plain words seemed to travel out across the prairie
and lose themselves in the heat.

Danby's bloodless lips moved before any sound came.

"If it is money—"

"Money? You think this is a commercial call?"

"You cannot escape."

"My dear Danby, you always exaggerated the importance of legal
formalities. There are no witnesses. You are armed. We quarrel.
We fire. The survivor explains."

Danby looked at the revolver in the dust.

"You have planned it."

"I once planned advertisements. One must employ one's talents."

"Then why talk?"

That question touched Strong unpleasantly, for there was sense in
it. Men who intend murder should not discuss their intentions
with the victim. Vanity, however, has interrupted more crimes than
conscience and completed nearly as many.

"Because," said Strong, "I have waited three years to see
you understand."

"I understand that you ruined us."

"You ruined us."

"You rejected every settlement."

"You stole the name."

"I bought it."

"You stole my life."

Danby's fear altered. It did not disappear, but hatred rose
through it, as a dark object rises beneath thin water.

"We ruined each other," he said. "You merely lacked the
courage to admit your share."

Strong drew his Colt.

"No more minutes of the previous meeting."

He fired.

The bullet struck Danby's right knee. There was a dry,
cracking sound, and Danby dropped beside the horse with a
cry so brief that it seemed bitten through. The startled
animal shied away, dragging its reins.

Strong dismounted.

Danby lay upon his side, gripping the shattered joint with
both hands. His face had become grey, and sweat stood
upon his forehead. Strong watched him with an expression of
almost judicial attention.

"That," he said, "is for the bankruptcy."

Danby looked up. No appeal remained in his face.

Strong raised the revolver again.

The second shot struck near the left eye. Danby's head
jerked backwards. He rolled once, his fingers tearing at the
grass, and lay still. Blood moved darkly through the dust
beneath his cheek.

The pistol report had been short, sharp and echoless. Silence
closed over the prairie with such completeness that the shots
might have occurred in another world.

Strong stood breathing heavily.

Murder, once committed, is a surprisingly practical affair. The passion
which precedes it belongs to tragedy; what follows is housekeeping.

Strong caught Danby's horse and tied it to a mesquite
bush. He picked up the nickel-plated revolver and examined it.
Five chambers were loaded. He fired one shot into the
ground some yards away, then another past his own shoulder.
After reflection, he removed his hat, placed it upon a
stone and sent a bullet through the crown. This pleased
him. The hole supplied danger without inconvenience.

He next walked to his own horse, took a spare
cartridge from his saddlebag and considered the scene as an
artist might consider a picture which lacked balance.

Danby must appear to have fired first. Strong would report
that they had met accidentally, quarrelled over the former business
and drawn together. His pierced hat would support him. Danby's
weapon would contain three spent cartridges. Strong's own shots could
be justified by necessity. In Texas, he had been assured,
juries were broad-minded where firearms were concerned.

He returned to the body.

Danby's face was turned partly downward. The wound had mutilated
the eye and temple, and dust clung to the blood
upon his cheek. Strong felt no pity. He felt, instead,
irritation that the dead man should still require arrangements.

"You always left me the difficult work," he muttered.

He knelt and pulled Danby's right arm forward. The hand
was tightly closed. Strong forced the fingers open one by
one. They were warm, but he noticed this without thought;
the sun had heated everything.

He placed the revolver in the palm.

It fell out.

Strong swore, lifted it again and curled the fingers round
the grip. The forefinger would not remain inside the trigger
guard. He changed his position, leaned closer and pressed the
finger into place.

Danby was not yet dead.

No sign of life showed in the ruined face; no
breath that Strong could hear passed the bloodless lips. But
somewhere beneath torn bone, shock and darkness, the old hatred
continued its business. The hand which Strong had armed closed
faintly.

Strong saw the movement.

For one instant neither man seemed human. The prairie, the
immense sky and the two horses stood remote and indifferent,
while the last remnant of one will confronted the sudden
terror of the other.

Strong sprang backward.

The small revolver exploded.

At that distance it could scarcely miss. The bullet entered
beneath Strong's chin and passed upward into the brain. He
rose upon his toes, dropped his Colt, and fell across
Danby's legs.

For several minutes the horses strained at their reins. Then
one began cropping the grass.

Towards evening a stage driver found the two men. The
pierced hat lay upon the stone; one revolver rested in
the dust, and the other remained locked in Danby's dead
hand. The driver looked at the bodies, the weapons and
the old blue sample case strapped behind the saddle. Being
a sensible man, he did not attempt to reconstruct passions
which had begun in a London counting-house and ended beneath
a Texan sky.

The lawyers afterwards experienced some difficulty over the trade-mark, though
considerably less than the partners had experienced.

The firm of Danby and Strong was dissolved.

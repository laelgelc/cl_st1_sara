## The Judicial Courtship of Mr. Scofield

Herbert Scofield had reached that period of life when a
man who has successfully avoided matrimony begins to regard his
escape with less complacency than formerly. At twenty-five he had
considered marriage an interruption; at thirty, a possibility; at thirty-five,
an investment which no prudent person should make without the
fullest knowledge of the property concerned. This last comparison was
his own, and, since he was a lawyer with a
modest fortune and a constitutional respect for evidence, he thought
it singularly apt.

It followed that when Miss Minnie Madison began to occupy
an undue portion of his reflections, he did not admit
that he was in love with her. He merely conceded
that, should her character sustain a sufficiently prolonged investigation, he
might ultimately decide to fall in love with her.

Miss Madison was twenty-six, handsome without appearing aware of the
fact, and clever without forcing others to remember it. She
possessed good sense, good temper, and a reserve which Mr.
Scofield found highly exasperating because it resembled his own prudence
while being much more gracefully worn. He had met her
in New York during the winter; had danced with her,
called upon her, taken her to the opera under the
shelter of an aunt, and conversed with her concerning books,
pictures, weather, music, flowers, travel, and such other subjects as
society provides when it is determined to prevent two people
from learning anything about each other.

When, therefore, he heard that she was to spend July
at a summer hotel on the Hudson, he discovered that
mountain air was precisely what his health required.

He communicated this discovery to his friend Walter Dane.

"You are going after Miss Madison," said Dane.

"I am going to rest."

"You will find her there."

"So I am informed."

"And you intend to marry her."

"I intend nothing of the sort. I have not yet
had an opportunity to study her character."

Dane looked at him with the benevolent pity which married
men sometimes bestow upon bachelors, as though the latter were
children playing at liberty.

"My dear Scofield, when a man follows a young woman
eighty miles in order to study her character, his own
is already settled."

This was not argument, and Scofield declined to answer it.

He arrived at the Hudson House upon a brilliant afternoon,
with two trunks, a case of books, fishing tackle which
he never used, and a mental resolution to remain wholly
uncommitted. Ten minutes after signing the register, he saw Miss
Madison upon the veranda between two attentive gentlemen, and experienced
the worst state of unrest which his proposed season of
repose could have produced.

One of the gentlemen was Mr. Merriweather, a tall young
man with a pale mustache and an income which had
relieved him from the necessity of acquiring convictions. The other
was Mr. Brice, who had opinions upon all subjects and
delivered them in paragraphs. Both had been established at the
hotel for a fortnight; both knew every walk, view, cascade,
ruin, ledge, and eligible boatman in the neighborhood; and both
appeared to have placed these resources, together with their persons
and prospects, at Miss Madison's disposal.

She greeted Scofield with a smile so pleasant that his
heart rose, and with a composure so complete that it
immediately descended again.

"Why, Mr. Scofield! Who would have imagined finding you here?"

"I should think the hotel clerk might," he replied. "I
wrote for rooms a week ago."

"I mean that I did not know you cared for
the country."

"I have always cared for it."

"You told me in May that the country was a
region where the roads were poor and the newspapers late."

"People sometimes revise their judgments."

"After careful study?"

"Always."

Minnie's eyes rested upon him for an instant with an
amusement which he attributed to the bagatelle under discussion. Then
she introduced him to the two gentlemen, who received him
with that elaborate cordiality which one rival extends to another
before hostilities have been formally declared.

Scofield's plan was simple. He would observe Miss Madison in
varied circumstances. He would note her manner toward inferiors, her
conduct under annoyance, her tastes, habits, sympathies, prejudices, extravagances, economies,
and general fitness to preside over a dignified household. If
the result proved satisfactory, he would pay her marked attention;
if otherwise, he would withdraw before his feelings had become
involved.

This judicial programme would have been admirable had his feelings
consented to await the verdict. Unfortunately, they behaved like an
unruly jury, arriving at a conclusion before the testimony began.

During the first week he learned very little of Minnie
and a great deal of Merriweather. That gentleman rode with
her on Monday, played tennis with her on Tuesday, went
sketching with her on Wednesday, and occupied the chair beside
hers during the greater part of Thursday evening. Brice was
scarcely less assiduous. Scofield, unwilling to appear eager, frequently arrived
after the expeditions had been arranged; and Minnie, unwilling to
make eagerness unnecessary, never saved him from the consequences of
his caution.

"You might have joined us," she would say on returning.

"I did not know that you wished me to."

"How could you know until you came?"

"How could I come until I knew?"

"That is a difficult question, Mr. Scofield. Perhaps Mr. Brice
can answer it. He understands political economy."

Mr. Brice would then explain that all human conduct depended
upon the distribution of capital, while Scofield retired in a
frame of mind wholly unfavorable to political economy.

Minnie was not indifferent to his discomfort. On the contrary,
she perceived it with a clearness which would have astonished
him, and valued it rather more than was charitable. She
liked him; she had liked him in New York; and
she suspected that beneath his deliberate manner there existed a
heart capable of moving with commendable rapidity when once it
had escaped from the custody of his reason. But she
had no intention of presenting herself for inspection like a
house whose drains, title, and southern exposure were to be
examined before purchase.

"If he means to care for me," she thought, "he
may do so without a certificate."

She therefore continued amiable, elusive, and scrupulously impartial. When Scofield
approached, she did not withdraw; when he remained away, she
did not summon him. This was feminine strategy, perhaps; yet
it was also feminine justice. A woman may properly hesitate
to surrender her heart to a man who appears to
be taking an inventory of it.

And so affairs went on. The river shone beneath the
hotel lawn; white sails moved between wooded shores; orchestras played
after dinner; young people made engagements which they forgot and
promises which they remembered; and Scofield, who had come to
examine Miss Madison's character, spent his time counting the occasions
upon which Merriweather sat beside her.

There came a day which brought him to a decision.

A party had been planned for a mountain excursion to
a rocky outlook several miles inland. Mrs. Van Sittart, who
undertook all enterprises with enthusiasm and abandoned them to others
at the first inconvenience, arranged the carriages, selected the provisions,
predicted perfect weather, and remained at the hotel with a
headache. Minnie, Scofield, Merriweather, Brice, and six or seven others
set out without her.

The morning was clear and cool. For the first two
miles they followed a road through fields in which the
hay stood high and fragrant; afterward they entered a wood,
where the path climbed among rocks and laurels. Scofield had
hoped to walk beside Minnie, but Merriweather secured that position
at the start, and Brice occupied the other side in
order to discuss municipal reform.

Scofield followed with Miss Felicia Trent, a young lady who
found every stone romantic and every insect alarming. He pulled
her up the steeper ascents, assisted her across three imaginary
dangers and one actual brook, and watched Minnie's blue dress
disappear and reappear among the trees ahead.

At the summit they lunched, praised the view, carved initials
upon objects which nature had taken several centuries to prepare,
and began the descent in scattered groups. Through some management
of fortune—or of Minnie, though Scofield never knew which—he at
last found himself beside her.

"You have been very useful to Miss Trent," she said.

"I am gratified that my usefulness attracted your notice."

"It attracted everyone's. Her cries were audible at a considerable
distance."

"She was afraid of falling."

"And you are always ready to prevent a catastrophe?"

"When I am permitted."

Minnie glanced at him. "Surely catastrophes do not wait for
permission."

Before he could reply, a shrill cry sounded from beyond
a turn in the path. It was not Miss Trent's
decorative little scream, but the frightened call of a child
in pain. Minnie ran forward; Scofield followed; and they came
upon a girl of perhaps nine years lying beside a
heap of fallen branches. A smaller boy stood near, white-faced
and speechless.

The children had been gathering sticks. The girl, stepping backward
from the bank, had fallen heavily, and one leg lay
twisted beneath her.

Minnie was upon her knees at once.

"Do not move, dear," she said. "We shall take care
of you. Where do you live?"

The child pointed through the trees and began to sob.

"Suthin's broke," said the boy solemnly. "I heard it."

The others gathered around, full of sympathy and conflicting advice.
Brice recommended a splint; Merriweather suggested carrying the child immediately;
Miss Trent cried; and a gentleman named Pollard expressed a
desire for brandy, though whether for the patient or himself
was not stated.

Minnie looked up.

"Please do not all speak. Mr. Scofield, can you find
the house?"

"I can try."

"You," she said to the boy, "show him. Mr. Merriweather,
will you help me keep her still? Mr. Brice, perhaps
you will bring those two straight branches. No, not so
thick. Miss Trent, take off your scarf, please."

Her voice was quiet, and everyone obeyed it. Scofield ran
after the boy down a steep path, through a strip
of woodland, and across a neglected clearing to a small
cottage, where a broad-shouldered woodchopper appeared at the door.

"Your daughter has fallen and injured her leg," Scofield said.
"Where is the nearest doctor?"

The man stared.

"Doctor? There ain't none short o' Ridgeville."

"How far?"

"Six mile—mebbe seven."

"Have you a horse?"

"Got the gray, but she ain't—"

"Put her in the wagon."

The man scratched his head. "I dunno as the wagon'll
hold together fer—"

"It will hold together," said Scofield. "Harness the horse."

There are times when circumlocution is a virtue and times
when it is merely a crime. The woodchopper recognized in
Scofield's manner the authority of a man who had no
intention of discussing distinctions. Within five minutes they were jolting
toward Ridgeville in a wagon whose wheels alternately threatened separation
and reconciliation.

The gray mare was old, and Scofield, who had never
before considered impatience a physical force, learned during that drive
how rapidly sympathy may be converted into speed. He urged
the animal up hills, ran beside her when the road
became steep, and, on reaching the village, brought a reluctant
physician from his luncheon with a directness bordering upon abduction.

The return journey was even more energetic. When they reached
the cottage, the child had been carried thither upon an
improvised litter. Minnie sat beside the bed, holding the little
girl's hand and telling a story about a princess who
had fallen from a tower and afterward become queen of
an exceedingly prosperous country. The child, though pale, listened intently.

Scofield stopped in the doorway.

Minnie's hat was off; her hair had loosened in the
exertion; her face was grave and tender, wholly unguarded because
wholly occupied with another's suffering. She did not see him
at first. He saw her.

There are investigations which proceed by questions, references, and prolonged
observation; and there are truths which reveal themselves in a
single unpremeditated act. Mr. Scofield's inquiry into Miss Madison's character
ended at that moment. The evidence was conclusive, and the
jury, having long since agreed, was discharged.

The physician pronounced the leg broken but the injury uncomplicated.
He commended the splint, rearranged it, and assured the parents
that the child would recover. Minnie rose only when there
was nothing more she could do.

"You found him," she said to Scofield, and her eyes
warmed with approval.

"I should have brought the entire medical faculty if you
had asked it."

"You nearly killed the horse bringing one member of it."

The warmth vanished from her voice.

"The child was in pain."

"The horse could feel pain also."

Scofield looked at the exhausted gray mare standing with lowered
head before the cottage, and his triumph suffered an unpleasant
diminution.

"You are right," he said after a moment. "I did
not think."

Minnie's expression softened. "No; you only acted."

"I wished to serve you."

"The little girl, Mr. Scofield."

"The little girl, of course."

She knew that he had spoken with more candor than
tact, and he knew that she knew it. Yet the
walk back to the hotel provided no opportunity for an
explanation. Minnie went with Miss Trent, who had recovered sufficiently
to describe her emotions; Merriweather remained close at hand; and
Brice occupied Scofield with a discourse upon rural medical administration.

Nevertheless, from that day Scofield abandoned his judicial attitude. He
no longer waited to discover whether Minnie wished him to
join an expedition. He joined it. He no longer concealed
his preference beneath equal attentions to six young women. He
sought Minnie whenever social usage permitted and sometimes when it
did not. He brought her flowers, secured her boat, carried
her shawl, chose her books, and stood ready, like a
well-dressed providence, to interfere with any inconvenience that threatened her.

Unfortunately, love, having defeated prudence, did not thereby acquire wisdom.

Minnie received his attentions with pleasure, but she declined to
reward them with certainty. If he brought roses, she wore
one and gave another to Mrs. Van Sittart. If he
chose a book, she thanked him and afterward discussed it
with Brice. If he asked her to row, she sometimes
accepted and sometimes had promised Merriweather. She did not torment
him from cruelty. She merely waited for him to say
plainly what every gesture announced, and he, who now thought
his devotion conspicuous enough to be visible from the opposite
shore, considered words unnecessary unless he had some assurance of
their reception.

One evening they stood at the end of the long
veranda while music sounded from the drawing-room.

"You have changed since you came here," Minnie said.

"In what respect?"

"You used to be so composed."

"I have ceased to value composure."

"That is a serious sacrifice."

"You may consider it an offering."

"To the mountain air?"

"To the cause which destroyed it."

Minnie lowered her eyes, but a smile touched her lips.
"You speak in riddles, Mr. Scofield."

"I believed my meaning sufficiently plain."

"Meanings are often plain to those who possess them."

"You cannot be ignorant of mine."

"Cannot I?"

At that fatal instant Merriweather appeared with Minnie's wrap. He
placed it about her shoulders with practiced solicitude, and Scofield,
instead of continuing, became as silent as an offended monument.

Another week passed. His ardor increased; his hopes declined. He
watched Minnie laugh with Merriweather beneath an elm, listen to
Brice upon the boat landing, and dance twice in one
evening with a visiting lieutenant. That she also walked with
Scofield before breakfast, consulted him about the injured child, and
kept the dried rose he had given her in a
book escaped his notice. Jealousy is an accountant that enters
every loss and omits each gain.

Minnie wondered how long his pride would struggle against his
affection. She had begun by wishing to punish his caution;
she continued because, having discovered the extent of his feeling,
she desired the dignity of an avowal. Yet she too
had pride. She could offer encouragement, but not surrender; indicate
the road, but not carry him over it. Thus each
stood upon one side of a very narrow gulf and
waited for the other to build a bridge.

At last Scofield concluded that she did not care for
him.

This conclusion was absurd, but it brought him relief of
that bleak kind which follows the abandonment of hope. He
had been made restless, jealous, ridiculous. He had neglected correspondence,
lost interest in business, and developed a detestation of two
men who, apart from their attentions to Minnie, had never
injured him. A retreat was necessary. He would return to
New York on Monday.

Having reached this heroic resolution on Saturday morning, he avoided
Minnie during breakfast and spent an hour informing the clerk
about his luggage. He then took a boat and rowed
alone up the river, intending to be gone until dinner.

The day was bright, though a haze lay upon the
distant hills. He pulled steadily, using his disappointment as if
it were a second pair of oars. Beyond a wooded
point the river widened, and near the eastern shore lay
a small island, little more than a ridge of trees
and grass surrounded at high tide by shallow water. Scofield
had passed it before without interest. Now, as he drew
nearer, he saw a woman standing among the alders and
waving a handkerchief.

He altered his course. A minute later he recognized Minnie.

She had gone out early in one of the hotel
boats and landed on the island to gather fern. While
she wandered beneath the trees, the tide had fallen. Her
boat, left in a narrow inlet, had settled into black
mud beyond her power to move it; and the channel
separating the island from navigable water was now a broad
expanse of glistening ooze.

Scofield brought his boat as near as he could.

"Good-morning, Miss Madison."

His tone was so ceremonious that she knew at once
he was suffering.

"Good-morning, Mr. Scofield. I am in a ridiculous situation."

"It does not appear comfortable."

"I have been here nearly two hours."

"You should not have landed alone."

"If I had known you were coming, I might have
waited."

This was an opening through which a less determined man
would have driven a declaration. Scofield merely looked at the
mud.

"I cannot bring the boat closer," he said. "The bottom
is too soft."

"I feared not."

"There is no firm path?"

"I have tried both sides. Unless I remain until the
tide returns—"

"That will not be necessary."

He removed his shoes and stockings, rolled his trousers above
the knee, stepped over the side, and sank at once
into mud halfway to that joint. Minnie uttered a little
cry.

"You cannot come through that!"

"I am already coming."

"But you will be covered with mud."

"That appears probable."

He dragged each foot free with labor, thrust it forward,
sank again, and advanced with a dignity poorly suited to
his appearance. The black slime clung to him; hidden shells
cut his feet; once he nearly fell and preserved his
balance only by plunging both hands into the mud. The
rescue lacked every conventional feature of romance. There was no
foaming torrent, no runaway horse, no burning building—only a hatless
lawyer proceeding slowly through malodorous ooze while the lady of
his heart begged him not to spoil his clothes.

Yet Minnie, watching, felt no inclination to laugh.

He reached the firmer edge of the island at last
and stood before her, breathless and stained.

"I am afraid," she said, "that I have caused you
a great deal of trouble."

"It is of no consequence."

His face, however, indicated that something in the world was
of considerable consequence.

"How am I to reach the boat?" she asked.

"I shall carry you."

Her color deepened. "Indeed, you will not."

"There is no other way."

"I can take off my shoes and wade."

"You would sink. Besides, there are sharp shells."

"You have come through them."

"That is different."

"Because your feet are of no value?"

"Because mine are mine."

"And mine?"

He looked at her, but pride locked the answer behind
his teeth.

"Please, Miss Madison. The boat is drifting against the anchor
rope, and we have no time for a debate."

She stood very straight. "I told you once, I think,
that a woman could not simply be picked up and
carried off."

"I remember."

"And now you propose to do it."

"Only to the boat."

The bitterness of the last words was so plain that
Minnie's amusement deserted her. She saw that he believed himself
tolerated, perhaps used, and that he intended to serve her
while asking nothing. The discovery touched her more deeply than
his flowers, his jealousy, or even his race for the
doctor. He had then acted partly for her praise; he
acted now in spite of what he thought was her
indifference.

She lowered her eyes.

"Very well," she said. "Since it is necessary."

He lifted her carefully. She placed one arm about his
shoulders and gathered her skirt with the other hand. She
was lighter than he had expected, but the mud made
each step a struggle. Once his foot slipped.

"You will fall," she whispered.

"Not while I am carrying you."

"That sounds boastful."

"It is not intended to sound anything."

"Are you angry with me?"

"No."

"You are very grim."

"I am occupied."

"With the mud?"

"With leaving the island."

She was silent for several steps. Then she said, close
to his ear, "You mean to leave something else, I
think."

He stopped, and the mud received him another inch.

"I leave for New York on Monday."

"So soon?"

"There is no reason for remaining."

"No reason at all?"

"None that is sufficient."

Minnie's arm tightened almost imperceptibly about his shoulder. Had Scofield
been less miserable, he might have interpreted the movement; being
wholly absorbed in renunciation, he attributed it to fear of
falling.

He reached the boat and placed her upon the stern
seat with a care that was almost impersonal. Then he
waded back, pushed off, climbed in, and washed the mud
from his hands and feet over the side. He put
on his stockings and shoes without speaking.

Minnie watched him.

"You have not asked what became of my boat," she
said.

"I shall send someone for it when the tide rises."

"You arrange everything."

"So far as I am able."

"And then go away?"

"Yes."

He took the oars. The sun had begun its descent,
and the river, ruffled by a southward breeze, showed changing
strips of blue and gold. For several minutes they moved
in silence. Minnie sat opposite him, so that every pull
brought her face toward him and every recovery carried it
away. The arrangement seemed to Scofield an intolerably accurate representation
of their acquaintance.

At length she said, "May I ask why you are
leaving?"

"Business."

"You came for rest."

"I have rested enough."

"I should not have thought so."

"Rest sometimes produces unrest."

"Is that another riddle?"

"No. It is a confession."

She waited, but he offered no more.

Near the point below the hotel a boat appeared, moving
rapidly toward them. Merriweather was at the oars. He had
learned from a servant that Minnie had not returned, and,
with a zeal sharpened perhaps by seeing Scofield's empty place
at luncheon, had set out to find her.

"There is Mr. Merriweather," said Minnie.

Scofield's mouth hardened.

"He will be relieved to see you safe."

"Will he?"

"He has naturally been anxious."

"And you?"

"I found you by accident."

"That does not answer me."

"No answer from me can be important."

Minnie looked away toward the wooded shore. "You have no
right to say that."

"I have every right to govern my own estimate of
my importance."

"But not mine."

The oars paused. The boat drifted.

Merriweather was approaching. His white shirt and pale mustache became
distinct; he raised one hand in greeting.

Scofield resumed rowing.

"Miss Madison," he said, "I have tried to avoid saying
what would embarrass you. Perhaps I have merely succeeded in
embarrassing myself. I came here because of you. I remained
because of you. I am leaving because—because I have learned
that what I hoped is impossible. You need not answer.
Indeed, I beg that you will not."

"That is very convenient."

"Convenient?"

"You follow me from New York in order to study
my character; you watch me as though I were a
witness under examination; you decide, without consulting me, first that
I may be worth caring for, and then that I
cannot care for you. Having conducted the whole case for
both sides, you now beg the court not to answer."

He stared at her.

She continued, though her cheeks were bright: "It is all
extremely judicial, Mr. Scofield."

"I ceased to be judicial weeks ago."

"I had begun to suspect it."

"Then you must also have suspected—"

"Many things may be suspected which ought to be stated."

Merriweather's boat was now near enough for his voice to
carry.

"Miss Madison! Is anything wrong?"

"Nothing now," Minnie called.

Scofield's heart gave one violent and wholly unjudicial bound.

Merriweather came nearer. "I saw your boat on the mud.
I feared you had met with an accident."

"I was stranded."

"May I take you back? My boat is cleaner, I
think."

This was true. Scofield's boat bore unmistakable traces of the
rescue, and Scofield himself, despite his efforts, still displayed mud
upon his sleeves and trousers.

Minnie glanced at the vacant place beside her on the
stern seat, then at Scofield, who still sat opposite with
the oars in his hands.

"Thank you, Mr. Merriweather," she said. "Mr. Scofield has brought
me safely so far."

"Then I will accompany you."

"There is scarcely room."

Merriweather looked puzzled. There was plainly room in both boats.

Minnie turned to Scofield. "You have rowed a long distance.
Perhaps you are tired."

"I am not tired."

"But if you were to sit here beside me for
a little while"—she moved slightly and laid one hand upon
the empty cushion—"Mr. Merriweather might understand that it is unnecessary
to accompany us."

The last remnant of Scofield's caution struggled nobly for existence.

"Understand what?" he asked.

Minnie's eyes met his. They were demure, but no longer
evasive.

"That I am not obliged to return with the first
gentleman who offers to carry me off."

For one second he remained motionless. Then he shipped the
oars and crossed to the seat beside her with such
eagerness that the boat rocked dangerously.

Minnie caught the gunwale.

"You will overturn us, Mr. Scofield."

"I beg your pardon."

"You are still too energetic."

"I shall never again mistreat a horse."

"I was not thinking of the horse."

"No," he said, taking her hand beneath the shelter of
her folded shawl. "Nor am I."

Merriweather had ceased rowing. He looked from Minnie to Scofield,
from Scofield to the narrow space between them, and finally
toward the hotel. He was not a brilliant man, but
there are situations which illuminate even moderate intelligence.

"Shall I send someone for your boat, Miss Madison?" he
called.

"Mr. Scofield has arranged it."

"Of course," said Merriweather.

He lifted his hat, turned his boat, and rowed another
way.

The sun descended behind the western hills, laying a broad
road of light across the Hudson. Scofield regarded it with
the expression of a man who has unexpectedly acquired not
only the prize for which he had contended, but also
a new conception of his own stupidity.

"Minnie," he said, and stopped, because the use of her
name seemed at once audacious and insufficient.

"Yes, Herbert?"

That completed his defeat.

"You knew," he said presently. "You knew all the time."

"Not all the time."

"Nearly all."

"I hoped."

"And you let me believe you preferred Merriweather."

"I never said so."

"You permitted him to be constantly near you."

"I also permitted you."

"But you never made it clear."

"Would you have valued anything made too clear?"

"I should have valued peace of mind."

"You came here to rest," she reminded him.

He laughed, though with some ruefulness.

"And my investigation?" he asked. "You knew of that too?"

"My aunt heard you tell Mr. Dane that you intended
to study my character."

Scofield closed his eyes.

"He had no right to repeat it."

"My aunt has never regarded the absence of a right
as an obstacle to conversation."

"And that is why you were so obdurate?"

"I was not obdurate. I merely declined to assist at
my own appraisal."

"You have punished me severely."

"Have I?"

He looked down at their joined hands.

"No," he said. "On reflection, I withdraw the complaint."

The tide had turned. Around the little island the water
crept back over the black mud, effacing the path by
which he had carried her. Her stranded boat would soon
float again, but neither of them paid it further attention.
Some vessels, once released, may be recovered by servants; some
opportunities, if lost, do not return with the tide.

Scofield had come to the Hudson to determine whether Minnie
Madison was worthy of his love. He went back to
the hotel knowing that love is not a judgment pronounced
after evidence, but an authority which first seizes the court,
then dismisses the case, and finally carries off the judge
nolens volens.

As for Minnie, she had insisted, with perfect truth, that
a woman could not simply be picked up and carried
away. Yet she had allowed precisely that proceeding when the
proper man, having abandoned all claim of ownership, came through
the mud to serve her.

This distinction may appear subtle. Minnie understood it. And in
time—under affectionate instruction—so did Mr. Scofield.

### The Wax Angel

I

The water in the pail had frozen during the night,
not solidly, for the room was never quite so cold
as the street, but with a thin, grey skin which
broke beneath Sashka's knuckles and cut them like glass. He
struck it twice, angrily, though no one had asked him
to wash, and watched the black pieces sink among threads,
cabbage leaves, and other small household refuse. Then he wiped
his hand upon his trousers.

"You'll tear them," said his father from the bed.

"Let them tear."

"They were mended yesterday."

"So they can be mended again."

His father moved under the blanket and began to cough.
The blanket was an old horse-cover, brown and hairless along
the folds; whenever he coughed, the whole shapeless heap rose
and fell, while from beneath it came a dull rattling,
as though somebody were dragging an iron chain through a
cellar. When at last his thin, yellow face appeared, the
eyes seemed too large and mild for it.

"You had better wash, Sasha," he whispered. "Your mother'll come."

"I'm not afraid of her."

"No, of course not. Still, you had better."

Sashka took a piece of ice from the pail and
held it against his cheek. The cold made his teeth
ache. In the cracked looking-glass over the washstand he saw
one black eye, half a mouth, and a projecting ear;
by moving to the right he could collect another portion
of himself, but never the whole. He grimaced at the
fragments.

Thirteen years had formed him badly but firmly. He was
small, broad-shouldered, and quick, with a low forehead from which
the coarse hair sprang in stubborn points. His clothes, altered
from those of his father's healthier days, were too wide
across the chest and too short at wrist and ankle;
the elbows had patches like round leather shields, and one
knee was covered by cloth of a different colour, so
that from a distance he seemed to be kneeling even
when he stood. His eyes, grey and restless, possessed that
weary vigilance which is seen in old dogs kicked from
too many doorways.

The room was still almost dark. Through the window, filmed
by frost and kitchen vapour, the reddish street lamp appeared
as a blurred and swollen eye. The stove had gone
out. On it lay an empty vodka bottle, a bent
spoon, two onions, and the blue pawnbroker's ticket for Sashka's
father's winter coat.

Sashka looked at the ticket and spat into the pail.

"What's that for?" asked his father.

"For nothing."

"You shouldn't spit indoors."

"Then let me spit outdoors."

Again the sick man coughed. Sashka waited with his head
turned away, his lower lip thrust out; yet when the
coughing lasted too long, he crept towards the bed and
shoved the pillow higher behind his father's shoulders.

"Don't fuss," he said savagely. "You lie like a sack.
How's a fellow to pull you up?"

"Thank you, Sasha."

"I didn't do it for thanks."

The key scraped in the outer door.

At once the boy stepped from the bed. His shoulders
rose, his face hardened, and the movement of tenderness, being
discovered by himself before another had discovered it, became shameful.
He kicked the stool into the middle of the room.

His mother entered carrying a bundle of laundry under one
arm. Snow had whitened the black woollen shawl over her
head, and her cheeks were red from cold and drink.
She was a tall woman, heavy in the breast and
hips, with powerful hands steamed raw by washing. Poverty, which
had dried and hollowed her husband, had thickened her. Every
year she became stronger, louder, and more indestructible, as though
all the nourishment missing from the others had, through some
malicious law, passed into her.

"What's this?" she cried, striking the stool with her boot.
"Why's it in the road? You devil's whelp! Want me
to break my neck?"

"You won't break it. It's too thick."

For a moment she stared at him. Then the laundry
fell to the floor and her hand shot out. Sashka
ducked. Her fingers grazed his hair, and before she could
seize his collar he had put the table between them.

"Come here!"

"Come and get me."

"I'll kill you!"

"You always say that."

His father raised one hand from the blanket.

"Now, now, Varvara—Christmas Eve—"

"You keep out of it! Christmas Eve! And what's Christmas
to the likes of us? Is Christmas bringing bread? Is
the holy infant paying the rent? You lie there and
preach while I slave my arms off."

"I didn't preach."

"Of course not. You never do anything."

She turned again upon Sashka, but he had already slipped
behind the stove. Only his head showed above the corner,
with bright, hostile eyes.

"He's been expelled," she said, addressing the room, the stove,
God, and any neighbour who might be listening through the
partition. "Expelled for good! The headmaster himself sent word. What
do you say to that?"

The father shut his eyes.

"What did you do, Sasha?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing!" cried Varvara. "They always throw boys out for nothing.
First he puts tar on the teacher's chair. Then he
locks the little ones in the privy. Then he calls
the priest a stuffed crow—"

"He does look like one."

"—and yesterday he struck the inspector in the stomach."

"He grabbed my ear."

"So you struck him?"

"He shouldn't grab."

The father opened his eyes and gazed at the ceiling.
Once he had been a clerk in a lawyer's office
and had owned three white shirts, a watch, and a
handwriting so elegant that strangers paused to admire his ledgers.
In those days he had believed education to be a
door which opened, if not upon happiness, then at least
upon a clean corridor leading towards it. Now the door
had shut upon his son with a bang, and behind
it stood the inspector clutching his stomach.

"What will become of you?" he asked faintly.

Sashka grinned.

"What's become of you?"

The words were hardly out before Varvara caught him. She
had overturned the table with a shove and seized the
hair at his temple. He twisted, bit her wrist, and
received a blow against the ear which filled the room
with a crimson ringing. His father called out. A cup
fell and broke. The mother swore, the boy swore more
inventively, and through the thin wall a baby began to
cry.

When it was over, Varvara stood panting amid the laundry,
with blood upon her wrist, and Sashka crouched by the
door. A red mark burned across his cheek. His cap
was in his hand.

"Go on, then," she said. "Get out! Go and freeze.
Go and steal. That's what you're fit for."

He jammed the cap over his ears.

"I will steal."

"Mind they don't cut your hands off."

"They ought to cut your tongue out."

The door slammed behind him.

In the passage, where it was dark and smelled of
kerosene, wet boots, and cats, Sashka stood quite still. His
breath came in hard little jerks. He wanted to return,
to throw something, to smash the remaining cup or drag
the vodka bottle from the stove and fling it at
his mother's head. Instead he pressed his bitten lip against
his sleeve.

From within he heard his father say, "You shouldn't have
struck him so hard."

Then came Varvara's heavy voice:

"And who strikes me softly?"

Sashka went down the stairs.

The street had the blue, uncertain light of a winter
morning which seems unwilling to become day. Snow lay in
ridges along the gutters, trodden black where carts had passed.
A baker's boy hurried by with a basket covered in
white cloth; warm, yeasty fragrance escaped beneath it, and Sashka
followed for half a block, not because he had any
plan, but because hunger drew him as a chain draws
a dog.

At the corner he met two schoolboys in belted coats,
their books strapped together, their ears pink and polished from
washing. They saw him and moved aside.

"Well?" said Sashka.

They said nothing.

"Going to school?"

The smaller nodded.

"Fine place, school. They'll teach you wisdom."

He snatched the boy's cap, threw it into a snowbank,
and waited. The child stared, astonished rather than frightened, which
irritated Sashka more than tears would have done.

"Pick it up," said the older boy.

"You pick it up."

"It isn't mine."

"Then leave it."

The smaller child began to cry. Sashka wanted suddenly to
strike him, to strike the round clean face and feel
beneath his fist the softness of a life in which
breakfast stood ready upon a table and mothers did not
throw cups. But a policeman appeared at the crossing, and
Sashka sauntered away, whistling.

Freedom, when he had dreamt of expulsion, had seemed immense.
No bell, no inspector, no ink-stained desk; no standing in
the corner with his face to the wall while behind
him thirty boys breathed and tried not to laugh. The
streets would belong to him, the markets, the frozen river,
the railway yard where engines exhaled white clouds into the
sky. Yet after three days liberty had narrowed to a
stomachache, wet feet, and the necessity of keeping out of
sight of policemen.

He spent the morning in the lumber yard, where stacks
of timber made yellow passages sheltered from the wind. There
he found two smaller boys smoking a cigarette-end. He demanded
it, smoked until nausea came, and then cuffed them for
watching him turn pale. At noon he carried a sack
for a market woman and was paid with half a
frozen turnip. He ate it behind a church, crunching the
icy fibres with aching teeth while worshippers passed in furs
and the bells proclaimed peace above the roofs.

Towards evening the snow began again, dry and fine as
flour. Lamps appeared one after another. Windows glowed amber; behind
their frost flowers one caught fleeting glimpses of other existences—hands
setting dishes upon cloth, heads bent beneath shaded lamps, children
running with ribbons and parcels. Every light seemed to say:
Here is warmth, but not for you; here is bread,
but not for you; here people are awaited, and you
are not awaited anywhere.

He was thinking of going home when someone called his
name.

A servant girl in a green coat stood beneath an
umbrella at the gate of a large stone house. He
knew her: she belonged to the Svechnikovs, old acquaintances of
his father from the days of the white shirts and
the admired handwriting.

"Alexander Ivanovich!" she called again, giving his dirty clothes a
doubtful look. "Your mother's been hunting for you."

"Let her hunt."

"You're invited upstairs. The mistress sent me. There's a children's
tree."

"I'm not a child."

"Well, you're not a general. Come along."

"I won't."

"They've a present for you."

He looked past her into the courtyard, where carriage lamps
shone upon smooth snow.

"What present?"

"How should I know? Gloves, perhaps. Or a book."

"I don't need their rubbish."

But he followed her.

II

The Svechnikovs' entrance hall was so warm that the snow
upon Sashka's cap melted at once and ran down his
neck. His boots, whitened by salt and split at the
sides, left dark stains upon the waxed floor. The servant
girl took his coat and held it away from herself
with two fingers.

"You might at least have brushed," she muttered.

"You brush it."

He entered a room full of mirrors, palms in brass
pots, and people who all seemed to be speaking without
listening. Light poured from a crystal chandelier and lodged in
polished furniture, gold frames, silk shoulders, smooth hair. Sashka stopped
just inside the doorway. He had meant to appear indifferent,
even insolent; but in this brightness every patch upon him,
every roughness of his hands, every stale household smell imprisoned
in his clothes, became separately visible.

Anna Petrovna Svechnikova came towards him. She was a plump,
agreeable lady with fair hair arranged high above her forehead
and a soft double chin which moved when she spoke.
Her husband had once worked beside Sashka's father and, being
prudent, healthy, and capable of agreeing with his superiors, had
risen until he possessed this apartment, these mirrors, and a
waistcoat crossed by a gold chain.

"So here is our young rebel," said Anna Petrovna.

Sashka looked at the buttons of her dress.

"Why don't you greet me?"

"Good evening."

"And wish me a happy Christmas?"

"It isn't Christmas yet."

Several adults smiled. Their smiles were gentle, but Sashka heard
laughter in them.

"Still literal," said a gentleman with whiskers.

"Still independent," said Anna Petrovna. "Your poor father must be
terribly distressed."

"He isn't."

"We have heard about the school."

"They lie."

"My dear boy, the inspector himself—"

"He lies too."

The gentleman with whiskers laughed aloud. Anna Petrovna sighed and
placed a scented hand upon Sashka's head. He jerked away.

"No, no," she said. "We shall not be rude this
evening. You are among friends. Remember that kindness is worth
more than pride."

To Sashka, kindness at that moment appeared to consist of
standing him beneath the chandelier while clean persons examined his
dirt. He shifted his weight and thrust both hands into
his pockets.

"May I go?"

"You have only just arrived."

"I didn't want to come."

A flush rose over her throat.

"Then perhaps you came for your present?"

"I don't want that either."

"What do you want?"

"Nothing."

He said it triumphantly, for nothing was the only possession
they could not withhold from him.

From the next room came a sudden opening of doors,
a cry of children, and a blaze so powerful that
even the adults turned. The Christmas tree had been lit.

It rose almost to the ceiling, broad, green, and astonishing,
its branches burdened with candles, gilded walnuts, chains of coloured
paper, sugar fruits, little drums, silver cones, red apples, dolls,
horns, and boxes tied with ribbon. At its foot lay
parcels in shining heaps. The many flames burned upright in
the motionless air and made a strong, unwavering light in
which every ornament had a sharp edge and every child's
face seemed newly varnished.

The children rushed forward. Their voices climbed over one another.

"That horse is mine!"

"Look at the ship!"

"Mamma, may I have the orange?"

"Don't touch the candles."

A little boy in a sailor suit saw Sashka and
approached with the fearless curiosity of one who has never
been struck for staring. His name was Kolya, and his
pale curls hung almost to his collar.

"Who are 'oo?" he asked.

"Nobody."

"Are 'oo a bad boy?"

"Yes."

Kolya considered this.

"Did 'oo hit the schoolmaster?"

"Inspector."

"Was he naughty?"

"Very."

"Did he cwy?"

"He will next time."

Kolya smiled, pleased with the answer. He held a wooden
horse against his chest, a splendid bay animal with real
hair in its mane.

"I dot this," he said. "What did 'oo det?"

"Consumption."

"Is it a toy?"

"It makes you cough till you die."

Kolya's mouth opened. A nurse hurried over and drew him
away.

"You mustn't talk nonsense to him," she whispered sharply.

"Then stuff cotton in his ears."

Sashka withdrew into a corner behind a tall piano. Here
the light was dimmer. No one offered him a parcel,
and he was glad; or rather he told himself that
he was glad, clenching his fingers in the empty pockets
until the nails hurt. Children received soldiers, books, dolls, skates,
paint boxes. They thanked their parents, kissed the hostess, tore
paper, and complained when one gift was less splendid than
another. A red-haired girl began to cry because her doll's
hat would not come off. Sashka watched her with narrowed
eyes.

To destroy the tree would be easy. One tug at
the green baize covering its stand, one shove, and the
proud, blazing thing would descend upon them in fire and
broken glass. Their ribbons would catch; their curls would burn.
Then they would know. They would run and scream, while
he stood laughing in the smoke.

He moved nearer.

At that moment Anna Petrovna called him.

"Alexander, here are your gifts."

On a small table lay a pair of knitted mittens,
a woollen scarf, and a book with a picture of
a kneeling boy on its cover. The kneeling boy had
smooth hair and an expression of such repentance that Sashka
hated him immediately.

"You see," said Anna Petrovna, "we did not forget you."

"I don't need them."

"The scarf is very warm."

"I'm warm already."

"Take them for your father's sake."

"My father doesn't wear mittens."

The whiskered gentleman made a sound in his throat. Anna
Petrovna's voice became firmer.

"Your pride is misplaced. Those who have little should be
grateful when others remember them."

"Keep them, then."

"Sasha," said Mr. Svechnikov, approaching, "a gift is not payment.
No one means to insult you."

"Then why's everybody looking?"

Indeed everybody was looking: adults openly, children from behind their
toys, servants at the door. Beneath those eyes he felt
himself becoming smaller and dirtier, a captured street animal dragged
indoors for entertainment. He wanted his mother's blows, the frozen
pail, the dark passage—anything but the mildness of these faces.

Anna Petrovna held out the scarf.

"Come. Let us be friends."

He struck it from her hand.

There was a silence. The scarf fell beside the table,
one end trailing like a tongue.

"Pick it up," said Mr. Svechnikov.

Sashka did not move.

"Pick it up and apologize."

"No."

The man seized his arm. The grip was not painful,
yet its restraint contained all authority—school, police, money, warm houses,
people who never shouted because others obeyed before shouting became
needful.

Sashka bent suddenly and tried to bite him.

Several women cried out. The children laughed. Mr. Svechnikov released
him with disgust.

"Little savage!"

"There, you see?" said the nurse.

"Send him away."

"Poor child," somebody murmured, and these two words, more than
"savage," more than laughter, caused a hot darkness to pass
before Sashka's eyes.

He backed towards the tree. His heel crushed a paper
box. A little girl screamed that it was hers.

Then, while turning to curse her, he saw the angel.

It hung high enough to have escaped the children's hands,
but not at the bright outer edge of the tree.
A heavier branch curved before it, making a small green
chamber into which the candlelight entered softened and golden. There,
as though it had flown from some far quiet place
and paused among the needles, was a little figure made
of pale wax.

It was no longer than Sashka's hand. A slender robe
descended from its breast in folds almost transparent at the
edges; two wings rose behind its shoulders, each feather delicately
marked. The head inclined a little, not in sorrow and
not in joy, but in that grave tenderness with which
one might look upon suffering already understood and forgiven. Its
hands were crossed. A thread of silver held it to
the branch, yet the thread could scarcely be seen, and
the angel appeared to rest in the dark air by
its own will.

Sashka ceased to hear the room.

The candles still burned, mouths moved, the piano's black surface
received and returned a hundred points of light; but all
this withdrew. Even the tree itself, insolent in its abundance,
laden with sweetness and possessions, disappeared around that quiet face.

He stared.

The angel's wax was not white. In shadow it had
the faint blue of snow at evening; where a candle
shone through one wing, it became rose-coloured and living. The
little lips seemed about to open. What would it say?
Nothing that the school priest said, certainly; nothing about obedience,
sin, punishment, gratitude. Those words belonged to canes, corners, hungry
mornings, and the contemptuous charity of full people. This being
possessed another language, or needed none. It knew what could
not be told: why his father coughed in darkness, why
his mother drank and struck, why he himself injured the
weak and hated the strong; it knew, and knowing did
not turn away.

His mouth fell open. The hard line between his brows
relaxed. His hands came out of his pockets and hung
helplessly at his sides.

Kolya crept near and tugged his sleeve.

"Do 'oo like the twee?"

Sashka pushed him off without looking, but gently, so that
the child merely sat down upon a parcel and blinked.

The angel trembled. Someone had brushed the lower branches, and
from the tips of its wings there passed a scarcely
visible shiver. It seemed preparing to fly, not upward towards
the painted ceiling, but away from the whole room, through
walls and snow and night, to a place whence no
one was expelled and no one came home drunk. Terror
seized Sashka. It might go before he touched it. It
might vanish, and then he would again be what he
had been five minutes ago, only with the knowledge that
somewhere there existed this beauty and that it was not
his.

He turned to Anna Petrovna.

She was speaking to the whiskered gentleman, and when Sashka
caught her sleeve she drew back instinctively.

"What is it now?"

"Give me that."

"What?"

"That thing."

He pointed. His finger shook.

"The angel? No, my dear. It belongs on the tree."

"Give it."

"You refused your gifts."

"I'll take the scarf. I'll take all of it. Only
give me that."

She looked at him with surprise, then with the pleased
caution of an educator who sees an opportunity.

"Why do you want it?"

He glared. How could she ask? If she could look
at the angel and still ask why, no answer would
pass between them.

"I want it."

"For yourself?"

"Yes."

"And what will you do with it?"

"Nothing."

Anna Petrovna smiled.

"That is not very convincing."

Mr. Svechnikov joined them. "What does he want?"

"The wax angel."

"That? It is part of the decoration. Give him a
sweet instead."

"I don't want a sweet!" cried Sashka.

Heads turned again. The angel quivered in the stirred air.

"Quietly," said Anna Petrovna. "Ask properly."

"Please give it."

"That is better. But you must also apologize for your
behaviour."

He swallowed.

"I'm sorry."

"For what?"

"For the scarf."

"And for trying to bite Pavel Nikolayevich."

"I'm sorry."

The words came hard, dry, and separate. She considered him.

"Do you mean it?"

"Yes."

"Look at me when you speak."

He looked. His face had become pale beneath the grime,
and there was something in his eyes that disturbed her,
though she did not yet understand it.

"Perhaps," she said, "if you promise to return to school
and be obedient—"

"They threw me out."

"Then promise to behave at home."

"I promise."

"And not torment little boys."

"I promise."

"And say your prayers."

"I promise everything. Give it here."

His voice broke upon the last word. A snicker came
from among the children. At once the familiar fury flared
in him; he wheeled round, searching for the laugher, and
Anna Petrovna shook her head.

"You see? Already you forget."

"I didn't do anything."

"You were about to."

"I won't. I swear I won't."

She folded her hands. To her, the angel was one
ornament among many, perhaps bought that afternoon for fifteen kopecks;
and yet, precisely because it was insignificant, she felt she
must not surrender too quickly. Charity, like medicine, ought to
have an unpleasant taste, else the poor might acquire a
liking for it.

"One does not swear," she said. "One asks humbly."

"Humbly?"

"Yes. Pride has been your great fault. Kneel and ask
forgiveness."

Mr. Svechnikov frowned.

"That is unnecessary, Anya."

"It may do him good."

Sashka looked from her to the angel. The room had
grown very still, or seemed so to him. His ears
rang as they had after his mother's blow. Kneel? Before
these people, before the red-haired girl and the nurse and
Kolya with his wooden horse? Kneel upon the polished floor
in his patched trousers, that they might carry the story
from house to house: the savage begged, the savage crawled,
and we taught him humility?

"No," he said.

Anna Petrovna shrugged.

"As you wish."

She turned away.

The angel moved again. Its wings trembled in the mild
upward current from the candles, and its face, entering shadow,
appeared already more distant.

"No—wait."

Anna Petrovna looked back.

Sashka stood with teeth clenched. His knees would not bend.
He had been made to kneel at school upon dried
peas, and had stood up with blood spotted through his
stockings; he had fallen beneath his mother's fists but always
risen cursing. The whole poor kingdom of his self-respect consisted
of never asking, never yielding, never showing where pain entered
him. If he knelt now, that kingdom would be gone.

But the angel would be his.

He dropped to one knee.

Someone laughed softly. He lowered the other knee and rested
upon both. The patches showed. His fists pressed against his
thighs.

"Forgive me," he muttered.

"What did you say?" asked Anna Petrovna.

"Forgive me."

"And the angel?"

"Give it to me."

"Please."

"Please."

The hot darkness rose again, but now it dissolved strangely,
becoming water. Tears filled his eyes. He bent his head
so no one might see them, and whispered:

"Please. Dear lady, please. Give me the little angel."

Anna Petrovna's face changed. Whether she understood at last, or
merely became ashamed of the watching room, she stepped quickly
to the tree.

"Pavel, lift it down."

"No!" cried Sashka, springing up. "You'll break it. Let me."

A chair was brought. He climbed upon it, then upon
the lower rung of a small ladder held by a
servant. The brilliant branches brushed his cheeks and gave out
their bitter resinous fragrance. A candle flame leaned towards his
sleeve; someone warned him, but he paid no heed.

Near at hand the angel was more beautiful and more
fragile. A small dent showed in one wing; a thread
of dust lay across the robe. Its humanity was not
diminished by these faults. Rather it seemed to Sashka that
the little being had travelled far and suffered gently upon
the way.

His rough fingers hovered. He feared to touch it. Then,
with a tenderness which none present would have believed those
hands capable of, he loosened the silver thread and lowered
the angel into his palm.

The wax was cool.

A deep breath escaped him. He bent his head over
it, sheltering it with his body, and for one instant
the angry boy, the expelled pupil, the tormentor of smaller
children, disappeared altogether. There remained only a child in patched
clothes holding beauty as one holds a wounded bird.

"My dear little thing," he whispered. "My dear, dear little
thing."

"What did he say?" asked Kolya.

No one answered.

Anna Petrovna gave Sashka the scarf and mittens. He accepted
them without protest, rolled them carelessly beneath his arm, and
asked for paper for the angel. When a maid brought
tissue, he refused to let her wrap it. He did
so himself, awkwardly but with infinite care, leaving the angel's
face uncovered lest it be smothered.

At the door Anna Petrovna touched his shoulder.

"You see how pleasant it is to be good?"

He drew away, yet without malice.

"You didn't give it because I was good."

"Why, then?"

He looked at her with a grave, exhausted contempt beyond
his years.

"Because I wanted it more than you did."

Then he went out into the snow.

III

The cold took him by the throat, but he scarcely
felt it. He carried the angel inside his coat, between
shirt and breast, where no fall could hurt it. Every
few steps he put in a hand to assure himself
that it remained there. The wax, warmed slightly by his
body, seemed almost alive.

The streets had become quieter. Above the roofs the sky
was black and clear, with a few hard stars. From
tavern doors came yellow light, steam, laughter, and the smell
of spirits; sleigh bells passed and faded. Sashka walked rapidly,
avoiding drunken men and boys who might jostle him. For
the first time he feared a blow not for his
own sake.

At the corner near home he remembered his father.

The thought came not as an obligation but as a
discovery. His father must see it. Of all people, perhaps
only he would see it truly. The others had looked
at a tree ornament; Anna Petrovna had weighed it against
an apology; the children would have broken it and asked
for another. But his father, who lay shivering beneath the
horse blanket and spoke softly even when cursed, would understand.

Sashka ran.

The room was lit by the smoky cooking lamp. His
mother was gone. On the table stood a crust of
bread and a cup; the broken china had been swept
into a neat white pile by the wall. His father
lay as before, but his eyes were open.

"Sasha?"

"Where's she?"

"Gone to the tavern, I think. Or to your aunt's.
What have you there?"

"Wait."

He shut the door, pushed a rag into the crack
beneath it, and came to the bed. His movements had
an air of secrecy and ceremony. First he placed the
scarf and mittens on the stool. Then he drew the
packet from his shirt.

"They gave you something, then," said his father.

"They gave me rubbish."

"And that?"

"This isn't rubbish."

He unfolded the paper.

The angel appeared in the poor room.

At once, by its presence, the soot upon the walls
seemed blacker, the lamp more smoky, the damp stains larger;
yet the room was not merely shamed by it. A
faint radiance entered the misery, not abolishing one object, not
mending the blanket or filling the bread box, but revealing
that these things, too, existed beneath some gaze gentler than
human judgment.

The father raised himself upon one elbow.

"Let me see."

"Don't touch."

"I won't."

Sashka held it near the lamp. The wavering flame passed
through the thin wings, and they glowed softly against the
darkness.

For a long while the father said nothing. His breathing
became shallow. He looked first with surprise, then with an
attention so profound that the boy glanced from the angel
to him, almost jealous.

"Well?" Sashka demanded.

"It is beautiful."

"You've seen one like it?"

"No. Yes—perhaps. Not like that."

His eyes had filled, though he smiled. Memory, which in
the sick and defeated often sleeps for years beneath daily
humiliation, had awakened all at once, and not in fragments
but as a whole country opens beneath lightning.

He saw a garden in spring, the wet black branches
of apple trees and a young woman walking beneath them
in a pale dress. She was not beautiful according to
others; he remembered that distinctly, and remembered being angered by
it. Her mouth was too serious, her shoulders too narrow.
Yet when she listened, inclining her head, there came over
her face that same expression which now rested upon the
wax angel—tenderness without pity, sorrow without reproach, a knowledge of
happiness made grave by its passing.

Her name had been Sophia.

Before the lawyer's office, before Varvara, before illness and vodka
and rooms where frost grew inside the windows, there had
been summer evenings, books carried beneath his arm, clean cuffs,
and Sophia's hand resting lightly upon a gate. He had
meant to study. He had meant to become worthy, to
earn money, to take her away from a harsh uncle
and a provincial house where everyone quarrelled over inheritance. He
had believed his life stood before him like a bright
road.

Then her family removed to another town. Letters came, then
fewer letters, then one in an unfamiliar hand. She had
married. Years afterwards he heard that she had died giving
birth to a daughter who also died. By that time
he was already married to Varvara and had begun to
cough.

The memories contained little injustice when considered separately. No villain
had stolen Sophia; no storm had destroyed a wedding carriage;
no curse had been pronounced. There had been only delay,
poverty, timidity, other people's decisions, and that weak compliance by
which a man assists in the ruin of his own
life. Yet their sum seemed monstrous. Looking at the angel,
he felt not merely his private sorrow but the vast
sorrow of all that is possible and never comes to
pass.

"Sophia," he whispered.

"What?" said Sashka.

"Nothing."

"Who's Sophia?"

"Someone I knew."

"A woman?"

"Yes."

"Before Mother?"

The father smiled faintly.

"Everything good was before your mother, eh? Don't say that.
She too was different once."

"I don't believe it."

"She laughed easily. She had a red ribbon. I remember
it."

"She drinks."

"So do I."

"You don't beat people."

"I have done worse, perhaps."

Sashka frowned. Such talk threatened the clear arrangement by which
his mother was cruel, his father weak, rich people false,
teachers tyrants, and he himself answerable to no one. But
the angel, held between them, seemed to trouble every judgment.

"Did this Sophia look like it?" he asked.

"No. Not in her face. There was something—" His father
stopped and coughed into the blanket. "Something one cannot put
into words. When you are young, Sasha, you think words
can hold everything. Justice, love, truth—one says them loudly and
believes the matter settled. Afterwards one learns that the truest
things have no names, or else their names have been
worn out by liars."

Sashka considered this suspiciously.

"You talk like a book."

"Yes. A bad habit."

"Was she rich?"

"No."

"Then why didn't you marry her?"

The father looked again at the angel.

"I thought there would be time."

The answer was so simple that Sashka could not mock
it.

They remained silent. The stove clicked as its iron contracted.
In the passage a door opened, voices quarrelled briefly, and
somewhere below a child was singing the same three notes
over and over. The angel's wings trembled whenever a draught
reached the lamp.

"Give it here," said the father.

"You'll drop it."

"I won't."

Sashka hesitated, then placed the figure upon his father's palm.
The hand was narrow, hot, and almost transparent; blue veins
showed beneath the skin. It shook so badly that Sashka
at once put his own hands underneath.

Thus they held it together.

Neither looked at the other. Between the sick man and
the embittered boy lay years of small estrangements: the father's
feeble advice, the son's contempt; questions unanswered, tenderness concealed, shame
converted into rudeness. They did not vanish. Sashka did not
suddenly forgive his father for being broken, nor did the
father imagine that beauty had made his son obedient and
pure. Yet for those minutes they faced the same thing
and knew that they faced it together.

"I knew you'd understand," said Sashka.

His father's lips moved.

"What?"

"I didn't say anything."

"You did."

"Well, I knew."

The father turned his head towards him. Sashka's hair was
damp with melted snow; a bruise darkened one cheek, and
beneath the sharp, defiant mouth there still lingered the helpless
softness with which he had looked upon the angel. The
father wanted to put an arm around him. He even
lifted it. But embarrassment, that vigilant jailer of the poor,
stopped the movement halfway, and he merely brushed a bit
of fir needle from the boy's shoulder.

Sashka noticed and pretended not to.

"Where shall we put it?" he asked.

"On the wall?"

"Too dark."

"By the window?"

"Someone might steal it."

"Who?"

"Anybody."

They searched the room. The safest and most visible place
was upon the cord stretched over the stove, where stockings
and rags were usually hung to dry. Sashka dragged over
the stool and tied the silver thread carefully. The angel
hung above them, turning a little, its face inclined towards
the bed.

"There," he said.

His father lay back. "Yes. There."

The stove had been lit while Sashka was away, and
beneath its iron plate the embers still possessed a dull
red life. The warmth was slight, but precious. It kept
the water from freezing solid; it eased the father's cough;
it made sleep possible. Sashka pushed in two bits of
wood and closed the door.

"Not too much," said his father. "We've little left."

"Let it burn."

He lay down upon a pallet beside the bed without
undressing. The mittens served as a pillow, the new scarf
as a blanket over his chest. From there he could
see the angel. The cooking lamp had been turned low,
and its yellow flame made a narrow halo around the
waxen head.

"Are you asleep?" he asked.

"No."

After a pause the father said, "Sasha."

"What?"

"I am sorry."

"For what?"

The sick man searched among many answers—poverty, illness, weakness, the
mother, the school, the whole inheritance of anger which he
had given his child without intending it.

"For not having done better."

Sashka shifted on the pallet.

"You couldn't."

It was not forgiveness. It was perhaps only fatigue. Nevertheless
the father closed his eyes and received it as mercy.

A few minutes later Sashka whispered, "Do you think it
can fly?"

"What?"

"The angel."

"In a fashion."

"It hasn't moved."

"You would not see it."

Sashka gazed upward. "If it flew away, it might come
back."

"Perhaps."

"Here?"

"Why not here?"

The boy smiled in the darkness, a small and unpractised
smile which made him look younger. His breathing slowed.

The father did not sleep for some time. Behind his
closed lids he saw Sophia's narrow shoulders beneath apple blossoms,
Sashka kneeling before strangers, Varvara with the red ribbon she
had worn twenty years ago, and himself walking upon the
bright road that had never existed except because he once
believed in it. These images were sad, but the sadness
had become clear and almost sweet. The angel seemed to
gather the scattered pieces of his life and hold them,
not reconciled, not justified, but illuminated.

At last he too slept.

The stove grew hotter.

There was no miracle in what followed. Wax obeys warmth
without hatred and without pity. The same heat which loosened
the sickness in the father's chest, which dried Sashka's wet
boots and protected the sleeping household from the winter night,
rose steadily towards the cord.

First the lower folds of the angel's robe became glossy.
A tiny bead formed at the hem, trembled, and dropped
upon the stove with a faint hiss. Then the crossed
hands softened. One arm bent from its prayerful position; the
head inclined farther, as though under an unbearable sorrow.

The room remained still.

Outside, a sleigh passed. Its bells sounded briefly beneath the
window and were gone.

The angel's wings, being thinner than the body, yielded next.
Their delicately marked feathers blurred; their edges curled inward. For
a moment the figure appeared truly to struggle in flight,
one wing lifted, the other drooping, while the silver thread
turned slowly above the heat. Then the face lost its
features. The grave eyes closed into wax; the mouth which
had spoken without words disappeared.

Drop after drop fell.

On the pallet Sashka stirred and murmured something. His hand
reached upward, perhaps in dream, but did not wake him.
His father coughed once and sank deeper beneath the blanket.

By the time the lamp exhausted its oil, nothing recognisable
remained upon the thread. A pale lump had gathered on
the stove plate, mixed with soot and a burnt strand
of silver. One small wing endured a little longer, translucent
and bent, then slid down and collapsed upon the rest.

Towards dawn a cockroach emerged from a crack behind the
stove. It paused, moving its feelers in the cooling air,
then crawled across the shapeless wax, over what had been
the breast and folded hands, and vanished beneath the bread
cupboard.

Grey-blue morning entered through the frozen panes. It showed the
overturned stool, the mended clothes, the empty bottle, the father's
hollow face, and Sashka's bruised cheek resting upon his new
mittens. In distant yards gates opened. Cart wheels groaned. Factory
whistles began calling men and women back to labour.

Father and son slept on, each guarded for a little
while longer by the angel that no longer existed.

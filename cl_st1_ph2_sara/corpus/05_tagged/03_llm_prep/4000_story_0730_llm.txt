"Of course," said Ellison, from the darkest corner of the
studio, "you went because you wanted to see a ghost."

"I went," I replied, "because I did not believe in
one."

"That is not quite the same thing."

"It was quite the same thing at twenty-three."

They laughed at that—all except Dr. Vale, who continued to
scrape a match along the box without lighting it. The
rain ticked at the long skylight overhead, and somewhere behind
the curtains the wind worried an ill-fitting frame. We had
turned down the gas for effect, though no one would
admit it; and the red points of three pipes made
a little constellation about the stove.

"If I tell it," I said, "I shall give you
only the essentials."

"You?" said my uncle.

"Yes, I. And if I mention the colour of the
wallpaper, it will be because the wallpaper matters."

"Wallpaper never matters," Ellison observed.

"You have never been frightened," I said. "When you are
frightened, wallpaper, bootlaces, unpaid bills, the name of your first
governess, and the fact that you have left your best
umbrella in an omnibus all matter enormously. That is one
of the essentials."

So I began.

The house stood in Mallow Street, which, as you know,
runs behind one of the busiest roads in London and
contrives, nevertheless, to look at night as though no human
foot had crossed it since the Great Fire. There were
lamps at either end, but none near the house. A
narrow churchyard backed upon it, and the houses opposite had
those secretive, flat faces which seem to close their eyes
when the shutters are drawn.

It belonged then to Sir Henry Vane, my uncle's friend—not
my uncle here, but my mother's brother, Colonel Lascelles. Sir
Henry had inherited the place, could neither sell nor let
it, and was fond of saying at dinner that Londoners
would pay ten guineas to hear a doubtful medium knock
under a table but would not take a sound house
at half rent if a genuine ghost were included.

The ghost, according to him, was a woman.

She had been murdered in an upper room by a
jealous lover. Her body had been hidden beneath the boards,
discovered years afterward, and removed; but she herself remained. Tenants
heard her pacing. Servants saw her on the stairs. A
solicitor who spent one night there was found at dawn
in the street without his collar, unable for some hours
to say how he had got out. There were variations,
naturally. In one version the woman wore grey; in another,
white. Sometimes she wrung her hands; sometimes she had no
hands to wring. But all agreed that she wept.

Now, I had been reading psychical literature with the appetite
other girls give to romances. I played hockey, rode a
bicycle, smoked when I could do so without offending elderly
relations, and held the cheerful belief that nerves were an
invention of badly educated women. I had attended séances and
caught one medium moving a trumpet with her foot. I
had slept in a room where a suicide was alleged
to walk, and slept excellently. Therefore, when Sir Henry said
no one would remain alone in Mallow Street between midnight
and dawn, I said I would.

There was an argument about propriety.

There always is, when a woman proposes to do anything
interesting.

In the end, my uncle agreed to put me into
a cab at eleven, the caretaker agreed to meet me,
and a policeman on the beat was informed that a
young lady with a latchkey had not necessarily robbed the
premises. I took a candle-lantern, matches, cigarettes, a flask of
coffee, a packet of sandwiches, and a small revolver that
I did not know how to use. The last object
gave me courage until I remembered that ghosts are seldom
shot; after which it made me nervous by suggesting burglars.

The caretaker, Mrs. Briggs, was waiting on the step. She
was a square woman in a bonnet, and disapproved of
me before I had spoken.

"You won't stay, miss," she said.

"I intend to."

"They all do."

"Have you seen the woman?"

"No, miss, and don't want to. Hearing's sufficient."

"What have you heard?"

Mrs. Briggs glanced past me toward the black windows.

"One crying where there wasn't anybody. One walking where there
wasn't room. And once"—she lowered her voice—"a gentleman speaking under
the stairs."

"What did he say?"

"I didn't stop to ask."

She unlocked the door, pushed it inward, gave me the
key, and refused my offer of half a crown with
the air of one rejecting blood money. She had arranged
a chair and table in the front drawing-room. There was
a gas jet, a rug, a kettle, and, to my
surprise, a bright fire.

"You'll keep to this room, if you've sense," she said.

"Where was the murder?"

"Top floor, back."

"Then why should I keep here?"

She looked at me with a kind of mournful contempt.

"Young ladies nowadays," she said, "seem to think the Lord
put stairs into houses for them to go looking for
trouble."

Then she left.

The door closed. Her steps crossed the pavement, diminished, and
ceased. A cab went by at the end of the
street. Its lamps moved across the ceiling like two pale
hands and vanished.

I was alone.

At first it was delightful. I drew off my gloves,
warmed myself, poured coffee, and wrote down the time—eleven forty-seven—in
a notebook. I examined the room. The wallpaper was yellow,
Ellison, with a design of brown pomegranates; and it matters
because, later, I counted those pomegranates to prevent myself from
screaming.

The furniture had gone, except for what Mrs. Briggs had
provided. Rectangles upon the walls showed where pictures had hung.
The window curtains were dusty, the grate smoked a little,
and the ceiling rose so high that the gas did
not illuminate it. Beyond the half-open door lay the hall,
faintly grey. The staircase ascended from it and turned sharply
out of sight.

At twelve, a church clock struck. Others answered from farther
off, until all London seemed to be announcing midnight in
different tempers. Then silence returned—not country silence, but the enormous,
muffled silence of a city: wheels far away, a train
underground, water ticking in a pipe, the uneasy respiration of
millions behind brick.

Nothing happened.

At twelve twenty I smoked a cigarette.

At twelve forty I ate a sandwich.

At one I began to feel foolish, which was the
effect I had expected and desired. I imagined Sir Henry's
face when I told him. I considered a spirited article
on the credulity of men. I even wished that Mrs.
Briggs had left something to read.

Then a man said behind me:

"You should not have come."

I turned so violently that the chair fell.

He stood between me and the door.

My first sensation was not supernatural terror but outrage. He
had no business there. He was tall and thin, in
dark clothes neither old-fashioned nor quite modern, and his face
was colourless in the weak gaslight. His hair fell untidily
over his forehead. He looked about thirty-five, perhaps forty. He
looked tired. More than tired—desolate.

But he was a man, and I was alone with
him in an empty house at one in the morning.
You may imagine that all my bold theories concerning feminine
independence became instantly less abstract.

"How did you get in?" I demanded.

He did not answer.

"I have a revolver."

His eyes moved toward my bag, where the revolver lay
beneath two sandwiches and a powder compact.

"You are frightened," he said.

"I am not."

"You are. I feel it."

His voice was very low, yet it seemed to come
not merely across the room but through the floor, touching
the soles of my boots. I backed toward the fireplace.

"If Sir Henry sent you," I said, "the joke has
gone far enough."

At the name, his face altered.

"Henry?"

"Sir Henry Vane. The owner."

"The owner," he repeated. "Yes. They own houses. They own
keys. They own stories. But not what is left in
the dark."

There is a point at which odd speech ceases to
sound theatrical and begins to sound mad. I preferred a
ghost to a madman. A ghost might pass through one;
a madman might wring one's neck.

I seized my bag.

"Stand aside."

"No."

"Then I shall call the police."

"He cannot enter while you fear."

That sentence, which made no sense, terrified me more than
a threat. I moved to the window and tugged at
the sash. It would not rise. The man took one
step nearer.

"Do not bring fear," he said. "Not more. I cannot
endure more."

I threw the coffee at him.

It was not dignified, but any one of you men
now listening to me would have thrown the kettle as
well.

The cup struck the wall.

I saw it strike. I heard it break. Brown liquid
ran down the pomegranates. But the man had not dodged.
For an instant—less than an instant—the cup appeared to pass
through his shoulder.

Then he came toward me.

I dashed for the door.

I do not remember touching him. I remember cold, and
a sensation like cobweb across my mouth. Then I was
in the hall, tearing at the front door. The key
would not turn. I pulled, twisted, bruised my hand against
the lock, and behind me came the voice:

"Do not leave me."

That did it. I fled upstairs.

The staircase was broad for one flight, narrow for the
next. My lantern banged against the banister; my skirts caught
at my knees; dust rose under my feet. I passed
empty rooms where scraps of paper stirred though there was
no draught. Behind me there were no footsteps, but at
each landing the voice seemed close to my ear.

"You are frightened."

"Stay back!"

"You carry it with you."

On the second floor I slammed a door and locked
it. I found myself in a bedroom with bare boards,
a black mantelpiece, and two windows looking over the churchyard.
The clouds had covered the stars. A lamp beyond the
wall made one wet gravestone shine.

I listened.

Nothing.

I was safe.

Then he said, from within the room:

"This is where I died."

I screamed. I am not ashamed of it. I screamed
with all the breath I possessed and flung myself against
the locked door, but the key spun uselessly in my
fingers. He stood by the windows, his dark figure drawn
against the paler dark.

"I won't hurt you," he said.

"You are a ghost."

"Yes."

The word quieted me.

That seems absurd, but uncertainty had been worse. A ghost
belonged to a category. There were books about ghosts, societies
for ghosts, rules—at least proposed rules—concerning apparitions, vibrations, and telepathic
impressions. Once he admitted what he was, I could begin
to think.

"You are the murdered woman," I said.

For the first time his expression showed something like astonishment.

"Woman?"

"The woman murdered here."

He laughed.

It was not a pleasant laugh. Nor was it cruel.
It was the noise a man might make on learning
that his own funeral had been held for someone else.

"There was no murdered woman."

"Then who are you?"

He came nearer, and I pressed myself to the door.
I could see him more clearly now, though no light
had increased. His face was lean, the mouth bitter, the
eyes deeply shadowed; but in those eyes was terror—fixed, ancient,
shameful terror. Not the terror of something presently approaching. The
terror of something that had happened and would never cease
happening.

"I am the man who was frightened to death," he
said.

The pipes in the studio had all gone out by
then. I noticed it because Ellison, who always relights his,
did not move.

I asked the ghost what he meant.

"I lived here," he said. "I hated people, and I
believed them evil. That is a kind of madness, though
clever men call it philosophy. I shut myself away. Every
cruelty I heard of proved me right; every kindness I
called hypocrisy. I studied death because life offended me. I
read of hauntings, possessions, forces without bodies, thoughts surviving the
brain. I filled these rooms with expectation."

He touched the mantelpiece. Or seemed to. His fingers lay
upon it without quite obscuring the stone.

"Then fear came. Not from outside. Do you understand? I
made it. Fed it. Gave it rooms, hours, names. At
first I feared that something watched me. Later I heard
it move. At last I saw it—always behind me, always
at the edge of sight. Physicians said exhaustion. Henry said
I must travel. I accused them all of plotting to
imprison me."

"Were they?"

"No. I wish they had."

He looked toward a corner where the darkness had gathered
in a triangular mass.

"One night I locked myself here, as you have done.
I heard a sound on the stair. I knew it
was coming. I knew it would open the door. The
sound was my own heart, perhaps; but knowledge has less
power than fear. The door moved. Something entered. I saw
nothing—and I died."

"From fright?"

"From fright."

"The man who was frightened to death," I whispered.

"Yes."

His face twisted at the words, and I understood that
he had repeated them through years as both confession and
sentence.

"But why remain?" I asked.

"Because death changes less than you think. A miser carries
his hunger where there is no gold. A tyrant carries
his will where there is no one to command. I
carried fear. Fear bound me to the place that held
its shape. Hatred strengthened the walls. Remorse locked the doors."

He took another step.

I raised both hands.

"No nearer."

Immediately he stopped.

"You see?" he said. "You see what you do?"

"I see that I am shut in a room with
a dead man."

"You fear me, and your fear enters me. Every one
who comes gives me more. The curious are worst. They
sit below and invite horror. They desire a sign, but
they tremble lest it come. Their minds reach through the
house like cold fingers. They make shapes for me. A
woman weeping. A murderer. A thing beneath the floor. And
I—who have almost forgotten form—I become what they dread."

"Then let me go."

"I cannot."

"Will not."

"Cannot! Your fear holds both of us in its condition."

He lifted his hands, and I saw that they shook.

That changed something. Only a little. I still felt as
though hot water had been poured beneath my skin; my
knees were weak, my tongue dry, my thoughts ridiculous and
multitudinous. I remembered that my left stocking had been darned
with darker wool. I wondered whether Mrs. Briggs would find
my body and tell everyone I had thrown coffee at
the wallpaper. I thought of my mother saying that smoke
made a woman's hair smell coarse. I counted six brown
pomegranates, then seven, though there was no wallpaper in that
room.

But I looked at his hands, and they shook more
than mine.

"What do you want?" I asked.

"To cease."

"Can I help you?"

"Yes."

"How?"

He was silent so long that the wind seemed to
circle the house twice.

"Do not fear me."

"I cannot help it."

"Try."

"I am trying."

"No. You are controlling your body. That is not the
same."

This annoyed me. Terror can coexist with annoyance, and sometimes
annoyance is useful.

"You are asking a great deal," I said. "You appeared
behind a woman alone at midnight, pursued her upstairs, admitted
to being dead, and now criticize the quality of her
self-command."

"I know."

"If you know, be reasonable."

"I was never reasonable alive."

There was such humility in it that, against my will,
I nearly smiled.

He saw.

For one instant his outline sharpened. He became less like
a stain upon darkness and more like a human being.
I saw threads at the edge of his sleeve and
a small scar beside his mouth.

"What happened?" I said.

"You pitied me."

"Perhaps."

"Do it again."

"One cannot pity to order."

"Then know me."

His voice changed. It lost its hollow resonance and became
simply a man's voice, tired and urgent.

"I was cruel," he said. "Not with blows. I had
not that courage. I wounded those who loved me because
love made claims. My mother came to this house and
I refused to see her. Henry sent letters; I burned
them. A woman—" He paused. "There was a woman who
would have married me. I told her tenderness was vanity
and marriage a trap. She wept in the room below,
and I watched the carriage take her away with satisfaction.
I believed myself free."

"And were you?"

"No. I had only emptied the prison."

He turned his face from me.

"After death I remembered every offered hand. Remorse is love
recognized too late. It calls and calls, but the living
cannot hear in the direction from which the dead speak."

I no longer pressed against the door. My fear had
not vanished, but it had shifted, as a dark landscape
shifts when one realizes the distant crouching beast is only
a broken tree.

"What would release you?" I asked.

"Love."

The word sounded extravagant in that bare room, almost indecent.
We modern girls used it jestingly or not at all.

"Whose love?"

"Any love without fear."

"You expect someone who never knew you to love you?"

"I expect nothing. I have expected nothing for years. But
you asked."

He looked at me again, and now the dread in
his eyes was unbearable because it was no longer dreadful
to me. It was suffering—naked suffering, which is the most
terrible sight in the world.

"Those who came before," he said, "hated what they feared.
Even when they prayed, they prayed against me. They wished
to banish, compel, expose, investigate. Their curiosity entered like a
knife. One good thought might have opened the way, but
they guarded themselves. People guard love as though Heaven issued
only a little and extravagance would beggar them."

"What must I do?"

"Do not ask unless you mean it."

"I mean to help you."

"Then come near."

I took one step.

He shuddered, and the room darkened. At once my courage
failed. His face blurred; his arms lengthened strangely; a cold
breath swept from him and extinguished my lantern. I cried
out and retreated.

"No!" he groaned. "That is your fear. Not me. Do
not make me monstrous."

"I can't see you."

"See what I was."

"I'm frightened."

"I am frightened too."

"I'm frightened," I repeated.

"So am I."

There we stood, the living woman and the dead man,
confessing fear to one another in the dark.

Then, I cannot tell you by what process, I understood
him completely. Not his metaphysics; Dr. Vale may arrange those
afterward. I understood the simple misery. He had been afraid
of life and therefore rejected love; afraid of death and
therefore could not die; afraid of judgment and therefore judged
himself eternally. Every terrified visitor had cried, in effect, "Keep
away! You are not one of us!" And that cry
had been the chain.

Any one of you might say compassion is easier when
the sufferer has ceased to be dangerous. But I did
not know that he was harmless. I knew only that
if I ran from him then, he would remain in
that empty house, hearing forever the footsteps of the thing
he had made.

I stepped forward again.

"Don't change," I said.

"I do not change. You change me."

"Then help me."

"How?"

"Speak."

"What shall I say?"

"Your name."

"Adrian Vane."

"Adrian," I said.

The darkness yielded. He stood close before me, no longer
luminous, no longer shadowy, but frail and distinct. I could
have believed him alive except that I felt no warmth
from him.

"Tell me you are not afraid," he whispered.

"I won't lie."

His mouth trembled.

"Tell me you could love me."

"I could pity you."

"Pity looks down. I cannot rise by that."

"What, then?"

"Love me."

He said it without command, without seduction, with the desperate
simplicity of a child outside a locked door.

"Love me."

I thought of all the women whose affection he had
scorned, and of his mother turned from the house, and
of that unnamed woman watching the windows from her departing
carriage. Perhaps what I felt was theirs, arriving late through
me. Perhaps it was mine. At such moments ownership is
a small question.

I put my hands upon his shoulders.

They met resistance—light, uncertain, like pressure against running water. Cold
went through my palms to my heart, and for one
dreadful second the old terror rose, shrieking at me to
pull away.

He closed his eyes.

"Do not fear."

"I am trying."

"Love me."

"I do."

"Kiss me."

That, I confess, was harder.

To embrace an apparition in a metaphysical spirit is one
thing; to kiss a strange man in an attic is
another, and the conventions persist in the most inconvenient circumstances.
I heard all London judging me—my mother, Mrs. Briggs, Sir
Henry, the policeman, every matron at every tea party I
had ever endured.

Then Adrian opened his eyes, and there was no demand
in them. Only the certainty that I would fail.

So I kissed him.

His lips were cold, but not with the coldness of
death. They were cold like rain. He made a sound—not
a groan, not a sigh—and his arms came around me.

Again he whispered, "Kiss me."

I kissed his forehead, his eyes, his mouth. I held
him as I would have held someone rescued from deep
water. The cold altered. It became heat, but a heat
that did not burn the flesh; it ran through me
like light through coloured glass. His body grew lighter in
my arms and at the same time the whole room
filled with him—with grief, remorse, gratitude, and a sweetness so
fierce that I could scarcely endure it.

"Again," he said, though his voice seemed far above me.

I kissed him again.

Then he dissolved.

There is no other word. He did not vanish from
before me. He vanished within my embrace. My arms closed
upon cool wind and burning fire. For an instant the
empty room was full of radiance—not light seen by the
eyes, perhaps, but glory felt in every nerve. I knew
a joy that was not mine. It rushed upward, outward,
through roof and darkness, and the house gave one long
shudder, like a sleeper released from nightmare.

Afterward, I stood alone.

The door opened at my touch.

Downstairs, the gas still burned. The fire had gone low.
The broken cup lay by the wall and coffee stained
the brown pomegranates. My notebook showed twenty-three minutes past two.

I stayed until dawn.

Nothing else happened. The house was merely empty—wonderfully, peacefully empty.
Its silence had become ordinary. When Mrs. Briggs arrived, she
found me asleep in the chair.

Sir Henry and my uncle came at nine. They expected
triumph or hysteria and were disappointed by both.

"Well?" Sir Henry asked. "Did she weep?"

"There was no woman," I said.

His expression changed.

My uncle began fussing about breakfast, but Sir Henry sent
him to speak with Mrs. Briggs. Then he shut the
drawing-room door.

"What did you see?"

"A man."

He sat down.

"What sort of man?"

"A very unhappy one."

"Did he give a name?"

I hesitated. "Adrian."

Sir Henry went grey.

The story of the murdered woman, he told me, had
been invented. He had planted it deliberately, altering details for
each investigator. He wanted to discover whether apparitions arose from
suggestion. Some saw grey, some white, some heard weeping, because
those were the things they had been led to expect.

But there had been an Adrian Vane—his cousin. Adrian had
lived in the house, withdrawn from everyone, become obsessed with
unseen presences, and died in the upper back room during
an attack of morbid terror. The physician's certificate named failure
of the heart.

"No one outside the family knew," Sir Henry said. "Not
Briggs. Not your uncle. Certainly not you."

I said nothing.

"What did he tell you?"

"That he was frightened to death."

Sir Henry covered his face.

After a while he asked, "Is he still there?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

"I know."

"What happened in that room?"

Here, if you please, I followed my original rule and
gave only the essentials.

I told him Adrian was free.

I did not tell him how.

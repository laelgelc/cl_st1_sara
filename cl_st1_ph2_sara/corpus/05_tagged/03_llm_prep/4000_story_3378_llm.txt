## Mrs. Dacre Takes the Line

"Central! Central, I say! What ails the woman? Has she
fell down the well?"

Mrs. Dacre shook the receiver hook with an energy surprising
in one who had not set a foot upon the
floor for seven months.

A mild buzzing inhabited the instrument. Then came a click,
a sigh, and the operator's weary voice.

"Did you ring, Mis' Dacre?"

"Ring? I've near pulled the contraption out by the roots.
Gimme two-long, one-short."

"The doctor's line?"

"I know whose line it is, Lyddy Pike. I helped
bring Doctor Ames into this world before you was thought
of. Two-long, one-short."

There followed two bells, a pause, and another bell, all
sounding faintly through the black receiver. Mrs. Dacre settled deeper
among her pillows. Her bed, a high walnut structure with
four carved posts and a white counterpane as rigid as
Sunday behavior, stood in the downstairs sitting room because, as
she said, stairs were not made for invalids and no
earthly affliction should deprive a decent woman of her front
windows.

The telephone had been fastened to the wall within reach
of her right hand. On her left stood a little
table bearing a glass of water, witch-hazel, camphor, peppermint drops,
three tracts, a blue china bowl of apples, and the
village weekly folded to the deaths and marriages. Above the
mantel the great clock ticked solemnly, and Mrs. Dacre maintained
that it was "tickin' off her mortal span loud enough
to be heern in Jericho."

The telephone, happily, ticked nothing off. It rang, clicked, buzzed,
whispered, coughed, and occasionally uttered such shrill fragments of human
intimacy as made mortality seem an exceedingly crowded condition.

Mrs. Dacre pressed the receiver close.

"Hello?" said Doctor Ames.

Before she could answer, another voice broke in.

"Ed, is that you? The brindle cow's took bad again."

Mrs. Dacre's eyes sharpened.

"I told you, Hiram Pote," said a woman's voice, "not
to take that beast into the south medder. There's bitterweed—"

"Hush up, Melissy. I'm askin' Doc."

"And I'm tellin' ye—"

Mrs. Dacre clicked the hook severely.

"This is Mis' Dacre," she announced. "Hiram, if you'd strained
the slops same as I advised last March, you wouldn't
have a brindle cow with the colic now. Doctor, Nancy's
lookin' peaked."

There was a brief silence of the sort which had
lately begun to occur whenever Mrs. Dacre entered a telephone
conversation that did not belong to her.

"Is she feverish?" asked Doctor Ames.

"No fever I can find. But she's white as a
June lily and don't eat enough to keep a canary
Christian. Yesterday mornin' she let her buckwheat cakes git cold."

"I was speaking about my cow," protested Hiram.

"Your cow'll live. She always does. Melissy, give her warm
mash and keep her from that fool medder. Doctor, Nancy
lays awake."

"Does she complain of pain?"

"Nancy'd not complain if she was bein' quartered by wild
horses. That's Dacre blood."

From the doorway came a soft voice.

"Mother, please."

Nancy Dacre stood with a basket of mending on her
arm. The late September sun, entering through the western window,
lay upon her pale hair and her white apron, and
she had that transparent brightness sometimes seen in a flower
whose petals are already loosened though no wind has touched
them.

Mrs. Dacre turned.

"Don't 'mother, please' me. The truth's the truth, an' no
Dacre ever died from havin' it spoke. Doctor, you come
by when you're over this way."

"Yes," said Doctor Ames. "But I think—"

A click cut him off. Mrs. Dacre hung up with
decision.

"There. It's arranged. If folks would only come to the
point on these wires, instead of clackin' about cows and
weather, there'd be less waste of time."

Nancy put down her basket.

"You were listening before you called."

"Listenin'! Land alive, the voices come straight into my room.
Am I to stuff the receiver with cotton? Besides, Hiram
would lose every creature he owns if somebody didn't oversee
him."

"The calls are private, Mother."

"Nothin's private on a party line. That's why they call
it a party. Anyway, I don't repeat half what I
hear."

This was strictly true. Mrs. Dacre repeated perhaps three quarters,
but she refined it first through judgment, corrected improbabilities, supplied
motives, and gave the finished account the dignity of history.

The rural telephone had arrived in Fairmeadow that spring, striding
in on raw poles over hill and pasture, crossing stone
walls without so much as "by your leave," and fastening
the most distant households together with a single black wire.
The village received it as villages receive all inventions—with wonder,
suspicion, and an immediate determination to use it for purposes
never contemplated by its maker.

Old Cyrus Bell refused to have one because lightning would
"come huntin' down the wire after him." The Pote family
accepted theirs as an aid to agriculture and employed it
chiefly to discuss funerals. Mrs. Ruggles rang everybody whenever bread
fell in price. The young people discovered that one might
whisper under cover of static. The elderly discovered that whispers
traveled farther than shouts. And Mrs. Dacre, whose illness had
cut her off from roads, kitchens, sickbeds, sewing circles, and
church vestries, regarded the telephone not as an instrument but
as Providence admitting an error and making amends.

Until the previous winter she had been the moving force
of Fairmeadow.

No child had been born, no widow left comfortless, no
minister underfed, no stranger buried, no carpet put down, no
preserves spoiled, and no domestic quarrel brought to a proper
conclusion without Mrs. Dacre's presence. She had crossed Miller's Brook
on a plank in flood time to nurse the Fenner
twins through scarlet fever. She had climbed Pine Hill in
snow to carry broth to old Miss Wren. She had
walked three miles in an August thunderstorm because Hattie Bell
was "expectin'" and Hattie's mother had lost her head. Wind
could not turn her, darkness could not frighten her, and
mud, which clung to lesser women, appeared to understand that
Mrs. Dacre had no patience with it.

She knew when to apply hot bricks, when to open
a window, when to close one, when a child required
castor oil, and when a husband required plain speaking. She
scoured, stitched, prayed, commanded, and consoled. If there was vanity
in her goodness, there was also goodness in her vanity;
for she never asked another to endure cold, fatigue, or
disagreeable smells that she would not endure first.

Yet service, continued over many years, had become authority, and
authority had ripened into ownership. Fairmeadow belonged to Mrs. Dacre
because she had done so much for it. Its babies
were partly hers, its marriages subject to her review, its
virtues her handiwork, and its faults a personal disobedience.

Most of all, Nancy belonged to her.

Nancy had been born after twelve years of childless marriage,
when hope had thinned almost to resignation. She was a
small, fair baby wrapped in white flannel and worshiped by
a mother whose love had the intensity of a late
spring after a long winter. Mr. Dacre died when Nancy
was six. Thereafter the two lived alone in the old
house beneath the apple trees, and Mrs. Dacre poured into
her daughter every tenderness, ambition, memory, and unspent dream of
her energetic heart.

She dressed her in white when other children wore serviceable
brown. She taught her to read from the family Bible
and the poems of Mrs. Hemans. She guarded her from
croup, damp grass, rough playmates, green currants, thunderstorms, low company,
and excessive imagination. Nancy grew gentle beneath this shelter, as
a plant beneath an orchard bough may grow pale and
sweet from filtered sunlight.

Then Saul Manley came home from the West.

He had been a long, loose-jointed boy when he left
Fairmeadow, with red-brown hair, grave eyes, and patched trousers. He
returned at twenty-seven, broad-shouldered and quiet, with enough money to
purchase the neglected Avery farm and enough experience to make
it yield. He repaired the barn before the house, which
Mrs. Dacre held to show deficient refinement but which every
farmer in the neighborhood called sound sense. He drained the
lower field, set new apple trees, kept his fences straight,
paid his debts before they were asked for, and drove
his old mother to church every Sunday in a washed
buggy.

Within six months the village had forgiven him for being
a Manley.

Mrs. Dacre did not.

"Blood tells," she said. "You can whitewash a smokehouse, but
wait till August."

What particular crime attached to the Manley blood was not
easily stated. Saul's grandfather had traded horses and was alleged
to have known too much about the infirmities of those
he sold. An aunt had married a traveling photographer. Saul's
mother said "seen" where she ought to say "saw," and
his father, Aleck Manley, had been handsome beyond what Mrs.
Dacre considered safe in a man who owned no silver
teaspoons.

That Nancy loved Saul was discovered by Mrs. Dacre on
an evening in apple-blossom time.

The orchard was floating in whiteness; the old limbs, dark
with rain, carried their blossoms like snow caught and warmed.
Twilight had veiled the fields, and the first star hung
above Pine Hill. Mrs. Dacre, returning from taking jelly to
Miss Wren, entered by the side path and saw Nancy
beneath the largest tree. Saul stood before her, holding both
her hands.

They were not embracing. They were only looking at one
another with that grave astonishment by which youth recognizes its
own immortal happiness.

Mrs. Dacre stopped. Something old and long buried stirred in
her—not memory alone, but the very warmth of a vanished
sunset, the smell of fern and dust, the ache of
a girl's heart before it has learned pride.

Then she said, "Nancy."

Nancy's hands fell.

Saul turned. His face changed, but he did not step
away.

"Mis' Dacre," he said, "I was comin' to speak to
you."

"You've spoke sufficient."

"Mother—"

"You go into the house, Nancy."

Nancy went, because obedience was the habit of her life.
Saul remained beneath the blossoms.

"I love her," he said.

"Likely you do."

"And she loves me."

"You're bold."

"I don't mean disrespect."

"Folks that don't mean it manage to carry a sight
of it around."

Saul took the words steadily.

"I can give Nancy a good home. The Avery place
is paid for except twelve hundred dollars, and I'll clear
that in three years. I've got forty acres in corn
and twenty in hay. The house needs paint, but the
roof's sound. Mother can live in the east room, or
with my sister if Nancy would rather. I won't ask
Nancy to drudge."

Mrs. Dacre's nostrils whitened.

"You reckon a Dacre girl's value in corn acreage, do
ye?"

"No, ma'am. I was tellin' you what I had because
it's what a man has to tell."

"You've got Manley blood. You didn't mention that."

His quiet face colored.

"I can't help my name."

"You can help offerin' it to my daughter."

He looked beyond her toward the lighted house. "I'll wait."

"For what?"

"For as long as need be."

"You may wait till Gabriel sounds."

"Then I'll be on hand," said Saul.

It was the one speech Mrs. Dacre could never forgive,
perhaps because it bore the mark of a constancy equal
to her own.

She forbade him the house. She forbade Nancy to meet
him. Nancy listened, white and still, and when her mother
had finished she said, "I will not leave you."

Mrs. Dacre drew a deep breath of victory.

"But," Nancy continued, "I cannot stop loving Saul."

From that position no argument moved her. She did not
defy; she endured. She performed every duty, answered gently, read
aloud in the evenings, arranged her mother's flowers, attended church,
and grew a little paler every week.

Saul kept his word. He did not enter the house,
nor persuade Nancy to secret rebellion. But he sent Mrs.
Dacre the first strawberries from his garden because Nancy said
she liked them; repaired the Dacre gate after a storm;
broke a path to their well after the great snow;
and once, finding Mrs. Dacre's old mare loose upon the
highway, led it home without waiting to be thanked.

These actions annoyed her beyond open wickedness.

"Tryin' to buy his way in with fence rails and
fruit," she said. "Manley all through."

Yet her hatred was not simple, and in the still
hours, when the clock pronounced each minute and Nancy's light
burned late above, Mrs. Dacre knew it.

Long before she became Mrs. Dacre, she had been Martha
Binns, daughter of a tenant farmer at the edge of
Clay County. The Binnses had owned no family silver, no
framed ancestors, and no particular grammar. Martha had been a
strong, handsome girl, black-haired, high-colored, and swift of foot. At
nineteen she could pitch hay beside a man, dance until
sunrise, and sing across a field so that laborers paused
to hear.

Aleck Manley had heard.

He was poor, laughing, reckless, beautiful; his eyes were Saul's
eyes before experience had made them grave. One October evening
he walked Martha home through the beech woods. Frost had
reddened the leaves, sunset burned between the trunks, and Aleck
caught her hand.

For one wild instant she had loved him—or believed she
did, which in youth is commonly the same.

But the next week Silas Dacre came courting in a
polished buggy. He owned eighty acres, a fine house, a
pew near the church pulpit, and a name pronounced in
Fairmeadow with respect. Martha's mother advised her. Martha's ambition agreed.
She married Silas and learned to say "we Dacres" with
such conviction that, in time, she almost forgot she had
ever been Martha Binns running bareheaded through autumn woods.

Almost.

Aleck married a mill girl, grew careless, lost money, and
died young. Mrs. Dacre mentioned him rarely and condemned the
Manleys often.

When Saul stood beneath the apple tree holding Nancy's hands,
she saw not merely an unsuitable suitor but the return
of that October twilight, wearing an honest face and demanding
judgment. If Nancy married Saul and was happy, what became
of the grand distinction for which Martha had traded her
first bright hunger? If a Manley could be worthy, then
perhaps the wall she had spent a lifetime building between
refinement and commonness was no wall at all, but only
a screen set up to hide a frightened girl's choice.

And beneath pride, beneath memory, beneath even jealousy, there lay
the plain sorrow that Nancy would go away.

The Avery farm was less than two miles distant, but
to a possessive heart two miles may be an ocean.
Another kitchen would claim Nancy's hands, another roof shelter her
sleep, another love stand first. Mrs. Dacre, who had carried
warmth and courage into half the homes of Fairmeadow, could
not imagine her own home emptied.

So she took to her bed.

Her collapse occurred in January after Nancy asked, quietly, if
she might marry Saul in the spring.

Mrs. Dacre pressed one hand to her side, sat down,
and said, "You've killed me."

For several days there was genuine illness. Shock, anger, sleeplessness,
and a winter cold combined to weaken her. Doctor Ames
prescribed rest. Mrs. Dacre obeyed with a thoroughness he had
not intended. A week became a month, a month became
seven, and the bed in the sitting room developed the
permanence of a public institution.

She could not rise. She could not walk. Any suggestion
that she might try produced palpitations, chills, or a solemn
inquiry whether the speaker desired to attend her funeral before
haying.

Nancy refused to leave her.

Saul waited.

Then came the telephone, and Mrs. Dacre's body remained captive
while her spirit raced along the wires.

She listened to calls about births, debts, sermons, poultry, love
affairs, rheumatism, and the price of peaches. She advised without
invitation and corrected without appeal. She learned that the minister's
wife had burned three pies, that Hattie Bell expected again,
that young Tom Ruggles was keeping company with a schoolteacher
from New Hampshire, and that Deacon Price had bought an
automobile but could not make it stop.

When subscribers heard the small betraying click of her receiver,
conversations altered.

"I was sayin'," Mrs. Ruggles would announce loudly, "that some
folks' sufferin' gives 'em uncommon good hearin'."

Or two women would begin reciting seed catalogues until Mrs.
Dacre, disgusted, hung up.

Such resistance wounded her. She had never considered herself a
gossip. A gossip scattered news merely to see it run;
Mrs. Dacre directed information toward moral ends. But the distinction,
clear in her own mind, was less visible from neighboring
receivers.

One afternoon in late September, Mrs. Ruggles came to call.

She entered carrying a jar of damson preserves and wearing
the expression of one who has brought both a gift
and a duty, and likes the duty better.

"How be ye, Martha?"

"Failin' gradual," said Mrs. Dacre. "Set the jar on the
table, not in the sun. Damsons turn if they're warmed.
Nancy's gone to Miss Wren's. Fool trip, with rain threatenin'.
Sit down. That chair creaks, but it's safe if you
don't throw yourself."

Mrs. Ruggles seated herself carefully.

"You look hearty."

"My looks have deceived stronger minds than yours."

"Color's good."

"Fever."

"Eyes is bright."

"Pain does that."

"Voice carries same as ever."

"The dyin' sometimes speak powerful clear."

Mrs. Ruggles folded her hands over her reticule.

"Martha, I come to say somethin'."

"I know it. Your upper lip's been drawed tight ever
sence you crossed the yard."

"It's about the telephone."

"What's broke now?"

"Nothin's broke."

"Then there's no reason to discuss it. The line's a
blessing, barrin' Lyddy Pike, who takes an age to answer
and then acts as if she'd conferred salvation."

Mrs. Ruggles cleared her throat.

"Folks don't like your listenin'."

Mrs. Dacre's head rose from the pillow.

"My what?"

"You heard."

"Folks has got mighty delicate all at once. I remember
when your Ezra went bankrupt and the whole town knew
it before breakfast."

"That wa'n't by telephone."

"No. You told 'em face to face."

Mrs. Ruggles reddened but held her ground.

"Maybe I did, and maybe I've repented. But every time
anybody takes down a receiver now, there you be, breathin'
soft."

"I do not breathe soft. My lungs is oppressed."

"You broke in yesterday while Lem Green was askin' his
wife what kind of ribbon she wanted."

"She wanted heliotrope, and at her age it'd make her
look jaundiced. I saved him money."

"You told the minister who he'd ought to visit."

"He'd skipped the widow Tame."

"You advised Hiram about his cow."

"The beast improved, didn't she?"

"You heern Tom Ruggles talkin' to Clara Wynn."

"I heard sufficient to know the girl lacks judgment."

"That's my Tom."

"I'm aware of his misfortune."

Mrs. Ruggles shut her eyes briefly. When she opened them,
anger had yielded to something like pity.

"Martha, you used to go where folks needed ye. Now
you lay here and reach after 'em through a wire.
It ain't the same."

The great clock ticked four times into the silence.

"I go," said Mrs. Dacre coldly, "where the Lord permits."

"Do ye?"

Mrs. Dacre's fingers tightened on the counterpane.

"You come to accuse a sick woman, Almiry Ruggles?"

"I come to warn an old friend. Folks is beginnin'
to make sport. When they hear your click, they say,
'There's the Queen of Fairmeadow puttin' on her crown.'"

Mrs. Dacre's face went scarlet.

"They say that?"

"Some."

"Name 'em."

"No."

"Cowards always travel unnamed."

"They ain't all cowards, neither. Some's only tired."

Mrs. Ruggles stood.

"I fetched the preserves because you always favored damsons. Nancy
helped pick 'em."

The name softened something in Mrs. Dacre's rigid face.

"She'd no business. Wet grass chills her."

"It was dry then. Saul spread sacks under the tree
so her shoes wouldn't stain."

"Saul!"

"He was helpin' me gather. He helps most folks, one
time or another."

"Fetch your preserves home."

"No. I give 'em."

"I won't eat Manley damsons."

"They grew on my tree."

"He touched 'em."

"So did Nancy."

The two women looked at one another across the little
table—the one upright and broad in her brown dress, the
other enthroned among whitened pillows, sovereign still, but suddenly old.

At last Mrs. Ruggles said, "Martha, love can hold on
so tight it strangles. Think on that."

Then she went away.

Mrs. Dacre lay with her eyes closed. She did not
hear the first drops of rain strike the apple leaves,
nor Nancy's light step in the hall half an hour
later. She said she wanted no supper. She complained of
oppression and asked that an extra hot brick be placed
at her feet. When Nancy bent to kiss her, Mrs.
Dacre caught the girl's wrist.

"You ain't sick, be ye?"

"No, Mother."

"You're thin."

"I have always been thin."

"You're white."

"That is Dacre blood, you say."

There was no bitterness in the answer, which made it
harder to bear.

"You'd tell me if you felt a cough comin'?"

"Yes, Mother."

"Your father's sister had weak lungs."

"I know."

"Don't go out tomorrow."

"All right."

"And don't have dealin's with Saul's damsons."

Nancy smiled faintly. "All right."

She withdrew, and Mrs. Dacre watched the closing door.

Rain thickened after dark. It whispered through the orchard and
ran from the eaves with a monotonous murmur. The clock
on the mantel ticked enormously. Mrs. Dacre imagined each stroke
shortening the invisible thread of Nancy's life.

At eight the telephone rang: one long, two short—the general
signal used when somebody wanted half the line and expected
the other half to listen.

Mrs. Dacre stared at the instrument.

After Mrs. Ruggles's warning, dignity demanded that she ignore it.

The bell rang again.

Dignity, though a respected member of the Dacre household, had
never been permitted to interfere with necessary information.

Mrs. Dacre lifted the receiver cautiously.

"Hello?" called Mrs. Ruggles.

A second voice answered. It belonged to Melissa Pote.

"Hello, Almiry. I'm ready. What become of that story you
was tellin'?"

"What story?" asked Mrs. Ruggles, with an innocence too broad
to be entirely natural.

"The one out of the paper. About that lovely young
woman and the wicked old aunt."

"Oh, that. I'd forgot."

"Well, I ain't. You left off where the poor girl
wa'n't allowed to marry the faithful young man."

Mrs. Dacre's brows drew together. She had heard no beginning,
but continued stories exercised a peculiar power over her. She
adjusted the receiver.

Mrs. Ruggles began in the slow, emphatic tones of one
reading aloud, though no rustle of paper could be heard.

"The young woman was beautiful and mild, with hair like
pale sunshine and a heart that never disobeyed. Her father
was dead, and she lived with an aged female relative
who had raised her from infancy."

"Aunt, wa'n't it?" Melissa interrupted.

"Might've been aunt. Might've been mother. It don't signify."

"It would to the girl."

"Do you want the story or don't ye?"

"Go on."

"The aged female was a proud, masterful person, once useful
in her neighborhood, but now laid up through a complaint
no doctor could name."

Mrs. Dacre's mouth opened.

"She was confined to a great bed," Mrs. Ruggles continued,
"where she spent her time listenin' to a newfangled talkin'
machine and interferin' with innocent folks."

"That sounds familiar," said Melissa.

"You hush. The lovely girl had a suitor. He was
a noble young farmer, patient as Job and strong as
an ox, though descended from a family the aged female
despised."

"What'd they do?"

"Nothin' much. One of 'em traded a lame horse fifty
year before."

"Sho!"

"But the old female was set. 'Wed him,' says she,
'and I perish before your eyes.'"

Mrs. Dacre gripped the receiver.

"That ain't the exact words," Melissa objected.

"It's the moral sense of 'em. So the girl, bein'
tender, sacrificed her happiness. Day by day she faded. She
ate no food. She slept no sleep. Her cheeks grew
white, her hands transparent, and a cough took hold."

Mrs. Dacre's free hand flew to her throat.

"Did she have consumption?" Melissa whispered.

"The gallopin' kind," said Mrs. Ruggles darkly. "No walkin' about
it."

"Almiry!"

"The faithful lover watched from afar. His heart broke regular,
but he never married another. At last a physician come
out of the city and says, 'She must have joy,
or she dies.'"

The rain hissed in the wire.

"And what did the old one say?" asked Melissa.

"She said her family pride was more important than the
young woman's life."

"No!"

"Yes. She clung to the girl with hands of iron.
'Mine, mine,' she says. 'If I can't keep ye, the
grave shall.'"

"I never said such!" cried Mrs. Dacre into the receiver.

The line went silent.

Then Melissa said, faintly, "Who's that?"

Mrs. Dacre could not answer. The room had altered. The
white bed, the ticking clock, the bowl of apples, the
limp folds of the curtain—all seemed objects belonging to a
dreadful stage upon which she had been discovered playing the
villain.

She saw Nancy beneath the apple tree. Nancy at the
supper table, breaking bread she did not eat. Nancy bending
over a lamp late at night. Nancy's wrist within her
hand, slight as a flower stem.

She heard again her own words: You've killed me.

And for the first time she understood that a love
willing to die in order to command another's life was
not sacrifice. It was tyranny wearing a widow's cap.

The receiver slipped and swung against the wall.

"Martha?" called Mrs. Ruggles from the dangling mouthpiece. "Martha, be
ye there?"

Mrs. Dacre's eyes fixed upon the floor.

For seven months that floor had been as distant as
a foreign shore. She had spoken of it with terror.
To touch it, she said, would stop her heart.

Now another terror surpassed the first.

She threw back the counterpane.

Her feet, in gray wool stockings, appeared at the edge
of the bed, oddly small and defenseless. She swung them
downward. The room swayed. She seized a bedpost.

The soles of her feet met the braided rug.

Nothing happened.

The clock continued. The rain continued. Her heart, though behaving
with scandalous violence, did not stop.

Mrs. Dacre stood.

At first she bent like one forced beneath a great
burden. Then her knees steadied. Her back straightened by degrees.
The old force ran through her limbs—not the invincibility of
youth, but the sterner strength of a will that has
at last turned against its own falsehood.

She took one step, then another.

"Martha?" cried the telephone.

Mrs. Dacre snatched up the receiver.

"Almiry Ruggles, was that story true?"

Mrs. Ruggles hesitated. "Parts of it."

"Does the girl die?"

"Not yet."

"She ain't goin' to."

"Martha—"

"Git off the line. I got business."

She rang central so fiercely that Lyddy Pike answered at
once.

"Gimme Elder Snow. Three short."

"Mis' Dacre, is somethin' wrong?"

"Everything's been wrong, and I'm puttin' it right. Ring!"

Nancy came hurrying in, rain pearled upon her hair.

"Mother!"

Mrs. Dacre stood beside the telephone, one hand gripping the
wall. Nancy stopped as if before an apparition.

"Don't gape," said Mrs. Dacre. "Fetch my black silk—the good
one, not the bombazine. And my lace collar. Heat the
irons. My sleeves is crushed."

"You're standing."

"I'm aware of my posture."

"You'll fall!"

"I ain't got time. Elder? That you?"

A distant male voice answered.

"This is Martha Dacre. You hitch up and fetch Saul
Manley here."

"What's happened?"

"A weddin's happened, or it will if this town's got
a minister with any usefulness left in him."

Nancy made a small sound.

Mrs. Dacre turned. The girl's face had gone from white
to rose, like an apple blossom touched by sunrise.

"Mother, what do you mean?"

"I mean you're marryin' Saul. Tonight if Almiry's story has
any medical foundation; tomorrow if it ain't. I won't have
consumption in this family merely to gratify my pride."

Nancy came toward her slowly.

"Do you want us to marry?"

"Want! What's want got to do with it? The thing's
got to be done. That man's waited long enough to
grow gray, and you've moped till you look like a
cemetery angel."

"Mother—"

"Don't cry on me. I've got to dress."

Nancy flung her arms about her.

Mrs. Dacre resisted for perhaps the space of one tick
from the great clock. Then her arms closed around the
slender white figure. She held Nancy fiercely, but not as
before. There are embraces that imprison and embraces that release;
outwardly they may look the same, but the heart knows
the difference.

"I wa'n't goin' to lose ye," she whispered.

"You never could."

"I near did."

Nancy's tears warmed her cheek.

From the telephone came Elder Snow's anxious voice.

"Mis' Dacre? You still there?"

Mrs. Dacre drew herself up.

"Course I'm here. Where else would I be? Fetch Saul,
and tell your wife to come button me. Nancy shakes
too much. Stop by the Ruggles place for witnesses. And,
Elder—"

"Yes?"

"Don't you tell Saul I've changed my mind."

"What shall I tell him?"

"Tell him I've come to my senses. There's a difference."

Within twenty minutes the Dacre house shone from every window.
Mrs. Ruggles arrived carrying the damson preserves, Melissa Pote brought
a frosted cake originally intended for Sunday, and Elder Snow's
wife came with pins in her mouth. Somebody gathered wet
asters. Somebody else spread the parlor rug. The telephone rang
without ceasing, bearing questions, offers, congratulations, and corrections.

Mrs. Dacre, clothed in black silk and old lace, sat
only once while her shoes were fastened. Then she rose
and crossed the room without assistance.

When Saul entered, rain on his shoulders and amazement in
his grave eyes, Nancy went to him.

Mrs. Dacre watched. Pain touched her, clean and sharp, but
it was no longer the pain of possession thwarted. It
was the honorable sorrow by which love pays for another's
freedom.

Saul held out his hand.

"Mis' Dacre—"

"Don't speechify. You said you'd be on hand when Gabriel
sounded. Elder Snow's bell ain't Gabriel, but it's near enough
for Fairmeadow."

His mouth trembled.

"I'll take care of her."

"You'll do more than that. She ain't a piece of
china. Let her take care of you some. And mind
the east chamber's damp. Put the stove in before frost."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And your lower orchard needs pruning."

"Yes, ma'am."

"You've let the stone wall sag by the road."

"Yes, ma'am."

Mrs. Ruggles whispered to Melissa, "He's married already, whether the
elder says so or not."

Mrs. Dacre heard, and for once she did not rebuke
the listener.

They stood beneath the great clock while rain softened to
a murmur among the apple trees. Nancy wore no bridal
veil, only her white dress and the asters in her
hair. Saul's hand closed over hers with reverence. Elder Snow
opened his book.

At that moment the telephone rang.

Everyone looked toward it.

Mrs. Dacre looked longest. For months it had been her
road, her window, her throne, and her spyglass. Through it
she had reached into life rather than rise to meet
life. Now the black receiver hung silent between rings, no
more magical than a kitchen ladle.

"Let it ring," said Nancy softly.

Mrs. Dacre hesitated.

Then she marched to the wall, lifted the receiver, and
said, "Line's occupied. There's a weddin' here."

A chorus of village voices burst from the instrument—cheers, questions,
laughter, and Hiram Pote asking whether anybody had remembered to
give the brindle cow her mash.

Mrs. Dacre's old commanding brightness flamed.

"Melissy, see to that cow. Lyddy, quit listenin'. Elder, proceed.
Saul, stand straight. Nancy, stop tremblin'. Almiry, pass them damsons
after the ceremony."

She hung up, turned her back upon the telephone, and
took her place beside her daughter.

"Now," said Mrs. Dacre, "let nobody interfere."

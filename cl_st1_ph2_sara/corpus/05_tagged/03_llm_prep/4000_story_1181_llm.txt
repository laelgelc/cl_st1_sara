### The Blue-Bordered Handkerchief

In a country where cattle wandered farther than witnesses, and
brands supplied the testimony which fences, deeds, and constables supplied
elsewhere, the charge against Jim Basset was not a light
one. No man had seen him run the iron; but
two steers from the Double Spur had appeared upon his
claim with a blurred mark at the ribs, and Abel
Strake had been prompt to explain both the blur and
Jim.

Abel was a steady young cattleman, the owner of six
hundred head, a new board house, and a jealousy which,
being prosperous, passed readily for moral indignation. Jim had eighty
poor cattle, a dugout above Willow Creek, and no evidence
except his word. Under such circumstances the valley accepted Abel's
account.

To alter a brand was more than theft. It disturbed
that rude scripture by which ownership was read upon the
moving herds. A horse might be borrowed, a fence cut,
even a man struck, and reconciliation remain possible; but the
man who laid a false iron on another's stock carried
a levitical curse. He might ride the same trails and
drink at the same bars, yet he was socially outside
the firelight.

Thus, when the Christmas dance was announced at Rhodes's ranch,
Jim received no invitation.

He heard of it at the corrals, from boys who
fell silent after mentioning it. He heard that there would
be two fiddlers, and oysters sent up in tins from
the railroad, and that Mrs. Rhodes had turned flour sacks
into curtains for the shed-room. Last, he heard that Hetty
Rhodes was going with Abel Strake.

That hurt him worse than the rest.

Hetty had not promised Jim anything. Of these things a
girl may not testify. She had ridden with him twice
to the upper crossing; she had allowed him to mend
the latigo of her saddle while she stood close enough
for the wind to carry the scent of her hair;
and once, when he had said he meant to build
a house with two glass windows, she had asked which
one would look toward the Rhodes ranch. But she had
also laughed with Abel at the post office and accepted
peppermints from the schoolmaster. Her favors were democratic, while the
conclusions drawn from them were private and frequently disastrous.

Three days before Christmas Jim rode into Cottonwood for cartridges,
coffee, and a new cinch. Snow had fallen thinly during
the night, whitening the bunch-grass without concealing it, and the
country beyond town fell away in pale undulations, a ghostly
land seeking some remote and ghostly sea.

At Pomeroy's store he found Hetty before the glass case,
buying dancing shoes.

They were absurdly delicate shoes for Willow Creek—black, narrow, and
bright at the toes. Mrs. Rhodes had sold two hens
and a crock of butter to pay for them. Hetty
lifted one upon her gloved hand, considering it with an
expression of almost religious vanity.

"Been buying out the store?" Jim asked.

She turned. The color rose under her brown skin, but
her eyes remained cool.

"Only what I can pay for."

Jim laid down the cinch he had been examining. His
"shaps" were whitened at the knees, and a split in
one glove showed the reddened knuckle. Hetty noticed these things,
as she noticed that his face had grown leaner and
that he looked directly at her without the old half-smile.

"You going to the dance?" he asked.

"I expect so."

"With Strake?"

"He asked me."

Jim glanced at the shoes. "He'll admire them."

Mr. Pomeroy, who possessed the tact of a man accustomed
to selling on credit, moved noiselessly toward the stove. Jim
turned to a display of holiday goods hung behind the
counter: celluloid combs, red suspenders, perfumed soap, and silk handkerchiefs
in paper boxes. One of the handkerchiefs was white, with
a border of clear blue. It was a fragile and
unnecessary thing, suggestive of cleanliness beyond the soilure of work
and weather; and because Jim's life contained little that was
either fragile or unnecessary, he desired it at once.

"How much?"

Pomeroy named a sum fit for three sacks of flour.

"I'll take it."

Hetty stared while the salesman folded the box in brown
paper. Outside, beside the hitching rail, Jim overtook her. Her
new shoes were wrapped under her arm.

"Here," he said.

"What is it?"

"Something for the dance."

She opened the parcel and caught her breath, which pleased
him before her expression hardened.

"I can't take it, Jim."

"You kin."

"No." She touched the blue edge with one finger. "Folks
would talk."

"They do that now without anything to go on."

Her face changed; but whether from pity or anger he
could not tell.

"I'm going with Abel," she said.

"I heard you."

"And you ain't invited."

"I heard that too."

He held the handkerchief toward her. She stepped back. The
little square of silk, exposed between them in the cold,
seemed to Jim suddenly ridiculous. He crumpled it and thrust
it into his coat.

"I'll be there," he said.

"Jim—"

"You kin bank on it."

He mounted and rode out with the cartridges forgotten on
Pomeroy's counter.

The Rhodes house stood eleven miles from Cottonwood, in the
broad valley below Twin Buttes. On Christmas night every hitching
rack was full, and saddle horses crowded the corral, steaming
under blankets. Sleigh bells had been fastened above the door;
lanterns shone from the windows; and from the shed-room came
the scrape of a fiddle tuning above laughter and stamping
boots.

The women changed their overshoes in the kitchen. Men left
slickers, gloves, and belled spurs in the lean-to, but retained
their revolvers, for to appear unarmed at a dance would
have suggested either innocence or poverty, neither of which a
Western gentleman willingly advertised.

Abel Strake stood near the musicians, broad and complacent in
a black coat. Hetty wore a pale wool dress altered
from her mother's wedding gown, and the new shoes. She
had looked for Jim's face at every opening of the
door and was angry with herself for looking.

At ten o'clock a rider stopped outside.

The talk diminished by degrees, like water draining from a
trough. Jim came through the lean-to with snow on his
shoulders. He had dressed carefully. His boots were polished; his
red neckerchief was clean; his revolver rode low at his
thigh.

Old Rhodes met him at the door.

"You know you ain't wanted here, Jim."

"I came to dance."

"You came to make trouble," Abel said.

Jim looked past him to Hetty. "Might depend on the
dancing."

Several men moved away from the walls. Their movement was
slight, but significant. Jim rested one hand above his gun.

Then Silas Crew, the elder fiddler, peered over his spectacles.

"A man don't need a gun to dance," he observed.

"No," Jim said. "But he might need one getting invited."

Mrs. Rhodes, frightened for her curtains and her guests, began
whispering to her husband. The girls crowded together, their alarm
mingled with a lively appreciation of the unprecedented proposal which
Jim now made.

"I'll give up my gun," he said, "if I get
one dance with any woman I choose."

"Not in my house," Rhodes answered.

"Ask the women."

This appeal to the timid suffrages of the gentler sex
was audacious, but effective. The girls giggled; matrons protested that
they wanted no killing at Christmas; and Hetty, from beside
Abel's shoulder, said distinctly:

"Let him have his dance."

Abel turned on her. "You keep out of it."

"I shan't."

Thereafter the matter escaped male administration. Jim unbuckled his belt
and handed it to Rhodes. The old man passed it
to his wife, who carried it into the bedroom as
if it were a snake in a basket.

Silas lifted his fiddle.

"Choose your partner."

Jim crossed the room. The people divided before him, leaving
a polished lane of puncheon floor. He stopped in front
of Hetty and bowed.

She gave Abel one quick look, then placed her hand
in Jim's.

The tune began.

At first they danced with grave propriety. Jim's right hand
rested lightly at Hetty's waist; her skirt brushed his boots;
the blue shadows of the lamps swayed upon the walls.
They went down the center, turned at the musicians, and
came back between two rows of watching faces.

Hetty's heart beat high, though her expression remained almost disdainful.

"You made a fool of yourself," she murmured.

"Likely."

"What did you come for?"

"You."

She missed a step.

Abel stood with his arms folded. Each time they passed
him, Jim's face grew calmer and Abel's less so. This
disparity was not lost upon the company.

The figure ended. Silas lowered his bow.

"That's your dance," Rhodes said.

Jim did not release Hetty.

"Keep right on," he told the fiddler.

Silas glanced toward Rhodes, then toward the girls. The girls
were smiling. With the moral cowardice peculiar to musicians and
philosophers, he raised his bow and began another figure.

A murmur ran around the room.

Hetty resisted for half a turn. "Jim, you said one
dance."

"I ain't stopped yet."

"Abel will kill you."

"Not while your pa's got my gun."

It was a reckless answer, and she should have rebuked
it. Instead she laughed. That laugh, bright and involuntary, did
more damage than a pistol shot.

Abel stepped forward. "That's enough."

Jim swung Hetty past him, her skirt flaring, the black
shoes flashing beneath it. Silas played faster. His companion, catching
the spirit of misrule, joined with a high, sawing harmony.
The dancers who had withdrawn now clapped time; even certain
elders, while preserving judicial faces, showed a dangerous amusement.

Around the room went Jim and Hetty—once, twice, three times.
Her vanity, which had often played with men's feelings because
her own remained safely cool, found itself overtaken by a
stronger excitement. She was afraid of Jim, afraid for him,
and proud that every woman there saw herself chosen.

"Stop 'em!" Abel shouted.

No one did. The community which had interdicted Jim was
discovering that its verdict, however righteous at the corrals, made
excellent theater at a dance.

Jim guided Hetty toward the shed-room.

She understood then.

"No," she whispered.

"Come with me."

"No."

But her hand tightened on his shoulder.

They reached the doorway. Abel sprang after them. Jim kicked
the door shut, dropped the wooden bar, and swept Hetty
into his arms. She gave one cry—not loud enough, perhaps,
for one who sincerely expected rescue.

Beyond the shed stood Jim's bay, saddled and waiting.

"You planned this!"

"Only since Cottonwood."

He set her upon the saddle, gathered the reins, and
mounted behind her. The bar crashed from its socket as
Abel struck the door. Jim wheeled the bay through the
yard, scattering tethered horses and jingling bells.

A shot cracked from the house; whether fired upward or
badly aimed, it harmed nothing but the dignity of Christmas.

They were gone.

They crossed the valley under a clouding moon, forded Willow
Creek where the ice broke black about the bay's knees,
and climbed through sagebrush toward the north road. Behind them
lanterns moved briefly near the Rhodes corrals, then vanished. The
chinook had begun to breathe along the ridges, smelling of
wet earth beneath the snow.

After six miles Hetty started shivering. Jim took off his
slicker and wrapped it around her.

"You're a cattle thief," she said, though without conviction.

"No."

"Abel says—"

"Abel lies."

She was silent a long time. Then: "Where are we
going?"

"Parson Dale's. Sixteen miles."

"And if I won't marry you?"

"I'll ride you home."

"That'd be thirty-two miles."

"Yes."

She considered the hardship, the scandal, and the singular economy
of accepting him.

"Jim," she said presently, "you crushed my handkerchief."

He drew it from his coat. Though wrinkled, it remained
white, and its border had the clear color of morning
above snow. Hetty folded it cornerwise and tied it over
her hair.

Before dawn they reached the parson's claim, riding double on
a tired bay. Hetty wore Jim's slicker over her pale
dress, her dancing shoes were muddied at the toes, and
the blue-bordered silk fluttered in the rising wind—her first accepted
gift, and the only wedding finery she possessed.

At the Cannes Flower Festival, Marguerite and Simone seemed buried
alive beneath blossoms.

Their landau, dressed from wheel-rim to lantern in garlands, had
lost the rigid appearance of a carriage and become a
strange, rolling, flowery beast. Roses covered the doors; pale carnations
trembled along the hood; violets, crushed by the ladies' skirts,
gave out a warm and melancholy perfume; and long sprays
of mimosa streamed behind like a golden mane. Only the
coachman's square back emerged from this fragrant confusion, upright in
dark green livery, with two rows of silver buttons flashing
in the sun.

All along the Croisette moved an interminable procession of equipages,
slow, glittering and noisy. Horses wore flowers at their ears
and on their bridles. Women, powdered and laughing, disappeared beneath
heaps of orchids, camellias, lilies and anemones. Men stood up
to hurl bouquets with the grave violence of soldiers serving
artillery. A cluster of carnations would rise, hesitate in the
light, and burst against a parasol; roses flew from hand
to hand like scented missiles; petals filled the air and
fell upon the road, where carriage wheels ground them into
a purple, yellow and scarlet paste.

Behind the lines of policemen, who prevented the poor from
mingling too freely with the rich, cooks, shopgirls, fishermen, soldiers
and barefoot children watched the battle of luxury. They shouted
whenever a handsome woman was struck full in the face
by a bouquet. Beyond them, the sea lay blue and
metallic, and three warships on the horizon resembled monstrous armoured
animals waiting patiently for the end of the fête.

Marguerite, weary of bowing, throwing, smiling and being admired, leaned
toward her companion.

"Let us escape."

Simone looked around with the mild regret of a queen
leaving a triumph arranged in her honour.

"Already?"

"I can smell nothing but bruised stems. I shall hate
roses for a month."

She ordered the coachman to turn into a side street.
The landau withdrew with difficulty from the bright river of
carriages, passed between villas half hidden by palms and pepper
trees, then reached the white coast road. At once the
uproar diminished. The cries became distant; the music dissolved behind
them; and the sea, no longer a decoration at the
edge of the festival, opened immense and solemn beneath the
afternoon sky.

The two women settled back among the cushions. Marguerite was
fair, lively and softly rounded, with that confident beauty which
accepts homage as naturally as the skin accepts sunlight. Simone,
darker and more slender, had a grave mouth that seemed
made for refusals. She chose her admirers with severity and
dismissed them with an almost religious calm.

They had been discussing, before the noise overwhelmed them, the
passion of a young secretary for his employer's wife.

Simone resumed:

"No. I cannot understand how she is flattered. The man
copies her husband's letters and carries her parcels."

"What has that to do with love?" Marguerite asked.

"Everything. There are men one does not see."

"And yet they see us."

"That is their affair."

Marguerite laughed.

"You are monstrous."

"I am particular."

"My dear, the love of any man is flattering."

"Any man?"

"Yes, certainly—provided one need not return it."

"A groom? A footman? Your concierge?"

"Why not? Sincerity has no livery."

Simone raised her eyebrows.

"If your coachman adored you, would you be touched?"

Marguerite glanced at the rigid back before them, at the
glossy collar, the flat cap and the shining silver buttons.

"Perhaps."

"You would be disgusted."

"Not in the least. Embarrassed, possibly. But secretly pleased. A
woman always forgives a man for finding her beautiful."

"Even a servant?"

"Especially a servant. A gentleman may pay compliments because he
has nothing else to do. A servant who loves risks
his place."

Simone plucked a bruised violet from her lap and threw
it over the side.

"You speak with experience."

Marguerite did not answer at once. Her smile narrowed; her
eyes, still turned toward the sea, became attentive to some
private and rather absurd memory.

"Yes," she said. "I had a strange experience once—though not
precisely of love."

"Tell me."

"You will laugh."

"Then you must tell me."

"It happened five years ago, in Paris. I had just
dismissed my maid. She was insolent, dishonest and perpetually in
tears—three qualities which are intolerable separately and ruinous when combined.
For eight days I lived in chaos. No gown could
be found, no ribbon matched another, and my bath was
either glacial or boiling. Then the concierge sent me a
girl named Rose."

"A pretty girl?"

"No; that is the curious part. At first I thought
her almost ugly. She was tall and rather heavily made,
with large hands, a sallow complexion and smooth black hair.
Her eyes were lowered in a modest fashion. She had
good references, spoke little and asked reasonable wages. I engaged
her at once because I was dining that evening at
the Austrian embassy and could not fasten my dress."

The landau rolled beside a low wall over which geraniums
poured in red cascades. Marguerite continued:

"Rose was wonderful. Within two days she understood me better
than women who had served me for years. She never
asked useless questions. She knew which gown I meant when
I said, 'The blue one'—though I possessed six blue gowns.
She remembered my gloves, my letters, my appointments, my powders,
my medicines. If I rang, she appeared; if I did
not ring, she seemed to guess that I needed her.

"And such strength! She lifted trunks as though they were
hatboxes. She tightened a corset without jerking me in two.
She could pin a sleeve, dress hair, remove stains, mend
lace, pack jewels and bargain with tradesmen. My household feared
her. The cook ceased stealing; the footman polished the silver;
even the concierge addressed her respectfully."

"You were fortunate."

"I adored her. We all search for devotion in love
and find it most often in domestics. Rose was grave
but tender. When I came home tired, she undressed me
with the solicitude of a mother. She prepared my bath,
tested the water, washed my hair, rubbed my shoulders with
almond paste, and afterward massaged my arms and legs, because
I suffered then from fatigue after riding."

Simone turned her head slightly.

"She bathed you?"

"Of course. A maid does."

"Yes—but you describe her as so strong."

"I noticed only that she was capable. Her hands were
large, certainly, yet extraordinarily gentle. I sometimes told her, 'Rose,
you have the hands of a man.' She would lower
her eyes and reply, 'Madame is too kind.' It was
a ridiculous answer, but servants always answer beside the point."

"And you suspected nothing?"

"Nothing! How could I? She dressed plainly, walked without swinging
her skirts, and possessed not one of those shrill little
vanities that make maids unbearable. I attributed this to good
sense. I trusted her completely. She slept in the room
next to mine, kept my keys and knew where I
put everything."

"How long did this continue?"

"About a month. Then, one morning, while Rose was brushing
my hair, there came a violent ringing at the front
door. We heard voices, boots, an argument with the concierge.
A moment later my butler entered, white as the cloth
on my dressing table.

"'Madame,' he stammered, 'the police.'

"I thought someone had been murdered in the house. Rose
did not move. She continued passing the brush through my
hair, slowly and evenly.

"A police inspector came in. Behind him were two agents.
He bowed and apologized, but with that manner policemen have
which proves that an apology is merely another kind of
order.

"'Madame,' he said, 'we are searching the neighbourhood for an
escaped convict. We must examine every person in the household.'

"I was offended. One always is when the law protects
one inconveniently. I told him my servants were honest people
and that his visit would create scandal. He answered that
the man was dangerous and might be disguised.

"'Disguised as what?' I asked.

"'As anything, madame.'

"Then he looked at Rose."

Marguerite paused. The carriage had entered a strip of shadow
cast by stone pines, whose trunks stood red and crooked
above the road. Far below, waves opened upon the rocks
in white fans.

"Well?" Simone asked.

"Rose put down the brush.

"The inspector said, 'Your name?'

"'Rose Delaunay.'

"'Your birthplace?'

"She gave the name of a village.

"'Your age?'

"'Twenty-eight.'

"He stepped closer. I remember noticing, absurdly, that one of
his boots had left mud on my carpet. Then he
seized Rose by the wrist.

"'Show me your hands.'

"She resisted—not violently, but with a sudden stiffening of the
whole body. The two agents sprang forward. One caught her
shoulders, the other her waist. Her cap fell off. Her
hair did not come down, as it does in the
theatre; it remained smooth and black. But her face changed.
It became hard, terrible, and—how shall I say it?—masculine. Not
because a beard appeared, naturally, but because the expression was
no longer a servant's expression. It was the face of
someone who commanded, struck, escaped and hated.

"The inspector cried, 'John Nicholas Lecapet, you are arrested.'

"I began to laugh. I thought the whole thing a
mistake.

"Then they tore open the collar of her dress."

Simone sat perfectly still.

"Rose was a man?"

"A man. John Nicholas Lecapet, sentenced to imprisonment for life—for
murder and rape. He had escaped from the penal settlement,
reached Paris by means no one could explain, and hidden
himself in my house as a woman. The police had
been searching for him for eleven months."

"And he had attended you?"

"For thirty-one days."

"Dressed you?"

"Yes."

"Bathed you?"

"Yes."

"Massaged you?"

"Yes—yes, everything."

Marguerite spoke impatiently now, almost angrily, as if Simone's questions
accused her of stupidity rather than innocence.

"What did he say?"

"Nothing at first. When they put the handcuffs on him,
he looked at me. That was all. Such a look!
Not insolent, not ashamed, not even imploring. Calm—almost sad. Then
he said, in the same soft voice Rose had always
used, 'Madame has nothing to fear. I never intended her
harm.'"

"How horrible!"

"Is it?"

"You had lived for a month beside a murderer."

"Yes."

"A man condemned for—"

"I know."

"He might have killed you."

"Certainly."

"He had seen you—touched you—when you were helpless."

"Obviously."

Simone pressed her hands together.

"And that did not drive you mad?"

Marguerite leaned back and watched the moving line of sunlight
along the carriage door.

"At first I fainted. Afterward I trembled for several days.
I had the locks changed, burned Rose's clothes and dismissed
the butler, though he was in no way responsible. I
could not endure being touched by another maid. But fear
was not what remained."

"What remained?"

Marguerite frowned. For the first time since beginning her story,
her composure appeared to fail her.

"It was something so foolish, so humiliating, that I have
never confessed it."

Simone waited.

"You understand—this man had been convicted of rape. The newspapers
described him as a brute, a monster, a creature governed
by abominable appetites. Yet for a month he had been
near me night and day. He had undressed me, bathed
me, carried me once from the sofa when I had
sprained my ankle. He had every opportunity. I trusted him.
I never called for help. And he—he behaved like the
most respectful old woman."

She stopped, then added in a lower voice:

"He had not desired me."

Simone made no reply.

Marguerite laughed, but the laugh was brief and without gaiety.

"That was the wound, my dear. Not the danger, not
the deception, not even the shame of remembering his hands.
I could forgive all that. But to learn that a
convicted ravisher, a man whom prisons and policemen called insatiable,
had lived beside me and remained perfectly virtuous—ah! There are
insults which no gentleman would dare offer."

The landau turned back toward Cannes. Already the distant tumult
of the festival returned across the warm air: horns, laughter,
wheels and the cries of vendors. A stray bouquet lay
in the road, flattened and muddy, its white roses marked
by horseshoes.

Neither woman spoke.

Simone's eyes rested upon the coachman's broad back. The dark
green cloth tightened faintly whenever he moved his arms; the
two straight rows of silver buttons shone and vanished, shone
and vanished, beneath the branches.

And while Marguerite looked out toward the warships asleep upon
the glittering sea, Simone continued to contemplate those buttons with
the secret, motionless smile of a sphinx.

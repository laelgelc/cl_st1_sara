### The Baroness's Journey

It was an accident, an accident pure and simple, one
of those absurd little arrangements of circumstance which alter two
lives while appearing hardly important enough to disturb a curtain.

The Baron d'Etraille had gone upstairs in search of the
Princess de Rayneval. Her reception was at its height. From
the long suite of rooms below came the dull, continuous
murmur of conversation, broken now and then by the sharp
crystal note of a laugh. The orchestra, hidden behind palms
in the winter garden, was playing a waltz which seemed
to float through the heated house like a perfume.

The baron had been told that the princess was in
her bedroom, detained there by a torn flounce. As he
had known her since childhood, he mounted without ceremony, crossed
the little boudoir, and pushed open the door.

The room was almost dark.

A single lamp, shaded with rose-colored silk, burned near the
bed, an enormous azure-and-gold structure half concealed beneath heavy draperies.
In that uncertain light the bed resembled a magnificent catafalque
prepared for the burial of love. The mirrors, the gilded
furniture, the silver toilet articles, all shone faintly, as if
seen under water.

He called:

"Are you there, princess?"

No one replied.

Thinking he had been misdirected, he was about to withdraw
when he perceived, in a tall mirror opposite the bed,
two faces close together.

At first he did not understand. They appeared far away
in the glass, suspended in a dark depth like drowned
faces beneath a lake. Then the man turned slightly, and
the baron recognized the Marquis de Cervigne. The woman's cheek
was hidden, but he knew the small ear, the curve
of the neck, and a diamond clasp which he himself
had given his wife on their wedding morning.

The marquis was kissing her.

D'Etraille did not move. The extraordinary thing was that neither
of them stood within his direct field of vision. A
screen concealed them; the mirror alone betrayed them, with the
cold exactness of an accomplice that has waited patiently to
speak.

The kiss lasted several seconds.

Then the baron stepped backward and closed the door without
noise.

He returned to the ballroom.

People later remembered that he had been in excellent spirits
that evening. He joked with an ambassador, danced with a
woman whom everybody desired, and lost a considerable sum at
cards without the slightest sign of annoyance. Toward two o'clock
he offered his arm to the baroness, placed her in
their carriage, and sat opposite her.

She was twenty-four, slender, fair, and exquisitely dressed. She had
the fragile elegance of certain Parisian women who appear to
have been made, not born: clever, spoiled, delicate, coquettish, witty,
and a little cruel from never having encountered resistance.

During the drive she spoke of the party.

"Madame de Varnet wore the same pearls last Thursday. I
wonder she dares."

The baron answered:

"Perhaps she believes pearls become more beautiful when frequently exhibited."

Bertha glanced at him. His tone was ordinary. He was
watching the dark streets through the window.

When they reached home, he accompanied her to the door
of her apartment.

"I should like to speak to you for a moment,"
he said.

Her maid had begun to remove her cloak. The baron
dismissed the woman with a movement. Bertha remained standing before
him, one hand resting upon the back of a chair.

He was thirty-seven then, tall, handsome, and correct in all
things. He had mistresses without passion, debts without embarrassment, opinions
without conviction. Good form was his morality; ridicule, the only
sin he sincerely feared.

"My dear," he said, "I entered the princess's bedroom this
evening."

Her fingers tightened on the chair.

He continued:

"I saw you in the mirror."

She became very pale, but she neither cried out nor
attempted one of those explanations which insult the intelligence more
than the offense itself. This silence pleased him. It seemed
to him worthy of his name.

After a moment she asked:

"What do you intend to do?"

"Nothing dramatic, I assure you."

He walked toward the fireplace, warmed his hands, and spoke
as though arranging a change in household accounts.

"A scandal would be disagreeable to both of us. A
duel would be ridiculous. Cervigne is a poor shot, and
I have no wish to kill him merely because you
find him attractive. Nor do I propose to kill you.
Such remedies are provincial."

She lifted her eyes.

"You are very calm."

"I am trying to be reasonable. We shall separate amicably.
You may live where you please, receive whom you please,
travel, amuse yourself, spend the income settled upon you. I
ask only that appearances be preserved. Before the world, you
remain the Baroness d'Etraille. You will give no one the
right to laugh at me."

"And if I refuse?"

He smiled.

"You will not refuse. You have as much taste as
I."

His calmness humiliated her more deeply than anger might have
done. Had he struck her, she could have hated him
simply; had he wept, she might have despised him comfortably.
But he treated her betrayal as an error in etiquette,
a spot on the family silver to be removed without
calling the servants.

She asked:

"Shall we ever meet?"

"It would be better not."

"So be it."

He bowed.

"Thank you."

The next month she left Paris. Their friends were informed
that her health required a warmer climate. Later it was
said that the baroness preferred Italy, that the baron could
not abandon his estates, that they possessed independent tastes. Society
accepted the explanation because society is obliging when a lie
is well dressed.

For six years they did not meet.

At first d'Etraille enjoyed his freedom. He traveled in Russia,
hunted in Hungary, went twice to Egypt, and spent one
winter in Vienna. He restored the eastern wing of his
château, drained a marsh, planted an avenue of limes, and
quarreled with an architect over a staircase. He had several
love affairs, all properly managed and all soon forgotten. He
was congratulated upon his life.

"You are fortunate," his friends told him. "A wife somewhere
in the distance, no children, a fine estate, complete liberty!"

He agreed.

Yet complete liberty, like an immense empty room, may after
a time become oppressive. A man crosses it in every
direction, admires its dimensions, and finally wonders where he ought
to sit.

The years altered him quietly. Time does not always announce
itself by great disasters. It removes a little brightness from
the eyes, a little firmness from the flesh; it places
a heaviness beneath the chin, a fatigue at the corners
of the mouth. One morning a man finds that his
tailor is too skillful, because the coat conceals what youth
once displayed.

At forty-three the baron was still called handsome by old
ladies and distinguished by young ones. He had grown heavier.
His hair had retreated at the temples; his face, reddened
by good dinners and autumn winds, no longer possessed its
former hard elegance. He noticed these changes only in other
men.

Of Bertha he heard almost nothing. She had lived in
Florence, then in Rome, then perhaps in Cannes. Cervigne had
married an American heiress and become immensely serious. There had
been rumors around the baroness—there are always rumors around a
pretty woman who lives independently—but none had acquired sufficient precision
to offend good form.

One February evening d'Etraille left Paris for Nice.

He had dined at his club before departure and arrived
at the station with only a few minutes to spare.
A servant placed his rugs and valise in a first-class
carriage. The baron climbed in, settled himself in a corner,
and discovered that he was not alone.

A woman occupied the opposite seat. She had already arranged
her wraps for the night and wore a small traveling
hat beneath a dark veil. Her figure was almost hidden
by a cloak trimmed with fur. She appeared to be
asleep.

The train moved.

D'Etraille opened a newspaper, read three lines, and looked over
the top of it. There was something familiar in the
position of the gloved hand upon the woman's lap. He
lowered the paper.

Who was she?

The veil concealed her features. He saw only a pale
cheek and a strand of fair hair. A perfume, very
faint, reached him whenever the movement of the carriage stirred
the air. He remembered that perfume, or believed he remembered
it. Memory is easily deceived when curiosity assists it.

Could it be Bertha?

He examined her more attentively. His wife had been slight,
almost thin. This woman possessed a fuller outline beneath the
cloak. Bertha's gestures had been restless; this traveler remained motionless
with the calm of a person accustomed to being observed.

No, it was not she.

Then the woman turned her head. The profile appeared for
an instant against the lamp—the short straight nose, the firm
little chin.

It was she and yet not she.

His heart gave a violent beat, which irritated him. Why
should the presence of a woman whom he had dismissed
six years earlier trouble him like this? He was master
of himself. He had proved it. Their situation was settled,
classified, dead.

But certain things which are dead retain a surprising warmth.

He did not speak. Perhaps she had not recognized him.
Perhaps the veil, the dim light, the years, protected them
both. To pass a whole night opposite one's wife as
strangers seemed to him both absurd and strangely agreeable.

At Dijon she woke, or ceased pretending to sleep. She
lifted her veil and looked directly at him.

"Good evening," she said.

He bowed.

"Good evening, madame."

There was no surprise in her face.

"So you recognized me?" he asked.

"At once."

"And you said nothing."

"There was nothing to say."

This reply displeased him. He had expected embarrassment, perhaps emotion,
at least the curiosity due to an unexpected resurrection. Instead
she drew off one glove and examined the fastening as
though he were an acquaintance met often and without importance.

He looked at her openly now.

She had become beautiful.

The slight Parisian doll had disappeared. Her shoulders were rounded,
her throat fuller, her face composed and luminous. The years
which had marked him had completed her. They had taken
the promise of the young woman and given it weight,
softness, assurance. Her fair hair showed warmer beneath the lamp,
and her eyes, once quick and capricious, had acquired that
steady gaze which comes either from experience or from the
habit of concealing it.

He felt a sudden resentment against time for having favored
her.

"You have changed," he said.

"Yes."

"Remarkably."

"So have you."

Her tone caused him to ask:

"For the better?"

"No."

He laughed, but his face grew hot.

"You retain your frankness."

"I learned it from you."

She wrapped the cloak more closely around herself and leaned
back. He tried to read, but the lines moved uselessly
before his eyes. He was conscious of her knees opposite
his, of the warmth enclosed within that narrow carriage, of
the night rushing past outside.

At every station lamps swept over her face and vanished.
Between the stations the glass reflected them both: two pale
travelers seated face to face, carried together through darkness after
six years of separation. The reflection reminded him of another
mirror. Once a mirror had exposed her; now this black
railway window seemed to unite them.

He began to talk.

He asked where she had lived, whether she liked Italy,
whom she saw, where she was going. She answered with
perfect civility and no detail.

"To Nice."

"For the season?"

"For some weeks."

"You travel alone?"

"My maid is in the next carriage."

"Still independent, then?"

"You required it."

There was no reproach in the words. That made them
a reproach.

Near dawn she fell asleep. Her head rested against the
cushion; the cloak slipped a little from one shoulder. D'Etraille
watched her.

Desire, when it returns after years, borrows masks from memory.
He told himself first that he was merely astonished, then
that he admired her objectively, then that he wished to
understand the woman she had become. But the body is
less diplomatic than the mind. He wanted her.

He remembered their first months of marriage, her bare arms
at dinner, the fragrance of her hair, her childish tempers,
her sudden laughter in dark rooms. He remembered, too, that
another man had kissed her behind a screen, and jealousy,
six years late, entered him with all the vigor it
had lacked at the proper moment.

The dawn came slowly, gray and cold. Fields emerged from
the mist. The sky grew pearl-colored above distant hills. Then
a slanting ray of sunlight entered the carriage and fell
across Bertha's face.

She opened her eyes.

For several seconds they looked at one another.

"You were watching me," she said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I find you astonishing."

She smiled slightly.

"You did not find me so six years ago."

"I was a fool."

"No. You were offended."

"Can you not forgive me for that?"

"Have I ever asked you to forgive me?"

Again she had struck without raising her voice.

He moved to the seat beside her. She did not
draw away, but her stillness was a refusal more decisive
than a gesture.

"Bertha," he said, "this comedy has lasted long enough."

"What comedy?"

"Our separation."

"It was your arrangement."

"I can alter my arrangements."

"Can you?"

He took her hand. She withdrew it calmly.

"You forget," he said, and now his voice had become
ceremonious, almost official, "that you are my wife."

"I have had little opportunity to remember it."

"The law remembers."

"Ah! You appeal to the law?"

"I appeal to my right."

She looked at him with attentive curiosity, as if observing
a familiar animal perform an unexpected trick.

"Your right to what?"

"To take you back."

"And suppose I do not wish to be taken?"

He hesitated. He had imagined resistance, but not this composed
examination of his authority.

"You have lived freely because I permitted it," he said.
"I behaved generously."

"You behaved prudently. You were afraid people would laugh."

"That is unjust."

"It is exact."

The train thundered over a bridge. Beneath them, briefly visible,
a broad river flashed in the morning light.

He lowered his voice.

"I desire you."

"That is at least more sincere."

"I desire you, and you are my wife. We will
forget the past."

"Which past? Mine, or yours?"

"All of it."

She smiled.

"Men always propose oblivion when memory ceases to serve them."

He rose and stood before her, bracing himself against the
motion of the carriage.

"I am serious, Bertha."

"So am I."

"I shall accompany you to Nice."

"No."

"You cannot prevent me."

"I do not intend to."

"You will return with me."

"No."

His dignity, his legal rights, his recovered passion—all seemed to
break against that quiet monosyllable. He had an impulse to
seize her, to prove by physical force the authority which
conversation denied him. But violence would have been ugly, and
ugliness terrified him almost as much as ridicule.

He resumed his seat.

For the next hour they did not speak.

As the train approached Marseilles, Bertha arranged her hair before
the little mirror and fastened her veil. She did this
without haste. The baron watched, thinking that her calm must
conceal uncertainty. Surely his declaration had moved her. Women reflect
after refusing; refusal is often only the first form of
negotiation.

The suburbs appeared, with factory roofs, white houses, telegraph wires,
and walls stained by smoke. Bertha put on her gloves.

"I should warn you of something," she said.

He leaned toward her.

"Yes?"

"Some friends are meeting me at the station."

He frowned.

"What does that matter?"

"It matters a great deal. They know this is my
carriage."

A suspicion, still shapeless, entered his mind.

"You expected them?"

"Certainly."

"And you knew that I was traveling by this train?"

She looked at him, and for the first time her
smile was open.

"Yes."

He stared.

"You arranged this?"

"I arranged that we should be seen arriving together."

The train slowed. Wheels shrieked beneath them.

She continued in the same even tone:

"My friends will see you descend from my carriage after
a night journey from Paris. By luncheon, half Marseilles will
know. By tomorrow, Nice and Paris will know. Everyone will
conclude that the Baron and Baroness d'Etraille are reconciled."

His face changed.

"Why?"

She lowered her veil.

"Because appearances must be preserved."

He understood then that the words spoken six years earlier
had returned to him, intact and sharpened.

"Bertha," he said, "what have you done?"

She paused with one hand upon the door.

"I may be pregnant."

The train stopped.

For an instant all sounds seemed to withdraw from him.
Then came the clamor of the platform: porters shouting, doors
opening, greetings, laughter.

"You—may be—"

"Yes."

"By whom?"

She gave a little shrug.

"That is precisely the question which no well-bred person will
now ask."

Outside, a group of elegant travelers had recognized her. A
woman waved her muff; two men approached smiling. D'Etraille saw
their smiles alter as they perceived him beside her—surprise first,
then comprehension, then the discreet pleasure with which society receives
a secret it can pretend not to notice.

Bertha stepped down.

"My dear baroness!" cried the woman. "And the baron! What
a delightful surprise."

"Yes," Bertha replied. "A delightful surprise."

D'Etraille remained at the carriage door, unable to speak. He
felt that every eye upon the platform had already calculated
the night, the closed compartment, the restored marriage, the possible
child. His own rules surrounded him like the bars of
a cage.

Bertha turned once.

Her face expressed neither hatred nor triumph. That was the
final cruelty. She merely appeared satisfied with a difficult arrangement
properly concluded.

"Good-bye," she said.

Then she took her friend's arm and went away through
the crowd.

The baron never saw her again.

Some months later he heard that she had gone abroad.
There were no announcements, no christening, no child presented beneath
his name. He never discovered whether she had spoken the
truth, whether she had feared a consequence and escaped it,
or whether the entire confession had been invented to punish
him for one hour with an anxiety that would last
as long as memory.

He made discreet inquiries, then abandoned them, since inquiries themselves
create the scandal they seek to prevent.

Whenever the story of their supposed reconciliation was mentioned, he
smiled.

A man of good form always smiles.

But mirrors and railway windows troubled him afterward, for both
repeat appearances faithfully, and neither has ever promised to tell
the truth.

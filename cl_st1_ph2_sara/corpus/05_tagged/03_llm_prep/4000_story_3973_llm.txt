### The Brotherhood of the Bleachers

There are moments in the life of a married man
when liberty, after having vanished for years like a dishonest
cashier, suddenly reappears at the window and whistles.

Such a moment had come to J. Wilmot Birdsey.

He stood beneath the gray London sky with his hat
pushed back, his cheeks glowing, and his soul performing cartwheels.
Around him flowed the dispersing crowd from the exhibition game
between the New York Giants and the Chicago White Sox.
Englishmen in reserved coats discussed the spectacle with the tolerant
curiosity of persons who had witnessed an obscure tribal ceremony;
Americans shouted across the pavement, collided affectionately, and explained to
policemen that what they had just seen was not merely
a game but civilization at its highest pitch.

Birdsey belonged to the latter school.

He was a small, stout, red-faced man whose figure suggested
that Nature, having begun to construct a senator, had changed
her mind and completed a cherub. In repose he looked
mild. In enthusiasm he expanded. At this moment he had
expanded until he seemed to occupy most of West London.

"Baseball!" he murmured. "Real baseball! In England!"

The words were inadequate, but no others presented themselves. For
eight months he had lived among people who spoke of
a "match" when they meant cricket, and who, on being
informed that the Giants had taken two straight from Pittsburgh,
replied that the weather had been unusually damp. He had
learned to bear much in silence. He had endured castles,
cathedrals, garden parties, portrait galleries, and conversations about the fox.
He had even sat through an amateur production of *The
Importance of Being Earnest* in which his son-in-law played Algernon
with a yellow mustache and the deliberate manner of a
man crossing a frozen lake.

But today exile had lifted its boot from his neck.

He had seen a runner steal second.

Birdsey breathed deeply. The air of London, though composed principally
of smoke, petrol, and history, tasted to him like that
of the Polo Grounds.

His attendance at the game had involved strategy. Mrs J.
Wilmot Birdsey did not approve of strategy unless she had
devised it. She was a tall, handsome woman with a
gaze that had stopped hotel clerks in mid-deception and caused
waiters to confess errors not yet committed. Five years earlier
their daughter, Eulalie, had married Percy, Earl of Thanet, and
from that hour Mrs Birdsey had regarded America as a
regrettable preliminary to life.

Birdsey's own opinion had not been requested.

He had been gathered from his favorite chair in his
New York club as neatly as an oyster from its
shell, packed, ticketed, and conveyed to England. He retained a
vague recollection of protesting somewhere in the Atlantic, but Mrs
Birdsey had been seasick at the time and had told
him not to be foolish. Since then his existence had
consisted largely of entering drawing rooms when summoned and withdrawing
when his presence ceased to ornament them.

Tonight, according to the domestic program, he was to have
dined at Thanet House with a duchess and two men
connected with museums. Instead, he had sent a message announcing
that a sudden headache required solitude, darkness, and the temporary
inaccessibility of his person. He had then put on a
soft hat, slipped down a service staircase, and taken a
cab to the game.

Even now, beneath his happiness, there stirred the cold little
worm of tomorrow's explanation. But Birdsey was an American optimist.
Tomorrow, he felt, might fail to arrive.

"Some finish," said a voice beside him.

Birdsey wheeled.

The speaker was a lean young man of about thirty-five,
with alert eyes and a sharp, vulturine face. He had
the air of one who found the world badly organized
and had appointed himself to collect evidence. His clothes were
English, but his accent came cleanly from Manhattan.

Birdsey gripped his arm.

"American?"

"New York."

"My boy!"

The young man, though surprised to find himself adopted in
the street, endured the embrace.

"Name's Waterall," he said. "Charles Waterall. *New York Sentinel*."

"A newspaper man! Shake again. Birdsey's my name. J. Wilmot
Birdsey. West Seventy-Third Street before they kidnapped me."

"Kidnapped?"

"By marriage. Legalized abduction. My daughter married an earl, and
my wife has been making me pay for it ever
since."

Waterall nodded with the impersonal interest of a journalist who
has heard of stranger crimes.

A few feet away stood another man, also unmistakably American.
One knew it not from his clothes, which were sober
and British, nor from his beard, which was black and
pointed, but from the expression with which he was watching
two English spectators attempt to describe a double play to
each other. It was the pained yet forgiving expression of
a missionary among the heathen.

Birdsey addressed him.

"You, too, sir?"

The bearded man started. For an instant his eyes flashed
toward the road as if measuring the distance to the
nearest cab.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You're from home."

The man hesitated.

"Yes."

"Knew it. Any man who looks like that when somebody
calls a foul ball an off-side has got to be
American."

A reluctant smile appeared beneath the beard.

"My name is Johnson."

"Where from?"

"New York."

Birdsey's heart, already in a molten condition, overflowed.

"Gentlemen," he said, "this is providential. I've spent eight months
eating dinner with people who think an RBI is a
railway. I have a suite at the Savoy, a private
dining room, and enough money to buy food and drink
for every genuine baseball fan in this island. Come along.
We'll have dinner and talk."

Waterall glanced at his watch.

"I'm free."

Mr Johnson glanced over his shoulder.

"I'm afraid—"

"No, you're not," said Birdsey warmly. "Nobody's afraid after a
game like that. You're coming. It's a reunion. It's the
landing of the Pilgrim Fathers in reverse. We three have
got to stick together."

"I have another engagement."

"Side-step it."

"It is important."

"Nothing is important tonight except that catch in the seventh."

The bearded man's resistance trembled. He had the look of
one who had lived so long behind bolted doors that
a friendly hand upon the latch terrified him more than
an enemy's fist. Then, quite suddenly, his face softened.

"All right," he said. "Thank you. I'd like to come."

Birdsey summoned a cab with the sweeping gesture of a
general ordering cavalry into action.

The dinner began magnificently.

Birdsey, fearing that sober reflection might return Mr Johnson to
his engagement, had insisted upon champagne. The first bottle arrived
like a relieving army; the second consolidated the occupied territory.
Soup was followed by sole, sole by a saddle of
lamb, and lamb by a cheese whose aroma asserted its
nationality from across the room.

Birdsey talked.

For eight months his conversation had been dammed behind drawing-room
politeness. Now the barriers went down. He spoke of pennant
races, pitching staffs, disputed trades, and the wickedness of selling
a dependable infielder merely because his knees had begun to
resemble hinges on a barn door. He reviewed the Giants'
prospects. He condemned Boston. He reconstructed the afternoon's game pitch
by pitch, pausing occasionally to drink to individual players.

"To McGraw," he said.

They drank to McGraw.

"To the fellows who came over here and gave us
exiles one square look at life."

They drank to the fellows.

"To baseball."

They drank to baseball.

Under this treatment Mr Johnson's reserve gradually dissolved. At first
he had sat rigidly, his chair angled so that he
could see the door. He answered questions with care and
touched his wine as though it might contain a detective.
But as Birdsey described a famous ninth-inning rally, the man
forgot caution. His eyes gleamed. He interrupted. He contradicted. He
struck the table with his fist while arguing that a
certain runner had been safe at third.

Birdsey beamed. Here was no casual spectator. Here was one
of the elect.

Waterall contributed less. He ate, drank, and watched Mr Johnson
with increasing concentration. His profession had trained him to notice
what other people wished unnoticed. He observed the pallor beneath
the beard, the trembling hand that reached for the salt,
the momentary tightening of the mouth whenever footsteps sounded in
the corridor.

"Been in England long, Mr Johnson?" he asked.

The effect of the question was slight but distinct. Johnson's
fork paused.

"Some years."

"London?"

"No. Various places."

"What line are you in?"

"Business."

"What sort?"

Johnson crumbled bread.

"Importing."

"From the States?"

"Sometimes."

Birdsey intervened. He disliked biography when baseball was available.

"Tell him about the home run," he said. "You saw
it better than I did. There was a hat in
front of me the size of Rhode Island."

But Waterall did not look away.

"Funny," he said. "I have an idea we've met before."

Johnson's eyes rose.

"I don't think so."

"Your face is familiar."

"A face with a beard is apt to remind people
of other faces with beards."

"Possibly."

Waterall took out his cigarette case. His movements were casual,
but casualness, like a badly fitted toupee, was not something
he wore convincingly.

"Were you ever in Albany?"

"No."

"Chicago?"

"No."

"New York, of course."

"I said I was from New York."

"What part?"

Johnson's fingers closed on his wineglass.

"Downtown."

"That narrows it."

Birdsey frowned.

The dinner, which had started out as a patriotic festival,
was developing the atmosphere of a cross-examination. He felt that
Waterall was committing an offense against hospitality comparable to stealing
the spoons.

"Say," he said, "what is this? You fellows can fill
out census forms some other time. We were discussing whether
a left-handed batter—"

"Your beard," said Waterall. "How long have you worn it?"

Silence entered the room.

It did not enter timidly. It came in, took the
best chair, and stared at everybody.

Johnson put down his glass.

"Why do you ask?"

"Curiosity."

"You're a reporter. Your curiosity has a salary attached to
it."

Waterall smiled without warmth.

"Sometimes."

Birdsey looked from one to the other. The cheese continued
to proclaim itself, but nobody listened.

Johnson leaned back. The animation produced by the game and
the champagne had vanished. His face seemed to have sharpened
behind the beard.

"I think I'll go."

He pushed his chair away.

Waterall spoke quietly.

"Sit down, Benyon."

Johnson stopped.

The name hung in the air with the disagreeable precision
of a knife thrown into a wall.

Birdsey blinked.

"Benyon?"

"John Benyon," said Waterall. "Former assistant cashier of the Mercantile
National Bank, New York. Disappeared five years ago with three
hundred thousand dollars."

The machinery of Birdsey's mind, designed for moderate speeds and
familiar roads, struck a hill. He stared at his guest.

"Is that right?"

Johnson—or Benyon—remained standing. His lips had gone white.

Waterall continued in the clipped voice of a man laying
cards on a table.

"The police traced him to Montreal. From there he sailed
under another name. They thought South America. I thought so,
too, until tonight."

Birdsey's first emotion was resentment at the intrusion of banking
into a baseball dinner. His second was amazement. His third,
arriving late but making noise, was moral concern.

"Three hundred thousand?" he said.

"Approximately."

"That's a lot of money."

"It was considered so by the bank."

Birdsey looked at Benyon. The man's shoulders had sagged. The
fear in him was no longer hidden, and because it
was visible it ceased, for Birdsey, to be sinister. It
became merely human.

"Is it true?" Birdsey asked.

Benyon sat down slowly.

"Yes."

Waterall's expression brightened by a fraction. A journalist loves truth,
particularly when it can be telegraphed collect.

"All of it?"

"The essential part."

"You robbed the bank?"

"I took the money."

"And came to England?"

"Yes."

"Where have you been living?"

Benyon laughed. It was a short, cracked sound.

"You want the address, too?"

"It would improve the story."

Birdsey stirred uneasily.

"Now listen, Waterall—"

"No, Mr Birdsey. You listen. This man has been wanted
for five years. There's an extradition treaty. I'm going to
call Scotland Yard."

Benyon's hand moved, then stopped upon the tablecloth.

"You don't have to do that."

"I think I do."

"For a newspaper story?"

"For justice."

Waterall uttered the word with dignity, though not so much
dignity that it obscured the front-page possibilities.

Benyon looked toward Birdsey.

"I'm sorry. I shouldn't have come."

Birdsey's moral concern, which had been standing uncertainly in the
background, received a blow.

"Why did you?"

Benyon did not answer at once. He stared at the
bubbles rising in his glass. Somewhere beyond the curtained windows
a motor horn sounded, and London rolled on, ancient, indifferent,
and entirely without a ninth inning.

"Because of the game," he said.

Waterall made an impatient movement.

"Don't be absurd."

"I'm not. I read about it three weeks ago. I
told myself I wouldn't go. Every day I told myself.
There were notices in the papers, posters at the stations.
Giants against the White Sox in London. It was like
having someone call your name in a crowd."

He paused.

Birdsey leaned forward.

"I've been hiding for five years," Benyon continued. "First in
Glasgow. Then in a village in Wales. Then near Exeter.
I changed my name twice. I let the beard grow.
I never spoke to Americans if I could help it.
When I heard an American voice in a hotel, I
left."

His hands had begun to tremble again.

"You don't know what that's like."

Birdsey made a low sound of sympathy. He knew what
it was like to be unable to speak to Americans,
though in his case the prohibition had usually taken the
form of Mrs Birdsey steering him away from tourists on
the ground that their clothes were noisy.

"I thought exile would be easy," said Benyon. "You read
about men going abroad and beginning new lives. They don't
tell you about the little things. Newspapers arriving two days
late. Nobody knowing what you mean when you say a
team has folded in the ninth. Sitting in a room
at night and trying to remember the sound of the
bleachers."

He swallowed.

"I hadn't seen a game in five years. Not one.
I knew it was dangerous. There would be reporters, detectives
perhaps, people who remembered me. I came anyway. I sat
in the cheapest seat I could get, pulled my hat
over my eyes, and told myself I'd leave after the
first inning."

"You stayed," said Birdsey softly.

"I stayed until the last man was out."

There was a pause.

"And it was worth it," Benyon said.

Now, by all accepted standards, this was a peculiarly immoral
remark. A man accused of stealing three hundred thousand dollars
should not declare that the risking of arrest for an
afternoon's entertainment had been worth it. He should express remorse,
mention an aged mother, and offer to lead the police
to the place where the unspent portion was buried.

Birdsey, however, was not measuring the statement by accepted standards.

He saw Benyon alone in a cheap seat, hidden beneath
a hat, while the crack of bat against ball traveled
across an alien city. He saw five years of silence
broken by the roar of a crowd. He saw, in
short, not an absconding cashier but a fan.

And fans, like shipwrecked sailors and men married to commanding
women, recognize one another's sufferings.

"Well," Birdsey said, clearing his throat, "you've got to hand
it to him."

Waterall stared.

"What?"

"I'm not endorsing the bank proposition. That was raw work.
Very raw. But coming to that game with the police
after him—well, for the love of Mike, it took nerve."

"It took stupidity."

"There's a thin line."

"There is also a law."

Birdsey's face reddened more deeply.

"I know there's a law. I've spent eight months being
introduced to judges. But there are other things."

"Such as?"

"Baseball."

Waterall looked at him as a physician might look at
a patient who had swallowed a small clock.

Benyon covered his face with one hand.

"Let it go, Mr Birdsey. He's right. I knew what
I was doing."

Waterall rose.

"I'm glad somebody does."

He walked toward the telephone on a side table.

The change in Benyon was instantaneous. Despair dropped from him.
He sprang to his feet, his chair crashing backward, and
his right hand went inside his coat.

When it emerged, it held a pistol.

Birdsey had never liked pistols. They belonged, in his opinion,
to the noisier districts of life. This one was black,
compact, and aimed directly at Waterall.

The journalist froze with his hand over the receiver.

"No telephone," said Benyon.

His voice was scarcely audible.

Waterall turned his head. The vulturine face had become pale,
but he kept control of it with creditable firmness.

"You won't shoot."

"Don't make me."

"You're already a thief. Do you want to be a
murderer?"

Benyon flinched.

Birdsey rose halfway from his chair.

"Now, boys—"

"Stay out of this," said Waterall.

Birdsey sat down again automatically. He had been told to
stay out of things for so many years that obedience
had become muscular.

Waterall faced Benyon.

"You can't escape. Even if I don't call, someone else
will recognize you."

"Perhaps."

"Put that down."

"No."

"Then shoot."

Benyon's arm trembled.

Waterall's nerve, though genuine, was assisted by calculation. He had
recognized that the man before him possessed the temperament of
a rabbit complicated by remorse. He took one step toward
the telephone.

"Stop!"

Waterall took another.

"Stop, I tell you!"

The pistol wavered.

For a moment the room became a melodrama of the
strictest school: the hunted criminal, the fearless representative of justice,
the weapon, the locked door, the champagne bottle catching the
light. Birdsey alone failed to rise to the scene. He
stood beside the table with a napkin tucked into his
collar, looking less like an arbiter of destiny than a
diner who had just discovered an insect in the salad.

Waterall reached for the receiver.

Benyon shut his eyes.

Nothing happened.

His hand dropped. The pistol fell to the carpet. He
followed it more slowly, sinking into a chair and then
forward, his arms upon the table.

"I can't," he whispered. "I can't do it."

Waterall snatched up the telephone.

"Operator. Give me Scotland Yard."

And then J. Wilmot Birdsey underwent a transformation.

It would be misleading to say that he thought. Thought
had no part in the affair. His intellect, accustomed to
having all difficult questions referred to Mrs Birdsey, remained peacefully
absent. What acted was something older: the instinct which, thirty
years before, had made a surprisingly useful member of a
college football team out of a short young man with
stout legs and no concern for consequences.

The ancient warrior awoke.

Birdsey lowered his head and charged.

Waterall, who was saying, "This is urgent," had time to
look around. He saw advancing upon him a red, spherical
object decorated with a dinner napkin. Then Birdsey struck him
amidships.

The telephone receiver flew one way, Waterall another. He collided
with a sofa, revolved upon its arm, and disappeared behind
it with a noise like a wardrobe falling downstairs.

Birdsey rebounded but remained upright.

"Run!" he shouted. "Run, you fool! Run!"

Benyon looked up stupidly.

"What?"

"Beat it! Make tracks! Do you want a written invitation?"

Waterall emerged from behind the sofa, disordered but active.

"Birdsey, you lunatic!"

Birdsey seized a champagne bottle and brandished it.

"You stay where you are."

"You're helping a criminal escape."

"I know it. It's the first thing I've done on
my own initiative since 1908, and I'm going through with
it."

Benyon had reached the door. He paused, one hand on
the knob.

"Mr Birdsey—"

"Don't make a speech. They always catch fellows during speeches.
Run!"

Benyon's face worked. For a second he appeared about to
say something important; then wisdom prevailed. He opened the door
and vanished.

Birdsey slammed it behind him and planted his back against
it.

Waterall stood in the middle of the room, brushing dust
from his sleeve. His dignity had suffered, and dignity, once
dented, can be vicious.

"You realize what you've done?"

"Sure."

"You assaulted me."

"You were asking for it."

"I was calling the police."

"That's what I mean."

"You have deliberately assisted an embezzler."

Birdsey considered this.

"Technically."

"There's nothing technical about it."

"The man hadn't seen baseball in five years."

Waterall's mouth opened.

Birdsey raised a hand.

"Don't argue. I'm not saying he ought to have taken
the money. If he'd asked me, I'd have advised against
it. Banks get touchy about that sort of thing. But
he came out of hiding to see the Giants and
White Sox. He risked everything for nine innings. And then
he sat down to dinner with two fellows from home
because he couldn't stand being alone another minute."

Waterall retrieved the receiver and replaced it.

"That does not excuse robbery."

"Maybe not to you."

"Not to anyone sane."

Birdsey's eyes hardened with the lofty fire of the fanatic.

"You're not an exile."

"I've lived in London for three years."

"Yes, but you're a newspaper man. You can go around
asking strangers where they were born. It gives you company.
I've been shut up with earls."

Waterall stared at him.

Birdsey pressed his advantage.

"Exiled fans have got to stick together. It's a brotherhood.
You may not understand, but that's because you've got the
heart of a tax collector."

"My newspaper will understand. This will make an excellent story."

Birdsey's heroic glow flickered.

"Story?"

"Prominent American resident aids fugitive bank robber after assaulting journalist."

"You wouldn't print my name?"

"I can't imagine omitting it."

Birdsey's jaw fell.

Until this moment he had regarded the evening as private.
It now opened before him in headlines. He saw the
paper on the breakfast tray at Thanet House. He saw
Eulalie's eyebrows rise. He saw the earl adjust his monocle.
Above all, he saw Mrs Birdsey.

The resurrected warrior instinct quietly packed its bag and departed.

Waterall observed the collapse with interest.

"There may also be legal consequences."

Birdsey sank into a chair.

"Legal consequences?"

"Obstructing justice. Assault. Aiding escape."

"Would they put me in jail?"

"It's possible."

Birdsey brightened faintly. Jail, though containing inconveniences, did not contain
duchesses. Then he remembered that Mrs Birdsey would visit.

Waterall took his coat from the back of a chair.

"I shall report the matter."

"Now, look here, Waterall. We're both Americans."

"So is Benyon."

"Exactly. That's the spirit."

"It is not my spirit."

Birdsey became persuasive.

"Suppose we say the dinner got lively. Three old baseball
fans, you know. Champagne. Argument about the infield fly rule.
These things happen."

"A pistol was produced."

"Bad taste, certainly. But he put it down."

"He fled."

"Had another engagement."

"I was knocked over a sofa."

"You slipped."

"You charged me like a bull."

"I was coming over to help."

"With your head down?"

"I'm near-sighted."

Waterall put on his coat.

Birdsey watched him in misery. It was clear that reason,
sentiment, patriotism, and creative reconstruction had failed. Only money remained,
and Birdsey was too fundamentally honest to offer it. Also,
he suspected that a journalist who smelled a front page
would cost more than he had on him.

At the door, Waterall paused.

"I ought to call Scotland Yard at once."

Birdsey waited.

"But?"

"But Benyon has several minutes' start, and London is large.
Also, I dislike wasting the police's time."

Birdsey sat upright.

"You mean—"

"I mean I may telephone from the lobby. Or I
may go to my office first."

The vulturine face relaxed slightly.

"Your tackle was not bad for a man of your
build."

Birdsey glowed.

"Used to play football."

"I inferred something of the sort."

"And about the story?"

Waterall opened the door.

"I'll decide in the morning."

He went out.

Birdsey remained motionless. Hope and terror pulled him in opposite
directions like two dogs disputing a cushion.

Presently he rose and crossed to the window. Far below,
cabs moved along the Strand, each one potentially containing John
Benyon, Scotland Yard, or Mrs Birdsey. Birdsey found himself hoping
that Benyon had reached a station, shed the beard, changed
his coat, and disappeared into the vast machinery of England.
He did not inquire too closely into the morality of
this wish. Morality, like evening dress, is apt to become
restrictive after dinner.

He poured the last of the champagne into his glass.

"To the brotherhood," he said.

He drank.

The champagne steadied him. He surveyed the wreckage of the
room—the overturned chair, the fallen cushions, the abandoned pistol beneath
the table—and felt for one splendid instant that he had
become a man of action. He had defied a journalist,
saved a fellow fan, and revived the lost technique of
the flying tackle. Somewhere in London a fugitive was running
free because J. Wilmot Birdsey had risen against tyranny.

Then the telephone rang.

Birdsey stared at it.

It rang again with the measured confidence of an instrument
that knows the worst.

He lifted the receiver.

"Hello?"

"Is that you, Wilmot?" said Mrs Birdsey.

The champagne ceased to be an army and became a
memory.

"Yes, my dear."

"I have telephoned three times. The hotel said you were
dining."

"Just a little soup."

"With whom?"

Birdsey looked at the overturned furniture and at the pistol
on the carpet.

"Two Americans."

"I thought you had a headache."

"So did I."

"Eulalie's dinner was ruined. The duchess asked after you."

"That was kind."

"Where have you been all afternoon?"

Birdsey swallowed.

He had faced a fugitive with a gun. He had
hurled a journalist over a sofa. He had placed friendship
above law and baseball above three hundred thousand dollars. Yet
now, at the sound of his wife's voice, the hero
of the Savoy suite felt his knees loosen.

"Wilmot?"

"Yes, my dear."

"Where have you been?"

Birdsey closed his eyes. A man, he reflected, could escape
Scotland Yard, shave off a beard, take ship under another
name, and begin life anew in some remote corner of
the earth.

But there was no hiding from Mrs Birdsey.

"At the British Museum," he whispered.

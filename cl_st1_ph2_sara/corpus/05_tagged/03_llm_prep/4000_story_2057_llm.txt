## THE THIRD EVENING

I heard this story at an inn near the western
edge of Cornwall, from a man who declared that every
word of it was true, and then, perceiving that I
doubted him, added several details which could not possibly have
been true. This is the usual method of the conscientious
narrator. Truth, like a modest lady at a ball, is
seldom permitted to enter society without a little borrowed ornament.

I have removed the ornaments that were plainly unsuitable, retained
those that improved the figure, and supplied one or two
of my own. The result is, I believe, as accurate
as history commonly is, and much more probable than most
marriages.

The place where Charles Seabohn first saw Mivanway Evans was
called the Witches' Cauldron. The name suggests midnight ceremonies, green
flames, and elderly females riding kitchen implements through the air.
In reality it was a narrow cove between black cliffs,
where the Atlantic spent its afternoons hurling white water against
rocks which had endured the insult for several thousand years
and were consequently unimpressed.

Charles was twenty-two, though he had the grave forehead of
a man who expects to be misunderstood until forty. He
had been reading Shelley upon the cliff and had reached
that condition in which a young man regards himself as
a solitary spirit standing beyond the common crowd, when the
common crowd appeared below him in the form of a
young woman bathing.

She rose through the foam with wet hair upon her
shoulders, and for one magnificent instant Charles believed that Aphrodite
had returned to Cornwall. Then Mivanway put her foot upon
a slippery stone, waved both arms, and sat down sharply
in six inches of water.

The sea laughed. A large breaker roared, for the Atlantic
is pompous; but a little retreating wave ran back over
the shingle with a sound unmistakably malicious.

Charles descended to help her. The path was dangerous, but
young men in love with apparitions will climb where experienced
mountaineers hesitate. By the time he reached the beach, Mivanway
had risen and was arranging her dignity, which had suffered
more than her person.

"You were watching me," she said.

"I thought you were drowning."

"I was not."

"I know that now."

This was not an inspired beginning, yet it answered. Many
lifelong attachments have opened with less.

Mivanway was nineteen, pretty, and accustomed to having small inconveniences
removed by other people. Her father, a Presbyterian merchant of
Newcastle, had brought his family to Cornwall for the summer,
partly for Mivanway's health and partly because a physician had
remarked that sea air was beneficial. Physicians often say this
when they cannot think of anything else.

They met again the following morning by accident. They met
by accident on the next four mornings. After that they
had sufficient honesty to dispense with accident.

Charles was on holiday from a civil engineering firm in
London. He disliked civil engineering, because bridges and drainage systems
did not sympathise with his soul; but the firm paid
him, which is a coarse argument and generally conclusive. He
wrote verses in the evening and believed that poverty, properly
shared, would be romantic. Mivanway believed so too, having never
shared it.

Within a fortnight they loved each other eternally. Eternity is
the natural unit of calculation at nineteen.

Their elders objected. Charles's mother said that Mivanway was spoiled.
Mivanway's father said that Charles had no prospects. Both statements
were correct, and therefore useless. Had somebody merely observed that
the young people would quarrel over breakfast, damp towels, money,
weather, visiting relations, and the proper place for muddy boots,
he might have frightened them. But parents in such crises
insist upon abstractions, and abstractions are the very food of
love.

Opposition transformed Charles into Hercules and Mivanway into a princess
guarded by dragons. The dragons were chiefly two aunts and
a solicitor. They married before Christmas.

That was the first mistake.

Not the marriage itself, perhaps. I am not a theologian,
and therefore hesitate to pronounce upon mysteries which theologians explain
so confidently. Their mistake was marrying with the conviction that
love abolishes character. Love may illuminate character; it may forgive
it; it may even, after years of careful work, improve
it. But it does not abolish it. A girl who
has been indulged remains accustomed to indulgence after the wedding
service, and a young man who has always thought himself
a hero does not become a sensible husband merely by
buying a ring.

They took a cottage in a lonely part of Northumberland
for their honeymoon. Charles had chosen solitude because poets recommend
it. Poets also recommend dying young, wandering barefoot, and addressing
remarks to the moon. Their advice should be treated cautiously.

The cottage had three rooms, a smoking chimney, a pump
that froze, and a woman from the village who came
on alternate mornings unless the weather was unpleasant, in which
case she did not come at all. Rain fell steadily.
The lanes became mud. The nearest neighbour was a retired
clergyman who collected beetles and spoke only of their legs.

For the first week Charles and Mivanway were exquisitely happy.
During the second they began to discover each other.

Charles discovered that Mivanway disliked silence unless she had chosen
it. Mivanway discovered that Charles, when composing poetry, wished to
be left alone but, when not composing it, expected to
be entertained. He wished her to admire his verses without
criticism. She wished him to admire her hats without noticing
their cost. He forgot to order coal. She cried because
he laughed when she burned the pudding. He apologised and
she forgave him; but unfortunately she explained at length why
forgiveness had been necessary, so that by the end he
was offended again.

They dreamed of making sacrifices for each other. Mivanway imagined
nursing Charles through fever in a hut while hostile natives
beat drums outside. Charles imagined renouncing fame, fortune, and possibly
a throne for Mivanway's sake. No fever, natives, or throne
presenting themselves, she asked him to give up smoking.

Charles smoked a meerschaum pipe. It was not a beautiful
object, though he believed it to be one. Its bowl
had attained that brownish colour which the smoker calls mellow
and everybody else calls dirty. He loved it with the
modest but tenacious affection men reserve for things incapable of
answering back.

Mivanway said smoke made her ill.

Charles threw the tobacco pouch into the fire.

It was a superb gesture. Marie Antoinette could not have
performed it more royally, though she might have reflected that
the pouch was nearly empty.

For three days he was cheerful. On the fourth he
became thoughtful. On the fifth he began to speak bitterly
of tyranny. He did not smoke, but he talked so
continuously about not smoking that Mivanway would have suffered less
had he filled the cottage with fumes from morning till
night.

The honeymoon ended. They went to Newcastle, where Charles found
employment in a dingy engineering office and Mivanway lived within
visiting distance of her family. This arrangement looked practical. That
was the next mistake.

Her relations treated Charles with elaborate kindness, which is often
harder to bear than rudeness. Her father lent them money
and never mentioned it, except indirectly. Her sisters called him
"poor Charles" when he was in the room. Her aunts
gave Mivanway advice in whispers loud enough to penetrate masonry.

Charles's work was tedious. He left home early in damp
weather and returned late, bringing with him the temper accumulated
from incompetent draughtsmen, unreasonable contractors, and a managing clerk whose
collar seemed designed to express moral superiority. Mivanway passed lonely
days in small rooms and received visitors who asked whether
she was happy in tones implying that she could not
be.

Neither had yet learned the indispensable domestic art of allowing
an irritation to die unfed.

Their quarrels were petty, which made them dangerous. Great calamities
sometimes unite people; small discomforts recruit them separately. A burned
chop can achieve what war, pestilence, and theological controversy attempt
in vain.

The end came one evening after dinner.

Mivanway had discovered tobacco ash upon Charles's coat. He had
been smoking at the office. Strictly speaking, this was not
a crime, though it was certainly bad tactics.

"You promised," she said.

"I promised not to smoke because you said it poisoned
you. You were not at the office."

"A promise does not alter with the furniture."

"I suppose I must ask permission to breathe when you
are absent."

"You never loved me."

This phrase is to marriage what a lighted match is
to a powder magazine. Charles replied that she did not
know what love meant. Mivanway said she knew enough to
recognise its absence. Charles said something cruel about her family.
She struck his shoulder with her open hand.

He struck her face.

There was silence.

It was not a violent blow. That makes no difference.
He stared at her, horrified less by what he had
done than by the sudden sight of the man capable
of doing it.

Mivanway put her fingers to her cheek.

"Go away," she said.

He went.

The next morning he returned determined to confess himself a
brute, kneel if necessary, and accept any punishment. Unfortunately, during
a sleepless night, Mivanway had also prepared a speech. Reconciliations,
like parliamentary debates, seldom prosper when both parties bring notes.

Charles said, "I can never forgive myself."

Mivanway replied, "It is not for you to forgive."

He tried to explain that he had meant precisely that.
She accused him of turning even his repentance into vanity.
He said she could wring poison from an apology. She
said his apology was an insult. In ten minutes they
had left the blow far behind and were quarrelling about
the quarrel concerning the apology for the quarrel.

Had an elderly person entered and knocked their heads together,
happiness might have been restored. No elderly person came. Elderly
persons are generally present when young people wish to be
alone and absent when they might be useful.

Within a week Charles accepted a post with an engineering
company in South Africa. He told himself that separation would
be best for Mivanway. Pride often disguises itself as sacrifice,
and the fit is excellent.

She returned to her father's house. At the railway station
Charles hoped she would ask him to remain. Mivanway hoped
he would refuse to go. Each waited for the other
to save them.

The train left punctually.

Three weeks later, the steamer carrying Charles southward struck submerged
wreckage during a storm. She sank before dawn. A few
boats reached safety, but the passenger lists were confused, and
the newspapers reported Charles Seabohn among the dead.

He was not dead. He had been pulled from the
water by a Dutch trader, spent six days feverish at
a small port, and then travelled inland with a party
whose letters were infrequent and whose interest in English domestic
arrangements was limited.

When, after some weeks, Charles learned of his reported death,
he wrote a letter to Mivanway. He addressed it, sealed
it, and carried it in his pocket for two days.

Then he burned it.

This was the worst mistake.

He believed Mivanway had never loved him. He imagined her
first shocked, then relieved, then free to begin again without
the disgrace of divorce or the burden of a husband
she despised. It seemed noble to remain dead for her
convenience. We are never so ingenious as when constructing noble
reasons for cowardly conduct.

Thus Charles Seabohn disappeared.

Mivanway received the news at breakfast. Her father took the
telegram, read it twice, and sent the younger girls from
the room. Mivanway did not faint. Persons in fiction faint
at once; persons in life generally attend first to some
absurd detail. She folded her napkin, moved the sugar bowl,
and asked whether Charles had suffered.

That afternoon she fainted.

For months she wore black and spoke little. Her family,
who had thought the marriage disastrous, now discovered admirable qualities
in the dead man. Death is the most persuasive advocate.
Her father remembered Charles's independence; her aunts recalled his pleasant
manners; her sisters preserved remarks he had never made.

Mivanway preserved the truth.

She remembered his generosity, his foolish verses, his wet hair
after walking in rain, the way he had warmed her
gloves before the fire. She remembered also the quarrels and
the blow, but these no longer proved that he had
not loved her. They proved merely that he had been
young and angry—and she had been young and cruel.

She would have forgiven him. That knowledge arrived too late,
as the most important knowledge commonly does.

Meanwhile Charles worked.

South Africa was good for him, which he resented at
first. The wide veldt cared nothing for his wounded soul.
Rivers had to be bridged, roads surveyed, machinery repaired, and
men persuaded to labour under a sun that made poetry
appear a criminal occupation. Charles became useful. Usefulness is one
of the few reliable cures for excessive interest in oneself.

Years passed. He grew brown, quiet, and successful. He learned
to listen before answering. He learned that hunger worsens an
argument, that fatigue may resemble hatred, and that a man
who apologises should stop speaking immediately afterward. In short, he
acquired in exile the education that marriage had demanded in
advance.

He smoked again.

At first he smoked defiantly, as if each pipeful were
a declaration of independence addressed across the ocean to Mivanway.
Later he smoked because he liked tobacco. This was progress.

He kept the old meerschaum. Its colour deepened until even
he admitted it was ugly.

Then, in his thirty-second year, he began to dream of
Mivanway.

In the first dream she stood upon the Cornish cliff
above the Witches' Cauldron, dressed in grey, looking toward the
sea. He called, but the wind carried his voice away.

In the second she descended the path and vanished into
mist.

In the third she turned to him and said, "Come
now, Charles. I have waited."

He woke convinced that she was dead.

Dreams are unreliable guides. They have advised kings to fight
disastrous battles, merchants to invest in nonexistent mines, and respectable
gentlemen to flee from harmless black cats. Nevertheless, when a
man has spent ten years pretending not to love his
wife, a dream may tell him what daylight has failed
to make him admit.

Charles arranged his affairs and sailed for England.

He reached Cornwall in October. The innkeeper remembered the Evans
family and informed him that one of the daughters—"the widow
lady"—was staying with her elder sister in a house above
the bay.

Charles did not ask which daughter. He dared not. Being
certain Mivanway was dead, he preferred not to risk contradiction
until he had visited the place of their first meeting.
There is no superstition so unreasonable that a remorseful man
cannot make it orderly.

At twilight he walked to the Witches' Cauldron.

The cliffs were black against a sky of faded gold.
White water moved far below, appearing and disappearing among the
rocks. The wind travelled over the heather with the long
whisper of a silk dress. Charles stood where, ten years
before, he had read Shelley and mistaken a wet girl
for a goddess.

Then he saw Mivanway.

She came along the upper path slowly, dressed in grey.
Grief and time had altered her, though less than Charles
would afterward claim. She was no longer the petulant girl
who demanded heroic devotion before breakfast. Her face was quieter,
her movements gentler. To Charles she appeared exactly as she
had in his dream.

He could not move.

Mivanway saw him beside the cliff.

She, too, stood still.

Charles had grown leaner and broader. His beard, foreign clothes,
and sun-darkened face should have made him seem a stranger.
But love, even when buried, is an excellent portrait painter.
She knew him at once.

She knew also that he was dead.

Each looked upon the other with the fearful tenderness appropriate
to seeing a ghost. Neither spoke. The sea boomed below
with indecent enthusiasm.

After perhaps a minute, Mivanway bowed her head. Charles, interpreting
this as a gesture from the world beyond, removed his
hat. Then she turned and walked away.

Charles watched until the twilight swallowed her.

That night he slept little. He was convinced that Mivanway's
spirit had returned to forgive him, or summon him, or
reproach him; he was uncertain which. Ghosts, like government notices,
are often deficient in precise instructions.

Mivanway told her sister Eleanor that she had seen Charles.

Eleanor, being forty, practical, and unmarried, said, "You have been
walking too far without tea."

"I saw him by the Cauldron."

"You saw somebody like him."

"He took off his hat."

"A civil ghost."

Mivanway refused to argue. Deep emotion may survive ridicule, but
it seldom enjoys the experience.

The following evening both returned to the cliff.

Charles arrived first. He had told himself he would not
go, and had therefore set out half an hour earlier
than necessary. Mivanway approached at the same hour as before.

Again they stood apart.

This time Charles whispered her name. The wind moved between
them, and Mivanway could not be certain she had heard.
She raised one hand, but fear checked her. If he
were a spirit, perhaps speech would banish him. If he
were a delusion, speech would make the delusion ridiculous. Human
beings are often more afraid of being ridiculous than of
being haunted.

They watched one another until darkness gathered. Then Mivanway left.

Charles almost followed. He did not. Though he had acquired
courage among floods, blasting powder, and angry labourers, he remained
timid before the possibility of forgiveness.

On the third evening the weather changed. Clouds covered the
sunset, and a pale mist crept upward from the cove.
The path glimmered, the heather darkened, and every rock prepared
itself to be mistaken for something supernatural.

Charles waited near the edge. In his pocket lay the
old meerschaum pipe. He had been smoking to steady his
nerves, tobacco serving many moral purposes not recognised by temperance
societies.

Mivanway appeared through the mist.

She came nearer than before. Charles could see the sorrow
in her face; she could see the lines time had
drawn about his eyes.

"Mivanway," he said.

She trembled but did not retreat.

"Charles."

No ghost had ever answered to its name with more
satisfaction.

"I came because you called me," he said.

"I called you every day for ten years."

This reply puzzled him. The dead, he had supposed, possessed
clearer information.

"I thought you had forgiven me."

"I did. Too late."

"It was not too late for me to know."

She looked at him wonderingly. "Do the dead know what
we think?"

"Surely you can tell me that better than I."

They paused.

It is astonishing how long a misunderstanding may survive when
nourished on both sides.

Charles stepped nearer. "I was cruel to you. I was
vain and violent. I wanted you to love me without
troubling me. I blamed you for every discomfort because I
had promised to make you happy and could not bear
to know I had failed."

Mivanway's eyes filled.

"I was cruel too," she said. "I made a virtue
of hurting you. I asked for sacrifice because I wanted
proof, and when you gave it I despised you for
resenting it. Oh, Charles, if you had come back the
next morning—"

"I did."

"No. I mean after you had gone away."

"I could not. I believed you hated me."

"I loved you."

He bowed his head. "I know now."

"Why did you not let me tell you while you
lived?"

Charles looked up sharply. "While I lived?"

She came still closer. "I have prayed that I might
see you once, if only to ask forgiveness. Perhaps this
is wrong. Eleanor would say it is shockingly improper to
meet a spirit alone upon a cliff, though I do
not see who could chaperon us."

Charles stared.

"You think me a spirit?"

Mivanway gave a faint, wondering smile. "What else could you
be?"

It was then that the truth, after wandering for ten
years through two continents, reached Charles Seabohn. It reached him
slowly; truth sometimes loses breath on steep paths.

"Mivanway," he said, "you are alive."

She drew back.

"Of course I am."

"I thought you dead."

"How could you?"

"I dreamed—"

She almost laughed, but remembered she was speaking to a
ghost.

Charles stretched out his hands. "The ship sank, but I
was saved."

"You were reported drowned."

"I know."

"You knew?"

He lowered his hands.

Here the supernatural difficulty ended and the matrimonial one recommenced.

"You let me believe you dead?"

"I thought you would be happier."

"For ten years?"

"I was a fool."

"You were worse than a fool."

"Yes."

"You allowed me to mourn you, to pray for you,
to wear black until Aunt Margaret said it made me
look sallow—"

"I am sorry."

"You could have written."

"I did write."

Her face softened.

"Then the letter was lost?"

"I burned it."

Her face hardened again.

Even at the entrance to paradise, Charles possessed a genius
for choosing the wrong explanation.

He told her everything: his cowardice disguised as sacrifice, his
work, his regret, the dreams, the journey home. Mivanway listened
with tears upon her cheeks. Anger struggled with joy; joy,
having waited longer, was stronger.

"You struck me once," she said, "and I thought that
the great injury. But letting me believe you dead was
worse."

"I know."

"Do you always know everything after it is useless?"

"Formerly. I hope I have improved."

She looked at the altered face before her, at the
humility which youth had lacked and suffering had supplied.

"If you are truly Charles," she whispered, "then let me
bless you before you go back."

"Go back where?"

She did not answer. Some portion of her still clung
to the ghost. The dead may confess, but a husband
who has remained voluntarily dead for ten years is almost
too improbable even for marriage.

Mivanway bent and took his hand.

At that moment the meerschaum pipe slipped from Charles's pocket
and fell upon the rock between them.

They both looked down.

The pipe was unmistakable. It had survived the honeymoon, the
shipwreck, Africa, remorse, three supernatural evenings, and Charles's moral development.
Few human affections are equally durable.

Mivanway stared at it. Then she stared at Charles.

He stooped quickly and attempted to hide it behind his
back.

That guilty movement convinced her.

A spirit might appear in foreign clothes. A spirit might
carry the face of a living man, speak with his
voice, and offer badly reasoned explanations. But no spirit, however
depraved in life, would smuggle a meerschaum pipe into eternity
and then conceal it from his wife.

"You are alive," she said.

"Yes."

"And smoking."

Charles, who had faced death in the Atlantic and dynamite
upon the veldt, answered meekly, "A little."

Mivanway began to laugh.

At first it was no more than a broken sound
among tears. Then the absurdity of ten years' mourning, three
evenings' haunting, Charles's solemn repentance, and that detestable brown pipe
overwhelmed her. She laughed until the cliffs gave back the
sound, until the sea seemed to laugh with her, until
she could neither speak nor breathe.

Then she fainted.

Charles caught her.

He carried her up the path toward the house, an
undertaking which would have been more romantic had Mivanway not
lost one shoe and had Charles not been obliged twice
to stop for breath. Love may lend strength, but it
does not repeal gradients.

Eleanor opened the door.

She saw in the porch a tall, bearded man carrying
her unconscious sister. The lamp shone upon Charles's face.

Eleanor screamed and shut the door.

Charles knocked.

There was no answer.

He knocked again. "Miss Evans!"

From within came Eleanor's voice: "Go away!"

"I cannot. I have Mivanway."

"That is precisely why you should go away."

"She has fainted."

There was a silence during which practical duty fought superstition.
Practical duty won, as it generally does in elder sisters.

Eleanor opened the door a few inches.

"Are you Charles Seabohn?"

"Yes."

"You are dead."

"No."

"You look dead."

"I am tired."

Mivanway stirred, and Eleanor admitted them.

They laid her on a sofa. Eleanor fetched brandy, though
she disapproved of it, and held the glass while Charles
explained. It was not a good explanation. No explanation involving
a shipwreck, ten years of voluntary decease, prophetic dreams, and
a pipe can be made good at short notice.

When Mivanway opened her eyes, she saw Charles beside her
and smiled.

"He is alive, Eleanor."

"So he asserts."

"I am going to forgive him."

"That," Eleanor said, "is your affair. But he cannot remain
in this room after midnight."

"It is only half past nine," said Charles.

Eleanor looked at him severely. One does not return from
the dead to correct a lady's clock.

Mivanway held out her hand, and Charles took it. He
bent over her, no longer a poet, a hero, a
ghost, or a martyr—merely a man who had behaved very
badly, suffered considerably, and been granted the rare privilege of
trying again.

Eleanor withdrew to send a telegram to the family. She
paused in the hall, pen in hand, attempting to decide
whether the message should begin, CHARLES ALIVE, which sounded indecently
abrupt, or MIRACULOUS NEWS, which would alarm her father's heart.

From the drawing room came Mivanway's low voice, then Charles's
answer, then a sound suspiciously like laughter.

Eleanor shook her head.

She had lived respectably for forty years, had never mistaken
a living man for a ghost, and had always regarded
smoking as a vulgar habit. Yet it now appeared that
Providence, romance, and tobacco had combined to arrange matters without
consulting her.

This was unreasonable.

But, as she afterward admitted, it was very like marriage.

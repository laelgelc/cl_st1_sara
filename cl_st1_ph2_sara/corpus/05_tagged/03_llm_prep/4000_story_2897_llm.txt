### Memoranda Concerning the Fogo Hands

Among the numerous varieties of mankind encountered by the practical
mariner—who, without troubling Salamanca, Oxford, or any other inland establishment
of conjecture, sees more nations before breakfast than a professor
does in his natural life—there is none concerning which greater
misinformation prevails than the people commonly denominated by forecastle authority
the "'Gees." I place the apostrophe carefully, since it represents,
as near as punctuation may, that portion of the original
word which sailors have thrown overboard.

The primitive word is Portuguese; its commercial abbreviation, Portugee; and
its final maritime concentration, 'Gee. Thus is language purified by
use, as rosewater becomes attar, or a lengthy sermon becomes,
under judicious sleep, a brief benediction. Some philologists assert that
the term derives from a native cry. Others, with greater
probability, hold that Jack Tar, having no leisure for syllables
not immediately useful in reefing topsails, knocked the head and
tail off Portugee and retained the middle for ballast. I
offer both theories impartially, though the latter has cost me
more reflection and should therefore weigh more.

The 'Gee particularly intended in these memoranda is native to
Fogo, one of the Cape Verde islands, where his ancestors,
according to the best cabin-table authorities, were partly Portuguese settlers
and partly Africans, with such additional ingredients as Providence, piracy,
miscalculation, and hot weather may have introduced. Fogo signifies fire,
from the volcano there situated; though the mountain, having once
enjoyed an extensive eruptive practice, has in recent years thrown
up business altogether. The inhabitants remain.

Upon a scientific view, the Fogo 'Gee is neither this
nor that, but a profitable intermediate article. He is commonly
brown, though the precise shade depends on sunlight, ancestry, occupation,
and the observer's disposition. To the humane captain he is
coffee-colored; to the mate who has lost a boat by
him, burnt molasses; to the cooper, mahogany; and to the
sailor who owes him money, black. Such distinctions, being moral
rather than optical, should not perplex the student.

His exterior varies. Some are tall, some short, some broad,
some thin; which singular diversity has led experienced men to
conclude that no two are exactly alike, except in being
'Gees. He generally possesses a great appetite but little acknowledged
imagination; strong shoulders but no political influence; a large eyeball,
according to some observers, but small insight into the mysteries
of American wages. His hair curls with a pertinacity that
resists salt water, whale oil, theology, and the ship's comb.
His teeth, where complete, are excellent for biscuit, which is
fortunate, biscuit being the chief article by which the American
whale fishery communicates civilization.

The island lies conveniently in the track of vessels wanting
men and inconveniently in the track of men wanting fair
wages. Hence a commerce has arisen, founded on those immutable
principles which regulate the relations of strength and necessity. A
whaleship from Nantucket, New Bedford, or Dartmouth arrives short-handed, her
original crew having deserted, died, been discharged, been lost, or
merely discovered the voyage to be longer than represented. The
captain goes ashore with an interpreter, a bag of tobacco,
several promises, and that benevolent expression which commerce assumes when
buying below value.

Presently the candidates collect. They come down the black slopes
in patched trousers and bright caps, bringing recommendations from cousins
who never saw the sea and testimonials from priests who
never saw the candidate sober. One has caught turtles; another
has rowed between islands; a third once stood on a
rock during a gale and is therefore entered as an
able seaman. All are willing. Willingness, among shipowners, ranks just
below skill and considerably above expense.

The ordinary seaman ships for money. The Fogo hand ships
for money also, but by a beautiful refinement receives much
of it in biscuit, slops, tobacco, postponed accounts, mysterious deductions,
and blows. The latter are seldom entered upon the articles,
because their number cannot be accurately foreseen. A careful owner
dislikes an open account.

It would be unjust to say the 'Gee is paid
nothing. He is paid whatever remains after his advance, clothes,
medicine, boat gear, lost time, damaged property, and the captain's
recollection have been deducted. I once knew a man who,
after forty months' cruising, owed the ship three dollars and
a quarter. This astonished him, for he had never possessed
three dollars and a quarter in his life; but the
steward explained that debt, like longitude, might exist though invisible.
The man then requested another voyage in order to discharge
the obligation, thereby proving that the system encourages industry.

On board, the new recruit is termed a green 'Gee
until he has learned to pull, steer, reef, splice, cut
in, try out, duck a handspike, distinguish the mate's right
fist from his left, and answer to any name beginning
with an oath. After this education he becomes a knowing
'Gee, though the advancement brings no increase of pay. Knowledge
is its own reward, especially when acquired by another man's
labor.

Sailors, who are jealous guardians of equality whenever anyone above
them is concerned, regard the Fogo hand with contempt for
working cheaply. They blame him for lowering wages, as a
drowning man might blame the rope for being thin; and
having beaten him for taking less, they beat him again
for asking more. "There's too many of 'em," says Jack.
"Then why ship them?" asks an inexperienced landsman. "Because they're
cheap," says Jack, closing the argument in the manner of
Socrates, Fox, and the second mate.

During ordinary weather the recruit is a 'Gee. In a
gale, when top-hamper must come down and the watch is
worn hollow, he becomes a man.

"Men aloft! Hands by the halyards! Where are those men?"

The storm passes; the title is withdrawn.

"Here, you 'Gees, scrub up that blubber!"

Thus humanity, like extra sail, is set only when required
and taken in at the first moderation of weather.

The selection of these islanders is an art, and captains
who practice it are sometimes called 'Gee jockeys, though they
do not ride the article except figuratively. The inexperienced master
judges by face and assurances. The prudent one examines bone,
wind, bottom, joints, teeth, temper, and probable endurance, much as
he would inspect a horse, with this commercial advantage: a
horse may kick during inspection, while a poor man hoping
for wages commonly stands still.

The candidate is ordered to walk, run, stoop, leap, cough,
shout, and lift a stone. His chest is thumped as
a cask is sounded; his arms are pulled fore and
aft; his knees are discussed in his presence; and his
age, stated by himself as twenty-two, is corrected by the
captain to thirty-six, or eighteen, according to the berth requiring
him. If he smiles, he has a good temper. If
he does not, he is sulky. If he understands the
questions, he is cunning; if he fails to understand, he
is stupid. A perfect examination cannot be failed, because every
answer proves the examiner correct.

Yet deception will occur. The islander, having observed that sound
legs possess a market value, sometimes endeavors to exhibit them
whether Providence has supplied them or not. In this he
differs little from ships advertised as copper-fastened, politicians advertised as
incorruptible, or patent medicines advertised as universal; but captains, who
tolerate exaggeration only in their own statements, resent the practice.

A notable case occurred aboard the *Jeroboam*, commanded by Captain
Peleg Snipe. Needing one additional hand, Snipe selected a lively
fellow named Manuel, who sprang over a wall, ran twice
around a tamarind tree, lifted a keg, and executed several
movements suggesting a close acquaintance with Apollo Belvedere. He wore
loose trousers, as is customary in warm climates and convenient
in commercial demonstrations. The captain, delighted with his agility, shipped
him at a rate which would hardly have tempted a
respectable Newfoundland dog.

For the first day Manuel worked cheerfully. On the second
he moved slowly. On the third he remained in his
bunk. The mate, attributing this to laziness, prescribed rope's-ending, that
universal maritime remedy recommended for fatigue, homesickness, fever, defective English,
and fractured confidence. The remedy failed.

It then appeared that the man suffered from elephantiasis, and
had concealed beneath his capacious garments a leg of such
heroic dimensions that Hercules might have claimed it after death.
During his exhibition ashore he had performed chiefly upon the
other leg, assisted, some said, by two cousins behind the
wall. Once at sea the concealed member swelled until it
became less a limb than a passenger, and demanded accommodations
of its own.

Captain Snipe was outraged—not at the disease, which he regarded
as an act of God, but at the loss of
labor, which he regarded as personal fraud. He entered in
the log that Manuel had "hung fire." The mate called
him lumber. The crew called him worse. Unable to climb,
pull, or stand in a rolling boat, he was carried
about the Pacific for nearly three years, from Fogo to
the Brazil Banks, around Cape Horn, among the islands, north
toward Japan, and home again, occupying space, eating biscuit, and
reminding the captain daily that a bargain may have a
soul inconveniently attached to it.

Manuel bore all this with a silence which the officers
praised as evidence that his intellect was small. I sometimes
sat near him during the middle watch. He knew enough
English to ask where the ship was bound, and I
knew enough honesty to tell him I did not know.
"Home?" he would say.

"In time," I answered.

His home receded astern at six knots.

When the *Jeroboam* finally entered New Bedford, Manuel was set
ashore with eight dollars, a Bible in Portuguese, and an
account proving that he had cost the owners much more.
The eight dollars were mentioned in every subsequent telling; the
three years were generally omitted, being a detail injurious to
the moral.

Captain Hosea Kean, however, never suffered such imposition. Kean was
a man of science, suspicion, and very little neck. He
maintained that a candidate viewed in the marketplace was a
candidate in theatrical condition. "A 'Gee before a captain," said
he, "is all leg and willingness. Catch him natural."

Accordingly, when recruiting at Fogo, Kean first inspected the applicants
publicly and rejected them all. At dusk he armed the
mate and four boatsteerers, hired a boy as guide, and
marched inland to observe the rejected men in their domestic
state. His theory was that disease might lie to a
captain, but would tell the truth to a wife.

The expedition approached each cottage silently. At a signal the
party rushed in, lanterns raised and pistols ready, surprising families
at supper, prayer, quarrel, sleep, or such other suspicious pursuits
as islanders practice when not seeking employment. One man was
ordered to rise from his mat and jump three times.
Another was made to carry his mother across the room.
A third, discovered eating stew, was commanded to cough while
the mate listened at his back with an ear made
accurate by rum.

"Turn him round," Kean would say. "Make him walk. Now
run, you rascal! Ge-e-e-e-e!"

If the fellow ran, the captain nodded. If he attempted
to escape through the window, Kean marked him especially sound.

By this method he obtained nine men without elephantiasis, concealed
fractures, advanced consumption, or visible reluctance. One had a wife,
who clung to his arm and demanded to know where
he was being taken. Kean, through the interpreter, explained that
the man was going to earn money. The wife asked
how much. This question, being foreign to the purpose of
the expedition, was not translated.

Kean's recruits proved excellent. Only one died, two deserted, three
were injured, and the remaining three returned after four years
with enough clothing to be mistaken for prosperity. The captain's
system was thereafter much admired, though never generally adopted, because
it required walking uphill after supper.

As to the moral nature of the 'Gee, I hesitate
to pronounce. Sailors say he is thievish, chiefly because he
sometimes takes food when hungry; cowardly, because he dislikes being
struck; and ungrateful, because after a voyage of cuffs, cold,
danger, and biscuit he does not always thank the captain
in polished terms. On the other hand, I have known
him to share his tobacco, pull until blood stood in
his palms, and go over a boat's side after a
drowning shipmate whom better-paid men had already committed to Providence.
These facts are difficult to reconcile with established opinion and
may therefore be exceptional.

His religion is Roman Catholic ashore and meteorological at sea.
In calm weather he remembers saints; in a typhoon he
invokes every power from the Virgin to the cook. This
has been cited as theological confusion, though I have heard
Protestant captains perform equal enlargements of doctrine when a whale
stove their boats.

If he survives the voyage and settles in an American
port, identification becomes troublesome. He buys boots, acquires English oaths,
marries, keeps a boardinghouse, and occasionally lends money to sailors
who once kicked him. His children attend school and refuse
to answer to 'Gee. The knowing observer may still detect
the ancestry by surname, complexion, or the anger displayed when
asked to explain either; but such tests are uncertain. Prosperity
is a great confounder of natural history. Give a despised
man a coat, a vote, and a rent book, and
before long some philosopher will discover that he belongs to
mankind.

There are, I am told, former Fogo hands in Providence,
New London, and New Bedford who own small vessels and
hire crews themselves. Whether they pay in biscuit, I cannot
say. It would be philosophically pleasing if they did, for
oppression, like whale oil, is most valuable when refined and
passed through several hands.

Anyone desiring fuller information should apply to a whaling captain
of long experience, who will describe the Fogo people with
complete authority, particularly if none are present to contradict him.
Captain Hosea Kean is the best of these naturalists. At
last report he was somewhere in the Pacific—north of one
island, south of another, and westward of the truth—examining mankind
for defects.

Should the inquirer find him, let him ask how many
'Gees make a crew, how many biscuits make wages, how
many blows make discipline, and at what exact degree of
danger a 'Gee becomes a man.

Kean will answer every question.

That is his defect.

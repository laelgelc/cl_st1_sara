### THE IMPERIAL PIG

*There are hunters who follow the sambhur's slot,*
*And hunters who wait by the pool;*
*But the sport that is played with a minute and
note*
*Is the sport of the diligent fool.*
*Oh, heavy the spear, and uncertain the shot,*
*And brief is the tiger's spring—*
*But the hunting of Man through an office file*
*Is a year-long delicate thing.*

I knew Pinecoffin before Nafferton bought the horse, and Nafferton
after Pinecoffin sold it; which gives me, I think, a
right to speak with authority upon the matter. In India
every quarrel has at least three origins—the true one, the
official one, and the one told at the Club. This
quarrel was exceptional, for all three began with the same
horse.

The beast was a long, yellow gelding, with one white
eye and the moral nature of a dismissed tahsildar. Pinecoffin
had ridden him twice, both times in a soft field
with a syce at his head. He told Nafferton that
the animal required "a firm but sympathetic hand," which is
horse-dealer's language for a saddle, a chain, and the assistance
of Providence.

Nafferton paid four hundred rupees. On the following morning the
horse removed him first from the saddle and then, by
a backwards kick, from the immediate neighbourhood of the stable.
Nafferton lay among the irrigation plans with a torn coat
and a bruised hip, while the gelding tried to eat
a table of district statistics.

Pinecoffin came to see him.

"A little fresh," he said. "You held him too tight.
These up-country horses dislike interference."

Then he laughed.

You may think the sale of the beast, or the
danger of the kick, was the offence. It was not.
Men forgive fraud daily, because to admit fraud is to
admit that another man has been cleverer. They forgive attempted
murder if it is carried out with sufficient confusion. But
laughter fixes the account. Pinecoffin laughed for perhaps eight seconds,
and Nafferton, looking up from the floor, said: "I shall
remember that."

He remembered for three weeks before moving. Nafferton belonged to
that quiet order of Civilian who never attacks a difficulty
in front. He went round it, measured it, labelled it,
requested information regarding its probable cost, and then brought Government
upon it from the rear. Pinecoffin, on the other hand,
was Earnest.

There are several kinds of official earnestness. There is the
earnestness which arrives by dak, marks everything "Immediate," and leaves
by the next cold weather. There is financial earnestness, which
saves nine annas at the expense of nine thousand rupees.
There is frontier earnestness, which believes all questions may be
settled by a road, a levy, or both. Pinecoffin possessed
Agricultural Earnestness. He loved bullocks, temporary wells, seed-distribution, village manure,
opium-scrapers, and the comparative virtues of deep and shallow ploughing.
Give him a stalk of diseased millet and he would
produce eighty foolscap pages, with appendices.

Nafferton knew this.

One evening he asked Pinecoffin, quite casually, whether pork might
not be introduced more generally into the ration of the
British soldier in India.

Pinecoffin looked grave. "The subject," said he, "has an Imperial
aspect."

"Exactly," said Nafferton.

That was the first beat of the pig-hunt.

Within a week Nafferton wrote privately—not officially, you understand—to Pinecoffin,
asking whether, in view of the increasing cost of preserved
meats and the desirability of developing local resources, he could
furnish a brief note upon the breeds of Pig most
suited for cantonment supply.

He capitalized Pig. This was the lure.

Pinecoffin replied in twenty-three pages, exclusive of a table showing
measurements from snout to tail in the black, white, grey,
piebald, long-eared, short-eared, hairy, and comparatively hairless varieties. He added
observations on fecundity, comparative longevity, and "the influence of humid
conditions upon the essentially hirsute characteristics of the exotic Pig."

Nafferton thanked him warmly and asked how the animals should
be distributed.

Pinecoffin considered distribution. Distribution involved railways; railways involved freight; freight
involved crates; crates involved timber; timber involved the Forest Department.
He wrote to deputy commissioners, veterinary surgeons, two railway boards,
and a missionary in the hills who was reported to
possess a Chinese boar of singular constitution.

Nafferton filed every answer.

Then he asked about feeding.

Here Pinecoffin flounced shoulder-deep into the trap. He investigated gram,
maize, refuse, oil-cake, melon-rinds, kitchen slops, and a wild root
eaten in years of scarcity by the poorer inhabitants of
a district which neither he nor Nafferton had visited. He
calculated how much flesh-former existed in one seer of each,
allowing for climate, age, sex, exercise, and "temperamental wastage." He
devised a piggery on sanitary principles, with drains, sleeping platforms,
shade, and a separate apartment for the maternity cases.

Government has built barracks on less evidence.

At the end of two months Nafferton sent a friendly
memorandum:

> "Would the advantages claimed for the system remain valid
in tracts where maize is scarce, water brackish, and the
inhabitants either unwilling or, from religious prejudice, unable to engage
in porcine husbandry?"

Pinecoffin answered the question of religion.

It was a noble answer. It travelled from the Punjab
to Madras; glanced at caste; paused over custom; distinguished objection
to touching Pig from objection to feeding Pig; and concluded,
after an ethnological excursus of some splendour, that local sentiment
should be respected, except where economy demanded otherwise. This last
phrase was Pinecoffin's own, and Nafferton underlined it in blue
pencil.

Next came acclimatization.

Could the English Pig survive the hot weather? If not,
could the country Pig be improved by judicious crossing? Would
the half-bred Pig inherit the delicacy of its European sire
or the reprehensible hardiness of its oriental dam? What effect
had dust-storms upon the appetite? Was wallowing a luxury, an
instinct, or a hygienic necessity? Might tanks be provided? If
tanks were provided, what was the estimated evaporation per superficial
foot?

Pinecoffin hunted the Pig through seven climates and three hundred
pages.

Meanwhile he continued his proper work. He inspected wells, checked
crop returns, rebuked negligent patwaris, advised on locusts, and attended
conferences where men who had never owned an acre explained
the Native Cultivator. At night he wrote Pig. His eyes
reddened; his collars wilted; and his bungalow became fortified with
reports.

I called on him once and found the drawing-room occupied
by diagrams of pork.

"The difficulty," he told me, "is not production, but coordination."

It generally is.

Nafferton was very sympathetic. He sent Pinecoffin cigars and another
question.

What of economics?

Pinecoffin now prepared estimates for model piggeries of twenty, fifty,
one hundred, and five hundred head; for slaughter-houses attached and
detached; for transport by hoof, rail, cart, and river; for
mortality ordinary, epidemic, and conjectural. He considered insurance. He compared
the Pig with the goat, the sheep, the hen, and,
in one moment of fever, the commissariat mule. The mule
comparison ran to forty-six pages and proved nothing except that
a mule cannot profitably be converted into bacon.

Nafferton asked whether the hides had commercial value.

They had not, but Pinecoffin would not say so until
he had consulted the tanners of Cawnpore. The tanners differed.
One believed pigskin suitable for saddlery; another for bookbinding; a
third condemned it on account of the pores. Pinecoffin reconciled
them in an appendix, and passed to glue, gelatine, hoof
substitutes, bone-meal, bladder manufacture, and the possibility of exporting pickled
ears.

Then came bristles.

For six weeks all India seemed to Pinecoffin one enormous
brush. He collected specimens from cantonments and bazaars, measured stiffness,
tested elasticity, and corresponded with a Birmingham firm on the
manufacture of shaving, painting, varnishing, scrubbing, tooth, and bottle brushes.
He discovered that certain imported bristles returned to India in
finished articles at an enhanced price. This awoke the Fiscal
side of him, and he wrote with passion upon the
economic drain.

Nafferton congratulated him upon "opening an entirely new and patriotic
branch of the inquiry."

The year turned. The yellow gelding had long since been
sold to a subaltern, whom he deposited in a canal,
but the Pig endured.

It spread into cold storage, military hygiene, veterinary education, municipal
taxation, female employment, technical schools, and the disposal of offal.
Nafferton's questions were always modest and always inevitable.

"Would it be possible—?"

"Has any attempt been made—?"

"May there not be grounds for supposing—?"

"Before troubling Government, ought we not to ascertain—?"

That last was especially deadly. Pinecoffin believed that Government ought
never to be troubled until everything had been ascertained. As
everything cannot be ascertained, he worked without hope of release.

After fourteen months, Nafferton asked whether cooked refuse gave better
results than uncooked refuse under conditions of extreme humidity.

Pinecoffin's nerve broke. He had dealt with the matter, he
wrote, in paragraph 117 of his Preliminary Note on Feeding,
submitted eleven months earlier. If Nafferton would do him the
honour to read material already supplied, unnecessary repetition might be
avoided.

It was not a rude letter. But it was tired,
and tiredness in official correspondence is like blood on a
trail.

Nafferton pulled the string.

He wrote to Government—through the proper channels—that, while pursuing a
private inquiry into the possibility of reducing the cost of
Army meat supplies, he had sought technical assistance from Pinecoffin;
that Pinecoffin had at first responded with commendable zeal; but
that, when asked for a small clarification bearing directly upon
the practical issue, he had referred the inquirer to an
inaccessible and apparently irrelevant document in terms which suggested flippancy
toward a matter of public importance. Nafferton regretted the necessity
of mentioning the affair. Nafferton was reluctant to complain. Nafferton
desired only the advancement of Imperial interests.

Government gushed.

Government is often represented as a machine. It is really
a large, sleepy animal which can be made to kick
if the correct hair is pulled. A Secretariat gentleman, who
had read none of the papers and therefore approached them
impartially, minuted that officers should afford reasonable assistance to bona
fide inquiries connected with administrative economy. Pinecoffin received an official
expression of surprise and regret.

That should have ended it.

But Nafferton had not forgotten the laugh.

He gathered Pinecoffin's finest passages—the half-bred Pig, the maternity apartment,
the evaporation from wallowing tanks, the patriotic bristle, the pickled
ear, and the mule as a bacon-producing agency. To these
he attached Pinecoffin's final letter and the Government reprimand. Then,
under a modest pseudonym, he sent the packet to a
Calcutta newspaper which had lately discovered that the Indian administration
was both comic and expensive.

The newspaper printed extracts beneath the heading:

> **PIGS AND PROGRESS**
> **HOW THE TAXPAYER IS INSTRUCTED**

It asked whether the Empire would perish unless Pinecoffin determined
the temperamental wastage of the porcine ration. It recommended him
for appointment as Director-General of Bristles. It suggested that a
special Department of Pig, Pork, and Pickled Ear be created,
with Pinecoffin as its first and permanent secretary.

Other papers copied the article. The Club read it aloud
after dinner. Men who had never opened an agricultural report
quoted "maternity apartments" across the bar. A wag sent Pinecoffin
a shaving-brush marked "Imperial Produce." Another addressed a parcel to
"The Honourable Member for Pig."

Pinecoffin could not explain. To say that he had spent
a year answering a private succession of questions was worse
than silence. To say that Nafferton had made him do
it seemed incredible. There was no false statement in the
correspondence. There was only Pig—enormous, solemn, and accurately documented.

THEN Pinecoffin understood.

He came to Nafferton's bungalow at dusk. I was there,
though I had the decency to move behind the newspaper
when he entered. Pinecoffin's face was grey, and the famous
article shook in his hand.

"You did this," he said.

Nafferton rose. "Did what?"

"You know. The questions—the complaint—the papers. You made a fool
of me."

"My dear Pinecoffin, your information was invaluable."

"There was no scheme!"

"There was an inquiry."

"You never meant to submit it."

"I have submitted portions to the public."

Pinecoffin made a weak movement with the newspaper. "Why?"

Nafferton looked at him with the mildness he reserved for
successful operations.

"Do you remember the yellow horse?"

Pinecoffin stared.

"You sold me a dangerous brute," Nafferton continued, "but I
might have forgiven the sale. He threw me, but I
might have forgiven the fall. You came to see me
lying among the district statistics—and you laughed."

"That was fourteen months ago."

"Exactly. You laughed for eight seconds. I wished to give
you something that would last longer."

Pinecoffin said nothing.

Nafferton's voice became almost kind. "You told the Club I
could not ride. They forgot it in a week. The
newspapers have called you the Director-General of Bristles. That will
be remembered for years. We are even, I think."

Pinecoffin went out without closing the door.

Nafferton watched him cross the compound. "Poor fellow," he said.
"He takes Pig too seriously."

That was all. There was no duel, no appeal, and
no confession to Government. The files remained perfectly regular; the
questions had been reasonable; the answers were Pinecoffin's own; and
Nafferton had at every stage appeared industrious, courteous, economical, and
public-spirited.

Which shows that, in India, a man may be hunted
without horse or hound, shot without powder, and skinned without
a knife—provided only that his enemy understands official procedure and
he himself is Earnest.

## THE PASSENGER IN ROOM EIGHTEEN

When the Hot Cross Bun Steam Navigation Company paid Mrs.
Richard Keeling the sum of two thousand one hundred pounds,
the circumstance was very properly described by the newspapers as
an act of munificence without parallel in the history of
the mercantile marine.

One journal, whose editor possessed a heart of unusual dimensions
and shares in the company, declared that such generosity reconciled
Capital with Labour, the Sea with the Land, and Humanity
with the payment of dividends. It added that strong men
had wept upon the Stock Exchange when the news became
known. This may have been true, though tears in that
neighbourhood are more commonly associated with a fall of three
points.

The company itself said nothing. Corporations, like oysters, are most
impressive when closed.

Yet there were persons who remembered that, twelve months earlier,
the same company had refunded Mrs. Keeling her husband's fare,
amounting to eighteen pounds ten shillings, and had considered even
that payment excessive. How, then, did eighteen pounds ten become
two thousand one hundred?

It is a story worth telling, if only because it
shows that justice, though frequently seasick, sometimes reaches port.

Richard Keeling was a small, pale man who kept accounts
for a seed merchant in Clapham. Having inherited a few
hundred pounds from an aunt, he resolved to begin life
anew in America, where, as he told his wife, clerks
were paid like princes and princes, presumably, kept no accounts.

He took passage to New York in the *Gibrontus*, the
largest and most advertised vessel of the Hot Cross Bun
Line. He occupied Room 18, upon the starboard side, and
on the third night of the voyage fell through an
open hatch into the after-hold.

His neck was broken.

There had been no lamp at the hatch, no rope
around it, and no sailor stationed near it. These facts
were admitted. The company, however, denied legal liability, because legal
liability is a shy animal which seldom appears where it
is most confidently expected.

Mrs. Keeling maintained that her husband had been killed by
negligence. The company maintained that he had fallen through the
hatch of his own accord. Both parties were probably sincere,
but the company possessed solicitors, expert witnesses, and a managing
director, while Mrs. Keeling possessed three children and seven shillings.

She accepted the refund of the fare.

The *Gibrontus* sailed again six weeks later.

On the second morning the purser, Mr. Baines, found a
passenger sitting alone at breakfast. He was a thin gentleman
in a black coat, with damp hair, grey cheeks, and
cold, fishy eyes. Before him stood a cup of coffee
which he did not drink.

Mr. Baines had an excellent memory for passengers, especially those
likely to complain, and he could not place the stranger.

"Good morning, sir," he said. "I fear I have not
had the pleasure—"

"Keeling," replied the passenger. "Richard Keeling."

The purser's smile remained upon his face, though it had
ceased to express anything.

"Indeed, sir?"

"Room 18."

Mr. Baines consulted his pocket list. Room 18 was vacant.

"There appears to be some mistake."

"Not on my part."

"May I see your ticket?"

Keeling produced one. It was a genuine Hot Cross Bun
ticket, issued for the present voyage, stamped, numbered, and initialled
at Liverpool. No clerk afterwards admitted selling it, but clerks
will admit little when dismissal stands behind the question.

"Quite so," said Mr. Baines. "Everything seems regular. Yet your
name is not on my list."

"That," said Keeling, "is your difficulty."

The purser disliked the remark, because it was just.

He went to the chief steward. The chief steward pooh-poohed
the matter and said that passenger lists were always wrong.
He went to the captain. The captain said that pursers
were always wrong. Thus the incident was officially explained.

That evening, shortly after eleven, a cry was heard near
the after-hold. Sailors came running and found the hatch open.
At the bottom lay Richard Keeling, with his neck broken.

The hatch had been secured ten minutes earlier.

The body was brought up, identified by Mr. Baines, and
placed in the ship's mortuary. In the morning it was
gone.

This disappearance greatly annoyed the captain. A dead passenger is
an inconvenience; a missing dead passenger suggests carelessness.

The crew were warned against idle talk, and consequently discussed
nothing else for the remainder of the voyage. Mr. Baines
made a report to the directors, who replied that fatigue,
indigestion, and excessive attention to nautical superstition had temporarily impaired
his judgement. They enclosed a memorandum reminding him that the
company discouraged hallucinations while on duty.

The next voyage began quietly. Before sailing, Mr. Baines inspected
Room 18 himself. It was locked and unoccupied. He then
inspected every hatch, and had each one fastened with chains,
padlocks, and such earnestness as can be inspired only by
fear disguised as official zeal.

At dinner, a passenger touched him on the arm.

"Good evening," said Richard Keeling.

Mr. Baines dropped the soup list.

Keeling looked precisely as before, except that his head inclined
slightly to one side, as though his collar were uncomfortable.

"You!" whispered the purser.

"Room 18," said Keeling.

"It is locked."

"I have the key."

He displayed it. He also displayed a ticket, perfectly valid
and horribly businesslike.

Mr. Baines took him into his office and shut the
door.

"Now, look here," he said. "You are dead."

"I am aware of it."

"You fell into the hold."

"Twice."

"But why have you come back?"

Keeling regarded him with eyes in which there was no
anger, no warmth, and very little interest.

"I purchased a passage to New York. Your company undertook
to convey me there. It has not done so."

"But you were killed!"

"That is not performance of contract."

The purser wiped his forehead.

"What can I do for you?"

"Deliver me in New York, or compensate my widow."

"We refunded your fare."

"So I heard."

"And she accepted it."

"She was hungry."

This was outside Mr. Baines's department.

"How much compensation?"

"Two thousand pounds, with one hundred pounds added for each
further voyage."

"Two thousand!"

"Two thousand one hundred now."

Mr. Baines stared.

"You mean to go on coming?"

"Again and again."

"And falling down that hatch?"

"Again and again."

"But it is chained!"

"Your company made a similar observation last time."

Keeling rose.

"Wait!" cried the purser. "Where are you going?"

"To take the air."

"Not near the after-hold?"

"That is the shortest way."

Mr. Baines sprang after him, but the office door, which
had opened easily for Keeling, refused to move for the
purser. By the time he forced it, there came from
below the dull crash of a body striking timber.

They found Keeling in the hold.

The hatch above him remained chained and padlocked.

There are moments when even sailors become unreasonable. Six men
immediately announced their intention of leaving the ship at New
York. Three others requested transfer to vessels without Room 18.
The chief steward, who had pooh-poohed the first appearance, now
pooh-poohed nothing whatever and slept with a Bible beneath his
pillow and a life-belt around his waist.

Once again the corpse vanished before morning.

Once again the directors attributed the affair to nervous strain.

Unfortunately for the company, one of the resigning sailors told
a reporter. The reporter, being a conscientious man, added only
such details as were necessary to make the truth credible.
He described Keeling as luminous, seven feet tall, and accompanied
by blue flames. He further stated that the ghost had
demanded the blood of the chairman.

The public took an immediate interest in Room 18. Passengers
cancelled bookings. Those who did sail insisted upon inspecting every
hatch before paying their deposits. A clergyman wrote to say
that the apparition was a judgement. A barrister replied that,
as the deceased had accepted the conditions printed on the
back of his ticket, no judgement could possibly lie. Both
letters were widely admired.

The managing director, Sir Joshua Pike, remained firm.

"There is no ghost," he said. "Ghosts are contrary to
company policy."

He ordered the name of Room 18 changed to Room
16 A, an expedient which had proved successful with unlucky
numbers in hotels. Keeling, however, booked it under its new
designation without difficulty.

On that voyage he fell through the after-hatch at midnight,
despite the fact that the hatch had been covered with
an iron plate and guarded by two able seamen. The
guards resigned before breakfast. One had seen Keeling pass through
the plate; the other had not seen him, which was
worse, for he had heard the dead man politely say,
"Excuse me," while passing through his body.

The newspapers became less friendly. Cartoons appeared. One showed the
Hot Cross Bun Line carrying passengers "to New York or
the next world, at the company's option." Another represented Sir
Joshua counting sovereigns while a queue of ghosts waited at
the booking office.

Shares declined.

A decline in shares will induce belief where all the
spectres of antiquity have failed. Sir Joshua announced that he
would investigate personally.

He sailed in the *Gibrontus* and occupied Room 18—or 16
A, as the directors still preferred to call it. He
brought two detectives, the company solicitor, and a revolver. The
detectives guarded the door, the solicitor guarded the company's interests,
and Sir Joshua guarded the revolver.

For two nights nothing happened.

At eleven on the third, Sir Joshua was awakened by
a man sitting at the foot of his berth.

"Keeling?" he gasped.

"Quite so."

"How did you get in?"

"With my key."

"What do you want?"

"You know."

Sir Joshua recovered something of his boardroom manner.

"Your widow accepted a settlement."

"She accepted a refund."

"The company admitted no liability."

"It need not."

"Then upon what legal ground—"

"I am not proceeding legally."

This silenced the solicitor, who was listening through the keyhole.

Sir Joshua tried another course.

"My good man, this persecution is most damaging."

"Yes."

"It affects innocent shareholders."

"They invested in the company."

"It may ruin the line!"

"Then you will understand my widow's position."

From the corridor came a suppressed cough, which may have
been the solicitor's conscience, though he afterwards denied possessing one.

Sir Joshua gripped the blanket.

"If we pay, will this stop?"

"Immediately."

"Two thousand one hundred?"

Keeling's cold eyes rested upon him.

"Until midnight."

"And after midnight?"

"Two thousand two hundred. This is another voyage."

Sir Joshua looked at his watch. It was seventeen minutes
to twelve.

Business training triumphed. Within six minutes a wireless message had
been sent to Liverpool authorising payment of two thousand one
hundred pounds to Mrs. Keeling. Within ten, confirmation arrived. Sir
Joshua handed the message to the dead man.

Keeling read it carefully.

"Thank you," he said. "I shall not trouble you again."

He rose and passed through the locked door.

At twelve precisely there came no cry from the after-hold,
no crash, and no dead passenger requiring attention. It was
the quietest midnight the *Gibrontus* had known for months.

Upon his return Sir Joshua informed the press that prolonged
reflection upon the hardships of widowhood had inspired the directors
to make a purely voluntary gift. The newspapers praised him;
the shareholders approved; and Mrs. Keeling received her money.

Room 18 was afterwards converted into a linen cupboard.

The Hot Cross Bun Line still denies that it ever
carried a ghost. This is understandable. It carried Richard Keeling
badly enough when he was alive, and no corporation cares
to admit that its most persistent customer was a dead
man who insisted upon getting what he had paid for.

### Our Woodland Neighbours

When we first built our house in the woods, we
imagined that we had gone quite beyond the reach of
society. The nearest highway was hidden by a mile of
oak and chestnut; the river, broad and darkly glassy, slipped
under alders at the foot of the hill; and all
about us lay an ancient congregation of trees, which seemed
to have been holding solemn council there since before men
had invented taxes, door-bells, or afternoon calls.

The place was beautiful beyond our sober expectations. Checkerberry shone
underfoot with polished leaves and scarlet beads; partridge-plum crept over
the rocks; prince's-pine made little green temples in the shade;
and wild bignonia swung its orange trumpets from the branches
as if fairy heralds were forever announcing some royal progress
which never came. There were plumy ferns in the hollows,
grape-vines as thick as a child's arm, and mosses so
deep that one trod upon them with compunction. Through all
went the river, showing here a silver scale, there a
black mirror, and farther on breaking into light among the
stones.

Into this paradise we introduced carpenters.

The ring of axes, the scream of saws, and the
crash of unwilling trees made a startling commentary upon our
love of nature. We preserved everything, we said, which it
was possible to preserve; but the house must stand somewhere,
and sunshine must enter it, and a garden could not
flourish in the precise spot occupied by three hemlocks, two
beeches, and a thicket of mountain-laurel. Thus we sacrificed our
principles by the acre, and consoled ourselves by planting roses.

When at last the house, with its broad veranda and
glass conservatory, looked peacefully down the slope, we supposed the
country had accepted us. We had not yet learned that
ownership recorded on paper is viewed with indifference by the
older inhabitants of a wood. We had bought the land
from a gentleman in town; the snakes, woodchucks, frogs, owls,
muskrats, and flying squirrels had not signed the deed.

Our first callers were snakes.

They came in spring, when the wet earth was full
of roots and promises, and the pale green points of
our lilies had scarcely pushed aside the leaves. We found
one little striped gentleman lying across the garden path, warming
his back in the sun. He was neat, harmless, and
not more than two feet long; yet the alarm which
announced him might have served for a dragon.

"A snake! A snake!"

Every member of the household arrived equipped according to temperament—with
a hoe, a broom, a stick, a basket, or advice.
The visitor, meanwhile, slipped into the grass with an air
of wounded dignity. Soon another appeared by the stone wall,
and then a third beneath the veranda steps. Evidently the
whole serpent family had come out for its spring shopping,
and our new paths happened to run through the market-place.

We grew almost philosophical about them. Were they not creatures
of the same beneficent Providence? Had they not glossy skins,
modest habits, and an excellent appetite for vermin? It was
only inherited prejudice, we declared, dating from that unfortunate affair
in Eden, which prevented mankind from appreciating their virtues.

This tolerant opinion endured until one afternoon, when we saw
a large snake beside the lily-pond with a frog half
swallowed. The poor frog's hind-legs were still outside, spread helplessly
upon the grass, and now and then they gave a
convulsive kick. Philosophy forsook us. The hoe descended; the snake's
noontide repast was interrupted forever; and the frog, alas! was
too far gone to profit by our intervention.

Afterward we reflected that the snake had been merely dining,
and that we ourselves had eaten chicken at noon without
inquiring into the moral sentiments of the chicken. But comparisons
of this kind are seldom welcome when one has just
performed an act of righteous violence. We buried neither victim,
and spoke of something else.

Our next neighbour was a woodchuck, who resided under a
ledge beyond the garden and led, as we at first
supposed, the innocent life of a woodland hermit. He was
a broad, brown, contemplative person, with small ears and a
coat which suggested that he had slept in it through
several winters. At sunset he sat upright near his door,
folded his paws upon his waistcoat, and surveyed us with
the composure of an original proprietor.

We admired him greatly until the lettuce began to disappear.

At first we blamed the weather. Then we blamed cutworms.
Then, early one morning, we discovered our hermit among the
beans, helping himself without asking. He had tastes of singular
refinement. He ignored the coarse outer leaves and selected the
tender hearts; he sampled peas, rejected radishes, approved young cabbage,
and cut down beans chiefly, it seemed, for the pleasure
of seeing them fall. A human guest who behaved in
this fashion would have been considered imperfectly civilized.

We fenced the garden. He dug under the fence. We
blocked the passage with stones. He opened another. We hung
fluttering rags, tin pans, and the fragments of an old
umbrella about the beds, until our garden looked like the
encampment of a defeated army. He became accustomed to these
decorations and ate beneath them.

At last, driven to desperation by the loss of our
second sowing, we set a trap—not, we explained to one
another, with murderous intention, but merely to detain him for
removal to some distant and agreeable mountain. The distinction comforted
us at bedtime.

In the morning he was dead.

There he lay in the trap, the shrewd little eyes
closed, the paws which had wrought so much mischief drawn
helplessly inward. All his crimes became trivial. The lettuces rose
before us in hateful abundance; the beans appeared not worth
eating. We remembered him sitting at sunset, a solitary old
fellow before his rocky door, and we accused ourselves of
cruelty with that complete sincerity which often follows an accomplished
purpose.

What should be done with him? Burial under the beeches
was proposed, but the gardener, whose sentiments were less literary,
carried him to a farmer's pig. The pig received our
lamented neighbour without prejudice, ceremony, or delay.

So ended the hermit, and so ended, for several days,
our appetite for pork.

The conservatory had been designed as a refuge for flowers
and ourselves. Its glass roof gathered winter sunshine; vines climbed
the pale walls; and in the centre a little fountain
spilled from one stone basin to another with a murmur
which encouraged dreams of Italy, water-sprites, and enchanted princes. It
was therefore natural that a frog should establish himself there.

He appeared one morning on the rim of the fountain—a
large bullfrog, green-brown and solemn, with a cream-colored throat and
gold-rimmed eyes. He wore, to our imagination, an invisible-green coat,
a gray satin vest, and spectacles. His hands rested upon
his knees; his mouth expressed judicial reserve. He looked like
a German professor who had suffered enchantment but retained his
degree.

We called him Unke.

Unke soon became one of the family. He permitted us
to approach within a respectful distance and listened to conversation
without interrupting, unless the subject displeased him, when he descended
into the basin with a heavy splash. In the evening
he uttered a deep note from among the calla-lilies—"Unk! Unk!"—as
if delivering, one syllable at a time, a discourse upon
the vanity of human affairs.

We fed him flies, which he received without gratitude, and
small worms, which disappeared with astonishing speed into a countenance
otherwise devoted to philosophy. Visitors were introduced to him. Children
knelt beside the fountain to see him blink. We speculated
upon his past, his opinions, and the probability that a
kiss from the right princess might restore him to a
tall castle on the Rhine.

Presently there came another frog—a small, lively, bright-eyed creature, hardly
half Unke's size. He sat on a mossy stone and
made a cheerful chirruping sound, like the refrain of some
nursery song heard through an open door. We named him
Cri-Cri.

Cri-Cri had none of Unke's academic gravity. He sprang from
stone to leaf, hid behind the fern-pots, peeped at us
from the water, and sometimes perched upon the bronze shell
from which the fountain flowed. If Unke was the philosopher
of our little court, Cri-Cri was its minstrel, page, and
licensed jester. We fancied the two were friends. Often they
sat together beside the basin—Unke massive and meditative, Cri-Cri slim
and alert—and we congratulated ourselves upon having established an ideal
society beneath glass.

Nature, however, had established another.

One warm noon a cry summoned us to the conservatory.

"Oh, look at Unke!"

We looked—and may we never again behold philosophy under such
circumstances! Cri-Cri had vanished except for two slender hind-legs, which
projected from Unke's mouth and palpitated feebly against his vest.
The old villain sat with half-closed eyes, swallowing by measured
efforts. At every gulp the legs grew shorter.

We reproached him; we threatened him; we offered flies in
exchange; but he remained firm. One final movement of his
throat completed the tragedy of the deepest dye, and our
lively little singer passed entirely into the person of his
friend.

Unke's character was ruined.

Yesterday he had been a sage, an enchanted prince, a
genial guest; today he was a cannibal, a hypocrite, and
a monster in green broadcloth. It was useless to remind
ourselves that bullfrogs have not studied domestic ethics, and that
Cri-Cri had possibly regarded every insect in the conservatory with
equally criminal intentions. Affection is rarely scientific. We had named
the victim, and therefore the victim possessed a soul; we
had named the murderer, and therefore he ought to have
known better.

A family council was called. Capital punishment, advocated with warmth,
was rejected because of the woodchuck. Perpetual imprisonment seemed inconvenient.
Transportation was finally decreed.

Unke was captured after a pursuit among flower-pots, during which
he overturned a heliotrope and leaped into a visitor's bonnet.
We bore him down the hill in a covered pail.
At the river-bank the lid was removed, and the condemned
stood for one instant looking at us with his old
gold-spectacled gravity, as if we, not he, had behaved discreditably.
Then he sprang into the water and vanished under the
shadow of an alder.

We never saw him again. Sometimes, on damp evenings, a
deep "Unk!" sounded from the river. We chose to believe
that it was he, lecturing a less prejudiced audience upon
the injustice of civilization.

Of other woodland neighbours there were many. Muskrats occupied the
river-bank and occasionally came up to inspect our premises. They
were industrious-looking bodies, though their perfume preceded them and lingered
after their departure. One sat beneath the veranda during a
shower and washed his face with such exemplary care that
we forgave him everything except the air.

Owls called from the hemlocks at night. One gave a
melancholy question, another answered with wild laughter, and a third
seemed to be strangling in the chimney. We never saw
them clearly; they belonged to the dark, and the dark,
we found, has a population and business of its own.

Most mysterious of all were the noises in the house.

They began after midnight—a soft scrambling behind the walls, a
sudden thump overhead, then a patter across the floor of
an unoccupied room. We blamed mice, branches, loose shutters, burglars,
and, in moments of candor, ghosts. The sounds continued. Something
raced along the upper passage, stopped when we opened a
door, and began again when we returned to bed.

"What could it be?"

One night we discovered the intruder in a chamber. A
small shape flew—or seemed to fly—from the curtain to the
top of a wardrobe, and clung there with enormous shining
eyes. It was a flying squirrel, soft gray above, white
beneath, with folds of skin stretched between his limbs like
a gentleman's traveling-cloak.

How had he entered? Why not here? Doors and chimneys
are human distinctions; to a determined squirrel, a house in
the woods is merely a tree with unusually square hollows.

The fine gentleman refused to depart. When approached, he launched
himself across the room, flattened like a furry kite, alighted
upon a picture-frame, and looked down with spirited contempt. We
opened the window. He ignored it. We moved a chair.
He sailed to the bedpost. We advanced with sheets held
like nets; he vanished behind a bookcase and reappeared upon
the mantel.

At last we closed the other doors and gently drove
him along the passage toward the conservatory. There, among the
vines and moonlit panes, he appeared suddenly at home. He
climbed a tall plant, crouched upon a branch, and surveyed
the fountain where Unke had once dispensed wisdom and Cri-Cri
gaiety.

We opened a high window.

For a moment the squirrel hesitated, his small body outlined
against the night; then he sprang, spread his velvet cloak,
and glided outward to an oak. The leaves received him
with scarcely a sound. We saw him run up the
trunk, pause once to regard us, and disappear into his
proper world.

Or so we thought. A week later there was another
thump in the roof.

Living in the woods did not, after all, remove us
from society. It only introduced us to neighbours who made
fewer calls and observed fewer conventions. They ate our vegetables,
swallowed their companions, entered without knocking, laughed after midnight, and
sometimes presented an odour in place of a visiting-card. We
condemned them according to our convenience, sentimentalized them according to
their appearance, and forgave them when it was too late
to be useful.

Yet, taking them together—the hermit who loved lettuce, the philosopher
who devoured his friend, the perfumed river-folk, the midnight musicians,
and the gray rogue in his flying-cloak—we cannot honestly say
they were worse than neighbours we had known elsewhere. Their
appetites were franker, perhaps, and their manners less disguised. But
in mischief, vanity, intrusion, violence, and the art of looking
innocent afterward, they differed from civilized society chiefly in having
no words with which to defend themselves.

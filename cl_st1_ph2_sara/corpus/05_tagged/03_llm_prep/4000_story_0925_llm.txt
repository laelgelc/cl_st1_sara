# THE SWIMMING SENECA;
## OR, THE VOICE OF THE LAKE

Among the curiosities which, from the earliest settlement of the
country, have exercised the ingenuity and disturbed the philosophy of
those who dwell upon the borders of Seneca Lake, two
have preserved a reputation so general and so well attested
that it would be rash either to reject them as
inventions or to receive, without inquiry, all that has been
affirmed concerning them. The first is a tree—or, more properly,
the trunk of a tree—which has been seen, at uncertain
intervals, floating upright in the lake; the second is a
deep, sudden, and apparently subterranean report, resembling the discharge of
heavy ordnance, and known throughout the neighboring country by the
appellation of the Lake Gun.

The tree was first observed, according to a memorandum preserved
in the family of one of the older settlers, during
the autumn of 1796. It appeared about three miles south
of the outlet, standing nearly perpendicular in the water, with
five or six feet of its body exposed, and moving
against a wind which was sufficiently strong to wrinkle the
whole surface of the lake. The observer, who was a
man of veracity, and who had no motive for deceiving
either himself or his descendants, followed it in a skiff
until the increasing violence of the weather obliged him to
seek the shore. On the succeeding morning the tree had
disappeared.

Within twenty years it was seen again near the western
bank, more than thirty miles from its former position. A
party of fishermen approached it, threw a rope about the
projecting portion, and endeavored to tow it into a cove;
but the rope—whether from decay, bad seamanship, or the opposition
of the mysterious voyager—parted at the instant when success appeared
certain. One of the fishermen declared that, as the line
broke, the trunk inclined toward them, "like a man making
a bow before he leaves company." This circumstance, though entitled
to no great weight in a court of law, obtained
extraordinary authority in the taverns, where it was commonly repeated
after the hour at which men cease to distinguish accurately
between what they have witnessed and what they desire to
have witnessed.

Many other appearances were reported. The trunk was said to
have been caught in ice, buried beneath it, and liberated
in the spring; to have drifted south with a northerly
gale and north with a southerly one; to have remained
for weeks in a particular bay, like a ship lying
at anchor, and then, without visible cause, to have unmoored
itself at midnight and departed. It had been struck with
oars, fired at with rifles, and assailed—if local testimony may
be trusted—with a boat-hook wielded by a miller of uncommon
bodily strength. From all these adventures it escaped with no
injury which could be satisfactorily proved.

The settlers, whose imagination had been nourished upon scriptural histories
and Old-World traditions, bestowed upon this restless fragment the name
of the Wandering Jew; for the obvious reason that a
being condemned to travel until an appointed consummation, and a
tree condemned to cruise without harbor from one end of
Seneca Lake to the other, appeared to them members of
the same unfortunate family. Whether the comparison flattered the tree
or wronged the man was not considered.

The Lake Gun rested upon testimony still more extensive. Its
report had been heard at noonday beneath an unclouded sky;
at midnight, when the stars were visible; in winter, when
the lake was sheathed with ice; and in summer, when
not a breath moved the leaves of the vineyards. Sometimes
it seemed to proceed from beneath the water; sometimes from
the southern hills; and sometimes from the vacant air midway
between the two. The lake spoke, the hills answered, and
a succession of echoes rolled northward, until the last murmur
expired among cultivated fields.

Natural causes had, of course, been broached. One philosopher attributed
the noise to electrical discharges; another to the collapse of
caverns beneath the lake; a third to the escape of
imprisoned gases; while a fourth, who possessed the advantage of
having heard the sound only once and at a great
distance, was confident that it arose from the cracking of
rocks under sudden changes of temperature. The boatmen shook their
heads at these solutions. They had observed that persons who
knew least concerning a matter were often best provided with
explanations; and, although this discovery did not induce them to
distrust their own judgment, it rendered them properly suspicious of
the judgment of others.

Such was the condition of the controversy when Augustus Fuller
came to Geneva.

Fuller was one of those travelers who cannot look upon
a ruin without restoring it in imagination, nor hear a
strange noise without desiring to know what made it. He
was not a philosopher by profession, though he had read
philosophy; not an antiquary, though he respected whatever was old
enough to have gathered dust; and not wholly credulous, though
his reason, like a prudent householder, sometimes admitted a marvelous
guest for the pleasure of hearing him converse. He had
traveled much, written a little, and formed the commendable resolution
of believing nothing which could be disproved without difficulty.

The view from Geneva delighted him. Northward, the shores, softened
by cultivation, presented a succession of smiling pictures—orchards, dwellings, fields,
and vineyards descending toward the water as if prosperity herself
had chosen the lake for a mirror. Southward, the scene
assumed another character. The hills rose more sternly; cultivation became
less obtrusive; ravines opened darkly between wooded heights; and distance
conspired with shadow to invest the landscape with that sober
dignity which is not beauty merely, but one of the
elements that characterize the noble in nature.

Fuller admired the prospect for an hour, asked concerning the
best road to Watkins, the depth of the lake, the
quality of its trout, and finally, with an appearance of
indifference which deceived nobody, inquired whether the Wandering Jew had
lately been seen.

"You mean the old tree," replied the landlord.

"I mean whatever has been mistaken for one."

"That depends on who's telling it. Peter Cornelius saw him
last spring. Peter never calls him a tree."

"Where may Peter be found?"

"At the lower wharf—unless he's fishing, sleeping, or explaining the
lake to somebody."

Peter Cornelius was discovered in the stern of a flat-bottomed
boat, repairing a foresheet which seemed, from the number of
knots already applied to it, to have passed the period
when repair may properly be distinguished from reconstruction. He was
a small, dried man, with a face browned by weather,
gray eyes which had acquired the habit of looking over
water, and hands whose enormous joints appeared to have been
tied together by pieces of cord. His hat had once
belonged to a larger person, and descended upon his ears
whenever he inclined his head.

"You are acquainted with the floating tree?" asked Fuller.

Peter suspended his labor and surveyed the stranger.

"I've seed the crittur."

"Frequently?"

"Often enough to know him, and seldom enough not to
get familiar."

"Can you take me to him?"

"No man can take another man to that tree. I
can take you where he was, where he might be,
and where he'd ought to be if he had any
Christian regard for currents; but as for where he is,
that's for him to say."

Fuller smiled. "You regard him as having a will?"

"A will! Look here, stranger: a dead log goes where
wind and water send it. This one goes where he's
a mind to. I've knowed him to beat up the
lake ag'in a breeze that would've made a sloop shorten
sail. I've knowed him to lie under the eastern shore
for three days, with his bald head out, watching folks
cut wheat; and then, when a boat come nigh, down
he'd settle, shift his helm, and be off."

"What acts as his helm?"

"The part you don't see."

This answer, being equally applicable to politics, theology, and floating
timber, impressed Fuller as containing more wisdom than Peter intended.

After some bargaining, a cruise of indefinite duration was agreed
upon. Fuller supplied provisions; Peter supplied boat, sail, fishing tackle,
and knowledge; while the Wandering Jew was expected to supply
himself. They departed the following morning beneath a sky of
singular purity.

For two weeks they traversed the lake. They searched bays
where driftwood gathered in desultory councils; they examined black stumps
protruding from marshes; they pursued shadows, fence-rails, fishing buoys, and
once a swimming bear, which Peter, until it reached shore
and climbed a bank, maintained to be the tree "in
one of his contrary shapes." Every report received at a
landing sent them across the lake, and every crossing produced
a new report locating the object somewhere behind them.

At Dresden, a farmer had seen it at sunset.

"How far out?" asked Fuller.

"A mile, maybe."

"Did you approach it?"

"No. I was milking."

At Lodi, a boy affirmed that he had stood upon
it.

"How did it look?"

"Wet," replied the boy.

Near Peach Orchard Point, an elderly woman declared that the
tree had passed her house at midnight.

"How could you see it?" Fuller inquired.

"I didn't see it. I heard it."

"What sound did it make?"

"None at all; and that was how I knew. Every
honest boat rattles somewhere."

Peter received all testimony, however contradictory, with the liberality of
a mind already convinced. The object had been north and
south on the same evening? It had traveled rapidly. It
had been observed upon opposite shores at the same hour?
The witnesses' clocks disagreed. It had stood in shallow water
where no upright trunk of its reputed length could float?
It had drawn in its lower branches. That no explanation
disconcerted Peter arose less from stupidity than from a principle
common among mankind: facts are easily accommodated when belief has
engaged lodgings before their arrival.

On the ninth day Fuller heard the Lake Gun.

They were becalmed near the middle of the water. The
afternoon was warm, the sky without a cloud, and even
the gulls seemed unwilling to disturb the universal tranquillity. Suddenly
there came a concussion—not sharp like a rifle, nor prolonged
like thunder, but deep, round, and heavy, as though some
gigantic gun, planted beneath the lake, had discharged its ball
against the foundations of the hills. An echo sprang from
the western heights, another from the eastern, and a third,
fainter than the others, wandered southward and died.

Fuller started. Peter merely nodded.

"There," said he. "He's about."

"Who?"

"Who d'ye suppose? Old Bald-head. The Gun speaks when he
gets into mischief."

Fuller examined the sky, watched the water, and listened for
a repetition. None followed.

"Perhaps a blast in a quarry."

"No quarry that-a-way."

"A distant cannon."

"Fired under us, was it?"

"Sound is deceptive on water."

"So is a man that won't believe his own ears."

But although Peter searched with renewed energy, they found nothing;
and at the end of the fourteenth day Fuller abandoned
the expedition, not because curiosity had been satisfied, but because
provisions, patience, and clean linen had all been exhausted.

It was on the following afternoon, while walking upon a
high promontory south of the village, that he encountered the
person by whom the two wonders were eventually connected.

The stranger sat upon a ledge overlooking the lake. He
was young, scarcely more than five-and-twenty, tall, erect, and of
a gravity remarkable even in repose. His dress was that
of an educated gentleman, plain and carefully ordered; yet in
the firm outline of his features, the breadth of his
brow, and the dark, steady eye which seemed less to
receive than to examine whatever it beheld, Fuller recognized the
lineage of the ancient possessors of the country.

The young man rose when addressed, and returned Fuller's salutation
with courtesy.

"You are acquainted with this region?" Fuller asked.

"It was my father's country."

The emphasis was slight, but the words descended between them
with the weight of a history. Fuller looked from the
speaker to the lake, the farms, the white dwellings, and
the roads by which the tide of another people had
entered. He understood that country might belong to the living
by law, to the dead by memory, and to the
dispossessed by a title which neither surveys nor parchments can
extinguish.

"I have lately been seeking one of its oldest inhabitants,"
said Fuller, after a pause. "A tree called the Wandering
Jew."

"The pale-faces have given it a foreign name. My people
call it the Swimming Seneca."

"You have seen it?"

"When I was a child. My grandfather pointed to it
from yonder shore and told me what I shall never
forget."

"A tradition?"

"You would call it so."

"And you?"

"I have been taught to call many things traditions which
my fathers called truths."

There was no reproach in his voice, yet Fuller felt
the reproof which belongs to any civilization that demands of
another people not only their lands, but the surrender of
their memories. He seated himself upon the rock, and the
young Seneca resumed his place.

"Will you tell me the story?"

The Seneca looked long over the water. At last he
spoke.

"Many winters ago, when the villages of my nation stood
thick beside the lakes, and when the wisdom of the
aged was a wall around the rashness of the young,
there arose a chief named See-wise. His tongue was smooth,
his face was open, and he had learned that men
often reward most generously those who praise them for virtues
which they do not possess.

"When the old men said, 'The law is ancient,' See-wise
answered, 'The people are older than the law.' When the
counsellors said, 'Our fathers found wisdom through suffering,' he replied,
'The sons are wiser because they have not suffered.' When
the priests warned that restraint preserves a nation, he declared
that restraint was invented by the few to humble the
many.

"The idle loved him because he called idleness independence. The
ignorant loved him because he named ignorance uncorrupted judgment. The
violent loved him because he described passion as courage. Each
man heard his fault praised as a virtue; and therefore
all pronounced See-wise the truest friend of man."

Fuller turned his eyes toward the speaker. The young man's
mien had altered. He no longer appeared merely a relater
of an obsolete legend; his voice had acquired the measured
force of one accustomed to weigh public words, and his
glance, though directed upon the lake, seemed to survey a
wider congregation.

"Did he become chief?" Fuller asked.

"He became more than chief. He became the voice of
those who had voices but no counsel. Men repeated what
he said until repetition appeared proof; they shouted until noise
appeared unanimity; and they called every doubt treason, for the
obvious reason that a falsehood is safest when questioning it
is made a crime.

"At that time sacred laws governed the taking of fish.
The people might cast their nets in certain seasons, but
during the spawning moons the fish were protected, that the
lake might remain fruitful for children yet unborn. The law
had come from the fathers, and behind the fathers stood
the command of the Great Spirit.

"See-wise desired greater power. He therefore went among the young
fishermen and asked why dead men should govern living men.
He told the poor that the lake belonged especially to
them; he told the rich that larger catches would enlarge
their wealth; he told every village that other villages were
already violating the law, and that obedience was merely surrender
to cunning neighbors.

"Some answered, 'If all take fish in the forbidden season,
soon there will be none.'

"See-wise said, 'The lake is deep.'

"Others said, 'Our fathers knew its depth and yet established
the law.'

"He replied, 'Our fathers were timid.'

"The priests said, 'The Great Spirit commands.'

"He answered, 'The Great Spirit speaks through the people.'

"Thus he made appetite sacred by giving it a sacred
name. He did not tell men to be selfish; he
told them their selfishness was justice. He did not bid
them destroy the law; he declared that law had already
destroyed liberty. He did not confess his wish to rule;
he protested that he sought only to obey the popular
will, which, as often happens in such cases, was the
name he gave to his own desire after teaching others
to desire it."

The young Seneca paused. Far below, a hawk swept across
the cliff, diminished to a dark point above the shining
water, and vanished among the trees.

"What happened?" asked Fuller.

"The nets were cast before the appointed moon. At first
the catches were abundant, and See-wise laughed at the old
men. 'Behold,' he cried, 'how fear deceived our fathers! The
lake rewards freedom.' The people feasted. Songs were made in
his honor. Mothers named their sons for him; warriors compared
him with the founders of the nation; and those who
had secretly doubted his counsel became the loudest in praise,
because men often proclaim a public error with vehemence in
order to silence a private shame.

"But the young fish were destroyed with the old. Nets
grew empty. Villages accused one another. The people who had
broken the law at his persuasion demanded that See-wise repair
the evil by another speech.

"He blamed the priests. He blamed the weather. He blamed
hidden enemies. He blamed the fish for fleeing their natural
friends. Last of all, he blamed the people for not
following his counsel with sufficient boldness; for the demagogue, though
he obtains power by calling the multitude infallible, commonly retains
it by declaring that their failures proceed from disobedience to
himself.

"Then famine approached. Children cried in lodges where plenty had
dwelt. The aged, whose warnings had been mocked, divided their
small stores with those who mocked them; because wisdom, unlike
pride, does not refuse to save a fool merely because
it foresaw his danger.

"At last the people assembled beside the lake. They demanded
that See-wise restore the ancient law. He would not. He
ascended a stone, spread his arms, and told them they
were free, enlightened, and unconquerable. He said their suffering proved
the greatness of the struggle; their hunger proved the malignity
of their enemies; and their ruin, if they would but
understand it, was a triumph.

"Then the sky darkened."

At this instant a faint wind moved across the promontory.
The leaves turned their paler surfaces outward, and the lake,
which had lain smooth beneath the afternoon light, became mottled
with traveling shadows.

"The Great Spirit," continued the Seneca, "had heard enough. A
cloud descended until it rested upon the water. Out of
it came a voice—not loud, but heard by every man
within his own breast.

"'See-wise,' it said, 'you have praised the people that they
might praise you. You have called desire wisdom, law oppression,
and disobedience courage. You have consumed the inheritance of the
unborn to purchase the favor of the living. Therefore you
shall become what you have made your nation—a thing without
root, without harbor, and without rest.

"'For a thousand winters you shall swim in the lake
whose laws you despised. The storms shall drive you, the
ice shall imprison you, and the sun shall dry your
uncovered head; yet neither shore shall receive you. Whenever you
approach the fish, my Gun shall speak, that they may
flee from the destroyer. Men shall look upon your face
and know you; but you shall possess no tongue with
which to flatter them.'

"Then lightning struck the stone upon which See-wise stood. The
cloud rose. The people saw no chief, but upon the
lake there floated a black trunk, upright in the water,
bearing above the surface the likeness of a bald and
withered head.

"That was the Swimming Seneca."

The narrative ceased. Fuller, who had listened with interest not
wholly due to the supernatural machinery of the tale, remained
silent. The lake before him, touched by alternating light and
shadow, seemed at once a real body of water and
the ancient theatre of judgment. He was too well acquainted
with the habits of mankind not to perceive that See-wise
had descendants beyond the Seneca villages and disciples in communities
which had never heard his name.

"You speak," he said at length, "as though you believe
the tradition."

The young man's expression softened.

"I have learned the sciences of your schools. I know
that wood floats, that currents move beneath a surface apparently
still, that echoes deceive the ear, and that gases confined
under water or earth may break forth with noise. I
know also that a people may preserve wisdom beneath a
story when they cannot preserve it in a book. Whether
See-wise became a tree, or whether men saw a tree
and remembered See-wise, the warning remains."

Before Fuller could reply, there burst from the lake a
report more tremendous than that which he had heard while
sailing with Peter. The air appeared to strike the cliff;
the cliff returned the blow; and the reverberation went leaping
from hill to hill, until the southern valleys swallowed it.

Both men sprang to their feet.

"The Gun!" cried Fuller.

"The warning," said the Seneca.

At the same moment a voice arose from below.

"Halloo! Mr. Fuller! He's unmoored! He's a-goin' north with his
helm down!"

Peter's boat came into view around the base of the
promontory. The old boatman stood in the stern, one hand
upon the tiller and the other flourished above his head.
Beyond him, perhaps half a mile from shore, a dark
object rose from the disturbed water.

Fuller and his companion descended by a narrow path, entered
the boat, and within minutes were beating outward. The wind,
which had awakened with the report, came in irregular gusts.
Peter managed sail and tiller with an activity that proved
his joints more serviceable than their appearance promised.

"Seed him just afore the Gun," he said. "Knew he'd
show. Always does when a stranger quits looking. That's his
pride. Keep your eye sou'west of the bow."

The object disappeared in a hollow of the waves, reappeared,
and seemed larger. As they drew nearer, Fuller distinguished the
trunk of a gigantic tree, stripped of branches and blackened
by long immersion. It floated nearly upright, though with a
slight inclination, and moved in a direction which did not
precisely accord with either the wind or the visible set
of the water.

"There!" Peter exclaimed. "Tell me a common log carries sail
like that."

"I see no sail."

"Nor sheet, neither. That's the uncommonness of it."

"May there not be heavy roots below?"

"Roots! If he'd respected roots he wouldn't be here."

The young Seneca stood in the bow, silent and motionless.
To him the object was evidently more than a curiosity.
Education had taught him to question the literal faith of
his childhood, but education had not supplied a sentiment equal
to that which it removed; and now, confronted by the
visible image of an ancestral warning, he appeared suspended between
two worlds—too instructed to believe without resistance, too reverent to
disbelieve without pain.

They lowered the sail and approached under oars.

The projecting end of the trunk was of a pale,
weather-beaten gray. Time, water, frost, and blows from drifted ice
had carved it into irregular forms; but accidents, when contemplated
by an imagination already prepared, soon become artists. A broad
swelling resembled a brow; beneath it two dark knots occupied
the places of eyes. One side had been splintered into
what appeared a compressed and sneering mouth, while a long
strip of loosened wood, bending now to one side and
now to the other, gave to the whole countenance an
expression at once selfish, crafty, and irresolute.

No face could have been less noble. It seemed to
promise everything and determine nothing; to smile upon whatever stood
before it and betray whatever had passed behind; to solicit
applause, resent counsel, and consult, amid every public calamity, the
safety of itself. In one light it wore audacity, in
another fear. As the waves turned it, its features altered
from arrogance to complaint, from complaint to menace, and from
menace to that injured innocence which powerful offenders so readily
assume when required to answer for their deeds.

Peter removed his hat.

"There's his old wicked phiz," he whispered. "Hasn't altered since
I was a boy, only weather's took some politics out
of him."

Fuller leaned over the gunwale. Natural explanations presented themselves in
sufficient number. The submerged roots, if roots remained, might act
as ballast; unseen currents might account for the direction; resemblance
to a human head was plainly the sport of decay.
Yet explanations are not impressions, and a man may understand
each cause while still being moved by the whole. The
great trunk rose and fell; water streamed from its bald
summit; the false eyes stared toward the boat; and every
motion appeared purposeful.

The Seneca spoke.

"Look upon him. That is the face of one who
asked every man what he wished, and called the answer
wisdom. See how the mouth turns toward each shore, as
though promising both what can be granted to neither. See
how the head inclines before the wind, but the hidden
weight beneath opposes the open course above. He is all
submission in appearance and all appetite below."

Peter nodded gravely, though it is doubtful whether he comprehended
the distinction.

Fuller said, "Such faces may be found on land."

"They are found wherever men prefer praise to truth," replied
the Seneca. "See-wise is not alone. He floats in councils;
he swims through assemblies; he rises in every nation where
ambition discovers that the shortest road to mastery is to
assure the ignorant they have nothing to learn.

"First he flatters the multitude; then the multitude flatters him.
He tells them they are wise because they choose him;
they call him great because he tells them they are
wise. Thus each becomes the mirror of the other, and
neither perceives that the image is false.

"He teaches the young to despise the old, not because
the old are always right, but because inherited counsel stands
between appetite and indulgence. He laughs at sacred restraints, not
because every restraint is just, but because a law above
himself limits his power. He promises abundance without labor, liberty
without obedience, greatness without sacrifice, and virtue without self-denial.

"When consequences arrive, he does not repent. He searches for
an enemy. If the harvest fails, an enemy poisoned the
field; if the treasury is empty, an enemy concealed the
wealth; if brother rises against brother, an enemy taught them
hatred. Yet it was he who sowed suspicion, spent the
inheritance, and instructed passion to wear the garments of justice."

The wind increased. The trunk dipped, recovered itself, and slowly
revolved. For one extraordinary moment the splintered mouth seemed to
grin directly at Fuller.

"Easy, now!" cried Peter. "He's shiftin' his cutwater. Don't let
him foul us. I've knowed sich critturs to be spiteful
when talked about plain."

But the Seneca continued, and the solemn cadences of his
speech mingled with the slap of waves against the boat.

"Men ask why such leaders prosper. They prosper because truth
is slow and flattery immediate; because law promises distant safety
while appetite promises present pleasure; because wisdom speaks with a
calm voice and passion with a trumpet. They prosper as
the storm-cloud prospers when it shuts out the sun. For
an hour it possesses the whole sky. The hills darken
beneath it; the timid call it eternal; the foolish worship
its thunder.

"But the cloud did not create the heavens, and cannot
destroy them. It passes. The hills remain.

"This trunk has wandered through many lustrums. It has endured
tempests and ice; it has escaped rope, hook, and shore.
Yet its freedom is punishment. It goes everywhere and arrives
nowhere. The water that bears it is also its prison.
So it is with the man who casts off truth
that he may command events. Events carry him for a
season, and he mistakes movement for mastery; but the same
flood that exalts him bears him onward to judgment."

A gust struck the boat. Peter seized the sheet and
shouted to Fuller to fend off. The trunk, drawn by
some cross-current, swung suddenly toward them. Its submerged portion struck
the hull with a hollow thump. The boat heeled; water
came over the side; and for an instant the gray
head towered above the Seneca like a living antagonist.

Peter smote it with an oar.

"Keep your berth, you old heathen!"

Whether offended by the insult, persuaded by the blow, or
merely obedient to the current, the trunk inclined, sank until
only its uppermost point remained visible, and then disappeared altogether.

The three men watched the spot. Circles widened upon the
surface; a few bubbles rose; thereafter nothing remained but waves.

Peter looked beneath the seats, as if the object might
have entered the boat by stealth.

"He'll come up astern," he said.

It did not.

They circled the place, sounded with an oar, and waited.
The tree had vanished into the lake as completely as
though a door had opened beneath it. Peter declared this
proof of intelligence. Fuller thought it proof of great depth
and imperfect knowledge. The Seneca offered no explanation.

The wind subsided almost as rapidly as it had risen.
In the west, the descending sun broke through a line
of clouds and laid a broad path of light across
the water. The shadows withdrew from the hills; fields emerged,
then houses, then distant windows shining like small fires. What
had been stern became beneficent; what had appeared confused acquired
order beneath illumination.

They raised sail and turned toward shore.

Fuller sat thoughtful in the stern. He had set out
to explain a tree and a noise; he returned with
both phenomena materially unexplained, yet morally interpreted. This result would
not satisfy a society of natural philosophers; nevertheless, it has
often happened that men who fail to discover what a
thing is may learn, with profit, what it signifies.

"Your tradition," he said to the Seneca, "belongs not to
one nation."

"No warning belongs to one nation."

"Nor to one age."

"The name changes. The man remains."

"And will justice always overtake him?"

The young man looked toward the luminous west.

"Not always when the injured demand it; not always in
the manner the impatient prescribe. The wicked may flourish through
many seasons. Falsehood may sit in high places, surrounded by
those who repeat it for reward. A people may forget
its fathers, deride its laws, consume its inheritance, and call
the ruin progress.

"But there is an order which men do not establish
and cannot repeal. A lie may obscure truth; it cannot
alter it. A cloud may darken the sun; it cannot
extinguish it. The lake may bear See-wise proudly upon its
bosom; yet the lake is his prison, the Gun his
accusation, and every shore he approaches another shore from which
he must depart.

"Therefore we should not trust the cloud because it is
large, nor fear the darkness because it is present. We
should keep the counsel proved by suffering, the law made
sacred by justice, and the humility which remembers that no
generation is the first to be wise. Above all, when
the loud voice flatters and the multitude answers, we should
trust neither the voice nor the answer, but God, who
needs no applause and cannot be deceived."

Peter, who had listened with an expression of laborious attention,
nodded.

"That's what I've allers said," he observed, though he had
never said anything resembling it. "A bad tree'll float crooked,
put him in what water you may."

The remark destroyed the grandeur of the silence which might
otherwise have followed; but truth, like royalty traveling incognito, is
sometimes obliged to accept an undignified carriage.

They reached the landing after sunset. Peter secured the boat
with two ropes, explaining that one was enough for an
honest craft, but that no man could be too careful
after carrying the acquaintance of the Wandering Jew. The young

Fuller remained several days in the neighborhood. He questioned farmers,
fishermen, quarrymen, and men of science. He collected theories of
the Lake Gun until their number became more mysterious than
the sound itself. Concerning the tree, he learned that upright
floating trunks are not unknown; that roots, stones, or saturated
branches may preserve a perpendicular position; and that the varying
currents of a long, deep lake may produce motions apparently
contrary to the wind.

All this he accepted. It must be confessed, however, that
when he afterward related the adventure, his scientific explanations commonly
occupied the beginning of the narrative, while the face of
the trunk, the Seneca's warning, and the sudden voice of
the lake possessed the end. This arrangement was injurious to
philosophy, since listeners remember best what they hear last.

Years passed. New farms appeared upon the shores; villages enlarged;
steamboats disturbed the old routes of sail and canoe; and
a generation arose which considered the marvels of its fathers
evidence chiefly of their ignorance. Yet from time to time,
in some lonely bay or upon the open water after
a storm, fishermen reported an upright trunk moving with singular
independence. At other times the deep report was heard, the
hills answered, and children ran to doors while their elders
pretended not to be startled.

Some called the object the Wandering Jew; others, remembering an
older title, named it the Swimming Seneca. There were persons
who laughed at both names and saw nothing but timber.
They may have been right. There were others who heard
in the Lake Gun only air, rock, water, and echo.
They also may have been right.

But whether it was merely a tree which accident had
carved into the likeness of selfish ambition, or a guilty
chief whom divine power had condemned to a thousand winters
of unrest, it told the same story. It spoke of
the leader who rises by flattering the ignorant, who inflames
passion and calls it virtue, who discards inherited wisdom because
wisdom restrains him, and who consumes tomorrow's portion to purchase
today's applause.

And the Gun, whether fired by the Great Spirit beneath
the waters or by those hidden forces through which the
laws of nature execute the will of their Author, repeated
the same admonition: that noise is not truth, that popularity
is not innocence, that motion is not freedom, and that
no man, however loudly praised, can forever escape the order
he has violated.

The clouds may gather; the hills may darken; the false
pilot may seize the helm and promise a harbor where
no harbor exists. For a season the frightened may submit,
the foolish applaud, and the wise appear forsaken. Yet clouds
exhaust themselves, darkness passes, and the sun, which seemed conquered,
proves only to have been concealed.

So the old lake keeps its legend. Beneath morning brightness
and evening shadow, beneath summer sails and winter ice, it
holds the wandering image of See-wise; and whenever men become
too confident that flattery can repeal justice, or that a
multitude may make falsehood true by shouting it together, the
deep water lifts its voice, the steadfast hills return the
warning, and the sound rolls onward—solemn, invisible, and unanswerable—until it
dies among the habitations of men.

### The Campaign Against "Have"

I had been in Florence three weeks when I established,
beyond peradventure, that the Italian language is constructed incorrectly.

This discovery cost me considerable labor. At first I suspected
the nouns. They conduct themselves suspiciously, changing their coats according
to sex and number, though a table has no more
business being masculine than a thunderstorm has being an aunt.
Yet nouns are stationary property. Once fenced, labeled, and entered
in the tax-book, they may be trusted not to escape.
Adjectives are worse, because they agree with everybody; but this
is weakness of character rather than active crime. Prepositions are
small and can be crushed.

The Verb is the true disturber of the peace.

A Verb will present itself in the morning as one
thing, dine under another name, and by nightfall deny the
acquaintance of its own relatives. It has moods, like a
poet; persons, like a census; numbers, like a lottery; and
tenses enough to stock a watchmaker. You no sooner recognize
it by its termination—which is the tail provided by Nature
for identifying the animal—than it drops that termination, puts on
whiskers, and enters by the kitchen.

I therefore resolved to master the Italian Verb.

I do not use "master" figuratively. I meant to take
one alive, examine its mechanism, break its temper, teach it
discipline, and thereafter employ it against the others. When a
frontier is infested by brigands, the right and wise course
is not to memorize each brigand separately. It is to
capture one, turn him State's evidence, furnish him a uniform,
and send him ahead with a lantern.

My servant, Luigi, was the man for the enterprise. He
had been successively a groom, a horse doctor, a waiter,
a scene-shifter, a corporal, and assistant to a man who
restored cracked saints. He could shave a mule, bleed a
tenor, mend a fresco, or arrange a procession. His present
title was facchino, which means, as nearly as I can
determine, a person who does everything that the person paying
him declines to do.

"Luigi," I said, "bring me a Verb."

He looked doubtful.

"Regular or irregular, signore?"

"Regular, certainly. I have no dealings with irregulars. They are
pathetic outcasts who have abandoned their proper tails and ought
not to be encouraged."

He brought a grammar and proposed amare, "to love."

At first the choice pleased me. Love has always enjoyed
a sound commercial reputation, and I supposed that any civilized
language would make its facilities simple. But when Luigi opened
the schedules, amare spread over four pages. It loved in
the present, loved in the past, had loved before it
loved, would love after it had ceased loving, might have
loved under favorable weather, and appeared capable of loving conditionally,
imperatively, and without the knowledge of either party.

"How many forms?"

"One hundred and ten, perhaps, including—"

"Take it away. A man who requires one hundred and
ten arrangements for loving is not affectionate; he is merely
methodical."

Luigi suggested avere, "to have."

"That is the ticket," I said. "Having is plain. Either
you have a thing or you have not. There can
be no sentimental embroidery about possession. How many forms?"

He counted.

"Fifty-seven."

I was staggered, but I concealed it. A commander must
never let a subordinate see him stagger, unless the subordinate
has put obstacles on the stairs.

"Fifty-seven is excessive," I said, "but not fatal. A Gatling
gun has more pieces and is fatal at a mile
and a half. We will organize these forms into a
company. Put each tense in uniform, divide the persons into
squads, and drill them until I can tell them by
their gait."

Luigi's face brightened. He had not been a corporal for
nothing.

We hired the Rope-Walk, a long, bare hall behind an
abandoned warehouse, and recruited the company from the neighborhood. The
landlord furnished six peasants, his wife supplied three laundresses, Luigi
obtained a baker, two stable-boys, a deaf gardener, and a
young fellow who sold lottery tickets. I engaged them by
the hour, which was a mistake, as will appear.

The uniforms were magnificent. The Present wore blue jackets with
brass buttons; the Past Definite, red shirts and black sashes;
the Imperfect, green coats of a melancholy cut; the Future,
silver paper helmets, because the future should always shine until
reached. The Conditional wore gray, being uncertain whether it had
enlisted. The Subjunctive was given purple plumes and masks, on
the ground that nobody knows precisely what it means, though
educated persons use it at dinner.

Each recruit carried a placard. On one side stood the
Italian form; on the other, its alleged English equivalent. Luigi
arranged them by rank: First Person in front, Second Person
behind, Third Person last. Plurals marched abreast, which made them
easy to recognize and dangerous in narrow passages.

At ten o'clock I took my position on a packing-case.
Luigi stood below with a whistle and an umbrella, the
umbrella representing authority.

"Bring on the Present!"

He whistled. The blue squad entered at quickstep and halted
before me.

"Io ho!" cried the first peasant.

"I have!" translated Luigi.

"Tu hai!" cried the second.

"Thou hast!"

"Stop," I said. "Who is Thou?"

"It is the second person singular."

"I have never met him."

"It means you."

"Then let it say you. We are conducting a grammar,
not a séance."

Luigi made a note.

"Egli ha!" shouted the lottery youth.

"He has!"

"No objection."

"Noi abbiamo!"

"We have!"

"Voi avete!"

"You have!"

"Essi hanno!"

"They have!"

The squad wheeled and marched past. It was a handsome
spectacle, though somewhat barren. They had, certainly; but what had
they? A man who says "I have" and declines to
exhibit the property creates uneasiness. He might have money, measles,
or a concealed weapon. Grammar should develop confidence, not undermine
it.

"They require an object," I said.

Luigi agreed and sent a stable-boy for one. The boy
returned with a yellow dog whose social standing was uncertain.

We formed the squad again.

"Io ho il cane!" said the first man. I have
the dog.

"Tu hai il cane!" You have the dog.

"He has the dog! We have the dog! You have
the dog! They have the dog!"

The animal passed from hand to hand with increasing discouragement.
The recruits recited without spirit. Nobody wanted the dog, and
the dog plainly did not want the conjugation. At "they
have the dog," he escaped, overturned the Future, and retired
beneath the Conditional, where he bit a supposition in the
ankle.

"This object is grammatically sound but dramatically weak," I said.
"We must choose something which excites ownership."

At that instant six chickens wandered in through the open
door. They belonged to a widow next door and were
unattended.

The peasants changed at once. Their eyes sharpened; their backs
straightened. Here was property with feathers on it, property which
could be boiled, roasted, sold, hidden under a coat, or
denied before a magistrate.

"Excellent," I said. "Pipe the Past Definite to the assault!"

The red-shirted squad charged in.

"Io ebbi le galline!" roared the first peasant, seizing a
hen. I had the chickens!

"Tu avesti le galline!" cried the second, taking two.

"Egli ebbe le galline!" shouted the third, pursuing a rooster
into the Present.

The plurals advanced with terrible enthusiasm.

"Noi avemmo le galline!"

"Voi aveste le galline!"

"Essi ebbero le galline!"

At this final declaration every soldier fell upon the poultry.
Feathers filled the air. The widow appeared at a window
and contributed a prolonged passage which Luigi said was not
in the grammar, though it possessed every indication of the
Imperative. The chickens fled in six directions, hotly conjugated. One
entered a barrel; another flew upon my packing-case and accused
me personally.

I was moved. For the first time the Verb lived.
It had blood in its cheeks and poultry in its
hands. I understood the Past Definite perfectly. It meant that
at a certain, decisive point in history somebody had the
chickens, and the former owner had better consult a lawyer.

"Magnificent!" I cried. "Again!"

But the widow came down with a broom, and diplomacy
required an interval. Luigi paid her two lire from funds
I had set aside for educational casualties. Then he marshaled
the next squad.

They wore green and entered slowly, with the languid tread
of men who had been ill for years and expected
the Government to inquire into it.

"What are these?" I demanded.

"The Imperfect."

"Imperfect in what respect?"

"In time."

"Then repair them before parade."

"It is only the name, signore. This tense expresses an
action prolonged or customary in the past, or one whose
beginning and ending are not precisely fixed."

"Let us see it."

The first green soldier advanced.

"Io avevo."

"I had," said Luigi.

I looked at the red squad. "But that man already
said he had."

"Yes, signore, but he said io ebbi."

"Which means?"

"I had."

"And this green bankrupt says io avevo."

"Yes."

"Which means?"

"I had."

I descended from the packing-case. Matters of principle should be
discussed on the ground, where a man can pace.

"Luigi, I will state the case fairly. Red says he
had. Green says he had. Both refer to past ownership.
Both claim the same English translation. Yet they draw separate
uniforms, separate wages, separate quarters, and probably separate pensions. Is
this grammar, or is it political patronage?"

"They are different."

"They are dressed differently. That is established."

"The first—ebbi—is used for an action completed at a definite
time."

"I had the chickens yesterday at four o'clock?"

"Precisely."

"And avevo?"

"You had them for some time, or used to have
them, or had them when something else happened."

"I see. A sickly, lingering kind of Had; a Had
that sits by the window watching the barometer; a Had
without a fixed address."

Luigi considered this unjust.

"For example," he said, "Quando ero giovane, avevo un cavallo—when
I was young, I had a horse."

"How young?"

"That is not specified."

"What horse?"

"It does not matter."

"How long did you have him?"

"It is indefinite."

"Then the statement is gossip and should not be admitted.
A language ought not to furnish special machinery for persons
who cannot remember their dates."

The green squad stood uneasily. One soldier concealed a yawn
behind his placard.

Luigi persisted. "Suppose you say: I had a horse when
the war began. The having continued; the war interrupted—"

"Why should a war interrupt my horse? Wars have enough
to answer for."

"It interrupts the sentence."

"Then discipline the sentence."

He tried again. "While I had the horse, it rained."

"That is worse. If it rained, the horse and I
both knew it. No Imperfect was needed."

"But the distinction exists."

That phrase inflamed me. Whenever a useless institution is cornered,
it says it exists. Mosquitoes exist; unpaid bills exist; distant
cousins exist. Existence is not a recommendation.

I mounted the packing-case once more.

"Soldiers of the Verb!" I said. "We are confronted by
duplication. Two Hads occupy the same territory. One is vigorous,
definite, and wears red. The other is prolonged, vague, green,
and expensive. The Italian people, patient under many burdens, are
taxed to support both. Children must learn both; printers must
print both; travelers must stumble over both; schoolmasters must explain
the difference and, failing that, give additional examples. This is
not language. It is a Had Trust."

Luigi raised the umbrella. "But, signore—"

"I don't want any stock in a Had Trust. Abolish
the Imperfect."

The green soldiers murmured. They had expected another hour's employment.

Luigi said, "You cannot abolish a tense."

"Why not?"

"It has always been used."

"So had the rack."

"Everyone would misunderstand you."

"Then everyone must be corrected. Reform has always begun with
one man and a packing-case."

I ordered the green squad to surrender its placards. The
first man refused on the ground that he had been
paid to say avevo and had not yet said it
often enough. The second insisted that he used to have
the placard, had it now, and intended to continue having
it, thus demonstrating the very abuse under investigation. The third
declared that if his employment ended before noon, custom required
payment until noon.

"Observe," I said to Luigi. "The Imperfect has already become
a labor question."

He whispered with them. They whispered back. The widow reappeared
at the door, now allied with the dog. The Past
Definite had secured three chickens and denied all knowledge of
feathers. The Present wanted to know whether it presently had
wages. The Future asked if it would have them. The
Conditional said it would have them if the lesson continued.
The Subjunctive, waiting in purple masks at the far end
of the hall, looked capable of having anything under doubtful
circumstances.

"Silence!" I shouted. "Front—face! We proceed to the Future. Luigi,
pipe the next squad to the assault!"

He lifted the whistle.

At that moment the noon gun boomed over Florence.

Every soldier dropped his placard.

Then the church bells began—one bell, then ten, then a
whole bronze parliament declaring the hour with overlapping authority. The
baker removed his helmet. The laundresses untied their sashes. The
peasants released the surviving poultry and moved toward the door
with that instantaneous unanimity found only among persons whose wages
continue during dinner.

"Halt!" I cried.

Nobody halted.

"Luigi! Recall them!"

"It is noon, signore."

"What has noon to do with grammar?"

"They work only until noon."

"But the lesson is not completed."

"The labor agreement is completed."

I pointed to the silver squad escaping through the door.
"The Future has not even occurred!"

"It will occur tomorrow."

"That is an evasion."

"It is also the Future."

Within half a minute the Rope-Walk was empty except for
Luigi, myself, the yellow dog, two disputed feathers, and a
purple plume left behind by the Subjunctive—which, appropriately, might or
might not have been there.

I surveyed the field. It could not be called a
defeat. We had captured "to have," exposed its excessive armament,
animated the Past with chickens, and abolished one entire tense
by executive order. True, the Italian language had not yet
heard of the abolition; but this is a common delay
in reform. A principle must first be established, then announced,
then resisted, then misunderstood, and finally adopted under another man's
name.

"Tomorrow," I told Luigi, "we will reduce the remaining forms
to twelve."

He shook his head.

"Eight, then."

He continued shaking it.

"Very well—six. One for each person. No moods, no disguises,
no green Hads drawing salaries for not knowing when they
happened."

"And the irregular verbs?"

"We will deport them."

"To where?"

"France," I said. "They are accustomed to complications there."

Luigi picked up the abandoned placards.

As we left, the church bells were still talking all
at once, repeating the same news in a dozen sizes
and tones. I remarked that this proved my argument: Italy
tolerated needless forms even in the announcement of noon.

Luigi replied that each bell belonged to a different church.

"That," I said, "is precisely the trouble."

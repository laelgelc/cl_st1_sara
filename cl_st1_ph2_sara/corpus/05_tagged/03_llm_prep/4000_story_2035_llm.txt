I was twenty-three when I first met Mrs. Clifton Courtenay,
though I generally gave myself the intellectual age of forty.
In justice to my arithmetic, I had published a volume
of impressions entitled *Grey Hours*, and one of the smaller
reviews had called it "not wholly negligible." At twenty-three this
is equivalent to immortality.

Mrs. Courtenay sat beside me at Lady Merton's dinner. She
was a fair woman, beautifully arranged, and of that uncertain
age which tact places at thirty and experience, with equal
confidence, at forty-two. Her eyes were blue, candid, and slightly
fatigued, as though they had looked upon every pleasure and
found the management of them exhausting.

"You are not," she said, before the fish had completed
its journey round the table, "the Mr. Elwood who wrote
*Grey Hours*?"

I admitted it with the modesty of a man waiting
to be contradicted.

"I knew it," she murmured. "One feels these things."

No one had ever felt me before. Editors had occasionally
expressed an opinion about me, but that was different.

"There is a passage," she continued, "about the rain on
empty streets. It made me cry. Clifton asked what was
the matter, and of course I could not tell him.
Men like Clifton do not understand tears that have no
practical cause."

Across the table Mr. Clifton Courtenay was explaining to a
duchess how he had reduced the cost of his stables.
He was a handsome, well-fed man, and I instantly perceived
that his soul was hollow.

"It was written in great loneliness," I said.

"I knew that too."

Her voice crept round one like a warm caress. I
forgot that the passage concerning rain had originally been introduced
because I could think of no transition between a page
about ambition and another about death.

"How wonderful," she said, "to be able to utter what
other people can only suffer!"

I forgave her the word *wonderful*. Indeed, I thought it
exact.

After dinner she drew me toward a little conservatory where
two palms, a marble nymph, and several draughts struggled for
existence. Music floated to us from the drawing-room.

"Do you know," she said, resting her fingers for one
instant upon my sleeve, "I am very tired of all
this?"

Her gesture included Lady Merton, the music, the duchess, the
stables, and probably civilisation.

"People think me frivolous," she went on. "Perhaps I seem
so. One must wear a mask in Society. If I
showed my real self, they would either laugh or be
frightened."

I was quite certain that I should do neither.

"There are moments," she said, looking into the middle distance,
"when a woman needs one sincere friend—someone clever, but kind;
someone who has suffered enough to understand."

I had suffered chiefly from publishers, but suffering, like port,
improves in the telling.

"You may trust me," I said.

She turned her eyes upon me. For a second I
contemplated kneeling at her feet. The floor was damp from
the palms, however, and I decided that devotion might be
equally well expressed standing up. Had she commanded it, I
believe I should have stood upon my head; youth does
not object to appearing ridiculous, provided it may do so
romantically.

"I knew I might," she whispered.

At that moment Mr. Courtenay entered.

"My dear," he said, "the carriage is here."

The weary soul vanished; the social smile appeared as neatly
as a light turned on behind blue glass.

"Already? How tiresome! Mr. Elwood, you must come to me
on Thursday. I collect a few people—only those who matter."

She went away upon the arm of the hollow Society
man, discussing whether they should sup at Ranelagh.

For three days I lived in a condition of spiritual
engagement. I composed letters to her and destroyed them because
they were inadequate. I composed poems to her and preserved
them for the same reason. I pictured our friendship moving,
grave and stainless, above the chatter of London. She had
beauty, sorrow, and a misunderstood nature; I had genius and
Thursday afternoon.

On Wednesday I lunched with Gerald Vane, a young novelist
whose first book had been praised rather more warmly than
mine, a circumstance which had not impaired our friendship, though
it had made it watchful.

"You know Mrs. Clifton Courtenay?" he asked.

I endeavoured not to start.

"Slightly."

"Extraordinary woman."

"In what way?"

"She understands. That is so rare in a woman who
lives as she does."

I drank some water.

"I met her at the Baxters'. She had read *The
Red Orchard* twice."

"Twice?"

"She remembers whole passages. She said the chapter about the
deserted child made her cry. Her husband asked what was
the matter, but naturally she could not tell him. He
is apparently an excellent fellow, but utterly material."

"Did she speak of masks?" I inquired.

Gerald looked surprised.

"She did. Society forces her to wear one. Beneath it
she has a passionate, almost terrible sincerity. She said that
sometimes a woman needs one true friend—someone strong enough to
understand and gentle enough not to judge."

"Did she touch your sleeve?"

"My hand," said Gerald, with pardonable superiority.

I began to dislike him.

"She is giving a little reception to-morrow," he added. "Only
the people who matter."

Mrs. Courtenay's little receptions provided seats for eight and invitations
for eighty. The people who mattered stood upon one another's
toes and shouted intimate observations through a din composed of
politicians, painters, minor royalties, major tenors, and ladies who had
discovered new religions during the previous week.

She received me warmly.

"My dear Mr. Elwood! How good of you. I have
been thinking of your rain ever since."

Before I could reply, a celebrated actor claimed her, and
she gave him the look of one who has waited
through several incarnations for precisely that handshake.

Later she found me behind a Japanese screen, where I
had taken refuge with a sandwich.

"Tell me," she said quickly, "you know Gerald Vane?"

"He is my friend."

"Is he really clever? I have read so little. There
is never time for anything real."

I mentioned *The Red Orchard*.

"What is it like?"

"It contains one admirable sentence," I said, for generosity is
easiest when confined to a sentence. "'A woman's tenderness is
the one lifebelt Heaven throws to a drowning man.'"

She closed her eyes.

"How beautiful! Say it again."

I repeated it.

"A woman's tenderness," she whispered, "is the one lifebelt Heaven
throws to a drowning man. Ah! Only a man who
had nearly drowned could have written that."

Then an attaché approached, and she asked him whether diplomacy
did not sometimes make him feel horribly false.

A week later Gerald came to my rooms in triumph.

"She understands the book better than I do myself," he
announced. "She has treasured one sentence for months. She repeated
it word for word."

I did not ask which sentence. There are truths one
prefers to meet gradually, like elderly relations and winter.

After that I observed Mrs. Courtenay with less emotion and
more profit. She never altered her method, because perfection has
no need of novelty. What varied was the vocabulary.

The Reverend Septimus Bale, an earnest clergyman whose parish consisted
largely of wealthy widows, told me that he had found
her upon the threshold of a profound spiritual change.

"Worldliness has never touched her heart," he said. "Beneath the
glitter there is a simple Protestant soul crying out for
guidance. I spoke to her quite plainly about amusement, ritual,
and the moral danger of Sunday concerts. She grasped my
hand and said, 'Why has no one told me this
before?' I believe that, with patience, we shall see her
taking an active interest in the Girls' Friendly Society."

Three days afterward I encountered the Abbé Morel, who smiled
when I mentioned her name.

"A noble nature," he said, "but starved by the grey
northern creed in which she has been imprisoned. She said
to me, 'I want a faith that is not afraid
of beauty.' Her eyes filled with tears when I spoke
of the ancient Church as a mother opening her arms
to a tired child. She is nearer Rome than she
knows."

Mr. Partridge, the Theosophist, knew better than either of them.

"Creeds are shells," he explained at dinner, refusing the mutton
because it retarded vibration. "Mrs. Courtenay has passed beyond sectarian
forms. I recognised at once that she possesses an unusually
old soul. When I described the Seven Ascending Planes, she
became silent and said, 'All my life I have felt
that truth was somewhere above me.' I shall take her
to our lecture on Astral Wisdom. She has promised to
come, unless she is dining with the Bishop."

Colonel Branksome claimed her for the Empire.

"No nonsense about that woman," he said. "Damme, sir, she
sees what the country needs. We had a long talk
after supper, and I told her plainly that universal military
service is the only remedy for national softness. 'Colonel,' she
said, 'you have made me ashamed of my easy life.'
Spirit! That's what she has. Told me she longs to
do something for England. We may get her to preside
over the Ladies' League for Preparedness."

Yet Jocelyn Pryde, the radical reformer, described the same easy
life as a cage forged by privilege.

"She is awakening," he said, tossing back the hair which
he considered a protest against property. "You see merely a
fashionable hostess; I have heard the imprisoned woman beneath. She
confessed that every jewel she wears seems to her a
drop of an artizan's blood. I spoke of emancipation, of
the breaking of class barriers, of the coming dawn when
no woman shall be owned by husband, Church, or State.
She whispered, 'You have given words to my secret rebellion.'"

That evening Mrs. Courtenay wore all her jewels at the
opera, accompanied by her husband, the Bishop, Colonel Branksome, and
a foreign prince whose views upon emancipation were believed to
be reserved.

In time I ceased trying to discover which of these
gentlemen had penetrated to her real self. Perhaps they all
had; a house with many visitors requires many rooms. Or
perhaps Mrs. Courtenay's real self was merely the gracious mirror
in which every man beheld his favourite convictions wearing a
beautiful expression.

Bale said she was fundamentally Protestant; the Abbé that she
was inevitably Catholic; Partridge that she had outgrown Christianity; Branksome
that she would save the Empire; and Jocelyn that she
intended to abolish it.

But they all agreed that she was a charming woman.

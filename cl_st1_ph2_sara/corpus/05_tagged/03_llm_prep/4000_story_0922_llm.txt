### The Honor of the Saplings

The smoke which hung above the ruined mission had scarcely
ceased to ascend in a straight and slender column—for the
morning was still, and the leaves of the forest had
not yet begun their customary whispering—when the last prayer of
Father Ignatius was interrupted by the stroke of a hatchet.

He fell beside the rough cross which his own hands
had raised. Whether the old man was dead before his
knees touched the earth, or whether some remnant of consciousness
permitted him to complete, in silence, the petition which had
been commenced aloud, none who witnessed the event could afterward
declare. The warriors, having satisfied a vengeance which they considered
public rather than personal, bestowed little further attention upon him.
There remained another captive, and there were, beyond the ridge,
seven settlers, three women, and two children whose fate required
immediate consultation.

Corporal Gideon Brace stood at the foot of an oak,
his elbows drawn behind its trunk and secured with strips
of green hide. He had seen men killed in the
open field, in blockhouses, in ravines, and once, at Monongahela,
in water so shallow that the dead seemed to kneel
beneath its surface; yet the quiet despatching of the missionary
impressed him more painfully than many a noisier spectacle. The
circumstance was not owing, as the reader might charitably suppose,
to any especial tenderness in Brace's disposition. He was a
hard veteran, shaped by thirty years of service into something
which resembled the lock of his own musket—dark, battered, and
useful chiefly because repeated blows had made it insensible. But
the priest had spoken to him kindly during the night;
and kindness, when offered to an old soldier without design,
is sometimes more dangerous to his composure than menace.

The Weasel wiped his hatchet upon the dead man's robe
and turned.

He was not the principal chief of the band, though
his temper frequently led strangers to mistake noise for authority.
The true direction of the party rested with Red Oak,
an elderly war leader whose countenance, though marked by paint,
wounds, and the habitual severity of command, possessed a gravity
not inconsistent with reflection. Near him stood Little Crane, younger
by twenty years, celebrated for fleetness and for an eloquence
which, when interpreted into English, lost much of its imagery
but none of its intention. The Weasel was feared for
suddenness. He loved the instant in which anger becomes action,
and he resented every pause imposed between desire and blood.

"Now the coat-soldier," he said.

Brace regarded the speaker from beneath shaggy brows.

"Ay," he answered, "now the coat-soldier. Ye've been long enough
comin' to the rear rank. Step up, Mister Weasel, an'
take yer ration."

The Weasel understood enough English to apprehend the contempt, though
perhaps not the military figure. He advanced; but Red Oak
extended one arm, and the younger man stopped.

"Brother," said the chief to Brace, "the praying man is
gone. He did not cry out."

"He'd no cause," replied the corporal. "Ye struck him from
behind."

"He knew the hatchet was lifted."

"Then he know'd more than you'll know when yours comes."

Several warriors uttered a low exclamation, not altogether of anger.
Defiance in a helpless enemy has seldom failed to produce
a certain admiration among peoples whose safety depends upon fortitude;
and though cruelty may pervert that admiration into a desire
to test what it praises, the sentiment itself is not
counterfeit.

Red Oak continued:

"Brother, you have eaten our meat. You have slept in
our camp. You have heard our council. Soon you will
travel where the trail is hidden."

"Untie me," said Brace, "an' I'll travel it through the
middle of ye."

The Weasel sprang forward again. In his eagerness to strike
he committed the mistake against which all prudent combatants, whether
in forest or field, should be guarded—he approached too closely
an enemy whose powers had not been effectually ascertained. The
hide thongs about Brace's wrists had been cut nearly through
during the night against a projection of bark. He had
labored patiently, despite cramped fingers and bleeding skin, concealing each
movement whenever the firelight strengthened or a watcher turned. Until
that moment he had not known whether one violent effort
would free him; but despair has often furnished the last
ounce of strength denied to calculation.

He drew his arms apart.

The weakened thong snapped. At the same instant he stooped
beneath The Weasel's blow, seized the haft of the descending
weapon with his left hand, and drove his shoulder against
the warrior's breast. The two men turned half around in
a struggle so brief that those nearest had not time
to interfere. Brace wrenched the tomahawk free and buried it
above The Weasel's ear.

The chief fell without a sound.

The corporal did not pause to recover the weapon. He
tore it loose, struck backward at the first man who
reached him, and opened that warrior's forearm from wrist to
elbow. A second received the handle in his teeth; a
third, more cautious, caught at Brace's hunting-shirt and was rewarded
with a cut across the shoulder. The prisoner then made
for the rifles, which had been stacked near the council
fire.

He covered perhaps six yards.

Little Crane flung himself upon Brace's knees; Red Oak seized
the lifted arm; and four others, attacking from the flanks
and rear as men accustomed to the capture of desperate
animals, bore him to the ground. Even there the corporal
continued the contest. He broke one man's finger between his
teeth, kicked another in the belly, and did not relinquish
the hatchet until Red Oak struck his wrist with a
club and deprived the hand, for several minutes, of all
sensation.

When at length Brace lay bound, panting, and bleeding from
a spear-scratch along the ribs, The Weasel was dead, three
warriors were disabled, and the intended attack upon the settlers
had been delayed.

It might be imagined that these results would have insured
the captive's immediate destruction. They did not. The same exploit
which demanded signal vengeance rendered an ordinary death unworthy. The
Weasel had been a chief, and something more than a
blow was required to answer for him; while the courage
of the prisoner, having been publicly manifested, imposed upon his
captors—according to their stern conception of honor—the obligation of providing
an ordeal equal to it.

Red Oak ordered that Brace be tied more securely. This
was done with no unnecessary violence, although the precaution was
now so elaborate that a regiment could scarcely have released
him without tools. The chief then summoned the principal men
to council.

The deliberation lasted nearly an hour.

During that period the sun climbed above the pines and
dissolved the smoke over the mission. Flies gathered about the
dead. From the valley came the faint, regular knocking of
an axe, showing that the settlers beyond the ridge still
pursued their labor in ignorance of the danger which had
halted so near them. Brace heard the sound and counted
the strokes, as if they formed the beat of a
distant drum. He knew that every minute gained might be
of service to those people; but he knew, also, that
the price of the delay would be collected from himself.

He had endured flogging, fever, hunger, a broken thigh, and
once the extraction of a musket-ball by a surgeon who
fortified himself with rum but offered none to his patient.
Yet he had never been tortured. His imagination, therefore, enjoyed
that dreadful freedom which ignorance bestows. It presented fire, knives,
splinters, heated iron, and mutilations too confused to be named.
The sweat which stood upon his brow was not produced
by the morning heat.

Outwardly, however, he maintained the expression of a man inconvenienced
by foolish ceremony.

When Little Crane approached at the end of the council,
Brace spat a blade of grass from his lips.

"Well?" he demanded. "Have ye settled which brave gentleman gets
my boots?"

Little Crane seated himself before the captive.

"Brother," he said, "you have killed The Weasel."

"'Twas the first wise thing done with his hatchet."

"The Weasel was quick in council and strong on the
trail."

"He was slow enough when I got him."

"He has brothers."

"So have I, maybe. Haven't counted 'em these twenty years."

Little Crane allowed this irreverence to pass. His office was
not merely to communicate the judgment but to enlarge it,
so that its justice, dignity, and inevitable character might be
understood by prisoner and witnesses alike.

"Brother, you are brave. A rabbit may be killed with
a stick. A wolf is met with a spear. The
little bird falls and is forgotten; the eagle is carried
home, and men speak of its wings. We will not
make you a rabbit."

"Much obleeged."

"You shall have the death of a strong man."

"Give me my musket, then, an' stand forty paces off."

"No. A musket kills too quickly."

Here Little Crane smiled, not because he found pleasure in
the corporal's fear—for he could not yet be certain that
fear existed—but because the promised distinction contained that grim contradiction
by which men in every age have decorated what humanity
would be ashamed to view plainly.

"We will bend two young trees," he continued. "One hand
shall be tied to one tree, and one hand to
the other. When the trees rise, one part of our
brother will travel toward the sunrise, and one part toward
the sunset. The Weasel will see. He will know that
a brave enemy followed him."

Brace's face did not alter; but the back of his
neck became cold.

It may be necessary to explain that the punishment thus
described, though less laborious than many inventions of cruelty, possessed
an infernal ingenuity. Two supple saplings, growing at a suitable
distance from each other, were bent inward until their tops
nearly met and were held in that unnatural position by
ropes fastened to a central stake. The victim's limbs were
then attached to the opposing trunks. Upon the cutting of
the retaining cords the trees resumed, with tremendous violence, the
upright posture from which they had been forced. Nature herself
appeared to become executioner; and the very strength by which
living wood seeks light and heaven was converted into an
agency of destruction.

Whether this usage was as common as frontier tradition later
asserted may reasonably be doubted. Terror enlarges whatever it repeats,
and many a solitary outrage, passing from cabin to cabin,
has become in recollection the settled practice of a whole
people. But no qualification of history can lessen the horror
of a particular act to the person selected for its
subject.

Brace had seen saplings bent to lift traps, drag logs,
and hoist game beyond the reach of wolves. He understood
the mechanism at once.

"Is that all?" he asked.

Little Crane's eyes narrowed. "Does my brother wish more?"

"I was thinkin' ye'd found some new trick. I've seen
two companies of recruits pulled apart worse than that over
one keg o' rum."

A murmur ran through the circle. Red Oak raised his
hand, and it ceased.

The corporal had now formed his plan. It was a
simple one, requiring no weapon, no freedom, and no hope.
If he could anger one of the warriors beyond restraint,
a hatchet or knife might end what the saplings would
otherwise prolong. The Weasel would have obliged him; but The
Weasel, unfortunately, had already been rendered incapable of further usefulness.

Brace looked from face to face and selected a broad
warrior whose cheek bore a black stripe and whose right
arm was wrapped where the tomahawk had cut it.

"Here, Split-Arm," he called. "D'ye understand me?"

The wounded man stepped forward.

"I understand."

"Then understand this: I've seen better fighters run from half
as many men. At Fallen Timbers ye flew so fast
the wind couldn't keep yer feathers straight."

The warrior's left hand moved toward his knife.

Now the corporal's historical assertion, like many boasts produced under
circumstances not favorable to accuracy, was too broad. Native forces
had suffered defeat at Fallen Timbers; but they had not
lacked courage, nor had all fled in disorder. Military history,
when recited by veterans, commonly resembles a coat altered to
fit the wearer: seams are shifted, stains covered, and inconvenient
portions cut away. Brace had not been present at that
battle, having at the time been confined by fever two
hundred miles distant. This did not prevent him from describing
it with the authority of an eyewitness.

"Wayne drove ye like geese!" he continued. "One platoon—one little
platoon with baggonets—could've chased the whole pack into the lakes."

The wounded man drew his knife.

Red Oak spoke one word.

The blade remained in its sheath.

"Our brother wants a little death," said the chief. "He
shall have a great one."

"A great one!" Brace laughed. "D'ye call tree-work great? I
call it carpenterin'. Ye can't stand to cold iron. Never
could. Show ye a baggonet, an' yer knees knock louder
than a militia drum."

There have been numerous disputes concerning the comparative value of
the bayonet in woodland warfare. The weapon, so formidable when
disciplined ranks advance over open ground, loses much of its
authority among trees, where formations break and an unseen rifleman
may decline the invitation to close. Nevertheless, it possessed a
peculiar place in the corporal's affections. He regarded it not
merely as sharpened steel but as the final argument of
civilization; and no catalogue of reverses, ambuscades, abandoned posts, or
annihilated detachments could have persuaded him that men who preferred
not to be stabbed were therefore superior in wisdom rather
than deficient in courage.

Little Crane comprehended the prisoner's purpose. "Brother," he said, "your
tongue runs ahead to find death. It will come back
disappointed."

Brace cursed him with considerable invention.

"Words do not cut the cords," Little Crane replied.

The preparations commenced.

Two saplings were selected at the edge of a small
opening north of the mission. They were hickories, each as
thick at the base as a man's thigh, rising straight
for twelve feet before dividing into pliant crowns. The warriors
climbed them, attached rawhide lines near their upper branches, and
drew them inward by degrees. The labor was difficult. Stakes
were driven; ropes tightened; additional hands summoned. As each tree
descended, the fibers groaned with a sound so like complaint
that Brace found himself listening against his will.

He was placed where he could observe the whole procedure.

This was no accident. His captors wished him to measure
the engine prepared for him, to watch its completion, and
thus to suffer in anticipation what the body had not
yet endured. In this they displayed a knowledge of fear
by no means peculiar to uncultivated warfare. Civilized tribunals have
led condemned men past scaffolds; armies have fired preliminary volleys;
jailers have tested ropes within hearing of occupied cells. Cruelty
differs in costume more often than in principle.

Brace continued his insults.

He mocked their knots, their weapons, their painted faces, their
dead chief, and even the method by which one warrior
drove a stake. He declared that six artillery horses could
have pulled the trees down in half the time; that
soldiers at the fort would laugh when told how many
men were needed to kill one corporal; that The Weasel,
if permitted to witness the proceedings from the next world,
was doubtless ashamed of his friends.

But every taunt was now guarded. Red Oak had stationed
himself between the prisoner and the most excitable warriors, while
Little Crane repeated, with tormenting composure, that a brave man
must not be cheated of his honor.

"Honor!" Brace cried. "Ye call it honor when twelve men
tie one?"

"Would my brother tie a bear before he skinned it?"

"I'd shoot him first."

"My brother is not a bear."

"No," said Brace. "The bear's got better company."

At noon a young warrior returned from the ridge and
reported that the settlers remained near their cabins. The attack,
Red Oak announced, would take place after the execution. The
words afforded Brace his only comfort. However slowly the business
advanced, the delay continued to shield the unsuspecting families. There
was even a possibility—faint, but not entirely unreasonable—that the missionary's
missing guide had reached the nearest post.

That guide, a mixed-blood hunter named Elias March, had escaped
during the attack of the previous evening. Brace had seen
him slip beneath a collapsed section of palisade while the
mission roof burned. Whether March had been shot in the
darkness, lost in the swamp, or gone in search of
assistance, the corporal could not know. He had heard no
friendly signal and expected none.

Still, whenever the wind stirred the brush beyond the clearing,
he looked toward it without turning his head.

The saplings were at last bent until their tops crossed.
Their accumulated force could be judged from the strained cords,
each of which sang sharply when touched. A final stake
was driven midway between the trunks. To this stake the
trees were temporarily fastened while loops of rawhide were prepared
for the prisoner's wrists.

Brace was raised.

His legs, numbed by confinement, failed beneath him, and two
warriors caught his arms. Their assistance humiliated him more than
a blow would have done.

"Leave go," he growled. "I can march to my own
parade."

They released him. He stood swaying for a moment, summoned
command over his limbs, and walked the remaining distance without
support.

The body of The Weasel had been carried to the
clearing and laid upon a robe. His face was turned
toward the place of execution. Brace glanced at him.

"Keep yer eyes open," he said to the dead man.
"Ye started this inspection."

The Weasel's brother uttered a harsh cry and lifted his
hatchet, but Red Oak caught his wrist.

"No," said the chief. "The coat-soldier is making his heart
hard. Let him strike it with words."

The observation was just. Brace's abuse had ceased to be
directed wholly at his enemies. He used it as a
man in a tempest may sing against the wind—not in
the belief that his voice will overcome the storm, but
because silence would leave him alone with it.

His right wrist was placed in one loop and his
left in the other. The rawhide was doubled, knotted, and
tested. The ropes drew his arms upward and outward until
his shoulders burned. His feet scarcely rested flat upon the
ground.

For the first time, the exact physical meaning of the
punishment entered his mind without disguise.

The trees would not merely kill. They would take possession
of his body in opposing directions. He imagined the wrench
at the shoulders, the tearing of the chest, the instant
when pain might destroy thought yet leave life. His breath
shortened. A cold sweat ran from his temples into his
beard. He fixed his eyes upon a patch of sky
between the interlaced leaves, lest the captors discover the terror
which he believed must now be plainly written upon him.

Courage, as has often been said, is not the absence
of fear; it might be more true to say that
courage is fear compelled to wear the uniform of duty.
Brace was afraid—afraid with every nerve and every inward faculty—yet
he kept his knees from bending and his lips from
supplication. That victory, invisible to those around him, was the
greatest he had won.

Little Crane came near.

"Brother, the trees are strong."

"Stronger than you."

"They will carry your name far."

"Then tell 'em to carry it to the fort. There's
men there'll come collectin'."

"Does my brother wish to speak before he goes?"

Brace intended another curse, but none came. From some chamber
of memory, long closed by years of profanity, drink, marching,
and battle, there arose the image of a small white
meetinghouse beside a field of winter stubble. He saw his
mother's gloved hand upon a worn Bible. He heard a
minister speak of One who had died upon a tree
and forgiven those who killed Him.

The recollection did not transform the corporal. He was no
saint, and bodily dread pressed too urgently upon him to
permit sustained devotion. Yet his mouth moved once without sound.

Little Crane mistook this for the commencement of a plea.

"Speak, Brother."

Brace looked at him and recovered his rough smile.

"Ay. Tell Red Oak he's a liar, tell Split-Arm I
marked him proper, an' tell the rest that Gideon Brace
never begged quarter of better men—so he surely won't beg
it here."

The wounded warrior lunged forward. Three chiefs restrained him.

"Cut the cords," ordered Red Oak.

A man took position beside the central stake, where the
two retaining lines were secured. He raised a tomahawk.

At that instant the world seemed to Brace to divide
into separate and unnatural particulars. He saw an ant crossing
the gray surface of the stake. He saw a drop
of blood, shaken from his wounded side, darken the dust
between his boots. He heard the rasp of Little Crane's
breath, the creak of the hickories, the distant axe in
the valley, and, beyond all these, a faint movement among
the leaves at the edge of the thicket.

His eyes turned toward it.

Nothing was visible but laurel, shadow, and one narrow bar
of sunlight.

The tomahawk descended.

A rifle cracked from the thicket.

The retaining cords parted under the blade. The two saplings
sprang upright with a violence that swept leaves and dust
into the air. Brace's body was jerked from the ground;
but before the wrench of the trees could reach his
consciousness, the rifle-ball entered behind his right ear and passed
through his brain.

He was dead in the same fraction of time in
which the torture began.

For several seconds no one moved. The report rolled away
among the hills, multiplying itself into fainter reports, as if
hidden marksmen occupied every ridge. Then Red Oak shouted, and
half the warriors rushed toward the thicket while the remainder
raised their guns and searched the brush for smoke.

They found a trampled fern, the impression of one knee
in damp soil, and the mark where a rifle-rest had
pressed across a fallen limb. The shooter was already gone.
Elias March, who had crawled within sixty yards of the
clearing but possessed neither the men nor the time required
for rescue, was descending the far side of the ridge
toward the settlers. He had chosen the only mercy left
within his power, and he had aimed as carefully as
affection and grief permitted.

The saplings stood nearly straight once more. Between them hung
the corporal, his arms extended, his boots a little above
the earth. The force had dislocated both shoulders, but it
had not torn the body apart; that further labor, had
life remained, would have been completed by additional violence and
time.

Little Crane approached and examined the wound. The opening made
by the ball was so small as to be nearly
imperceptible. Only a thin thread of blood descended along the
neck and lost itself beneath the collar of the faded
military coat.

"The shot killed him first," he said.

Red Oak looked toward the silent thicket. Anger darkened his
face, for vengeance had been cheated of its ceremony; yet
he could not wholly refuse admiration to the hand which,
from concealment and at fearful risk, had bestowed so terrible
a kindness.

"The hidden brother loved him," he said.

Little Crane considered the dead soldier, whose features, relieved at
last of their labor, wore neither defiance nor pain.

"Yes," he answered. "He loved him enough to kill him."

Behind them, unnoticed for the moment, the knocking in the
valley ceased. Elias March had reached the cabins, and the
settlers were taking up their rifles. The intended surprise was
lost; the women and children were already moving toward the
stone storehouse beside the creek; and before Red Oak's scattered
men could be recalled from their fruitless pursuit, the narrow
approach to the settlement would be defended.

Thus the corporal, who had failed to escape, succeeded in
delaying the attack; and the friend who could not save
his life saved him from the only death he feared.

Of Father Ignatius, The Weasel, and the wounded men, frontier
accounts afterward spoke according to the loyalties of those who
repeated them. One party made martyrs, another heroes, and all
made monsters of their enemies. Such is the common manufacture
of history where grief supplies the material and hatred holds
the tools. But concerning Gideon Brace there was less disagreement.
Even Red Oak, years later, when telling the story beside
a council fire from which much bitterness had departed, acknowledged
that the old soldier's heart had not failed.

He was buried near the mission, beneath the two hickories.

Freed from their bonds, the trees continued to grow. Their
upper branches, once forced together for an office of cruelty,
crossed again in later years by the slower and gentler
action of wind and sunlight. Travelers who knew the story
sometimes paused beneath them; and if they observed, high upon
either trunk, an ancient scar where the rawhide had bitten
through the bark, they were reminded that nature preserves the
marks of human violence long after she has hidden its
victims.

Yet in spring the scars put forth leaves.

### September 2, 1813

I am three hundred and twenty-three years of age, and
yet the hand that traces these characters is smooth, vigorous,
and unshaken as that of a youth of twenty.

Am I immortal?

It is the question which has followed me through the
lengthened night of three centuries; it has sat beside me
at the hearths of strangers, walked with me over battle-fields,
watched from the mast while tempests raved around my vessel,
and whispered from every new-made grave into which I have
seen the beloved and the familiar descend. Am I immortal?
I know not. I have outlived dynasties, friendships, hatreds, cities,
and almost the memory of my native hills; yet there
may be a limit. The invisible sand may still be
falling, though the glass that contains it be hidden from
my sight.

To-day I prepare for a voyage from which few expect
me to return. Before I go, I would leave behind
me this confession—not to claim credence, for credulity itself would
recoil from it; not to excite compassion, for who will
pity one whom Death refuses?—but that, should my design fail
or triumph, some record may remain of Winzy, the mortal
immortal; the poorest servant of Cornelius Agrippa, the happiest bridegroom
in Christendom, the most desolate creature beneath God's stars.

I was born among the mountains in the year 1490.
My father, a woodcutter, died beneath a falling pine when
I had seen but seven winters; my mother followed him
before I had completed my twelfth. Poverty became my guardian,
hunger my tutor, and the open forest my first habitation.
Yet even then I possessed one treasure, and I thought
myself richer than emperors.

Bertha was the daughter of a soldier who had perished
in the Italian wars. Her mother, being distantly related to
the Lady Ursula of Rosenheim, implored that haughty dame to
receive the orphan beneath her roof. Lady Ursula consented—not from
tenderness, but because charity, when publicly displayed, is a jewel
which pride is willing to wear. Thus Bertha was lodged
in a turret chamber, clothed in cast-off silks, taught to
embroider altar-cloths, and reminded each day that she ate another's
bread.

Before she entered that splendid prison, she had been my
playmate. Together we chased moths by moonlight, gathered strawberries beneath
the beeches, and listened, trembling yet delighted, to the torrent
roaring in its rocky chasm. She was a slender, dark-haired
child, swift as a fawn and proud as a captive
princess. If I brought her a bird's nest, she praised
the eggs and scolded me for alarming the mother; if
I climbed the highest crag to gather a blue flower
she had admired, she called me mad, then wore the
blossom in her bosom until it withered.

I loved her. Nay, that poor word is pale beside
the truth—I adored, worshipped, idolized her. Before I knew the
name of passion, its chain was about my heart.

Lady Ursula's castle stood above our village like a grey
threat. Its windows glittered in the sunset, its iron gates
opened only to riders in velvet and mail, and its
terraces were guarded by stone saints whose mutilated faces appeared
to condemn the humble creatures below. Once Bertha entered there,
I saw her rarely; and when I did, she was
accompanied by a severe old waiting-woman who measured every glance
and counted every word.

Still, love is cunning. A folded leaf placed beneath a
loose stone, a ribbon tied to the branch of a
hawthorn, a song sung after dusk beyond the garden wall—these
were our messengers. When fortune permitted us a meeting in
the wood, Bertha would come flying down the path, her
dark curls loosened, her cheeks warm with fear and joy.
Then the castle, Lady Ursula, poverty, duty, the whole dull
machinery of the world, disappeared. There were only two beings
beneath heaven—Bertha and Winzy.

But childhood vanished; and with manhood came that ancient persecutor
of love—gold.

"What have you?" asked Bertha one evening, when I had
spoken of marriage with all the magnificence of a king
bestowing provinces.

"Two strong arms," I replied, "a faithful heart, and courage
enough to conquer fortune."

She smiled, but there were tears in her eyes. "Lady
Ursula says courage cannot buy bread."

"Lady Ursula," cried I, "has so much bread that she
has forgotten its taste."

"Hush! She would send me to the convent if she
knew I met you."

"A convent! Bertha, you would not go?"

"Would I have the choice?"

The question struck me dumb. I saw, as if a
curtain had been torn aside, the truth of our condition.
I was poor; therefore my love was presumption. She was
dependent; therefore her will was a crime. Lady Ursula could
separate us by a word, immure Bertha behind holy walls,
or bestow her upon some rich suitor as reward for
a flattering bow.

I sought employment with the desperation of a storm-tossed sailor
who sees one light upon a limitless shore. It was
then that I first entered the service of Cornelius Agrippa.

His name was already uttered with fear from Cologne to
Milan. Some called him philosopher, some magician, some servant of
Satan. Peasants crossed themselves when his black horse passed; scholars
denounced the books they secretly desired to read; priests warned
their flocks that his furnaces were fed by the bones
of unbaptized children. I knew nothing of his learning and
cared little for his reputation. He offered wages. That was
sufficient.

Agrippa inhabited an ancient house beyond the town wall, where
the ground descended toward a marsh. The building had once
belonged to monks; its chapel served as his library, its
refectory as a laboratory, and its burial vaults—of their use
I never inquired. All night strange lights moved behind the
narrow windows. Acrid vapours mingled with scents delicious as the
breath of Arabia. At times the earth seemed to throb
beneath the furnaces, and blue flame crept over the floor
without consuming aught it touched.

Yet the terrible alchymist, whom the vulgar imagined commanding legions
of spirits, was frequently in need of a boy to
carry charcoal, grind minerals, scour vessels, and keep a steady
fire while he slept. Such was my office.

For nearly a year I served him. I saw metals
dissolve, powders explode, and drops of colourless fluid eat holes
through iron. Once a glass globe filled itself with moving
shadows, though the chamber was empty and the lamp extinguished.
Again, I heard a voice issue from a sealed vessel,
murmuring in a language unknown to me. Agrippa rebuked my
terror.

"Fear," said he, "is ignorance kneeling before its own image."

He was severe, absorbed, capricious; but not cruel. When paid
by some wealthy patron, he paid me generously; when abandoned
by fortune, he forgot both his own hunger and mine.
His eyes burned with that inward fever which consumes men
who seek to tear from Nature the secrets she has
hidden beneath rock, star, and grave.

I left him at last because Bertha entreated me.

"You grow pale in that accursed house," she said. "Your
clothes smell of sulphur; your dreams are filled with demons.
Come away, Winzy. Better poverty beneath the sun than gold
earned in a sorcerer's dungeon."

I obeyed. Ah, happy obedience—brief and ruinous! I laboured where
I could, but work was scarce and wages mean. Lady
Ursula discovered our attachment, redoubled her vigilance, and forbade Bertha
to leave the castle except on feast-days. Soon a new
figure appeared upon its terraces: Albert Hoffer, the son of
a wealthy magistrate.

He rode a white horse, wore a chain of gold,
and had never in his life desired what money could
not procure. Lady Ursula received him with smiles reserved for
the prosperous. He hunted in her forests, supped at her
table, and sang beneath Bertha's window with a voice which,
though not melodious, was richly accompanied by lutes.

At first I laughed. Could Bertha prefer that perfumed popinjay
to the companion of her childhood? Could a gold buckle
outweigh ten years of devotion? I laughed—then doubted—then watched.

Jealousy does not enter the breast like an armed invader;
it steals in as mist creeps through a ruined casement.
A look becomes a confession, silence a conspiracy, a smile
an oath of inconstancy. I saw Bertha walking beside Albert
in the linden avenue. She listened while he spoke; once
she laughed. That laugh! It had been mine, the music
of our forest rambles, and now the rich fool possessed
it.

When at last she contrived to meet me, I was
sullen.

"You have found cheerful company," said I.

She coloured. "Lady Ursula commanded me to attend him."

"And commanded you to laugh?"

"Perhaps I laughed at him."

"You did not appear contemptuous."

"Then watch less closely."

"Or love less dearly?"

Her proud eyes flashed. "If your love consists of suspicion
and reproach, less would be a blessing."

She turned from me. I caught her hand; she withdrew
it. Words followed—hot, foolish, poisonous words, such as passion forges
and repentance can never wholly destroy. She accused me of
idleness; I accused her of vanity. She spoke Albert's name;
I called her false. At this she grew white.

"False?" she repeated. "Then seek one truer."

She departed. I remained beneath the trees, listening to the
retreat of her footsteps until silence closed over them like
earth upon a coffin.

The next morning I returned to Agrippa.

He regarded me narrowly. "Love has sent you back."

"Hatred," I answered.

"They are neighbouring kingdoms, and the frontier is ill marked.
Can you keep vigil?"

"Until doomsday."

"Boast not. Men who promise doomsday commonly sleep before midnight."

He led me into the innermost chamber, where a furnace
glowed beneath a blackened arch. Upon a tripod stood a
small crystal vessel. Within it trembled a rose-coloured liquid, clear
as sunset reflected in mountain snow. Agrippa's face was gaunt
from labour; his hands shook.

"I have watched three nights," he said. "I must rest.
Keep the flame at this height—neither more nor less. If
the liquid change from rose to white, awaken me instantly.
Touch nothing. Taste nothing. Should you disobey, you may purchase
knowledge at a price no repentance can repay."

Then he went into the adjoining cell and threw himself
upon a couch.

I sat before the furnace.

For an hour my attention remained fixed upon the vessel.
The liquid pulsed with faint radiance, as though a tiny
heart beat within it. Presently my thoughts wandered. I saw
Bertha under the lindens; Albert bent toward her; she smiled.
I heard again the word false in her wounded voice.
Anger burned, then grief, then a longing so intense that
I pressed both hands against my breast, as if I
might tear out the tormenting organ.

Upon Agrippa's table lay an open manuscript. My eyes fell
on a line written in red:

**A draught to free the soul from love.**

I started. Was this the labour that had consumed my
master? Could science indeed extinguish that fever which prayer, pride,
and absence failed to cure? The thought was terrible—and delightful.

To forget Bertha! To behold her beside Albert and feel
no wound; to pass the castle without raising my eyes;
to sleep without dreaming of her; to awake without hunger
for her voice! Liberty opened before me. I imagined my
heart a quiet chamber from which the tyrant had been
expelled.

At that instant the potion changed.

The rosy hue faded; the fluid became white—whiter than moonlight,
whiter than the shroud of an innocent child. Golden flashes
sprang through it, and a fragrance filled the chamber. No
flower of earth ever breathed such perfume. It seemed compounded
of spring rain, crushed violets, warm fruit, sea-wind, and the
first breath of morning. My veins thrilled. Every sense cried
out to partake.

I should have awakened Agrippa.

Instead I lifted the vessel.

"Freedom," I whispered. "Only freedom from love."

I quaffed.

Fire—yet not pain—rushed from my lips to my heart, from
my heart to the extremities of my frame. The chamber
expanded; the walls receded; the dying lamp blazed like a
captured sun. I heard the minute crackling of charcoal, the
stirring of a moth's wing near the ceiling, the deep
breathing of Agrippa in the next room. Strength, joy, and
boundless confidence possessed me. Methought I could have uprooted the
mountains, commanded kings, or laughed Satan from his throne.

I raised the vessel again. Half the liquid remained.

Then Agrippa awoke.

His cry shook the vault. I started; the glass slipped
from my hand and shattered upon the stones. White flame
ran along the floor; for one dazzling moment every object
stood revealed with unnatural sharpness—books, skulls, retorts, the alchymist's horrified
countenance—and then the fire vanished, leaving only a dark stain.

"Wretch!" he exclaimed. "What have you done?"

"I sought to cure myself of love."

"Love?" His terror changed to amazement, almost to laughter; but
he mastered it. "Begone! Leave me. The labour of years—lost
through a jealous boy!"

He seized my arm and thrust me from the chamber.
I ran from the house, not in shame, but in
exultation. Dawn reddened the east. Every leaf glittered, every bird
sang for me, every cloud appeared a triumphal banner. I
leapt brooks, climbed walls, and sped uphill toward the castle
with the swiftness of a deer.

Had I ceased to love Bertha? Folly! The supposed cure
had armed love with wings. I loved her more vehemently
than before, but all fear was gone. Albert's wealth seemed
ridiculous; Lady Ursula's pride, a cobweb; the castle itself, a
cage whose bars I could rend apart.

I reached the terrace as Bertha came from the chapel
with her guardian and Albert. My sudden appearance shocked them.
My hair was disordered, my poor garments stained, yet I
felt clothed in celestial splendour.

"Bertha," I cried, "come with me."

Lady Ursula drew herself up. "Insolent beggar!"

Albert stepped forward. "Remove yourself before you are beaten from
the gate."

I looked at him, and he recoiled. Whether some remnant
of the elixir's fire shone in my eyes, or whether
courage alone confounds those accustomed to submission, I cannot say.

"Bertha," I repeated, "yesterday I wronged you. I was mad
with jealousy because I loved you beyond reason. I have
nothing—no land, no gold, no name that men honour. But
I have life, strength, fidelity, and a heart that is
wholly yours. Come with me now, or command me never
to seek you again."

She trembled.

Lady Ursula caught her wrist. "You will enter the house."

Bertha looked at the iron door, the high windows, the
stern faces; then at me. In that moment I saw
childhood, hope, fear, and love contend within her. Love conquered.

"I will go with Winzy," she said.

The old lady's grasp tightened. Bertha tore herself free and
flew to me—flew like the dark-haired fawn of our early
days. Albert called for servants; Lady Ursula threatened convent, prison,
damnation. We heard them as birds may hear the barking
of dogs far below while they cleave the upper air.

Before noon we had left the valley. Within a month
Bertha became my wife.

Those who say that happiness is brief speak truth, yet
not the whole truth; for one hour of perfect happiness
may spread its radiance over years, even as one lamp
illumines a vast and gloomy hall. For five years we
were poor, industrious, and blessed. I hired a cottage beside
a vineyard; I laboured among the vines, repaired wheels, cut
timber, and undertook whatever honest task came to hand. Bertha
spun, tended our garden, and made our humble room beautiful
by that domestic enchantment which no alchymist has ever imitated.

Sometimes she rallied me concerning the miraculous draught.

"A noble cure," she would say, resting her head upon
my shoulder. "It delivered you from love so completely that
you married the object of your indifference."

"I drank too little."

"Then return for the remainder."

"Never! A second dose might make me hate you."

She laughed; and I, remembering the broken vessel and Agrippa's
despair, felt a passing uneasiness. But youth is credulous toward
joy and sceptical toward danger. My health was perfect; my
strength seemed inexhaustible; these I attributed to temperance, labour, and
contentment.

Five years afterwards a messenger came from Agrippa. The great
philosopher lay dying and desired to see me.

I found him in the chamber of the experiment. Dust
covered the instruments; cobwebs hung from the arch; the furnace
was cold. Agrippa, once so commanding, had dwindled to a
shadow upon his bed. Yet his eyes retained their ancient
fire.

"You drank it," he murmured when we were alone.

"The cure for love? Yes—and a poor cure it proved."

Again that strange smile touched his mouth.

"Did you think I wasted years to compose a remedy
for a boy's sighs? The manuscript spoke of another preparation.
What you stole was the work of my life."

"What work?"

He raised himself with difficulty. "The Elixir of Immortality."

I stared, then laughed. I could not help it. The
chamber of death, the solemn alchymist, the monstrous declaration—all appeared
the machinery of some grim jest.

"Immortality! Then why do you lie there?"

"Because you drank it."

"Half only. The rest was spilled."

His fingers fastened upon mine with startling force. "Half! Then
your destiny is uncertain. Perhaps eternal youth—perhaps centuries—perhaps only a
life extended beyond the common measure. I cannot tell. No
man can tell. The experiment is made in you."

"Master, fever wanders in your brain."

"Would that it did! Look at yourself. Five years have
passed. Has one line appeared? Has one hair altered?"

"I am young."

"You will say so when these walls are dust."

He sank back. I wished to summon an attendant, but
he detained me.

"Hear me. If death forgets you, do not bless the
gift. Nature permits no solitary triumph. The immortal man would
be severed from his kind—an eagle chained among insects, yet
chained still. You will love; they will perish. You will
build; time will overthrow. You will remember; no one will
share remembrance. Beware hope—it will become your cruellest jailer."

His voice failed. After a moment he added, "Had you
awakened me—"

The sentence remained unfinished. Cornelius Agrippa died before dawn, and
with him perished the knowledge that might have explained or
ended my fate.

I returned to Bertha troubled, but silence seemed wiser than
an alarming tale founded upon a dying man's delirium. For
several years I observed myself with secret vigilance. Each birthday
I examined my face in a small Venetian mirror. At
first the practice amused me. Then amusement became wonder.

I did not change.

At thirty, I appeared twenty. At forty, I still appeared
twenty. The sun browned my skin; fatigue made me pale;
grief hollowed my eyes for a night—but rest restored the
same intolerable youth. No wrinkle remained, no silver thread appeared
among my hair. Illness avoided me. Wounds closed with unnatural
speed. Once a falling beam broke my arm; before the
surgeon believed the bone secure, I had resumed my labour.

Bertha changed.

Ah! Why does my hand hesitate? Age is no disgrace;
its marks are honourable inscriptions written by sorrow, endurance, maternity,
thought. Yet love—earthly love—has a selfish eye. I saw the
first faint line upon her brow and wished it away.
A year later it deepened. A few dark hairs turned
grey. Her step lost something of its woodland lightness. These
alterations would have been nothing had they visited us together;
but beside my unaltered face they became accusations.

At first Bertha jested.

"You have made a covenant with your mirror," she said.
"It flatters you and insults me."

Later she sought remedies. She washed her face with dew
gathered before sunrise, purchased oils from wandering women, stained her
whitening hair, and wore youthful colours that once became her
but now proclaimed what they attempted to conceal. I smiled
too eagerly; my kindness wounded her more than ridicule.

"Do not look at me so," she cried one evening.

"How do I look?"

"As if you pitied me."

"I love you."

"You love what I was."

"No, Bertha."

"Yes! You remain the bridegroom, while I become the nurse,
the mother—the grandmother! Strangers already wonder. Yesterday the innkeeper asked
whether you were my son."

I turned away, for indignation and grief contended in me.

She seized the mirror and placed us before it. Her
face, still noble and beloved, bore the record of nearly
fifty years; mine retained the smoothness, brightness, and insolent promise
of twenty.

"What are you?" she whispered.

The hour for concealment was past. I told her all—the
rose-coloured liquid, the golden flashes, Agrippa's last confession, my uncertainty.
She listened without interruption. When I finished, she crossed herself.

"Immortal," she said.

"Perhaps."

"And you let me marry you?"

"I did not know."

"You knew something unnatural had occurred."

"I believed it a cordial, a fever of the blood—anything
but this."

She paced the room. Fear made her unfamiliar to me.
"When I am dead, you will marry another."

"Never."

"Then a third, when she too is dust. A fourth!
Young women will love you through the centuries."

"Bertha!"

"Why should they not? You are beautiful. You will always
be beautiful."

There was bitterness in the word. I knelt before her.

"If my existence torments you, I will go. Men already
whisper. They will accuse you of witchcraft, or me of
some darker commerce. I can disappear; you may say I
died. I will leave you all I possess."

"And wander young and free?"

"Alone and accursed."

She studied my face long and mournfully. Then the jealous
anger left her; my Bertha returned.

"No," she said. "You shall not go. If you are
a demon, I chose you before I knew it, and
must abide by my choice. If you are a man,
you need me more than ever. We will leave this
place together."

Thus she, who had once fled from a castle to
share my poverty, now abandoned home and familiarity to share
my monstrous secret.

We travelled into France, assuming another name. There strangers saw
only a young husband and his elder relative; to prevent
questions, we allowed them to think Bertha my mother. The
deception humiliated her, though she had herself proposed it. Sometimes,
when neighbours praised my filial tenderness, her lips compressed. Once,
in a fit of wounded pride, she declared before them
that she was my wife. They laughed, believing it a
jest. She laughed also—too loudly—and wept the whole night afterward.

Yet her love endured. So did mine, though time purified
it of youthful intoxication. Her beauty faded; her spirit remained
ardent, imperious, affectionate, and easily wounded. I became her servant,
companion, physician, and child. If she envied my youth, she
also guarded it. When reckless despair tempted me to dangerous
feats, she rebuked me. When villagers stared, she devised another
removal. When I sat silent, pondering the abyss of coming
solitude, she forced me to speak of ordinary things—the garden,
the weather, the price of flour—as though common cares might
tether me to human life.

Years passed away.

Her hair became white. Her body, once so swift, bent
beneath infirmity. A palsy seized her left side; thereafter she
seldom left her chair. I carried her to the window
each morning, and at evening bore her back to bed.
She grew light in my arms—terribly light, like a child
returning in reverse through the gates of existence.

One winter night snow covered the fields. The fire sank
low, and moonlight lay upon the floor. Bertha awoke and
called my name.

"I am here."

"You always are," she said.

I raised her, and she touched my cheek with her
withered fingers.

"Still smooth," she murmured. "I hated you for it once."

"I know."

"Forgive me."

"There is nothing to forgive."

"Oh, there is much. I was vain. I wished you
to grow ugly so that I might not grow old
alone."

I pressed her hand to my lips.

"Winzy, do you remember the blue flower?"

"The one upon the crag?"

"You nearly broke your neck to gather it."

"You scolded me."

"I wore it."

"Until it died."

She smiled. "Everything dies."

The words froze my blood.

"Not love," I answered.

"Perhaps love too—only it leaves a seed."

Toward dawn her breathing became faint. I called for the
physician, though both of us knew that no art could
detain her. She would not release my hand.

"You will not follow me yet," she said.

"I shall try."

"No violence. Promise."

I was silent.

"Promise me. Do not murder the life God permitted, even
if He permitted too much."

"I promise."

Her eyes closed, then opened once more.

"You were never cured of love," she whispered.

Those were her last words.

For three days I sat beside her body. Death restored
a solemn beauty to her face, smoothing the bitterness of
age while leaving its dignity. I waited for some sign
that the elixir might extend its protection through my touch,
my breath, my longing. None came. The beloved clay grew
cold.

They buried her in a small churchyard near Tours. Rain
fell while the priest spoke; drops gathered upon the coffin,
and each seemed a world in which our whole life
was reflected and extinguished. When earth struck the lid, I
understood immortality—not as endless time, but as endless bereavement.

Since then I have wandered.

Three centuries! Reader, if reader I obtain, pause before those
words. You count a century as history; I count it
as remembrance. Kings rose, quarrelled, conquered, and became names in
books. Tongues altered. Garments, laws, prayers, and weapons changed. Roads
crossed forests where I had once been lost; towns vanished
where I had once begged shelter. Men disputed truths for
which their grandfathers had slain one another, then abandoned both
the truths and the disputes.

I moved among the children of Adam like one of
another creation. At first I attempted friendship. But friendship demands
equality, and what equality can exist between one who changes
and one who does not? A boy became my companion;
soon he appeared my elder, then my father, then a
trembling ancient who blessed me as though I were his
son. I attended his funeral and fled before his grandchildren
questioned me.

I altered my name, my country, my occupation. I was
soldier, sailor, clerk, tutor, apothecary, gardener. I fought in wars
whose causes I scarcely understood and whose victories are now
forgotten. Steel entered my side; fever consumed entire camps around
me; a ship sank beneath me in the Atlantic, and
for a night I clung to wreckage while black water
promised peace. Yet dawn found me alive.

Water has ever tempted me. Its depths appear tranquil, secret,
absolving. To sink below the last ray, to feel the
great cold close overhead, to descend where memory itself must
be crushed—what annihilation could be sweeter? Yet when waves covered
my face, instinct fought. I gasped, struggled, survived. The body
is a tyrant more vigilant than any jailer.

Fire tempted me also; but I remembered the white flame
running from Agrippa's broken vessel and imagined that perhaps the
same element which bestowed this curse could alone destroy it.
I approached burning houses, volcanoes, furnaces—and recoiled. Courage before accidental
danger is easy; deliberate annihilation requires another species of soul.

Besides, Bertha had compelled my promise.

Is suicide forbidden to one who cannot die? The commandment
says, Thou shalt not kill. But what if the victim
be beyond killing? Would the attempt be crime, folly, or
experiment? I have debated until thought became a wheel turning
upon emptiness.

At times I sought another hand. A condemned murderer once
offered, for gold, to plunge a dagger into my heart.
I bared my breast; he raised the weapon; then I
beheld his face—not cruel, but terrified—and dismissed him. Why should
I burden another conscience with my despair? Why make a
stranger the executioner of a sentence pronounced by no court
save sorrow?

Thus I live.

Do not imagine that perpetual youth is perpetual delight. Appetite
continues, but novelty perishes. Spring returns; I have seen too
many springs. Beauty smiles; behind the young face I behold
already the skull. Children play; I calculate the year of
their death. The lover vows "for ever," and I alone
know how brief his eternity will prove.

Mirrors are my enemies. They show no spectre, no mark
of Cain, no ruin answering to the ruin within. My
eyes retain brightness, my limbs vigour, my hair its dark
abundance. Yet my heart is older than Nestor's, heavier than
the Wandering Jew's fabled burden. I am an old-hearted youth,
a living contradiction, a prison whose walls renew themselves each
morning.

Still—am I immortal?

I drank but half. This thought is the last star
above my desolation. Perhaps the draught merely prolonged life to
some gigantic term. Perhaps four hundred years are allotted me,
or five. Perhaps even now invisible decay advances toward some
hidden citadel. Agrippa did not know. His assertion was inference,
not proof. The elixir may resist age, yet fail before
cold, flame, crushing weight, or deprivation.

There are mornings when I awake weary and cry, "At
last!" But food restores me. There are nights when my
pulse seems slow; hope dawns—the hope of death!—and then the
detestable engine resumes its perfect labour.

I have studied legends. The Seven Sleepers awoke after centuries,
but they had not endured the centuries consciously. Tithonus obtained
endless life without endless youth; I possess, it seems, the
opposite horror—youth without renewal of the soul. The Wandering Jew
expects judgment and has prophecy for companion. I have only
doubt. Even Satan knows himself immortal; certainty gives shape to
his despair. Mine has none.

Therefore I have determined upon my voyage.

A company departs within the week toward the northern seas,
seeking a passage through regions where the ocean is locked
beneath mountains of ice and the sun deserts the world
for months. Men of science accompany it; sailors hardened by
tempests tremble at the proposed route. They speak of cold
that stops the blood, winds sharp as knives, gigantic fields
of ice that close upon ships and grind oak beams
to splinters. They speak of hunger, darkness, madness, and white
wastes where no grave can be dug.

I have offered my service. They think me a reckless
young adventurer hungry for fame.

They are not wholly mistaken.

If I perish there, freedom is mine. No churchyard need
receive me; the snow shall be my winding-sheet, the polar
ocean my tomb. Bertha's prohibition concerned violence by my own
hand; I go to encounter danger, not to strike the
blow. Is this a coward's evasion? Perhaps. The conscience, like
a subtle advocate, can make innocence from desire.

But if I survive—if cold cannot freeze me, famine waste
me, or the crushing ice destroy this frame—then I shall
possess proof approaching certainty. I will return not as a
nameless wanderer, but as one who has penetrated where mortal
strength failed. Fame may become the substitute for companionship. Men
who would shun a sorcerer may honour an explorer; they
may preserve my name even while time erases theirs.

And afterward?

Afterward I shall seek some more decisive means. Fire at
the earth's heart; descent into the deepest ocean; the summit
where no breath sustains life; explosive force sufficient to scatter
every particle of this self-renewing prison—surely Nature contains an instrument
stronger than Agrippa's art.

Yet another thought intrudes. Suppose my body be torn asunder
and still consciousness remain? Suppose immortality belongs not to flesh
alone, but to sensation—to the imprisoned "I" that cannot escape?
To lie scattered, aware, incapable even of motion—ah! There are
abysses beneath the abyss.

I close my eyes and see Bertha as she was
when she ran from Lady Ursula's terrace: dark hair streaming,
hand extended, love victorious over fear. Sometimes I imagine that
death has not annihilated her but carried her beyond a
veil through which I alone cannot pass. She waits; I
linger. Each year is another locked door between us.

At other times I fear she has forgotten me. Why
should the dead preserve those passions which tormented the living?
Perhaps she has entered a peace too vast for remembrance.
Perhaps my love, which I once called eternal, is merely
the chain binding me to a shadow.

No—let philosophers dispute. I loved her. I love her still.
If three hundred years have not cured me, no medicine
can.

The lamp burns low as I write. Dawn will soon
touch the harbour, and the masts will emerge one by
one from darkness like crosses in a drowned churchyard. My
chest is packed. Within it lie warm garments, instruments, this
memoir, and the little Venetian mirror I have carried since
the first years of my marriage. Why take that hateful
witness? Because if I return, I shall look once more
and ask whether the ice has written a single line
upon my face.

If it has—joy! If one hair is white, if one
wrinkle appears, I shall hail it as a messenger more
beautiful than Hope. Decay will be my sunrise; infirmity my
bridal garment; Death, no longer an ill-visaged enemy, but the
long-expected friend who leads me to Bertha.

If nothing changes, then the question will darken but not
vanish.

Am I immortal?

To-morrow I go northward to demand an answer from cold,
solitude, and night. Perhaps my bones will lie beneath an
unnamed field of ice, and these pages alone return. Perhaps
I shall come back unchanged, while every companion has perished.
Perhaps centuries hence I shall read these words and pity
the credulous youth of three hundred and twenty-three who still
believed Nature capable of mercy.

The harbour bell sounds.

For ever?—No. I will not write it.

Not yet.

Our introduction to the classical mythology was effected by Miss
Crewe, who had been engaged to improve the minds of
the girls, but who, owing to a mistaken liberality on
the part of Providence, improved ours as well. She read
aloud beneath the cedar on fine afternoons; and though we
lay beyond the laurel hedge, technically engaged upon private affairs
of our own, the adventures of gods and heroes penetrated
to us through the leaves. Thus we learned, in no
proper order, that ladies might be turned into trees for
declining an acquaintance, that kings habitually exposed their infant relations
on mountains, and that a young man of spirit, when
denied his inheritance, had only to obtain a ship, a
mixed crew, and a fleece of gold.

For several days afterward Harold was Jason.

Harold's imagination was magnificent in conception but parsimonious in equipment.
He had selected an ancient pig-trough behind the unused stable
as the Argo, and, by means of a broken rake,
two lengths of clothes-line, and a red pocket-handkerchief, had fitted
it for navigation. When we found him he was seated
in it, wearing the gardener's discarded straw hat with the
brim turned up in a manner which he maintained was
Corinthian.

"You can be the crew," he said. "I shall steer."

"There's no water," Edward observed.

Harold replied that this was a detail; the great thing
was to imagine properly.

Edward had no objection to imagination in its place, but
held that a voyage without water was rot. He also
pointed out that the trough had a crack along the
bottom, had contained pigs within recent memory, and, morally considered,
was unworthy of heroes. Edward's criticism was usually destructive only
until he had substituted a more dangerous proposal of his
own.

"Farmer Larkin's boat is down by the osiers," he said.
"Why not take that?"

There followed one of those councils in which the public
voice, after affecting dubiety, discovers that it has been unanimous
from the outset. The boat was undoubtedly Farmer Larkin's; Farmer
Larkin had on more than one occasion expressed an unreasoning
desire that we should keep out of it; and the
taking of property against its owner's explicit wishes might, under
certain narrow interpretations, be confused with theft. On the other
hand, Jason had not waited for a signed permit from
the harbour-master of Iolcos. The ethical authorities being thus divided,
adventure obtained the casting vote.

The girls, who had now joined us, demanded inclusion. We
refused it on historical grounds.

"There were no girls among the Argonauts," Harold stated.

"There was Atalanta," said Mary.

Harold, who disliked facts when they arrived from a female
quarter, said that Atalanta had probably remained on shore to
look after things.

"She did not," said Mary. "Miss Crewe read it."

Edward intervened with statesmanlike impatience. The boat was small; the
girls would scream if it rocked; and somebody had to
stay behind to mislead Nurse, should that functionary institute inquiries.
This last argument, being both insulting and practical, ended the
debate. Mary said she hoped we should be drowned, while
assuring us in the same breath that she would tell
no one where we had gone. Such inconsistencies are commonly
attributed to women, though at the time we considered them
peculiar to sisters.

We reached the osier-bed by way of the lower orchard,
avoiding the yard where Farmer Larkin was engaged in disputation
with a cow. The Argo lay among reeds under a
willow, green-painted, flat-bottomed, and smelling of mud, rope, and the
honest labour of other people. It contained one pair of
oars, a rusty boat-hook, and a quantity of rainwater. The
latter we removed with Larkin's own tin baler, thereby leaving
the vessel in better condition than we found it and
establishing, as Harold remarked, our position as benefactors rather than
pirates.

The chain was secured by a peg. Edward pulled it
up.

"There!" he said. "Now shove."

There is a solemn instant in every great enterprise when
conjecture becomes deed, when retreat remains possible but has ceased
to be respectable. The boat's nose slid from the rushes;
brown water widened between us and the bank; a moorhen
fled with an imprecation; and the familiar meadow, viewed from
three yards offshore, assumed the remoteness of a foreign kingdom.

Edward took the oars because he could row. Harold sat
in the stern because Jason was commander. We occupied the
middle and performed, as opportunity offered, the functions of Orpheus,
Castor, Pollux, and the common sailor. Harold had intended to
deliver an address on leaving harbour, but the boat swung
broadside into the reeds and a trailing bramble removed his
Corinthian hat. His speech was consequently postponed while we backed,
pushed, and accused one another.

Once clear of the osiers, however, the expedition took on
dignity. The river, which at ordinary times was a sluggish
and rather ill-smelling stream passing between cow-pastures, now stretched before
us as the sea beyond Thessaly. Its bends hid unknown
continents; pollarded willows became monstrous guardians of the shore; and
the mud-banks, perforated by water-rats, were unquestionably the habitations of
hostile tribes. A white horse standing knee-deep at a ford
was first identified as Poseidon and afterward, when it whisked
its tail, as one of the man-eating mares of Diomedes.
The distinction did not concern us greatly. In childhood, as
in diplomacy, a formidable name is often more important than
exact classification.

We rowed upstream because the current, though mild, gave resistance
enough to make conquest perceptible. At the first narrow passage,
where two willows leaned toward each other, Harold shouted that
we had reached the Symplegades. Edward told him not to
be an ass and pulled hard, which was precisely the
practical assistance Jason himself must often have desired from his
crew. We passed safely, though a branch scraped our heads
and knocked one of the oars from its rowlock. Harold
declared the incident a fulfilment of the prophecy that the
ship should lose a feather from its stern. Edward said
it was not the stern, and not a feather; but
by then we were occupied with a family of ducks,
who took wing ahead of us like six disreputable sirens.

At noon—or what we supposed to be noon, having no
watch and a liberal attitude toward chronology—we ate our provisions.
These consisted of two apples, a heel of cake, three
biscuits intended for the spaniel, and some acid drops which
Harold had been preserving since Sunday. Division raised constitutional difficulties.
Jason claimed the cake by prerogative; Orpheus maintained that the
artist must be sustained; and Edward, who had furnished half
the supplies and all the rowing, threatened to land us
unless equity prevailed. We therefore divided everything with exactness, except
the acid drops, which had adhered permanently to the lining
of Harold's pocket.

Beyond the second bridge the river entered country less familiar
to us. Meadows gave place to thickets; the banks rose;
and elder, hazel, and wild hop leaned over the water
until the channel seemed a green corridor, cool and secret
beneath the afternoon. Here we lowered our voices, partly because
the place required it, and partly because Edward, who had
rowed farther than he admitted, wished to rest.

A little water-gate appeared on the left, almost concealed by
loose sprays of honeysuckle. Behind it, shallow steps climbed from
the river into a garden. No house could at first
be seen. There was only a gravel path, grass whitening
beneath daisies, and flowers growing in such profusion that cultivation
had plainly retreated and left them to their own politics.

"Colchis," Harold whispered.

The name settled the matter. We brought the boat alongside,
tied it with an elaborate knot which Edward afterward replaced
with a useful one, and landed in search of the
Golden Fleece.

Quiet had lapped over that garden until even our footsteps
seemed to make rings in it. Roses hung heavily from
unpruned arches; tall lilies leaned across the paths as though
listening; and heliotrope poured out a sweetness so dense that
we fancied one might have gathered it in handfuls. At
the centre of the lawn stood a grey sundial, severe
amid the disorder, recording hours which no one there appeared
willing to use. The house showed itself at last behind
cedar branches: long, pale, and withdrawn, with blinds pulled down
against the sun.

We knew at once that it was enchanted. No ordinary
household closed its eyes on so brilliant a day unless
a spell, a death, or an elderly relation were involved.

Harold led us toward the sundial. Hanging from a bush
near it was a yellow shawl, and he gave a
cry of triumph.

"The Fleece!"

It was not, upon examination, particularly golden, nor was it
fleece; but mythological necessities are elastic, and we had begun
to remove it when a voice behind us said:

"What are you doing?"

We turned. A young woman stood at the edge of
the shadow.

She wore a white dress, not the firm, practical white
of our sisters' summer frocks, but something soft that fell
about her as water falls, and her dark hair was
loosely fastened behind. Her face was pale, and for an
instant we judged her to be angry; then she smiled,
and the whole garden seemed to draw breath.

"We are the Argonauts," Harold said. He added, because courtesy
was due even in hostile kingdoms, "I am Jason."

"Of course," she answered.

This immediate acceptance established her quality. Most adults, when informed
of an obvious fact, sought to diminish it with questions;
she merely stepped out into the sunlight and looked toward
the river.

"Then you have come for the Fleece."

"Yes," said Harold. "Are you Medea?"

A curious change passed over her face—amusement first, and afterward
something we had no name for.

"I expect I am," she said. "I have been waiting
long enough."

Thus allied, we set about the conquest of Colchis. Lucy—for
that was her mortal name—entered the game with an ardour
that surprised and finally outstripped us. She knew where the
dragon ought to lie, beneath a copper beech whose roots
broke from the earth like claws; she found us a
sword in the shape of a rusted pruning-hook; and she
manufactured fire-breath from scarlet poppy petals, which she blew through
her fingers while Harold assaulted the tree. When the dragon
had been slain, resurrected owing to a legal objection by
Edward, and slain again under revised conditions, she crowned us
with ivy and led a procession round the sundial.

She ran like one released from a locked room. The
colour rose in her cheeks; her hair came down; and
she laughed not in the moderate fashion of grown-up ladies
but helplessly, stopping with one hand against the cedar because
she could go no farther. We, who accepted joy as
the normal climate of existence and sorrow as a shower
arranged by adults, wondered why laughter should appear to astonish
her.

"Do you live here all alone?" one of us asked.

"With my aunt."

"Is she a witch?"

Lucy considered.

"No," she said. "Witches can do things."

This seemed conclusive.

We sat beneath the cedar, and she gave us lemonade
from a jug which had been standing in the cool
of the house. There were only two glasses, so we
drank by turns, a ceremonial which made the beverage more
precious. Harold, now secure in his position as Jason, asked
whether she was waiting for anyone.

Lucy looked across the lawn toward the water-gate.

"Yes," she said.

"Who?"

"A traveller. He went away to seek his fortune."

"Like Ulysses?"

"Perhaps. Though Ulysses came back."

"He may have been detained by Cyclopses," Harold suggested kindly.
"Or turned into a pig."

"Then I hope he is a happy pig."

She said this smiling; yet her hand tightened round the
long grass she had been plucking, and suddenly we perceived—not
clearly, but as one sees a light beneath a closed
door—that the game had approached a country where our passports
were invalid. We did what children generally do before mysteries
of grief: accepted them as facts of geography and began
another expedition.

Lucy was now Persephone, imprisoned in the underworld; Edward became
Cerberus, because he could bark convincingly; and we were to
rescue her without eating anything belonging to Pluto. This condition
proved difficult, since Lucy discovered strawberries under netting and insisted
that the laws of Hades ought not to apply to
visitors. We had just effected her release and were bearing
her, with some inconvenience, upon crossed hands, when a voice
from the terrace froze the pageant in mid-triumph.

"Lucy!"

An old lady stood before the darkened French windows. She
was narrow, upright, and grey, with a black lace arrangement
on her head which gave her the appearance of a
highly respectable bird of prey. Her eyes moved from Lucy's
loosened hair to the broken poppies, from the open strawberry
net to ourselves, and finally to Harold, who still wore
ivy and carried the pruning-hook.

"Who are these children?"

"They came by the river," Lucy said.

"So I see. Put her down."

We obeyed. Even Perseus would have obeyed that voice, though
he might subsequently have employed the Gorgon's head.

"You know you are not to excite yourself," the old
lady continued. "Go indoors at once."

"I am not excited, Aunt. I am only happy."

The answer produced a silence which, though we could not
interpret it, made retreat seem dishonourable. Harold stepped forward.

"She's Medea," he explained. "We need her."

The aunt regarded him with a severity several centuries older
than himself.

"You must leave the garden immediately."

"But—"

"Immediately."

Lucy gave us a little farewell movement of the hand.
Already the brightness was leaving her face; already the white
dress, crossing the shadowed terrace, seemed less the robe of
a princess than the garment of a prisoner. The French
windows closed behind her. A blind descended with slow finality.

We withdrew to the water-gate, subdued but indignant. Harold proposed
returning at night with ropes. Edward said ropes would be
useless unless we had somewhere to take her. We suggested
our schoolroom, the hayloft, and an unoccupied dog-kennel; but each
refuge presented deficiencies. It is one of the stern facts
of life that rescuing princesses is easier before questions of
accommodation arise.

At the boat we discovered that Edward was missing.

He had separated from us during the aunt's admonishment, believing,
as he afterward said, that all Colchis could not belong
to one old woman and that there might be another
way out. We waited; we called softly; we called less
softly. No answer came. Since Edward was both our chief
rower and our best authority on trespass, his absence was
serious.

At length there arose from beyond the garden wall a
clamour of dogs, female denunciation, and rapid feet. Edward appeared
on top of the wall, dropped into a bed of
nettles, and ran toward us.

"Push off!" he cried.

Behind him came a stout woman in a blue apron,
brandishing a broom. She was followed by a black cat
of extraordinary indignation and by two smaller children who had
attached themselves to the pursuit on public grounds.

"He tied a tin to my Tibby!" shouted the woman.

"I didn't tie it," Edward protested, tumbling into the boat.
"I only put it over his tail to teach him
manners."

This distinction failed to soothe her. The broom descended just
short of the stern as we shoved away, and the
black cat, from the top step, delivered an imprecation which
followed us round the bend.

Our return voyage began under favourable omens, but presently practical
anxieties displaced the mythological. The sun had declined; Nurse would
have completed her preliminary inquiries; and Farmer Larkin must by
now have noticed a vacancy among his maritime possessions. The
current carried us swiftly homeward, but speed only hastened the
accounting.

At the last bend Edward raised his hand. Through the
willows we could see the landing. Farmer Larkin stood upon
it.

He had removed his coat and held a length of
rope. His attitude was not, in itself, violent; but neither
was it that of a man preparing to welcome distinguished
voyagers. Beside him waited his brown dog, whose prejudices coincided
with his master's.

"We're done," Harold whispered.

Edward surveyed the banks. On our right the river ran
close beneath a wooded slope. Farther down, beyond Larkin's landing,
the road crossed by a low stone bridge.

"Not yet," he said. "Row into those bushes."

We concealed the boat under an alder on the opposite
bank. Edward then explained his policy. The boat was to
be released empty and allowed to drift past Larkin. The
farmer, concluding that his property had escaped, would follow it
downstream toward the bridge. Meanwhile we should run along our
bank, intercept the vessel below, cross back, restore it to
the osiers, and disperse.

Harold objected that an empty Argo was contrary to legend.

"Then stay in it," said Edward.

The objection was withdrawn.

We pushed the boat into the current and crouched among
the alders. It went broadside at first, revolving with an
air of innocence, then righted itself and drifted solemnly toward
the landing. Farmer Larkin saw it. He shouted, ran to
the edge, and attempted to reach it with the rope.
The rope fell short. The boat continued downstream.

Larkin said several things unconnected with classical literature and set
off along the towpath, the brown dog preceding him like
Nemesis.

We ran.

Branches whipped our faces; roots rose maliciously beneath our feet;
Harold lost the Corinthian hat for the second and final
time. At a muddy inlet before the bridge, Edward waded
thigh-deep and caught the stern. We scrambled aboard. With the
boat-hook, one oar, and our hands against the bank, we
forced the Argo upstream while Farmer Larkin's voice receded on
the farther side.

At last we crossed, shot into the osier-bed, drove the
peg home, coiled the chain, and even returned the tin
baler to its original position. Then we fled through the
orchard, separating according to a plan invented while running.

Punishment awaited us, naturally, but it concerned lateness, torn clothes,
and the mysterious disappearance of provisions; the greater piracy remained
undiscovered. Harold persisted for weeks in schemes for Lucy's liberation,
though none survived contact with detail. Edward maintained that she
could escape perfectly well if she chose, but he never
again went upstream without looking toward the hidden water-gate.

As for the manoeuvre by which we restored the boat,
Edward accepted our admiration with becoming modesty. When we compared
him to Ulysses, however, he was pleased; for ordinary praise
he mistrusted as flattery, but the authority of Homer, like
the pursuit of an angry farmer, was a thing he
understood.

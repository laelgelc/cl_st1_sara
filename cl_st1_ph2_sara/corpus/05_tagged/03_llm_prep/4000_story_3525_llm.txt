Alyosha was the youngest son in a poor peasant family.
He was small and thin, with a long nose and
large ears that stood out from his head. When he
ran, the ears seemed to move like two little wings.
The village boys laughed at him, but Alyosha laughed too.
He was seldom angry, and when he was, it was
not with people but with things: with a knot that
would not come loose, with a pail that leaked, or
with his feet when they ached and slowed him down.

He began work as soon as he could carry anything.
He watched the geese, fetched water, chopped kindling, drove the
old mare, and helped his mother knead bread. At twelve
he went with his brothers into the fields. He was
not strong, but he did not stop. If a load
was too heavy, he bent lower. If he was cold,
he ran. If he was hungry, he waited.

His father liked him for this.

"Alyosha, bring the harness."

Alyosha brought it.

"Alyosha, go see where the calf has got to."

He ran to look.

"Alyosha, take this sack to your aunt."

He put the sack on his shoulder and went.

His mother sometimes said, "Let the boy eat first."

But his father answered, "He can eat when he comes
back. He is young."

So Alyosha ate when he came back, unless there was
another errand.

At nineteen his father sent him to the town to
serve a merchant. Alyosha's elder brother had worked there before,
but the brother had been taken as a soldier. The
merchant needed someone in his place.

"You will do as they tell you," said his father.
"Do not spoil matters. Your wages come to me."

"Yes, Father," said Alyosha.

His mother gave him a clean shirt and patched his
trousers once more. His boots had been his brother's. They
were wide in the heel and narrow at the toes,
and the leather had split along one side. His mother
bound the split with cord.

"Mind you dry them at night," she said.

Alyosha smiled, put on his short red knitted jacket, and
walked to town.

The merchant dealt in grain, candles and iron goods. He
had a wife, two married sons, an unmarried daughter, a
clerk, a coachman and a cook named Ustinia. There were
horses in the yard, sacks in the storeroom, and people
coming in and going out all day.

At first they called Alyosha only for heavy work.

"Alyosha, unload those sacks."

"Alyosha, clean the stable."

"Alyosha, carry wood to the kitchen."

He did it all quickly, and because he did it
quickly, they began calling him for small work too.

"Alyosha, take this note to the warehouse."

"Alyosha, polish my boots."

"Alyosha, light the samovar."

"Alyosha, run to the baker."

"Alyosha, bring the young master's coat."

"Alyosha, the gate is open."

"Alyosha, the dog is loose."

"Alyosha, where are you?"

"I'm coming," he would answer, even when he was already
doing something for someone else.

He rose before the household. He fed the horses, swept
the yard, shook out the rugs, carried water and cleaned
the lamps. Then the merchant sent him to the shop,
the merchant's wife sent him to market, and the daughter
sent him back because she had forgotten to ask for
blue thread. At noon he swallowed his cabbage soup standing
by the kitchen door. In the evening he brought in
wood, heated the bathhouse, blacked boots and waited in case
the young masters returned late.

He was tired, but he did not think much about
it. Work followed work, and the days passed faster than
they had in the village.

Only his boots troubled him. His toes pressed against the
hard leather until the nails grew dark, and when he
ran over frozen ground, pain climbed from his feet to
his knees. Once, coming back from the warehouse, he was
angry with his feet.

Why cannot you go properly? he thought. Everyone is waiting.

When spring came, the cord broke and two toes showed
through the split. The merchant saw them.

"You cannot wait on customers like that," he said. "Get
boots."

Alyosha went to a shoemaker and bought the cheapest pair.
When his father came for the wages, the merchant deducted
the price.

"What is this?" his father asked.

"Boots," said the merchant. "The boy was nearly barefoot."

On the way out, his father frowned at Alyosha.

"You are here to earn, not to dress like a
gentleman."

"The old ones were broken," said Alyosha.

"Things break if a man does not care for them."

Alyosha looked at the new boots. They pinched worse than
the old ones, but they shone.

"I'll be more careful," he said.

He received no money himself. At home he had never
expected any, and in town it was the same. He
had food, a place beside the stove in winter, and
his red jacket. That seemed enough.

Ustinia, the cook, was a broad young woman with strong
red hands. At first she scolded him because he came
late for meals.

"The porridge turns to paste waiting for you," she said.

"They sent me for candles."

"They always send you."

She put the pot back near the fire and kept
his portion warm.

After that she began setting aside food before the others
ate. If there was a meat bone, she left more
meat on it for him. If there were pies, she
hid one beneath a cloth. She watched him eat, and
if he slowed down, she asked whether he was ill.

No one had asked Alyosha that before.

His mother had loved him, but there had always been
children, bread, hens, washing and the cow. She gave him
what she could while doing something else. Here Ustinia looked
at him as though his hunger belonged to her.

Once she saw that his trousers had torn at the
knee.

"Take them off tonight," she said. "I'll mend them."

"You have enough to do."

"And you have nothing to do, I suppose?"

He laughed. That night he left the trousers by the
kitchen stove and lay down wrapped in an old coat.
In the morning the tear was neatly sewn, and a
second weak place had been patched too.

He turned the trousers in his hands.

"Why did you mend that part? It wasn't torn."

"It would have torn tomorrow."

Alyosha put them on and smiled all morning.

After that he tried to help her when he could.
He split her firewood into smaller pieces, carried full pots
without being asked, and cleaned the soot from the kitchen
chimney. When the merchant's wife complained that the floor was
dirty, Alyosha came at night and scrubbed it.

"You must sleep," Ustinia said.

"I sleep enough."

"You don't."

"I'm used to it."

He feared sometimes that talking with her might keep him
from work. If he heard his name called while she
was speaking, he jumped as if he had done wrong.
But often no one called, and then they stood by
the woodpile or sat for a moment near the cooling
stove.

They did not say much. Ustinia told him about her
married sister and her sister's baby. Alyosha told her of
the old mare at home and how it knew which
gate to turn into. Sometimes they only sat.

One evening Ustinia said, "If you had your wages, you
could buy cloth for a proper coat."

"My father needs the money."

"Does he need all of it?"

"He knows."

"And if you married?"

Alyosha looked at her.

"What then?" she asked.

He understood and became frightened and glad at once. He
had never made a plan for himself. The thought that
he might have a room, a table and someone waiting
for him seemed almost improper.

"We could serve together," Ustinia said. "Later we might rent
a little place."

"Yes," he said. "We might."

For several days he worked more quickly than ever. Even
his aching feet did not anger him. When he passed
Ustinia, they looked at each other and smiled. She began
knitting him woollen mittens from leftover yarn.

The merchant's wife noticed.

"What is going on with the cook and Alyosha?" she
asked the clerk.

The clerk laughed, and soon the whole family knew.

The merchant called Alyosha into the shop.

"You are thinking of marrying?"

"If it is allowed," said Alyosha.

"Allowed? A married servant wants a room, more food, time
for his wife, and then come children. We do not
keep married men in the house."

"I could work the same."

"You say that now. Put the idea away."

The merchant wrote to Alyosha's father. A week later the
old man came to town. He sat in the kitchen,
drank two glasses of tea, and then called his son
into the passage.

"What foolishness have you started?"

Alyosha looked down.

"She is a good worker," he said.

"Did I ask whether she works? You have no land,
no house and no money. Your wages support the family.
Are we to starve so you can please yourself?"

"I thought we might both stay here."

"They will not have it. And if you leave, who
will pay you as well? You will forget this."

Alyosha was silent.

"Do you hear?"

"Yes, Father."

"You give me your word."

Alyosha glanced toward the kitchen door. Ustinia was there, though
the old man did not see her.

"I give it," he said.

His father finished his tea and went away.

Ustinia came into the passage. Her face was pale.

"So that is settled," she said.

"Yes."

"You agreed quickly."

"He is my father."

"And what am I?"

Alyosha did not know what to answer. His lips made
their usual smile, but tears stood in his eyes.

"I'm sorry," he said.

She covered her face with her apron and went back
to the stove. That evening she burned the onions, and
the merchant's wife scolded her.

Afterward they spoke less. Ustinia still saved food for him,
and he still carried her pots. But now each kindness
hurt them, so they did it quietly.

Winter came. Snow lay deep on the roofs and pressed
against the yard gate. One morning the merchant ordered Alyosha
to clear the roof over the storehouse before the thaw
made the snow heavy.

Alyosha took a wooden shovel and climbed the ladder. The
day was bright. Meltwater had begun to run beneath the
snow, and the roof boards were slick.

"Clear the edge too!" the merchant called.

"Yes, sir."

Alyosha pushed one load down, then another. He stepped backward
to reach a ridge of snow. His boot slipped. He
caught at the roof, but his mitten slid over the
wet boards. He fell to the yard and struck the
stone step.

For a moment he did not move. Then he opened
his eyes and tried to rise.

"I've made trouble," he said.

They carried him into the lodge. The doctor came, felt
his body and said his back was broken. He could
do nothing.

Ustinia sat beside Alyosha. At first he was ashamed that
she had to help him drink. Then the pain grew,
and shame no longer mattered.

"Are you angry with me?" he asked.

"With you? No."

"It is better we did not marry."

"Don't speak."

"It is better," he repeated. "You would have had a
cripple, and then no husband at all."

She bent over his hand and wept.

"Thank you," he said. "For the porridge, and the trousers,
and everything."

Toward evening he became quieter. A priest came and prayed.
Alyosha listened, but the words escaped him. He had never
learned many prayers. All his life he had prayed by
carrying, fetching, giving way and doing what was asked. Now
there was nothing left for his hands to do, so
he prayed with his heart.

He looked surprised.

"What is it?" Ustinia asked.

"Nothing. Only—how strange."

"What is strange?"

"That I must go."

He lay still, thinking that death too was something given
him to do. He need not understand it. He need
only not refuse.

His face eased, and the old smile returned, small and
almost shy. He looked at Ustinia once more, then upward,
as though someone had called his name from a place
he had never seen.

"I'm coming," he whispered.

And before morning he died.

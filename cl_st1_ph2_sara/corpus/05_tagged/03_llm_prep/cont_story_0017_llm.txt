The professor sees the woman in mourning before the woman
sees her. She is standing at the end of the
footbridge, one hand resting on the iron rail, her black
coat open although the morning is cold. Behind her, two
rusty chains sag between stone posts to keep scooters from
crossing. People pass on either side of her, adjusting their
course without appearing to notice.

For a moment the professor studies her friend's profile. A
year has altered it less than grief has. The cheeks
are thinner, the mouth held firmly even at rest. She
has cut her hair to the jaw. Then she turns,
and the firmness gives way.

"You're here."

They embrace with an awkwardness that belongs not to their
friendship but to the coat, the bags, the suddenness of
touch after twelve months. The woman in mourning smells of
pharmacy soap and the cigarettes she claims not to smoke
anymore.

"When did you arrive?"

"Last night. You?"

"Sunday."

The funeral was on Tuesday. The professor does not ask
how it went, not there, with cyclists ringing their bells
and the river beneath them carrying scraps of pale reflected
sky. Instead she takes her friend's arm, and they begin
across the bridge.

Rome has been washed by rain. The paving stones are
dark, the plane trees bare, the domes and apartment roofs
sharpened by the clean air. The professor has returned for
the spring term, officially to work on a book during
her sabbatical, though her colleagues assume she has also come
for the libraries. This is only partly true. She has
come because, after three years of teaching Rome in a
northern city where winter enters every conversation, she has begun
to feel that she is describing a person she misses.

"I can breathe here," she says.

The woman in mourning looks at the traffic moving along
the embankment. "You say that every time."

"It continues to be true."

"Give it a month."

They smile. This, at least, is familiar. They met fifteen
years ago at a school gate when their sons were
small, one child refusing to release his mother's skirt, the
other already climbing the bars. Later there were birthday parties,
feverish telephone calls, afternoons in playgrounds, husbands arriving late, and
eventually the discovery that they liked one another independently of
their children. The professor's son is now at university. The
other boy lives in Milan and has returned there after
the burial.

"Is he all right?" the professor asks.

"He is twenty-two. He has decided that being all right
is a political principle."

"And your mother?"

"My mother is making lists."

"Useful lists?"

"Lists of towels. Lists of plates. A list of things
Papa kept in the cellar. Yesterday she found six bottles
of oil from 2009 and asked me whether oil dies."

"What did you say?"

"That everything dies."

The woman in mourning says this plainly. The professor presses
her arm, and neither speaks until they have reached the
other side.

The trattoria is in a street behind the hospital, hidden
between a locksmith and a shop that sells clerical shirts.
The woman in mourning chose it because her father used
to bring her there on Saturdays when her mother visited
an aunt. In those days the owner's parents ran it,
and there were barrels near the entrance and paper tablecloths.
She describes bread dipped in sauce, the illicit half-glass of
watered wine, her father removing his tie and folding it
into his pocket.

"He wasn't affectionate," she says, "but he gave me his
artichoke heart."

"That is affection."

"I know that now."

They almost pass the entrance. It lies at an angle
to the street behind a frosted-glass door with no sign
except a faded red curtain. Inside is a narrow counter,
a refrigerator for desserts, and an L-shaped dining room whose
second half disappears around a corner. Marble wainscoting rises to
shoulder height. Above it hang framed photographs of actors, priests,
and footballers, some signed, most smoke-yellowed.

A heavy woman in a blue cardigan comes from behind
the counter.

"Dottoressa!"

The woman in mourning is received into her arms. The
owner holds her by both elbows afterward and examines her
face.

"Your poor papa. A gentleman. Always a gentleman."

"Thank you, Signora Carla."

"We heard too late. My brother saw the notice. Sit,
sit. Not there, it's cold. Here."

Only then does she look at the professor.

"And who is this? La moretta?"

The owner says it cheerfully, almost as if presenting a
sweet from a tray. The professor has heard the word
before, in songs, in jokes, in the mouths of old
women admiring babies. It may mean the little dark one;
it may mean less, or more. She feels it touch
the skin beneath her ear.

"My friend," the woman in mourning says. "She teaches Roman
history."

"Ah."

"At a university."

"Brava."

The owner turns back toward the kitchen. "Two ladies! The
Dottoressa and her friend."

They are shown to a small table near the bend
in the room. One chair faces the entrance, the other
a wall hung with a photograph of the Forum under
snow. The woman in mourning takes the chair facing the
door. The professor sits opposite and puts her bag between
her feet.

A young waiter brings menus in cracked plastic sleeves. The
menu is handwritten and copied so many times that the
blue letters are gray at the edges. Before they can
read it, Signora Carla returns and tells them what they
should eat.

"There are tonnarelli with artichokes. Ciccia, you liked artichokes. And
the lamb is beautiful today."

Ciccia, the professor notices, has replaced Dottoressa. The grieving woman
does not appear to hear the change, or hears it
as part of the warmth she has come for.

"I don't know if I can eat lamb," she says.

"You are too thin. Your papa would complain."

"My papa always complained."

"He had standards."

The owner laughs, touching her shoulder. She asks whether the
professor wants lamb but directs the question to the woman
in mourning.

"She eats everything," the woman says.

"I can answer," the professor says lightly. "And yes, lamb
is fine."

Signora Carla looks at her, then at the menu. "Good.
Two lamb."

"No," says the professor. "She said she might not want
it."

The woman in mourning rubs her forehead. "I'll have the
pasta."

"Then one pasta, one lamb," the owner says, and leaves.

The professor watches the blue cardigan vanish around the corner.

"You're famous here."

"My father was."

"Ciccia."

"I haven't been called that since I was sixteen."

The woman in mourning smiles, but her eyes fill unexpectedly.
She lowers them and begins to straighten the paper napkin
under her fork.

"I went to his flat this morning," she says. "His
slippers were beside the bed. My mother hasn't moved them.
She says she doesn't notice them, but she has dusted
around them."

The waiter brings water and two slices of bread in
a steel basket. The professor waits.

"I keep thinking I should call him," her friend continues.
"Not because I have anything to say. Just because there
are times when I call him. Friday after lunch. Sunday
evening. I look at the clock and my body remembers
before I do."

The professor reaches across the table. The woman's hand remains
beneath hers for a few seconds, then slips away to
pour the water.

"And Paolo?" the professor asks.

The woman in mourning looks toward the door, where a
family is entering. "Paolo sends messages."

"What kind?"

"Practical ones. The boiler has been serviced. He paid the
insurance. He wants to know when I'm coming back."

"Are you?"

"I don't know."

Three months earlier she discovered that her husband had been
seeing someone from his office. Not once, and not recently:
for nearly two years, throughout her father's illness, during the
weekends when she traveled to Rome to take him to
appointments. Paolo admitted it only after she showed him the
hotel receipts. Since then they have occupied different rooms in
the same house, moving around one another with the politeness
of tenants.

"Does he still see her?" the professor asks.

"He says no."

"Do you believe him?"

"I believed him before."

The pasta comes. It is bright with parsley and oil.
The lamb arrives on a white plate with potatoes darkened
at the edges. Signora Carla sets the pasta before the
woman in mourning herself.

"For you, ciccia. Eat."

She tells the professor's plate where to go with a
movement of her chin. The waiter sets it down.

The woman in mourning takes two bites, then puts her
fork aside. "It tastes exactly the same."

"Is that good?"

"I don't know."

At the table beyond the bend, a child begins singing.
The words are indistinct, the melody made of three notes
repeated without variation. A man tells her to sit down.
A woman laughs. The child appears a moment later, a
small girl in pink leggings and silver shoes, with two
ponytails fixed high on her head. She stops beside their
table and stares.

"Hello," the woman in mourning says.

The girl presses a finger into her mouth.

"What's your name?"

The child does not answer. Her gaze moves to the
professor and remains there.

The professor smiles. "Those are splendid shoes."

The girl retreats around the corner.

"Splendid?" says the woman in mourning.

"I teach undergraduates. I have lost the language of children."

"You never had it."

This, too, is familiar, and they laugh. The professor begins
to eat. The lamb is tender, heavily salted. Lunch settles
into its ordinary sequence: bread broken, water poured, the woman
in mourning describing the paperwork required after a death. She
has learned that institutions need the deceased to be repeatedly
certified as dead. The bank requires one document, the pension
office another. Each clerk speaks of her father in the
present tense while asking for proof that he no longer
exists.

Her phone vibrates. She glances at it and turns it
face down.

"Paolo?"

"My cousin."

The child returns carrying a yellow plastic horse. She comes
close to the woman in mourning and lays the horse
beside her plate.

"For me?"

The child nods.

"That's kind. Thank you."

"You nice," the girl says.

"So are you."

The child looks at the professor.

"And she is my friend," the woman in mourning adds.

The girl snatches up the horse and runs away.

The professor cuts another piece of lamb. "A severe jury."

"She's shy."

"She doesn't seem shy."

A burst of laughter comes from around the corner. Signora
Carla calls the child's name, Livia, in a voice of
indulgent warning. The professor cannot see who is seated there.
The room's angle makes the family present as noise, a
sleeve at the edge of sight, a hand lifting a
glass.

After the plates are cleared, the professor asks for the
bathroom.

"Back there," the waiter says, pointing past the bend.

She walks around the corner. The second part of the
room is smaller, with four tables crowded close together. Livia
sits between a young woman and an older man. Signora
Carla is standing beside them with a coffee pot. All
four look up when the professor appears.

"The bathroom?" she asks.

No one answers immediately. Then the young woman gestures toward
an unmarked door near the kitchen.

The professor moves toward it. Livia slides off her chair
and plants herself in the narrow passage, legs apart, one
hand against each table.

"Excuse me," the professor says.

The child shakes her head.

"Livia," the young woman says, but without urgency.

"I need to pass."

"No."

Signora Carla smiles as if the child has performed a
trick. "Let the signora through."

Livia remains where she is.

The professor waits. She is conscious of her height, her
dark coat, the attention of the table. She could step
over the child, but the space is too tight. She
could move her aside, but the thought of putting her
hands on those pink-clad shoulders seems suddenly impossible.

"Please," she says.

Livia narrows her eyes. "Bad."

The young woman gives a small laugh. "Come on, amore."

"Bad," the child repeats, louder.

"Who is bad?" asks the old man.

"She bad."

The professor feels the word strike with less force than
the amusement surrounding it. Signora Carla takes the child by
one wrist and draws her half a step aside.

"Children," she says to the professor, as if supplying a
full explanation.

The professor passes. Before she closes the bathroom door, she
hears Livia sing, "Bad, bad, bad," to the same three
notes as before.

The bathroom is cold and very clean. There is a
small sink, a mirror with a chipped corner, and a
high window covered by wire mesh. The professor turns on
the tap. She has no need to use the toilet
now. She washes her hands, dries them, washes them again.

In the mirror her face seems composed. Her hair is
pinned back, and there is a gray thread on the
shoulder of her sweater. She removes it. She tells herself
that Livia is four, perhaps five. Children choose enemies for
reasons no adult can recover: a voice, a coat, the
refusal to surrender a toy. She tells herself that moretta
can be affectionate. She tells herself that Signora Carla is
old-fashioned, that the family was embarrassed, that laughter often comes
from embarrassment.

Outside, the child is still singing.

When the professor opens the door, the passage is clear.
No one looks at her. Signora Carla is pouring wine
for the old man. The young woman is examining her
phone. Livia sits beneath the table, silver shoes showing between
the hanging cloth.

Back at her place, the woman in mourning is reading
a message.

"Everything all right?"

"Yes."

"Was there a queue?"

"The child wouldn't let me pass."

"Which child?"

"Livia."

Her friend looks around. "Why?"

"She says I'm bad."

The woman in mourning waits for the smile that would
make this a joke. When it does not come, she
puts down her phone.

"She's a little girl."

"I'm aware."

"What happened?"

"I asked to go past. She blocked the passage. Everyone
watched."

"Did her mother move her?"

"Eventually Carla did."

The waiter brings two coffees they did not order. One
cup is set carefully before the woman in mourning; the
other lands near the professor's elbow, spilling a thin brown
crescent into the saucer.

The woman in mourning says, "Thank you," and the waiter
leaves.

The professor looks at the coffee. "No one told her
to apologize."

"Children don't understand apologies."

"Adults do."

Her friend's expression changes, not into disagreement exactly, but fatigue.
"What do you want me to say?"

The professor does not know. The possible answers all seem
excessive in the small room. She wants her friend to
turn toward the hidden table and ask what the child
has heard, what she has seen, why only one of
the two strangers has become bad. She wants her to
mention the name used at the door. She wants the
room to lose its confidence.

Instead she says, "Nothing."

Signora Carla returns with the bill and places it beside
the woman in mourning.

"This is too much," the woman says after looking at
it.

"The coffee is from me."

"You're charging us for two desserts."

"No, no, that is the cover."

"For four people?"

Signora Carla takes the bill and studies it. "A mistake."

The professor reaches for her wallet.

"You are my guest," her friend says.

"We'll divide it."

"No."

The owner returns with the corrected bill. She pats the
woman in mourning's cheek. "Next time bring your mother. Poor
soul. She must not stay alone."

She does not say goodbye to the professor.

Outside, the street seems brighter than before. A delivery scooter
blocks half the pavement, and they walk single file until
the corner. The professor feels lunch in her stomach, compact
and immovable.

"I'm sorry," the woman in mourning says.

"For what?"

"That she was rude."

"Who?"

"The child. Carla. Whoever."

The professor stops beside a row of parked cars. "It
wasn't only rudeness."

"What was it, then?"

"You heard what she called me."

"Moretta? She calls everyone something. She called me ciccia."

"It isn't the same."

"No," the woman says, after a pause. "It isn't the
same word."

The professor looks at her. Her friend's face is closed
now, as it had been on the bridge before she
saw her. Grief has given her a limited supply of
attention, and the professor understands that she has reached its
edge.

"I'm not asking you to conduct a trial," she says.

"I know."

"I just don't want to be told it was nothing."

"I didn't say it was nothing."

"You said the child was shy."

"That was before."

"And now?"

"Now I think she was badly behaved."

The professor begins walking again. Behind them, a truck reverses
with a series of shrill beeps.

At the bridge they slow. Their routes divide there: the
woman in mourning must go toward her father's flat; the
professor has arranged to spend the afternoon at the library,
though she no longer wants to read.

"When will I see you?" her friend asks.

"Whenever you're free."

"Friday?"

"Send me a message."

They embrace again. This time the woman in mourning holds
on longer, and the professor nearly speaks. She nearly says
that Rome is the only place she has chosen repeatedly,
the only place where streets encountered twenty years ago can
still make her feel recognized. She nearly says that affection
for a city may be no protection from it. But
her friend's father is dead, her marriage is breaking apart,
and these facts stand between them like objects too heavy
to move.

"Take care of yourself," she says.

"You too."

They cross in opposite directions.

On the far embankment, the professor finds a bench facing
the river. The wood is damp, but she sits. Above
the water, gulls turn in the pale air. A tourist
unfolds a map nearby and asks his companion where they
are. The professor knows the answer. She knows the older
name of the bridge, the date of its restoration, the
reason one arch differs from the others. Knowledge gathers in
her and offers no shelter.

The child's three-note chant returns, stripped now of the child's
voice. The professor hears it in the brakes of buses,
in the squeak of a bicycle, in the repeated cry
of a gull. She looks down at her hands and
feels humiliation arrive late, spreading with the dull heat of
a bruise. What hurts is not the word alone. It
is the room's patience with it, the soft laughter, her
friend's tired refusal to see beyond discourtesy.

Across the river, the woman in mourning sits on another
bench with her father's phone in her lap. Her mother
has asked her to clear it. There are condolence messages
from cousins, former colleagues, neighbors, a pharmacist, a man her
father knew in military service. They begin in different ways
and end alike: a good man, a dear friend, unforgettable,
generous, correct. Several are addressed directly to him, as if
he might still read them.

She opens each message, copies the sender's name into a
notebook, and adds a mark for those who must receive
a reply. The work steadies her. In the accumulation of
words she finds the outline of a father more affectionate
than the one she remembers, a man dispersed among other
people's gratitude.

On the opposite bank, beyond the traffic and the brown-green
water, her friend remains seated. Neither woman can see the
other clearly. Each appears only as one dark figure among
the bare trees, keeping still while Rome proceeds around her.

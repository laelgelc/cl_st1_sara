Among the many proofs which history affords of that constancy
wherewith a faithful wife may cleave unto her husband in
calamity, there are some which the heart can never contemplate
without mingled admiration and sorrow.

Arria, a Roman matron, beheld her husband Paetus condemned to
die. He shrank, as even brave men may shrink, when
the fatal hour drew near; whereupon she took the weapon
appointed for his death, plunged it into her own breast,
and, presenting it to him, said with a countenance undisturbed,
"Paetus, it is not painful." There was undoubtedly greatness in
the action, and pagan Rome has preserved her name with
honour; yet we must not confound courage with duty, nor
suppose that self-destruction is rendered lawful by affection. Arria could
show her husband how to die, but she could not
teach him how patiently to endure.

More blessed was Natalia, the wife of Adrian. Her husband,
newly converted to the Christian faith, was cast into prison,
scourged, and subjected to fearful torments. When word was brought
to Natalia that Adrian had been released, she trembled, not
with joy, but lest he had purchased liberty by denying
his Lord. She barred the door against him until she
learned that he came only to bid her farewell before
returning to martyrdom. Then did she accompany him, strengthen his
wavering spirit, and entreat him to remember the crown of
life promised to those who remain faithful unto death. After
his limbs had been broken and his hands severed, she
preserved one of those poor mangled hands as a sacred
memorial; and, refusing every later offer of marriage, lived out
her remaining years in widowhood and prayer.

Yet there is a devotion which, without approving a husband's
offence, neither abandons him to shame nor flies from the
spectacle of his punishment; and of this tenderer, more prolonged,
and perhaps more difficult fidelity, Gertrude von der Wart has
left an example worthy to be remembered whilst love and
compassion are honoured amongst mankind.

In the early years of the fourteenth century, Albrecht of
Austria wore the imperial crown. He was a stern prince,
little fitted to win affection, and his government was especially
hateful to the stout and honest inhabitants of the Swiss
valleys, who valued ancient liberty above the favour of emperors.
But public discontent was joined to a private quarrel of
inheritance. Johann of Hapsburg, the emperor's nephew, believed that possessions
justly belonging to him were withheld by his powerful kinsman.
His petitions were disregarded, his complaints answered with contempt, and
resentment, long nourished, ripened at last into a dark conspiracy.

Among those who became connected with Johann was Rudolf von
der Wart, a knight of honourable birth and hitherto unblemished
reputation. That he knew of some design against the emperor
cannot reasonably be denied; but he solemnly declared, even when
falsehood might have seemed his only hope of life, that
he had never understood murder to be intended. He believed,
as he said, that Albrecht was to be seized and
compelled to render justice. Alas! he who enters the path
of violent counsels can seldom choose where it shall end.
Evil men conceal from the less guilty the worst portion
of their purpose, and when blood hath once been shed,
all who stood near the assassin are stained in the
judgment of the world.

The opportunity came as Albrecht journeyed with a small attendance.
Johann and his companions fell upon him suddenly, and the
emperor sank beneath their weapons. Thus a prince, surrounded by
power and accustomed to command nations, perished by the hand
of his own kindred; and those who had imagined that
one murderous stroke would end their grievances found that it
had but opened the gates of vengeance.

The children of Albrecht pursued every person suspected of sharing
in the conspiracy. Leopold, his son, was relentless; but Agnes,
his daughter, surpassed even her brother in the fierceness of
her wrath. Houses were destroyed, estates confiscated, families reduced to
beggary, and the innocent made to suffer with the guilty.
It is recorded that Agnes afterwards founded a religious house
upon the spot where her father had fallen, as though
stones consecrated to devotion could atone for deeds from which
mercy had been banished. A holy hermit rebuked her with
courageous plainness.

"Woman," said he, "God is not served by blood. Thou
mayest build convents, enrich altars, and fill churches with chanting;
but no prayer rises acceptably from a heart that refuseth
compassion."

This was severe language to the daughter of an emperor;
yet it was righteous. Religion without forgiveness is but a
splendid garment cast over an unhealed wound.

Rudolf was taken and brought to judgment. There was little
hope for one accused in an hour when grief demanded
victims rather than truth. He confessed that he had been
with the conspirators, but denied all foreknowledge of their intention
to slay Albrecht. His protestations availed nothing. He was sentenced
to be broken upon the wheel and left suspended there
until death should release him.

Gertrude had meanwhile been placed under restraint, lest her presence
should disturb the execution or excite compassion for the condemned.
But love, when joined to courage and guided by duty,
discovers paths which vigilance overlooks. She escaped from confinement, travelled
with all possible speed, and reached the place of punishment
whilst Rudolf yet lived.

The wheel had been raised above the scaffold. Upon it
lay her husband, his limbs shattered, his body bound fast,
and his face already marked by that pallor which suffering
brings before death. Soldiers stood around; a rude crowd watched
from below; and the executioners, hardened by their dreadful office,
regarded the anguish of their victim with indifference.

Then Gertrude came through the multitude.

Her garments were disordered by haste, and her breath failed
her; but when she beheld Rudolf, she uttered no wild
cry. For one moment she clasped her hands as though
her heart must break; then, approaching the scaffold, she called
him by his name.

At the sound of that beloved voice, Rudolf opened his
eyes.

"Gertrude!" he exclaimed. "Why hast thou come? Oh, leave this
place. Let not thy gentle spirit be tortured by my
misery."

"I have come," she answered, "because thou art my husband.
I could not save thee from judgment; I will not
forsake thee in suffering."

The officers commanded her to withdraw. She knelt at the
foot of the wheel and refused.

"Woman," said one, "thou canst do him no service. Go,
ere force be used."

"I can pray," she replied; "I can speak comfort to
him; I can remain where God hath appointed my duty."

Even the roughest were for a little while silenced. Yet
Rudolf, fearing that she might be imprisoned or punished, entreated
her more earnestly.

"My beloved, save thyself. Remember that I am already dead
in the eyes of men. Carry not my sentence into
thine own life."

But Gertrude looked up at him with a tenderness stronger
than fear.

"Whilst thou hast breath, thou art my husband, and where
thou sufferest I will remain. If they slay me, I
will die with thee; if they spare me, I will
comfort thee until the end."

The daylight faded. The idle and curious departed, but Gertrude
stayed. A priest was permitted to approach and speak to
the dying man of repentance, forgiveness, and the mercy which
no earthly tribunal can withhold. Rudolf confessed whatever guilt belonged
to his rash association with violent men; but he still
protested that the emperor's murder had been hidden from him.

"I forgive those who have condemned me," he said, "as
I desire to be forgiven. May God judge between them
and me, not in wrath, but in mercy."

Night descended coldly. The wind rose and swept across the
open ground; rain followed, beating upon the scaffold and upon
the broken body which could neither turn nor seek shelter.
Gertrude removed her cloak and contrived to spread it over
him. When the gusts displaced it, she rose, though ordered
to keep back, and arranged it anew with patient hands.
She stroked the damp hair from his forehead and spoke
to him of the compassion of Christ, who had Himself
endured mockery, wounds, and death.

At intervals Rudolf's agony forced from him a groan; then
Gertrude prayed aloud, her voice sometimes lost in the storm,
yet never wholly ceasing. The sentinels changed, the torches burned
low, and the long hours passed. No sleep visited her.
She watched every movement of his face, and whenever consciousness
returned, he found her still beneath him.

Toward morning he whispered, "Water."

None had been provided. Gertrude implored the guards, but they
answered that the condemned man must receive no relief. She
looked about her and perceived rainwater gathered in a hollow
of the ground. Having no vessel, she drew off her
shoe, filled it as well as she could, and carried
the poor draught to the scaffold. Much was spilled before
she reached him, yet a little remained; and dipping her
fingers therein, she moistened his lips.

That small act of mercy, denied by armed men and
performed by a weary woman, is more worthy of remembrance
than many victories. Power had raised the wheel; love brought
the water.

Morning broadened into day. Persons came again to gaze, and
some mocked at Rudolf, calling him traitor and murderer. Gertrude
made no bitter answer. She only said, "Pray for him.
Whether guilty or innocent of the worst charge, he is
a soul passing into eternity."

There were those who turned away ashamed. Compassion is seldom
entirely dead, even in a crowd assembled to behold suffering;
it waits only for some nobler heart to awaken it.

The second day dragged onward. Rudolf still lived, though every
breath appeared his last. His wife, exhausted by cold, hunger,
and grief, leaned against the scaffold, but would not depart.
He begged her once more to seek shelter.

"Gertrude," said he, "thy face is pale. I cannot bear
that thou shouldst perish for me."

She answered, "Think not of my body. God gives strength
according to the need. When thou art at rest, I
too shall find rest."

Again came evening, and with it a sharper cold. She
wrapped the cloak about his limbs as far as their
cruel fastenings allowed, and repeated portions of Scripture which they
had learned in happier days. She spoke of their home,
not to awaken vain regret, but to assure him that
all its innocent joys had been gifts of God and
pledges of a better dwelling. Thus memory, which might have
increased despair, became in her mouth an instrument of consolation.

The second night was more terrible than the first, for
weakness had nearly deprived Rudolf of speech, and Gertrude could
not always tell whether he heard her. Still she prayed.
Sometimes she raised herself and listened for his breathing; sometimes
she laid her hand upon his brow; sometimes, overcome by
sorrow, she bowed her head and wept silently. Yet no
complaint against Providence escaped her lips.

At length another dawn touched the sky. Rudolf opened his
eyes with a clearness which had long been absent, and
looked upon his wife.

"My faithful Gertrude," he murmured, "thou hast been true unto
death."

"And beyond death," she replied, "through the mercy of God."

"Tell me once more that thou forgivest me for bringing
sorrow upon thee."

"I have nothing to forgive. Where thou didst err, God
forgive thee; where thou hast been falsely accused, God vindicate
thee. I love thee still."

A faint peace passed over his countenance.

"Then farewell," he said. "Thy love hath lightened the wheel.
May Christ receive me, and may He reward thee when
thy own hour comes."

She placed her hand within his, so far as the
bonds permitted, and commended his spirit to God. A few
moments later his breathing ceased.

Gertrude remained kneeling. The world had become strangely silent to
her, though men moved and spoke around the scaffold. At
last she arose, kissed the hand now insensible to her
touch, and suffered herself to be led away.

She did not seek revenge upon those who had condemned
Rudolf, nor did she spend her remaining days accusing Heaven.
Having buried earthly hope, she entered a convent, where prayer,
care of the sick, and charity toward the poor became
the quiet occupations of her widowhood. The sorrow which might
have hardened another heart only enlarged hers. She who had
carried water to one tormented sufferer learned to see in
every afflicted creature a claim upon her tenderness.

Thus Agnes and Gertrude, women alike in strength of will,
stand opposed before us. The one, clothed in princely power,
pursued vengeance and vainly sought to sanctify cruelty by religious
gifts; the other, poor and helpless beneath a scaffold, conquered
hatred by compassion. Agnes raised walls of stone; Gertrude built
within her soul a temple of faithfulness. And when at
last she was called from labour and mourning, we may
reverently trust that she entered that true rest where no
wheel is raised, no sentence unjustly uttered, and those whom
love hath kept faithful unto death are parted no more.

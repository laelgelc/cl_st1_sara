# The Comforts of Friendship

When Captain Cairnes died—which he did after forty-one years of
matrimony, taken for better or worse, with a considerable preponderance
of the latter—his widow found herself possessed of a square
brick house in Westbury, an income sufficient for one person
of disciplined habits, six silver teaspoons, a black cat, and
more leisure than she had expected to enjoy.

Mrs. Julia Cairnes was not, however, a woman to sit
down under leisure as under an affliction. Before the mourning
border had faded from her stationery, she had resumed her
duties in the Dorcas Society, the Foreign Missionary Circle, the
Ladies' Aid, the Flower Committee, the Temperance League, the Improvement
Association, and that nameless but powerful body which concerned itself
with the morals, maladies, marriages, debts, servants, gowns, and probable
destinies of every family in Westbury. She carried soup to
the sick, tracts to the doubtful, flannel to the poor,
advice to everybody, and returned home each afternoon with the
comfortable consciousness of having done her duty and learned the
news.

Yet there were hours, particularly after tea, when societies ceased
meeting, the minister's wife had been sufficiently visited, the cat
had gone to sleep upon the hearthrug, and even the
neighborhood had, for the time being, exhausted its capacity for
incident. At such moments Mrs. Cairnes would sit beneath the
shaded lamp, with her knitting arrested in her capable hands,
and listen to the solemn tick of the tall clock.

A clock, when one is alone, has an offensive way
of insisting upon the fact.

It was then that she thought most often of Sophia
Maybury.

Sophia had been Sophia Bell in those distant days when
she and Julia went to Miss Pender's school in matching
blue capes; when they divided peppermints during Scripture recitation, whispered
after candles-out at each other's houses, and vowed—as young girls
have vowed since the first two discovered that grown people
were unsympathetic—to live together forever. Life, with its usual disregard
of private arrangements, gave Julia to Captain Cairnes and Sophia
to Mr. Maybury. The friends went different ways, wrote at
first weekly, then monthly, then at Christmas, and afterward only
upon births, deaths, marriages, and those attacks of rheumatism which
seem, in advancing years, to claim the dignity of public
events.

Mr. Maybury had kept the Webster House in Bell's Crossing,
fourteen miles away. He was a mild, agreeable man who
liked a good table, a bright fire, a hand at
whist, and the society of travelers, provided they paid their
reckonings. Sophia, during two score years, had lived amid ringing
bells, slamming doors, steaming kettles, disputed bills, arrivals after midnight,
departures before dawn, horses in the yard, commercial gentlemen in
the parlor, and a cook who resigned every spring. She
frequently lamented the bustle. She longed, she said, for peace.

When Mr. Maybury died, Sophia received a life interest in
the Webster House, which was managed by a nephew; but
she could not remain there. Every chair recalled her husband,
every joint of meat suggested his carving, every pack of
cards seemed to wait for his deliberate hand. Worse still,
the nephew's wife had modern notions respecting curtains, breakfast, children,
and the proper place of an elderly relict in a
commercial establishment. Sophia took a room at Mrs. Peebles's boardinghouse,
where the coffee was weak, the conversation strong, and six
persons shared one bath on principles never clearly explained.

Then Julia wrote.

"My dearest Sophia," the letter began, "why should two lonely
women, who have loved each other from childhood and agree
upon all essential matters, remain separate? I have a house
too large for me. You have no home properly so
called. Come to Westbury. We shall talk together, read together,
sew together, attend church together, and pass a sweet, comfortable,
peaceful time until one of us is called Higher."

Sophia wept over the letter. Mrs. Peebles had served liver
for the third time that week, and the gentleman in
the next room coughed through the nights with a conscientious
regularity. A sweet, comfortable, peaceful time! She saw Julia and
herself beside an open window, the sunlight falling across their
work, while they spoke gently of the past. She would
no longer be required to hear Mrs. Peebles accuse the
grocer, the laundress, the gas company, and Providence before breakfast.

She answered at once.

Julia read the acceptance aloud to the cat.

"There, Pharaoh," she said, "we shall have somebody with us
now."

Pharaoh opened one yellow eye. He had not been consulted.

The village approved the arrangement in advance. Everybody remembered the
friendship of Julia Cairnes and Sophia Maybury, though most people
had not seen them together for thirty years. Miss Trimmer
called it beautiful. The minister preached the following Sunday upon
the consolations appointed for widowhood, and looked twice toward Mrs.
Cairnes's pew. Mrs. Larkin said it was well Julia had
found some use for the spare room. Mrs. Dodd wondered
whether Sophia paid board. Mrs. Abner Mills declared that two
women who had reached sixty-five without learning submission were not
likely to begin upon each other.

Mrs. Mills was considered sour.

Julia prepared the southeast chamber. She removed Captain Cairnes's boots
from the closet, though not without reflecting that Sophia had
always been somewhat careless of property. She put fresh muslin
curtains at the windows, a Bible and a volume of
sermons on the table, lavender among the sheets, and three
potted geraniums upon the broad sill. She also cleared one
drawer in the highboy and half a shelf in the
wardrobe. This seemed ample, for Sophia had written that she
meant to bring "very little."

Sophia arrived with two trunks, a hatbox, a shawl strap,
a workbasket, a framed portrait of Mr. Maybury, a foot
warmer, seven small parcels, and a rosewood box which she
would not entrust to the expressman.

The meeting at the station was affecting. Julia cried, "Sophia!"
Sophia cried, "Julia!" They embraced beside the baggage cart while
young Mr. Larkin, who had come for the trunks, looked
respectfully toward the telegraph office.

"My dear, you have not changed," said Sophia, when she
could see through her tears.

"You have grown a little thinner," said Julia, which was
not the same thing at all, "but we shall soon
set that right."

They drove home through the elm-shaded street. Julia identified every
house, its occupant, the occupant before that, and the reason
the former occupant had departed. The reason was not always
death.

At the gate she pointed out her lilacs, the vegetable
patch, the minister's chimney beyond the wall, and a pear
tree which had failed for nine years but which she
retained on principle.

"And here we are!" she said. "At home, Sophia—at home!"

Sophia pressed her hand. It was indeed a pretty house,
with white steps, green blinds, a brass knocker, and a
parlor so neat that one felt an apology might be
due for entering it. The carpet was dark red, the
chairs stood stiffly against the walls, wax flowers bloomed beneath
glass, and Captain Cairnes regarded the room from an enlarged
photograph in which his expression suggested that death had confirmed
all his worst opinions.

Pharaoh sat upon the sofa.

"What a very large cat," said Sophia.

"He is as gentle as a lamb."

Pharaoh rose, stretched, and fixed Sophia with the meditative hostility
of a landlord discovering an unauthorized tenant.

"I am not much accustomed to cats."

"You will adore him. Everyone does."

Pharaoh came forward and sniffed Sophia's skirt.

"He has left hairs upon me."

"Only two or three."

There were fourteen, but at the outset of domestic happiness
it is best not to count too closely.

The first evening fulfilled every expectation. They ate toast and
preserves from Julia's best china, exchanged recollections, corrected each other
upon the names and fates of school companions, and agreed
that the present generation possessed neither manners, gratitude, modesty, endurance,
handwriting, nor sound religious principles. They read the ninety-first Psalm
together. At bedtime Julia placed a candlestick in Sophia's hand
and kissed her.

"Good night, dear Sophia."

"Good night, dear Julia. I am so thankful."

Each went to bed believing the experiment blessed.

The first slight shadow fell at breakfast.

Julia's breakfast, after years of careful adjustment, consisted of oatmeal,
weak tea, and one slice of bread. On alternate days
there was an egg, unless eggs were dear. Sophia, accustomed
to the flesh-pots of the Webster House, looked about for
what she believed must follow.

"Shall I ring?" she asked.

"For what?"

"The remainder of breakfast."

Julia laughed. "My dear, this is the remainder."

Sophia also laughed, though not immediately. "I have been spoiled.
Mr. Maybury always liked something savory in the morning—a chop,
or a little fish, or kidneys when they were to
be had."

Julia's nostrils contracted at the last word.

"Such foods are unnecessary at our age."

"I have never found age to diminish appetite."

"No, but it should diminish indulgence."

The egg was divided. Julia cut it with strict justice,
and Sophia received the larger half; nevertheless, she spent the
morning remembering a certain broiled trout at the Webster House.

At dinner there was mutton. Sophia praised it warmly. On
Tuesday it appeared cold. On Wednesday it was minced with
potato. On Thursday it returned as broth, accompanied by dumplings
of a firmness which Julia attributed to wholesome flour. Friday
produced hash.

"We waste nothing here," Julia said with satisfaction.

"I see that," said Sophia.

She had begun to perceive that thrift, admired at a
distance, may at table take an alarmingly material form. Julia,
for her part, thought Sophia's palate luxurious. A widow dependent
upon a modest hotel income ought not to expect five
different deaths for one sheep.

Still, food was a small matter. They had affection, faith,
memories, companionship. They would talk together, read together, sew together.

But Julia went out.

She went to the Dorcas Society on Monday, the Ladies'
Aid on Tuesday, the minister's on Wednesday, the Temperance League
on Thursday, and on Friday to see Mrs. Peter Vane,
whose daughter had become engaged in circumstances requiring immediate investigation.
Saturday was occupied by marketing, distribution of church notices, and
a call upon a sick woman who had shown poor
judgment in the management of her illness. Sunday, naturally, belonged
to public worship, Sunday school, evening service, and conversation upon
the sermon.

Sophia was invited to all these activities and declined most
of them.

"I have passed so many years among people," she explained.
"I thought it would be restful to sit quietly."

"Of course, dear. Do exactly as you please."

Yet Julia returned from her errands to find Sophia wistful.

"You have been gone a very long while."

"Only two hours."

"Three hours and twenty minutes."

"I had no notion you were counting."

"I was not counting. The clock struck."

"Well, you might have come."

"And hear Mrs. Vane tell me that her neuralgia rises
from her left shoulder at new moon? My dear Julia,
I have heard symptoms enough in boardinghouses to last me
into eternity."

Julia took off her bonnet with offended briskness. "Mrs. Vane
is an excellent woman."

"I did not question her excellence."

"You spoke lightly of her affliction."

"I spoke lightly of her account of it."

"They are not the same thing?"

"Not to the hearer."

Julia hung up her shawl. Sophia had always been considered
sweet-tempered; but perhaps sweetness was easier to maintain when one
had servants, guests, and Mr. Maybury to humor every whim.

Sophia watched Julia close the cupboard door with unnecessary decision.
Julia had always been lively; but surely liveliness in childhood
had not meant rushing about the village all day and
returning laden with ailments, scandals, committee disputes, and particulars concerning
people whom Sophia did not know.

We shall not repeat all that Julia related over tea
concerning the Vane engagement. The privilege of privacy, though seldom
granted in Westbury, may at least be extended here.

The matter of the curtains followed.

On a golden October afternoon Sophia entered the parlor, drew
back the heavy curtains, raised the blind, and sat in
a wide pool of sunlight. She had arranged yellow chrysanthemums
in a blue bowl, placed Mr. Maybury's portrait upon the
table, and taken up a bit of embroidery. For the
first time since leaving Bell's Crossing, she felt tranquil.

Julia came in and stopped.

"Sophia!"

"What is it?"

"The sun!"

"Yes, is it not lovely?"

"It is upon the carpet."

"So it is."

"It will take every particle of color out."

Sophia looked down. "The carpet is exceedingly dark."

"That is why it has lasted. I have taken care
of it."

Julia lowered the blind, drew the curtains, and restored the
room to a respectable twilight.

"I cannot sew in this gloom," Sophia said.

"There is abundant light."

"For an owl."

"You may use the lamp."

"At three in the afternoon?"

"Oil is cheaper than carpet."

Sophia folded her embroidery. "Mr. Maybury always said sunshine was
the cheapest luxury."

"Mr. Maybury did not purchase this carpet."

It was the first time either had used the name
of the departed husband in argument. Sophia's face changed.

"No," she said. "He purchased many things, but never comfort
at the expense of cheerfulness."

Julia heard the rebuke, and Sophia intended that she should
hear it; nevertheless both ladies maintained that no rebuke had
been offered.

That evening Julia found the lamp turned too high. The
next morning Sophia found the curtains closed again. Thus began
a silent warfare of blind cords, lamp wicks, curtain rings,
shades, matches, and moral principles. Sophia opened; Julia shut. Sophia
brightened; Julia preserved. Each believed herself defending not merely preference
but civilization.

Pharaoh meanwhile declared war independently.

He slept upon Sophia's shawl, sharpened his claws upon her
trunk, drank from her water glass, and once deposited a
small dead mouse beside Mr. Maybury's portrait. Julia called this
last a tribute.

"He brings things only to those he loves."

"He may withhold his affection."

"You hurt his feelings."

"I had supposed he was asleep."

"Cats feel more than some persons imagine."

"Then he may imagine how I feel."

Julia gathered Pharaoh into her arms. "Poor innocent!"

Sophia looked at the creature's complacent face and thought innocence
had seldom been so well furred.

There were also the plants.

Julia's plants occupied the southeast chamber in winter because that
room received the morning sun. The three geraniums on Sophia's
sill proved merely an advance guard. As the weather cooled,
Julia brought in two fuchsias, a rubber plant, a fern,
a pot of ivy, four slips of wandering Jew, a
calla lily which had not bloomed in six years, and
an oleander afflicted with scale.

"They cannot stay in my room," Sophia said.

"They always have."

"But I stay here now."

"They purify the air."

"They fill the air with damp earth."

"They require sunlight."

"So do I."

Julia was wounded. Had she not opened her house, her
spare chamber, her very life? Was a faithful friend to
be weighed against a geranium? Certainly not. But neither, after
years of patient tending, was a geranium to be put
into the dark passage for a friend who might perfectly
well sit downstairs.

Two pots were removed. Julia spoke of the sacrifice to
no one except Miss Trimmer, Mrs. Larkin, the minister's wife,
and the treasurer of the Dorcas Society.

Sophia heard of it from all four.

"Julia tells me she has moved her beautiful fuchsias for
you," said Miss Trimmer kindly.

"Only two pots."

"She values them like children."

"Then it is fortunate they do not require schooling."

Miss Trimmer repeated this as an example of Sophia's wit.
By the time it returned to Julia, it had become
an attack upon the childless.

Village speech travels by immutable decrees of its own.

Yet the ladies still loved each other. They could sit
for an hour recalling Miss Pender's false front, the day
Julia fell through the schoolroom ice, the picnic at Burnt
Hill, and the young divinity student who had admired Sophia
and afterward married a missionary's daughter. Their laughter then was
genuine and young. But the past is a commodious country
because nobody must wash dishes there, regulate lamps, or decide
what shall be done with yesterday's gravy.

Cards precipitated another difficulty.

One rainy evening Sophia brought down a pack.

"Let us play cribbage," she said. "Mr. Maybury and I
always played after supper."

Julia drew back.

"Cards?"

"Only cribbage."

"Playing cards?"

"I know no other sort."

"I have never permitted them in my house."

Sophia stared. "We played old maid as girls."

"We were unconverted."

"At twelve?"

"The heart may be hardened early."

"My husband and I played every evening for twenty years."

Julia's expression made Sophia suspect that Mr. Maybury's present location
had become doubtful.

"There can be no harm in an innocent game," Sophia
continued.

"The instruments of vice cannot be made innocent by domestic
use."

"A knife may murder a man, but you used one
upon the mutton."

"That is different."

"It was certainly severe upon the mutton."

"Sophia!"

"Julia!"

They sat facing one another, each shocked by the other's
tone.

Julia spoke first. "I cannot compromise where principle is concerned."

"Your principles appear to multiply wherever my pleasures begin."

"And your pleasures are very numerous for one who professes
to desire peace."

Sophia's eyes filled. This was an advantage she disliked employing
but could not always prevent.

"I did not know, when you invited me, that I
was entering a convent."

"A convent! In a Protestant household!"

"I meant only—"

"I know what you meant. I suppose next you will
want dancing."

"I am sixty-eight."

"That has never restrained foolishness."

The cards went upstairs. That night Sophia played a solitary
game upon the counterpane, using the rosewood box as a
board and keeping one ear directed toward the passage like
a criminal. The absurdity of this made her angry; the
secrecy made her ashamed; and the memory of Mr. Maybury
counting "fifteen-two, fifteen-four" made her cry.

Julia, in the adjoining chamber, heard the faint movement.

She is playing, she thought. Under my roof!

Then she heard Sophia's sob and lay rigid in the
darkness.

Poor Sophia. Poor foolish, worldly, lonely Sophia.

In the morning Julia was especially kind and gave her
the entire egg. Sophia recognized the peace offering and disliked
herself for being appeased by it.

Winter drew the house closer around them. The street filled
with snow, the committee meetings became fewer, and the evenings
grew longer. They had desired leisure for companionship; now they
possessed it in such abundance that each began to value
solitude with almost sinful intensity.

Julia knitted rapidly by the lamp. Sophia sewed slowly. Pharaoh
occupied the warmest point between them.

"Mrs. Peebles had a letter from her niece," Julia would
begin.

"Indeed?"

"She says the young man is not what was represented."

"What young man?"

"The one in the shoe business."

"I do not know Mrs. Peebles's niece."

"You met her cousin."

"Did I?"

"At the church supper."

"There were many people."

"The tall woman with the wart."

"I do not remember."

Julia felt snubbed. Sophia felt persecuted. Silence followed. Then Sophia
sighed.

"Are you uncomfortable?" Julia asked.

"No."

"You sighed."

"Did I?"

"You have sighed seven times."

"I was not aware you were counting."

"I was not counting. I noticed."

Another silence.

Presently Sophia said, "Mr. Maybury used to read aloud on
winter evenings."

"What did he read?"

"Scott. Dickens. Sometimes the newspaper."

"I do not approve of novels."

"Mr. Maybury did."

"Men are often careless in such matters."

"He was not a careless man."

"I did not say he was."

"You imply more than you say."

Julia's needles clicked. "That is a gift I have never
been accused of possessing."

Sophia almost smiled; but she was determined to remain hurt
and therefore lost the opportunity.

Theology, which both had supposed an immutable foundation of harmony,
proved peculiarly treacherous. They agreed upon the authority of Scripture,
the necessity of salvation, the vanity of earthly things, and
the certainty of judgment; but Julia believed judgment would be
conducted according to strict regulations with which she was fortunately
acquainted, while Sophia entertained hopes of mercy that seemed to
Julia dangerously broad.

"The gate is narrow," Julia said.

"Yes," Sophia answered, "but we do not know that we
are appointed to stand beside it and measure everybody."

"I make no judgments."

"You make little else from breakfast until tea."

Julia dropped her knitting.

"If you consider my conversation uncharitable—"

"I consider it very well informed."

"What does that mean?"

"It means exactly what it says."

"It means something unpleasant. You have acquired a habit of
disguising unkindness as refinement."

"And you have acquired a habit of disguising curiosity as
Christian concern."

There are remarks which, once made, cannot be reclaimed by
any number of muffins. Both ladies knew this. Both immediately
wished the words unsaid. Neither apologized.

Instead Julia rose to mend the fire and said, "You
will find the room cold if you sit so far
from the stove."

Sophia moved her chair nearer.

This was their manner of forgiveness.

The village continued to praise the arrangement.

"How happy you must be together!" cried Mrs. Dodd.

"We are very comfortable," said Julia.

"It is a privilege to have an old friend always
at hand," said Sophia.

"Such perfect companionship!"

"Perfect," they agreed.

They meant, at that moment, that disagreement before Mrs. Dodd
would be intolerable.

In private, each began to form plans which did not
yet amount to escape. Julia wondered whether Sophia might spend
the summer at Bell's Crossing. Sophia considered whether Mrs. Peebles's
coffee had truly been as weak as memory represented. Julia
calculated the cost of extra food, oil, coal, and breakages,
then rebuked herself for calculation. Sophia looked at her trunks
and imagined packing them, then remembered the boardinghouse cough, the
liver, the common bath, and sat down again.

Moreover, affection remained. Julia warmed Sophia's bed when her rheumatism
troubled her. Sophia sat through two nights when Julia suffered
bronchitis, brewing drinks, reading Psalms, and keeping Pharaoh from the
blankets. Julia secretly ordered a small jar of the marmalade
Sophia liked. Sophia embroidered a spectacle case for Julia, though
Julia said the colors were too light to be serviceable.

Neither was ungenerous. Each merely wished to be generous according
to her own plan.

With spring came fresh disputes.

Sophia wished to take tea upon the porch. Julia said
the wind was treacherous.

Sophia wished to walk at sunset. Julia said evening air
bred chills.

Sophia wished to plant mignonette beneath her window. Julia had
reserved the bed for onions.

Sophia wished to invite the rector's widowed sister for a
quiet afternoon. Julia invited eight members of the Ladies' Aid
at the same hour because the parlor was already to
be used.

Sophia bought sardines. Julia called them extravagant.

Julia served turnips four times in nine days. Sophia called
them turnips every time, which Julia thought unnecessary.

Then came the affair of the blue vase.

Sophia's blue vase had stood in her room at the
Webster House. It was not valuable, but Mr. Maybury had
bought it during their wedding journey. One morning Julia placed
in it a bunch of lilacs and carried it to
the church for a missionary tea.

"I thought it would look so pretty," she explained.

"You took it without asking."

"I knew you would not object."

"Then asking would have been easy."

Julia stiffened. "I have allowed you the freest use of
everything I possess."

"That does not make what I possess common property."

"I was only trying to help the missionary cause."

"The missionaries have no claim upon my wedding vase."

"A vase is a vase."

"Then you might have taken your own."

"My own is too heavy."

Sophia said nothing. That silence, Julia felt, accused her of
theft, sacrilege, tyranny, and indifference to the late Mr. Maybury.
She fetched the vase from the church in a light
rain and set it upon Sophia's table.

"There," she said.

"Thank you."

"I hope no permanent injury has been done."

Sophia examined it. "There is a chip."

"The chip was there before."

"It was not."

"It must have been."

"I washed that vase every week for forty years."

"Then perhaps you chipped it washing."

Sophia turned pale. Julia went out in dudgeon. At the
gate she remembered that she herself had struck the vase
against the church door.

All morning she fought a battle between conscience and dignity.
Conscience prevailed at noon, though dignity negotiated terms.

"Sophia," she said after dinner, "it is possible that the
vase touched the door as I carried it. I cannot
say positively. If it did, I am sorry."

Sophia replied, "Thank you, Julia."

The words were correct; their temperature was not. Julia wished
she had left conscience defeated.

By early summer, the sweet, comfortable, peaceful time had become
a condition of mild bondage, tolerable because each woman supposed
the other dependent upon it.

Julia believed Sophia could not live alone. Sophia had never
managed money, ordered coal, hired repairs, dealt with insurance agents,
or killed a spider larger than a pinhead. Without Julia
she would be at the mercy of boardinghouses and designing
tradesmen.

Sophia believed Julia could not bear solitude. Beneath all her
societies and briskness, Julia was lonely. Without Sophia she would
dine upon scraps, talk to the cat, supervise strangers, and
listen to the clock.

Therefore each endured the other as an act of benevolence.

Then the Webster House burned.

The news arrived at seven in the morning by telegram.
The fire had begun in the kitchen, passed through the
rear stairwell, consumed the office and dining room, and left
by noon only two chimneys and the iron hotel sign
standing. No lives were lost. The building was uninsured.

Sophia read the message twice.

"No insurance?" Julia demanded.

"The premium was due last month. Edmund had postponed it."

"But your income!"

Sophia sat down. "There will be none."

For once all domestic grievances vanished. Julia put her arm
around her friend. Sophia leaned against her and trembled.

"What shall I do?"

"You shall do nothing," Julia said firmly. "You are here,
and here you remain."

"But I cannot pay anything."

"Have I asked payment?"

"No, but I have bought my own clothes and little
things. I cannot even—Julia, I have only seventeen dollars in
the bank."

"Then you will keep it."

"I cannot be a burden."

"Burden! Sophia, I will not hear the word."

Julia meant every syllable. In the swift expansion of catastrophe
she became her best self—decisive, loyal, tender, almost magnificent. She
saw at once what must be sacrificed: the new roof
postponed, meat reduced, one fire instead of two, the gardener
dismissed, perhaps the southeast chamber's carpet turned. Sophia should never
know anxiety. They had been friends from childhood. Providence had
placed one beneath the roof of the other. Was not
this the very purpose of friendship?

Sophia wept with gratitude and despair.

For three days Julia made plans. She calculated, economized, rearranged.
She told the minister's wife, in strict confidence, that Sophia
had lost everything but would always have a home with
her. The minister's wife told only those whose prayers might
be useful. By Sunday the whole village knew that Julia
Cairnes had undertaken the permanent support of Sophia Maybury.

"What devotion!" said Miss Trimmer.

"It is noble," said Mrs. Dodd.

"I always knew Julia had a large heart," said Mrs.
Larkin, who had often criticized the size of Julia's coal
orders.

Mrs. Abner Mills asked, "Has anybody inquired what Sophia intends?"

No one had.

Sophia herself hardly knew. Gratitude pressed upon her more heavily
than poverty. Until the fire, she had at least possessed
the secret possibility of departure. Her income, modest but regular,
had stood behind her like a half-open door. Now the
door had shut. Julia's house, Julia's mutton, Julia's curtains, Julia's
plants, Julia's committees, Julia's cat, Julia's theology—forever.

Forever is a solemn word at any age; at sixty-eight
it may be short, but it is quite long enough.

Julia began to speak of necessary economies.

"We shall manage beautifully," she said. "I have always maintained
that most people eat twice what they require."

Sophia looked at the oatmeal.

"We can close the southeast chamber next winter and sleep
together in mine. One fire will answer."

"Sleep together?"

"It is a large bed."

"I am wakeful."

"I sleep soundly."

"I know."

"And we shall not need two lamps. You may sit
with me every evening."

"Every evening."

"What a blessing that we are so congenial!"

"Yes," Sophia said. "A blessing."

That afternoon she took a walk alone. The sun was
warm, the roadside grass full of clover, and the air
moved freely over fields that belonged to no parlor and
required no curtains. At the far end of the village
stood the Westbury Home for Respectable Aged Women, a broad
white building with a veranda, twelve bedrooms, a common dining
room, and a garden.

Sophia had passed it often. Julia disapproved of it mildly
because its committee was controlled by ladies from the Episcopal
church.

Now Sophia stopped at the gate.

Three old women sat on the veranda. One knitted; one
shelled peas; one read a newspaper through steel spectacles. From
an open window came the unmistakable click of cards.

Cards!

A small woman in a lavender cap looked up.

"Good afternoon. Won't you come in?"

Sophia entered.

The superintendent, Miss Prout, showed her the parlor, the dining
room, the sunny bedrooms, the bath, the garden, and the
little cupboard assigned to each resident. There were rules, naturally,
but they were rules of an impersonal sort and therefore
less exasperating than another person's habits. Breakfast was varied. Residents
might receive visitors. They might go out after signing the
book. Cards were permitted except on Sundays. Curtains might be
opened.

"What is the entrance fee?" Sophia asked.

"One hundred dollars, with a small annual allowance if the
resident possesses it. In cases of reduced circumstances, the board
sometimes makes provision."

Sophia touched the watch pinned beneath her collar.

It was a heavy gold watch, Mr. Maybury's wedding gift,
seldom worn except on Sundays. She had always imagined leaving
it to his niece. For a moment her throat tightened.

"What if one could pay the entrance fee but nothing
more?"

"That has been arranged before."

The lavender-capped woman appeared in the doorway.

"Tell her about Thursdays," she said.

"We have music on Thursdays," Miss Prout explained.

"And whist on Saturdays," added the woman. "Mrs. Pike cheats,
but only when she is losing."

"I do not cheat," called a voice from the veranda.
"I miscount."

Sophia laughed. It was the first unguarded laugh she had
given in weeks.

She returned to Julia's at sunset.

"Where have you been?" Julia asked.

"Walking."

"For nearly three hours?"

"I stopped somewhere."

"You should not tire yourself. Now that you have had
this shock, I must watch you carefully."

Sophia looked at her old friend—the strong face, the anxious
eyes, the hand already reaching to straighten Sophia's collar. Julia
loved her. That was the difficulty. It would have been
easier to resist cruelty than devoted management.

The next morning Sophia went to the jeweler.

The watch brought one hundred and thirty-five dollars. She retained
the little key and chain. She then visited Miss Prout,
paid the entrance fee, completed the application, and learned that
a sunny room overlooking the garden would be free on
Monday.

Only after all was settled did she tell Julia.

They sat in the parlor. The curtains were closed against
the afternoon sun. Pharaoh occupied Sophia's chair, so she remained
standing.

"Julia, I am going to the Home on Monday."

Julia stared at her.

"The Home?"

"The Westbury Home."

"For Respectable Aged Women?"

"Yes."

"You?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

Sophia had prepared six explanations. All deserted her.

"Because I wish to."

Julia rose slowly. "You would rather live among strangers than
with me."

"They will not remain strangers."

"And I suppose I have remained one?"

"No, Julia."

"After I offered you my home, my support, everything I
have!"

"I know what you offered, and I shall never forget
it."

"You have forgotten already."

"I have not. I am trying to save our friendship."

"Save it? From what?"

Sophia looked toward the curtained windows, the rubber plant, the
shaded lamp, the cat.

"From too much of it," she said.

Julia's face reddened. "I do not understand."

"Yes, you do."

"I understand that you have deceived me."

"I did not know until yesterday."

"And how do you propose to pay for this grand
independence?"

"I sold my watch."

"Mr. Maybury's watch!"

"He gave it to me."

"To keep!"

"He gave it to me to use for my happiness.
I believe I have used it so."

Julia was too shocked for speech. Mr. Maybury, dead fifteen
years, had again been brought into the house and placed
on Sophia's side.

At last she said, "People will think I drove you
out."

"They need not know anything beyond the fact that I
preferred the Home."

"They will certainly know more than that. This is Westbury."

"I will tell them you begged me to stay."

"That will sound as if you had to beg me
to let you go."

"Then what shall we say?"

The question, being practical, steadied them.

They considered several forms of announcement. "Mrs. Maybury has elected
to enter the Home" sounded judicial. "Mrs. Maybury requires a
wider social circle" reflected upon Julia. "The arrangement was always
temporary" was false and could be disproved by letters. At
length they chose: "Mrs. Maybury, desiring to secure her future
upon an independent basis, has accepted a room at the
Westbury Home, with Mrs. Cairnes's affectionate approval."

Julia's approval was not yet affectionate, but she hoped to
acquire it before the sentence circulated.

For the remainder of the day they were ceremoniously polite.

That night Julia lay awake and listened. No cards moved
in the adjoining room. Sophia, too, was awake.

Julia thought of the empty chamber, the silent meals, the
long evenings, the village's questions. Ingratitude! Eccentricity! Pride! She had
opened her house. She had planned to divide everything. She
had even contemplated sharing her bed, an offer she now
recognized as heroic.

Then another thought appeared—small, indecent, liberating.

The southeast chamber would be free.

Her fuchsias could return to the window. The calla lily
might bloom. Pharaoh could sleep wherever he pleased. The parlor
curtains would remain closed. There would be no sardines, no
cards, no sighing, no portrait of Mr. Maybury upon the
dining-room mantel. She could attend the Ladies' Aid without calculating
how long she had been absent. She could eat the
same mutton for six days.

Julia turned upon her pillow and rebuked herself.

In the next room Sophia imagined the Home: the sunny
window, the garden, the card table, the little cupboard, the
other women talking on the veranda. She would possess only
one room, but it would be hers. No one would
count her lamp oil. No cat would lay tribute beside
her husband's picture. No geranium would dispute her house-room.

Then she remembered Julia warming her bed, bringing marmalade, standing
strong and tender beside her when the telegram came. Sophia
wept quietly.

Affection and relief, though commonly thought opposites, slept beneath the
same roof that night.

On Monday young Mr. Larkin came for the trunks. This
time there were still two trunks, a hatbox, a shawl
strap, a workbasket, six small parcels, and the rosewood box.
The framed portrait went in Sophia's lap.

At the door Julia and Sophia embraced.

"You will come tomorrow?" Sophia asked.

"If you are settled."

"Come whether I am settled or not."

"You must send word if you need anything."

"I shall."

"And do not sit in a draft."

"I shall sit where I please."

"Sophia!"

Sophia smiled.

Julia smiled also, unwillingly at first and then with affection.

When the wagon disappeared, Julia entered the house. The silence
met her, but it was not the old silence. It
seemed broad, clean, almost musical.

Pharaoh came into the hall.

"Well," Julia said, "we are alone again."

Pharaoh rubbed against her skirt.

She went upstairs, opened the southeast chamber, and began moving
plants. The fuchsias returned first, then the fern, the ivy,
the oleander, the wandering Jew, the calla lily, and three
geraniums. She opened the wardrobe, spread her winter quilts upon
the shelves, removed the last of Sophia's hairpins from the
bureau, and stood in the center of the room with
her hands upon her hips.

Her house.

At four o'clock she attended a committee meeting and remained
until six without once looking at the clock.

At the Home, Sophia's room proved smaller than Julia's spare
chamber and infinitely more spacious. She drew the curtains wide.
Sunshine lay across the faded carpet without objection from anyone.
She placed Mr. Maybury's portrait on the bureau, the blue
vase beside it with three sprays of mignonette, and the
rosewood box upon the table.

At supper there was fish, potatoes, stewed tomatoes, bread, and
custard.

"Do they always give so much variety?" Sophia whispered to
the lavender-capped woman, whose name was Mrs. Bellows.

"My dear, this is Monday. Wait until Thursday. We have
chicken."

Afterward four ladies played whist.

Mrs. Pike did not cheat, precisely, but she possessed a
flexible understanding of the deal. Sophia enjoyed herself exceedingly.

Julia visited the next afternoon carrying marmalade, two sermons, and
a cutting from the healthier fuchsia.

Sophia received the marmalade warmly, the sermons gratefully, and the
cutting with composure.

"You have a great deal of sun," Julia observed.

"Yes."

"It will fade the carpet."

"It belongs to the Home."

Julia considered this and conceded the force of it.

They sat on the veranda for an hour. Sophia described
the residents. Julia supplied what was known in the village
concerning their former histories. Mrs. Bellows joined them and proved
to be a distant cousin of someone Julia had once
assisted during scarlet fever. By the end of the visit,
Julia had been invited to the Thursday music and had
volunteered to advise the Home committee about its coal supplier.

The following day Sophia went to Julia's.

The parlor was dim, the carpet safe, Pharaoh enthroned, and
the plants restored upstairs. Julia served cold mutton and pickles.
Sophia ate with appetite, knowing chicken awaited her on Thursday.

"How pleasant everything looks," she said.

"I have put the house to rights."

"So I see."

"And you are comfortable?"

"Very."

"Not lonely?"

"Not at all. Mrs. Pike snores through the wall."

"That does not sound agreeable."

"It reminds me that I am not alone."

They exchanged news. Julia recounted a dispute in the Flower
Committee; Sophia told of Mrs. Bellows's courtship by a drummer
in 1859. Each listened with fresh interest because neither was
obliged to continue listening after the visit ended.

At five Sophia rose.

"You need not hurry," Julia said.

"I promised Mrs. Pike a game before supper."

"Cards?"

"Yes, Julia."

"Not for stakes?"

"Only buttons."

Julia shook her head, but she was smiling. "I fear
you are incorrigible."

"I have attained the privilege."

After Sophia left, Julia lowered the lamp, fed Pharaoh, and
ate a small bowl of the previous day's turnips. Peace
settled upon the house—not the grand peace of sentimental expectation,
but the modest, serviceable peace of having one's belongings, meals,
hours, windows, pets, plants, and opinions under one's own government.

Sophia likewise sat beside her open window, shuffled the cards,
and felt the evening sun upon her hands.

Thereafter the two friends met almost every day.

Julia walked to the Home after committee meetings. Sophia came
to tea on Tuesdays and Sundays. They attended church together,
though Sophia occasionally went with Mrs. Bellows to the Episcopal
service, a liberty Julia bore with visible fortitude. Julia brought
flowers, preserves, village news, and advice. Sophia supplied sympathy, card
scores, hotel reminiscences, and a readiness to admire sunsets which
Julia could enjoy well enough when no carpet was endangered.

Separated, they recovered all the virtues they had lost in
proximity. Julia's energy became entertaining rather than oppressive. Sophia's delicacy
became charming rather than burdensome. Julia could praise independence because
it no longer inconvenienced her. Sophia could praise generosity because
she was not required to live upon it.

They even spoke tenderly of the months together.

"We had some beautiful evenings," Sophia said once.

"We did," Julia replied.

Neither specified which evenings.

The village regarded their friendship as remarkable.

"Julia goes to see Sophia every day," said Miss Trimmer.

"And Sophia is forever at Julia's," said Mrs. Dodd.

"It is touching," said the minister's wife. "Separation has not
weakened their devotion."

"Nothing could," said Mrs. Larkin.

Only Mrs. Abner Mills, who had been considered sour and
therefore permitted a certain liberty of truth, remarked that some
friendships flourished best with a road between them.

No one attended to her.

Thus Julia Cairnes and Sophia Maybury became celebrated throughout Westbury
as an example of perfect constancy: two hearts united from
childhood, consoled in widowhood, harmonious in faith, tender in adversity,
and inseparable in age.

And so they were—provided each could go home before supper.

### Hanrahan's Song Against Age

It was after many years of roads and fairs and
wakes, and sleeping under hedges or in the lofts of
friendly houses, that Owen Hanrahan came to a little empty
cabin between Ballygawley and the foot of Slieve Daeane. There
was a roof of black thatch on it, though the
rain came through in two places, and there was a
hearth, and a stool with three legs, and a patch
of ground where nettles and dock leaves had taken possession.

And Hanrahan said, "It is enough for a man that
has followed queens in dreams and women in the world,
and has had neither thanks nor peace from the one
or the other."

He cut the nettles, stopped the holes with rushes and
clay, and borrowed a pot from a widow. The people
brought him turf, eggs, oaten cake, and sometimes a cup
of sour milk. In return he gathered the children into
the cabin and taught them their letters, and the histories
of Cuchulain and Finn, and a little Latin he had
kept from his young days. But he taught them more
songs than prayers, and more of the doings of poets
than of kings; for it is I know he believed
a poet's word would outlast a king's stone house.

So the seasons went over him quietly. He had a
bed of heather in the corner, a brown hen scratching
at the threshold, and smoke rising from his roof in
the evenings. And some said wandering Hanrahan was settled at
last.

But there is no settled place for a man whose
pride is quicker than his judgment.

On the night before May Day he went to a
house beyond Collooney, where there was music and dancing, and
where they had asked him to make verses for a
wedding. He stayed until the candles were gutters of wax
and the young people had gone out under the whitening
sky. It was near sunrise when he took the mountain
road home, his coat about him and his head heavy
with drink and remembrance.

The hawthorn was white along the ditches, and birds were
calling from every little wood, and the grass had the
shining greenness that comes only once in the year. Yet
Hanrahan noticed none of these things, for he was thinking
of a girl with yellow hair who had danced with
every young farmer in the house and had not once
looked toward him.

A hare ran across the road before his feet and
stopped upon a bank. It looked back at him with
one dark eye.

"Go your way," said Hanrahan. "I have followed your kind
before, and little good came of it."

The hare went into the hazels, but he heard a
laugh where it had vanished, or thought he heard one,
and this left an uneasiness upon him.

It was not long till he came to a well
under an old ash tree, and there he saw a
young woman sitting with her shawl drawn over her head.
She was crying, but when she heard his step she
dried her eyes and stood up.

"Good morrow, Owen Hanrahan," she said.

"Good morrow, Nora Burke," said he, for he knew her
family. "And what sorrow is on you at the beginning
of May, when sorrow should be shut in the house
and gladness walking the roads?"

"My father will have me married to Paddy Doe of
Knocknaroy," she said. "He has cattle in three fields, and
money put away, and a slated house. My father says
a full haggard is better than a handsome face."

"And is Paddy Doe hateful to you?"

"He is stiff in the knees and grey in the
beard. His breath whistles, and he counts every potato put
into the pot. He has buried two wives, and maybe
it is a third grave he is wanting filled."

Hanrahan lifted his head, for there was nothing he liked
better than being called to defend beauty against wealth.

"O Nora Burke," he said, "there were women before you
that were sold for cattle, and kingdoms went down because
of it. Helen was carried over the sea, and Deirdre's
sorrow reddened the swords of Ulster. Let your father make
no delay in hearing this: gold can buy a roof,
but it cannot buy the month of May."

"It is a song I want from you," said Nora.
"Make a song that will put shame on Paddy Doe,
and the boys will sing it at the fair. Then
maybe he will leave me in peace."

"That I will do," said Hanrahan. "I will make such
a song as will loosen the slates on his house
and turn his cattle's faces from him. The curse-making bards
had power to raise blemishes on kings, and there is
some spark of that power in me yet."

Nora smiled through her tears. "You will know well what
to say of an old man, Hanrahan, being near his
age yourself."

There was no more malice in her words than there
is in a bird picking a seed from the road.
But Hanrahan stood as if she had struck him.

"Near his age?" he said.

"Indeed, I meant no offence."

"Paddy Doe is an old stump with moss on him."

"He is not so much older than yourself, maybe."

Hanrahan turned from her and looked into the well. There
was his own face trembling in the water, lined about
the eyes, the beard mixed with grey, the cheeks fallen
in from what they had been. He put up his
hand and felt the loose skin beneath his chin.

And all at once many small things came into his
mind: how girls no longer made room for him beside
the fire; how married women called him "poor Hanrahan" and
gave him food; how young men listened to his songs
but laughed when he spoke of love; how his back
ached after a night on hard ground; and how, that
very morning, the yellow-haired girl had passed him as if
he were a gatepost.

"Nora Burke," he said, and his voice was low, "there
was a time women did not speak to me of
age."

"I ask your pardon," she said. "It was thoughtless talk."

But he went by her without farewell, and all the
whiteness of the hawthorn seemed an insult to him.

When he came to his cabin the children were waiting,
seven or eight of them, with books under their arms
and mischief in their eyes. Hanrahan sent them away, saying
there would be no schooling until the next day. Then
he shut the door and sat before the cold hearth.

He thought of the eagle that had lived since before
the saints came into Ireland, its feathers dropping one by
one upon a bare mountain. He thought of the pike
in Lough Gara, long as a man's arm and scarred
with years, lying among weeds in the dark water. He
thought of old yew trees twisted over churchyards, and of
ravens croaking on ruined towers.

"Why should anything live beyond its beauty?" he cried. "What
right has Time to leave the husk when the grain
is gone?"

He walked up and down the narrow floor, naming every
old man he disliked and every ache he feared. There
was Paddy Doe with his buried wives; Mat Hession, whose
nose had a drop forever hanging from it; Darby Flynn,
bent double over his stick; and old Shawn Grogan, who
had three teeth and used them all for gossip.

And because anger is a strong wind in the house
of poetry, the words began coming to him. He wrote
them with a burned stick upon the whitewashed wall when
paper failed him, and before noon he had made a
song.

The next day he called the children in. He stood
before them with his coat buttoned and his hair combed
back, as solemn as a priest at Mass.

"Listen now," he said, "and keep every word, for this
is a judgment on Age, and Weariness, and the greedy
hand that would lay winter upon spring."

Then he sang:

> A curse on the eagle that moults on the
stone,
> And counts all the kingdoms that wither and groan;
> A curse on the pike in the cold reedy
mere,
> That has swallowed young fishes a hundredth year.
>
> A curse upon Darby, bent down like a bow,
> And Mat with the winter hung under his brow;
> On Grogan, whose teeth are but three in a
row,
> And twice seven curses on old Paddy Doe.
>
> His knee gives a crack and his shoulder a
sting,
> Yet he reaches a claw for the youth of
the spring;
> May his money grow mould and his chimney smoke
low,
> And no girl share the blanket of old Paddy
Doe.
>
> But a blessing on blossom that whitens the spray,
> For it dies in its beauty at ending of
May;
> No wrinkle can touch it, no cold blood can
slow—
> God shelter the blossom from old Paddy Doe.

The children were silent for the length of a breath,
and then they shouted with laughter. Before an hour had
passed they knew the song better than their alphabet.

"Go east and west with it," said Hanrahan. "Sing it
at the crossroads and beside the wells. Give it to
every beggar and cattle-driver between Tubbercurry and Ballysadare. And if
any man asks where it came from, say Owen Hanrahan
made it by the authority of poets."

They went like starlings let loose from a barn.

By evening the boys minding cows were singing the second
verse. By the next day it was heard at the
forge, and the blacksmith beat time to it on the
anvil. A blind fiddler carried it to the market. Women
sang the blessing on the blossom while washing clothes in
the river, and young fellows added verses concerning Paddy Doe's
bald head and the squeaking of his boots.

Nora's father heard it and swore. Paddy Doe heard it
and swore longer.

But it was not Paddy alone who felt the hook.
Darby Flynn's grandchildren sang the verse outside his door. Someone
chalked an eagle upon Mat Hession's gate. Shawn Grogan found
three white pebbles set upon his window ledge, in remembrance
of his teeth. And every grey head in the district
began to think Hanrahan's song had been aimed at him.

On the third evening a little boy came running to
the cabin.

"They are coming, Master Hanrahan!"

"Who is coming?"

"All the old men."

Hanrahan went to the door. Along the road, under a
sky red with sunset, there came a company of men
with sticks. Paddy Doe was at their head in a
tall hat, carrying a blackthorn knobbed like a bishop's crozier.
Behind him limped Darby Flynn, Mat Hession, Shawn Grogan, and
a score more from scattered farms, some bent, some stiff,
some coughing, but every one driven onward by rage.

There were bald heads, white whiskers, watery eyes, hooked shoulders,
swollen knuckles, and legs that went unwillingly beneath them. Yet
as Hanrahan watched, they seemed to grow beyond themselves. They
were no longer merely neighbours he had mocked. They were
Old Age and Time and Weariness and Sickness, marching together
to claim him.

He barred the door and looked about for a weapon.
There was only the school rod, the pot, and a
sod of turf.

Then a stone struck the wall.

"Come out, curse-maker!" cried Paddy Doe.

"Come out till we teach you respect for your elders!"
shouted Grogan.

"I respect elders that have wisdom," Hanrahan called through the
door, "but not withered hands reaching for young women."

A roar answered him, and the door shook beneath the
first blow of a stick.

Hanrahan remembered the back window, little more than a hole
where the clay had fallen away. He pushed through it,
tearing his coat, and ran into the field. A child
saw him and gave a warning cry, but by then
he was over the ditch and among the hazels.

From there he looked back.

The old men broke open the door. They dragged out
his stool, his heather bed, his books, and the little
board on which the children wrote their letters. Paddy Doe
kicked apart the hearth. Then Mat Hession thrust a burning
wisp of straw beneath the thatch.

The flame went slowly at first, blue and uncertain. But
the evening wind took it, and in a moment the
roof was red. Sparks rose among the May branches, and
the hen flew squawking from the smoke. The children cheered
until they remembered it was Hanrahan's home, and then some
began to cry.

He stood hidden while the walls blackened and the roof
fell inward. All his settlement, his school, his pot, his
poor bed, and the little peace he had gathered after
so many roads went up together into the darkening sky.

And he thought, "My song has power indeed, for it
has brought all Age against me; but it had not
the wisdom to tell me I was inside the thing
I cursed."

When night had come, he went away over the mountain
with no possession but the torn coat on his back.
At the edge of the field a hare crossed before
him again, and this time he did not rebuke it.

"O Blessed Queen of Heaven," he said, "keep me from
old eagles, old fishes, old trees, old lovers, old enemies,
and most of all from the old man walking within
myself. And if there is no shelter from him, let
my feet be light and the roads long to the
end of life and time."

Behind him the last of the thatch fell in a
shower of sparks, and before him the white May blossom
shone along the road.

So Owen Hanrahan became a wanderer once more.

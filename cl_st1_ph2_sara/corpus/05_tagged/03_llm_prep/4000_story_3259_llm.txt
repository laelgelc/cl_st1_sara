## THE PRICE OF CLEOPATRA

### I

Charsky was one of those unfortunate men whom nature has
endowed with a talent and society has punished for possessing
it. He wrote verses.

This occupation, innocent enough when practised by a councillor's daughter
in an album, by a guardsman during his confinement to
barracks, or by a vice-governor on the occasion of His
Majesty's birthday, became in Charsky a settled vocation; and for
that reason alone it exposed him to attentions which his
pride found intolerable. Wherever he went, the same questions awaited
him.

"Haven't you written anything new?"

"When will you give us another little poem?"

"Do improvise something upon my fan!"

One lady begged him to celebrate the death of her
spaniel; another required an inscription for a grotto on her
estate; a young cornet, having quarrelled with his mistress, demanded
a satire upon the entire female sex. Even people who
had never opened a book considered themselves entitled to discuss
his inspiration, as though poetry were a mineral spring maintained
for the refreshment of the public.

Charsky suffered more acutely because he belonged by birth, fortune
and inclination to the fashionable world. His house stood in
one of the best quarters of St. Petersburg; his carriage
was faultless; his linen came from Paris; his dinners, though
he understood little of cuisine, were praised by people who
understood still less. He attended races and spoke of English
horses, although he could not distinguish a hunter from a
post-horse without first observing the saddle. At cards he lost
with a composure mistaken for skill. In the theatre he
occupied a conspicuous box and listened chiefly to the conversation
behind him. In short, he spared no pains to pass
for an agreeable and useless young man.

But fame, that indiscreet chambermaid, continually betrayed him.

A new poem, copied by a friend, would circulate through
twenty drawing rooms before Charsky had revised its final stanza.
An elegy written at midnight in solitude was recited next
morning by a young lady between a polonaise and supper.
Gentlemen who would have been offended had anyone inquired into
their accounts asked Charsky, without ceremony, how much he had
received from his publisher. Certain venerable Maecenases offered advice. One
recommended that he write something national; another urged him toward
morality; a third, who had once seen Goethe in Carlsbad,
assured him that genius required simplicity and thereafter addressed him
as "our Russian Goethe."

We must confess that Charsky did not bear these trials
with philosophical mildness. He denied his poems, mocked poetry, and
sometimes spoke of literature with a coarseness borrowed from men
who imagine contempt to be a proof of worldly experience.
If a stranger praised him, he grew cold; if an
old acquaintance asked, "Haven't you written anything new?" he became
positively savage.

Yet beneath this affectation lay neither emptiness nor ingratitude. Charsky
loved poetry with the jealous devotion of a man unwilling
to see his mistress dragged into a rout. At certain
hours he locked the doors of his study, dismissed his
servants, laid aside the embroidered dressing gown in which he
received intimate visitors, and became what he was ashamed to
appear—a poet. Then the streets, the dinners, the officers' jokes,
the ladies' smiles, even his own vanity disappeared in sweet
oblivion. He emerged from such hours pale, happy and exhausted,
and would have forgiven the world everything had the world
not immediately asked:

"Well, my dear fellow, haven't you written anything new?"

One morning in November, while Charsky was standing before a
mirror and allowing his valet to arrange a neckcloth of
serious importance, a servant entered to announce that a foreigner
wished to see him.

"What sort of foreigner?"

"An Italian, sir, I believe."

"Has he a name?"

The servant looked at the card.

"Giovanni Battista—there is more, sir, but the writing—"

"Send him away. Say I am not at home."

"I already told him so, sir. He answered that he
would wait."

Charsky frowned. He expected to dine with a newly arrived
diplomat, and his waistcoat had not yet been chosen. Nothing
renders interruption more offensive than the conviction that one is
engaged in a necessary frivolity.

"What does he look like?"

"Poorly dressed, sir. Perhaps a musician."

The valet smiled. Charsky, who noticed the smile in the
mirror, was irritated by it.

"Show him into the study."

A few minutes later he entered that room and found
a man of about thirty-five standing near the door. The
stranger was tall and thin. Thick black hair fell in
disorder about his forehead; a beard, equally dark, sharpened the
pallor of his face. His clothes had once possessed elegance,
but travel and misfortune had disputed them between themselves. The
seams of his black coat were whitened; his boots bore
the mud of several capitals; in his cravat glittered a
stone which proclaimed itself a diamond with the same insistence
by which certain adventurers proclaim themselves counts.

On seeing Charsky, the stranger bowed profoundly.

"Signor," he began in Italian, "forgive my boldness. I have
come to appeal to a brother."

Charsky understood Italian perfectly, but the word brother displeased him.
Brotherhood is often claimed with particular eagerness by persons who
intend to borrow money.

"You are mistaken," he replied in French. "I do not
have the honour of knowing you."

"Ah, but Apollo knows no frontiers!" cried the Italian, placing
one hand upon his breast. "I am a poet."

Charsky's face grew colder.

"A poet?"

"An improvisatore, signor. I arrived from Naples by Vienna and
Warsaw. In Moscow I was told, 'Go to St. Petersburg;
there is Charsky, the celebrated poet, generous protector of genius—'"

"Who told you that?"

"Everyone! Fame, signor, fame!"

Charsky rang the bell.

The Italian stopped. His expressive face, which a moment before
had shone with confidence, changed so quickly that Charsky felt
an involuntary embarrassment. Pride remained in the stranger's posture, but
alarm had entered his eyes.

"I cannot be of service to you," said Charsky. "You
have been badly advised."

The servant appeared.

"Show this gentleman out."

The Italian bowed, not now with theatrical grace but with
painful dignity.

"Capisco. I understand. Poverty is a bad letter of introduction."

He turned toward the door. At that moment his worn
cuff slipped back, revealing a wrist of almost feminine delicacy;
Charsky noticed that the man's hand trembled. On the table
lay a purse, negligently thrown there after the previous night's
cards. The stranger did not glance at it.

"Wait," said Charsky.

The Italian stopped.

"You call yourself an improviser. What precisely do you intend
to do in Petersburg?"

"To give a public performance, if I can find a
hall, an audience, patronage—one name upon the notice, signor! In
Italy I have improvised at Florence, Milan, Venice. At Paris
they applauded me. Here I know no one. The landlord
asks payment; the printer asks payment; even hunger, which elsewhere
sometimes grants credit, is in this country punctual."

Charsky dismissed the servant with a gesture.

"And you compose verses upon any subject proposed?"

"Any subject."

"In Italian?"

"Naturally."

"And instantly?"

The stranger smiled. It was the first unstudied smile Charsky
had seen upon his face.

"Give me a theme."

Charsky examined him. The request was so direct that suspicion
yielded to curiosity.

"Not now," he said. "Come this evening at seven."

Hope flashed across the Italian's features.

"At seven! Precisely! Ah, signor, you shall see—"

"And meanwhile," Charsky added, turning away, "my servant will arrange
a room for you at the Hôtel de Paris."

The Italian's gratitude burst forth in exclamations, bows and half-finished
phrases. Charsky cut them short. He was ashamed both of
his first cruelty and of the kindness by which he
had corrected it; for there are men who conceal their
good actions almost as anxiously as others conceal their bad
ones.

When the improvisatore had gone, the valet entered carrying two
waistcoats.

"The pearl-grey, sir, or the black?"

"The black."

"The Italian was a poet, sir?"

Charsky looked at him sharply.

"No. A dealer in coral."

### II

At seven o'clock the Italian returned. He had evidently devoted
part of Charsky's assistance to his appearance. His coat had
been brushed; his beard was trimmed; fresh linen set off
the southern pallor of his face. The false diamond, however,
still burned in his cravat like a tiny lighthouse warning
the experienced away from dangerous shores.

Charsky received him in the study. The lamps were shaded;
the curtains closed against the blue winter darkness; books, manuscripts
and scattered sheets covered the desk. No fashionable visitor was
ever admitted there. The Italian glanced about him and understood
that he had crossed a boundary.

"You wished to test me," he said.

"I wished to hear you."

"È lo stesso—almost the same. Give me a subject."

Charsky sat down. He had spent the afternoon inventing themes
and rejecting them. Love seemed commonplace, death pretentious, spring impossible
in November, and the taking of Constantinople too much like
an examination in history.

"At what age," he said at last, "would you say
that a poet's genius is greatest?"

The improvisatore considered.

"That is not a theme. It is a question for
an academy."

"Then make it one. The poet chooses neither the hour
of inspiration nor the season of his power."

"Good," said the Italian quietly. "Very good."

He walked twice across the room. All restlessness left him.
His shoulders straightened; his head sank slightly; his lips moved
without sound. Charsky watched with professional curiosity and, we must
admit, professional distrust. He expected preparatory tricks—the raised eye, the
hand upon the brow, the noble sigh by which mediocrity
announces its intention of being sublime.

There was nothing of the kind.

The Italian stopped near the lamp. His face grew pale;
drops of perspiration appeared at his temples. For several seconds
he seemed to listen to something at a distance. Then
his eyes flashed.

He began.

At first the verse flowed slowly, as though feeling its
road through darkness; then image summoned image, rhythm strengthened rhythm,
and the whole poem rushed onward with an ease that
concealed its power. He spoke of the youthful poet who
waits impatiently for the god, and of the old singer
whom the god visits at the grave; of genius as
a flame which neither youth commands nor age extinguishes; of
the lyre hanging silent through years of feasting, until one
accidental breath wakens all its strings. There were bold transitions,
noble harmonies, and one comparison—between inspiration and lightning imprisoned in
winter clouds—which Charsky would gladly have stolen, had envy not
been lost in astonishment.

The final line fell. The Italian lowered his head.

Silence followed.

"Well?" he asked.

Charsky did not answer.

"Well?" repeated the improvisatore, approaching him. "Is it good? Tell
me! One cannot judge one's own child at the instant
of birth."

"It is extraordinary," said Charsky.

"Truly?"

"Truly."

The Italian seized his hand.

"I knew it! No—I did not know it; one never
knows. During the first lines I feared the comparison of
the lamp was weak, so I abandoned it. Did you
observe? Then came the winter cloud. That was better. The
conclusion—perhaps too abrupt?"

"It was perfect."

"Perfect!" cried the Italian. He walked about the room, laughing
and muttering verses to himself. "Ah, when it comes, when
it comes! There is no emperor richer than I!"

Charsky looked at him with enthusiasm. At that moment the
shabby coat, the theatrical bows, the counterfeit jewel—all disappeared. Before
him stood a man transfigured, a chosen vessel of that
incomprehensible force which the wise explain after it has vanished
and the poet obeys while it is present. Charsky felt
the generous delight of recognising a gift different from, and
perhaps greater than, his own.

"My dear signor," he said warmly, "your success here is
certain. We shall arrange a performance. Princess N—— gives a
salon next Thursday; all Petersburg will be there. If she
permits you to appear, fashion will do the rest."

"All Petersburg?" asked the Italian.

"All that calls itself Petersburg."

"How many persons?"

"Two hundred, perhaps three."

The improvisatore's eyes became attentive.

"And the tickets?"

"Tickets?"

"What price should one ask? Five roubles? Ten? At Paris,
under favourable circumstances—"

Charsky's exaltation descended to earth with unpleasant speed.

"We shall speak of that later."

"Certainly, certainly! Art first. But one must calculate. If there
are three hundred persons at ten roubles, that is three
thousand. The hall, printing, lights, attendants—perhaps five hundred. If the
princess lends her salon, the expense is reduced. Does she
lend the candles also?"

Charsky stared at him.

The Italian, entirely occupied with arithmetic, took a pencil from
the desk.

"Suppose only two hundred attend. At five roubles—no, five is
too little. The public distrusts what is cheap. Eight roubles
is inconvenient for change. Ten is dignified."

"You spoke a moment ago of being richer than an
emperor."

"Precisely! But emperors must dine, and so must poets."

The answer was reasonable; nevertheless it offended Charsky. We willingly
pardon genius every vice except a concern for its own
dinner. A nobleman may spend half an evening discussing the
price of a horse he does not need, but if
a poet asks payment for the bread he does need,
the nobleman feels that the sanctity of art has been
insulted.

Charsky rose and went to the fireplace.

"You will leave these details to me."

"With joy, signor! Only do not allow them to place
me too near the doors. People come and go, there
is noise, the final rows do not hear; and if
admission is collected in the ante-room, one must have a
trustworthy person. At Bologna an impresario—ah, un ladro!—declared that half
the audience entered by invitation."

"I said I would arrange it."

The Italian instantly perceived the change in Charsky's tone. He
laid down the pencil.

"Forgive me," he said. "You think me avaricious."

Charsky made a polite gesture.

"You think it," repeated the improvisatore. "And perhaps you are
right. Yet inspiration lasts an hour; hunger returns every day.
When I improvise, I do not remember money. When I
have finished, I remember everything—the hotel, the tailor, the road
home, my mother in Naples. Is that so contemptible?"

There was neither declamation nor self-pity in his voice. Charsky
felt rebuked.

"No," he answered. "It is not contemptible."

"Among your great lords, a poet may be ashamed to
sell poetry because he has estates, peasants, houses, bankers. I
possess this." He touched his forehead. "If I do not
sell what is here, must I sell my coat? I
have nearly done so already."

Charsky laughed and held out his hand.

"You are right, signor. I was playing the Maecenas, which
is the most economical manner of playing the rich man.
We shall make your fortune—at ten roubles a ticket."

"Ten!" cried the Italian, brightening. "I was sure you would
agree."

The next morning Charsky visited Princess N——. The princess was
a widow of that uncertain age at which beauty becomes
authority and gallantry is transformed into influence. Her Thursdays brought
together ambassadors, guardsmen, elderly statesmen, foreign travellers, two celebrated beauties,
one celebrated bore, and several young men who were admitted
because their fathers had been important or their uniforms were
becoming.

She received Charsky in a small boudoir crowded with porcelain,
miniatures and flowers which had never known soil.

"An improviser?" she said. "Are there still improvisers? I thought
they belonged to Tasso, moonlight and Mme de Staël."

"This one belongs chiefly to his hotelkeeper," replied Charsky, "but
he has genius."

"Genius? How alarming."

"You will be astonished."

"Must I understand Italian?"

"Not necessarily. Half the audience will not understand him, which
may increase the effect."

The princess smiled.

"And what does he require?"

"A platform, a table, several lamps, paper, and permission to
sell tickets."

"To sell tickets in my house!"

"Only to those who would otherwise boast of having entered
without them."

The princess objected, hesitated, suggested invitations, remembered the expense of
candles, reproached Charsky for turning her salon into a theatre,
and finally consented—with the condition that the tickets should be
distributed so discreetly that everyone might speak of them openly
without having seen them sold.

By evening the performance was known throughout the fashionable quarter.
Some declared the Italian a genius; others had heard him
fail miserably in Vienna; one diplomat remembered a man of
the same name who trained canaries in Turin. Tickets vanished.
Those who obtained them complained of the price; those who
did not obtain them complained of the exclusiveness. The improvisatore,
on receiving the accounts, embraced Charsky and asked whether standing
places might be added.

### III

On Thursday, Princess N——'s house shone from foundation to cornice.
Carriages crowded the street; footmen shouted titles into the frosty
air; the ante-rooms overflowed with cloaks, swords, feathers and the
grave impatience of servants waiting for masters who were amusing
themselves.

The great salon had been arranged as a small theatre.
At one end stood a raised platform covered with dark-red
cloth. Upon it were a table, an armchair and two
bronze candelabra. The audience formed a brilliant semicircle: uniforms embroidered
with gold, black coats adorned with stars, bare shoulders, white
gloves, jewels, fans, ribbons and flowers. Every face wore an
expression of expectation carefully moderated by good breeding.

The ladies were curious about the Italian. The gentlemen were
curious about the ladies' curiosity. A few literary men had
come prepared to disapprove on principle. One critic announced that
improvisation could never be true poetry because it lacked revision;
another replied that revision commonly removed whatever poetry had survived
composition. Neither had heard the performer.

Charsky stood near the platform, outwardly calm and inwardly anxious.
He feared a failure as though he himself were to
perform. The improvisatore had spent the afternoon discussing receipts, yet
upon entering the salon he appeared to forget them. He
wore a black suit supplied by Charsky's tailor, who had
protested against the haste and then accomplished the miracle for
twice the usual price. His hair remained loose about his
pale forehead. The false diamond had disappeared.

At half past nine Princess N—— entered, and the murmur
subsided. Charsky led the Italian forward.

The improvisatore bowed. At first his manner seemed constrained. The
magnificence of the room, the cold examination of so many
aristocratic eyes, and perhaps the memory of his unpaid landlord
weighed upon him. He explained in French that subjects would
be proposed by the audience, written on separate slips and
placed in an urn; one would then be drawn by
lot.

His accent delighted the ladies.

"Any subject?" called a young officer.

"Any, monsieur."

"Even a political one?"

The Italian glanced quickly at Charsky.

"Any subject approved by the company," Charsky interposed.

Several persons laughed. Slips of paper were distributed. Pens scratched;
fans concealed consultations; wit, which is seldom so shy as
when officially requested to appear, took refuge in historical names
and philosophical abstractions.

The urn soon contained a harvest of folded papers.

"Read them!" cried several voices.

The improvisatore drew out the first slip and handed it
to Charsky.

"'The triumph of winter.'"

"A Russian subject!" observed someone.

The second read: "'The last day of Pompeii.'"

"Too easy for an Italian."

The third: "'A poet commanded to sing before a tyrant.'"

Charsky glanced toward a councillor known for submitting verses to
ministers. The councillor looked grave.

Other subjects followed: the fall of the angels; the return
of the prodigal son; Napoleon at Moscow; a flower found
upon a battlefield; the invention of champagne; Sappho; Mazeppa; the
North and the South; and, in a lady's delicate hand,
"What does a woman mean when she says no?"

"That subject," said the princess, "would occupy the signor until
morning."

The last slip was drawn. Charsky unfolded it and read:

"Cleopatra and her lovers."

A slight stir passed through the room.

"What is meant by that?" asked the princess.

Charsky hesitated. The subject came from an obscure anecdote preserved
by Aurelius Victor, or attributed to him by commentators who,
when antiquity is silent, possess the admirable courage to speak
in its name.* According to the tale, Cleopatra, weary of
ordinary submission, proclaimed that her love might be purchased at
the price of life. The man admitted to her bed
must die at dawn. The threat, far from discouraging candidates,
lent desire the solemnity of sacrifice; three men, it was
said, accepted the bargain.

Charsky gave the explanation with as much delicacy as history
permitted.

The ladies listened with heightened attention. One elderly countess raised
her lorgnette. A young girl blushed and asked her neighbour
what Cleopatra had demanded; the neighbour whispered an answer which
made her blush still more. The men smiled with the
assurance of persons convinced that, under ancient conditions, they would
have behaved heroically.

"Charming morality," said Princess N——.

"Then this is the chosen subject?" asked Charsky.

The slips were placed again in the urn and shaken.
A white-gloved hand—belonging to the youngest lady present—drew one.

"Cleopatra and her lovers," she read, scarcely above a whisper.

The improvisatore took the paper.

For an instant disappointment crossed his face; perhaps he had
hoped for the last day of Pompeii, with its fires,
temples and terrified multitudes, all excellent material. Then he repeated:

"Cleopatra… and her lovers."

He sat at the table, leaned his forehead upon his
hand and remained motionless.

The salon waited.

A chair creaked. Someone coughed and was frowned into silence.
From the distant street came the muffled roll of a
carriage over snow. The Italian did not stir.

Charsky watched him closely. He had seen the approach of
inspiration once before, but here, amid silk, jewels and curiosity,
it seemed more mysterious. The performer's face lost its social
expression, as a landscape loses familiar outlines beneath moonlight. His
breathing deepened. His fingers tightened upon the slip of paper;
then slowly opened. He raised his head.

His eyes no longer saw the salon.

He rose.

A change, immediate and indescribable, passed through the audience. The
fashionable assembly, which had come to judge an entertainment, felt
itself judged by something greater than entertainment. Smiles vanished; fans
became still. Even the critic who opposed improvisation bent forward.

The Italian extended one arm. When he spoke, his voice
was low, but every syllable reached the farthest corner.

> The sun went down in molten gold,
> And Nilus, purple, vast and old,
> Bore on his consecrated tide
> The lamps of Egypt's evening pride.
> From marble quay to temple stair
> Rose flute and incense through the air;
> While, crowned above the festal noise,
> Sat Egypt's queen among her joys.

His voice gathered richness.

> Beneath a roof of cedar beams
> The banquet burned like fevered dreams;
> Phoenician purple clothed the wall,
> And roses, doomed before their fall,
> Lay crushed beneath the dancers' feet.
> The wine was dark, the music sweet;
> Gold leopards watched with jewelled eyes
> The slaves from distant merchandise.

At the word slaves, his glance swept over the glittering
company with such sudden fire that several listeners started.

> There princes bowed from isles of Greece,
> And chiefs who purchased war with peace;
> There Syria's lord, in silver mail,
> Told how his horse outran the gale;
> There Roman envoys, sternly mute,
> Despised the harp, but marked the flute;
> And every heart, though proud or wise,
> Paid secret tribute to her eyes.

The rhythm slowed. The improvisatore's voice became caressing, almost languid.

> She leaned upon her ivory throne,
> A woman—and a world—alone.
> The lotus trembled in her hair;
> No mortal crown had made her fair.
> Her lips, where love and empire met,
> Smiled as if Rome were but a debt;
> And in her gaze the wise could see
> Delight, disdain and destiny.

A murmur, scarcely audible, moved through the women. The Italian
paused; his pallor had deepened, and perspiration shone upon his
brow. Yet the pause was not uncertainty. It was the
silence before a door opens.

> The cymbals ceased. The dancers fled.
> The last low chord of music bled
> Into the perfumed, breathless night.
> Then Cleopatra rose upright.
> She raised her cup. The rubies cast
> Red sparks, like drops from battles past;
> And all the guests, by wonder bound,
> Heard death before she gave it sound.

The audience seemed afraid to breathe.

> "Lords of the East, and Rome's grave sons,
> Whose will commands, whose valour runs
> Through provinces beneath your tread—
> Who among you will love the dead?
>
> "Not me—the dead. For she who lies
> One night beneath these burning eyes
> Shall greet, when morning gilds the wave,
> No second kiss this side the grave.
>
> "My love is royal; royal be
> The price of one short hour with me.
> Not pearls, not cities, crowns nor breath
> Can buy it. I am bought—with death."

She ceased. Beyond the palace wall
The Nile flowed black beneath them all;
The torches bent, the roses shed
Their petals like bright drops of red.
No courtier laughed, no goblet rang—
But from the gate a bronze gong sang…

And in that ominous note, while the queen stood smiling
above the silent feast and three unknown figures stirred among
the guests, the improvisatore lifted both hands, as though the
vision opening before him were too immense for speech—

# PHILO GUBB AND THE WILL ON BOTH SIDES

When Philo Gubb completed the paper-hanging of the three upper
rooms of Judge Mackinnon's residence, he received forty-eight dollars and
sixty cents, cash in hand. The sixty cents represented two
rolls of border which the Judge had purchased but which
Mr. Gubb had not used. The forty-eight dollars represented labor,
skill, paste, and the Judge's desire to have Mr. Gubb
out of the house before Mrs. Mackinnon decided to have
the hall done also.

Mr. Gubb folded the money into his pocketbook and proceeded
directly to the office of Mr. Jonas C. Lattimer, father
of Syrilla Lattimer, whom Mr. Gubb hoped at some future
time and occasion to marry.

Mr. Lattimer had sold Mr. Gubb one thousand shares of
the Great Geyser Silver, Gold, Copper, Mica, Tin, and Natural
Gas Consolidated Mining Company for five hundred dollars. The shares
had originally been printed to sell for ten dollars each,
but Mr. Lattimer had reduced them because he liked Mr.
Gubb and because the printing on several of them was
blurred.

The company owned a mine in Nevada. At least, it
owned the rights to a hole in Nevada, and Mr.
Lattimer had frequently assured Mr. Gubb that mineral wealth was
bound to come out of that hole as soon as
enough money had been put into it.

So far the process had been all put and no
come.

Mr. Gubb had paid one hundred and seventy-five dollars on
the stock. He now took twenty-five dollars from his pocketbook
and laid it on Mr. Lattimer's desk.

"I come to reduce down the present outstanding indebtedness," he
said, "so that when the mine becomes a dividend-paying proposition
I will own the shares more completely than heretofore."

Mr. Lattimer looked at the money with regret. He was
always sorry to receive money on the mining stock, because
every payment reduced the amount still due.

"You don't want to strain yourself, Philo," he said. "A
man ought to keep a little ready cash."

"I retain twenty-three dollars and sixty cents," said Mr. Gubb.
"The sixty cents is an emergency reserve."

Mr. Lattimer pushed a cigar toward him.

"You heard from Syrilla lately?"

Mr. Gubb's long face brightened. The effect was much like
lighting one candle in an empty railway station, but it
was an improvement.

"This present morning I received a telegraphic communication," he said.
"It was of a highly satisfactory nature."

He drew the telegram from his coat and read it
aloud:

> LOST SEVEN POUNDS THIS WEEK STOP PROFESSOR PULTZ SAYS
IF THIS KEEPS UP CONTRACT ENDS BY OCTOBER STOP NOW
EAT NO POTATOES CABBAGES TURNIPS PARSNIPS BEETS CARROTS ONIONS SQUASH
PUMPKINS CUCUMBERS CORN TOMATOES OR PEAS STOP AM ALLOWED ONE
RADISH SUNDAYS STOP LOVE SYRILLA

Syrilla Lattimer was, at the time, appearing with Pultz's Colossal
Congress of Living Wonders under the professional title of Princess
Muscavado, the Mountain of Maidenly Magnificence. When Mr. Gubb had
first become engaged to her, Syrilla weighed six hundred and
twelve pounds. Professor Pultz had immediately placed her under contract,
and the contract stated that she might leave the show
when her weight fell below four hundred pounds.

Love gave Syrilla a reason for reducing, and Professor Pultz
gave her a diet. Between them they had reduced her
to four hundred and thirty-six pounds.

"Seven pounds!" said Mr. Lattimer. "That's fine."

"It is more than fine," Mr. Gubb said. "It is
hopeful. If Syrilla continues losing seven pounds per week, she
will become free from the side-show bondage in a little
more than five weeks. Naturally, I do not expect her
to lose the entire seven pounds every week. A portion
may be regained by the Sunday radish."

"Could be," said Mr. Lattimer.

Mr. Gubb replaced the telegram tenderly in his pocket. Having
discharged a part of his debt and received news of
his future bride's continued decrease, he felt prepared to resume
the practice of his other profession.

This profession was detective work.

Mr. Gubb was a graduate of the Rising Sun Correspondence
School of Detection, Investigation, Shadowing, and the Complete Unmasking of
Crime. He had completed Lessons One through Thirty-Seven, with the
exception of Lesson Twenty-Two, which had been lost in the
mail. He considered himself a paper-hanger by trade and a
deteckative by intellectual preference.

His office occupied one room over the Riverbank Ice, Coal,
and Feed Company. On the door he had painted:

> PHILO GUBB
> INTERIOR DECORATOR
> AND PRIVATE DETECKATIVE
> STRICT CONFIDENCE USED

When he reached this office, he found Lawyer Higgins waiting
outside.

Lawyer Higgins was a small, brisk man whose manner suggested
that every minute had been entered on his time sheet
and would be charged to somebody.

"Gubb," he said, "I want you to find Mustard Bilton."

Mr. Gubb unlocked the door.

"You have come to the correct place and location," he
said. "Kindly enter in."

Lawyer Higgins entered, but he did not sit. Sitting, in
his opinion, was something a man could do after winning
a case.

"You know Mustard Bilton?" he asked.

"I know him by visual appearance and general reputation," said
Mr. Gubb. "He is an unemployed person who smokes a
cob pipe and considers fishing a commercial occupation."

"That's the man. He has disappeared."

"Disappeared away entirely?"

"He hasn't been seen for nine days."

Mr. Gubb sat behind his desk and put his fingertips
together in the manner recommended by Lesson Six, "How to
Impress the Client Before Learning What the Trouble Is."

"Was there," he asked, "any blood stains, blunt instruments, cries
in the night, footprints leading toward water, mysterious women in
veils, or portions of torn correspondence?"

"No."

Mr. Gubb appeared disappointed but professional.

"Then the crime is unusually deep," he said.

"It may not be a crime at all. Mustard is
wanted as a witness in the O'Hara will matter."

Mr. Gubb nodded. Everyone in Riverbank knew of the O'Hara
will matter, but not everyone understood it. Those who understood
it least explained it most frequently.

Haddon O'Hara had been Riverbank's leading practical joker. He had
never become wealthy enough to be called an eccentric during
life, but when he died and left an estate valued
at eighty thousand dollars, his jokes were remembered as eccentricities.

He had once sent each member of the town council
an official-looking notice ordering him to report at the freight
depot with a shovel. He had nailed a silver dollar
to the courthouse steps. He had placed a sign reading
FRESH PAINT on a bench that had not been painted
for six years. He had entered a pig in the
county baby parade under the name of Aloysius O'Hara, and
the pig had received honorable mention because the judges were
afraid it belonged to an influential family.

In spite of these habits, Haddon O'Hara was liked. His
jokes were troublesome, but they were seldom cruel, and he
laughed as readily when one was played on him.

Then he died.

He died and left a will.

More exactly, he left two wills, and this was the
difficulty.

The wills were written on opposite sides of one sheet
of heavy legal paper. One side left five hundred dollars
to Dolly O'Hara, his orphaned niece; one hundred dollars to
the Riverbank Home for Aged Horses; fifty dollars to Judge
Mackinnon for "legal services never rendered and therefore worth the
price"; and the entire remainder of the estate to Miss
Ardelia Doblin.

The other side contained the same words, line for line,
comma for comma, and flourish for flourish, except that the
name Sarah Kinsey appeared where the name Ardelia Doblin appeared.

Both wills were dated April 17. Both were signed by
Haddon O'Hara. Both were witnessed by Mustard Bilton and two
men who had since died. The ink appeared equally old.
The handwriting appeared equally shaky. Neither side bore a blot,
thumbprint, grease spot, fold, or other mark by which it
might be proved to have been written before the other.

If the Ardelia side had been written last, Ardelia inherited.
If the Sarah side had been written last, Sarah inherited.

Miss Doblin and Miss Kinsey had disliked each other for
twenty-two years. The disputed estate gave this dislike a financial
foundation.

Lawyer Higgins represented Ardelia Doblin.

"I'll pay you fifty dollars when you produce Mustard Bilton,"
he told Gubb. "He witnessed both writings. He may know
which one was last."

"I comprehend the legal crisis," said Mr. Gubb. "The final
side written is the final will made."

"Exactly."

"And until Mustard is produced into court, Miss Doblin remains
deprived of funds which may or may not rightfully belong
to her, depending on what Mustard knows."

"Exactly."

"And you pay fifty dollars."

"When he is found."

Mr. Gubb arose and extended his hand.

"I accept the case under strict silence," he said.

Lawyer Higgins had barely gone when Lawyer Burch entered.

Lawyer Burch represented Sarah Kinsey. He was a large, slow
man who believed no statement should be made until it
had been allowed to mature in the mind. His pauses
often lasted long enough to become recesses.

"Gubb," he said, and paused, "I wish you to find
Mustard Bilton."

Mr. Gubb considered the professional ethics involved. Lesson Eleven warned
the student never to betray a client's confidence. It did
not warn him against accepting another client who wanted the
same thing.

"You desire the same missing personage who is wanted elsewhere
in certain legal circles?" he asked.

"Yes."

"I am not at liberty to tell you whether I
have been hired by another party, person, or lawyer," said
Gubb. "But if I was, it would be a confidence.
Therefore I do not tell you I was."

"Quite right," said Lawyer Burch. "I will pay you fifty
dollars to locate Bilton and bring him to my office."

Mr. Gubb frowned.

"It may be impossible to bring one entire man to
two separate law offices simultaneously at the same instant," he
said.

"Bring him to Judge Mackinnon's office, then. Both sides can
question him there."

"That removes the ethical obstacle from the path," said Gubb.
"I accept."

Lawyer Burch left, and Mr. Gubb took from a drawer
a magnifying glass, a tape measure, a notebook, two small
plaster boxes for preserving footprints, a pair of false whiskers,
and the correspondence lesson entitled "Missing Men: Where They Go
and Why They Should Not."

He had just arranged these on his desk when there
came a light knock.

The young woman who entered was Dolly O'Hara.

She was not beautiful in the aggressive manner of the
actresses whose pictures were displayed in the drugstore window, but
she had gentle eyes and the sort of face that
made a man wish he had not said anything foolish
during the previous five minutes. Mr. Gubb stood at once
and brushed a shred of wallpaper from his sleeve.

"Mr. Gubb," she said, "I've come about Waffles."

Mr. Gubb looked toward the door.

"Is he waiting outside?"

"No. He's gone."

"Gone where?"

"If I knew that, he wouldn't be lost."

This was so exact that Mr. Gubb wrote it in
his notebook.

"What is Waffles?" he asked.

"My dog."

Mr. Gubb's professional expression weakened slightly. A missing witness in
an eighty-thousand-dollar will case was deteckative work of the first
order. A missing dog was lower in the lessons, between
"Unexplained Chicken Losses" and "The Anonymous Valentine."

Dolly saw the change in his face.

"I know he's only a dog," she said. "But Uncle
Haddon gave him to me. Waffles is almost the only
thing of Uncle's I expected to keep. I'm not going
to get the estate, no matter which of those women
wins it."

Mr. Gubb immediately closed the lesson on missing men.

"A dog," he said with dignity, "is no less missing
because he travels on four legs. The Rising Sun course
recognizes footprints regardless of quantity. When was Waffles last observed
in a present condition?"

"Three nights ago. I put him in his house before
I went to bed. In the morning the chain was
unfastened and he was gone."

"Was the chain broken?"

"No."

"The collar?"

"Gone with him."

"Did Waffles possess enemies?"

"He barked at Mrs. Mullarky."

"Many persons do," said Mr. Gubb.

Mrs. Mullarky had been Haddon O'Hara's housekeeper and remained in
Dolly's service. She was considered a useful woman in the
house and a dangerous woman in conversation.

"Did your late uncle have especial affection toward the dog?"
Gubb asked.

"Oh, yes. He brought Waffles home as a puppy. He
said Waffles was the only creature in Riverbank who understood
his jokes, because Waffles never asked him to explain one."

"A clue," said Mr. Gubb.

"What is?"

"I cannot state until subsequent developments disclose whether it is
one. Many clues do not become clues until after they
are no longer needed."

Dolly offered him two dollars.

Mr. Gubb looked at the money and then at her
anxious face.

"The professional deteckative fee will be collected when the dog
is found," he said. "If he is not found, the
fee is nothing, plus expenses."

"What expenses?"

"None as yet."

Mr. Gubb accompanied Dolly to the O'Hara house.

It was an old square house near the edge of
town, with a porch that had settled in the middle
as if tired of holding itself up. Waffles's house stood
in the rear yard beneath an elm. It was a
substantial doghouse, shingled, painted green, and large enough to accommodate
a dog, a child, or a private detective of unusually
narrow construction.

Mrs. Mullarky met them at the back door.

"So this is the great detective man, is it?" she
said. "Well, praise be, Dolly, we'll have the dog back
before supper, unless Mr. Gubb mistakes himself for a clue
and follows his own legs home."

Mr. Gubb accepted this as encouragement.

"You may joke, Mrs. Mullarky," he said, "but the deteckative
intellect is frequently hidden beneath an exterior surface that deceives
the untrained observer."

"Hidden deep, is it?" she asked.

"Extremely."

"That I believe."

Mr. Gubb began with the ground surrounding the doghouse. He
knelt, measured three impressions, and made sketches of them. One
was the heel mark of Dolly's shoe. One was made
by the milkman. The third proved, after prolonged examination, to
be the place where Mrs. Mullarky had set down a
flatiron to cool.

He examined the chain.

He examined the staple.

He examined the empty water pan and found water.

He scraped a stain from the door and placed it
in a folded paper.

"What is that?" Dolly asked.

"Paint," said Gubb. "But frequently paint is used by criminals
to resemble paint."

He then requested specimens of every brush, blanket, rug, and
garment with which Waffles had recently come in contact. Dolly
supplied a worn piece of carpet on which the dog
had slept, his brush, and a blue woolen shawl she
had sometimes thrown across him in cold weather.

Mr. Gubb carried these articles to his office.

For two days he subjected them to microscopic examination.

The Rising Sun Correspondence School had sold him the microscope
as a special graduation accessory, although he had not graduated.
It magnified objects with great vigor while leaving their meaning
unchanged.

From the brush he obtained thirteen brown hairs, four gray
hairs, one red hair, two human hairs, three carpet fibers,
and a bristle that may have come from the brush.

From the carpet he obtained eleven brown hairs, a piece
of cracker, sand, coal dust, lint, and the wing of
a moth.

From the shawl he obtained blue wool, brown dog hair,
white cotton, a strand of Dolly's hair, and a flea.

Mr. Gubb arranged the discoveries in envelopes and labeled them
Exhibit A through Exhibit H. He compared the brown hairs
one with another. They all resembled brown dog hairs. He
compared the sand with sand from the riverbank, from Main
Street, and from the schoolyard. It resembled all three, as
sand will.

At the end of the second day he had reached
no conclusion, unless the flea led to the conclusion that
Waffles had at some period been acquainted with fleas.

The inductive method had again failed Mr. Gubb, as it
usually did. He therefore adopted the more reliable method of
walking around town and asking questions.

He questioned the station agent, the freight agent, the express
agent, the ferryman, two butchers, three boys, the chief of
police, and a traveling umbrella-mender. None had seen Waffles. The
boys claimed to have seen dogs of every description except
the description Mr. Gubb gave. One of them offered to
sell him a yellow dog for seventy-five cents.

Nor did these inquiries locate Mustard Bilton.

Mustard's room over Kling's harness shop had not been occupied
for nine days. His fishing pole was gone, but his
best coat remained. His landlady said he owed three weeks'
rent, but this supplied no information because Mustard had often
remained visible while owing considerably more.

Mr. Gubb searched the riverbank. He searched the railway yards.
He visited two barns, a smokehouse, and the abandoned brick
kiln. He found a broken bottle, a horseshoe, three buttons,
and a handkerchief marked "E. P." None belonged to Mustard.
One button, however, exactly matched a missing button on Mr.
Gubb's own vest.

Meanwhile Riverbank discussed the O'Hara wills.

Miss Ardelia Doblin declared that Haddon had always admired her.

Miss Sarah Kinsey declared that Haddon had always admired anyone
he could tease.

Miss Doblin said Sarah had pursued Haddon shamelessly.

Miss Kinsey replied that nobody could pursue Haddon shamelessly because
shame would be the only reason to pursue him.

Each woman was sixty or thereabouts, and each had been
expecting to marry Haddon for twenty years. Haddon had encouraged
both expectations just enough to keep them lively and had
avoided marriage by the simpler process of not marrying either.

On the fourth morning Mr. Gubb received a visit from
Judge Mackinnon.

"Any progress?" the Judge asked.

"Progress has been made in numerous directions," said Gubb. "Unfortunately
none has reached a destination."

"Find Mustard?"

"Not in a located condition."

"Find the dog?"

"Equally absent."

The Judge smiled.

"Haddon O'Hara used to say you'd be the best detective
in Iowa if criminals would only leave wallpaper paste on
everything."

Mr. Gubb bowed.

"I am obliged for the high compliment."

"It wasn't altogether— Never mind. Gubb, there's something peculiar here.
O'Hara dies. Mustard vanishes. Then Dolly's dog vanishes. Haddon was
fond of that dog, and Mustard witnessed both sides of
the will."

"I have connected all three facts mentally," said Gubb.

"And?"

"They are all connected."

"How?"

"That is what remains concealed."

Judge Mackinnon looked about the office. On the desk lay
a dog collar borrowed for comparative purposes, a lump of
river mud, the three buttons, the microscope, and a photograph
of Syrilla in her professional costume.

"Who is that?" he asked.

"My future bride previous to reducing," said Mr. Gubb.

"Ah."

"She is now considerably less."

"Glad to hear it."

Judge Mackinnon departed, and Mr. Gubb sat with his elbows
on the desk. The Judge's words had moved something in
his mind, but not far enough for him to see
what it was.

Haddon had been fond of Waffles.

Mustard had witnessed the wills.

Haddon had joked about everything.

The dog was gone.

Mustard was gone.

Mr. Gubb arose abruptly.

When the inductive method fails, Lesson Seventeen advised the student
to return to the scene and inspect whatever he had
neglected to inspect the first time. This, the lesson stated,
was especially useful when the student had neglected almost everything.

Mr. Gubb returned to the O'Hara house.

Mrs. Mullarky was beating a rug on the clothesline.

"Back again, are ye?" she called. "Did the microscope tell
ye the dog was brown?"

"It communicated several scientific indications," said Mr. Gubb. "I am
about to inspect the doghouse interiorly."

"And did ye not do that before?"

"I inspected the vicinity, surrounding locality, chain, fastenings, earth, and
exterior surface."

"Meaning ye looked everywhere but where the dog lived."

"The obvious," said Mr. Gubb, "is generally overlooked because it
is too plainly visible."

"Ye overlooked it beautifully."

He went to the doghouse and knelt before the entrance.
The opening was low, but Mr. Gubb was thin and
had the advantage of being shaped somewhat like a folded
carpenter's rule. He removed his coat, placed his hat on
the grass, and entered headfirst.

The interior smelled of dog, dust, and old carpet. Mr.
Gubb examined the floorboards. He examined the rear wall. He
examined the side walls. Then, while turning, he struck his
head against the roof.

The blow was not severe, but it caused him to
observe that one board sounded hollow.

He tapped it again.

It still sounded hollow.

Mr. Gubb backed out, obtained a screwdriver from Mrs. Mullarky,
and entered once more. Two screws held the board. When
he removed them, the board dropped and struck him on
the nose. Behind it was a narrow compartment, and within
the compartment lay an envelope.

Across the envelope was written:

> TO PHILO GUBB, IF HE HAS SENSE ENOUGH TO
LOOK HERE.

Mr. Gubb read this with satisfaction. The confidence of the
late Haddon O'Hara in his professional abilities was touching.

He backed out of the doghouse with the envelope in
his teeth.

Mrs. Mullarky stood with her hands on her hips.

"And what have ye fetched from the kennel?" she asked.
"A bone?"

"A documentary clue," said Mr. Gubb, brushing dust from his
knees.

The envelope was sealed. Beneath Mr. Gubb's name were the
words:

> Open this when found. Follow directions exactly. Do not
deliver the enclosed sealed paper until Waffles has been returned
to Dolly.

Inside was a note and another envelope. The second envelope
was heavy, sealed with red wax, and addressed to Judge
Mackinnon, Executor of the Estate of Haddon O'Hara.

Mr. Gubb read the note:

> Dear Gubb:
>
> If you have reached this point, Dolly has hired
you to find Waffles, as I expected she would. You
have probably examined hairs through a microscope, measured footprints, and
bothered half the town. That is why I allowed four
days before the next step.
>
> Take the County Road west to the old Dempsey
cattle shed beyond Miller's Creek. Enter by the small rear
door. Bring Dolly's dog home. Bring anything else you find
there. Then deliver the sealed envelope to Judge Mackinnon in
the presence of the interested lawyers and claimants.
>
> Do not try to reason this out. You will
only delay matters.
>
> HADDON O'HARA

Mr. Gubb read the note twice.

It was impressive evidence of O'Hara's understanding of scientific detection.
The dead man had predicted the microscope, the measurements, the
questions, and even the necessary four days. Mr. Gubb did
not regard the final sentence as insulting. To him it
merely meant that speed was essential.

He placed the sealed envelope inside his shirt.

"Mrs. Mullarky," he said, "inform Miss Dolly that professional developments
have reached a climax. I go to recover her dog,
together with anything else located in association with him."

"Would that include your senses?" she asked.

"If found," said Mr. Gubb gravely, "all related articles will
be brought back."

The old Dempsey cattle shed stood five miles west of
Riverbank, beyond Miller's Creek, in a pasture no longer used
except by weeds. Mr. Gubb hired Alf Sproat's delivery wagon
for fifty cents and drove there.

The front doors of the shed sagged on rusty hinges.
He went around to the rear and found the small
door. From within came a bark.

"Waffles!" cried Mr. Gubb.

The bark became a series of joyful explosions.

Mr. Gubb opened the door. A brown dog rushed upon
him, placed muddy paws on his shirt, knocked off his
hat, and licked him across the mouth.

"Your identity is practically established," said Mr. Gubb, when he
could speak.

"Shut that door!" called a voice from the dimness. "He'll
run clean to town if you let him."

On a pile of hay sat Mustard Bilton. He wore
old trousers, a faded shirt, and an expression of complete
comfort. Beside him were a fishing pole, a cob pipe,
a sack of provisions, and seven empty sardine tins.

Mr. Gubb stared.

"Mustard Bilton!"

"That's what folks mostly call me."

"You are missing."

"I ain't missing to myself."

"You have been sought for throughout Riverbank by two legal
representatives, one private deteckative, various boys, and possibly the chief
of police, although he did not appear greatly active."

Mustard filled his pipe.

"I knowed you'd turn up," he said. "Haddon said you
would."

Mr. Gubb drew himself erect.

"The process by which I have located you is confidential,
but not accidental."

"Found the letter in the doghouse, did you?"

"I did."

"How long'd it take?"

"The exact duration is professionally immaterial."

Mustard grinned.

"Haddon said four days. Said you'd spend two days looking
at dog hair, one day asking everybody that didn't know,
and one day thinking. He allowed half a day either
way."

Mr. Gubb chose not to dispute the accuracy of this
schedule.

"What are you doing here with the dog?"

"Waiting for you."

"Why?"

"Because Haddon paid me to."

Mustard struck a match on his shoe and lighted the
pipe.

"A month before Haddon died," he continued, "he come to
me and says, 'Mustard, I got a job that calls
for a man with patience, no curiosity, and no regular
employment.' I says that sounds like me. He gives me
fifty dollars then and promises fifty more from Judge Mackinnon
after it's over.

"He told me when he died I was to wait
a week, then take Dolly's dog out of his house
at night and bring him here. He'd stocked the place
with food for me and the dog. Said I was
to stay till you come with a note from the
kennel."

"You stole Waffles?"

"Borrowed him. Haddon was particular about that. Said the dog
had to go back in good condition. I been feeding
him better than I feed myself."

Waffles, who was eating from a pan, agreed with this
by wagging his tail.

"And why did you disappear before the dog did?" asked
Mr. Gubb.

"Haddon's orders. He said the lawyers had to get worried
enough to hire you. If the dog went first, you
might hunt the dog without paying attention to me. If
I went first and the dog followed, he said even
you might connect us."

Mr. Gubb felt the need to defend himself.

"I had already mentally connected both disappearances with the deceased
party."

"Sure," said Mustard. "Haddon allowed you might."

"You witnessed the two wills," Gubb said sternly. "Do you
know which side was written last?"

"I know," said Mustard.

Mr. Gubb breathed deeply. Here was the secret for which
Riverbank had argued, two women had quarreled, and two lawyers
had offered fifty dollars each.

"Which?" he asked.

"Neither."

Mr. Gubb looked at one wall of the shed and
then at another.

"Both sides cannot be neither," he said.

"They can if there's a third will."

Mr. Gubb's hand went to the sealed envelope inside his
shirt.

Mustard nodded.

"That's it."

The return to Riverbank was triumphal in everything except appearance.
Mustard drove the wagon. Mr. Gubb sat beside him holding
the sealed envelope. Waffles occupied the rear, where he barked
at cows, fence posts, and one stump.

They stopped first at Dolly's house.

Dolly came running from the porch when she heard Waffles
bark. The dog leaped from the wagon and reached her
in three bounds. She dropped to her knees and put
both arms around him.

"Waffles! Oh, Waffles!"

The dog whined, licked her cheek, and attempted to climb
entirely into her lap.

Mr. Gubb stood solemnly by. A private deteckative, he believed,
should show no emotion at the successful conclusion of a
case. He therefore looked at the elm tree until his
eyes stopped watering.

Dolly rose and caught his hand.

"You found him!"

"I recovered him into your possession," said Mr. Gubb. "He
has suffered no apparent injury except association with Mustard Bilton."

Mustard touched his hat.

"Dog's fine, Miss Dolly."

"I don't care how you did it," she said to
Gubb. "I don't care where he was. You brought him
back."

She tried to give Gubb the two dollars.

He took one.

"The other dollar is deducted," he said, "because the case
contained certain assistance furnished by the late Mr. O'Hara."

Mrs. Mullarky looked into the wagon and saw Mustard.

"So ye found the loafer too!" she exclaimed.

"I was instructed to bring anything else discovered in the
shed," said Mr. Gubb.

"Did ye look for the shed?"

"I had no authority to remove real estate."

Within half an hour Judge Mackinnon's office was crowded.

Judge Mackinnon sat behind his desk. Lawyer Higgins and Miss
Ardelia Doblin occupied one side of the room. Lawyer Burch
and Miss Sarah Kinsey occupied the other. Dolly stood near
the door with Waffles, who had been admitted because Haddon's
instructions seemed to make him an interested party.

Mustard Bilton leaned against the wall. Mr. Gubb stood in
the center with the sealed envelope.

Miss Doblin was a tall, sharp woman dressed in black.
Miss Kinsey was a shorter, rounder woman also dressed in
black. Each wore mourning for Haddon, but neither had yet
decided whether the mourning would be permanent or merely until
she lost the estate.

"There he is!" cried Miss Doblin, pointing at Mustard. "Make
him tell which will was last."

"Yes," said Miss Kinsey. "And make him tell the truth.
Ardelia has probably promised him money."

"I have done nothing of the kind!"

"You would if you thought of it."

"Ladies," said Judge Mackinnon, "we'll proceed in order. Gubb, you
located Mustard?"

"I did," said Mr. Gubb.

"You also found Dolly's dog?"

"The dog and the witness were simultaneously present in one
abandoned agricultural structure."

"Why?"

"Haddon told him to," said Mustard.

Both women began talking at once. Judge Mackinnon struck his
desk with a paperweight.

"Mustard," he said, "tell us the whole thing."

Mustard removed his cob pipe from his pocket. Judge Mackinnon
pointed at it.

"Don't light that."

"I wasn't figuring to. Just holds my hand steady."

He told the story he had told Mr. Gubb. When
he finished, Lawyer Higgins stepped forward.

"You admit witnessing both sides of the sheet?"

"Yep."

"Which side was executed last?"

"The Ardelia side," said Mustard.

Miss Doblin gave a cry of triumph. Miss Kinsey gave
a cry of outrage.

Then Mustard added, "Unless it was the Sarah side."

The cries reversed owners.

"What do you mean?" demanded Lawyer Burch.

"Well, Haddon wrote one side and signed it, and me
and the other two witnessed. Then he turned the paper
over and done the other. I can't recollect which came
first."

"Then why did you say the Ardelia side?"

"I was thinking it might've been."

"You just said you knew!" cried Miss Doblin.

"I know neither one was his last will."

All eyes turned toward Mr. Gubb and the envelope.

Judge Mackinnon held out his hand.

Mr. Gubb did not surrender it.

"The written instructions specifically require delivery in the presence of
the interested lawyers and claimants," he said. "Are all present?"

"Everyone except Haddon," said the Judge.

"He is represented by written documentary communication," said Gubb, and
handed over the envelope.

The red wax cracked under Judge Mackinnon's thumb. Inside was
a folded sheet and a smaller note.

The Judge unfolded the sheet first. He studied it. Then
he looked at Dolly.

"This is a will," he said.

Miss Doblin rose.

"Another will?"

"Dated June 3, two months after the double-sided document. Properly
signed and witnessed. It revokes all former wills."

"And who gets the estate?" Sarah Kinsey demanded.

Judge Mackinnon read:

> I, Haddon Patrick O'Hara, being sound in mind although
frequently accused of the contrary, do declare this my final
will. I give all property of every kind to my
niece, Dolly O'Hara, except one hundred dollars to Mustard Bilton
for services to be rendered, and twenty-five dollars each to
Ardelia Doblin and Sarah Kinsey, on condition that each spend
her twenty-five dollars on a gift for the other. I
name Judge Mackinnon executor and direct him to pay all
reasonable expenses caused by my final joke.

For several seconds nobody spoke.

Dolly stared at the Judge.

"I get—everything?"

"Everything except the amounts named."

"But Uncle's first wills only gave me five hundred dollars."

"Those weren't his final wills."

"Then why did he make them?"

Judge Mackinnon opened the smaller note.

"I expect this explains."

He read aloud:

> Dear Mackinnon:
>
> Ardelia and Sarah each spent twenty years trying to
marry me for my money. It seems unfair that neither
should get any return for the effort, so I have
left each twenty-five dollars and the opportunity to improve her
Christian disposition.
>
> Dolly never asked me for anything except to stop
teasing people. She likes Waffles better than she likes money,
which is why she should have both.
>
> I made the two-sided wills because one bad will
causes ordinary trouble, while two bad wills on one sheet
cause first-class trouble. I instructed Mustard to witness them and
later arranged for him to disappear. I knew Higgins and
Burch would hire Gubb. I also knew Dolly would hire
Gubb when Waffles vanished. Gubb would eventually search the doghouse,
provided nobody told him to begin there.
>
> Pay Gubb whatever rewards he has been promised. He
will have earned them, although not in the way he
thinks.
>
> If Ardelia and Sarah are still arguing, tell them
I said the one who stops first was always my
favorite.
>
> Yours deceasedly,
> HADDON O'HARA

Miss Doblin stopped speaking.

Miss Kinsey also stopped.

Each then watched the other to see whether she would
begin.

Lawyer Higgins drew Mr. Gubb aside and paid him fifty
dollars. Lawyer Burch paid him another fifty. Dolly tried to
pay him more, but he refused.

"One dollar was the agreed professional charge," he said. "The
additional reward is already sufficient to compensate the deteckative faculties."

Judge Mackinnon paid Mustard his remaining fifty dollars. Mustard counted
it twice and asked whether he was still required as
a witness.

"Not unless the will is contested," said the Judge.

"Anybody contesting?"

Miss Doblin looked at Miss Kinsey.

Miss Kinsey looked at Miss Doblin.

"No," they said together.

Waffles, bored with legal procedure, put his front paws on
Judge Mackinnon's desk and seized Haddon's explanatory note in his
teeth.

"Drop that!" cried four people.

Dolly rescued it before he could eat more than one
corner.

"He always did like Uncle's jokes," she said.

The next morning Philo Gubb returned to Mr. Lattimer's office.
From the hundred dollars paid by the lawyers, he placed
fifty on the desk as an additional payment on the
Great Geyser Silver, Gold, Copper, Mica, Tin, and Natural Gas
Consolidated Mining Company stock.

"You're doing well, Philo," said Mr. Lattimer. "Found that missing
witness, did you?"

"I found the witness, the dog, the final last will,
and the rightful heiress."

"That sounds like real detective work."

"Deteckative work," corrected Mr. Gubb.

"Did you reason it all out?"

Mr. Gubb considered the question. He thought of the microscope,
the hairs, the sand, the flea, the questions, the doghouse,
and the dead man's note advising him not to reason.

"The solution," he said at last, "was obtained by following
the advanced principle contained in Lesson Seventeen of the Rising
Sun Correspondence School course."

"What principle is that?"

Mr. Gubb drew himself up with professional dignity.

"When everything else has failed," he said, "look where you
ought to have looked in the first place."

At nine o'clock on a November morning two persons issued
separately from the same prison, each carrying a brown paper
parcel and a few shillings. They had entered by different
gates, upon different indictments, and at different seasons; but liberty,
which is careless of such distinctions, delivered them together upon
the pavement.

The man was perhaps thirty-five, squarely made and pale in
the places where other men are brown. His cropped hair
had begun to bristle with independence, though his new boots
still creaked institutionally. The woman, some years younger, wore a
black jacket which had not been cut for her and
held her parcel against her breast. Her eyes, accustomed to
doors that opened by rule, went continually to the prison
gate, as if expecting it to correct a mistake.

They had neither relations nor acquaintances worth mentioning. We may
therefore know them as Adam and Eve.

Adam looked east, where the fog had swallowed the street;
then west, where it had swallowed the street also.

"Which way?" Eve asked.

He took a penny from his pocket.

"Head, west. Tail, t'other."

The penny showed the Queen, and the Queen sent them
west.

Eve followed at his heels.

* * *

London received them without ceremony. Carts ground past, omnibuses loomed
red and enormous, newsboys darted between wheels, and shop doors
opened upon caverns of meat, cloth, brass, tobacco, bread, and
gaslight. The fog hung overhead like a low tent-cloth, yellow
at the seams and black where chimney smoke had soaked
it. Beneath its frayed edge the lamps floated without posts,
each in a private stain of orange; beyond them went
a continual sound, like the sea heard from under a
blanket.

Adam walked quickly. He had imagined freedom as a road
with room on either side. It proved to be elbows,
horses, umbrellas, wheels, and faces—thousands of faces, none of which
knew him, and all of which seemed to possess urgent
business.

"Keep close," he said.

"I am close."

A gentleman struck Adam's shoulder and called him a fool.
A woman with a basket thrust Eve into the gutter.
At a crossing they waited while vehicles passed in such
profusion that Adam suspected them of returning by another street
in order to pass again.

"They don't stop," Eve whispered.

"Nobody asked 'em."

They had eaten before release, because even the State does
not cast out its children fasting; nevertheless the steam on
a coffee-shop window detained them. Adam counted his money. It
looked considerable when arranged on his palm, but less so
when interpreted by the prices painted on the glass.

"We'll get summat farther on," he said.

Eve looked once at a loaf, golden in the gaslight,
and followed.

By afternoon the fog thickened and the crowds acquired the
formless hostility of water. Adam turned aside wherever the pressure
was greatest, first into one street, then another, until the
streets grew quieter and tall houses watched them from behind
curtains. Presently an opening appeared between railings. Trees, dim and
multitudinous, leaned out of vapour; gravel ran inward; there were
no carts.

"A garden," said Eve.

"Public," said Adam, reading the board with difficulty. "Means we
can."

They entered Kensington Gardens.

At first its emptiness comforted them. Their feet made a
proper sound upon the path, and no one shoved. Leaves
lay in wet heaps; the grass, whitening toward evening, stretched
between trunks whose upper branches had been erased. Here and
there a nursemaid wheeled a carriage through the mist, or
a mounted figure passed like a king in an old
tapestry. Then these vanished. The lamps outside became a pale
procession behind the railings.

They walked west, as the penny had ordered. Yet every
path divided into two, and each division displayed more trees.
The garden, which upon a map would have appeared a
respectable green patch, enlarged itself for those who had no
map. Darkness came early. Bushes became thickets, lawns became limitless
plains, and the Serpentine breathed through the fog with a
cold animal breath.

"We've passed that tree," Eve said.

"There's plenty trees alike."

"That one's broke."

Adam looked at the splintered branch and said nothing.

A bell sounded far away.

They returned toward the railings, but found only more grass.
When at last the iron bars emerged, tall, wet, and
admirable, Adam shook a gate. It was locked.

"Hoy!" he shouted.

His voice went a little way and died.

They ran along the boundary. Each gate was fastened. Beyond
the bars, London rolled and glittered; inside, the darkness appeared
to breed acres. Seen from the road they might have
resembled two caged beasts. To themselves, however, the cage was
all the world, and the free people went safely past
outside it.

"Call again," said Eve.

Adam called until a policeman's lantern paused beyond the railings.

"Locked in, are you?" said the policeman.

"Yes."

"Then you'll be locked in till morning."

"Can't you open?"

"Not my garden."

The lantern moved away.

Eve began to cry without noise. Adam put his arm
about her, an action new to them both, and they
crouched under a tree. The fog settled upon their clothes;
unseen water spoke at intervals; leaves scurried over the ground
like small pursued creatures.

"It's too big," Eve said.

"Only a park."

"It's too big for a park."

At dawn a keeper found them beside the gate. He
rebuked them for being there, opened it, and let them
out.

* * *

Beyond Hammersmith the town loosened by degrees. Houses fell apart
into villas, villas into cottages; chimneys diminished, hedges increased, and
the road displayed long portions of itself. Adam preferred the
pavement when there was one. Eve still followed at his
heels.

They called themselves man and wife because the truth required
explanations and marriage did not. Adam asked at yards, stables,
and nurseries for work.

"What sort?" men asked.

"Any sort."

"What have you done?"

"'Cumulated jobs."

This answer, though comprehensive, was not persuasive. His hands were
too white for a labourer's, his speech too rough for
a clerk's, and his clothes had the melancholy exactness of
charity. Eve fared no better. Housewives glanced at her short
hair, at her uncertain boots, and at Adam waiting by
the gate.

By the second evening their money was gone. They slept
beneath a hayrick, which was smaller than Kensington Gardens and
therefore almost pleasant. In the morning they walked on with
empty stomachs. The road shone after rain, running west between
bare hedges toward a horizon which continually retreated.

Near Reading they came to a house standing alone behind
a wall. A brass tube projected from a little dome
upon the roof. In the garden an elderly gentleman, narrow
as a folded umbrella, was fastening a ladder.

Adam stopped at the gate.

"Work wanted?" asked the gentleman.

"Food," said Adam, being tired beyond diplomacy. Then, seeing the
old man's eyes pass to Eve, he added, "My wife's
expecting."

Eve stared at him.

The old gentleman removed his spectacles, polished them, and looked
again. Benevolence, like justice, is assisted by a category.

"My dear girl!" he exclaimed. "Come in at once."

They were given stew, bread, cheese, and tea in a
kitchen bright with copper pans. Eve ate delicately for the
first minute and thereafter with attention. Adam kept his eyes
upon his plate. When nothing remained but crumbs and the
old gentleman's kindness, his discomfort became greater than hunger had
been.

"She ain't," he said.

The astronomer blinked.

"Ain't what?"

"Expecting. I told you wrong."

Eve's face reddened.

Adam pushed back his chair. "You can fetch the police
if you like. Only she was hungry."

The old man considered them. He was a student of
remote fires and had consequently learned some tolerance for small
terrestrial darknesses.

"I shall not fetch anyone," he said. "But you should
not invent a child."

"No," said Adam. "There's enough people."

This answer pleased the astronomer more than it ought to
have done. He laughed, and because the night had cleared
after many foggy ones, he asked whether they would like
to see the stars.

Adam looked toward Eve. "Never seen 'em proper."

"Then you shall."

* * *

They climbed a narrow stair to the dome. Its slit
stood open, admitting a blade of black sky so pure
that Eve shrank from it. The great telescope rested upon
its mounting with the solemnity of a cannon.

The astronomer directed it first to the moon. Eve looked
and drew back sharply. Mountains stood there, white and dead,
their shadows black as pits.

"How far?" Adam asked.

"A quarter of a million miles, approximately."

Adam frowned. Numbers above a thousand had always seemed to
him a gentleman's ornament.

The old man, mistaking silence for appetite, proceeded joyfully. He
showed them Jupiter with its belts and little attendants; Saturn,
imprisoned in its miraculous rings; a double star, one flame
golden and the other blue. He spoke of light travelling
one hundred and eighty-six thousand miles in a second, yet
requiring years to cross the distances between neighbouring suns. He
named Sirius, Vega, Aldebaran, and the pale swarms beyond them;
he described worlds rushing through darkness at speeds which reduced
express trains to hesitation.

"The nearest star," he said, "is more than twenty-five million
million miles away."

Eve gripped the edge of the table.

"And after that?" she asked.

"More stars."

"After them?"

"More still. Systems, nebulae, islands of suns—space beyond calculation."

The dome seemed to tip backward. Adam looked through the
opening and saw, not a roof ornamented with lights, but
an abyss crowded with fires, all falling or flying, none
enclosed. The earth beneath him became a loose stone. England
shrank; Reading vanished; the road, London, and even the prison
dwindled to less than a grain. He seized the telescope
as though it were a railing.

The astronomer, delighted by the majesty of his subject, continued.
He mentioned a certain swift-moving star in Ursa Major, travelling—according
to the latest estimate—at an appalling velocity toward an appointment
no man would witness.

"Then to hell with your star!" Adam cried.

There was a silence.

Eve had crouched upon the stair. Her eyes were wide
and bright, like a hare's under a lantern.

"Shut it," she said.

"My dear—"

"Shut the sky."

The old man closed the shutter.

At once the dome became a room again: boards, brasswork,
a smell of oil, four walls. Adam's breathing quieted. The
astronomer offered them beds, but before sunrise they had gone.

* * *

They did not continue west. The open road had lost
its innocence. Every gap in the hedge admitted infinity; every
puddle held a depth of stars. Eve walked eastward, and
now Adam followed at her heels.

It took them several days. They begged little, spoke less,
and slept where roofs could be found. London's smoke at
last rose before them like a wall. The crowd closed
round; wheels thundered; fog lowered its familiar ceiling. These things,
formerly monstrous, had acquired limits.

At the prison they waited by the gate until a
blue-clad warder appeared.

"Well?" he said.

Adam removed his cap. "Any chance of coming back?"

The warder laughed, supposing this wit.

Eve put one hand between the bars. "Please."

There are many doors into custody, and the law, though
ceremonious, is seldom unwilling to open one. Before the week
ended Adam broke a window under observation, while Eve took
a shawl and remained politely beside the constable until claimed.

Thus they returned to their separate cells, to bells, measured
corridors, numbered garments, and walls of an intelligible height. Paradise
closed behind them without thunder. There was no flaming sword;
only a warder with keys, who turned the lock and
bade them settle down.

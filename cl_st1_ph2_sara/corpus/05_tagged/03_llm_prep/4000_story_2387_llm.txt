I I have been frequently requested, by persons to whose
judgment I am disposed to pay respect, to commit to
writing the particulars of certain occurrences which took place during
my residence at a watering-place on the southern coast; and
as the lapse of years has neither materially weakened my
recollection of them, nor supplied any explanation more satisfactory than
that which was available at the time, I have at
length consented to do so. I shall omit names and
precise dates, partly because several persons concerned are, I believe,
still living, and partly because nothing would be gained by
directing an inconvenient curiosity toward a house which may now
be occupied by persons wholly ignorant of its former reputation.

I was, at the period in question, recovering from a
severe illness, which, though not immediately dangerous, had left me
so reduced in strength that my medical adviser recommended sea
air and complete cessation from business. My wife accordingly took,
for a term of some months, a house in a
newly arranged terrace at the eastern extremity of the town.
Our family consisted of ourselves, three children—two girls and a
boy, the eldest being then about nine years of age—and
the usual servants: a nurse, housemaid, cook, and a man
who acted indifferently as butler and valet. This last, whom
I shall call R., had been with me during the
greater part of my married life. He was a steady,
grave person, of intelligence considerably above the common run of
persons in his rank of life, and had, I may
add, a positive contempt for all tales of ghosts, warnings,
second sight, and the like.

The house appeared, in every respect, admirably fitted for our
purpose. It was cheerful, well lighted, and, as we believed,
recently built. Its windows commanded the sea; its fittings were
modern; the principal bed-rooms were airy; and there was nothing
in its exterior suggestive of antiquity or neglect. It formed
one of a short row, the uniform stucco fronts and
bright green shutters of which gave rather the appearance of
speculative newness than of any thing romantic or venerable.

At the back there was a paved yard, beyond which
stood a stable and coach-house, with a small inclosure between
them and the boundary wall. Under the kitchen level were
two cellars, one used for wine and stores, and the
other as a coal vault. The latter was entered by
a stout door from the basement passage, and had also,
as is usual, an iron plate or shutter communicating with
the pavement above, through which coal might be delivered. This
plate was secured from within by a cross-bar, and the
vault itself, when examined, presented nothing unusual. I mention these
particulars because the coal vault became, in the sequel, a
principal point of inquiry.

The internal arrangement was simple. From the front door a
passage ran backward to the staircase. The drawing-room and dining-room
occupied the ground floor; our bed-room, dressing-room, and a spare
chamber were on the floor above; and the children, with
their nurse, slept up stairs, in two rooms communicating by
a door which generally stood open at night. The servants
slept at the back of the upper story, except R.,
who had a small apartment near the basement stairs. I
caused these dispositions to be particularly observed, partly on account
of my own helpless condition, and partly because my wife
was, at first, apprehensive that the distance of the nursery
from our room might prove inconvenient.

For nearly a fortnight nothing occurred which could attract notice.
I improved in health, sat for some hours daily near
an open window, and was carried, when the weather permitted,
to the beach. The children were delighted with the novelty
of the place; the servants considered the house unusually comfortable;
and my wife more than once congratulated herself upon having
secured it at a moderate rent.

The first circumstance of an extraordinary nature was related to
me by my wife on the morning after it happened.

She had awakened, she said, at some hour after midnight,
though she could not state the exact time. A night-light
burned upon a table at my side of the bed,
because I then required occasional assistance; and there was, besides,
a faint moonlight at the window. She was perfectly awake—not
merely conscious in that confused manner which sometimes attends the
transition from sleep—and, turning toward the foot of the bed,
perceived a man standing there.

The figure was of unusual height; so tall, indeed, that
its head appeared nearly to reach the upper part of
the bed-curtains. It wore a long, dark garment, but whether
a coat or cloak she could not determine. The face
was indistinct, not from any covering, but because the head
was a little inclined and the light fell badly upon
it. She saw, however, the outline of the shoulders and
arms with sufficient exactness to be certain that she beheld
the form of a man, and not a coat hung
upon a chair or any projection of the furniture.

Her first supposition was that R. had entered in consequence
of some sound from me. She said, "What is it?"
The figure made no reply. She then raised herself upon
her elbow and repeated the question, whereupon it seemed to
move backward—not walking, as she thought, but passing with a
smooth movement toward the dressing-room door. At that instant she
called me by name.

I awoke, but not quickly enough to see any thing.
The dressing-room door was shut. The door opening upon the
landing was also shut, though not locked. I rang for
R., who came almost immediately, carrying a candle and wearing,
I recollect, a grey dressing-gown. We searched the dressing-room, looked
beneath the bed, examined the cupboards, and subsequently went over
the landing and the vacant chamber adjoining. No person was
discovered; no door or window had been opened; and nothing
in the room corresponded, even remotely, to the appearance described.

I attributed the matter, in limine, to a dream. My
wife, who is not of an imaginative disposition, maintained that
she had been as completely awake as she then was,
but did not dispute my conclusion with warmth. The occurrence
made, so far as I could judge, less impression upon
her than upon me; for she spoke of the figure
as curious rather than alarming, and slept in the same
room without hesitation on the following night.

Some days later the housemaid, M., came to my wife
and requested permission to leave our service. This was unexpected.
She was a strong, active young woman, about twenty-six years
of age, and had been with us more than two
years. Her character for courage was established by several trifling
incidents which need not be detailed; and she was, in
fact, sometimes blamed by the other servants for a roughness
and self-confidence subversive of proper order. On being questioned, she
at first alleged that the sea air disagreed with her.
My wife, observing the obvious insufficiency of this reason, pressed
her further; and the girl then admitted that she had
twice seen "a dreadful-looking woman" in the basement passage.

I was informed of this, and had her brought to
me separately, before she could compare her recollections with those
of the other servants. Her account, reduced to its material
parts, was as follows.

On the first occasion she had gone down at about
half-past ten in the evening to ascertain whether the kitchen
fire was safe. The cook had already retired. M. carried
a flat candlestick in her right hand. As she reached
the bottom of the stairs she saw a woman standing
with her back against the coal-vault door. The person was
dressed in a ragged brown gown, or gown and petticoat
of nearly the same colour, with a dark handkerchief crossed
over the breast. Her hair, which was partly grey, escaped
from beneath a dirty cap. Her face was marked with
smallpox, and one eye—M. believed the left—was closed or destroyed,
the lid being drawn and discoloured. The other eye was
fixed upon the servant in what she described as an
angry, side-long manner.

M., supposing her to be a beggar or thief who
had obtained entrance from the yard, demanded what she did
there. The woman turned, pulled at the handle of the
coal-vault door, and passed in. M. immediately followed. The door,
she said, did not visibly open; yet the woman was
no longer in the passage. When M. tried the handle,
she found the door locked. The key was in the
cook's possession.

The girl, though startled, said nothing that night. Two evenings
later, at almost the same hour, she again saw the
woman crouching beside a basket under the basement window, apparently
searching among some pieces of wood. On being approached, she
rose, crossed the passage, and disappeared at the coal-vault door
in the same manner. This second occurrence determined M. to
leave.

I asked whether she had ever heard any account of
the house before entering it. She declared she had not.
I asked whether the woman might have been the cook,
or my wife, or some reflection in the glass of
the basement window. She appeared offended by the first two
suggestions, and answered the last by observing, with justice, that
the window was at right angles to the door, and
could not reflect any person standing beside it. She was
quite positive respecting the injured eye and the marks upon
the face.

The coal vault was forthwith examined. The door was thick,
the lock good, and the key, according to the cook,
had not been out of her pocket. The iron delivery-plate
was barred; the walls were solid; and the small ventilating
aperture toward the yard would scarcely have admitted a cat.
R. tapped the masonry, measured its dimensions against those of
the passage and yard, and expressed the opinion that no
recess or concealed communication existed. Still, I considered it more
probable that we had overlooked some entrance than that a
woman could pass through a locked door.

I persuaded M. to remain, promising that R. should accompany
her whenever she had occasion to descend late. For a
week or more she saw nothing further.

The next incident was of a different kind. The cook,
a middle-aged woman of sober habits and strong religious opinions,
was sitting one evening near the kitchen table, mending an
apron. M. stood at the dresser, and R. was in
the servants' hall adjoining, the door between the rooms being
open. One candle burned on the table, and another upon
a shelf near the fireplace.

While the cook was sewing, a shadow passed slowly along
the wall opposite her. It resembled, in outline, the head
and shoulders of a woman wearing a cap. As the
table candle stood between the cook and the wall, the
shadow could not, in the ordinary course of light, have
been cast by any one who crossed behind her; and
no person crossed either before or behind her. The shadow
moved from the direction of the yard door toward the
coal-vault passage, where it disappeared.

The cook called out. M. turned in time, as she
asserted, to see the latter portion of the shadow; and
R., entering immediately, found both women greatly agitated. They differed
in one small particular: the cook thought the profile turned
toward the wall, while M. believed she distinguished the projecting
outline of the blind eye on the side presented to
the room. That any shadow should exhibit a blind eye
may appear an absurdity; I give only the expression she
used.

We made several experiments on the following morning and evening.
R. and I moved candles to different positions, while M.
walked along the kitchen and passage. No arrangement produced a
shadow in the place and direction described. Indeed, with the
table candle where it had stood, the wall was strongly
illuminated; and an opaque figure moving near it would have
cast a shadow in the contrary direction. The phenomenon was
thus rendered more perplexing by examination, though I by no
means concluded that it was not referable to natural agencies.
Servants, when once alarmed, are apt to see a contrariety
to ordinary laws in the simplest effect of light.

About this time knocking began to be heard in the
upper part of the house. It occurred chiefly after the
family had retired, and consisted of three or four blows
upon a door, followed by a pause, and then by
a single blow. The nursery door seemed generally to be
the point from which the sound proceeded. The nurse would
say "Come in," supposing it to be one of the
servants; but no one entered. On several occasions she opened
the door immediately, and found the landing empty. Once, while
she held the door open and looked along the passage,
the handle of the adjoining empty bed-room was seen to
turn downward, slowly and completely, though the door did not
open.

The nurse was a sensible widow of nearly forty, singularly
free from nervous apprehension. She at first imagined that the
children played some trick, and watched them narrowly. On the
night when the handle turned, all three were in their
beds, two asleep and the eldest reading under the bed-clothes
by the aid of a little lamp—which offence, I may
say, was discovered only through this investigation. The landing had
no recess in which a person could conceal himself, and
the stairs were visible from the nursery threshold.

R. suspected that the sounds were communicated through the walls
from the adjoining house. This was a reasonable conjecture; but
the next house was unoccupied, and its owner courteously permitted
us to inspect it. No corresponding door existed on that
side of the party-wall, nor were there pipes or other
wooden communications by which a blow could readily have been
transmitted. We nevertheless adhered for some time to this explanation,
as being less objectionable than the alternative.

The sounds presently changed their locality. A rythmical hammering was
heard over our bed-room, apparently upon or immediately beneath the
roof. It began with six measured strokes, paused, and recommenced,
sometimes continuing for twenty minutes. R. climbed through the skylight
and examined the slates, gutters, chimney-pots, and every accessible portion.
He discovered neither loose metal nor branch of a tree;
indeed, no tree stood near enough to touch the house.
Wind could not account for it, because the hammering occurred
on perfectly still nights and ceased during at least one
gale.

On the third occasion I determined to address it, less
from any expectation of a reply than from impatience. The
strokes had continued for perhaps five minutes, and were sufficiently
loud to wake my wife. I called, "Stop that noise."
It stopped instantly.

After an interval I said, "If any person is there,
knock again."

Nothing followed. R., who had heard the disturbance from below
and came up with a lantern, searched the roof. He
found no one. I was disposed to regard the cessation
as accidental; yet when the noise returned on another night,
my wife said quietly, "Tell it to stop," and upon
my doing so it again ceased in the middle of
a series.

No damage was ever found upon the roof. No slates
were displaced, no marks of tools were discernible, and nothing
was stolen from any part of the house.

It will naturally be asked why, under these circumstances, we
remained. The answer is that the occurrences, though odd, were
neither continuous nor violent; that my health had already improved;
that moving would have involved inconvenience; and that I still
believed some mischievous person, possibly connected with smugglers upon the
coast, was making use of a concealed passage. The idea
of a ghost was repeatedly proposed below stairs, but never
received encouragement from me. I may have been obstinate; yet
incredulity, when not assumed for display, is surely more respectable
than an appetite for marvels.

My suspicion of an intruder was strengthened by a circumstance
which happened to R. He had gone into the town
one afternoon to make certain purchases. On returning by a
narrow lane behind the fish-market, he saw, at a distance
of perhaps forty yards, a woman whose dress and appearance
struck him as remarkable. She wore a torn brown gown
and a dark handkerchief; she was bent, walked rapidly, and
turned her face toward him as she passed beneath a
lamp. One eye was closed, and the face was deeply
pitted.

R., who had heard the servants' description, followed her. She
turned twice, entered a crowded street, and disappeared near a
passage leading toward the quay. He searched the passage and
inquired of two women selling fruit whether they had noticed
her. One believed such a person had gone by, while
the other had seen no one. R. returned persuaded that
a real woman, probably a vagrant, had been entering our
premises and frightening the servants.

Two days afterward I saw, or believed I saw, the
same person.

I was then able to walk a short distance with
a stick. My wife and I had gone out in
the carriage, and I had alighted near a row of
shops while she continued to a circulating library. Standing beneath
an awning, I observed on the opposite pavement an old
woman in brown, with a ragged shawl drawn about her.
Her face was turned partly away; but as she looked
across the street I distinctly perceived the closed eye and
the peculiar scars described by M. She moved behind a
passing cart. I crossed as quickly as my infirmity allowed,
but when the cart had gone she was nowhere visible.

There were two shops into which she might have entered,
and a narrow court between them. I sent R., who
attended me, into the court, while I made inquiry at
the shops. No one had observed her. The court terminated
in a locked gate, and contained no open doorway.

This incident greatly confirmed me in the belief that the
supposed apparition was a living person. If R. and I
could see her in a public street, it appeared absurd
to connect her with any supernatural agency. We therefore made
a more exact search of the house and grounds. A
builder examined the cellar walls, measured the coal vault externally
and internally, removed portions of boarding, and probed several cracks.
The paving near the stable was lifted in two places.
Nothing was found. The builder stated positively that the vault
had no concealed outlet and that any person entering it
must pass through the door or coal plate.

The apparitions—or persons, as I continued to call them—were not
confined to the servants. Our eldest daughter, E., had an
experience which she concealed for several days, from fear, as
she afterward said, that we should accuse her of invention.

The nursery consisted, as I have mentioned, of a large
front room and a smaller room behind it, in which
the nurse slept with the youngest child. E. and her
sister occupied two little beds in the front apartment; the
boy, being then only four, slept in the inner room.
A tall press stood against the wall beside the fireplace.
Between it and the outer door was a space of
about three feet. Toys were kept in a chest beneath
the window.

One night E. was awakened by a sound of drawers
being opened. The fire had burned low, but a lamp
in the inner room threw sufficient light through the open
communicating door to render the furniture discernible. She saw a
man standing before the press. He was short and broad,
with a large red face, coarse black hair, and what
she called "shining buttons" upon a dark jacket. His trousers
were loose, like those worn by sailors. He opened one
of the press doors, felt among the folded linen, shut
it, and then passed to the toy-chest.

E. supposed him to be a thief. She lay still,
watching. The man lifted the lid, rummaged among the toys,
and seemed to become angry at not finding whatever he
sought. He next stooped, looked under her sister's bed, rose,
and came toward E. His face was then fully visible.
She described it as red, swollen, and wet, though whether
with perspiration or water she could not say. There was
a dark bruise or patch over one temple.

He did not look at her. He passed between the
beds, approached the communicating door, and vanished before reaching it.
E. did not see him fade; in her words, "he
was there, and then there was only the door."

She covered her head and remained silent until morning. The
press and chest showed no sign of disturbance, though she
asserted that she had heard the wooden lid strike the
wall. Nothing had been taken.

I questioned her with care, avoiding suggestions. She had never
heard the servants' account of the one-eyed woman, and she
did not connect the man with any other occurrence in
the house. Her description of sailor-like clothing may have arisen
from the many fishermen she saw daily; while the red
face might have been a firelight effect. The nurse, however,
declared that there had been almost no fire and that
E. could not, from her bed, see the lamp itself.
The child was observant and habitually exact, though not free,
of course, from the imaginative tendencies natural to her age.

The second girl, L., who was seven, supplied a more
distressing relation. She was playing alone in the upper passage
one afternoon, arranging a doll's table near the spare bed-room
door. The day was bright, and all the doors and
windows were, so far as I recollect, in their ordinary
condition. Hearing what she thought was her mother's dress rustling,
she looked up and saw a young woman come from
the spare room.

The woman was pale, with long dark hair hanging loose
upon her shoulders. She wore a light-coloured gown, not white,
but of a faded grey or lavender. Both hands were
pressed against her throat. Across the front of the throat
was a broad red line, which L. called a cut;
blood had stained the gown below it. The young woman
walked slowly, looking downward, until she came opposite the child.
She then raised her eyes, made a movement as if
endeavouring to speak, and turned toward the nursery.

L. called for the nurse. Before the latter reached the
passage, the figure had disappeared. The nursery was searched immediately.
The windows were closed; the children's rooms contained no stranger;
and the spare chamber from which the woman seemed to
have issued was locked, the key being in my wife's
work-basket below.

L. related this occurrence while still crying and before she
had spoken with her sister. Her account never materially varied.
She had not injured herself, seen blood that day, or
heard any tale of murder. My wife at first suspected
that the red ribbon upon a discarded bonnet had suggested
the wound; but the bonnet had been put away in
a trunk before our arrival, and L. denied having seen
it.

There were now, it will be observed, four appearances to
consider: the tall, indistinct man beside our bed; the ragged
one-eyed woman; the red-faced person searching the nursery; and the
young woman with the wound in her throat. I do
not assert that the first was connected with the others.
Indeed, its want of characteristic detail distinguishes it from them,
and my wife never saw it again.

The one-eyed woman, on the contrary, was repeatedly observed by
the children in the yard. Their nursery window overlooked the
stable and the narrow strip of ground beside its eastern
wall. On several mornings they saw an old woman there,
crouching or kneeling, and scraping at the earth with her
hands. Sometimes she appeared to use a short piece of
iron; sometimes she merely pulled away weeds and loose soil.
She always worked at the same spot, near a disused
water-trough.

The children supposed her to be a poor person employed
by the landlord, and for some time said nothing. The
boy once called to her from the window, asking what
she was doing. She raised her face. He became frightened
by the blind eye and ran to the nurse. When
the nurse looked out, no one was there. The yard
gate was bolted on the inside.

Upon hearing this, I examined the place. The soil did
appear disturbed, but the children had played there, and the
stable-boy—who attended only during the day—might have loosened it. I
ordered R. to watch from an upper window on the
following morning. He saw nothing. M., however, declared on the
next evening that she had observed the woman pass the
stable door and bend down by the trough.

At this period our mare began to behave in an
unaccountable manner. She was a quiet animal, accustomed to town
traffic, and had never shown vice. Yet on several nights
she was heard plunging and striking the partition of her
stall. The stable-boy found her covered with sweat, her halter
pulled tight, and her eyes fixed upon the wall adjoining
the disturbed ground. She refused oats when placed near that
side of the manger, though she ate readily if the
feed was moved.

R. slept one night in the coach-house to detect any
person who might be tormenting her. At about two o'clock
she began to tremble, snort, and draw backward. R. entered
with a lantern. He saw no person or animal in
the stable, but the mare's attention remained fixed upon the
lower portion of the wall. He led her into the
yard, where she became quiet; when brought back she resisted
so violently that he left her in the coach-house until
daylight.

Stupid dog! you will say—for I had also a spaniel
which, during the whole of these proceedings, neither barked at
the mysterious noises nor displayed the smallest apprehension. He slept
before the kitchen fire while shadows crossed the wall, and
once lay against the coal-vault door at the very hour
M. alleged that the woman had passed through it. The
mare, an animal commonly credited with less sagacity, appeared to
perceive what escaped him.

As continued watching produced no discovery, I directed that the
ground near the stable wall should be dug up. I
did this chiefly to satisfy the children and servants, and
with no expectation of finding any thing more remarkable than
a broken drain.

The stable-boy removed the surface soil. At a depth of
perhaps eighteen inches his spade struck something which he supposed
to be a stone. On clearing the earth with his
hand, he drew out part of a human lower jaw.
It was brown, fragile, and incomplete at one end, but
several teeth remained in it. These teeth, though loosened by
decay, were sound and unusually even.

The discovery caused considerable commotion. I sent for a medical
gentleman, who examined the fragment and pronounced it unquestionably human.
He could not determine the sex with certainty from so
small a portion, but thought it had belonged to a
young or middle-aged adult. It did not appear recent. Whether
it had lain in the earth twenty years or a
hundred he would not undertake to say.

We enlarged the excavation along the wall and beneath the
trough. A quantity of dark soil, a corroded buckle, and
two scraps of material resembling leather were found, but no
other bone. The foundations had been repaired at some former
period, and it was possible that the jaw had been
brought with rubbish used to fill the trench. The medical
gentleman favoured this explanation. R. pointed out, however, that the
surrounding soil was natural sand, while the darker patch formed
an oval space about four feet long. This observation was
not professionally verified, and I give it only for what
it may be worth.

The jaw was delivered to the local authorities. A constable
made inquiries, but no record of a missing person connected
with the house could be discovered. The landlord professed total
ignorance and appeared more annoyed than interested. He asserted that
the terrace had been erected within the preceding twelve years,
upon ground previously occupied by sheds and waste inclosures. The
builder who had examined the vault confirmed that the interior
fittings and upper masonry were comparatively modern.

After the excavation, manifestations became less frequent. The knocking occurred
twice; the roof hammering once. M. saw the ragged woman
at the end of the passage, but she did not
approach the coal vault, and disappeared when the candle was
raised. E. believed she saw the red-faced man looking round
the nursery door, though only for an instant. The younger
girl never again saw the wounded woman.

One occurrence, not in itself important, deserves mention because it
was witnessed by several persons. My wife, R., and I
were seated in the dining-room shortly after dusk, discussing our
arrangements for departure. The door into the hall stood open.
A shadow, resembling a woman stooping beneath a burden, passed
across the illuminated wall of the hall from right to
left. Immediately afterward the handle of the basement door turned,
and three distinct knocks sounded from below.

R. took a candle and went down. I followed more
slowly. The coal-vault door stood open.

This was the first time we had found it so
after any disturbance. The key remained in the cook's pocket;
the lock had not been forced; the iron plate was
still barred. There was fresh coal dust upon the floor,
marked by a broad trail from the threshold to the
far wall, as though a heavy sack had been dragged
across it. The trail ended abruptly at the masonry.

R. searched the coal with an iron rod and afterward
had a considerable portion removed. Nothing was discovered. The lock,
when tested, worked properly. The cook was quite positive that
she had locked the door after the morning delivery and
had retained the key.

I could form no useful conjecture. If smugglers had access,
their ingenuity in entering a sealed vault was equalled only
by their folly in removing nothing and leaving a theatrical
mark upon the floor. If the servants combined to deceive
us, their purpose was equally obscure; moreover, R., whose fidelity
I had no cause to question, would have had to
participate.

My health being by then substantially restored, we prepared to
leave at the conclusion of our term. Two days before
our departure an elderly gentleman, a former officer in a
government department, called upon me with a letter of introduction
from a mutual acquaintance. In conversation he happened to ask
where we were staying. When I named the terrace, he
looked surprised and said that he remembered the site before
any terrace existed.

I told him that the houses were said to be
about twelve years old.

"The fronts may be," he replied, "but not the whole
of the building."

He then gave, in substance and purport—for I cannot pretend
at this distance of time to reproduce his exact expressions—the
following information.

More than fifty years earlier, when he was a boy,
a long, low government store had stood upon that ground.
It had been used at different periods for naval cordage,
seized goods, and miscellaneous materials connected with the coast service.
The position was then isolated. There were dunes toward the
sea, a rough road toward the town, and few inhabited
houses within half a mile. Smuggling was prevalent, and the
store, though nominally watched, was sometimes left for days in
charge of a single keeper.

The building had thick walls and vaulted lower rooms. After
disposal by the government it passed through several hands, was
partly heightened, divided by party-walls, and supplied with new fronts,
so that the present terrace incorporated a considerable portion of
the old structure. My visitor believed our coal vault to
have formed part of one of the original storage chambers.

He remembered also, though imperfectly, a local story concerning a
young woman who had disappeared in that neighbourhood. She was
said to have been connected either with a sailor or
a man employed about the store. Suspicion had fallen upon
several persons, but no body was found, and no charge
could be sustained. An old woman of disreputable character, blind
of one eye, had been questioned because she was believed
to shelter smugglers; and a rough fellow, remarkable for his
red complexion, had left the town soon afterward. Whether these
circumstances belonged to one occurrence or had been combined by
village gossip my informant could not say.

I asked if he recalled the name of the missing
young woman. He gave one, but as I have no
means of verifying it I shall not repeat it. He
thought she had dark hair and was the daughter of
a small tradesman. Of the alleged manner of her death
he knew nothing. The rumour, he said, was that she
had gone away voluntarily, though her family never heard from
her.

This information, coming as it did after the discovery near
the stable wall, impressed us more than it might otherwise
have done. I took the gentleman over the basement. He
declared the thickness and curvature of the coal-vault wall to
be characteristic of the old store, and pointed out that
the apparently modern door-frame had been inserted in earlier masonry.
In the yard he could not identify the exact position
of any former room, because the ground level and boundaries
had been altered.

I communicated his statement to the local authority, together with
a request that the excavation should be continued if judged
proper. I do not know whether any further search was
made. We left two days later, and the occupations to
which I returned prevented my revisiting the place. R. remained

It would be easy to construct a sort of story
out of the facts. The red-faced man may have murdered
the young woman in or near the old store; the
one-eyed woman may have assisted him in concealing the body;
the tall figure may have been some watcher, accomplice, or
wholly unrelated appearance; the searching of cupboards and boxes may
have represented an attempt to recover an article; the digging
beside the stable may have indicated the place where remains
were hidden; and the hammering, shadows, locked doors, and terror
of the mare may have been subordinate repetitions of circumstances
which we could not interpret.

But this is conjecture, and conjecture only. The jaw may
have been deposited with builders' rubbish. The elderly gentleman's recollection
may have been influenced by our questions. The children may
have dreamed; the servants may have exaggerated; my wife may
have experienced a waking illusion; R. and I may have
mistaken a living beggar in the town for the person
described in the house. Each incident, considered separately, admits some
such explanation. It is their accumulation, their correspondence in particulars
not always communicated between the witnesses, and their relation—if relation
there was—to the concealed age of the building, which constitute
the difficulty.

I have stated nothing of which I was not either
myself a witness or directly informed by the person concerned,
generally within a few hours of the event. I have
omitted several minor circumstances because they rested upon hearsay or
appeared capable of ordinary explanation. I do not affirm that
the house was haunted; still less do I profess to
know by what agency the appearances were produced. I record
only that such appearances were reported by honest, sensible, and,
in several instances, independent witnesses; that repeated searches failed to
discover an intruder or secret entrance; and that part of
a human jaw was found at the place where a
figure had been observed to dig.

The affair remains, in my judgment, a very pretty puzzle.
I had neither the health at first, nor the leisure
afterward, to pursue it further; and perhaps the want of
a conclusion preserves it more exactly in the form in
which it presented itself to us—not as a tale contrived
for terror, but as an inconvenient series of facts, each
trifling in isolation, and all together inexplicable.

[The foregoing statement was placed in our hands by a
gentleman whose position and character preclude any reasonable suspicion of
intentional misrepresentation. We have been permitted to compare it with
memoranda made by another member of the family, and with
a brief written declaration formerly supplied by the servant designated
R. These documents agree in all material circumstances. The house
has since undergone extensive alteration, and no satisfactory account of
the jawbone, or of any further excavation, has been obtained.—ED.]

The roses were the first things to offend Frank.

They burned along the white wall of the cottage in
great red clusters, too full, too loose, dropping petals on
the path as if the place belonged to them. When
he pushed open the gate, one branch caught the shoulder
of his new grey coat and left a green smear
near the seam.

"Confound the things," he said.

Eleanor, who had already gone up the path, turned and
looked at the mark without seeming to see it.

"It will brush off."

"It had better."

He rubbed the cloth with his handkerchief. The coat was
the best he had ever owned, narrow at the waist,
padded a little at the shoulders, and of a grey
which the tailor had called silver. It gave him, he
felt, the look of a man accustomed to leisure. Yet
here it was, scarcely three hours from London, made ridiculous
by a common briar.

Eleanor stood waiting before the cottage door. She was dressed
in white, with a small veil drawn from her hat
and a cream-coloured parasol hooked over her wrist. Against the
blazing garden she had an exhausted purity, as if all
the colour had been taken out of her and given
to the flowers.

"Well?" she said.

"The key."

"You have it."

Frank found it in the breast pocket beneath his railway
tickets. The door stuck at the sill. He put his
shoulder against it and entered with unnecessary force.

The cottage smelt of closed rooms, matting, and sea damp.
There were blue plates on the dresser, muslin curtains, an
ornamental ship under a glass case, and a clock which
had stopped at twenty minutes after six. From the back
window one saw the slope of the field and, beyond
it, a piece of the sea, hard and glittering as
metal.

Frank crossed the parlour, opening doors, lifting covers, examining chairs.
He had hired the cottage from a photograph and was
determined not to be cheated.

"Not bad," he announced. "They've done us fairly well. Though
the grate's small. You'd never get a decent fire in
that."

"In August?"

"There'll be wet evenings. At the seaside there always are."

Eleanor remained at the door, holding her parasol. Her gaze
moved over the room and came to rest on the
little ship. She knew it. The glass case had stood
in the same place twelve years ago. Dust had gathered
then, as now, upon the painted waves.

Frank followed her eyes.

"Ugly old thing. We can have it put away."

"No."

He looked at her. "You like it?"

"I remember such things."

Such things. Again there was that tone, faint and superior,
as though she had discovered a private meaning which he
was too clumsy to perceive. He pulled the end of
his moustache between his lips and sucked it thoughtfully.

"So this is where you lived."

"For a summer."

"You never told me much about it."

"There was not much to tell."

"And you're sure nobody's likely to know you?"

She moved then. She put the parasol on the table,
took off one glove, finger by finger, and laid it
beside the parasol.

"That is what I wanted to speak of. Frank, if
anyone asks, we have never been here before."

"Why on earth?"

"I should prefer it."

He laughed. "Running away from your creditors?"

"Don't be absurd."

"Old admirers, then?"

She turned quickly, but his face was genial. He was
pleased with the phrase and with himself for having made
it. In the silver-grey coat he felt capable of treating
all former admirers generously, provided they were not too numerous
and had not been too successful.

"Well?" he persisted.

"There were people here whom I knew. I don't want
to renew acquaintance."

"Too grand for them now you're Mrs. Frank Cresswell?"

She stiffened almost imperceptibly.

He had married her six weeks earlier. Sometimes, on waking,
he was still astonished by the fact. Eleanor Verney, with
her quiet voice, her long white hands, her friends who
spoke of Italy as though it were a neighbouring county—Eleanor
had married him. At the works, men who had once
slapped his back had become respectful. His mother had cried
from pride and then complained that the bride looked at
her "as if she was something brought in with the
coal." Frank had defended Eleanor. She was shy, he said.
She had had troubles. Her father had died owing money;
her mother had followed within the year; the grand friends
had thinned away. But he, Frank Cresswell, had come forward.
He had not cared that she was thirty-two. He had
not asked what money she brought. He had taken the
whole risk, and he had taken it cheerfully.

Yet she would never let him feel magnanimous for more
than five minutes.

"Mrs. Frank Cresswell," she repeated. "Yes. Perhaps."

He heard the perhaps.

"I don't see why we should sneak about."

"There is no need to sneak. Only don't tell people
my maiden name. That is all."

"What am I to call you if they ask?"

"Your wife, I suppose."

He gave another laugh, not because he was amused but
because laughter proved him easy. "All right. Mysterious lady. Have
it your own way."

At luncheon she ate nothing but a fragment of bread.
Frank made an excellent meal of cold beef, pickles, and
bottled beer which the caretaker had left. The train had
made him hungry, and her silence increased his appetite by
giving the meal the character of a contest.

Afterwards, while he lit his pipe, she stood at the
open window. The sea sent up a distant dragging noise.
Gulls turned over the field, flashing white, then grey, then
white again.

"What's the programme?" he asked. "A walk along the front?"

"You go."

"Aren't you coming?"

"I have a headache."

"Already?"

She placed her hand against her temple, but the gesture
was too late. "The journey tired me."

"You slept most of the way."

"I did not sleep."

"You had your eyes shut."

"There is a difference."

He knocked his pipe against the grate. "Of course there
is."

She looked back at him, and for a moment each
waited for the other to acknowledge the quarrel. Neither did.

Frank rose, tugged his waistcoat down, and checked his watch.

"I may go as far as the harbour. There ought
to be fishing. You're sure you won't come?"

"Quite sure."

"And if anyone calls?"

"No one will."

He went to the door, paused, and tried once more
to make a joke of it.

"I shall say my wife is a nameless princess under
a curse."

"Say nothing about me."

This time her seriousness pricked him.

"You know, Eleanor, a man might think you were ashamed
of him."

"Would he?"

"Are you?"

She bent over the table and began arranging the knives
which already lay straight.

"No."

He waited. Nothing followed.

The smart coat had seemed to lead him confidently into
the room; now it appeared to hang upon him, stiff
and conscious. He put on his hat.

"Well, I'm off."

She did not answer until the gate clicked behind him.

"Good-bye."

The word fell into the empty passage.

For several minutes Eleanor remained beside the table. She listened
to his boots on the road. Their sound grew faint,
returned for a moment where the road bent between walls,
then disappeared beneath the long wash of the sea.

She went upstairs.

In the bedroom she locked the door, though she was
alone. She removed her hat and pressed both hands against
her hair. The face in the glass had the dulled,
uncertain look of a face seen beneath water. She had
thought herself prepared. On the train she had repeated every
fact: the village would be altered; the people dispersed; the
rectory perhaps sold; the garden neglected or destroyed. Twelve years
had passed. There was nothing here but geography.

But the painted ship remained. The roses still struck against
the cottage wall. And from the window she could see,
beyond the roofs, the dark points of the rectory cedars.

She had not chosen the village. Frank had chosen it
from a list.

When he had said the name in the dining-room at
home, she had dropped the spoon into the sugar bowl.
He had laughed and asked whether she had seen a
ghost. Then she had told him, lightly, that she once
spent a summer there. He wanted cliffs, cheap lodgings, and
good sea-fishing. Why should a dead man prevent them?

A dead man could not.

She opened the small travelling case and took out a
folded handkerchief. Inside it was a ring, a thin gold
circle set with a dark blue stone. She did not
put it on. She held it in her palm.

Archie had worn its fellow on the little finger of
his left hand. He had bought the pair in a
hot, crowded shop at Portsmouth and had said, laughing, that
secret marriages required secret jewellery. They had not been married,
secretly or otherwise. They had been too young, too watched,
too certain that time would obey them.

He had sailed in September.

From Africa came three letters. The first was full of
dust, flies, incompetent officers, and a ridiculous monkey. The second
spoke of heat so violent that the ink dried on
the nib. The third, written in another hand, said Captain
Archibald Wray had been struck down while inspecting an outpost
and had died after two days without recovering consciousness.

She had read the letter in the rectory garden, beneath
the white roses.

For months afterwards the world possessed no weight. Cups slipped
from her fingers. People spoke and their voices did not
arrive. She walked through rooms without touching the floor. Then
her father's ruin came, and illness, and lodgings, and the
long discipline of accepting favours. Years closed over the wound,
not healing it but hardening around it. When Frank asked
her to marry him, she had said yes because the
word no seemed theatrical. She was thirty-two. She had no
money. He was honest and energetic and wanted her with
a force that resembled love sufficiently to warm her.

Now the ring lay in her palm, warm from her
flesh.

She wrapped it again and replaced it beneath the linen.
Then she washed her face, put on the hat, and
took the parasol.

She left by the back door.

The path crossed the field and descended between high banks.
In childhood—or what now seemed childhood—she had walked it daily,
gathering seed heads and striking them against her skirt. The
banks were higher than she remembered. Foxgloves leaned from the
shadow, their spotted throats open; small blue flowers trembled beneath
the nettles. The air held the hot green odour of
crushed fern.

At the lane she stopped.

To the right was the village and the sea. To
the left, beyond a stand of elms, stood the rectory.

Its chimneys appeared first, then the roof, then the pale
face of the house behind iron railings. The place looked
inhabited, though scarcely awake. Several upper blinds were drawn. The
windows below reflected the garden and showed nothing within.

Eleanor went past the gate.

Her heart struck painfully. She continued for perhaps fifty yards,
turned, came back, and passed the gate again. There had
once been a small entrance in the wall farther down,
used by the gardener. Ivy covered it now, but when
she pushed, the warped door yielded with a dry groan.

She entered.

At once the village vanished.

The garden had outgrown its old obedience. Laurel pressed upon
the path; honeysuckle netted the trees; tall lilies stood among
the weeds with an air of astonished nobility. Sunlight descended
in separate golden chambers. Beyond the kitchen garden lay the
long border where roses climbed over arches and posts, poured
across beds, mingled with lavender and scarlet sage.

The sight of them stopped her breath.

They were not the same roses. Many must have died;
others had been planted. Yet colour had kept faith where
everything else betrayed. Crimson blossoms opened darkly, almost black at
the centre. Pink roses crowded in little faces upon a
trellis. White ones floated under the cedar, pallid in the
shade. A flame-coloured climber had escaped its support and flung
itself along an apple tree, burning among the leaves.

She moved towards them.

The years loosened. They did not retreat in order; they
fell sideways, silently, like scenery in a dream. Archie was
on the lawn, striking matches in the wind. Archie leaned
over the sundial. Archie whispered through the library window while
her aunt slept upright with a book upon her knee.
His hands smelled of tobacco and crushed box. His laugh,
always slightly delayed, came after his smile.

Here he had kissed her first.

No—there, by the yew hedge.

No, it had been under the arch, because one rose
had caught in her hair and he had freed it,
clumsily, hurting her. Pain and delight had entered together. Afterwards
she had treasured the scratch on her temple until it
faded.

She reached the arch and put up her hand.

A petal detached itself and rested against her glove. She
turned her wrist, and it remained there, soft and pink,
as though choosing her. The garden knew her. That was
the dangerous thought. The garden had been waiting while she
endured cheap rooms, black dresses, polite humiliations, and Frank's broad,
hopeful face. It received her without astonishment. Its scents came
about her, intimate and accusing. She could surrender to them.
She could become again the girl in a thin summer
frock, moving towards a future so brilliant that it blinded
her.

She shut her eyes.

"Good afternoon."

The voice was near.

Her eyes opened.

A man stood on the farther side of the arch.

He was tall and spare, dressed in light flannels with
a dark military coat thrown over his shoulders despite the
heat. One hand rested on a walking stick. The other
hung beside him, palm slightly turned forward.

For a moment Eleanor noticed only the hand.

It was brown, narrow, and beautifully shaped. On the little
finger was a gold ring set with a blue stone.

The garden drew inward. Every leaf hardened. A bee moved
inside a rose with a sound enormous and intolerable.

The man stepped forward.

His hair had gone grey at the temples, and his
face was thinner than the remembered face, deeply marked beside
the mouth. But the eyes were the same clear hazel,
flecked with yellow. The small scar above his right eyebrow
was the same.

"Archie," she said.

He bowed.

"I beg your pardon."

She held the arch with both hands. Thorns entered her
glove.

"Archie."

He smiled, courteously and without recognition. "It is possible. They
sometimes call me that."

She stared.

He was alive. That single fact rose in her like
a shout. Alive. His chest lifted with breath; sunlight lay
upon his cheek; the pulse moved in his throat. All
these years he had existed somewhere beneath the same sky.
She had grieved for a lie. She had married upon
a lie.

"Don't you know me?"

His smile altered. It became careful.

"I know a great many people. One must be cautious
about names. They have a habit of changing them."

"I am Eleanor."

"Indeed."

"Eleanor Verney."

At the second name he looked past her towards the
yew hedge. His eyes narrowed as if he listened to
something a long way off.

She waited. Hope became a physical agony, stretching every nerve.

He said, "The Verneys left on Thursday."

"No. Look at me."

"They took the south train. Or perhaps that was the
regiment." He tapped the stick twice upon the gravel. "The
regiment is always leaving. Very wasteful. Men should remain where
they are put."

She came through the arch.

He retreated.

The movement was slight, but she saw it. Archie had
stepped away from her.

"It's Eleanor," she repeated. "Ellie. You called me Ellie."

"That would have been familiar."

"We were—" Her voice failed. She began again. "Before you
went away, here in this garden—"

"Gardens are not reliable." His tone was confidential now. "At
night they move the paths. I have mentioned it to
Wilkes, but he is in league with them."

He glanced sharply at the roses.

Eleanor saw then that his coat had been buttoned wrongly.
One brass button was missing. A dead leaf clung to
his shoulder. His shoelace was untied, though the shoes themselves
were polished to a military brightness.

"Were you ill?" she whispered.

"Oh, no. The sun was ill."

"In Africa?"

"They put it too near us. An error in calculation."
He leaned towards her, suddenly grave. "You mustn't drink the
light. However thirsty you are. It burns the names out."

His gaze touched her face and slipped away.

The words were absurd. The voice was Archie's.

That voice had once spoken her name in the dark.
It had trembled when he promised to return. Now it
moved through broken meanings with perfect manners, like a guest
wandering from room to room in a ruined house.

She reached for his hand.

He raised it quickly beyond her grasp.

"No," he said. "That belongs to someone."

The ring flashed.

"To whom?"

He examined it as if he had never seen it.

"A young lady, I believe."

"What young lady?"

"She was drowned."

Eleanor made a sound.

He watched her with mild concern. "Not recently."

"I am the young lady."

"That is unlikely."

"I gave you that ring. There are two. I have
the other."

"You should return it to the authorities."

His face was Archie's. It was not Archie. His hand,
his scar, the fine fold at the outer corner of
the eye—all Archie. Yet the man to whom these things
belonged had gone. Something wore his body gently, innocently, and
knew nothing of her.

She had imagined death as absence. She had fed her
love upon absence until it became pure, incorruptible. But this
was a worse subtraction. The beloved form remained, denying its
own history. It stood before her and made her memory
indecent.

"Archie, please."

He frowned. "You are distressed."

"Yes."

"Has someone died?"

The question entered her without pain because there was no
place left to hurt.

"Yes," she said. "Someone has died."

He nodded, satisfied by the orderliness of the answer.

"You should speak to the Rector. He keeps records."

"Captain!" a voice called from near the house. "Captain Wray!"

Archie turned at once. "They require me."

A stout woman in a dark dress appeared at the
end of the path. When she saw Eleanor, her expression
changed from alarm to suspicion.

"I'm sorry, madam. He doesn't generally come this far alone."

"I knew him."

The woman looked more closely at her. "Before, do you
mean?"

"Yes."

"Ah."

That one syllable contained knowledge, weariness, and warning.

"They told me he died," Eleanor said.

"Very near did. Fever and sunstroke together. They sent the
wrong report. By the time it was put right, he'd
been taken to a place in Cape Town. No papers
on him, you see. There was confusion for months."

"And afterwards?"

"His sister brought him home. She's the Rector's wife now.
He's quiet mostly." The woman lowered her voice. "Best not
to stir him. It does no good."

Archie had begun to walk towards the house, tapping each
paving stone with his stick before he trusted his foot
to it.

"Does he remember nothing?"

"Bits. Never in a way you can make use of."

As he reached the bend, he glanced back.

Eleanor's heart gave one violent leap.

For an instant his face seemed illuminated from within. He
saw her. Surely he saw. His lips parted; the old
delayed smile hovered.

Then he raised his hand in a formal salute.

"Good afternoon, Miss Thursday," he called.

He disappeared behind the laurels.

The stout woman murmured something, but Eleanor was already moving.
She passed under the arch, through the side door, into
the lane. Her parasol dragged from her wrist. The point
struck stones, tore leaves, caught in the hem of her
skirt. She did not notice.

The path across the field swelled and sank beneath her.
Heat pressed upon her head. She felt no grief, only
a vast alteration of substance, as if her bones had
been removed and she must somehow transport herself without them.

At the cottage she entered by the back door and
went upstairs.

She did not cry.

She sat on the bed in her white dress, holding
the torn parasol across her knees. Light moved slowly along
the wallpaper. Once she heard Frank return, whistling. Cupboard doors
opened below. A cork was drawn. He called her name.

She made no answer.

His tread came up the stairs.

The handle turned and struck the lock.

"Eleanor?"

She watched the strip of light beneath the door.

"Are you in there?"

"Yes."

"What's the door locked for?"

"My head is bad."

There was a pause.

"I brought some prawns. Fellow down at the harbour had
them alive in a basket. Come and look."

"I don't want to."

He tried the handle again.

"You've been out."

She looked at the dust on her shoes.

"Yes."

"I thought your head was bad."

"It became better."

"And now it's bad again?"

"Yes."

He said nothing. She could feel him standing on the
other side, his fingers still enclosing the brass handle.

"Where did you go?"

"For a walk."

"You might have waited for me."

"I preferred to go alone."

His hand left the handle. The boards creaked beneath his
weight.

"Dinner's at seven," he said.

When she came down, he had changed his coat and
was carving the cold mutton. The prawns stood on a
plate between them, curled and pink, their black eyes shining.
He had bought a bottle of wine and placed it
beside her glass.

"Thought it might do you good."

"Thank you."

He watched while she lifted the glass. She touched it
to her lips and put it down.

"Not right?"

"Quite right."

"Then drink it."

"I don't want it."

The meal proceeded beneath small noises: knife against plate, the
clock newly wound and ticking, Frank's breath through his nose.
Eleanor broke a potato with her fork and looked into
its floury interior.

Frank ate heavily at first. Then her stillness began to
make each mouthful difficult. She had always possessed the trick
of withholding herself while remaining in the room. Tonight the
trick became an outrage. Her face was pale and fixed.
Her eyes seemed to rest upon objects a great distance
behind him.

He poured more wine.

"Meet anyone?"

"No."

"You were gone a good while for a walk in
the field."

"I went farther."

"So you did meet someone."

She laid down her fork. "Why ask if you have
decided the answer?"

"Because I'd like to hear yours."

"I met no one who matters to you."

The sentence was quietly spoken. Its quietness struck harder than
anger.

Frank put both hands on the table. They were broad
hands, marked despite scrubbing by the black ingrained shadows of
the engineering works. He had once been proud to show
her those hands. They proved he had made himself. Tonight
beside her white fingers they appeared coarse and accused.

"Who matters to me?" he said.

She did not answer.

"I suppose your own wife ought to."

"Please, Frank."

"Please what?"

"Not tonight."

"Why not tonight? You've been odd ever since we came.
First I'm not to say who you are. Then you
send me off alone. Then you sneak out and lock
yourself in. If there's something going on, I've a right
to know."

"No."

"No?"

"You have no right to every thought I possess."

"I'm not asking every thought. I'm asking where you went."

"To the rectory garden."

He sat back.

"The place with the cedars?"

"Yes."

"Who lives there?"

"I don't know."

"That's a lie."

She looked at him at last. There was no heat
in her gaze, and this frightened him more than contempt.

"I knew the people who lived there twelve years ago."

"Ah. One of the acquaintances you're too fine to renew?"

"Something like that."

"A man?"

Her eyes moved away.

The jealousy which had been lying in him all day
rose swiftly, almost gratefully. It gave shape to her secrecy.
A man. Of course. Some polished gentleman with smooth hands
and a name that opened doors. Frank's mind created him
at once: tall, careless, educated, probably idle. The sort who
had possessed Eleanor before misfortune lowered her within reach of
Frank Cresswell.

"An old admirer," he said. "So my joke was near
the mark."

"Don't."

"Did you meet him?"

She stood.

"I am going upstairs."

He caught her wrist.

The contact shocked them both. He released her immediately, but
the marks of his fingers seemed to remain around the
white skin.

"You'll answer me."

"No."

"I'm your husband."

"Is that meant as a threat?"

"As a fact."

She turned and went from the room.

For perhaps a minute he sat motionless. Then he pushed
back his chair so violently that it fell. The clock
continued its tidy ticking. He righted the chair, because leaving
it fallen seemed childish, and followed her.

The bedroom door was not yet locked. He entered and
shut it behind him.

Eleanor stood at the window. She had removed her dress
and wore a white wrapper fastened at the throat. Her
clothes lay folded across a chair, the dusty hem turned
inward with exact care.

"You can't run away in a room," he said.

"I am not running away."

"You've done nothing else since we married."

She closed the shutters. At once the room contracted. The
lamp flame trembled in the glass chimney.

"What do you want me to say?"

"The truth."

"You would not know what to do with it."

"I'll chance that."

"No, Frank. You will punish me for it, and yourself
too."

His moustache was in his mouth again. He pulled it
free with an angry movement.

"You think I'm too common to understand."

"I did not say that."

"You needn't. You say it every time you look at
me. You married beneath you, didn't you? That's what you
tell yourself. Never mind that your fine relations had left
you without sixpence. Never mind who paid the bills."

Her face sharpened.

"There it is."

"What?"

"The account. You have kept it after all."

"I never asked gratitude."

"You ask it every day."

"I ask you to behave like a wife."

She gave a low laugh. "What does that mean? To
eat when you tell me? To walk when you tell
me? To open every locked drawer in my mind because
you have bought the furniture?"

The blood climbed into his face.

"I didn't buy you."

"Didn't you?"

He moved towards her. She did not retreat. Her composure
seemed to place him below her, and he hated her
for that height.

"Who was he?"

She was silent.

"Who was the man at the rectory?"

Still she said nothing.

"Was he your lover?"

The word struck through the room. It was not a
word Frank commonly used. In his mouth it sounded both
theatrical and obscene.

Eleanor's lips parted. For one instant he saw fear. Then
something in her yielded—not to him, but to destruction.

"Yes."

He had expected denial. The simple answer left him stranded.

"You mean—before?"

"Yes."

"How long before?"

"Twelve years."

"What was his name?"

"Archibald Wray."

"Captain Wray?"

"Yes."

Frank knew the name. A brass tablet in the church
porch bore it among the parish dead of foreign service.
He had read it that afternoon while sheltering from the
sun.

"He's dead."

"I believed so."

The room became strangely orderly in Frank's sight. He noticed
a crack in the washbasin, two dead moths within the
lamp shade, the hairbrush lying crooked on the dressing table.

"You believed so."

"I was told he died in Africa."

"And you were going to marry him?"

"Yes."

The answer came without apology.

"Your family wouldn't have it, I suppose."

"We did not tell them."

"So it was all secret."

"Yes."

"The whole hogger?"

She frowned. "What?"

"You and him. Were you his mistress?"

Colour rushed into her face.

"I loved him."

"That's not what I asked."

"It is the only answer you will have."

He turned away and planted himself before the empty grate.
The hearthrug showed sailing ships in blue wool. His boots
covered two of them.

"You loved him," he repeated.

"Yes."

"And then he died."

"I thought he died."

"And then you married me."

"Eleven years later."

"That's no answer either."

"It is the truth."

He stared at the black grate. Inside him something laboured
to remain reasonable. Eleven years. He had not known her.
A man could not demand a woman be born on
her wedding day. He had always said he was not
narrow-minded. Other men might rage at a past; he was
larger than that.

But it was not the past. Her face at dinner,
her secrecy, the locked door—these were present. The dead man
had sat between them from the beginning, wearing Frank's ignorance
like an invisibility cloak.

"Did you love me when you married me?"

She turned her head.

He wheeled upon her. "Answer."

"I respected you."

His body seemed to diminish inside his clothes.

"Did you love me?"

"I hoped I might."

"Hoped?"

"You were kind. You wanted me. I was tired."

"So I was rest and lodging."

"That is cruel."

"Cruel!"

"You insisted on the truth."

"I didn't insist on being flayed with it."

He crossed the room and stopped near her. His hands
opened and closed.

"All those times I said it. All those letters I
wrote. You let me make a fool of myself."

"I never laughed at you."

"No. You were too sorry for me."

"That is not fair."

"Did you think of him when I kissed you?"

She shut her eyes.

He saw the answer and felt rage pour through him,
hot and cleansing.

"You did."

"At first."

"At first! And afterwards?"

"I tried not to think."

"You tried."

"Yes."

He began to laugh. The sound came harshly, in separate
breaths. "There's my marriage. A woman trying not to think
of another man."

"I believed he was dead."

"What difference does that make to me?"

"None, perhaps."

He caught the bedpost and gripped it until the polished
wood hurt his palm.

"And today you went to mourn him prettily in the
garden."

"No."

"You thought you'd have your romance while your husband looked
at fishing boats."

"No."

"What, then?"

She stood very straight. Her face had become almost transparent,
all its life withdrawn behind the skin.

"I saw him."

Frank's grip loosened.

"Who?"

"Archie."

He waited, unable to attach meaning to the name.

"You said he was dead."

"He is alive."

"No."

"I spoke to him this afternoon."

Frank stared at her. Suspicion came first. Was this a
trick, some refinement of cruelty? But she had no look
of triumph. She looked not even miserable, but emptied.

"The tablet says—"

"The report was mistaken. He nearly died. They lost him
in a hospital. He was brought home afterwards."

"And you knew he was here?"

"No. I went to the garden because I wanted to
see the place. Then he came."

A new image entered Frank's mind: Eleanor moving beneath the
flowers towards a living man, her old lover, her true
lover. Their recognition. Their hands reaching. The embrace. He felt
sick.

"You met him there."

"Yes."

"Alone."

"For a little while."

"What happened?"

She did not answer.

"What happened between you?"

"Nothing."

"You expect me to believe that?"

"Nothing could happen."

"Why? Because someone came?"

"Because he did not know me."

Frank's anger checked itself.

"What do you mean?"

"He did not know me."

"He'd forgotten?"

"He is mad."

The words seemed too small to alter the room, yet
everything altered.

Frank took his hand from the bedpost.

Eleanor continued in the same level voice. "The sunstroke destroyed
his mind. He speaks. He walks about. He looks almost
as he did. But he remembers nothing properly. He called
me Miss Thursday."

Her mouth twisted. She turned from him, but there was
nowhere to turn except the closed shutters.

"I showed him—or told him—about the ring. He said the
girl who gave it to him was drowned. Then he
asked whether someone had died."

Frank said, "Good God."

"Yes."

The anger in him did not vanish. It lost its
object and rushed uselessly through his body. He had wanted
a rival he could hate: a gentleman, a seducer, a
living thief. Instead there was a broken man tapping stones
with a stick, and Eleanor's white face beside the lamp.

"You saw him alive," he said slowly, "after all these
years."

"Yes."

"And he didn't know you."

"No."

She sat upon the edge of the bed. Her hands
fell loosely in her lap.

For the first time he understood that the thing had
happened to her before it happened to him. All evening
he had taken her silence as an insult carefully prepared
for his benefit. But she had come home from the
garden carrying a death more absolute than the death she
had mourned. She had found the body and lost the
man.

Pity moved in him. At once jealousy seized the pity
and poisoned it.

"You're grieving for him now."

She looked up.

"Of course."

"Before me. In my room. Six weeks after marrying me."

"I cannot arrange this for your comfort."

"My comfort! You've never cared a curse for that."

"No."

The answer was not defiant. It was merely exhausted.

He stepped back as if she had struck him.

"No," he repeated. "You haven't."

She seemed about to speak, but he raised his hand.

"Don't. If you say you're sorry, I may do something
I'll regret."

"I was not going to say it."

His face contracted.

There again: even now she would not give him the
poor coin of a lie. He saw his own history
with humiliating clearness—his visits with flowers too large for her
vases, his jokes before her silent friends, the engagement ring
she had accepted after looking at it for a long
time, his pride at the wedding, his careful tenderness on
their first night. He had believed her reserve to be
delicacy. He had boasted inwardly that he alone could warm
her. But she had been cold because all her fire
belonged to a grave.

And now the grave had opened, not to return the
beloved, but to show her the wreck of him.

"What am I to you?" he asked.

She pressed her fingers together.

"I don't know."

"You must know."

"I knew this morning. Or thought I did."

"What did you think?"

"That you were my husband. That I had made a
choice and must live within it."

"Must?"

"Yes."

He gave a short, wounded breath. "Like a sentence."

"I did not mean—"

"You always mean it. That's the devil of you. You
mean more than you say, and when a man hears
it you look surprised."

He went to the door. The small room forced him
to pass close beside her. Her sleeve touched his hand,
and both drew away.

At the threshold he stopped.

She sat bent now, no longer proud, no longer even
distinctly Eleanor to him. She was a woman in a
white wrapper beneath a smoking lamp, and suffering had made
her remote as a figure viewed across water. He felt
remote too. Husband and wife were words that had belonged
to them an hour ago. Now they floated loose, requiring
other people.

He wanted her to call him back.

If she called, he did not know whether he would
comfort her or curse her. But he waited.

She lifted her head. Her eyes met his with the
terrible innocence of complete desolation.

"Frank," she said.

His heart struck.

Then she added, "Please leave me alone."

He placed both hands flat against the door, the broad
workman's hands spread upon the painted wood. For one moment
his shoulders shook. He thought she must see it; he
hoped she did not.

"All right," he said.

He opened the door.

Below, the clock ticked in the parlour. The prawns remained
on the table with their bright shells and blind black
eyes. The bottle of wine stood uncorked. Outside the window
the roses stirred against the cottage wall, brushing and withdrawing,
brushing and withdrawing, with a soft persistent sound.

Frank went down the stairs without looking back.

Eleanor heard each step.

When the front door closed, she did not move. She
had thought solitude was what she required. Now it came
upon her immense and exact.

Beyond the shutters the sea dragged itself along the shore.
Somewhere inland, concealed among flowers and dark windows, Archie moved
through a world in which names burned away and paths
changed their places. Frank walked out into the evening carrying
a wound she had given him and could not mend.
Between the two men she seemed to have no body,
no history, no certain self—only the memory of having once
been recognised.

She put her hand to her mouth.

At last the tears came, but they brought no release.
They fell soundlessly, one after another, upon her fingers, while
the lamp flame diminished and the locked room filled with
the scent of roses.

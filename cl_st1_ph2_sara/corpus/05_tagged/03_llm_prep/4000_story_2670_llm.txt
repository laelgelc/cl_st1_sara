At four o'clock, when the winter light had thinned to
the colour of skimmed milk, the bell rang.

She had been expecting it. Nevertheless she sat quite still
at her desk, one hand resting on a page she
had not read for ten minutes, and listened to the
small, impatient vibration pass through the studio.

So he had come.

Instantly the room drew itself together. The orange shade over
the lamp burned more warmly; the two red chairs turned
their broad backs toward the darkening windows; even the blue
teapot, waiting on its tray, appeared to swell with importance.
She looked round with the absurd pride of a mother
whose brilliant children are about to be inspected.

The bell rang again.

"Coming!"

She opened the door.

He stood there smiling, cold and narrow in his dark
coat, with drops of fog shining on his shoulders.

"You had forgotten me."

"I had not."

"You always say that after forgetting."

"And you always accuse me before taking off your coat."

This was their way. It delighted them—the briskness, the little
polished blows that never wounded. He stepped inside, and she
took his coat as though accepting a challenge. Beneath it
he seemed younger, less formal, almost defenseless.

"Tea?" she asked.

"Is there cake?"

"There is always cake."

"Then why ask?"

"Because one day I shall answer, 'No cake,' and see
whether the friendship survives."

They laughed. How easy it was! How clean! Other people,
poor entangled creatures, required compliments, assurances, explanations. But they had
passed beyond all that. They were thirty, or nearly; they
had suffered, observed, read immensely. Passion—what an old-fashioned mechanism!—had long
ago been examined, labelled, and set aside. Their friendship was
founded on the mind. Two open cities, each visible from
the other's highest tower; no walls, no ambushes, no foolish
flags.

He walked to the fire and held out his hands.

"You have moved the little table."

"Three inches."

"I knew it."

She paused over the teapot. "Did you?"

"And there were white flowers in that bowl."

"Last week."

"There should still be white flowers."

The words were nothing, but the tone—no, not the tone,
something beneath the tone—made her glance at him. He was
looking into the fire.

So he notices, she thought. He notices everything here.

A warmth, separate from the warmth of the kettle, ran
up her neck and settled behind her ears. At once
she busied herself with the cups.

"You are becoming domestic," she said. "It is alarming."

"I am exact, not domestic."

"Exact people marry domestic people and spend their lives correcting
the position of cushions."

"Then save me."

The kettle began to sing. Its thin, silvery note rose
between them, climbed, trembled, and seemed suddenly too loud.

She lifted it quickly. Quickly! Pour the water, turn the
pot, cut the cake. The cake was dark and damp,
with a rum smell; the knife entered it softly. He
watched her hands. She felt him watching and could not
remember whether she had already given him sugar.

"One or two?"

"You know."

"Do I?"

"You have known for seven years."

Two, then. Of course, two.

For a moment neither spoke.

It ought to have been a pleasant silence. They had
always been proud of their silences. With ordinary people, silence
was a cupboard where embarrassment hid among old coats; with
them it had been a clear space, a sunlit courtyard.
They could sit opposite one another and understand—so they said—without
the crude machinery of words.

But this silence was not clear.

It was a dark pool. A thought had fallen into
it; the ripples widened, touched them both, vanished into a
glittering depth. She stirred her tea though the sugar was
dissolved. He broke his cake into three pieces and left
them untouched.

His secret self leaned toward her.

Say it.

Her secret self drew back.

Say what?

You know.

No. Nothing. There is nothing to know.

He raised his cup. "Excellent tea."

She almost laughed with relief. "You sound like an aunt."

"Your tea deserves elderly praise. It has body. It has
moral seriousness."

"And the cake?"

"The cake is corrupt. Entirely without principle."

"Then you may have another piece."

"I shall sacrifice myself."

The surface was restored. They skimmed over it beautifully. He
described a luncheon at which a poet had worn a
green scarf and spoken for twenty minutes about sincerity. She
invented a society for the suppression of young men who
used the word "cosmic." He proposed a rival society for
women who placed bowls of pebbles on writing desks.

She looked at the bowl of pebbles on hers.

"You beast!"

"You invited candour."

"Candour, yes. Savagery, no."

They laughed again, but now the laughter had a little
crack in it. She heard the crack; he heard her
hearing it. Their eyes met.

What happened?

Nothing. It lasted less than a second. Yet in that
second the studio fell away—the bright shade, the blue pot,
the red chairs, the crumbs on the plate—and they stood,
it seemed, under an enormous night sky, with no shelter
anywhere. His face was strange to her and terribly familiar.
There was an appeal in it, and fear behind the
appeal, and behind the fear—she would not look. She could
not.

She bent over her cup.

He thought: I have hurt her.

She thought: He has seen me.

Then each, with identical panic, reached for the nearest safe
idea.

"What are you reading?" he asked.

She touched the open book on the desk. "A novel.
Allegedly."

"Bad?"

"Very capable."

"Ah. Fatal."

"It contains detail."

"Detail is not necessarily fatal."

"This detail is. The author harvests detail. Page after page
of harvest. Buttons, railway tickets, spoons, the precise condition of
a widow's left boot—"

"Perhaps the future of the novel lies in the left
boot."

"There is no future in boots."

"Psychologically—"

"Yes, psychologically," she cried, too brightly, "that is exactly the
trouble."

And now they were off. The psychological novel—what was it?
What might it become? Was consciousness a river, a room,
a series of lantern slides, a badly managed hotel? Did
one reveal character through action, or was action merely the
vulgar shadow cast by character? They spoke rapidly, eagerly, interrupting
each other with pleasure so exaggerated that it became work.

He sat forward, elbows on his knees.

"The old novelist says, 'He loved her.' The new novelist
must ask what 'he' is, what 'loved' means, whether 'her'
existed except as an arrangement of his perceptions."

"And after three hundred pages," she said, "she has missed
the train."

"The train is irrelevant."

"The train is the one honest thing in the book."

"But surely—"

"Surely nothing."

Their ideas, usually alive and flashing, seemed tonight to arrive
padded in wool. Heavy ideas. Stodgy ideas. Elderly, upholstered minds
wheeled out to sit between them. They piled argument upon
argument as children pile furniture against a door.

Behind the door—what?

A cry sounded far away. They were hunters beside a
fire in a jungle, pretending not to have heard it.
One said, "The modern use of time is interesting." The
other answered, "Only where form supports it." Meanwhile the cry
came again, nearer, unmistakable.

You've hurt me.

No, you have hurt me.

Why did you look at me like that?

Why did you answer?

She rose and carried the cake plate to the little
table, though there was no reason to move it. The
window had turned black. Beyond the glass, the garden was
only a few tangled lines: the pale droop of the
willows, the ivy shouldering over the wall. One star had
appeared above the chimney.

He watched her reflected face floating in the pane.

If I went to her now, he thought, if I
simply stood beside her—

But do what? Touch her shoulder? Take her hand? Such
gestures belonged to another language, one they had spent seven
years mocking. They had said friendship was finer because it
demanded nothing. They had congratulated themselves on being free. Free!
The word now seemed to flap idiotically over an empty
field.

She turned.

He looked away too late.

Again the silence opened.

This one was worse, because it contained the memory of
the first. It entered her body like cold. She could
feel every object in the room waiting: the cup against
his fingers, the folded napkin, the round brass clock, even
the fire, holding a bright red breath.

She wanted him to speak. She dreaded his speaking. The
two desires pressed against each other until she could scarcely
breathe.

Say something—anything.

He put down his cup.

"I'm afraid I must go."

The clock ticked once.

"Go?"

"I have an engagement."

"Of course."

"I had forgotten the time."

"Of course."

He did not have an engagement. He knew she knew.
The lie lay on the carpet between them, small, grey,
shameful. Yet having placed it there, he was obliged to
step over it.

She crossed to the door and brought him his coat.
Her movements were neat and swift. No accusation; that would
have been easier. She held the coat open. He put
one arm in, then the other, while some absurd intimate
knowledge of his shoulder, the shape and warmth of it
beneath the cloth, stabbed through her.

"Thank you for the psychological novel," he said.

"My pleasure."

"We settled its future."

"Completely."

They were grinning now—two painted puppets jigging above nothingness.

At the outer door the night met them with a
damp breath. The little garden path gleamed. Ivy scratched faintly
at the wall; the willows bent like tired women over
the fence. Above them the sky had deepened, star after
star pricking through.

He turned on the step.

She stood framed by the yellow hall light. For one
instant he thought she would call him back. Her lips
parted.

"Good night," she said.

"Good night."

He walked away.

She watched until the gate clicked behind him. Even then
she remained at the door, looking at the place where
he had disappeared, as if the dark might reconsider and
give him back. It did not. A gust shook the
ivy. Somewhere a motor horn sounded, jaunty and remote.

She closed the door.

At once fury took her.

Stupid! Stupid! She marched into the studio and snatched up
the cups. His still held a mouthful of cold tea.
That offended her beyond endurance. She tipped it into the
fire, where it hissed viciously.

An engagement! The coward. The solemn, clever coward. And she—had
she not been worse? Standing by the window like a
heroine in a cheap play, waiting for a man to
cross a carpet! How grotesque. How humiliating. They, who had
believed themselves honest! Two open cities indeed—two locked cupboards, each
boasting of its architecture.

She pushed the red chair back so violently that it
struck the table.

Something had ended. She knew it with a certainty that
was almost luxurious in its pain. The friendship, or their
belief in it, or that unspoken possibility which had come
alive only long enough to be strangled—ended, ended. He would
return, perhaps. Next week they might discuss style, eat corrupt
cake, laugh at green scarves. But beneath it would be
tonight's absence, a missing stair over which they must forever
step carefully.

She sat at her desk and covered her face.

Anguish—anguish. What a theatrical word. She despised it. Yet there
it was, not grand at all, but hot and childish
and beating behind the eyes.

The bell rang.

She sprang up.

He had come back.

The knowledge flooded her so swiftly that before thought could
intervene she was in the hall, one hand at her
hair, the other already turning the latch. Of course he
had come back. He had reached the gate, the corner,
perhaps the next street; then the truth had seized him.
He would stand there breathless, ashamed—

She opened the door.

An elderly woman waited on the step.

For a moment neither moved.

The visitor wore a brown coat shiny at the seams
and a hat with a tired feather. In both gloved
hands she held a shabby bunch of violets wrapped in
damp paper. Their leaves were bruised; the flowers, dark and
slightly soiled, drooped against her wrist.

"Oh," said the woman. "I hope—I'm not too late?"

Disappointment struck so brutally that the younger woman almost stepped
back. She knew the visitor: a faithful, lonely admirer who
attended lectures, sent clippings, and wrote letters in a tiny
sloping hand. She had once invited her to call—carelessly, generously,
never imagining the invitation would be used.

"I saw your light," the woman continued. "I thought perhaps
just for a moment. I brought these. They're not very
good, I'm afraid. The man said they were fresh, but
on the omnibus—"

The younger woman looked at the violets. She wanted to
say she was busy. She was tired. Another evening. The
phrases assembled themselves, smooth and reasonable.

Then the visitor raised her eyes.

There it was again.

Not the same question, and yet the same: the naked,
silent question which asks nothing because asking might make refusal
inevitable. The old woman's smile trembled. She had come through
the damp night carrying sixpence worth of dying flowers and
her foolish, unprotected affection.

How easy to close the door. How easy to call
fear wisdom, indifference freedom, silence dignity.

The younger woman felt something within her give way—not break,
exactly, but loosen, like ice under a sudden warm rain.

"My dear," she said.

The old woman blinked.

"My dear, you are cold."

She took the bruised violets. Their scent rose faintly, astonishingly
sweet. Then, before the visitor could offer another apology, she
put her arms around the narrow brown shoulders and kissed
the powdery cheek.

The little body stiffened. For a heartbeat it remained rigid
with surprise; then it softened against her. A gloved hand
touched her sleeve.

Neither spoke.

But this silence was different. No dark pool, no jungle
cry, no abyss disguised by words. It was small and
warm and mortal. It asked to be lived in, that
was all.

When they drew apart, the old woman's eyes were shining.

"I only wanted you to know," she whispered, "how much
your work has meant to me."

"Yes," said the younger woman. Her own voice shook. "Yes.
I do know."

She led her through the hall, past the orange light
and the disordered tea things, and settled her in the
red chair beside the fire. She relit the kettle. There
was one piece of cake left, a little dry at
the edge, but the visitor ate it with such grateful
delicacy that it seemed a feast.

They talked for ten minutes—no, hardly talked. The old woman
told her of the omnibus, the flower seller, a sister
dead many years. The younger woman listened. She did not
improve the story, interpret it, or fit it into a
theory. She listened.

At last the visitor rose.

At the door, the garden showed itself again: black ivy,
silvered path, willows bowed beneath the stars. Yet it no
longer seemed immense or threatening. The night had boundaries now—the
gate, the wall, the circle of light around two women.

The younger woman took both gloved hands.

"Good night, my friend. Come again soon."

"Oh!" The old woman's face opened like a lamp. "May
I?"

"You must."

She watched her pass carefully down the path, the tired
feather nodding, and this time when the gate clicked there
was no desolation in the sound.

Back in the studio, she placed the violets in the
white bowl. They were battered, certainly; one or two were
almost dead. Still, against the pale china they possessed a
dark, secret splendour.

She sat at her desk.

The anger had gone. Not resolved—nothing was resolved—but washed clean
of its vanity. She took a sheet of paper and
began to write to him.

She wrote that their discussion of the psychological novel had
interested her. She thought they had been wrong about detail;
perhaps detail was not harvest but seed. She wrote that
consciousness might be less like a river than a room
repeatedly entered by different doors. She made a joke about
the widow's boot. She did not mention the silence, his
lie, the look at the window, or the moment when
the world had opened beneath them.

Yet all these were in the letter.

They lived between the lines, breathed in the crossings-out, waited
in the space before the final sentence. She held her
pen there for a long while. The kettle gave one
last sleepy murmur. In the fire, a piece of coal
shifted and released a brief wing of flame.

Then she bent her head and wrote:

"Good night, my friend. Come again soon."

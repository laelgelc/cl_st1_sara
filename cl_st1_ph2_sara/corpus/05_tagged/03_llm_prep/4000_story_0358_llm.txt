Once on a time there were five wives who lived
in five little houses under one green fell, and not
one of them had a bairn to rock in a
cradle or carry at her kirtle-tail. So they sighed when
the spring came and the lambs skipped, and they sighed
when the summer came and the birds fed their young,
and in winter they sat together spinning and said that
a house without a child was like a grange without
corn.

One day, as they went to the wood for sticks,
they found an egg lying under a juniper bush. It
was no hen's egg, nor goose egg, nor eagle's egg,
for it was as big as a goodman's head and
speckled red and brown like an old iron pot.

"Here's luck," said the first wife. "We'll hatch it."

"Change and change about," said the second.

"And if there's a bairn inside," said the third, "we'll
have a fifth share apiece."

So they carried the egg home in a meal-sack between
them, though it weighed as much as a millstone, and
the first wife sat upon it all Monday, the second
all Tuesday, the third all Wednesday, the fourth all Thursday,
and the fifth all Friday. On Saturday and Sunday they
all sat round it to keep off the draught.

Thus they sat and sat, and warmed and warmed, for
five weeks, then five months, and then five days over.
At last there came a crack that shook the pans
from the shelf, and a fist broke through the shell.
Then came a head, then two shoulders, and then a
boy as broad as a chopping-block tumbled out upon the
floor.

He was the ugliest bairn ever seen. His hair was
like scorched heather, his nose turned east while his chin
turned west, one eye was grey and the other green,
and his mouth went almost twice round his face.

Still, the five wives were glad.

"What shall we call him?" asked one.

"Call him Fairface," said another.

"Nay," said the third, "that would be a lie."

Just then the bairn opened his mouth and grumbled so
loudly that soot fell down the chimney. After that he
swallowed the eggshell, all but one splinter, and cried for
more.

"We'll call him Grumblegizzard," said the eldest wife, and Grumblegizzard
he was called.

The wives brought him a bowl of milk.

"More," said he.

They brought him a pan of brose.

"More."

They brought him twelve herrings and a rye loaf.

"More."

Before sunset he had eaten five pots of porridge, five
cheeses, a ham, two baskets of turnips, and the leather
strap off the meal-chest.

"What would you have now?" cried the wives.

"Herrings and brose, porridge and milk," said Grumblegizzard. "And plenty
of it."

He grew as fast as he ate. On Monday he
was as tall as the table; on Tuesday he was
as tall as the door; on Wednesday his head went
through the thatch. By Friday he could lift all five
houses, one after another, to hunt for crumbs beneath them.

The wives baked and boiled, churned and brewed, but it
was no use. Grumblegizzard ate them out of house and
home. He swallowed the winter meal, the spring seed, and
the last salt herring in the tub. Then he gnawed
the wooden spoon.

"Well, bairn," said the eldest wife, "we love you as
well as five mothers may, but if you stay till
morning, you'll eat the beds under us."

"What shall I do now?" asked Grumblegizzard.

"Go out and seek work," said they. "And here's a
scrip with five bannocks, one from each of us."

Grumblegizzard hung the scrip round his neck, put all five
bannocks into his mouth, and went over the fell.

Before long he came to a great farm where the
goodman stood in the yard, scolding his grieve because twelve
horses could not drag a stone from the middle of
a field.

"Will you hire me?" asked Grumblegizzard.

"What can you do?" said the farmer.

"Eat," said Grumblegizzard. "And maybe work, if there's enough eating."

The farmer looked him up and down.

"I'll give you a man's wage and a man's board."

"Make the board for twelve men," said Grumblegizzard, "and I'll
do the work of twelve."

The goodman thought this a fine bargain. First he set
Grumblegizzard to plough the stony field. There were six horses
before the plough, and each horse was as stout as
a church door.

But Grumblegizzard unharnessed them.

"They pull awry," said he.

Then he took the plough in one hand, drew it
himself, and cut furrows so deep that springs bubbled in
the bottoms. When he struck a boulder, he did not
turn aside. He drove straight through it, and the stone
flew in two like a rotten cheese.

At noon the maid brought him a bowl of porridge
and a jug of milk.

Grumblegizzard peeped into the bowl.

"Is this for tasting?" said he.

"It's your dinner," said the maid.

"Then where's the dinner after it?"

So the farmer had twelve bowls filled, and twelve more,
and Grumblegizzard ate the bowls along with the porridge because
he was in haste.

Next day the goodman told him to clear the wood
beyond the grange. It would have taken twenty men all
winter.

Grumblegizzard tore up the fir trees by their roots, three
in each hand, and laid them down like windfalls. Then
he bound the whole wood together with a twisted oak
and dragged it home. But he came too near the
barn, and the roots swept barn, byre, pigsty, and henhouse
into one heap.

"You've ruined me!" cried the farmer.

"I've cleared the wood," said Grumblegizzard. "What shall I do
now?"

The farmer thought awhile.

"Yonder lies a stone that twelve horses cannot stir. Carry
it to the boundary."

Grumblegizzard lifted the stone, but beneath it was another, and
beneath that a third. He gathered all three into his
scrip, where they rattled like hazelnuts, and strode to the
boundary. Yet when he set them down, he set them
atop the farmer's new mill, and mill, wheel, and millstones
sank into the earth.

Now the goodman saw that another week of such service
would leave him neither roof nor field.

"You're too fine a worker for the likes of me,"
said he. "The king needs strong men."

"Does the king give herrings and brose, porridge and milk?"

"He has whole storehouses."

"Then I'll go to the king."

So Grumblegizzard went to the palace. At the gate stood
twelve guards with silver pikes.

"What's your business?" said they.

"To eat the king's dinner and do the king's work."

They laughed and crossed their pikes, but Grumblegizzard gathered guards
and pikes into one bundle and set them on the
gatehouse roof. Then he walked in.

The king sat at table with his court, and there
were peacocks, pies, puddings, salmon, sweetmeats, and seven sorts of
wine before him.

"That will do for a beginning," said Grumblegizzard.

Before anyone could stop him, he swept the feast into
his mouth, silver dishes and all.

The king went pale.

"What manner of monster are you?"

"A hungry one," said Grumblegizzard. "What shall I do now?"

Just then a messenger rushed in. A mighty army had
crossed the eastern border. There were ten thousand footmen, five
thousand horsemen, and three hundred cannon, and their general had
sworn to eat supper in the king's hall.

The king looked at Grumblegizzard and thought, "If the army
kills him, I am rid of him; and if he
kills the army, I am rid of the army."

"Go and drive away my enemies," said the king. "If
you return, you shall have a sack of gold."

"And my board?"

"Herrings and brose, porridge and milk, as much as you
can hold."

"That's fair speech," said Grumblegizzard.

He went eastward and found the army filling a whole
valley. The musketeers fired first, and the bullets pattered on
his hide.

"Bilberries," said Grumblegizzard. "But sour ones."

Then the cannon roared, and a ball struck him on
the knee.

"Big peashooters," said he. "Children should take care with such
toys."

He pulled up a fir tree and used it for
a broom. With one sweep he brushed away three regiments,
and with the next he scattered the horsemen like chaff
on a threshing-floor. The general galloped at him, waving a
sword.

"That's a pretty bodkin," said Grumblegizzard.

He pinched sword, horse, and general between finger and thumb
and thrust them into the mouth of the largest cannon.
Then he fired it, and they flew so far that
no man knows where they came down.

The rest of the army ran. Grumblegizzard caught them in
his cap, shook them till they promised never to return,
and emptied them over the border.

Then he went back.

"What shall I do now?" he asked.

The king did not like to see him.

"Your gold is not yet weighed," said he. "But there
is another service. Beyond the black fell lies a manor
belonging to Old Nick's mother. She has paid no land
tax for seven hundred years. Go and collect it."

The king felt sure the Devil's mother would cook Grumblegizzard
in her oven.

So Grumblegizzard crossed the black fell, where the stones smoked
and the streams ran warm, until he came to a
house roofed with red tiles and fenced with dead men's
ribs. An old woman sat at the door, combing her
hair with a rake. She was Old Nick's mother, and
so ugly that Grumblegizzard seemed almost handsome beside her.

"What do you want?" she screeched.

"Land tax," said he.

"I pay none."

"Then I'll take the land."

He stooped, put both arms round the house, garden, pigsty,
and seven acres of ground, and began to lift. The
old woman saw her chimney leaning and her cabbage beds
sliding into the pit.

"Hold! Hold!" she cried. "What tax do you claim?"

"Seven horse-loads of gold, seven of silver, and dinner."

She paid the gold and silver, but at dinner she
brought one black loaf and a dish of smoking toads.

"Is that all?"

"All."

"Then I'll eat the cook."

At that the old woman ran to her pantry and
fetched twelve hams, twelve cheeses, twelve barrels of meal, and
a copper of porridge big enough to wash an ox
in. Grumblegizzard ate and drank, drank and ate, till not
so much as a bone remained.

Just as he slung the gold and silver over his
shoulder, Old Nick came home. He had horns like hayforks,
feet like millstones, and a tail that could wind three
times round a church.

"Who has robbed my mother?" he roared.

"I've collected the king's tax," said Grumblegizzard.

Old Nick rushed at him, but Grumblegizzard struck him across
the nose with a horse-load of silver. The Devil fell
into the cabbage bed, and while he was pulling his
horns from the earth, Grumblegizzard set off.

Old Nick soon followed, leaping a mile at each stride.
Grumblegizzard heard him coming, so he emptied the horse-loads of
gold into his left shoe and the silver into his
right. Then he ran both far and long, and every
step drove the treasure deeper under his heels.

At the first valley Old Nick nearly caught him. Grumblegizzard
snatched up a lake and threw it behind. The Devil
had to drink it dry before he could pass.

At the second valley he nearly caught him again. Grumblegizzard
tore a crag from the fell and cast it behind.
Old Nick bored through it with his horns.

At the third valley the Devil stretched out his claws
and seized Grumblegizzard's coat-tail. Grumblegizzard slipped out of the coat,
and Old Nick tumbled backward with it into a bog.

So Grumblegizzard reached the palace first and poured the gold
and silver upon the floor. There was so much that
it buried three councillors to their necks.

"What shall I do now?" he asked.

The king wished the gold had buried him altogether.

"Well," said he, "there's a seven-headed troll in the north
wood. He has stolen my grandfather's sword, and he keeps
an enchanted goodman shut in stone. Bring me the sword,
and perhaps we'll reckon your wages."

Grumblegizzard went north. At dusk he found the troll's cave.
Outside it lay bones enough to fence a parish, and
inside the seven-headed troll sat eating seven roasted oxen, one
head to each ox and the middle head sharing between
two.

"Good evening," said Grumblegizzard.

"It may be your last," said all seven heads.

"That meat smells thin," said Grumblegizzard. "Have you porridge?"

The troll sprang up. He had a club smithied from
fifteen tons of iron, and he swung it so hard
that the wind of it flattened the wood for half
a mile.

Grumblegizzard caught the club.

"This might crack nuts," he said, "if the nuts were
small."

Then he bent it round the troll's seven necks and
tied a knot. The heads bit one another, cursed one
another, and called for room. While they quarrelled, Grumblegizzard took
the king's ancestral sword from the wall.

The sword was twelve ells long and broad as a
barn door.

"Too light for chopping wood," said he, "but it may
shave trolls."

With one sweep he cut off all seven heads. Six
rolled into the fire, and the seventh flew out of
the cave and frightened an owl from its nest.

Behind the troll's chair stood a black stone with a
human voice crying inside it.

"Strike me three times with the sword!" called the voice.

Grumblegizzard struck once, and the hill trembled. He struck twice,
and the cave roof cracked. He struck three times, and
the black stone burst. Out stepped a grey-bearded goodman in
a blue coat.

"I was lord of the northern marches," said he, "until
the troll enchanted me. You have set me free."

"Then you can cook," said Grumblegizzard. "I've had no supper."

The enchanted man touched the wall, and a table appeared
laden with beef, bread, fish, puddings, and milk. Grumblegizzard sat
down and ate the table bare. Then he put the
ancestral sword under his arm and carried the freed man
to the palace in his pocket.

The king rejoiced to see the sword, but not Grumblegizzard.

"What shall I do now?" asked Grumblegizzard.

The king rubbed his chin.

"On the western moor stands an empty castle. No one
can sleep there three nights and live. If you do
so, I'll pay all I owe you and give you
my daughter besides."

The princess was fair and sensible, and when she heard
this, she said, "Father, you mean to cheat him."

"A king cannot cheat," said the king. "Whatever a king
does is policy."

So Grumblegizzard went to the western castle with a sack
holding twelve barrels of meal, twelve tubs of herrings, and
a churn of milk.

The castle had no glass in its windows, no fire
on its hearths, and no living thing beneath its roof
but rats as big as sheep. Grumblegizzard made porridge in
the copper, ate six dozen rats for relish, and lay
down upon the hall floor.

At midnight twelve headless men came marching down the stair,
each carrying his head under his arm.

"This is our hall," said the heads.

"There's room enough," said Grumblegizzard. "Lie down and be quiet."

They rolled their heads at him like bowls. Grumblegizzard caught
them and tossed them into the porridge copper. Then the
headless bodies ran round and round, feeling beneath tables and
chairs.

"If you want them," said Grumblegizzard, "wait till breakfast."

At cockcrow the ghosts vanished, but the heads remained, and
Grumblegizzard ate them with salt.

The second night there came a woman as tall as
a fir-stump, with iron teeth and a tongue like a
red blanket.

"I'll grind your bones," she cried.

"You may grind my meal first," said Grumblegizzard.

She took the hand-mill and ground twelve barrels before she
understood the trick. Then she flew at him. He seized
her by the tongue, swung her round his head, and
flung her up the chimney. She shot into the moon
and left a black mark there, which may be seen
to this day when the weather is clear.

On the third night Old Nick himself came stamping in,
still wearing Grumblegizzard's torn coat round his horns.

"Now I have you," said the Devil.

"Sit down," said Grumblegizzard. "You look heated."

There stood in the hall an iron chair that Grumblegizzard
had found in the smithy. He had hammered its arms
inward and smeared the seat with twelve pots of pitch.

Old Nick sat, and at once Grumblegizzard bent the iron
arms round him.

"Let me loose!" roared Old Nick.

"Not till morning."

The Devil kicked so hard that the castle jumped from
one side of the moat to the other. He breathed
fire till the roof melted, and he stretched his tail
through seven doorways. But Grumblegizzard sat on his horns and
ate herrings.

At cockcrow Old Nick began to whine.

"What will you take to free me?"

"Build the king a bridge across the Great Sound," said
Grumblegizzard. "A bridge of stone, wide enough for twelve carts
abreast, and have it ready before sunrise tomorrow."

"What payment?"

"The first soul that crosses shall be yours."

Old Nick thought that would surely be Grumblegizzard or some
royal lord.

"Done," said he.

So Grumblegizzard untwisted the chair, and the Devil flew off.
All that day and night he and his imps quarried
the fells, tore rocks from the sea bottom, and laid
arch upon arch. They worked and worked, and the sparks
from their hammers looked like stars. Before sunrise the bridge
stood complete, stretching over the Great Sound from shore to
shore, with towers at either end and paving-stones smooth as
cheese-rinds.

Old Nick waited beneath the first arch with his sack.

Grumblegizzard came from the castle carrying a basket.

"Cross over," called Old Nick. "The bridge is ready."

"A bargain is a bargain," said Grumblegizzard.

He opened the basket and took out a billy goat.
He tied a bladder to the goat's tail, filled the
bladder with dried peas, and struck it smartly.

The goat dashed over the bridge, while the peas rattled
behind like musketry.

"There goes your first soul," said Grumblegizzard.

"I'll not have that stinking goat!" cried Old Nick.

"Do you refuse him?"

"I do."

"Then you've refused your toll, and no second payment is
due."

Old Nick saw he was outwitted. He tried to seize
the goat, but it lowered its horns and butted him
from the bridge into the sea. The water hissed for
seven miles round, and the Devil came ashore no bigger
than a drowned black cat. He ran home to his
mother and did not show himself in that kingdom for
a hundred years.

Grumblegizzard returned to the palace.

"The castle is quiet," said he. "The bridge is built.
Old Nick has had first choice and refused it. What
shall I do now?"

The king looked from the bridge, which was the wonder
of the world, to the ancestral sword, which hung again
above his throne, and then to the heaps of gold
and silver. He could think of no more deadly errands.

"You shall be paid tomorrow," said he.

Tomorrow came.

"You shall be paid when the moon is full."

The moon filled and emptied.

"You shall be paid after harvest."

Harvest came, and Grumblegizzard ate half of it while waiting.

At last he strode into the throne room.

"My wages," said he. "And the princess, as promised."

The king drew himself up.

"As for wages, you have eaten more than your service
was worth. As for my daughter, she cannot marry a
fellow hatched from an egg."

"Father," said the princess, "your word is pledged."

"A king's word," said the king, "must bend with affairs
of state."

Grumblegizzard scratched his scorched-heather hair.

"Then I must pay myself."

He went to the royal kitchen and filled a sack
with twelve hams, twelve cheeses, twelve loaves, a barrel of
herrings, and a copper of brose. He tied the sack
tightly.

"Thief!" cried the king. "Put down my victuals!"

"They're my wages."

"They are not!"

"Then fetch them back."

With that Grumblegizzard took the king in one hand and
the sack in the other. He swung them once round
his head, twice round his head, and the third time
hurled them through the roof.

Up they flew, king and sack, past the weathercock, past
the clouds, past the wild geese. The king clutched the
sack, and the sack caught on the horns of the
new moon. There he hung between heaven and earth, with
the food just beyond his reach, for whenever he stretched
toward the hams, the moon tipped him toward the ground,
and whenever he drew back, the sack swung away.

The councillors stared through the hole in the roof.

"Who governs us now?" they asked.

"The princess," said Grumblegizzard. "She has more sense."

The princess became queen, and because she kept her father's
promise, she gave Grumblegizzard the sack of gold, the sack
of silver, and the broad lands beyond the new bridge.
Whether she married him is told two ways. Some say
she did, because he was direct in speech and no
man in the kingdom could frighten him. Others say she
gave him twelve farms instead, which pleased him better, since
each farm had a dairy, a smokehouse, and a meal-bin.

But all agree that he built one great hall for
his five mothers, and each had a silver chair beside
the fire. Every morning he asked them, "What shall I
do now?" and every morning they answered, "Eat your breakfast
first."

Then twelve kettles were hung, twelve griddles heated, and twelve
milkmaids went to the byres. Yet after he had eaten
the herrings and brose, the porridge and milk, the beef
and bannocks, he always peeped into the pots and said:

"Is that all?"

As for the dishonest king, he hangs between heaven and
earth with his sack of victuals to this very day.
On clear nights you may see him kicking below the
moon, and when a ham or a cheese drops from
the sack, folk call it a falling star.

But Grumblegizzard never troubles to look up.

He has supper to mind.

# THE CRYSTAL BRIDAL-CUP

## I
## THE DREAM-BIRTH

I know not how long I have dwelt in this
chamber above the sea.

Time, that once moved with golden feet toward my bridal
hour, hath become a blind gaoler who counts neither sunrise
nor sunset, but only the strokes of my chisel and
the wasting pulses of my heart. Day follows day; night
drops her black veil; the sea rises and falls beneath
my barred casement; and still I labour.

Work, work!

So commanded the King. So commands Love. So whispers Death.

The chamber is wrought wholly of white marble, veined as
though ancient sorrow had congealed within it. Its roof is
high and solemn; its pillars shine in the morning like
frozen priests; its floor returns my footsteps with a cold,
accusing echo. Beyond the narrow window I behold the immeasurable
water, sapphire beneath noon, iron beneath tempest, silver when the
moon walketh upon it. Often have I pressed my brow
against those bars until the flesh grew numb, and cried
unto the waves:

"Bear me to Aurora! Bear but my name to her!
Tell her I live—tell her I love—tell her I die!"

But the waves, those smiling slaves of the Wind, toss
their foam-crests and flee. They touch every shore save mine;
they know every freedom save that of giving Freedom.

Aurora! Bride of one hour! Dawn of one brief and
perfect morning!

Canst thou remember the garden wherein we stood beneath the
almond trees, whilst pale blossoms fell upon thy hair? The
priest had spoken; the ring trembled upon thy finger; thy
hand rested in mine as softly as a sleeping dove.
There was music in the leaves, music in the fountains,
music in the blood that hastened through my veins. Thou
didst sing the song which we had fashioned together in
those happy evenings when the young moon hung above thy
father's roof. I gave the answering line, and our voices,
meeting, became one voice.

Then came the raiders from the sea.

Black sails darkened the haven; torches flew like fiery birds;
iron rang against iron; men shouted; women shrieked; the bridal
garlands were trampled into mud. I remember thy white arms
about me. I remember a sword-hilt descending like a thunderbolt.
I remember awakening in the belly of a ship, chained
among sculptors, singers, poets, metal-workers, dancers, and builders stolen from
a hundred coasts.

Of thee I knew nought.

Perhaps thou wert spared. Perhaps thou wanderest beside the sea
and watchest every sail. Perhaps—oh, let me not shape the
darker thought! The mind is itself a sculptor, but too
often it carveth horrors from the black marble of uncertainty.

When first I stood before the King, I deemed him
not a man, but some colossal image endowed by enchantment
with speech. He sat beneath a canopy purple as a
wound. Lions of gold upheld his throne; behind him burned
seven lamps whose flames bent neither to draught nor breath;
about him stood guards with naked swords crossed upon their
breasts.

He looked long upon my hands.

"These," said he, "have made beautiful things."

"They have made things beloved by a few," I answered.

"And therefore imperfect. Thou shalt make a thing beloved by
all."

"Beauty obeyeth no command."

At this, anger moved through the court like wind through
a field of grain; yet the King smiled.

"Beauty hath ever obeyed kings," said he. "I prepare a
Feast such as the world hath never seen. The greatest
workers in every art shall contend before me. Poetry shall
rise against Painting, Sculpture against Music, Music against Dance, and
all against all. He who creates the one unrivalled thing
shall receive gold, honour, and freedom."

"And those who fail?"

"They shall labour again."

"Until when?"

"Until they succeed—or die."

Thus was I brought to this marble cell. Thus was
given unto me a block of crystal, clear as imprisoned
water, flawless as ice from the mountain's secret heart. Tools
finer than needles were laid before me; food and wine
appeared each dawn; a guard stood without the door; and
I was commanded to bring Beauty forth from stone.

For many days I touched not the crystal.

What is Beauty without Love? What is Art without Freedom?
A flower beneath glass; a bird gilded in its cage;
a holy word uttered by a tongue that understandeth it
not.

Yet one evening, when the sunset reddened the sea, I
seemed to hear thy voice. It came faintly from afar—or
from within me—singing the first phrase of our bridal song.

I arose. The crystal caught a scarlet beam and burned
like a heart.

Then was the vision born.

I saw a cup—not such a cup as kings lift
amid trumpets and flatterers, but a Bridal-Cup, fashioned to contain
no wine save memory, no treasure save two souls made
one. Its bowl should be rounded like the horizon; its
stem should spring like a flower from entwining hands; about
its rim should pass the signs of dawn and moon,
of wave and wing, of grief conquered and love fulfilled.
And within its hollow, unseen yet living, should sleep our
song.

"To work!" I cried. "To work, whilst one breath remaineth!"

The chisel struck.

A note sprang from the crystal.

I trembled; for it was the very note thou hadst
sung beneath the almond trees.

* * *

Day by day the cup awakens, and day by day
I perish.

At first my hand was strong. The useless crystal fell
away in bright scales, scattering rainbow sparks upon the marble.
I carved the foot as two waves bending toward each
other, opposed yet inseparable; above them I wrought lilies, for
thy innocence, and thorns, for our sorrow. The stem I
shaped as two slender figures ascending with arms intertwined. Their
faces were too small for mortal tool, yet I knew
them: thine and mine.

Whenever despair weighed upon me, I sang.

Low at first, lest the guard should mock me; then
louder, because the sea answered; then louder still, because the
crystal remembered. Always I sang our song, and at certain
tones a faint murmur awakened in the growing hollow of
the cup. Was it echo only? Nay! An echo repeateth
sound as a slave repeats command; but this voice changed,
lingered, loved. It seemed to choose among my notes, keeping
those most burdened with tenderness.

I began to believe that the crystal drank my life.

Each stroke entered it. Each sigh passed into it. Each
memory became a thread of light imprisoned in its transparent
walls. After labouring, I would sink upon my couch, cold
though the air burned with summer; yet the cup upon
my table glowed warmly, as if what fled from my
blood had entered there.

"Drink," said the physician when he visited me.

"I am drinking."

"Thy cup is empty."

"Thou seest not its wine."

He placed his fingers upon my wrist and frowned. He
spoke of rest, air, nourishment. What know physicians of the
maladies which Love inflicts and Art consumes? They would measure
the tempest by the trembling of one leaf.

The King came once.

He entered without herald, wrapped in a dark mantle, and
stood behind me whilst I worked. His presence filled the
chamber, yet I would not turn.

"At last," he said, "thou obeyest."

"I obey not thee."

"Whom, then?"

"My bride."

"Is she dead?"

The chisel slipped and wounded my hand. Blood ran over
the crystal, but left no stain.

"I know not."

He was silent. Perhaps some buried human thing stirred beneath
his crown; perhaps not. Kings dwell so long among kneeling
bodies that they forget the stature of a soul.

He bent above the cup.

"I have seen the temples of the South," he murmured;
"the ivory towers beyond the desert; the jewelled masks of
buried emperors; the painted caverns where dead gods still smile.
Yet never have I seen crystal made to seem alive."

"It is alive."

"With what?"

"With all thou hast taken from me."

He drew himself upright. "Complete it, and I restore all."

"All?"

"Freedom, gold, rank—whatever thou demandest."

"Canst thou restore one bridal hour?"

His face darkened. He departed without reply.

That night the wind rose. The palace shook under the
blows of the sea. I dreamed the King stood upon
a mountain of crowns, reaching toward a star; but every
crown beneath him was a human skull, and when his
hand touched the star it became an eye and wept
blood.

I awoke crying thy name.

"Aurora!"

From somewhere—far below, beyond many walls—I thought another voice answered.

But the storm swallowed it.

* * *

To-day I carved the frieze about the bowl.

Therein are our almond trees, forever flowering. There is the
fountain beside which thou didst first confess thy love, though
thy lips spoke only of the weather and thy eyes
betrayed the heart's sweet treason. There is the little bridge
where we paused beneath the moon. There is the chapel
door; there the priest's uplifted hands; there the bridal ring;
there, beyond all, the black sail entering the harbour.

I had not meant to carve the sail. My hand
fashioned it against my will.

Oh Art, merciless Truth-teller! Why wilt thou not permit the
happy lie? Why must Beauty carry her wound visibly, as
the pearl carries within its radiance the memory of pain?

My strength fails swiftly. The physician hath ceased to command
and begun to entreat. The guard, who once cursed my
slow labour, now sets food beside me with downcast eyes.
Even the sunbeams seem gentler when they enter, as though
the bright children of the Day-Spirit feared to hurt me.

Yet the cup lacketh its crown.

Around the rim must run a ring of tiny stars,
each shaped to answer one note of our song. When
a voice awakeneth the first, the others shall stir in
order, and the whole vessel shall become a Throne of
Sound. This was not in my first design. The crystal
itself taught me.

At times I place my ear against it.

Within I hear a vast and distant murmuring: waves on
unknown coasts, leaves in unborn forests, children laughing before they
have entered the world, bells rung in cities whose stones
are not yet quarried. Above these I hear my own
voice wandering like a lost pilgrim. It seeketh thee, Aurora.
It goeth round and round the transparent walls, finding no
gate.

Would that I could make one opening through which it
might escape!

But then the work would be broken.

And so I understand: perfection is also a prison.

* * *

I have seen thee.

Not with the eyes of flesh, which grow dim, but
with that inward sight which Death cleanseth before he closes
it forever.

Thou stoodest in a hall of black pillars. A chain
encircled thy throat—not of iron, but of silence. Men commanded
thee to sing, and thou wouldst not. Then a moonbeam
touched thy lips, and they opened; but instead of song
there issued a white bird, which flew above a multitude
and entered my cup.

Was this a dream? A prophecy? A cruel sport of
fever?

I called the guard and questioned him concerning the other
captives.

"There are many," he said.

"Women?"

"Yes."

"Singers?"

"Many singers."

"One called Aurora?"

At that name he started. Though he denied all knowledge,
his face became my answer. I seized his robe. Strength
returned to me like lightning to a dead tree.

"She is here! Speak! By the Universal Mother who bore
thee, speak!"

He freed himself, but not roughly.

"I may tell thee nought."

"Then tell the King that I will break the cup
unless Aurora is brought to me."

His eyes moved toward the crystal.

"Thou couldst not break it."

"Could I not?"

I raised my hammer. The cup shone beneath it; within
its depth I heard our bridal melody utter one pleading
note.

My hand fell powerless.

The guard bowed his head. "Finish thy work," he whispered.
"The Feast is near. All competitors will be summoned."

"All?"

"So it is said."

Hope, thou most beautiful and most treacherous of spirits! Thou
enteredst me then, not as dawn enters the sky, gently
and by degrees, but as flame enters dry grass. I
worked through the night. My fingers opened; I bound them
with linen. Blood rose in my throat; I swallowed it.
The stars about the rim came forth one by one.

Aurora lives! Aurora is near! There are but walls between
us—walls, doors, guards, the pride of a King. What are
these against Love?

Work, work!

* * *

The cup is finished.

It stands before me upon the marble table, and the
declining sun hath clothed it in sevenfold fire. No earthly
words can tell its beauty, nor would I speak them
if they could; for praise is a garment too coarse
for that which is perfect. It seems not fashioned, but
born; not solid, but woven from light; not silent, but
holding silence as a musician holds breath before the first
note.

I placed my lips upon the rim and sang our
song for the last time.

At once the stars trembled. The lilies along the stem
opened with pale radiance. The crystal figures lifted their tiny
faces. From the hollow arose an answering voice—my voice, and
yet purer than mine; freed from flesh, freed from pain,
freed from the harshness of mortal breath.

It sang thy part, Aurora.

Then I knew the truth. The cup awaiteth thee. My
labour is but one half of a prayer; thou alone
canst utter the Amen.

The door opens. The King enters with physicians, guards, and
men who bear a laurel crown. He speaks, but his
words come from far away.

"Thou hast conquered."

Nay, King. I have surrendered.

He bids them raise me. They cannot. My limbs are
already marble; my blood moves reluctantly; the chamber recedes as
a shore recedes from a departing ship.

"Freedom," says the King. "Thou art free."

Too late!

Yet is it too late? The bars have vanished. The
sea spreads beneath me, no longer iron, no longer separating.
I behold a road of light across it. At the
end stands Aurora in her bridal robe, her hair full
of almond blossoms.

Wait, beloved!

I come.

But first—listen. I must speak, though no man hear.

O cup, child of my agony, crystal heart born from
the marriage of Love and Sorrow, keep thou my voice!
When she sings, answer her. When she weeps, console her.
When tyrants name thee theirs, remember thou wert made for
Freedom. Hold me until she comes; then break, if break
thou must, and release me.

Aurora!

Aur—

## II
## THE FEAST OF BEAUTY

I was seventy years of age when the King commanded
the dead to attend his Feast.

During fifty-three of those years I had served within the
Palace of the Sea: first as keeper of the lower
seals, afterward as master of ceremonies, and at last as
counsellor in those matters which kings commonly resolve before counselling
any man. I had seen ambassadors crawl upon carpets; rebels
smile beneath the axe; princes born amid trumpets and buried
amid whispers. I believed myself beyond astonishment.

Yet when the King looked upon us and said, "All
shall attend," I dared to question him.

"All, Majesty?"

"All."

"The living competitors are assembled. But some have died."

"All," he repeated.

There are words which gain vastness from the mouth that
utters them. That second "All" seemed to pass beyond the
audience chamber and enter the prisons, the sickrooms, the vaults;
yea, even the narrow country of Death.

The King stood beside an open window. He had not
slept since the crystal-worker died. Upon a table near him
lay a list of those taken by his raiders during
seven years: poets from islands, painters from cities of dust,
sculptors from the northern mountains, dancers from the cedar kingdoms,
singers from temples and marketplaces. Against many names appeared the
black mark signifying death.

Until that hour they had been numbers to him.

Now he touched each mark as though it burned.

"I desired Beauty," he said.

None answered.

"I sent my ships abroad, commanding them to gather the
greatest makers. I called it tribute. I called it necessity.
I called it the right of a throne whose glory
must outlive stone. Why did no man call it cruelty?"

We knelt; for courtiers kneel most quickly when accused of
silence.

He turned, and for the first time I saw not
the Lord of the Seven Harbours, the Shadow of the
Sun, the Master of Life and Law, but an aging
man whose ambition had awakened before his conscience and grown
monstrous whilst that conscience slept.

"The crystal-worker is dead," he said.

"Yes, Majesty."

"He was promised freedom."

"He completed the work before death."

"Then freedom is his."

I thought grief had clouded his reason.

"Majesty, how shall the dead use freedom?"

"How shall the living use it, if kings may steal
it?" he replied. "Bring him to the Feast. Place the
cup before him. Crown him. Let no man say that
a King's promise is cancelled by a grave."

"And the others?"

"The living shall be freed when they have shown their
works."

"The victors?"

His eyes flashed. "Victors or martyrs! Have I not learned
the difference is sometimes but a breath?"

Thus did repentance enter the Palace, not humbly, but wearing
the King's own thunder.

* * *

The Feast of Beauty was prepared in the Hall of
a Thousand Columns. Lamps of perfumed oil hung like constellations
beneath the dome; tapestries clothed the walls in victories which
now seemed accusations; fountains ran with wine; tables bore fruits
from every shore. At the northern end rose the royal
throne, but the King ordered another seat to be placed
beside it—a chair of ivory draped in white.

There we set the dead artist.

The embalmers had touched him lightly. His face remained thin
and fierce; his hands, folded upon his breast, were bound
with the stained linen of his last labour. A laurel
crown rested upon his dark hair. Before him, on a
pedestal of black stone, stood the crystal cup.

When the lamps were kindled, it gathered their flames and
multiplied them until the whole hall appeared to breathe through
it.

The competitors entered.

First came the poets, carrying scrolls chained in silver; then
the painters with veiled panels; sculptors followed beside images of
gods, beasts, griefs, and dreams; architects bore cities in miniature;
metal-workers displayed crowns delicate as frost; dancers moved under nets
of bells; musicians came with harps, pipes, drums, shells, and
instruments whose names were unknown even to me.

Last came the singers.

Among them walked a young woman in grey.

She wore no jewel. Her hair, though unadorned, shone with
that deep brightness seen in the sky before sunrise. Her
face was pale, and about her throat lay the faint
red sign of a chain recently removed. She looked neither
toward the King nor toward the assembled wonders.

Then she saw the dead man.

No cry escaped her. That silence was more terrible than
lamentation.

She advanced as if drawn by an invisible cord. The
guards moved to restrain her, but the King lifted his
hand.

She reached the ivory chair and knelt. Slowly she touched
the artist's wounded fingers. Then she raised them to her
lips.

"Aurora," murmured one of the singers.

At the name, the cup gave forth a low, clear
tone.

Every flame leaned toward it.

The King descended from his throne.

"Thou art his bride?" he asked.

She looked up. Her eyes had in them neither fear
nor reverence—only a grief so measureless that sovereignty became trivial
before it.

"For one hour," she said.

I heard the King draw breath as a man wounded.

He knew then, as did we all, that his raiders
had taken them separately during the confusion: the bridegroom carried
to the western holds, the bride hidden among captives in
another ship. She had resisted every command to sing. They
had imprisoned her beneath the eastern tower, whilst he laboured
above the sea, each ignorant that the other lived within
the same palace.

The King bowed his head.

No chronicle records that gesture, for official histories are ashamed
when monarchs resemble men. But I saw it.

"Lady," he said, "I cannot restore what I have taken.
I can release thee."

"Release?" She glanced at the dead artist. "Whither?"

"Home, if thou wilt. Or anywhere beneath heaven."

"My home is there."

Again she touched his hand.

The King's face worked strangely. "He wrought this cup for
thee."

"For me?"

"He spoke thy name with his final breath."

She arose. The stillness in the hall deepened until I
could hear the small rippling of the fountains.

"What must I do?" she asked.

"Nothing," said the King. "Thou art free."

But Aurora turned toward the cup.

"No. He hath done his part. I must do mine."

One of the elder singers whispered to her. She shook
her head.

"I will sing the song that was ours."

Then, for the first time, fear entered me.

The physician who had attended the artist seized my arm.
"Stop her," he muttered.

"Why?"

"He believed the cup contained his life."

"A dying man's fancy."

"Perhaps. But I heard it answer."

I approached the King, yet before I could speak Aurora
lifted her face.

The first note came.

It was soft—so soft that the great hall seemed to
bend downward in order to hear. It carried the tenderness
of morning before birds awaken, of a mother watching beside
a cradle, of a bride saying within her heart that
word which her lips dare not utter.

The cup trembled.

From its hollow arose an echo.

No, not an echo.

A man's voice.

Aurora swayed. Her hands flew to her breast. The dead
artist sat motionless beneath his laurel, yet his voice moved
through the hall, answering her phrase precisely as a lover
answers from beyond a garden wall.

She sang again, louder.

The cup replied.

Then began that duet which no mortal pen can describe
and no mortal ear could forget. Her voice was grief
made radiant; his was memory released from stone. They met
above us, parted, pursued one another among the columns, climbed
the dome, descended like rain, and entered every heart. Warriors
wept without shame. Old enemies embraced. The chained scrolls of
the poets rustled. Painted faces seemed to turn toward the
sound. Harp-strings stirred though no hand touched them.

The King stood beside the cup, entranced.

"Majesty," I cried, "command her to cease!"

He did not hear.

Or hearing, would not.

For he had sought Perfect Beauty through blood, gold, conquest,
captivity; and now that dread divinity stood before him, not
as a possession but as judgment.

Aurora's voice rose.

The crystal blazed.

A crack, fine as a hair, appeared along the rim.

"Cease!" cried the physician.

But the dead voice answered, stronger than before.

The King stretched out both hands.

"More," he whispered. "More!"

And the lamps went dark.

## III
## THE STORY OF THE MOONBEAM

I was born that night from the eastern edge of
the moon.

We children of the Moon-Mother are numberless, yet no two
journeys are the same. Some fall upon quiet graves and
sleep there until dawn; some dance on the shields of
armies; some enter lovers' chambers and are blessed; some touch
the eyes of prisoners who mistake us for Freedom and
weep.

I descended toward the sea.

Far below, the palace rose from black water, vast and
pale, like the skull of a dead god. Its marble
terraces drank the waves; its towers pierced the night; its
thousand windows glimmered with imprisoned fire. Music moved within it—not
outward, as mortal music commonly moves, but inward, drawing all
things toward its heart.

I heard a woman sing.

I heard a dead man answer.

At once I knew that the Great Spirit had appointed
my path.

The winds sought to carry me westward, but I slipped
from their fingers. Clouds advanced like armies in grey mail;
I passed through their broken ranks. The sea lifted mirrors
to receive me, yet I would not rest upon them.
Upward I flew along the palace wall, over balconies where
guards stood forgetful of duty, past a chamber whose barred
window looked upon an empty couch and a table scarred
by tools.

There lingered the scent of blood and crystal dust.

"Here," whispered the stones, "he suffered."

I entered the Hall of a Thousand Columns through a
high aperture in the dome.

Darkness filled it, for the lamps had died; but the
music had become light. Each note shone. The woman's voice
streamed upward in gold; the man's answer flowed about it
in silver. Together they made a luminous tree whose roots
entered two hearts, one living and one dead, and whose
branches touched the unseen stars.

Mortals crowded below. Some knelt, some clasped one another, some
hid their faces. At the centre stood the crystal cup,
glowing from within like a captive moon.

Beside it was Aurora.

I knew her by her name, for all beams are
kin to Dawn. She was our elder sister, though born
in flesh: pale at first, then kindled; sorrowful, yet carrying
within her the prophecy of morning. Her hair had fallen
about her shoulders. Her eyes were fixed upon the crowned
dead man.

She sang:

"Beyond the wave, beyond the night,
I seek the heart that was my light—"

And from the cup came the answer:

"Beyond the breath, beyond the tomb,
I wait for thee where dawns shall bloom."

The words were simple, but through them moved that First
Music which the All-Father breathed before the worlds were separated.
I remembered the birth of tides. I remembered stars turning
in their invisible choirs. I remembered the Moon-Mother young and
stainless, looking for the first time upon the sleeping sea.

The cup remembered more.

Within its crystal walls I beheld the artist's labour: each
stroke of the chisel, each drop of blood, each whispered
name. His life lay there, folded like fire within a
gem. I saw the bridal garden; almond blossoms fell through
the clear substance; a fountain rose; two hands joined; black
sails came; chains flashed; the marble chamber closed.

The vessel was cup, tomb, heart, prison, and womb.

Yet one thing it lacked.

Light.

Not the red spears of the Sun-Lord, which conquer whatever
they touch; not the yellow flames of men, which devour
even whilst they illuminate; but our light—the tender light that
enters without command and reveals without possessing.

Therefore had I been sent.

I descended.

As I passed the dome, the music shook me into
a thousand fragments; yet each fragment remained myself. I touched
a column, and its carved leaves stirred. I crossed the
face of an old courtier, and saw tears in the
channels of his age. I fell upon the King.

His crown had slipped backward. Beneath it his brow appeared
naked and mortal. His eyes burned with terror and desire.

"Perfect," he murmured. "At last—Perfect!"

Poor King! He thought Perfection had come to reward him.
He knew not that she cometh to consume all falsehood.

I left him and entered the cup.

Oh wonder beyond telling!

Crystal is no dead thing. Deep within its ordered silence
dwell innumerable gates, corridors of brightness, palaces smaller than dew,
abysses wherein one ray may travel for a thousand mortal
years. I passed through memories hardened into radiance. I heard
the artist call Aurora's name in youth, in fear, in
hope, in fever, in death. I found his voice beating
against the walls like a bird.

"Who art thou?" it asked.

"A child of the Moon."

"Canst thou lead me forth?"

"Not alone."

"Is she near?"

"She sings beside thee."

Then his imprisoned spirit rushed toward her voice. The cup
rang so mightily that the marble floor quivered.

Aurora heard. She smiled.

It was not the smile of happiness, nor the smile
of madness, but that holy and terrible smile worn by
those who have discovered that the gate called Death opens
both ways.

The old courtier cried, "Lady, cease! The crystal breaks!"

She looked upon the artist's body.

"He waits," she said.

The King came closer. "Sing!"

One word—yet in it dwelt the whole tragedy of his
reign. Sing, though chains bind thee. Sing, though love bleed.
Sing, that power may feast upon Beauty.

But Aurora was no longer obedient to him. She sang
because the dead had answered.

Her voice climbed higher.

The crack about the rim spread from star to star.
Each little crystal sign awakened as it broke, uttering its
appointed tone. One sounded like rain upon spring leaves; one
like a child's laugh; one like a sword dropped after
battle; one like earth falling upon a coffin; one like
the first bird before dawn.

I ran along those fissures, widening them with silver fingers.

"Not yet," said the cup.

"Thou must break," I answered.

"I shall cease to be beautiful."

"Thou shalt cease to be possessed."

"I shall lose the life within me."

"Thou shalt release it."

The vessel trembled. Long had it been proud of its
perfection, as all created things are proud when they approach
the dream of their creator. It feared destruction. Yet deeper
than fear lay obedience to Love.

"Break me, then," it whispered.

Aurora sang the final verse.

Her voice was failing now. Blood coloured her lips; her
breath came like wind through a dying forest; but every
weakness of the flesh became strength in the song. The
dead man's voice rose to sustain her. They were no
longer singer and echo. They were bride and bridegroom at
the threshold of a second marriage.

Around them the hall vanished from mortal sense.

The captives beheld their homes. One saw the blue mountains
of his childhood; another the red roofs of his city;
another a mother's door; another a little grave beneath cypress
trees. Chains fell from memory before they fell from limbs.
Pain became transparent. Even those who had hated the King
looked upon him with pity, for he stood alone amid
all that communion, wealthiest and poorest of men.

He clutched his breast.

"Mine," he gasped.

Was he speaking of the cup, the Feast, the kingdom,
or his own heart? I know not.

Aurora answered him, though her gaze never left her beloved.

"Beauty is no man's slave."

The crystal gave a cry.

Not the sound of glass breaking, but the cry of
a star when its age is accomplished; the cry of
ice when spring delivereth the river; the cry of the
soul when the body's last gate opens.

The cup shattered.

Its fragments did not fall at once. They hung about
Aurora in a bright circle, each holding my light, each
singing one note. In their midst arose the artist's spirit,
visible for a breath: young, unwounded, clad in the same
white garment he had worn at his wedding.

He extended his hands.

Aurora stepped toward him.

Her body remained where it was, trembling; yet from it
came another Aurora, luminous as dawn upon snow. The two
spirits met above the broken cup.

No word passed between them.

What need hath fulfilled Love of words?

Their foreheads touched. About them appeared almond blossoms, though no
almond tree grew within a thousand miles. The fragrance filled
the hall. The dead artist's laurel became green; his wounded
hands opened; the faintest smile rested upon his face.

Then Aurora's mortal body sank.

The old courtier caught her before she struck the marble.
He called physicians, servants, gods; he commanded life to return
as though he too were a king. But her heart
had followed her song into the crystal, and when the
crystal broke, it chose not to remain behind.

The spirits rose together.

I wrapped myself around them. Never had I carried so
sweet a burden. We ascended toward the dome, but below
us another cry sounded.

The King had fallen beside the pedestal.

He lay among the fragments, his crown broken beneath him.
One hand grasped the ivory chair; the other was stretched
toward the place where the cup had stood. His eyes
remained open, beholding perhaps that Beauty for whose shadow he
had filled prisons and sent black ships across the sea.

The physician bent over him.

"All?" whispered the King.

It was the last word of his life.

Perhaps he asked whether all the captives were free. Perhaps
whether all Beauty must end thus. Perhaps he saw the
dead gathered beyond the veil and wondered whether kings also
must attend the final Feast.

The old courtier answered, "All, Majesty."

Then the King died.

* * *

Dawn approached.

We moonbeams feel her coming before men do. Our strength
wanes; our silver roads grow pale; the stars withdraw like
pilgrims who have heard a greater hymn. I bore the
lovers upward until the palace became small beneath us and
the sea opened vast and blue-black around it.

"Whither go we?" asked Aurora.

"Beyond the wave," answered her beloved.

"Beyond the night?"

"Beyond all thrones."

They looked back once.

Below, the doors of the prisons were opening. Captives poured
upon the terraces: singers supporting dancers, sculptors carrying the tools
of dead companions, poets bearing not their chained scrolls but
the names of those who had perished. No guard restrained
them. Ships were made ready. The old courtier stood upon
the highest stair and read aloud the King's final decree
of Freedom.

In the hall, servants gathered the crystal fragments; but whenever
any hand sought to claim one, it dissolved into a
drop of clear water. Thus no treasury received the masterpiece.
No crown possessed it. By sunrise nothing remained upon the
pedestal save a ring of moisture and two almond blossoms.

Yet the cup was not destroyed.

Its music had entered the pillars, the sea, the freed
captives, the old man's memory, and the silence following the
King's last breath. Its light had entered me. Its love
had entered the dawn.

I carried the spirits eastward, where Aurora's namesake lifted her
first rose-coloured banners above the world. The Day-Spirit stretched forth
arms of fire, and I, a frail child of the
Moon, could go no farther.

"Must we part?" I asked.

"For a little while," said the artist.

Aurora bent and kissed me. At her touch I became
so bright that, for one moment, I shone visibly amid
morning.

Then they passed into the dawn together.

I faded.

But fading is not death to Light. We return to
the Moon-Mother; we sleep in her tranquil heart; we are
born again whenever darkness needs a witness.

Many ages have passed since that night. The Palace of
the Sea is fallen. Fish move through its audience chamber;
coral grows upon the throne; waves wander freely where guards
once stood. Men remember neither the King's titles nor the
boundaries of his dominion. Of his conquests, nought remaineth. Of
his Feast, only a legend.

Yet sometimes, when moonlight enters clear water or touches the
rim of a glass, a low note awakeneth. If one
who truly loves is near, another note answers. Then the
waves grow still, the night leans down, and two voices
may be heard singing beyond the borders of mortal breath.

One is Aurora's.

The other is the voice that the crystal kept.

And their song, being no longer imprisoned in a perfect
thing, hath become part of the music of the spheres—free,
unending, and owned by none.

### What the Tree Kept

The first thing you notice is that the avocado tree
is smaller.

For eighteen years it has lived in your mind as
a green country of its own, its branches shouldering the
sky, its roots spreading beneath Grandmama's yard and under the
walls and perhaps beneath the red road outside. Now the
highest branch barely reaches the roof. Lichen powders the trunk.
One limb has been cut away, leaving a pale circular
scar in the wood.

You stand beneath it with your suitcase still in the
car and think: This cannot be the tree.

Then Dozie says your name.

He comes around the side of the house wearing rubber
slippers and a white shirt gone yellow at the collar.
He is taller than you remember, although this is a
foolish thought. Of course he is taller. He was eleven
when you last saw him. You were ten. Children become
adults even when you stop looking at them.

He hugs you carefully, touching only your shoulders. His body
smells of sun and soap.

"Nwadiana," he says.

Cousin, daughter of our daughter. The word enters you more
easily than you expect. You have not heard it spoken
to you since Grandmama's funeral, when women wrapped in black
cloth held your face between their palms and called you
poor child, poor motherless child, although your mother was alive
and standing nearby.

"I thought you might not come," Dozie says.

You tell him you had promised.

He looks at the tree. Or perhaps you only imagine
this because you are looking at it.

The summer Nonso died was the summer Grandmama taught you
how to test mangoes with your thumbs. Too firm meant
sourness that tightened your jaw. Too soft meant worms or
brown rot. The right mango gave a little beneath the
skin, as if it had been waiting for your touch.

It was the summer rain fell in furious sheets and
stopped without warning, leaving the yard steaming. The summer millipedes
curled into black bracelets along the veranda. The summer Grandmama
killed a chicken for Nonso because he had beaten Dozie
in a race from the gate to the breadfruit tree.

It was the summer your mother began to smoke again.

It was the summer your father said America had made
your mother too sharp, and your mother said Nigeria had
taught him to mistake silence for obedience.

It was the summer Nonso died.

Inside the house, the air is sealed and medicinal. Dozie
has opened the shutters, but dust still floats in the
bars of light. Grandmama's furniture remains where it always was:
the brown sofa with wooden arms, the glass cabinet crowded
with cups nobody was allowed to use, the clock shaped
like a little house. The clock stopped at twelve minutes
past four. You wonder whether this happened on the day
Grandmama died or whether Dozie left it that way because
twelve minutes past four looked final.

Her photograph sits on the cabinet. In it, she is
younger than you are now. She wears a head tie
with stiff wings and looks beyond the photographer as if
someone more important has entered the room.

"You kept everything," you say.

"Not everything."

Dozie goes to the kitchen to bring water. You remain
beside the cabinet, where a tiny rust-colored stain marks the
wall. When you were ten, you believed it was Nonso's
blood, carried in somehow on Grandmama's wrapper or on the
hands of the men who lifted him. Later you understood
that blood did not travel in this way, secretly and
upward, to fix itself above a cabinet.

Still, you touch the stain.

Dozie returns with two glasses on a tray. He watches
your fingers leave the wall.

"Rust," he says. "The nail behind it."

You nod.

He does not ask what you thought it was.

Your mother had not wanted you to make this trip.
She said there was nothing left in Grandmama's house except
mildew and quarrels about land. She said this while standing
at the granite counter in her Maryland kitchen, slicing pears
into transparent moons.

"Dozie invited me," you said.

"Dozie invites everyone. It is how people there keep accounts."

She said there as though Nigeria were a room she
had escaped from and locked behind her.

You told her Grandmama's lawyer needed signatures before the house
could be sold. This was not quite true. Your signature
was not needed. Dozie could have handled the sale without
you.

Your mother placed the knife beside the pear.

"Be careful," she said.

It was what your father had said before each journey
when you were children: Be careful what you eat, be
careful near the road, be careful in the rain. After
Nonso died, the words changed shape. Be careful meant Do
not trust Grandmama. It meant The dead can be used
against you. It meant You came back once without your
brother.

Your mother has not spoken Nonso's name in eleven years.

Before that, she said it only by accident.

Dozie leads you through the rooms. Grandmama's bedroom is empty
except for the iron bed and a tower of old
newspapers. In the room where you and Nonso slept, the
mosquito net hangs from a hook, gray with dust. You
remember Nonso breathing beside you, his breath sweet with guava.
You remember hating the sound because it proved he remained
alive even in sleep, taking the night's air into himself
and returning it warm.

You remember loving him, too. Perhaps.

Memory is greedy for simple words. Love. Hate. Jealousy. Grief.
It wants one label tied neatly to each thing. But
you loved Nonso when he let you ride on his
back. You hated him when Grandmama called him her little
husband. You loved the soft hair near his temples and
hated his narrow feet, his quick laugh, the way adults
turned toward him before he had spoken.

Most of all, you hated the space made for him.

At breakfast, Grandmama gave him the browned skin from the
top of the custard. At Mass, she sat him on
her right. When visitors came, she presented him first.

"This is Nonso," she would say. "He will carry his
grandfather's name."

Then she gestured toward you and added that you were
the American girl, as though America were your name and
not the place where you lived.

Once you asked what you would carry.

Grandmama laughed. "You will go to another family."

You were ten. You knew enough to understand that going
meant vanishing.

Dozie opens the back door and heat presses against you.
Beyond the veranda, the yard is crowded with cassava, plantain,
weeds, and fallen leaves. A hen scratches beside a cracked
basin. The air smells of damp soil and the bitter
smoke of someone burning rubbish.

"The agent is coming tomorrow," he says. "We can sign
then."

"You want to sell?"

He shrugs. "I cannot repair all this."

He has lived here since Grandmama became ill, first caring
for her, then caring for the house. You know this
from the occasional messages he sent: Grandmama is weaker. Grandmama
no longer eats meat. Grandmama asked whether you married. You
answered some messages and ignored others. After she died, he
wrote, She called Nonso's name at the end.

You had deleted that one, then recovered it from the
trash and read it until the letters blurred.

Now Dozie walks toward the avocado tree. You follow because
refusing would reveal too much, although you are no longer
certain what can still be concealed.

The ground beneath the tree is bare. The roots rise
through it like knuckles.

"Do you remember how much fruit it used to give?"
he asks.

You say yes.

In truth, you remember fruit striking the earth in the
night. One heavy thud, then another, then several together when
the wind shook the branches. For years afterward, any sudden
falling sound—a book dropped in the apartment above yours, a
sack of rice unloaded behind a grocery store—made your stomach
contract.

At school in Philadelphia, a boy once jumped from a
low wall and landed behind you. You vomited into a
trash bin. When the teacher asked what was wrong, you
said the cafeteria milk had gone bad.

You became good at explanations.

That summer, Grandmama told stories after dinner while moths battered
themselves against the kerosene lamp. Her favorite story concerned the
echi eteka, the snake whose poison did not wait for
tomorrow. If it bit you, you died before you could
call for your mother. Sometimes Grandmama said its skin was
green. Sometimes black. Once she said it had no color
at all and could pass through grass like water.

"Have you seen it?" Nonso asked.

Grandmama said she had seen what it left behind.

The next morning, she showed you a shed snakeskin caught
on the wire fence. It was delicate and empty, complete
down to the cloudy caps where eyes had been. Nonso
touched it. You did not. Dozie stood close enough that
his arm rubbed yours.

Dozie followed you everywhere that summer.

When you climbed the low wall, he climbed it. When
you spat watermelon seeds at the chickens, he did the
same. He gave you the larger share of roasted groundnuts
and laughed before you reached the end of your jokes.
At eleven, he had begun to look at you in
a way that made your skin alert. You did not
know what to call it. You only knew you could
ask him to do things.

Once, behind the storage shed, you told him to close
his eyes. You pressed your lips to his for three
seconds, counting in your head. His mouth was dry and
motionless. Afterward, he smiled as if you had given him
a prize.

"Don't tell Nonso," you said.

He promised.

You thought this promise proved that you had something Nonso
could not enter. A room with only you and Dozie
inside.

But later that afternoon, Grandmama called Nonso to sit beside
her while she sorted egusi seeds. She told Dozie to
move away and make space for the heir. Dozie obeyed.
Everyone obeyed Grandmama then.

You watched Nonso lean against her knee. You watched her
palm rest on his head.

Your hatred was sudden and clean.

Or maybe it had been growing for years.

On your second evening back, rain comes before sunset. It
rattles the roof and pours from the gutters in silver
ropes. The electricity fails. Dozie lights a lamp and sets
it on the dining table, and for one moment the
room is restored to that summer: the same unsteady shadows,
the same thickened air, the same insects throwing themselves toward
flame.

You and Dozie eat yam porridge from chipped bowls. He
tells you about his work at a printing shop in
town, about the woman he almost married, about Grandmama's last
months. Her legs swelled. She accused neighbors of stealing her
yams. She mistook him for your father and asked why
his wife had taken the children away.

"She did not know me at the end," he says.

"I'm sorry."

He smiles without looking up. "Maybe it was easier."

Rainwater gathers beneath the closed window. You trace the crack
in your bowl.

"Did she talk about Nonso?"

The question is out before you can pull it back.

Dozie stops eating. In the lamp's light, his face seems
briefly young, the bones softened.

"Sometimes."

"What did she say?"

"That he was waiting."

"For her?"

"For all of us, maybe."

You laugh, but the sound is too hard. "She always
liked to frighten people."

Dozie's spoon rests in his bowl. "She frightened you."

You look toward the dark window.

"At the funeral," he continues, "I heard what you told
Aunty."

The room tightens.

You see your mother again in Grandmama's bedroom, still wearing
the clothes she had traveled in. Her face was swollen
from crying, her braids frayed from the flight. She held
your wrists and asked you what happened, what happened, what
happened.

You said Grandmama ordered Nonso to climb the avocado tree.

You said Nonso did not want to go, but Grandmama
called him cowardly. You said that when he reached the
weak branch, Grandmama shouted about the snake. You said Grandmama
watched him lose his grip. You said she stood there
for a long time before calling anyone.

Your mother believed you because grief required a body to
punish and Nonso's body was already beyond punishment.

Grandmama denied it. Your mother slapped her.

People pulled them apart. Your father said everyone was mad
with sorrow. Grandmama called your mother an ungrateful daughter. Your
mother said she would never allow you near that family
again.

By Christmas, your parents slept in different rooms.

By the following summer, your father had moved to New
Jersey.

There were other reasons, you were told. There are always
other reasons adults offer when one reason is too ugly
to hold.

You stare at Dozie across the table.

"You were there," you say.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you tell them?"

He turns the spoon over. The metal catches the flame
and releases it.

"I was a child."

"So was I."

"I know."

The rain softens. Somewhere in the house, water begins to
drip with patient regularity.

You want him to accuse you. You have imagined the
accusation for eighteen years—in airplanes, in therapists' rooms, beside sleeping
men whose names you later forgot. You have imagined Dozie
pointing at you across Grandmama's yard and saying, She did
it. You have imagined policemen, judges, your mother's face. In
some versions you confess and are forgiven because you were
ten. In others, forgiveness is refused, and the refusal feels
almost like mercy because at last the punishment has edges.

Instead Dozie says, "I used to dream of him."

You wait.

"In the dream he is still in the tree. He
asks me to help him down, but my arms are
very small." Dozie studies his hands. "Sometimes you are there.
Sometimes it is only me."

You press your thumbnail into your palm.

"Do you still dream it?"

"Not often."

You want to say you have never dreamed of Nonso.
This is true, or nearly true. You dream of the
branch springing upward after his weight leaves it. You dream
of a snake with a child's fingers. You dream of
fruit bursting open on the ground. But Nonso himself refuses
to appear.

Perhaps the dead have their own refusals.

The next morning you wake before Dozie. The rain has
washed the yard bright. Drops hang from the clothesline and
the leaves shine as though varnished. You walk barefoot to
the avocado tree.

At ten, Nonso was taller than you by half a
head. He climbed well but cautiously. Dozie climbed quickly, careless
of scratches. You disliked this difference because Grandmama praised Nonso's
caution as wisdom and called Dozie wild.

The three of you had gone into the yard after
lunch. Grandmama was asleep on the veranda, a folded cloth
over her face. The heat made everything slow except the
insects.

You pointed to an avocado hanging near the upper branches.

"Grandmama said whoever brings that one down gets the chicken
gizzard tonight," you told Nonso.

Grandmama had said no such thing.

Nonso looked at the tree. "There are lower ones."

"That one is biggest."

"Then you climb."

"She wants you to do it. You're the heir."

You knew exactly how to say the word. You made
it heavy with mock respect.

Dozie laughed because you wanted him to laugh.

Nonso glanced toward the veranda. Even then, he wanted Grandmama's
approval more than the gizzard. Or perhaps he wanted to
prove something to you. You had not considered that until
years later: that he, too, might have felt the narrowness
of the space assigned to him.

He began to climb.

The trunk forked low, and he pulled himself up easily.
Leaves closed around his yellow shirt. You stood below, giving
directions.

"Left. Higher. It's above you."

Dozie stood beside you. His shoulder touched yours.

Nonso reached the branch Grandmama had warned all of you
about. It was gray at its base and hollowed by
ants. He tested it with one foot.

"Don't," Dozie said softly.

You told him to be quiet.

Nonso edged outward. The branch bent. The avocado hung just
beyond his fingers, dark and shining.

You could have stopped then.

This is the place where memory always pauses, offering you
an exit. You could have called him back. You could
have shouted for Grandmama. You could have said it was
a joke.

Instead you looked at Dozie, making sure he watched you.

Then you screamed, "Echi eteka! Behind you!"

Nonso jerked around.

There was no snake.

His foot slipped first. His hands seized at leaves, then
at air. For one impossible second he hung level with
the branch, as if the tree were deciding whether to
keep him.

It released him.

The sound was not one sound. It was leaves tearing,
wood cracking, breath leaving a body, the dull collapse of
something that had believed the ground was farther away. It
was like a basket of hard fruit overturned at once.

Dozie did not scream.

Neither did you.

Nonso lay on his side, one arm tucked beneath him.
His eyes were open. A thin red line ran from
his nose into the soil.

Grandmama woke because a hen began shrieking.

For eighteen years you have remembered her slowness: the cloth
falling from her face, her feet searching for slippers, her
confusion when she saw the two of you beneath the
tree. Yet she was not slow. You know this now.
She ran. She lifted Nonso's head and called his name.
She sent Dozie for the neighbors. She pressed her mouth
against Nonso's mouth, once, twice, again.

You stood close enough to smell the bitter oil in
her hair.

When she looked at you and asked what happened, you
said nothing.

Silence was your first lie.

Dozie finds you beneath the tree after sunrise. He is
dressed to meet the agent, dark trousers and a blue
shirt. He carries two cups of tea.

"You'll catch cold," he says, although the morning is warm.

He places one cup on a root. You do not
take it.

"Dozie."

He waits.

You have practiced the sentence in private for years. I
told him to climb. I frightened him. It was me.
Each version has seemed both too small and too enormous.
Words cannot return breath to a body. They cannot repair
your mother's grief or your father's leaving or Grandmama's final
years of accusation. They can only move the burden, dividing
it among those still alive.

"I'm sorry," you say.

Dozie looks toward the cut branch, the pale scar where
wood has been removed.

"For what?"

The question is not innocence. It is an opening, and
also a blade.

Your mouth dries. You see your mother slicing pears. You
hear your father saying be careful. You feel Grandmama's hand
resting on Nonso's head, claiming him for the future he
never reached.

"For not coming sooner," you say.

Dozie closes his eyes.

When he opens them, whatever was there has folded itself
away.

"I wondered why you did it," he says. "For many
years."

You cannot breathe deeply enough.

He does not say which thing.

"I thought maybe you hated him," he continues. "Then I
remembered how children are. I remembered myself."

"You didn't do anything."

"I watched."

"You told him not to climb."

"But when you told me to be quiet, I was
quiet." He looks at you fully now. "Back then, what
you wanted was the only thing that mattered to me."

The words enter softly. That is what makes them unbearable.

You want to tell him he was eleven. You want
to absolve him so that he might absolve you in
return. You want to say Grandmama made Nonso too large
and you too small, that adults had built the room
and taught you there was space for only one child.
You want to say you did not mean death. You
meant fear. You meant humiliation. You meant to make him
climb down shaking so you could laugh and, for one
afternoon, become the child everyone watched.

But intention is a weak branch. It does not hold.

Dozie picks up the untouched cup. Tea has spilled into
the dirt, darkening it.

"The agent will come at ten," he says.

Then he walks toward the house.

You remain under the avocado tree. The bark is rough
beneath your palms. You press harder until its ridges mark
you, until pain runs cleanly through your hands, but the
tree does not open, does not speak, does not give
back what it took.

Above you, the leaves move in the rinsed morning light.
An avocado loosens somewhere among them. You hear it strike
one branch, then another, before landing in the wet grass
with a sound your body recognizes before your mind does.

You call Dozie's name.

He does not turn.

The tears come without warning, hot and graceless. Your chest
jerks. A sound leaves you, low and broken, almost an
animal's sound. You lean your forehead against the trunk and
weep for Nonso, for Grandmama, for your mother, for Dozie
walking away, for the ten-year-old girl whose hunger for space
became a space no one could fill.

On the bark beside your face, ants enter a narrow
crack and disappear.

The tree holds still.

It has had eighteen years to learn how.

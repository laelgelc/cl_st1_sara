# The Hospital Where the Affliction Is Flight

My father called at six in the morning and said
his arm hurt.

"Which arm?"

"The left."

That was the arm people mentioned in television heart attacks.
They grabbed it before falling into wedding cakes or swimming
pools. My father said it was not his heart. He
had already decided this.

"How do you know?"

"Because my heart doesn't hurt."

I was standing in my kitchen in my underwear, watching
oatmeal make one large, thoughtful bubble on the hot plate.
The stove had been shut off for three months because
of a gas leak the landlord called a disagreement between
the pipes. I had twenty-seven dollars in checking. My most
recent story had earned two contributor copies of a magazine
that smelled like a church basement.

"Can you move it?"

"I can move it. It hurts when I move it."

"That's not really an answer."

"It answers the question."

He was sixty-seven then. Later, when people asked when the
cancer started, I would think of that call, though the
pain had nothing to do with the cancer. Or maybe
everything does, afterward. That is one of the cheap tricks
time performs. It runs a red string backward through every
harmless morning.

"I'll come get you," I said.

"You don't have to."

"I know."

"Bring a coat. It's cold."

He meant for himself, though he would never have said
so.

The oatmeal burst. A white blister opened and folded over.
I turned off the hot plate, put on my clothes,
and left the oatmeal where it was. It hardened during
the day into a gray disk. When I came home
I threw away the whole saucepan.

At my father's apartment, he was waiting on the curb
in a short-sleeved shirt.

"Where's your coat?" I asked.

"You brought one."

"I brought my coat."

"You said you were bringing a coat."

This was true in the sense that I had arrived
wearing one.

He got into the car. I took off my coat
and handed it to him. He laid it over his
knees like a restaurant napkin.

"You're supposed to wear it."

"I'm not cold now."

My father believed needing something was the quickest way to
lose it. Warmth, money, assistance. Children.

He held his left arm stiff against his stomach while
I drove. Every few blocks I looked at him. He
noticed on the fourth look.

"Watch the road."

"I am."

"You're watching my arm."

"The road is less likely to die."

He turned toward the window.

That was a cruel thing to say. I knew it
while saying it. Knowing did not stop me. This is
a major theme in my life.

Traffic was light. The sky had the pale, rubbed-out look
of paper erased too many times. My father asked whether
I had sold anything.

"A story."

"For money?"

"Technically."

"How much?"

"Forty dollars."

"That's pretty good."

"It took seven months."

"You need to work faster."

Under my shirt, between my shoulder blades, the XII began
to itch.

I pressed my back against the seat.

My father said, "What's this one about?"

"A kitten."

"You don't like cats."

"The kitten is symbolic."

"Of what?"

"Loneliness."

He considered this. "Does it find a home?"

"Yes."

"That's nice."

In the story, the kitten did not find a home.
It crawled into the engine of a delivery van and
was carried from Ohio to Nebraska, where its body was
discovered by a woman recently divorced from a tax attorney.
She buried it beneath a cottonwood and understood something vague
about endurance. I had written the ending three times. In
the first version, the woman kept driving and never noticed.
In the second, the kitten survived. The editor bought the
third and asked if I could make the kitten less
dead.

So yes. It found a home.

The emergency entrance was crowded with ambulances and people smoking
under NO SMOKING signs. A woman in slippers stood beside
a garbage can, holding a clear bag of yellow liquid
attached to her by a tube. She smoked with concentration.
A boy with a bandaged forehead tried to catch snowflakes
on his tongue, though it was not snowing. He was
catching ash from the woman's cigarette.

I dropped my father at the door.

"You coming in?" he asked.

"I have to park."

"You could leave it here."

"There's an ambulance behind me."

"They can go around."

The ambulance driver leaned on the horn.

My father got out slowly. He wore my coat now.
It was too narrow across his shoulders and too long
in the sleeves. For a moment he looked like a
child dressed as his future self.

I parked four blocks away because the hospital garage charged
eighteen dollars, which was close to half a literary story.
The meter accepted cards but rejected mine twice before approving
it with visible reluctance. I paid for two hours.

Adulthood is mostly predicting how long your father will require
emergency medical care and feeding the appropriate amount into a
machine.

Inside, he was arguing with the receptionist.

"I gave you the card," he said.

"And I gave it back."

"You did not."

"Sir, I did."

"Then where is it?"

"I don't know where you put it."

"You had it."

The receptionist looked at me as if I had manufactured
him in my basement.

"Are you family?"

"Unfortunately," my father said.

The insurance card was under his elbow.

We waited.

The emergency room had plastic chairs bolted together in rows,
perhaps so no one could steal one and begin a
better life. A television showed a cooking program with subtitles.
A chef whisked cream into something glossy while a man
beside us vomited into a blue bag. The bag had
helpful measurement lines.

My father sat with his coat over his lap again.

"You can wear it or give it back," I said.

"I'm using it."

"For what?"

He lifted the right side and showed me his hand
beneath it. "My hand was cold."

His left hand hung uncovered.

Across from us, a girl had wrapped both arms around
her abdomen. She was maybe sixteen. Her mother rubbed small
circles between her shoulders and talked on the phone about
deductibles. An old man slept with his mouth open. His
wife—or sister, or daughter who had become elderly through service—held
his hat and cane. A construction worker stared at a
bloody rag around his thumb. Nobody looked at anybody except
to see whether their suffering was getting ahead in line.

I took out my notebook.

My father said, "Now?"

"I have an idea."

"What idea?"

"I don't know yet."

"How do you know you have it?"

"It hurts."

He nodded as though that made sense.

The page was blank. I wrote: My father called at
six in the morning and said his arm hurt.

The letters warmed under my hand.

It had been nine years since I gave away my
tongue.

I should be exact. I still possessed a tongue. It
was pink and muscular and occupied the normal location. Dentists
had admired it without alarm. What I no longer possessed
was the tongue I was born with.

The Twelve-tongued God had that one.

I met the god in the basement of a public
library where I had gone to hear a famous novelist
explain perseverance. I was twenty-four, unemployed, and had written eleven
stories about young men who were unemployed and wanted to
write stories. The novelist wore red glasses and said rejection
was part of the process. He had received a million-dollar
advance.

Afterward, I followed him toward the elevators to ask what
kind of rejection built character without also making rent impossible.
He moved quickly. Famous novelists are fast in the presence
of unfamous novelists.

I took the wrong stairwell.

It was a basement.

There were pipes. A mop bucket. A humming vending machine.
A steel door with no handle. The fluorescent lights flickered
in order, one after another, leading me down the hall.

The god was sitting on a crate of printer paper.

At first it looked like a woman in a janitor's
uniform. Then a young man in a wool suit. Then
my fifth-grade teacher, dead for twelve years. Its face altered
whenever I tried to settle on it. Only the mouth
stayed consistent. It was too wide and contained twelve tongues
arranged like petals around a dark center. Each tongue moved
independently. One licked the upper teeth. One appeared to be
sleeping. Two whispered to each other. One wore a tiny
silver ring.

"You want to be heard," the god said.

It spoke without moving those tongues. Or all of them
moved. Memory gets decorative when frightened.

"I took the wrong stairs."

"There are no wrong stairs in a library."

"I'm looking for the elevator."

"You are looking for permission."

I wanted to leave. I also wanted to know whether
it had read my work.

The god smiled. The tongues leaned forward.

"I can make your sentences live," it said.

"What does that mean?"

"It means they will not lie dead on the page."

"Are you an agent?"

Its faces cycled faster. Old woman. Infant. Dog. Man with
burned cheeks. Me.

"I have represented everyone."

The humming vending machine went quiet.

"What do you want?" I asked.

"Your tongue."

I laughed because a person has to contribute something.

The god reached into its mouth and pulled out one
of its tongues. It came free with a sound like
a boot lifted from mud. It was longer than my
forearm. The severed end dripped black fluid onto the floor
and wrote small, crawling words between our shoes.

The tongue in the god's hand curled around its wrist.

"Take mine," it said. "Give me yours. You will write
what other people cannot bear to look at."

"Will I be successful?"

"Possibly."

"That doesn't sound very divine."

"I am not the Twelve-tongued God of Royalties."

One of its remaining tongues laughed.

"What happens if I refuse?"

"You take the elevator. You go home. You write honestly
about unemployed young men until you become an unemployed old
man. This is also a life."

I thought of my apartment. The mattress on the floor.
The late notices. My father asking whether I had considered
working for the post office. The famous novelist upstairs describing
persistence to a room full of people who would persist
until they disappeared.

"What happens if I agree?"

"You will never run out."

"Of ideas?"

"Of need."

The god stood. It was much taller standing. Its head
scraped the pipes.

"You must make suffering into form," it said. "Again and
again. No waste. No silence. No looking away because looking
hurts your feelings. You will make what happens useful."

That should have warned me. Useful is a knife word.

I opened my mouth.

The exchange was not ceremonial. The god seized my tongue
with two fingers and pulled. I heard roots tearing through
my neck. The basement flooded with the taste of pennies
and childhood fevers. My tongue stretched to my chest, my
knees, the floor. Every word I had ever failed to
say traveled through it as light: I'm sorry. I'm afraid.
Please stay. I love you. I need money.

Then it came free.

I fell against the vending machine.

The god pressed its severed tongue into my mouth. It
burrowed backward with horrible eagerness. It attached itself somewhere behind
my heart.

The pain was white. The white became a room. In
that room every living person stood with a sentence above
the head, and every sentence opened into other rooms, and
those rooms contained people with more sentences, and soon the
world was all doors and no walls.

When I woke, the god was gone.

A Roman numeral had been burned into my back.

XII.

My own tongue was gone too. I sometimes imagined it
among the others in the god's mouth, whispering practical advice
no one followed.

The first story I wrote afterward was published within a
month. The second won a prize. The third made my
father ask why everybody had to be miserable.

Then the luck became less pure.

The sentences still came alive, but living things are difficult.
They bite. They soil the carpet. Editors said my work
was vivid but relentless, ambitious but uneven, haunting but not
right for them at this time. I began writing advertisements
for a company that sold customized urns. I wrote product
descriptions in the morning and stories at night.

The god visited occasionally.

It appeared as a bus driver and called my novel
cowardly. It appeared as a child at a supermarket and
asked why I had killed the mother when the father's
death would have been less predictable. Once it came as
a moth with twelve soft mouths. It landed on my
laptop and ate the final paragraph of a story. The
story improved.

I kept writing because if I stopped, the borrowed tongue
swelled. At first it only pressed against my teeth. After
three days of silence it filled my mouth and slid
down my throat, tasting my lungs. After a week, words
began appearing in the skin of my arms. They moved
under the surface like parasites.

No waste. No silence.

In the emergency room, my father looked at my notebook.

"Any good?"

"No."

"You just started."

"I can tell."

The mark on my back burned. The borrowed tongue tasted
metal.

A nurse called his name.

She pronounced it wrong. My father corrected her. She pronounced
it the same way again.

We followed her past double doors. She took his blood
pressure in a narrow cubicle and asked him to rate
the pain from one to ten.

"Depends what ten is," he said.

"Worst pain imaginable."

"I can imagine a lot."

"Pick a number, sir."

"Four."

In the car he had said seven.

The nurse asked about medications. He said none. I listed
three.

"I stopped those."

"You stopped the blood-pressure pills?"

"They made me dizzy."

"They made you not have a stroke."

"Do you have medical training?" the nurse asked me.

"No."

"Then let him answer."

"He lies."

"I simplify," my father said.

The nurse typed this into the computer, or perhaps ordered
shoes. Her face revealed nothing.

She sent us to radiology.

We got lost immediately.

The hospital had signs, but the signs seemed designed by
a person who believed arrows were an emotional suggestion. RADIOLOGY
pointed left, then vanished. IMAGING appeared in blue. DIAGNOSTIC SERVICES
appeared in green. We passed a gift shop, a chapel,
a coffee stand, and a locked door labeled FAMILY CONSULTATION,
behind which someone was crying.

At an intersection of corridors, an orderly pushed an old
man in a wheelchair. The man wore a hospital gown
and one sock. His head leaned backward at such an
angle that he seemed to have been installed as a
warning against low ceilings.

"Radiology?" I asked.

The orderly pointed with his chin. "Down, left, right at
the mural, elevator B, basement."

"Which mural?"

"You'll see it."

We saw three murals. One showed sailboats. One showed children
releasing balloons. One showed a forest where every tree appeared
terminally ill.

The elevator took us to the first floor, then the
third, then opened on a mechanical room.

My father said, "You pressed the wrong button."

"I pressed B."

"That's three."

"The button said B."

"It looked like an eight."

"There is no eighth-floor button."

"Then why press it?"

On our second attempt, we reached the basement.

Radiology was there beside a leaking water fountain. A towel
had been placed around the base. It was soaked through,
but still there, fulfilling the moral obligation of towels.

The waiting area was full.

A woman with a scarf wrapped around her hair slept
against a man's shoulder. An elderly couple sat holding hands,
and I could not tell which one was sick. A
boy with a cast drew teeth on it with a
green marker. Near the vending machines, six people stood in
a tight circle, heads bowed, arms across one another's backs.
A football huddle waiting to learn whether God had made
the play.

Someone in the middle sobbed.

My father looked away.

I wrote them down.

Not their names. I did not know their names. I
wrote: Family like a football huddle. Grief calling the play.

The sentence squirmed.

I crossed it out.

The borrowed tongue pressed against the roof of my mouth.

"You always do that?" my father asked.

"What?"

"Write people down."

"Sometimes."

"Do they know?"

"No."

"What if they don't want to be in it?"

"They won't recognize themselves."

He looked at the huddled family. "You recognize them."

A technician called him.

I waited with the coat and his paperwork.

The grieving family remained by the vending machines. Their crying
became quieter, then louder, then quiet again. A doctor came
to them and said words I could not hear. The
group tightened. One of the women made a sound that
seemed too large for a body and had to be
produced by the building itself.

I tried not to listen.

I listened expertly.

I opened my notebook.

My father comes to the hospital with pain in his
arm. The pain is not his heart. It is a
herald.

No.

A herald was exactly the kind of word the god
liked and my father distrusted.

I wrote: In eleven months my father will be diagnosed
with multiple myeloma.

The page shivered.

I did not yet know the name. Not in the
ordinary way. But the borrowed tongue knew. It tasted the
depleted blood cells, the calcium flooding his kidneys, the small
punched-out holes that would appear in his bones. It tasted
the chemotherapy he would call "the stuff." It tasted hospital
oatmeal and plastic tubing and the diaper I would one
day help him change while both of us discussed a
basketball game as if his body had not become a
room we were embarrassed to share.

I saw him thinner.

I saw him without hair, though he would keep insisting
he had lost it naturally.

I saw him asleep in a recliner with a blanket
over his knees. My coat had long since disappeared. I
saw his hands tremble around a paper cup. I saw
the final week, which I will not put here because
I have used enough of him already.

The vision passed.

The family by the vending machine groaned and groaned.

I tore out the page. The paper was hot. I
folded it twice, then four times, then put it in
my mouth.

It tasted like bleach and walnuts.

I swallowed.

A nurse sat down beside me.

"You're not supposed to eat those," she said.

She was broad-shouldered and middle-aged, with purple glasses and a
badge turned backward. Her scrubs were patterned with smiling clouds.
One cloud held an umbrella.

"I was hungry."

"You have always confused hunger with permission."

I looked at her.

Her face changed. Not dramatically. The changes happened in the
details. Her nose shortened. Her eyes became my eyes. Her
cheeks sagged into my grandmother's cheeks. For one second she
had my father's face, only frightened in a way he
would never permit.

Her mouth opened.

Tongues moved behind her teeth.

I stood too quickly and banged my knee on the
plastic table.

"You," I said.

"That's usually how identification works."

"What are you doing here?"

"Working a double."

The Twelve-tongued God checked an imaginary watch.

"You're wearing clouds."

"They are mandatory."

The grieving family cried behind us. The god glanced at
them with professional interest.

"Don't," I said.

"Don't what?"

"Look at them like that."

"Like material?"

"Yes."

The god smiled with the nurse's mouth.

"You wrote down their posture."

"I crossed it out."

"After admiring it."

I closed the notebook.

"You showed me something."

"I show you many things. Your species is constantly happening."

"My father."

"Ah."

"Is it true?"

The god leaned back. The smiling clouds stretched over its
chest.

"What is true?"

"What I saw."

"Truth is a draft with funding."

"Will he get cancer?"

"Eventually he will get everything. So will you."

"Answer me."

"Make me."

The mark burned sharply enough that I reached behind myself.

The god watched.

"You have become timid," it said.

"I write what happens."

"You decorate what happens. You take a corpse, place a
cottonwood over it, and call the roots mercy."

"You read the kitten story?"

"I read the kitten."

I wanted to strike it. I wanted to ask whether
the editor had been right.

The god continued. "You were given a living instrument and
you use it to produce tasteful sorrow. Small domestic tragedies.
Earned ambiguity. A father dies, but the son notices a
bird. A marriage ends, but someone cooks an egg. Rain
on a window. Light on a floor."

"Those things happen."

"So does resurrection."

"Not often."

"Because writers like you are cowards."

It stood.

The badge turned around. Instead of a name, it showed
XII.

"You can make what you want to see," the god
said.

"That wasn't the deal."

"It was always the deal. You misunderstood because you were
young and interested in publication."

"I can't change what happens."

The god looked at the notebook beneath my hand.

A black word pushed through the cover like a small
insect, walked across my knuckle, and vanished into my sleeve.

"You have been changing it," the god said. "You only
choose changes that resemble accidents."

A radiology technician came through the door and called another
name. The old couple stood together. They took three steps,
stopped, exchanged a look, and the woman sat back down.
The man followed the technician. So he was sick. Or
she was, and he had gone to ask directions. I
had wanted the answer and now it disappointed me.

"Give him a happy ending," the god said.

"My father?"

"Everyone."

The family near the vending machines began crying again.

The god tilted its head toward them.

"Be bold."

Then the nurse's body folded inward like a coat dropped
from a hook. For one impossible moment the scrubs lay
empty on the chair.

A real nurse walked past. When I looked again, the
chair was empty too.

My father came out rubbing adhesive from his arm.

"What happened to your knee?"

"Nothing."

"You're bleeding."

I looked down. A dark spot spread through my jeans.

"They did pictures," he said. "Now we go back."

"Did they say anything?"

"They aren't allowed. Only the doctor is allowed to know
what the pictures mean."

We retraced our route.

At the elevator we passed the grieving family. A young
woman had collapsed into a chair. The others surrounded her.
One man stared at the floor with both fists closed.
There was nothing I could do, which was terrible. There
was nothing I should do, which was worse.

In the elevator, my father said, "You know them?"

"No."

"You kept looking."

"They got bad news."

"Everybody here gets bad news."

"That's not true."

He shrugged.

The elevator opened on the wrong floor.

We passed the sailboat mural again, then the locked consultation
room, then the old man in the wheelchair. He had
been parked beside a door and left there. The door
opened against his foot each time someone passed through. He
was a human doorstop in one sock. I hated myself
for thinking it. I wrote it down in my head
so I would not forget to hate myself later.

A volunteer gave us directions.

A second volunteer corrected the first.

A security guard told us to follow the yellow line
on the floor. The yellow line ended at a supply
closet.

My father became angry, which meant he became cheerful.

"Pretty good hospital," he said. "You could die before they
find you."

"They'd bill the hallway."

"Hallway is out of network."

We laughed.

It was the best moment of the day.

Back in emergency, a doctor saw us after another hour.
He was young and had the bruised eyes of someone
who had been awake since medical school. He asked my
father to lift his arm.

My father lifted it halfway.

"Any injury?"

"No."

"He was moving a bookcase," I said.

"It wasn't a bookcase."

"It was full of books."

"It was a shelf."

"A shelf becomes a bookcase when you put books on
it."

The doctor pressed my father's shoulder. My father flinched.

"Probably a strain," the doctor said.

"Probably?" my father said.

"Your cardiac enzymes are normal. X-rays don't show a fracture.
I'd follow up with your primary-care physician if it continues."

"I don't have one."

The doctor looked at the computer. "It says you do."

"He retired," my father said.

"When?"

"Four years ago."

The doctor prescribed an anti-inflammatory and left before either of
us could invent another question.

My father began buttoning my coat.

"That's it?" I asked.

"That's it."

I looked at the computer screen. I could not read
most of it from where I stood, but I saw
the words NORMAL and NO ACUTE FINDINGS. Beneath those words,
for a second, other words surfaced.

Abnormal protein.

Lesions.

Further testing recommended.

The future pressed against the screen like a face beneath
ice.

I opened my notebook.

My father said, "Can we go?"

"Wait."

"For what?"

I wrote: There was nothing wrong with him.

The borrowed tongue twisted.

On the monitor, the hidden words blurred.

I wrote: His blood was ordinary. His bones were whole.
There was no dark multiplication in the marrow, no secret
white machinery preparing to eat him from inside.

The room warmed. The fluorescent lights brightened until every surface
looked newly created and cheap.

My father rubbed his shoulder.

"Actually," he said, "it feels better."

The computer chimed.

The doctor returned. He seemed confused to see us.

"I reviewed everything again," he said. "You're fine."

"You said it was a strain."

"No strain."

"What was it?"

The doctor blinked. "Nothing."

My father looked at me as if this were somehow
my fault.

"Nothing hurts?" he asked.

"Sometimes nothing hurts."

The doctor smiled. It was a bad smile, but a
smile.

My back burned with pleasure.

I had done it.

I was happy.

That is the embarrassing truth. I did not consider causality
or ethics or whether a future can be removed without
leaving a hole. My father's bones were whole. In the
world I had written, no doctor would say multiple myeloma.
No chemotherapy would drip into him. No diaper. No paper
cup trembling in his hand.

I had saved him with a sentence.

Outside the cubicle, someone screamed.

The happiness cracked.

A gurney rushed past. A child lay on it, small
beneath oxygen tubing. His father ran beside him barefoot, carrying
one tiny shoe.

I looked at my notebook.

Be bold, the god had said.

My father handed me the discharge papers. "Hold these."

"Wait here."

"Where are you going?"

"I need to fix something."

"What?"

I was already in the corridor.

I followed the gurney but lost it at the double
doors. A nurse blocked me.

"Family only."

"I can help."

"Are you family?"

"No."

"Then you can't help."

This was the governing rule of the hospital. Help had
categories. Help required a badge, a bracelet, a blood relationship,
or the correct billing code.

I wrote against the wall.

The child could breathe.

Beyond the doors, the screaming stopped.

A monitor alarmed once, then settled into a calm electronic
rhythm.

I laughed.

The nurse stared at me.

"Sir?"

"It worked."

"What worked?"

The mark on my back was hot as an iron.
Words crowded my mouth. I could feel them moving in
my gums, impatient to be born.

The god had been right. I had wasted years arranging
suffering into attractive containers when I could have ended it.
I had mistaken restraint for morality because restraint reviewed well.

I ran.

At the nurses' station, I wrote: The woman in bed
fourteen opened her eyes. The clot dissolved. Her brain remembered
every name.

A nurse's coffee slid sideways as the floor trembled.

I wrote: The man in cardiac care grew a clean
red heart.

Somewhere above us, people began shouting.

I wrote: No malignant cell could survive beneath this roof.

The sentence lifted from the page in bright threads. It
passed through walls, vents, elevator shafts. The whole hospital inhaled.

Lights burst one after another down the hall.

A man came out of a room holding his IV
pole.

"My scan," he said. "They said it's gone."

A woman tore tape from her wrist.

"My daughter woke up."

An orderly sat against the wall, laughing.

I kept writing.

Kidneys cleared. Bones sealed. Skin knitted. Blood replenished itself. Infections
surrendered. Pain packed its small black bags and left through
the service entrance. Tumors shrank like embarrassed fruit. The blind
saw, though one man complained immediately about the wallpaper. The
deaf heard alarms and asked if there was a quieter
miracle available.

Doors opened.

Patients stepped into the corridors.

Some still wore gowns. Some dragged blankets. One woman carried
the bag of her own urine over her head like
a lantern. Nurses tried to stop them, then gave up
because their charts were changing faster than they could read.

NO ACUTE FINDINGS multiplied across screens.

DISCHARGE appeared everywhere.

The Twelve-tongued God spoke from the intercom.

"Now you're showing off."

Its voice was pleased.

I reached the radiology basement.

The water fountain no longer leaked. The old couple stood
side by side, both apparently cured of whatever had brought
either of them there. The boy's cast split down the
middle and fell from his arm. He raised the arm
and shouted.

The woman in the scarf removed it. New hair poured
over her shoulders in a black wave and kept growing
until the man beside her had to lift it off
the floor.

The grieving family remained by the vending machines.

They were still there.

They were no longer crying. They looked at one another
with stunned, exhausted faces.

A young woman stepped out from the middle of them.

"You," she said.

I stopped.

She was the one who had collapsed into the chair.
Her eyes were swollen. She held a phone in one
hand.

"You did this?" she asked.

"I think so."

"Then bring him back."

Behind her, the family opened to show the absence at
its center.

I had cured every sickness. Death was not technically a

I looked at the notebook.

The god appeared beside the vending machine as a hospital
custodian. Its gray mustache was wet with twelve narrow lines

"Go on," it said.

I wrote: The dead man returned.

Nothing happened.

"Who?" the god asked.

I looked at the family.

"What was his name?"

The young woman told me.

I wrote the name.

The letters crawled off the page and died against my
wrist.

"Again," said the god.

I described a man I had never seen. He opened
his eyes. His heart resumed. He sat up beneath a
white sheet.

The family waited.

Somewhere, a stranger screamed.

A morgue drawer slammed open down the hall. Footsteps approached,
slow and dragging.

The young woman stared past me.

A very old man came around the corner wearing only
a sheet. His skin was gray. A tag hung from
his toe. He smiled at us with gums.

"That isn't him," she said.

The old man raised both arms as if expecting applause.

The family recoiled.

"You didn't describe him well enough," said the god.

"I didn't know him."

"You wanted universality. It has administrative problems."

The old man sat down on the floor. He seemed
disappointed to be alive and cold.

The young woman struck me.

It was a good strike. My head hit the vending
machine. Candy bars dropped behind the glass.

"Stop using us," she said.

Her family gathered around her. Not a football huddle now.
A wall.

I closed the notebook.

But the story had momentum.

The cured patients flowed through the corridors toward the exits.
There were hundreds of them, then thousands. People rolled their
own wheelchairs. They pushed empty gurneys like parade floats. A
surgeon threw masks into the air. Someone rang a bell
used for the completion of chemotherapy, and then everyone wanted
to ring it, so the bell kept sounding and sounding.

I found my father near the gift shop.

He held my coat under one arm and a crushed
bouquet under the other.

"What did you do?" he asked.

"I made you better."

"I was already better."

"No, you weren't."

"I had a sore arm."

"You were going to get sick."

"Everybody is going to get sick."

"Not anymore."

A woman passing us shouted, "Sickness is over!"

The crowd answered.

SICKNESS IS OVER.

The phrase moved through the hospital, louder each time.

My father looked around at the barefoot patients, the dangling
tubes, the nurses standing stunned beneath the fluorescent lights.

"This is your writing thing," he said.

I almost laughed. "Writing thing?"

"The basement thing."

I went cold.

"What do you know about the basement?"

"You came home bleeding from the mouth. You think I
believed you bit your tongue?"

"You never asked."

"You didn't want me to ask."

The crowd pushed us toward the entrance.

"Give me the notebook," he said.

"No."

"You fixed me. Fine. Now stop."

"I can fix everyone."

"You don't know everyone."

"I don't have to."

"That sounds like you."

The sliding doors opened.

Cold daylight entered.

The first cured patients crossed the threshold.

A woman in a blue gown stepped onto the sidewalk
and rose six inches into the air.

People cheered.

She rose higher.

Her face changed from surprise to joy. She spread her
arms. Her gown opened behind her. For one clean moment
she hung above the ambulance lane, pale feet pointed downward,
hair lifting around her head.

Then she dropped.

Her knees struck first. The sound was small and hard.

The crowd went quiet.

A man crossed the threshold behind her and floated to
the height of the NO SMOKING sign. He turned slowly,
laughing, until gravity remembered him. He landed on his shoulder.

Another woman lifted.

A child.

A doctor.

Each one rose when passing from the hospital into the
world. Each hovered for a few seconds. Then each fell.

It kept happening.

The doors could not close because people filled them. Those
behind could not see the falls and continued pushing. Patients
spilled outside and went up. Three feet. Six feet. Ten.
Their gowns flapped. Their wristbands shone. Some shouted with delight.
Some prayed. Some tried swimming through the air.

Then they came down.

Bodies hit pavement, cars, hedges, one another. A man landed
across the hood of my car four blocks away, though
I did not see that until later. A woman caught
briefly in the branches of a decorative pear tree. Her
IV tubing hung like tinsel.

The groaning began.

It spread across the entrance plaza. The healed lay with
broken wrists, shattered hips, opened scalps. Those unable to walk
crawled back toward the hospital, but at the threshold their
bodies lightened again. A man gripped the doorframe to keep
from rising. His legs floated behind him like streamers.

Inside, the crowd panicked.

I opened the notebook.

"They can fly," said the god.

It stood outside now, above the revolving doors, wearing the
nurse's smiling-cloud scrubs. Wind stirred its twelve tongues around its
face.

"This isn't flying," I shouted.

"Readers enjoy ambiguity."

I wrote: They did not fall.

A young man rose above an ambulance. He remained there,
kicking his feet.

Others stayed up too.

For one second I thought I had repaired it.

Then the wind moved them.

The young man drifted over the road. Another patient struck
a streetlamp and wrapped both arms around it. A baby
in a hospital blanket rose from its mother's hands. The
mother screamed and caught one corner of the blanket. The
baby hung above her, staring down with solemn interest.

People who did not fall could not descend.

They accumulated against buildings, trees, power lines. Their gowns ballooned.
Their tubes tangled. They called for help from twenty feet
up.

I crossed out the sentence.

Everyone dropped.

The world filled with impact.

My father grabbed my wrist.

"Stop writing."

"I have to fix it."

"You keep saying that."

A siren approached, though ambulances could not reach the entrance
because the ambulance drivers were also floating. One clung to
the roof of his vehicle.

I tried another sentence.

Inside this hospital, all endings are happy.

The words ignited.

Fire ran along the page without consuming it. It climbed
my fingers and entered the XII on my back. The
borrowed tongue swelled until I could not close my mouth.

Around me, injured people began smiling.

They smiled through broken teeth and dislocated jaws. The woman
with shattered knees laughed while trying to stand. The mother
laughed as blood ran from her forehead. Her baby laughed
above her. The sounds rose together, cheerful and unbearable.

The god applauded.

"Cheap," it said. "But bold."

I tore out the page.

The laughter stopped.

The woman with broken knees began screaming again. This was
better. I was relieved by her pain because it meant
I had returned something true to her.

My father slapped the notebook from my hands.

It landed open on the pavement.

Wind caught the pages. They turned rapidly, showing pieces of
everything I had used. The kitten. The divorced woman. My
father lifting a bookcase. The grieving family. The old man
in one sock. Patients rose from the paper in sentences,
each bent beneath the metaphor I had assigned them.

The old man pointed at me.

"Doorstop?" he said.

The family gathered around the notebook.

"Football?" said the young woman.

The kitten dragged its flattened body over a paragraph.

A version of my father sat in a recliner, thin
and bald, shaking a paper cup.

"You got rid of me," that father said.

"I saved you."

"You got rid of the part you were afraid to
write."

Another figure appeared. A woman from a story I had
abandoned years earlier. I had kept her husband in a
coma for twenty-six pages because I liked the tension.

"How long do I have to sit beside that bed?"
she asked.

"I never finished."

"I noticed."

The figures crowded around me. All the people I had
injured for structure. All the people I had cured for
applause. Their mouths moved, and the voices came from my
own borrowed tongue.

My father kicked the notebook shut.

The figures vanished.

He picked it up.

"Burn it," he said.

"I can't."

"Why?"

I looked above the doors.

The god was gone.

The XII cooled beneath my shirt. It had left me
alone with authorship, which was its cruelest face.

"If I stop," I said, though the swollen tongue made
the words thick, "it gets worse."

My father stared at my mouth.

"What gets worse?"

"Everything."

Behind him, a patient floated through the doors, rose gently
to the level of the awning, and fell onto a
stack of wheelchairs.

My father looked back.

The hospital staff had begun tying people to gurneys before
wheeling them outside. This helped until the gurneys lifted too.
Orderlies hung from the frames to provide ballast. A nurse
shouted for sandbags. Someone from maintenance brought chains.

Those who had already fallen were carried inside, healed, and
sent out again because the crowd kept moving. They rose.
They fell. They broke. They were healed. They rose again.

The same people groaned and groaned.

"It keeps happening," my father said.

"I know."

"Make it not happen."

"I don't know how."

"You made it."

"That's different."

"How?"

I had no useful answer.

I took the notebook from him and wrote slowly.

The hospital was once again a place where sickness occurred.

Nothing changed.

I wrote: Cancer returned. Hearts failed. Blood clotted. Children struggled
to breathe. The dead remained dead.

The words sat flat.

I had wanted suffering gone. I could not invite it
back in a sentence without choosing each victim. I could
not decide whose tumor should return, whose child should close
his eyes, whose father should begin the long walk toward
a room with a recliner and a paper cup.

The happy ending had locked behind us.

At the entrance, another man rose.

He looked peaceful during the ascent. Most of them did.
That was the worst part. For a few seconds they
believed.

He reached the height of the hospital sign. Sunlight touched
his face. He opened his arms, and his gown fluttered
around him like the robes of a minor saint.

Then he fell into the hedge.

My father touched his left arm.

"Does it hurt?" I asked.

"No."

"Good."

"Is it?"

I looked at him.

His face was not the face from the future. It
was full, irritated, alive. He would not get the cancer
I had seen. I was almost certain. I had torn
that road away.

But roads go somewhere even when removed.

Across the plaza, the woman with the yellow bag floated
waist-high while two security guards held her ankles. The boy
with the bandaged forehead sailed over a parked ambulance, delighted.
His mother ran beneath him with both hands raised. The
grieving family remained inside the doors, unwilling to cross. Their
dead stayed dead. Their healthy bodies had become another kind
of trap.

Snow began, actual snow this time.

White pieces gathered on the blood and melted.

My father held out the notebook.

"What are you writing?" he asked.

I looked at the pages. They were blank now. Every
word had crawled away or burned through.

"A story," I said.

"About what?"

A nurse drifted above the parking meters, turning slowly in
the gray morning. Three patients clung to her legs. Beneath
them, people spread blankets that would not help.

I could have told him it was about fear. Or
ambition. Or how love becomes monstrous when it refuses limits.
I could have said it was about class and medicine
and the arrogance of turning bodies into symbols. I could
have told him flight meant transcendence, except transcendence usually has
better landing instructions.

Those would have been writer answers.

My father waited.

"It's about a hospital where people can fly," I said.

He looked at the bodies rising from the doors. He
looked at the bodies on the ground. He looked at
me.

"What have you done?"

## The Master of Transom's

The room in which Ebenezer Transom had been born and
was now preparing to die had a look of having
endured both events too often to be much impressed by
either. Its whitewash, once renewed at weddings and confinements, had
settled into the colour of old bone; the beams were
smoke-blackened, the boards bare and uneven, and the narrow casement
admitted less light than weather. Yet the chamber possessed that
distinction which long service sometimes gives to mean things. Four
generations had drawn breath there, cried there, quarrelled there, and
ceased from troubling; and the walls seemed to have taken
from them something of the gravity of a human face.

Elizabeth Rundle sat beside the bed, knitting a grey stocking
which would never be worn by the man for whom
it had been begun. She was called 'Lizabeth by everybody
within six miles, and had been called so since childhood;
not from tenderness, but because country speech grudged a syllable
where none was needed.

The candle upon the chest guttered whenever the wind found
the crack below the door. Beyond the window lay the
yard, black with December rain, the linhay, the cattle-house and
the ash tree whose naked branches scraped at the stone
like the fingers of an importunate creditor. Christmas Eve had
gone by without bell or visitor. It was now nearly
midnight.

"Be the beasts fed?" Ebenezer asked.

"They be."

"Daisy'll kick if Joe milks her from the wrong side."

"I told him."

"An' the red heifer—"

"Her's shut in."

He moved his head querulously upon the pillow. The long
labour of dying had not softened him; it had merely
reduced the compass of his complaints. For thirty years he
had governed Transom's Farm with a close fist, a sharp
eye and no unnecessary speech. It offended him that death
should require so much attendance.

"You'll not let 'em waste hay," he said.

"No."

"Nor burn two candles where one'll serve."

"No."

His eyes opened, pale and vexed. "You do answer short."

"You haven't breath to spare for long answers, uncle."

"A woman's answer."

She laid aside the knitting, lifted his head and gave
him water from a cracked cup. Her hands were brown
and capable, with small scars across the fingers from bramble,
knife and oven. She was thirty-two; tall, spare, and composed
rather than handsome. In youth her mouth had been ready
to smile, but the readiness had worn away. There remained
in her face something firm and clean, as in a
field after rain.

Ebenezer swallowed with difficulty.

"William," he said.

The name fell into the room as a live coal
might have fallen upon the bedclothes. 'Lizabeth replaced the cup
without haste.

"What of him?"

"He'll come when I be under."

"Maybe."

"To see what's left."

"Maybe not."

"He'll come." The old man's lips drew back in the
shadow of a smile. "A bad son's got a rare
nose for a dead father's money."

"He may be dead himself."

"Nay. We'd ha' heard. Bad news travels, but not so
fast as good."

For seven years William Transom had been in the army,
or said to be in it when he last wrote.
Before that he had been many things for short periods
and nothing for long: horse-dealer's man, carrier, ostler, farm labourer
when compelled, and the admiration of those girls who mistook
a red cheek and a ready laugh for evidence of
a generous heart. He had left the parish after borrowing
ten pounds from his father, two from 'Lizabeth, and promises
from several women. From one of these last creditors he
had taken more than he ever meant to repay.

Ebenezer's breathing caught and rattled.

"You'll stop here till—" he began.

"Yes."

"Then send Hooper."

"Yes."

"For the measuring."

She looked at him.

"For your coffin?"

"What else?"

"You'd best leave that till morning."

"Always putting off," he muttered, though if any postponement were
occurring it was not hers.

A little later he became restless and tried to sit
up. She supported him while the rain drove hard against
the pane. His head, which in health had been carried
like that of an old hawk, sank against her shoulder.
She felt the last effort go from him, not dramatically,
but by degrees, as warmth leaves an iron set aside
from the fire.

"Uncle?"

His lids were closed. She waited, counting unconsciously the ticks
of the clock below. There came no breath.

Ebenezer Transom was dead.

For several minutes 'Lizabeth did not move. The candle flame
recovered itself; the ash branch scraped; somewhere in the cattle-house
a chain clinked as a beast turned in its stall.
Death had entered without grandeur and was already being absorbed
into the business of the place.

She straightened the dead man, tied up his jaw with
a folded handkerchief, and drew the sheet. Then she took
the candle downstairs.

The kitchen looked altered, though nothing had changed. Ebenezer's boots
remained beneath the settle, their toes pointing toward the hearth.
His mug stood on the dresser. A piece of bread,
cut by his hand and rejected at supper, lay beside
the salt. But the room had lost its master, and
every object appeared conscious of having been left without instructions.

'Lizabeth stirred the fire and put on the kettle. It
was not grief that made her do so, nor hunger,
but habit, which in houses of sickness serves as courage.
She had just taken down the tea caddy when the
outer latch rattled.

She stood still.

The latch rattled again, cautiously at first, then with impatience.
The dogs had not barked. They were old, and the
rain confused their senses; nevertheless a stranger coming across the
yard at midnight was no Christmas comfort.

"Who's there?" she called.

No answer.

She crossed to the mantel, reached behind Ebenezer's account books
and took down the pistol. It had been loaded against
poultry thieves five years earlier, but she did not trust
five-year-old powder. Keeping her eyes upon the door, she opened
the drawer, measured fresh powder, rammed it home and set
the cap. The movements were exact and quiet.

A fist struck the oak.

"Open, can't 'ee? I be drowned out here!"

She knew the voice before the last word.

The pistol lowered a little.

"William?"

"Who else d'ye reckon?"

She unbarred the door.

He came in with the night behind him, bringing rain,
mud and a gust that sent smoke over the hearth.
He was broad now where he had once been merely
well-made. A military coat strained across his shoulders; his moustache
was waxed at the ends; and his complexion had the
polished redness of beef displayed for a prize. Time had
thickened him without improving his substance.

"Well, 'Lizabeth!" he cried. "You do keep a welcome like
a gaol-house. Put that thing down. You'd not shoot an
old friend?"

"I might shoot what rattled the latch and wouldn't answer."

"Still sharp, then." He shut the door and looked round.
"Where's father?"

"Upstairs."

"Asleep?"

"Dead."

The joviality left his face, though not quite so quickly
as decency required.

"Dead? When?"

"Ten minutes gone."

"God's truth." He stared at the ceiling. "I've come close,
then."

"Too late."

"Aye." He drew off his wet coat and flung it
across Ebenezer's chair. "Poor old fellow."

The words had the smoothness of counters worn by frequent
use. 'Lizabeth took his coat and hung it from a
peg.

"You'd best go up."

"In a minute. I've walked from the turnpike. Got naught
hot?"

"There's tea."

"Tea!" He laughed. "I've been living amongst men, cousin. Haven't
'ee brandy?"

"Uncle kept some for sickness."

"Well, he's past it."

She looked at him, and the laugh dwindled.

"Tea'll do," he said.

While she set bread and cheese upon the table, William
held his hands to the blaze. She was aware of
every detail of him: the scar by his ear, the
new heaviness beneath his eyes, the ring on his smallest
finger. Seven years had not erased the younger William from
her memory; it had only set this coarser figure over
him, as a clumsy inscription may be cut across an
old and cherished one.

He, meanwhile, considered her with greater simplicity.

"You haven't altered much."

"I have."

"Not to look at. A bit leaner, maybe. You always
was one for work."

She poured the tea.

"Did father speak of me?"

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

"That you'd come when he was dead."

William gave a short laugh. "That's father, sure enough. Thought
the worst of every man and generally found it. Still,
I'd ha' come sooner if I'd known."

"You knew he was failing. I wrote."

"Letters don't always reach a soldier."

"You answered it."

"Did I? Then maybe I meant to come." He bit
cheese. "Campaigning puts things out of a man's head."

"Where have you come from?"

"Bristol, last."

"Are you discharged?"

He tapped his breast pocket. "Papers all proper."

"Honourably?"

His eyes sharpened. "Proper, I said."

She sat opposite him, the pistol still beside her hand.
Between them stood the teapot which she had bought years
ago at the fair, on a day when William had
told her she had eyes like sloes after frost. She
had believed this observation to be poetry. It seemed now
merely botanical.

He drank noisily.

"So father held on to the place."

"Yes."

"Mortgage?"

"No."

"Debts?"

"None worth naming."

"Stock sound?"

"Sound enough."

William leaned back, satisfaction broadening him. "Then it's not so
bad. A man comes home with little in his pocket
and finds a roof waiting. There's worse ends to soldiering."

"Your father's body is under that roof."

"Aye, and I'm main sorry. But dead's dead, 'Lizabeth. Farm
won't stop for mourning."

"No."

"You been managing?"

"Mostly."

"Then you can show me the ways of it." His
glance travelled over her with an easy impudence which he
mistook for charm. "Might be you'll not have to quit
in haste."

"I haven't thought of quitting."

"No need. There's room enough, if folk agree." He smiled.
"You and me agreed well once."

She rose to fetch more water though the kettle was
full.

"We were younger."

"And you were kinder."

"I was more foolish."

"Nay, you were a handsome maid. Not that you're amiss
now." He stretched his boots toward the fire. "A farm
wants a mistress. I've learnt that much, tramping about."

"Then you should have brought one."

For an instant something passed across his face. Before she
could read it, there came a faint sound from outside:
not the latch this time, but a knock against the
wall, followed by a woman's cough.

'Lizabeth turned.

William remained seated.

"Who's that?"

"Wind."

"The wind's got a chest complaint."

The cough came again, and then a querulous voice called,
"Will! I can't stand here no longer!"

William swore beneath his breath.

'Lizabeth opened the door. A woman was huddled under the
penthouse, sheltering among sacks and broken tools. She wore a
bonnet absurdly decorated with sodden artificial cherries, a yellow cloak,
and thin town shoes submerged in farmyard mud. Her face,
beneath its rain-streaked powder, was small, pretty and peevish.

"Come in," said 'Lizabeth.

The woman entered and sagged upon the settle like a
half-empty bag.

"Oh, mercy! Such a place! Will, you said it weren't
half a mile."

"Road was longer in the dark."

"You left me by that horrid shed."

"I had to see how matters stood."

"What matters?"

William got up. His swagger had become defensive.

"This here's Maria," he said. "My wife."

The kitchen was very quiet. A drop of rain fell
from one of the bonnet cherries and struck the hearthstone
with a small hiss.

"Your wife," repeated 'Lizabeth.

"Married legal in Bristol. Three months come Tuesday."

Maria peered at her. "You're the cousin, I suppose."

"Yes."

"He told me about you."

"I doubt it."

"He said you kept house for the old gentleman. Is
he truly dead?"

"Yes."

"How dreadful! And us come on Christmas." Maria looked toward
the ceiling and shivered. "I couldn't sleep beneath a corpse."

"You won't be asked to sleep beneath him. His room
is over the parlour."

"Well, I mean in the house. Will, why didn't you
tell me he was so bad?"

"I did."

"You said he might last till spring."

"Men don't die by timetable."

Maria began to cry. Her tears, unlike the rain, appeared
to improve her complexion. "I have been dragged through mud,
shut in a cart with poultry, and left in a
shed, and now there's death in the house. I wish
I'd never come."

William looked at 'Lizabeth as though she were somehow accountable
for these arrangements.

"Take her up," he said. "Get her dry."

"There's the spare room."

"Not the little one," Maria protested. "I must have air.
I'm delicate."

"The large room has no fire."

"Then light one."

'Lizabeth picked up the candle. "Come."

As Maria rose, William detained 'Lizabeth by the sleeve.

"You see how 'tis," he muttered.

"I do."

"No use making it awkward."

"I'm not making it."

"Maria's used to gentler ways."

'Lizabeth glanced at the gingerbread ornaments on the wet bonnet,
the cheap cloak and the broken heel.

"So I see."

She led Maria upstairs. At the first landing they passed
the chamber of death. Maria drew away from its closed
door and clutched 'Lizabeth's arm.

"Was he terrible at the end?"

"No."

"Did he speak?"

"A little."

"About William?"

"A little."

Maria's curiosity fought with fear. "What did he say?"

"That William would come."

"How strange. Almost like second sight."

"It was knowledge, not sight."

At the top of the stairs 'Lizabeth stopped. A line
of light showed beneath the door of Ebenezer's room.

She had left the candle burning there, but the light
moved. Something scraped across the boards; then came the unmistakable
chink of metal.

Maria whispered, "Is somebody in there?"

'Lizabeth opened the door.

William stood beside the old oak chest at the foot
of the bed. The lid was raised. Papers lay upon
the floor, account books upon the chair, and a canvas
bag in his hand. The dead man's covered face made
the occupation appear not more wicked, perhaps, but considerably more
vulgar.

Maria gave a little scream.

William swung round. "What in thunder—"

"You were quick," said 'Lizabeth.

"I'm looking for papers. A man's got a right."

"Before he's cold?"

"He'd not be warmer tomorrow."

Maria edged past 'Lizabeth. "Is that money?"

"Only a few sovereigns." William thrust the bag into his
pocket. "Father's, and therefore mine."

"Not till the law says so," said 'Lizabeth.

"The law won't trouble itself between father and son."

He knelt again and pulled out a bundle of receipts.
Maria, reassured by the ordinary nature of avarice, came closer.
She picked up a silver watch from the chest.

"That's handsome."

"Put it down," said 'Lizabeth.

Maria replaced it, offended.

William broke the string on another bundle. "No will here.
Did he make one?"

"Not that I know."

"He say aught?"

"No."

"Where did he keep deeds?"

"In the bottom drawer."

The drawer resisted him. He cursed, seized the handle with
both hands and wrenched it out. Behind it lay a
long envelope, brown with age but newly sealed. William caught
it up.

"Here we be."

Upon the outside was written: *The Last Will and Testament
of Ebenezer Transom, of Transom's Farm.*

Maria clasped her hands. "Oh, Will!"

He tore it open. The document within was not long.
He read the first lines rapidly; then his face darkened,
and he began again, moving his lips.

"What is it?" Maria demanded.

He did not answer.

"William?" said 'Lizabeth.

His head came up.

"You knew."

"Knew what?"

"You damned, sly, canting—"

"Mind your tongue," she said, and the quietness of it
stopped him where shouting might not.

He held the paper toward the candle and read aloud:

"'I, Ebenezer Transom, being of sound mind—' Sound! 'Do give
and bequeath all my lands, goods, monies, stock, household effects
and whatsoever else I may die possessed of, to my
niece Elizabeth Rundle, she having served me faithfully in sickness
and health when my natural son deserted his duty.'"

Maria sank upon the chair.

William crushed the edge of the will in his fist.
"All. Every stick and stone."

'Lizabeth took the paper from him and read it herself.

The words were plain. The signature was Ebenezer's, witnessed by
Lawyer Cade and Thomas Hooper. The date was two years
old.

She lowered it.

"I didn't know."

"No, of course not." William laughed harshly. "Never guessed, did
'ee? Only sat by him, turned him against me, wrote
your lies, kept me away—"

"I wrote asking you to come."

"To see me watch you take the place?"

"I knew nothing of this."

"D'ye expect Maria to believe that? D'ye expect me?"

Maria began weakly, "Perhaps she truly—"

"Hold your noise." William pointed at 'Lizabeth. "You always wanted
it."

"The farm?"

"Me."

The word struck with greater accuracy than he understood.

"You wanted me," he continued. "Everybody knew. You thought you'd
get the land, and then I'd come begging, and you
could buy me with my own father's acres. That was
the trick, wasn't it? Make yourself mistress here and take
me as your man."

Colour rose slowly in 'Lizabeth's face. She looked at the
dead bed, at Maria in her ruined finery, and finally
at William.

There had been a time when she had indeed wanted
him. She had saved ribbons for Sundays because he liked
blue; had waited by gates; had listened for his step
in the lane; had given him money when she could
ill spare it and forgiveness when he had not asked.
Worse than all these follies, she had believed her constancy
noble merely because it was painful. His departure had wounded
her, but it had not cured her at once. For
years she had kept some inner door unbarred, though no
guest came to it.

Now shame entered where love had lingered. It was not
shame that she had loved without return; that misfortune may
befall the wise. It was shame that the object had
been this man.

William mistook her silence.

"There. You can't deny it."

"I do deny it."

"Burn the will, then."

Maria stared at him. "Will!"

"If she's innocent, let her prove it. Father's son should
have father's land. Everybody knows that. 'Tis nature."

"Nature didn't feed him," said 'Lizabeth. "Nor sit awake with
him. Nor fetch the doctor through snow."

"So you charged high for it."

She looked down at the will.

The fire in the grate had sunk, but a red
core remained beneath the ash. The document in her hands
was a poor-looking instrument to contain fields, barns, beasts, money
and the future relations of three human beings. Ebenezer's dead
face was turned toward the ceiling. It offered neither command
nor absolution.

Maria rose. "Don't do anything foolish. The paper's legal. If
the old gentleman wished—"

"He was my father!" William cried.

"And I was his wife's niece," said 'Lizabeth. "Blood can
name its claims all night."

Her voice had changed. It was still low, but each
word seemed to have passed through some severe inward judgment
before being uttered.

"I earned what he left me. Maybe I earned it
better than you'd have done. But I'll not hold land
that makes you say I bought your love; nor keep
one roof-tile under which such a thought could live. You
reckon the farm is yours by nature. Take it by
nature, then, and see what nature does for you."

She folded the will once.

William watched her, breathing hard.

"'Lizabeth," Maria said, "don't—"

She crossed to the grate and laid the folded paper
upon the coals.

For one second nothing happened. Then a blue flame ran
along the edge, sharpened, and turned yellow. The seal blistered.
Ebenezer's name blackened, curled and lifted like a dead leaf.
William made an involuntary movement toward it, but stopped. The
three of them watched until the last written clause collapsed
into red ash.

The rain beat against the window.

"There," said 'Lizabeth.

William stared at the grate. Triumph came into his face,
but uneasily, as though it had entered by the wrong
door.

"You've done sensible."

"No."

"You said—"

"I've done what I chose. Sense had little part in
it."

Maria began to cry again, from agitation, fatigue or a
dim perception that she had witnessed something beyond her measure.

'Lizabeth went to the cupboard and took out her aunt's
old travelling box.

"What be you doing?" William asked.

"Packing."

"At this hour?"

"At this hour."

"You needn't go. I told you, there's room."

"Not for me."

"Don't be proud. Maria'll want help, and I'm not one
to bear a grudge."

She faced him. "You cannot bear what you haven't weight
enough to carry."

His mouth opened.

"Good night, William. Your father lies there. If you've any
prayer in you, say it before you count his sovereigns."

She left him standing between the open chest and the
bed.

Before dawn 'Lizabeth had packed two dresses, her boots, three
books, some linen and the teapot. She dressed the body,
set bread to rise, wrote down the fodder measures for
the cattle, and covered the kitchen fire. These things she
did not for William, nor for Ebenezer, but because neglected
work had always appeared to her a species of falsehood.

At six she walked across the fields to Hooper's, carrying
the box in one hand and her umbrella in the
other. The rain had diminished to a grey drizzle. Behind
her, Transom's Farm lay low under the wet sky, its
chimneys smoking and its windows dark. She did not turn
until she reached the stile. When she did, she looked
once, as a person may look upon a grave not
because the dead can see, but because the living must
learn that they have left it.

Thomas Hooper opened his door in his stockings.

"Lord save us, 'Lizabeth! Who's dead?"

"Uncle."

"Ah." He rubbed his chin. "Well, he'd been at it
a long while. Come in."

Mrs. Hooper appeared behind him, round, warm and immediately lachrymose.

"You poor soul! On Christmas morning too! There, now, don't
speak. I know all. You shall have bacon."

"I need work," said 'Lizabeth.

"Work?"

"And a room, if you've one."

The Hoopers had both. Their eldest daughter had married in
November, leaving an attic chamber and more dairy labour than
Mrs. Hooper cared to acknowledge. By breakfast, 'Lizabeth was installed.

The will, once burned, could not conveniently bear witness to
its own destruction. Lawyer Cade remembered drawing it, but since
Ebenezer had retained the document and William denied knowledge of
any testament, remembrance alone availed little. Thomas Hooper, who had
witnessed it, was inclined to speak; 'Lizabeth forbade him.

"Old man meant you to have all," he protested.

"Old man's dead."

"Meaning don't die with a man."

"Paper did."

"You had no right to burn it."

"No," she said. "That may be."

This answer, being less defensible and more final than argument,
silenced him.

William inherited as the only son. He paid the funeral
expenses with an ostentation which would have surprised Ebenezer, bought
himself a new trap, and put a brass plate on
the gate. Maria, who now called herself Mrs. Transom in
a manner suggestive of recent promotion, had the parlour papered
in crimson roses and removed the old dresser because it
looked common. She was much concerned that neighbouring families should
understand she had been accustomed to society in Bristol. Bristol,
if consulted, might have shown less concern to establish the
acquaintance.

At first William worked with spasmodic violence. He rose early
for three mornings, scolded Joe, bought two unnecessary machines and
spoke of improving the breed of cattle. By February he
rose at eight, by March at nine, and by April
he had discovered that farming was impossible without frequent visits
to the market town. The farm survived because land has
a stubborn patience with its owners, and because Joe knew
more than William supposed.

'Lizabeth heard these matters at Hooper's table and made no
comment. She milked, churned, baked, mended harness, kept accounts and
nursed Mrs. Hooper through an attack of rheumatic fever. She
passed Transom's lane only when necessity required it. If she
saw William's trap in the distance, she took another path;
if Maria came to church in a hat decorated with
feathers of an egregious height, 'Lizabeth looked at the hymn
book.

Her reserve, never slight, became complete. She did not brood
visibly. Brooding interferes with work, and 'Lizabeth's hands were seldom
unoccupied. Yet something within her had been shut and clasped.
The clasp was pride, the lock knowledge; and if pain
remained behind it, she gave no person leave to test
the door.

Maria was not happy at Transom's. The winters were damp,
the lanes deep, the neighbours insufficiently impressed, and William less
gallant at breakfast than he had been in Bristol taverns.
She lacked the constitution for rural discomfort and the temper
to make discomfort tolerable. Before their first year was out
she developed a cough. It improved in warm weather, returned
with the fogs, and by the following January had settled
upon her lungs.

Mrs. Hooper carried broth to her and came back with
reports.

"Poor little thing," she said. "Not made for farm life.
Hands white as candles."

"Candles do some work," said Thomas.

"You men have no tenderness."

"I've tenderness enough not to compare a woman with tallow."

'Lizabeth strained milk through muslin.

"Did she ask for me?" she said.

Mrs. Hooper hesitated. "She did happen to mention your name."

"What did she want?"

"Said you knew where the household linen was kept. William
can't find half."

"I told him. Bottom press in the passage."

"She says it isn't."

"It is."

The next day 'Lizabeth went.

Maria lay in the crimson parlour because she feared the
bedroom in which Ebenezer had died. The roses upon the
wall crowded behind her with a vulgar abundance; her face
among them looked small and colourless. She had lost her
prettiness, but gained something less fragile than charm.

"I knew you'd come," she said.

"You sent."

"I thought you hated me."

"No."

"You never looked at me in church."

"I go there to look elsewhere."

Maria smiled faintly. "You always did speak queer."

The linen was where 'Lizabeth had said. She brought sheets,
aired them, made broth and stayed through the afternoon. William
came in at dusk smelling of rain and ale. He
appeared surprised, then relieved.

"Good girl," he said. "You know what she wants."

'Lizabeth did not answer.

After that Maria sent for her often. There are offences
which diminish beside sickness, not because they are forgiven, but
because the body's distress is older than pride. 'Lizabeth sat
with her, changed the damp pillow, measured medicine and listened
to complaints of the mud, the doctor, the neighbours and
William.

One evening Maria caught her wrist.

"You truly didn't know of the will?"

"No."

"I believe you now."

"That's late."

"I was frightened then. I thought we'd have nothing."

"You have the farm."

Maria glanced toward the door. "Sometimes nothing might be quieter."

In March, a little more than a year after her
arrival, Maria died. She had been twenty-seven. Her artificial cherries
remained in a bandbox upstairs; her crimson roses remained upon
the parlour walls; and William, who had neglected her in
health with ordinary marital carelessness, mourned her in death with
considerable sincerity. Remorse flattered his sense of feeling, and loneliness
was a hardship he could understand.

'Lizabeth laid Maria out. While fastening the clean gown at
the throat, she remembered the wet figure sagging upon the
settle and felt neither triumph nor resentment—only that grave pity
which the dead exact even from those they have misunderstood.

William stood at the window.

"You were good to her," he said.

"She was ill."

"Not everybody would've come."

"No."

He turned. "'Lizabeth—"

"Fetch the pins from the dresser."

He fetched them.

After the funeral, five months passed.

The year moved toward summer. Grass rose in the meadows;
apple blossom fell; calves grew broad-backed beside their mothers. At
Hooper's, 'Lizabeth's days took their customary shape. She rose before
five, skimmed the cream, fed poultry, baked twice weekly and
kept Thomas from ruining his accounts by optimism. Outwardly, life
had become level.

On a warm June morning she sat beneath the elder
tree by the dairy door, shelling peas into a blue
basin. The yard was washed with sunlight. Bees worried the
wallflowers; a hen scratched dust over herself with indecorous satisfaction.
Mrs. Hooper had gone to visit a married daughter, and
Thomas was in the far field.

William entered through the wicket.

'Lizabeth saw his shadow before she looked up.

He wore his best coat and carried a switch. His
mourning band was narrow but visible. For a moment he
stood arranging his expression, which wished to be manly, sorrowful
and attractive at once.

"Morning."

"Morning."

"Fine weather."

"Yes."

"Peas come early."

"They were sown early."

He sat on the bench without invitation. She continued splitting
pods with her thumb. The peas fell through her fingers,
round, cool and exact.

"You never come by now," he said.

"No."

"Place is lonesome."

"So I've heard."

"I've made changes. Put drainage in the lower field."

"That field needed it."

"Maria always said so."

"Maria knew little of drains."

He frowned, then sighed as if indulgent toward her severity.

"You were with her at the end."

"Yes."

"She spoke well of you."

"She spoke little."

"She'd have wanted us to be friends."

This employment of the dead was characteristic but not unexpected.
'Lizabeth opened another pod.

"What do you want, William?"

He tapped the switch against his boot.

"Straight as ever. Well, no harm in straight speaking. I've
thought things over."

"Have you?"

"A man does, alone in that house. A farm wants
a woman. Servants waste, dairy's going wrong, and there's no
comfort coming home."

"You can hire a housekeeper."

"Not the same."

"No."

"You know the place. Better than any woman. Father trusted
you."

She looked at him then.

He shifted.

"I don't mean to rake up old trouble. We both
spoke hasty that night."

"I did not."

"Well, I did, maybe. A man finding himself cut off
with his wife beside him—there's excuse."

"Is there?"

"You burnt it. That showed where your heart was, whatever
you said."

Her fingers stopped among the peas.

"What did it show?"

"That you couldn't see me wronged."

"It showed I wouldn't be thought to purchase you."

"Same thing, near enough."

"No."

He leaned toward her. "Listen, 'Lizabeth. You'd have had all.
I've often thought on that. Every acre would've been yours,
and I'd have had naught. But you gave it back.
Now I'm offering fair. Marry me, and you'll be mistress
after all. Your share comes home to you, like. Half
mine, half yours—though man and wife don't count halves."

He smiled. It was the old smile, thickened by years
but still confident in its office. Once it had disturbed
her sleep.

The pea pod in her hand was empty. She laid
it with the husks.

"You think this is payment?"

"Don't twist words. I'm offering marriage."

"Five months after Maria's death."

"A man can't mourn forever."

"Five months isn't forever."

"She'd understand. She knew I couldn't manage alone." He softened
his tone. "And I'm fond of you, 'Lizabeth. Always was,
in a fashion. We were young then. I had wild
blood. Soldiering settles a man."

"It settled you badly."

His cheek deepened in colour. "No call for insults. I
come honourable."

"You come because your dairy's going wrong."

"That's woman's work. Why shouldn't my wife do it?"

"No reason, if you find one."

"I have found one."

"No."

He stared. "What d'ye mean, no?"

"I mean I won't marry you."

He laughed, but the laugh required effort. "Still punishing me?"

"No."

"Want me to ask prettier?"

"No."

"I could say things if that's what you're after. Never
had much tongue for courting."

"You had enough once."

"Aye, and you liked it."

"I did."

The admission checked him. He had prepared himself against denial,
reproach and coyness; calm assent gave him no purchase.

She resumed shelling.

"I loved you," she said. "That is true. I loved
you when there was little in you to love, and
I made that little greater by looking at it too
long. When you went, I thought you had broken my
heart. You had not. You had only bruised my vanity,
and vanity makes a powerful noise."

"That's fine talk."

"It has taken me years to find plain words for
it."

"You burnt a fortune for me."

"I burnt it against myself."

He frowned. "What's that to mean?"

"It meant I would not profit by your vileness, nor
leave you to call my love a bargain. It meant
I was ashamed that such a man could wound me.
And because I was proud, I wounded myself deeper, so
that your stroke might seem the lesser."

He rose.

"You're saying I'm not good enough."

"I'm saying I did not know myself, and knew you
less. Now I know both."

"I own Transom's."

"Yes."

"I've money."

"Some."

"You're a hired woman at Hooper's."

"Yes."

"And you turn me down?"

"Yes."

His incredulity was genuine. Property, masculine privilege and remembered desire
had combined in his mind into an argument so conclusive
that refusal seemed not merely insulting but unnatural.

"You'll be sorry."

"I have been sorry. It passed."

"You'll grow old."

"If I live."

"Alone."

"There are worse companions than solitude."

"You're hard," he said. "Hard as father."

"No. Your father kept what he valued. I gave it
away."

He paced to the wicket and back.

"What was I to do? Marry you when I was
twenty? Tie myself before I'd seen life?"

"No."

"You'd have had me, then."

"Yes."

"But not now, with land under me and sense in
my head?"

"Not now."

"So it's spite."

"No."

"Pride, then."

"Yes," she said. "Some pride. A person must keep enough
to stand upright."

He stopped before her. "Last word?"

She tipped the full basin gently, settling the peas.

"Last."

"Then you can stay servant here till your back breaks."

"I may."

"And don't come to me when Hooper dies and his
wife turns you out."

"I shall not."

"You women never know your own minds."

"I know mine now."

He struck the elder trunk with his switch. Small white
blossoms shook down upon his sleeve.

"Good-bye, then."

"Good-bye, William."

He waited, perhaps expecting some movement, some tremor, some recalling
word. None came. She picked up another pod.

William went through the wicket and crossed the yard. His
boots sounded on the hard ground, diminished along the lane
and ceased.

'Lizabeth sat beneath the elder tree until the peas were
shelled. Then she carried them into the kitchen, put the
husks in the pig bucket and set the basin in
cold water. Her hands did not shake. Only when she
lifted the kettle did she find it empty; she had
forgotten to fill it, which was rare enough to mark
the morning more surely than tears.

Mrs. Hooper returned at noon and learnt of the visit
from Thomas, who had seen William crossing the far field.

"What did he want?" she asked.

"He asked me to marry him."

Mrs. Hooper dropped her bonnet upon the table. "He didn't!"

"He did."

"And you?"

"I said no."

Mrs. Hooper stared with the solemn amazement of one before
whom Providence had committed an error.

"But, 'Lizabeth—the farm!"

"I know the farm."

"You'd be mistress."

"I have been near enough to it."

"And him a widower, poor man, with that great house
and no woman to see to his buttons. Why, it
sounds appointed!"

"By whom?"

Mrs. Hooper ignored this impious difficulty. "You always had a
leaning toward him. Everybody said so."

"Everybody was right."

"Then what more d'ye want?"

"Nothing."

Thomas, entering with mud on his gaiters, heard the matter
and scratched his head.

"Women be queer cattle," he pronounced. "Chase a man when
he's running, run when he turns."

"I never chased him," said 'Lizabeth.

"Not with your feet, maybe."

Mrs. Hooper sighed. "You'll think better by Michaelmas."

"I shall not."

"By Christmas, then."

"No."

And since ordinary minds are often most persistent where they
are least able to enter, the Hoopers continued for years
to suppose that 'Lizabeth had refused William in order to
encourage him. When he did not ask again, they revised
their judgment and held that she had carried the game
too far. It comforted them to reduce a matter of
pride, renunciation and self-knowledge to a mishandled courtship; such explanations
keep the world familiar.

William remained at Transom's. He farmed neither disastrously nor well,
and in later life was heard to say that women
had ruined his peace: one by dying and another by
refusing to take her place. He never understood that Maria's
death and 'Lizabeth's refusal were the two events in which
neither woman had considered his convenience.

'Lizabeth stayed with the Hoopers until both were dead. Afterward
she kept house for their son, then for his widow,
and at last lived in a cottage near the common
with a strip of garden, six hens and furniture enough
for one person. She was useful at births, illnesses and
deaths; she could be trusted with money, secrets and the
laying-out of the dead. Children feared her at first and
loved her later. Men called her proud, women called her
unfortunate, and neither judgment greatly disturbed her.

She never married.

On certain winter nights, when rain came slantwise across the
common and the latch moved in the wind, she would
look up from her sewing. Those who sat with her
saw only a tall old woman listening for weather. They
could not know of the candle in the death-room, the
open chest, the paper curling red upon the coals, or
the young soldier's face which had once occupied her heart
and had afterward been expelled from it at so great
a cost.

But she did not regret her answer beneath the elder
tree.

Regret belonged to the years before knowledge, when she had
mistaken endurance for love and suffering for proof. What remained
afterward was sterner and less consoling, yet it was her
own. She had lost a farm, a husband she had
once desired, and the common story by which a woman's
life was judged complete. In return she had gained no
romance, no wealth and no public vindication.

She had gained only the truth.

It was not much to look at; but, unlike William's
inheritance, she knew how dearly it had been bought.

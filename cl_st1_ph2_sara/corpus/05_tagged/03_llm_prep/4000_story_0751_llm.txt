## The Other Side of the Ridge

It was my wife who first discovered that the Bredes
had three children, and it was I who discovered, not
twenty-four hours later, that they had none.

I make this statement with the caution proper to a
man whose memory has more than once misplaced a railway
ticket, an umbrella, and the principal point of his own
argument. Amelia declares that I forget only what is useful
and remember with unnatural exactness whatever may be brought against
her. This is unjust. I have forgotten many excellent things
that might have been brought against Amelia.

We were spending August at Jacobus's boarding house in the
Helderberg country, where the air was advertised as "tonic," the
food was abundant, and the beds resembled geological formations. Jacobus
himself was a broad, slow-moving man with a beard of
uncertain color and the habit of carrying a hammer. What
he did with the hammer no one knew. I never
saw him drive a nail, though I saw him carry
it to meals, to the spring, and once to family
prayers.

The house stood on a shelf of the mountain. Below
us, field fell into field, divided by stone walls that
became threads in the distance; white roads wandered among orchards;
villages lay like handfuls of pale counters; and beyond them
the low blue ridges rose one behind another, each fainter
than the last, until earth and sky seemed to have
entered into a silent agreement not to say where one
ended and the other began.

Into this peace came the Bredes.

They arrived on a Tuesday afternoon in a glossy hired
carriage, with two trunks, a dressing case, a golf bag,
a square leather box, and a parasol wrapped with remarkable
care in tissue paper. Mr. Brede was a handsome fellow
of thirty, perhaps thirty-five—though Amelia said twenty-eight—with clear eyes, a
brown mustache, and a manner that made every woman feel
he had remembered her name when in fact he had
not heard it. Mrs. Brede was small, fair, and charmingly
serious. She wore gray traveling clothes and looked at her
husband before answering even the simplest question, as if his
opinion might affect not only the answer but the question
itself.

"They are delightful," Amelia said that evening.

"They are," I agreed.

"And devoted."

"Obviously."

"Not offensively devoted."

"Not yet."

Amelia looked at me. "What do you mean by that?"

"Nothing whatever."

"You always mean something when you say nothing whatever."

This is one of those matrimonial deductions against which logic
is powerless.

The first discrepancy occurred at breakfast. Mrs. Brede sat beside
Amelia, while I sat across from Mr. Brede. Little Mrs.
Hoogencamp, who had six children and regarded motherhood as a
competitive profession, was questioning Mrs. Brede.

"You have children, of course?"

Mrs. Brede hesitated and glanced at her husband.

"Three," she said.

At precisely that moment Major Halkit, at the other end
of the table, asked Mr. Brede whether he intended to
educate his boys for business.

"My boys?" said Brede. "Oh, I have no children."

There was a brief silence, during which Jacobus dropped a
spoon.

Then Mrs. Brede laughed.

"Edward means no children with us," she explained. "They are
with his mother."

"Her mother," said Brede quickly.

"With my mother," said Mrs. Brede.

"In Newark," he added.

"In Albany," she said.

They looked at each other.

"My mother is in Newark," Mrs. Brede continued, coloring. "Edward's
mother is in Albany. The children have been divided."

"Divided!" cried Mrs. Hoogencamp.

"For the summer," said Brede. "Not permanently."

Everyone laughed, and the matter might have ended there had
Mrs. Hoogencamp not asked their ages.

"Six, four, and two," said Mrs. Brede.

"Seven, five, and—" Brede began.

"The birthdays are all in September," she said rapidly.

It was August.

I confess that I noticed this. I further confess that
I said nothing until Amelia and I had reached our
room.

"Well?" she demanded.

"Well what?"

"The children."

"What about them?"

"There aren't any."

"My dear Amelia, three children do not cease to exist
merely because their parents are uncertain of their ages. If
that were the rule, half the younger generation would disappear
before luncheon."

"She said six, four, and two."

"He said seven and five."

"He stopped before the third."

"Perhaps he had forgotten it."

"A father does not forget the age of his child."

I regarded her mildly.

"You forgot Ruth's age last Christmas."

"Ruth is my niece."

"You sent her a doll."

"She is twelve."

"She is seventeen."

Amelia turned away. "That is entirely different."

It generally is.

---

The Bredes soon became the most popular people in the
house. Brede played tennis with Miss Biggle, listened to Major
Halkit's explanation of copper shares, and permitted Mrs. Hoogencamp to
tell him how children ought to be raised. Mrs. Brede
embroidered, sang French songs in a small, agreeable voice, and
gave the impression of having lived everywhere and met everybody
without having been spoiled by either experience.

Yet their history would not remain still.

On Wednesday Brede told the Major that he had been
born in Boston. On Thursday Mrs. Brede remarked to Amelia
that her husband had never visited Boston. He had, she
said, been born in Baltimore, in the same house where
his grandfather, Judge Brede, had died.

"Judge Brede?" asked Miss Biggle, who read society columns as
theologians read Scripture. "Wasn't he the Philadelphia Brede?"

"No," said Mrs. Brede. "The Philadelphia Brede was Edward's uncle."

"My father," said Brede, who had approached unheard.

"Your father's brother," she corrected.

"My father had no brother."

"Then it must have been your mother's."

"My mother was an only child."

Mrs. Brede bent closely over her embroidery.

"Families are so confusing," she murmured.

This observation is sound, and should have protected them. It
did not.

Their home, according to Brede, was at 41 West Seventy-Sixth
Street. According to his wife, it was at 14 East
Seventy-Sixth Street. He said they had occupied it five years;
she said they had taken it in May. She described
a red drawing room; he remembered it as blue. Their
cook was at once Swedish, Irish, and absent. Their house
had a garden until Major Halkit pointed out that neither
address allowed room for one, whereupon it became a roof
garden and subsequently a window box.

Nor could they agree upon their travels. Mrs. Brede had
seen the Matterhorn at sunrise. Mr. Brede had never been
in Switzerland. When reminded of his wife's experience, he explained
that she had viewed the mountain from Italy.

"You cannot see the Matterhorn from Italy," said Miss Biggle.

"Certainly you can," Brede replied.

"Have you?"

"Not exactly."

"Then how do you know?"

"I have seen photographs."

"Taken from Italy?"

Brede gazed at her with the expression of a man
who perceives that civilization has become too complicated to maintain.

"Possibly they were taken in New Jersey," he said.

Even Amelia began to be troubled.

"They tell lies," she whispered as we walked after supper.

"They tell very poor lies," I said. "That is in
their favor. A practiced deceiver would at least keep his
mountains in the same country."

"But why lie at all?"

There lay the question, small, hard, and indigestible, like the
solitary lump of sugar Mrs. Hoogencamp put into her tea.

The sugar itself later became evidence.

On Friday morning Mrs. Brede took two lumps. Brede, watching
her, said, "But, Clara, you never take sugar."

"Always," she replied.

"At home you drink it plain."

"At home we have coffee."

"You told me yesterday you disliked coffee."

"I dislike our coffee."

"Then why do we have it?"

Mrs. Brede placed both lumps in her cup with tragic
deliberation.

Mrs. Hoogencamp looked around the table. Miss Biggle raised her
eyebrows. Major Halkit ceased describing a gold mine. Even Jacobus
stood motionless with the coffeepot.

Afterward, on the veranda, Mrs. Hoogencamp said what everyone had
begun to think.

"They are not husband and wife."

The sentence passed through the house with the quiet rapidity
of a mouse behind a wall. No one could say
who had first repeated it, though everyone could say from
whom he had heard it. By noon the Bredes were
suspected; by dinner they were condemned; by bedtime their entire
careers had been reconstructed.

Miss Biggle believed Mrs. Brede an heiress lured from her
family.

Major Halkit believed Brede a bankrupt broker.

Mrs. Hoogencamp believed the worst, but declined to specify it
in the presence of unmarried women, thereby ensuring that the
unmarried women imagined several things worse than anything she could
have specified.

Old Mr. Tibb, who was deaf, understood that Brede had
three wives in Albany.

"And where are the children?" he shouted.

No one knew.

"That proves it!" said Mr. Tibb.

I do not pretend that Amelia and I remained above
this tide. We floated on it with the rest, though
perhaps we waved our arms more apologetically.

"There is one simple test," Amelia said. "A genuine married
couple knows where the marriage license is."

I laughed.

"Do you know where ours is?"

"In the black tin box."

"There is no black tin box."

"Then it is in the escritoire."

"We sold the escritoire."

"We did not sell it. We gave it to Cousin
Harriet."

"Then Cousin Harriet has our marriage license."

Amelia became thoughtful.

As a matter of fact, I have never understood why
the legal proof of a union intended to last forever
should be entrusted to a sheet of paper that cannot
be found after six months. Deeds go into safes, bonds
into banks, and receipts for plumbing are preserved with an
almost religious care; but the marriage license wanders from drawer
to drawer, passing through old trunks and envelopes marked "Important,"
until some descendant uses it to wrap collar buttons. Yet
we continue to believe that happiness, respectability, and the legitimacy
of children depend upon our ability to produce it before
breakfast.

"I could find ours if I had to," Amelia said.

"So, doubtless, could Mrs. Brede."

"She could if she were Mrs. Brede."

That "if" followed us downstairs.

---

The following afternoon the Bredes walked with us to Eagle
Ledge. It was a warm, luminous day. Clouds moved slowly
across the valley, trailing islands of shadow over farms and
woods, while the higher slopes remained in full sunlight. From
the ledge the boarding house itself appeared no larger than
a child's toy, its many windows reduced to bright points,
its veranda to a pale line.

For some minutes no one spoke.

The silence up there was not emptiness but fullness—a vast,
suspended attention. One heard the faint wind in the dwarf
pines, the click of an insect among stones, and from
immeasurably below the barking of a dog, softened until it
seemed no more than the memory of sound.

Brede stood with his hands behind him.

"Odd," he said, "how complete the world looks from one
place."

"And how little of it we see," Mrs. Brede added.

He pointed toward the nearest ridge.

"We see this side green in the sun. On the
other side there may be a storm, a burned forest,
a town, a circus—anything. Yet because we cannot see it,
we imagine it is much like this."

Mrs. Brede gave him a quick look.

"Or much worse," Amelia said.

Brede smiled. "That too."

There was nothing suspicious in the remark. Nevertheless, when a
man has become suspicious, his most innocent observations acquire the
dignity of confession.

On the return walk, Brede told me that he had
studied law.

At supper, Mrs. Brede said he was an engineer.

That night Major Halkit assembled what he called "the gentlemen"
in the smoking room. The gentlemen consisted of the Major,
Mr. Tibb, myself, young Hoogencamp—who was sixteen and had entered
by the window—and Jacobus with his hammer.

"This cannot continue," said the Major. "There are ladies under
this roof."

"There have always been ladies under this roof," said Jacobus.

"That is precisely my point."

"Don't see it," said Jacobus.

The Major lowered his voice. "These people may not be
married."

Jacobus scratched his beard with the hammer handle.

"Ain't they?"

"We have reason to doubt it."

"What reason?"

The Major presented the children, the addresses, Boston, Baltimore, Switzerland,
the sugar, the red-and-blue drawing room, and the contradictory professions.
Jacobus listened with increasing discomfort.

"Maybe he's two men," suggested young Hoogencamp.

His father ordered him out. He went by the door,
which showed that the meeting had sobered him.

"You keep a respectable house," the Major continued. "You owe
it to your guests to make inquiries."

"Me?" said Jacobus.

"You are the proprietor."

"I ain't never asked folks was they married. They says
they is, I puts 'em in a double room. They
says they ain't, I puts 'em separate. That's business."

"It is now a moral question."

Jacobus looked genuinely alarmed. Business he understood; morality, when brought
into direct contact with paying guests, was another matter.

"What d'you want me to do?"

"Ask for proof."

"Proof o' what?"

"Their marriage."

Jacobus laid the hammer on the table.

"Major, if I ask every married pair in this house
to prove it, there won't be no breakfast till noon."

Nevertheless, public sentiment was too strong. Jacobus agreed to speak
with Brede in the morning.

I slept badly.

---

Breakfast was a constrained meal. Conversation began repeatedly and expired.
Spoons struck cups with unnatural violence. Mrs. Brede, unaware or
pretending to be unaware, wore a white dress with small
blue flowers. Brede appeared cheerful, but he forgot to put
sugar in his coffee, which Mrs. Hoogencamp plainly regarded as
another lie.

When the meal ended, Jacobus approached him.

"Mister Brede, could I speak to ye private?"

"Certainly."

They went into the office. The door did not close
properly, and although no one intentionally listened, twelve persons found
reasons to remain in the hall.

Jacobus coughed.

"Mr. Brede, this here's a respectable house."

"So I have observed."

"An' I ain't sayin' nothin' ag'in you nor the lady."

"I am glad to hear it."

"But folks is talkin'."

"Folks frequently are."

"They're sayin'—well, they're sayin' mebbe you an' her ain't exactly—"

"Aren't exactly what?"

"Hitched."

There was a pause.

"You mean married?"

"That's the word."

Brede's voice changed.

"And what do you say?"

"I don't say nothin'. Only, seein' how folks is stirred
up, if you got a license, or a letter from
a preacher, or somethin'—"

"A letter from a preacher!"

"Not necessary a long letter."

The office door opened. Brede came out, white with anger.
Mrs. Brede rose from the veranda chair where she had
been waiting.

"Clara," he said, "we are leaving."

Her face lost its color. "Leaving?"

"At once."

He turned to the assembly.

"I do not know which of you has chosen to
make our private affairs the entertainment of this house. Mr.
Jacobus has asked me to prove that my wife is
my wife. I shall not do so. We will remove
ourselves from the society of persons who require documentary evidence
before offering ordinary civility."

No one answered.

Mrs. Hoogencamp looked at the floor. The Major inspected the
ceiling. Miss Biggle adjusted a bracelet that had not moved.

I stepped forward.

"My dear Brede—"

"You too?" he asked.

There are short questions into which a man may put
an entire indictment. I felt myself convicted.

"I am afraid," I said, "that I too wondered."

Amelia came beside me. "We are very sorry."

Mrs. Brede looked from face to face. Her lips trembled.

"We did not mean—" she began.

Brede took her arm. "You need explain nothing."

Their trunks were brought down. The hired carriage was summoned
from the village. Jacobus protested that there was no need
for haste; Brede replied that there was every need. Mrs.
Brede went upstairs and returned carrying the carefully wrapped parasol.

By then all the guests had gathered on the lawn,
drawn by remorse, curiosity, or that strange social instinct which
makes us attend the departures of persons we have driven
away.

The carriage came rattling up the road.

Brede helped his wife toward it. She was crying quietly
now—not with theatrical sobs, but with the helpless tears of
one who has been frightened and humiliated beyond the power
of concealment.

The sun was strong. At the carriage step she tore
the tissue from her parasol and pressed the catch.

The parasol sprang open.

From its folds there poured a white, pattering shower.

Rice bounced on the carriage seat, scattered over the drive,
lodged in Brede's hair, rolled beneath Jacobus's boots, and lay
shining in the grass. For a moment we stared at
it as though it were snow in August.

Mrs. Brede gave a little cry.

Then she covered her face.

"Oh, Edward! We should have told them!"

Brede looked at the rice. His anger collapsed with such
completeness that he seemed suddenly very young.

Mrs. Brede turned to us.

"We were married last Monday."

"Last Monday!" cried Amelia.

"At noon," Mrs. Brede sobbed. "And everybody at the station
knew. They tied labels to our trunks and rang bells
and sent telegrams to the hotels. Edward said if we
came here as bride and groom, people would stare and
laugh and put flowers at our places and ask how
long we had known each other."

"They would have," said Miss Biggle faintly.

"So we said we had been married for years," Mrs.
Brede continued. "Then Mrs. Hoogencamp asked about children, and I
thought married people ought to have them—"

"Three?" demanded Mrs. Hoogencamp.

"I was nervous."

"And I said none," Brede confessed, "because I did not
hear Clara's answer."

"The houses?" asked the Major.

"We have no house," said Brede. "We are to look
for one in September."

"Boston and Baltimore?" asked Miss Biggle.

"I was born in Syracuse," he said.

"My grandfather was born in Baltimore," said Mrs. Brede. "Or
perhaps it was Boston."

"The Matterhorn?"

"We saw it in a lecture," they said together.

At this, Amelia began to laugh. I joined her. Miss
Biggle followed, then the Major, then Mrs. Hoogencamp, though she
struggled against it on moral grounds. Even old Mr. Tibb
laughed after asking what had happened.

Jacobus pushed through us and seized Brede's hand.

"You ain't goin' nowheres," he declared. "Not till you've had
your vacation. An' I'll tell ye what—anybody asks me for
your license, I'll show 'em my hammer."

Mrs. Brede laughed through her tears.

"But we have lied dreadfully."

"Not dreadful," said Amelia. "Only incompetently."

This distinction comforted everyone.

The trunks were carried back. The carriage was dismissed. Jacobus
ordered beer and lemonade to be brought onto the lawn,
and Mrs. Hoogencamp, who had done more than anyone to
exile the Bredes, now took charge of welcoming them home.
By luncheon she had arranged flowers at their plates, tied
white ribbon to their chairs, and persuaded old Mr. Tibb
that they had been married three times in Syracuse.

As for the license, Brede produced it that evening—not because
anyone asked, but because he had discovered it in the
lining of his hat. How it came there he could
not explain. I refrained from comment. A man whose own
certificate may be in Cousin Harriet's escritoire should not be
severe.

At sunset the four of us walked again to Eagle
Ledge.

Behind us the boarding house windows shone warmly through the
trees. Below, the valley lay open and tranquil; yet beyond
the nearest ridge a bank of clouds had gathered, dark
with rain. Brede pointed to it.

"There," he said. "The other side."

Amelia slipped her arm through Mrs. Brede's.

"And we made it worse than it was."

Mrs. Brede looked at her husband.

"We did give you some assistance."

"That," I said, "is usually how judgment works. One person
supplies a foolish fact, another supplies a foolish conclusion, and
the community supplies refreshments afterward."

When we returned, the last of the wedding rice still
whitened the drive. Pigeons had descended from the barn roof
and were pecking at it industriously, caring nothing for Boston,
Baltimore, three imaginary children, two incompatible street numbers, or the
sacred distinction between sugar and no sugar.

Jacobus sat on the veranda with his hammer across his
knees. The Major was explaining investments to Brede; Mrs. Hoogencamp
was advising the bride; Miss Biggle had begun a French
song she did not quite know. Lamps were lit, glasses
touched, and laughter went out over the darkening lawn.

We had seen only one side of the ridge, of
course.

Fortunately, there was beer on the other.

## The Ball at Bézina's

Bobint did not care for dancing. He was too large
in the shoulders, too deliberate in the feet, and too
conscious of both deficiencies when fiddles began to scrape. Yet
on the evening of Bézina's ball he blacked his boots,
tied a blue handkerchief at his throat, and rode six
miles over the prairie with the gravity of a man
going to confession.

Calixta would be there.

That should have been reason enough for going. It was
also reason enough for staying away. When Calixta laughed with
a man, she leaned toward him as if the laughter
belonged to the two of them alone; and when she
danced, the red ribbons in her black hair and the
little gold pendants at her ears appeared to dance more
wickedly than she did. Bobint had endured these things before.
He could endure them again. But Alcée Laballière might come.

Why should Alcée come to a country ball? He belonged
to the broad galleries and silver coffee services of Laballière;
he owned fields where a man could ride half a
morning without reaching the end of the rice. He danced
in New Orleans beneath chandeliers, with ladies who wore gloves
past the elbow. Yet wealth had never prevented a Laballière
from seeking amusement wherever it happened to be warmest. And
Bobint had not forgotten Assumption.

Nobody had forgotten Assumption.

The women still lowered their voices when they spoke of
that afternoon, though what had happened was known precisely by
no one. Alcée had ridden with Calixta toward the old
cypress grove. A storm had come. They had returned separately,
late, muddied, and silent. That was all. It was quite
enough.

So Bobint rode to Bézina's, telling himself that Alcée would
not be there.

At Laballière, meanwhile, no one imagined Alcée was thinking of
a ball.

Three days earlier a cyclone had crossed the lower fields.
It came roaring out of a green-black sky, flattening young
rice, twisting two oaks from the earth, carrying half the
roof of the western barn into the bayou. The rain
afterward had lain on the fields like tarnished metal. Men
went about in mud to their knees, speaking softly, as
people speak in the presence of death.

Alcée had worked beside them until his hands blistered. He
had cursed the wind, the water, the rice, and finally
himself. Before the storm he had counted upon a harvest
generous enough to pay old debts and furnish the east
rooms of the house. After it, there was scarcely a
harvest to count.

But it was not the rice alone that drove him
from the supper table that evening.

Clarisse sat opposite him, pale and composed in a dress
the color of cream. She was his cousin, though the
relation was sufficiently distant for the family to discuss other
possibilities whenever they saw the two together. Clarisse had lived
at Laballière since childhood. She knew which hound Alcée favored,
how he drank his coffee, what hymn he disliked at
Mass, and where to find the keys he was always
losing. She knew everything about him except, it seemed, the
thing he most desired her to know.

He had told her on the gallery at dusk.

The storm-broken trees made dark wounds against the sky. Water
dripped from the eaves. Clarisse stood beside a jasmine vine,
plucking away leaves that the wind had bruised.

"I love you," Alcée said.

She did not start. She did not blush. She merely
looked at the leaf between her fingers.

"You are tired, mon cousin."

"I know when I'm tired."

"And you are unhappy about the crop."

"I know that too."

"Then wait till you are yourself."

"This is myself, Clarisse."

She smiled, but it was the smile she gave a
child who had asked to hold a candle. "No. This
is a man with mud on his boots and ruin
in his head."

He seized her wrist. Not roughly; nevertheless she drew herself
upright, all whiteness and offended dignity. She could be a
lily when she wished, cold enough to grow in moonlight.

"Let me go, Alcée."

He let her go.

At supper she spoke to his mother about preserves. Alcée
watched her lift a spoonful of fig conserve to her
plate. The small sound of silver against china seemed unbearable.
Before coffee he rose.

"You not going out again, hein?" his mother asked.

"For a ride."

"In the dark?"

"The horse knows the road."

Clarisse did not look up. That settled it. If the
house had fallen upon them at that instant, she would
have asked whether the china was damaged.

In the stable yard Alcée met Bruce, an old groom
who had served his father and considered the Laballière men
a troublesome inheritance.

"Where you gwine, M'sieur Alcée?"

"Anywhere the road goes."

"Road go plenty places. Don't fetch a man back from
all of 'em."

Alcée laughed and swung into the saddle.

"Tell them I rode toward town."

But beyond the gate he turned south, toward Bézina's.

The ball had already shaken Bézina's house from gallery to
kitchen when Bobint arrived. Horses crowded the fence. Lanterns hung
under the oaks, making gold puddles in the dust; children
darted between skirts; old men smoked and discussed cane prices
while their feet marked the fiddle's rhythm in spite of
them. From the kitchen came the rich smell of chicken
gumbo, coffee, onions, and filé. Inside, the dancers moved through
heat made visible by lamp smoke and powdered dust.

Bobint paused at the door and saw Calixta at once.

She wore white muslin, too simple a dress for such
an effect. A scarlet sash wound twice about her waist.
Her hair, black and abundant, had been forced upward with
combs, but curls escaped at her temples and neck. Her
blue eyes—too blue for that dark head, people always said—were
half closed with laughter.

She was dancing with Frédéric Savoie.

Frédéric said something. Calixta threw back her head. The gold
drops at her ears flashed.

Bobint's heart descended heavily into his boots.

"Tiens, Bobint!" called a woman near the wall. "You come
dance at last?"

"I come to see."

"To see Calixta, yes!"

Everyone laughed, including Bobint, because denial would have made it
worse. He stationed himself near a window, where he could
breathe and suffer privately.

Calixta had known for two years that he wished to
marry her. He had asked first after Easter Mass, while
she pinned her veil under her chin. She told him
she could not think with the church bells ringing. He
asked again at a fais-dodo in August. She said it
was too hot to decide anything for life. At Christmas
she kissed his cheek beneath a sprig of mistletoe, then
informed him a kiss was not a promise.

Still he waited.

A man could wait for rain. He could wait for
rice to sprout, for a heifer to calve, for a
fever to break. Why not for Calixta? But waiting for
natural things did not generally require a man to watch
them waltz with Frédéric Savoie.

The music stopped. Calixta caught sight of Bobint.

"Ah, there he is! Bonsoir, Bobint. You bring me something
good?"

He had, in fact, brought pralines wrapped in paper. They
were in his coat pocket, becoming soft.

"I bring myself."

"Then keep yourself cool by the window," she said, sweeping
past him.

That was Calixta.

Before Bobint could decide whether her answer had been playful
or cruel, hoofbeats sounded outside. A murmur ran through the
gallery and entered the room ahead of the newcomer.

Alcée Laballière stood in the doorway.

He had ridden fast. His dark hair was damp at
the forehead, and his coat hung open over a fine
shirt marked with mud at one cuff. He brought with
him the night air, the smell of horse, and the
easy assurance of a man accustomed to finding a welcome
whether or not one had been prepared.

"Mais, look who come!" cried Bézina.

Alcée bowed to the elders, shook hands with the men,
and accepted a cup of coffee he did not want.
Then his gaze passed over the room and found Calixta.

Bobint saw the finding.

It was nothing more than a look, but some looks
have hands in them. Calixta became still. The teasing curve
of her mouth vanished, returned, and deepened. She lifted one
shoulder in greeting.

"So, M'sieur Laballière," she said when he reached her. "The
grand people let you escape?"

"The grand people never knew they held me."

"You come to dance with country girls?"

"With one, perhaps."

Her eyes narrowed pleasantly. "Perhaps she don't dance with you."

"She danced with me in Assumption."

The words fell between them, soft as ash.

Calixta glanced toward the matrons. "You remember too much."

"I've been trying to remember something agreeable."

"And you find only me? Pauvre Alcée!"

He smiled then, for the first time since the cyclone.
The ruined fields, Clarisse's cool wrist beneath his fingers, his
mother's anxious face—all receded. The fiddle began a quadrille.

"Dance," he said.

Calixta gave him her hand.

Bobint remained near the window. The pralines melted.

Alcée danced as he rode, lightly and as if direction
were his private invention. Calixta followed no one, yet with
him her movements seemed not submission but conspiracy. They crossed,
turned, separated, met again. Her skirt brushed his boots. His
hand found her waist for a moment longer than the
figure required.

People noticed. People always noticed most when pretending not to
see.

"Dat gal got fire under her foot," whispered a cook
from the kitchen doorway.

"Fire don't stay under foot," another answered. "It climb."

After the quadrille came a waltz. Alcée and Calixta did
not leave the floor.

"You look tired," she told him.

"I'm ruined."

"Ruined men don't wear boots like those."

"My rice is lying under water."

She studied his face, and the mockery went out of
hers. "The cyclone?"

"The cyclone. Half the crop gone, maybe more."

"Then why you laughing?"

"Because you are here."

"Ah, you always talk pretty when you want something."

"What do I want?"

She looked away. "You tell me."

The room turned around them, lantern light and white sleeves,
dark heads and red mouths. Alcée felt her breath near
his cheek. Calixta's fingers rested in his hand with surprising
softness. He remembered those fingers wet with rain in Assumption,
clutching his sleeve beneath the cypress trees. He remembered her
saying, Go away—then holding him when he moved.

"You've changed," he murmured.

"I got a new dress."

"Not the dress."

"You mean I got respectable?"

"Have you?"

She laughed low. "Ask Bobint."

"Bobint!"

The name amused him more than it should have. He
looked toward the window. Bobint's broad, patient face was turned
away.

"He asks me to marry him," Calixta said.

"And you?"

"I ask him why he want all that trouble."

"Why does he?"

"Because he's foolish."

Alcée bent nearer. "Then he isn't alone."

His lips almost touched the shell of her ear. One
gold drop trembled there, tapping her neck. He touched it
with a fingertip.

Calixta's breath caught.

"Don't," she said.

He touched it again.

"Alcée!"

But her reproach was no more substantial than steam above
gumbo. Her eyes were drowsy now, darker at their centers.
The waltz ended. Neither moved until another couple collided with
them, laughing.

They went out to the side gallery, where the air
was cooler and the noise came through the walls in
rhythmic bursts. Beyond the railing, the prairie lay flat beneath
the moon. Mosquitoes whined among the vines.

Calixta leaned against a post. "You shouldn't have come."

"No."

"Why you come?"

"To forget."

"Forget your rice?"

"And other things."

Her face sharpened. "Some lady?"

"You're jealous."

"I don't care enough."

"That sounds like jealousy."

"It sounds like truth."

She turned away, but he caught the end of her
sash and wound the red silk once around his hand.
The gesture drew her backward until her shoulder nearly touched
his chest.

"Let go."

"You said that in Assumption."

"I said plenty things."

"And meant none?"

She turned suddenly. They were very close. Moonlight silvered her
cheek but left her mouth in shadow.

"Maybe I meant all."

From inside came a shout for partners. On the front
gallery a baby began crying. The ordinary world persisted with
vulgar determination, yet Alcée and Calixta stood inside that narrow
red tether as though the night had tied them there.

Then someone called his name.

Not loudly. Still he released the sash at once.

Clarisse stood at the end of the gallery.

She had ridden without changing her cream-colored dress. Its hem
was dark with dew; her hair, usually smooth, had loosened
and streamed over one shoulder. Against the moonlit yard she
looked first like a pale flower, then like a tall
reed bent by wind, and finally like no delicate thing
at all, for her eyes were bright with command.

"Alcée."

Calixta straightened. Alcée stared.

"What happened?"

"You must come home."

"My mother?"

"She is well."

"The house?"

"It stands."

"Then what—"

"You must come."

Her voice shook. That astonished him more than her presence.
Clarisse's voice never shook. It could be soft, cool, amused,
but never uncertain.

Calixta folded her arms. "Mademoiselle rode far to fetch you."

Clarisse looked at her only then. One glance took in
the scarlet sash, the loosened curls, Alcée's hand still half
lifted between them.

"Yes," she said. "I rode far."

Alcée's face changed. Calixta saw it and hated him immediately.
A moment before his whole being had leaned toward her;
now some invisible cord drew him across the gallery toward
Clarisse.

"Where is your horse?" he asked.

"At the gate."

"You came alone?"

"Bruce followed partway. I sent him back."

"You had no business riding alone at night."

"You had no business leaving."

It was almost a quarrel, yet tenderness moved beneath every
word, unmistakable and humiliating.

Calixta laughed. "Go, then, M'sieur Laballière. The grand people found
out you escaped."

He looked back at her. "Calixta—"

"Go!"

She snapped the word like a fan shutting. Alcée hesitated
only an instant. Then he followed Clarisse around the gallery.

Calixta remained beside the post. In the yard she heard
bridles ring, Clarisse's low answer to some question, Alcée's voice
giving an order. Two horses moved off at a walk.
A third followed riderless, led by Alcée.

The fiddles began again.

Calixta entered the room with her head high and danced
with the first man who asked. She danced badly. Her
steps were too quick, her laughter too loud. During the
next figure she trod upon her partner. Afterward she scolded
him for putting his foot where hers wished to go.

Near midnight the room grew hotter. Babies slept on shawls
beneath benches. Old women drank coffee and watched the young
commit errors that appeared new only to the young. Calixta
danced until her scarlet sash sagged and damp curls clung
to her forehead. She would not look at the window.

Bobint had gone from it.

He was outside, sitting on an overturned barrel behind the
kitchen. He had eaten two of the pralines because they
could no longer be presented respectably. The rest stuck together
in his pocket like brown mortar.

Bézina's little boy found him.

"Mamzelle Calixta say you hitch her horse."

"Now?"

"She say right now, if you please. But she don't
say please."

Bobint rose.

Calixta waited beneath an oak, fanning herself with her hand.
The moon was lower, yellow and swollen. Her face looked
exhausted.

"You leaving early," Bobint said.

"It's near morning."

"For you that's early."

"Don't begin."

He did not know what he was forbidden to begin,
so he brought her mare and tightened the girth. Calixta
watched his large hands work. Those hands had mended her
father's fence after the flood. They had carried her little
brother home with a broken ankle. They would never touch
an earring merely to make her tremble.

"Bobint."

"Yes?"

"You still want marry me?"

He stopped with one hand under the leather strap.

"What you say?"

"You hear."

He stared at her. She appeared impatient, almost angry, as
though he had forced the question from her.

"You making fun?"

"No."

"Calixta, I ask you three time."

"You counting? Then this make four."

"I ain't ask tonight."

"I'm asking for you."

From the house came the stamping beat of a final
dance. Bobint could scarcely breathe. He had imagined her consent
in a hundred forms: shy, laughing, tearful, whispered after Mass.
Never like this, beneath an oak while she looked down
the road another man had taken.

"You love me?" he asked.

She pressed her lips together.

There it was—the one question he should not have asked.
Even Bobint knew it when it was too late.

Calixta glanced toward the paling east. "I like you. You
good. You don't run off when somebody call."

It was not love. But it was Calixta, offering what
she had at that hour.

Bobint picked up her hand. Her fingers lay limp in
his palm, then closed a little.

"I'll take care of you," he said.

"You better."

"And you won't be sorry?"

"I'm sorry already about plenty. One more don't frighten me."

He smiled because he could not help it. His happiness
was too large to distinguish jest from warning. He took
the ruined pralines from his pocket.

"I bring you these."

She opened the paper and stared. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a
tired, genuine laugh.

"They look terrible."

"They got warm."

"You kept them all night?"

"I ate two."

She chose the least damaged and bit into it. Sugar
stuck to her thumb. Bobint watched as though she had
accepted a ring.

Before mounting, Calixta leaned down and kissed his cheek. Her
lips were cool now.

"Tell them," she said. "Tell everybody, if you want."

Bobint wanted.

By the time the last lantern was extinguished, half the
parish knew Calixta had agreed to marry him. Bobint rode
beside her through the dawn with his shoulders squared and
his heart beating like dance music. The prairie shone wet
and gray; larks sprang from the ditches; far off, the
church bell called early worshippers. He spoke of a house,
a cow, curtains she might choose. Calixta listened with her
face turned toward the mist.

Sometimes she answered.

. . . . . . .

Clarisse and Alcée rode in silence until Bézina's music had
faded behind them.

The road crossed open prairie, then entered a stretch of
woods where branches knitted overhead. Moonlight dropped in broken pieces
upon the horses. Wet grass brushed Clarisse's skirt. Alcée kept
near enough to seize her bridle, though she rode perfectly
well.

"What is wrong?" he demanded again.

"Nothing."

"You come six miles at midnight for nothing?"

"I wanted you home."

"Why?"

Clarisse urged her mare forward. Alcée caught up.

"My mother sent you?"

"No."

"You were afraid of the road?"

"I was afraid before I took it."

"Of what?"

She made no answer.

His anger returned, sharpened by bewilderment. "You refuse me, then
follow me to a ball. You order me home and
won't tell me why. Clarisse, I'm not one of the
children."

"No," she said. "Children are easier."

They emerged from the trees onto a narrow bridge. Beneath
it water moved blackly among reeds. Clarisse stopped her mare
in the middle.

Alcée stopped too.

The eastern sky had begun to whiten. In that uncertain
light Clarisse no longer resembled the composed young woman at
the supper table. Her cream dress was stained; a scratch
marked one wrist; strands of hair lay across her mouth.
She looked wild, and very dear.

"I saw you leave," she said.

"You would not look at me."

"I saw."

"And?"

"I waited for you to return."

He gave a short laugh. "You made that likely."

"Then Bruce said you had not gone toward town. He
heard about the ball. He said—" She stopped.

"What did Bruce say?"

"That a road does not fetch a man back from
every place."

Despite himself, Alcée smiled. "Bruce considers himself a philosopher."

"I hated him for saying it."

A bird called once from the marsh.

Clarisse dismounted so abruptly that Alcée did the same. She
went to the bridge rail, hiding her face against the
sleeve of her riding arm. He approached but did not
touch her. After the coldness of the gallery at Laballière,
he could not trust this new, trembling creature.

"Clarisse."

"I thought of you there," she said into her sleeve.
"With music and lights—and women who would be glad you
came."

"One woman, perhaps."

She flinched.

He regretted the cruelty as soon as it was spoken,
but some masculine remnant of misery had wished to see
whether she could be wounded.

Clarisse turned. "That Calixta?"

"You saw her."

"Everyone knows about Assumption."

"No one knows anything."

"I know enough."

"What do you know?"

"That I could not stay in the house. That I
could not let you be there with her. That I
would have ridden through another cyclone rather than—"

Her voice broke.

Alcée caught both her hands. "Rather than what?"

She tried to pull away, but he held her.

"Say it."

"You know."

"I knew nothing two hours ago."

"I was wrong."

"About what?"

"About you being tired. About the crop. About everything."

Dawn moved into the woods, touching the upper leaves, then
the reeds, then Clarisse's face. Her eyes were wet and
furious.

"I love you," she said. "There. Are you satisfied?"

Satisfied!

The word was too small, absurd as measuring a flood
with a coffee spoon. Alcée drew her against him. For
one instant she resisted from habit; then all her tall,
proud slenderness yielded. She hid her face against his shoulder
as she had hidden it against her sleeve.

The ruined rice crop vanished.

It still lay drowned beneath muddy water. The barn roof
was still in the bayou. Debts remained debts, and no
declaration had ever coaxed a stalk of grain upright. Yet
Alcée, holding Clarisse while morning opened over the marsh, could
think of the cyclone almost tenderly. Had it not broken
the fields, he might never have spoken on the gallery.
Had she not refused him, he might never have ridden
away. Had he not ridden away, she might never have
followed.

Disaster had displayed excellent judgment.

He kissed her hair, her temple, the scratch upon her
wrist.

"And Calixta?" Clarisse asked at last.

"Calixta?"

She drew back enough to study him.

Already the name seemed to belong to music heard in
another parish, to a red sash glimpsed through smoke, to
an old rumor from Assumption. Alcée had desired Calixta intensely
less than an hour before. Now that desire had become
so insubstantial he could scarcely remember its shape.

"A foolishness," he said.

Clarisse should have objected to the answer. Instead she smiled.

They mounted and rode on slowly, their horses close. The
sun lifted red beyond the cane. At Laballière the wet
fields would soon begin to shine, and men would be
waiting for instructions. Alcée would give them grand ones. He
felt capable of commanding the water to retreat.

. . . . . . .

The ball ended with chairs overturned, lamps smoking, and sleepers
waking crossly beneath benches. Bézina counted spoons. The fiddler wrapped
his instrument in green cloth. News of two victories traveled
outward with the departing guests.

Bobint had won Calixta.

Clarisse had fetched Alcée home.

So people said.

Bobint believed it while Calixta rode beside him, wearing his
promise as she might wear a shawl accepted against the
morning chill. Alcée believed it while Clarisse's gloved hand rested
in his and the drowned rice flashed gold beneath the
rising sun.

Both men were happy.

And Calixta, between one road and another, ate the last
crushed praline from its paper and watched the mist lift
from the prairie.

Once there lay in the harbour of St. Orison a
fleet of twenty-seven vessels, all fully rigged, adequately provisioned, and
prepared, according to the declarations of their captains, to sail
at a moment's notice.

This moment, however, did not immediately occur.

At first the delay was caused by a contrary wind.
When the wind changed, the tide was judged unsuitable; when
the tide rose, clouds appeared in the west; and when
the clouds dispersed, it was remembered that the almanac predicted
rain before the end of the month. Therefore the fleet
remained at anchor, now in sunshine, now in mist, without
special damage and without special merit. The captains visited one
another, drank toddy, compared charts, and agreed that no prudent
mariner would exchange a safe harbour for a dangerous sea
merely because ships had been built for sailing.

Men are not so good as they ought to be,
but they are wonderfully adaptable. The crews, having at first
polished the brass, repaired the cordage, and watched the horizon
with expectation, gradually discovered quieter occupations. Some carved boxes, some
bred pigeons in the longboats, and some established a choir
on the forecastle. Decks became gardens of geraniums; signal lockers
became cupboards; and more than one gun, being useless for
defence, served admirably as a chimney for a cabin stove.

Nevertheless, one ship departed. Her captain was called rash, and
when she returned six months later with spices and torn
sails, it was generally observed that the torn sails proved
the wisdom of remaining at home. Then two more vessels
left, and after them five. Years passed. Masts vanished from
the harbour as trees vanish from a wood under an
industrious axe. Some ships were wrecked, some prospered, and some
were never heard of; but since those that prospered were
away and those that stayed could speak every evening at
the tavern, public opinion remained for a long time on
the side of immobility.

At last only one vessel was left.

She was a brig named the *Patient Virtue*, though the
younger sailors, with that want of reverence peculiar to youth,
called her the *Permanent Fixture*. She was broad in the
beam, short in the bows, and chubby as a cherub
painted upon a church ceiling. Her hull was old-fashioned, but
her decorations were not. She possessed a new figurehead representing
Progress, a lady in gilt boots who pointed boldly seaward,
though the brig herself pointed chiefly toward the customs house.
Painted gunports made her look formidable at a distance, and
an enormous quantity of rigging made her look industrious near
at hand.

Her skipper, Captain Ormont, was no common man. In early
life he had painted a picture of a lighthouse, and
he could sing the second verse of a naval hymn
without assistance. He had also crossed the harbour in a
rowing boat during a squall, an exploit which increased in
danger whenever he related it. His admirers maintained that he
had commanded the *Patient Virtue* for thirty-two years without losing
her. Persons unfavourable to him replied that he had never
taken her anywhere; but this was most probably a lie,
since no one can deny that a ship at anchor
may drag.

Captain Ormont's chief adviser was the steersman, Mr. Cramp. Cramp
had stood so long beside the tiller that he appeared
to have grown there. He distrusted compasses manufactured after his
fiftieth birthday and believed that steam was a vulgar vapour.
In discussion he commonly said "H'm," which was regarded as
profound; but, if pressed, he could discharge such volleys of
words as made ordinary men feel ashamed of knowing what
they meant.

The captain and the steersman lived comfortably in the after-cabin.
Every morning they examined the weather through a small window,
and every evening they played cards beneath a large chart
of the world. The chart had faded until the continents
resembled stains caused by damp, but Captain Ormont often pointed
to them with his pipe and spoke of global navigation.
There were two toddy kettles, one for ordinary deliberation and
one for emergencies. The second had never been used, and
its brightness was frequently cited as proof of vigilance.

The crew did not at first complain. They received their
rations, sang in the choir, and slept without being washed
from their hammocks. But a new generation had grown up
aboard the brig—lads who had entered as cabin-boys and become
able seamen without ever seeing the lighthouse from the other
side. These young men began to look beyond the breakwater.
They heard tales from foreign ships, and it seemed to
them that waves, though inconvenient, were not necessarily unconstitutional.

One morning a sailor named Noll climbed to the quarterdeck
and touched his cap.

"Captain," said he, "the wind is fair. The stores are
aboard, the water-casks are full, and there is open sea
beyond the mole. Why do we not sail?"

Captain Ormont stared at him. His face, ordinarily the colour
of pale leather, became first pink, then purple, then white.

"Sail?" he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Do you presume to instruct your captain in seamanship?"

"No, sir. I only ask why we remain here."

"There!" cried Captain Ormont, turning to Cramp. "You hear the
spirit of mutiny. First he asks why we remain; next
he will ask where we go; and presently he will
demand that the ship be navigated."

Cramp said, "H'm."

This so encouraged the captain that he continued.

"You juvenile agitators suppose motion to be progress. You mistake
rest for stagnation and danger for development. We have preserved
this vessel through gales, tides, rumours, and changes of ministry.
Other ships have sailed, and where are they?"

"Mostly in other ports," said Noll.

The captain trembled.

"Impertinence! They have deserted the harbour which sheltered them. But
we—we have maintained continuity."

"We have also grown barnacles nine inches long," said another
sailor from the main deck.

At this there was an ugly laugh among the crew.
Captain Ormont retired to the after-cabin, where he and Cramp
held a conference lasting until supper. During the conference the
ordinary toddy kettle was emptied twice, which showed how seriously
they regarded the crisis.

For several days nothing happened. Then rumours spread through the
brig. The cook had seen the captain oiling his telescope.
The carpenter had been ordered to hammer something, though none
knew what. A sailmaker was told to inspect a sail,
and the boatswain discovered that the anchor chain had not
become inseparably united with the bottom. Finally, the cabin-boy, Peep,
came running forward with round eyes.

"It is Initiative," he whispered.

"What is?"

"What they are getting ready in the cabin. I heard
the captain say we must have Initiative."

"Is it a weapon?" asked the cook.

"It may be. Mr. Cramp said it ought to be
handled with discretion."

The news passed from mouth to mouth.

"Initiative!" cried the sailors.

They pictured a new kind of compass, a patent sail,
or perhaps a cannon capable of firing round corners. The
word restored hope because nobody understood it, and hope is
often strongest under that condition.

On the following Sunday Captain Ormont appeared in full uniform.
His coat had been cut for him twenty years earlier
and had preserved its shape better than he had. Beside
him stood Cramp, clasping a roll of charts and wearing
an expression which suggested that the oceans had been arranged
without consulting him.

"Men," said the captain, "certain irresponsible elements have accused the
command of inertia. We reject the accusation. Nevertheless, responding not
to clamour but to the logic of events, we have
resolved upon a controlled maritime evolution."

"Are we sailing?" called Noll.

Captain Ormont frowned.

"If you insist on reducing a comprehensive policy to crude
language—yes."

A cheer went up so suddenly that pigeons flew from
the longboat, hymn books fell from the capstan, and the
cook dropped three onions into the harbour. The geraniums were
carried ashore; the stove pipes were removed from the guns;
sails were shaken out, stiff with disuse; and the capstan
went round to a tune which no one had sung
for years. Even the brig seemed in a good humour.
She creaked from stem to stern as the anchors came
home, and raised more foam than was strictly necessary when
a tug pulled her clear of the quay.

Beyond the mole the wind caught her canvas. The *Patient
Virtue* leaned, hesitated, and then moved forward.

The effect upon the crew was extraordinary. Old men straightened
their backs. Young men ran aloft. The painted lady at
the bow pointed seaward with new credibility, and the false
gunports appeared almost capable of firing. Water hurried beneath the
counter; gulls wheeled astern; and the harbour, which had so
long seemed the whole world, became a collection of roofs
and chimneys behind them.

Captain Ormont stood upon the quarterdeck with one hand on
his telescope and the other gripping the rail. He had
intended to maintain this attitude until sunset, but the brig
had not been under canvas half an hour before a
gentle swell caused him to sit down. After another swell
he descended the companion with dignity, explaining that he must
consult documents below.

Cramp remained at the tiller. He steered by recollection, since
he considered the compass excitable. Whenever the brig wandered from
her course, he said the coast had shifted. Still, the
day was bright, the wind moderate, and the sea no
rougher than any sea having self-respect may reasonably be.

Toward afternoon a wave appeared on the port bow.

It was not a monstrous wave. It had not swallowed
fleets, drowned kingdoms, or been mentioned in Scripture. Yet it
was larger than the ripples of St. Orison harbour, and
it approached with a broad green shoulder, as if it
had lain down at a distance, taken aim, and now
meant to slap the *Patient Virtue* on the cheek.

The look-out shouted.

Cramp peered over the rail.

"H'm," he said, but this time the word lacked depth.

The wave struck. Foam flew over the forecastle, a bucket
rolled down the deck, and the brig shuddered with surprise.
Below, Captain Ormont fell from the sofa, together with the
chart of the world and the emergency toddy kettle.

"We have collided!" he cried.

"With what?" asked Peep, who was collecting cups.

"With the ocean, you fool!"

The captain rushed on deck without his hat. His face
passed rapidly through all its colours and settled upon green.

"Where are we?"

"Off the Black Tooth skerries," said Noll, after consulting the
new compass which Cramp refused to acknowledge.

"Rocks!" cried the captain. "As I predicted. Cramp, save the
ship!"

Mr. Cramp pulled the tiller one way, then the other,
and finally held it in the middle, which had the
advantage of appearing impartial.

"Drop anchor!" he shouted.

"We are in forty fathoms!" replied the boatswain.

"Then use more chain."

"But we are making good way past the skerries."

"Drop both anchors!" cried Captain Ormont. "I will not have
my authority undermined by speed."

The crew obeyed, for obedience, like sleep, returns readily to
those long accustomed to it. The best bower plunged, then
the second; chains roared through the hawseholes; sails cracked and
flapped; and the brig swung round so violently that the
cook's remaining onions escaped from a basket. When all was
done, the *Patient Virtue* lay pitching uneasily within sight of
black rocks, with two anchors down upon a foul bottom
and the wind pressing her toward the coast.

Any competent mariner would have called the position dangerous. Captain
Ormont called it stabilized.

That evening he summoned the crew. He wore his hat
again, and beside him stood Cramp, wrapped in an oilskin
and moral superiority.

"Men," said the captain, "you have witnessed the result of
agitation. Against our mature judgment, and solely to satisfy demands
for reckless innovation, this brig was exposed to the open
sea. We had scarcely commenced the experiment when we encountered
a violent convulsion of nature, followed by imminent collision and
geographic hostility."

"It was one wave," said Noll.

"There speaks the theorist!" cried Captain Ormont. "To the practical
seaman, one wave is sufficient. Must we be struck twice
before acknowledging the first?"

"The anchors put us nearer the rocks than we were."

"The anchors," replied the captain, "are ancient and honoured institutions.
If the rocks object to them, the responsibility rests with
the rocks."

There was some murmuring.

Cramp stepped forward. He had been saving his words all
day and now poured them out.

"The present conjuncture," he said, "is the inevitable culmination of
premature evolutionary propulsion, aggravated by hydrodynamic intransigence and the unseasonable
geographical proximity of non-negotiable coastal formations."

The crew became silent. Several men believed they had been
insulted; others feared a disease had broken out. Peep hid
behind the cook, supposing hydrodynamic intransigence to be the Initiative,
at last discharged.

Captain Ormont raised his hand.

"We disclaim responsibility. We warned you that the sea contained
water. We told you that progress might produce movement. Yet
you clamoured. We yielded. Behold the consequences."

"But you command the brig," Noll said.

"I command her when she is properly obedient. When she
is driven into experiments by juvenile agitators, command becomes advisory."

"And what do we do now?"

"We preserve unity."

This proved difficult. The older sailors blamed the younger for
disturbing a comfortable arrangement. The younger blamed the captain for
anchoring under the skerries. The cook blamed everybody because his
onions were gone. Those who had cheered loudest at departure
now declared that they had always doubted the sea; and
those who had doubted it claimed they would have managed
the voyage better. Hymn books were brought out, not for
singing but for throwing. Men disputed in the forecastle, at
the pumps, and in the rigging. Only Captain Ormont and
Mr. Cramp remained united, being secure in the knowledge that
each had agreed with the other.

Days passed. The wind freshened and the chains strained. Twice
Noll proposed weighing anchor and standing out to sea, but
the captain replied that further action would inflame divisions. Once
he proposed returning to harbour, but Cramp explained that retreat
would compromise continuity. Thus neither advance nor return was possible,
and the precise place which all admitted to be unsafe
became, by this reasoning, the only safe place to remain.

Barnacles gathered again. The sails rotted in their gear. The
painted figurehead lost first her gilt boots, then her outstretched
hand, though her face preserved an expression of official confidence.
New cabin-boys grew into old sailors and learned that the
Black Tooth skerries were unfortunate but unavoidable. On calm evenings
Captain Ormont lectured upon the historic voyage of the *Patient
Virtue*, dwelling especially upon the courage with which he had
arrested it. Cramp, still glued to the tiller, answered "H'm,"
and the word recovered its profundity.

Other ships sometimes passed in the offing. They signalled warnings
or offered towlines, but the brig replied that outside interference
would endanger her independence. In storms she dragged nearer the
rocks; in calms she swung upon her chains. No man
trusted his neighbour enough to raise one anchor, and no
officer would order it, lest a movement follow and responsibility
with it.

And therefore, between the harbour she abandoned and the sea
she never entered, with two anchors down, quarrels on deck,
and Progress pointing nowhere, there she lies to this day.

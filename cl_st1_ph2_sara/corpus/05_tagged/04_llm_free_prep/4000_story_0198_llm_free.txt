The Last Lighthouse Keeper

On the morning the sea disappeared, Elias Vale was polishing
the lighthouse lens.

He had climbed the iron stairs before dawn, carrying a
bucket of rainwater and three clean cloths. The storm lantern
at his belt swung with each step, scattering shadows around
the circular walls. Outside, the wind was quiet, but the
tower groaned in the old familiar way, as though remembering
storms.

At the top, Elias opened the brass door to the
lantern room and stopped.

Beyond the glass, where the sea should have stretched to
the horizon, there was only darkness.

Not night. Not fog. Darkness.

The lighthouse stood on a black cliff two hundred feet
above the shore. From the lantern room, Elias could usually
see white waves breaking against the rocks and, farther out,
fishing boats returning to the village with lamps trembling on
their masts. Now there were no waves, no boats, no
gray line where water met sky. It looked as if
someone had cut the ocean from the world and left
behind an empty space.

Elias set down his bucket.

He pressed his face to the cold glass. The darkness
below was perfectly still. Even the sound of the sea
had vanished.

For forty-two years, he had slept to the rhythm of
water. Its absence filled the tower like a scream.

He hurried down.

The village of Bellwether was already awake. People gathered along
the cliff road in nightclothes and heavy coats, staring toward
the vanished sea. Some shouted names into the emptiness. Others
stood with their hands over their mouths.

Mara Quinn, the baker, had brought her brass telescope. She
adjusted it with shaking fingers.

"What do you see?" Elias asked.

"Nothing."

"Fog?"

"Fog reflects light."

"Then what is it?"

Mara lowered the telescope. Her face had gone pale beneath
its dusting of flour.

"I think it's a hole."

By sunrise, the whole village had crowded onto the cliffs.

The sun rose behind them, gilding roofs, chimneys, and the
lighthouse tower. Its light reached the edge of the land
and stopped. The darkness did not brighten. It swallowed every
ray without reflection.

A stone mason named Perran tied a rope around a
boulder and threw the loose end over the cliff. They
listened as the rope hissed through his hands.

Fifty feet.

One hundred.

Two hundred.

At three hundred feet, the rope suddenly jerked sideways. Perran
cried out and released it. The entire length slid over
the edge, dragging sparks from the rocks before disappearing.

After that, no one wanted to go closer.

The mayor ordered the church bell rung and sent riders
inland for help. Elias returned to the lighthouse. He did
not know what else to do.

At noon, he lit the great lamp.

"You can't guide ships on a sea that isn't there,"
Mara said. She had followed him up the stairs, bringing
bread and a flask of coffee.

"The lamp is meant to be lit."

"It's daytime."

"The sea is dark."

Mara leaned against the curved glass. She had been Elias's
friend since childhood, and for nearly as long she had
considered him unreasonable.

"Your lamp won't fix this," she said.

"I'm not trying to fix it."

"What are you trying to do?"

Elias looked toward the emptiness.

"Let them know where the land is."

No one returned from the capital that day.

At sunset, the first voice came from below.

The villagers had gathered in the inn, speaking in frightened
whispers, when a boy named Tom Arlen ran through the
door.

"My mother!" he cried. "I heard my mother!"

Tom's mother had sailed the previous morning on a trading
ship bound south. Half the village followed him to the
cliff road.

At first, they heard nothing.

Then, from the darkness, faint and distant, came a woman's
voice.

"Tom?"

The boy pushed through the crowd.

"Mama!"

"Tom, where are you?"

The voice seemed to rise from far beneath the cliff.
It sounded thin but unmistakably human.

"I'm here!" Tom shouted. "Come toward the lighthouse!"

"I can't see it."

Elias looked up. The lighthouse beam turned steadily overhead, but
when it passed across the emptiness, it vanished.

"Make it brighter!" someone yelled.

Elias ran.

The lamp burned whale oil through six concentric wicks, magnified
by a crystal lens taller than a man. Elias opened
every fuel valve. He trimmed the wicks until the flames
shone white and hot. The mechanism clicked and groaned, rotating
the lens.

Below, the villagers shouted encouragement into the dark.

One by one, more voices answered.

Fishermen. Sailors. Passengers. People who had been on the water
when the sea disappeared. They called from different distances, some
close enough to recognize, others so faint their words could
not be understood.

None could see the light.

Before midnight, the voices began to change.

"Elias," said Mara, standing beside him in the lantern room.
"Did you hear that?"

He had.

Among the desperate calls floated another sound: a deep, low
murmur, like a choir singing through stone.

"What is it?" she whispered.

The murmur grew louder whenever the beam crossed the darkness.

Something was responding to the lamp.

Elias reduced the fuel. The beam dimmed, and the sound
faded.

"Turn it off," Mara said.

"No."

"Whatever is out there can see it."

"So might our people."

At that moment, a sharp knock came from the lantern-room
glass.

Elias and Mara froze.

Another knock followed.

The glass overlooked a sheer drop. There was no ledge
outside, no balcony, nothing to stand on.

A third knock sounded.

Mara lifted the storm lantern. Its glow revealed a wet
hand pressed against the outer surface.

Then a face rose behind it.

Tom's mother stared through the glass.

Her hair drifted around her head as if she were
underwater. Her eyes were wide, and her lips had a
bluish cast. Yet she was alive. She moved her mouth,
silently forming words.

Let me in.

Mara stepped forward.

Elias caught her arm.

"How did she climb the tower?" he asked.

The woman outside struck the glass again.

Cracks spread beneath her palm.

Elias pulled the main lever. Metal shutters slammed around the
lamp, blocking its light. The lantern room went dark.

The woman vanished.

A scream rose from the cliff road below—not one scream,
but many. The voices from the emptiness cried out in
anger.

Then everything fell silent.

At dawn, the inland road was gone.

The cobbled way should have wound east through fields toward
the neighboring town. Instead, it ended twenty yards beyond Bellwether's
last house. Past that point lay the same lightless void
that had swallowed the sea.

The village had become an island.

Not an island in water, Elias thought. An island in
nothing.

People panicked.

Some locked themselves indoors. Some broke into the tavern cellar.
The mayor ordered the remaining food counted and placed under
guard. There were supplies for perhaps two months, longer if
the goats survived and the wells remained fresh.

The church filled with prayers.

Elias spent the morning examining the lighthouse lamp. The outer
glass bore no crack, though all four panes were smeared
with saltwater.

Mara found him cleaning them.

"You saw her," she said.

"Yes."

"Was it really Tom's mother?"

"I don't know."

"I think it was."

"People don't climb empty air."

"People don't wake to find the world missing."

Elias wrung seawater from his cloth.

Mara watched the drops fall. "What if the sea is
still there?"

"It isn't."

"What if we are the ones who disappeared?"

Elias looked at her.

She took a crust of bread from her pocket and
held it up.

"We see the village. We see each other. But we
can't see beyond the cliffs. Perhaps, to the rest of
the world, Bellwether vanished."

"Then why can we hear the ships?"

"Because they're searching for us."

He looked toward the shuttered lamp.

"And the woman outside?"

Mara did not answer.

That night, they lit the lighthouse again.

They agreed to use only one wick, creating a weak
amber beam. The villagers gathered beneath the tower, waiting.

For an hour, there was nothing.

Then a bell rang in the darkness.

It was the bell of the Saint Orra, the southern
trading ship. Its pattern was familiar: two short notes, one
long.

Tom stood at the cliff edge, held back by his
father.

The bell sounded again.

A tiny light appeared in the void.

It looked like a star, impossibly distant. Slowly, it grew
brighter. The villagers cheered.

"Saint Orra!" Tom's father shouted. "Follow the lighthouse!"

The light approached. Elias watched through Mara's telescope. At first
he could make out only a lantern swinging from a
mast. Then the prow emerged.

The ship sailed through blackness as if on invisible water.
Its hull was slick, its sails hung limp, and its
decks were crowded with people.

But something was wrong.

The Saint Orra was sailing upright toward a cliff face.

"Turn!" Elias shouted, though he was far too high to
be heard. "Turn north!"

The ship continued.

Its passengers waved. Their mouths opened in joyous cries.

The prow reached the cliff.

And passed into the rock.

There was no impact. Timber, mast, sails, and people slid
silently through solid stone and disappeared.

Tom's father collapsed.

In the lantern room, Mara gripped the telescope.

"They didn't see the cliff," she said.

Elias stared at the place where the ship had vanished.

"No," he replied. "They saw something else."

A moment later, the Saint Orra's bell rang from inside
the lighthouse wall.

The villagers fled.

For the next six days, the void crept inward.

It took the northern pasture first. At noon, the field
was green and crowded with sheep. By evening, it was
gone. A perfectly straight line of darkness cut across the
grass. Three sheep stood near the edge, bleating at companions
they could still hear but no longer see.

The darkness swallowed the cemetery next.

Mara's bakery vanished on the fifth day. She watched from
the street as the black boundary passed through the building.
There was no noise, no wind, no collapse. One moment
the bakery stood whole; the next, only its front steps
remained.

Bellwether shrank toward the lighthouse.

The villagers moved into the church, the inn, and the
keeper's house. Each night, voices called from the void.

Some belonged to the living.

Others belonged to the dead.

Elias heard his wife on the seventh night.

Clara had died twelve years earlier, in the upstairs bedroom
of the keeper's house, while winter waves battered the cliffs.
Elias had sat beside her until dawn, holding her hand
and counting the seconds between flashes of the lighthouse.

Now her voice drifted from the dark.

"Elias."

He stood alone in the lantern room.

"Elias, open the shutters."

He closed his eyes.

"I know you're there," Clara said. "You always were."

The voice was warm, amused, exactly as he remembered it.

He touched the brass lever.

"Open them," she said. "I'm nearly home."

His hand tightened around the lever. Then he noticed something.

Clara had hated the lighthouse beam at night. During her
final illness, even with the curtains closed, light had slipped
through the seams every twelve seconds. Near the end, she
had begged him to stop the turning lens.

She would never have asked him to open the shutters.

Elias stepped away.

The voice changed.

It deepened, becoming many voices at once.

"WE ARE NEARLY HOME."

The tower shook.

Below, people screamed as wet hands struck every window in
the village.

Elias kept the shutters closed until morning.

By the tenth day, only the lighthouse, church, inn, and
a cluster of houses remained. The void circled them less
than a mile away.

The wells turned salty.

The goats refused to eat.

Mayor Clegg demanded that the lighthouse be extinguished permanently.

"It's drawing the darkness," he said. "Every time you light
it, the boundary moves closer."

"It moves whether the lamp burns or not," Elias replied.

"You don't know that."

"And you don't know it doesn't."

They argued in the church before the whole village. Fear
had hollowed their faces. Mothers held sleeping children. Old sailors
clutched knives and prayer beads.

"The lamp guides those things to us," Clegg said. "We've
all seen them."

"It also guides our people."

"Our people sail into stone!"

"Because they cannot see what we see."

"Then the light is useless."

Elias looked around the church.

"No. It tells them we are still here."

A weary laugh came from the back.

"What good is that?" someone asked.

Elias had no answer.

The vote was nearly unanimous. The lighthouse would remain dark.

That evening, Elias sat on the keeper's house steps with
Mara. Above them, the tower stood unlit against a sky
crowded with stars.

Strangely, the stars remained visible. The void had not swallowed
the sky.

"Do you think they made the right choice?" Mara asked.

"No."

"Do you think they made the wrong one?"

"I don't know."

She handed him a tin cup of bitter tea.

He drank and listened to the unnatural silence.

"When I was nine," Mara said, "my father took me
out in his boat. A storm came from nowhere. The
mast broke, and we drifted until night. Do you remember?"

"I was fifteen. I remember everyone searching."

"I remember the lighthouse. Every twelve seconds, the beam crossed
the clouds. My father told me to watch it. He
said as long as I could see the light, the
storm couldn't change where home was."

Elias turned the cup between his hands.

"We can't see home from the dark," she continued. "But
perhaps home can still see us."

At midnight, the stars began to disappear.

Not all at once. One after another, from the horizon
upward, they blinked out.

The darkness was climbing the sky.

Elias stood.

Mara looked at him. "What are you going to do?"

"My job."

They ran to the lighthouse.

Mayor Clegg had locked the door and posted two guards.
Mara distracted them by throwing a stone through the church
window. While the guards turned at the crash, Elias struck
one with his shoulder, snatched the key, and rushed inside.

They climbed the stairs as bells rang through the village.

At the top, Elias opened the fuel valves.

Nothing happened.

"The pipes are empty," he said.

Clegg had drained the lamp reservoir.

Mara held up her storm lantern. "Can we use this?"

"Not enough."

Below, villagers pounded on the lighthouse door.

Elias searched the cabinets. A bottle of cleaning alcohol, half
full. Lamp oil from two spare lanterns. Grease from the
rotation gears.

"Bring every candle you can find," he told Mara.

She hurried down the stairs.

Elias poured the alcohol and oil into the reservoir. It
would burn badly, perhaps explode. He no longer cared.

The door below burst open.

Footsteps thundered up the stairs.

Mara returned ahead of them, her apron filled with candles
stolen from the church. Elias melted them in the wick
tray and struck a match.

The first flame rose blue, then yellow.

The second wick caught.

Mayor Clegg entered the lantern room with half the village
behind him.

"Stop!" he shouted.

Elias pulled the lever.

The shutters opened.

Light swept across Bellwether.

The crowd fell silent.

The void had reached the base of the lighthouse.

The inn and church were gone. The remaining villagers stood
on a circle of grass no more than fifty yards
wide. Beyond it waited endless dark.

Within that dark were thousands of lights.

Ships surrounded them.

Fishing boats, merchant vessels, warships, tiny rowboats—vessels from Bellwether's waters
and vessels no one recognized. Some were ancient, with square
sails and painted eyes. Others were made of gleaming metal.
They sailed above, below, and sideways, moving along invisible currents.

Their passengers stared toward the lighthouse.

Beyond the ships floated islands, cities, fragments of forests, towers,
bridges, roads, and mountains. Pieces of lost worlds revolved slowly
in the dark, each surrounded by its own thin haze
of light.

Bellwether was not alone.

Mara whispered, "They're all looking for a way back."

A shape moved behind the drifting worlds.

It was larger than the sea. Larger than the night.
Elias could not understand its form, but he saw the
curve of something like an eye opening in the distance.

The murmur began again.

Not a song, he realized.

Breathing.

The lamp flickered as the creature drew nearer.

Mayor Clegg fell to his knees.

"What do we do?" he asked.

No one answered.

Then, from one of the distant towers, a light flashed.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Another light answered from an upside-down ship. Then another from
a floating city. Across the void, lamps began to shine
in deliberate patterns.

They had seen Bellwether's lighthouse.

Elias understood.

"They're lighthouses," he said.

Mara stared at the countless points igniting in the dark.

"Not all of them."

"No. But they're doing the same thing."

The eye in the void turned toward the growing lights.

Elias's lamp sputtered. The makeshift fuel was almost gone.

"More oil," he said.

"There is none," Clegg replied.

"Then bring wood. Cloth. Furniture. Anything that burns."

The villagers ran.

They tore doors from the keeper's house. They smashed chairs,
ripped curtains, emptied bottles of spirits, and carried everything up
the iron stairs. Elias removed the delicate burner assembly and
built a fire inside the great lens itself.

It was an act of destruction. The heat cracked crystal
prisms that had survived a century of storms. Smoke blackened
the brass. The rotation gears warped.

But the light grew.

Around them, the void filled with answering beacons.

Thousands became millions.

Every lost ship raised lanterns. Every drifting town lit windows.
People burned signal fires on fragments of broken land. The
lights formed a vast constellation, not in the sky, but
below it.

The creature recoiled.

Its breathing became a roar. Shadows struck the tower. Glass
burst inward, showering the villagers with glittering fragments. Wind rushed
through the lantern room, cold and salt-laden.

Elias held the shattered lens frame as the floor pitched
beneath him.

"Keep it burning!" he shouted.

The darkness folded.

For one impossible instant, Elias saw every lost place occupying
the same point: seas beneath deserts, cities inside forests, ships
sailing through mountains. At the center of them all stood
the lighthouse, its broken lamp blazing.

Then came the sound of a wave.

A single wave, crashing against rock.

The world returned.

Morning sunlight poured through the ruined lantern room.

Below, the sea shone blue and restless. Fishing boats crowded
the harbor. The village stood whole again: church, inn, bakery,
cemetery, and every house. The northern pasture was green, its
sheep grazing calmly.

The Saint Orra floated half a mile offshore.

For a long time, no one moved.

Then Tom saw his mother standing on the ship's deck
and began to shout.

The villagers rushed down the tower.

Elias remained.

The lighthouse lens was destroyed. The lamp was a heap
of melted brass and ash. The walls were cracked, and
most of the windows were gone.

Mara stood beside him, looking at the restored horizon.

"Do you think it's over?" she asked.

Far out at sea, something flashed beneath the water.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Elias watched until another light answered from beyond the horizon.

"No," he said. "But now we know we're not alone."

In the months that followed, the people of Bellwether rebuilt
the lighthouse.

They brought a new lens from the capital, larger than
the first. They installed mirrors behind the lamp and stocked
enough oil for years. Every house received a signal lantern,
and every child learned the patterns used on the night
the world disappeared.

Sailors began bringing strange stories from distant ports.

They spoke of islands that had returned after centuries, of
ships arriving with crews who claimed to have been lost
for a single night though their names belonged to old
legends. On moonless evenings, lights appeared where no land had
ever been charted.

Elias listened to every story.

He remained keeper until his hair turned entirely white and
his knees could no longer manage the iron stairs. Mara,
who had rebuilt her bakery, brought bread each morning and
complained that he overfilled the oil reservoir.

On his final night as keeper, Elias climbed to the
lantern room alone.

A storm was gathering. Waves broke white against the cliffs,
and rain moved across the sea in silver veils. Behind
him, Bellwether glowed with warm windows.

He lit the lamp.

The great lens began to turn.

Every twelve seconds, the beam crossed the water. On its
third passage, a small light answered from the storm.

Elias raised his hand.

Perhaps it was a ship.

Perhaps it was a lost city or a distant lighthouse
shining through a crack in the world.

Perhaps it was only lightning.

It did not matter.

The purpose of a lighthouse, Elias had learned, was not
to defeat the darkness.

It was to tell anyone wandering within it that somewhere,
however distant, there was still a shore.

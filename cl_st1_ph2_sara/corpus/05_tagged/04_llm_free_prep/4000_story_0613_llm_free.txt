# The Cartographer of Lost Roads

By the time Mara Venn reached the village of Bellwether,
the road behind her had vanished.

She noticed this only because the rain stopped.

For three days she had traveled beneath a steady gray
curtain, following a narrow path through the western hills. The
road appeared on no royal map, but Mara had found
it in the notebook of a dead surveyor, drawn in
faded blue ink between two pages that smelled of smoke.
According to the surveyor's notes, the road led to Bellwether,
a village abandoned fifty years ago after a winter famine.

Yet smoke rose from its chimneys.

Mara stood beneath an oak at the edge of the
village and looked back. There should have been a track
descending toward the valley. Instead, wet grass stretched unbroken to
a wall of black pines.

She took out her compass. Its needle spun once, twice,
and then pointed directly at her.

"That's unhelpful," she muttered.

Mara was the youngest cartographer employed by the Royal Office
of Roads, though "employed" was a generous word. The office
paid her late, dismissed her reports, and assigned her to
map routes no senior cartographer wanted. Haunted woods, flooded plains,
disputed borders—these were Mara's specialties.

She folded the compass away and entered Bellwether.

The village consisted of thirty cottages, a church with a
crooked steeple, a blacksmith's yard, and an inn called the
Lantern House. Lamps burned in every window, though it was
not yet evening. Villagers in dark woolen clothes moved quietly
through the rain-washed streets. They glanced at Mara, then quickly
looked away.

A wooden sign stood beside the village well.

WELCOME TO BELLWETHER, it read.

BENEATH, in smaller letters, someone had carved:

DO NOT LEAVE BY A ROAD YOU REMEMBER.

Mara read the warning twice.

The door of the Lantern House opened. A tall old
woman in a red apron beckoned to her.

"You'll catch cold standing there."

"I've been wet for three days."

"Then you're three days overdue for soup."

The woman stepped aside, and Mara entered.

The inn smelled of onions, damp boots, and woodsmoke. Seven
people sat at tables, speaking in low voices. When Mara
removed her blue traveling cloak, the silver compass embroidered on
her coat became visible. Conversation stopped.

The old woman frowned.

"You're a mapmaker."

"Cartographer," Mara said.

"That's a longer word for the same trouble."

"My name is Mara Venn. I'm surveying the western roads
on behalf of the Crown."

"The Crown has no roads here."

"That is what I'm trying to determine."

The woman studied her with pale, sharp eyes. "I'm Elianor
Thatch. This is my inn, and you may have a
room if you promise not to draw anything."

Mara glanced at the patrons. All of them were listening.

"I can promise not to draw your inn."

"Anything," Elianor repeated.

Mara was cold, hungry, and in no position to bargain.

"Very well."

Elianor served her a bowl of soup and a heel
of black bread. For several minutes, the only sound in
the room was the scrape of Mara's spoon.

Then a little girl approached her table.

She was perhaps eight years old, with two dark braids
and a serious expression. She carried a wooden horse with
one wheel missing.

"Did you come from outside?" the girl asked.

Mara nodded.

"What does the sea look like?"

"Big."

A man near the hearth gave a nervous laugh.

The girl frowned. "That's not a proper answer."

"No," Mara agreed. "It looks like a piece of the
sky fell down and forgot how to stay still."

The girl considered this and smiled.

"My name is Tessa. I'm going to see it one
day."

Elianor set down a cup hard enough to spill ale.

"Tessa."

The girl lowered her head and returned to the hearth.

Mara looked from Tessa to Elianor. "Is no one allowed
to leave?"

"No one is forbidden," Elianor said.

"Then why the sign by the well?"

"That is advice, not law."

"What happens if someone leaves by a road they remember?"

Elianor gathered Mara's empty bowl. "They return."

"That doesn't sound terrible."

"Not all of them return in the same year."

She walked away.

That night, Mara was given a room beneath the sloping
roof. The bed was narrow but dry, and the window
overlooked the village square.

She waited until the inn fell silent.

Then she lit a candle, locked her door, and opened
her leather map case.

Inside lay rulers, ink, quills, a brass divider, six completed
charts, and the dead surveyor's notebook. Mara turned to the
page showing Bellwether.

The road was drawn as a thin blue line. Beside
it, the surveyor had written:

The village remains, though it cannot be approached deliberately. Road
visible only to the lost. Residents hostile to maps. Church
bell rings at midnight, but the tower contains no bell.

At the bottom of the page, in shakier handwriting:

I have mapped the way out. God forgive me.

The following page had been torn away.

As midnight approached, Mara heard doors opening below. She blew
out her candle and looked through the window.

The villagers were gathering in the square. Each carried a
lantern. They formed a circle around the well, facing the
church.

A wind stirred, although the trees were still.

Then the church bell rang.

Its voice was deep and sorrowful. The sound passed through
the glass and seemed to vibrate in Mara's bones.

Once.

Twice.

At the third toll, pale lines appeared in the village
streets.

Mara pressed closer to the window.

The lines shone faintly beneath the mud, crossing and branching
like moonlit veins. Roads. There were dozens of them, far
more than the village streets could hold. Some passed through
houses. Others curved up walls or vanished into the well.
One climbed into the sky.

The villagers raised their lanterns.

The bell rang a fourth time.

From every road came footsteps.

Travelers emerged from the darkness.

A soldier in rusted armor walked through the blacksmith's wall.
A woman carrying a swaddled infant stepped out of the
well. Two boys in summer clothes crossed the square hand
in hand, their faces pale with cold. An old man
crawled down from the church roof, weeping.

The people of Bellwether moved among them, offering blankets, water,
and food.

The bell rang twelve times. At the final toll, the
glowing roads faded.

The new arrivals remained.

Mara did not sleep.

At dawn, there were four more names written on a
slate behind the innkeeper's counter. The soldier was not among
them.

"Where did he go?" Mara asked.

Elianor wiped a cup. "He remembered his road."

"And?"

"He took it."

"When will he arrive where he's going?"

"If he's fortunate, before he left."

Mara stared at her.

Elianor sighed. "You should speak to Ansel Vale. He tends
the church."

The church door was unlocked. Inside, morning light fell through
blue glass, painting the floor in watery colors. There were
no saints, no altar, and no religious symbols. Instead, hundreds
of maps covered the walls.

Most were crude, drawn on cloth, bark, scraps of paper,
even flattened tin. They showed roads leading from Bellwether to
places real and impossible: the capital, the northern desert, a
city beneath a lake, a house labeled MOTHER'S KITCHEN, a
battlefield, a grave.

Ansel Vale stood at the front of the church. He
was a broad, stooped man with white hair and ink-stained
hands.

"I wondered when you'd come," he said.

"You knew I was here?"

"Bellwether knows when a cartographer arrives."

Mara looked up toward the empty tower. "What rings the
bell?"

"The roads."

"Roads do not ring bells."

"Most villages do not swallow travelers, yet here we are."

He led her to a circular table covered with a
large sheet of yellowed parchment. At its center was Bellwether.
Thousands of roads spread outward, tangling across the page. Many
ended abruptly. Others looped back upon themselves.

Mara leaned closer.

"This is extraordinary."

"This is a prison."

"Who drew it?"

"I did. With help."

"Why?"

"To find a way out."

Elianor's warning came back to her.

"You're a cartographer."

"I was, once."

Ansel touched a road that curved east before disappearing at
the parchment's edge. "Bellwether is not a place in the
ordinary sense. It is where lost roads go."

"Roads don't get lost. Travelers do."

"Travelers vanish. Roads are forgotten. A path is abandoned, a
bridge collapses, a border moves. A husband dies, and his
wife never again walks the lane where they first met.
A child takes a shortcut home and grows old elsewhere.
Those roads do not cease to exist. They come here."

Mara studied the map. "And they bring travelers with them."

"Sometimes."

"Why can't the villagers leave?"

"We can. But every road out of Bellwether is a
lost road. If you remember where it should lead, it
changes. It bends toward memory instead of place."

"Then take a road you don't remember."

"Many have tried. A stranger's road may carry you into
a stranger's life. You might arrive at a wedding where
everyone knows your name, or a prison where they have
been waiting to hang you. You might walk out yesterday,
or a hundred years from now."

Mara thought of the warning by the well.

"Do not leave by a road you remember."

"Memory gives the road power," Ansel said. "Maps give it
more."

He pointed to a black stain near the center of
the parchment.

"This village was ordinary once. Then a royal cartographer came.
He believed every road should be measured and fixed. He
mapped Bellwether so precisely that the village became pinned between
where it was and where the roads believed it to
be."

"The dead surveyor," Mara said.

"Dead?"

She took out the notebook and showed him the blue
map.

Ansel sat heavily.

"Corren Vey. He came fifty years ago."

"He died last winter. I bought this among his papers."

"Then he escaped."

"It seems so."

Ansel turned the notebook over in his hands. "Corren drew
a master chart, a map containing every lost road. He
thought he could command them. Instead, the roads multiplied. Bellwether
slipped away from the world."

"What happened to the chart?"

"Elianor burned it."

Mara remembered the notebook's smoky scent.

"If the chart was destroyed, why is the village still
trapped?"

"Because one piece survived. The center. Bellwether itself."

"Where is it?"

"In the bell."

"You said there was no bell."

"There isn't. Not anymore."

Before Mara could question him further, the church doors opened.
Tessa stood outside, breathless.

"Master Vale, it's my mother."

They found Elianor in the inn's kitchen, gripping the edge
of a table. Before her lay a faded red ribbon.

"It was on the stairs," she whispered.

Tessa reached for it, but Mara stopped her.

"What is it?"

"My daughter's," Elianor said.

Tessa's eyes widened. "My mother is your daughter."

"My elder daughter. Lysa."

Elianor picked up the ribbon with trembling fingers. "She left
Bellwether sixteen years ago. She took the north road because
she remembered snow at its end. I tried to stop
her."

"Did she return?" Mara asked.

"No. But if the ribbon is here, her road is
near."

That evening, the villagers gathered inside the church. Fear moved
among them like a draft.

Ansel placed the red ribbon on the circular map. It
twisted by itself, pointing toward a road drawn in pale
ink.

"The road will appear at midnight," he said. "We must
not let Elianor take it."

"You expect me to leave my child out there?" Elianor
demanded.

"She may not be out there. The road could lead
to a memory of her."

"That is enough."

"No," Mara said. "It isn't."

Elianor turned on her. "You know nothing about it."

"I know maps. And I know a road is not
the same as its destination."

"Spoken like a cartographer."

Mara ignored the insult. "If Corren escaped, he must have
discovered something. The missing page in his notebook probably contained
the route. Perhaps the page wasn't destroyed."

"Corren took nothing when he vanished," Ansel said.

"Are you certain?"

Elianor went very still.

Everyone looked at her.

At last, she reached beneath her apron and drew out
a small iron key.

"There is a box," she said.

The box was hidden under a loose stone in the
inn's cellar. It was made of oak and bound with
tarnished brass. Inside lay a single scrap of paper, blackened
around the edges.

Mara recognized Corren's handwriting.

No map can show the way out, because the way
out is not a road. To escape, Bellwether must be
lost.

Below the words he had drawn half a circle.

"That's all?" Elianor said.

Mara held the paper near the lantern. "No."

She turned it over.

On the back was a rough picture of the village
well, with an arrow pointing down.

At eleven, Mara descended into the well.

The villagers lowered her by rope. Ansel came with her,
carrying a lantern and Corren's scrap of paper. The well
was deeper than it appeared. Its stone walls gave way
to dark earth, then to layers of broken paving. Mara
saw pieces of roads buried in the soil: white marble,
red brick, wooden planks, gravel, black glass.

At last, her boots touched solid ground.

A tunnel stretched away beneath the village.

"The well was here before Bellwether," Ansel said. "No one
knows who dug it."

"It isn't a well," Mara replied.

She raised the lantern.

Milestones lined the tunnel. Each bore a different distance and
destination.

THREE MILES TO HOME.

ONE DAY TO THE WAR.

SEVEN STEPS TO FORGIVENESS.

NO DISTANCE AT ALL TO THE END.

The tunnel opened into a circular chamber beneath the church.
At its center hung a bell-shaped emptiness.

Mara could not look directly at it. Her gaze slid
aside, catching only an outline where torchlight bent and disappeared.

"The bell," Ansel whispered.

Within the emptiness floated a torn piece of parchment.

Bellwether was drawn upon it in perfect detail.

Every roof tile. Every tree. Every stone around the well.

"The center of the master chart," Mara said.

The first toll of midnight shook dust from the ceiling.

Above them, the roads were waking.

The parchment fluttered inside the bell-shaped void. Mara reached toward
it, but Ansel seized her wrist.

"Anything that touches the bell becomes a road."

"How do we destroy the map?"

"Fire failed before."

"Because Elianor burned the chart but preserved this piece. Corren
must have cut it out first."

A second toll sounded.

From the tunnel came distant footsteps.

Mara stared at the map. Corren's words repeated in her
mind.

Bellwether must be lost.

Not destroyed. Lost.

A third toll.

"Mara," Ansel said, "if we cannot remove the map, we
must go."

She opened her leather case.

"What are you doing?"

"Breaking my promise to Elianor."

She took out ink and a quill. Then, kneeling on
the chamber floor, she began to draw.

Not Bellwether. Something else.

She drew the coastline where she had grown up, the
sharp cliffs and narrow bays. She drew the road to
her father's cottage. She drew the bent pear tree by
the gate and the hill where her brother had taught
her to fly a paper kite.

The bell rang again.

Each toll pulled at the drawing. The ink shimmered. The
road to her father's cottage lifted from the page like
a strand of black thread.

Ansel recoiled. "Stop. You're feeding it a memory."

"Yes."

The road stretched toward the empty bell.

Mara kept drawing.

She mapped the academy in the capital, where instructors told
her women lacked the temperament for serious fieldwork. She drew
the alley behind the Royal Office where she had waited
in the rain to receive her first assignment. She drew
every road she had traveled since: mountain passes, forest tracks,
causeways, frozen rivers.

The map inside the bell began to blur.

"What are you doing?" Ansel asked.

"Making Bellwether somewhere else."

"That won't free us."

"No. But it may confuse the roads."

At the edge of her drawing, Mara sketched Bellwether without
detail—a circle, a well, a crooked church. Then she connected
it to every road she had drawn.

The chamber trembled.

The map in the bell changed. Tiny streets twisted. Houses
shifted. The church appeared beside Mara's childhood sea. The village
well rose in the courtyard of the royal academy.

A fifth toll boomed.

From above came shouting.

Elianor.

"She's taken Lysa's road," Ansel said.

Mara drew faster. "Go."

"I won't leave you."

"She'll listen to you."

"She has not listened to me in forty years."

"Then try something new."

Ansel hesitated, then ran into the tunnel.

Mara was alone.

The sixth toll rang.

The footsteps in the tunnel grew louder. Shapes appeared in
the darkness: travelers walking forgotten roads. A queen in golden
robes. A barefoot shepherd. A wounded man dragging a sword.
A young woman with a red ribbon in her hair.

Lysa.

She looked exactly like Elianor, but younger.

"Are you real?" Mara asked.

Lysa smiled sadly. "Does it matter?"

Behind her, the road unfolded—a snowy lane beneath stars.

Elianor appeared at the far end, running toward her daughter.
Ansel followed, shouting.

Mara understood then. The road was not approaching Bellwether.

Bellwether was being pulled onto the road.

The seventh toll cracked the floor.

Mara dipped her quill, but the ink bottle was empty.

The map in the bell had become a confusion of
overlapping places. Yet Bellwether remained at its center, exquisitely detailed,
fixed by the certainty of Corren's hand.

No map can show the way out.

Mara tore her drawing from the pad.

She thought of all the rules she had learned: mark
north clearly, measure distance twice, use permanent ink, never invent
what has not been observed. A map was a promise
that the world could be known.

Perhaps that promise was the trap.

She crumpled her drawing.

The roads screamed.

It was not a sound made for human ears. The
chamber filled with grinding stone, breaking wheels, horses in panic,
slamming doors, and the last breaths of people who had
died far from home.

Mara thrust the crumpled map into the bell-shaped emptiness.

The eighth toll never finished.

Every light went out.

In the darkness, someone caught Mara's hand.

"Come," said a girl's voice.

"Tessa?"

But the hand felt older than Tessa's, the fingers callused
and strong.

Mara was pulled forward. Wind struck her face. Snow, hot
sand, and sea spray whirled around her. She saw a
thousand roads beneath a sky with no stars.

"Which way?" Mara shouted.

The unseen girl answered, "A way we don't remember."

So Mara closed her eyes and ran.

She awoke on wet grass.

Morning sunlight warmed her face. Birds sang nearby. For a
long time, she listened, afraid to move.

Then Tessa said, "Is the sea supposed to be making
that noise?"

Mara opened her eyes.

They lay on a green cliff above a vast blue-gray
ocean. Waves struck the rocks below, bursting into white spray.

Around them, the people of Bellwether were waking among the
grass. Elianor sat nearby, holding both her daughters. Lysa was
no longer young. Silver streaked her dark hair, and deep
lines framed her eyes, but the red ribbon remained tied
around her wrist.

Ansel stood at the cliff's edge, laughing.

Behind them was the village.

Every cottage, every chimney, the inn, the blacksmith's yard, and
the crooked church had arrived intact. The well stood in
the square, filled with clear water. Beyond the last house,
a broad road led inland.

A wooden sign stood beside it.

WELCOME TO BELLWETHER.

The warning beneath had vanished.

Mara found her leather map case near the cliff. The
instruments inside were broken. Her charts were blank, washed clean
of ink. Even Corren's notebook contained only empty pages.

She should have felt devastated. Years of work had disappeared.

Instead, she laughed.

Weeks later, a messenger from the nearest coastal town carried
news of Bellwether to the Royal Office of Roads. The
office sent three senior cartographers, two historians, a magistrate, and
a man whose only duty seemed to be objecting to
expenses.

They demanded an explanation.

Mara gave them one.

She said Bellwether had always stood on the western coast.
Its omission from royal maps was an administrative error.

The senior cartographers protested. The historians searched archives. The magistrate
interviewed villagers, all of whom agreed that their families had
fished those waters for generations. Elianor produced tax records dating
back eighty years, though no one knew where she had
found them.

At last, the officials accepted Mara's account. Governments, she discovered,
were more comfortable admitting incompetence than magic.

The Royal Office offered to restore Mara's position. She declined.

Instead, she remained in Bellwether and opened a small shop
beside the well. Above the door hung a sign:

MARA VENN
MAPS OF PLACES THAT MAY OR MAY NOT EXIST

Travelers came from distant countries to purchase her charts. Mara
mapped secret gardens, buried cities, roads seen in dreams, and
paths that appeared only in winter. She never promised accuracy.

Tessa became her apprentice.

The girl learned quickly, though she disliked rulers and refused
to draw straight lines. When Mara complained, Tessa reminded her
that straight roads caused most of the trouble.

As for the church bell, it never rang again.

Sometimes, however, on rainy nights, strangers arrived at the Lantern
House wearing clothes from other lands and other centuries. Elianor
gave them soup. Ansel listened to their stories. Lysa helped
them decide where to go next.

No one was turned away.

Years later, when Mara's hair had begun to gray, Tessa
asked her to draw the road by which she had
first entered Bellwether.

They sat together on the cliff, the sea moving below
them like a fallen piece of sky.

"I can't," Mara said.

"Because you don't remember it?"

Mara looked inland. Many roads crossed the hills now. Some
led to farms, some to towns, and some, perhaps, to
places no map could hold.

"Oh, I remember," she said. "But that road was lost."

Tessa waited for more.

Mara smiled and handed her the compass. Its needle spun
once, twice, then pointed toward the open sea.

"Let's leave it that way."

The town of Bellwether had no clocks.

There were empty circles on the railway station wall, pale
squares in kitchens where clocks had once hung, and a
rusted frame above the courthouse door. In the schoolhouse, children
learned to count, to read, and to name the seasons,
but no one taught them hours or minutes.

Time in Bellwether was kept by Mara Venn.

Every morning, before the first chimney smoked, Mara climbed the
hill east of town and unlocked the black door of
the tower. She wound the great mechanism inside, adjusted the
brass wheels, and listened to the heartbeat of the pendulum.
Then, at the proper moment, she pulled a rope.

The bell's first note told the bakers to light their
ovens. The second sent the shepherds toward the northern fields.
The third woke the schoolmaster, who always claimed to have
been awake already. Other chimes followed throughout the day: one
for the market, one for midday, one for closing the
shops, and a final low toll that told families to
bar their doors and leave the streets to darkness.

No one asked how Mara knew when to ring.

Her mother had known before her, and her mother's father
before that. The tower keeper's knowledge passed through the family
like the shape of a nose or a silver streak
in the hair. Bellwether trusted the Venns because it always
had.

"The bell does not tell the time," Mara's mother had
once explained. "The bell makes the time. Remember the difference."

Mara had been nine then. At thirty-seven, she still did
not understand.

She knew only that the mechanism in the tower was
older than the town and unlike any clockwork she had
seen in books. Its gears turned even when they were
disconnected. Its pendulum sometimes swung against the wind. Behind its
face—visible only from inside the tower—there were no numbers, only
a painted black river winding through a golden landscape.

Each dawn, Mara set a thin iron needle on that
river. If she placed it too far upstream, morning mist
clung to the streets and roosters refused to crow. If
she set it too far down, shadows shortened before breakfast
and bread burned in the ovens.

She had made such mistakes while learning. Her mother always
corrected them before the town noticed.

Now there was no one to correct Mara.

Her mother had been dead for twelve years.

For twelve years, Bellwether's mornings had arrived cleanly, its afternoons
had passed at an orderly pace, and its nights had
ended exactly when they should. Mara had no husband and
no child, but she told herself there was still time.
The phrase had become a private joke.

Then, one autumn morning, she found a boy sleeping beside
the pendulum.

He lay curled on the stone floor, barefoot and muddy,
wearing a coat several sizes too large. A canvas satchel
was clutched to his chest. Each sweep of the brass
pendulum passed within an inch of his dark hair.

Mara nearly cried out. Instead she seized the pendulum, stopping
it mid-swing.

The world outside went silent.

Not quiet—silent. The wind froze against the tower windows. A
crow hung motionless above the hill, its wings extended. Smoke
stood like gray pillars over the town.

The boy opened his eyes.

"You shouldn't stop it," he said.

Mara released the pendulum. It resumed its arc.

Outside, the crow completed its flap and vanished below the
trees. Wind struck the glass. Somewhere in town, a dog
barked.

Mara stared at the boy. "Who are you?"

He sat up and pushed hair from his face. He
was perhaps twelve, though hunger made him look younger. His
eyes were a peculiar shade of gray.

"My name is Silas."

"How did you get in?"

"The door."

"The door was locked."

"Not when I used it."

Mara glanced toward the stairwell. The black door at its
foot required two keys, both of which hung from her
belt.

Silas rose. His coat dragged around his ankles. "Is this
Bellwether?"

"It is."

"Then I've come a long way."

"From where?"

He considered the question. "Tomorrow."

Mara had dealt with children before. Bellwether's children often dared
one another to approach the tower, though none had entered
it. She knew their tricks, their solemn faces, their confidence
that adults were fools.

"Empty your satchel," she said.

Silas hesitated, then knelt and opened it.

Inside were three hard biscuits, a glass jar filled with
rainwater, a broken pocket watch, and a newspaper.

Mara picked up the paper.

It was called the Bellwether Chronicle. The town had no
newspaper. Messages arrived on the weekly train and were pinned
to a board at the station.

The date at the top of the page was twenty-three
years ahead.

Beneath it, in bold black letters, was a headline:

TOWER COLLAPSE MARKS TENTH YEAR OF THE STILLNESS

The photograph below showed Bellwether's tower split open like a
lightning-struck tree. Vines covered the stone. In the square, dozens
of people stood with vacant faces.

Mara looked more closely and felt cold spread through her
hands.

None of the people had aged.

She recognized Master Pell outside the schoolhouse, wearing the same
narrow spectacles he wore now. The baker's twins stood near
the dry fountain, still seven years old. At the edge
of the picture was Mara herself.

She looked thirty-seven.

"What is the Stillness?" she asked.

Silas watched the pendulum. "It began when the bell stopped."

"Why did it stop?"

"You stopped it."

Mara folded the newspaper carefully, buying herself a moment to
think.

"Why?"

"I don't know. No one knows. You went into the
tower on First Frost morning. The bell didn't ring, and
the sun never rose. After that, nothing changed. People didn't
grow older. Crops didn't grow. Fires burned but wood was
never consumed. No one could leave."

"And you?"

"I was born outside the boundary. My father entered the
town ten years later." Silas touched the broken watch. "He
wanted to study the Stillness. He never came back."

"You entered after him?"

"Not exactly."

Silas explained that the Stillness had made Bellwether into a
knot. Days and years tangled around it. Most travelers who
approached found themselves walking in circles. Some emerged a few
moments after entering; others returned before they had arrived.

Silas had spent two years studying his father's notes. On
the anniversary of the tower's collapse, he had found a
narrow path through the knot. It led not to the
frozen Bellwether of his own day, but to the tower
twenty-three years earlier.

"To today," Mara said.

"To three days before First Frost."

The tower mechanism clicked around them.

Mara looked at the painted river. The iron needle trembled
over its golden bank.

Three days.

She had expected to spend them as she spent every
three days: winding, listening, ringing. Now they had become a
corridor leading to a locked door.

"What happened to your father?" she asked.

Silas lowered his eyes. "He's in the town. I saw
him once from beyond the boundary. He was standing near
the station."

"Unchanged?"

"Yes."

"Alive?"

"I think so."

"Then we have three days to prevent the Stillness."

Silas gave her a weary look. "Or cause it."

Mara brought him home.

Her cottage stood behind the hill, hidden from the town
by alder trees. Silas ate four bowls of stew and
fell asleep beside the stove. Mara remained at the table
with the future newspaper spread before her.

Most of its articles described the world beyond Bellwether. There
had been a war in the south, a flood along
the coast, and the invention of an engine that crossed
the sky. The language seemed familiar but subtly altered, as
though the words themselves had traveled.

On the second page she found a short article about
Silas.

MISSING SCHOLAR'S SON BELIEVED LOST

The photograph showed him older than he was now. Beneath
it was a statement from his aunt, expressing hope for
his return.

Mara folded the page before he woke.

The next morning, Silas accompanied her to the tower. He
watched her set the needle and wind the mechanism.

"You don't know how it works?" he asked.

"I know what to do."

"That isn't the same."

"It has always been enough."

"Until First Frost."

Mara disliked him for saying it, chiefly because he was
right.

After the morning bells, they searched the tower. Mara knew
every stair, stone, and gear, yet Silas noticed what she
had not: tiny letters carved around the pendulum's hinge.

They cleaned away a century of oil and dust.

The inscription read:

WHAT IS KEPT MUST ALSO BE GIVEN.

"Kept and given," Silas murmured. "Like a promise?"

"Or a secret."

"Or time."

Mara remembered her mother standing before the painted river.

The bell does not tell the time. The bell makes
the time.

They spent the day in the tower archives, a circular
chamber beneath the belfry. The oldest records had been written
by the first Venn keeper, Elian Venn, two hundred and
eleven years earlier.

Elian had arrived when Bellwether was only a gathering of
cottages in a valley where winter lasted most of the
year. His journal described failed harvests and children dying in
the cold. In the forest, he had discovered the bell
tower—already ancient, already waiting.

One passage had been cut from the book.

Mara held the damaged page near a lamp. Faint impressions
remained where the missing sheet had pressed against it. Silas
shaded the paper with charcoal, revealing broken lines.

A bargain with the river.

One life of years, freely offered.

The town shall have gentle seasons.

Each keeper to surrender what is owed.

At the bottom, in Elian's hurried hand, were six final
words:

If we refuse, the river stops.

Mara's mouth went dry.

"What does that mean?" Silas asked.

She did not answer.

Instead, she went to the mechanism and examined its base.
For years she had polished the engraved names of previous
keepers without considering the numbers beside them.

Elian Venn, 73.

Orra Venn, 81.

Camril Venn, 69.

Sera Venn, 76.

Lysa Venn, 58.

Her mother, Lysa, had not been fifty-eight when she died.
She had been seventy-one.

Mara had prepared the body herself. She remembered the deep
lines around her mother's eyes, her white hair, her thin,
spotted hands.

But everyone in Bellwether remembered Lysa as fifty-eight.

Mara ran a finger over the number. The brass was
warm.

"What is it?" Silas asked.

"The years they gave."

At sunset, the answer came.

As Mara reached for the rope, a compartment opened inside
the mechanism. She had seen it only once before, on
the last morning of her mother's life. Then, dazed with
grief, she had mistaken it for a broken panel.

Inside rested a small silver cup.

First Frost was not three days away.

It was two.

The time had changed.

Silas stepped back. "What goes in the cup?"

Mara knew.

She had known from the moment she saw it.

The keeper surrendered years so the town could continue. Not
all at once, but gradually. Each First Frost, the tower
claimed a measure of the keeper's future. One year, perhaps
more, poured invisibly into the river.

Her mother had been seventy-one but had died with only
fifty-eight years acknowledged by the tower. Thirteen years had been
given to Bellwether, vanishing from the memories of everyone except
the next keeper.

Mara had never used the cup.

Her mother had died in late spring, and First Frost
had not come since.

For twelve years, there had been no First Frost.

The tower had stretched summer and autumn, waiting for payment.
Mara, ignorant of the bargain, had set the needle farther
and farther upstream, preserving gentle days.

Now the debt had ripened.

That night, frost formed on the cottage windows.

Mara dreamed of her mother. Lysa sat at the kitchen
table, winding a ball of red yarn.

"Why didn't you tell me?" Mara asked.

"I did."

"No. You taught me to pull ropes and turn keys."

"I taught you what I could."

"What happens on First Frost?"

Her mother's hands continued winding. "You choose."

"Between what?"

"Time and time."

Mara woke before dawn.

Silas was gone.

She found his footprints leading up the hill. By the
time she reached the tower, he had opened the mechanism
with a tool from his satchel. The silver cup stood
on the floor.

"You can't take it," Mara said.

"I'm not taking it."

He held the broken pocket watch. Its back had been
removed, revealing a lock of dark hair wound around the
gears.

"My father made this. He said every watch contains a
little life because someone spends life making it. If the
tower needs years, perhaps it doesn't matter whose."

"No."

"I have years."

"They aren't yours to give."

"They are exactly mine to give."

"You came here to find your father."

"I came to save him."

Silas placed the watch in the cup.

For an instant, nothing happened. Then the hair inside the
watch turned white. Its metal casing corroded, collapsed, and became
dust.

The mechanism shuddered.

Outside, the eastern sky brightened and immediately darkened again.

The tower groaned. A crack shot across the stone floor.

Mara pulled Silas away as the cup leapt into the
air and struck the wall. The bell overhead gave a
harsh, broken cry.

In the town below, people shouted.

"You've damaged it," Mara said.

Silas stared at the ruined watch. "I thought—"

"You gave it an object. A memory. Not years."

"It was all I had of him."

The crack widened beneath their feet.

From deep inside the tower came the sound of rushing
water.

The painted river was moving.

Black waves rolled across the inner clock face, washing away
the golden landscape. As the river rose, Mara saw images
within it: Bellwether under snow, Bellwether in sunlight, Bellwether ruined
and overgrown. Faces surfaced and vanished. Children became adults, adults
became bones, and bones became trees.

Then she saw herself on First Frost morning.

In the vision, the tower broke. Mara, terrified, seized the
pendulum. The world stopped.

The image repeated.

Tower breaking. Hand reaching. Pendulum stopping.

"That's how it happens," Silas whispered. "You try to save
the town."

Mara watched another future appear. In this one, she placed
her hands around the silver cup. Her hair whitened. Her
body bent. The mechanism calmed, but only briefly. Numbers burned
across the face: twelve.

Twelve years owed.

If she paid them, she would become forty-nine in a
breath.

No, she realized. The tower did not want twelve years
of age.

It wanted twelve years of future.

There was a difference.

Her mother had surrendered thirteen years and died thirteen years
before her natural end. No one else remembered those stolen
years because, for Lysa, they had ceased to exist.

Mara thought of all the things she had postponed. Marriage.
Children. Travel. A red coat in the tailor's window. Learning
to play the violin. Seeing the ocean.

There had always been time.

A stone fell from the ceiling.

Silas grabbed her arm. "Tell me what to do."

"We have to ring the morning bell."

"It isn't morning."

"Morning hasn't known when to come for twelve years."

Mara climbed the stairs.

Wind tore through new cracks in the belfry. Below, townspeople
streamed up the hill, carrying lanterns. Above, clouds raced in
circles around the tower.

The bell waited in its wooden frame.

Mara took the rope. It felt alive, twisting against her
palms.

Silas appeared on the stairs. "If you pay the debt,
will the future change?"

"Yes."

"How do you know?"

"I don't."

She descended to the mechanism and lifted the silver cup.

It reflected no face, only the black river.

"What is kept must also be given," she said.

She thought of twelve lost years. They seemed vast when
imagined as seasons, but small when pictured as a row
of marks on brass. She could not know what they
contained. That was what made them valuable.

She might have loved someone.

She might have become a mother.

She might have lived quietly, changing nothing.

She might have been happy.

Mara closed her eyes.

"I give the tower what I owe."

The cup became cold.

Something passed through her—not pain, exactly, but absence. She remembered
events that had never happened: a kiss beneath summer rain,
a child asleep against her shoulder, blue water reaching to
the horizon. They flashed like sunlight on glass and were
gone before she could tell whether they were prophecies or
dreams.

Her knees struck the floor.

When she opened her eyes, her hands were older.

The mechanism stood still.

"Did it work?" Silas asked.

A new inscription appeared beneath the names of the dead
keepers.

Mara Venn, 37.

Not forty-nine.

Thirty-seven.

The tower had not added the years to her age.
It had taken them from the end.

Mara looked at Silas. "Ring the bell."

Together they climbed.

The rope had grown heavy. Silas wrapped both hands around
it, and Mara placed hers above his.

They pulled.

The bell swung.

Its voice rolled over Bellwether, through the streets and houses,
across fields silvered by sudden frost. It traveled beyond the
valley, beyond the roads, perhaps even beyond the years.

The clouds broke.

Dawn came like a flood.

Sunlight struck the tower, and every clock in Bellwether reappeared.

In kitchens and shops, on the station wall and above
the courthouse door, hands began moving across faces that had
been blank for generations. People cried out as twelve delayed
winters arrived in a single breath, not as cold, but
as memory. They remembered snow that had never fallen, harvests
that had never failed, days borrowed from a woman they
had trusted without understanding.

The cracks in the tower sealed.

The black river slowed.

Silas stood at the eastern window, staring down at the
station.

A man waited on the platform.

He was thin, bearded, and dressed in unfamiliar clothes. A
leather case hung from one hand. He looked up at
the tower as if he had heard his name.

"Father," Silas breathed.

He ran.

Mara followed more slowly. Each stair seemed steeper than before,
and a new weakness lived in her bones. Yet when
she stepped outside, the air tasted sharper than it ever
had.

Silas reached the platform just as the man turned.

For a moment, neither moved.

Then the man dropped his case.

Silas crossed the remaining distance and collided with him. His
father sank to his knees, holding the boy so tightly
that Mara had to look away.

The townspeople gathered around her.

Master Pell removed his spectacles. The baker's twins stared. The
mayor opened his mouth, but Mara silenced him with a
glance.

"What time is it?" someone finally asked.

Mara looked toward the courthouse clock.

Its hands pointed to a little after seven.

She almost laughed. Bellwether had never had seven in the
morning before. It had only had the first bell, breakfast
smoke, and light touching the western roofs.

"Seven minutes past," she said.

"Past what?"

"Past the hour."

The crowd considered this strange new idea.

That evening, Mara did not ring the closing bell.

She stood in the square while the courthouse clock marked
the hour without her. All over town, people watched their
restored clocks. Some distrusted them. Others delighted in them, announcing
each minute as it passed.

Silas and his father came to find her.

The man introduced himself as Elias Vale. For him, only
four days had passed since he entered the boundary. For
Silas, it had been two years. They did not know
which era waited beyond the valley now.

"Come with us," Silas said. "We'll find out together."

Mara looked up at the tower.

The bell would still need a keeper. The seasons would
still need guidance, though perhaps not control. She sensed that
the old bargain had changed. By paying the debt freely,
she had ended the inheritance of sacrifice. The mechanism's new
inscription bore no number of years surrendered, only her age
when she chose.

Still, she could not leave that night.

"Ask me in spring," she said.

Silas smiled. "When will that be?"

Mara listened to the ticking sounds drifting from every house.
Beyond the rooftops, frost glittered in the fields. Winter waited
at the valley's edge, no longer an enemy, merely a
season that had been denied too long.

"When it comes," she said.

Years later, travelers passing through Bellwether often remarked upon its
clocks. There were hundreds of them by then: elaborate clocks,
plain clocks, watches on chains, painted sundials, and little wooden
birds that announced the hours. The townspeople loved time with
the enthusiasm of those who had once mistaken order for
abundance.

Yet the tower bell rang only twice each year.

In spring, it welcomed what was beginning.

On First Frost, it honored what must end.

Mara remained its keeper for a long while, though not
as long as she might have. She did buy the
red coat. She learned three songs on the violin and
played all of them badly. She saw the ocean once
with Silas, who grew into a scholar and wrote a
book no one in Bellwether entirely believed.

She never married and never had a child. Sometimes she
dreamed of a life in which she had done both.
She would wake grieving people who had never existed, then
rise, open the window, and listen to the honest ticking
of the clock beside her bed.

When her final winter came, the whole town climbed the
hill.

Mara was old, though not very old. Snow rested in
her silver hair. Silas stood beside her, no longer a
boy, holding the rope they had once pulled together.

"Are you afraid?" he asked.

"Yes," she said. "But fear is only love looking at
a closed door."

At sunset, she rang the bell.

Its voice crossed the valley, deep and clear. Clocks paused
to hear it. Children fell silent. Birds lifted from the
bare trees.

Mara listened until the note faded.

Then she smiled, because the silence afterward was not the
Stillness. It was not trapped or empty. It held footsteps,
breath, falling snow, and the small persistent movement of hands
around a clock face.

It was time continuing without her.

And that, at last, was the gift.

Mara Vale first heard the bell beneath the sea on
the morning the lighthouse went dark.

It was a low, patient sound, too deep to belong
to any church in Greyhaven. The note passed through the
cliffs, through the village walls, through the iron frame of
Mara's narrow bed. Cups trembled on shelves. Gulls rose from
the harbor in a white spiral.

Then silence returned.

Mara opened her eyes and stared at the ceiling.

Her grandfather was already awake. She heard him moving downstairs,
dragging a chair across the kitchen floor.

"Grandfather?" she called.

No answer.

She dressed quickly and found him standing by the western
window, his silver hair uncombed, his hands gripping the back
of the chair. Across the bay, Blackstone Lighthouse stood on
its island like a broken tooth. Its lamp should have
been shining, even after dawn. Instead, the tower was blind.

"The bell," Mara said. "Did you hear it?"

Her grandfather nodded.

"What was it?"

"Trouble."

He crossed the kitchen, locked the door, then drew the
curtains although the sun had begun to rise.

Mara frowned. "Shouldn't we go to the harbor? Mr. Thorne
may need help."

"No one is going to Blackstone."

"But the lamp—"

"No one," he repeated.

Outside, doors opened along the village street. People gathered in
coats and shawls, pointing toward the island. Fishermen left their
boats tied to the pier. The harbor master shouted orders,
but nobody obeyed him.

Mara watched through a gap in the curtain. "They're afraid."

"They should be."

Her grandfather opened a wooden cupboard and removed a long
bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Mara had seen it once before,
many years ago, after her mother's funeral. He placed it
on the table and unfolded the cloth.

Inside lay a brass key, green with age.

It was almost as long as Mara's hand. Its handle
had been fashioned into the shape of an eye.

Her grandfather touched it reluctantly.

"The bell beneath the sea rings only when the gate
is open," he said.

Mara waited.

Greyhaven was full of legends, most of them designed to
frighten children away from cliffs, caves, strangers, storms, wells, forests,
and midnight. Her grandfather had told her none of them.
He disliked stories. He said they made people careless with
the truth.

"What gate?" she asked.

"The one under Blackstone."

He sat and gestured for her to do the same.

Long before Greyhaven had a name, he told her, the
people of the coast discovered a city beneath the bay.
At the lowest tide of the longest winter, towers could
be seen under the water, and streets paved with white
stone appeared between the waves.

The drowned city was older than any kingdom. No one
knew who had built it. Its windows were narrow and
tall, its doors enormous. In the center stood a bronze
gate leading into the earth.

The first villagers opened it.

Only three survived.

They sealed the gate and built Blackstone Lighthouse above it,
not merely to guide ships, but to keep watch. A
keeper remained on the island at all times. The brass
key passed from one guardian to another.

"And now Mr. Thorne has failed," Mara said.

"Or something has happened to him."

"Why do you have the key?"

Her grandfather looked at her. In the dim kitchen, his
face seemed suddenly older.

"Because I was the keeper before Thorne."

Mara remembered how rarely he looked toward the island. She
remembered the pale scars around his wrists and the nights
when he woke shouting words in a language she did
not understand.

"You never told me."

"I hoped I would never need to."

The bell sounded again.

This time a hairline crack appeared in the western window.

Her grandfather wrapped the key and tucked it inside his
coat.

"Stay here," he said.

"You can barely climb the hill to the market."

"I can still row."

"In calm water, perhaps. Have you seen the bay?"

Beyond the houses, the sea moved in conflicting directions. Waves
traveled east while the wind blew west. Dark circles widened
around Blackstone Island.

"I said stay here."

"And I heard you."

Mara took her red woolen hat from its peg.

He glared at her. "Your mother had exactly that expression."

"Did telling her to stay ever work?"

"Not once."

"Then we've saved ourselves an argument."

They left by the back door.

At the harbor, nearly everyone in Greyhaven had gathered on
the pier. Harbor Master Pell stood aboard his launch, begging
for volunteers. None came forward.

When Mara and her grandfather approached, the crowd fell silent.

Old Nessa Ward spat into the water. "Elias Vale," she
said. "I knew this day would come."

"Then you might have warned someone," Mara's grandfather replied.

"And be called mad?"

"You're called mad already."

Nessa smiled without humor. "Fair enough."

Harbor Master Pell stepped onto the pier. He was a
heavy man with a red beard and a voice built
for storms.

"Elias, what's happening out there?"

"The light is out."

"I have eyes."

"Then use them to keep everyone ashore."

Pell noticed the oilcloth bundle. His face lost its color.
"Is that what I think it is?"

"Yes."

A murmur passed through the crowd.

Mara looked from face to face. They knew. Perhaps not
the whole story, but enough. Greyhaven's adults had kept the
truth hidden under ordinary life, beneath fish markets, weddings, leaking
roofs, and summer festivals.

"You all knew about the gate," Mara said.

No one answered.

Her grandfather climbed down into a small rowing boat.

Mara followed.

"You can't mean to cross in that," Pell said. "Take
the launch."

"Engines won't work near the island now."

"I'll come with you."

"No. Keep the harbor clear. If we don't return before
sunset, take everyone inland. Go as far as you can."

Pell gripped the edge of the pier. "And if we
hear the bell again?"

"Don't listen."

Her grandfather pushed away before Pell could ask how.

The current seized them at once.

Mara rowed while her grandfather steered. She had crossed the
bay a hundred times, but that morning distance had become
unreliable. Blackstone Island seemed close enough to touch, then impossibly
far away. Once, Mara looked back and saw Greyhaven upside
down in the sky, its chimneys descending into pale clouds.
When she blinked, it was normal again.

"Don't trust your eyes," her grandfather warned.

"What should I trust?"

"The oars."

Something struck the underside of the boat.

Mara froze.

A shape passed beneath them, longer than a fishing vessel.
She saw a row of pale lights under the water
and realized, with a jolt, that they were windows. A
roof emerged between the waves, covered in shells and black
weeds. It remained visible for a moment, then sank.

The drowned city was rising.

The bell rang a third time.

The sea became still.

Not calm—still. The oars rested on water as hard and
smooth as glass. All around them, shadows sharpened below the
surface: towers, bridges, domes, courtyards. The drowned city stretched across
the floor of the bay.

Mara leaned over the side.

Far below, something moved along a white street.

"Row," her grandfather whispered.

"The water—"

"Row!"

She pulled. The glassy surface broke like a thin sheet
of ice, and the boat lurched forward. Behind them, long
fingers rose through the crack.

Mara did not look down again.

They reached Blackstone Island near noon.

The lighthouse door stood open. Deep scratches marked the black
rock around it. Her grandfather took an iron knife from
beneath his coat and entered first.

"Mr. Thorne?" Mara called.

The circular ground-floor room was empty. A kettle rested on
a cold stove. A chair lay overturned. On the wall,
the lighthouse clock had stopped at thirteen minutes past three.

A wet trail crossed the floor and disappeared up the
stairs.

Her grandfather touched it with one finger. "Seawater."

"Maybe a wave came through the door."

"No."

Mara swallowed. "Of course not."

They climbed.

The wet footprints appeared on the next landing. They were
human in shape, but twice the size of a man's
feet and webbed between the toes.

In the keeper's bedroom, they found Mr. Thorne.

He sat upright on the bed, facing the wall. His
clothes dripped seawater. His white beard was tangled with green
weed.

"Mr. Thorne?" Mara said.

His head turned.

He had no eyes. In their place were two small,
shining shells.

Her grandfather pushed Mara behind him.

Thorne smiled. "Elias."

His voice sounded like pebbles dragged by the tide.

"What did you do?" Mara's grandfather asked.

"I grew tired."

"You opened the gate."

"For thirty-seven years, I kept the lamp. Thirty-seven years of
storms and fog and voices under the floor. They promised
me rest."

"They lie."

"Yes." Thorne's smile widened. "I know that now."

He stood.

His body unfolded to an unnatural height. Water poured from
his sleeves. Beneath his soaked shirt, something shifted as though
many small creatures were crawling inside him.

"Where is the lock?" Elias demanded.

"Below."

"Where below?"

Thorne lifted one finger and pointed down.

The tower shook.

From beneath the floor came a vast metallic groan.

"Too late," said Thorne. "The king is waking."

He lunged.

Mara's grandfather raised the iron knife, but Thorne struck him
aside. Elias crashed into the wall and fell.

Mara snatched the kettle from the stove and swung it
with both hands. It hit Thorne's head with a dull
clang. He staggered, more surprised than hurt.

The shells in his eye sockets turned toward her.

"Little Vale," he whispered.

Mara hit him again.

This time one shell cracked. A stream of black water
spilled down his cheek. Thorne shrieked and stumbled backward into
the stairwell. He fell without attempting to catch himself.

They listened as his body struck step after step.

Then all was quiet.

Mara helped her grandfather sit up. Blood ran from a
cut above his eyebrow.

"You were told to stay home," he muttered.

"You're welcome."

He almost smiled.

At the top of the tower, the lamp chamber was
dark. The great lens stood motionless. Its central flame had
been extinguished, but the chamber smelled not of smoke, but
of salt and decay.

Mara examined the mechanism. "Can we relight it?"

"The lamp is only part of the seal." Her grandfather
went to the north wall and pressed his palm against
the stones. "Help me find a latch."

They searched until Mara discovered a metal loop hidden behind
a support beam. Together they pulled it. A section of
floor opened, revealing steps that spiraled downward inside the lighthouse
wall.

A breath of cold air rose from the darkness.

"The tower stairs go up," Mara said.

"These go where they must."

They descended past the bedroom, past the kitchen, past the
foundations of the lighthouse. The stone changed from rough black
rock to smooth white blocks carved with unfamiliar letters.

At the bottom, they reached the drowned city.

An invisible barrier held back the sea. Water arched above
the streets like a dark glass roof. Fish swam overhead.
Blackstone Lighthouse rose behind them, its foundations merging with an
ancient tower.

Mara stared.

The city was enormous. Pale buildings leaned over silent avenues.
Statues stood in empty squares, their faces scratched away. At
the end of the widest street loomed the bronze gate.

It was open.

Beyond it lay no chamber, no tunnel, only darkness threaded
with dim blue light.

The bell hung above the gate. No rope was attached
to it, yet it began to move.

Mara clapped her hands over her ears.

The sound passed through her bones.

Shapes stirred in the buildings. Doors opened. Tall figures stepped
into the street, draped in rotting robes. Some had too
many arms. Others walked on jointed limbs. All turned toward
Mara and her grandfather.

Elias unwrapped the brass key.

"We have to reach the gate," he said.

"How?"

"Quickly."

They ran.

The drowned inhabitants followed.

At first they moved slowly, as if remembering how. Then
their pace increased. Claws clicked on the white stones. Something
screamed from a rooftop.

Mara and Elias crossed a square where hundreds of stone
faces lay arranged in circles. As Mara passed, their mouths
opened.

"Mara," they whispered.

One spoke in her mother's voice.

She stopped.

At the center of the square stood a woman in
a blue dress, her dark hair tied back with a
silver ribbon. She looked exactly as Mara remembered her, though
twelve years had passed since the fever took her.

"Mara," the woman said. "Come here."

Elias grabbed Mara's arm.

"That isn't her."

The woman's expression crumpled. "Father, how can you say that?"

Elias flinched.

Behind the woman, the pursuing figures entered the square.

"She knew you'd bring me," Mara's mother said. "She knew
because you always put duty before love."

"Keep walking," Elias said, but his voice shook.

"You let me die."

"No."

"You had the key. You knew what waited below. You
could have asked the old king to save me."

"And what price would he have taken?"

The woman smiled. Her teeth were thin and pointed.

"Only the light."

Mara pulled her grandfather onward.

Her mother's voice followed them. It changed as they fled—first
pleading, then angry, then deep and inhuman.

They reached the bronze gate.

Up close, Mara saw thousands of figures engraved across it.
They depicted the drowned city before the sea covered it:
crowds kneeling, a dark shape descending from the sky, people
tearing out their own eyes in worship.

A keyhole waited at the center.

Her grandfather inserted the brass key and turned.

Nothing happened.

He tried again.

The key would not move.

On the other side of the gate, the darkness gathered.
Blue lights opened one by one, each the size of
a shield.

Eyes, Mara thought.

A voice came from beyond the threshold.

ELIAS VALE.

The bronze gate trembled.

YOU RETURNED.

Elias gripped the key. "Mara, go back to the lighthouse."

The creatures filled the street behind them. Mara recognized Thorne
among them, his broken shell-eye dangling against his cheek.

"I'm not leaving you."

"The key needs a keeper."

"You're the keeper."

"Not anymore."

He showed her his hand.

Dark veins spread from his fingertips, where he had touched
Thorne's seawater. The skin was already becoming translucent.

Mara shook her head.

"I knew before we left the tower," he said. "It
moves quickly."

"No."

"I can hold them off long enough for you to
seal the gate."

"You said the key doesn't work."

"It does. But it knows I'm no longer fit to
carry it."

The darkness beyond the gate pressed closer. A shape became
visible: a vast head crowned with antlers, fins, and ruined
towers. Its skin was the color of the deepest ocean.

CHILD, it said.

The city shook.

I CAN RETURN WHAT YOU HAVE LOST.

Mara looked at the key. "What do I do?"

"Take it."

She closed her hand around the brass handle.

Pain shot up her arm.

Suddenly she saw every keeper who had held it: men
and women standing in storms, tending the lighthouse flame, descending
into the city. She saw her grandfather as a young
man. She saw her mother beside him, laughing as she
polished the great lens.

Then she saw Thorne open the gate.

He had not done it willingly. For years the drowned
king had sent dreams into the lighthouse, showing him his
dead wife waiting beneath the sea. On the final night,
Thorne had broken. He turned the key and learned that
promises made in darkness vanish when brought to light.

The vision ended.

The key turned.

With a roar, the bronze doors began to close.

The drowned creatures rushed forward.

Elias drew his iron knife. "Go!"

He met the first creature at the threshold. The knife
flashed, and the thing collapsed in a tangle of limbs.
Another seized his shoulder. He drove the blade into its
neck.

Mara held the key with both hands.

The doors moved slowly. Too slowly.

The drowned king pushed one enormous hand through the narrowing
gap. Its fingers sank into the street, cracking stone.

MARA VALE, it said inside her mind.

Her mother appeared beyond the gate. She stood in sunlight
now, healthy and warm, holding out her arms.

You have been lonely for so long.

Mara's grip loosened.

She remembered winter evenings after her mother died, when her
grandfather sat silently by the fire because neither of them
knew how to speak about grief. She remembered waking from
dreams and almost calling for someone who was no longer
there.

I can give you every stolen year.

The gate stopped.

"Mara!" Elias shouted.

The creatures dragged him to his knees.

Her mother stepped closer. "Please," she said. "I've been waiting."

Mara began to turn the key backward.

Then she noticed the silver ribbon.

Her mother had hated silver. She always wore red.

"You don't remember her," Mara whispered.

The thing wearing her mother's face tilted its head.

"You found her in me, but you don't remember her."

Mara turned the key with all her strength.

The bronze doors slammed shut.

A shock wave raced through the city. Windows shattered. The
invisible roof above the streets rippled. The drowned creatures fell,
shrieking, and dissolved into torrents of black water.

Thorne remained longest.

For a moment his true face returned. He looked at
Elias, then at Mara.

"Keep the light," he said.

Then he was gone.

The bell cracked from top to bottom.

Silence fell.

Mara ran to her grandfather. The dark veins had reached
his neck, but they were fading.

"You said it moved quickly," she said.

"It seems the king's influence weakens when the gate is
shut."

"So you knew you might survive?"

"I suspected."

"You sent me away."

"I was trying to be noble."

"You were being an idiot."

"That, too."

The city groaned around them.

A thin stream of seawater curled down from the invisible
roof.

"We should go," Elias said.

They climbed the hidden stair as the barrier failed. Water
chased them upward, flooding the lower steps. They reached the
lighthouse kitchen just as the secret door slammed shut behind
them.

Outside, the sea had begun to rise.

Mara and Elias climbed to the lamp chamber. The mechanism
still would not move. Rust clogged the gears, and black
weed twisted around the central shaft.

Together they tore it free.

Mara cleaned the fuel line while Elias adjusted the gears.
The water climbed the lighthouse stairs below them. First it
filled the kitchen. Then it reached the bedroom.

"Hurry," Elias said.

"I am hurrying."

The sea struck the trapdoor beneath the lamp chamber.

Mara opened the fuel valve. Elias handed her a match.

The first would not light.

Neither would the second.

Water burst around the edges of the trapdoor.

Mara struck the final match against the stone floor. A
small yellow flame appeared.

She touched it to the wick.

Fire blossomed in the heart of the lens.

The gears turned.

Slowly, the great glass lens began to rotate, sending a
brilliant beam across the bay. Wherever the light passed, the
unnatural water fell away. The circles around the island vanished.
The hidden rooftops sank. The sea released a long breath.

Far across the bay, the lamps of Greyhaven flickered in
answer.

By sunset, a rescue boat reached the island.

Harbor Master Pell climbed into the lamp chamber and stared
at the broken trapdoor, the flooded stairs, and Elias's bruised
face.

"I suppose," he said, "there's an explanation."

"No," Elias replied.

Pell looked at Mara.

She held up the brass key, now bright and warm
in her hand.

"There is," she said. "But you won't like it."

In the weeks that followed, Greyhaven returned to its ordinary
habits. Fishermen mended nets. Children raced along the pier. Old
Nessa told anyone who listened that she had predicted everything,
though her predictions changed with each telling.

Mr. Thorne's name was carved on the memorial stone above
the harbor. Beneath it, at Mara's request, they added five
words:

HE KEPT THE LIGHT UNTIL DAWN.

Elias moved back to Blackstone Island. Mara went with him.

"You don't have to stay," he told her as they
carried boxes into the keeper's quarters.

"Yes, I do."

"You could train someone else."

"I will. Several people. No more solitary keepers listening to
voices for thirty-seven years."

"Tradition—"

"Tradition nearly drowned Greyhaven."

Elias considered this. "Your mother used to say the same
sort of thing."

"About ancient gates?"

"About me."

They repaired the lighthouse over the summer. Harbor Master Pell
installed a radio and three emergency engines. Nessa sent curtains,
all of them different colors. The village children visited on
calm days, and Mara taught them to polish the lens.

She told them the truth.

Not all of it, and never at night, but enough.

She told them that the lighthouse was more than a
guide for ships. She told them there were doors in
the world that should remain closed, and promises that should
not be trusted simply because they were spoken in the
voice of someone loved.

Years passed.

Elias grew slower, while Mara became the keeper in fact
as well as name. Each evening she climbed the tower
and lit the lamp. The beam swept across the water,
steady and gold.

Sometimes, in winter, she saw pale towers beneath the waves.

Sometimes she dreamed of a bronze gate and blue eyes
opening in darkness.

Once, many years after the gate had closed, the bell
rang again.

Only once.

Mara woke before dawn. She was no longer young. Her
hair had begun to silver at the temples, and a
new apprentice slept in the room below.

She climbed to the lamp chamber and looked across the
bay.

The sea was calm. Greyhaven shone beneath the stars. The
lighthouse flame burned strongly.

On the table lay the brass key.

A drop of seawater rested in the center of its
eye-shaped handle.

Mara wiped it away.

Then she turned the lamp toward the darkest part of
the sea and kept it there until morning.

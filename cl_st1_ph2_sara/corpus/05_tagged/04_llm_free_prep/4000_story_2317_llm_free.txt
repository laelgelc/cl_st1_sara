## The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, Mara Vale climbed the east tower
of Bellwether Station and wound the great brass clock.

The task took forty-three turns of an iron key. No
more, no fewer. At the forty-third turn, the clock shuddered
awake, its gears caught one another, and the black hands
began their patient journey around the white face high above
the sea.

Bellwether Station had once been a railway terminal. That was
before the cliffs collapsed, taking the tracks and half the
old town into the water. Now the station stood at
the edge of the land with its platforms pointing toward
empty air. Gulls nested beneath the rusted awnings. Salt covered
the ticket windows. When the tide was low, the rails
could be seen under the waves, shining like ladders laid
across the seabed.

No trains had arrived in thirty-seven years.

Still, Mara kept the clock.

She kept it because her father had kept it, and
his father before him. She kept it because the townspeople
wanted to hear the bell strike each hour, though few
of them admitted listening. Most of all, she kept it
because the clock did not merely tell time.

It remembered what time had taken.

On the first morning of each month, when Mara opened
the narrow maintenance door beneath the clock face, she found
something that had not been there before: a lost object
resting among the gears.

Once, it was a child's blue mitten. Another time, a
silver wedding ring engraved with the words *Until the sea
runs dry*. She had found keys, spectacles, coins from vanished
countries, a soldier's medal, a lock of red hair tied
with thread, and hundreds of letters whose ink had faded
before she could read them.

Her father had taught her never to remove these things.

"They're anchors," he had said. "Memories need weight, or they
drift away."

Mara had been twelve then, impatient and thin-kneed, with the
wind forever tangling her hair.

"What happens if they drift?"

"People forget."

"Forget what?"

Her father had looked toward the clock's turning gears.

"Whatever the hour wants them to forget."

Mara had not understood. At twelve, forgetting seemed harmless. It
was what happened to homework, umbrellas, and names heard at
dull parties. She learned differently after her father died.

He disappeared on a clear afternoon in March. Mara had
gone downstairs to make tea, leaving him oiling the clock's
central axle. When she returned, the oil can lay on
its side, but her father was gone.

No one in town remembered him.

The baker denied that Mara's father had bought bread from
her every Sunday for twenty years. The mayor insisted Mara
had always lived alone. Even the family photographs changed. In
every picture, an empty space appeared beside Mara, a curtain
or tree or shadow filling the place where her father
had stood.

Only Mara remembered.

Inside the clock, caught between two small gears, she found
his pocket watch.

She left it there.

For eleven years, Mara had wound the clock and listened
for her father's footsteps in the tower. She heard only
gears, wind, and the sea striking the cliffs below.

Then, one November morning, the clock gave her a train
ticket.

It waited inside the maintenance door on a ledge dusted
with iron filings. The ticket was stiff, cream-colored, and perfectly
dry. Across the front, in faded green ink, were printed
the words:

**BELLWETHER TO LASTLIGHT**
**DEPARTURE: 11:59 P.M.**
**PLATFORM 3**
**PASSENGER: ELIAS VALE**

Mara read it three times.

Elias was her father's name.

Behind her, the clock ticked with the heavy rhythm of
a distant engine.

She carried the ticket downstairs.

It was the first object she had ever taken from
the mechanism.

At once, the station bell rang.

The sound rolled across the town, deep enough to shake
dust from the rafters. It rang once, though the time
was six seventeen. Out on the sea, every gull rose
from the water together.

Mara closed her hand around the ticket.

Nothing else happened.

By breakfast, however, she had forgotten the color of her
father's eyes.

She stood in the station kitchen, staring at the kettle
while steam clouded the window. Brown, she thought. No, gray.
Perhaps blue. Panic tightened around her ribs.

She ran upstairs and returned the ticket to the clock.

His eyes came back to her at once: green, with
amber around the pupils.

"An anchor," she whispered.

The clock continued ticking.

Mara did not sleep that night. She sat in the
old waiting room beneath a broken timetable, watching moonlight move
across rows of wooden benches. Platform 3 lay beyond the
tall windows. Weeds grew between the paving stones, and the
track ended at the collapsed cliff.

At eleven fifty, she climbed the tower and took the
ticket again.

This time she brought her father's pocket watch with it.

The effect was immediate. His face blurred in her memory.
She could no longer hear his voice. Even the knowledge
that he was her father became strangely fragile, like a
word repeated until it lost meaning.

Mara pressed both objects to her heart.

"I remember you," she said, though the sentence felt less
certain with every breath.

At eleven fifty-nine, a light appeared beneath the sea.

It began as a pale glow far below the cliff.
Then it rose, swelling through the black water. The submerged
rails shone. Waves drew back from them in silver curtains,
and a railway line emerged from the deep, streaming foam.

A whistle sounded.

A train climbed out of the ocean.

Its engine was black and gleaming, crowned with white steam.
Water poured from its wheels but never seemed to touch
the ground. Behind it came seven carriages, their windows lit
gold. The train moved along the drowned tracks, reached the
broken cliff, and continued over empty air as though the
missing rails remained beneath it.

It stopped at Platform 3.

The station doors, locked for decades, opened by themselves.

Mara stepped onto the platform.

A conductor stood beside the first carriage. He wore a
dark blue uniform cut in an old style, with brass
buttons and a cap pulled low over his eyes. He
was very tall, and seawater dripped steadily from his sleeves.

"Ticket," he said.

Mara offered the cream-colored card.

The conductor examined it. "This is not yours."

"It belonged to my father."

"Then he is expected."

"Where?"

"Lastlight."

"What is that?"

"A terminus."

"Is he alive?"

The conductor tilted his head. Somewhere beneath his cap, Mara
saw the faint shine of pearl.

"No passenger aboard this train is alive in the ordinary
sense," he said. "Nor are they dead in the useful
sense."

"I want to see him."

"Then you must travel."

Mara glanced at the station clock. Its hands had stopped
one minute before midnight.

"What's the fare?"

"One hour."

"An hour of what?"

"Your life."

Mara almost laughed. "Only one?"

"One hour for each station. Bellwether to Lastlight is a
considerable distance."

"How many stations?"

The conductor smiled without warmth.

"That depends on what you are willing to forget."

The doors of the train began to close.

Mara thought of her father's green eyes. She thought of
eleven years winding the clock, of speaking to a man
who existed nowhere but inside her memory. Then she stepped
aboard.

The carriage smelled of coal smoke and rain.

Passengers sat in red velvet seats beneath brass lamps. An
old woman held a cracked porcelain bowl. A young soldier
carried a muddy violin. A little boy embraced a wooden
bird with only one wing. None of them spoke. Some
had faces Mara felt she ought to recognize, though she
was certain she had never seen them before.

The conductor punched her ticket.

A jolt passed through her body.

For one hour, she saw herself at nine years old,
lying in summer grass while her father pointed out cloud
shapes. Then the memory vanished.

Mara gasped.

The conductor handed back the ticket. A small round hole
had appeared beside Bellwether's name.

"Fare collected," he said.

"That was not an hour of my future."

"No."

"You took an hour I already lived."

"All hours are already lived somewhere."

The train moved.

Outside the windows, Bellwether Station slid away. The drowned rails
descended beneath the waves, but darkness did not swallow the
carriage. Fish flashed past the glass. Forests of seaweed bent
in the current. The rooftops of the sunken town appeared
below, chimneys and church spires silver with moonlight.

The old woman across the aisle looked at Mara.

"First journey?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Keep hold of why you came."

"I came for my father."

The woman's expression softened. "Then say his name at every
stop."

"Why?"

"Because the train likes empty seats."

The whistle cried, and the carriage lights dimmed.

Their first stop was a platform built in the middle
of a wheat field. Dawn stretched across the horizon, though
it had been midnight moments before. A sign read **YESTERDAY'S
END**.

Doors opened.

The little boy with the wooden bird stood and walked
toward them.

"Don't," Mara said.

He looked at her calmly. "My mother is here."

On the platform, a woman waited among the wheat, her
arms open.

The boy ran to her. As soon as they embraced,
both became a flock of blackbirds and scattered into the
brightening sky.

The doors shut.

"Elias Vale," Mara whispered.

The train entered a tunnel.

The next station hung over a city of glass. Rain
fell upward from the streets and vanished into the clouds.
**UNSAID**, the sign declared.

Here the soldier rose with his violin.

"No," the old woman said, gripping his sleeve. "Not yet."

"I hear them calling my name."

"That is not your name."

But the soldier pulled away. When he stepped onto the
platform, every window in the glass city shattered. The noise
became a thousand voices speaking all the apologies he had
never made.

He fell to his knees.

The train left him there.

"Elias Vale," Mara said again.

At each station, the conductor collected another hour.

She lost the morning her father taught her to swim.
She lost a winter evening when they burned supper and
ate apples instead. She lost the hour after his funeral,
though no one else had attended. She lost the memory
of sitting on his shoulders during the midsummer parade. With
each vanished hour, his face grew less familiar.

Between stations, Mara asked the other passengers why they traveled.

The old woman had lost her daughter to a fever
sixty years before.

"She was seven," the woman said. "Everyone who knew her
is gone now. Everyone except me."

"What happens when you die?"

"My daughter dies completely."

Mara looked at the cracked bowl in the woman's lap.

"Was that hers?"

"She made it. Badly." The old woman smiled. "It leaks."

"Why take it to Lastlight?"

"I heard lost things can be returned there."

"Who told you?"

"The clock keeper at Bellwether."

Mara stared at her. "My father?"

"A tall man, green eyes. Very kind."

"When?"

"Many years ago. Or next week. Time is poorly behaved
on this route."

"Did he travel with you?"

"No. But he told me the way."

Hope, painful and sudden, rose in Mara's throat.

At the next stop, the platform was empty except for
a white door standing upright with no wall around it.
The sign read **IF ONLY**.

The old woman began to weep.

"My daughter is behind that door."

"How do you know?"

"I can hear her."

Mara heard nothing, but the woman stood.

"Remember what you told me," Mara said. "Keep hold of
why you came."

"I came for her."

"No. You came to return the bowl."

The old woman looked confused. "What bowl?"

The cracked porcelain had vanished from her lap.

"No," Mara whispered.

The conductor stood at the far end of the carriage,
holding the bowl in one dripping hand.

"She has forgotten her anchor," he said.

"You took it."

"I collect abandoned luggage."

The white door opened.

Warm light spilled across the platform. A child laughed beyond
it.

The old woman stepped off the train.

Mara seized her hand.

"What was your daughter's name?"

The woman's eyes widened with terror.

"I—I don't know."

The train lurched. Mara lost her grip, and the old
woman fell onto the platform. The white door closed behind
her.

A moment later, the station was gone.

Mara turned on the conductor. "Give me the bowl."

"She no longer requires it."

"You made her forget."

"I collect fares. Forgetting is merely the shape payment takes."

He placed the bowl inside his coat.

Mara wanted to strike him, but something in his stillness
frightened her. Instead, she reached into her pocket and touched
the watch and ticket.

"Elias Vale," she said.

The name sounded foreign.

"How much farther?"

"Three stations."

"And how many hours will you take?"

"Three, if you remain aboard."

"How many have you taken already?"

"Eight."

Eight hours. Such a small sum. Yet those hours had
contained almost everything she remembered clearly about her father.

"What happens at Lastlight?"

"You will be offered a choice."

"What choice?"

"The same choice offered to every keeper."

Before Mara could ask more, the train entered darkness.

Not a tunnel. Not night. True darkness, vast and absolute,
pressed against the windows. The lamps went out one by
one. The train slowed.

Mara heard whispering beneath the wheels.

Names, she realized. Thousands of names.

Some were spoken tenderly, some in grief, some as curses.
They rose around the carriage until the air trembled.

Then one voice spoke close to her ear.

"Mara."

Her father's voice.

She stood so quickly her knees struck the seat.

"Papa?"

The dark window reflected a man sitting beside her.

He looked older than she remembered. His hair was white,
and deep lines crossed his face, but his eyes were
green with amber around the pupils.

"Mara," the reflection said. "Get off at the next station."

She turned. The seat beside her was empty.

"Where are you?"

"Lastlight."

"Then why should I get off?"

"Because Lastlight is a trap."

The train whistle screamed. The reflection flickered.

Her father leaned closer from the other side of the
glass.

"The clock does not preserve memories," he said. "It feeds
on them."

The carriage lights burst back to life, and his image
disappeared.

The conductor stood in the aisle.

"Passengers must not converse with the exterior," he said.

"That was my father."

"That was a possibility wearing his face."

"He said the clock feeds on memories."

"Clocks consume everything eventually."

"What are you?"

"An employee."

"Of whom?"

The conductor looked toward the front of the train.

"The Keeper of Lost Hours."

The next station appeared ahead. It was a plain wooden
platform beside a quiet river. A cottage stood among willow
trees, smoke rising from its chimney. The sign bore no
name.

The train stopped.

Through the cottage window, Mara saw herself at a table.
She was older, perhaps forty. A dark-haired woman stood behind
her with one hand on her shoulder. Two children played
on the floor.

There was peace in that room. A life Mara had
never lived, waiting with terrible patience.

"Disembark here," the conductor said, "and the clock will release
you."

"Is it real?"

"Real enough."

"Will I remember my father?"

"No."

The cottage door opened. The older Mara looked toward the
train and smiled.

For one moment, Mara wanted to step down. She wanted
mornings that did not begin with iron keys. She wanted
to be known by people who did not forget her.
She wanted years untouched by salt, gears, or ghosts.

Then she drew out her father's watch.

Its hands had begun to move backward.

"No," she said.

The doors closed.

The conductor punched her ticket again.

Mara lost the last breakfast she had eaten with her
father. She remembered the table and the sunlight, but not
what he had said. Soon she could not remember whether
he had been happy that morning.

"Elias Vale," she murmured.

The conductor moved on.

The second-to-last station was called **THE HOUR BEFORE**.

Everyone who had ever been forgotten seemed to wait there.

The platform stretched beyond sight, crowded with men, women, and
children in clothes from every age. Some held flowers. Some
carried suitcases. Some stood empty-handed, faces lifted toward the passing
windows.

Mara recognized the baker's husband, who had vanished at sea.
She recognized the mayor's elder brother, though the mayor claimed
to be an only child. She recognized schoolmates, neighbors, and
strangers whose portraits had been emptied by the clock.

Near the end of the platform stood her father.

Not a reflection. Not a voice. A man of flesh
or something close to it.

He ran beside the slowing train.

"Mara!"

She forced open the window.

"Papa!"

His face changed when he heard the word—joy, then horror.

"You remember me?"

"Of course."

"Listen. At Lastlight, it will ask you to become the
next Keeper. You must refuse."

"How do I bring you home?"

"You can't."

"There must be a way."

"The station clock holds the town's forgotten hours. I tried
to stop it, but it took me instead. If you
accept its offer, it will release me and take you."

"Then I'll do it."

"No!"

"I've looked for you for eleven years."

"And I have spent eleven years hoping you would live."

The train began to accelerate.

Mara reached through the window. Her father caught her hand.
For a few seconds, they held on.

"Break the watch!" he shouted.

"What?"

"The watch is the first anchor. Break it before Lastlight!"

The conductor seized Mara around the waist and pulled her
back. Her fingers slipped from her father's.

"Mara!" Elias cried.

The platform fell away.

She struggled, but the conductor held her easily.

"Unwise," he said.

Mara struck his face with the iron winding key.

His cap flew off.

Beneath it, he had no eyes, only two clock faces
set into pale skin. The hands spun wildly.

He released her.

Mara pulled out the pocket watch and threw it onto
the floor.

The conductor lunged.

She brought the iron key down.

The glass cracked.

The watch stopped.

All around them, the train groaned.

The velvet seats split open. Brass lamps swung violently. Through
the floor came the thunder of enormous gears breaking apart.

The conductor collapsed, clutching his eyeless face.

"What have you done?" he howled.

The watch's hands began spinning forward.

Memories struck Mara like waves.

Her father laughing in summer grass. Her father carrying her
home after she broke her ankle. Her father teaching her
to turn the key exactly forty-three times. His voice. His
eyes. The roughness of his hands. Every hour returned at
once, not only to Mara but to everyone.

The remaining passengers cried out as forgotten names filled the
carriage.

Outside, the platform of THE HOUR BEFORE had begun to
move beside the train. The lost climbed aboard through windows
and doors. Hundreds poured in, then thousands, filling the aisles
without crowding them.

Elias entered last.

He reached Mara and held her as he had when
she was a child.

"I told you to get off," he said.

"You knew I wouldn't."

"I hoped you had become sensible."

"You raised me."

"That explains it."

The train plunged toward a distant light.

Lastlight rose ahead: an immense station beneath a sky without
stars. Its towers were made of clocks stacked one upon
another, their hands turning in different directions. At the center
of the platform waited a figure in white.

The Keeper of Lost Hours was neither old nor young,
neither man nor woman. Its coat was sewn with thousands
of pockets. From every pocket came the ticking of a
stolen watch.

The train crashed through the station.

Clocks exploded as the carriages struck them. Glass rained like
ice. The Keeper raised one hand, and time stopped.

Fragments of glass hung in the air.

Mara stood frozen beside her father. Only her thoughts could
move.

The Keeper walked toward her.

"You have broken the first anchor," it said. "Now every
lost hour must return."

*Good*, Mara tried to say.

"The living cannot bear all they have forgotten."

*They should be allowed to try.*

"Sorrow will return. Guilt will return. Old wounds will open.
The dead will be mourned again."

*They should be mourned.*

The Keeper studied her.

"You would destroy mercy."

*Forgetting is not mercy when someone else chooses it.*

For the first time, the Keeper looked uncertain.

Then Elias's broken watch chimed.

It was a small sound, no louder than a spoon
against a cup, but time shuddered and resumed.

The train burst through Lastlight's far wall.

Behind it, the station collapsed. Clocks tumbled into darkness, each
striking a different hour. The Keeper vanished beneath the sound.

Dawn came suddenly.

The train stood at Platform 3 in Bellwether.

Its doors opened.

The lost stepped onto land.

Some faded as soon as sunlight touched them. Others became
solid. A sailor walked into town, where an old woman
recognized the husband she had mourned fifty years before. Two
brothers embraced beside the station gate. Names returned to gravestones.
Faces reappeared in photographs.

Elias stepped down beside Mara.

"Are you real?" she asked.

He considered this. "Real enough."

The station clock began to strike.

Once.

Twice.

It continued beyond twelve, beyond twenty-four, ringing once for every
hour it had stolen. The townspeople came from their houses
and gathered in the street, remembering as the bells rolled
over them.

Some fell to their knees. Some laughed. Some cursed the
tower. Most wept.

At the forty-third bell, the clock face cracked from top
to bottom.

Its hands stopped forever.

Years later, the old station became a place where people
brought things they were afraid to forget. They filled the
ticket hall with photographs, letters, chipped cups, children's shoes, dried
flowers, and watches that no longer worked.

Mara kept no clock in the tower.

Each morning, she climbed the stairs and opened the windows
to the sea. Sometimes her father joined her. Sometimes he
did not. He had returned changed, as lost things often
do, and there were days when light passed through him.
Yet he remained long enough to grow older, which was
more than either of them had expected.

Travelers occasionally asked Mara for the time.

She would look toward the sun, the tide, or the
first evening star.

Then she would give them her best guess.

And if they complained that her answer was not exact,
she smiled.

"No hour is ever exact," she told them. "That is
why you must decide for yourself what to keep."

Every morning at seven, the bell in the tower rang
thirteen times.

No one in Bellwether spoke of the extra note. Children
learned to count around it. Bakers removed bread from their
ovens on the twelfth chime, and fishermen pushed away from
the harbor on the thirteenth. Visitors sometimes looked up in
confusion, but the townspeople merely smiled and continued with their
day.

The tower had stood in the market square longer than
any house, longer even than the sea wall. Its stones
were black with salt and weather, and ivy climbed halfway
to the clock face. Above the clock hung thirteen bronze
bells, though only twelve could be seen from the street.

The thirteenth was hidden in the tower's narrow heart.

Mara Vale had rung the bells for forty-seven years.

She was a small, sharp-backed woman with hands like roots.
Three times a day, she climbed the spiral stairs, wound
the mechanism, checked the ropes, and polished the brass teeth
of the great clock. The work had belonged to her
father before her, and to his mother before him. Someday,
everyone assumed, it would pass to Mara's apprentice, Tomas Reed.

Tomas was nineteen and incapable of leaving a question alone.

"Why thirteen?" he asked on his first day.

Mara handed him a bucket of oil. "Because twelve would
be wrong."

"Why would twelve be wrong?"

"Because the clock rings thirteen."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only one you'll get while that gear is
squeaking."

For three years, Tomas asked in different ways. He asked
over tea, while tightening bolts, while sweeping pigeons' nests from
the belfry. Mara never answered. Once, he tried to find
the hidden bell himself, but the passage leading to the
tower's center was sealed by an iron door without a
handle.

The door had a single keyhole.

Mara wore a long iron key around her neck.

On the first morning of winter, she did not come
to the tower.

Tomas waited until six forty-five, then wound the clock himself.
At seven, the visible bells began their chorus.

One.

Two.

Their voices rolled over tiled roofs and sleeping chimneys.

Three.

Four.

Lights appeared in windows.

Five.

Six.

Tomas watched the largest wheel turn.

Seven.

Eight.

Footsteps sounded on the stairs. He expected Mara, late and
furious, but it was Doctor Pell. The doctor's gray cap
was twisted between his hands.

Nine.

Ten.

"Tomas," he said.

Eleven.

Twelve.

The final visible bell swung.

Then, from deep within the tower, came the thirteenth note.

It was lower than the others, too low to be
merely heard. Tomas felt it in his ribs and teeth.
Dust shivered from the beams.

Doctor Pell lowered his eyes.

"Mara died before dawn."

The town buried her on the hill, in the long
grass overlooking the sea. Nearly everyone attended. The mayor spoke
about duty. The baker spoke about kindness. Tomas said nothing
because he had not yet decided whether he was angry
with Mara for dying or with himself for believing she
never would.

After the service, Doctor Pell gave him a small wooden
box.

"She left this for you."

Inside lay the iron key.

There was also a note in Mara's narrow handwriting.

Before you open the door, ring the bells.

Tomas read the sentence three times. There was no explanation,
only the command.

He returned to the tower at dusk. Beyond the harbor,
the sea reflected the red sky like hammered copper. The
evening ringing was due at eight, but Tomas could not
wait.

He climbed to the mechanism room and pulled the master
lever.

The bells rang across Bellwether.

People paused in the streets. Heads lifted from supper tables.
A flock of starlings erupted from the courthouse roof.

When the thirteenth note faded, Tomas fitted the key into
the iron door.

It turned easily.

The door opened inward onto darkness and a stairway descending
through the center of the tower.

Tomas lit a lantern. He had expected the stair to
lead to the hidden bell, perhaps suspended in some secret
chamber, but the steps continued far below ground level. Moisture
glittered on the walls. The air smelled of earth, metal,
and something sweetly rotten.

At the bottom stood another door.

This one was made of wood.

Tomas pushed it open and entered a room larger than
the tower should have been able to contain. Hundreds of
clocks covered the walls. Mantel clocks, pocket watches, sundials, water
clocks, hourglasses, and instruments he did not recognize ticked together
in a soft, insect-like murmur.

At the center of the room hung the thirteenth bell.

It was no bigger than a soup pot.

A silver hammer rested beside it, attached to a complicated
web of wires. Beneath the bell stood a stone table.
On it lay a book bound in cracked blue leather.

Tomas opened the book.

The first pages contained names and dates.

Elsa Vale, Keeper, 178 years.

Jon Orrow, Keeper, 53 years.

Mina Vale, Keeper, 91 years.

The list continued for centuries. Mara's name appeared near the
end.

Mara Vale, Keeper, 47 years.

Under it, in fresh black ink, was another entry.

Tomas Reed, Keeper—

The space after his name was blank.

He turned the page.

A map of Bellwether had been drawn there, though it
was not Bellwether as he knew it. The coastline lay
farther west. Where the market square now stood, the map
showed open water. Thirteen black circles formed a ring around
the town.

On the next page, Mara had written:

The bell does not tell the hour. It gives it.

Tomas heard movement behind him.

He spun around.

A boy stood in the doorway.

He was perhaps ten years old, barefoot and dripping wet.
His clothes were old-fashioned, and strands of seaweed clung to
his dark hair. His skin had the pale, swollen look
of something long submerged.

"You rang early," the boy said.

Tomas backed toward the table. "Who are you?"

"I've had many names."

"Are you a ghost?"

The boy considered this.

"Not exactly."

Every clock in the room stopped.

The silence was enormous.

The boy stepped closer to the hidden bell. "Your keeper
is gone."

"My keeper?"

"Mara. She kept you all very well."

"What did she keep us from?"

The boy smiled, and seawater ran from the corners of
his mouth.

"Tomorrow."

Then the clocks began ticking again.

Tomas blinked. The doorway was empty.

He spent the night reading the blue book.

Long before Bellwether had a name, a village stood on
the same shore. One summer, the sea withdrew beyond the
horizon. The villagers walked across the exposed seabed, collecting stranded
fish and treasures from wrecks. At sunset, a child discovered
a silver bell buried in the mud.

He rang it once.

The sea returned.

It came as a wall taller than the cliffs, swallowing
the village, the farms, and every living soul. Only a
watchmaker named Elsa Vale survived. She had been on the
hill gathering herbs when the water struck.

At dawn, she descended to find the village gone. In
its place stood the child who had found the bell.

The child offered her a bargain.

The drowned town could be restored, but each day of
its existence had to be borrowed. At sunrise, noon, and
evening, the silver bell would take one hour from the
future and divide it among the living. The people would
not remember drowning. They would rebuild, marry, argue, work, and
grow old.

But borrowed time accumulated like debt.

If the bell ever failed to ring, the future would
arrive all at once.

Elsa accepted.

For more than four hundred years, the keepers rang the
bell.

Tomas read until his lamp burned out.

At seven the next morning, he rang thirteen times.

The thirteenth note trembled beneath the town, and the sun
rose over a day that did not belong to anyone
yet.

For several weeks, Tomas followed Mara's routine. He rang at
seven, twelve, and eight. Between ringings, he studied the book.

The records were incomplete. Some keepers wrote only maintenance notes.
Others filled pages with guilt.

We have stolen 438,000 hours, wrote Mina Vale.

Another hand replied beneath it:

From whom?

No one had an answer.

Tomas began noticing things he had never seen before. The
same white gull landed on the same chimney every morning.
The baker dropped a wooden spoon every third Thursday. Mayor
Foss told the same joke whenever it rained. Children grew
taller, but the town itself seemed trapped in a performance
too often rehearsed.

One evening, Tomas asked Doctor Pell how Mara had died.

"She was old," the doctor said.

"She was sixty-three."

"That can be old."

"Was she ill?"

The doctor stared toward the tower. "She was tired."

"That isn't an illness."

"Sometimes it is."

Tomas took the iron key from beneath his shirt.

"Did you know about the bell?"

The doctor's face went still.

"That is the keeper's business."

"So you did."

"My grandfather knew. He told me never to ask questions
about time. Said time resents being noticed."

"Do you know what happens if I stop ringing?"

Doctor Pell's eyes filled with fear. "Mara would never have
chosen you if she thought you might do that."

"She didn't choose me. I was a boy who liked
clocks."

"She chose you because you asked why."

Tomas laughed bitterly. "She never answered."

"Perhaps she hoped you'd find a better answer than hers."

That winter, the storms came early.

Waves struck the sea wall hard enough to shake windows.
Boats remained tied in the harbor, and black clouds covered
the sun for days. Each time Tomas entered the hidden
chamber, the drowned boy was waiting.

"You're late," the boy would say, even when Tomas was
early.

"What happens if I don't ring?" Tomas asked.

"You've read the book."

"The book was written by frightened people."

"All books are."

"How much time have we borrowed?"

The boy pointed to the clocks.

Tomas studied them. Some ran clockwise. Others ran backward. A
glass vessel dripped blue water upward. An hourglass contained bright
particles that vanished before reaching the lower chamber.

"I don't understand."

"No."

"Could we repay it gradually?"

"You already are."

"How?"

The boy smiled. "Everything that lives is repayment."

Then he faded into the dark.

On the longest night of the year, lightning struck the
tower.

Tomas was halfway up the stairs when the mechanism screamed.
A gear the size of a cartwheel tore free and
shattered against the wall. The clock hands stopped at seven
fifty-nine.

Outside, the townspeople gathered in the rain.

Tomas climbed through smoke to the belfry. The ropes had
tangled. The master lever was broken. He pulled each rope
by hand, counting the notes.

One.

Two.

His palms burned.

Three.

Four.

The storm swallowed the sound.

Five.

Six.

Below, people counted with him.

Seven.

Eight.

The tower shifted in the wind.

Nine.

Ten.

A beam collapsed across the stairwell.

Eleven.

Twelve.

Tomas ran to the iron door. The key slipped twice
before entering the lock. He descended through smoke, stumbling into
the chamber.

The silver hammer had fallen from its mechanism.

The thirteenth bell was silent.

Every clock had stopped.

The drowned boy stood beside the stone table.

"Ring it," Tomas said.

"I cannot."

"Help me lift the hammer."

"I cannot."

The room tilted. Water seeped between the stones.

Above them, the town waited.

Tomas seized the hammer. It was impossibly heavy, heavier than
iron or stone. He pulled with both hands, but it
did not move.

"What is it made of?" he gasped.

"Hours."

Water covered his boots.

The boy watched without sympathy.

Tomas braced his feet against the table and pulled again.
The hammer rose an inch.

Images flashed through his mind.

A woman he had never met blowing out candles on
her hundredth birthday.

A city of glass towers beneath a green sky.

A child stepping onto a red desert and leaving the
first human footprint.

An old man watching the last ship depart from a
dying Earth.

Possible hours. Future hours. Hours Bellwether had taken.

Tomas released the hammer.

It struck the floor.

Above, people began shouting.

The chamber walls turned transparent. Beyond them, Tomas saw not
earth but ocean. Roofs lay beneath black water. Fish moved
through bedrooms. The skeleton of the sea wall curled across
the sand.

"This is what Bellwether is," the boy said. "A memory
kept warm by theft."

"My family is up there."

"Yes."

"My friends."

"Yes."

"They didn't choose this."

"Neither did those whose hours you borrow."

Water climbed to Tomas's knees.

"What happens to you if I don't ring?"

For the first time, the boy looked uncertain.

"I finish drowning."

"You're the child who found the bell."

"I was."

"Why did you offer Elsa the bargain?"

"I was afraid."

The answer was so ordinary that Tomas almost laughed.

The boy looked no older than ten, despite carrying four
centuries in his hollow eyes. He had destroyed the village
by accident, then trapped himself and everyone else in the
moment before the consequence.

Tomas looked at the book. His name waited on the
final page.

Tomas Reed, Keeper—

All his life, he had believed a clock measured time.
Mara had known better. A clock was a promise that
one moment would end and another would be allowed to
begin.

He picked up the blue book and tore out the
page bearing his name.

The drowned boy recoiled.

"What are you doing?"

"Answering my question."

The water reached Tomas's waist.

Above, the townspeople called for the thirteenth note.

Tomas imagined his mother standing outside the tailor's shop, rain
in her silver hair. He imagined Doctor Pell clutching his
cap. The baker. The fishermen. Children counting to twelve and
waiting for the sound that made the world continue.

He loved them.

That was what made the choice terrible.

That was what made it necessary.

Tomas held the torn page over his lantern flame.

The paper caught.

The drowned boy screamed.

The sound came from everywhere: from the flooded walls, from
the clocks, from the stones of the tower. The silver
bell split down the middle.

For one suspended instant, nothing happened.

Then tomorrow arrived.

The sea struck Bellwether.

It poured through streets and windows, over gardens and chimneys.
The tower broke apart. Its twelve visible bells fell through
white water, turning soundlessly. Houses folded into waves. The market
square vanished.

Yet the townspeople did not drown in terror, as Tomas
had feared.

They remembered.

In the final instant, the borrowed centuries returned to them.
They remembered the first flood. They remembered Elsa's bargain. They
remembered every morning they had woken beneath a sun that
was not theirs, every meal, every kiss, every grief. Four
hundred years passed through each mind like light through glass.

Then Bellwether was gone.

Tomas opened his eyes on the hill.

He lay in wet grass under a clear morning sky.
Beside him, people stirred and sat up. There were not
thousands, as there had been in the town, but perhaps
two hundred—the descendants of those who had been outside the
village when the first wave came, lives the bargain had
tangled with Bellwether but not created from the drowned.

Doctor Pell knelt nearby, staring at his hands.

Tomas's mother found him and held him so tightly he
could not breathe.

Below the hill, the sea was calm.

No tower rose from the shore. No harbor cut into
the land. There were only cliffs, wheeling birds, and broken
stones visible beneath the shallow water.

A boy stood at the edge of the waves.

He was no longer pale. No seaweed clung to his
hair. He looked back at Tomas, raised one hand, and
walked into the sunlight. With each step he became less
distinct, until there was only brightness on the sea.

The survivors built a new village inland.

They did not call it Bellwether. They named it Morrow.

Tomas became a clockmaker, though he never made a clock
that chimed. His clocks had quiet faces and simple hands.
On the back of each, he engraved the same words:

Time is not given. Time is allowed.

Years passed. Morrow grew. Children were born who had no
memory of the tower, the bargain, or the thirteenth bell.
They counted normally, argued loudly, and wasted whole afternoons without
guilt.

Tomas grew old.

On his final morning, he asked to be carried to
the hill above the sea. His granddaughter, Mara, sat beside
him in the grass.

Far below, waves moved over the ruins.

"What are you listening for?" she asked.

Tomas smiled.

"Nothing."

The sun rose.

No bell rang, and the day began.

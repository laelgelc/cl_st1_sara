Mara Vale repaired clocks in a town where no one
was ever late.

Every morning at seven, the bakery opened. At seven ten,
the milk cart rattled over the eastern bridge. At eight,
the school bell rang twice, and at eight fifteen, Mayor
Bell crossed the square carrying a black umbrella, whether it
rained or not. Even the gulls seemed to wheel above
the harbor according to a schedule posted somewhere in the
clouds.

The town was called Bellwether, and at its center stood
the Great Clock.

It had four white faces, each wide as a house,
and hands of hammered copper that flashed green in the
sun. Beneath the clock faces, behind walls of gray stone,
hung a pendulum taller than three men. Its swing governed
every watch, bell, oven, ferry, and appointment in town.

At least, that was what people said.

Mara's shop stood in the clock tower's shadow. Over the
door, a wooden sign read VALE & DAUGHTER, though her
father had been dead for nine years and there had
never been another daughter. She kept the sign because the
gold lettering was still good.

Inside, hundreds of clocks ticked at different volumes. Brass carriage
clocks, walnut mantel clocks, painted cuckoo clocks, silver watches, iron
alarm clocks, and one tiny clock set inside a glass
ring all spoke at once. Visitors often found the sound
overwhelming. Mara heard it as weather.

On the first Monday of October, at precisely nine minutes
past ten, a stranger brought her a clock that did
not tick.

He was old, though not in the ordinary way. His
coat was carefully mended, his boots were dusty, and his
hair was white except for one black strand above his
left ear. He placed a small wooden box on her
counter.

"I need this repaired," he said.

Mara opened the lid.

Inside lay a pocket watch of dark metal. Its case
was engraved with a bird in flight. There were no
numbers on its face, only a single black hand pointing
upward.

"It has no winding crown," Mara said.

"No."

"No hinge on the back."

"No."

"And only one hand."

"Yes."

"Then how am I supposed to open it?"

The stranger smiled. "At the right time."

Mara disliked riddles before lunch.

"What's wrong with it?"

"It stopped."

"So I gathered."

"Can you make it go again?"

"Perhaps. Who made it?"

"You did."

The shop seemed to become louder. A dozen pendulums swung.
A cuckoo called once from the wall, although the hour
was nowhere near.

Mara looked at the stranger. "I've never seen this watch
before."

"Not yet."

She nearly pushed the box back to him. Then she
noticed the engraving beneath the bird: a small letter V
crossed by a line. It was her mark, the signature
she scratched into every mechanism she built.

The mark was imperfect. The left stroke leaned too far
outward, exactly as hers always did.

"Where did you get this?"

The stranger glanced toward the window. Outside, townspeople crossed the
square in neat streams. Above them, the Great Clock showed
ten thirteen.

"You gave it to me," he said. "A long time
from now."

Mara shut the lid.

"Get out."

"At noon tomorrow, the Great Clock will lose one minute."

"The Great Clock has not lost a minute in two
hundred and six years."

"I know."

"And you expect me to believe you came from the
future?"

"No," said the stranger. "I expect you to believe the
clock."

He left the box on the counter and walked out.

Mara hurried after him, but when she reached the street,
he was gone. There was nowhere he could have hidden.
The square was broad and bare, and every shop door
was closed against the autumn wind.

At ten fifteen, Mayor Bell passed under the tower with
his umbrella.

Mara took the dark watch into her workshop and tried
every tool she owned. The metal resisted files. Oil rolled
off it. Magnets had no effect. When she held it
to her ear, she heard no mechanism, only a faint
rushing sound like waves in a shell.

She worked until midnight.

Before going to bed in the room above the shop,
she placed the watch inside her father's old safe. Then
she locked the safe, locked the workshop, locked the shop,
and slept badly.

In her dream, the whole town stood knee-deep in black
water. The people stared at the clock tower while fish
swam through open windows. The Great Clock's hands spun backward,
and somewhere beneath the water a man knocked against glass.

The next morning, the dark watch lay on her bedside
table.

Mara sat up so quickly she struck her head on
the wall.

The safe downstairs was still locked. So were the workshop
and shop doors. She examined the watch again. The single
hand now pointed slightly to the right.

At eleven fifty-five, Mara carried it into the square.

She felt foolish. The sky was clear. Shopkeepers stood in
doorways, enjoying the cold sunlight. Children marched back to school
after their midday meal. The milk cart returned over the
bridge. Everything happened as it always had.

At noon, the tower bell began to ring.

One.

Two.

Three.

Mara counted each stroke.

At twelve, the final note rolled over the rooftops and
out across the harbor.

The Great Clock read eleven fifty-nine.

For a moment, no one moved.

Then the minute hand jumped forward.

The baker laughed uncertainly. A teacher clapped as if a
trick had been performed. People checked their watches and found
them correct. The Great Clock showed noon again.

Conversation resumed. Carts rolled. Doors shut. Within five minutes, the
square had returned to order.

Only Mara remained still.

The dark watch's single hand had moved another fraction to
the right.

That afternoon, Mayor Bell entered her shop.

He was a tall, narrow man with a silver beard
and a manner so polished that even his anger seemed
ceremonial. His black umbrella hung from one arm.

"I understand you were in the square at noon," he
said.

"So were many people."

"You were watching the tower."

"I often watch clocks. It's my profession."

The mayor did not smile. "Did anyone speak to you
about the Great Clock?"

Mara thought of the stranger. "Why would they?"

"An idle question."

"From a busy man?"

He examined the clocks on her walls, none of which
required repair. "Your father cared for the tower mechanism."

"He did."

"And after his death, the council appointed Mr. Quill."

"Mr. Quill oils what he can reach and lies about
the rest."

"Nevertheless, you are not authorized to enter the tower."

"I haven't tried."

"Good."

The mayor turned to leave.

"Why did the clock lose a minute?" Mara asked.

His hand tightened around the umbrella.

"Old machines," he said, "occasionally surprise us."

"So do old men."

Mayor Bell looked back at her. For an instant, his
expression was not angry but afraid.

Then he left.

At closing time, Mara found a folded piece of paper
beneath the dark watch. She had not put it there.

The note contained four words:

BELOW THE PENDULUM. MIDNIGHT.

At eleven forty-five, Mara locked her shop and crossed the
square.

The tower's main entrance was guarded at night, but Mara
knew another way in. As a child, she had followed
her father through a drain tunnel beside the canal. The
tunnel smelled worse than she remembered and was narrower than
it had been when she was twelve, but it still
ended behind a loose grate in the tower cellar.

She emerged into darkness filled with machinery.

The Great Clock's mechanism occupied nine floors. Gears turned in
the walls. Chains vanished into shafts. Counterweights rose and fell
with grave patience. At the center, the pendulum swung through
the tower like a golden blade.

Mara stood beneath it.

At midnight, the bells shook dust from the ceiling.

On the final stroke, the dark watch opened.

Its back unfolded into six metal petals. Inside, instead of
springs and wheels, a disk of pale glass shone with
blue light. Lines appeared across it, forming a map of
the tower cellar.

A red point blinked beneath Mara's feet.

She found the trapdoor under a layer of coal dust.

Below it, a spiral staircase descended farther than the tower
foundations should have allowed. The air grew warm as Mara
followed it down. At the bottom, she reached an iron
door bearing the seal of Bellwether: a sheep with a
bell around its neck.

The door was unlocked.

Beyond it stood a vast underground chamber.

Mara forgot to breathe.

Hundreds of glass cylinders rose from floor to ceiling. Copper
tubes connected them in a shining web. Within each cylinder
floated something that looked like smoke underwater—silver, restless, alive. Labels
had been fixed to the glass.

APRIL 3, 1791—ELEVEN MINUTES.

DECEMBER 19, 1834—THREE HOURS.

JULY 8, 1880—TWO DAYS.

Some cylinders were small enough to hold in one hand.
Others were wider than houses.

At the center of the chamber, pipes descended from the
Great Clock into a black reservoir. A gauge on its
side trembled near a red mark.

"You came."

The stranger stepped from behind the reservoir.

Mara raised the dark watch like a weapon. "Who are
you?"

"Jonas Vale."

Her mouth went dry.

"My son," she whispered.

"Eventually."

"I don't have a son."

"Not yet."

The stranger—Jonas—looked up at the cylinders. Blue light deepened the
lines in his face.

"What is this place?"

"The reason no one in Bellwether is ever late."

He touched a cylinder labeled MAY 14, 1902—TWENTY-SEVEN MINUTES.

"The Great Clock does not measure time," he said. "It
takes it."

Mara waited.

"Small amounts, usually. A second from a conversation. Five minutes
from a walk. An hour from someone's sleep. The machine
smooths the stolen time across the town. It keeps ships
on schedule, prevents accidents, delays storms, and ensures that every
harvest comes before the first frost."

"That's impossible."

"You are standing under a tower full of stolen Tuesdays."

She looked again at the silver currents trapped in glass.

"Where does it come from?"

"Everyone. No one notices a few seconds. When more is
needed, the council takes larger pieces."

Mara remembered her father in his final months. He had
been fifty-three, strong and loud, with hands that could straighten
a bent gear by touch. Then, in a single winter,
he had become ancient. His hair whitened. His back curved.
By spring, he was dead.

"What did they take from my father?"

Jonas lowered his eyes.

"How much?"

"Seventeen years."

The chamber blurred.

Mara gripped the edge of the reservoir until its bolts
cut her palm. "Why?"

"He discovered the machine was failing. Mayor Bell demanded that
he repair it. Your father refused. He said the town
had lived too long on borrowed time."

"And Bell took his life?"

"Bell's father did. The mayors are all from the same
family. They've guarded this place for generations."

Above them, the mechanism groaned.

The gauge's needle jumped closer to the red mark.

"What happens when that reaches the end?" Mara asked.

"The reservoir breaks. All the stored time returns at once."

"To the people it was taken from?"

"To everything."

She imagined buildings aging into dust, children becoming adults in
a heartbeat, dead trees blooming and rotting, waves freezing over
the harbor and racing away.

"When?"

"Tomorrow at noon."

"The clock lost one minute today."

"The first crack in the reservoir. It will lose an
hour in the morning. Then everything."

Mara stared at him. "Why come to me?"

"Because you made the watch."

"You keep saying that."

"In my time, Bellwether no longer exists. The clock failed
when I was a child. You saved me by putting
me on a boat before the collapse, but you stayed
behind."

Jonas took the watch from her gently.

"Years later, I found this among your tools. It can
move against the current of time, but only once in
each direction. I used it to come here."

"Then we warn the town."

"They won't believe us."

"We show them the chamber."

"Mayor Bell will stop us."

"Then we stop him first."

Jonas smiled, and for an instant Mara saw something of
herself in his face.

"That," he said, "is what you said before."

Footsteps rang on the staircase.

Mayor Bell entered the chamber with six guards.

"I had hoped," he said, "that grief had taught you
not to interfere with the tower."

The guards surrounded Mara and Jonas. Each carried a short
iron club.

Mara looked at the mayor. "You knew this would happen."

"I knew the machine required adjustment."

"It is about to burst."

"Mr. Quill assures me the reservoir can be stabilized."

"Mr. Quill thinks a differential gear is a disease of
the liver."

The mayor's jaw tightened.

Jonas stepped forward. "You need to empty the reservoir gradually.
Release the stored time before noon."

"Into what?" asked Bell.

"The town."

"Absolutely not."

"Then the town dies."

The mayor walked to a cylinder and laid one hand
against the glass.

"You see theft," he said. "I see two centuries without
famine. Ships that returned before storms. Children pulled from roads
before runaway carts arrived. Fires that burned slowly enough to
be extinguished. Bellwether prospers because the clock gives us what
the rest of the world lacks: order."

"It killed my father," Mara said.

"It saved thousands."

"You always say thousands when you mean yourself."

For the first time, Bell's composure broke.

"My family has given more than any other. Do you
imagine the machine runs without judgment? Every day we choose
where seconds must be added and where they can be
removed. Every day we carry the burden of keeping this
town alive."

"No," said Jonas. "You carry an umbrella."

One of the guards laughed before turning it into a
cough.

Mayor Bell struck Jonas across the face.

Jonas fell against the reservoir. The gauge needle shuddered into
the red.

A thin crack appeared in the black metal.

Silver light spilled through it.

All at once, Mara remembered a day that had never
happened.

She stood on the harbor pier holding a red-haired baby.
Her son. Jonas. He grabbed her finger and laughed as
gulls cried overhead.

The memory vanished.

Another took its place: her father teaching her to polish
brass; a summer evening stretched warm and endless; her mother
singing in a kitchen that had been torn down twenty
years ago.

The stolen time was leaking.

Everyone in the chamber felt it. A guard dropped his
club and began to weep. Another gasped the name of
someone he had forgotten.

The mayor stared at his hands. For a moment, they
appeared young.

Then the crack sealed itself.

Silence returned.

Mara understood.

"The time wants to go back," she said.

Jonas rose, blood on his lip. "Yes."

"The pipes can reverse."

"The main escapement must be disengaged."

"From the clock room."

Mayor Bell motioned to the guards. "Take them."

The tower lurched.

Far above, bells rang once.

Then again.

Then a third time, too quickly.

Everyone looked up.

"It's begun," Jonas said.

Mara threw the dark watch at the nearest lamp.

Glass shattered. Oil flared across the floor. In the sudden
confusion, she seized a guard's club and struck the valve
beside the reservoir. Steam burst into the chamber. Jonas tackled
another guard. Mayor Bell shouted orders no one obeyed.

Mara ran.

She climbed the spiral stairs two at a time. The
tower mechanism hammered around her. Gears spun too fast. Chains
whipped through pulleys. On the fourth floor, she saw the
minute hand's enormous axle turning backward.

Outside, the town screamed.

Through a window, Mara glimpsed chaos in the square. People
staggered as lost seconds returned. A woman dropped a basket,
then caught it before it fell, then dropped it again.
A cart crossed the same patch of road three times.
Autumn leaves rose from the gutters and fastened themselves to
branches.

Mara climbed higher.

At the clock room, Mr. Quill knelt beside the escapement,
turning a wrench in the wrong direction.

"Stop!" she shouted.

He looked up. "The mayor ordered—"

A gear tooth snapped and flew past his head.

Mr. Quill fainted.

The main escapement stood behind the pendulum shaft: a steel
lever controlling the release of the entire mechanism. Mara pulled
it. It did not move.

A keyhole sat at its base.

The dark watch.

She had left it below.

The stairs shook as Jonas stumbled into the room.

He held the watch.

"Thought you might need this."

Behind him came Mayor Bell, carrying a guard's club.

"You do not know what you are releasing," Bell said.

"I know exactly what was taken," Mara replied.

"You'll destroy everything we built."

"Then we'll build it again honestly."

Bell raised the club. Jonas moved between them.

The mayor swung.

Mara caught his arm, but the blow struck Jonas's shoulder.
The dark watch fell and skidded beneath the pendulum. Mara
dived after it. The copper blade swept over her, close
enough to lift her hair.

She seized the watch.

Its petals opened as she brought it near the keyhole.
The single black hand pointed straight down.

The watch fitted perfectly.

Mayor Bell tore free from Jonas and rushed toward her.

Mara turned the key.

The escapement released.

The Great Clock stopped.

For the first time in two hundred and six years,
Bellwether was silent.

Then every cylinder beneath the tower shattered.

Time rose through the mechanism in a silver flood. It
poured from windows, burst through clock faces, and rolled across
the town without heat or sound.

Mara saw her father standing beside the pendulum. He was
young, his beard still red, his eyes bright with pride.

"You found it," he said.

She reached for him.

The wave struck.

Morning and evening passed in the same breath. The tower
walls cracked, healed, and cracked again. Bells became green with
age, then shone as new. Mara felt seconds entering her:
forgotten meals, unfinished dreams, pauses between words, hours taken from
sleep. She lived a hundred small moments all at once.

She saw Jonas as a child.

She saw herself hiding him aboard a fishing boat beneath
a gray, broken sky.

She saw years that might never come.

Then the silver light vanished.

Mara lay on the clock room floor.

Sunlight entered through the western clock face. The hands had
stopped at twelve.

Jonas sat nearby, no longer old. He looked about thirty.
The white had gone from his hair, including the single
black strand that had once remained.

Mayor Bell leaned against the wall. His beard was dark,
but his face was full of bewilderment. In one instant,
decades of inherited memories had returned to him: choices made
by fathers, grandfathers, and mayors whose names were carved beneath
the tower. He lowered the club.

"What time is it?" he asked.

No one answered.

Below, the people of Bellwether emerged into the square.

The bakery opened late the next morning.

The milk cart did not arrive at all because the
horse had wandered into an orchard and refused to leave.
School began at nine twenty-three, though the teacher claimed it
was nine fifteen. The ferry departed without three passengers and
returned for them. Mayor Bell crossed the square at noon
carrying no umbrella, only to be caught in the first
rain.

At first, the town hated it.

People missed meetings. Bread burned. Fishermen argued over tides. Watches
disagreed. The great hands on the tower remained fixed, and
no one could decide whether to repair them.

But no storms paused at the harbor after that. No
harvest waited politely for the farmers. Accidents happened, as did
narrow escapes. Children grew at the proper speed. Old people
complained that years passed too quickly, and young people complained
that they passed too slowly.

Mara kept the dark watch in her workshop.

Jonas stayed through winter. They spoke carefully about the future,
like travelers comparing maps of a country whose borders were
changing. He told her nothing about his father. Mara did
not ask. Some doors, she decided, should remain closed until
one reached them.

In spring, the watch's black hand began to point upward
again.

"It's time," Jonas said.

They stood on the eastern bridge at dawn. Gulls wheeled
above the harbor without apparent instruction.

"Will you remember me?" Mara asked.

"I think so."

"Will I still have you?"

Jonas smiled. "I think that depends on whether you're late."

He pressed the watch into her hand.

"I made this for you," she said.

"You will."

The metal petals opened. Blue light wrapped around him.

Mara wanted to say something wise, something a mother might
say at the end of a long life. But she
had not yet become his mother, and her life was
not ending.

So she said, "Take care of your shoulder."

Jonas laughed.

Then he was gone.

Years later, Mara married a boatbuilder who had once missed
the ferry because his horse wandered into an orchard. Their
son was born red-haired and loud on a rainy afternoon.
They named him Jonas, though Mara never explained why.

The Great Clock was not repaired.

Vines climbed the tower. Birds nested behind its frozen hands.
The underground chamber was emptied, its broken cylinders melted down
and made into bells for ships and schools and front
doors.

Mara's shop remained full of ticking.

Above the entrance, the old sign still read VALE &
DAUGHTER, though she had a son and no daughter. She
kept it because the gold lettering was still good, and
because people no longer expected every sign, clock, promise, or
life to be perfectly accurate.

Sometimes a customer would hurry in, apologizing for being late.

Mara always smiled.

"For what?" she would ask.

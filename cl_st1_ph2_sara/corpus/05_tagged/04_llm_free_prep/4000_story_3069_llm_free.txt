### The Museum of Lost Weather

By the time Mara reached the Museum of Lost Weather,
the rain had already forgotten how to fall.

It hung above the city in silver beads, each droplet
trembling in place as if caught on invisible threads. People
hurried beneath the suspended storm with umbrellas closed, glancing upward
but pretending not to be afraid. The city had become
skilled at pretending.

Mara stopped at the museum gates and held out her
hand. A drop hovered above her palm, cold and perfect.
She raised herself on her toes and touched it with
one finger.

It burst soundlessly.

Inside the museum, a brass clock struck nine, though the
hour hand pointed to three and the minute hand spun
backward.

"You're late," called a voice from the doorway.

The curator stood beneath the stone arch, wrapped in a
coat the color of old snow. His name was Mr.
Vale, and he had been ancient when Mara first came
to work for him seven years earlier. He wore round
spectacles with one cracked lens and carried a silver tuning
fork in his breast pocket.

"The rain stopped me," Mara said.

"The rain has stopped everyone."

Mr. Vale turned and disappeared into the dim entrance hall.
Mara followed.

The museum occupied the oldest building in the city, a
former railway station abandoned when the sea swallowed the eastern
tracks. Its main hall still bore the great iron ribs
of the original roof. Beneath them stood glass cabinets containing
the remnants of climates that no longer existed.

A fist-sized thunderstorm grumbled inside a blue bottle.

A ribbon of desert wind coiled around a wooden spindle.

Frost from the last white winter feathered a pane of
black glass.

In one cabinet lay a small brass key struck by
lightning so many times that it glowed faintly in the
dark.

Mara had dusted, labeled, fed, cooled, warmed, and occasionally sung
to every weather specimen in the museum. She knew that
the northern wind preferred the smell of pine resin, that
fog became anxious in sealed jars, and that hailstones would
shrink if stored near bells.

But she had never seen the rain stop in the
air.

Mr. Vale led her through the hall to the conservation
room. On the central table sat a wooden box bound
with copper wire. Water had gathered around it, though the
box itself was dry.

"What is that?" Mara asked.

"It arrived at dawn."

"From whom?"

"The courier had no face."

Mara waited. Mr. Vale often said alarming things in the
same tone other people used to discuss breakfast.

"No face?" she repeated.

"None worth remembering."

He handed her a card. The ink had run, but
she could make out a single line:

FOR THE GIRL WHO REMEMBERS SUMMER.

Mara looked toward the shuttered windows. She had not been
a girl for many years, and she did not remember
summer.

Not truly.

She remembered photographs: children in bright clothes, sunlight spread like
honey on brick walls, old men sleeping in parks beneath
green trees. She remembered her mother speaking of heat with
reverence, describing peaches warm from the branch and evenings when
people opened every window.

But those were inherited memories. Mara had been born during
the Long Autumn, when the sun became a pale coin
and the leaves fell for eleven years.

"This isn't for me," she said.

Mr. Vale regarded her over the rims of his spectacles.
"Open it."

The copper wire was warm. Mara unwound it carefully and
lifted the lid.

Inside lay a glass sphere no larger than an apple.
At first it appeared empty. Then she turned it toward
the light.

A golden field burned within it.

Grass bowed under a hot wind. Insects flashed above red
flowers. Beyond a low stone wall, a girl ran barefoot
toward a river, her dark braid striking the back of
a yellow dress.

Mara nearly dropped the sphere.

The girl turned.

Though the face was tiny and blurred by the curve
of the glass, Mara recognized herself.

A pain opened behind her ribs. She saw brown hands
on a garden gate. She smelled dust, mint, and sun-warmed
stone. Somewhere, very far away, her mother was laughing.

Then the vision vanished.

The sphere filled with cloud.

Mr. Vale gripped the table. The museum windows rattled. From
the main hall came a chorus of noises: the bottled
thunderstorm cracking, the northern wind shrieking against its seals, hailstones
leaping in their drawer.

"What did you do?" Mara asked.

"Nothing."

"You always say that when you've done something."

"This time I am offended by the accuracy."

The cloud inside the sphere began to turn. A tiny
spiral formed at its center.

Mr. Vale snatched up his tuning fork and struck it
against the table. A pure note rang through the room.
The spiral shuddered and slowed.

"The Heart of Summer," he whispered.

Mara stared at him. "That's a story."

"Most dangerous things are, until someone finds them."

According to museum legend, every season possessed a heart: a
small, indestructible memory from which all its weather could be
reborn. The Heart of Winter was said to sleep beneath
a glacier. Spring's heart wandered in the roots of forests.
No one agreed where Autumn had hidden its heart, though
Mr. Vale suspected it was tucked inside a migrating bird.

Summer's heart had disappeared forty years ago.

Soon afterward, the seasons began to fail.

First summer shortened, then vanished. Winter arrived but brought no
snow. Autumn settled over the world and refused to leave.
In recent years, even autumn had begun to unravel. Winds
blew in circles. Clouds formed indoors. Dawn sometimes came twice,
while noon failed to arrive at all.

"You knew this existed," Mara said.

"I hoped it did."

"And the girl?"

Mr. Vale did not answer.

Mara picked up the card again. The wet ink shifted
before her eyes. New words bled across the paper.

RETURN IT BEFORE THE LAST DROP FALLS.

A heavy tap sounded against the roof.

Then another.

Mara looked up.

The suspended rain was beginning to rise.

They climbed to the observatory at the top of the
museum. From there, the city spread beneath them: slate roofs,
black chimneys, narrow streets shining with damp. Above it all,
millions of raindrops drifted upward toward the clouds.

People had stopped pretending. Crowds filled the squares. Some knelt.
Some shouted. Children leaped from fountains as streams of water
uncoiled from the basins and climbed into the sky.

"The world is taking its water back," said Mr. Vale.

"Why?"

"Because the seasons are not separate machines. They are one
motion. Summer lifts the seas. Autumn carries the clouds. Winter
releases snow. Spring opens the rivers. Break one season, and
the others may limp along for a while, but eventually
the whole wheel stops."

Mara held the sphere inside her coat. It pulsed against
her chest like a second heart.

"Where do we return it?"

Mr. Vale pointed west.

Beyond the city walls, beyond the salt marshes and drowned
villages, a line of dark hills marked the horizon. At
their center stood Mount Orra, its summit hidden by cloud.

"To the Sun House," he said. "Before it was a
museum, this building was the final station on the western
railway. Trains carried pilgrims to the foot of the mountain.
At the summit, they kept the first flame of every
summer."

"The railway is gone."

"Mostly."

"And the mountain is three days away."

"Under ordinary weather."

Mara looked at the rising rain.

"What happens when the last drop reaches the clouds?"

Mr. Vale removed his spectacles and polished them slowly.

"No more rivers. No more wells. No blood moving in
any living vein."

Mara closed her hand around the sphere.

"When do we leave?"

"We don't."

She turned sharply.

Mr. Vale leaned against the observatory wall. Without his coat
buttoned, he looked suddenly thin and breakable.

"You do," he said. "I must keep the museum stable.
Every specimen downstairs is reacting to the Heart. If their
cases fail, the city will have more immediate problems than
thirst."

"You expect me to climb a mountain and repair summer
alone?"

"No. I expect you to remember the way."

"I told you. I don't remember summer."

"You remembered enough to wake the sphere."

He gave her a railway ticket, yellow with age. Printed
across the front were the words WESTERN LINE — ONE
WAY.

"Platform Seven," he said.

"There is no Platform Seven."

"There is when the clock runs backward."

In the old station hall, the clock's hands were spinning
so quickly they had become a blur. Mr. Vale struck
his tuning fork beneath it. The note rose into the
iron rafters.

The clock stopped.

For one breath, everything was silent.

Then it began to chime.

Thirteen. Fourteen. Fifteen.

With each impossible stroke, the far wall faded. Tiles became
mist. A platform emerged where no platform had stood before,
lined with lamps burning under green glass.

A train waited beside it.

The engine was black, wet, and breathing. Moss covered its
wheels. Ferns grew from cracks in the carriages. There was
no driver.

Mara stepped aboard.

Mr. Vale remained on the platform.

"If I knew summer," she said through the open door,
"why did I forget?"

The old curator lowered his eyes.

"Because someone made you."

The doors slammed shut.

The train began to move.

It passed through the museum wall and into a country
without rain.

Mara watched from a dusty window as the city fell
behind. Canals lay empty, their fish gasping in black mud.
Orchard trees bent beneath leaves as gray as ash. In
the fields, farmers stood beside buckets while water rose out
of the soil in glittering threads.

The train gathered speed.

Mara explored the carriages. Most were empty, though she found
evidence of former passengers: a red glove, a deck of
swollen playing cards, a child's shoe filled with sand.

In the last carriage sat a boy.

He appeared to be ten or eleven, dressed in a
school uniform of an old-fashioned cut. His hair was damp,
and drops of water floated around his head.

"You're not supposed to be here," Mara said.

"Neither are you."

He was drawing circles on the fogged window.

"What's your name?"

"Eli."

"How long have you been on this train?"

He shrugged. "Since it rained."

"Today?"

"The first time."

Mara sat opposite him.

Eli studied the shape beneath her coat. "You've got the
hot thing."

"How do you know?"

"It's making my teeth hurt."

The sphere pulsed.

For an instant, summer opened around them. The carriage filled
with the scent of grass and river water. Mara saw
the girl in the yellow dress again, but this time
she was not alone. A younger child followed her along
the riverbank, laughing as he tried to catch dragonflies.

Eli.

The vision snapped away.

Mara's hands began to shake.

The boy looked pale. "I know you."

"No," she whispered.

"You left me."

Memory came not as a picture but as heat.

A summer evening. Thunder beyond the hills. Mara—eight years old,
perhaps nine—running beside the river with her little brother. Their
mother calling from the Sun House garden.

Then men in gray uniforms at the gate.

A machine of mirrors.

Her mother pushing the golden sphere into Mara's hands.

Run.

Mara lurched to her feet. "You're my brother."

Eli wiped the window with his sleeve. Behind the glass,
the landscape rushed past in shades of brown.

"You left me on the train," he said again.

"I don't remember."

"That doesn't mean you didn't."

A whistle screamed from the engine. Ahead, the western tracks
vanished into a lake.

The train did not slow.

Mara grabbed the seat as the engine struck the water.
Instead of sinking, the train raced across the surface. Waves
rose on both sides, then lifted into the air, joining
the upward rain.

Below the clear water lay a drowned town. Chimneys, streets,
and church roofs passed beneath them. Pale figures stood in
the submerged square, looking up.

Eli pressed his face to the glass.

"They're waiting for weather," he said.

"Who are they?"

"People who forgot to leave."

The lake ended at a wall of fog.

As the train entered it, the world disappeared.

Voices moved outside the windows.

Give it back.

Mara pulled the sphere from her coat. Golden light spilled
over the carriage walls. Shadowy hands pressed against the glass.

Not yet, she thought.

The whispers grew louder.

Give it back, little thief.

The fog carried another memory.

Her mother kneeling before her in a room full of
mirrors.

"Summer is not heat," she said, closing Mara's fingers around
the sphere. "It is the promise that warmth will return.
Whatever happens, take it to the mountain."

Outside, boots struck the stairs.

Mara remembered running.

She remembered Eli following.

At the station, soldiers filled the platform. They belonged to
the Ministry of Constant Skies, the government that had promised
to end storms, droughts, and dangerous winters by regulating every
cloud.

They wanted the Heart.

Their machines had already weakened the seasons. With Summer's heart,
they believed they could create perfect weather forever.

Mara remembered shoving Eli aboard the train.

"Wait for me," she had told him.

Then she had turned back for their mother.

The mirrors flashed.

After that, nothing.

"I didn't leave you," Mara said. "I meant to come
back."

Eli was silent.

"What happened to you?"

"I waited."

"For how long?"

"I don't know. Trains don't have years."

The fog cleared.

The train had reached the mountain.

It stopped at a ruined station where weeds grew through
the platform. Above it, a stone stairway climbed toward the
hidden summit.

Mara stepped down. Eli followed.

"Can you leave the train?" she asked.

"I just did."

Behind them, the carriages began to fade. The engine gave
one tired sigh, then dissolved into mist.

They climbed.

The first section of the stairway wound through a forest
of bare trees. Water streamed upward from their roots. Dead
leaves lifted from the ground and fastened themselves to branches.
It looked almost like autumn in reverse.

Higher up, the trees gave way to black rock. The
wind sharpened. Mara's lungs burned, and the sphere grew hotter
with every step.

Eli seemed untouched by exhaustion.

Near sunset—or what passed for sunset beneath the permanent clouds—they
reached an iron door built into the mountainside.

A symbol was carved above it: a circle surrounded by
twelve rays.

Mara pressed the sphere to the lock.

The door opened.

Beyond lay the Sun House.

Its central chamber was vast and round, lined with tarnished
mirrors. At its center stood a stone basin filled with
ash. Above it, a hole in the roof revealed a
patch of black sky.

Mara approached the basin.

"This is where it goes," she said.

Eli remained at the doorway.

"No."

She turned.

The boy's face had changed. He looked older now—fifteen, then
twenty, then older still. Years passed across his features like
shadows of clouds.

"You can't put it back," he said.

"Why?"

"Because they'll find it again. People always do. They'll build
another machine. They'll cut winter short because they hate the
cold. They'll stop the rain because they don't like getting
wet. They'll pull all the clouds apart and call it
progress."

His voice deepened as he aged, then became a child's
again.

Mara understood.

"You're not Eli."

"I was."

"What are you now?"

He looked toward the basin.

"The part of summer that never arrived."

The lost years. The vanished season. All the days of
warmth that had been trapped between one moment and the
next.

The sphere flared in Mara's hand.

Eli stepped closer. "Keep it. You can make one summer.
Your summer. The one they took from you."

The mirrors brightened.

In them, Mara saw the garden of her childhood restored.
Her mother stood at the gate, alive and smiling. Eli
chased dragonflies beside the river. The table beneath the peach
tree was set for supper.

Mara could smell bread.

Her mother held out a hand.

"Mara," she called.

The sound broke something inside her.

For seven years, Mara had preserved weather for strangers. She
had kept frost cold for children who had never touched
snow. She had cared for a thunderstorm though lightning terrified
her. She had saved fragments of a world she barely
remembered.

But she had never believed she might recover her own
lost day.

She stepped toward the nearest mirror.

The garden grew clearer.

"Mara," her mother called again.

Eli stood beside the basin, watching.

"You only have to break the sphere," he said. "The
summer inside it will become yours."

"And the world?"

"The world had its chance."

Far below, the last lakes were emptying. She could feel
their water rising through the mountain. The air itself was
drying. Her lips had cracked. When she inhaled, her throat
tasted of dust.

Mara raised the sphere.

In its curved surface, she saw herself as a child
in a yellow dress.

Then the child shook her head.

Not a reflection. A memory.

Her mother's final words returned with sudden clarity.

Summer is the promise that warmth will return.

Not that it would remain.

Not that it belonged to anyone.

Return it.

Mara turned from the mirror and dropped the sphere into
the stone basin.

Nothing happened.

The golden glass rested in the ash.

Eli's face crumpled. "You chose them?"

"No," Mara said. "I chose what comes after us."

She took the silver tuning fork from her pocket.

Mr. Vale must have slipped it there before she boarded
the train.

Mara struck it against the basin.

The note rang through the Sun House.

Every mirror shattered.

The sphere broke.

Light erupted from the basin.

It passed through Mara without burning her. It filled her
bones, her eyes, the empty rooms of memory. She saw
wheat bending under noon, lizards sleeping on walls, storms building
above blue mountains. She saw every summer that had ever
been and all those that might yet come.

Eli stood in the center of the light.

For one moment, he was simply her brother: five years
old, damp-haired, furious at being left behind.

"I did wait," he said.

"I'm sorry."

"I know."

"Will I remember you?"

"If you let me go."

She knelt and opened her arms.

He came to her.

He felt like sunlight through a window.

Then he was gone.

The beam shot upward through the broken roof.

Above the mountain, the clouds ignited.

Gold spread across the sky, racing from west to east.
The rising rain stopped. For a heartbeat, every drop hung
motionless between earth and cloud.

Then gravity remembered its ancient work.

Rain fell.

It struck the Sun House roof, the mountain, the forests,
the drowned villages, and the thirsty city. It poured into
empty rivers and hammered on windows. People ran into the
streets with bowls, cups, hats, and bare hands.

In the museum, glass cases burst open.

The northern wind roared through the halls. Frost bloomed across
the floor. The bottled thunderstorm escaped into the rafters and
boomed with joy.

Mr. Vale stood beneath the backward clock and laughed as
rain flooded through the broken roof.

On Mount Orra, Mara stepped outside.

The storm had already begun to pass. At the edge
of the clouds, a strip of blue appeared.

Real blue—not the faded gray-blue of old paintings, but a
color so deep and clear that it seemed to open
forever.

Warmth touched her face.

The mountain streams reversed themselves and rushed downhill. Leaves shook
free of branches, green beneath their autumn skins. In the
valley, fields lifted from brown to gold.

The world did not become perfect.

In the years that followed, winter returned bitterly, freezing pipes
and closing roads. Spring brought floods. Summer scorched the southern
farms, and autumn storms tore roofs from houses. People complained,
adapted, planted, rebuilt, and learned again to look at the
sky with respect rather than expectation.

The Ministry of Constant Skies was dissolved. Its weather machines
were dismantled, except for one placed in the museum as
a warning.

Mara became curator after Mr. Vale retired, though he continued
to appear every morning and criticize the labels.

She added a new gallery called The Seasons That Returned.

There were snowflakes that never melted, the scent of the
first spring soil sealed in a green bottle, and an
October wind that whispered through copper leaves.

At the center stood an empty case.

Its label read:

SUMMER
Not preserved.

Visitors often asked why the case contained nothing.

Mara would lead them to the courtyard, where peach trees
grew along the old railway wall. If the day was
warm, she invited them to sit in the sun. If
rain came, she let them get wet.

Sometimes, in the bright hour before evening, she saw a
boy beside the fountain, trying to catch dragonflies. He never
stayed long, and she never called him back.

She remembered him.

That was enough.

Years later, on the first truly hot day of a
new century, Mara climbed to the museum roof. The city
shone below her. Laundry moved in the warm wind. Children
splashed in canals that had once been dry. Far away,
thunder gathered over the western hills.

A raindrop fell from an empty patch of sky and
landed in her palm.

For just a moment, it remained there, silver and trembling.

Then it burst against her skin.

Mara smiled.

Above her, the weather went on.

The Clockmaker of Bellweather Street

Every clock in Mara Venn's shop stopped at 3:17 on
the morning her father died.

The brass carriage clocks ceased with their tiny hammers lifted.
The painted cuckoo froze halfway through its door. Even the
great walnut regulator, which had kept time through storms, blackouts,
and three generations of Venns, halted with its pendulum leaning
toward the window.

Mara found them that way when she came downstairs.

For several minutes she stood in the middle of the
shop, listening to the rain tap against the glass. Without
ticking, the room felt too large. The cabinets, workbenches, and
rows of patient faces seemed not merely silent but watchful.

Then the telephone rang.

By sunrise, she knew.

Her father's ship, the Mercy Bell, had struck the northern
reef while returning to Bellweather. Eleven sailors had been rescued.
Two had not.

Among the missing was Elias Venn, clockmaker, widower, and a
man who had gone to sea only once in his
sixty-two years.

No body was found.

For the first week, neighbors filled the shop with bread,
soup, flowers, and the soft, useless sentences people bring to
the bereaved.

"He may still be found."

"The currents are strange this time of year."

"At least he was not alone."

Mara accepted everything. She thanked everyone. At night, she carried
the untouched food upstairs and sat on the edge of
her father's bed.

He had left his spectacles folded beside a book. His
gray coat hung behind the door. One boot lay on
its side beneath the chair, as if he had just
kicked it off.

The room waited for him more faithfully than Mara could.

On the eighth day, she opened the shop.

Bellweather Street smelled of wet stone and chimney smoke. Across
the road, Mr. Vale raised the shutters of his bakery.
Farther downhill, the sea appeared between rooftops, dull and iron-colored
beneath the November sky.

Mara turned the sign from CLOSED to OPEN.

The clocks remained stopped at 3:17.

She had tried winding them, oiling them, replacing springs, and
resetting hands. Nothing worked. A few ticked for several seconds,
but each eventually shuddered back to the same hour and
minute.

All except one.

In the bottom drawer of her father's workbench, beneath a
stack of invoices and a square of blue velvet, Mara
found a silver pocket watch she had never seen before.

It was plain, heavy, and cold. No maker's mark appeared
on its case. When she pressed the crown, the lid
sprang open.

Inside, delicate black hands circled a white enamel face.

It was keeping perfect time.

Tucked beneath the watch was an envelope bearing her name.

Mara,

If the clocks stop, do not repair the silver watch.

Do not open the inner case.

And whatever you hear, do not answer.

Forgive me.

—Father

Mara read the note three times.

Then the watch chimed.

The sound was impossibly deep for such a small object.
It passed through the room like the single toll of
a distant harbor bell.

The stopped clocks answered.

Not with chimes. With whispers.

At first Mara thought it was rain sliding down the
window. Then she heard her name.

"Mara."

She turned.

No one stood behind her.

"Mara, love."

Her knees weakened.

The voice came from the walnut regulator.

It was faint and warped, as though traveling through miles
of wind, but it belonged to her father. She knew
the roughness at the edge of his words, the slight
upward turn he gave her name.

She crossed the shop and pressed both hands against the
clock's glass.

"Father?"

The regulator's pendulum stirred.

"Mara, can you hear me?"

She remembered the note.

Whatever you hear, do not answer.

Her reflection stared from the glass, pale and wide-eyed.

"I can hear you," she whispered.

Every clock in the shop began to tick.

The sound crashed around her, hundreds of mechanisms springing to
life at once. The cuckoo burst from its door. Bells
rang. Springs shrieked. Hands spun forward and backward in silver
blurs.

Then all was silent again.

The regulator showed 3:18.

"Mara," her father's voice said, clearer now. "Listen to me.
There isn't much time."

She pulled a chair close.

"Where are you?"

"I don't know."

"Are you alive?"

A long pause followed.

"No."

The word should have broken her. Instead, the certainty steadied
something inside her. For eight days she had been trapped
between hope and grief, unable to enter either. Now the
door had closed behind her.

She sat down.

"What happened?"

"The reef took the hull. I was below deck." His
voice faded, then returned. "I had the watch with me.
I thought I could destroy it."

"What is it?"

"A clock that doesn't measure time."

Mara looked at the silver watch on the workbench.

"What does it measure?"

"The distance between the living and the dead."

Outside, a cart rattled down Bellweather Street. Somewhere a dog
barked. The ordinary world continued within arm's reach, yet it
seemed less real than the voice inside the clock.

Her father said, "Your mother made it."

Mara stopped breathing.

Lena Venn had died when Mara was five. Mara remembered
dark hair, warm hands, and songs without words. Her father
rarely spoke of her. When asked, he said only that
grief was a room best visited with the door left
open.

"She was a clockmaker?"

"The finest I ever knew."

"You told me she painted miniatures."

"She did that too."

"Why did she make this?"

The regulator's minute hand trembled.

"For you."

Mara glanced at the note again. Forgive me.

"What do you mean?"

"You were born too early. The doctor said you wouldn't
survive the night. Your mother refused to accept it. She
believed death wasn't a wall, only a mechanism—something with laws,
gates, and moving parts. She believed every mechanism could be
altered."

Mara wanted to dismiss the story as impossible, but impossibility
was speaking to her from a clock.

"What did she do?"

"She built the watch. She opened the inner case. And
she called you back."

A chill moved through the shop.

"From where?"

"I don't know. But something came with you."

The windows darkened as clouds passed over the street.

Her father's voice grew urgent. "The watch creates a passage.
Each time someone answers a voice from the other side,
the passage widens. Your mother heard her own father. Then
her sister. Then strangers. By the time she understood, it
was too late."

Mara remembered almost nothing of her mother's death. There had
been a winter morning, a locked room, and her father
carrying something wrapped in a red blanket toward the sea.

"You said she died of fever."

"I lied."

The regulator ticked once.

"She opened the watch too far," Elias continued. "The dead
began using her memories to enter the house. They wore
familiar voices. Familiar faces. She tried to close the passage,
but closing it required someone living to carry the watch
beyond the reach of clocks."

"The sea," Mara said.

"Yes."

"You took it on the Mercy Bell."

"I should have done so years ago. I couldn't. The
watch was the last thing she touched."

Anger rose through Mara, sharp and clean.

"So you kept it. Here. Beneath your workbench."

"I was weak."

"And now you're dead."

"Yes."

The plainness of his answer exhausted her anger. She pressed
her forehead to the regulator's case.

"What am I supposed to do?"

"Take the watch to Saint Orra's Tower. At the top
is the town's first clock. It was built before the
streets, before the harbor wall. Place the watch inside the
bell chamber when the clock strikes midnight."

"And then?"

"Break it."

The silver watch chimed again.

This time the sound did not fade. It deepened until
the windowpanes shook.

From the clocks came a hundred breaths.

Then voices filled the shop.

Some wept. Some laughed. Some called names Mara did not
know. Others begged to be heard. The cuckoo clock spoke
in the voice of a child. A brass alarm clock
muttered prayers. From a little porcelain clock on the mantel
came the low singing Mara remembered from her earliest dreams.

She turned toward it.

"Mara," said her mother.

The voice was warm as lamplight.

Mara took one step.

The regulator struck its case from within.

"Don't listen," her father said.

"My little bird," Lena's voice murmured. "You grew up."

Mara covered her ears, but the words seemed to form
inside her bones.

"I waited so long."

The porcelain clock's hands moved to 3:19.

A hairline crack appeared across its painted face.

"Open the watch," Lena said, "and let me see you."

Mara seized the silver watch and snapped its lid shut.

All the voices stopped.

The shop smelled suddenly of seawater.

From beneath the front door, a dark tide crept over
the floorboards. It spread around Mara's boots, though outside the
street was dry.

In the black surface she saw faces.

Not reflections. Faces beneath water.

Mara stumbled backward.

Her father shouted from the regulator, "Go now!"

She wrapped the watch in the blue velvet, shoved it
into her coat pocket, and ran.

Saint Orra's Tower stood on the hill above Bellweather, its
copper roof green with age. The town had grown around
it in crooked rings, every street descending eventually toward the
sea.

Mara pushed uphill through the afternoon fog.

Behind her, bells began ringing.

First one, then dozens.

Shop bells. Church bells. Harbor buoys. Dinner bells in private
homes. They rang without rhythm, spreading the silver watch's call
across Bellweather.

Doors opened as Mara passed.

People stepped into the street.

Mrs. Tallow, the schoolmistress, emerged from her house with tears
streaming down her cheeks.

"Edwin?" she called.

At the cobbler's shop, old Mr. Pike pressed his ear
to the wall.

"My sons," he said. "Both of them. I hear them."

More townspeople appeared, following voices only they could hear. Some
smiled. Some sobbed. All began walking toward Mara.

The watch pulsed in her pocket.

She quickened her pace.

At the base of the tower, she found the iron
gate locked. A sign announced that repairs had been suspended
until spring.

Mara rattled the bars.

Behind her, the crowd entered the square.

There were perhaps fifty people now. Mr. Vale still wore
his flour-dusted apron. The harbor master carried an unlit lantern.
A young woman Mara did not know hugged a framed
portrait to her chest.

"Mara," Mr. Vale said gently, "you have something that belongs
to us."

She backed against the gate.

"My wife is in there," he continued. "I heard her.
After nine years, I heard her."

"It isn't her."

His expression hardened.

"You don't know that."

Mara thought of her mother's voice. My little bird.

No imitation should have known those words.

Or perhaps the dead truly waited inside the watch. Perhaps
her father wanted to destroy the only bridge that remained
because he feared what might cross it. But what if
bridges could be guarded? What if the passage could be
controlled?

The watch chimed through her coat.

The gate unlocked itself.

Mara slipped through and slammed it behind her. The crowd
surged forward, hands reaching between the bars.

She ran into the tower.

A spiral staircase wound upward through darkness. The air smelled
of rust, dust, and old rain. High above, the great
clock's mechanism groaned.

Mara climbed.

At the first landing, she heard footsteps below.

The gate had opened.

At the second landing, her father spoke from inside her
pocket.

"You must hurry."

"How do I know it's you?" she gasped.

No answer came.

She stopped.

That was the horror of it. Her father had warned
that the dead wore familiar voices, but he too was
dead. Every direction required trust, and trust itself had become
a trap.

"Mara," he said.

She took the watch from her pocket.

"Tell me something only you would know."

"The first clock you repaired was a green alarm clock
from the sailors' hostel. You were eight. You put the
spring in backward."

"Everyone in the shop knew that."

"When you were twelve, you ran away to the western
cliffs. I found you asleep under the signal bridge."

"People saw us return."

His voice weakened. "On the night your mother died, you
asked me why the moon followed our house."

Mara closed her eyes.

She remembered sitting wrapped in a red blanket, not her
mother but herself, while her father carried her toward the
sea. She remembered the moon above his shoulder. She remembered
asking the question.

"What did you say?"

"I said the moon followed everyone, but lonely people noticed
it most."

Mara resumed climbing.

Below, the crowd entered the stairwell.

The third landing opened into the clock room. Gears taller
than Mara turned with ancient patience. Iron teeth caught and
released. Chains descended into darkness. The four clock faces glowed
blue through the fog outside.

The mechanism showed eleven minutes to midnight.

That was impossible. It had been afternoon when Mara entered
the tower.

She looked through the western clock face.

Night covered Bellweather.

The streets below were filled with people. Hundreds surrounded the
tower, each holding a lamp or candle. The town shone
like a fallen constellation.

"The watch bends time around itself," her father said. "You're
close now."

A ladder led to the bell chamber.

Mara climbed through the hatch.

The bell hung above her, vast and black. Wind moved
through the open arches. Beyond them, the sea stretched into
moonless dark.

Ten minutes remained.

Mara placed the watch on the stone floor.

"How do I break it?"

"When the bell strikes, open the inner case. There's a
glass wheel beneath the face. Stop it before the twelfth
stroke."

"And if I can't?"

The hatch burst open.

Mr. Vale climbed into the chamber, followed by Mrs. Tallow
and the harbor master. More people crowded the ladder beneath
them.

"You don't have to do this," Mr. Vale said.

Mara stood over the watch.

"Yes, I do."

"You would take them from us again?"

"They're already gone."

Mrs. Tallow flinched as if struck.

"No," she said. "Gone is silence. Gone is an empty
chair. My Edwin spoke to me."

The watch opened by itself.

Its black hands spun.

Light poured from the face, pale and fluid, and gathered
into the shape of a woman.

She appeared first as a reflection on glass. Then color
touched her cheeks. Dark hair fell over her shoulders. She
wore the blue dress from the portrait above Mara's childhood
bed.

Lena Venn stood beside the bell.

The crowd fell silent.

"Mara," she said.

Mara could not move.

Her mother smiled, and the years between them collapsed. Mara
was five again, drowsy beside the stove while Lena sang
and painted tiny gold stars on a clock face.

"My little bird."

"Are you really her?" Mara asked.

"Yes."

"Father said something came back with me."

Pain crossed Lena's face.

"Your father was afraid of what he couldn't understand."

"Did you make the watch?"

"I made a door. Doors are not evil because wolves
may use them."

From the watch, Elias shouted, "That is not your mother!"

Lena looked down sadly.

"He has always mistaken love for possession."

Mara's throat tightened. Her father had kept the watch for
seventeen years. He had lied about Lena's death. He had
taken it to sea only when—why?

A thought came to her.

"Father," she said, "why did you finally decide to destroy
it?"

Silence.

"Why that night?"

The watch hands continued spinning.

Lena's smile faded.

At last Elias answered. "Because I heard you."

Mara stared at the watch.

"You heard me?"

"Calling from inside it. Crying for help."

"But I was upstairs."

"I checked. You were asleep. Then I understood that it
had learned your voice."

The figure beside the bell flickered.

Elias continued, "It doesn't bring back the dead, Mara. It
consumes what the living remember and builds masks from those
memories. Every answer gives it more. Every longing opens the
way wider."

Lena stepped forward.

"Or perhaps he wants you to believe that."

Mara looked into her mother's face.

She searched for some flaw and found none. The same
dark eyes. The small scar near the left eyebrow. The
silver ring on her thumb.

Then Mara remembered something her father had once said while
teaching her to repair painted dials: Perfection is the signature
of a machine. Human hands always leave a mistake.

She looked at the blue dress.

Every gold button was fastened.

In the portrait above Mara's bed, the third button had
always been missing.

The tower clock began to strike.

The first blow shook dust from the beams.

Mara dropped to her knees and seized the watch.

Lena screamed.

The beautiful figure stretched upward, her mouth widening beyond the
limits of a human face. Darkness spilled from her eyes.

The second stroke sounded.

Mara dug her fingernail beneath the inner case. It would
not lift.

Hands grabbed her shoulders. Mr. Vale pulled her backward.

"My wife!" he shouted.

The third stroke.

Mara kicked free and struck the watch against the stone
floor. The crystal cracked, but the hands kept spinning.

The crowd surged around her.

From the open watch came their beloved dead: children, parents,
husbands, sisters. Pale figures filled the chamber, each calling to
someone.

The fourth stroke.

Mrs. Tallow walked into her brother's arms. His body opened
like smoke and folded around her. She vanished without a
sound.

The crowd recoiled.

The fifth stroke.

Mr. Vale stared at the place where Mrs. Tallow had
stood. Then he lifted his boot and brought it down
on the watch.

The case split.

A shriek rose from every clock in Bellweather.

The sixth stroke.

Beneath the enamel face, Mara saw a wheel of clear
glass turning in a nest of silver gears.

She reached for it.

Lena's hand closed around her wrist.

The touch was cold, but not empty. Memories flooded Mara:
her mother laughing in summer rain, her mother leaning over
a cradle, her mother burning her fingers as she fitted
the final spring into the silver watch.

Some of the memories were Mara's.

Some could not be.

The seventh stroke.

"You came back wrong," Lena whispered. "He never told you
that part."

Mara looked at her.

"The child I called from death was not alone. You
carry the door too."

The eighth stroke.

"You cannot destroy it without destroying what keeps you here."

Mara's fingers hovered above the glass wheel.

Across the chamber, the false dead tore through the crowd
like shadows in a storm. People fled toward the hatch.
Mr. Vale held the ladder, helping them descend.

The ninth stroke.

"Father?" Mara called.

His voice came faintly from the broken watch.

"I don't know what will happen."

"Did you know?"

"No."

"Is that why you kept it?"

A pause.

"Yes."

The tenth stroke.

Mara understood then. Her father had not kept the watch
only because it was the last thing Lena touched. He
had kept it because the machine might also be the
last thing holding his daughter in the world.

He had chosen, day after day, the danger of grief
over the certainty of losing her.

The eleventh stroke began.

Lena smiled.

"You see? He couldn't let you go."

Mara gripped the glass wheel.

"No," she said. "But he tried."

She tore it free.

The twelfth stroke fell.

For one instant, every light in Bellweather went out.

The bell chamber disappeared.

Mara stood on a shore beneath a sky without stars.
Countless figures waited in the dark water. Some wore faces.
Some had forgotten how.

Her father stood among them.

He looked younger, his gray hair dark again, his shoulders
straight beneath his old work apron.

"Am I dead?" Mara asked.

"I don't know," he said.

Behind him, far across the water, her mother waited. Not
the perfect figure from the watch, but a tired woman
in a blue dress with one button missing.

Lena raised her hand.

Mara wanted to run to her.

Instead, Elias stepped forward and placed his palm against Mara's
cheek.

"The moon follows everyone," he said.

The dark shore cracked like glass.

Mara woke at dawn.

She lay on the floor of the bell chamber. The
great bell was silent above her. Around her, the townspeople
stirred among fragments of silver and glass.

Mr. Vale knelt nearby.

When he saw her open her eyes, he began to
laugh.

Mara sat up.

No voices came from the broken watch. Its gears were
still. The glass wheel had shattered in her hand, leaving
a thin red cut across her palm.

Below, Bellweather's clocks began striking six.

Not together. Not perfectly.

One rang early. Another was slow. The bakery clock chimed
seven times, as it had for years. From somewhere near
the harbor came the ridiculous call of a cuckoo.

Mara listened until the last bell faded.

Months later, she reopened the shop on Bellweather Street.

The clocks that had stopped at 3:17 could not be
restored. Mara left them as they were, their hands marking
the moment the old passage opened for the final time.

Customers often asked why she kept so many broken clocks.

"They aren't broken," she would say. "They have finished."

The silver watch remained in a locked drawer beneath her
workbench. Nothing of its mechanism had survived except one black
hand and a sliver of white enamel. Mara could have
thrown them into the sea, but she did not.

Some doors, once closed, did not need to be erased.

Years passed.

Mara became a better clockmaker than her father, though she
never believed it. She repaired tower clocks, ship clocks, kitchen
clocks, and watches worn smooth by generations of hands. She
married no one, but she was not lonely. Children gathered
in her shop after school, and she taught them to
listen for bent springs and tired gears.

On certain winter nights, when rain tapped the windows and
fog pressed close to Bellweather Street, all the clocks seemed
to pause between one second and the next.

In that pause, Mara sometimes thought she heard her father
clearing his throat at the neighboring bench.

She never answered.

She only smiled, bent over her work, and waited for
time to move again.

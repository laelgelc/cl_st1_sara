# The Museum of Unfinished Things

Every morning at nine, Mara Vale unlocked the museum's brass
doors and listened for the sound of rain.

It did not matter whether the sky was clear. Inside
the Museum of Unfinished Things, rain fell somewhere every day.

Sometimes it whispered against an invisible roof above the Hall
of Abandoned Journeys. Sometimes it tapped inside a blue porcelain
cup that had never been glazed. Once, for three weeks,
it poured steadily behind a framed canvas on which an
artist had painted only a horizon.

The rain never left puddles. It simply sounded, as if
the building were remembering weather.

Mara had worked at the museum for thirty-two years. She
knew every incomplete object in its collection: the violin without
strings, the clockmaker's final gear, the book ending in the
middle of a sentence, the wedding dress with one sleeve,
and the wooden staircase that climbed nine steps before stopping
in empty air.

Visitors often laughed when they first entered.

"So it's a museum of rubbish?" they asked.

"Of intentions," Mara would reply.

That usually quieted them.

The museum stood in the oldest part of Bellwether, squeezed
between a bakery and a narrow house whose windows had
been bricked up. Its founder, Silas Vale, had been Mara's
grandfather, although she had never met him. According to family
legend, he began collecting unfinished things after returning from a
war he refused to discuss. He believed that abandoned work
retained a trace of the future intended for it.

"Completion closes a door," he had written in the museum's
charter. "The unfinished leaves it open."

Mara had always considered this beautiful nonsense.

Then, on a gray Tuesday in November, a boy brought
her the moon.

He arrived five minutes before closing, soaked from the rain
and carrying a small cardboard box under one arm.

"I have something for the museum," he said.

He was perhaps twelve, thin and pale, with black hair
dripping into his eyes. His coat was too large for
him. One cuff had been repaired with red thread.

"Donations are evaluated on Thursdays," Mara said. "You'll need to
fill out a form."

"I can't come Thursday."

"Then leave your name and telephone number."

"I don't have a telephone."

Mara looked past him toward the street. The rain had
emptied it. The lamps glowed in the early dark, and
water ran silver along the gutters.

"What's in the box?"

The boy set it on the admissions desk.

"An unfinished moon."

Mara had heard every possible joke about her museum, but
the boy did not appear amused. He lifted the lid.

Inside lay a sphere no larger than an apple. Most
of it was dark and rough, like a lump of
charcoal. One curved section, however, shone with a soft white
light.

The light did not flicker. It gathered around Mara's fingers
when she reached toward it, cool and fine as flour.

She withdrew her hand.

"What is it made of?"

"I don't know."

"Where did you get it?"

"My father made it."

Mara stared at the glowing crescent. "Your father made the
moon?"

"Not the real one. A new one."

The boy's voice remained calm, but his hands tightened around
the box.

"What's your name?" Mara asked.

"Eli."

"And your father's?"

"Jonas Or."

The name meant nothing to her. "Is he an artist?"

"He repaired streetlights. But at night he made things."

"What sort of things?"

Eli shrugged. "Things people needed."

The museum clock chimed five. In the galleries, the automatic
lights began to dim.

Mara closed the box.

"I'll give you a receipt, and our committee will—"

"No committee." Eli pushed the box toward her. "You have
to keep it safe. He said this was the only
place."

"The only place for what?"

But the boy was already backing away.

"Eli."

He paused at the door.

"Why didn't your father finish it?"

For the first time, his expression changed. Beneath wet hair,
his face became suddenly younger.

"He disappeared."

Then he ran into the rain.

Mara stood behind the desk for several minutes, listening to
the fading slap of his shoes on the pavement.

She should have called the police. She should have found
the boy, asked where he lived, and contacted whoever was
responsible for him. Instead, she carried the box to her
office.

The sphere lit the room even after she switched off
the lamp.

Mara examined it with a magnifying glass. The dark portion
was pitted and dull, while the completed crescent contained delicate
plains, mountains, and craters. A white thread of cloud drifted
across its surface.

She blinked.

The cloud had moved.

Mara fetched the museum's curator, Mr. Saye, who had been
preparing to leave.

He was a nervous man with a pointed beard and
the habit of polishing his spectacles when confronted by anything
surprising. By the time Mara finished explaining, the lenses were
immaculate.

"It's a mechanism," he said.

"Where is the power source?"

"Hidden."

"Inside a solid sphere?"

"Apparently."

They brought the object to the conservation room and placed
it beneath an X-ray scanner. The image showed only a
pale curve surrounded by darkness.

"No wires," Mara said.

"Then the machine is malfunctioning."

They tried another.

The second machine stopped working altogether.

Mr. Saye folded his arms. "We should lock it in
storage."

"It was donated for display."

"By a child five minutes before closing."

"The museum has accepted stranger objects."

"Not glowing ones."

Mara looked at the sphere. The crescent had widened slightly.

She was almost certain of it.

They placed the unfinished moon in a glass case in
Gallery Seven, between a composer's silent opera and a chessboard
carved with only black pieces. Mara attached a temporary label.

UNTITLED SPHERE
Maker: Jonas Or
Materials: Unknown
Date: Unknown
Status: Incomplete

That night, she dreamed of her sister.

In the dream, Lena was seventeen again, standing on the
museum's unfinished staircase. She wore the yellow dress she had
worn the summer before she died, and her brown hair
was tied with a green ribbon.

"Come up," Lena called.

"There's nowhere to go," Mara said.

"You always say that."

Lena climbed beyond the ninth step and continued upward, placing
her feet on stairs that were not there.

Mara woke before dawn with the sound of rain inside
her wardrobe.

By the time she arrived at the museum, a crowd
had gathered outside Gallery Seven.

The moon was larger.

It now filled most of its case, nearly twice the
size it had been the previous evening. Half its surface
shone. Tiny clouds moved over silver seas. Along the bright
edge, mountains rose from darkness like teeth.

Mr. Saye stood beside the case, pale with astonishment.

"It's impossible," he whispered.

Mara asked the guards whether anyone had entered overnight. No
alarms had sounded. The cameras showed an empty gallery, though
at 3:17 in the morning every recording had turned white
for exactly one minute.

News traveled quickly.

By noon, reporters crowded the museum steps. The mayor arrived
with two scientists from Bellwether University. They tested the glass,
measured the light, and argued in low voices. One insisted
that the sphere was a highly advanced projection. The other
placed his ear against the case and began to cry.

"What did you hear?" Mara asked.

"My mother singing," he said.

Visitors reported different sounds.

Some heard waves, others wind through grass. A little girl
heard bells from a village where she had lived before
crossing the sea. A soldier heard the voice of a
friend killed twenty years earlier. Mr. Saye heard applause.

Mara heard nothing.

"Perhaps you aren't listening correctly," he suggested.

"I know how to listen."

Still, when the gallery emptied that evening, she remained beside
the case.

The moon had grown to the size of a melon.
Its bright half illuminated the other exhibits, casting long shadows
from the unfinished chess pieces.

Mara pressed her palm to the glass.

At first there was silence.

Then Lena laughed.

The sound was so clear that Mara turned around.

No one stood behind her.

"Mara," the voice whispered from inside the case.

She pulled away.

The glowing sphere continued its slow expansion.

That night, the city council ordered the museum closed. The
object might be dangerous, they said. It could produce radiation,
contaminate the air, or explode. A government agency sent a
black van and six people carrying sealed metal containers.

They attempted to remove the moon.

The moment they touched the glass case, every unfinished object
in the museum began to move.

The stringless violin played a single low note.

The unglazed cup overflowed with invisible rain.

Letters appeared in the half-written book, racing across the final
page before vanishing at the edge.

Most alarming of all, the staircase acquired a tenth step.

The government officials left without the sphere.

By the third day, the moon had broken its case.

It hovered a meter above the gallery floor, perfectly round
and as broad as a dining table. Three-quarters of its
surface shone, and tides moved across its miniature oceans.

No one could explain how it floated. No one could
approach closer than six paces. Those who tried found themselves
remembering a promise they had broken.

A police officer remembered telling his daughter he would return
from the hospital.

The mayor remembered a bridge he had pledged to repair.

Mr. Saye remembered that, as a boy, he had intended
to become a pianist.

Mara remembered Lena's hand slipping from hers above the river.

She left the gallery trembling.

The sisters had been fourteen and twelve when Lena died.
They had climbed the flood barrier during a storm, daring
each other to stand closer to the edge. Their father
had forbidden them to go near the river, but rules
had always felt negotiable when Lena smiled.

A wave struck the wall.

Lena slipped.

Mara caught her wrist.

For one stretched and endless second, they looked at each
other. Lena's green ribbon tore free and flew into the
rain. Mara tried to pull her up, but the stone
was slick and Lena was heavy.

"Don't let go," Lena said.

Mara had not meant to.

That fact had never mattered.

For fifty years, she had carried the unfinished act of
saving her sister.

Now the moon knew.

On the fourth day, Eli returned.

The museum was surrounded by barricades, news vans, and frightened
officials, but he entered through the bakery's cellar. There was
an old connecting door, a relic from when both buildings
had belonged to the same monastery. Only Mara and the
baker knew it existed.

Eli found her in the Hall of Abandoned Journeys, sitting
beneath the suspended frame of an airplane that had never
flown.

"You put it on display," he said.

"You said it belonged here."

"I said to keep it safe."

"You failed to mention it would grow."

"I didn't know."

Mara stood. "Where is your father?"

"I told you. He disappeared."

"That isn't an answer."

Eli looked toward Gallery Seven, where white light poured beneath
the doors.

"He started making the moon after my mother died," he
said. "She was sick for a long time. On the
last night, she wanted to see the moon, but the
sky was cloudy. My father promised he would bring it
to her."

Mara said nothing.

"He built the little sphere from things he found. Broken
mirrors. Clock parts. Stones from the river. He worked until
morning, but he couldn't finish. My mother died before sunrise."

"And afterward?"

"He kept working. The moon never changed, not for years.
Then last week he said he understood what was missing."

"What?"

"An ending."

The rain began somewhere overhead. It sounded heavier than usual.

Eli reached into his coat and removed a small brass
key.

"He told me the museum had many unfinished things but
no unfinished people. He said people were different because they
could choose whether to remain unfinished."

"That sounds like something my grandfather would have written."

"Maybe he did. My father came here a lot."

Mara looked at the key. "What does it open?"

"I don't know. The night he disappeared, he gave it
to me and told me to bring the moon here.
He said someone would know what to do."

"Who?"

"A woman who had forgotten that letting go and giving
up are not the same thing."

Mara felt a coldness unrelated to the rain.

"Did he know my name?"

Eli nodded.

A deep crack sounded from Gallery Seven.

They ran.

The moon had grown again. It nearly filled the room,
pressing exhibits against the walls. Its surface was almost complete.
Only a small dark patch remained at its southern edge.

The unfinished objects around it were changing.

The silent opera poured from sheets of music in a
storm of voices. White chess pieces formed from dust. The
book's final sentence burned across its page:

When at last she opened the door, she found—

Then the words stopped.

The staircase now had dozens of steps. They twisted upward
through a hole that had appeared in the ceiling, climbing
into a sky full of stars.

At the base of the stairs stood a man.

He was tall and tired-looking, wearing the blue uniform of
a city lamplighter. Gray threaded his dark beard. One hand
rested on the railing.

"Father," Eli breathed.

Jonas Or turned.

Eli tried to run to him, but Mara caught his
shoulder. The space between the man and the staircase shimmered
like summer heat.

Jonas smiled sadly. "You brought it."

"You said you'd come back," Eli cried.

"I said I would try."

"You lied."

"No." Jonas looked up the impossible staircase. "I failed."

The moon pulsed. For an instant, Gallery Seven vanished, replaced
by a vast white plain under a black sky. Mara
smelled cold dust. Above them hung the Earth, small and
blue.

Then the gallery returned.

"What is happening?" Mara asked.

Jonas studied her. "The moon is finishing itself."

"How?"

"With everything in this building. Every abandoned plan, every broken
promise, every life that might have been. It is drawing
possibility into itself."

"And when it is complete?"

"It will become real."

Eli stared at him. "A real moon?"

"A new world," Jonas said. "Made from unfinished futures."

"That sounds almost beautiful," Mara said.

"It is. But this world will lose them."

From somewhere beyond the gallery came a crash. Mara pictured
the thousands of objects in the museum becoming ordinary debris,
emptied of all intention.

"What does that mean for people?"

"They will lose what they have not yet done."

Mara thought of the city outside: letters unwritten, apologies unspoken,
children unconceived, journeys postponed but still possible. A world without
unfinished futures would not end dramatically. It would simply become
fixed.

No one would change.

"Stop it," she said.

"I can't."

"You made it."

"I began it. There is a difference."

The dark patch on the moon narrowed.

Jonas pointed toward the top of the staircase. "There is
a door. The key opens it. Beyond the door is
the last unfinished moment of whoever enters."

Eli held up the brass key.

"If the moment is completed," Jonas continued, "the moon will
be completed too. It will depart and take every possibility
with it."

"And if the moment is abandoned?" Mara asked.

"The moon will break apart. Everything it has taken will
return."

"Then why are you standing here?"

Jonas looked at his son.

"My unfinished moment is the promise I made to his
mother. If I open the door, I can finish the
moon and bring it to her. I can keep my
word."

"She is dead," Eli said.

"Yes."

"You can't give it to her."

"Behind that door, perhaps I can."

Eli's face twisted. "And what happens to us?"

Jonas closed his eyes.

Mara understood then. The moon did not feed merely on
regret. It offered a trade: the living future for the
chance to repair the dead past.

She thought of Lena on the river wall, her wrist
warm in Mara's hands.

A door waited somewhere above.

Mara could climb to it. She could use the key.
She could return to the storm and grip Lena with
both hands. Perhaps this time she would pull her up.
Perhaps they would go home together, shivering and laughing, and
grow old side by side.

The desire struck with such force that she nearly fell.

The moon brightened.

It had felt her longing.

Mara turned to Eli. "Give me the key."

He hesitated, then placed it in her palm.

Jonas stepped forward. "You don't know what you're choosing."

"Yes," Mara said. "I do."

She began to climb.

The staircase creaked beneath her. After ten steps she passed
through the broken ceiling, but instead of reaching the floor
above, she entered darkness.

Stars surrounded the staircase. Below, Gallery Seven dwindled to a
square of white light.

As she climbed, doors appeared on either side.

Behind one, she heard her mother singing while she prepared
supper.

Behind another, her father said, "I'm proud of you."

A third door opened by itself, revealing Mara at twenty-five,
standing on a station platform as a train pulled away.
She had once planned to board that train, leave Bellwether,
and study architecture in the capital. Instead, she had remained
to care for her grieving parents.

The young Mara watched the train disappear.

"Not that one," the older Mara whispered.

She climbed on.

At the top stood a narrow green door, wet with
rain.

The brass key fit.

Mara opened it and returned to the river.

She was twelve again, lying on slick stone. Wind tore
at her coat. Below, brown water raged against the barrier.

Lena dangled over the edge.

"Don't let go!" she screamed.

Mara felt the strain in her shoulder, the terrible slipping
of skin against skin.

But she was also herself at sixty-two. She carried five
decades of grief, anger, memory, and endurance.

She knew how this moment ended.

That was what made it unfinished—not that its outcome remained
uncertain, but that Mara had refused to live beyond it.

She had spent her life gripping Lena's wrist.

The river waited.

"Mara!" Lena sobbed.

"I'm sorry," Mara said.

With her free hand, she reached down.

For one impossible moment, she held her sister's wrist with
both hands. Strength flooded her arms. She could pull Lena
upward. She saw the future opening: Lena surviving, Lena marrying,
Lena quarreling with her, Lena growing old. A thousand bright
scenes unfolded across the storm.

All she had to do was choose them.

And outside the green door, the moon would become whole.

Mara looked into her sister's eyes.

"I love you," she said.

Lena's frightened face softened.

"I know."

Mara opened her hands.

Lena fell into the river and vanished.

This time, Mara watched.

This time, she did not tell herself that she had
chosen it, or caused it, or could still undo it
if she suffered long enough.

She knelt in the rain and screamed until the world
became only sound.

Then the river dissolved.

Mara stood before the green door, old again. The key
was warm in her hand.

Behind her, the staircase shook.

The moon cracked.

A line of darkness split its glowing surface. Light burst
through Gallery Seven, pouring upward between the stars. The staircase
twisted. Doors flew open all around Mara, releasing voices, music,
wind, and the noise of countless unwritten days.

She ran downward.

The steps vanished behind her one by one.

When she reached the gallery, Eli was pulling his father
away from the collapsing moon. Mara seized Jonas's arm, and
together they fell through the doorway as the sphere broke
apart.

There was no explosion.

The moon became a million white moths.

They filled the museum, streaming through halls and stairwells, settling
briefly on every unfinished object. One touched the stringless violin,
which sighed. Another vanished into the unglazed cup. Hundreds disappeared
between the pages of the half-written book.

The remaining moths flew through windows and cracks, out over
Bellwether.

For several minutes, the whole city shone.

Then darkness returned.

Jonas lay unconscious on the gallery floor. Eli knelt beside
him, gripping his hand.

"Is he alive?" Mara asked.

Jonas coughed.

Eli began to laugh and cry at once.

Mr. Saye emerged from beneath the chess table. His spectacles
were broken, and one half of his beard had turned
white.

"Is it over?" he asked.

Mara looked around.

The staircase ended at nine steps.

The opera was silent.

The chessboard held only black pieces.

In the display case, among shards of glass, lay a
dark sphere the size of an apple. A thin silver
crescent curved across its surface.

"Not over," Mara said. "Unfinished."

The museum reopened in spring.

Jonas recovered, though he remembered little about the staircase. He
took a job repairing the museum's lights, most of which
had failed during the incident. Eli visited after school and
did his homework at the admissions desk.

The unfinished moon remained in Gallery Seven, now protected by
a simple wooden box. It did not grow again. Visitors
occasionally claimed to hear voices from it, but Mara never
did.

She heard only rain.

One evening, she found Eli standing beside the half-written book.

"Do you think it knows the ending?" he asked.

"Which ending?"

"The book's."

"Perhaps."

"Why doesn't it show us?"

Mara considered the final line.

When at last she opened the door, she found—

"Because then it would be finished," she said.

Eli frowned. "Isn't that the point of a story?"

"Sometimes."

Outside, bells marked the hour. Jonas called from the front
hall, and Eli hurried away.

Mara made her final round alone.

She passed the wedding dress, the silent violin, the one-sided
chess set, and the airplane that had never flown. At
the base of the staircase, she stopped.

For years she had disliked that exhibit. It had seemed
foolish, nine steps leading nowhere.

Now she climbed to the top.

The museum spread below her, dim and quiet. Beyond the
high windows, Bellwether's evening lights appeared one by one. Each
lamp marked a home, a room, a person still capable
of becoming someone else.

Mara stood on the ninth step.

There was no tenth.

There did not need to be.

She descended, turned off the lights, and locked the brass
doors behind her.

Above the city hung the old moon—scarred, distant, and complete
enough to shine.

Mara looked at it for a while.

Then she walked home through the rain.

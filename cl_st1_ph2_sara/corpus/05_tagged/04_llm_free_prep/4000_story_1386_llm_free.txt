# The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, Mara Vale unlocked the clock shop
on Bellweather Street and listened to the town begin.

First came the bakery shutters rattling open across the square.
Then the milk cart, its wheels complaining over the stones.
At half past six, pigeons descended upon the fountain. At
seven, the church bell struck seven times, always a little
too slowly, as if reluctant to surrender the previous hour.

Mara repaired clocks beneath a blue wooden sign that read:

**VALE & DAUGHTER
CLOCKS RESTORED, TIME KEPT**

Her father had painted the sign when she was ten.
He had been dead for twelve years, but Mara had
never changed it. She sometimes imagined that the words still
held a place for him, somewhere between the ampersand and
the fading gold letters.

The shop was narrow and deep. Hundreds of clocks covered
its walls: walnut mantel clocks, brass carriage clocks, cuckoo clocks
with painted doors, pocket watches no larger than walnuts, and
tall grandfather clocks that stared down like solemn judges. They
ticked at different speeds, producing a steady rainfall of sound.

Visitors often found the noise unsettling.

"How can you bear it?" they asked.

Mara always gave the same answer.

"I don't hear ticking. I hear things working."

Then, one November evening, a boy brought her a clock
that did not work at all.

He arrived just before closing, when fog had swallowed the
lamps in the square. He looked about twelve, though his
coat was so large it made him seem smaller. Rain
shone in his black hair.

"Miss Vale?" he asked.

"Yes?"

"I need you to fix this."

He placed a parcel on the counter. The cloth around
it was tied with red thread and smelled faintly of
smoke.

Mara untied the knot.

Inside lay a small silver clock.

It had no hands.

Its face was blank except for a thin black circle
and a keyhole at the center. Around its edge ran
an inscription in a language Mara did not recognize. The
case was cold enough to sting her fingers.

"Where did you get this?" she asked.

"My mother left it."

"Is she a collector?"

The boy looked down. "She's gone."

Mara softened. "I'm sorry."

"She said that if she didn't come back, I should
bring it here."

"To this shop?"

"To you."

Mara turned the clock over. On the back, scratched into
the silver, were two letters: **E.V.**

Her father's initials.

A chill passed through her that had nothing to do
with the metal.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Jonas Reed."

"And your mother?"

"Elianor."

Mara knew the name.

She had not heard it since childhood, but memory returned
at once: a woman with copper hair sitting at the
Vale family table; low voices after midnight; her father locking
the kitchen door before opening an old leather book. Elianor
had been younger then, impatient and bright-eyed. She had called
Mara "little moth" because the girl always hovered near lamps.

"When did you last see her?" Mara asked.

"Three nights ago. She told me to wait at home.
I did. Then I found the note."

He produced a folded scrap of paper. The writing was
hurried.

*Jonas, if the bells ring thirteen, go to Mara Vale.
Trust no clock but the silver one.*

Mara read it twice.

"Did the bells ring thirteen?"

"Last night."

"That's impossible."

"I counted."

Outside, the church bell began to strike eight. Each note
moved through the fog like a stone dropped into dark
water.

They both counted.

Eight.

Mara looked again at the silver clock.

"I can't promise anything," she said. "But I'll examine it."

"I'm staying."

"You should go home."

"My home is empty."

There was no self-pity in his voice, which made the
words worse.

Mara sighed, locked the front door, and turned the sign
to **CLOSED**.

"Very well. You can sit by the stove. Don't touch
anything."

Jonas obeyed. Mara carried the silver clock to her workbench
and lit the green-shaded lamp.

Ordinary clocks were arguments between tension and release. A wound
spring wanted to uncoil; gears controlled its impatience; the escapement
divided force into equal pieces; the pendulum gave those pieces
rhythm. However ornate the case, the principle remained honest and
mechanical.

The silver clock contained none of those things.

When Mara opened its back, she found no gears, no
spring, no balance wheel. Instead, the case held a disk
of dark glass. Beneath the glass, points of light drifted
like stars under ice.

Jonas appeared at her shoulder.

"I told you not to touch anything."

"I'm not touching."

"You're breathing on my neck."

He stepped back.

Mara studied the inscription through a magnifying lens. One symbol
resembled an eye, another an open door. Between them were
tiny numbers.

Not hours, she realized.

Dates.

She recognized one immediately: the day her father died.

Her hand jerked, and the clock slipped. Jonas caught it
before it struck the floor.

At his touch, the dark glass flashed.

The shop vanished.

Mara stood in sunlight.

For one bewildering moment she smelled hot grass and river
mud. A child laughed nearby. She turned and saw herself
at ten years old, running through a meadow with a
wooden sword. Her father chased her, roaring like a dragon.

He was alive.

Not merely an image, not a pale dream. Alive. His
boots crushed daisies. Sweat darkened his collar. His laughter rolled
across the field.

Mara could not breathe.

She stepped toward him.

"Father?"

He did not hear her.

The child Mara turned, grinning, and the sight of her
own young face broke something open in her chest.

Then the meadow folded inward like burning paper.

She returned to the shop with both hands gripping the
workbench. Every clock on the wall had stopped.

Jonas still held the silver clock.

"What happened?" he whispered.

Mara took it from him.

"You opened a memory."

"Whose?"

"Mine."

A second later, the clocks resumed. The cuckoos burst from
their doors all at once. Bells chimed, mechanisms whirred, and
pendulums swung in wild disagreement before settling.

Mara stared at the silver clock.

Her father had once told her that time left a
residue. Most people felt it only in certain places: old
bedrooms, abandoned stations, a chair belonging to someone dead. Clockmakers,
he claimed, learned to sense it more clearly because they
spent their lives measuring what could not be held.

She had thought it one of his stories.

Now she remembered the leather book and Elianor's midnight visits.

"Your mother knew my father," Mara said.

"I know. She said they worked together."

"Doing what?"

"Keeping lost hours."

The phrase stirred another memory. Her father at his bench,
believing Mara asleep.

*An hour can be lost in many ways, Elianor. In
grief. In fear. In the moment before disaster. And anything
lost may be found by the wrong person.*

Mara went to the tallest cabinet at the rear of
the shop. It had remained locked since her father's death.
She wore its key around her neck but had never
opened it. Grief had turned the cabinet into a kind
of tomb, and she had respected its silence.

Now she inserted the key.

The lock resisted, then yielded with a groan.

Inside were no tools or clock parts. There was only
a leather book, a brass key, and a map of
Bellweather.

Red circles marked seven locations: the church, the railway station,
the old hospital, the river bridge, the schoolhouse, the Vale
shop, and a house on Larkspur Lane.

Jonas pointed to the house.

"That's where I live."

Mara opened the book. Her father's handwriting filled the pages.

The first entries described experiments. He and Elianor had discovered
that intense moments left impressions on time, like fingerprints in
wax. Most faded naturally. Others remained trapped, repeating themselves in
dreams, instincts, and déjà vu.

But some memories were too powerful to fade.

Those became lost hours.

The silver clock had been built to locate and contain
them.

Near the end of the book, the writing grew uneven.

*The Bellweather fire produced a fracture larger than any we
have seen. Forty-seven people died at the old mill, yet
the town remembers only forty-six. One hour is missing from
every public record. Elianor believes someone removed it deliberately.*

Mara turned the page.

*His name is Silas Venn. He calls himself a horologist,
but he repairs nothing. He believes a lost hour can
be entered and altered. He intends to use the mill
fracture to prove it.*

The next page had been torn out.

After that came a final entry, dated the morning of
her father's death.

*If I fail, the fracture must remain sealed beneath the
church. The silver clock is the lock. My key is
the path. Elianor knows the cost.*

Mara looked at the brass key in the cabinet.

"What cost?" Jonas asked.

"I don't know."

"You're lying."

"I'm uncertain," she corrected.

"You made the same face my mother makes when she
lies."

Before Mara could reply, someone knocked at the front door.

Three slow taps.

The fog beyond the window had thickened. A man stood
on the pavement in a gray coat and tall hat.
He was thin, almost elegantly so, with silver hair and
a black cane.

Mara covered the silver clock with a cloth.

"We're closed," she called.

The man smiled through the glass.

"I know, Miss Vale."

Jonas seized Mara's sleeve.

"That's him."

"Who?"

"He came to our house the night Mother disappeared."

The stranger tapped again.

This time, every clock in the shop stopped.

The silence was immediate and enormous.

The man raised one gloved hand and pressed it against
the glass. Frost spread beneath his palm, branching across the
window.

"Silas Venn," Mara said.

He bowed.

The lock on the door turned by itself.

Mara grabbed the silver clock, the book, and the brass
key. With her other hand she pushed Jonas toward the
rear door.

They slipped into the alley as the shop bell chimed
behind them.

"Miss Vale," Venn called pleasantly. "You cannot keep time by
running from it."

They ran anyway.

Bellweather had disappeared into fog. The streetlamps were soft yellow
blurs. Mara pulled Jonas through narrow lanes toward the church,
guided by instinct and the map she had glimpsed.

"Why the church?" Jonas asked, breathless.

"The fracture is sealed beneath it."

"And my mother?"

"If Venn needs the clock, she may still be stopping
him."

"You don't know that."

"No."

They crossed the square. The church doors stood open.

Inside, candles burned along the aisle, though no service had
been held that evening. Their flames leaned toward the altar
as if drawn by wind.

Mara and Jonas entered.

The doors slammed shut behind them.

A figure lay beside the front pew.

"Mother!"

Jonas rushed forward.

Elianor Reed was pale but breathing. A bruise darkened her
temple. Her copper hair, now threaded with gray, spread across
the stone floor.

She opened her eyes when Jonas touched her.

"You shouldn't have come," she murmured.

"You told me to."

"I told you to go to Mara."

"She came too."

Elianor gave Mara a faint, exhausted smile. "Little moth."

Mara knelt. "What happened?"

"Venn took the hour but couldn't open it. He needs
the clock and Edwin's key."

"My father died protecting this?"

"Your father died making certain Venn could never possess all
three pieces."

"All three?"

Elianor's gaze moved to Jonas.

Understanding came too late.

The church doors opened. Silas Venn walked down the aisle,
his cane tapping the stones.

"The clock," he said, "the key, and the keeper."

Jonas stood. "I'm not a keeper."

"Not yet."

Venn removed his hat. His face was smooth but strangely
colorless, as if no sunlight had touched it in years.

"Elianor was wise enough to bind the missing hour to
a living bloodline," he continued. "Hers. Only she or her
son can enter it. Edwin kept the key. The clock
finds the door."

Mara held the clock behind her back. "Why do you
want to enter?"

Venn's pleasant expression tightened.

"Because my daughter died in the mill fire."

The answer echoed beneath the vaulted ceiling.

For the first time, Mara saw grief in him. Not
fresh grief, but something preserved beyond its natural life—hard, bright,
and poisonous.

"She was eight," Venn said. "Her name was Clara. The
fire began at nine minutes past four. At twenty-two minutes
past, the roof collapsed. Thirteen minutes. That is all I
require."

"You want to save her," Jonas said.

"I will save her."

Elianor pushed herself upright. "And the other forty-six?"

"Forty-seven," Venn snapped. "The town forgot Clara because I pulled
the hour loose before it settled. I thought I could
change it. Instead, I trapped it."

"If you alter the hour," Elianor said, "everything after it
may alter too."

"Good."

"People who lived may die. Children may never be born."

"People say such things because fate has been kind to
them."

Mara thought of her father in the meadow. For one
instant she had wanted nothing more than to step into
that memory, take his hand, and refuse to leave. The
desire had not been reasonable. It had been absolute.

"You saw him, didn't you?" Venn asked her softly.

Mara said nothing.

"The clock showed you Edwin. It knows what you want."

"I want my father alive."

"Then help me. The mill hour first. After that, we
can recover others. Death is only a clock no one
has learned to repair."

Behind Venn, the church bell began to move.

No rope pulled it.

One strike shook dust from the rafters.

Two.

Elianor struggled to rise. "He's opening the fracture."

Venn lifted his cane. Its black wood split down the
middle, revealing a long metal instrument like the hand of
a clock.

Mara took out the brass key.

His eyes brightened.

Three strikes.

"You mistake grief for love," Mara said.

Venn's face hardened. "And you mistake surrender for wisdom."

Four.

The silver clock trembled in Mara's hand. A narrow beam
of light spilled from its central keyhole and pointed toward
the altar.

Five.

Stone ground against stone. The altar shifted aside, revealing steps
descending into darkness.

Venn moved swiftly. He thrust the metal rod toward Mara,
but Elianor caught his coat. He struck her aside.

Jonas leaped at him.

"Run!" Mara shouted.

Six.

Venn flung the boy against a pew. Mara seized a
brass candlestick and swung it. Venn blocked the blow, but
the impact sent his instrument skittering across the floor.

Seven.

Mara ran toward the hidden steps.

The silver clock pulled her downward, cold and eager in
her palm.

Eight.

She descended beneath the church into a round stone chamber.
At its center stood a door of black iron. It
had no hinges and no handle, only a keyhole surrounded
by words carved in the same language as the clock.

Nine.

Venn followed.

Blood ran from a cut above his eyebrow. His calm
had vanished.

"You know what waits beyond that door," he said. "Everything
you have lost."

Ten.

Mara inserted the brass key into the iron door.

The silver clock began forming hands upon its blank face.
One pointed to four. The other pointed to nine minutes
past.

Eleven.

Jonas and Elianor reached the chamber.

Venn snatched up his fallen instrument and drove it into
the silver clock.

The clock screamed.

There was no other word for the sound. The dark
glass cracked, and light flooded the chamber.

Twelve.

The iron door opened.

Fire roared on the other side.

Smoke poured through the doorway, carrying the shouts of trapped
workers. Mara saw the mill floor, machines, burning rafters, men
and women running beneath a red sky. A little girl
stood beyond the flames, crying for her father.

"Clara!" Venn shouted.

He stepped toward the door.

Then the bell struck thirteen.

The chamber vanished.

Mara found herself inside the burning mill.

Heat pressed against her face. The silver clock lay whole
in her hands. Venn ran toward Clara. Elianor held Jonas
near the door, but the doorway was already fading.

"This is the lost hour!" Elianor shouted. "It's repeating. We
have only thirteen minutes."

Venn reached his daughter and swept her into his arms.

Clara stared at him in terror.

"It's me," he said. "It's Papa."

"My papa's at home," she cried.

Venn froze.

Of course, Mara thought. The Venn before them was decades
older than the father Clara knew.

A beam crashed behind them. Sparks scattered across the floor.

Venn carried the struggling girl toward the doorway.

The world flickered.

For an instant, the mill disappeared. Mara stood in Bellweather
Square beneath a white sky. The buildings shifted like reflections
on broken water. Faces appeared and vanished. The bakery became
a butcher's shop, then an empty lot. The church tower
crumbled, rebuilt itself, and crumbled again.

"Time is changing!" Elianor called.

Venn saw it too, but tightened his hold on Clara.

"I don't care."

Clara stopped struggling. Over his shoulder, she saw something in
the smoke.

"Miss Lyle!" she cried.

A woman lay pinned beneath a fallen loom. Flames crawled
toward her dress.

Clara kicked free of Venn's arms and ran to her.

"No!" Venn followed.

Mara understood then what the records had forgotten. Clara had
not merely died in the fire. She had spent her
final minutes helping others.

The girl pulled at the broken timber. A young worker
joined her. Together they freed Miss Lyle. That woman, perhaps,
had later led three others to safety. Remove Clara, and
those lives would be lost. Their children would vanish, and
their children's children. Time was not a row of separate
clocks. It was one mechanism, every wheel touching another.

Venn grabbed Clara's hand.

The mill flickered again.

Jonas disappeared.

Elianor screamed.

A second later he returned, translucent and gasping.

"Let her go!" Mara shouted.

"I have already let her die once!"

"You didn't let her. It happened."

"There is no difference!"

"There is to her."

Venn looked down.

Clara was not pleading to be saved. She was pointing
toward the far wall, where two smaller children crouched beneath
a table.

"They can't get out," she said.

The roof groaned.

Venn's face collapsed.

All the years of obsession left him at once, and
beneath them was only a father in a burning room.

He knelt before his daughter.

"You must listen to me," he said.

Clara touched the blood on his brow. "Are you hurt,
mister?"

He laughed, though the sound broke halfway through.

"Yes."

"Miss Lyle says we have to be brave when we're
hurt."

Venn closed his eyes.

Then he released her hand.

Clara ran toward the children.

The silver clock's new hands advanced. Four twenty. Four twenty-one.

Mara felt the lost hour pulling away.

"The door!" Elianor shouted.

They ran. Venn remained on his knees, watching Clara disappear
into smoke.

Mara caught his arm.

"Come with us."

He looked toward the collapsing roof.

"I have spent thirty years trying to reach this moment."

"And now you have."

For a heartbeat, he resisted.

Then he rose.

They crossed the doorway as the mill roof fell.

The iron door slammed shut behind them.

Silence filled the underground chamber.

The silver clock cracked down the center.

Its hands stopped at four twenty-two.

Above them, the church bell rang once.

A normal bell. A normal hour.

Jonas embraced his mother. Elianor held him fiercely, one hand
pressed to the back of his wet hair.

Silas Venn sat against the stone wall. He had aged.
His silver hair was now white, his face deeply lined.
Without the lost hour sustaining him, the years had reclaimed
their place.

"Will I remember her?" he asked.

Elianor looked at the broken clock.

"Yes," she said. "Now everyone will."

They emerged from the church near dawn.

The fog had lifted. Across the square, workers were gathering
around a stone monument that Mara had never seen before.
It stood beside the fountain, dark with rain.

Forty-seven names were carved upon it.

The last was **CLARA VENN, AGE EIGHT**.

Below the names were the words:

**THE HOUR PASSES. THE DEED REMAINS.**

Silas touched his daughter's name. Then he walked away without
his cane, slowly, into the waking town.

Months later, Mara rehung the blue sign outside her shop.

The new lettering read:

**VALE & REED
CLOCKS RESTORED, TIME KEPT**

Jonas apprenticed under her after school. He was impatient with
tiny screws, careless with oil, and far too curious about
mechanisms that did not concern him. Mara considered these promising
qualities.

Elianor took the room above the bakery and began writing
a true history of the mill fire. Sometimes she and
Mara stayed up late, reading Edwin Vale's old notebooks and
arguing over what should be preserved and what should be
destroyed.

The silver clock could not be repaired.

Mara placed it in the tall cabinet beside the leather
book. Its face remained frozen at four twenty-two, the minute
when a little girl had chosen to help two children
rather than save herself.

Once a year, on the anniversary of the fire, every
clock in Bellweather stopped for thirteen minutes.

No one knew why.

During those minutes, bakers lowered their hands, trains waited at
the station, teachers closed their books, and families stood before
the monument in the square. The town held the lost
hour together, not to change it and not to forget
it, but to give it a proper place among all
the hours that had followed.

Mara would stand in the doorway of her shop and
listen to the silence.

She no longer believed that everything broken could be repaired.

Some things could only be remembered. Some wounds could only
be carried. Time did not heal them by erasing them,
but by building around them—minute after minute, life after life—until
grief became part of a larger mechanism.

Then the thirteen minutes would end.

The church bell would ring.

The clocks would begin again.

And Mara, surrounded by all their separate voices, would hear
the town working.

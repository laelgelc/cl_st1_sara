## The Cartographer of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, Mara Venn unlocked the brass door
beneath the eastern clock tower and went to work mapping
the previous day.

Her office had no windows. The city had decided long
ago that windows encouraged inaccuracy in civil servants, particularly those
whose duties involved time. Instead, a hundred glass cabinets lined
the walls, each containing clocks that had stopped for reasons
no watchmaker could explain.

One showed thirteen minutes past midnight, though midnight had never
arrived on the date engraved beneath it. Another ran backward
whenever someone lied nearby. The oldest clock had no hands
at all. It ticked only during thunderstorms.

At the center of the room stood Mara's desk. On
it lay a blank map, a silver compass, and the
official ledger of missing hours.

The city of Bellwether lost time in small quantities. Seven
minutes might vanish from a bakery between the kneading of
dough and the lighting of ovens. A schoolroom might misplace
an entire afternoon. Now and then, a citizen would leave
home on Monday and reach the market on Wednesday, convinced
the journey had taken half an hour.

These losses were rarely dangerous. Time, like rainwater, found cracks.

Mara's task was to locate those cracks, measure them, and
draw them on the municipal maps in violet ink. The
city council used her work to determine where new clocks
should be built and which streets required warning signs.

CAUTION: TUESDAYS UNSTABLE.

DO NOT LINGER AFTER THE NINTH BELL.

SENIOR CITIZENS MAY EXPERIENCE YOUTH.

Mara had been the city's cartographer of lost hours for
eleven years. She was precise, patient, and entirely alone.

At eight minutes after six on the first day of
winter, a boy appeared inside the office.

He did not enter through the door. One moment Mara
was drawing the outline of a missing quarter-hour near Weaver's
Square; the next, he was standing beside Cabinet Forty-Three, dripping
rain onto the tiled floor.

He was perhaps twelve years old. His coat was too
large, his boots were tied with string, and in his
arms he carried a black wooden box.

"You're late," he said.

Mara looked at the locked door, then at the boy.

"For what?"

"For finding me."

He set the box on her desk.

Mara reached for the alarm bell beneath the ledger, but
the boy placed one hand over it.

"If you call the guards, I'll disappear again."

"That sounds like their problem."

"It becomes yours in three days."

His voice was calm, but his fingers trembled.

Mara studied him more carefully. There was a bruise on
his cheek, yellow at the edges as if it were
several days old. His wet hair clung to his forehead.
Around his neck hung a copper disk stamped with the
emblem of the city: a bell inside a circle.

"Who are you?"

"My name is Eli."

"Family name?"

"I don't know yet."

Mara had heard many strange answers in that room, but
never that one.

"What's in the box?"

"You should open it."

"I should do no such thing."

"You already did."

Eli lifted the lid.

Inside lay a pocket watch.

Its case was badly scratched, and its chain had been
broken. The watch's single hand spun so quickly that it
was only a silver blur. Engraved inside the lid were
the words:

TO MARA, WHO ALWAYS FINDS THE WAY BACK.

Mara's breath caught.

She knew the handwriting.

Her father had written those words on the morning of
her tenth birthday, forty years ago.

The watch had vanished with him the following winter.

She picked it up. It was warm.

"Where did you get this?"

"You gave it to me."

"I've never seen you before."

"You will."

Mara closed the lid and put the watch down with
deliberate care.

"My father disappeared with this watch. The inspectors searched every
unstable district in the city. They found nothing."

"They were looking in the wrong year."

Something clicked in Cabinet Twelve. A clock began striking, though
it had no bell.

Mara stared at Eli.

There were approved ways to discuss temporal displacement. There were
forms, definitions, and legal distinctions. A person could be delayed,
repeated, reversed, or mislaid. The dead, however, remained dead. The
Department of Hours was emphatic on that point.

"My father died," she said.

"No," Eli replied. "He got lost."

"Where?"

"In the day after tomorrow."

The clock in Cabinet Twelve stopped.

Eli swayed. Mara caught him before his head struck the
desk.

He weighed almost nothing.

She carried him to the narrow bench by the wall
and wrapped him in her spare coat. When she turned
back, the black box was gone.

The pocket watch remained.

By seven, Eli was still asleep. By eight, three municipal
messengers had knocked on the brass door. Mara ignored them.
At half past nine, the chief inspector arrived with a
key of his own.

Orrin Vale filled the doorway like a badly built cupboard.
He wore a gray uniform fastened to the chin and
carried a stack of blue warrants under one arm.

"You failed to submit yesterday's map," he said.

"I'm revising it."

"You've never revised anything."

"There's a first time for all disasters."

Vale stepped inside. His eyes moved to the sleeping boy.

"Who is that?"

"My assistant."

"Your assistant is sixty-three and has a mustache."

"I replaced him."

"With a child?"

"He works cheaply."

Vale approached the bench. Mara stood between them.

The chief inspector frowned. "Where did he come from?"

"North district."

"Which street?"

"I didn't ask."

"You ask everyone."

"Perhaps I'm becoming careless."

"Then perhaps you're becoming unemployed."

Behind him, the clocks in the cabinets began ticking together.

It was a sound Mara had heard only once before,
during the collapse of the old observatory. A hundred mechanisms
found the same rhythm, and the room seemed to tighten
around it.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Vale glanced at the cabinets.

"Is that normal?"

"Perfectly."

Tick.

Tick.

The pocket watch on Mara's desk sprang open.

Its spinning hand stopped.

It pointed not to a number, but toward Vale.

The inspector's face changed.

He moved quickly for a large man. One hand seized
the watch while the other struck Mara across the mouth.
She fell against the desk, scattering pens and rulers.

Vale turned toward the door.

Eli sat up.

"Don't let him take it," he said.

Mara grabbed the silver compass and hurled it.

It struck Vale's wrist. He cursed and dropped the watch.
The moment it touched the floor, the office vanished.

Mara landed on wet cobblestones beneath a violet sky.

Rain hung motionless in the air. Each drop was a
clear bead, suspended between cloud and street. A horse stood
frozen beside an overturned carriage. Flames leaned from the windows
of a nearby house but gave no heat. Above the
rooftops, the eastern clock tower bent at an impossible angle,
its bells scattered upward like birds.

Eli lay beside her.

Vale was nowhere in sight.

Mara touched the blood on her lip.

"What happened?"

"The watch opened a door," Eli said.

"To where?"

He pointed down the street.

A newspaper floated above the pavement, caught in a wind
that had stopped. Mara plucked it from the air.

The date was two days ahead.

The headline read:

CATASTROPHIC TIME FAILURE
EASTERN DISTRICT LOST
THOUSANDS MISSING

Below it was a photograph of the clock tower collapsing
into a black circle in the sky.

"This is the day after tomorrow," Mara whispered.

"Part of it."

"Where are the people?"

"Between seconds."

"Why aren't we?"

"The watch keeps us moving."

Mara looked at the pocket watch in her hand. Its
silver hand now turned slowly, one revolution every few breaths.

"Where is Vale?"

"He has his own way through."

"You know him?"

Eli nodded. "He caused this."

They followed the frozen street toward the clock tower. As
they walked, Mara saw the city caught at the instant
of disaster. A woman knelt beside a fallen basket, reaching
for apples suspended above the gutter. Two children hovered in
mid-jump over a rope. A flock of pigeons filled the
square like gray stars.

At the entrance to the tower, a crack divided the
air.

It was thin as thread and blacker than darkness. Through
it, Mara glimpsed other versions of the square: one buried
in snow, one flooded to the rooftops, one crowded with
people wearing fashions from a century before.

She had mapped thousands of temporal fractures, but always from
the safe side. This one did not feel like a
gap in time. It felt like a wound.

"The city can't survive a break this large," she said.

"It doesn't."

"How do we stop it?"

"We find your father."

The tower doors were gone. Beyond them, the staircase spiraled
upward through the hollow center of the building.

Mara began to climb.

As a child, she had spent every Sunday inside that
tower. Her father, Jon Venn, had been keeper of the
eastern clock, and he had allowed her to polish the
gears while he adjusted the great brass pendulum.

He believed clocks were not machines for measuring time. They
were promises.

"Every tick says the next moment is coming," he once
told her. "Every tock says it arrived."

Then, one winter morning, he had gone to repair a
fault in the tower and never returned.

Mara had waited beneath the clock until nightfall. Afterward, she
had spent years learning the shape of time's cracks, convinced
that knowledge could prevent anyone else from being lost.

Now she climbed with her father's watch beating in her
palm.

At the seventh landing, they found a door that had
never been there before.

Eli opened it.

On the other side was a summer afternoon from forty
years ago.

Sunlight entered through the tower windows. The gears turned. Ten-year-old
Mara sat cross-legged on the floor, eating sugared plums from
a paper bag.

Her father stood at the workbench, engraving a pocket watch.

Mara stopped in the doorway.

She remembered this day: the smell of oil, the sticky
sweetness on her fingers, the golden dust in the air.
She remembered her father's rolled sleeves and the tune he
whistled when he was concentrating.

Young Mara looked up.

"Papa," she said, "there's a lady at the door."

Jon Venn turned.

For one reckless moment, Mara wanted to run to him.
She wanted to say everything at once: that she had
waited, that she had searched, that she had spent forty
years angry with him for being dead.

But Eli pulled her backward.

"This isn't him," the boy said. "It's only an hour
he left behind."

The summer room flickered.

Jon smiled politely at them, unaware that he was a
memory.

"Can I help you?" he asked.

Mara could not answer.

The scene repeated.

Young Mara ate a sugared plum. Jon engraved the watch.
Dust turned in the sunlight.

"Papa, there's a lady at the door."

They closed it.

On the ninth landing, they found Vale.

He stood before the central mechanism, where the tower's great
pendulum should have been. In its place spun a sphere
of black glass. Threads of violet light stretched from the
sphere into the walls, trembling with each distant tick.

Vale held the other end of the pocket watch's broken
chain.

"I wondered when you would catch up," he said.

Mara stepped in front of Eli.

"What is that thing?"

"A reservoir."

"For stolen time?"

"Recovered time," Vale corrected. "Do you know how much the
city wastes? Hours spent sleeping. Years lost to illness. Whole
lifetimes squandered by people who have no idea what to
do with them."

"You caused the fractures."

"I collected what leaked through them."

"For what purpose?"

Vale placed his hand against the black sphere.

The glass cleared.

Inside it, a young woman lay in a white bed.
Her dark hair spread across the pillow, and a bouquet
of dried flowers rested beside her. She was perfectly still.

"My daughter, Lysa," Vale said. "She died seventeen years ago."

Mara looked at him. "You can't bring back the dead."

"No. But I can return to a time when she
was alive."

"You're destroying the city."

"A city is stone. Stone can be rebuilt."

"There are people frozen in the streets."

"Then they'll never suffer. That's more mercy than time showed
my child."

His fingers tightened around the chain.

The pocket watch tore from Mara's hand and flew toward
him.

Eli lunged for it.

Vale caught the boy by the coat and dragged him
close.

For the first time, the inspector looked afraid.

"You," he whispered.

Eli struggled, but Vale held him fast.

Mara took one step forward.

"Let him go."

"Do you know what he is?" Vale asked. "He isn't
a child. He's a consequence."

The black sphere brightened. Within it, the image of Vale's
daughter faded, replaced by a web of streets and clocks.
Violet lines converged on Eli's heart.

"When the eastern district collapses," Vale said, "all its possible
futures collapse with it. Every life that might have been
lived becomes energy. The boy is one of those lives.
He slipped out before the failure was complete."

Mara stared at Eli.

"What does that mean?"

The boy's eyes filled with tears, though his voice remained
steady.

"My name is Eli Venn."

The tower groaned.

"Venn?" Mara said.

"You're my grandmother."

For a moment, the fractured tower, the ruined city, and
Vale's machine all became very far away.

"I have no children."

"Not yet."

"Then your mother—"

"Isn't born yet. She won't be if the city ends
today."

Mara understood his first answer then.

He did not know his full history because it had
not happened. He was a branch growing from a tree
not yet planted.

Vale pulled him toward the sphere.

"That's why he can repair it," he said. "An unlived
lifetime is enough to stabilize the reservoir."

"You mean kill him."

"He was never born."

"He's here."

"That is not the same."

"It is to him."

Mara moved.

She seized the loose chain and wrapped it around her
wrist. Vale jerked backward. The pocket watch swung between them,
opening and closing as if gasping for breath.

The sphere pulsed.

For an instant Mara saw dozens of rooms inside it:
hospital rooms, nurseries, kitchens, graves. She saw Vale holding his
dying daughter's hand. She saw Eli as a baby in
someone's arms. She saw herself older, laughing at a table
surrounded by people whose names she did not know.

Then the chain snapped.

Mara fell against the mechanism.

The world rang.

Not the tower bell. Not any sound made by metal
or air. It was the noise of a thousand lost
hours returning at once.

The sphere cracked.

Vale screamed and reached for it.

"Lysa!"

A hand appeared inside the glass.

The young woman was awake now. She sat upright in
the white bed and looked through the sphere at her
father.

Vale pressed both palms to the surface.

"I'm coming," he said.

Lysa shook her head.

Even through the glass, Mara saw the sadness in her
face.

She formed one word with her lips.

Live.

Then the sphere shattered.

Time poured out.

The staircase buckled. Rain crashed against the windows. Bells fell
through the tower, striking stone and wood. Somewhere below, the
city resumed its unfinished second: horses cried out, people shouted,
glass broke, and thunder rolled across the roofs.

A wave of violet light lifted Eli from the floor.

Mara caught his hand.

He was already becoming transparent.

"Hold on!"

"I can't."

"Yes, you can."

"You have to find him," Eli said.

"Who?"

"Your father. He's in the last room."

Another wave passed through him. Mara could see the gears
through his chest.

"Will I see you again?" she asked.

Eli smiled.

"You already did."

His hand vanished from hers.

The tower lurched. Vale lay beside the ruined sphere, alive
but unmoving.

Mara wanted to stay. She wanted to search the air
for any trace of Eli. But the floor split beneath
her, and beyond it she saw a final staircase leading
upward.

She climbed.

At the top of the tower was a small circular
room.

Her father sat beneath the bell.

He was older than she remembered. His hair was white,
his face thin, his workman's coat patched at both elbows.
Around him lay thousands of maps drawn in violet ink.

Jon Venn looked up and smiled.

"You took your time," he said.

Mara stood in the doorway, unable to breathe.

He rose slowly.

"I tried to get back," he continued. "But the fracture
carried me forward. I arrived here two days after I
left, except the two days were forty years apart."

"You were alive."

"Yes."

"All this time?"

"Not from where I was sitting."

She crossed the room and struck him in the chest.

He accepted the blow.

She struck him again, then clutched his coat and pressed
her face against his shoulder.

"I waited," she said.

"I know."

"You left me."

"I know."

"I thought you were dead."

"I'm sorry."

He held her while the tower shook.

After a while, Mara stepped back.

"Eli said you could stop this."

"Not me." Jon pointed to the maps. "You."

The drawings covered every year of the city's history. They
showed fractures Mara had mapped herself, as well as thousands
she had never seen. Together they formed a single vast
pattern.

A clock face.

At its center was the eastern tower.

"The missing hours were never random," Jon said. "They're pieces
of one mechanism. Vale tried to wind it backward. You
must let it move forward."

"How?"

"Complete the map."

He handed her a violet pen.

One section remained blank: a narrow curve through the city's
eastern district. Mara recognized it as the path she had
walked with Eli through the frozen rain.

She drew the line.

Below them, every clock in Bellwether struck at once.

The sound traveled through streets and houses, through kitchens, schools,
hospitals, and graves. Lost minutes slipped beneath doors. Mislaid afternoons
fell from rooftops. Forgotten birthdays returned in flashes of candlelight.
Citizens woke with sudden memories of conversations they had never
finished.

The great bell above Mara swung.

The tower became transparent.

For one heartbeat she saw the whole city not as
stone, but as a living map of moments: every beginning,
every ending, every choice opening into another.

Among them stood Eli.

He raised one hand.

Then the bell struck, and the vision disappeared.

Mara woke on the floor of her office.

Morning light did not enter, because the room still had
no windows. Yet she knew it was morning from the
ordinary, comforting disorder of the clocks. Some ticked. Some did
not. One chimed whenever dust settled on its case.

The pocket watch lay beside her.

Its hand moved normally.

On the bench sat her father, asleep beneath her spare
coat.

Chief Inspector Vale was found that afternoon in the eastern
tower. He confessed to seventeen years of unauthorized experiments and
was sentenced to spend the remainder of his career repairing
every public clock he had damaged. He accepted the judgment
without protest.

Once a year, on the anniversary of his daughter's death,
he stopped all the clocks in his workshop for one
hour.

Then he started them again.

The city council denied that any catastrophe had occurred. Governments
preferred disasters that left rubble. Nevertheless, new signs appeared throughout
the eastern district:

CAUTION: FUTURE UNDER CONSTRUCTION.

Mara continued mapping lost hours, though she no longer worked
alone. Jon took the desk beside hers and complained daily
about modern pens. Together they redrew the municipal charts, adding
not only the places where time had broken, but also
the places where it had healed.

Years passed.

Mara married late, surprising everyone, especially herself. She had a
daughter who grew up fascinated by bells and entirely uninterested
in maps. The girl traveled beyond the city, returned with
stories, and eventually brought home a quiet musician with wet
boots and kind eyes.

On the first day of winter, many years after the
tower nearly fell, Mara was summoned from her office by
a note written in her daughter's hurried hand.

She arrived at the hospital shortly after dawn.

Her daughter lay exhausted but smiling beneath white sheets. In
her arms was a small boy with dark hair.

"Would you like to hold him?" she asked.

Mara took the child carefully.

He opened his eyes.

Around his neck, her daughter had tied a copper disk
stamped with the emblem of the city: a bell inside
a circle.

"We haven't chosen a family name," the musician said, "but
we thought Eli for his first."

The baby wrapped his hand around Mara's finger.

From far across Bellwether came the sound of the eastern
clock striking six. Every promise in the city answered: tick
and tock, loss and return, the next moment coming and
the next moment arrived.

Mara held her grandson close.

"You're late," Eli said.

Or perhaps it was only the clock.

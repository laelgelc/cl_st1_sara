On the morning the sea returned the church bell, Mara
Vale was repairing a clock that had not worked for
forty-three years.

The clock belonged to Mr. Ossory, who claimed it had
stopped at the exact moment his wife decided not to
marry him.

"Then how did she become your wife?" Mara asked.

"She changed her mind at half past four."

The hands showed twenty minutes past three.

Mara examined the mechanism through her magnifying lens. "This stopped
because someone poured honey into it."

Mr. Ossory looked offended. "Nella hated honey."

"Perhaps the clock liked it."

Outside, gulls cried over the steep roofs of Bellweather, a
town built where the cliffs leaned close to the sea.
The streets smelled of salt, coal smoke, and bread. Every
house had blue shutters, though no one remembered who had
made the rule. At the bottom of the hill, waves
pushed white foam between black rocks.

Then came a sound so deep that the windowpanes trembled.

Bong.

Mr. Ossory stood.

Mara removed her lens.

Bong.

Dust drifted from the ceiling. In the shop below, every
clock began to chime, though the hour was ten minutes
past nine.

Bong.

People ran into the street.

There had once been a church in Bellweather, long before
Mara was born. It had stood on a low island
in the bay, connected to the town by a narrow
road exposed at low tide. One winter, during a storm,
the island collapsed. The church slid into the sea with
its windows shining and its bell ringing. All night, people
listened from the cliffs as the sound faded beneath the
waves.

That had been seventy-six years ago.

But the bell now tolling over Bellweather had the same
heavy voice.

Mara and Mr. Ossory hurried outside. The entire town was
moving downhill: bakers with flour on their arms, fishermen in
oilskins, children who should have been in school, and old
women clutching cups of tea. At the harbor wall they
stopped.

Beyond the boats, something dark swung between the waves.

The bell came rolling toward shore, tangled in red ropes
of seaweed. It was green with age, broad enough for
a child to hide inside, and marked with silver letters
no one could read from the wall. Each wave lifted
it and let it fall.

Bong.

"That's Saint Orison's bell," whispered Mr. Ossory.

No one disagreed.

By noon, the fishermen had pulled it onto the beach.
People gathered around while Mayor Finch ordered them not to
touch anything. This instruction caused at least twelve people to
touch it immediately.

Mara remained at the edge of the crowd. She disliked
mysteries when they were larger than clocks. A clock could
be opened. It could be cleaned, questioned, and made to
confess. The sea was different. It covered its machinery.

"Mara!"

Her younger brother, Tavin, wriggled through the crowd. At seventeen,
he was long-limbed and perpetually untidy, with dark curls and
a coat missing two buttons.

"You heard it?" he asked.

"So did three counties, probably."

"They say it rang underwater every year on the night
the church sank."

"They say the moon makes people steal spoons."

"The moon does make people steal spoons. Mrs. Fen stole
six last winter."

"Mrs. Fen steals spoons in every kind of weather."

Mayor Finch climbed onto an overturned boat and raised both
hands.

"My friends," he announced, "this is an important historic object.
Until we determine what should be done, it will be
moved to the town hall."

The bell rang.

It did not sway. No one struck it. Yet the
iron tongue moved once, and the sound rolled across the
beach.

Bong.

Everyone fell silent.

A crack appeared in the bell's green surface.

From it spilled seawater, then three silver fish, a handful
of white shells, and a small wooden box.

The box landed at Mara's feet.

No one moved.

Tavin bent down.

"Don't," Mara said.

He picked it up.

The box was plain, blackened by water, and locked with
a brass keyhole. Tavin wiped slime from the lid. Carved
into the wood was a symbol: a circle crossed by
two hands, one pointing forward and the other back.

Mara knew that symbol.

It appeared on every clock made by her grandfather.

She took the box from Tavin.

Mayor Finch pushed through the crowd. "That belongs to the
town."

"It has my family mark."

"It came out of the town's bell."

"It came out of the sea."

"Which falls under the authority of the harbor office."

"Then charge the sea with smuggling."

The mayor's mustache stiffened. Mara turned and walked away before
he could stop her. Tavin followed, grinning.

Their clock shop stood halfway up Lantern Street. A sign
above the door read VALE & DAUGHTER, although Mara's father
had been dead for nine years and no one had
thought to repaint it.

Inside, clocks covered the walls. Some ticked quickly, some slowly.
Their combined voices sounded like rain on leaves.

Mara set the wooden box on her workbench. She selected
a thin steel pick, leaned close to the keyhole, and
listened.

"You can open it?" Tavin asked.

"All locks are clocks with fewer ambitions."

"That means yes?"

"It means be quiet."

The lock was old but intricate. Its inner pins resisted
in a peculiar rhythm: two forward, one back; pause; three
forward. Mara recognized the pattern. Her grandfather had taught it
to her as a child by tapping on the kitchen
table.

Never force a stubborn thing, he used to say. Find
out what it is afraid of.

At last, the lock clicked.

Inside lay a brass key and a roll of oilskin.

Mara unfolded the oilskin. It contained a chart of the
bay, drawn in fading ink. The drowned island was marked
at its center. Beneath it, in her grandfather's handwriting, were
the words:

WHEN THE BELL RETURNS, THE DOOR WILL OPEN.

Tavin leaned over her shoulder.

"What door?"

"There's more."

On the other side was a letter.

My dear Elian,

If this reaches you, then I failed to return before
the bell. Do not let them wind the engine. Bellweather
was not built to keep time. It was built to
keep something outside it.

The key opens the stair at Saint Orison's. You must
go below before sunset and remove the final hour.

Forgive me.

A.V.

Tavin read it twice. "A.V.?"

"Arden Vale. Grandfather's father."

"Our great-grandfather?"

Mara had seen his name in family ledgers, but little
else was known about him. He had disappeared seventy-six years
earlier, the same night the church sank.

The clocks on the walls stopped.

Not one after another. All at once.

The silence pressed against Mara's ears.

Then the hands began to turn backward.

Tavin stared at them. "That seems bad."

From outside came shouting.

They rushed to the window. People stood in Lantern Street
looking uphill. At the top of the town, smoke was
folding into chimneys. A dropped apple leaped from the gutter
into a grocer's hand. A horse walked backward, pulling its
cart uphill. Mrs. Fen emerged from her house and returned
a spoon through her neighbor's window.

Time was reversing.

Mara watched a flock of gulls fly tail-first across the
sky.

"How long until sunset?" Tavin asked.

She looked west. The sun hung above the water, but
it had begun to climb.

"I don't think sunset is our present difficulty."

A pounding shook the shop door. Mayor Finch entered without
waiting.

"What have you done?" he demanded.

"Opened a box."

"Well, close it."

"The box isn't doing this."

"You can't know that."

"I can, because boxes are rarely responsible for the motion
of the sun."

The mayor glanced at the backward clocks. His anger drained
into fear. "Can you stop it?"

Mara folded the chart and put it in her coat.

"I can try."

They returned to the harbor. The tide was retreating at
impossible speed, pulling away from the beach in shining sheets.
Fish flapped on newly exposed sand. Anchored boats settled onto
their sides.

Far out in the bay, the drowned road emerged.

It ran from Bellweather toward a hill of black stone
rising from the seabed. Broken columns and arches appeared, streaming
with water. At the summit stood the ruins of Saint
Orison's Church.

The bell on the beach rang again.

This time, the sound swept outward, flattening the water. In
the town behind them, a chimney rebuilt itself brick by
brick.

Mara stepped onto the road.

Tavin followed.

"No," she said.

"Yes."

"It may be dangerous."

"That is why I'm coming."

"That is not a reason."

"It's the best reason."

Mara knew argument would only waste time, assuming they still
possessed any. Together they walked toward the island.

The road was slick with weed. On either side, the
seabed stretched beneath the gray daylight: rippled sand, rusted anchors,
broken bottles, and the pale ribs of ships. Crabs scuttled
backward into holes. Above, the sun rose toward noon.

Halfway across, they passed a row of stone figures. These
had once been saints lining the road, but the sea
had softened their faces. One still held a stone lantern.
Another seemed to be covering its eyes.

"Why would Great-Grandfather put a letter inside the bell?" Tavin
asked.

"Perhaps there was no time to deliver it."

"But the letter says 'before the bell.' He knew it
would return."

Mara studied the island ahead. "Maybe because he had seen
it happen before."

At the church, the roof was gone. Seaweed hung from
the walls like torn banners. Small fish flickered in pools
between the floor tiles. The empty bell tower pointed into
the sky.

They found the stair beneath the altar.

Its stone door bore the symbol of the crossed clock
hands. In its center was a keyhole.

Mara inserted the brass key.

The door opened inward, releasing a breath of cold, dry
air.

Steps descended into darkness.

Tavin took a candle from his pocket.

"Why do you have that?"

"I'm prepared."

"You don't have matches."

His face fell. Mara removed a matchbox from her coat,
lit the candle, and handed it back to him.

The staircase twisted deep beneath the island. Unlike the church,
it showed no sign of having been underwater. The stone
walls were dry. Brass pipes ran along the ceiling, vibrating
faintly.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

The sound grew louder as they descended.

At the bottom, they entered a circular chamber vast enough
to contain the town hall. In the center stood an
engine made of wheels.

Thousands of gears turned within gears. Pendulums swung from the
darkness overhead. Chains passed through holes in the floor. Brass
arms traced circles on glass plates. Some parts were delicate
as watch springs; others were larger than houses.

Around the chamber's outer wall stood twelve iron doors. Above
each was carved an hour, from one to twelve.

All were shut except the last.

The door beneath XII stood open.

Beyond it was not another room but a night sky
crowded with unfamiliar stars.

Wind poured through, carrying the smell of snow.

A man stood before the doorway, straining to pull it
closed. He wore an old-fashioned black coat. His white hair
floated in the wind, and his hands were wrapped in
bloodstained cloth.

When he saw Mara, his expression broke.

"Elian?"

Mara stepped closer. "No. Elian was my grandfather."

The man closed his eyes. "Then I have been here
longer than I feared."

"You're Arden Vale."

"Yes."

Tavin nearly dropped the candle. "You're our great-grandfather."

Arden looked at him, then back at Mara. "How long?"

"Seventy-six years."

"To you, perhaps."

A grinding noise shook the chamber. The open door widened
by another inch. Behind Arden, something enormous moved among the
stars.

He braced himself against it. "Help me."

Mara and Tavin seized the iron handle. Together they pulled.
The door resisted like a sail in a storm. Mara's
boots slid across the floor.

"What is this place?" she shouted.

"The last minute of the last hour," Arden said. "The
moment Bellweather was made to imprison."

"That explains nothing."

"It is difficult to be precise while holding back eternity."

They pulled until the door narrowed, but it would not
shut. A wedge of pale metal had been driven between
the door and its frame.

"The final hour," Mara said.

Arden nodded. "Remove it."

Tavin kicked at the wedge. It did not move.

Mara examined the nearby mechanism. The door was connected to
the great engine by a line of interlocking gears. Most
turned forward. One small wheel, black with corrosion, spun backward.

"Why is time reversing in town?" she asked.

"The bell was the anchor. When the church sank, it
held the island between moments. But the sea has returned
it. Now the engine is unwinding."

"Who built this?"

"No one knows. The first houses in Bellweather were raised
around its upper vents. Every generation added clocks and bells
without understanding why. We thought we were measuring time." Arden
laughed bitterly. "We were feeding it."

Another impact sounded beyond the doorway.

For an instant, Mara saw a shape against the stars:
a vast, many-jointed silhouette walking slowly toward them.

"What happens if it comes through?" Tavin whispered.

"Every hour will become the last hour," Arden replied.

Mara released the handle.

The door lurched open.

"What are you doing?" Arden cried.

"I need my hands."

She hurried to the blackened gear. Its teeth caught and
slipped against the next wheel, each failure sending a tremor
through the engine.

"It's worn," she said. "The gear can't hold its direction."

"Can you repair it?"

"In my shop, with proper tools and half a day."

"You have neither."

Mara searched her pockets: matchbox, screwdriver, pliers, two clock keys,
a piece of chalk, and Mr. Ossory's honey-clogged watch, which
she had carried from the workbench without noticing.

She opened the watch. Its tiny gears gleamed beneath amber
residue.

"Great-grandfather," she said, "if the engine were working, could the
door close?"

"Yes."

"Then keep pulling."

She removed the watch's main wheel. It was far too
small to replace the damaged gear, but its teeth had
the same fine spacing as the gear's inner ring. Mara
placed it against the mechanism.

It almost fit.

Almost was useless.

The chamber shook. Tavin cried out as the door dragged
him forward. Arden seized his coat and hauled him back.

The shape beyond the threshold was closer now. Starlight outlined
an arm longer than a ship's mast. At its end
turned a wheel of white fire.

Mara thought of her grandfather tapping a rhythm on the
kitchen table.

Never force a stubborn thing. Find out what it is
afraid of.

She watched the backward gear.

It was not merely slipping. It was avoiding the wheel
beside it, shuddering each time the teeth met.

Because the neighboring wheel was moving too quickly.

"It isn't broken," she said. "It's protecting the engine."

"What?" Tavin shouted.

"The reversal is a safety mechanism."

She traced the gear train. It led upward to a
glass dial marked with the phases of the tide. The
returning bell had released the island, but the engine was
reversing because the twelfth door had opened. It was trying
to retreat from the dangerous hour.

To close the door, she did not need to make
the gear turn forward.

She needed to let it finish going back.

Mara grabbed a brass lever beside the dial and pulled.

Nothing happened.

A small keyhole waited at its base.

She tried the brass key from the box. It fit,
but would not turn.

"Two forward, one back," she murmured.

She twisted the key in the rhythm of her family
lock: forward twice, back once, pause, forward three times.

The engine stopped.

For one breath, every pendulum hung motionless.

Then the entire mechanism began to run backward.

The open doorway darkened. The stars beyond it streaked in
reverse. The approaching creature stepped away, each movement undoing the
last. The wedge beneath the door lifted from the floor
and flew into Arden's hand.

"Now!" Mara shouted.

They pulled.

The twelfth door slammed shut.

The impact extinguished Tavin's candle.

In the darkness, the engine ticked.

Once.

Twice.

Then it began to move forward.

Lamps flared around the chamber. The great wheels turned smoothly.
Above the twelfth door, the carved numerals faded until only
blank stone remained.

Arden sank to the floor.

Mara knelt beside him. His face seemed thinner now, almost
transparent.

"You can come with us," Tavin said.

Arden smiled. "No. This hour has held me too long."

"You held the door for seventy-six years," Mara said.

"For me, it was perhaps a day. Perhaps a century."
He looked toward the ceiling as the engine gathered speed.
"Elian became a grandfather?"

"He taught me clocks."

"Was he happy?"

Mara thought of her grandfather in the sunlit kitchen, gears
spread across the table, laughing whenever she handed him the
wrong tool on purpose.

"Yes," she said. "Not always. But enough."

Arden nodded.

His outline blurred. For a moment he looked young, a
dark-haired man in a black coat. Then he scattered like
dust in a beam of light.

Where he had been lay a silver pocket watch.

Mara picked it up. The hands showed twenty minutes past
three.

The journey back was brighter.

By the time Mara and Tavin climbed the church stairs,
the tide was rising. The sun moved west again. They
hurried across the drowned road as waves folded over it
behind them.

At the harbor, half the town waited.

Mayor Finch stood knee-deep in water, shouting contradictory orders. When
Mara and Tavin reached the beach, a cheer rose from
the crowd.

The church sank slowly into the bay. First the road
vanished, then the broken walls, then the tower. At last
only the waves remained.

The green bell sat on the sand.

A final crack opened across its surface. The metal split
silently into two halves.

Inside was nothing but seawater.

The clocks in Bellweather had lost three hours. The bakers
found dough returned to flour, and several children were forced
to attend the same morning lesson twice. Mrs. Fen kept
the spoon that time had placed in her hand, insisting
the universe wanted her to have it.

Mayor Finch declared the incident a successful emergency exercise.

No one believed him.

That evening, Mara returned to her shop. She set Arden's
silver watch on the workbench beside Mr. Ossory's clock. Tavin
made tea while she opened the watch.

Its mechanism was unlike any she had seen. Tiny stars
turned beneath the glass. At its center, two hands crossed:
one forward, one back.

Engraved inside the case were four words:

NOT ALL HOURS RETURN.

Mara cleaned Mr. Ossory's clock, replaced the honey-stiffened spring, and
set the hands moving. At half past four, he arrived
to collect it.

"Did you fix the moment?" he asked.

"No," Mara said. "Only the clock."

He seemed satisfied.

After he left, she climbed a ladder and took down
the old sign above the door. Tavin watched from the
street.

"What are you changing it to?" he asked.

The next morning, Bellweather woke to the usual cries of
gulls and the ordinary forward pull of the tide. People
climbing Lantern Street stopped to read the freshly painted sign.

VALE & BROTHER, it said.

Beneath that, in smaller letters:

CLOCKS REPAIRED. LOST HOURS FOUND.

From somewhere far below the town came one faint, measured
sound.

Tick.

Mara heard it through the floorboards. She looked at Tavin.
He had heard it too.

Together they listened as the hidden engine marked another second,
then another, carrying Bellweather safely away from the final hour.

### The Keeper of Small Hours

Every night at exactly 2:17, the clock in Bellweather Station
stopped.

It did not lose a minute gradually, nor did its
hands tremble before surrendering. At 2:16, the brass second hand
circled the painted face with perfect confidence. Then, as it
reached the twelfth mark, everything froze.

At 2:17, the clock stopped.

At 2:18, it began again.

Most people never noticed. The last passenger train left at
midnight, and the first arrived shortly after five. The station
cleaners came at three, but they relied on their wristwatches.
The ticket office had its own little electric clock, which
ran three minutes fast because the clerk, Mrs. Vale, believed
punctuality should be encouraged by deception.

Only Elias Wren knew about the missing minute.

Elias was the night keeper of Bellweather Station, though no
such position officially existed. His badge identified him as a
maintenance supervisor, but the title seemed too grand for what
he actually did: polished the benches, checked the locks, chased
pigeons from the rafters, and made certain the ancient heating
pipes did not burst in winter.

He had worked at the station for thirty-eight years. In
all that time, the building had changed around him. The
café became a pharmacy, then a café again. Wooden signs
were replaced by screens. The old luggage room was sealed,
reopened as a bookstore, and sealed once more after a
flood. Porters vanished. Steam vanished. The grand hotel across the
road became offices, then expensive apartments in which no lights
ever seemed to turn on.

But the clock remained.

It hung above Platform One beneath a curved iron roof
blackened by a century of smoke. Its face was broad
and pale, its Roman numerals faded at the edges. No
one remembered who had made it. Local historians claimed it
had been installed when the station opened in 1889, although
one photograph from that year showed an empty patch of
brick where the clock ought to have been.

Elias first observed the missing minute during his second week
on the night shift.

He had been standing beneath the clock with a mop
in one hand and a cigarette in the other—this was
back when smoking indoors was tolerated—when the second hand stopped.
At the same moment, all sound drained from the station.

The hum of the lights disappeared. Rain ceased tapping the
glass roof. A pigeon hung motionless in the air above
the tracks, wings spread as though pinned to the darkness.

Elias dropped his cigarette.

Or rather, he tried to.

It remained between his fingers.

He found that he could move, but everything else had
become still. For one impossible minute, the world waited while
he wandered through it alone.

Then the clock resumed.

The cigarette fell. The pigeon flapped onward. Rain struck the
roof with such sudden force that Elias thought the sky
had cracked open.

He told no one.

For years, he spent the missing minute beneath the clock,
experimenting. He placed a coin on the floor and moved
it while time stood still. When the minute ended, the
coin remained where he had put it. He carried a
sleeping pigeon from one bench to another; the bird awoke
in its new location and flew away in outrage. He
once walked outside, but the station doors would not open,
though they had been unlocked. The frozen minute appeared to
belong to the station alone.

Living things that happened to be inside were caught like
insects in amber.

All except Elias.

He searched the clock for secret mechanisms. He climbed a
ladder and opened the iron case, finding only gears, dust,
and the tiny bones of a mouse. He inspected the
wiring after the clock was converted to electricity, but there
was nothing unusual. When the mechanism was replaced, the new
one also stopped at 2:17.

Once, a repairman removed the clock entirely for cleaning. At
2:17 the following morning, time stopped anyway. The bare square
of brick above Platform One seemed to watch Elias until
the missing minute passed.

He began to think the station itself was responsible.

Bellweather had always seemed larger at night. Corridors stretched farther.
Shadows formed doors where no doors existed. The tiled walls,
cream-colored by day, turned gray-blue under the lamps and appeared
to continue beyond the limits of the building. There were
times when Elias heard footsteps on closed platforms or saw
the reflection of someone hurrying behind him, carrying a suitcase
of an old-fashioned shape.

He never felt threatened. A station was made from arrivals
and departures, and perhaps some of those journeys left echoes.

As he grew older, the missing minute became the most
reliable part of his life.

His wife, Miriam, died. His son moved to Australia and
called every other Sunday until other Sundays became every third
Sunday and then once a month. Elias's hair whitened. His
knees began to ache when he climbed stairs. Through all
of it, the clock stopped each night.

At 2:17, he was no longer an old widower in
a fading station. He was the only moving man in
the world.

He used the minute to think.

Sometimes he read half a page of a book. Sometimes
he stood beside the frozen rain and studied each suspended
drop. Sometimes he spoke to Miriam, not because he believed
she could hear him, but because silence made a patient
listener.

Then, on a Thursday in November, a girl stepped out
of the stopped clock.

Elias had just finished checking Platform Four. A heavy fog
had rolled in from the river, filling the tracks with
white emptiness. He returned to the main concourse as the
clock approached 2:17.

The second hand reached twelve.

The station fell silent.

Elias took a breath.

A small door opened in the wall beneath the clock.

He had never seen it before. It was narrow, painted
green, and fitted with a polished brass handle. Beyond it
stood a dim corridor lit by yellow bulbs.

A girl of about twelve emerged, dragging a red suitcase.

She wore a navy coat fastened to the chin and
a wool hat pulled over her ears. Her dark hair
was braided down her back. She looked at Elias, looked
at the clock, and frowned.

"You're not Mr. Thorne," she said.

"No," Elias replied.

"Where is Mr. Thorne?"

"I don't know anyone by that name."

The girl sighed with the exhausted patience of someone surrounded
by incompetent adults.

"What year is it?"

Elias told her.

Her expression changed.

"No," she whispered.

Behind her, somewhere in the corridor, a bell rang.

The girl seized her suitcase and hurried back toward the
green door. Before she reached it, the clock resumed.

Sound crashed into the station. The door vanished.

The girl remained.

She stood facing a blank tiled wall, one hand stretched
toward it. Her suitcase rested beside her.

For a long moment, neither of them spoke.

Then she turned.

"I missed it," she said.

Elias glanced up at the moving clock. "It appears so."

The girl kicked the wall. "Stupid station."

Her name was Clara Venn. She had left Bellweather Station
on the night of February 3, 1941, during the war.
Her mother had sent her away from the city after
a factory near their home was bombed. She had carried
one suitcase, a gas mask, two sandwiches, and a label
tied around her neck.

"The train was full," Clara explained. "I got off to
find the lavatory. Then the siren started, and everyone went
into the shelter. I followed Mr. Thorne because he said
he knew a quicker way. Only the corridor kept changing.
He told me to wait near the green door while
he fetched the others."

She looked around the modern station with open suspicion.

"Did we win the war?"

"Yes."

"Good." She considered this. "Is the king still alive?"

"No."

"Oh."

Elias took her to the staff room and made cocoa.
Clara disliked the vending machine, mistrusted the microwave, and was
fascinated by the refrigerator light. She accepted the passage of
time more easily than Elias expected, perhaps because children understand
that the world is strange and adults merely pretend otherwise.

"What happened to my mother?" she asked.

Elias searched the station office computer. There were records available,
though not many. A Margaret Venn had lived in the
city until 1987. She had married again after the war
and had two sons.

There was also an old newspaper article about Clara's disappearance.

MISSING EVACUEE FEARED KILLED IN RAID.

Beneath the headline was a photograph of the same serious
face now watching Elias from across the desk.

Clara read the article twice.

"She thought I was dead."

"I'm sorry," Elias said.

Clara folded her hands. She did not cry, but her
mouth became a hard, pale line.

"My suitcase is still here," she said at last.

Inside were two dresses, a hairbrush, a book of fairy
tales, three letters from her mother, and a paper bag
containing something that had once been sandwiches. Everything was exactly
as it had been eighty-three years before.

There was nowhere obvious for Clara to go. Taking her
to the authorities would invite questions neither of them could
answer. Elias therefore brought her home at the end of
his shift.

His house was small and quiet. Miriam's gardening boots still
stood beside the back door, though no one had worn
them in eleven years. Clara slept in the spare room
beneath a quilt Elias's mother had made.

Over the following days, they established an uneasy routine. Elias
reported to work at ten each evening. Clara came with
him because neither wanted to discover what would happen if
she was outside the station at 2:17.

During the quiet hours, he taught her how to use
a computer. She taught him card games he had forgotten.
She learned about rockets, television, antibiotics, and the internet. He
learned that children from the past were no more obedient
than children from the present.

"Why do people stare at those little screens all day?"
she asked after seeing several early commuters on their phones.

"They contain almost everything people want."

"That man walked into a bin."

"They do not contain the bins."

Clara smiled for the first time.

At 2:17 each night, the clock stopped, but the green
door did not reappear.

Clara stood beneath it with her suitcase packed, waiting.

On the seventh night, she asked, "What if it never
comes back?"

"We'll manage."

"I don't belong here."

"Most people feel that way at twelve."

"I mean it."

"I know."

Elias contacted his son in Australia. He did not explain
the truth. He said only that he had become responsible
for a distant relative's child.

There was a long silence on the line.

"Dad," his son said, "we don't have distant relatives."

"We do now."

His son flew home three weeks later.

Thomas Wren was fifty-two, broad-shouldered, sunburned, and permanently hurried. He
arrived at Elias's house prepared to discuss doctors, retirement, and
assisted living. Then Clara showed him the newspaper article.

Thomas read it. He examined the suitcase. He asked Clara
questions about wartime rationing and the layout of streets demolished
before he was born.

At last, he sat heavily at the kitchen table.

"This is impossible."

Clara shrugged. "That's what your father said about video calls."

Elias had not said that, but he appreciated her strategy.

Thomas stayed for two weeks. The three of them searched
archives and station plans. They found no mention of a
green door or changing corridor. There had been a night
porter named Arthur Thorne, however. He disappeared during the same
air raid as Clara.

"Maybe he's still in there," Thomas said.

Clara stared at the clock.

The idea transformed their waiting. The missing minute was no
longer merely a door that might open. It was a
place in which someone might be trapped.

Elias began mapping the station during the pause. Each night,
he searched for irregularities: a draft from a solid wall,
a loose tile, a shadow falling against the light. Clara
followed close behind. Thomas, unable to move during the stopped
minute, stood frozen in the concourse with a notebook in
his hand.

On the nineteenth night, Elias heard the bell.

It sounded faintly from Platform Three.

He and Clara ran. Elias's knees protested, and Clara quickly
pulled ahead. At the far end of the platform, beyond
a locked gate, a staircase descended where no staircase should
have been.

They reached it with seconds to spare.

"Not tonight," Elias warned. "We don't know where it leads."

Clara gripped the railing.

The clock resumed.

The stairs disappeared. Clara nearly fell onto the tracks, but
Elias caught her coat.

The following night, they came prepared. Elias carried a flashlight,
rope, chalk, and the old pocket watch Miriam had given
him on their twentieth anniversary. Clara brought her red suitcase
despite being told not to.

"If I find the way home, I'll need it."

"There is no home to find," Elias said gently.

She looked at him with such fury that he regretted
the words immediately.

At 2:17, the staircase appeared again.

They descended.

The air grew colder with each step. At the bottom
was a tiled passage crowded with abandoned luggage. Some cases
were wooden and bound in leather. Others were bright plastic
with wheels. All had labels.

Paris. Glasgow. Bombay. Prague. Home.

Arrows painted on the walls pointed in contradictory directions.

They walked quickly. Elias marked each turn with chalk, but
when he looked back, his marks had vanished. His pocket
watch remained fixed at 2:17.

The missing minute did not end.

"It's bigger inside," Clara whispered.

The passage opened onto a platform.

A train waited there.

It was not one train but many. The front was
a black steam engine breathing silver vapor. Behind it were
carriages from different eras: polished wooden compartments, wartime coaches, modern
steel cars, even a dining carriage with lamps glowing behind
red curtains.

Figures waited along the platform, motionless.

A soldier with a bandaged head. A woman wearing a
wedding dress. A businessman clutching a briefcase. A boy in
a yellow raincoat. Their clothes belonged to different years, but
all stared toward the train with the same expression of
patient uncertainty.

Near the engine stood a tall man in a porter's
uniform.

"Mr. Thorne!" Clara cried.

The porter turned.

His face was lined, and soot covered one side of
his coat. When he saw Clara, grief and relief passed
over his features.

"Oh, child," he said. "I told you to wait."

"I did!"

"Yes," he replied softly. "You always were too good at
following instructions."

Mr. Thorne explained that the station had shelters beneath the
platforms during the war. When the bomb struck, he had
been leading Clara and several passengers through a service tunnel.
A wall collapsed. He found a green door and pushed
Clara through, believing it led back to the concourse.

Instead, it led to the platform between hours.

"The railway carries people from where they are to where
they're going," he said. "But some people lose their way.
Some leave too early. Some arrive too late. This is
where they wait."

"For what?" Elias asked.

"The last train."

The engine released a sigh.

Clara stepped closer to Mr. Thorne. "Does it go to
1941?"

"No."

"To my mother?"

"In a way."

Elias understood before Clara did.

"No," he said.

Mr. Thorne met his gaze. "Everyone chooses whether to board."

Clara looked through the nearest carriage window. Inside sat people
Elias could not see clearly. Their faces shifted behind reflections,
but Clara's eyes widened.

"Mum?"

She moved toward the train.

Elias caught her hand.

She pulled away. "She's there."

"Clara—"

"She waited for me."

"She lived a whole life."

"Without me!"

Her voice echoed along the endless platform. Several waiting figures
turned.

Elias had no answer that could overcome eighty-three stolen years.
He thought of Miriam's boots by the door, Miriam's empty
chair, Miriam's voice fading in his memory. If a train
appeared and promised to take him to her, what right
would anyone have to stop him?

Clara touched the carriage handle.

Then the bell began to ring.

Mr. Thorne looked toward a clock hanging above the platform.
Its hands, unlike all the others, were moving backward.

"You must decide," he said.

The train doors opened.

Wind rushed through the station, pulling tickets and scraps of
newspaper into the air. The waiting passengers began to board.

Elias felt suddenly tired. Not the ordinary weariness of a
long shift, but the weight of every night he had
crossed alone. He saw, through another window, a woman with
silver in her hair.

Miriam.

She sat in a corner seat, wearing the green coat
in which he had buried her. She lifted one hand.

The train had not come for Clara alone.

He stepped forward.

Clara looked at him. Tears streaked her face, but her
expression was calm.

"Is that your wife?"

"Yes."

"Are you going?"

Elias watched Miriam through the glass. Every part of him
leaned toward that carriage.

Then he noticed the way she was shaking her head.

Not yet, her lips formed.

Elias stopped.

Miriam smiled.

The backward-moving clock was nearing 2:16.

Elias seized Clara's suitcase.

"What are you doing?" she shouted.

"Giving you a choice."

"I've made it!"

"No. You're choosing because the past hurts and the present
frightens you."

"She's my mother!"

"And if she is anything like a mother, she will
tell you the same thing my wife just told me."

Clara looked through the window again.

Whatever she saw there made her sob.

The bell rang faster.

Mr. Thorne climbed aboard and stood in the doorway. "Come
now, Clara, if you're coming."

Elias released her hand.

He could not force her to live.

Clara stood between him and the train, trembling.

Then she threw her arms around Mr. Thorne. The old
porter bent and embraced her.

"Tell her I waited," Clara whispered.

"She knows."

The clock reached 2:16.

Clara stepped back.

The train doors closed.

As it began to move, Mr. Thorne saluted. The steam
engine gathered speed, pulling its impossible collection of carriages past
them. Miriam's face appeared once more at a window, then
vanished into smoke.

The platform shook. Lights burst overhead.

Elias grabbed Clara, and the world turned white.

They awoke beneath the clock in Bellweather Station.

Thomas stood beside them, his notebook still raised. To him,
no time had passed at all.

The clock showed 2:18.

For the first time in thirty-eight years, Elias heard it
tick through every second.

The green door never appeared again. The station clock never
stopped again.

Elias retired the following spring. Thomas remained in England longer
than planned, then permanently. There were legal difficulties concerning Clara,
but people are often willing to accept a complicated lie
rather than an impossible truth. Records were created. Explanations were
signed. A school admitted her.

She hated mathematics, loved airplanes, and eventually became an engineer.

Years later, when Elias was very old, Clara took him
to Bellweather Station in a wheelchair. The building had been
renovated again. The bookstore was now a bakery. The ticket
barriers recognized faces. Platform Three had been closed, and ivy
covered the wall where the staircase had once appeared.

The great clock remained above Platform One.

They arrived shortly before 2:17 in the morning. The station
manager, a friend of Clara's, had allowed them inside after
hours.

"You know it won't stop," Clara said.

"I know."

They waited anyway.

The clock ticked onward.

At 2:17, nothing happened.

At 2:18, Elias smiled.

"What?" Clara asked.

"I was thinking of all the things a person can
do in a minute."

"Not much."

"You'd be surprised."

She took his hand.

High above them, the second hand moved steadily around the
pale face. Trains slept on distant tracks. Rain whispered against
the roof. Somewhere in the walls, old journeys settled like
dust.

Elias closed his eyes.

For just a moment, the station became perfectly quiet.

And in that small hour, between one breath and the
next, he heard a final train approaching—not with the roar
of iron wheels, but with the soft certainty of someone
returning home.

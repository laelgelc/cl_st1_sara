The Last Lighthouse Keeper

On the island of Orison, the lighthouse beam turned once
every twelve seconds.

For forty-three years, Elias Vale had counted those seconds whenever
sleep refused him. The light swept over his bedroom wall,
slipped across the ceiling, and vanished toward the mainland. Darkness
followed. Then the beam returned.

One, two, three.

At twelve, the room became bright again.

The rhythm had survived winter storms, summer fogs, three broken
lenses, and one lightning strike that split the old cedar
beside the keeper's cottage. It had continued through Elias's marriage,
his daughter's childhood, his wife's illness, and the long quiet
years after both wife and daughter were gone.

But at dawn on the first Monday of October, a
red government boat arrived with a brass plaque and a
young engineer.

The plaque read:

ORISON LIGHT
AUTOMATED NAVIGATION STATION 47

The engineer was named Mara Finch. She wore an orange
raincoat and carried a black case filled with instruments. Her
dark hair had been cut short, perhaps for convenience, and
the wind kept pressing it against her forehead.

"Mr. Vale?" she called from the jetty.

Elias watched from the top of the stone steps.

"No," he said.

She looked uncertain. "I was told Elias Vale was the
keeper."

"He is."

"And you're not him?"

"I am."

Mara waited for an explanation. When none came, she lifted
the black case.

"I'm here to install the automatic system."

"I know what you're here to do. That's why I
said no."

"The order was delivered last month."

"I burned it."

"You signed the receipt."

"I wanted the postman to get home before the tide
turned."

Mara closed her eyes for a moment. She appeared to
be counting, though not to twelve.

"May I come up?"

"It's a free island," Elias said.

It was not, strictly speaking, a free island. The Maritime
Authority owned the lighthouse, the cottage, the jetty, and all
nine wind-battered acres of Orison. Elias owned a wooden dinghy,
six sweaters, a silver watch that no longer worked, and
a blue teacup with a crack down one side.

Still, Mara came up.

The lighthouse stood at the island's highest point, a white
tower rising from black rock. Behind it crouched the keeper's
cottage, its roof pinned with heavy stones against the Atlantic
wind. Beyond the cliff, the sea stretched cold and metallic
beneath a pale sky.

Mara stopped at the tower door.

"I expected it to be larger," she said.

"Most important things look small from the outside."

"Is that something lighthouse keepers say?"

"It is now."

Inside, the spiral stairs wound upward around the tower's hollow
center. Elias climbed first. He was seventy-one and disliked being
followed, especially by young people who might notice how carefully
he placed his feet.

At the top, the lamp room smelled of hot metal
and salt. The great Fresnel lens stood at its center,
a palace of glass prisms taller than Mara. Sunlight broke
through it and scattered rainbows over the curved walls.

She set down her case.

"This is beautiful."

Elias, prepared to dislike everything she said, paused.

"Yes," he answered. "It is."

She opened the case and began removing tools.

The new system, she explained, would need less fuel and
no constant supervision. It would monitor its own lamp, report
faults by radio, and activate a backup beacon if the
main light failed. Sensors would adjust brightness according to visibility.
The rotation would be controlled by a sealed electric motor.

"And if the motor breaks?" Elias asked.

"A signal goes to the station on the mainland."

"And if the radio breaks?"

"The backup transmitter sends a signal."

"And if that breaks?"

Mara looked at him. "Then someone comes out."

"In a storm?"

"If necessary."

"No one comes out in a storm."

"The rescue service—"

"No one," Elias repeated, "comes out in a real storm."

Mara returned a screwdriver to its slot with unnecessary care.

"The system has been tested in the North Sea."

"The North Sea can write me a letter."

"It has reduced operating costs by sixty percent."

"Ships rarely sink from expensive lighthouses."

She stood and faced him.

"Mr. Vale, this isn't a discussion. I'm required to complete
the installation by Friday. After that, the station will operate
remotely, and you'll be transferred to shore."

"Transferred where?"

"There's accommodation in Bellweather."

"I've seen it."

"It has heating."

"So does fire."

"It has shops, a clinic, and regular buses."

"Sounds dangerous."

For the first time, Mara smiled.

It was a quick smile, there and gone, but it
altered her face. Elias found this irritating.

She spent the rest of the morning measuring brackets and
tracing cables. Elias followed her from room to room, offering
advice she did not request. He told her the western
wall leaked only when rain came sideways, which was most
of the time. He told her the third stair from
the bottom complained but was trustworthy. He told her never
to leave food uncovered because gulls had no morals.

At noon, the sea grew rough enough to prevent the
government boat from returning.

Mara stood at the kitchen window, watching waves strike the
jetty.

"The forecast said calm until evening."

"The sea doesn't read forecasts."

"Does anything here read?"

"I do."

"You burned the last thing you received."

"I read it first."

She stayed the night in the spare room, which had
once belonged to Elias's daughter. He had removed most of
Anna's belongings years ago, but pale squares remained on the
walls where her drawings had hung. A carved wooden whale
still rested on the windowsill.

At supper, Mara asked about it.

"My daughter made that," Elias said.

"Does she live nearby?"

"No."

He scraped his spoon along the bottom of his bowl.

Mara waited, but unlike most people, she did not fill
the silence with an apology. Elias was grateful for that.

Afterward, he climbed the tower to light the lamp.

Though machinery rotated the lens, the lamp itself still required
tending. Elias checked the fuel line, cleaned the burner, polished
the glass, and listened to the gears. Every machine had
a voice. The lighthouse ticked, sighed, clicked, and hummed. A
good keeper knew which sounds belonged and which did not.

Mara watched from the stairs.

"Would you like to light it?" Elias asked.

She seemed surprised. "Am I allowed?"

"No."

He handed her the taper.

She touched flame to the wick. The lamp rose slowly,
dim gold becoming white brilliance. The lens caught it and
shaped it into a beam that swept across the sea.

For a few seconds, Mara said nothing.

Then, very softly, she said, "My father would have loved
this."

Elias pretended to adjust a valve. "Sailor?"

"Engineer."

"Engineers don't love things. They improve them."

"He did both."

The beam turned.

One, two, three.

At twelve, it returned.

Mara stayed beside the lamp until midnight.

Over the next two days, she worked while Elias resisted
her in increasingly useless ways.

He misplaced her wrench, then helped her look for it.
He claimed the north cable duct was blocked, only to
discover she had already cleared it. He argued that the
sensor housing would never survive an Orison winter. She showed
him photographs of the same housing buried in Arctic ice.

By Wednesday afternoon, the automatic motor was fixed beneath the
lens platform. New wires ran through the old walls like
nerves. A gray transmitter box had been mounted beside the
door.

The future had arrived, and Elias disliked its color.

That evening, Mara found him on the eastern cliff, sitting
on a stone bench built by some forgotten keeper. The
mainland was a dark line on the horizon. Between island
and shore lay a strip of water known as the
Widow's Mile, where hidden rocks waited beneath the surface.

Mara sat at the other end of the bench.

"Why is the island called Orison?" she asked.

"It means prayer."

"I know that."

"Then why ask?"

"I wondered if there was a story."

"There's always a story. Usually invented after the name."

"Tell me anyway."

Elias pulled his cap lower against the wind.

"They say a fishing boat was caught in fog before
the lighthouse was built. The crew heard a church bell
ringing from the island and followed the sound to safety.
When they landed, they found no church and no bell.
Only a woman praying on the cliff."

"Was she a saint?"

"Probably someone's wife."

"And did it happen?"

"No."

Mara laughed. "You tell stories badly."

"I tell them honestly."

They watched the light begin its evening circuit behind them.

After a while, Mara said, "I know you think I've
come to take your life apart."

"You've come to put it in boxes."

"I'm here to change a lamp."

"You're here to remove the reason I wake up."

"You might find another."

"At my age?"

"People don't become incapable of surprise at seventy-one."

"You're very sure about seventy-one."

"My father was seventy-one when he decided to build a
boat."

"Did he?"

"He bought the wood. Drew the plans. Spent six months
arguing with everyone about the correct shape of the hull."

"Sounds sensible."

"He died before he cut the first plank."

The wind lifted spray from the rocks below.

Elias looked at her. "I'm sorry."

"So was I. Mostly because he had waited. He talked
about that boat for twenty years. There was always some
reason not to begin."

"You think I've been waiting?"

"I think you've been staying."

The words annoyed him because they were close enough to
truth to cast a shadow.

He stood.

"Storm tomorrow," he said.

Mara glanced at the clear western sky. "The forecast says
rain Friday."

"The gulls are inland. Pressure's falling. And the sea's breathing
from the south."

"The sea's breathing?"

"Badly."

By dawn, the sky had turned the color of a
bruise.

The radio reported a fast-moving Atlantic depression, stronger than predicted.
Fishing vessels were ordered to harbor. Ferries were canceled. Wind
struck the cottage in sudden blows, rattling crockery and pushing
smoke back down the chimney.

Mara checked her equipment.

"We should delay activation."

Elias looked offended. "I assumed it could survive the North
Sea."

"It can. But I'd rather test it before relying on
it in severe weather."

"Almost like having a keeper."

"Almost."

At noon, a message arrived from the mainland. The storm
had changed course again and would pass directly over Orison
after dark.

Mara's return boat would not come.

They secured the cottage shutters, carried fuel to the tower,
and inspected the lamp. Waves climbed the cliffs in white
explosions. By sunset, the island had vanished inside rain.

Elias lit the lamp early.

Its beam cut through the storm, illuminating sheets of water
and the wild backs of waves. Wind screamed around the
glass. The tower trembled, not with weakness but with the
strain of standing.

At nine, the radio crackled.

"Orison Light, Orison Light, this is trawler Mercy Bell. Engine
failure south of Widow's Mile. Four persons aboard. Drifting north-northeast.
Visibility near zero."

Elias seized the receiver.

"Mercy Bell, this is Orison. We have your signal. What
is your position?"

A burst of static answered.

Mara unfolded a chart across the worktable. "If they're south
of the Mile and drifting north-northeast—"

"They're heading for the Teeth."

The Teeth were three granite spires just below the surface
at high tide.

"Can rescue reach them?"

"Not before they strike."

The radio crackled again. "Orison, we can see your light
intermittently. Repeat, intermittently."

Elias looked at the beam. Rain swallowed it almost as
soon as it left the tower.

"Lamp to full power," he said.

"It's already near maximum."

"Near isn't full."

They opened the fuel feed. The flame surged, and the
lens threw a stronger blade into the dark.

"Mercy Bell," Elias said, "keep the light off your port
side. Do you understand? Off your port side. Prepare anchor."

Static.

Then: "Anchor lost. Trying to keep her turned."

Mara's finger traced the chart.

"At their drift rate, they have fifteen minutes."

"Less."

A violent metallic crack sounded beneath them.

The lens stopped turning.

For one impossible moment, the beam remained fixed toward the
east. Then the lamp flickered.

Mara dropped to the motor housing. "The drive chain's gone."

"The old chain never broke."

"The old chain is what broke."

Elias knelt beside her. A corroded link had split where
it ran beneath the gear assembly.

"Can you repair it?" he asked.

"Not in fifteen minutes."

The radio spat out a frightened voice. "Orison, we've lost
your signal."

Mara reached for the control panel. "The automated backup."

She pressed the switch.

A small beacon on the gallery rail flashed to life,
weak and red.

Elias stared at it. "That couldn't guide a moth."

"It marks the island."

"They don't need the island. They need the channel."

He grabbed the emergency hand crank from the wall and
fitted it to the lens mechanism.

The crank resisted. He leaned into it, and slowly—painfully—the immense
glass lens began to turn.

The beam moved across the storm.

"One rotation," Mara said. "Keep it steady."

Elias pushed.

The mechanism had been designed for two keepers. In the
early years, Elias and his assistant had taken turns during
power failures. But the assistant's post had been eliminated long
ago, and Elias had not used the crank in sixteen
years.

The iron handle dragged against him like a living thing.

"One, two, three," Mara counted.

At twelve seconds, the beam completed its circle.

The radio came alive. "Light sighted!"

"Keep it off your port side," Elias shouted. "You're coming
north. The channel bends west near the island."

The crank shuddered. Elias's shoulder burned.

Mara took hold opposite him.

"No," he said. "Watch the chart."

"I can push and read."

"You'll ruin your hands."

"I'm an engineer. They're already ruined."

Together they turned the lens.

Each revolution became a journey. Push through the eastern arc.
Pull past the north window. Brace at the stiff western
gear. Let the weight carry it south, but not too
fast.

One, two, three.

Lightning filled the lamp room with blue fire.

At twelve, the beam returned.

Mara called instructions into the radio. Elias watched the compass
ring below the lens and judged the trawler's position from
its broken transmissions.

"Mercy Bell, turn five degrees west."

"We have no power."

"Use your storm sail."

"It's torn."

"Then use your sea anchor."

"We told you, we lost it."

Elias closed his eyes. Rain hammered the glass.

"Your nets," he said.

"What?"

"Throw your nets from the starboard stern. All of them.
They'll drag and turn you."

Mara stared at him.

The radio operator hesitated. "They'll be ruined."

"So will your boat."

Static swallowed the answer.

They continued cranking.

Elias's breath shortened. Pain spread from his shoulder into his
chest, thin at first, then sharp. He said nothing. The
beam had to turn. The light had always turned.

One, two, three.

At twelve, brightness crossed the sea.

After what felt like an hour, though the clock said
nine minutes, the trawler called again.

"Nets deployed. Bow coming west."

Mara bent over the chart. "That puts them parallel to
the Teeth."

"Not enough. They'll scrape the southern rock."

"Can they see anything?"

"Mercy Bell, look for the dark interval after the beam
crosses you. When the red backup light appears, steer toward
it."

Mara understood first.

"The main beam shows the channel. The backup marks the
tower."

"Two points make a line."

"They have no engine."

"They have wind, nets, and fear. Sometimes that's enough."

The crank bucked. Elias lost his grip and struck the
floor.

Mara caught the handle before it spun backward.

"Elias!"

"I'm fine."

He was not fine. His left arm had gone numb.

Mara saw his face and knew.

"Sit down."

"Turn the light."

"I can't do both."

"Then choose correctly."

She looked at him for one furious second, then set
both hands on the crank.

Alone, she forced it forward.

The first rotation took sixteen seconds.

"Too slow," Elias said from the floor.

"I am aware."

"Use your weight at the western gear."

"I am using everything."

"Your feet are wrong."

"My feet are excellent."

"Left foot by the bolt."

She moved it.

The next rotation took thirteen seconds.

"Better," Elias said.

"Be quiet."

The third took twelve.

The lamp circled through darkness and rain, around and around,
while Elias spoke into the radio from where he sat
against the wall.

"Mercy Bell, report."

Nothing.

"Mercy Bell, report."

Mara pushed. Her face shone with sweat. Blood marked one
palm where the crank had torn the skin.

One, two, three.

At twelve, the beam returned.

Then the radio cracked.

"Orison, we're past the rocks."

Mara did not stop turning.

"Confirm," Elias said.

"We saw breakers ten meters starboard. We're inside the channel
now. Drifting toward the lee shore."

"Beach her by the jetty."

"Understood."

Only then did Elias let his head rest against the
wall.

Mara continued until the rescue station confirmed that the Mercy
Bell had grounded safely in the island's sheltered cove. Three
crew members and a twelve-year-old boy made it ashore. The
trawler was damaged, but afloat.

The storm passed shortly before sunrise.

By then, a rescue helicopter had landed on the eastern
field. Two medics carried Elias out of the lighthouse on
a stretcher.

As they crossed the grass, he saw the first clear
light of morning spread over the sea. The Mercy Bell
rested crookedly near the jetty, her nets streaming behind her
like torn black wings. Four figures stood beside the cottage
and raised their hands as he passed.

Mara walked alongside the stretcher, her injured palm wrapped in
white cloth.

"The lamp," Elias said.

"I'll repair it."

"The bearing needs oil."

"I know."

"Not too much."

"I know."

"The western pane—"

"Elias."

He looked at her.

"I know," she said.

He believed her.

Winter settled over the mainland while Elias recovered in Bellweather.
His apartment had heating, as promised. It also had beige
curtains, silent radiators, and a view of a pharmacy.

He hated it for eleven days.

On the twelfth, the boy from the Mercy Bell visited
with his mother. He brought a paper model of Orison
Light, badly folded and colored with yellow pencil. Elias placed
it on the windowsill.

Other things followed.

Letters arrived from sailors, fishermen, and people who had never
been to sea but had heard the story. A newspaper
called him a hero, though Mara's name appeared only in
the final paragraph. Elias wrote an angry correction. The newspaper
printed half of it.

In December, Mara visited.

She brought the cracked drive chain in a wooden box.

"Thought you'd want this," she said.

"I don't collect failures."

"You kept the government's first automation order."

"I burned it."

"You kept the ashes."

"That proves nothing."

She looked around the apartment. The beige curtains had been
replaced with blue ones. Charts covered one wall. On the
table stood a half-built model boat.

"You started," she said.

"It's only a model."

"It has a hull."

"Most boats do."

Mara sat by the window.

"The Authority completed the review. They're keeping Orison automated."

Elias nodded.

"But they've changed the system. Manual override outside the motor
housing. Stronger backup beacon. Emergency guidance protocol based on what
you did with the two lights."

"Common sense has become a protocol?"

"Everything becomes a protocol if you put it in a
binder."

"And the rotation?"

"Once every twelve seconds."

"Good."

She hesitated.

"There's something else. The Maritime Museum wants to restore the
old signal station at Bellweather Harbor. They need someone to
advise them. Someone who knows the equipment and can tell
stories to visitors."

"I tell stories badly."

"I warned them."

He looked toward the paper lighthouse on the windowsill.

"Would there be children?"

"Probably."

"Children touch lenses."

"They've been known to."

"Disaster."

"Almost certainly."

He took the museum job in January.

In spring, when the weather softened, Elias returned to Orison
for one final night.

The cottage had been emptied of most of his belongings.
The kitchen seemed larger without his chipped plates and hanging
pans. The spare room was bare except for the wooden
whale on the windowsill.

He carried it to the tower.

Mara had kept her promise. The lamp room shone with
polished brass and clean glass. The new motor turned with
a quiet hum. Beside it, fixed securely to the floor,
stood the emergency hand crank.

Outside, evening gathered over the Atlantic.

Elias placed the whale beside the window and watched the
lamp ignite automatically. The lens began to turn.

No hand fed the flame. No keeper listened to the
gears. Signals traveled invisibly toward the mainland, reporting that all
was well.

At first, Elias felt only absence.

Then the beam passed over him.

It swept across the sea, touched the hidden Teeth, crossed
the Widow's Mile, and reached toward ships beyond the horizon.
It did not know who had lit it. It did
not remember storms or names or hands torn by iron.
It simply performed its task.

Perhaps that was not betrayal.

Perhaps that was what all keepers worked toward: not to
be needed forever, but to leave the light turning when
they were gone.

Behind him, the tower door opened.

Mara entered carrying two cups of tea.

"I thought you might be counting," she said.

"I was."

"Still twelve?"

"Close enough."

They stood together as darkness settled over Orison.

The beam circled.

One, two, three.

Far out at sea, a ship answered with a low
horn.

At twelve, the light returned.

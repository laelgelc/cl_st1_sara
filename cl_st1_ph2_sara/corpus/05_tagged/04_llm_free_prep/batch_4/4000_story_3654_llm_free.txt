## The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, Mara Venn wound the town clock.

She climbed one hundred and twelve steps inside the tower,
carrying a brass key as long as her forearm. At
the top, beneath the great iron bell, she fitted the
key into the clock's heart and turned it seven times.
The gears stirred. The pendulum resumed its solemn sweep, and
the people of Bellweather began another day.

At least, that was what they believed.

Mara knew the clock did more than measure time. It
collected what people lost.

A second dropped while a baker stared into an oven.
Three minutes forgotten by a child daydreaming beside the river.
An hour misplaced by an old man who entered a
room and could not remember why. All of it flowed
unseen through the streets and gathered inside the tower.

Her father had shown her the hidden chamber when she
was nine.

"You must never take from it," he had warned.

Behind the clock face, where the afternoon sun turned the
numerals black, there was a small green door. Beyond it
lay a room without windows. Hundreds of glass bottles lined
its walls, each sealed with red wax. Some glowed pale
blue; others shone gold, violet, or the deep green of
summer leaves.

Each bottle contained time.

Her father had lifted one from a shelf. Inside, silver
sparks spun in a slow spiral.

"Seven minutes," he said. "Lost by someone waiting for bad
news."

"Can we give them back?"

"Sometimes."

"To whom?"

"To those who need them most."

"Then why mustn't we take any?"

His face had become grave. "Because need is a door
with no lock."

For forty-two years Mara obeyed him.

When her father died, she inherited the brass key, the
tower, and the duty of tending the lost hours. She
learned how to collect them from the clock's inner reservoir,
how to separate minutes from seconds, and how to hear
the faint differences between them.

Happy time chimed when shaken. Lonely time sighed. Time lost
in sleep was soft and almost weightless. Time lost in
fear clung to the glass like smoke.

Once each winter, Mara chose a bottle and carried it
to someone in town.

She gave six minutes to a fisherman swept beneath the
ice. The rescue party swore they had reached him just
before he drowned, though their watches stopped during the attempt.

She gave half an hour to a woman struggling to
deliver twins. The church bell rang twelve times that night,
paused, and rang twelve times again. Both babies lived.

She gave eight seconds to a boy who stepped into
the path of a carriage. No one saw what happened.
One instant he was beneath the horse's hooves; the next
he stood unharmed on the pavement, holding a red apple.

Such gifts had to be rare. Too much returned time
could knot the hours together. Clocks ran backward. People met
themselves in mirrors. Doors opened into rooms that had not
yet been built.

Mara's father had told her of a keeper who tried
to save an entire city from a flood. He opened
forty years at once. The river vanished—but so did the
city. In its place stood a forest of ancient trees,
each bearing small silver clocks instead of fruit.

"Time is not kind," her father used to say. "It
is only exact."

Mara believed him until Elias Vale came home.

He arrived on the last train in November, wearing a
dark coat dusted with snow. Mara was crossing the station
square when she saw him step onto the platform.

For a moment, she was nineteen again.

Elias had the same crooked smile, though sorrow had drawn
lines around it. His hair was gray at the temples.
He carried a doctor's bag in one hand and a
wooden case in the other.

"Mara," he said.

She had imagined this meeting so often that every possible
answer felt rehearsed. In the end she said only, "You
missed the summer."

"By twenty-three years."

"Twenty-four."

"Of course you counted."

"I keep the clock."

His smile faded. "Yes. I heard about your father."

"He died sixteen years ago."

"I'm sorry."

"You had sixteen years to say so."

Snow settled between them.

When they were young, Elias had promised to marry her.
Then a university in the capital offered him a place,
and he promised to return after four years. His letters
came weekly, then monthly, then not at all.

Mara had kept the final one. It contained only three
sentences.

There is work here that matters. I hope you understand.
Do not wait for me.

She had not waited. Not exactly. She had simply continued
living in a town where every street remembered him.

"What brought you back?" she asked.

"My daughter."

He gestured toward the train. A conductor emerged, pushing a
wheeled chair. In it sat a girl of perhaps fourteen,
bundled beneath gray blankets.

Her face was thin, but her eyes were bright and
watchful.

"This is Lysa," Elias said. "Lysa, this is Mara Venn."

"The clock keeper," the girl said.

"Among other things."

Lysa looked up at the tower. "Does it always run
three seconds slow?"

Mara followed her gaze. "No."

"It does today."

The clock began to strike six. The first toll rolled
over the snowy rooftops. By the sixth, Lysa had closed
her eyes as if listening to a distant song.

That evening, Elias came to Mara's house.

She did not invite him in, but the wind did.
It pushed snow across the threshold, and he entered with
it.

"I need your help," he said.

"You're a doctor."

"That is why I know no doctor can save her."

Mara hung his coat by the stove. "What is wrong?"

"Her heart. She was born with a defect. There have
been surgeries, treatments, every possible medicine. She might have a
month. Perhaps less."

Mara poured tea because her hands needed a task.

"You heard stories," she said.

"I remembered stories."

"Children's tales."

"I remember a boy beneath a carriage. I was there,
Mara. I saw him fall. I saw the horse strike
him. Then the clock stopped, and he was standing beside
me."

"Memory changes."

"Not yours."

She set the teapot down too hard. "If I could
cure every illness, do you think the churchyard would be
full?"

"I'm not asking you to cure everyone."

"No. Only your daughter."

"Is that so terrible?"

"It is always only one person to the person asking."

Elias lowered his head. "She is all I have."

Mara wanted to answer cruelly. She wanted to tell him
he had chosen what he had, that the years were
exact, that need was a door with no lock.

Instead she asked, "Where is her mother?"

"Dead. Nine years ago."

"I'm sorry."

"You had nine years to say so."

Mara looked at him, and despite herself she laughed. It
was a brief, bitter sound, but it broke something open
between them.

Elias reached into his coat and removed an envelope.

"I wrote to you," he said.

"I received one letter."

"I wrote dozens."

"You expect me to believe they all vanished?"

"No. Only that your father sent them back."

The room became very still.

"He came to the capital," Elias continued. "He said the
keeper could not belong to anyone. That you had agreed
to take his place. He told me you had changed
your mind about us."

"My father would not—"

Elias placed the envelope on the table. It was yellow
with age, addressed in his handwriting, and stamped RETURN TO
SENDER.

On the back, in the unmistakable script of Mara's father,
were the words: She has no time for you.

Mara sat down.

All those years she had believed Elias had chosen ambition
over her. All those years he had believed she had
chosen the tower.

"Why didn't you come back?" she whispered.

"Pride. Anger. Cowardice. Choose whichever answer makes me easiest to
forgive."

"And now?"

"Now I have no pride left."

He knelt beside her chair.

"Please," he said. "Give Lysa time."

The hidden room was colder than Mara remembered.

She entered alone after midnight, carrying a lantern. Bottles gleamed
on every wall, more than she could count. Bellweather had
grown older, and age was careless with its hours.

She passed shelves labeled in her father's hand.

MOMENTS OF HESITATION.

FORGOTTEN DREAMS.

WAITING.

The waiting shelf stretched from floor to ceiling.

Mara found bottles large enough to hold days, but most
were dark or troubled. A week lost in fever would
only give Lysa another week of fever. A month spent
in grief might burden her heart beyond endurance.

Time retained the shape of its loss.

At the back of the chamber stood a locked cabinet.
Mara had seen her father open it only once. The
brass key fit.

Inside was a single bottle.

It was no larger than her hand, yet sunlight burned
within it. When she touched the glass, she smelled warm
grass and river water. She heard a young man laughing
under an elm tree.

A paper label hung from the neck.

MARA VENN. ELIAS VALE.
SUMMER, AGE NINETEEN.
FORTY-SEVEN DAYS.

She nearly dropped it.

Memory returned with painful clarity.

That summer had always seemed strangely brief. Elias and she
had walked beside the river, planned a house, argued over
the names of children. Yet whole afternoons were missing from
her mind. She had thought happiness caused time to fly.

Her father had stolen those days.

Perhaps he had believed he was protecting the tower. Perhaps
he had seen love as a threat to duty. He
had taken forty-seven days from them, bottled the days, and
later driven Elias away.

Mara held the summer to the lantern. Tiny shapes moved
inside: two figures beneath an elm, forever almost touching.

Forty-seven days would not cure Lysa. But they might become
years if properly released. Joyful time was generous. Shared time,
more so.

It was also dangerous.

The label carried a warning in red ink.

UNFINISHED. RETURN ONLY TO BOTH.

The time belonged to Mara and Elias together. If opened
in their presence, it would seek its original path. It
would make them nineteen again for forty-seven days—or perhaps erase
everything that had happened since.

Lysa might never be born.

Mara put the bottle back.

Then she took it out again.

At dawn, she found Lysa awake in the station hotel.

Elias slept in a chair beside the bed. Mara touched
his shoulder, and he woke instantly.

"Bring her to the tower," she said.

The snowstorm had covered the streets. Elias carried Lysa while
Mara walked ahead with a lantern. The girl rested her
head against her father's shoulder.

"Is this a rescue?" she asked.

"I don't know," Mara replied.

"Those are the interesting kind."

Inside the tower, Elias climbed slowly, pausing at every landing.
By the time they reached the clock chamber, his face
was pale with effort.

Mara spread blankets beneath the pendulum. Lysa lay down and
stared at the gears turning above her.

"It sounds alive," she said.

"It is," Mara told her. "But not in the way
we are."

She showed Elias the bottle.

He read the label twice.

"You kept this?"

"My father took it from us."

Understanding passed over his face. "If we open it—"

"It may return to us. We might become young again.
We might lose everything after that summer."

"Including Lysa."

"Yes."

He handed the bottle back at once. "Then no."

Lysa pushed herself upright. "Wait."

"No," Elias repeated.

"You brought me here for magic, and now you're refusing
it before anyone explains?"

"It could make it so you were never born."

"Dying will do that eventually."

"That is not the same."

"No," she said. "But it's still my time."

Mara knelt beside her. "Time never belongs to only one
person. That is the problem."

Lysa studied the bottle. Golden light moved across her face.

"What if you don't return it to yourselves?" she asked.

"It must go where it came from."

"Does it say that, or do you think that?"

Mara looked at the label.

Unfinished. Return only to both.

An idea appeared—impossible, reckless, shining.

"There may be another way," she said.

She carried the bottle to the mechanism. At the clock's
center was a narrow silver basin where lost seconds gathered
like drops of rain. If she poured the summer there,
the clock would not return it directly. It would strike
the time across the town.

Shared between hundreds of people, forty-seven days would become minutes
for each. Harmless.

But if Lysa stood inside the bell when it rang,
she might receive more. Sound carried time, and the bell
was its loudest voice.

"How much more?" Elias asked.

"I don't know."

"Could it kill her?"

"Yes."

"She is already dying," Lysa said.

"Stop saying that!" Elias shouted.

The gears seemed to recoil from his voice.

Lysa reached for his hand. "You taught me not to
lie about a diagnosis."

"I taught you to hope."

"I do hope. I hope this works."

"And if it doesn't?"

She smiled, and Mara saw Elias's crooked smile within hers.

"Then I climbed a forbidden clock tower in a snowstorm,"
Lysa said. "That's better than another morning in bed."

At six, Mara opened the summer.

The wax seal cracked.

Warm wind burst through the chamber. Snow melted on the
windows. The scent of grass and river water filled the
tower, and for an instant Mara stood beneath the elm
tree again.

Nineteen-year-old Elias was beside her, a green leaf caught in
his hair.

"If we could keep one day forever," he said from
the past, "which would you choose?"

"Tomorrow," young Mara answered.

The memory pierced her more deeply than grief.

She poured the glowing time into the silver basin.

The clock stopped.

Far below, Bellweather fell silent. A spoon hung in the
air above a breakfast bowl. A dog remained suspended mid-leap.
Snowflakes paused over chimneys.

Only Mara, Elias, and Lysa could move.

The golden summer ran through the gears, coating each tooth
and spring in sunlight. The clock hands spun wildly. Around
them, the tower changed. New wood became old, old stone
became new. Vines burst through the walls and withered in
a breath.

"Now!" Mara cried.

Elias lifted Lysa into the bell.

Mara pulled the striking lever.

The bell sounded.

Golden light exploded over the town.

The first toll carried the afternoon beneath the elm. The
second carried laughter by the river. The third carried a
kiss that had almost happened. With every stroke, the stolen
summer scattered across Bellweather.

People received it in fragments.

The baker remembered a happiness he had never lived.

A widow woke with the certainty that she had once
danced barefoot in grass.

Children dreamed of a river shining under green branches.

The clock struck forty-seven times.

On the final toll, the bottle shattered in Mara's hand.

Time resumed.

Snow plunged from the sky. Somewhere below, dishes broke. Horses
cried out. Every clock in Bellweather began ringing at once.

Inside the bell, Lysa collapsed.

Elias lowered her to the floor.

She did not breathe.

He pressed his ear to her chest, then began the
desperate rhythm of a doctor refusing his own knowledge. Mara
stood frozen beside him.

"Come on," he whispered. "Come on, Lysa."

Nothing.

The golden light faded from the gears.

Mara thought of the forty-two years she had obeyed. She
thought of her father's warnings, his lies, his certainty that
duty mattered more than love.

Need is a door with no lock.

She ran to the hidden room.

Bottles shook on their shelves. Some had cracked during the
striking. Blue seconds leaked across the floor. Green hours rose
like mist.

Mara seized bottles at random.

Three minutes lost in prayer.

Twelve minutes forgotten during a song.

An hour spent watching the sea.

She carried them to the clock chamber and broke them
over Lysa.

Light washed across the girl, but she remained still.

"Mara," Elias said. His voice was empty.

She returned to the chamber.

There, on the highest shelf, she saw a bottle she
had never noticed. It was clear and unlabeled. Inside rested
a single white spark.

When she touched it, she heard her father's voice.

Time is not kind. It is only exact.

She understood then.

This was not lost time. It was her father's final
second—the instant between life and death. He had trapped it
because he had been afraid to cross.

Mara carried the bottle to Lysa.

"One second," Elias said. "What can one second do?"

"Everything, if it is the right one."

She broke the bottle.

The white spark fell into Lysa's chest.

The girl inhaled.

It was a small breath, hardly louder than the ticking
clock, but Elias heard it. He gathered her into his
arms, laughing and sobbing.

Beneath his hand, Lysa's heart beat once.

Then again.

Then found a rhythm it had never known.

Outside, the snow stopped.

Lysa lived.

The doctors in the capital later claimed that the change
in her heart was medically astonishing but not impossible. They
used words such as spontaneous correction and delayed response. Elias
let them.

Bellweather noticed other changes.

For years afterward, no one in town felt they had
enough time. This should have made them anxious, but it
did the opposite. The baker closed early to walk with
his wife. The schoolmaster lengthened recess. People lingered at doors.
They listened to stories they had heard before.

On summer evenings, nearly everyone went to the river.

Mara remained the keeper, though she no longer followed all
her father's rules. She gave time when she could and
refused when she must. She learned that caution and cruelty
often wore the same coat, and that wisdom did not
always mean obedience.

Elias reopened the abandoned clinic near the square.

He did not ask Mara to recover the years they
had lost. Some things, they agreed, could not be returned
without breaking what had grown around the absence.

Instead, he called on her every evening at seven.

Often Lysa came too. She grew stronger, then taller. She
studied clocks, anatomy, and every forbidden subject she could find.
At seventeen, she could climb the tower steps faster than
Mara.

One evening, beneath the elm by the river, Elias asked,
"If we could keep one day forever, which would you
choose?"

Mara remembered sunlight trapped in glass. She remembered a question
stolen from them and scattered across a town.

She took his hand.

"Tomorrow," she said.

Above Bellweather, the great clock ran three seconds slow.

Mara never corrected it.

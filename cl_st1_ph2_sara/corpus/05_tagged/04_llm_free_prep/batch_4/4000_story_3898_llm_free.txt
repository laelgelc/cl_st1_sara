# The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every evening at six minutes past midnight, the clock shop
on Bellweather Street opened its door.

No hand turned the brass knob. No bell chimed above
the frame. The door simply swung inward, allowing the cold
night air to wander between the shelves, where hundreds of
clocks whispered in the dark.

Some ticked quickly, nervous as mice. Others took their time.
A grandfather clock in the corner sighed once every minute,
while a row of silver pocket watches clicked together like
beetles under leaves.

At the back of the shop sat Elias Vale, Keeper
of Lost Hours.

He was a thin man with white hair, though he
was not as old as he looked. He wore a
black waistcoat with many pockets, each holding a different tool:
tweezers, winding keys, tiny screwdrivers, a magnifying lens, and one
narrow silver knife that had never cut anything solid.

Before him lay a glass jar filled with golden light.

Inside the jar, an hour was waiting.

It shifted like sunlight on water. Sometimes it glowed softly;
sometimes it flared against the glass, as if remembering the
life from which it had been taken.

Elias lifted the jar and read the label.

MRS. ADELA PINE
14 OCTOBER, 1987
4:00 P.M.–5:00 P.M.
LOST TO REGRET

He carried it to a wall of cabinets and placed
it in a drawer among dozens of similar jars.

There were hours lost to boredom, hours lost to fear,
hours wasted in waiting rooms and railway stations. There were
childhood afternoons forgotten by adults and sleepless nights that seemed
endless while they happened but vanished by morning.

Elias kept them all.

Most people never noticed when an hour went missing. They
would look up from a window or a newspaper and
wonder where the time had gone. By then, it was
already on its way to Bellweather Street.

The work was quiet, precise, and lonely.

Elias preferred it that way.

At twenty-three minutes past midnight, the open door admitted a
girl wearing a red coat.

She stepped inside without hesitation.

Elias stared at her over the rim of his spectacles.

Customers did not come to the clock shop. In fact,
ordinary people could not even see Bellweather Street after midnight.
They passed its entrance and found themselves looking down an
alley or at a brick wall.

Yet the girl stood before him, snow melting in her
dark hair.

"Are you the Keeper?" she asked.

Elias glanced at the clocks. Several had stopped.

"Who wants to know?"

"My name is Mira Quill."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

She was perhaps twelve years old. She carried no bag
and wore no gloves. Her fingers were red from the
cold, but her gray eyes were steady.

Elias closed the ledger on his desk.

"You should go home, Mira Quill."

"I can't."

"Everyone can go home."

"Not if their home is gone."

The clocks resumed ticking, though unevenly.

Elias studied her more carefully. The hem of her coat
was burned. One sleeve had been torn and repaired with
blue thread. Around her neck hung a brass key.

"What do you want?" he asked.

"An hour."

"We do not sell time here."

"I don't want to buy one. I want mine back."

Elias felt the room grow colder.

"People lose hours every day," he said. "They are not
entitled to their return."

"This one was stolen."

"No hour can be stolen."

"Then your rules are wrong."

A small clock on the counter gave an offended click.

Elias rose.

He was taller than Mira had expected, but she did
not step back.

"Time may be forgotten," he said. "It may be squandered,
ignored, regretted, or surrendered. But it cannot be stolen."

Mira pulled the brass key from her neck and placed
it on the counter.

Elias stopped breathing.

The key was small, with four teeth and a handle
shaped like a crescent moon. Along its stem, in letters
almost too fine to see, was engraved the name VALE.

"Where did you get this?"

"My mother gave it to me."

"What is her name?"

"Lydia Quill."

The grandfather clock in the corner failed to sigh.

Elias picked up the key. His hand trembled.

Many years before, when he still possessed black hair and
foolish hopes, he had taken an apprentice. Lydia had been
clever, impatient, and fearless. She could repair a watch by
sound alone. She could distinguish an hour lost to grief
from one lost to anger by holding its jar against
her heart.

She had also asked too many questions.

Why must lost time remain lost? Why couldn't the hours
be returned? Why should one terrible decision last forever when
somewhere, locked in a cabinet, waited the moment in which
it could be changed?

Elias had warned her that returned time did not behave
like ordinary time. It tangled with memory. It opened doors
that had been sealed. It demanded payment.

Lydia had called him a coward.

Then one winter morning, she left with the key to
the lower vault.

Elias had not seen her since.

"Where is your mother?" he asked.

"Dead," Mira replied.

The word was quiet, but every clock heard it.

Elias set the key down.

"When?"

"Three days ago. Our house burned."

"And the hour you seek?"

"The hour before the fire."

Elias removed his spectacles and rubbed his eyes.

"You remember it?"

"No. That's why I came. I was in the kitchen,
and Mother was upstairs. I heard the clock strike eight.
Then it was nine, and the house was burning, and
I was standing in the snow."

"You lost an hour to shock."

"She took it."

"Who?"

"My mother."

Elias looked at the brass key.

Mira continued. "Before she died, she said, 'Find Bellweather Street.
Tell Elias I'm sorry. Tell him the hollow clock has
started.' Then she gave me this."

At once, every clock in the shop fell silent.

The silence was so complete that Elias heard snow touch
the windows.

From beneath the floor came a deep metallic knock.

Once.

Twice.

Then a third time.

Mira looked down. "What was that?"

Elias slipped the key into his pocket.

"The reason your mother stole your hour."

He crossed the shop and pulled aside a green curtain.
Behind it stood an iron door with no handle, only
a crescent-shaped keyhole.

"You will stay here," he said.

"No."

"This is not a request."

"Then I refuse anyway."

"Mira—"

"My mother sent me. Whatever is down there killed her."

"You do not know that."

"Neither do you."

The floor shook. Several jars rattled in their cabinets.

Elias considered locking the girl outside, but Lydia had once
worn that same expression: chin lifted, eyes bright with terror
disguised as determination. Locking a Quill anywhere had never accomplished
much.

He inserted the key.

The iron door opened.

Beyond it, a spiral staircase descended into darkness.

Elias lit a lantern with a blue flame and handed
Mira a second one.

"Stay close," he said. "Do not touch the walls. Do
not answer if you hear someone call your name."

"What if it's you?"

"Especially then."

They began to descend.

The staircase curled far below the shop. At first, the
walls were brick. Then brick gave way to stone, stone
to packed earth, and earth to something pale and smooth
that seemed disturbingly like bone.

Mira kept one hand against her coat pocket.

"What's the hollow clock?" she asked.

"The first clock."

"The first ever made?"

"The first that mattered."

"That sounds like something old people say when they don't
want to explain."

Despite himself, Elias smiled.

"Long ago," he said, "before hours had names, people experienced
time differently. A summer could pass in a breath. A
single night of fear could last a year. Then a
clockmaker named Orra Bellweather built a machine that taught time
to move evenly."

"How?"

"No one knows. Some say she trapped a star inside
it. Some say she carved its gears from her own
bones. But after the clock began to turn, seconds became
equal. Days learned to follow one another. The world grew
measurable."

"And the clock was hollow?"

"Not then. At its center was a golden pendulum called
the Heart of Measure. Without it, the clock could still
move, but it would consume time instead of counting it."

Mira's lantern flickered.

"My mother took the heart."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"To return an hour."

"Whose?"

Elias did not answer.

The staircase ended at a circular chamber.

Twelve passages opened around them, each marked with a number.
Wind blew from one tunnel, carrying the smell of salt.
From another came the sound of distant music. A third
was filled with autumn leaves, though there were no trees.

In the center stood a stone pedestal. On it rested
a jar glowing with golden light.

Mira stepped toward it.

Her name was written on the label.

MIRA QUILL
21 DECEMBER
8:00 P.M.–9:00 P.M.
TAKEN FOR SAFEKEEPING

"That's it," she whispered.

Elias caught her shoulder.

"Wait."

A shadow moved within the passage marked twelve.

It entered the chamber wearing the shape of a woman.

"Mira," it said.

The girl froze.

The woman had dark hair, a burned dress, and a
face much like Mira's. Smoke curled from her skin.

"Mother?"

Elias seized Mira as she tried to run forward.

"That is not Lydia."

The figure smiled.

It opened its mouth, and the sound of a great
clock emerged.

Tick.

The chamber shifted.

Tock.

The twelve tunnels stretched outward like spokes on a wheel.

Tick.

The false Lydia split from throat to waist, and inside
her was an emptiness filled with turning gears.

Tock.

"Give me the hour," said the hollow clock.

Its voice came from everywhere.

The jar on the pedestal cracked.

Elias drew the narrow silver knife from his waistcoat and
sliced through the air. The blade opened a bright seam,
and for one instant the creature staggered.

"Take the jar!" he shouted.

Mira tore free and grabbed it.

The moment her fingers touched the glass, the chamber vanished.

She stood in her kitchen at home.

Snow pressed against the windows. A kettle sang on the
stove. The clock above the fireplace showed eight minutes past
eight.

"Mira?"

Her mother stood in the doorway.

Not burned. Not a shadow. Alive.

Mira dropped the jar, but it disappeared before striking the
floor.

Lydia crossed the room and embraced her.

For a moment, Mira could do nothing. Then she clung
to her mother so tightly that the woman gasped.

"You're here," Mira whispered.

"Of course I am."

"No. You died."

Lydia became very still.

"So Elias returned the hour."

"I took it."

"That was dangerous."

"You stole it first."

"To protect you."

"From what?"

The kitchen clock ticked backward.

Lydia led Mira to the table.

"I once had a brother," she said. "His name was
Thomas. When we were children, he drowned in the river.
I saw him fall through the ice, but I was
too frightened to move."

The kettle's song deepened as time slowed.

"Years later, I became Elias's apprentice and learned about lost
hours. I found the hour in which Thomas died. I
thought I could return to it. I thought I could
save him."

"Did you?"

"Yes."

Mira waited.

Lydia's eyes filled with tears.

"When I returned, I pulled him from the river. But
time requires balance. The death meant for Thomas followed us.
It took our mother a week later. Then our father.
Then strangers. Every future I created cracked apart."

"So you changed it back?"

"I tried. I removed the Heart of Measure from the
first clock. For one minute, all time became possible at
once. I chose the world in which Thomas died, and
the others lived."

"That's horrible."

"It was necessary."

"Those aren't opposites."

Lydia looked at her with sad pride.

"No," she said. "They rarely are."

A dark stain appeared on the ceiling. Smoke crept down
the walls.

"The hollow clock has searched for the heart ever since,"
Lydia continued. "I hid it where the clock could never
willingly look."

"Where?"

"In an hour of grief."

The fire began upstairs.

Mira smelled burning wood.

"This hour," she said.

Lydia nodded. "The heart is hidden inside the moment of
my death. The hollow clock cannot enter without experiencing the
loss. So it took your memory instead and waited for
you to bring the hour back."

"Then we'll leave it here."

"We cannot. Once a lost hour is returned, it must
be lived."

Flames crawled beneath the kitchen door.

Mira stood.

"I can save you."

"No."

"I know the fire is coming. We can run now."

Lydia caught her hands.

"If I live, the heart remains hidden inside me, and
the clock will continue consuming hours. At first, people will
lose minutes. Then years. Eventually, whole lives will vanish before
they are lived."

"There has to be another way."

"That is what I said to Elias."

Glass shattered upstairs.

The room filled with smoke.

Lydia removed a golden object from beneath her dress. It
was shaped like the pendulum of a clock, no longer
than a finger. Warm light pulsed inside it.

"When the fire reaches this room, you must take the
heart."

"I won't."

"You must carry it back to Elias."

"I won't let you die again!"

Lydia placed a hand against her daughter's cheek.

"You think this hour was taken because I wanted to
spare you the pain," she said. "I did want that.
But pain is not only suffering. Sometimes it is proof
that love was present."

The ceiling groaned.

"You will remember this time," Lydia said. "You will remember
that I was afraid. You will remember that I made
mistakes. And you will remember that I loved you beyond
every hour the world could hold."

The kitchen door burst open.

Fire entered like a living animal.

Lydia pushed Mira toward the back door. A burning beam
fell between them, scattering sparks.

"Mira, go!"

Mira pulled at the beam, but the heat drove her
back.

Her mother's figure wavered behind the flames.

The golden heart rolled across the floor.

Mira snatched it up.

The moment she did, she felt the hour fold around
her. The kitchen, the fire, and her mother became thin
as reflections on glass.

Lydia smiled through the smoke.

Then the hour broke.

Mira found herself again in the chamber below the shop,
kneeling beside the shattered jar.

The hollow clock towered above her.

Its borrowed human shape was gone. It had become a
vast circle of black metal, with hands that spun across
a faceless dial. At its center gaped an empty space.

Elias lay against the wall, his silver knife broken beside
him.

"Mira," he said weakly. "Do not let it take the
heart."

The golden pendulum burned in her palm.

The clock's gears accelerated.

Around the chamber, the twelve passages flickered. Mira saw glimpses
of days that had been and days that might be:
herself as an old woman, herself as a child, her
mother laughing in a garden, Elias closing the shop forever.

"Give me measure," the clock said, "and I will return
her."

The vision changed.

Lydia stood in the kitchen doorway, alive.

Mira took one step forward.

"You can have every morning," said the clock. "Every birthday.
Every word you did not say."

Another step.

Her mother extended a hand.

Behind Mira, Elias whispered, "A returned past is paid for
with the future."

The clock's voice shook the chamber.

"Your future is empty without her."

Mira stopped.

She looked at the vision of her mother.

Then she remembered Lydia's face in the fire—not peaceful, not
fearless, but certain. She remembered her mother saying that pain
proved love had been present.

An empty future was still a future.

And emptiness could be filled.

Mira closed her fingers around the Heart of Measure.

"You don't want this," she told the clock.

"I require it."

"No. You're afraid of being hollow."

The machine shrieked. Its hands spun so quickly they vanished.

Mira approached, not the vision of her mother, but the
dark opening at the clock's center.

"Being empty isn't the same as being broken," she said.

Then she pushed the golden heart into the hollow space.

Elias shouted her name.

Light erupted through the chamber.

The clock's black gears turned gold. Its hands slowed. Once
more, it began not to consume time, but to count
it.

Tick.

Mira saw her mother carrying her as a baby.

Tock.

She saw Elias as a young man, opening the shop
for the first time.

Tick.

She saw Lydia stealing the crescent key.

Tock.

She saw countless people looking up from their lives and
wondering where the time had gone.

The clock struck one.

The sound traveled up the staircase, through the shop, over
the sleeping city, and out across the world.

Every clock answered.

Then darkness fell.

When Mira woke, she was lying behind the counter in
the shop. A blanket covered her. Morning sunlight warmed the
windows.

Elias sat nearby with his arm in a sling.

"You put the heart back," he said.

"Wasn't I supposed to?"

"I believed it would destroy you."

"You could have mentioned that."

"You were already doing it."

Mira sat up. The clocks ticked calmly around them.

"What happens now?"

"The hollow clock keeps the hours in balance."

"And my mother?"

Elias looked toward the window.

"She remains dead."

Mira swallowed.

This grief felt different from the blank space she had
carried before. It was heavier, sharper, and strangely precious.

"Do the lost hours stay lost?" she asked.

"Most of them."

"Even the good ones?"

"Especially those. People often fail to notice happiness while it
is happening."

Mira considered the cabinets filled with golden jars.

"That seems unfair."

"It is."

"Can we change it?"

Elias raised an eyebrow. "We?"

"My house is gone. You need an apprentice. And someone
has to correct your rules."

"I do not need an apprentice."

A cuckoo emerged from a clock above him.

"Liar," it announced.

Elias glared at it.

Mira smiled for the first time in three days.

Months passed.

She learned to clean gears, polish springs, and catch loose
minutes before they slipped beneath the furniture. She learned that
hours lost to laughter were pale blue, while hours lost
to longing shone like moonlight. She learned never to open
a jar labeled FIRST LOVE indoors.

Under her influence, the shop changed.

Elias still collected lost hours, but not all of them
remained in the cabinets. Sometimes a tired father received an
extra bedtime story with his son. Sometimes an old woman
woke from a dream remembering the exact sound of her
sister's voice. Sometimes a lonely child discovered that a rainy
afternoon could last as long as needed.

Every evening at six minutes past midnight, the door on
Bellweather Street opened by itself.

The clocks whispered in the darkness.

At the back of the shop sat Elias Vale, Keeper
of Lost Hours, and beside him worked Mira Quill, who
was becoming something the world had never known before.

Not a keeper.

A giver.

Above them, on a narrow shelf, stood an empty jar
bearing Lydia's name.

Mira never filled it. She kept it as a reminder
that some hours could not be recovered and should not
be remade.

But beside the jar, she placed a clock of her
own design.

It had no numbers and only one hand.

On its face she painted a single word:

NOW.

And no matter how the other clocks hurried or hesitated,
that one always kept perfect time.

# The Cartographer of Rain

In Bellweather, rain never fell where it was expected.

It might pour for three days over a single chimney
while the neighboring roofs remained dry. It might rise from
the gutters in silver threads and vanish into a cloudless
sky. Once, during the mayor's wedding, rain fell inside the
church but nowhere else, drumming upon the pews and soaking
the bride while the stained-glass saints looked on with suspiciously
bright eyes.

For this reason, the town employed a Cartographer of Rain.

His name was Elias Vale, and every morning he climbed
the narrow stairs of the weather tower carrying a roll
of blank paper, a brass compass, and a box of
blue pencils. From the tower's round window, he watched the
rain arrive, depart, turn corners, hesitate over houses, and gather
in alleys. Then he drew its movements on maps.

The maps were not forecasts. Forecasts tried to say what
rain would do, and Bellweather's rain disliked being told. Elias's
maps showed only where it had been.

Still, the townspeople valued them.

Farmers consulted the archives before planting. Children traced old storms
and imagined them as dragons, wolves, or ships. Lawyers used
the maps to settle disputes over flooded cellars and ruined
hats. Lovers searched them for romantic patterns.

"There," a young man might say, pointing to a curl
of blue ink around two neighboring streets. "You see? Even
the rain wanted us to meet."

Elias did not encourage such interpretations. To him, rain was
neither romantic nor cruel. It was simply difficult.

He had been the Cartographer for thirty-one years, taking the
position after his father died. His father had inherited it
from his mother, who had inherited it from an uncle
no one remembered. Elias knew every rooftop, drainpipe, orchard, and
weather vane in Bellweather. He knew which windows leaked and
which doors swelled in damp seasons. He could identify the
sound of rain on slate, copper, glass, canvas, and leaves.

But he had never once been caught in a storm.

Rain avoided him.

If Elias walked into a downpour, the clouds thinned above
his head. If he stood beneath a dripping tree, the
branches ceased dripping. When he was a boy, other children
had invited him to picnics as a kind of living
umbrella. His father had called it a useful gift.

Elias considered it an embarrassment.

How could a man map a thing that refused to
touch him?

Nevertheless, he did his work faithfully until the morning he
found a girl asleep beneath his desk.

She was perhaps twelve years old, with tangled black hair
and a coat several sizes too large. Mud covered her
boots. She slept curled around a glass jar, holding it
against her chest as if it were warm.

Elias cleared his throat.

The girl opened one eye.

"The tower is closed at night," he said.

"I know."

"How did you get in?"

"Window."

"We are six floors above the ground."

"Yes."

Elias waited for further explanation, but none came.

"What is your name?"

"Lina."

"Your full name."

"That is all of it."

She sat up. The jar in her arms was sealed
with a metal lid. At first Elias thought it was
empty. Then something moved inside—a drop of water crawling slowly
up the glass.

Elias bent closer.

The drop stopped.

"What is that?"

"Rain," Lina said.

"Rain does not crawl upward."

"This one does."

As if offended by his doubt, the drop leaped from
the bottom of the jar to the lid with a
tiny tap.

Elias stared. "Where did you find it?"

"I didn't. It found me."

He straightened. "Rain does not find people."

Lina gave him a patient look. "This one did."

Outside, the town bells rang seven times. Elias crossed to
the window and opened the wooden shutters.

Bellweather stretched below in gray morning light. Smoke rose from
chimneys. Bakers lifted awnings. Shopkeepers swept their steps. In the
eastern hills, clouds gathered in long dark bands.

Elias picked up his compass.

The needle spun once and pointed west, away from the
clouds.

"That is unusual," he murmured.

"What is?"

"The storm is moving backward."

"Maybe it forgot something."

Storms did not forget things, but Elias had learned that
arguing with children delayed work. He laid a fresh sheet
of paper across his desk.

"You must leave," he said. "I have a map to
make."

Lina rose, still holding the jar. "I need your help."

"No."

"You don't know what I need."

"People who climb six floors through windows carrying impossible rain
rarely need anything sensible."

She placed the jar on his map.

The blue drop inside split into two. Then four. Then
eight. Within seconds, hundreds of tiny drops speckled the glass.

They began to tremble.

Elias felt a pressure in his ears. The window rattled
in its frame.

Below, people shouted.

He hurried to the tower window.

Every puddle in Bellweather had begun to rise.

Water lifted from the streets in flat, shining sheets. Drops
flew upward from roofs. Gutters emptied into the sky. The
river shivered, and silver beads pulled free from its surface,
rising like sparks from a fire.

All across town, people stopped and stared as yesterday's rain
returned to the clouds.

Elias looked at Lina.

She had gone pale.

"That," she said, "is what I need help with."

By noon, the river had sunk three feet.

Mayor Thorne called an emergency meeting in the tower. He
arrived with the chief engineer, three councilors, the head gardener,
and Mrs. Quill from the bakery, who attended every emergency
meeting whether invited or not.

"The wells are dropping," said the engineer.

"My roses have been sucked dry," said the head gardener.

"My morning buns collapsed," said Mrs. Quill. "I cannot prove
it is related, but I know what I saw."

Mayor Thorne planted both hands on Elias's desk. He was
a broad man with a mustache like two sleeping squirrels.

"You are the Cartographer of Rain," he said. "Explain this."

"I map rain after it falls. I do not command
it."

"What are we paying you for, then?"

"Historical accuracy."

"This is no time for honesty."

Lina sat on the windowsill, swinging her legs. Elias had
tried twice to send her away. Twice, the rain in
her jar had begun rattling so violently that the tower
stones shook. For the moment, he had decided she should
remain.

One councilor pointed at her. "Who is that?"

"My assistant," Elias said.

"I thought you worked alone."

"So did I."

The compass needle continued to point west. Elias unfolded a
map of the lands beyond Bellweather. To the east lay
the hills and the sea. To the west stood a
region known as the Empty Country, though old maps showed
villages there.

No one had lived in the Empty Country for nearly
forty years.

Rain never fell there.

Elias marked a line across the paper.

"The water is traveling west," he said.

"Why?" asked the mayor.

"I don't know."

Everyone spoke at once.

Elias raised his voice. "But I can follow it."

The room quieted.

Mayor Thorne frowned. "You cannot leave. What if it rains
while you are gone?"

"At present, that seems unlikely."

Mrs. Quill nodded gravely. "He has you there."

Elias packed two blue pencils, a compass, measuring instruments, bread,
cheese, and his father's oldest map. Lina brought the jar.

They left Bellweather before sunset in a small wagon pulled
by a patient horse named Candle. Behind them, the river
continued to unravel into the sky.

The road west had not been repaired in decades. Grass
grew between its stones. As evening deepened, they passed abandoned
farms with broken fences and hollow barns. Wells stood open
like black mouths.

Overhead, water streamed through the sky in glittering ribbons, flowing
toward the Empty Country.

Elias made notes.

"Speed increasing. Altitude uncertain. Direction west by southwest."

"You write everything down?" Lina asked.

"Everything important."

"How do you know what's important?"

"Experience."

"What if experience is just a list of things that
have already happened?"

"That is precisely what experience is."

"Then how does it tell you about new things?"

Elias lowered his pencil.

Lina smiled.

He returned to his notes.

They camped beside an old milestone. The air was dry,
but thunder murmured somewhere beyond the horizon.

While Candle grazed, Elias studied the jar. The rain inside
drifted toward Lina's hand whenever she touched the glass.

"Tell me where you got it," he said.

Lina sat opposite him by the fire. "My village."

"In the Empty Country?"

She nodded.

"There are no villages there."

"There's one."

"It is not on any current map."

"That doesn't mean it isn't there."

Elias thought of the dozens of officials who had made
that same mistake in reverse, insisting that if something appeared
on a map, it must still exist.

"What is your village called?"

"Mercy."

He unrolled his father's oldest chart. The paper had yellowed,
and several edges were worn through. Near the center of
the Empty Country, faded letters marked a town named Mersey.

"Is this it?"

"You spelled it wrong."

"I did not make the map."

"Then someone else spelled it wrong."

Elias studied the faded blue lines surrounding the town. Forty
years ago, rivers had crossed that region. Lakes had dotted
the lowlands. Then the rainfall had shifted east, toward Bellweather,
and Mercy had disappeared from later maps.

"What happened there?"

Lina poked the fire with a stick.

"Long ago, it rained every day. That's what my grandmother
says. People grew tired of it. Clothes never dried. Houses
went green. Children were born knowing the sound of thunder
better than their mothers' voices."

"That seems unlikely."

"She says it anyway. Then a man came to the
village. A maker of clocks."

"Clocks?"

"He built a machine beneath the meeting hall. He said
it could save rain the way a clock saves time.
Whenever too much rain fell, the machine would keep the
extra for later."

Elias glanced at the streams moving across the stars.

"I assume it did not work."

"It worked too well. It took every storm that came.
After a while, the rain learned to avoid us. The
ponds dried. The fields turned to dust. Everyone left except
a few families who didn't have anywhere else to go."

"And the machine?"

"Still taking."

"Why has it begun taking Bellweather's rain now?"

Lina lifted the jar. Inside, the drops had gathered into
a tiny spinning cloud.

"Because I woke it."

In the morning, they continued west.

By afternoon, the land had changed. Trees stood bare beneath
the summer sun. Creeks were only scars in the soil.
The air smelled of stone and old roots.

Yet water crowded the sky above them.

It flowed westward in vast rivers, darkening the day. Fish-shaped
shadows swam within it. Lightning flickered silently in its depths.

Elias had never seen so much rain.

He felt the old shame return. Here was the greatest
weather event in Bellweather's history, and he remained dry. Not
a single drop touched his face.

He drew until his fingers cramped.

Near dusk, Candle refused to go farther.

Ahead stood the village of Mercy.

Its houses had sunk beneath dunes of dust. A church
leaned sideways. The meeting hall occupied the village square, its
clock tower frozen at twelve minutes past three.

A dozen people waited outside.

They were thin and sun-browned, from a white-haired woman with
a walking stick to a boy no older than five.
When Lina climbed from the wagon, the woman struck her
sharply across the cheek.

Then she pulled Lina into her arms.

"You foolish, impossible child," she whispered.

"I brought a cartographer," Lina said into her shoulder.

The woman looked at Elias. "Can he fix it?"

"No," Elias answered.

Lina pulled away. "You haven't seen the machine."

"I draw maps. I do not repair ancient weather machinery."

The villagers stared at him.

Elias sighed. "But I can look."

Beneath the meeting hall, they found a staircase spiraling into
darkness.

The air grew colder as Elias and Lina descended. Their
lantern cast weak light over pipes, gears, and copper tanks.
Some were green with age. Others shone as if polished
that morning.

Far below, something ticked.

They reached a circular chamber.

The machine filled it.

It resembled the skeleton of a gigantic clock wrapped around
a glass sphere. Hundreds of pipes entered the sphere from
above. Within it churned a storm—black clouds, blue lightning, and
endless rain compressed into a space no larger than a
cottage.

At the machine's base stood a narrow slot.

Lina drew a key from beneath her coat.

"I found this in my grandmother's cupboard," she said. "I
wanted to see if the stories were true."

"So you turned the machine on."

"I thought I was turning it off."

Elias approached the glass sphere. The storm inside pressed against
the surface, following him.

"What happened after you used the key?"

"It started ticking. Then the jar filled."

The machine gave a deep metallic knock.

Dust fell from the ceiling.

A needle on a nearby gauge crept toward a red
line.

Elias examined the engraved instructions beside the keyhole. Most had
been worn away, but one sentence remained:

WHAT IS SAVED MUST BE RETURNED BY THE HAND THAT
DREW IT FORTH.

"You have to turn the key again," he said.

"I tried. It won't move."

Together they forced the key into the lock. Elias gripped
Lina's hand, and they pulled.

Nothing happened.

The machine ticked faster.

In the sphere, lightning opened a white crack across the
storm.

Elias looked more closely at the keyhole. Around it were
dozens of tiny lines, not random scratches but marks shaped
like rivers, streets, and borders.

A map.

He recognized the style.

His grandmother's.

Elias took his father's old chart from his bag and
spread it on the floor. The faded blue lines around
Mercy matched the marks on the machine. Rivers flowed into
the keyhole. Rain spiraled outward.

At the bottom of the map, nearly erased by age,
were the initials A.V.

Ada Vale. The first Cartographer of Rain.

Elias suddenly remembered his father's stories of her: how she
had traveled west during the great floods; how she had
returned alone; how, after that journey, the rain had begun
falling strangely in Bellweather.

"She helped build this," he said.

Lina traced the map with one finger. "Can it tell
us how to stop it?"

"No. It tells us what she did."

The rain had not simply abandoned Mercy. Ada had redirected
it. The machine saved rain, and her map gave that
saved rain a path eastward. Bellweather's impossible storms—rain inside houses,
rain circling chimneys, rain turning corners—were not natural. They were
forty years of water struggling to follow an old cartographer's
lines.

Now the machine was calling it all back.

"What is saved must be returned," Elias murmured.

"The key won't turn," Lina said.

"Because the machine does not need a key. It needs
a new path."

He pulled out a blank sheet of paper.

For thirty-one years, Elias had mapped only the past. He
had insisted that maps should never command the world. A
river drawn where no river ran was a lie. A
road drawn through a mountain was vanity. A storm drawn
before it fell was worse than a forecast; it was
a dare.

But experience, Lina had said, was only a list of
things that had already happened.

The machine knocked again.

Above them, bells began ringing in the abandoned village, though
no one pulled their ropes.

Elias sharpened a blue pencil.

"What are you doing?" Lina asked.

"Something professionally irresponsible."

He drew Mercy first: the square, the church, the houses,
the buried wells. Then he drew the dry riverbed leading
east. He extended it across the Empty Country, but not
all the way to Bellweather. He added branches through farms
and valleys, ponds and forests, giving the water room to
spread.

The pencil moved faster.

The machine's ticking matched his strokes.

Yet when the map was nearly finished, he hesitated.

Every line led away from the glass sphere, but none
opened it.

"What's missing?" Lina asked.

"A beginning."

She held up the jar. The tiny storm inside spun
wildly.

"This rain found me," she said.

Elias understood.

Maps did not begin with roads, rivers, or borders. They
began with a person standing somewhere and saying, I am
here.

He placed the jar at the center of the paper.

The glass cracked.

One drop fell onto the map.

At once, every blue line shone.

The key turned by itself.

The machine stopped.

For a breath, the world held still.

Then the glass sphere burst.

Elias threw himself over Lina. He expected crushing water, shattered
glass, darkness.

Instead, a single raindrop struck the back of his hand.

He stared at it.

Another touched his neck.

Then another.

Rain passed through the chamber in a silver rush without
flooding it, flowing through stone as moonlight passes through a
window. It whirled around Elias and Lina, cold and bright,
lifting the map into the air.

For the first time in his life, Elias Vale stood
in rain.

It soaked his coat. It ran into his eyes. It
filled his shoes. He laughed once, unexpectedly, and then could
not stop.

Lina laughed too.

"You sound ridiculous," she shouted.

"I am aware."

They climbed from beneath the meeting hall into a storm.

Rain fell over Mercy.

It drummed on roofs, washed dust from windows, and poured
from the church gutters. The villagers stood in the square
with faces lifted. Some danced. Some wept. The white-haired woman
sat down in the mud and pressed both palms against
the wet earth.

Across the Empty Country, dry riverbeds filled. Wells swallowed water.
Seeds that had waited years in darkness began, cautiously, to
open.

The storm continued east, following Elias's map, but it no
longer traveled as one great flood. It divided over fields
and forests. It wandered into valleys. It paused above thirsty
orchards. By the time it reached Bellweather, three days later,
it arrived as a gentle evening shower.

Elias returned home to find the town unchanged except for
several wilted gardens, a cracked fountain, and Mrs. Quill's temper.

Mayor Thorne demanded a complete report.

Elias gave him a map instead.

It showed Bellweather in the east and Mercy in the
west, with rivers, roads, and wandering blue lines connecting them.

"What does it mean?" the mayor asked.

"It means our rain was borrowed."

"From whom?"

"People we forgot to put on newer maps."

Mayor Thorne looked uncomfortable, as officials often do when confronted
with a fact that is also an accusation.

Within a year, the western road was rebuilt.

Families returned to Mercy. They repaired the houses and planted
crops. They dismantled the weather machine piece by piece, though
Lina kept the key. Trade began between the two towns.
Bellweather sent glass, tools, and bakers. Mercy sent apples, river
clay, and stories that grew less accurate with every telling.

As for Elias, he remained the Cartographer of Rain, but
he changed the rules of his profession.

He still mapped storms after they passed. Historical accuracy, he
maintained, had its uses. But in the corner of every
map, he left a small blank space.

"For what?" visitors asked.

"For what has not happened yet," he said.

Lina became his apprentice, though she continued to enter the
tower by the window despite repeated objections. She drew reckless
maps—rivers that looped like ribbons, roads ending at unnamed doors,
clouds shaped like keys. Elias corrected her measurements but not
her imagination.

And rain no longer avoided him.

Sometimes it found him at inconvenient moments: halfway to the
market, on the tower stairs, or just after he had
hung his washing. Each time, he stopped whatever he was
doing and stood beneath it for a while.

Years later, people would say Elias Vale had learned to
predict the rain. This was not true.

He had learned something more useful.

He had learned that no map was innocent, that every
line remembered a choice, and that the empty places were
not always empty.

Most of all, he had learned that rain, like history,
eventually returns to wherever it has been taken from—and when
it does, the wisest thing anyone can do is open
the windows and let it in.

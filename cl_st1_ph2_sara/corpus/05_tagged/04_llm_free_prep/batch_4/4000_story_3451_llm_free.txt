## The Cartographer of Rain

In the town of Bellwether, rain was never allowed to
fall without a name.

A light spring shower might be called a Mercy, while
a sudden summer downpour was often recorded as a Quarrel.
Drizzle that lingered for days was a Grey Guest. If
rain came while the sun was shining, it was known
as a Fox's Wedding, though no one remembered why.

Every kind of rain had its own symbol, and every
symbol belonged on a map.

For forty-three years, Tomas Vale had served as Bellwether's official
Cartographer of Rain. From a narrow office above the clockmaker's
shop, he drew each storm in blue-black ink upon sheets
of waxed paper. He marked where it began, which streets
it crossed, how long it rested over the slate roofs,
and where the final drops disappeared into the earth.

His maps were precise enough to show that a Mercy
had touched the eastern wall of the bakery but spared
the ovens. They could prove that a Quarrel had flooded
the mayor's cellar while leaving the neighboring house completely dry.
They recorded the direction of wind, the height of clouds,
and the exact moment gutters began to sing.

No one in Bellwether knew what the maps were for.

That did not stop them from trusting Tomas.

Farmers consulted him before planting. Brides asked whether rain on
their wedding day would be fortunate. Children visited his office
to see storms transformed into rivers of ink. Even Mayor
Pike, who claimed to believe only in brick, law, and
compound interest, requested a forecast before every public speech.

Tomas always gave the same answer.

"Rain goes where it is needed."

People usually found this comforting.

Tomas himself did not.

Every morning, before opening his office, he climbed the steep
stairs to the roof and examined the horizon through a
brass telescope. Then he measured the water in six copper
cups arranged along the railing. He tested the wind with
a silk ribbon, checked the barometer, and wrote the date
in a red leather ledger.

The first sign of trouble appeared in late April, when
three days passed without rain.

Bellwether was not alarmed. Three dry days were a blessing
after a damp winter. Rugs appeared over balconies. Windows opened.
Children made chalk kingdoms in the square.

But three days became twelve.

Twelve became thirty.

By the end of May, the river had fallen low
enough to expose the rusted bell that gave the town
its name. It lay half-buried in mud near the western
bank, green with age, its broken mouth turned toward the
sky.

The oldest story in Bellwether claimed that the bell had
once hung in a tower on the hill. During a
storm, lightning struck the tower and sent the bell rolling
all the way into the river. When townspeople tried to
retrieve it, the bell rang beneath the water, and rain
began falling upward from the river into the clouds.

Tomas considered this nonsense.

Still, when he saw the bell emerge, he closed his
telescope more quickly than usual.

By June, the gardens had browned. Dust gathered in the
fountains. The fields outside town cracked into hard, uneven plates.

Tomas had never drawn a map of no rain.

He tried. He spread a fresh sheet across his desk
and stared at its blankness. Usually, even the smallest drizzle
gave him something to record: a curve, a cluster of
dots, a shaded border. But drought had no direction. It
had no footprint. It was an absence that covered everything.

At noon, someone knocked.

"Come in," Tomas called.

A girl entered carrying a yellow umbrella.

She was perhaps twelve years old, with a narrow face,
tangled black hair, and an expression of grave impatience. Dust
coated her boots. The umbrella was open, though there was
no rain and barely enough room beneath the sloping ceiling.

"You'll knock over the lamp," Tomas said.

The girl closed the umbrella.

"My name is Elianor Wren," she said. "I've come to
report a storm."

Tomas looked toward the window. The sky was blue and
merciless.

"Where?"

"In my bedroom."

He removed his spectacles.

"Your bedroom."

"It rains every night between midnight and one."

Tomas had received unusual reports before. Once, a widow insisted
that rain fell only on her late husband's chair. A
tavern keeper claimed he could hear thunder inside sealed wine
barrels. Such cases generally involved leaking pipes, mice, grief, or
too much brandy.

"Is there a hole in your roof?" he asked.

"No."

"A loose window?"

"No."

"Do you keep a washbasin on a high shelf?"

"No."

"Then perhaps you were dreaming."

Elianor leaned her umbrella against the desk. Its yellow cloth
was stained with dark circles.

"If I were dreaming, would I need this?"

Tomas touched one of the marks. It was damp.

He drew his hand back.

"Where do you live?"

"Number Eight, Candle Lane."

"I'll visit tomorrow morning."

"The rain comes at midnight," she said.

"I am rarely awake at midnight."

"Then tonight will be exciting for you."

She left the umbrella in his office and walked out.

At eleven thirty that night, Tomas stood before Number Eight,
Candle Lane, feeling foolish.

The house belonged to Elianor's aunt, Mrs. Wren, who taught
music and distrusted all municipal officers. She opened the door
only after Tomas showed her the mayor's seal on his
measuring case.

"I told Elianor not to bother you," she whispered as
she led him upstairs. "The child has a dramatic nature.
Her mother was the same."

"Where are her parents?"

"Gone."

Mrs. Wren said it in a way that made further
questions seem improper.

Elianor's bedroom was small and neat. A bed stood beneath
the window. Books were stacked against one wall, and paper
birds hung from the ceiling on threads. There were no
pipes above, no gaps in the roof, and no stains
on the plaster.

Elianor sat cross-legged on the bed.

"You're late," she said.

"It's three minutes to midnight."

"Storms don't wait for officials."

Tomas placed copper cups around the room. He set his
barometer on the windowsill and laid a sheet of waxed
paper across the floor.

The town clock began to strike.

On the twelfth note, a drop of water landed on
the paper.

Then another touched Tomas's cheek.

He looked up.

A cloud no larger than a dinner table had formed
against the ceiling.

Rain began to fall.

It was gentle at first. The drops descended in silver
threads, tapping the floorboards and whispering against the bedframe. Yet
nothing in the room became wet except the map paper
and the yellow umbrella when Elianor opened it.

Tomas stepped beneath the cloud. Water passed through his coat
without soaking the cloth. It felt cold on his skin,
with a faint taste of salt.

Not ordinary salt.

Tears.

He looked at Elianor.

She stared down at her hands.

"What do you dream about?" he asked.

"I told you it isn't a dream."

"What do you remember, then?"

Her face tightened.

"That isn't your business."

The rain grew heavier. The paper birds spun overhead. From
somewhere within the cloud came a low trembling sound—not thunder,
exactly, but the rumble of a cart crossing a distant
bridge.

Tomas knelt beside the map. Ink would not be necessary.
Where each drop struck, a blue mark appeared by itself.

The marks were not random.

They formed lines.

Roads.

He watched as the rain drew a coastline, a crescent
bay, and a chain of mountains. In the center appeared
a tiny circle around the outline of a house.

Tomas knew the place.

He had seen it on an old map in the
town archives: Lume Valley, a settlement north of Bellwether, abandoned
after a landslide seven years earlier.

"Elianor," he said carefully, "did you once live in Lume
Valley?"

The cloud vanished.

Every drop disappeared before reaching the floor.

Elianor's eyes were wide.

"My mother said we must never tell."

"Tell what?"

But the girl pulled the blanket over her head and
refused to speak.

The next morning, Tomas went to the archives beneath the
town hall. Dusty ledgers filled the shelves, along with tax
records, birth certificates, property deeds, and maps drawn by every
Cartographer of Rain before him.

He found the volume concerning Lume Valley.

The official account was brief. After weeks of heavy rain,
the northern mountain had collapsed. Mud buried fields and houses.
Forty-one people died. A handful escaped. The survivors scattered, and
the valley was declared unsafe.

Attached to the report was a rain map.

Tomas unfolded it and immediately saw something wrong.

According to the map, no rain had fallen on the
mountain during the month before the landslide.

He carried the record upstairs to Mayor Pike.

The mayor was a large man with silver whiskers and
a chain of office that strained across his waistcoat. He
listened while signing letters.

"An inaccurate map," he said. "Hardly a catastrophe."

"Rain maps are never inaccurate."

"Everything made by human hands is inaccurate."

"This one was altered. The original lines were scraped away."

Mayor Pike stopped writing.

"Tomas, the town has six weeks of grain remaining. Wells
are failing. Yesterday, two farms caught fire from a spark
off a plow. If you have come to discuss seven-year-old
paperwork, I suggest you return when the present has become
less demanding."

"A storm appeared in a child's bedroom."

"Then put a bucket under it."

"It drew Lume Valley."

The mayor's pen hovered over the page.

For a moment, Tomas saw recognition in his eyes.

Then Pike laughed.

Not well, but loudly.

"You spend too much time alone," he said.

"You know something."

"I know the town needs rain."

"So do I."

"Then find it."

Tomas left with the altered map beneath his arm.

That afternoon, Bellwether's council announced strict water rationing. Each household
received two jugs a day. Bathhouses closed. The fountain in
the square was dismantled so no one would be tempted
to steal from its empty pipes.

The drought continued.

At midnight, the cloud returned to Elianor's room.

This time Tomas was waiting.

The rain drew the same road north, but the map
extended farther. Beyond Lume Valley, it showed a narrow path
climbing into the mountains. At the top stood a black
shape like an eye.

When the rain stopped, Elianor spoke.

"My mother could call storms," she said.

Tomas did not interrupt.

"She never meant to. They came when she was sad
or frightened. In Lume Valley, people learned about it. At
first they were grateful. Farmers asked her for rain. Then
they asked for more. When she refused, they locked her
in the old meeting hall."

Elianor's voice remained steady, but the yellow umbrella trembled in
her hands.

"She cried for three weeks. It rained for three weeks.
The mountain broke."

"And you escaped?"

"My father got us out. We came to Bellwether. He
died the next winter. My mother went north two years
ago and never returned."

"Why did she go?"

"She said the rain remembered her."

Tomas looked at the map.

"Did she mention a place in the mountains?"

"The First Well."

He had heard the name only once, during his apprenticeship.
His teacher, Master Orra, had spoken of it while feverish.

Every drop of rain, she claimed, began at the First
Well—not as water, but as memory. The sky carried what
people could not bear to keep. Grief became drizzle. Anger
became storms. Joy rose as mist from summer fields.

Tomas had assumed the fever was talking.

Now Bellwether's sky was empty, while a grieving child rained
indoors.

"Your mother may still be alive," he said.

Elianor shook her head.

"You don't know that."

"No," Tomas admitted. "But I know where we must look."

They left before dawn.

Tomas carried instruments, maps, food, and three carefully sealed bottles
of water. Elianor brought the yellow umbrella and refused to
leave it behind, despite the cloudless sky.

The road north followed the shrinking river. By midday, they
reached Lume Valley.

Ruined chimneys rose from hardened mud. Roof beams lay tangled
among stones. Wild grass had once covered the wreckage, but
the drought had burned it pale.

Elianor stood where the road divided.

"Our house was there," she said, pointing to a low
mound.

Tomas waited while she searched through the dirt. At last
she found a blue glass marble. She cleaned it on
her sleeve and placed it in her pocket.

They climbed into the mountains.

The path shown on the rain map did not appear
on any official chart, but Elianor found it easily. It
wound between black pines and over ridges where the wind
smelled of dust. At sunset, they reached a wall of
stone marked by a circular opening.

Beyond it lay a staircase descending into darkness.

"The First Well," Elianor whispered.

Tomas lit a lantern.

The stairs led deep into the mountain. The air cooled
as they descended, and soon Tomas heard water—not flowing, but
breathing, as if an underground sea slept below.

The staircase ended at a vast cavern.

Thousands of glass vessels hung from the ceiling. Some were
tiny as perfume bottles; others were larger than houses. Each
held a moving storm.

Lightning flashed within one jar. Snow whirled in another. In
a green bottle, rain fell over a miniature forest. Every
vessel was connected by silver pipes that converged above a
stone well in the center of the cavern.

Beside the well stood Elianor's mother.

She was alive, though thin and grey-haired. Both hands gripped
an iron wheel attached to the largest pipe.

"Don't come closer," she said.

Elianor ran to her anyway.

For a long moment, mother and daughter held each other
without speaking.

Then the mountain groaned.

The wheel turned a fraction beneath Mrs. Wren's hands. Pipes
shuddered. Inside the hanging vessels, storms spun faster.

"What is this place?" Tomas asked.

"A lock," Elianor's mother said. "And a prison."

She explained that after the destruction of Lume Valley, she
had followed the pull of the rain to the mountain.
Here she discovered that the First Well had been sealed
many years before.

Bellwether's earliest mayors had feared floods, failed harvests, and unpredictable
storms. They built a machine to trap the rain's memories
underground, releasing only what the town required. The Cartographers' maps
had once been used to guide that release.

Over time, the purpose was forgotten. The machine weakened. Seven
years ago, a blockage sent too much rain toward Lume
Valley. Officials altered the records to hide their responsibility.

"Two years ago, the final valve began to fail," Mrs.
Wren said. "I came here to hold it shut. If
I release the wheel, every storm in these vessels will
escape at once."

"Why has Bellwether received no rain?" Tomas asked.

"Because Mayor Pike closed the northern gate. He thought it
would ease the pressure."

"It trapped everything."

"Yes."

Elianor touched the iron wheel. "Can't we open it slowly?"

"The controls are above the well. Someone must map a
path for the water."

Tomas looked at the silver pipes, then at his empty
paper.

For forty-three years, he had drawn rain only after it
fell. He recorded its choices, traced its wanderings, and pretended
that observation was the same as understanding.

Rain goes where it is needed, he had always said.

Perhaps that was not a comforting proverb.

Perhaps it was an instruction.

He unrolled his largest map of Bellwether across the cavern
floor.

Using blue-black ink, he drew a line from the First
Well through Lume Valley. He divided it around the ruined
houses and sent it into dry streams. He guided it
south to the farms, the orchards, and the town reservoir.
He marked the bakery, the square, Candle Lane, and the
roof above his office.

But the storms in the vessels remained wild.

"It isn't enough," Mrs. Wren said. "A true rain map
must carry a memory."

Tomas thought of all the years he had spent watching
clouds from his rooftop while life unfolded below. His wife
had died long ago, and he had returned to work
the next morning because grief seemed less dangerous when measured.
He had recorded the rain at her funeral as a
Grey Guest lasting two hours and seventeen minutes.

He had never written that he missed her.

Tomas dipped his pen again.

Beside the line leading into Bellwether, he wrote her name.

Mara.

A drop struck the map.

Then another.

Tomas looked up. A small cloud had formed beneath the
cavern roof.

His own rain.

It fell across the paper, carrying forty-three years of words
he had not spoken. The ink spread outward, filling rivers
and wells, branching through every street.

The iron wheel began to turn.

"Now!" Mrs. Wren cried.

Together, she and Elianor released it.

The vessels opened.

Storms poured through the silver pipes in ribbons of cloud.
The cavern roared with thunder. Wind lifted Tomas from his
feet and threw him against the well. The map tore
loose, rising into the air like a blue bird.

For one terrible instant, every storm rushed toward Bellwether at
once.

Then the rain found the lines.

It divided.

Some flowed into mountain streams. Some sank gently into Lume
Valley. Some crossed the fields in dark curtains, while the
fiercest storms turned east toward the empty plains.

The four of them—Tomas, Elianor, her mother, and the yellow
umbrella—climbed out of the mountain at dawn.

Clouds covered the sky.

Rain fell over Lume Valley, soft as breath.

By the time they reached Bellwether, the river was rising.
Townspeople danced barefoot in the square. Children opened their mouths
to the sky. Farmers laughed as water ran between the
cracked rows of their fields.

Mayor Pike waited beneath the town hall awning.

"This is too much rain," he said as Tomas approached.

"No," Tomas replied. "It is exactly as much as we
remember."

The altered records were made public. Mayor Pike resigned before
the council could remove him, though he continued to insist
that history was best kept dry and locked away.

Elianor and her mother moved into a small house near
the river. On most nights, no cloud appeared in Elianor's
bedroom. When one did, she opened the window.

Tomas returned to his office above the clockmaker's shop.

He still mapped every storm, but his maps changed. Alongside
measurements of wind and water, he recorded other things: the
names spoken beneath an awning, the songs heard through open
windows, the number of children who jumped in a particular
puddle.

A summer shower was no longer merely a Mercy. It
was the rain that ended a quarrel between two brothers.

An autumn drizzle became the rain in which Mrs. Wren
planted blue flowers beside her new home.

The first snowmelt was marked as the morning Elianor became
his apprentice.

Years later, when Tomas was too old to climb the
stairs to the roof, Elianor took his place as Cartographer
of Rain. Her maps were less precise than his, but
more truthful. She drew storms in bright colors and left
room in every corner for what could not yet be
understood.

Above her desk hung the yellow umbrella.

And whenever someone asked what the maps were for, Elianor
gave the answer Tomas had taught her—though she added a
sentence of her own.

"Rain goes where it is needed," she said. "The map
only helps us admit where that is."

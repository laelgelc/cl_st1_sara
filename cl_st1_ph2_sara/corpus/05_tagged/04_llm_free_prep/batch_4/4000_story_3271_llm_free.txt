# The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, before the fishing boats coughed awake
in the harbor, Elias Vale climbed the hill to the
clock house.

It stood above the village on four weathered pillars, a
narrow wooden tower with salt-stained windows and a copper roof
turned green by the sea. Inside, three hundred and sixty-five
clocks covered the walls. There were brass carriage clocks, painted
kitchen clocks, jeweled watches under glass, and long pendulum clocks
that leaned like elderly gentlemen listening to a secret.

None of them told the same time.

Elias's duty was not to correct them.

He wound each clock according to a black ledger left
by the keeper before him. One required seven turns every
Monday. Another needed winding only during rain. A tiny silver
watch was never touched except on the first frost of
winter. The largest clock, built into the northern wall, had
no hands at all.

The villagers believed the clocks governed the tides.

This was not true, but Elias never said so. People
preferred a useful mystery to a difficult fact.

The truth was that every clock held an hour someone
had lost.

Some hours had been wasted in railway stations or hospital
corridors. Some had vanished during sleepless nights. Others had been
lost when a person became so happy that time slipped
unnoticed through their fingers. These hours drifted from their owners
and gathered like mist. If no keeper caught them, they
settled over the world, making flowers bloom too early, bread
burn in ovens, and children grow old before their parents
were ready.

So the keepers collected them.

Elias had tended the clocks for forty-two years. In that
time, he had learned to recognize the character of a
lost hour by its sound.

An hour of boredom ticked heavily.

An hour of fear raced.

An hour of loneliness made almost no sound at all.

His favorite clock was a blue porcelain one painted with
swallows. It contained an hour lost by two lovers dancing
at a wedding in 1931. Whenever Elias wound it, the
tower smelled faintly of roses, candle wax, and summer rain.

His least favorite was a square iron clock that had
arrived after a mine collapsed in the northern mountains. It
held the hour during which fourteen families waited for news.
Its ticking was slow and hollow, and Elias always wound
it last.

He lived alone in a cottage beneath the hill. The
villagers brought him fish, lamp oil, and bread, but they
rarely visited the tower. They treated him politely, as people
do when they suspect a man knows the exact date
of their death.

Only one person came without fear.

Her name was Mara Bell, and she was eleven years
old when she first knocked on the clock-house door.

Elias opened it and found a thin girl with wind-tangled
hair holding a broken alarm clock.

"My mother says you repair clocks," she said.

"Your mother is mistaken."

"Can you repair this one anyway?"

"No."

Mara looked past him at the walls. The clocks clicked,
chimed, whispered, and sighed.

"Why do you need so many?"

"I am very bad at waking up."

She frowned. "That's a lie."

Elias was not accustomed to being accused so efficiently.

"Go home," he said.

Instead, she placed the alarm clock on the floor and
sat beside it.

"I can wait until you change your mind."

"I am seventy years old. Changing my mind may take
longer than you expect."

"I brought lunch."

From her coat she produced an apple, two biscuits, and
a wedge of cheese.

Elias closed the door.

An hour later, he opened it again. Mara was still
there, eating the second biscuit.

"You will not touch anything," he said.

"I promise."

"You will not ask questions."

"I can't promise that."

He should have sent her away. Instead, he stepped aside.

Mara entered the clock house with the reverence of someone
walking into a forest after snow. Her gaze moved from
clock to clock. She did not laugh at the painted
faces or the chains and counterweights. She listened.

"This one is sad," she said, standing before a plain
oak clock.

Elias stiffened.

Most people heard only ticking. "Why do you say that?"

"It sounds like my mother sewing after my father died."

The oak clock held an hour lost by a widow
who had sat mending her husband's coat before remembering he
would never wear it again.

Elias looked at Mara more carefully.

"Your alarm clock," he said. "Let me see it."

It was cheap, red, and dented on one corner. Nothing
in it was worth repairing. He repaired it anyway.

After that, Mara came every Saturday.

At first, Elias gave her harmless tasks. She polished the
glass cases and swept dust from beneath the cabinets. Then
he taught her how to oil a gear, reset a
pendulum, and listen for fatigue in a mainspring. By thirteen,
she could identify a clock's lost hour almost as quickly
as Elias. By fifteen, she had memorized half the black
ledger.

Yet there was one rule he repeated more often than
any other.

"A lost hour must never be returned."

"Why?" Mara asked.

"Because time heals around what is missing. Put the hour
back, and the wound opens again."

"What if it was a happy hour?"

"Especially then."

"That makes no sense."

"Few true things do at first."

Mara disliked the answer, but she obeyed.

Until the winter her mother became ill.

It began with a cough. Within a month, Mrs. Bell
could no longer walk to the harbor market. By February,
she rarely left her bed.

The village doctor came every evening and departed with his
mouth set in a hard line. Mara stopped visiting the
clock house. Elias saw the yellow light burning all night
in the Bells' window and knew what kind of waiting
filled the rooms there.

One morning, the doctor climbed the hill.

"She is asking for you," he told Elias.

Mrs. Bell looked small beneath the blankets. Mara sat beside
her, holding a cup neither of them touched.

When Elias entered, Mrs. Bell smiled.

"You never told me what you were teaching my daughter,"
she said.

"Clock repair."

"That is a lie."

"So she tells me."

Mrs. Bell's smile faded into a cough. When it passed,
she looked toward Mara.

"I would like to speak with him alone."

Mara hesitated, then left.

Mrs. Bell waited until the door closed.

"My daughter believes you can give me more time."

Elias pulled a chair closer. "I cannot."

"But there are hours in the tower."

"Not hours that belong to you."

"Would one make a difference?"

"Yes," he said. "And that is why I cannot give
it."

She studied him, perhaps deciding whether to hate him. At
last she nodded.

"Good."

Elias had expected pleading, anger, even bargaining. "Good?"

"Mara would steal one if she thought it could save
me. You must prevent her."

"I will."

"You care for her."

It was not a question.

Elias looked at his hands. Oil had settled permanently into
the lines of his fingers.

"I had a daughter once," he said.

Mrs. Bell waited.

"Her name was Livia. She drowned when she was nine.
I was already the keeper then. I searched the clocks
for an hour I could return to her. An hour
before she went to the shore. An hour in which
I might stop her."

"Did you find one?"

"Yes."

The word barely crossed his lips.

"What happened?"

"I was afraid."

"Of breaking the rules?"

"No. Of finding that even with another chance, I might
fail again."

The room was quiet except for the sea tapping frozen
fingers against the window.

Mrs. Bell reached from beneath the blanket and touched his
hand.

"Then don't fail Mara."

She died three nights later.

Mara did not cry at the funeral. She stood beside
the grave in a black coat while snow gathered on
her shoulders. When the villagers left, she remained until darkness
covered the churchyard.

That night, Elias woke before midnight with the certain knowledge
that something was wrong.

He climbed the hill without dressing properly, pulling his coat
over his nightshirt. The wind drove snow across the path.
Above him, a light moved behind the clock-house windows.

The door stood open.

Inside, Mara was turning the pages of the black ledger.

"Stop," Elias said.

She looked up. Her face was pale, her eyes fierce
from sleeplessness.

"Which clock holds the hour?"

"What hour?"

"The one when my mother was well."

"There may be thousands."

"You keep records."

"Not names."

"Then help me listen."

"She asked me to prevent this."

Mara froze. "You saw her?"

"She knew what you might try."

"She wanted to die?"

"She wanted you to live."

Mara slammed the ledger shut. Several clocks shuddered on their
hooks.

"What does that mean? Everyone says that as if living
is something I can do for her. It isn't. It's
only something I have to do without her."

Elias had no answer large enough for her grief.

Mara turned toward the clocks.

"I only need one hour."

"One hour becomes two. Then a day. Then a year.
Grief is hungry, Mara. It will eat every minute you
offer and ask for more."

"I don't want a year. I want to say goodbye."

"You did."

"No. I told her I would see her in the
morning. Then I went to sleep."

Her voice broke at last.

The clocks seemed to quiet around her.

Elias understood then that rules were often built from fear
and preserved by those who had forgotten its name. He
had told himself for decades that lost hours must remain
lost because time healed around absence. Yet the truth was
less noble: he had once stood where Mara stood, and
he had been too frightened to discover what mercy might
cost.

He walked to the northern wall.

The great clock without hands was taller than either of
them. Its wooden case was black with age, its face
blank except for a small keyhole at the center.

"What is that?" Mara whispered.

"The first clock."

He removed a chain from beneath his shirt. On it
hung a long brass key.

"The other clocks hold hours lost accidentally," he said. "This
one holds hours surrendered willingly."

He inserted the key.

"Why would anyone surrender an hour?"

"To give it to someone else."

The lock turned with a sound like ice breaking on
a lake.

Inside the case hung a pendulum of clear glass. Light
moved within it: gold, crimson, and blue, layered like sunsets.
Thousands of voices murmured together. Farewells. Confessions. Lullabies. Laughter.

Elias felt tears gather in his eyes.

"When a keeper dies," he said, "their final hour enters
this clock. It can be given to one person in
need. But there is a price."

"What price?"

"The hour must end. You cannot change what comes after
it. You cannot carry anything back except the memory."

"Will I see my mother?"

"Yes."

Mara stepped forward.

Elias caught her wrist.

"You must understand. The clock will offer you choices. It
may show you ways she could have lived. It will
ask whether you wish to remain. You must refuse."

"What happens if I don't?"

"You become a lost hour yourself."

She looked at the glass pendulum, then at him.

"Have you ever used it?"

"No."

"Because you were afraid?"

"Yes."

Mara nodded. "Then be afraid with me."

Elias placed her hand against the pendulum and laid his
own over it.

The tower vanished.

He stood on a summer beach under a clear blue
sky. Mara was beside him, no longer wearing her black
funeral coat but a yellow dress. A short distance away,
Mrs. Bell sat on a blanket, slicing apples.

Beyond her, near the water, a little girl searched for
shells.

Elias stopped breathing.

Livia looked exactly as she had on the final morning
of her life: bare feet, dark braids, red ribbon around
one wrist.

She lifted a shell toward him.

"Papa, look!"

He nearly went to her.

Then Mara gripped his hand.

"Is that your daughter?"

"Yes."

Livia ran closer. Sunlight flashed on the wet shell in
her palm.

"You can stay," said a voice from the waves.

It was not cruel. It sounded like every person Elias
had ever loved.

Mrs. Bell rose from the blanket.

"Mara," she called.

Mara's hand slipped from his.

Her mother opened her arms, and Mara ran into them.
They held each other in the bright afternoon while gulls
wheeled above the sea.

Elias knelt as Livia approached.

He touched her face. Warm. Real. Impossible.

"I waited for you," she said.

"I know."

"Are you staying?"

The lost hour expanded around him. He saw years unfolding
that had never happened. Livia at sixteen, teasing him for
his old-fashioned coat. Livia at twenty, leaving the village with
a suitcase. Livia returning with children of her own. Christmas
mornings, letters, quarrels, reconciliations. A whole life stood open like
a door.

All he had to do was enter.

Then, across the beach, he heard Mara crying.

"I'm sorry," Mara told her mother. "I should have stayed
awake. I should have known."

Mrs. Bell held her face between both hands.

"My darling, you were tired."

"But you were alone."

"No," her mother said. "I was loved."

The sky darkened at the edges. Their hour was ending.

The voice in the waves grew louder.

Stay.

Elias looked at Livia.

For forty-two years, he had imagined the words he would
say if given another chance. Now every one of them
seemed inadequate.

"I am sorry," he whispered.

Livia smiled. "I know."

"I should have watched you."

"I know."

"I have missed you every day."

"I know that too."

The light behind her became golden.

Elias pressed his forehead to hers.

"Did you suffer?"

"No."

Whether it was true or merely kind, he accepted it.

"Are you angry with me?"

Livia considered this with a child's seriousness.

"I was angry that you didn't come when I called.
But then I wasn't angry anymore."

He closed his eyes.

"I can stay."

"Yes."

He wanted to. The desire was greater than hunger, greater
than pain. Yet behind him was a cold clock house
full of fragile hours, and a grieving girl who had
asked him not to be fearless, only to be afraid
beside her.

Elias stood.

"No," he said.

The beach trembled.

Livia's smile did not vanish. Instead, it deepened with something
that looked almost like pride.

"Goodbye, Papa."

"Goodbye."

He turned from her as the sea rose without sound.

Mara clung to her mother.

"I don't want to go."

"You must," Mrs. Bell said.

"I can't do this without you."

"You have already done many things without me. You just
did not know you were practicing."

The water reached their feet, though the air remained warm.

"What if I forget you?" Mara asked.

"You will forget some things. My voice, perhaps. The exact
color of my eyes. That is not the same as
losing me."

Mrs. Bell kissed her forehead.

"Now wake up."

The beach shattered into a thousand ticking pieces.

Elias opened his eyes on the clock-house floor.

Dawn filled the windows. Mara lay beside him, still holding
his hand. The great clock stood open, but its glass
pendulum was dark.

For a long time, neither spoke.

Then Mara asked, "Was she real?"

"Yes."

"How do you know?"

"I don't."

She gave a tearful laugh. "That makes no sense."

"Few true things do at first."

Outside, the storm had passed. Snow covered the village roofs,
and the harbor shone under the rising sun.

Elias tried to stand, but his knees failed. Mara helped
him to a chair.

"You're very old," she said.

"I have been telling you that for years."

The clocks began to strike six, not together but one
after another, filling the tower with bells. As each rang,
its hands moved forward and stopped at the correct time.

Elias stared.

All around them, clock faces that had disagreed for decades
now showed the same hour.

"What's happening?" Mara asked.

"The surrendered hour has been spent."

"And the others?"

"Released."

The blue porcelain clock chimed. For one last moment, the
tower smelled of roses and summer rain. Then its ticking
ceased.

Across the walls, pendulums slowed. Watches fell silent. One by
one, the lost hours departed into the morning.

In the village below, a baker suddenly remembered the afternoon
his brother taught him to knead dough. An old woman
woke laughing from a dream of her first dance. A
fisherman paused on the pier, hearing in the gulls the
voice of a friend dead thirty years.

Time returned not as extra minutes, but as memory.

By sunrise, every clock in the tower was still.

Elias opened the black ledger. Its pages were blank.

"What will happen now?" Mara asked.

"I suppose people will have to keep their own hours."

"And us?"

He looked around the silent tower. Without its ticking, it
seemed smaller, merely a room full of old machines.

"We repair clocks," he said.

Mara smiled.

Years later, travelers passing through the village often climbed the
hill to visit the clock shop. They found an old
wooden tower with a green roof and a sign painted
above the door:

VALE & BELL
REPAIRS, RESTORATIONS, AND TIME ENOUGH

The shopkeeper was a woman with wind-tangled hair. She could
repair any clock brought to her, though she warned customers
that no mechanism could recover the past or delay the
future.

In the northern wall stood an empty clock case with
a blank face.

Beside it hung two photographs.

One showed a laughing woman slicing apples on a beach.

The other showed an old man beside a dark-haired child
holding a shell.

Customers sometimes asked who they were.

Mara always gave the same answer.

"They were people we could not keep," she said, "but
were lucky enough to have."

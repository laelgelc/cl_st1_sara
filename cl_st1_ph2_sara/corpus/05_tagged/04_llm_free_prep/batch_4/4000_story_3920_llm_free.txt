### The Cartographer of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, Mara Vale unlocked the narrow green
door beneath the railway bridge and entered the Department of
Missing Time.

The department occupied three underground rooms, none of which appeared
on official maps. Trains passed overhead every seven minutes, shaking
dust from the ceiling and setting the brass instruments trembling
on their shelves. There were clocks without hands, calendars full
of blank squares, and glass jars labeled with dates no
historian recognized.

Mara's job was to draw maps.

Not maps of countries or cities, but of time that
had gone missing.

Most losses were small. A baker stared out his window
and misplaced nine minutes. A child walking home from school
arrived before she remembered leaving. An entire audience emerged from
a concert unable to account for the silence between the
second and third movements.

Such incidents were reported through pale blue forms collected by
municipal clerks. The forms eventually reached Mara, who plotted each
disappearance in black ink on a long roll of paper.
Over weeks and months, patterns emerged: spirals around old churches,
clusters beneath hospitals, crooked rivers running through crowded markets.

"Lost time gathers," her supervisor, Mr. Pell, had told her
on her first day. "Just like rainwater."

Mr. Pell was a thin man with silver spectacles and
a voice so soft that people leaned toward him without
realizing it. He had worked at the department for forty-three
years. Some whispered that he had founded it, although he
looked no older than sixty.

"What happens when enough gathers in one place?" Mara had
asked.

Mr. Pell gave her a long, measuring look.

"Flooding."

That had been seven years ago.

Since then, Mara had mapped nearly eleven thousand missing hours
and never seen a flood.

Until the Tuesday when she lost one herself.

It happened at 8:13 in the morning.

She was sitting at her desk, shading a region near
Bellweather Square where twenty-three people had simultaneously forgotten a quarter
of an hour. Her pen touched the paper.

Then it was 9:14.

The pen was still in her hand, but the ink
had dried on its nib. Her tea had gone cold.
On the map before her, someone had drawn a perfect
circle around the northern district of the city.

Inside the circle was a single word.

HOME.

Mara stared at it.

The handwriting was hers.

She called for Mr. Pell, but his office was empty.
His coat hung behind the door, and his silver watch
lay open on the desk, ticking patiently.

Mara waited through the morning. At noon, she sent a
message to Central Records. At two, she telephoned his apartment
and heard only a dull mechanical hum. By closing time,
no one had seen him.

Before leaving, she returned to the circle on her map.

The northern district had once been a neighborhood called Saint
Orison, built around an ancient clock tower. Thirty years earlier,
a fire had destroyed most of it. The surviving buildings
were condemned, the streets sealed, and the district abandoned. On
modern maps, it was represented as a gray rectangle marked
MUNICIPAL STORAGE.

Mara knew that this was a lie. The city stored
nothing there.

She also knew something else.

She had been born in Saint Orison.

Her mother had died in the fire. Her father had
disappeared the same night. Mara, then four years old, had
been found alone at dawn on the southern side of
the river. She remembered almost nothing of the event—not the
flames, not the rescue, not even her parents' faces.

Only a clock striking thirteen.

At 6:07, after the last train rattled overhead, Mara took
the map, Mr. Pell's watch, and the ring of departmental
keys. Then she locked the green door behind her and
went north.

Saint Orison was separated from the rest of the city
by a rusted iron fence. Behind it stood rows of
blackened buildings, their windows blind with soot. Weeds rose through
cracks in the streets. The old clock tower leaned above
the rooftops, its four faces frozen at 8:13.

Mara found a gate beneath a sign warning of unstable
structures. It was secured by three locks. The smallest key
on Mr. Pell's ring opened them all.

Beyond the gate, the air felt strangely warm.

She walked past a burned school, a roofless chapel, and
a public fountain filled with dry leaves. Although the evening
sky remained bright, the shadows in Saint Orison were deep
and long. They seemed to point, all of them, toward
the clock tower.

At the center of the district, Mara reached a square.

A man sat beside the fountain.

He wore a gray suit and silver spectacles. His hands
rested neatly on his knees.

"Mr. Pell?"

He looked up. "You came sooner than I expected."

Mara hurried toward him, torn between relief and anger. "You
vanished. I contacted Records. I called your home."

"I'm sorry."

"You left your watch."

"I knew you would need it."

She held it out, but he did not take it.

"Why did I draw a circle around this place?" she
asked. "What happened during that hour?"

Mr. Pell sighed.

"You told yourself where to go."

"I don't remember doing it."

"No. You wouldn't."

A train sounded somewhere far away, though no railway ran
through Saint Orison.

Mr. Pell stood. He looked older than he had that
morning. Not by a year or two, but by decades.
His shoulders had sunk, and the skin around his eyes
had become paper-thin.

Mara stepped back.

"What is happening to you?"

"Time is catching up."

He led her across the square to the tower. Its
entrance was blocked by a heavy oak door that had
somehow survived the fire. Above it, carved into the stone,
were the words:

WHAT IS LOST IS NOT GONE.

Inside, the tower smelled of rain.

A spiral staircase wound upward around a hollow shaft. Gears
as tall as houses moved in the darkness below. Their
teeth turned without sound. Thin streams of golden light ran
between them like glowing threads.

Mara leaned over the railing.

"What is this place?"

"The reason your department exists," said Mr. Pell.

They climbed.

As they passed the second floor, Mara heard laughter behind
the walls. On the third, she heard someone weeping. On
the fourth came the roar of an unseen crowd. Each
landing had a door, and each door bore a date.

Some lay in the future.

"Every hour lost in the city comes here," Mr. Pell
explained. "Most arrive as fragments. A few seconds of distraction.
A minute of sleep. A memory abandoned because it hurt
too much to keep. The tower collects them."

"Why?"

"Because time cannot be destroyed. Only moved."

At the seventh landing, he stopped to catch his breath.

Mara looked up. The staircase seemed endless.

"Did you build the department?"

"No. Your father did."

The words struck with the force of a fall.

"My father was a watchmaker."

"He was that too."

"You knew him?"

"Very well."

Mr. Pell sat on the step. For a moment, the
years seemed to settle more heavily over him.

"Elias Vale discovered the tower when he was a young
man. In those days, Saint Orison was full of clockmakers,
bell founders, and repair shops. People thought the tower was
merely old. Your father learned otherwise. He learned how to
enter its machinery and guide the flow of lost time."

Mara gripped the railing.

"What happened the night of the fire?"

"Your father tried to stop a flood."

Mr. Pell looked down into the shaft.

"For centuries, the tower absorbed what the city misplaced. But
people began losing more and more. Factories stole sleep. Wars
erased years. Grief swallowed whole seasons. The tower filled faster
than it could empty. Your father predicted it would rupture."

"And the fire?"

"Was not a fire. Not at first."

He rose and continued climbing.

Mara followed, though every instinct urged her to leave. At
the ninth floor, they passed a door marked with the
date of her birth. A baby cried behind it.

At the tenth, she heard her mother singing.

Mara stopped.

The voice was faint, muffled by wood, but she knew
it. Not from memory, exactly. It was deeper than memory,
woven into some part of her that had never learned
language.

She reached for the handle.

Mr. Pell caught her wrist.

"Don't."

"My mother is in there."

"No. An hour she lost is in there. Perhaps an
afternoon. Perhaps a dream. It may feel like her, but
it cannot become more than it was."

Mara pulled away. "You expect me to walk past?"

"Yes."

"You've had no right to keep this from me."

"No," he said. "I haven't."

The singing continued. It was a lullaby without words.

Mara pressed her forehead against the door. For seven years
she had mapped strangers' vanished moments while her own past
waited inside a tower. She imagined opening the door and
seeing her mother alive, sunlight on her face, arms extended.

Then she noticed the warning scratched beneath the date.

AN HOUR ENTERED MUST BE AN HOUR LIVED.

"What does that mean?"

Mr. Pell's eyes lowered.

"You may enter a lost hour, but you cannot observe
it. You become part of it. The tower will give
the moment substance by taking time from the life you
have left."

"How much?"

"Sometimes a day. Sometimes twenty years."

The song ended.

Behind the door, a woman whispered, "Mara."

Mr. Pell began climbing again.

Mara stood frozen, her hand inches from the handle.

Then, from above, the clock struck once.

The tower shook.

A crack split the wall beside her. Golden light poured
through it, bright as morning. Within that light, Mara glimpsed
thousands of images: a sailor asleep at his post, a
woman dropping a glass, two brothers arguing in an alley,
an old man waking beneath an apple tree.

The crack widened.

"Flooding," Mr. Pell said.

He climbed faster.

Mara tore herself from the door and followed.

They reached the clock chamber as the second strike sounded.

Four great clock faces surrounded a circular room. Beyond their
dusty glass, Saint Orison had vanished. In its place, different
versions of the city flickered with every turn of the
gears: streets covered in snow, towers not yet built, bridges
collapsed, rivers running backward.

At the center stood a metal chair beneath a pendulum.

Straps hung from its arms.

Mara stopped at the top of the stairs.

"No."

Mr. Pell moved toward the chair.

"This is how your father stopped the first flood. Someone
must enter the regulator and guide the lost hours back
into the city."

"You said my father disappeared."

"He did."

"You mean he sat in that chair."

"Yes."

The clock struck a third time. More cracks opened across
the walls.

"Then he's dead."

Mr. Pell shook his head. "Death is a kind of
destination. Your father has none. He is scattered through the
hours he released."

Mara looked at the shifting city beyond the clock faces.
In one instant, the streets were empty. In the next,
crowds flowed across the square. Faces appeared and vanished too
quickly to recognize.

"Why did you bring me here?"

"Because the regulator is failing. Elias has held it for
thirty years, but he cannot hold it forever. The tower
needs someone who shares his sense of time."

"You mean his blood."

"Yes."

Mara laughed once, without humor. "So that is what the
department was for? You kept me underground, drawing maps until
I was useful?"

"I kept you there so you could understand the pattern."

Mr. Pell removed his spectacles. His eyes were wet.

"I was supposed to prepare you. Instead, I delayed. I
told myself you deserved an ordinary life, though I knew
ordinary lives are only stories we tell when time is
behaving."

The fourth strike came.

The floor tilted. One clock face burst outward, but instead
of falling, the glass fragments hovered in the air. Through
the open frame, Mara saw the city beneath a violet
sky. Winged shapes circled distant buildings.

Mr. Pell seized the chair.

"There is another way," he said. "Someone else may enter
the regulator, but without your connection to Elias, the tower
will consume them quickly. Minutes, perhaps."

"You?"

"I hoped your lost hour this morning would be enough
to convince you. I see now it wasn't."

"What happened during that hour?"

"You came here."

Mara stared at him.

"In one possible version of today," he continued, "you reached
the tower before me. You entered the regulator willingly. The
tower sent the memory backward as a warning, but your
mind could not contain it. All that remained was the
map."

"Then this has happened already?"

"No. Almost. Time is full of almost."

The fifth strike sounded.

From the staircase came a rush of voices. The doors
below had opened. Lost moments climbed the tower in a
storm of sound: unfinished conversations, forgotten names, last breaths, first
kisses, cries from battlefields, birthday songs, the private silence after
terrible news.

The chamber filled with translucent figures.

A boy chased a red ball between the gears. A
bride lifted her veil. A wounded soldier called for his
sister. Each appeared for a heartbeat and vanished.

Then Mara saw her mother.

She stood near the broken clock face, wearing a yellow
dress. Her dark hair moved in a wind Mara could
not feel. She held a four-year-old child in her arms.

Young Mara was crying.

Her mother whispered into the child's ear, then handed her
to a man beside the chair.

Elias Vale.

Mara knew him at once. He had her narrow hands
and serious eyes. Blood covered one side of his face.

Mr. Pell appeared too, younger by decades. He was shouting,
though the roar of the tower swallowed his words.

The memory unfolded around them.

Smoke poured up the stairs. Elias strapped himself into the
regulator. Mara's mother held the child close and ran toward
the exit, but the floor split. A river of golden
light rose through the chamber.

Time flooded outward.

For one terrible moment, Mara experienced everything at once.

She was four years old in her mother's arms. She
was thirty-four, standing beside Mr. Pell. She was unborn. She
was ancient. She was running through a burning square. She
was sitting underground, drawing a circle. She was opening a
green door on her first day of work.

Then the vision broke.

Her mother vanished.

The sixth strike began.

It did not end.

The sound deepened until the tower shook like a bell.
Mr. Pell stumbled toward the chair.

Mara caught him.

"If I take his place," she shouted, "what happens to
me?"

"You become the keeper."

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

"Can I release the hours?"

"Some. Not all. Open the tower completely, and the city
will drown in its past. People will live every moment
they have lost, all at once."

"Can I choose where the hours go?"

"If you learn the mechanism."

She thought of the department's maps: black rivers, spirals, clusters,
the secret geography of absence.

Not records, she realized.

Instructions.

Mr. Pell had not taught her to observe lost time.
He had taught her how to navigate it.

The endless sixth strike grew louder.

Mara pushed him away and sat in the chair.

The metal was warm.

Straps coiled around her wrists before she touched them. The
pendulum descended until its sharpened point rested above her heart.

Mr. Pell gripped the chair.

"You don't have to forgive me."

"I don't."

"I am sorry."

"I know."

The pendulum entered her without breaking skin.

The chamber disappeared.

Mara fell through a darkness filled with clocks.

She felt the city spread around her: millions of lives,
each leaving small absences behind. Forgotten mornings pooled in gutters.
Unnoticed seconds clung to railway platforms. Years of loneliness settled
beneath locked doors.

And among them, stretched impossibly thin, was her father.

She did not see his face. She felt his presence
in the rhythm of the tower, in the pause between
one second and the next.

Mara, he said.

Or perhaps the tower said it for him.

I'm here, she answered.

You shouldn't be.

Neither should you.

She reached toward him, not with her hands but with
memory. She found fragments: Elias repairing a silver watch; Elias
laughing as flour covered the kitchen floor; Elias holding his
newborn daughter; Elias entering the regulator while Saint Orison burned
around him.

For thirty years, he had fed his own life into
the mechanism one moment at a time.

Only a few minutes of him remained.

You have to let go, Mara told him.

I couldn't leave it to you.

You didn't. You left me the maps.

She felt his surprise, followed by something like pride.

The tower showed her the flood building behind its walls.
Centuries of lost time pressed against the present. She could
not destroy it. She could not contain it forever.

But perhaps containment had always been the mistake.

Lost time gathered because no one welcomed it back.

Mara searched the city's pattern.

She found a widower who would wake tomorrow unable to
remember his wife's voice. She sent him eight forgotten minutes
from their wedding morning.

She found a woman who had worked for twenty years
without watching a sunrise. Mara returned one to her in
a dream.

She found an old teacher who believed his life had
changed nothing. Into his sleep, she released a thousand brief
memories of former students speaking his name.

The pressure eased.

Not much, but enough.

The endless sixth strike faded.

Mara continued. She returned misplaced seconds carefully, matching each to
a space where it could be received. Not all hours
belonged to those who had lost them. Some grief was
too heavy to return to its owner. Some joy could
save a stranger.

Time, she understood, was not a road.

It was rain.

It rose from one life, crossed an unseen sky, and
fell into another.

As the tower steadied, Elias began to dissolve.

Wait, Mara said.

I am tired.

I have questions.

I know.

I don't remember you.

That was my choice. The flood touched you. To save
your mind, I gave the tower your memories of us.

Anger moved through her like lightning.

Give them back.

They will cost you.

Everything does.

For one final moment, she felt her father smile.

Then thirty lost years opened around her.

She remembered riding on his shoulders through Saint Orison. She
remembered her mother singing in the kitchen. She remembered hiding
beneath his workbench and collecting fallen springs. She remembered the
night of the flood—his bloody face, his arms around her,
his voice saying that no hour given in love was
ever truly lost.

The memories entered her, bright and painful.

Elias disappeared.

The tower became silent.

Mara opened her eyes.

She was still in the chair. Dawn shone through the
repaired clock faces. The pendulum had withdrawn, though a small
clock-shaped mark remained above her heart.

Mr. Pell sat on the floor beside her.

He looked very old.

"How long?" Mara asked.

"Here? Nine hours."

"And outside?"

He glanced through the glass.

"Three years."

Mara closed her eyes.

Three years. Gone in a night.

"What happened to the city?"

"People remembered things."

"Too much?"

"Enough."

He helped her from the chair.

Far below, Saint Orison was no longer empty. Lights shone
in windows that had been dark for decades. Figures moved
through the streets, uncertain and pale, as if waking from
a shared dream.

"Are they real?" Mara asked.

"Some are. Some may only be hours given shape."

"How do we tell the difference?"

Mr. Pell put on his spectacles.

"I suspect they will tell us."

They descended the tower.

At the tenth landing, Mara paused before her mother's door.
The warning remained carved beneath the date.

AN HOUR ENTERED MUST BE AN HOUR LIVED.

She touched the handle but did not turn it.

Not yet.

Outside, the city's bells began to ring. None agreed on
the hour.

People gathered beyond the iron fence, staring into Saint Orison.
Some called out names. Others covered their mouths in wonder
as figures approached from the square.

Mr. Pell stood beside Mara beneath the leaning tower.

"What will you do now?" he asked.

She looked south, toward the railway bridge and the narrow
green door beneath it. The department would need new maps.
Different maps. Not merely of where time vanished, but where
it might be returned.

"I'm going to work," she said.

Years later, citizens would speak of the morning Saint Orison
came back.

Some claimed the district had been rebuilt overnight. Others insisted
it had never burned. A few remembered both versions and
learned to live with the contradiction.

The Department of Missing Time eventually moved above ground. Its
new offices had wide windows and clocks with hands. Anyone
could enter and report an absence.

Mara still arrived at six.

She drew maps in black ink, tracing the hidden rivers
that flowed between one life and another. On difficult days,
she opened her father's silver watch and listened to its
patient ticking.

She grew older, though not always at the usual speed.

Sometimes, while walking home, she passed a stranger who carried
a memory she recognized: sunlight on a kitchen table, the
smell of machine oil, a woman singing without words. Mara
never asked for those moments back.

She had learned that time was not precious because it
could be kept.

Time was precious because it could be given.

And beneath the clocks and streets, beneath every hurried morning
and sleepless night, the lost hours moved like deep water—waiting
not to be found, but to find their way home.

The Last Lighthouse Keeper

On the morning the sea disappeared, Mara Venn was polishing
the lighthouse lens.

She noticed the silence first.

For thirty-two years, the sea had struck the black cliffs
below Saint Orra with the persistence of a fist on
a locked door. Even on calm days, water hissed among
the rocks, gulls complained over the harbor, and the wind
carried the deep, slow breathing of the tide through every
crack in the lighthouse walls.

That morning, the world held its breath.

Mara set down her cloth and climbed the iron stairs
to the gallery. Dawn had only begun to color the
horizon, but she could already see what had happened.

The sea was gone.

Where yesterday there had been miles of gray water, there
now stretched a plain of glistening mud. Fish twisted in
shallow pools. Ropes of seaweed lay across the exposed earth
like veins. Far in the distance, perhaps twenty miles away,
a narrow silver line trembled beneath the rising sun.

Mara gripped the railing.

In the harbor below, boats rested on their sides. Bells
began to ring in the village.

She hurried downstairs and found Elias Ward waiting outside the
lighthouse door. He was fourteen, thin as a reed, with
a patched blue coat and an untamable storm of black
hair. Since his father had vanished in a winter gale,
Elias had come to the lighthouse each morning to help
with the lamps.

"Where did it go?" he asked.

Mara looked again at the empty horizon.

"I don't know."

"Will it come back?"

"I don't know that either."

Elias watched her carefully. The adults in Saint Orra had
a habit of giving children false answers wrapped in comforting
voices. Mara had never done that, which was why he
trusted her.

From the village came another burst of bells.

"We should go down," she said.

Everyone had gathered in the square by the time Mara
and Elias arrived. Fishermen stood beside merchants, sailors beside farmers
from the hills. Children climbed the fountain to see over
the crowd. At the church steps, Mayor Vale waved a
telegram above his head.

"The withdrawal has been observed along the entire eastern coast,"
he announced. "Perhaps farther. The Royal Office advises everyone to
remain calm and await instructions."

A fisherman named Dorrin laughed bitterly.

"Instructions for what? Planting potatoes on the seabed?"

Several people shouted questions. Had there been an earthquake? Was
it a sign of war? Had the moon changed its
path? The mayor had no answers.

Old Mother Sen, who sold herbs and charms near the
quay, pushed to the front.

"It is the lighthouse," she said.

The crowd quieted.

Mara frowned. "What about it?"

Mother Sen pointed a crooked finger toward the white tower
on the cliff.

"The old light has failed."

"The light was burning all night," Elias protested. "I trimmed
the wicks myself."

"Not that light," said Mother Sen. "The first light."

A murmur passed through the square.

Every child in Saint Orra knew the legend. Long before
the lighthouse had been built, before the kingdom had a
name, a star had fallen into the sea beside the
island. The people found it burning beneath the waves and
carried one piece of its fire to the highest cliff.
As long as the star-flame shone, the story said, the
sea would remember its proper place.

It was a tale told during storms, when windows rattled
and nervous children needed to believe that someone, somewhere, was
keeping the world in order.

Mayor Vale raised both hands.

"We will not govern by nursery stories."

"Nursery stories outlive governments," Mother Sen replied.

Mara left before the argument ended.

Back at the lighthouse, she unlocked a narrow storeroom beneath
the stairs. The room smelled of oil, salt, and damp
stone. Shelves held rusted tools, cracked lanterns, and journals written
by generations of keepers.

Mara had read them all—or thought she had.

She began removing boxes from the lowest shelf. Elias joined
her without being asked. After an hour, they uncovered a
small iron door set into the wall behind a cabinet.

There was no keyhole.

At its center was the shape of a seven-pointed star.

Mara touched it. The iron was warm.

"My father once said there were stairs under the lighthouse,"
Elias whispered.

"Your father said many things."

"He was right sometimes."

"Yes," Mara said. "He was."

She pressed her palm against the star.

Something clicked inside the wall.

The door opened.

Beyond it, steps descended into darkness.

They took two lanterns and followed the staircase beneath the
lighthouse. The walls narrowed as they went down, and the
stone changed from rough gray blocks to smooth black glass.
After seventy-three steps, they reached a round chamber.

At its center stood a pedestal. Resting upon it was
a brass lantern no larger than Mara's hand.

Inside the lantern lay a pinch of pale ash.

Elias stared at it. "The first light."

"Or what remains of it."

Words had been carved around the base of the pedestal.
Mara wiped away the dust and read aloud:

"When the sea forgets, carry the sleeping flame to the
place of falling. Give what was taken. Speak what was
lost."

Elias looked toward the ceiling, as if he could see
the empty seabed above them.

"The place of falling must be where the star landed."

"If the legend is true."

"And where is that?"

Mara lifted the lantern. Beneath it lay a map engraved
into the brass pedestal. It showed Saint Orra, the surrounding
coast, and a line extending eastward into the sea. At
the line's end was a seven-pointed star.

By noon, the villagers had begun walking across the seabed.

At first they moved cautiously. Then curiosity overcame fear. People
collected stranded crabs in baskets, cut anchors from ancient wrecks,
and pried coins from the mud. Children chased silver fish
through shallow pools. Men spoke excitedly of new roads, new
farmland, and treasures lost over centuries.

Mara stood on the quay with the brass lantern beneath
her coat.

"We need a cart," she told Mayor Vale.

When she explained what they had found, he rubbed both
hands over his face.

"You intend to walk twenty miles into what was, until
this morning, an ocean?"

"According to the map, the falling place is closer to
thirty."

"Even better."

"If the sea returns—"

"Exactly. If the sea returns, you drown."

"And if it doesn't?"

They both looked across the vast plain. Already, fish lay
dying by the thousands. The smell of salt and rot
thickened in the warming air.

The mayor lowered his voice.

"The Royal Office has ordered us to stay inland. They
believe the withdrawal may precede a great wave."

"Then evacuate the village."

"And you?"

"I'm going to return the flame."

"There is no flame."

"Then I'll return the ash."

Mayor Vale studied her. Mara had known him since childhood,
when they had stolen apples from the same orchard and
blamed the same goat. At last he took a ring
of keys from his pocket.

"Use my mule," he said. "She bites, but only people
she dislikes."

The mule disliked both Mara and Elias.

By midafternoon, they were traveling east across the exposed seabed
with the brass lantern tied securely inside the cart. The
map showed no obstacles, but the drowned landscape was difficult
to cross. Channels cut through the mud. Forests of kelp
tangled around the wheels. Twice, they passed shipwrecks, their broken
ribs rising from the earth.

Elias was fascinated by everything. He collected a blue shell,
a brass button, and a water-smoothed piece of green glass.
Mara let him keep the shell and glass but made
him return the button to the skeleton wearing it.

Toward evening, they found a road.

It ran north to south beneath a layer of silt:
fitted stones, pale and almost perfectly preserved. On either side
stood the remains of buildings. Roofs had collapsed long ago,
but walls and doorways remained.

"A town," Elias said.

Mara consulted the map. "It shouldn't be here."

They left the mule beside a stone arch and explored
on foot. Small fish flickered in flooded cellars. Coral covered
statues in a square. At the center stood a clock
tower whose hands still pointed to eleven minutes past two.

Elias wandered into a house. Mara followed and found him
looking at a wall of faded blue tiles. They showed
people carrying a seven-pointed flame from the sea. In the
next image, the people placed the flame in a tower.
In the last, the water rose and covered their city.

"They took it," Elias said. "The star-flame."

"So the sea took them."

"Was that the bargain?"

Before Mara could answer, a sound rolled across the seabed.

It was so low she felt it through her boots.

The mule screamed outside.

Mara ran into the square. On the eastern horizon, the
silver line had grown darker. Above it, birds wheeled in
enormous clouds.

"The water," Elias said.

"It's returning."

They climbed back into the cart and urged the mule
onward.

Night fell. The lighthouse beam swept over the distant cliffs
behind them, a white finger turning through the darkness. Mara
had left the ordinary lamp in the care of her
assistant, but from the seabed it looked weak and terribly
far away.

The brass lantern began to glow.

At first Mara thought it was reflecting moonlight. Then she
saw a dull red spark beneath the ash.

Elias saw it too.

"It's waking."

"Or warning us."

The earth shook again.

Water began rising through the mud around the cart wheels.
Not flowing from the east, Mara noticed, but welling up
from below. Pools joined into streams. Streams spread into a
shining sheet.

The mule stumbled.

Ahead, something enormous emerged from the darkness: a ring of
black stone half buried in the seabed. Seven pillars leaned
inward around a central hollow.

"The falling place," Elias said.

They abandoned the cart and ran.

By the time they reached the stones, water covered their
boots. Mara placed the lantern in the hollow. Its ember
brightened, but no flame appeared.

"Give what was taken," she said. "Speak what was lost."

Elias turned in a circle, searching the dark. "Give what?"

"I don't know."

Another tremor knocked him to his knees.

Mara stared at the lantern. The people of the drowned
city had taken fire from a fallen star. They had
carried it to land, and the sea had covered their
streets. Later, somehow, the flame had reached Saint Orra.

A stolen light passed from one shore to another.

"Give what was taken," she repeated.

She thought of every lighthouse keeper before her, polishing the
lens, trimming the wicks, guarding a flame they believed belonged
to them. She thought of ships guided home and sailors
saved from reefs. She thought of Elias's father, whose vessel
had never returned.

A light could guide. A light could warn.

A light could also call something toward it.

"What was lost?" Elias asked.

Mara looked west. The lighthouse beam crossed the night again.

"Names," she said.

"What?"

"The sea takes names. We say 'the drowned' or 'the
missing,' and after a while even their faces fade."

She reached into her coat and removed the keeper's book
she had carried from the tower. Its pages listed every
wreck recorded near Saint Orra for nearly two hundred years.

Mara opened it.

"Jon Aren," she read. "Lysa Bell. Corren Bell. Tomas Vey.
Anna Horne."

The ember flared.

Elias understood. He knelt beside her.

"Peter Ward," he said.

His father's name.

The water reached their knees.

Mara continued reading. She spoke the names of captains, deckhands,
fishermen, passengers, and children. Some had been buried in the
churchyard. Most had no graves. With each name, the light
grew stronger.

The wind rose. From the east came a roar like
mountains breaking.

A wall of water had appeared beneath the stars.

It was still miles away, but it crossed the seabed
with impossible speed, swallowing roads, wrecks, and the drowned town.

Mara turned the pages faster.

"Edric Shaw. Mina Shaw. Abel Tern. Violet Grey."

The water climbed to their waists.

"There are too many!" Elias shouted.

"Then help me!"

They read together, voices breaking, while the wave raced toward
them. The brass lantern burned white now, though the ash
inside remained untouched.

At the final page, Mara found one name written alone.

Neris Venn.

Her younger sister.

Forty years ago, when Mara was eleven and Neris was
eight, they had gone walking on the rocks below the
cliff. Mara had dared her sister to cross a narrow
ledge. A wave came. Neris slipped. Her body was never
found.

Mara had become a lighthouse keeper because of that day.
She had spent her life warning others away from rocks
she could not forget.

Her mouth refused to shape the name.

The wave was close enough now to block half the
sky.

Elias seized her hand.

Mara closed her eyes.

"Neris Venn," she whispered.

The lantern opened.

It did not break. The brass unfolded like the petals
of a flower, releasing a spear of white fire. Light
shot upward, brighter than noon. For an instant, Mara saw
the entire seabed: the ancient city, the scattered ships, the
countless bones resting beneath the mud.

And among them stood figures made of pale water.

Thousands upon thousands.

Neris was nearest.

She looked eight years old. Her hair drifted around her
face. She smiled, not with forgiveness, but with something gentler
and more difficult: recognition.

Then the wave struck.

Mara felt Elias's hand torn from hers. There was thunder,
cold, and a darkness filled with turning stars.

When she opened her eyes, she was lying on the
gallery of the lighthouse.

Rain fell softly.

Elias lay beside her, coughing seawater. The brass lantern rested
between them, dark and empty.

Below, waves broke against the cliffs.

The harbor was full again. Boats floated at their moorings,
some damaged, none lost. Across the village, people emerged from
houses and shelters. One by one, bells began to ring.

Elias rolled onto his back.

"How did we get here?"

Mara looked toward the eastern horizon.

"I don't know."

"Did we save the sea?"

She considered the question.

"No," she said. "I think the sea saved us."

In the weeks that followed, officials arrived from the capital.
They measured tides, questioned witnesses, and wrote reports full of
careful words that explained nothing. The road and the drowned
city vanished beneath the water once more. The Royal Office
declared the event an unusual geological disturbance.

Mayor Vale had a copy of the declaration framed and
hung in the town hall because, he said, nonsense deserved
a proper display.

Mara returned to her duties. Every evening she lit the
great lamp. Every morning she polished the lens.

But one thing changed.

Beside the lighthouse, the villagers raised a long wall of
black stone. Upon it they carved the names from the
keeper's book. Families added others: sailors lost far from Saint
Orra, children taken by currents, travelers who had disappeared between
one shore and another.

Elias carved Peter Ward himself.

Mara carved Neris.

Years later, when Mara's hands became too stiff to manage
the lamps, Elias took her place as keeper. He grew
tall, though his hair remained untamable. Ships came to know
the light of Saint Orra as the brightest on the
eastern coast.

Sailors also told another story.

On certain nights, especially when fog covered the water and
the stars were hidden, a second light appeared far below
the surface. It moved through the deep like a lantern
carried along a sunken road. No chart marked its course,
but vessels that followed it always found safe harbor.

Mara saw it once before she died.

She was sitting outside the lighthouse at dusk, wrapped in
a blue coat Elias had given her. The sea was
calm. The memorial stones shone with rain.

Far beyond the cliffs, a white flame opened beneath the
waves.

For a moment, the water became clear as glass, and
Mara glimpsed towers, streets, and figures walking through a city
untouched by time. At the end of a long road
stood a little girl with drifting hair.

Mara raised one hand.

The girl raised hers.

Then the sea folded over the light.

Night came, and the lighthouse began its slow, faithful turning.

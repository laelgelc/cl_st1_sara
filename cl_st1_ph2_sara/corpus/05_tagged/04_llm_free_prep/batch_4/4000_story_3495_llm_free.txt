# The Keeper of Lost Hours

On the morning the clocks began to vanish, Mara Vale
found a minute trembling beneath her kitchen table.

It was a small thing, no larger than a silver
coin, though not quite round. Its edges fluttered like moth
wings, and when she touched it, she heard the faint
echo of rain against a window.

Mara knew at once what it was.

She carried the minute upstairs to her workshop and placed
it inside a glass jar. The jar already contained seventeen
lost seconds, two afternoons, and the final hour of a
summer that no one in Bellweather could remember.

The minute settled at the bottom. Rain whispered against invisible
glass.

"Where did you come from?" Mara asked.

The minute did not answer. Time rarely did.

From her window, she saw people gathering in the square.
They stood beneath the clock tower with their faces raised.
The tower's four great clock faces had disappeared, leaving only
pale circles in the brick, like the eyes of something
that had gone blind.

Soon there came a pounding on Mara's door.

Mayor Finch stood outside, red-faced and breathless. He wore his
ceremonial coat over his nightshirt.

"You've seen it?" he demanded.

"I've seen."

"The clocks are gone."

"So I gathered."

"All of them," he said. "Pocket watches, kitchen clocks, the
sundial outside the school—even old Mrs. Fen's hourglass. Nothing left
but the sand."

Mara glanced past him. The square was filling with frightened
townspeople. Bakers clutched unfinished loaves. Children wandered in their bedclothes.
The stationmaster held his empty watch chain as if it
were a severed vein.

"And you came to me," Mara said.

"You're the clockmaker."

"I repair clocks."

"You also keep…" The mayor lowered his voice. "Other things."

Mara's gaze sharpened.

Everyone in Bellweather knew her as a clockmaker. Only a
few knew that people sometimes lost more than objects. They
misplaced moments, conversations, birthdays, winters. Mara found such pieces of
time where they had fallen and kept them safe in
her workshop.

"I don't steal them," she said.

"I didn't say you did."

"You were about to."

Mayor Finch removed his hat. "Please. Without clocks, the town
is already coming apart."

Behind him, a woman shouted that the bakery had opened
too early. The baker shouted back that perhaps she had
arrived too late. Somewhere, a church bell rang thirteen times,
paused, and rang twice more.

Mara sighed.

"Show me the first place a clock disappeared."

The mayor led her to the station.

The station clock had hung above the ticket window for
sixty-three years. Now only a darker circle marked its place
on the wall. Beneath it stood Stationmaster Pell, twisting his
cap.

"It was there before dawn," Pell said. "I looked up,
and it said ten minutes to six. Then the morning
train arrived."

"The train arrives at six fifteen," Mara said.

"Exactly. When I looked back, the clock was gone."

"Did the train driver notice anything?"

"He never arrived."

Mara turned. "You said the train arrived."

"It did. Empty."

The engine waited beside the platform, breathing steam. Its metal
was wet with dew, but not a single passenger sat
inside. No driver, no conductor, no luggage. On the side
of the engine, where its brass identification plate should have
been, there was only a blank rectangle.

Mara climbed aboard.

Every seat was cold. She walked through the carriages until
she reached the driver's cabin. On the floor lay another
lost minute, fluttering weakly.

She picked it up.

This one carried the sound of a man laughing.

Mara placed it in a tin box and listened carefully.
Beneath the laughter was the rhythmic clatter of wheels and,
farther away, a low tolling bell.

She knew that bell.

It belonged to Saint Orra's chapel, abandoned forty years earlier
on the northern hill.

"I need to go home," she said.

Mayor Finch hurried after her. "You've solved it?"

"No."

"But you know something."

"I know where to begin."

Mara's workshop occupied the top floor of her narrow house.
Clocks covered every wall, though none showed the same hour.
Some ran backward. Some had no hands. One marked only
moments of regret and ticked so loudly that Mara kept
it beneath a blanket.

At the center of the room stood a tall black
cabinet.

Mayor Finch watched as she unlocked it.

Inside were shelves of bottles, boxes, and paper packets, each
carefully labeled.

A Tuesday Misplaced by Arthur Bell.

Three Seconds Before a Kiss.

Half an Hour Lost in the Winter of 1888.

The Last Morning of Elian Vale.

The mayor read the final label. "Your father?"

Mara shut the door slightly, blocking his view.

"What do you know about Saint Orra's bell?" she asked.

"Only stories."

"Tell me."

He hesitated. "They say it rang during the Fever Year.
No one was there to ring it, but every midnight
it tolled once. People began losing track of the days.
Some slept for a week. Others aged a decade overnight."

"And?"

"And your father stopped it."

Mara looked toward the covered clock in the corner.

Her father had vanished twenty years ago, when she was
twelve. The town said he had died. Mara knew only
that, on the morning after his disappearance, she had found
a sealed bottle on his workbench.

Inside the bottle was his final hour.

She had never opened it.

"My father didn't stop the bell," she said. "He borrowed
its voice."

She removed the blanket from the clock.

It was a crude object, built from black wood and
tarnished copper. Instead of numbers, its face bore twelve names.
One of them was Orra.

The single hand pointed to that name.

Mayor Finch stepped back. "What is it?"

"A debt clock. My father made it. It measures what
time is owed."

"Owed to whom?"

"To whatever lives beneath the chapel."

The mayor laughed nervously. "Nothing lives beneath the chapel."

The debt clock ticked.

For one instant, Mayor Finch became an old man. His
hair whitened, his shoulders bent, and his cheeks hollowed. Then
he was himself again.

He stopped laughing.

Mara took a leather satchel and filled it with tools.
At the bottom she placed the bottle labeled The Last
Morning of Elian Vale.

"You're coming," she told the mayor.

"Why?"

"Because your grandfather was mayor during the Fever Year."

"What does that have to do with me?"

"Debts are patient. They don't care who inherits them."

Outside, Bellweather had fallen into confusion.

With no clocks, people searched the sky for answers, but
clouds hid the sun. Shopkeepers argued over opening hours. Teachers
dismissed their students and called them back moments later. At
the station, the empty train departed without warning and rolled
south into the mist.

As Mara and Mayor Finch climbed the northern road, they
passed more fragments of lost time.

A second clung to a thornbush, filled with the smell
of burnt toast.

An hour drifted in a pond like an oil stain.

By the old cemetery, they found a whole year curled
beneath an oak. It was pale and sleeping, its surface
covered with images of weddings, funerals, harvests, and snow.

"Whose year is that?" the mayor whispered.

"Everyone's, perhaps."

"Can you keep it safe?"

"Not in any jar I own."

They continued uphill.

Saint Orra's chapel stood at the summit, surrounded by dead
grass. Its roof had collapsed, but the bell tower remained.
A bell hung in the open air, green with age.

As Mara approached, it began to swing.

No sound came.

The silent motion made her teeth ache.

Mayor Finch gripped her arm. "Look."

Behind the chapel, where the hill sloped toward Bellweather, the
town was changing.

Houses flickered between new and ruined. Trees grew tall, shed
their leaves, and became saplings again. People moved through the
streets as infants, adults, and ghosts. The river flooded and
dried in the space of a breath.

Time was not merely disappearing.

It was coming loose.

Mara entered the chapel.

The stone floor had cracked down the center, revealing a
spiral staircase. A cold wind rose from below. It carried
thousands of sounds: lullabies, arguments, clocks ticking, doors closing, and
voices saying words they wished they could take back.

On the wall beside the stairs, someone had carved a
message.

MARA, DO NOT FOLLOW.

She recognized her father's handwriting.

Mayor Finch saw it too. "Perhaps we should listen."

"For twenty years, I have."

She lit a lantern and descended.

The staircase wound deep beneath the hill. The farther they
went, the stranger the air became. Mara saw moments drifting
through the darkness like leaves in water. A boy dropped
a red ball. A soldier kissed a letter. A woman
closed a book and never opened it again.

At the foot of the stairs lay a vast chamber.

In its center stood a clock.

It was taller than a house, built from stone, bone,
iron, and roots. Its pendulum hung motionless. Around its face
were not twelve numbers, but thousands, each glowing faintly.

At the base of the clock sat a man.

He looked no older than forty. His hair was dark,
his shirtsleeves rolled to the elbows. He held a small
brass tool in one hand.

Mara's lantern slipped from her fingers.

"Father?"

Elian Vale looked up.

For a moment, joy transformed his face. Then fear replaced
it.

"You shouldn't be here."

Mara crossed the chamber and struck him across the cheek.

He blinked.

"That," she said, "was for the message."

Then she embraced him.

He held her tightly, but only for a second. When
he pulled away, his eyes shone.

"How long?" he asked.

"Twenty years."

He closed his eyes. "I thought it might be two
or three."

Mayor Finch approached cautiously. "Mr. Vale, Bellweather is losing its
clocks."

"Not its clocks," Elian said. "Its claim on time."

He pointed to the great mechanism.

"This is the Orra Clock. It was here before the
town, perhaps before the hill. It does not measure time.
It distributes it."

"To Bellweather?" the mayor asked.

"To many places. But Bellweather took more than it was
given."

During the Fever Year, Elian explained, Mayor Finch's grandfather had
begged Mara's grandfather, also a clockmaker, to stop the disease.
Together they had found the Orra Clock and turned back
its pendulum.

They restored one month.

The dead returned to life. The sick became well. Funerals
became weddings and final breaths became morning conversations.

"But the month was borrowed," Elian said. "Every person in
town lived those days twice, though only a few remembered."

Mara understood. "And borrowed time must be repaid."

Elian nodded. "I came here when the debt first woke.
I offered the clock my own years, but one life
was not enough. So I jammed the mechanism. I thought
I could hold it while I repaired the balance."

"For twenty years?"

"Time does not pass properly here."

A deep groan rolled through the chamber.

One of the great clock's iron teeth snapped. It struck
the floor and shattered.

Above them, the silent bell began to toll.

Its voice did not enter through Mara's ears. It sounded
inside her bones.

The mayor fell to his knees. With each toll, he
changed. Boy, man, old man, boy again.

"How do we stop it?" Mara shouted.

"We cannot," Elian said. "The debt must be paid."

"How much?"

"One month from every bloodline that received it."

"That would kill half the town."

"More than half."

Another tooth broke.

The glowing numbers around the clock face began going dark.

Mara opened her satchel. She removed jars, tins, and packets—the
fragments she had collected that morning. Minutes flew into the
machine and vanished.

The pendulum shifted a fraction.

"Lost time," Elian whispered.

Mara looked at him.

"How much lost time exists?"

"All time is lost eventually."

"No. I mean truly lost. Forgotten birthdays. Wasted afternoons. Hours
spent waiting for people who never came."

"Those are not worthless."

"I didn't say they were."

Mara thought of her workshop. Decades of collected moments lined
its shelves. Time misplaced by the people of Bellweather. Time
they no longer remembered enough to miss.

Would it be sufficient?

The bell tolled again.

A crack opened across the clock face.

Mara took out the bottle containing her father's last morning.
Golden light moved within it. She had guarded it for
two decades, believing it was all that remained of him.

Now he stood before her.

She broke the seal.

The morning rushed free.

Sunlight filled the chamber. Mara saw herself at twelve, asleep
at the kitchen table. Her father draped a blanket over
her shoulders. He stood watching her for a long moment,
then wrote the warning and walked north toward the chapel.

The memory entered the Orra Clock.

Its gears turned once.

Elian stared at his daughter. "You kept that?"

"Yes."

"I'm sorry."

"Be sorry later."

Mara gathered the empty bottle. "We need every lost moment
in my workshop."

"We're beneath a hill," Mayor Finch said. "The town may
not exist by the time we return."

"We don't have to return."

She held the empty bottle beneath the clock and struck
it against the stone.

Glass shattered.

Far above, in Mara's workshop, seventeen lost seconds broke free
from their jars. Two afternoons slipped from their boxes. A
summer's final hour unfolded its bright wings.

Labels peeled away. Lids burst open.

Throughout Bellweather, forgotten time stirred.

The hour a widow had spent beside her husband's empty
chair rose from the floorboards.

A childhood winter climbed out of an old man's dream.

Thousands of wasted minutes emerged from queues, quarrels, sleepless beds,
and roads not taken. They streamed toward the northern hill
in ribbons of silver and gold.

The chapel bell rang louder.

The ceiling of the chamber dissolved into light.

Mara saw the flood of lost hours pour down through
the earth and enter the Orra Clock.

The mechanism shuddered.

Its broken teeth mended. The pendulum swung forward.

Once.

Back.

Again.

The bell fell silent.

For several breaths, no one moved.

Then Mayor Finch looked at his hands. They were steady
and middle-aged.

"Is it over?" he asked.

Elian listened to the clock.

"The debt is paid."

"With forgotten time?" Mara said.

"Not forgotten." Her father smiled sadly. "Given."

They climbed to the surface together.

Dawn waited outside.

It was the same dawn that had begun when the
clocks vanished, though it felt older now. The clouds had
parted. Bellweather lay below them, whole and still.

Then, one by one, its clocks returned.

The tower faces appeared with a flash of white. Watches
settled into pockets. The school sundial cast its shadow. In
kitchens and bedrooms, hands resumed their interrupted journeys.

Every clock in town showed six fifteen.

At the station, an engine whistled.

This time it carried passengers.

Mara, Elian, and Mayor Finch walked down the hill. Along
the road, they passed townspeople waking from memories they had
not known they possessed.

A woman stood beneath an oak, weeping with joy because
she remembered her mother's voice.

Two brothers embraced after recovering the afternoon they had decided
never to speak again.

Stationmaster Pell laughed as the driver stepped from the train
and demanded to know why everyone looked so surprised.

In the square, the mayor climbed the fountain and told
the truth.

He spoke of the Fever Year, of his grandfather's bargain,
and of the debt inherited by the town. He expected
anger. He received silence.

At last, old Mrs. Fen raised her empty hourglass.

"I remember dying," she said. "I was seven. My mother
was holding me. Then it was morning, and I was
well."

Others began to remember.

The restored month lived within them—not as a wound, but
as a second shadow. Bellweather had been given an impossible
mercy, and generations had unknowingly carried its price.

"What do we do now?" someone asked.

Mayor Finch looked at Mara.

She looked at her father.

Elian had aged during their walk from the hill. His
dark hair was streaked with gray, and lines had appeared
around his eyes. Time had found him again.

"We use what remains," Mara said.

That answer became Bellweather's new law, though it was never
written down.

The people removed the tower bell that rang every hour.
After that, it rang only twice a day: once at
dawn, as a promise, and once at dusk, as thanks.

Mara reopened her workshop. She no longer stored lost time
in bottles. When someone brought her a forgotten hour or
a misplaced afternoon, she asked whether they wished to recover
it, share it, or let it go.

Most chose to recover it.

Some chose to share.

A few discovered that letting go was not the same
as losing.

Elian moved into the room above the kitchen. He and
Mara often argued while repairing clocks together. They argued about
springs, tea, dust, and the twenty years between them. Such
arguments were precious, and neither tried to shorten them.

One autumn evening, a young girl entered the shop carrying
a closed fist.

"I found something," she said.

Mara knelt.

The girl opened her hand. On her palm trembled a
bright, winged second.

"What does it sound like?" Mara asked.

The child held it to her ear.

"Someone saying my name."

"Do you know who?"

"No."

Mara fetched a small mirror instead of a jar.

"Look carefully."

The girl placed the second against the glass. The mirror
clouded, then revealed a woman standing beside a cradle, whispering
a newborn's name with exhausted joy.

The girl smiled.

"Can I keep it?"

"It's yours."

"What should I do with it?"

Mara glanced across the workshop.

Her father sat at the long table, bent over the
mechanism of an ordinary kitchen clock. Beyond him, the windows
glowed with sunset. In the square, people hurried home, late
for supper or early for tomorrow, while the clock tower
kept its patient silence.

"Remember it," Mara said. "And then spend the rest wisely."

The girl closed her fingers around the second and ran
home.

At dusk, the tower bell rang once.

Every clock in Bellweather paused to listen.

Then time continued.

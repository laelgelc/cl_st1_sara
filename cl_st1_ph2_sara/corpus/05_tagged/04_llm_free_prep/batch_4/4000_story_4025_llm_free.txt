The Last Lighthouse Keeper

On the morning the sea disappeared, Elias Vale was polishing
the lighthouse lens.

He had worked his way around the great glass rings
with a square of linen and a bottle of vinegar,
rubbing away salt that had gathered during the night. Outside,
gulls screamed over the cliffs. The lamp had gone dark
at dawn, as it had every morning for eighty-three years,
and sunlight poured through the high windows in pale yellow
bars.

Elias noticed the silence only when he stopped humming.

The gulls were still crying. The wind was still moving
around the tower. But the deep, steady thunder beneath the
cliff—the sound that had followed every hour of his life—was
gone.

He set down the cloth and climbed the iron stairs
to the gallery.

Beyond the railing, where the sea should have stretched to
the horizon, there was only a vast floor of wet
sand.

It shone under the early sun, rippled and silver, scattered
with fish that flashed like dropped coins. Far in the
distance, perhaps twenty miles away, a thin blue line trembled
beneath the sky.

Elias gripped the railing.

The tide around Bellweather Island withdrew twice each day, uncovering
black rocks and narrow beaches. But it had never gone
beyond the old wreck at Gull Point, and now even
Gull Point stood high and dry. The seabed continued past
it, broad and empty, toward the vanished water.

On the exposed sand directly below the lighthouse lay a
road.

It was paved with white stones.

The road began at the foot of the cliff and
ran straight across the seabed until it disappeared into the
morning haze.

Elias watched it for a long time. Then he went
downstairs to wake his daughter.

Mara was asleep in the keeper's cottage, one hand tucked
beneath her cheek. At seventeen, she had her mother's dark
hair and her father's habit of sleeping through storms. Elias
stood in the doorway, unwilling to disturb her. She looked
younger asleep, almost like the child who had once searched
tide pools with a red bucket and asked whether starfish
fell from the night sky.

"Mara," he said.

She opened one eye. "Is the lamp broken?"

"No."

"Is the roof leaking?"

"No."

"Then it can wait until breakfast."

"The sea is gone."

She opened the other eye.

They ate bread and jam on the cliff while staring
at the empty ocean. Mara said nothing for several minutes.
She had inherited that from him too: the belief that
words should not be used until they were necessary.

At last she pointed to the white road.

"Was that always there?"

"No."

"Maybe it was buried."

"Under twenty fathoms of water?"

"Things get buried in strange places."

Elias looked toward the horizon. "The water will come back."

Mara tore a crust from her bread. "How soon?"

"The tide tables say it should already be rising."

"But it isn't."

"No."

Across the island, doors opened. The fishermen and their families
emerged from stone cottages and followed the path to the
cliff. Soon nearly all the inhabitants of Bellweather—forty-six people, counting
the new baby at the eastern farm—stood looking over the
edge.

Old Tomas Rill crossed himself. Mrs. Fen held a brass
telescope to her eye. The twins from the bakery threw
pebbles onto the exposed sand until their mother pulled them
away.

Mayor Cale arrived last, still fastening his coat.

"We must keep everyone off the seabed," he announced.

"Why?" Mara asked.

"Because the sea may return."

"When?"

Mayor Cale looked at Elias.

Elias was the lighthouse keeper. On Bellweather, this made him
responsible not only for the lamp but, by some unspoken
agreement, for the behavior of fog, tides, whales, lightning, and
any ship foolish enough to strike a rock. He had
spent half his life explaining that he did not command
the ocean. No one had ever believed him.

"I don't know," he said.

The mayor formed a committee.

By noon, the committee had met three times and accomplished
nothing. The island's radio received only static. Fishing boats rested
on their sides in the harbor mud. The old mainland
cable, which carried telephone messages beneath the channel, had stopped
working. Two men were sent to examine it and returned
saying that the cable had been cut cleanly where the
white road crossed it.

Meanwhile, clouds of flies gathered over the stranded fish.

The islanders carried buckets down the cliff path and collected
what they could. Children discovered crabs in shallow pools. Mr.
Fen found a cannon half buried in the sand and
claimed it had belonged to a pirate king. By midafternoon,
despite the mayor's warnings, people had spread nearly a mile
across the seabed.

Only Elias and Mara stood at the beginning of the
road.

The white stones were smooth and dry.

That troubled Elias more than anything else. Everything around them
glistened with seawater, but the road showed no trace of
moisture. No weed clung to it. No shell had rooted
in its cracks. It looked newly built.

Mara crouched and touched one of the stones.

"It's warm," she said.

Elias pulled her hand away.

She frowned. "I was only touching it."

"And now you've touched it."

"That is generally how touching works."

He almost smiled. Then he saw the footprints.

A trail of bare human prints ran along the center
of the road. They came from the horizon and ended
a few steps from where he and Mara stood.

The final footprints faced the lighthouse.

Mara saw them too.

"Someone came here."

"Last night, perhaps."

"The road would have been underwater."

"Yes."

"Then whoever made them walked twenty miles under the sea."

Elias turned back toward the cliff. "We're going home."

That evening, the water had still not returned.

The island smelled of salt, mud, and dying things. Without
the sea, Bellweather seemed taller than before. The cliffs rose
from the seabed like the walls of a fortress, and
the lighthouse, built to guide ships through water, appeared ridiculous
above an empty plain.

Nevertheless, at sunset Elias climbed the tower and lit the
lamp.

Mara followed him.

"Who is it for?" she asked as the lens began
to turn.

The beam swept across the darkening seabed.

"For anyone who needs it."

"There are no ships."

"It is not our task to decide who needs a
light."

This was what Elias's father had taught him, and what
his father's father had taught before that. The Vale family
had kept the Bellweather lamp for five generations. They had
lit it during wars, epidemics, and winters when ice sealed
the harbor. Elias had lit it on the night his
wife, Lena, died.

Especially that night.

Mara leaned against the window. "Father."

He heard the change in her voice.

Far out on the white road, a figure stood in
the beam.

It was too distant to see clearly. Each time the
lens turned, the light found it again: a person in
a long dark coat, walking toward the island.

Elias stopped the mechanism.

The beam remained fixed on the road.

The figure came closer.

"Go to the cottage," he said.

"No."

"Mara."

"If you stay, I stay."

He wanted to argue, but the words failed him. In
seventeen years he had never managed to win an argument
when she spoke in Lena's tone.

Together they descended.

By the time they reached the cliff, half the island
had gathered there with lanterns, hooks, rifles, and kitchen knives.
News traveled quickly in small places, especially impossible news.

The figure walked steadily toward them.

At fifty yards, Elias saw that it was a woman.
Her black dress streamed behind her, though the air on
the seabed was still. She wore no shoes. Her pale
feet followed the footprints already marked upon the road.

At twenty yards, he recognized her.

The lantern slipped from his hand.

Mara made a sound like a gasp and a sob
together.

The woman stopped at the end of the road.

She looked exactly as she had eleven years before: thirty-two
years old, dark hair tied with a blue ribbon, gray
eyes bright with patience and amusement.

"Hello, Elias," said Lena Vale.

No one moved.

Mara stepped forward.

Elias caught her wrist. She fought him, but he held
on.

"That is not your mother."

Lena's expression did not change.

Mara stared at her. "You died."

"Yes," Lena said.

"I saw you."

"Yes."

"We buried you."

"I remember."

Elias felt the world tilt beneath him. He remembered the
fever, the useless medicines, Lena's hand cooling inside his own.
He remembered digging her grave because the ground was too
frozen for the undertaker's spade. He remembered Mara, six years
old, asking why her mother could not simply wake up.

"What are you?" he demanded.

Lena looked toward the distant sea.

"A messenger."

"From whom?"

"From what waits beneath the water."

Behind Elias, someone whispered a prayer.

Lena continued. "The sea has withdrawn to open the road.
At midnight it will return."

"Then we have to reach high ground," Mayor Cale said.

"There is no ground high enough."

The mayor's face emptied.

Lena lifted one hand and pointed along the white stones.
"The road leads to a gate. Beyond it is a
place untouched by the flood. You may follow me there."

"All of us?" Mara asked.

"All who leave before midnight."

Old Tomas pushed through the crowd. "And if we stay?"

"The wave will cover Bellweather. It will cover the mainland.
By dawn, there will be no shore left anywhere."

A murmur passed among the islanders.

Elias studied the woman's face. Every detail was right, even
the small scar near her eyebrow from the time she
had fallen against the bakery steps. Yet there was something
absent. Lena had never stood completely still. She tapped her
fingers, shifted her weight, tilted her head when listening. This
woman did none of those things.

"Why wear her face?" he asked.

The messenger looked at him.

"Because you would not have listened to another."

The islanders began packing at once.

They took blankets, water, bread, knives, family Bibles, silver spoons,
framed photographs, seeds, and bottles of brandy. Mayor Cale tried
to organize them, but fear had dissolved his authority. Families
argued over what to carry. Mr. Fen attempted to drag
a wooden chest until the bottom broke and coins rolled
across the floor.

Elias returned to the lighthouse.

Mara followed.

"You are not coming," she said.

He climbed the stairs without answering.

"You heard what she said."

"I heard."

"She knew Mother."

"It knew her shape."

"It remembered dying."

"Perhaps it took that from us."

At the lantern room, Elias checked the oil reservoir.

Mara stood in the doorway. "Why are you doing that?"

"The lamp must remain lit."

"For whom? The sea is coming back. Everyone will be
gone."

"Then it will shine for the empty island."

She stared at him as though he had become a
stranger.

"You would rather die here than admit the lighthouse no
longer matters."

Elias tightened a valve. "It matters."

"To you."

"Yes."

"What about me?"

He looked at her then.

She was crying silently, which was worse than if she
had shouted.

"Come with me," she said. "Please."

Below them, lanterns moved toward the road. The first islanders
had begun their journey across the seabed.

Elias turned to the glass. The lamp flame burned steadily
at its center.

"When your mother was ill," he said, "she asked me
to let the light go dark. Only for one night.
She knew she was dying, and she did not want
to be alone."

Mara wiped her face.

"I told her ships depended on me. I told her
duty did not pause for grief. So I climbed this
tower, and while I was here, she died."

"You never told me."

"I have regretted it every day."

"Then don't do it again."

"That is why I must stay."

She shook her head. "That makes no sense."

"Perhaps not."

Elias gazed down at the messenger wearing Lena's face.

"When I found your mother dead, I wished the lamp
had failed. I wished every ship in the world would
break upon the rocks and sink, if only I could
have those hours again. Grief makes selfish bargains, Mara. And
sometimes, I think, something hears them."

"You think this is because of you?"

"No. But I think that road offers people what they
most want to see. I think it is dry because
nothing living made it. And I think a gate at
the bottom of the sea may not lead to safety."

Mara looked through the window. Nearly everyone was now on
the road.

"What if you're wrong?"

"Then staying is a terrible mistake."

"And if you're right?"

"Leaving is worse."

The lighthouse clock struck eleven.

Mara closed her eyes.

Then she removed her pack and set it on the
floor.

Elias felt both relief and terror.

"You should go," he said.

"So should you."

"I mean it."

"So do I."

Below, the messenger lifted her face toward them. For the
first time, her calm expression changed. It hardened.

The islanders on the road had become a chain of
lights stretching into darkness. Only Elias, Mara, and Old Tomas
remained behind. The old man sat beside the cliff path,
smoking his pipe.

"Aren't you going?" Elias called.

Tomas shrugged. "My knees hurt."

At half past eleven, a sound rolled across the seabed.

The sea was returning.

It appeared first as a silver line beneath the moon.
Then the line rose, impossibly high, until it hid the
stars. A wall of black water stretched from one horizon
to the other.

On the road, people began to run.

The messenger turned and raised both arms. The white stones
brightened, casting a cold glow. Far away, a doorway of
green light opened in the advancing water.

The first islanders reached it and vanished.

More followed.

The wave came closer, swallowing the seabed. Shipwrecks disappeared beneath
foam. Fish, stones, and broken anchors tumbled before it.

The messenger looked back at the lighthouse.

"Elias!" she called in Lena's voice.

He flinched.

"Elias, please!"

It was exactly the voice from that last night. Weak
with fever. Frightened. Human.

His hand moved toward the door.

Mara seized it.

"That is not Mother."

The words broke the spell.

The messenger screamed.

Her familiar face split like a reflection struck by a
stone. Beneath it was a smooth gray surface without eyes
or mouth. Her body lengthened. Her arms bent backward. She
sprang onto the road and raced toward the gate as
the water thundered behind her.

At midnight, the wave reached Bellweather.

Elias held Mara on the gallery. Old Tomas stood below,
his pipe finally extinguished. The lamp burned above them, its
golden beam aimed into the oncoming dark.

The wave struck.

But it did not cover the island.

Water rose around the cliffs in a roaring circle. It
climbed higher than the harbor, higher than the cottages, higher
than any tide in memory. Yet when it reached the
lighthouse beam, the wave divided.

For one endless moment, walls of water towered on either
side of Bellweather. Elias saw shapes moving inside them: pale
hands, enormous eyes, cities of coral, and crowds of human
figures walking in the deep.

Then the sea collapsed.

The lighthouse shook. Windows shattered. The gallery rail tore loose
on the western side. Mara cried out, and Elias pulled
her against the tower.

The lamp remained lit.

By dawn, the sea had returned to its old boundaries.

The cottages were damaged, the harbor ruined, and every boat
except one had vanished. But Bellweather still stood. Elias, Mara,
and Tomas searched the island, calling names that received no
answers.

Of the white road, there was no sign.

Weeks later, a navy ship arrived. Its captain reported strange
tides but no worldwide flood. Along the mainland coast, people
had seen the sea withdraw for seven minutes before returning
harmlessly. No roads had appeared elsewhere.

The forty-three missing inhabitants of Bellweather were never found.

Years passed.

The island was repopulated slowly. New fishermen repaired the harbor.
New children climbed the rocks. The official report blamed a
sudden storm and mass panic, though no one could explain
the vanished bodies.

Elias kept the lighthouse until his hands became too unsteady
to clean the lens.

Then Mara took his place.

On his final evening as keeper, they climbed the tower
together. His hair was white, and the iron stairs forced
him to stop twice for breath. At the top, Mara
lit the lamp while he watched.

Beyond the glass, the sea darkened under the setting sun.

"Do you ever wonder?" she asked.

"About the gate?"

"About where it led."

Elias nodded. "Every day."

"Do you think they died?"

"I don't know."

"Do you think we saved the island?"

"I don't know that either."

Mara smiled faintly. "You know very little for someone who
spent his life being asked questions."

"That may be the only thing I have learned."

Night gathered. The lens began to turn, sending its beam
across the water.

On the horizon, something flashed.

Once.

Twice.

Then a third time.

Not lightning. Not a ship's lantern. It was a pale
green light, opening and closing far beneath the surface.

Mara reached for the telescope, but Elias stopped her.

Together they stood beside the lamp and watched the distant
signal answer their own.

After a while, Elias took his daughter's hand.

"Keep the light," he said.

Below them, the sea whispered against the cliffs, patient as
memory.

And this time, neither of them mistook its silence for
peace.

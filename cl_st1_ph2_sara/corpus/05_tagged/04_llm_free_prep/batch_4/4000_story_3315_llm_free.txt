## The Last Lighthouse Keeper

On the morning the sea disappeared, Elias Vale was polishing
the lighthouse lens.

He noticed nothing at first. The tower walls were thick,
the windows narrow, and the great brass mechanism groaned loudly
as it turned. Elias worked with his sleeves rolled to
his elbows, rubbing salt from the glass with a square
of linen, while below him the clockwork gears clicked like
patient teeth.

Then the gulls went silent.

Elias paused.

For thirty-seven years he had lived on Beacon Island, and
in all that time the gulls had never been silent.
They screamed at dawn, fought at noon, and muttered through
the night. Their voices belonged to the island as surely
as the black rocks and the wind-bent grass.

He climbed to the outer gallery and opened the iron
door.

The sea was gone.

Beacon Island stood in the middle of an immense plain
of wet sand. The water had withdrawn beyond the horizon,
leaving behind stranded boats, glistening channels, and silver fish that
flashed helplessly in the early light. Far to the east,
where no land had ever been, dark shapes rose from
the exposed seabed.

Towers, Elias thought.

A drowned city.

He gripped the rail. The lighthouse lamp continued turning behind
him, sending its pale morning beam over a world that
no longer needed warning of rocks.

At the foot of the tower, someone rang the bell.

Elias leaned over the railing. A girl stood beside the
green door, holding a red bicycle. She was perhaps sixteen,
with a yellow coat and a braid blown nearly horizontal
by the wind.

"Mr. Vale!" she shouted.

He knew her. Everyone in Bellweather knew everyone else, and
Mara Venn was known especially well. She delivered telegrams, repaired
radios, and had once driven the mayor's automobile into the
fountain while demonstrating that she could steer with her knees.

"How did you get here?" Elias called.

"Across the seabed!"

"That's impossible."

"It was easier than swimming."

He hurried down the spiral stairs.

Mara met him in the doorway. Her boots and bicycle
tires were coated in gray mud. A canvas message bag
hung from her shoulder.

"The harbor master sent me," she said, catching her breath.
"The telephone lines are dead. He says you're to extinguish
the light and come ashore."

"Extinguish it?"

"There's no sea."

"There's still fog."

Mara glanced over her shoulder at the endless seabed. "Not
much chance of a ship hitting the island now."

"A lighthouse is lit from sunset to sunrise. Those are
the regulations."

"The regulations did not anticipate the ocean leaving."

"Good regulations anticipate everything."

She studied him with an expression that made him feel
like an exhibit in a museum.

Elias was sixty-three and as narrow as the tower in
which he lived. His beard had gone white years ago,
though his eyebrows remained stubbornly black. He wore the old
keeper's uniform even though the Lighthouse Authority had stopped requiring
it, and he kept three identical brass watches because he
believed one watch invited uncertainty and two invited argument.

"The harbor master also says," Mara continued, "that if you
won't come willingly, I should remind you the island may
become unsafe."

"It has survived ten thousand years of storms."

"Yes, but never the sudden absence of several trillion tons
of water."

Elias looked past her.

A fishing boat lay tilted in the sand halfway between
Beacon Island and Bellweather. Men and women moved around it
like ants, unloading baskets. Farther away, crowds wandered over the
exposed seabed. Some collected fish. Others dug for coins, anchors,
bottles, and whatever else the tide had hidden over the
centuries.

Beyond them, the drowned towers waited on the horizon.

"Do you see those?" Elias asked.

Mara followed his gaze. "The ruins?"

"You're very calm about them."

"I used up my surprise on the missing ocean."

Elias went back inside. He packed bread, cheese, two lanterns,
a coil of rope, his weather log, and the oldest
of his three watches.

"What are you doing?" Mara asked.

"Investigating."

"The harbor master told me to bring you back."

"And you have. I can see the harbor from here."

"You know that isn't what he meant."

"No regulation forbids walking on the seabed."

Mara smiled despite herself. "Good regulations anticipate everything?"

"Only the important things."

He locked the lighthouse door and slipped the key into
his pocket.

They set out toward the drowned city.

Walking on the floor of the sea was nothing like
walking on a beach. The mud pulled at their boots.
Long ridges of sand rose and fell like frozen waves.
Here and there lay fields of shells, pale as fallen
stars, and gardens of seaweed flattened beneath the sky.

Mara pushed her bicycle. Its wheels sank so deeply that
after half a mile she abandoned it beside the remains
of a ship.

The wreck's ribs curved from the sand. Elias recognized its
shape.

"The Mercy Bell," he said.

"You know it?"

"It sank when I was eight. My father helped search
for survivors."

Mara stepped closer. Inside the broken hull lay barrels, rope,
and a row of green bottles still sealed with wax.

"Was anyone saved?"

"Three sailors."

"And the rest?"

"The sea kept them."

Mara looked around uneasily, as though expecting the missing water
to overhear.

They continued east.

By noon the sun burned white above them. People had
spread across the seabed behind, shouting whenever they found something
valuable. Elias and Mara passed iron cannons furred with rust,
the stone jaws of an ancient harbor, and the skeleton
of a whale with a small anchor caught between its
ribs.

In a shallow pool, hundreds of fish crowded together.

Mara knelt. "They'll die."

"Yes."

"We should do something."

"With what? A bucket?"

She took off her yellow coat and soaked it in
the pool. The fish stirred beneath the shadow.

Elias said nothing. He remembered being young enough to believe
that because one could not save everything, one had to
begin immediately.

They shared bread beneath the whale's bones. When Elias checked
his watch, he found it had stopped at 9:17.

"That's strange," he said.

Mara opened the compass attached to her message bag. Its
needle spun without settling.

"The city is closer," she said.

It was. The ruins now rose distinctly against the sky:
domes, arches, crooked towers, and bridges that had once crossed
streets filled with water. Dark weed hung from balconies. Bronze
roofs gleamed beneath centuries of green corrosion.

Between Elias and the city stood a solitary door.

It was blue, upright, and unsupported, set into no wall.
Its brass knob shone as if polished that morning.

Mara walked around it. "I don't like this."

"That is a sensible response."

"Should we open it?"

"That is not."

Elias reached for the knob.

Before he touched it, the door opened inward.

On the other side was the sea.

Not the distant sea beneath a horizon, but the sea
itself—a vertical wall of blue water filled with drifting sunlight.
Fish moved through it. A jellyfish opened and closed like
a ghostly hand.

A woman stood within the water.

Her gray hair floated around her face, and her long
black dress moved in the current. She appeared neither drowned
nor troubled by the fact that she was standing several
feet below the surface of an ocean contained inside a
doorway.

"Elias Vale," she said.

Her voice came from everywhere: the door, the wet sand,
the empty shells.

Elias stepped back.

Mara whispered, "You know her?"

"No."

The woman smiled. "You knew me once."

Elias stared at her eyes. They were dark, almost black,
with a small amber ring around each pupil.

Memory struck him with such force that his knees weakened.

"Lena?"

His sister had been nine when she vanished.

It had happened on a calm afternoon, fifty-five years earlier.
Elias and Lena had rowed beyond the harbor wall despite
their father's warning. A sudden fog had surrounded them. Elias
remembered Lena leaning over the side, reaching for something bright
beneath the water. He remembered the boat rocking.

Then she was gone.

The town searched for six days. Elias searched for fifty-five
years.

The woman in the doorway raised one hand. The gesture
was exactly as he remembered: two fingers slightly bent from
where a dog had bitten her when they were children.

"You're dead," Elias said.

"No."

"You drowned."

"No."

"I saw you fall."

"You saw me taken."

Mara moved closer to him. "Taken by what?"

The woman looked toward the drowned city. "By them."

A sound rolled beneath the sand.

It was low and vast, a groan felt more than
heard. In the distance, something moved between the towers.

Elias thought at first it was a cloud shadow. Then
it curled around a dome.

A tentacle.

It was broader than the Bellweather clock tower and covered
in pale scars. It slid through the ruins, lifting curtains
of mud.

Mara gripped Elias's sleeve. "We should go."

The woman in the doorway nodded. "Yes."

Elias could not move. "Where have you been?"

"Below."

"All this time?"

"Time is different in the deep places."

"Why has the sea gone?"

"To open the gate."

"What gate?"

But the woman only looked at him sadly.

Then the doorway slammed shut.

For one second, the blue door stood alone on the
seabed.

The next, it collapsed into water. A wave spread around
their boots and vanished into the sand.

The deep sound came again.

All across the exposed plain, stranded fish began to leap.

Elias and Mara ran.

They stumbled over ridges and splashed through channels. Behind them,
the drowned city woke.

Bells rang in the towers, though no hands pulled their
ropes. Windows glowed green. Great shapes unfolded from plazas and
courtyards. Some crawled on jointed limbs; others floated above the
streets as though the vanished ocean still held them.

Mara looked back once.

"What do you see?" Elias shouted.

"I don't know!"

"Then stop looking!"

The seabed trembled. A crack split the sand ahead of
them. Black water bubbled from it, carrying the smell of
old storms.

They jumped across.

The crowds near Bellweather had noticed the danger. People abandoned
their treasures and fled toward shore. A man dragged a
chest until its lock broke and gold coins spilled into
the mud; after one desperate glance, he left them. Children
cried. Horses strained against carts piled with artifacts.

Far behind, the largest of the creatures rose above the
drowned city.

Elias saw a crown of bone, an eye like a
moon behind fog, and a mouth that opened not in
its face but across the sky.

A voice entered his thoughts.

LIGHTKEEPER.

He fell to one knee.

Mara pulled him upright. "What happened?"

"It knows me."

"Wonderful. Keep running."

The voice returned.

THE LAMP IS DARK.

Elias looked toward Beacon Island. Even in daylight, the lighthouse
stood clear against the western sky.

"The lamp," he said.

"What?"

"I left it burning."

"You did."

"But it's dark."

"It isn't night yet."

"No. You don't understand. The lighthouse wasn't built for ships."

Mara stared at him. "What else are lighthouses for?"

Elias remembered the old records stored in the tower basement—logbooks
written before Bellweather had a harbor, before the Lighthouse Authority,
before any map named Beacon Island. Many pages used strange
terms: watcher in the deep, hour of retreat, covenant of
flame.

He had dismissed them as sailors' superstitions.

The voice pressed into his mind again.

THE LAMP IS DARK. THE GATE IS WIDE.

"The lens," Elias said. "The light isn't pointing at the
city."

"The lens turns."

"It turns too slowly."

Another crack opened nearby. From it emerged a white arm
with too many elbows. Mara struck it with her message
bag. The arm retreated, leaving smoking fingerprints on the leather.

"Go to Bellweather," Elias told her.

"I'm not leaving you."

"I have to return to the lighthouse."

"The island is the other way!"

"Yes."

"So is everything trying to eat us!"

He grabbed her shoulders. "The light is a lock. I
don't know how I know that, but I do. If
the beam is kept on the city, perhaps the gate
will close."

"Perhaps?"

"Good regulations cannot anticipate everything."

Mara laughed once, sharply. "I knew you'd admit it eventually."

They turned toward Beacon Island.

By then the sea had begun to return.

It did not appear as a wave. Water seeped upward
through the sand, first covering their soles, then their ankles.
The stranded fish revived and darted between their boots. Channels
filled, joining one another in winding silver veins.

Elias and Mara reached the lighthouse as the water rose
to their knees.

The green door would not open.

Elias twisted the key until his hands hurt.

"The foundation shifted," he said.

Mara took a screwdriver from her bag, along with a
coil of copper wire and three objects Elias could not
identify. She knelt at the lock.

"You carry tools to deliver telegrams?"

"Sometimes people don't want their messages."

She worked for several seconds. The lock clicked.

Inside, they climbed the spiral stairs. The tower shook around
them. Dust drifted from the ceiling, and the clockwork mechanism
groaned in protest.

At the top, the lamp burned steadily, but the lens
rotated with painful slowness. Once every thirty seconds its beam
swept east, briefly illuminating the drowned city.

Each time the light touched the ruins, the creatures recoiled.

"Stop the rotation," Elias said.

"How?"

"The brake lever."

He pulled it.

The gears screamed. The lens stopped facing south.

"Wrong direction," Mara said.

Together they pushed the brass frame. It refused to move.

Below them, water surged against the tower.

Elias found the manual crank and forced it into its
socket. He leaned on it with all his strength. The
lens shifted an inch.

Mara joined him. Metal shrieked.

Slowly, they turned the lens east.

The beam crawled across the returning sea. When it reached
the drowned city, green fire erupted from every window. The
creatures twisted away. Towers bent like reeds in a gale.

The enormous crowned figure did not retreat.

Its eye opened fully.

Lena appeared inside it.

She stood behind the dark pupil, one palm pressed against
its surface.

"Elias," she said, though the tower windows were closed.

He let go of the crank.

The lens began to slip.

Mara seized it. "Help me!"

"My sister is there."

"That thing isn't your sister."

"I saw her."

"You saw what it wanted you to see!"

Through the glass, Lena raised her scarred hand.

"Please," she said.

Elias remembered the rowboat in the fog. Lena leaning over
the side. Her fingers reaching for a golden light beneath
the water.

No.

Not reaching.

Pointing.

She had been pointing behind him.

Warning him.

Elias remembered turning too late. Something pale had risen beside
the boat. Lena had pushed him down as it reached
over the gunwale.

She had not been taken because she was careless.

She had been taken because she saved him.

He grasped the crank again.

"I'm sorry," he whispered.

He and Mara forced the lens into place.

The full beam struck the city.

Light filled every street and doorway. The ruined towers turned
transparent, revealing staircases that descended miles into darkness. For a
moment Elias saw the gate beneath the city: two colossal
doors of black stone, open just wide enough for nightmares
to escape.

Figures stood between them.

Thousands of people—sailors, travelers, children—watched from the deep. Lena stood
at their front, no longer old but nine years old,
wearing the blue ribbon she had worn the day she
disappeared.

She smiled at Elias.

Then she took hold of one of the great doors.

The others joined her.

Together, the lost pushed.

The crowned creature screamed.

The sound shattered every window in the lighthouse. Elias and
Mara fell, but the beam remained fixed. Across the sea,
the black doors closed. The escaping creatures were dragged backward
into cracks, pools, and ruined halls. The crowned figure folded
in on itself, its moonlike eye shrinking to a spark.

Lena vanished last.

The sea returned all at once.

A wall of water rose beyond the city and swept
west. It swallowed the ruins, the wrecks, the whale bones,
and the abandoned gold. It struck Beacon Island with the
force of a mountain.

The lighthouse bent.

For one impossible instant, Elias saw the foundation stones beneath
it and the empty air beyond the broken windows. Then
the tower sprang upright.

Water exploded over the gallery.

The lamp went out.

When Elias opened his eyes, he was lying on the
lantern-room floor. Mara was beside him, soaked and bleeding from
one eyebrow.

"Are we dead?" she asked.

"No."

"Are you certain?"

"One of my knees hurts."

"Convincing evidence."

They crawled to the gallery.

The ocean had returned to its proper place. Waves rolled
toward Bellweather beneath a clean evening sky. Boats floated where
the seabed had been. The drowned city was gone.

Along the distant shore, people gathered around fires. Bells rang
from the town, wild and uneven but unmistakably joyful.

Elias checked his watch.

It had begun ticking again.

The time was 9:18.

Behind them, the lamp remained dark. The lens had cracked
from top to bottom.

Mara touched the glass. "Can it be repaired?"

"Yes."

"How long will it take?"

"With help? A week."

"And without help?"

"Three months."

She nodded thoughtfully. "Then it's fortunate you have an apprentice."

"I don't recall hiring one."

"You need someone who understands regulations."

"You crossed the seabed during a maritime catastrophe."

"There was no regulation against it."

Elias almost laughed.

Below, something knocked against the lighthouse door.

They descended cautiously and found Mara's red bicycle floating in
the entrance, returned by the sea. Tangled around its handlebars
was a strip of blue ribbon.

Elias held it for a long time.

That night he lit a temporary lamp and placed it
in the eastern window. It was small, scarcely brighter than
a candle, but its reflection stretched across the dark water
like a golden road.

Mara fell asleep in the keeper's chair with a repair
manual open on her lap.

Elias stood watch until dawn.

Near midnight, a fog gathered beyond the harbor. For a
while he thought he saw the outline of a blue
door standing upright on the waves. A child waited beside
it, one hand raised in farewell.

Then the lamp flickered, the fog shifted, and there was
only the sea.

Elias touched two fingers to the glass.

Behind him, the clockwork gears clicked like patient teeth, and
the little light kept turning.

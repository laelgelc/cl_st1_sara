The Clockmaker's Last Appointment

Every clock in Bellweather stopped at 3:17 on the morning
Elias Vale died.

Pocket watches froze in waistcoat drawers. Grandfather clocks fell silent
in their dark wooden cases. The brass clock above the
railway station held its black hands at a narrow angle,
as if pointing accusingly toward the eastern hills. Even the
cheap alarm clock beside Mayor Rusk's bed ceased ticking, though
he slept through the sudden silence and woke four hours
later convinced he had risen early.

Only one clock continued to run.

It stood in the workshop of Mara Vale, Elias's nineteen-year-old
apprentice and only child. The clock was no larger than
her palm, with a silver case and a face of
blue glass. It had no numbers, only twelve tiny stars
painted around its edge. Its single golden hand moved backward.

Mara discovered it after dawn.

She had spent the night beside her father's body, seated
on the floor with her back against the workbench. Elias
had died in his chair without drama, one hand resting
on his chest and the other closed around a little
iron key. There had been no final speech, no mysterious
warning. He had taken one quiet breath and then no
more.

At sunrise, gray light entered the workshop. Mara opened her
eyes and heard a delicate ticking.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Backward went the golden hand.

She rose, stiff and cold, and searched among the crowded
shelves. The sound led her to a locked cabinet that
had always stood behind Elias's desk. He had forbidden her
to open it, saying only, "A clockmaker must respect the
hours that do not belong to her."

The iron key from his hand fitted the lock.

Inside was the silver clock, a sealed envelope, and a
coil of red thread.

Mara reached for the letter first. Her name was written
across it in her father's careful hand.

My dear Mara,

If you are reading this, Bellweather has stopped.

The clock inside this cabinet does not measure time. It
measures what remains of it.

Before its hand completes one backward turn, you must carry
it to the tower on East Hill and place it
inside the mechanism there. Take the red thread. Trust no
one who claims to remember tomorrow.

I am sorry I could not tell you sooner.

Your loving father,

Elias

Mara read the letter twice.

Then every bell in town began to ring.

They rang without rhythm. Church bells, shop bells, school bells,
handbells, and the enormous bronze bell in the railway tower
all cried out together. Their clamor shook dust from rafters
and sent pigeons wheeling above the streets.

Mara snatched up the clock and thread. Outside, people poured
from their houses in nightclothes and work clothes, shouting questions
no one could answer.

"Seven o'clock!" called the baker.

"Half past eight!" insisted a woman carrying a stopped kitchen
clock.

"It's still dark in my bedroom!"

"The sun is moving, isn't it?"

"Has the train arrived?"

The sun had risen, but uneasily. It hovered low above
the rooftops, flattened by clouds. Shadows pointed west, though Mara
could not tell whether they were growing shorter.

At the center of town, Constable Orr stood on a
barrel and begged for calm.

"Mechanical failure," he announced. "A disturbance in the air, perhaps.
Everyone is to continue as normal until the clocks are
repaired."

"All of them?" someone shouted.

Orr glanced toward Mara. His expression sharpened.

"Miss Vale," he called. "Where is your father?"

The townspeople turned.

Mara's fingers tightened around the silver clock concealed in her
coat pocket.

"He died last night," she said.

A silence spread outward from her, heavier than the bells
had been.

Elias Vale had repaired Bellweather's clocks for forty-three years. He
had adjusted the station clock every Monday, wound the council
hall clock every Thursday, and visited private houses whenever their
timepieces lost a minute. The town had trusted him with
anniversaries, departures, examinations, factory shifts, wedding mornings, and medicine taken
by the hour.

Now he was dead, and time had stopped with him.

Constable Orr climbed down from the barrel. "I am sorry,
Mara. Truly. But we must inspect his workshop."

"No."

The word came too quickly.

Orr heard the fear in it. So did everyone else.

Mayor Rusk pushed through the crowd, buttoning his coat over
his nightshirt. "This is no time for sentiment. If Elias
left instructions, tools, anything that might start the clocks again,
it belongs to Bellweather."

Mara felt the silver clock ticking against her palm.

The golden hand had already moved past the first painted
star.

"My father left instructions," she said. "I must go to
East Hill."

At once, Mayor Rusk's face changed.

It was a small change. His brows lifted, and his
lips parted. But beneath his surprise, Mara saw recognition.

"You know the tower?" she asked.

The mayor recovered quickly. "There is no tower on East
Hill."

"Yes, there is," said a voice behind them.

An old woman stood at the edge of the crowd.
Mara knew her as Mrs. Finch, who lived alone above
the shuttered glove shop and often brought broken clocks to
Elias. She was dressed in a green coat, though one
foot was bare.

"I saw it tomorrow," Mrs. Finch said.

No one spoke.

The old woman smiled sadly. "Or perhaps it was yesterday.
It becomes difficult near the end."

Mara remembered the letter.

Trust no one who claims to remember tomorrow.

Constable Orr moved toward Mrs. Finch, but Mayor Rusk seized
Mara's arm.

"Give me whatever your father left you," he whispered.

She pulled away.

Rusk's pleasant public expression had vanished. "Listen to me. Elias
was a good man, but he did not always understand
the cost of his decisions. If you go to that
hill, you may make things worse."

"You said there was no tower."

"There isn't. Not yet."

Mara stared at him.

The mayor looked toward her pocket. "Give me the clock."

She ran.

Mara slipped between two market stalls, vaulted a crate of
cabbages, and sprinted down Copper Lane. Behind her, Rusk shouted
for Orr to stop her. The constable called her name,
but his boots were heavy and the street was crowded.

At the old fountain, Mara cut north. She knew Bellweather's
alleys as well as she knew the gears of a
carriage clock. She crossed the laundry yard, ducked through the
ruined arcade, and emerged beside the cemetery.

Beyond its iron fence, East Hill rose against the pale
sky.

There was no tower on its summit.

Mara slowed.

She had expected to see one, though she had no
reason to. The hill was bare except for winter grass
and a ring of blackthorn trees. Sheep trails wandered across
its slope.

The clock ticked in her pocket.

Three stars gone.

"Not yet," Mayor Rusk had said.

A hand closed around Mara's elbow.

She spun, ready to strike, but found Mrs. Finch beside
her, breathing hard.

"You run like your mother," the old woman said.

"You knew my mother?"

"I will."

Mara stepped back.

Mrs. Finch raised both hands. "Your father told you not
to trust me. Sensible of him."

"How do you know what he told me?"

"Because you'll tell me."

"Tomorrow?"

"If we are fortunate."

Behind them, shouts echoed from Copper Lane.

Mrs. Finch opened the cemetery gate. "You cannot reach the
tower by climbing a hill that has not yet decided
to exist."

Mara did not move.

The old woman sighed. "Your father should have explained more."

"He never explained anything."

"Yes. That was always his particular cowardice."

Anger flared through Mara's grief. "Do not speak about him
that way."

"I loved him too."

That stopped her.

Mrs. Finch touched the cemetery wall, where a carving of
an hourglass had nearly worn away. "Elias believed silence could
protect you. In fairness, it did for nineteen years. Now
you may choose whether it protects anyone else."

The voices drew nearer.

Mara looked at the empty hill. "Show me the way."

Mrs. Finch led her among the graves to a stone
mausoleum belonging to no family Mara recognized. Its door bore
neither name nor date, only the same hourglass symbol. The
old woman pressed her thumb into its carved center.

With a groan, the stone door opened inward.

A staircase descended into darkness.

"After you," Mrs. Finch said.

Mara hesitated. "Why?"

"Because I remember where the steps will be, but not
whether they were repaired."

Mara entered.

The door closed behind them, shutting out the day.

Mrs. Finch struck a match and lit a wall lantern.
Its flame was blue. The staircase curved deep underground, far
below the graves, into air that smelled of oil and
rain.

At the bottom stood a circular chamber filled with clocks.

Hundreds covered the walls. Some were finely made, others crude:
sundials, water clocks, pendulums, sandglasses, watches, candle clocks, and mechanisms
Mara could not name. Most had stopped.

In the center of the room stood a stone archway
with darkness inside it.

Red threads had been tied from the arch to hooks
around the chamber. Some were taut. Others lay snapped on
the floor.

Mara drew the coil from her coat.

Mrs. Finch nodded. "Your father prepared well."

"What is this place?"

"The first workshop. Bellweather was built above it."

"By clockmakers?"

"By refugees."

Mara waited.

The old woman looked at the dead clocks. "Long ago,
people discovered that time is not a road. It is
a sea. Most of us float upon its surface, carried
from one moment to the next. But certain minds can
dive beneath it. They can swim backward, sometimes forward. They
can change the currents."

"That's impossible."

"So is a town where every clock stops at the
exact instant its clockmaker dies."

Mara touched the silver clock through her pocket.

Mrs. Finch continued. "The first divers tried to prevent wars,
plagues, murders. Every change created another need for change. A
saved king became a tyrant. A prevented fire allowed a
worse disease to spread. Children were born twice or not
at all. Entire cities remembered lives they had never lived."

Mara looked at the arch. Darkness moved inside it like
deep water.

"The survivors founded Bellweather around a fixed point," Mrs. Finch
said. "The tower on East Hill. Its mechanism anchors one
chain of events and closes all others."

"But there is no tower."

"There was. There will be. At present, we are caught
in the hour before it was built."

Mara took out the silver clock. Five stars had passed
beneath the golden hand.

"How do I reach it?"

"Through there."

Mrs. Finch pointed to the arch.

Mara tied one end of the red thread around her
wrist, as her father's letter had implied. She fastened the
other to an iron hook.

"What will I see?"

"Possibilities. Memories. Lies. They often look alike."

"And you?"

"I cannot go with you."

"Why not?"

The old woman extended her bare foot. Around her ankle
was a faded red thread, severed close to the skin.

"I already failed once."

From the staircase came the sound of stone grinding. Constable
Orr had found the entrance.

Mrs. Finch took Mara's face between her wrinkled hands.

"You must put the silver clock inside the tower mechanism
before its hand returns to the first star. Whatever you
see, do not let go of your thread."

"How do I find a tower that isn't there?"

Mrs. Finch smiled. "Build it."

She pushed Mara through the arch.

There was no sensation of falling. One moment, Mara stood
underground; the next, she stood in her father's workshop on
a summer afternoon.

Elias was alive.

He sat at the bench, younger by several years, oiling
the spring of a music box. Sunlight warmed the floor.
Somewhere outside, a dog barked.

Mara could not breathe.

Her father looked up. "There you are."

She knew it was impossible, yet every detail was right:
the crooked collar, the burn mark on his thumb, the
spectacles forever sliding down his nose.

"Father?"

"Hand me the narrow pliers, would you?"

She almost obeyed.

Red thread trailed from her wrist, passing through the closed
cabinet and vanishing into the wall.

"Are you real?" she asked.

Elias considered. "Real enough to miss you."

Mara began to cry.

He put down the music box. "Ah, my little gear.
Don't."

"You left me alone."

"I know."

"You knew this would happen."

"Yes."

"And you told me nothing."

"I hoped I might solve it before it reached you."

Anger steadied her. "That is not an answer."

"No." His eyes were tired. "It is an apology."

The clock ticked in her pocket.

Mara drew it out. Seven stars gone.

Elias's face filled with alarm. "Where did you get that?"

"You left it for me."

"I would never give you that clock."

"You did."

"No." He stood. "Mara, listen carefully. Whatever version of me
sent you into the current was mistaken. Give me the
clock, and I can stop this."

She took a step toward him.

He was her father. Not a ghost or dream, but
Elias as she remembered him: impatient, gentle, secretive, alive.

He held out his hand.

Behind him, on a shelf, stood a framed photograph of
Mara at the age of twelve. In the photograph, she
wore a red scarf.

Mara remembered the day it had been taken. It was
winter, not summer, and the scarf had been blue.

She wrapped the thread twice around her wrist.

"You aren't him," she said.

Elias's expression emptied.

The workshop peeled apart like wet paper.

Mara stood on a railway platform beneath a storm-black sky.
The station clock had no hands. Hundreds of people crowded
around her, all silent.

A woman in a green coat waited beside the tracks.

Mrs. Finch was young. Her dark hair reached her waist,
and both her feet were shod. She looked barely older
than Mara.

"You came," she said.

"Who are you?"

"Lena Finch."

"You knew my father."

"I married him."

Mara stared.

Lena laughed without joy. "Not in your life. In mine."

A train whistle sounded far away.

"We were both apprentices," Lena said. "Elias and I guarded
the anchor. When the mechanism began to fail, we disagreed.
He believed the fixed history should be preserved. I thought
we could improve it."

"You changed the past."

"I tried to save my sister from drowning. A small
thing, I thought. One child in one river."

"What happened?"

"I saved her. She grew up, had three children, and
one of them became the engineer who built the North
Viaduct. It collapsed during opening celebrations. Four hundred people died."

The rails began to hum.

"I went back again," Lena continued. "I prevented the viaduct.
Caused a famine. Prevented the famine. Caused a war. Each
attempt split the current further. Elias followed, repairing what he
could."

"And my mother?"

Lena looked toward the tracks. "Anna Vale was the person
who finally stopped us."

The name hurt. Mara had been five when her mother
disappeared. Elias always said she had died while traveling, though
no grave had ever been shown to Mara.

"What did she do?"

"She entered the tower and sealed the divisions. The cost
was her place in the fixed history. Most people forgot
she existed."

"But I remember."

"You were holding her hand."

The train burst from the darkness, racing toward the station
with no lamps in its windows.

Lena caught Mara by the shoulders. "The tower needs a
living keeper. Your father took Anna's place, but he died
before another could be chosen. Put the clock in the
mechanism, and you will become the keeper."

"For how long?"

"Until you die."

The train screamed closer.

"Is that why Father didn't tell me?"

"He wanted you to have a life."

"Do I have a choice?"

"There is always a choice. You can drop the thread.
Remain in any moment you desire. Your childhood. Your mother's
last morning. A future where Elias lives."

The train roared into the station.

Its doors opened.

Inside, Mara saw a bright kitchen. Her mother stood at
a table kneading bread, flour on her cheek. Elias sat
by the window, older but alive. A second place had
been set for Mara.

Anna looked up and smiled.

"Mara," she called. "Come home."

Every lonely year inside Mara answered.

She moved toward the open door.

The red thread pulled tight behind her.

Her mother's smile trembled. "Please."

Mara reached out. Their fingers almost touched.

Then she saw the kitchen clock.

Its hands stood at 3:17.

Mara stepped back.

The train vanished.

She was halfway up East Hill beneath a colorless sky.

Now the tower stood above her.

It was enormous, built of black stone, with a clock
face shining white near its summit. Its hands spun wildly.
Around the hill, Bellweather flickered through seasons: snow covered rooftops,
vanished into spring rain, returned as ash, then became summer
sunlight. Buildings rose and fell in seconds.

The silver clock had passed ten stars.

Mara climbed.

Wind tore at her coat. The red thread streamed behind
her, stretching down the hill into nothing. With every step,
voices whispered from the grass.

Turn back.

Choose again.

He can live.

She can remember you.

At the tower door stood Mayor Rusk.

He appeared unsurprised to see her.

"You remember tomorrow," Mara said.

"I remember many tomorrows."

He removed his gloves. His hands were scarred with thin
red lines.

"I was a keeper once," he said. "Not in this
current. Elias replaced me."

"Why?"

"Because I learned the truth. The tower does not preserve
the best history. It preserves one history. There may be
kinder worlds outside it."

"And worse ones."

"Yes."

Mara approached the door. Rusk blocked her path.

"You would sacrifice your whole life to maintain a world
filled with suffering?" he asked. "Children still drown. Bridges still
fall. Your mother was erased, and your father died afraid.
What exactly are you saving?"

Mara thought of Bellweather: the baker dusted white with flour,
Constable Orr on his barrel, schoolchildren racing sticks in the
gutters after rain. She thought of funerals and fevers, petty
cruelty and ordinary disappointment. She also remembered the first strawberry
of summer, lamps lit in winter windows, her father laughing
when a cuckoo clock exploded soot across the workshop.

"Not a perfect world," she said. "Just the one that
belongs to us."

Rusk's face hardened. "Spoken like Elias."

He lunged for the clock.

Mara twisted away. His hand closed around the red thread
and pulled. Pain cut into her wrist. The world changed.

She was five years old, standing beside a river.

Her mother's hand slipped from hers.

Changed again.

She was ten. Elias slept at his desk, and Mara
draped a blanket over his shoulders.

Changed again.

She was thirty, laughing in a house she had never
seen, while a child with her eyes chased a wooden
horse across the floor.

Changed again.

Bellweather burned beneath a red sky.

Mara fell to her knees at the tower door. Rusk
towered above her, still gripping the thread.

"You see?" he said. "There are countless lives. Why should
this one rule them all?"

The silver clock ticked.

Eleven stars gone.

Mara looked at the red thread between them. Then she
took the little iron key from her pocket—the key her
father had died holding—and drove it into Rusk's hand.

He cried out and released the thread.

Mara opened the tower door.

Inside, a spiral staircase wound upward around a vast open
shaft. Gears filled the darkness below, each wheel larger than
a house. Many had stopped. Others jerked backward and forward,
their teeth grinding.

She climbed until her legs shook.

At the top waited a circular room beneath the clock
face. In its center stood an empty socket shaped like
the silver clock.

Beside it sat Elias.

Not a false summer version. Not a memory assembled by
the current. He wore the same clothes in which he
had died. His face was pale, and his hands were
translucent.

Mara stopped.

He smiled. "You took your time."

A laugh broke from her, half sob. "Are you real?"

"For another minute or two."

She knelt and embraced him. Her arms passed partly through
his body, but she felt the warmth of him nonetheless.

"I'm angry with you," she whispered.

"You should be."

"I may stay angry forever."

"That seems inconvenient."

She laughed again.

The backward hand approached the final star.

"What happens when I put it in?" she asked.

"The current closes. Bellweather continues from 3:17. The tower will
need you, but not every hour. You can leave, travel,
grow old. You simply must return when the clock calls."

"You said a living keeper."

"Living, Mara. Not imprisoned."

She looked at him suspiciously. "Mrs. Finch made it sound
permanent."

"Lena has a gift for tragedy."

"Was she really your wife?"

"In one history."

"Did you love her?"

"In several."

Mara nodded, deciding there would be time for questions if
time itself survived.

"And Mother?"

Elias looked toward the great white clock face. "Anna is
part of the tower now. That is why you could
see her. Perhaps, with patience, you may find a way
to free her without breaking the world."

"Did you?"

"I came close."

"Why didn't you tell me any of this?"

"Because each year I believed I had one more year."

The golden hand touched the final star.

"Now," Elias said.

Mara placed the silver clock into the socket.

For one vast instant, she heard every clock that had
ever existed.

She heard sundials whisper beneath ancient sun, sand slipping through
glass, water falling drop by drop into bronze bowls. She
heard watches carried into battles, kitchen clocks counting through sleepless
nights, station clocks announcing departures, and tiny mechanisms resting over
human hearts.

Then the tower bell rang.

Once.

The sound traveled across the hill, through the town, over
rivers and roads, backward into memory and forward into possibility.

The gears turned.

The great clock hands stopped spinning and settled at 3:18.

Elias began to fade.

Mara held his hand.

"I'm not ready," she said.

"No one ever is."

"What should I do?"

"Repair what you can. Forgive what you cannot. And charge
Mayor Rusk double whenever he brings in that dreadful mantel
clock."

She smiled through tears.

Elias looked at her as if memorizing her face.

"My little gear," he said.

Then he was gone.

Mara sat alone until the tower's machinery found a steady
rhythm.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

She descended at dawn.

The world outside was still.

Not unnaturally still. Morning still. Dew shone on the grass.
Smoke rose from chimneys. In Bellweather, bells rang the quarter
hour with modest dignity.

At the foot of the tower, Mayor Rusk sat holding
his injured hand. Constable Orr stood beside him, confused and
out of breath.

"I followed you through the cemetery," Orr told Mara, "but
I cannot remember why."

Rusk remembered. She saw it in his eyes.

He bowed his head.

Mrs. Finch waited by the blackthorn trees. Her green coat
stirred in the wind. When Mara approached, the old woman
held up her ankle.

The broken thread was gone.

"Do you still remember tomorrow?" Mara asked.

Mrs. Finch considered. "No."

"Do you remember my father?"

Her eyes filled with tears.

"Yes," she said. "Only the yesterdays that happened."

They walked back to town together.

By noon, Elias Vale's body had been carried from the
workshop, and black cloth hung over the door. Neighbors brought
soup, bread, flowers, and clocks. Dozens of clocks. Though time
had resumed, the interruption had left them running fast or
slow, chiming at odd hours, or ticking backward.

Mara stood at her father's bench and surveyed the work.

Constable Orr placed a small alarm clock before her.

"Can it be fixed?" he asked.

She picked it up, listened, and opened the case.

A spring had slipped loose. Nothing more.

"Yes," she said. "It can."

Outside, the clock above the railway station showed 12:06. The
council hall insisted it was 12:09. Somewhere down Copper Lane,
a cuckoo announced thirteen o'clock, startling a horse.

Bellweather had not returned to perfect time.

Perhaps it never had.

Mara fitted the spring into place and wound the alarm
clock. It began to tick in her hand, uncertain at
first, then steady.

On the shelf above the bench sat the silver clock
with its blue glass face. Its golden hand no longer
moved backward. It pointed east, toward the dark tower on
the hill.

No one in town seemed surprised that the tower was
there. People claimed it had stood for centuries. They argued
about who had built it, whether visitors should be allowed
inside, and whether its bell was too loud before breakfast.

Only Mara, Mrs. Finch, and Mayor Rusk knew that the
tower was both ancient and new.

In the years that followed, Mara traveled often. She studied
clockmaking in distant cities and returned with foreign tools, unusual
watches, and stories that kept Bellweather's children gathered around her
door. She loved twice, married once, and had a daughter
who disliked clocks but adored astronomy.

Whenever the silver clock called, Mara climbed East Hill.

Sometimes the tower showed her histories that might have been.
In one, Elias lived to ninety. In another, Anna Vale
came home. In others, Bellweather was a lake, a forest,
a battlefield, or a place never built.

Mara watched, remembered, and did not interfere.

But she searched for her mother.

It took many years to find the smallest loose gear
hidden behind the tower's central wheel. It took several more
to understand that it did not belong to the mechanism
at all.

On Mara's sixty-third birthday, she removed it.

Every clock in Bellweather stopped.

Only for one second.

Then, in the workshop below East Hill, a woman appeared
beside the locked cabinet. She had flour on one cheek
and had not aged a day since Mara was five.

Mara stood very still.

The woman looked around at the unfamiliar room, at the
photographs, at the tools worn smooth by decades of use.
Finally, she looked at Mara.

Her eyes widened.

"Mara?"

The clock above the railway station struck 3:18.

Mara crossed the workshop and took her mother's hand.

This time, she did not let go.

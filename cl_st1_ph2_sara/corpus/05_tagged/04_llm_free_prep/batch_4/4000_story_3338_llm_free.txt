# The Museum of Unfinished Things

Every morning at nine, Mara Vale unlocked the bronze doors
of the Museum of Unfinished Things and listened to the
building sigh.

It was not a grand museum. It stood between a
watch repair shop and a bakery on a narrow street
where rainwater gathered in the cracks. Its sign was small,
its windows dusty, and its roof leaned slightly toward the
sea. Tourists often passed without noticing it.

Those who entered rarely stayed long.

The first gallery contained half-painted portraits, books ending in the
middle of sentences, violins without strings, and clocks whose makers
had forgotten to add hands. The second held abandoned inventions:
a bicycle designed to travel over water, a machine intended
to translate birdsong, and a brass helmet that, according to
its label, had been built to remember dreams.

The final room was always locked.

Mara had worked at the museum for twenty-eight years. She
dusted the exhibits, replaced faded labels, repaired leaking pipes, and
answered visitors' questions.

"No," she would say, "we do not finish the objects."

Or, "No, the artist's identity is unknown."

And sometimes, "Please do not touch the moon."

The moon was a plaster sphere no larger than an
orange. Its sculptor had shaped every crater perfectly but had
left one side smooth and blank. Children were especially tempted
by it.

Mara understood why.

Unfinished things invited the hand.

She herself had come to the museum at sixteen, carrying
an unfinished object wrapped in a green scarf. The curator
at the time, Mr. Ellison, had accepted it without asking
many questions. He was a thin man with enormous eyebrows
and a habit of speaking to the exhibits as if
they were nervous animals.

"Everything here," he had told her, "was stopped for a
reason."

"Even the bad things?"

"Especially the bad things."

He had placed Mara's object in the locked final room.
Years later, when he retired, he gave her the iron
key.

"Open that room only when you must," he said.

"How will I know?"

"You won't," he replied. "But the room will."

Then he sailed east on a cargo ship and was
never heard from again.

For twenty-eight years, Mara had opened the final room only
to clean. She did so on the first Monday of
every month, always before sunrise, when no visitors were nearby.

The room contained a single exhibit.

It stood beneath a linen sheet in the center of
the floor.

Mara never removed the sheet. She swept around it, polished
the brass rail, and left as quickly as possible. Even
covered, the object seemed to recognize her. Sometimes the sheet
stirred though the windows were closed. Sometimes a faint ticking
followed her into the corridor.

She had long ago learned not to look back.

Then, on a wet Thursday in November, a boy arrived
with a thunderstorm in a glass jar.

He entered just before closing. He was perhaps twelve, small
for his age, with rain-dark hair and a blue coat
several sizes too large. Both arms were wrapped around a
jar sealed by a copper lid.

Inside, black clouds churned. Silver lightning flashed against the glass.

"I want to donate this," he said.

Mara looked from the jar to the boy.

"Where did you get it?"

"My mother made it."

"And where is your mother?"

The boy's grip tightened. "Gone."

People often brought objects to the museum after someone had
died. Mara had received incomplete wedding dresses, interrupted diaries, unmailed
letters, and a wooden cradle with one side missing. Grief
was the most productive artist she knew.

"What was it supposed to be?" she asked.

"A storm that would rain upward."

The cloud inside the jar flashed. A moment later came
a tiny, furious crack of thunder.

Mara leaned closer. "Why didn't she finish it?"

"She ran out of time."

There was something rehearsed in the answer, as if the
boy had repeated it to several adults and none had
believed him.

"What is your name?"

"Leo."

"Well, Leo, we do accept donations, but I'll need to
examine it. Some unfinished things are dangerous."

"It only storms when it's sad."

"That does not reassure me."

The boy almost smiled.

Mara carried the jar to the workroom. Leo followed closely.
Under the bright lamps, she found symbols engraved around the
copper lid. They were not letters in any language she
knew. Some resembled waves; others looked like small doors.

She put on leather gloves and turned the jar.

The storm followed her fingers.

"Your mother was an engineer?" Mara asked.

"A weather-maker."

"There are no weather-makers."

"There was one."

Mara considered him. The museum had taught her that impossible
things were often merely unfashionable facts.

She fetched the donation ledger.

"Your full name?"

"Leo Arden."

Her pen stopped.

"Arden?"

"Yes."

Mara looked toward the corridor leading to the locked room.

The covered object waiting there had been made by a
man named Elias Arden.

"Was your father called Elias?" she asked.

Leo's face changed. "How do you know that?"

The thunderstorm struck the side of the jar so violently
that blue sparks leaped from the copper lid.

Mara closed the ledger.

"What did your mother tell you about him?"

"She said he built a machine and disappeared."

"When?"

"Before I was born."

Mara felt the old ticking in her bones.

Twenty-eight years earlier, Elias Arden had arrived at the museum
as a young man with brilliant eyes and burned hands.
Mara had been sixteen. Elias had been nineteen, and he
had seemed to her like someone who had stolen fire
and was surprised to find it hot.

He was an apprentice clockmaker, though ordinary clocks bored him.
He did not wish to measure time. He wanted to
persuade it.

He had built a machine that could return one hour
of a person's life.

At first Mara had thought it was only a box
of gears and copper wire. Then Elias demonstrated it in
the cellar of the watch repair shop next door.

He placed a cracked teacup inside the machine. The gears
turned backward. The crack sealed itself. Tea rose from the
floor and filled the cup.

"It works," Mara whispered.

"For small things," Elias said. "For short distances."

He wanted to make it powerful enough to recover his
younger sister, who had drowned the previous winter. He spoke
of her as if she were waiting on the other
side of a door.

Mara loved him enough to help.

For seven months they collected rare metals, recalculated equations, and
stole electricity from the streetlamps. But on the night they
planned to test the full machine, Elias's mother came to
the workshop. She had discovered what they were doing.

"You think grief is a mistake you can repair," she
told him. "It is not."

He refused to listen.

The machine began to turn.

Every clock in the district rang at once. The air
folded like paper. Through a silver opening above the machine,
Mara glimpsed a frozen river, a summer field, an old
woman blowing out candles, and a child beneath dark water.

Then Elias's mother struck the mechanism with a hammer.

The opening collapsed.

Elias vanished.

All that remained was the machine, still ticking, one final
gear missing from its heart.

Mara carried it to the museum before dawn. Mr. Ellison
covered it with a sheet and locked it away.

Until now.

"Your mother," Mara said slowly. "What was her name?"

"Celeste Arden."

Elias's younger sister had been named Celeste.

Mara sat down.

"That's impossible."

Leo lifted his chin. "People keep saying that."

"How old was she?"

"Forty-one."

"No. She died when she was eight."

"She said she almost died when she was eight."

The room seemed to tilt. Rain whispered against the windows.

Leo placed the storm jar on the table.

"My mother told me that years ago someone tried to
pull her out of the past. He failed, but he
opened something. A crack. She fell through it and landed
twenty years later."

Mara gripped the edge of the desk.

"She grew up," Leo continued. "She studied the crack. She
said time was like weather—always moving, always changing pressure. She
learned to make little storms because storms could feel openings
between one moment and another."

"Where is she now?"

"Three weeks ago, she started disappearing."

"Disappearing?"

"First it was her shadow. Then her voice would stop
in the middle of words. Yesterday morning, she was gone."

The cloud in the jar darkened.

"She left a note," Leo said. "It said I had
to bring the storm here and find the machine."

Mara stood so quickly that the chair fell backward.

"No."

"You have it."

"No."

"He tried to save her once. The machine knows her."

"The machine took your father."

"My uncle."

Mara stared at him.

"Elias was her brother," Leo said. "Not my father."

Relief struck Mara first, absurd and sharp. Shame followed.

Leo noticed both.

"My father left when I was little," he said. "You
knew Elias, didn't you?"

"Yes."

"Then you can help me."

"No."

The answer came too loudly. The thunderstorm flashed.

Mara lowered her voice. "That machine is not a rescue
boat. It is a hole cut into something we do
not understand."

"You used it."

"I helped build it. That is different."

"Not very."

"You could vanish. Or grow old in a second. Or
return to a time before you were born and have
no way back."

Leo looked at the storm jar. "I don't have anywhere
else to go."

Mara wanted to tell him there were homes, schools, kind
strangers, authorities who kept forms for children with nowhere else
to go. But the words felt like offering an umbrella
to someone in a flood.

A heavy knock echoed through the museum.

Once.

Twice.

Then all the unfinished clocks began to tick.

Clocks without hands. Clocks without springs. Clocks painted on canvas.
A sundial in the dark.

Mara and Leo stepped into the gallery.

The sound rolled around them like rain on a metal
roof.

At the far end of the corridor, the door to
the final room stood open.

The iron key remained in Mara's pocket.

"The room," she whispered.

The ticking stopped.

From behind the open door came a girl's laughter.

Mara had never heard Celeste Arden laugh. The child had
drowned months before Mara met Elias. Yet she recognized the
sound because Elias had described it so often: high, breathless,
ending in a hiccup.

Leo ran toward it.

Mara caught his arm.

"That might not be her."

"It is."

"It might be something pretending."

"Then it knows her better than anyone else."

He pulled free and entered the final room.

The linen sheet had fallen away.

Elias's machine was taller than Mara remembered. It resembled a
clock assembled by someone who had only heard clocks described
in dreams. Copper ribs surrounded a circular chamber. Glass tubes
twisted between black iron gears. At its center was an
empty socket shaped like a twelve-pointed star.

The missing gear.

Leo placed the storm jar on the floor. Clouds inside
it spun faster.

"She said the storm would complete the machine," he said.

"No. A storm is not a gear."

"Maybe it doesn't need to be."

Before Mara could stop him, Leo broke the copper seal.

The lid flew upward.

The storm escaped.

It did not expand into the room. Instead, it poured
like smoke into the empty socket. Lightning crawled over the
machine's frame. The gears began turning, slowly at first, then
faster.

A circle of silver light opened inside the chamber.

Mara saw the river.

Snow lined its banks. Broken ice floated on black water.
An eight-year-old girl stood barefoot in the current, looking up
at something beyond Mara's sight.

"Celeste!" Mara called.

The girl turned.

The vision changed.

Celeste was older now, sitting at a kitchen table while
a baby slept in her arms. Then older again, laughing
as she bottled a yellow cloud. Then pale and frightened,
writing a note while parts of her body flickered like
a failing lamp.

Behind each image stood a shadow.

Elias.

He remained nineteen, unchanged, following his sister through every stolen
year.

"Is he alive?" Leo asked.

Mara stepped closer to the chamber.

Elias seemed to hear him. His shadow turned.

The silver circle widened.

A hand reached through.

It was Elias's hand, burned across the palm exactly as
Mara remembered.

"Mara," he said.

Twenty-eight years disappeared from her.

She was sixteen again, standing in a cellar full of
stolen electricity, believing every locked door existed only to be
opened.

She reached for him.

Then she saw his face.

Elias had not aged, but time had marked him in
other ways. His eyes contained flickering seasons. Snow fell across
his hair and vanished. His skin shifted from sunburned to
deathly pale.

"Help me," he said.

Mara's fingers hovered inches from his.

"What happened to Celeste?"

"I pulled her out too far. I tried to guide
her back, but every year created another path. She is
scattered between them."

"Can we bring her here?"

"Yes."

His answer was too quick.

"What will it cost?"

The seasons in his eyes accelerated.

"The machine needs an anchor," he said. "A life fixed
in one time. Someone must hold the path open."

"For how long?"

He did not answer.

"Forever?" Mara asked.

Leo moved beside her. "I'll do it."

"No," Mara and Elias said together.

The machine shuddered. In the chamber, Celeste appeared as a
grown woman. She was pounding soundlessly against an invisible wall.

Leo cried, "Mum!"

She saw him.

For one instant, everything in the room stopped.

Then the machine dragged Leo forward.

Mara seized the back of his coat. His feet left
the floor. The silver opening pulled at them with the
force of a waterfall. Loose papers flew from the corridor.
Exhibit labels, feathers, dry flowers, and unfinished songs spiraled through
the room.

Elias reached from the other side.

"Turn it off!" Mara shouted.

"I can't."

"You built it!"

"I never finished it!"

The truth struck her with almost comic simplicity.

Of course.

The museum did not contain failed things. It contained stopped
things. Paintings stopped before the final stroke. Letters stopped before
the fatal sentence. Machines stopped before desire became disaster.

Everything there had been stopped for a reason.

Mara looked at the iron gears. One spun out of
rhythm, catching every fourth turn. She remembered Elias's sketches, the
nights of calculation, the correction he had refused to make
because it would reduce the machine's power.

There was a brake.

Not a lever or button, but an absence deliberately designed
into the mechanism: a narrow space where a small object
could jam the central gear.

Mara reached into her pocket.

Her fingers closed around the iron key to the final
room.

"Elias," she said, "let go."

His hand remained extended.

"If it closes, I lose her."

"You lost her a long time ago."

"No."

"Yes." Mara tightened her grip on Leo. "And then you
lost yourself."

Celeste's image flickered behind him. For a moment she was
eight, then twenty, then forty-one. Each version looked exhausted.

Elias turned toward his sister.

She placed her palm against the invisible wall.

He matched it with his own.

They remained that way for a heartbeat.

Then Elias stepped aside.

"Mara," he said, and smiled as he had in the
watchmaker's cellar, when the broken cup became whole. "You got
old."

"So will you," she said. "If you're lucky."

She thrust the iron key into the gears.

The machine screamed.

Teeth snapped. Glass burst. The storm roared out of the
central socket and filled the room with blinding light.

Mara felt Leo torn from her hands.

Then the world went dark.

She woke to the smell of bread.

Morning sunlight streamed through a broken window. Flour drifted in
from the bakery next door, coating the museum floor in
white dust.

Mara sat up.

The time machine had collapsed into a heap of metal.
The iron key was fused between two gears.

Leo lay near the wall.

Beside him was a woman.

She was thin, barefoot, and wrapped in a coat dusted
with snow. Though Mara had never seen her as an
adult outside the machine, she recognized Celeste immediately. She had
Elias's dark eyebrows and the same stubborn line to her
mouth.

Leo opened his eyes.

For several seconds he stared at the woman as though
afraid that recognition might frighten her away.

"Mum?"

Celeste woke.

She pulled him into her arms.

Neither spoke. Their silence held more than words could have
carried.

Mara looked toward the ruined chamber.

Elias was not there.

On the floor, beneath a curved piece of copper, she
found a small brass gear shaped like a twelve-pointed star.

It was warm in her hand.

The museum remained closed for three months.

Repairs took time, and explanations took longer. Mara told the
authorities that a lightning strike had damaged the building. They
pointed out that the storm had occurred inside. Mara blamed
old wiring. The electrician, after examining the melted gears and
finding snow beneath the floorboards, supported her story without question.

Celeste and Leo moved into the rooms above the museum.
Celeste recovered slowly. Some mornings she woke at eight years
old and called for her brother. Some evenings she remembered
events that had never happened: Leo's wedding, a city under
glass, Mara dying peacefully at ninety-six.

"Possibilities," she explained. "They fade."

She returned to weather-making, though she worked cautiously. Her new
jars contained harmless things: fog that smelled of pine, rain
that fell only on thirsty plants, and a tiny rainbow
that appeared whenever someone lied nearby.

The rainbow was especially popular with school groups.

Mara rebuilt the final gallery but left the center empty.
The broken time machine was divided among several display cases
so no one could restore it easily. She wrote a
new label:

**AN INSTRUMENT FOR THE RECOVERY OF LOST TIME**

**Maker: Elias Arden**

**Status: Finished**

For the first time in the museum's history, visitors complained.

"If it is finished," they asked, "why is it here?"

Mara would smile.

"Because finishing is not always the same as succeeding."

Years passed.

Leo grew taller than his mother. He became the museum's
assistant, then its conservator. He had a gift for knowing
which objects should be repaired and which should remain as
they were.

He finished the stringless violin but left the handless clocks
alone.

He completed three pages of an abandoned novel and then
stopped because, he said, the heroine preferred uncertainty.

He never touched the plaster moon.

On Mara's seventy-second birthday, a parcel arrived without a return
address.

It was wrapped in green cloth.

Mara carried it to the workroom, where Celeste and Leo
gathered beside her. Inside was a wooden box, scarred by
heat and water. A brass latch held it closed.

There was no note.

Mara opened the box.

Within lay a cracked teacup.

She knew it at once. Blue flowers circled its rim.
A stain darkened the bottom.

Underneath it was a folded sheet of paper.

The handwriting leaned impatiently forward.

**Dear Mara,**

**I took your advice.**

**It has been difficult to grow old because time here
does not travel in a straight line. Some years arrive
twice. Others do not arrive at all. Yesterday I was
thirty. Tomorrow I expect to be twenty-four. Still, I am
doing my best.**

**Celeste was never mine to save, but I am glad
we tried once, and gladder that you knew when to
stop.**

**The cup broke again. I have decided not to fix
it.**

**There are better things to do.**

**Elias**

Mara read the letter twice.

Then she laughed.

It began quietly and ended in a hiccup.

That evening they placed the cracked cup in the first
gallery. Leo wrote the label, Celeste adjusted the light, and
Mara set the letter beside it.

Before locking the doors, Mara walked alone through the museum.

The unfinished portraits watched her pass. The silent violins rested
in their cases. The helmet for remembering dreams reflected the
dim lamps. Everywhere she looked, she saw pauses instead of
failures, choices instead of absences.

At the end of the corridor, she entered the final
room.

The broken pieces of the machine gleamed behind glass. On
the wall, where the silver opening had once shown a
hundred stolen years, Leo had hung an empty frame.

Mara stood before it.

For an instant, she thought she saw Elias reflected there—not
young, not old, but somewhere in between. He raised one
burned hand.

She raised hers.

Then the image was only her own.

She switched off the light and closed the door.

Outside, rain began to fall upward.

Drops lifted from the street in silver lines, rising past
chimneys and rooftops toward the clouds. People came out of
shops to stare. Children ran laughing through the dry rain.
Bakers, watchmakers, tourists, and tired office workers turned their faces
to the sky.

Mara stepped into the street.

Celeste and Leo joined her.

No one tried to explain the weather.

Some things, Mara had learned, did not need to be
completed, repaired, or understood.

They needed only to be witnessed before they passed beyond
reach.

### The Museum of Unfinished Things

At the end of Bellweather Street, squeezed between a bakery
and a boarded-up cinema, stood a narrow building that nobody
seemed to own.

Its bricks were the gray of rain. Its windows reflected
skies that did not belong to the season: snow clouds
in July, summer lightning in December. Above the door hung
a brass sign, green at the edges with age.

**THE MUSEUM OF UNFINISHED THINGS**

Mara Venn had passed it twice each day for seven
years. She had never seen anyone enter or leave.

Then, on the morning of her thirty-fourth birthday, the door
was open.

She stopped on the pavement while commuters moved around her.
Inside, a corridor stretched farther than the building should have
allowed, lit by hanging lamps. A woman stood at the
far end, holding a feather duster.

Mara checked her watch. She was already late for work.

She went in.

A bell chimed above the door, though the bell had
no clapper.

The woman with the duster approached. She was very old,
or perhaps simply tired. Her white hair was braided down
her back, and she wore a dark blue suit with
hundreds of tiny pockets.

"Good morning," she said. "Admission is free, but leaving costs
something."

Mara glanced behind her. The open door framed Bellweather Street:
the bakery, the bus stop, a red scarf caught in
the wind.

"What does it cost?"

"That depends on what you find."

"I only have five minutes."

"No one comes here with more."

The old woman handed her a square paper ticket. On
it, in faded ink, was written:

**MARA VENN — ONE VISIT ONLY**

"I didn't tell you my name."

"You left it unfinished," said the woman. "We collect such
things."

Before Mara could ask what that meant, the old woman
turned and walked away.

The first gallery was called **WORDS NEVER SAID**.

Glass cases lined the walls. Inside each lay a sentence
written on a slip of paper.

*I knew it was you.*

*Please don't go.*

*I'm sorry I laughed.*

*Yes.*

*No.*

Some were written carefully in ink. Others had been scratched
in pencil or lipstick. One had been burned into a
strip of wood.

Mara moved slowly between them.

Near the end of the gallery, she found a small
display with no label. Under the glass lay a folded
birthday card. The front showed a cartoon moon wearing a
nightcap.

She recognized it at once.

She had bought that card when she was seventeen. It
had been intended for her younger brother, Eli, who was
turning twelve. Inside, she had written a long apology for
not believing him when he said their mother's boyfriend had
been stealing money from his bedroom.

Mara had discovered the truth two weeks later. By then
Eli had gone to live with their father in another
city. She had carried the card in her schoolbag for
months, then hidden it at the bottom of a drawer.

She had never sent it.

With a trembling hand, she lifted the glass lid.

The card smelled faintly of pencil shavings and the lavender
soap their mother used to buy. Mara opened it.

The apology was gone.

Instead, the card contained a single line in Eli's handwriting.

*I waited for this.*

Mara dropped it.

The old woman appeared beside her.

"That isn't possible," Mara said.

"Possibility is rarely relevant here."

"Did he write that?"

"In a manner of speaking."

"Where did you get this?"

"From your drawer, on the day you threw it away."

"I burned it."

"Yes. We have excellent collectors."

Mara looked again at the card. "Can I take it?"

"You can take anything that belongs to you."

"What's the price?"

"You must finish it."

"How?"

But the old woman was already walking through an archway
into the next gallery.

Mara picked up the card and followed.

The second gallery was immense. A painted sign announced **ROADS
NOT TAKEN**, although the room contained no roads. Instead, doors
stood in rows beneath a high glass ceiling.

Each door was different. Some were polished oak with brass
handles. Some were painted in bright colors. One was made
of steel and had three locks. Another was a child-sized
hatch with a sunflower carved into it.

"Do they open?" Mara asked.

"All doors open," said the old woman. "That is their
danger."

She stopped before a plain white door marked **2009**.

Mara knew the year. It was the year she had
been offered a place at the Royal Academy of Art.
It was also the year her mother's lungs began to
fail.

She had stayed home.

For three years Mara cared for her mother. She cooked,
cleaned, organized medications, argued with doctors, and slept on a
sofa near the bedroom. After her mother died, the academy
offer was long gone. Mara took a job processing insurance
claims and stopped drawing.

The old woman opened the white door.

On the other side lay a sunlit studio. Canvases leaned
against white walls. The air smelled of turpentine and coffee.
A woman stood at an easel with her back to
them.

She was Mara, older by perhaps five years, her hair
cut short and streaked with silver. She wore paint-splattered overalls.
On the easel was a portrait of Eli.

In the painting, he was no longer twelve. He was
a grown man with tired eyes and a crooked smile.

The painted Mara turned.

"You took your time," she said.

Mara stepped backward. "Who are you?"

"An unfinished possibility."

"Did you go to the academy?"

"Yes."

"And Mum?"

"She died while I was in Florence. I arrived home
a day too late."

The words struck like cold water.

"Were you successful?"

The other Mara considered the question. "Sometimes."

"Happy?"

"Sometimes."

"Did you stay close to Eli?"

"No." She glanced at his portrait. "That is why I
paint him."

Mara stared at the portrait. "I thought if I'd gone,
everything would have been better."

"Everything is never better. Everything is only different."

The old woman closed the door.

The bright studio vanished.

Mara stood silently among the rows of doors.

"People imagine the unlived life as a perfect country," said
the old woman. "They forget it has weather."

Mara held Eli's birthday card against her chest. "Why show
me that?"

"We display. Interpretation is the visitor's burden."

"My brother and I barely speak. Once a year, maybe.
Christmas. If one of us remembers."

"And you believe this is because you stayed?"

"I don't know what I believe."

"That is another unfinished thing."

The old woman led her into a room filled with
music.

Melodies drifted through the air without visible instruments. A violin
repeated four rising notes, always stopping before the fifth. A
drumbeat waited forever for a chorus. A woman's voice sang
the first half of a lullaby. Somewhere, a piano chord
hung unresolved, filling the room with a longing so precise
that Mara's eyes watered.

This was the **HALL OF ABANDONED SONGS**.

In the center stood a wooden table. On it lay
a sketchbook.

Mara did not need to open it to know it
was hers.

When she was nineteen, she had planned a series of
charcoal drawings about the women in her family. Her grandmother
shelling peas. Her mother asleep at the kitchen table. Herself
and Eli standing ankle-deep in the sea. She completed only
seven pages before hospital appointments and part-time shifts consumed her
days.

After her mother's death, she could no longer bear to
look at the book.

Now she opened it.

The first drawing showed her grandmother's hands, knotted by arthritis
but still graceful. The second was her mother laughing, head
thrown back. Mara had forgotten that laugh. She had remembered
only the coughing.

On the final used page was an outline of Eli
at eleven years old. He stood on a railway platform,
one hand raised. His face had never been drawn.

Mara ran her fingertips over the blank oval.

"Why did I stop?" she whispered.

The old woman said nothing.

"I could have finished it later."

"Yes."

"But later kept becoming later."

"It often does."

"I don't even know if I can draw anymore."

"Can you?"

Mara looked around. "Do you have a pencil?"

The old woman searched several of her pockets and produced
a short stick of charcoal.

Mara sat at the table.

At first, her hand felt wooden. The charcoal made uncertain
marks. She tried to remember Eli's face at eleven: the
narrow chin, the heavy eyebrows, the small scar near his
left ear. Yet every feature slipped away as she reached
for it.

She closed her eyes.

A memory came to her.

They were children sitting beneath the kitchen table during a
thunderstorm. Eli was six, Mara eleven. Each time lightning flashed,
he gripped her sleeve. To distract him, she drew animals
on the underside of the table with a green crayon.
A bear with wings. A fish wearing boots. A rabbit
as tall as a house.

Their mother discovered the drawings days later and tried to
be angry, but the booted fish made her laugh.

Mara opened her eyes and began again.

She did not draw Eli at eleven. She drew him
at six, face tilted toward imaginary creatures, fear giving way
to wonder.

The charcoal moved more easily now.

When she finished, the room's unresolved piano chord finally changed.

One note descended.

It was not a grand conclusion, but it was enough.

The old woman leaned over the sketchbook. "Not accurate."

"No," said Mara. "But true."

"Better."

Mara closed the sketchbook. "I'm taking this too."

"It will be heavy."

"It's a book."

"Not for long."

They entered a circular chamber with a black domed ceiling.
Thousands of glass jars rested on shelves climbing toward darkness.
Each jar contained something different: blue smoke, a moth, a
child's marble, a wedding ring, a flake of ash, a
tiny thunderstorm.

A sign above the shelves read **THINGS WE MEANT TO
KEEP**.

Mara walked between the shelves until she found a jar
containing a red plastic whistle.

She laughed aloud.

It had belonged to Eli. During one terrible summer, he
blew it constantly—in the house, in the garden, once during
a funeral. Mara stole it and hid it inside a
broken drainpipe. That autumn, a storm washed it away.

Eli searched for weeks.

She had never confessed.

Mara lifted the jar. The whistle rattled inside.

"This is ridiculous," she said.

"Most precious things are."

"He probably doesn't even remember it."

"Then why do you?"

She unscrewed the lid. The whistle dropped into her palm,
scratched and faded. Without thinking, she blew.

The sound was shrill enough to wake the dead.

All the jars began to tremble.

Blue smoke curled against glass. The moth beat its wings.
The tiny thunderstorm flashed. Somewhere high above, a jar shattered,
and a burst of golden laughter poured down like rain.

The old woman smiled.

"Please refrain from touching the exhibits."

"You gave it to me."

"I was hoping you had matured."

Mara put the whistle in her coat pocket.

Beyond the chamber was a narrow staircase descending into darkness.
At the bottom stood a red door.

The old woman stopped.

"This is where the tour ends."

"What's behind it?"

"Your last unfinished thing."

Mara looked down at the card and sketchbook in her
hands. "I thought these were my unfinished things."

"No. These are symptoms."

"Of what?"

The old woman pointed to the door.

Mara opened it.

She found herself in her own apartment.

Gray morning light fell through the curtains. A cup waited
beside the sink. A dead plant leaned toward the window.
The walls were bare except for a framed print purchased
from a furniture store.

At the dining table sat another Mara.

This one was very old.

Her hair was white, her shoulders curved. She wore Mara's
favorite green sweater, patched at the elbows. Before her lay
the unfinished sketchbook, still unfinished. Beside it sat a telephone.

Old Mara looked toward the doorway.

"I wondered when you'd arrive."

Mara did not enter. "Are you a possibility too?"

"Yes."

"Which one?"

"The easiest."

Something in the apartment frightened Mara more than the impossible
rooms of the museum. Everything was familiar. Nothing had changed.
The same curtains. The same print. The same silence.

"What happened to us?" Mara asked.

"Nothing dramatic."

"And Eli?"

"Christmas calls stopped. Neither of us remembers exactly when."

"Did you ever send the card?"

"No."

"Did you draw?"

"Sometimes I sharpened a pencil."

Mara looked at the telephone. "Why didn't you call him?"

Old Mara smiled without humor. "I was waiting until I
knew what to say."

"And did you ever figure it out?"

"I had several excellent opening sentences. They all sounded foolish
by morning."

Mara stepped into the room. "You could have just said
hello."

"Could I?"

"Yes."

"Then you do it."

The telephone rang.

Old Mara vanished.

The green sweater collapsed onto the empty chair.

The telephone continued ringing.

Mara approached it. Her heart beat painfully. She picked up
the receiver.

"Hello?"

For several seconds there was only static.

Then a man said, "Mara?"

She knew his voice, though it was deeper than the
one she remembered.

"Eli?"

"Are you all right?"

"I don't know."

"You called me."

"No, I—" She turned. The old woman stood in the
doorway. "Where are you?"

"At home. It's six in the morning."

"I'm sorry."

"Is Mum's old clock broken again?"

"What?"

"You only call this early when the clocks change."

Mara laughed, and the sound broke halfway out of her.

"Are you sure you're all right?" Eli asked.

She gripped the receiver. She wanted to explain the museum,
the card, the portrait in the sunlit studio, the old
woman with a hundred pockets. She wanted to apologize for
the money, the whistle, the years of silence, every birthday
reduced to a message sent too late in the day.

Instead she said, "Do you remember the drawings under the
kitchen table?"

Silence.

Then Eli laughed.

"The fish in boots?"

"Yes."

"Mum blamed me."

"She did not."

"She absolutely did."

"You cried until I confessed."

"I was six."

"You cried often."

"You dressed the cat in doll clothes."

"The cat liked it."

"The cat left home."

Mara sat in the chair. "I found the sketchbook."

"What sketchbook?"

"The one with the drawings of Mum and Grandma."

Another silence, softer this time.

"I thought you'd thrown it away," he said.

"So did I."

"What do you mean?"

"Nothing. I mean, I still have it." She opened to
the new charcoal drawing. "I drew you."

"Oh God."

"With hair."

"Then it's historical fiction."

She laughed again. This time the sound came cleanly.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"For the hair?"

"For not believing you. About the money. Back then."

Eli did not answer.

Mara forced herself to continue. "I wrote you a card,
but I never sent it. I kept thinking an apology
had to be perfect or it would only make things
worse."

"I know."

"You know?"

"Dad found the first draft in your schoolbag when we
came back for my clothes."

Mara closed her eyes. "Why didn't you say anything?"

"I was twelve. Then I was thirteen. Then it got
strange to bring up."

"I should have called."

"So should I."

"I stole your red whistle."

"I knew that."

"What?"

"You were a terrible liar. Where is it?"

Mara touched her coat pocket. "You wouldn't believe me."

"Try."

The red door behind her began to close.

The old woman beckoned.

"I have to go," Mara said.

"Work?"

"Something like that."

"Mara?"

"Yes?"

"I'm in town next Thursday."

"You are?"

"For a conference. I could come by."

Her instinct was to think of the apartment, the dead
plant, the empty cupboards, the unfinished tasks piled across her
life. She almost said that Thursday was difficult.

Instead she said, "Come for dinner."

"You cook now?"

"No. We can order something."

"Good. I was worried this call was a hallucination."

"It might be."

"Thursday, then."

"Thursday."

"And Mara?"

"Yes?"

"Bring the sketchbook."

The red door was almost shut.

"I will," she said.

She put down the receiver and slipped through the gap.

The door clicked closed behind her.

She was once again in the museum's entrance hall. Through
the glass front door, Bellweather Street looked exactly as it
had before. The red scarf remained suspended in the wind.
A bus waited at the corner. Not a second had
passed.

The old woman held out her hand.

"Payment," she said.

Mara clutched the sketchbook and card. "You said leaving costs
something."

"It does."

"What do you want?"

"The easiest life."

Mara looked back toward the corridor, but all the galleries
had vanished. A brick wall stood where the first archway
had been.

"I don't understand."

"You have carried many possible lives. The artist. The dutiful
daughter. The distant sister. The old woman beside the silent
telephone. You use them to judge the life you have,
but you refuse to release any of them."

"How do I pay with a life?"

"Choose one not to mourn."

Mara thought of the bright studio beyond the white door.
Canvases against the walls. Paint on her hands. Florence. Success,
sometimes. Happiness, sometimes. Her mother dying while she hurried home.

She had envied that life for fifteen years.

Slowly, she opened the birthday card. Eli's sentence was still
there.

*I waited for this.*

Beneath it, Mara wrote with the charcoal:

*I'm here now.*

The words smudged beneath her fingers.

"I give up the life where leaving solved everything," she
said.

The old woman nodded.

Something lifted from Mara—not pain, exactly, but the shape she
had forced pain to wear. For years, she had imagined
regret as proof that the other road was better. Now
she saw it was only proof that a road existed.

The museum door opened.

Mara stepped onto the pavement.

Behind her, the old woman said, "You've forgotten something."

Mara turned.

The old woman tossed her the red whistle. Mara caught
it in one hand.

"Thursday," the old woman said. "Don't be late."

The door closed.

The brass sign above it now read:

**TO LET — ENQUIRE WITHIN**

The windows reflected an ordinary gray sky.

Mara stood there until someone bumped her shoulder and muttered
an apology. Traffic began moving again. The red scarf flew
down the street and vanished beneath a parked car.

Her phone said she was nine minutes late for work.

She looked at the office tower in the distance, its
mirrored windows pale in the morning. Then she looked at
the sketchbook under her arm.

At the bakery next door, she bought a coffee, a
blueberry pastry, and a pencil. She sat at a table
by the window and emailed her manager to say she
would not be coming in that day.

For nearly an hour, she stared at a blank page.

The museum had made drawing seem like revelation. Outside it,
drawing was harder. The pencil needed sharpening. The coffee cooled.
A child at the next table kicked the leg of
her chair. Mara's first line was wrong, and the second
was worse.

She nearly closed the book.

Then the baker came out carrying a tray of bread.
Flour covered both his arms to the elbows. He paused
beneath the kitchen light, looking exhausted and proud.

Mara began to draw his hands.

She drew slowly.

She made mistakes.

When she reached the edge of the page, she continued
on the next one.

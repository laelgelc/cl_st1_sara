## The Keeper of Lost Hours

On the morning the town lost an hour, only Mara
Venn noticed.

At six o'clock, the bakery chimed its copper bell. At
seven, the ferryman sounded his horn from the river. But
between those two familiar notes, something had gone missing. The
sun jumped above the tiled roofs as if startled, shadows
shifted sharply across the market square, and every clock in
Bellweather moved from six twenty-three to seven twenty-four without passing
through the minutes in between.

People hurried from their houses, fastening buttons and carrying half-eaten
breakfasts.

"Late again," muttered the baker.

"Must have slept badly," said the ferryman.

Mara stood outside her father's clock shop, holding the key
she had been about to turn in the lock. She
remembered the missing hour clearly—not what had happened during it,
but the shape of its absence. It felt like reaching
for a step in the dark and finding empty air.

Inside the shop, one hundred and twelve clocks ticked along
the walls. Tall clocks, round clocks, brass clocks, clocks painted
with blue flowers, clocks small enough to fit in a
pocket. All of them showed seven twenty-four.

All except one.

On the highest shelf stood a black wooden clock with
no hands. It had belonged to Mara's father, who had
disappeared seven years before. The clock had never ticked in
all that time.

Now it whispered.

Mara climbed the ladder and lifted it down. A faint
scratching came from behind its face, like a moth trapped
under paper. On the back, where the wood had always
been smooth, a small door had appeared.

She opened it.

A folded note dropped into her palm.

Mara knew the handwriting before she read the words. The
letters leaned forward as though always in a hurry.

*Dear Mara,*

*If you are reading this, Bellweather has begun to lose
time.*

*Do not trust the clocks.*

*Follow the river after midnight, and bring the handless clock.*

*I am sorry.*

*Father*

She read the note three times.

Outside, the town carried on. Carts rattled over cobblestones. Market
vendors raised striped awnings. A woman complained about the price
of pears. No one shouted that the morning had cracked
open. No one ran through the square demanding the return
of sixty-one stolen minutes.

Mara closed the shop.

For seven years, people had offered explanations for her father's
disappearance. He had fallen into the river. He had run
away to escape his debts. He had gone mad from
listening to too many clocks. The mayor had ordered a
search of the woods and downstream banks, but nothing had
been found—not a coat, not a tool, not a footprint.

Mara had been thirteen. She had waited by the window
every evening until she was sixteen.

At twenty, she no longer waited.

At least, that was what she told herself.

That night, she wrapped the handless clock in a woolen
shawl and left home at eleven forty. She followed the
road toward the river, passing the shuttered bakery and the
dark bell tower.

At the edge of town, she met Elias Thorne.

He was sitting on the low stone wall beside the
road, wearing his ferryman's coat and holding a lantern.

"You're out late," Mara said.

"So are you."

"I'm taking a walk."

"With a clock?"

She tightened her grip on the bundle. "Why are you
here?"

Elias looked toward the town. "Do you remember this morning?"

Mara hesitated.

"The missing hour," he said.

"You noticed?"

"My ferry was in the middle of the river at
six twenty-three. Then it was tied to the western dock,
and the church bell was striking seven. I don't remember
crossing the rest of the way."

"Why didn't you tell anyone?"

"I told my sister. She said I needed more sleep."
He raised the lantern. "Then I remembered your father."

Mara stiffened. "What about him?"

"The night he vanished, he took my father's boat."

Elias's father had been the ferryman then. Mara remembered him
as a broad man with white eyebrows and a laugh
that carried across the river. He had died two winters
ago without ever speaking publicly about Tom Venn's disappearance.

"My father made me promise not to tell," Elias continued.
"He said your father crossed after midnight. Alone. He carried
a black clock."

Mara unwrapped one corner of the shawl.

Elias stared. "That one?"

"Yes."

"What's on the other side of the river?"

"Woods. Marshes. The abandoned observatory."

"Then we'll start there."

"We?"

"You'll need a boat."

"I can row."

"Not through the midnight current."

Mara almost argued. Then the clock moved inside her arms.

Not ticked—moved.

It gave a small, urgent shudder.

"Fine," she said. "But if we find trouble, you stay
behind me."

Elias smiled. "Naturally."

They reached the dock shortly before midnight. The river lay
black and smooth under the moon. Elias untied the narrow
ferryboat, and Mara sat in the bow with the clock
on her knees.

At twelve, the bell in town began to ring.

One.

The boat pushed away from shore.

Two.

Mist rose around them.

Three.

Elias pulled the oars, his face pale in the lantern
light.

Four.

The town faded behind a wall of silver fog.

Five.

The river widened.

Six.

Something splashed beside the boat.

Seven.

The handless clock grew warm beneath Mara's palms.

Eight.

The current caught them, spinning the boat once before Elias
forced it straight.

Nine.

Shapes appeared in the mist: doorways without walls, staircases climbing
into darkness, windows lit from within.

Ten.

Mara heard laughter from somewhere across the water. A child's
laughter. Her own, perhaps, from years ago.

Eleven.

The clock's hidden door sprang open.

Twelve.

A beam of golden light spilled from it and stretched
across the river like a road.

Then the bell fell silent.

Ahead, where only marshland should have been, stood a house.

It rose directly from the river on dozens of thin
wooden legs. Its walls were patched with stone, brick, glass,
and pieces of old clock faces. Hundreds of chimneys leaned
from its roof, breathing pale smoke into the night. Windows
glowed on every floor. Some were bright with summer sunlight;
others showed storms, sunsets, or star-filled skies.

A sign hung over the landing:

**THE HOUSE OF UNFINISHED HOURS**

Elias stopped rowing.

"I've crossed this river every day for fourteen years," he
whispered. "That has never been there."

"Perhaps it isn't always here."

The golden path led to the house. Elias guided the
boat alongside the landing, and they climbed out.

The front door opened before Mara knocked.

A woman stood inside.

She was very tall and very old, wrapped in a
gray dress fastened with silver pins. Her hair hung to
her ankles in one long braid, and around her neck
she wore a chain of tiny glass bottles. Inside each
bottle, something shimmered: rain, candlelight, drifting snow, a bird in
flight.

"At last," she said. "Tom Venn's daughter."

"Where is my father?"

The old woman smiled sadly. "Straight to the sharpest question.
You are like him."

"Is he alive?"

"That depends on what you mean by alive."

Elias stepped closer to Mara. "Usually it means not dead."

The woman studied him. "A ferryman. Good. Every difficult journey
needs one."

She turned and walked into the house.

Mara followed.

The entrance hall was larger than the whole town square.
Staircases wound up and down in every direction. Pendulums swung
from the ceiling. The air smelled of dust, pine smoke,
salt water, and bread fresh from the oven.

Doors lined the walls, each marked with a brass plaque.

**AN AFTERNOON MISSPENT**

**THE LAST TEN MINUTES BEFORE GOODBYE**

**A SUMMER NO ONE REMEMBERS**

**THREE SECONDS OF PERFECT COURAGE**

From behind the doors came muffled voices, music, sobbing, thunder,
and once the unmistakable roar of a crowd.

"What is this place?" Mara asked.

"The place where lost time goes," said the old woman.
"I am its keeper. You may call me Sable."

"Lost time doesn't go anywhere."

"Everything goes somewhere."

Sable touched one of the bottles at her throat. Inside
it, a candle flame bent in an invisible wind.

"A minute wasted in regret. An hour forgotten in sleep.
A childhood that seems to vanish overnight. Most lost time
arrives in fragments. Harmless pieces. But Bellweather is different."

"Why?"

"Your town has a wound."

They entered a circular room filled with clocks. Unlike those
in Mara's shop, none had numbers. Their hands turned backward,
sideways, or in slow spirals. In the center stood a
stone table with a hollow shaped exactly like the black
clock.

Sable pointed to it. "Put it there."

Mara held the clock tighter. "Not until you tell me
where my father is."

"Your father is the reason Bellweather still exists."

Mara said nothing.

Sable sighed.

"Many years ago, your town built its bell tower over
a natural crossing—a weak place between the present and all
the moments that might have been. Most such crossings remain
closed. But every time Bellweather's great bell rang, the crack
widened."

"The bell has rung for two hundred years," Elias said.

"Yes. And seven years ago, it broke the seal."

Mara remembered that night. A storm had shaken the town.
The bell had rung once without anyone pulling the rope.
Her father had gone out soon afterward, promising to return
before breakfast.

"He knew," she said.

"Tom repaired clocks, but he understood time. Those are not
the same skill. He discovered the crossing and came here
seeking help. Together we made a lock."

Sable looked at the bundle in Mara's arms.

"The handless clock," Mara said.

"It held the broken hour in place. But locks weaken.
This morning, one hour escaped Bellweather entirely. Tomorrow, the town
will lose two. Then four. Soon, days and years. Houses
will collapse before they are built. Children will grow old
between footsteps. The dead may return without knowing they died."

Elias glanced toward the countless doors. "How do we stop
it?"

"The lock must be repaired by the person who made
it."

Mara's heart lifted painfully. "Take me to him."

Sable did not move. "There is a cost."

"There's always a cost in stories told by old women
in impossible houses."

"That is because young people rarely listen when the price
is small."

"Tell me."

"To keep the crossing closed, your father placed himself inside
the missing hour. He has lived there ever since, repeating
the same sixty minutes, holding the edges together."

Mara imagined an hour as a room with no door.
Her father trapped inside, walking the same path, speaking the
same words, never reaching morning.

"He wrote this note."

"The clock carried it. Tom cannot leave, but his messages
sometimes can."

"If he repairs the lock, will he be free?"

"Yes."

The answer came too quickly.

"And?"

Sable's gaze rested on Mara. "Someone must take his place."

Silence filled the room, measured by the strange clocks.

Elias shook his head. "No."

Mara looked at him.

"No," he repeated. "Whatever you're thinking, no."

"You don't know what I'm thinking."

"I know exactly what you're thinking."

Sable extended a thin hand. "The clock."

Mara placed it on the stone table.

The room shuddered.

All the clocks stopped.

A doorway appeared in the air, outlined by golden light.
Beyond it was Mara's father's workshop as it had looked
seven years ago.

Her father stood at the workbench with his back to
her.

He wore a brown vest over a white shirt, sleeves
rolled to his elbows. His dark hair was tied at
the nape of his neck. He was humming the same
four notes he always hummed while working.

Mara could not breathe.

"Father?"

He turned.

The small screwdriver fell from his hand.

For a moment, neither of them moved.

Then he crossed the room and held her.

Mara had planned this meeting a thousand times. In some
versions she shouted. In others she refused to speak. Sometimes
she struck him, sometimes forgave him, sometimes walked away before
he could explain.

Instead, she buried her face in his shoulder and became
thirteen years old again.

"You took so long," she said.

"I know."

"You said you'd be back before breakfast."

"I know."

"I waited."

His arms tightened around her. "I know, little bird. I
am so sorry."

When they stepped apart, he touched her face in wonder.

"You're grown."

"You're not."

He looked exactly as he had on the night he
left. Not one line had deepened around his eyes.

"For me, it has only been an hour," he said.
"I saw you in the shop before I came here.
You were asleep in the chair with a book open
on your lap."

"The History of Mechanical Escapements."

"You said it was interesting."

"I lied."

"I knew."

She laughed, and the laugh broke into a sob.

Beyond the workshop windows, the same bolt of lightning flashed
again and again. Rain struck the glass, stopped, rose, and
struck once more.

Tom looked past her at Elias. "Jon Thorne's boy?"

"Elias."

"You have his eyebrows."

"So I'm told."

Sable entered last. "The lock is failing, Tom."

"I felt it." He approached the black clock. "How much
time?"

"Until dawn."

He opened the case and examined the empty face. "The
anchor spring is broken."

"Can you repair it?" Mara asked.

"Yes."

Hope flared again.

"But the lock still needs an anchor," he added quietly.

"I'll do it."

"No," said Elias and Tom together.

Mara ignored them. "You've been here seven years."

"One hour."

"Seven years for everyone who loved you."

Pain crossed his face.

"You should come home," she said. "I know the shop
now. I repaired Mayor Vale's moon clock, though he insisted
it was impossible. The blue regulator gains three seconds every
winter, and Mrs. Cress still brings in that ugly silver
watch twice a month because she likes talking to someone.
I kept everything."

"I never wanted you to have to."

"That doesn't matter. It's done."

Tom shook his head. "I will repair the clock and
remain."

"You can't."

"I chose this."

"You chose without asking me."

"You were a child."

"I was your child. I had a right to know
you were leaving."

The repeating lightning filled the workshop with white light.

Tom lowered his eyes.

"You are right," he said. "I told myself there was
no time to explain. That saving the town was all
that mattered. But part of me was afraid. If I
had looked at you and said goodbye, I might not
have been brave enough to go."

Mara wanted to stay angry. Anger had kept her company
for years. It had warmed the empty rooms above the
shop and steadied her hands whenever a customer spoke carelessly
about her father.

But the man before her was not the heroic figure
the town might have carved in stone, nor the coward
she had sometimes imagined. He was simply her father: loving,
frightened, clever, and wrong.

"There must be another way," Elias said.

Sable looked at him. "There is no lock without an
anchor."

"Then don't lock it."

They all turned.

Elias pointed at the glowing doorway. "You said the bell
tower stands on a crossing. The clock holds it shut.
Why repair the lock instead of destroying the crossing?"

"Because crossings cannot be destroyed," Sable said.

"Can they be moved?"

Sable went still.

Tom looked at the ferryman with sudden interest. "Moved where?"

Elias gestured around them. "Here. This house already holds lost
hours. Let it hold the broken one too."

"The house is not infinite," Sable said.

"It looks fairly infinite."

"Looks are generous. Reality is not."

Mara studied the bottles around Sable's neck. Fragments of time,
collected and contained.

"How large is the broken hour?" she asked.

"Large enough to swallow Bellweather."

"That isn't what I mean. Is every part of it
dangerous?"

Tom understood first. "No. The crossing is only open for
a moment within the hour. Midnight exactly—the instant the bell
rang during the storm."

"Then we don't need to move the whole hour," Mara
said. "Only that moment."

Sable's eyes narrowed. "A single second can be heavier than
a century."

"But it can be contained?"

"Perhaps."

"In the clock?"

"The clock was built as a lock, not a vessel."

Tom was already reaching for his tools. "Then we rebuild
it."

For the first time, Sable smiled.

They worked as dawn approached.

Tom removed the clock's wooden back and drew designs in
chalk across the bench. Mara corrected the measurements, having learned
methods developed during the seven years he had missed. At
first he frowned at her changes. Then he examined them,
grinned, and altered his design.

Elias dismantled spare clocks under their direction, bringing gears, springs,
pins, and bells. Sable vanished into the house and returned
with a glass bottle no larger than Mara's thumb.

Inside the bottle was absolute darkness.

"A second no one has lived," Sable said. "It should
have room for one that has been lived too often."

The workshop trembled. Outside, the storm flickered faster. The hour
was collapsing.

Mara fitted the bottle into the heart of the clock.
Tom installed a brass gear around it, while Elias held
the lamp steady.

"Now the anchor spring," Tom said.

They had no replacement.

Mara searched the bench. Every spring was too weak, too
short, or rusted through.

"What happens without it?" Elias asked.

"The clock cannot close around the captured second," Tom said.

A deep bell sounded somewhere beyond the repeating hour.

Dawn's first strike.

Cracks spread across the workshop walls.

Sable's voice came from the doorway. "Bellweather is losing another
hour."

Mara thought of her shop and its hundred clocks. She
thought of metal hands turning together, each driven by a
tightly coiled ribbon of steel.

"My watch."

She pulled a small brass watch from her pocket. It
had been the first mechanism her father taught her to
repair. Inside was a fine blue spring, rare and strong.

Tom hesitated. "That was your mother's."

"I know."

Once, she would have guarded it because it was one
of the few things time had not taken. Now she
opened the case.

Her father removed the spring.

The bell sounded again.

The ceiling split. Through the crack, Mara saw Bellweather beneath
a rising sun. Townspeople moved backward through the streets. Birds
hung motionless in the sky.

Tom fitted the blue spring into the black clock.

"Ready," he said.

Sable uncorked the glass bottle.

The storm roared into the workshop.

For one terrible instant, Mara stood in many times at
once. She was six, riding on her father's shoulders. She
was thirteen, waiting by the window. She was eighteen, taking
down the shop sign during a winter gale. She was
old, with silver hair and oil-stained hands. She was never
born. She was a mother. She was alone. She was
laughing beside Elias on a sunlit ferry.

Then midnight entered the bottle.

Sable drove in the cork.

"Close the clock!" she shouted.

Mara and Tom pressed the case together. Elias turned the
winding key.

The blue spring tightened.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

The handless clock struck a single clear note.

The golden doorway vanished.

Mara fell to her knees on the riverbank.

Morning sunlight spread over the water. The impossible house was
gone. Beside her, Elias lay in the grass, still holding
the winding key. Sable stood ankle-deep in the river, her
gray dress untouched by the current.

Tom was nowhere to be seen.

"No," Mara whispered.

Then someone coughed behind her.

She turned.

Her father sat against a willow tree, staring up through
its green leaves. He looked older now. Not seven years
older, but a little: silver at his temples, new lines
beside his mouth, time settling gently onto him as though
deciding where it belonged.

Mara crawled across the grass and embraced him.

Across the river, Bellweather's clocks began striking seven.

Every clock rang at once, but none agreed on the
number of chimes. The sound rolled over the rooftops in
a wild, joyful confusion.

"What happens now?" Elias asked Sable.

"Now your town must repair its clocks."

"I meant to time."

Sable looked at the black clock in Mara's hands. A
single golden hand had appeared on its face. It did
not move.

"Time will continue," she said. "It usually does."

She began walking into the river.

"Wait," Mara called. "What if the bottle breaks?"

"Then find me before midnight."

"How?"

Sable glanced back. Morning light shone through her until she
seemed made of mist.

"Everyone finds the House of Unfinished Hours eventually."

Then she disappeared.

They returned by ferry.

In the town square, people stood in confusion. Some insisted
it was Monday, others Thursday. The baker had produced twenty-three
loaves without remembering making them. Mayor Vale claimed to have
delivered the same speech four times. A little girl had
lost her first tooth and grown another in its place.

But Bellweather remained.

Mara reopened the clock shop that afternoon.

She placed the black clock on the highest shelf, behind
glass, and hung a sign beneath it:

**NOT FOR SALE. DO NOT WIND.**

Tom sat at his old workbench, turning tools over in
his hands and marveling at how their handles had worn
with use. Customers gathered at the windows when word spread
that he had returned. Some called it a miracle. Others
called it suspicious. Mrs. Cress brought her ugly silver watch
and cried for twenty minutes.

Elias repaired the ferryboat's cracked oar, though there was nothing
wrong with it before their journey. He began stopping by
the shop each evening, usually with bread or river gossip,
always claiming he happened to be passing.

The town council voted to silence the great bell forever.
Mara objected.

"A bell isn't guilty because someone built it in the
wrong place," she said.

Instead, they moved the tower stone by stone to the
northern hill.

It took a year.

On the day the work was finished, the whole town
gathered to hear it ring. Tom stood beside Mara, older
by one honest year. Elias stood on her other side.

At noon, the bell sounded.

Its voice traveled across Bellweather, over the river, and into
the woods beyond. Nothing vanished. No doors appeared in the
air. The clocks continued their steady ticking.

Yet Mara knew time had not become tame.

It could still rush and drag. It could steal and
heal. It could turn fathers into strangers and strangers into
family. It could not be kept, no matter how many
clocks lined the walls. It could only be noticed while
it passed.

That evening, Mara climbed to the highest shelf and checked
the black clock.

The golden hand had moved by one second.

Behind the glass, something flickered—a storm, a bell, an instant
held safely in darkness.

Then the hidden door in the back clicked open, and
a new folded note fell out.

The handwriting was unfamiliar.

Mara unfolded it.

*To the keeper of the clock,*

*My town has begun to lose time.*

*Please come before we lose each other.*

She read the note twice, then looked toward the window.

Outside, Elias was securing the ferry for the night. Tom
was closing the shutters. Beyond them, the river reflected the
first stars.

Mara took the black clock from its shelf.

"Father," she called, "how quickly can we pack?"

Tom saw the note in her hand. For a moment,
he looked afraid.

Then he smiled.

"Before midnight," he said.

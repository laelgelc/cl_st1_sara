## The Keeper of Lost Hours

At the far end of Bellweather Street, where the cobbles
descended toward the sea, there stood a shop with no
sign above its door.

It was narrow and crooked, squeezed between a bakery and
a boarded-up tailor's. Its windows were always dark, even at
noon, and filled with objects no sensible shopkeeper would expect
to sell: cracked sundials, stopped watches, hourglasses without sand, calendars
opened to vanished years.

Mara Vale passed the shop every morning on her way
to the post office. She had lived in Bellweather all
her life, yet she had never seen anyone enter or
leave it.

Then, on the first Tuesday of November, she noticed something
new in the window.

A silver pocket watch rested on a square of blue
velvet. Its lid was engraved with a small bird in
flight.

Mara stopped so suddenly that Mr. Phelps, hurrying behind her,
walked into her shoulder.

"Sorry," he muttered, stepping around her.

She scarcely heard him.

Her father had owned a watch exactly like that.

He had carried it through thirty years of tides and
storms, tucked safely into the inside pocket of his oilskin
coat. When Mara was a child, he would let her
wind it before he sailed. She remembered the resistance of
the little crown beneath her fingers, the solemn tick beginning
under her palm.

Eight years ago, his boat had disappeared in a winter
storm. No wreckage had been found. Neither had the watch.

Mara tried the shop door.

The handle turned.

A bell chimed somewhere deep inside, though no bell hung
above the frame.

The air smelled of cedar, dust, and rain. Shelves rose
to the ceiling, crowded with clocks of every size. Tall
grandfather clocks leaned beside painted cuckoo clocks. Brass carriage clocks
gleamed in glass cases. Hundreds of hands pointed to hundreds
of different hours, but the room was silent.

At the back, beneath a green-shaded lamp, sat an old
man in a dark waistcoat. He was repairing a clock
with a pair of silver tweezers.

"You're late," he said.

Mara closed the door behind her. "For what?"

The old man looked up. His eyes were pale gray
and surprisingly sharp.

"For coming in."

He returned to his work.

Mara glanced toward the window. From inside the shop, the
silver watch was nowhere to be seen.

"There was a pocket watch," she said. "In the window."

"There are many watches."

"Silver. A bird on the lid."

The old man's hands became still.

After a moment, he set down the tweezers. "Ah."

"That watch belonged to my father."

"Perhaps."

"It did."

"Belonging is a difficult matter," the old man said. "A
key belongs to a lock until the lock is changed.
A name belongs to a person until no one remembers
it. Time belongs to us only until we spend it."

Mara had no patience for riddles. "Is it for sale?"

"No."

"Then why put it in the window?"

"I didn't."

She stared at him.

The old man sighed, pushed back his chair, and crossed
to a tall cabinet. He was smaller than he had
appeared while seated, with narrow shoulders and a slight limp.
From the cabinet he removed the silver watch.

Mara recognized the scratches on the lid. One long mark
crossed the engraved bird's wing, made years ago when her
father had dropped the watch on the harbor steps.

She reached for it.

The old man closed his fingers around the watch.

"Where did you get that?"

"It arrived."

"How?"

"The way lost things usually arrive. By being lost."

"My father had it with him when his boat went
down."

"I know."

The words struck harder than she expected.

"You knew him?"

"No."

"Then how could you know?"

Instead of answering, the old man placed the watch on
the counter. Between them, it seemed to give off a
faint warmth.

"My name is Orin Quill," he said. "I keep this
shop. People lose all sorts of things. Gloves, coins, letters.
Most can be found again. But sometimes people lose minutes,
hours, even years. Those come here."

Mara gave a short, disbelieving laugh. "Lost hours."

"Yes."

"You sell time?"

"Certainly not. Selling time is the business of employers. I
keep it."

She should have left. The postmaster would already be wondering
where she was. There were parcels to sort, stamps to
count, and a shipment of winter catalogues waiting in the
back room.

Yet the silver watch lay within reach.

"Open it," Orin said.

Mara hesitated, then pressed the catch.

The lid sprang up.

The hands were moving.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

The sound filled the shop. All around her, the silent
clocks seemed to listen.

The watch showed six minutes past four.

"That's the time his boat left the harbor," Mara whispered.

"Wind it."

She touched the crown.

The shop vanished.

Cold spray struck her face. She stood on the deck
of a fishing boat beneath a bruised sky. Waves rose
like black hills, flinging foam over the rails. The mast
groaned. Ropes snapped and twisted in the wind.

A man stood at the wheel.

Her father.

He was younger than she remembered, his beard still more
red than gray. Blood ran from a cut above his
eyebrow. He shouted something into the storm, but the wind
tore the words away.

Mara tried to reach him. Her hands passed through his
coat like mist.

The boat plunged into a trough. For one impossible instant,
the sea towered overhead.

Her father released the wheel and pulled the silver watch
from his pocket. He stared at it, then toward the
distant shore hidden beyond rain.

"Mara," he said.

The wave fell.

She was back in the shop, gripping the counter. Orin
caught the watch before it slipped from her hand.

Mara could not breathe. The storm still roared in her
ears.

"That was real."

"It was."

"He said my name."

"He was thinking of you."

"Did he die?"

Orin looked down at the watch. "The hour ends before
the wave does."

"Then show me the next one."

"There is no next one in this watch."

"You said you keep lost hours. Find it."

"I cannot find what was never lost."

Mara wiped seawater from her face, only to discover it
was tears. "What does that mean?"

"This watch holds six minutes your father lost during the
storm. Six minutes in which he was neither certain to
live nor certain to die. Such moments sometimes fall out
of the world. They catch in objects."

"I want them."

Orin's gaze hardened. "No."

"They belong to me."

"They belong to no one."

"He was my father."

"And if you take those minutes, what will you do
with them? Stand helplessly on a sinking boat again and
again? Listen for words the wind will never let you
hear?"

"I could see him."

"You could see his fear."

"I've seen worse than fear. I've seen an empty chair
at every supper for eight years."

The clocks remained silent, but Mara felt their attention gathering
around her.

Orin closed the pocket watch. "Go to work, Miss Vale."

She did not remember telling him her name.

"Give it to me."

"No."

"I'll pay."

"Not with money."

"What, then?"

Orin studied her for a long moment. "An hour for
an hour."

"You want one of my memories?"

"Not a memory. An hour. You may choose it, but
once surrendered, it will be as if you never lived
it."

Mara thought of the countless dull hours she had spent
sorting letters. "Fine."

"Do not answer quickly. Time is easy to despise before
one knows its cost."

"I'll give you an hour from yesterday. Three until four.
I was at the post office tying bundles."

Orin shook his head. "Spent hours cannot be surrendered unless
they matter."

"That makes no sense."

"Time without meaning leaves no mark. I can take only
an hour that shaped you."

Mara understood then why the shop was dark. Why its
clocks did not tick. The shelves were not filled with
machinery but with choices.

"What happens to the hour I give you?"

"It stays here."

"And I forget it?"

"You lose more than the memory. The hour's effect on
you disappears. A kindness may no longer have softened you.
A lesson may no longer guide you. A grief may
no longer weigh upon you."

Mara looked at her father's watch.

"I choose the morning he taught me to swim."

Orin frowned. "Why that hour?"

"Because I was six. Because it mattered. And because I
haven't gone swimming in years."

He did not move.

"You offered a trade," Mara said.

"I did."

"Then take it."

Orin fetched a small glass bottle from beneath the counter.
It was no larger than Mara's thumb and stopped with
black wax.

"Hold it," he said.

The glass was cold. As Mara closed her hand around
it, she remembered sunlight trembling across the harbor. Her father
stood waist-deep in the water, holding out his arms.

The memory opened around her.

"Kick," he called, laughing. "The sea won't carry you if
you fight her."

Young Mara clung to the ladder at the end of
the pier.

"It's cold!"

"Only at first."

"You'll let me sink."

"Never."

She remembered releasing the ladder. She remembered the terror of
having nothing beneath her, then her father's hands supporting her
stomach. She remembered how proud he looked when she crossed
the distance between them.

The bottle grew warm.

The memory folded inward.

Mara gasped.

She was standing in the clock shop, though for a
moment she could not recall why she was holding an
empty bottle.

Orin took it from her. Golden light swirled inside.

A sudden, shapeless unease came over her.

"What did I give you?"

"I cannot tell you."

"But I chose it."

"And now it is no longer yours."

He set the bottle on a high shelf. Then he
placed the silver watch in her palm.

"It will open once each day," he said. "Six minutes.
No more."

Mara closed her fingers around it. Triumph should have warmed
her, but she felt only a strange chill, as if
she had left something important outside in the rain.

She returned to the post office two hours late.

For the next week, Mara lived for six minutes past
four.

Each afternoon, she locked herself in the records room and
opened the watch. Each time she found herself on her
father's boat. She learned every movement of his final preserved
minutes: the pull of his shoulders as he fought the
wheel, the sudden break of the forward rail, the gray
shape of a gull tossed helplessly above the mast.

She began to read his lips.

On the fourth day, she understood him shouting, "Cut the
net!"

On the sixth, "Hold together, old girl."

On the ninth, she saw him take the watch from
his pocket and say her name.

But there was more. After "Mara," his lips continued moving.

The wave always fell before she could understand.

Mara stopped sleeping. She spent nights writing possible phrases.

Mara, forgive me.

Mara, I'm sorry.

Mara, I love you.

None matched the shape of his mouth.

Meanwhile, odd things began happening.

She found herself afraid of deep water.

At first it was only the harbor. When the postmaster
sent her to deliver a parcel to the lighthouse keeper,
she stood at the end of the pier unable to
board the launch. The dark water moved between the pilings,
and her legs refused to bend.

She returned the parcel to the post office and claimed
she was ill.

Days later, she panicked while filling her bathtub. The sound
of water against porcelain made her heart hammer. She shut
off the tap and backed into the wall.

She had never feared water before. Her father had made
sure of that.

Hadn't he?

Mara searched her memories. She could remember watching him swim
beyond the harbor wall. She could remember waving from shore.
But when she tried to recall learning to swim herself,
she found only a blank brightness, like a page held
up to the sun.

The shop.

She returned to Bellweather Street after closing time and pounded
on the door.

Orin opened it before her third knock.

"What did you take from me?"

"What you offered."

"I can't swim."

"Then the hour taught you."

"I want it back."

Orin stepped aside, letting her enter. "And the watch?"

Mara held it protectively against her chest.

"An hour for an hour," he reminded her.

"You gave me six minutes."

"The length of time and the weight of time are
rarely equal."

"Then take something else."

"No."

"You have hundreds of clocks. Thousands of hours. You can't
need mine."

"I do not collect hours for pleasure, Miss Vale. Nor
can one hour simply replace another."

He lifted the lamp and led her past the counter.

A curtain hung behind a cabinet. Beyond it, a staircase
spiraled downward farther than the dimensions of the building should
have allowed. They descended into a cavernous chamber.

Shelves vanished into darkness, holding bottles of every shape. Some
glowed gold, others blue or white. Within each, tiny scenes
moved like reflections.

A woman stood beside a hospital bed, holding a stranger's
hand.

A boy released a paper boat into a gutter.

Two sisters argued beneath a pear tree.

A soldier watched snow fall on a ruined city.

"Lost hours," Orin said. "Not all are taken in trade.
Most are abandoned. People say, 'I wish that day had
never happened,' and sometimes the world listens. People bury joy
because remembering it hurts. They give away love to survive
grief."

Mara saw an old man dancing alone in a kitchen,
a laughing child covered in flour, a young woman waiting
at a railway station.

"What happens to them?"

"They wait."

"For what?"

"To be reclaimed."

"Do people come back?"

"Sometimes."

They stopped before a shelf where a small bottle shone
with warm, sunlit gold. Mara reached for it, but Orin
caught her wrist.

"That one is yours."

She stared into the glass.

A little girl clung to a pier ladder. A red-bearded
man stood in the sea with his arms open.

Pain pierced her so sharply she nearly dropped the watch.

"Give it back."

"You must return what you received."

Mara looked down at the engraved bird.

"No."

"Then keep your bargain."

"There must be another way."

"There is always another way," Orin said. "But rarely an
easier one."

He left her among the shelves.

Over the following month, Mara's world narrowed.

She could not approach the harbor. She stopped bathing and
washed with a damp cloth. Rain frightened her when it
gathered in gutters. Yet every afternoon she opened the watch.

Gradually, the six minutes changed.

At first she thought she was imagining it. Her father's
hand reached for the watch a second earlier. The wave
paused a fraction longer. The storm seemed to turn its
vast attention toward her.

Then, one afternoon, her father looked directly into her eyes.

Not through her. At her.

"Mara?" he said.

She forgot to breathe.

"Father?"

He left the wheel. The boat swung broadside to the
waves.

"Father, can you hear me?"

"Mara!" He stumbled toward her, astonishment breaking across his bloodied
face. "How are you here?"

"I don't know."

He reached out. This time, when his fingers touched hers,
they did not pass through.

The sea went silent.

Rain hung motionless in the air. Each drop became a
glass bead. The wave towered above the boat, frozen.

Her father pulled her into his arms.

He smelled of salt, wool, and pipe smoke.

Mara held him so tightly that her ribs hurt. She
was thirty-two years old, but the sound she made belonged
to a child.

"I waited," she said. "We all waited."

"I know."

"You never came home."

His arms tightened.

"Is this death?" he asked.

"No. It's six minutes."

He drew back to look at her. "You're grown."

She laughed through tears. "That happens in eight years."

"Eight." He said the number softly.

Behind him, the frozen wave began to tremble.

Mara seized his hand. "Come with me."

"To where?"

"The shop. The keeper can save you. He keeps lost
time."

Her father glanced at the storm. "This time isn't lost,
lass."

"Yes, it is. It was in your watch."

"No." Understanding entered his face, followed by sorrow. "It's yours."

"What?"

"The watch never kept my hour. It kept yours."

The sky cracked like breaking ice.

Mara felt the deck beneath her beginning to dissolve.

"I don't understand."

"You've been standing here with me instead of living."

"I can get you out."

"You can't."

"I can try."

"You always did fight the sea."

He smiled then, and she saw the words she had
been trying to read in the shape of his mouth.

Mara, let me go.

The wave moved.

She was thrown back into the clock shop.

The silver watch struck the floor, its lid open. Its
hands had stopped at twelve minutes past four.

Orin stood behind the counter, as if he had been
expecting her.

"He spoke to me," Mara said.

"Yes."

"You knew he would."

"I hoped."

She picked up the watch. "He said the time was
mine."

"Grief has hours, too. You lost six minutes every day
to a moment you could not change. Eventually the watch
learned to hold them."

"Then none of it was real?"

Orin tilted his head. "Must a thing be unreal merely
because it happened inside grief?"

Mara touched the engraved bird.

"Can I see him again?"

"Yes."

The answer was gentle, which made it crueler.

"All I have to do is keep opening the watch."

"Yes."

"And if I do?"

"One day the six minutes will become an hour. Then
a day. In time, you may step aboard and never
return."

Mara imagined it: the storm held forever, her father's arms
around her, the wave always about to fall and never
falling. No empty chair. No unanswered questions. No years passing
without him.

A life inside the final moment before death.

She closed the watch.

"Take it back," she said.

Orin nodded.

"And return my hour."

He led her downstairs. The golden bottle waited on its
shelf.

"What must I pay?" Mara asked.

"You have paid."

He handed her the bottle.

It dissolved at her touch.

The harbor opened around her, bright with summer. She was
six years old again, clinging to the ladder while her
father laughed in the water.

"Kick," he called. "The sea won't carry you if you
fight her."

"It's cold!"

"Only at first."

"You'll let me sink."

"Never."

She released the ladder.

Fear rushed through her, wild and clean. Then his hands
steadied her. She kicked. She floated. Sunlight broke into silver
pieces across the water.

Her father stepped backward.

"That's it," he said. "Come on, Mara."

For a moment she was moving alone.

Then she reached him.

The memory settled into its proper place—not only in her
mind, but in her muscles, her lungs, the deep chambers
of her heart where courage sometimes waits without a name.

When Mara opened her eyes, she was lying on the
shop floor.

Dawn shone through the front window.

Orin sat nearby, winding a small brass clock.

"The watch?" she asked.

"Gone."

"Where?"

"Where lost things go when they are no longer needed."

Mara stood. She felt exhausted, as though she had crossed
a great distance.

At the door, she looked back.

"Why did it appear to me?"

Orin considered this. "Perhaps your father lost it. Perhaps you
did. Perhaps some objects are faithful enough to return when
we are finally ready to release them."

"That isn't an answer."

"The best answers rarely feel like answers."

Outside, Bellweather Street smelled of fresh bread and salt. The
town was waking. Chimneys smoked. Gulls wheeled over the roofs.

Mara walked downhill.

At the harbor, she stopped.

The sea stretched gray and cold beneath the morning sky.
Fear moved through her, but it no longer filled every
part of her. She descended the stone steps to the
water.

She removed her shoes, stockings, coat, and dress, leaving them
folded on the lowest dry step. A fisherman across the
harbor shouted something she ignored.

The first touch of water stole her breath.

Only at first.

She waded deeper.

When the bottom dropped away, panic seized her. She nearly
turned back. Then she remembered strong hands beneath her, a
laugh above the glittering sea, and a promise no father
had the power to keep forever.

Never.

Mara kicked.

Her body remembered.

She swam beyond the harbor wall, where the water lifted
and lowered her beneath the enormous sky. The town grew
small behind her. Ahead lay open sea, brightening as the
sun rose.

She floated on her back.

For the first time in eight years, she did not
imagine her father sinking below her. She imagined him as
he had been on that summer morning: standing waist-deep in
sunlight, stepping backward so she could discover that the water
would hold her.

When Mara returned to shore, the shop at the end
of Bellweather Street was gone.

In its place stood a narrow alley between the bakery
and the boarded-up tailor's. No doorway, no window, no clocks.

Years passed.

Mara left the post office and became the keeper of
the harbor light. She learned to read weather, repair lenses,
and guide boats home through fog. Sailors said the Bellweather
lamp was the steadiest on the coast.

Sometimes storms woke her at night.

On those nights she climbed to the lantern room and
watched the beam sweep over the black water. She thought
of all the hours people lost to fear, regret, and
longing. She wondered if Orin still kept them somewhere beneath
a crooked shop, waiting for their owners to return.

In the bottom drawer of her desk, Mara kept a
small glass bottle.

It appeared one winter morning, many years after her visit
to the shop. No larger than her thumb, it was
sealed with black wax and filled with a soft golden
light.

Inside was a single hour.

Mara never opened it. She did not need to. When
she held the bottle, she heard her father laughing and
felt cold water beneath a summer sun.

On the night of the worst storm Bellweather had seen
in half a century, a young sailor washed ashore below
the lighthouse. Mara found him among the rocks, barely breathing.
She carried him inside, wrapped him in blankets, and sat
beside him until morning.

At dawn, he opened his eyes.

"I thought I was dead," he whispered.

"Not today," Mara said.

He began to weep. "I lost them."

"Who?"

"My brother. My friends. The whole crew."

Mara took his hand.

For one hour, while the storm withdrew and sunlight spread
across the floor, she listened.

Later, when the sailor was asleep, Mara opened her desk
drawer. The bottle was gone.

She searched the room, though she already understood.

Far away, perhaps, a bell chimed in a shop with
no sign above its door. An old keeper placed a
new bottle on a high shelf. Inside it, a woman
sat beside a grieving stranger as dawn rose over the
sea.

The hour was not lost.

It was simply waiting to be found.

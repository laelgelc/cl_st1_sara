The Clockmaker of Bellwether Street

Every clock in Bellwether stopped at 3:17 on the morning
Elias Vale disappeared.

Pocket watches froze in waistcoat drawers. Alarm clocks went silent
beside sleeping heads. The great brass clock in the railway
station halted between one breath and the next, its minute
hand pointing accusingly at the floor. Even the painted wooden
rooster above the bakery ceased pecking at the hour.

For seventeen minutes, the town existed without time.

Then, at 3:34, every clock began again.

No one noticed the loss until dawn.

By breakfast, Bellwether was full of people tapping watch faces
and comparing times. By noon, the stationmaster had written a
report. By evening, Mrs. Vale had unlocked her husband's shop
and found the place empty.

There had been no struggle. Elias's coat was still hanging
behind the door. His spectacles rested on his workbench beside
a half-repaired music box. The back window was latched from
within, and the shop bell had not rung all night,
according to the widow who lived upstairs and slept lightly
enough to hear moths strike the glass.

Only two things were missing: Elias himself and the oldest
clock in Bellwether.

The clock had no name. It had stood in the
back room of the shop for as long as anyone
could remember, taller than a man and twice as broad,
with a black walnut case carved with moons, stars, foxes,
owls, and faces that seemed almost human in poor light.
Its dial had thirteen numerals. It never showed the correct
hour.

Children dared one another to touch it. Adults pretended not
to believe the stories.

Lena Marrow believed them.

She was twelve when Elias vanished and had been his
apprentice for six months, though neither of them used that
word. Officially, she swept floors, fetched tea, and delivered repaired
clocks. Unofficially, Elias had taught her how to straighten a
bent pivot without snapping it, how to hear a cracked
gear tooth in a running mechanism, and how to hold
her breath when lowering a balance spring into place.

"A clock," he once told her, "is a promise made
by metal."

"A promise to do what?"

"To remember what people forget."

Now, standing in the empty back room, Lena stared at
the pale rectangle on the floorboards where the ancient clock
had been.

Mrs. Vale stood behind her, twisting a handkerchief between her
fingers.

"The constable says Elias must have left willingly," she said.

"Without his coat?"

"He says people do strange things."

"Without his boots?"

Mrs. Vale looked toward the workbench. Beneath it sat Elias's
heavy boots, toes polished by years of wear.

"The constable," Lena concluded, "is an idiot."

"Lena."

"A respectful idiot."

Mrs. Vale almost smiled, but the smile did not survive.
"Go home. Your mother will worry."

Lena knelt and examined the floor. Four deep grooves marked
the boards where the clock's feet had rested. Dust lay
everywhere except in a narrow line leading from the pale
rectangle to the blank brick wall.

Not to the door.

To the wall.

She put her palm against the bricks. They were cold
enough to hurt.

Behind her, something ticked.

Lena turned.

The clocks in the front room filled the shop with
their ordinary chorus: slow ticks, brisk ticks, nervous clicks, the
soft whir of escapements. Yet the sound she had heard
was different. Deeper. It seemed to come from beneath the
floor.

Tick.

She followed it to Elias's workbench.

Tick.

She opened the top drawer and found brass screws, cleaning
cloths, and a heel of bread hardened to stone.

Tick.

The second drawer contained invoices and magnifying lenses.

Tick.

In the bottom drawer, beneath a roll of blue velvet,
lay a silver pocket watch.

It was larger than any pocket watch Lena had seen.
Its case bore the same carvings as the missing clock:
moons, owls, foxes, and tiny faces. There were thirteen numerals
on its dial.

Its hands pointed to 3:17.

A scrap of paper had been tied to its chain.

Lena unfolded it.

If the clock takes me, do not follow.

Below this, in smaller letters, Elias had added:

Unless I fail to return before the first snow.

Lena glanced through the shop window. Outside, against a darkening
sky, a single white flake drifted down and vanished on
the pavement.

Within an hour, snow covered Bellwether Street.

Lena did not go home.

She told Mrs. Vale she would lock the shop, waited
for the woman's footsteps to fade, and carried the silver
watch into the back room. The ticking grew louder near
the brick wall.

"Do not follow," she whispered. "That's a foolish thing to
write if you know someone as curious as me."

The watch sprang open.

Its minute hand began moving backward.

At once, the shop changed.

Shadows lifted from corners and climbed the walls. Dust leapt
from the floor into cracks. Lena heard footsteps retreating above
her as the widow upstairs walked backward through her rooms.
Beyond the window, snow rose from the street in white
streams and flew into the clouds.

Time was reversing.

Lena tried to drop the watch, but its chain had
wound itself around her wrist.

The bricks before her shivered. Mortar dissolved between them, and
one by one they drew apart, revealing a narrow doorway
filled with golden light.

On the other side stood the missing clock.

Its pendulum swung in silence.

Lena pulled at the chain. "I changed my mind."

The watch dragged her forward.

She crossed the threshold, and Bellwether vanished behind her.

She landed on a road paved with clock faces.

Around her stretched a city under a motionless violet sky.
Towers rose at impossible angles, their windows bright with trapped
sunsets. Bridges curved upward and ended in midair. Thousands of
clocks hung from iron trees, chiming hours that had never
existed. Along the road hurried people in clothes from different
ages: a soldier in rusted armor, a woman carrying a
glass umbrella, a barefoot boy with flowers growing from his
hair.

None cast shadows.

At the roadside, a sign pointed in three directions.

YESTERDAY

TOO LATE

THE THIRTEENTH HOUR

Someone cleared his throat.

A fox sat beneath the sign. He wore a green
waistcoat, and a pair of spectacles balanced on his narrow
muzzle.

"You are not Elias Vale," he said.

"You're a fox."

"Neither observation is particularly useful."

Lena stared at him.

The fox took out a tiny watch and consulted it.
"You are early, late, and precisely where you should not
be. Very inconvenient."

"Where is Elias?"

"Taken."

"By whom?"

"The Keeper."

"Where?"

The fox pointed toward the tallest tower in the city.
It was black, windowless, and crowned by a clock with
thirteen hands.

"There."

Lena began walking.

The fox trotted after her. "I would not do that."

"You're welcome to stay here."

"I would, but you have something belonging to me."

She looked at the silver watch chained to her wrist.

"This?"

"Once. Long ago. Before your town was built, before your
language learned the word Tuesday, before humans decided minutes should
all be the same length."

"Minutes are the same length."

The fox laughed so hard he had to sit down.

Lena continued toward the tower.

The city grew stranger as she entered it. In one
square, rain fell upward from a fountain. In another, old
men played cards with their younger selves. Shop windows displayed
bottled summers, lost opportunities, and final words that had never
been spoken. At a market stall, a masked merchant sold
small parcels labeled AN AFTERNOON YOU THOUGHT WOULD LAST FOREVER.

Lena kept her eyes on the tower.

"What is this place?" she asked.

"The place between moments," said the fox. "Discarded time collects
here."

"And the Keeper?"

"Collects the rest."

"Why did he take Elias?"

"Because your clockmaker tried to steal a day."

Lena stopped. "Elias never stole anything."

"All clockmakers steal. They cut eternity into pieces and sell
it by the hour."

"That sounds clever, but it isn't an answer."

The fox's ears tilted back. "Your master came here to
bargain. His wife is ill."

Lena knew Mrs. Vale had been weak throughout autumn. Elias
had often closed the shop early to take her home,
though Mrs. Vale insisted it was only a stubborn cold.

"He wanted more time for her," Lena said.

"He wanted the day on which she died removed from
the calendar. The Keeper dislikes such requests."

"Then why didn't Elias come back?"

"Because the Keeper made him a better offer."

They reached the tower at midnight, though the sky had
not changed. Its door was made from black wood and
had no handle.

Thirteen keyholes surrounded its frame.

Lena examined them. "Do you have a key?"

"I have several. None for this."

She took Elias's watch from her apron pocket—the ordinary watch
he used while working. She had found it beside his
spectacles and carried it without thinking. Its winding key was
fixed to the chain.

The key fit the smallest keyhole.

She turned it.

Something vast shifted inside the tower.

The door opened.

Beyond it lay Elias's shop.

For one hopeful instant, Lena thought she had returned to
Bellwether. Then she saw hundreds of Eliases seated at hundreds
of benches, all working beneath cold blue lamps.

One Elias was a child. Another had white hair to
his waist. One had mechanical hands. One was no more
than a shadow. Each repaired a different clock, and every
clock showed 3:17.

In the center of the room stood the real Elias,
bent over the ancient walnut clock.

"Elias!"

He looked up.

A smile of relief crossed his tired face, followed at
once by horror.

"Lena, no."

She ran to him. "You said not to follow unless
it snowed."

"I wrote that before I knew what the snow meant."

"What does it mean?"

"That no time is passing in Bellwether."

Lena remembered snow rising from the street as the watch
turned backward.

"How long have you been here?" she asked.

"Perhaps a day. Perhaps forty years." Elias touched her shoulder
as if making sure she was real. "The Keeper stopped
our town when I crossed over. Bellwether is trapped inside
the missing seventeen minutes."

"But the clocks started again."

"Their hands did. Time did not."

Lena thought of everyone in town waking, eating breakfast, arguing
at the station, and going home. "That's impossible."

"People often continue after time stops. Habit is a powerful
spring."

The fox jumped onto a workbench. "Charming reunion. We should
now discuss escape."

Every lamp in the room went out.

A thirteenth chime sounded.

The Keeper stepped from inside the walnut clock.

He was tall and dressed in a coat stitched from
calendar pages. Instead of a face, he wore a polished
brass dial. Two black hands turned slowly across it, though
there were no numerals to indicate what they measured.

When he spoke, his voice came from every clock in
the room.

"Elias Vale. You have brought me an apprentice."

"I brought no one," Elias said.

"Everyone brings those who love them."

Lena stepped between them, although her knees had begun to
shake. "Let him go."

The hands on the Keeper's face paused.

"You possess eighty-one years, seven months, two days, and some
scattered hours," he said. "You offer commands as though you
possess more."

"I offer a bargain."

Elias seized her arm. "No."

The Keeper turned toward her. "What do you seek?"

"Mrs. Vale lives. Bellwether goes free. Elias and I go
home."

"And what do you offer?"

Lena had expected this question, but now that it had
come, she found her plans embarrassingly thin.

"I can repair your clock."

The hundreds of Eliases stopped working.

The Keeper's coat rustled.

"My clock is not broken."

"Yes, it is."

Elias stared at her. "Lena—"

She pointed to the ancient clock's pendulum. It swung left,
then right, but the movement was subtly uneven. The rightward
arc was shorter.

"You've had hundreds of versions of Elias working on clocks
showing 3:17," she said. "You trapped Bellwether at that minute.
You took the old clock because it's connected to the
town. But you still can't make the missing seventeen minutes
hold properly."

The Keeper's face hands began to turn faster.

The fox whispered, "Careful."

Lena continued. "You don't collect discarded time. You're losing it."

A crack appeared across the brass dial where the Keeper's
mouth might have been.

From inside his coat came a sound like distant thunder.

"Time cannot be lost."

"Then why are you afraid?"

The room shook. Clocks fell from shelves, striking the floor
without breaking. The many Eliases flickered.

The real Elias leaned close. "You're right," he whispered. "The
clock has been slowing ever since I arrived. But I
could not find the fault."

Lena studied the mechanism. The walnut case stood open. Within
it, gears turned in glittering layers, far deeper than the
case should have allowed. Some were the size of coins;
others seemed as vast as moons.

"A clock is a promise made by metal," she murmured.

The fox's ears rose. "And what promise did this one
make?"

Lena looked at the carvings: moons, stars, owls, foxes, and
human faces. She saw then that the faces were not
decorations. One resembled Mrs. Vale. Another looked like Elias. A
third looked disturbingly like Lena.

"It promised to remember," she said.

The Keeper raised one long hand. "Enough."

His fingers closed.

The room froze.

The falling clocks hung in midair. Elias stood motionless beside
Lena. Even her heartbeat stopped, yet somehow she remained conscious.

Only the fox moved.

He leapt from the bench and bit the Keeper's ankle.

The Keeper cried out in twelve voices.

Time crashed back into motion.

"Run!" shouted the fox.

Lena did not run. She plunged both hands into the
clock.

Cold shot through her fingers. The gears were not metal.
One contained the smell of rain on Bellwether Street. Another
held the sound of Mrs. Vale laughing. A silver wheel
flashed with Lena's memory of her father lifting her onto
his shoulders when she was five. Each piece was a
moment, cut and polished until it fit beside another.

Then she saw the fault.

At the heart of the mechanism was an empty space.

Not a broken gear. A missing memory.

"Elias!" she shouted. "What happened at 3:17?"

He ducked as the Keeper swept an arm across the
bench. "What?"

"The clock remembers the town. Something happened at 3:17 that
it can't remember."

The Keeper seized the fox by the tail and flung
him into a cabinet.

Elias stared at the mechanism. "At 3:17, I crossed the
threshold."

"That isn't all."

"I started the clock."

"Why then?"

"Because—"

His face changed.

Mrs. Vale had appeared at the tower door.

She wore only a nightdress beneath her winter cloak. Snow
dusted her hair, and in one hand she carried a
kitchen knife.

"I told you," she said breathlessly, "that I am not
dying."

Elias went pale. "Mara?"

"You left a note claiming you had gone to save
my life. That is not a note any sensible wife
can ignore."

The Keeper released a shriek of grinding machinery.

The entire tower leaned.

Mrs. Vale gripped the doorframe. "I woke at 3:17 the
night before Elias left," she called. "He was sitting beside
me, holding that silver watch."

Elias closed his eyes.

"I asked him what was wrong," she continued. "He said
nothing. He always says nothing when he means everything."

Lena reached deeper into the clock's mechanism. "What happened next?"

"I told him I knew the doctor's diagnosis. I told
him I had perhaps a month. Elias promised I would
not die."

The empty space within the mechanism began to glow.

"And you?" Lena asked. "What did you promise?"

Mrs. Vale looked at her husband.

"I promised not to forgive him if he wasted our
last month searching for a miracle."

The missing gear appeared in Lena's hands.

It was small, warm, and made of no metal she
knew. Along its edge were engraved two words:

LET GO.

The Keeper lunged.

Elias threw himself into the Keeper's path. They struck the
floor together. Calendar pages tore from the Keeper's coat and
whirled through the room: winters, birthdays, wars, quiet Sundays, afternoons
not yet lived.

"Now, Lena!" Elias shouted.

She placed the gear into the empty space.

It fit perfectly.

The clock struck 3:18.

The sound shattered the tower.

Walls split open, revealing the violet sky. Bridges uncurled over
the rooftops. Bottled summers burst in the streets below, flooding
the city with sunlight. The hundreds of Eliases vanished one
after another, each giving Lena a brief nod before fading.

The Keeper staggered upright. A crack divided his brass face.

"You have released what cannot be restored," he said.

"No," Lena replied. "I released what you couldn't keep."

The clock struck 3:19.

Wind tore the calendar coat from the Keeper's body. Beneath
it, he was small, ancient, and terribly human.

The clock struck 3:20.

The city dissolved.

Lena felt Elias grab one hand and Mrs. Vale the
other. The fox caught her apron in his teeth. Together
they were lifted into a storm of ticking, chiming, ringing
sound.

For one instant, Lena saw every clock in Bellwether at
once.

Then she fell onto the floor of the shop.

Morning sunlight streamed through the windows.

Outside, carts rolled along Bellwether Street, leaving tracks in fresh
snow. The railway clock chimed seven. Somewhere, a baby began
to cry, and somewhere else, someone burned the first batch
of breakfast rolls.

Time had resumed.

Mrs. Vale sat against the wall, laughing and weeping. Elias
knelt beside her.

The ancient clock stood in its old place, its pendulum
swinging evenly. Its dial now had twelve numerals.

The silver watch lay open on the floor. Its hands
moved normally.

The fox was gone.

For a while, no one spoke.

Then Mrs. Vale slapped Elias.

It was not a hard slap, but it echoed impressively
through the shop.

"That," she said, "is for bargaining away forty years of
your life without consulting me."

Lena blinked. "Forty years?"

Elias touched his cheek. "The Keeper's first offer."

Mrs. Vale slapped him again.

"And that is for leaving a note."

"I thought the note was clear."

"It said, 'Gone to correct an error. Back soon.'"

"I was under pressure."

Lena began to laugh. She laughed until she had to
sit on the floor. After a moment, Mrs. Vale joined
her. Even Elias smiled.

The doctor had been correct. Mrs. Vale lived twenty-seven more
days.

They were not miraculous days. She slept often. She suffered.
There were medicines, difficult mornings, and long nights when the
house seemed to hold its breath. But there were also
shared meals, music from the repaired music box, and afternoons
when sunlight filled the upstairs room.

Elias did not search for another doorway.

On the final evening, snow began falling over Bellwether. Mrs.
Vale asked him to open the window.

"It's cold," he protested.

"That is how winter works."

He opened it.

Lena sat beside the bed while Mrs. Vale watched the
snow. The town clocks began striking nine, never quite together.

"Untidy," Mrs. Vale whispered.

"Elias says no two clocks agree because each remembers a
different world," Lena said.

"Elias says many things when he wants to avoid admitting
his repairs are imperfect."

From downstairs came his offended voice. "I heard that."

Mrs. Vale smiled.

She died sometime after midnight, holding her husband's hand. No
clocks stopped. No thunder sounded. The universe did not break.

A single moment ended, and the next began.

Years later, when Elias's hands grew too unsteady for delicate
work, the sign above the shop changed from VALE, CLOCKMAKER
to MARROW & VALE. Later still, it read simply LENA
MARROW, HOROLOGIST.

The ancient clock remained in the back room. It kept
excellent time, though every winter, on the first snowy night,
it lost seventeen minutes.

Lena never corrected it.

Sometimes customers asked about the carved fox in its walnut
case. It seemed an odd detail, especially with the tiny
green waistcoat and spectacles.

Lena would smile and say it had always been there.

This was not entirely true.

On quiet evenings, when the town settled and all the
repaired clocks spoke softly to one another, she sometimes heard
claws crossing the back room. In the morning, a muddy
paw print would mark the floor, and the silver watch
would be wound though she had left it untouched.

Once, many years after the missing seventeen minutes, Lena found
a parcel on her workbench.

Inside was a tiny brass gear and a folded note.

The note read:

Minutes are not the same length.

You were right about everything else.

Lena laughed, placed the gear in her parts drawer, and
opened the shop for another day.

Outside, Bellwether hurried through rain, weddings, market mornings, funerals, missed
trains, first kisses, last words, and all the other hours
people imagined they could keep.

Above them, the railway clock struck eight.

For once, every clock in town struck with it.

Then one small watch hesitated, remembered something better, and fell
a second behind.

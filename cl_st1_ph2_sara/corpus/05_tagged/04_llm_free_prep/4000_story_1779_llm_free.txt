The town of Bellwether had no clocks.

There were clock towers, certainly. One stood above the courthouse,
its four stone faces blank as dinner plates. Another rose
from the abandoned railway station, where swallows nested behind frozen
iron hands. In the schoolhouse, a walnut case hung above
the blackboard, but inside it lived only a patient spider.

People in Bellwether woke when the eastern hills turned gold.
They ate when they were hungry, worked until their work
was finished, and slept when darkness settled over the roofs.
Trains no longer stopped there. Travelers rarely did.

Only Elias Vale kept time.

He lived in a narrow shop between the bakery and
the undertaker. Painted across its window, in letters faded by
forty summers, were the words:

**ELIAS VALE
REPAIRS—MECHANICAL & OTHERWISE**

The clocks in his shop ticked at different speeds. Some
hurried. Some lagged. Some ran backward when it rained. Elias
did not correct them unless a customer asked. He believed
clocks, like people, should be permitted a few harmless peculiarities.

Each morning, a little after sunrise, he climbed the ladder
behind his counter and wound a small brass clock kept
on the highest shelf. It had no numbers and only
one hand. Its face was black, and its single silver
hand moved not in circles but gradually from left to
right, like a finger tracing a sentence.

No one had ever seen Elias bring the clock down.

No one, that is, except Mara Finch.

Mara was eleven years old, sharp-eyed, and forever being accused
of listening at doors. Her father owned the bakery next
to Elias's shop, and her bedroom window overlooked the alley
between the two buildings. From there, on a sleepless summer
night, she once saw Elias standing in his yard beneath
the moon. He held the black-faced clock in both hands,
and its silver hand shone.

Across the alley, the shadow of an apple tree stretched
over the ground.

Elias turned a tiny key.

The shadow moved backward.

A fallen apple leaped from the grass and fastened itself
to a branch. A moth flew tail-first into the dark.
In the bakery kitchen, a cracked cup rose from the
floor, joined its scattered pieces, and settled on the table.

Then Elias stopped winding.

The yard became ordinary again.

Mara did not sleep at all that night.

The next morning she waited until her father began kneading
bread, then slipped into Elias's shop. The doorbell gave a
guilty jingle.

Elias sat at his workbench, peering through a magnifying lens
at the machinery of a silver pocket watch.

"You reversed the apple tree," Mara said.

Elias did not look up. "Good morning."

"And our blue cup."

"I wondered where that had gone."

"You put time backward."

Elias removed the lens from his eye. He was thin
and pale, with white hair that seemed permanently electrified. "Time
does not go backward."

"I saw it."

"You saw events go backward. Time continued exactly as it
always does."

"That sounds like cheating."

"It is precision."

Mara crossed her arms. "What is that clock?"

Elias glanced toward the highest shelf.

"A responsibility," he said.

"Can I see it?"

"No."

"Can you make me unbreak the plate I dropped last
Tuesday?"

"No."

"Can you make me uneat porridge?"

"No, and I'm alarmed by the ambition."

She frowned at him. "Why did you do it to
the tree?"

Elias returned to the pocket watch. "Practice."

"For what?"

He placed one microscopic gear beside another. "For something I
hope never happens."

That answer should have discouraged her. Instead, Mara began visiting
every day.

She swept floors, sorted screws, delivered repaired watches to farmers
who used them only when traveling elsewhere, and learned the
names of tools. Elias taught her how to replace springs
and balance wheels. He taught her that a clock could
be made accurate by attending carefully to its errors.

He did not teach her about the black-faced clock.

Months passed. Then years.

Mara grew tall enough to reach the highest shelf without
a ladder, though she never touched what rested there. Her
father began to speak of sending her to relatives in
the city, where she might attend a proper school and
learn a useful profession. Mara resisted. Bellwether contained mysteries. The
city, she suspected, contained only crowds.

"You cannot spend your life taking clocks apart," her father
said.

"Elias has."

"Elias is not a model for anyone."

From behind the wall came three indignant knocks. The old
clockmaker had been listening through a bread tin.

In Mara's sixteenth winter, snow buried the lower windows of
the shop. The roads vanished. For twelve days, Bellwether was
an island of chimneys in a white sea.

On the thirteenth morning, Elias did not come downstairs.

Mara found him sitting beside his bed, already dressed, one
boot laced and the other in his hand. He looked
irritated rather than frightened.

"My left side has gone on strike," he said.

She ran for the doctor.

Elias survived, but one hand remained weak, and his speech
occasionally tangled itself. Mara began opening the shop each morning.
She wound the clocks, lit the stove, and brought him
tea.

She did not wind the black-faced clock.

"Leave it," Elias told her.

The silver hand crept forward day by day.

"What happens when it reaches the other side?" she asked.

Elias stared into his tea.

"It stops," he said.

"That's all?"

"All is a large word."

He refused to say more.

Spring arrived. Snow slipped from the roofs in shining avalanches.
The river broke free of ice and carried branches south.
Elias recovered enough to walk with a cane, but he
tired easily and often fell asleep at his bench.

One afternoon, a stranger entered the shop.

He was young, perhaps twenty-five, dressed in a dark green
coat powdered with road dust. Unlike most visitors, he carried
no broken clock. He removed his gloves finger by finger.

"I'm looking for Elias Vale," he said.

Mara pointed to the old man sleeping near the stove.

The stranger's expression softened.

"Wake him," he said.

"No."

"It is important."

"It always is when people say that."

Elias opened one eye. "You sound like your father."

"Then I shall never say it again."

He noticed the visitor. For a long moment, neither man
spoke.

Elias gripped his cane.

"Jonah," he whispered.

The stranger stepped forward. "Hello, Father."

Mara looked from one to the other. Elias had never
mentioned a son.

Jonah took a chair beside the stove. He spoke quietly,
as if continuing a conversation interrupted only yesterday. He had
lived in the capital. He had studied engineering, then mathematics,
then subjects whose names Mara did not know. He had
returned because a letter from the town doctor had finally
found him.

"You should have written to me," Jonah said.

"I did. Many times."

"I received one letter in twelve years."

"I never sent the others."

"That is not generally how letters work."

Elias stared at the floor.

Mara left them alone, though she listened from the bakery
wall and heard almost nothing. Jonah stayed in Bellwether. He
rented a room at the inn and spent his days
repairing the roof of his father's shop, carrying coal, or
reading in silent corners.

He was courteous to Mara, but she did not trust
him. He studied everything too quickly. When he picked up
a clock, his eyes went not to its craftsmanship but
to its weaknesses.

One evening, Mara entered the shop and found him on
the ladder.

His hand was inches from the highest shelf.

"Don't," she said.

Jonah looked down. "You know what it is?"

"Do you?"

He descended, carrying the black-faced clock.

Mara lunged for it, but Jonah turned away.

"Careful," he said. "If you damage it, the consequences might
exceed Elias's patience."

"Put it back."

"It belongs to my family."

"It belongs on the shelf."

Jonah smiled, though not unkindly. "Has he told you anything?"

Mara said nothing.

"My grandfather built this clock," Jonah continued. "Or discovered it.
Elias changed the story each time he told it. He
said it could rewind a bounded space—one room, one field,
perhaps an entire town. The greater the space, the less
precisely one could determine the result."

"He said it only moves events."

"Ah. Precision."

"What happens when the hand reaches the end?"

Jonah's smile vanished.

"That depends on whether it has been wound."

Footsteps sounded on the stairs. Elias stood in the doorway,
leaning heavily on his cane.

"Put it back," he said.

Jonah did.

That night, a storm came from the west.

Bellwether had endured storms before. It knew how to bend
shutters closed and bring animals into barns. But this storm
carried a strange green light through the clouds, and thunder
rolled beneath the ground rather than above it.

At midnight, lightning struck the courthouse tower.

The blank stone clock face exploded. Burning fragments fell onto
the roof, and within minutes flames were climbing through the
dry old beams.

The bell began to ring.

People poured into the square with buckets. Rain thrashed the
streets, yet the fire grew. It jumped from the courthouse
roof to the records hall, then crossed a narrow gap
to the schoolhouse. Wind hurled sparks eastward.

Toward the bakery.

Mara was carrying water when she saw the bakery roof
catch. She screamed for her father. He had gone inside
to save the account books and the wooden box containing
her mother's letters.

A window shattered.

Flame breathed out.

Mara ran toward the door, but Jonah caught her around
the waist.

"Let me go!"

"You can't enter."

"He's inside!"

The bakery roof groaned. Then, with a sound like the
sea striking cliffs, it collapsed.

Mara stopped fighting.

For one suspended instant, the square held only fire, rain,
and the terrible ringing of the bell.

Elias appeared beside them. He wore no coat. In his
hands was the black-faced clock.

"No," Jonah said.

Elias looked at the burning buildings. The silver hand had
almost reached the right edge.

"How far?" he asked.

"The bakery?" Jonah shook his head. "You cannot define it
in this chaos."

"The town."

"You've never attempted anything that large."

Elias turned the key.

The sound was very small.

All around Bellwether, rain rose from the mud and flew
into the clouds.

Flames shrank into rooftops. Smoke poured down chimneys. Burning beams
lifted and assembled themselves. Sparks swept westward in golden rivers.
The courthouse bell swallowed its own ringing.

People moved backward through the square. Water leaped from puddles
into buckets, then from buckets into wells. Shouts rushed into
mouths. Footprints vanished beneath reversing feet.

Mara felt herself dragged away from Jonah's arms, yet she
remained aware. The world pulled at her bones as if
she were a scrap of fabric caught on a turning
wheel.

Elias cried out.

Blood ran from his nose.

"Stop!" Jonah shouted.

But Elias kept turning.

The bakery roof became whole. Its windows caught their broken
glass and sealed themselves. Mara's father flew backward through the
doorway, the box of letters leaving his hands and returning
to a cupboard. Lightning climbed from the courthouse into the
sky.

Elias released the key.

The storm froze.

Then thunder shook the town, and time resumed.

Rain fell.

Lightning struck the western hills instead of the courthouse tower.

The people in the square were gone. They had returned
to their beds, their kitchens, their late-night conversations. None remembered
fire.

Mara stood in the empty street with Jonah.

Elias lay between them.

The black-faced clock had shattered. Its silver hand rested in
his open palm.

They buried Elias three days later on a hill overlooking
Bellwether.

The townspeople remembered him as an odd but decent man
who repaired watches no one needed. Mara's father remembered waking
in the night with the powerful certainty that his daughter
was in danger. He had found her standing in the
square, soaked through, beside Elias's body. The doctor blamed the
old man's heart.

Only Mara and Jonah remembered the fire.

After the funeral, they sat in the clock shop. Every
clock had stopped at midnight.

Jonah spread the broken pieces of the black-faced clock across
the workbench.

"Can you repair it?" Mara asked.

"No."

"You haven't tried."

"I spent half my life studying because I wanted to
understand this machine. I wanted to prove my father was
a coward for hiding it." He touched the fractured face.
"Now I know he was afraid because he understood it."

Mara stared at the silver hand.

"What did he mean when he said it stops?"

Jonah drew a slow breath.

"The clock did not reverse events for free. Every use
required time taken from the person turning the key."

"How much?"

"No one could measure it. Seconds for a cup. Days
for a tree. To rewind the entire town…" He looked
toward the hill where Elias had been buried. "More than
he had."

Mara remembered the roof collapsing. She remembered her father emerging
backward from the flames.

"Then he traded his life for ours."

"Yes."

Outside, the bakery door opened. Mara's father came into the
alley carrying a tray of honey cakes. He looked older
than he had before the storm, though no time had
passed for him. Perhaps, Mara thought, one could become older
simply by nearly losing everything.

Jonah gathered the clock's pieces.

"What will you do with them?" she asked.

"Throw them into the river."

"No."

He looked at her sharply.

"No one should have such a machine," he said.

"It saved the town."

"It killed my father."

"Both can be true."

Jonah pushed back his chair. "That is exactly why it
must be destroyed."

He reached for the silver hand, but Mara took it
first.

The moment her fingers closed around it, the shop disappeared.

She saw Elias as a boy, sitting at the same
bench beside an older man whose hands resembled Jonah's. She
saw a woman laughing in the alley while summer rain
fell. She saw Jonah at six years old asleep beneath
the counter. She saw Elias alone, writing letters he folded
but never sent.

Then came other images.

The courthouse burning.

The railway station crowded with strangers.

The river covering the square.

A child not yet born standing before the black clock.

A hundred possible Bellwethers unfolded like cards in a magician's
hands. In some, the town prospered. In others, it vanished.
In one, every clock began ticking at once.

Mara dropped the hand.

The shop returned.

Jonah had gone pale. "What did you see?"

"The clock wasn't only measuring what it took."

"No," he said. "It was measuring what remained."

They wrapped the pieces in cloth and locked them in
the iron safe beneath the workbench. Neither spoke again of
throwing them into the river.

Years passed.

Jonah reopened the railway station and convinced the northern line
to stop at Bellwether twice a week. He argued that
a town without clocks was not necessarily a town without
schedules. The railway company disagreed, but Jonah was tireless when
irritated.

Mara inherited the bakery and the clock shop. She joined
them by cutting a doorway through the wall. Bread cooled
beside pendulums. Customers learned that their watches might smell faintly
of cinnamon.

She repaired ordinary clocks and refused all requests to repair
the extraordinary one.

Her father grew old in a peaceful, untidy fashion. Jonah
married the schoolteacher. Children played beneath the courthouse tower, where
the blank clock faces were never replaced.

And every year, on the anniversary of the storm no
one remembered, Mara climbed the hill and sat beside Elias's
grave.

She told him about the town.

She told him when the railway returned, when the river
flooded only the lower fields, when her father died in
his sleep with a book open on his chest. She
told him about Jonah's daughter, Nell, who could dismantle a
pocket watch before she could properly spell her own name.

Thirty-two years after the fire, the clocks of Bellwether began
to ring.

It happened at noon, though no one in town knew
that.

The courthouse bell struck twelve times. The clock in the
abandoned station moved its iron hands. The schoolhouse spider fled
as the walnut clock shuddered awake. In Mara's shop, hundreds
of mechanisms burst into life.

All showed the same time.

Mara stood behind the counter, flour on her sleeves.

Nell, now seventeen, emerged from the bakery carrying a tray
of rolls.

"Is that supposed to happen?" she asked.

"No."

From beneath the workbench came a metallic click.

The iron safe had opened.

Mara knelt slowly. Inside, the cloth wrapping had come undone.
The black face was whole again. Its silver hand pointed
to the far left, where it had begun long ago.

Nell crouched beside her.

"What is it?"

Outside, the townspeople gathered beneath the courthouse, pointing at the
moving hands. Beyond the eastern hills, a train sounded its
whistle. Clouds collected in the west, dark at their roots.

Mara lifted the clock.

It was warm.

For an instant she imagined carrying it to the river.
She imagined burying it beneath Elias's grave or smashing it
with a hammer. But destruction was not the same as
wisdom, and fear was not the same as care.

She looked at Nell.

"It is a responsibility," Mara said.

The girl reached toward it, then paused.

"May I see?"

Mara thought of Elias beneath the moon, reversing an apple
before he dared reverse a town. She thought of all
the truths he had withheld and all the dangers hidden
by silence. Secrets, like clocks, could be made accurate only
by attending carefully to their errors.

"Yes," she said. "But first, I will tell you what
it costs."

Outside, Bellwether's clocks continued to ring, welcoming a future that
had not yet decided what it would become.

## The Department of Lost Hours

On the morning Elias Vale discovered that three hours were
missing from Tuesday, he was buttering toast.

The clock above the kitchen door said 8:17. He looked
down to spread jam, and when he looked up again,
it said 11:17.

The toast remained warm in his hand.

Outside, rain hung motionless in the air, each drop fixed
like a glass bead. Then, all at once, the drops
resumed their fall and rattled against the window.

Elias stared at the clock. It ticked with the innocent
persistence of an object that had never done anything wrong.

His first thought was that the battery had failed. His
second was that he had suffered a stroke. His third
was that he would be late for work.

He checked his phone. 11:17.

He checked the oven, the brass watch that had belonged
to his father, and the little digital clock on the
microwave. All agreed.

There were no missed calls. No messages. The kettle was
still murmuring on the stove. The flame beneath it had
not burned away the water, though it should have boiled
dry hours ago.

Elias sat at the kitchen table and tried to remember.

At 8:17, he had been thinking about calling his daughter.
He had not spoken to Mara in four years. He
had imagined what he might say if she answered.

Then—nothing.

At 11:17, he was still holding the knife.

He called his office and claimed illness. His manager believed
him at once, perhaps because Elias had never before claimed
anything.

He spent the day examining the apartment for evidence of
those vanished hours. The dust beneath the chairs was undisturbed.
The tea in the kettle tasted freshly boiled. The book
beside his bed lay open to the same page he
had read the night before.

Only one thing had changed.

On the kitchen table, beneath the sugar bowl, was a
cream-colored card.

Elias was certain it had not been there before.

The card bore no name, only an address embossed in
fading blue ink:

**THE DEPARTMENT OF LOST HOURS**
**17 BELLWEATHER LANE**
**OPEN BETWEEN MOMENTS**

On the back, in smaller letters, someone had written:

**Bring identification and proof of loss.**

Elias read the card six times. Then he placed it
in a drawer and went to bed before sunset.

For the next week, nothing unusual happened.

He woke, dressed, took the number nine bus to the
municipal records office, and spent eight hours correcting minor errors
in property deeds. He returned home, ate soup, listened to
the radio, and stood for several minutes beside the telephone.

He did not call Mara.

On Friday evening, he took the card from the drawer.

Bellweather Lane did not appear on any map. Elias searched
online, consulted an old street directory, and even asked Mr.
Anwar at the corner shop, who knew every alley in
the city.

"Bellweather?" Mr. Anwar said. "There was a Bellweather Court once,
before the war. Bombed flat. Or perhaps that was Bellwether
Row."

"Do you know where?"

"If it was bombed flat?"

"The lane."

Mr. Anwar considered this. "Try behind the station. Old streets
hide behind stations."

So on Saturday, Elias went to the central railway station.

Behind it lay a district of narrow streets where repair
shops leaned against abandoned warehouses. He walked for an hour
without success. At last, as he stopped beneath a clock
mounted over a pawnshop, the minute hand moved backward.

Only once.

A gap appeared between two buildings where Elias could have
sworn no gap had been before.

Beyond it was a lane paved with black stones. At
the far end stood a narrow gray building with a
blue door. Seventeen was painted above it.

Elias entered.

The lobby resembled a government office designed by someone working
from an imperfect description. Rows of wooden chairs faced a
reception desk. Filing cabinets climbed the walls, even across the
ceiling. Hundreds of clocks hung among them, none showing the
same time.

People waited in silence.

A woman in a wedding dress held a ticket marked
43. An elderly man clutched a child's red mitten. A
boy in a school uniform carried a jar filled with
afternoon sunlight. In the corner, a businessman slept with his
briefcase chained to his wrist.

Behind the desk sat a receptionist with silver spectacles.

"Form," she said without looking up.

"I don't have one."

She slid a sheet of paper toward him.

The form was titled **APPLICATION FOR THE RECOVERY OF MISPLACED,
MISAPPROPRIATED, OR UNACCOUNTED-FOR TIME**.

Elias filled in his name, address, date of birth, and
occupation. Under **NATURE OF LOSS**, he wrote: *Three hours disappeared
while I was making breakfast.*

Under **EVIDENCE**, he hesitated, then wrote: *The toast remained warm.*

The receptionist examined the form.

"Proof of identity?"

He offered his driver's license.

"Proof of loss?"

"I don't understand."

"A stopped watch. A premature wrinkle. An unfinished conversation. Something
of that nature."

Elias placed the cream-colored card on the desk.

The receptionist frowned.

"Who gave you this?"

"I don't know."

"That is usually the answer."

She stamped his form with purple ink. **POSSIBLY MISPLACED.**

"Take the stairs to Chronological Assessments. Third floor, second past."

"Second what?"

"Past."

Before Elias could ask, she rang a brass bell. His
ticket, which he had not noticed receiving, showed the number
88.

The display above the door changed from 12 to 88.

"Next," she said.

The stairs turned upward, downward, and briefly sideways. Elias passed
the second-floor landing twice. On the third occasion, a sign
read:

**CHRONOLOGICAL ASSESSMENTS**
**SECOND PAST**

He entered a long office divided by green glass partitions.
Clerks sat at desks, opening envelopes from which summer evenings,
sleepless nights, and childhood birthdays fluttered like moths.

A young woman waved Elias over. Her black hair had
been cut unevenly, as though she had done it herself
in the dark. A nameplate on her desk read **MISS
ORRA FINCH, TEMPORAL AUDITOR, GRADE II**.

"Mr. Vale," she said. "Three hours, Tuesday morning."

"Yes."

"Are you certain they were stolen?"

"I didn't say stolen."

"Time is rarely lost. Usually it is spent, wasted, surrendered,
borrowed, or stolen. Misplacement is less common than people think."

"I was standing in my kitchen. Then it was three
hours later."

"Any history of fugues, enchantments, prophetic dreams, or prolonged committee
meetings?"

"No."

"Have you recently promised anyone forever?"

"No."

"Ever said, 'I'll only be a minute,' when you knew
you would be longer?"

"I suppose everyone has."

Miss Finch sighed. "Everyone is not currently making a claim."

She opened a drawer and removed a tuning fork. After
striking it against the desk, she held it near Elias's
chest. The note it produced was low and sorrowful.

"Well," she said, "you do have a temporal cavity."

"A what?"

"A hollow where the hours were attached. Fresh loss, clean
edges. That is encouraging."

"Can they be returned?"

"Perhaps."

"Where are they?"

"If I knew that, I would be in Retrieval."

She took a length of red thread and measured the
space around him. Then she examined his watch.

"This belonged to your father."

It was not a question.

"Yes."

"He lost time, too."

Elias stiffened. "Everyone loses time."

"Not like this."

Miss Finch opened the back of the watch with a
thin silver tool. Inside the case, beneath the gears, someone
had scratched a date.

14 October, twenty-seven years earlier.

Elias knew the date.

His wife, Anna, had died that evening.

He had never seen the inscription before.

"What does it mean?" he asked.

Miss Finch's manner changed. Her impatience vanished.

"Mr. Vale, where were you at 6:40 on the fourteenth
of October?"

"At the hospital."

"Think carefully."

He saw the white corridor. The blue plastic chairs. Rain
darkening his coat. Mara, eleven years old, asleep with her
head in his lap.

"I was with my daughter."

"And Mrs. Vale?"

"In surgery."

"What happened at 6:40?"

Elias looked at the clocks on the wall. One of
them had no hands.

"The surgeon came out."

Miss Finch closed the watch.

"There are losses," she said quietly, "that create an echo.
A person may try to escape a terrible moment by
refusing to live through it. Most people do this imperfectly.
They distract themselves. They work. They drink. They forget. But
occasionally, someone with the right object and sufficient grief can
remove the moment entirely."

"I didn't remove anything."

"Perhaps not consciously."

She placed the watch between them.

"Your father's watch is older than it appears. Devices like
this are called keepers. They hold minutes their owners cannot
bear."

Elias almost laughed, but Miss Finch's face prevented him.

"You think this watch stole Tuesday morning?"

"No. I think it stored something twenty-seven years ago. Now
the mechanism is failing. Old time is leaking out, and
new time is falling in."

"I don't understand."

"You will."

She gave him a stamped envelope and directions to Retrieval,
which was located in the basement on the ninth floor.

The Retrieval Department occupied an enormous chamber filled with shelves.
Jars, boxes, bottles, and sealed tins extended into a darkness
that seemed farther than the building's walls permitted.

A clerk led Elias through aisles labeled **INFANCY**, **WAR**, **COMMUTES**,
**REGRETTABLE MARRIAGES**, and **SUNDAY AFTERNOONS**.

They stopped before a metal door.

Miss Finch was waiting there.

"I thought you worked upstairs," Elias said.

"I do, later."

She unlocked the door.

Inside stood a single chair and a projector. On the
opposite wall hung a white screen.

"This is where we verify ownership," she said. "Recovered time
cannot be returned until the claimant demonstrates that it belongs
to them."

"And if I don't?"

"Then it is archived, auctioned, or used to extend the
lives of senior officials."

Elias could not tell if she was joking.

"Sit down," she said.

He sat.

Miss Finch placed his father's watch inside the projector. The
machine began to click.

An image appeared on the screen.

Elias saw his old kitchen as it had looked when
Mara was a child. Anna stood at the counter, laughing
as she tried to ice a birthday cake. Flour streaked
her cheek. Sunlight filled the room.

The image changed.

Anna asleep on a train, her head on Elias's shoulder.

Anna barefoot in the garden.

Anna in the hospital, thinner than he remembered, smiling for
Mara's sake.

Then came the corridor.

The clock on the wall showed 6:39.

Elias gripped the arms of the chair.

"This isn't Tuesday," he said.

"No," said Miss Finch.

"I came for Tuesday."

"You came because Tuesday was taken to replace this."

On the screen, the minute hand moved.

6:40.

The surgeon entered the corridor.

Elias remembered his damp shoes. The drop of blood on
his cuff. The way he looked first at Mara, then
at Elias.

But the memory stopped there. It always had.

For twenty-seven years, whenever Elias approached that moment, his mind
slid away. He remembered the funeral, condolences, casseroles from neighbors.
He remembered selling the house. Yet he had never recalled
the surgeon's words.

The projector rattled.

The scene moved forward.

"I'm sorry," the surgeon said. "We did everything we could."

On the screen, Elias bowed his head.

Then Mara woke.

She looked up at him, frightened and confused.

"Is Mum all right?"

Elias watched his younger self open his mouth.

The scene froze.

"What did you tell her?" Miss Finch asked.

"I don't know."

"Yes, you do."

"I said—I must have told her—"

The watch inside the projector ticked loudly.

Mara's face filled the screen. Eleven years old. Waiting.

Elias whispered, "I told her not to cry."

Miss Finch said nothing.

"I told her she had to be brave. I said
her mother wouldn't want her to make a scene."

The words returned with unbearable clarity.

Mara's mouth had tightened. She had nodded. And because Elias
had needed silence more than she had needed comfort, she
had not cried.

Not in the hospital. Not at the funeral. Not where
he could see.

The projector continued.

Weeks passed in moments. Elias returned to work. Mara ate
alone. He removed Anna's clothes from the wardrobe while Mara
was at school. When she asked where they had gone,
he said there was no sense living in the past.

Years passed.

Mara, sixteen, shouting that he had erased her mother.

Elias telling her not to be dramatic.

Mara, twenty-two, moving to another city.

Mara, thirty-four, standing at his door after a failed marriage,
asking if she could come inside. Elias had been preparing
for an early meeting. He told her they should talk
when she was calmer.

The screen went dark.

Elias felt as though the room had tilted.

"I thought she blamed me," he said.

"For her mother's death?"

"For everything."

"Did she?"

"I never asked."

Miss Finch took the watch from the projector. A hairline
crack crossed its face.

"Your three hours are inside," she said. "Tuesday morning became
entangled with the time you refused. You may recover them,
but only by accepting the original moment."

"What does that mean?"

"You must live it."

"I just watched it."

"Watching is not living."

She set the hands to 6:39 and wound the crown.

The hospital corridor appeared around him.

Not on the screen. Around him.

He smelled disinfectant and wet wool. He felt Mara's sleeping
head against his leg. His younger hands rested on the
blue chair. The watch ticked in his palm.

At the far end of the corridor, the surgeon approached.

Elias tried to stand, but grief pinned him in place.

Again, the words came.

"I'm sorry. We did everything we could."

Mara stirred.

This time Elias felt the full weight of the moment:
not an announcement, but a door closing on every ordinary
future he had expected. Anna would not grow old. She
would not complain about his driving, burn birthday cakes, or
leave books facedown on the table. Every room he entered
from then on would contain the shape of her absence.

He wanted to flee.

He understood now that he had fled.

"Is Mum all right?" Mara asked.

Elias looked down.

Her eyes were Anna's eyes.

He could tell her to be brave. He could demand
that she become quiet and manageable, so that he would
not have to carry her grief as well as his
own.

Instead, he fell to his knees.

"No," he said. "No, she isn't."

Mara stared at him.

"Your mum died."

The child made a sound he had not permitted himself
to remember.

Elias pulled her into his arms.

"I'm sorry," he told her. "You don't have to be
brave. I'm here. I'm here."

She struck his chest with both fists. She screamed at
him, at the surgeon, at the white walls, at the
universe that had continued to exist.

Elias held on.

Then he began to cry.

The corridor dissolved.

He was back in the Retrieval chamber, curled in the
chair. Miss Finch stood beside him, one hand on his
shoulder.

The watch had stopped.

"What happens now?" he asked.

"Now you take responsibility for the time that remains."

"And the three hours?"

"Returned."

Nothing happened.

Then Elias heard rain tapping glass.

He was standing in his kitchen.

Toast in one hand. Knife in the other.

The clock said 8:17.

The cream-colored card lay on the table.

For a long while he did not move.

Then he put down the knife and reached for the
telephone.

Mara's number had remained in his contacts through four years
of silence. He pressed it before he could decide not
to.

The call rang six times.

"Hello?"

Her voice was older than the one in the hospital
corridor, but not so different.

"Mara."

Silence.

He nearly apologized for calling. He nearly invented a practical
reason. A document needing a signature. News about a distant
cousin. Anything that would allow him to retreat into the
safety of facts.

Instead, he said, "I failed you when your mother died."

The silence changed.

Elias continued.

"I made you hide your grief because I was afraid
of mine. I kept doing it for years. When you
needed me, I acted as if needing anyone was a
weakness. I don't expect you to forgive me. I only
wanted to tell you that I know."

His hands shook.

On the other end of the line, Mara breathed slowly.

"Why now?" she asked.

Elias looked at the clock.

8:19.

"Because I've wasted enough time."

Another silence.

Then Mara said, "I have a train at noon."

Elias closed his eyes.

"All right."

"I could miss it."

He did not trust himself to speak.

"I could come over," she said. "For an hour."

"Yes."

"Just an hour, Dad."

"Yes," he said. "An hour."

After the call ended, Elias sat at the kitchen table.

The rain eased. A blade of sunlight entered through the
clouds and touched the sugar bowl. He picked up the
cream-colored card.

The address had vanished.

In its place was a handwritten note:

**CLAIM SETTLED. BALANCE RESTORED.**

Beneath that, in Miss Finch's narrow script, was another line:

**Please remember: all hours are lost eventually. That is what
makes them ours.**

Elias placed the card inside his father's watch and closed
the case.

At 9:03, the doorbell rang.

Mara stood in the hallway wearing a red coat. There
were silver strands in her hair he had never noticed.
She held no luggage, only a paper bag from the
bakery near the station.

"I brought breakfast," she said.

"I made toast."

"You always burn it."

He looked back toward the kitchen. Smoke had begun to
rise from the toaster.

For one uncertain second, neither of them moved.

Then Mara laughed.

The sound was sharp, surprised, and so like her mother's
that Elias had to grip the doorframe.

Mara's laughter faded.

"Dad?"

"I'm all right," he said.

This time, it was not a command. It was not
even entirely true.

He stepped aside.

She entered.

They ate pastries at the kitchen table while the clock
moved steadily above the door. At first they spoke of
safe things: the rain, the train she had decided not
to take, the bakery that had changed owners twice. Then
Mara told him about her divorce. Elias listened without offering
advice.

At 9:47, she asked what he remembered about Anna's last
day.

He told her.

At 10:26, Mara cried.

Elias moved his chair beside hers and waited. When she
leaned toward him, he held her as he should have
held her twenty-seven years before.

At 11:17, the hour he had once reached without living
through the morning, the clock gave a small metallic click.

For an instant, Elias saw Bellweather Lane reflected in the
dark kitchen window. He saw the gray building and the
blue door. Through an upstairs window, Miss Finch sat at
her desk, sorting envelopes while clocks turned around her.

She glanced up.

Elias raised one hand.

Miss Finch nodded, then drew the curtain.

The lane disappeared.

"Did you see something?" Mara asked.

"No," Elias said, and then corrected himself. "Yes. But it's
gone now."

Outside, rainwater shone on the rooftops. Cars moved along the
street. Somewhere a dog barked, a child shouted, and a
train departed without Mara.

Elias listened to the watch ticking in his pocket.

Each second arrived only once.

This time, he was there to receive it.

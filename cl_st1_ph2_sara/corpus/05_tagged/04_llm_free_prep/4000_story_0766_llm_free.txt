# The Cartographer of Rain

In Bellweather, rain had always fallen in straight lines.

It came down neatly, silver threads from a gray sky,
tapping roofs, rinsing windows, filling gutters. Children drew it as
they saw it: a cloud, a row of vertical strokes,
a black umbrella underneath.

Then, one Tuesday morning in April, the rain began to
fall sideways.

It swept eastward through the town as if chased by
an invisible broom. It slipped beneath doors, climbed staircases, and
soaked only the left sleeves of coats. At the railway
station, it entered the waiting room through a closed window
and left through an open one without wetting the floor
between. On Market Street, every raindrop curved around the statue
of the town's founder, leaving the bronze man perfectly dry
while drenching all the pigeons at his feet.

By Wednesday, rain was rising from wells.

By Thursday, it fell upward from the river and vanished
into the clouds.

By Friday, the people of Bellweather were frightened.

Only Mara Venn seemed pleased.

Mara lived in a narrow house behind the clockmaker's shop,
with her grandfather, Silas, and a white cat named Compass.
She was seventeen, restless, and famous for drawing maps of
places nobody needed mapped: the shortcut taken by ants across
the bakery wall, the territory of shadows in the town
square, the route followed by every lost button that rolled
beneath the floorboards.

Silas called these drawings "impractical."

Mara called them practice.

Her parents had been mapmakers too, though of a more
respectable kind. When Mara was six, they had left Bellweather
to chart the northern marshes. Their expedition never returned. Search
parties found a broken lantern, three tent pegs, and one
page torn from her mother's notebook. On it was a
single sentence:

The world is larger in bad weather.

Silas had hidden the page in a desk for years.
Mara had found it anyway.

On Friday afternoon, while townspeople hammered boards over their windows,
Mara stood in the garden with charcoal and paper. Rain
spiraled around her in a slow corkscrew. Compass sat beneath
a rosebush, dry and offended.

Mara watched the drops closely.

They did not move at random. They bent left above
the chimney, circled twice around the pear tree, divided at
the stone wall, and joined again over the alley.

"It's a road," she whispered.

"What is?" asked Silas from the doorway.

"The rain."

"The rain is water."

"So is a river. Rivers go somewhere."

Silas tightened his dressing gown. He had been a tall
man once, but grief and age had folded him inward.
"Come inside before you catch your death."

Mara did not move. She drew faster.

The rain made a path across the garden, over the
wall, between the bakery and the clockmaker's shop, then vanished
into a patch of empty air at the end of
Candle Lane.

No, not empty, Mara realized. Thin.

Some places in the world felt thin, as if distance
had been stretched too tightly over them. Her mother had
written about such places in the margins of atlases.

Mara rolled up her map.

"I know where it goes."

Silas looked at her in alarm. "That is exactly the
sort of sentence your mother used to say before something
exploded."

"She usually came back."

"Until she didn't."

The words fell between them, heavier than rain.

Mara lowered her eyes. "I have to look."

"No."

"I may be the only person who can."

"No."

"What if this is connected to the marshes?"

Silas turned away.

Mara regretted saying it, but not enough to take it
back. For eleven years, Bellweather had spoken of her parents
in softened phrases. A tragic disappearance. A brave expedition. A
terrible uncertainty. Mara hated the uncertainty most. It had no
shape, no edge, no place on a map.

Silas closed the door.

By midnight, the storm had grown stranger. Rain drummed beneath
the roof tiles. Puddles crawled uphill. Water traced words across
windows, though nobody could read the language.

Mara waited until the grandfather clock struck one. She packed
bread, matches, string, charcoal, three pencils, her mother's torn notebook
page, and a brass compass that had never pointed north.
The cat Compass tried to climb into her bag.

"Not this time," she whispered, lifting him out.

He climbed in again.

Mara sighed. "Fine."

She left a note for Silas and slipped into the
storm.

Candle Lane was deserted. The rain flowed along it like
a shining herd, all drops moving toward the brick wall
at the end. Mara followed.

The wall belonged to an abandoned soap factory. She knew
every crack in its surface. As a child, she had
drawn each brick. Yet tonight, between the seventy-third and seventy-fourth
bricks from the ground, a gap had appeared.

It was no wider than a needle.

Rain streamed into it.

Mara placed her palm against the wall. The bricks felt
soft and cold, like clay beneath a river. She pushed.
Her hand went through.

Then the wall opened.

Beyond it lay not the ruined factory yard but a
vast blue country under a sky of moving water.

Mara stepped forward.

For a moment she fell sideways. Rain rushed past her
ears. The world twisted, and the ground arrived unexpectedly beneath
her boots.

She stood on a road made of dark glass. It
curved through blue grass toward a forest where drops hung
motionless among the trees. Above, rivers crossed the sky, winding
through clouds like silver veins.

Compass climbed out of the bag and sneezed.

"Well," Mara said. "Mother was right."

She turned to mark the doorway, but the brick wall
had disappeared. Behind her stretched miles of glass road.

Mara's heart lurched. She took a breath, then another.

"First rule of mapmaking," she told Compass. "Never panic until
you've recorded where you are."

She drew the road, the forest, and three rivers in
the sky. Her brass compass pointed ahead, trembling.

They walked.

The blue grass chimed against Mara's boots. Occasionally, a drop
of rain rose from the ground and floated upward. Within
each drop, she saw a tiny reflection—not of herself, but
of Bellweather. A woman lit a stove. A boy searched
for a missing shoe. Silas stood in Mara's bedroom, holding
her note.

She looked away.

At the forest's edge stood a wooden sign:

ALL LOST WEATHER MUST BE DECLARED.

Beneath it sat a man in a yellow coat behind
a small desk. He had a long nose, damp hair,
and spectacles speckled with rain. A teapot steamed beside his
elbow.

"Name?" he asked.

"Mara Venn."

"Place of origin?"

"Bellweather."

"Purpose of visit?"

"Your rain is leaking into my town."

The man sighed and opened an enormous ledger. "It isn't
our rain."

"It came through here."

"Many things come through here. Few belong here." He dipped
his pen into the teapot instead of the ink bottle
and wrote with tea. "Do you have an appointment with
the Custodian?"

"No."

"Invitation?"

"No."

"Prophecy?"

"Not that I know of."

"Dead ancestor with unfinished business?"

"My parents disappeared eleven years ago."

The man paused.

"What were their names?"

"Elian and Sera Venn."

His chair creaked as he leaned back. "Ah."

Mara gripped the edge of the desk. "You know them?"

"I knew of them."

"Where are they?"

He removed his spectacles and polished them slowly. "Maps are
dangerous here, Miss Venn."

"So are unhelpful men behind desks."

Compass hissed in agreement.

The man glanced at the cat. "Follow the glass road
until it becomes a staircase. At the top, you'll find
the House of Storms. Ask for the Custodian. Do not
leave the road, do not drink from the sky, and
if you hear someone calling your name from inside a
puddle, be polite but do not answer."

"Why?"

"Because whatever is calling will learn your voice."

Mara wrote down the warning.

"One more thing," he said. "If you find what you
came for, it may not be what you wanted."

"That sounds like a prophecy."

"No. Just experience."

The road wound deeper into the forest. Rain hung everywhere,
suspended like crystal beads. When Mara touched one, thunder murmured
in the distance.

After an hour, the glass beneath her feet rose in
shallow steps. The forest fell away. Mara climbed into the
sky.

At the summit stood a black house balanced on the
edge of a cloud.

It was enormous and crooked, with chimneys shaped like trumpets
and windows flashing with lightning. Rain poured from its gutters
in every direction: down into the land, up into the
rivers overhead, and sideways toward doors that opened onto places
Mara could not see.

She knocked.

A woman with silver eyes answered. Her hair moved around
her face as though stirred by a constant wind.

"Mara Venn," she said.

Mara stiffened. "How do you know my name?"

"Your parents talked about you."

The woman stepped aside.

Mara entered.

The House of Storms contained a thousand rooms and, somehow,
no walls. Snow fell over one carpet. A desert wind
blew across another. Thunder slept curled beside a fireplace, rumbling
softly. Shelves rose into darkness, crowded with jars labeled FIRST
FROST, UNSHED TEARS, MONSOON—HANDLE WITH CARE, and THE LAST FOG
OF A FORGOTTEN ISLAND.

The silver-eyed woman moved through it all without getting wet.

"I am Aster Vale," she said, "Custodian of Unfinished Weather."

"Where are my parents?"

Aster studied her. "Direct as a lightning strike. You have
your mother's manners."

"Are they alive?"

"Yes."

The answer was so simple that Mara did not understand
it at first.

Alive.

The word opened inside her like a door.

"Take me to them."

Aster's expression held no joy. "Your parents came here while
mapping the northern marshes. They discovered a breach—a place where
the weather between worlds had begun to mix. They crossed
over to close it."

"They succeeded?"

"For a time."

Aster led Mara past a staircase made of hailstones. "Every
world has weather, but weather is more than wind and
water. It carries memory. Mood. Possibility. A storm from one
world can alter another. A rain of forgetting may erase
a city. A wind of longing may send an entire
population walking west. The barriers must be maintained."

"And now one is breaking over Bellweather."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because your parents stopped repairing it."

They arrived at a round door. Aster placed her hand
against it.

Beyond lay a circular chamber filled with maps.

They covered the walls, the floor, and the domed ceiling.
Some were painted on paper. Others were etched into metal,
woven from thread, or formed from tiny clouds. At the
center of the room, beneath a column of rain, stood
Elian and Sera Venn.

They had not aged.

Mara recognized them instantly. Her father's dark curls. Her mother's
sharp chin. The green coat Sera had worn on the
morning they left.

They stood side by side, drawing on a map suspended
in the air.

"Mother?" Mara said.

Sera's pencil stopped.

She turned.

The pencil fell from her hand.

"Mara?"

Her father looked up. His face broke open with wonder
and pain. They crossed the room together and pulled Mara
into their arms.

For a while, there was no room, no storm, no
missing years. Mara smelled lamp oil on her father's coat
and peppermint on her mother's breath. She felt six years
old, lifted between them as they laughed.

Then she felt the unchanged smoothness of their faces.

She stepped back.

"Eleven years," she said.

Her father's smile faded. "Here, it has been eleven months."

Mara's joy sharpened into anger. "You could have come home."

"We tried," Sera said.

"For eleven months?"

"The breach trapped us. Whenever we opened a path to
Bellweather, unstable weather escaped through it. We believed we could
finish the repairs."

"You left me."

"We thought we'd return before winter."

"You left Grandfather."

Elian reached for her, but Mara moved away.

Around them, maps shivered in the windless chamber. The central
column of rain had begun to bend eastward.

Aster watched from the door. "The breach is widening."

Sera turned to the floating map. "We've lost the western
seam."

A crack appeared across the map, bright as lightning. At
once, rain burst from the ceiling and swept sideways through
the chamber.

Aster raised both hands. The drops froze in place.

"For years in Bellweather," she said, "and months here, your
parents have tried to preserve both the passage and the
barrier. It cannot be done. The breach must be sealed
completely."

Elian looked at Mara.

She understood before he spoke.

"If we seal it," he said, "the road home disappears."

"Then come with me now."

"The storm would follow," Sera said. "Not only rain. All
of this."

She gestured toward the maps. Mara saw lines connecting Bellweather
to whirlwinds, blizzards, red clouds, and oceans that floated upside
down.

"What happens if the breach stays open?" Mara asked.

"Your town becomes the first crossing point," Aster replied. "Then
the borders fail elsewhere. Eventually, there will be no distinction
between climates, places, or worlds."

"And if we close it?"

"Bellweather is safe. Your parents remain here."

Mara stared at them.

It was crueler than uncertainty. For eleven years she had
imagined their death, their rescue, their sudden return. Never had
she imagined finding them exactly as they were and being
asked to leave them behind.

"There must be another path," she said.

Her parents exchanged a glance.

Mara knew that glance. They had used it over breakfast
when she proposed mapping the inside of the chimney.

"What?"

Sera picked up her pencil. "There may be another way,
but we cannot see it."

"Show me."

They led her to the largest map on the floor.
It depicted the breach as a black circle surrounded by
thousands of fine lines. Most were tangled beyond recognition.

"We mapped every path the storm takes," Elian said. "All
lead through the breach."

Mara knelt.

The map was beautiful, precise, and useless.

"You drew the rain," she said.

"Yes."

"You drew where it comes from and where it goes."

"Yes."

"But you didn't draw where it refuses to go."

Her parents were silent.

Mara remembered the statue in Market Street, dry amid soaked
pigeons. The rain curving around the pear tree. Water entering
a waiting room without touching its floor.

She pulled out her charcoal.

"Every road is shaped by the places it avoids."

She began marking the empty spaces between the lines.

At first, they seemed random. Then a pattern emerged: dry
islands forming a spiral from the House of Storms to
the glass road, from the road to Candle Lane, from
Candle Lane beneath Bellweather, where they joined under the statue
in Market Street.

Her mother knelt beside her. "A second boundary."

"Not a boundary," Mara said. "A drain."

Aster's silver eyes widened. "The founder's statue."

"What about it?"

"Bellweather's founder was Orrin Vale. My brother. He crossed from
this world three centuries ago and built the town around
the anchor he used to close his passage."

"The statue stands over it," Mara said.

"If the anchor still holds, the excess weather can be
drawn back through the old channel while the breach is
sealed."

"And my parents?"

Aster looked at the map.

"They would have one chance to cross."

The room erupted into motion.

Elian and Sera drew the channel. Aster opened jars from
the shelves and poured winds into the map. Mara charted
the dry path while Compass chased sparks of lightning under
a table.

The crack widened.

The House of Storms groaned. Doors flew open, revealing burning
rain, black snow, and clouds filled with whispering faces. Thunder
woke by the fireplace and began to roar.

"Now!" Aster shouted.

She struck the map with both palms.

The world folded.

Mara was running down the glass staircase with her parents
behind her. Storms poured after them. The forest bent beneath
a wind that smelled of salt and smoke. Rain called
Mara's name from every puddle.

"Mara," it said in Silas's voice.

"Mara," it cried in her mother's voice, though Sera was
beside her.

She did not answer.

At the forest's edge, the man in the yellow coat
stood on his desk, waving a teapot.

"Passage closes in two minutes!" he called. "Possibly three! Time
is metaphorical!"

The glass road shattered behind them.

Ahead, the blue world narrowed into a brick-shaped slit.

Mara jumped through.

She landed in Candle Lane under ordinary, downward-falling rain.

Her father tumbled after her. Then Compass. Sera was halfway
through when the opening snapped inward, catching the edge of
her green coat.

Elian grabbed her hands.

Mara grabbed Elian.

For one terrible second, the wall pulled back.

Then the cloth tore.

They fell together onto the cobblestones.

The slit vanished.

Beneath their feet, the ground trembled. Water rushed through the
gutters toward Market Street. They followed it.

The town square had flooded. Rain circled the founder's statue
in a shining whirlpool. Windows glowed as frightened people watched
from their homes.

Silas stood alone in the water, wearing boots and his
dressing gown.

When he saw Mara, fury crossed his face first.

Then he saw the two figures behind her.

He became very still.

Sera stepped forward. "Father."

Silas made a sound like a door opening in an
abandoned house.

They embraced beneath the statue while rain spun around them.

"Anchor!" Mara shouted.

At the statue's base, beneath the bronze pigeons, she found
a small metal plate engraved with the same spiral she
had drawn in the House of Storms. There was a
keyhole in its center.

Elian searched his pockets. Sera searched hers.

"The key was in our field case," she said. "We
lost it in the marsh."

The whirlpool rose to Mara's knees.

Her brass compass jerked in her hand.

It had never pointed north.

Now it pointed to the keyhole.

Mara understood. The compass had belonged to her mother, and
before that to Orrin Vale. It was not a compass
at all.

She pressed its needle against the keyhole and turned.

The statue rang like a bell.

The whirlpool collapsed.

Every drop of strange rain in Bellweather lifted at once.
Water streamed from roofs, cellars, coats, and chimneys. It rose
into a twisting silver column above the statue. For an
instant, Mara saw the blue forest inside it and Aster
standing beside the black house, one hand raised in farewell.

Then the column narrowed to a thread.

The thread snapped.

Rain fell straight down.

By morning, the storm had passed.

The official explanation involved unusual winds, damaged pipes, and collective
nervous exhaustion. Nobody believed it, but Bellweather had long understood
that an unbelievable explanation was often more comfortable than the
truth.

Elian and Sera returned to their narrow house behind the
clockmaker's shop. They had aged only eleven months, while Silas
had aged eleven years. This was awkward at first. Silas
alternated between embracing them and shouting at them. Sometimes he
did both together.

Compass refused to leave Mara's bag for a week.

As for Mara, she was given a room above the
town archive and the title of Municipal Cartographer, though the
mayor admitted he did not know what she was supposed
to map.

"Everything important," she told him.

She began with the places where rain did not fall.

Over the years, her collection grew. She mapped doors that
appeared only in fog, roads that shortened when travelers told
the truth, and a hill where lost voices gathered at
sunset. She charted ordinary rivers too, and property lines, and
the safest route from the schoolhouse to the bakery.

Her parents sometimes helped, though they had become cautious.

Silas called this improvement.

Every April, on the anniversary of the sideways rain, Mara
walked to the end of Candle Lane and placed her
hand against the wall. Usually it was only brick.

But once, many years later, when she was old enough
to have silver in her hair, she felt something cold
and soft beneath her palm.

A needle-thin gap appeared.

Through it came the scent of blue grass and distant
thunder.

Mara smiled.

Then she took a piece of charcoal from her pocket
and began to draw.

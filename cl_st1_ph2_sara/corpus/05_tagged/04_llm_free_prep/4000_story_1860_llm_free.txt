The Last Lighthouse Keeper

Every morning, Mara climbed the ninety-three iron steps to the
lantern room and polished a light that had not been
lit in seventeen years.

The lighthouse stood on Blackglass Island, a blade of rock
three miles from the mainland. Waves gnawed at its cliffs,
gulls nested in its chimneys, and wind worried endlessly at
the white paint. No ships passed close anymore. They followed
satellite routes far beyond the horizon, guided by instruments more
precise than any lamp.

Still, Mara polished the great crystal lens.

She cleaned salt from the windows, tested the dead clockwork,
and recorded the weather in a green ledger.

October 18. Wind northwest. Rain before dawn. Visibility poor.

There were forty-six ledgers on the shelf, filled by her
mother, her grandfather, and strangers whose black-and-white portraits hung in
the stairwell. Mara had known their names before she had
known the names of the months.

At eight o'clock each evening, she climbed again and sat
beside the dark lamp until midnight.

It was an unnecessary duty. She knew that. The Coastal
Authority knew it too. Every spring, an official came by
boat with forms asking whether the station should be decommissioned.
Every spring, Mara marked the box labeled OPERATIONAL REQUIREMENT and
returned the papers unsigned.

The official always sighed.

The lighthouse had no operational requirement. But neither, Mara believed,
did grief, music, or promises, and people kept those.

Seventeen years earlier, when Mara was eleven, her father had
disappeared at sea.

He had taken the little red boat to the mainland
to collect medicine for Mara's mother. A storm arrived before
sunset. The lighthouse lamp failed an hour later, its gears
split by a lightning strike. Mara's mother lit oil lanterns
in every window, but they were too small to penetrate
the rain.

The red boat never returned.

Two days afterward, broken pieces of its hull washed ashore.

Mara's mother kept the lighthouse for another nine years. During
that time, she replaced the damaged mechanism, converted the lamp
to electricity, and polished the lens every morning. Yet she
never lit it again.

"Why?" Mara once asked.

Her mother looked toward the mainland, a shadow beneath her
eyes.

"Because nothing is coming home."

When illness took her, Mara inherited the keys.

Unlike her mother, she did not believe the sea had
intentions. It did not punish, remember, or forgive. It moved
according to gravity and weather, innocent as an animal turning
in sleep. Yet on certain winter nights, when the waves
struck the cliffs hard enough to shake the tower, she
hated it anyway.

On the morning of October 19, Mara found a bird
in the lantern room.

It lay beneath the eastern window, a small gray creature
with a white stripe over each eye. At first she
thought it was dead. Then it opened one black eye
and trembled.

"You chose the wrong island," Mara told it.

She wrapped the bird in a cloth and carried it
downstairs. In the kitchen, she placed it beside the stove
with a saucer of water and crumbs of oatcake.

The radio crackled on the shelf.

"Mara, do you hear me?"

She recognized Tomas Vale, the mainland harbor master.

"I hear you."

"We've got weather moving in."

"I noticed."

"Bad weather."

"All weather is bad from your office."

"I'm serious. The pressure has dropped faster than anything I've
seen in twenty years. You need to come in."

Mara glanced through the window. The sky was pale and
empty, but the sea had changed. Long swells moved beneath
its surface like muscles under skin.

"When?"

"The supply boat leaves at noon."

"I'm not due for supplies until next week."

"This isn't about supplies. The storm could cover the island."

"Then the tower will be cleaner afterward."

Silence hissed through the radio.

Tomas had grown up with Mara. They had explored tidal
pools together and once stolen a bottle of plum wine
from his aunt. At sixteen, he had kissed her behind
the harbor warehouse and apologized immediately. He had spent much
of the following twelve years apologizing in other ways.

"Mara," he said, "get on the boat."

She looked at the bird beside the stove.

"I'll think about it."

"That means no."

"It means I'll think about it."

"You're impossible."

"And yet perfectly audible."

She switched off the radio.

At noon, the supply boat appeared in the channel. Its
captain sounded the horn twice. Mara stood at the top
of the cliff, her coat snapping around her legs, and
raised one hand.

The captain shouted something she could not hear.

She shook her head.

He remained there for several minutes, the boat rising and
falling on the swells. Then he turned toward the mainland.

By late afternoon, the horizon vanished.

The storm arrived not as rain but as darkness—a wall
rolling over the water. Gulls fled inland. The wind dropped,
and for one unnatural minute the whole island seemed to
hold its breath.

Mara fastened the shutters, filled the oil lamps, and carried
water from the pump. She checked the emergency generator in
the storage room. It coughed twice before starting.

The injured bird perched on the rim of its basket.
Its head moved sharply as it watched her.

"You can stay," Mara said. "But you'll have to earn
your keep."

At six, the rain began.

By seven, it hammered the roof so loudly that Mara
could no longer hear the clock. The radio spoke in
broken fragments: evacuation orders, wind speeds, warnings to fishing vessels.
Then the signal dissolved into static.

Mara climbed the lighthouse.

The tower groaned around her. Water pushed beneath the door,
chasing her boots up the stairs. When she reached the
lantern room, the windows were black with rain.

She turned on the lamp.

The bulb came alive, white and brilliant. For a moment
the entire room shone with fractured rainbows as the Fresnel
lens gathered the light.

Then the lens began to turn.

Its old brass machinery clicked into motion, rolling on wheels
polished by generations of hands. A beam swept across the
storm, vanished, and returned twelve seconds later.

Mara stood at the center of its slow rotation.

The lighthouse had not been lit since the night her
father died.

From below came a sharp metallic crack.

The lamp went out.

Mara swore and hurried downstairs. The generator had stopped. Smoke
drifted from its housing, and the room smelled of burned
rubber. She tried the starter, but the machine only clicked.

The storm struck the walls like thrown gravel.

On a shelf above the generator sat six cans of
lamp oil. They belonged to the lighthouse's older system, preserved
by her mother for no reason except that keepers preserved
things. Mara carried them upstairs two at a time.

The original oil burner still rested beneath the electric fixture.
Its wicks were brittle, its reservoir filmed with dust, but
the design was simple. Her grandfather had taught her how
to use it when she was six.

"Electricity is convenient," he had said. "Fire is loyal."

She cleaned the burner, trimmed the wicks, and filled the
reservoir. Her first match failed. The second burned her thumb.
The third caught.

A yellow flame rose beneath the lens.

The beam returned, dimmer but warmer, circling over the waves.

Mara watched it pass across the eastern window. For one
heartbeat, it illuminated something white in the water.

She waited.

Twelve seconds.

The beam swept back.

There—a boat.

It appeared and disappeared behind the waves, perhaps half a
mile from the island. Its sail hung in ribbons. The
hull was turned broadside to the storm.

Mara seized the radio handset.

"Unidentified vessel east of Blackglass Island, do you hear me?"

Only static answered.

She tried the emergency frequency.

"This is Blackglass Light. Vessel off the eastern shore, respond."

A voice flickered through the noise.

"—engine—three aboard—taking water—"

"Identify yourself."

"Mercy Belle. Research vessel. We lost navigation."

Mara knew the name. The Mercy Belle belonged to the
university's marine laboratory. It was a strong boat, but the
storm drove it toward Widow's Teeth, a reef of basalt
pillars north of the island.

"You're approaching rocks," Mara said. "Can you steer?"

"Barely."

"Keep my light on your port side. I'll guide you
into the southern cove."

"Our charts show no harbor."

"There isn't one. It's a cove, and it's your only
chance."

A wave covered the boat. When it emerged, its mast
was gone.

"How far?" the voice asked.

"Half a mile. Turn south."

"We can't see your light."

Mara looked at the lamp. The flame had shrunk. Wind
forced itself through gaps in the window frames, making the
wicks flutter.

She raised them. Smoke climbed the glass chimney.

"Look again."

A pause.

"I see it."

"Keep it on your right now. When it disappears behind
the cliff, turn toward it."

"That makes no sense."

"It will when you're closer."

The radio went silent.

Mara watched the Mercy Belle drift southward, appearing each time
the beam crossed the water. It moved too slowly. The
current was dragging it north toward the reef.

She ran to the western side of the lantern room,
where the fog bell's lever emerged from the floor. The
bell itself hung outside, green with age. Its clockwork striker
had rusted years ago.

Mara pulled the lever by hand.

The bell gave a deep, lonely note.

She waited five seconds and pulled again.

Two notes meant safe water south. Three meant danger north.
The pattern was older than radio, older than electric light,
old enough to be trusted by frightened people in the
dark.

Two notes.

Pause.

Two notes.

Below, the cottage door banged open.

Mara froze.

She had locked it.

She took the emergency lantern and descended. Wind howled through
the stairwell. At the bottom, she found the door hanging
from one hinge and rain flooding the entry.

The bird's basket was empty.

"Miserable little thing," she muttered.

She fought the door closed and wedged a chair beneath
the latch. As she turned, the radio crackled.

"Blackglass, we've lost sight of you."

She grabbed the handset.

"I'm here."

"Light's gone."

Mara looked up the stairwell. A faint amber glow still
circled overhead.

"You've passed behind the southern cliff. Turn east, toward the
island."

"East will put us on the rocks."

"Not where you are."

"We can't risk it."

"If you continue south, you'll be in open water when
the center of the storm arrives. Your hull won't survive."

A different voice came over the radio, younger and strained.
"How do you know there's a cove?"

"I've lived here all my life."

Lightning flashed through the kitchen, turning every object white.

The voice said, "Turn east."

The radio went dead.

Mara climbed again, pulling the bell lever every few steps.

Two notes.

Pause.

Two notes.

When she reached the lantern room, the flame was failing.
One of the windows had cracked. Rain sprayed across the
floor and hissed on the burner.

She shielded the lamp with her body. Heat pressed against
her arms.

"Come on," she whispered, though she did not know whether
she meant the flame or the boat.

The Mercy Belle had disappeared behind the cliff. If they
had turned east, they would be entering the cove. If
not, the current would carry them into open sea.

The bell rope jerked in her hand.

Something had struck the lower mechanism. The lever would no
longer move.

Mara opened the hatch leading to the gallery outside.

Wind tore it from her grip.

She crawled onto the narrow iron walkway that circled the
lantern room. Rain struck her face hard enough to blind
her. She wrapped one arm around the railing and moved
toward the bell.

It hung above her, black against the sky. The striker
had jammed beneath its rim.

Mara reached for it.

The lighthouse beam passed over the sea, showing waves white
as bone. No boat.

She climbed onto the lower rail.

Far beneath her, water exploded against the cliff.

Her fingers touched the striker. She pulled, but it would
not move. Rust and salt held it fast.

The beam circled again.

For an instant, she saw the entrance to the cove—and
a pale shape riding dangerously close to the rocks.

The Mercy Belle had turned.

Mara took the small hammer from her belt and struck
the bell.

The impact shook her bones.

Once.

Twice.

Pause.

Once.

Twice.

She kept striking.

The boat vanished between the cliffs.

Mara's arm weakened. The hammer slipped from her fingers and
fell into darkness. She clung to the rail as the
wind pressed her outward.

Then, from below, an answering horn sounded twice.

The Mercy Belle had entered the cove.

Mara laughed. The sound was carried away at once.

Inside, the lamp went out.

She crawled back through the hatch and closed it behind
her. The lantern room became completely dark. She felt along
the floor until she found the burner. The chimney had
cracked, and water filled the reservoir.

There would be no more light.

She sat with her back against the lens, breathing smoke
and salt.

The Mercy Belle was in the cove, but the cove
was not safe. Its stone landing lay only a few
feet above normal tide. Tonight's waves might lift the boat
onto the rocks. The three people aboard would need shelter.

Mara descended with a lantern and coil of rope.

Outside the cottage, the wind knocked her to one knee.
She crawled across the island, following the path by touch.
Grass flattened beneath the storm. Pebbles flew through the air.
Once, something large—a branch, though no trees grew on Blackglass—passed
over her head.

At the top of the cove, she tied the rope
to an iron ring set into the rock.

"Mercy Belle!" she shouted.

A light blinked below.

She threw the coil toward it. The wind caught the
rope and flung it aside.

She hauled it back and tried again.

This time, someone caught it.

One by one, three figures climbed from the cove. The
first was a woman with blood running down her forehead.
The second was a young man clutching a metal case.
The third was an older man who collapsed the moment
he reached level ground.

Together, they dragged him toward the cottage.

The storm continued through the night.

Mara gave the survivors blankets and dry clothes. The injured
woman, Dr. Elian Ward, had two broken fingers and a
deep cut above one eye. The older man had swallowed
seawater but remained conscious. The young man, named Kit, refused
to release the metal case.

"What's in there?" Mara asked.

"Water samples," he said.

She stared at him.

He looked embarrassed. "They took six months to collect."

Mara laughed until she had to hold the table.

Near midnight, the cottage windows shattered. They moved into the
lighthouse and climbed above the flooding. On the second landing,
wrapped in blankets, they listened to the tower tremble.

"You saved us," Dr. Ward said.

"The light saved you."

"There shouldn't be a working light here. It isn't on
the charts."

"It worked long enough."

Kit leaned against the wall, still hugging his samples. "Why
keep it if no one uses it?"

Mara thought of her mother polishing the lens year after
year. Because nothing is coming home.

She had misunderstood those words. Her mother had not meant
that the light was useless. She had meant that grief
had convinced her to stop expecting the world to return
anything it took.

Mara looked up the spiral stairs.

"Someone always uses a light," she said. "Even if they
don't know it's there."

The storm's center passed over Blackglass shortly before dawn.

For several minutes, the wind ceased. Stars appeared through the
broken windows, sharp and innumerable. The sea still raged below,
but the silence above it seemed vast enough to enter.

A small bird landed on the stair rail.

Its gray feathers were soaked, and white stripes ran above
its eyes.

"There you are," Mara said.

The bird shook itself, scattering drops, and hopped closer.

Then the wind returned from the opposite direction.

At sunrise, the storm moved inland.

Blackglass Island had lost part of its eastern cliff, both
chimneys, the cottage roof, and most of the path to
the cove. The Mercy Belle lay broken across the rocks,
its bow pointing toward the lighthouse.

But all four people in the tower were alive.

At nine o'clock, a rescue helicopter appeared. It circled twice
before landing on the patch of grass behind the cottage.
Tomas jumped out before the rotors had stopped.

He ran to Mara and gripped her shoulders.

"You stayed," he said.

"So did the lighthouse."

"You could have died."

"So could they."

He glanced at the three survivors waiting by the tower.

"That isn't an argument."

"It's the only one I have."

Tomas pulled her against him. For once, he did not
apologize.

By afternoon, the survivors had been flown to the mainland.
Mara remained on the island, though the rescue pilot called
her several imaginative names when she refused to leave.

She stood in the lantern room and examined the damage.
Three panes were broken. The electric lamp had burned out.
The oil burner was ruined, and one section of the
lens had cracked from top to bottom.

Yet the great clockwork mechanism remained intact.

Mara found the injured bird perched on the lens frame.
It tilted its head toward her and then flew through
the broken window.

She watched it cross the channel toward the mainland.

On her desk downstairs, beneath a pool of rainwater, lay
the green weather ledger. She opened it to a dry
page.

October 20. Storm clearing east. Visibility improving.

She paused, then added another line.

Three souls brought safely ashore.

The following spring, the Coastal Authority official arrived with his
forms. This time, he was accompanied by two engineers, a
historian, and a photographer from the mainland newspaper.

The official placed the decommissioning papers on Mara's kitchen table.

"I assume," he said, "you'll mark the usual box."

"No."

His eyebrows rose.

Mara took the pen and drew a line through the
entire form.

Then she handed him a different document.

The official read it slowly.

After the rescue of the Mercy Belle, donations had come
from sailors, universities, historical societies, and people who had never
seen Blackglass Island. The Coastal Authority had agreed to restore
the station as an emergency beacon. It would also become
a training center for maritime rescue and a refuge for
migratory birds.

At sunset, the engineers completed their work.

Mara climbed the ninety-three steps with Tomas beside her. Together,
they entered the restored lantern room. New glass filled the
windows, but the cracked section of the old lens remained.
Mara had insisted on that.

"Ready?" Tomas asked.

She looked across the darkening sea.

For seventeen years, she had imagined her father somewhere beyond
the horizon, always trying to return. Now she understood that
a light could not bring back the dead. It could
only help the living find their way.

That was enough.

Mara closed the switch.

The lamp blazed.

The great lens turned, sending a white beam over the
water. Each time it passed through the cracked glass, the
light divided briefly into a fan of colors—red, gold, green,
and blue—before becoming whole again.

On the mainland, a harbor horn answered.

Mara stood beside the shining lens, one hand resting on
the warm brass rail.

Far below, waves broke against Blackglass Island. Far above, birds
crossed the evening sky.

And for the first time in seventeen years, the lighthouse
did not seem to be waiting for someone who would
never return.

It was waiting for everyone.

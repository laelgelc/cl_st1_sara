On the morning the sea returned the bells, Mara Vale
was repairing a clock that had never told the right
time.

It was a little brass carriage clock, green at the
hinges, with a white face cracked neatly between the four
and the five. For thirty-seven years it had gained nine
minutes every day, no matter how often Mara cleaned its
gears or adjusted its spring. Its owner, Mrs. Wren, considered
the flaw a virtue.

"Nine minutes," she always said, "is exactly how late my
husband used to be."

Mr. Wren had been dead for twelve years.

Mara was fitting the minute hand back onto its pin
when the first bell rang beneath the harbor.

The sound came through the floor of her shop, deep
enough to stir the jars of screws on the shelves.
It was not the bright note of the church bell
on the hill, nor the cracked clang of the buoy
beyond the reef. This was a drowned sound, heavy with
distance.

Mara's hands became still.

A second bell answered, farther out.

Then a third.

Across the town, doors opened. Fishmongers stepped into the street
with silver scales shining on their aprons. Children ran barefoot
toward the seawall. Old people stood in upstairs windows, staring
beyond the roofs.

Mara put down her tools and walked outside.

The town of Bellwether had been built around a narrow
harbor where the cliffs leaned together like shoulders sheltering a
flame. Once, merchant ships had crowded its stone piers. Now
only fishing boats and the weekly ferry used them. The
great warehouses had become homes, taverns, and shops. Nets hung
from balconies. Gulls nested in old chimneys.

Beyond the harbor wall, the sea lay flat and gray
beneath a cloudless sky.

The bells rang again.

Six notes this time, rising from under the water.

Everyone in Bellwether knew the story. A hundred and eighteen
years earlier, on a winter night, a storm had driven
six ships onto the Black Teeth, the reefs east of
the harbor. The lighthouse keeper had failed to light the
lamp. The ships went down with their cargo, their crews,
and six bronze bells intended for the cathedral in the
capital.

No bodies were recovered.

The lighthouse keeper vanished.

And on rare mornings, people said, the bells could still
be heard beneath the sea.

Mara had never believed it. Bellwether carried too many stories.
There was a ghost in every alley, a curse on
every stone, a mermaid beneath every widow's bed. Legends were
what remained when facts became lonely.

But the bells were ringing.

Beside Mara, a boy in a red cap gripped the
seawall.

"They're coming closer," he whispered.

He was right.

With each peal, the sound shifted toward the harbor mouth.

The crowd drew back as the water began to ripple.
A line appeared on the sea, straight as a cut
in glass. It moved between the piers, pushing aside foam
and floating weeds. Something dark passed beneath the surface.

Then, in the center of the harbor, a bell rose
from the water.

It was enormous, taller than a man, green with age
and furred with barnacles. Seaweed streamed from its crown. No
rope pulled it. No chain supported it. It simply broke
the surface and stood there, balanced upon the waves.

The bell swung.

Its note struck the town.

Windows rattled. Gulls burst into the sky. Mara felt the
sound enter her chest and move through her bones.

The harbor disappeared.

For one breath she was standing on the deck of
a ship beneath a black, storm-torn sky. Men shouted through
rain. A mast split overhead. Beyond the rail, the lighthouse
was dark.

A young sailor clung to the rigging. He was no
older than seventeen. Blood ran from his forehead into one
eye. As the ship tilted toward the reefs, he took
a small silver watch from his pocket, kissed it, and
threw it into the sea.

Then the vision ended.

Mara found herself on her knees.

All around her, townspeople were crying out. Some embraced one
another. Some fled. Others stared at the bell with the
stunned expressions of sleepers abruptly awakened.

The bell swung a final time, though no sound followed.

An object flew from its mouth.

It arced across the water and landed at Mara's feet.

A silver watch.

The crowd went silent.

Mara picked it up.

Its casing was dented and blackened, but when she pressed
the latch, the cover opened. Inside, protected beneath cloudy glass,
the hands pointed to nineteen minutes past midnight.

The watch began to tick.

The floating bell sank. Water closed over its crown, and
the harbor became calm again.

"Mara," someone said behind her.

She turned.

Her father stood at the edge of the crowd.

Elias Vale had once been the finest clockmaker on the
western coast. Kings had owned his clocks, though he claimed
kings were no better at using time than anyone else.
When Mara was a child, customers traveled for days to
bring him broken watches.

Now he was seventy-six and remembered almost nothing.

He often forgot Mara's name. He forgot where their house
was, whether he had eaten, and why his wife no
longer came home. Some days he called Mara by her
mother's name. Other days he looked at her with polite
uncertainty.

But now his eyes were clear.

"I know that watch," he said.

Mara led him to the shop.

The crowd remained at the harbor, arguing about what they
had seen, but Elias sat quietly at the workbench while
Mara lit the lamps. He held the silver watch between
both hands.

On the inside of the cover, beneath the stains, were
three engraved initials: T. A. V.

"Thomas Arden Vale," Elias said. "My grandfather's brother."

Mara frowned. "You never told me about him."

"No." His expression tightened. "My father forbade it."

Outside, the church bell began summoning the town council. Elias
did not seem to hear.

"Thomas was on one of the six ships," he continued.
"The youngest officer on the Bright Mercy. He was seventeen
when it sank."

"The man I saw."

Elias looked up sharply.

Mara described the vision: the storm, the dark lighthouse, the
sailor throwing the watch.

When she finished, her father covered his face.

"The light was not extinguished by the storm," he said.
"Our family put it out."

Mara waited.

Elias lowered his hands. Age returned to them first; they
trembled against the bench.

"The lighthouse keeper was our ancestor, Gideon Vale. He received
word that the ships carried more than bells. Gold, too.
He and three others planned to darken the light so
the vessels would strike the reef. They meant to reach
the wrecks before anyone else."

Mara felt cold despite the summer heat.

"But Thomas was aboard."

"Gideon didn't know. Not until later."

"What happened to him?"

"He confessed to his wife, then vanished. The other men
became rich. Their families still live here."

"Which families?"

Before Elias could answer, the bells beneath the sea began
ringing again.

This time the sound came from all around the town.

Every clock in Mara's shop stopped.

Elias turned toward the window. "They want the truth," he
whispered.

The town council met in the old customs house, a
long stone building overlooking the harbor. By the time Mara
arrived, half of Bellwether had crowded inside. The other half
pressed against the open doors.

Mayor Corven stood beneath a faded painting of the six
lost ships. He was a broad man with a red
face and a talent for speaking as though every idea
had first occurred to him.

"We must remain calm," he declared. "Unusual tides, underwater tremors,
pockets of trapped air—these things can create extraordinary phenomena."

"A bell rose out of the sea," called Mrs. Wren.

"An unusual tide," the mayor repeated firmly.

"And threw Mara a watch?"

"Underwater pressure."

The room erupted.

Mara pushed through the crowd with Elias beside her. When
they reached the front, she placed the silver watch on
the council table.

The ticking carried through the room.

Mayor Corven stared at it. On his right sat Councilor
Pell, owner of the largest fishing fleet. On his left
sat Councilor Saye, whose family owned the ferry and most
of the eastern cliffs.

Elias leaned close to Mara.

"Corven," he murmured. "Pell. Saye. Their ancestors were the others."

Mara raised her hand.

"There is something the town should know."

Perhaps it was her voice, or perhaps it was the
strange ticking of the watch, but the room slowly quieted.

She told them what Elias had told her.

At first there was disbelief. Then shouting. Mayor Corven demanded
proof. Councilor Pell called the story an insult to the
dead. Councilor Saye claimed Elias was confused.

"He can barely remember his own daughter," Saye said. "Why
should we trust his memory of a story from a
century ago?"

Elias flinched.

Mara felt anger rise within her, hot and clean.

"My father remembers clocks," she said. "Even when he forgets
people, he remembers every clock he has repaired, every mechanism
he has studied, every fault in every spring. This watch
belonged to Thomas Vale. The sea returned it today."

"The sea returns all sorts of rubbish," said the mayor.

The watch stopped ticking.

From somewhere beneath the customs house came a violent crash.

The floor lurched. People screamed and stumbled as six wet
cracks spread across the stone tiles. Seawater surged through them,
filling the chamber to the depth of an inch.

Objects began rising from the water.

A rusted compass.

A woman's ring.

A carved wooden horse.

A broken pipe.

A child's shoe.

Hundreds of things surfaced from water far too shallow to
contain them. They floated across the floor, gathering around people's
feet.

Whenever someone touched an object, they froze.

A fisherman picked up the pipe and began sobbing. A
woman seized the wooden horse, then shouted that she could
see a ship breaking in half. Mayor Corven tried to
climb onto the council table, but the ring drifted toward
him against the current and struck his boot.

The instant he touched it, his face went white.

"No," he whispered.

In the flooded room, a voice began to speak.

It did not come from one person. It came from
every wall, every drop of water, every corroded object.

"Return what was taken."

Then the water vanished.

The objects struck the floor with a single clatter.

For a long moment, no one moved.

Mayor Corven sat heavily in his chair.

"There is a chamber beneath the eastern warehouse," he said.

Councilor Pell turned on him. "Be quiet."

"My grandfather showed it to me when I became mayor."
Corven's voice was hollow. "His grandfather built it. Some of
the gold is there."

Chaos followed.

By sunset, the hidden chamber had been opened. Behind a
brick wall lay fourteen iron chests, their wood long rotted
away. Gold coins spilled across the damp floor. There were
far fewer than legend would have suggested, but enough to
explain the Corven family's wealth.

Pell confessed next. His family had melted their share generations
ago and used it to purchase boats.

Saye refused.

"My family built this town," she said before locking herself
inside her cliffside house. "We owe nothing to ghosts."

That night, no one in Bellwether slept.

The bells rang every hour.

At midnight, the first cracks appeared in the eastern cliffs.

Mara stood outside her shop with her father. Lanterns moved
through the streets as families carried possessions uphill. Dust rose
from the direction of Saye's house.

"We should leave," Mara said.

Elias was staring at the lighthouse.

It stood on a narrow island beyond the Black Teeth.
No keeper had lived there for twenty years; an automatic
electric lamp now turned behind its glass. As Mara watched,
the beam swept across the sea.

Then went out.

All along the harbor, people cried out.

In the darkness, six ships appeared.

They were not solid. Stars shone faintly through their sails.
Their broken masts leaned at impossible angles, and water poured
endlessly from their decks. Yet they moved without wind, gliding
toward the reef exactly as they had on the night
they sank.

The bells below rang faster.

Elias gripped Mara's arm.

"It is happening again."

"What do we do?"

"The lighthouse."

They found a boat at the western pier. No one
tried to stop them; the town was too busy watching
the dead ships approach.

Mara rowed.

The sea resisted every stroke. Shapes moved beneath the boat—faces,
hands, coils of rope. Elias sat in the bow holding
Thomas's watch.

"I used to hate clocks," Mara said breathlessly.

Elias looked back at her.

"When I was little. Do you remember?"

He smiled faintly. "You took apart the blue mantel clock
because it chimed during your birthday."

"You were furious."

"I was impressed. You were six."

Mara nearly lost her grip on the oars.

"You remember that?"

"For the moment."

The ghost ships drew closer. On their decks stood pale
figures, all facing the dark lighthouse.

Mara rowed harder.

They reached the island as the first vessel approached the
outer reef. She dragged the boat onto the stones and
helped Elias up the stairs.

The lighthouse door was locked.

Mara smashed the silver watch against it.

The casing bent, but the lock broke.

Inside, they climbed the spiral stairs. The tower shook with
each toll of the underwater bells. Near the top, they
found the electric mechanism torn apart. Wires hung from the
wall. Glass glittered across the steps.

"There's no time to repair it," Mara said.

Elias knelt beside the machinery.

"There is always time to repair something."

He examined the broken assembly, his fingers moving with sudden
confidence. Mara held the lantern while he stripped two wires,
reset a gear, and removed a narrow spring from Thomas's
watch.

"You'll ruin it," she said.

"It has already done what it came to do."

He fitted the spring into the lighthouse mechanism.

Below them, wood screamed against stone.

The first ghost ship had reached the Black Teeth.

"Now," Elias said.

Mara pulled the switch.

Nothing happened.

She pulled it again.

Still nothing.

Elias stared at the gears. His clear expression faded. Confusion
entered his eyes.

"Where are we?" he asked.

Mara's heart broke, but there was no time to let
it.

"We're fixing a clock," she said.

"A clock?"

"The largest one in Bellwether. It's losing time."

Elias looked at the mechanism again.

Then he laughed.

"No," he said. "The escapement is reversed."

With one finger he shifted the spring.

The lamp ignited.

Light burst over the sea.

The ghost ships became bright as bone. The first vessel
had already begun to split against the reef, but as
the beam touched it, its broken hull mended. Torn sails
rose. Masts straightened. One by one, the pale sailors turned
toward the lighthouse.

The young man Mara had seen stood on the forward
deck.

Thomas Vale raised one hand.

Elias lifted the ruined watch.

"I'm sorry," he said.

The bells ceased.

For the first time that day, the sea was completely
silent.

Then the six ships turned.

They sailed between the reefs, through the harbor mouth, and
into Bellwether. People lined the piers, holding lanterns. As each
ship passed, objects rose from its deck and drifted ashore:
letters, lockets, tools, portraits, small treasures carried by ordinary people
who had expected to reach another morning.

The ships continued toward the eastern cliffs.

Councilor Saye's house stood above the water, dark except for
one window.

A figure appeared on the path, running downhill.

Saye carried a wooden chest in her arms.

"Wait!" she cried.

She reached the beach and fell to her knees. The
chest opened, spilling golden cups, coins, and fragments of bronze
onto the sand.

"All of it," she shouted at the ships. "Take all
of it!"

The nearest ship passed through the treasure.

The gold vanished.

Across the cliff, the cracks stopped widening.

At last the six vessels turned toward the horizon. As
they sailed away, they became transparent, then silver, then no
more than lines of moonlight.

Dawn found Mara and Elias sitting outside the lighthouse.

The sea was pale blue. Gulls circled above the island.

Elias held the empty casing of Thomas's watch.

"Was I useful?" he asked.

"You saved the town."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It was."

He nodded, satisfied. After a while, he glanced at her.

"You look like my daughter."

"I am your daughter."

"Ah." He considered this. "I thought you might be."

They returned to Bellwether beneath a clear sky.

In the weeks that followed, the recovered gold was used
to repair the harbor and build a memorial for the
drowned. The names of every lost sailor were carved into
stone, including those of cooks, cabin boys, and unnamed passengers
whom the old histories had ignored.

The Corven, Pell, Saye, and Vale families added their own
names beneath a confession of what their ancestors had done.

Some protested that the living should not answer for the
dead.

Mara disagreed.

Answering was not the same as being condemned. A clock
might inherit a flaw from its maker, but that did
not mean the flaw had to remain.

Mrs. Wren's carriage clock was still on Mara's bench when
she reopened the shop. Mara fitted its minute hand, wound
the spring, and watched it begin to move.

For the first time in thirty-seven years, it kept perfect
time.

Mrs. Wren was disappointed.

"It doesn't feel like him anymore," she said.

So Mara adjusted it to gain nine minutes a day.

That afternoon Elias came into the shop carrying two cups
of tea. He placed one beside her and sat near
the window.

"Do I work here?" he asked.

"You used to."

"Was I any good?"

"The best."

He smiled, though he did not seem to believe her.

From the harbor came the ordinary sounds of Bellwether: hammers
against wood, gulls arguing over fish, children calling from the
seawall. The sea beyond the rooftops was calm.

Mara listened for the bells.

They did not ring again.

But sometimes, when every clock in the shop reached the
same hour, she heard a silver watch ticking inside the
lighthouse lamp, holding the dark back one second at a
time.

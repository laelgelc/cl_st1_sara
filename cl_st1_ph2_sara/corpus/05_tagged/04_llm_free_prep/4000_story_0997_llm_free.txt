The town of Bellweather had three unusual rules.

No one was allowed to whistle after sunset. Every house
had to keep a blue candle in its easternmost window.
And whenever the clock in the railway station struck thirteen,
everyone was expected to ignore it.

The first rule was blamed on birds, the second on
an old superstition no one could explain, and the third
was considered a matter of civic pride.

"Perfectly ordinary clocks make mistakes," Mayor Vale often said. "Drawing
attention to them is impolite."

Most people in Bellweather accepted this. They heard the thirteenth
chime once or twice a month, usually near midnight, and
continued washing dishes, closing curtains, or arguing with their children.
By morning, life would be normal again.

Only Elian Voss listened.

He was sixteen and worked in his aunt's repair shop
across from the station. The shop was narrow, crowded, and
full of clocks that disagreed with one another. Tall clocks
groaned in corners. Tiny silver watches trembled inside glass cases.
Wooden cuckoos leaped from doors at improper hours, shouted into
the dusty air, and vanished again.

Aunt Mara could repair anything except the railway clock.

"That clock does not want to be mended," she would
say.

Elian disliked this answer. Clocks had gears, springs, weights, and
escapements. They did not have desires.

"What's wrong with it?" he asked.

"It's old."

"So are half your customers."

"Customers usually want to be mended."

On the first night of winter, the station clock struck
thirteen.

Elian was alone in the shop. Snow brushed the window
like grains of salt. The blue candle on the sill
had burned low, making the brass pendulums shine.

The first twelve chimes were familiar—deep, measured sounds that seemed
to roll beneath the street. The thirteenth was softer. Elian
felt it more than heard it. The flame of the
blue candle bent toward the station, though the windows were
closed.

Then every clock in the shop stopped.

For several seconds, the only moving thing was Elian's breath.

A moment later, the clocks resumed.

All except one.

It was a small travel clock in a cracked leather
case. A woman had brought it in that afternoon, saying
it had belonged to her missing son. Aunt Mara had
cleaned it and replaced its mainspring, but its hands had
remained fixed at seven minutes past four.

Now they were moving backward.

Elian leaned closer.

The minute hand reversed slowly until it reached four o'clock.
Then the clock clicked and sprang open. Beneath its dial,
where gears should have been, lay a tightly folded strip
of paper.

Elian removed it with tweezers.

Four words had been written in faded ink:

THE CLOCK KEEPS THEM.

A floorboard creaked behind him.

He turned. Aunt Mara stood in the doorway, wrapped in
her gray coat, snow in her dark hair.

For once, she did not look surprised.

"You heard it," she said.

"Everyone heard it."

"No. Everyone heard thirteen chimes. You heard what came after."

Elian held up the message. "What does this mean?"

Mara locked the shop door and pulled the curtains. Then
she crossed the room, pinched out the blue candle, and
sat at the workbench.

"Bellweather's railway clock was built one hundred and eleven years
ago," she said. "The town was growing then. Trains brought
merchants, engineers, musicians, families. They also brought accidents. Fires. Illness.
People arrived full of hope and disappeared full of regret."

"That happens everywhere."

"Yes. But Bellweather had Gideon Rusk."

Elian knew the name. Rusk's bronze statue stood in Founders'
Square, one hand resting on a stone gear. The inscription
called him inventor, benefactor, and keeper of civic time.

"He built the station clock," Elian said.

"He built more than a clock. Rusk believed regret was
wasted time. He claimed he could collect the minutes people
wished they had lived differently."

Elian almost laughed, but Mara's expression stopped him.

"How?"

"By invitation. A person stood beneath the clock and spoke
a memory they wanted taken away. A harsh word. A
missed farewell. A terrible choice. The clock removed the moment—not
from history, but from the person. They forgot their regret
and went home lighter."

"That sounds like mercy."

"At first."

Outside, snow slid from an awning.

Mara continued. "Rusk soon discovered that regret contains power. A
person may forget a moment, but the moment does not
vanish. It becomes trapped time. He used that time to
keep Bellweather prosperous. Factories ran efficiently. Crops survived frost. Trains
arrived safely. People praised him."

"And the missing people?"

"The clock grew hungry. Voluntary minutes were no longer enough.
It began taking them without permission. A minute here, an
hour there. Eventually, entire lives."

Elian looked toward the station. Through a gap in the
curtains, he could see the clock's pale face above the
empty platform.

"That's impossible."

Mara sighed. "You always say that as though impossibility were
evidence."

She took the message from him.

"The clock keeps them," she read. "This was written by
Tomas Arden."

"The owner's son?"

"He disappeared nine years ago. Last seen at the station."

Elian glanced at the little travel clock. "How did his
note get inside?"

"Clocks talk to one another, in their way. Especially when
one of them is frightened."

He waited for her to smile. She did not.

"Why does the town ignore the thirteenth chime?" he asked.

"Because attention opens doors. Each time the clock strikes thirteen,
it searches for someone who is listening."

Elian felt a coldness unrelated to winter.

"And I listened."

"Yes."

The station clock began ticking.

They could hear it across the street, through brick, glass,
snow, and darkness.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Each sound was as heavy as a footstep.

Then someone knocked on the shop door.

Three slow taps.

Elian and Mara did not move.

The taps came again.

"Elian Voss," called a man's voice. "You have lost something."

Elian's hands tightened around the workbench.

The voice was his father's.

He had not heard it in eleven years.

His father, Corren Voss, had left Bellweather when Elian was
five. Mara said he had gone north and died in
a mine collapse. Elian remembered almost nothing of him—only strong
hands, a red scarf, and a song about a sailor
who married the moon.

Now the voice outside began to sing that song.

Elian rose.

Mara seized his wrist. "It is not him."

"You don't know that."

"I do."

The song stopped.

"Elian," the voice said gently, "ask her why your father
really went to the station."

Mara's grip loosened.

Elian looked at her. "What does it mean?"

"Do not answer it."

"What happened to my father?"

The blue candle relit by itself.

Its flame stretched sideways, pointing toward the station.

Mara whispered a curse.

The shop door flew open.

Wind hurled snow through the room. Clock faces flashed. Pendulums
swung wildly. The candle flame lengthened until it became a
narrow blue road suspended in the air.

At its end stood the railway clock.

Not the station itself. Not the tower. Only the enormous
clock face, hanging in darkness.

Its hands pointed to thirteen.

A shadow stepped through it.

The figure had the outline of a tall man in
a red scarf. Where his face should have been, there
was a turning brass dial.

Elian heard his aunt shout, but the sound stretched thin
and distant. The shadow reached for him. Instinctively, Elian raised
the cracked travel clock.

The figure touched it.

The world folded.

Elian fell through a storm of bells.

He landed on a railway platform beneath a green summer
sky.

For a few moments, he could not breathe. Warm air
pressed against his skin. There was no snow. Flowers spilled
from station boxes, and sunlight shone on polished tracks.

The station looked new. People in old-fashioned clothes hurried past
him, carrying trunks and baskets. No one seemed to notice
his sudden arrival.

Above them, the railway clock ticked.

Its hands stood at seven minutes past four.

Elian still held the travel clock. It showed the same
time.

A young man rushed across the platform, bumping Elian's shoulder.

"Sorry," the man said.

Elian recognized him from the portrait on the repair ticket.

"Tomas Arden?"

The man froze. He was perhaps twenty, with windblown hair
and an ink stain on his collar.

"Do I know you?"

"Your mother is waiting for you."

"My mother?"

"In Bellweather. Nine years from now."

Tomas stared at him. Then his face changed.

"Oh," he whispered. "You came through a clock."

Before Elian could answer, Tomas grabbed his sleeve and pulled
him behind a luggage cart.

"Don't let the stationmaster see you," Tomas said. "He reports
strangers."

"Where are we?"

"Fourteen years ago. Or perhaps five minutes from now. It
depends which door you use."

Elian looked around the crowded station. "Are all these people
trapped?"

"Some are memories. Some are lost hours. Some are people.
It's difficult to tell until you speak to them."

A little girl ran past chasing a hoop. The moment
she reached the end of the platform, she vanished and
reappeared at the beginning, laughing exactly as before.

Tomas lowered his voice. "The clock is a house made
of stolen time. Every room is a moment someone wanted
forgotten."

"How do we leave?"

"We don't."

"There has to be a way."

"I've searched for nine years."

"You haven't aged."

"Nine years outside." Tomas glanced up at the clock. "Here,
I have lived this same afternoon perhaps three thousand times."

A whistle shrieked. A black train entered the station, though
its wheels did not turn. The people on the platform
became still. Their faces went blank.

A conductor stepped down.

He wore a red scarf.

His face was a brass dial.

"Elian Voss," he called.

Tomas pulled Elian toward a service door. They ran down
a corridor as the conductor followed with slow, measured steps.

Every door they passed stood open.

Behind one, a woman sat beside a hospital bed, saying,
"I forgive you," while an old man died. Then the
scene began again.

Behind another, a boy watched his dog run into a
road.

Behind a third, Mayor Vale—much younger—accepted an envelope from a
factory owner and whispered, "No one will know."

Thousands of discarded regrets, repeating forever.

At the end of the corridor, Tomas forced open a
metal door. They entered a vast chamber filled with gears.

Each gear was taller than a house. Between the teeth
gleamed trapped scenes: farewells, betrayals, accidents, confessions. Chains vanished into
darkness above. Beneath the machinery, a pendulum swung over a
bottomless shaft.

"This is the heart of it," Tomas said.

"Can we stop it?"

"I tried. The clock repairs itself with stolen time."

The conductor appeared in the doorway.

"Elian," it said in Corren Voss's voice, "you have come
to recover your father."

The brass dial rotated. Its numbers scattered, and a human
face emerged beneath them.

Elian knew the strong jaw and tired gray eyes from
an old photograph.

His father smiled.

"Elian."

Tomas stepped back. "Don't trust it."

But the man removed his red scarf and held it
out. A section near the end had been clumsily mended
with blue thread. Elian remembered that thread. He remembered sitting
under the kitchen table while his mother repaired the scarf.

"You're alive," Elian said.

His father's smile broke.

"Not exactly."

The gears thundered around them.

Corren looked toward the doorway, as though afraid of being
overheard. "I don't have long. It uses me when it
needs a familiar voice."

"Why are you here?"

"I tried to destroy the clock."

Elian thought of Mara's silence. "She knew."

"She helped me."

Corren approached, but Tomas raised a metal bar.

"Stay back."

"He's himself," Elian said.

"For the moment," Corren replied. "The clock cannot imitate love
perfectly. It must borrow the person who felt it."

He touched Elian's cheek. His hand was cold, but real.

"I'm sorry," he whispered. "You were five. I thought I
could save the town and return before breakfast."

"What happened?"

"We learned the truth about Bellweather's prosperity. Every fortunate year
had been purchased. For every fire that failed to spread,
the clock took someone's final hour. For every safe harvest,
it swallowed a traveler. The council knew. They still know."

Elian remembered the mayor's practiced smile.

"Why didn't you stop it?"

"We reached the heart, as you have. But the clock
offered us a choice. Destroy it, and all the stolen
time returns at once."

"What does that mean?"

"Bellweather loses everything the clock prevented. A century of accidents.
Fires. Illnesses. Deaths. All restored."

Tomas lowered the bar.

Elian stared at the enormous machinery. "Then it can't be
destroyed."

"That is what it wants you to believe," Corren said.
"Time cannot restore what never occurred. The clock is lying.
It does not protect Bellweather by changing history. It protects
Bellweather by feeding on possible futures."

"I don't understand."

"It takes people at crossroads—people who might expose corruption, invent
something, raise a child, forgive an enemy, leave town, return
home. It devours what they could become. Bellweather's good fortune
is not miraculous. The clock simply removes anyone who might
disturb the town's preferred path."

The machinery jolted.

Corren cried out. The brass dial spread across half his
face.

"Hurry," he gasped. "It knows I'm resisting."

"How do we destroy it?"

"You can't. But you can make it release what it
keeps."

He pointed toward the pendulum.

At its highest point, a small keyhole flashed.

"The clock was built to take regretted moments. It must
return them if someone freely accepts all the regret inside."

Tomas looked into the shaft. "That would be a century
of grief."

"Yes."

"It would kill him."

"Perhaps."

Elian stepped to the edge.

Below, the pendulum cut through darkness. Its keyhole appeared only
for an instant with each swing.

"Where's the key?"

Corren looked at the travel clock.

Elian opened its leather case. The minute hand had detached.
It lay across the dial, thin and silver, shaped like
a key.

Footsteps echoed from every corridor.

Stationmasters entered the chamber—dozens of them, all wearing red scarves,
all with dials for faces.

The clock spoke through them together.

"Bellweather is safe."

Corren staggered as brass covered his mouth.

"Bellweather is prosperous."

Tomas swung the metal bar at the nearest figure. Its
glass face shattered, but behind it was another face, and
another.

"Elian!" Tomas shouted.

Elian gripped the silver hand and watched the pendulum.

It swung close.

He missed.

The force spun him to the floor. The silver key
nearly slipped into the shaft.

The stationmasters advanced.

In their rotating faces, Elian saw possible lives. Himself as
a famous clockmaker. Himself old and content. Himself leaving Bellweather.
Himself marrying someone whose face remained hidden by sunlight.

"Give us one regret," the clock said. "Only one, and
you may go home."

A memory rose inside him: his mother's last morning. She
had been ill, asking him to sit with her, but
he had gone outside to play. By evening, she was
gone.

For years he had wished to erase that bright, ordinary
hour.

The station dissolved around him.

He stood in his childhood kitchen. His mother sat by
the window, pale beneath a blanket. Sunlight rested on her
hands.

"Stay a little," she said.

Beyond the door, children called his name.

Elian felt his eight-year-old impatience, sharp and innocent. He remembered
saying, "I'll come back soon."

He remembered not looking at her as he left.

"Give it to me," whispered the clock. "You do not
need to carry this."

Elian approached his mother.

She looked exactly as he remembered and nothing like the
final, still figure in her bed.

He knelt beside her chair.

"I'm sorry," he said.

The memory waited.

He could surrender it. He could let the clock remove
the guilt, the sleepless nights, the question of whether she
had died believing he did not care.

Instead, he took her hand.

"I'm keeping it."

The kitchen cracked apart.

Elian was back in the chamber. The pendulum rushed toward
him.

He leaped.

The silver hand entered the keyhole.

For one endless second, nothing happened.

Then the clock opened.

Grief poured through him.

He felt every word spoken too late. Every door left
unopened. Every hand not held. He was the woman at
the hospital bed, the boy watching the road, the worker
who ignored a warning, the father who boarded a train,
the sister who burned a letter, the mayor who accepted
the bribe.

He felt Tomas's mother waiting year after year beside an
untouched place at the table.

He felt Mara watching Corren disappear.

He felt Corren realize he would never see his son
grow.

The weight of it drove Elian to his knees.

Yet beneath the regret lay something the clock had never
understood.

Love.

Not clean love, or painless love, but love tangled with
failure. Love that mattered because time could be wasted. Love
that asked forgiveness precisely because the past could not be
repaired.

Elian seized that tangled weight and pulled.

The giant gears screamed.

Across the chamber, trapped moments broke free. Doors flew open.
The hospital woman rose from her chair. The boy turned
away from the road. The little girl dropped her hoop
and stared at the sky as though seeing it for
the first time.

The stationmasters shattered.

Corren fell.

Tomas caught him while Elian held the key in place.

"More!" Elian shouted, though he did not know to whom.

The clock resisted. Its hands spun backward. Cracks raced across
its face.

Then blue light flooded the chamber.

Aunt Mara stood in the doorway holding the candle from
the shop.

"You foolish child," she said, tears on her face. "You
were not supposed to do this alone."

She set the candle beneath the pendulum.

The flame split into hundreds of blue paths, each leading
toward a different door.

The trapped people began to run.

Some emerged old. Some remained young. Some faded into sparks
because they had been memories rather than bodies. Tomas helped
Corren toward the nearest path.

"Elian!" Mara called.

He tried to release the key, but his fingers would
not move.

The clock had wrapped thin brass wires around his wrist.

A voice boomed through the gears.

"IF I END, BELLWEATHER ENDS."

"No," Elian said. "Bellweather changes."

He tore his hand free.

The pendulum split down the middle.

Darkness swallowed everything.

When Elian woke, he was lying in the snow outside
the repair shop.

Dawn colored the sky. Across the street, the railway clock
was frozen at seven minutes past four.

People stood along the platform in bewildered groups.

A young man with an ink-stained collar was embracing an
elderly woman who had dropped her walking stick. Near them,
Aunt Mara sat in the snow beside Corren Voss.

Elian approached slowly.

His father looked older now. Gray threaded his hair, and
deep lines surrounded his eyes. Time, released from the clock,
had settled on him all at once.

But he was alive.

"Elian," Corren said.

There were too many things to say, so neither of
them tried. Elian knelt, and they held each other while
bells rang throughout the town.

In the weeks that followed, Bellweather became less fortunate.

The mayor resigned after records of bribes appeared inside his
locked desk. A factory closed. A storm damaged the eastern
bridge. Trains arrived late, crops suffered blight, and people discovered
that ordinary life was neither safe nor fair.

But lost citizens returned.

Some came from the station. Others woke in fields, attics,
or abandoned houses. Families met strangers who carried the faces
of people they had mourned. Not every reunion was joyful.
Time had separated husbands from wives, parents from children, promises
from those who had made them.

The town argued about what to do with the railway
clock.

Some wanted it repaired.

Most wanted it destroyed.

Elian climbed the tower alone and removed its hands. He
found no gears behind the face—only thousands of folded notes,
each bearing a regret someone had once tried to surrender.

He carried the notes to the square and burned them
in a public fire. People watched in silence. A few
wept. Others turned away.

The three unusual rules of Bellweather were soon abandoned.

People whistled after sunset whenever they pleased. Blue candles disappeared
from eastern windows. And the railway clock never struck thirteen
again.

A year later, Elian reopened the repair shop with Mara,
Corren, and, occasionally, Tomas, who had discovered he disliked repairing
clocks but enjoyed making tea.

They kept the broken station hands above the door.

Customers often asked why.

Elian would look around the shop, where dozens of clocks
ticked at slightly different speeds, none perfectly correct and none
expected to be.

Then he would smile.

"To remind us," he said, "that time should never be
trusted to keep what belongs to the heart."

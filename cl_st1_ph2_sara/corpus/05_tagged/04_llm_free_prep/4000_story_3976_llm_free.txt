The Bell Beneath the Lake

Every morning at six, Mara Venn climbed the eastern watchtower
and rang the brass bell over Bellweather Lake.

She rang it once for clear skies, twice for rain,
and three times for fog. In winter, when ice sealed
the lake and snow buried the road to the capital,
she rang four times to warn travelers that the mountain
pass was closed.

No one had appointed her to the task. The watchtower
had belonged to her father, and before him to her
grandfather. After her father died, the villagers simply continued to
expect the bell, and Mara continued to ring it.

She was seventeen when she first climbed the tower alone.
At twenty-nine, she could count the steps in darkness and
knew each one by its complaint: the sighing seventh, the
cracked thirteenth, the twenty-second that shifted beneath the heel. She
knew how the bell rope stiffened before snow and how
swallows nested behind the western shutters each spring.

She also knew the lake had once been a town.

On still days, roofs could be seen far below the
surface—dark triangles beneath the green water. There had been a
square, a mill, a chapel, and thirty-seven houses. The town
had been flooded sixty years earlier when the river was
dammed to power the mills downstream.

The villagers called the drowned place Old Bellweather. Mara's grandfather
had called it home.

"They moved the graves," he used to tell her. "They
carried out the chapel windows and the school benches. But
no one thought to take the bell."

The bell of Old Bellweather still hung in its submerged
chapel tower.

Or so the stories claimed.

On summer nights, fishermen sometimes heard it ringing beneath their
boats. A low, impossible note would pass through the hull,
trembling in their bones rather than their ears. The old
people said the drowned bell rang when the lake intended
to take someone.

Mara did not believe that.

She believed in rotting wood, sudden winds, deep water, careless
sailors, and the power of fear to turn an ordinary
sound into an omen. When fishermen failed to return, she
helped search for them. When bodies were recovered, she helped
wash and bury them.

The lake took no one, she would say. People entered
it, and sometimes they did not come back.

Then, one autumn morning, the bell beneath the lake rang
for her.

It happened before dawn.

Mara woke with her hand pressed against the bedroom wall.
The cottage was silent, but a vibration traveled through the
stone, faint and deep.

Once.

The water pitcher trembled on the table.

Twice.

Dust fell from the ceiling beam.

Three times.

Mara held her breath.

The sound was not like the bright bell in the
watchtower. It was older and heavier, a voice buried beneath
mud and water. The third note faded slowly, leaving the
cottage so quiet that Mara could hear rain ticking against
the window.

She dressed and went outside.

Fog had swallowed the village. The houses along the shore
were pale shapes without edges, their chimneys trailing smoke into
whiteness. No lamps moved on the road. No dogs barked.
The lake was invisible, but Mara could smell it: wet
stone, reeds, and autumn weeds.

She began climbing the hill to the watchtower.

At the twenty-second step, the loose board shifted.

At the top, she opened the eastern shutters and stared
into fog. She could not see the lake, let alone
the far shore. By custom, she should ring three times.

Instead, she stood with the rope in her hand.

The drowned bell had also rung three times.

"Mara?"

She turned. A boy stood at the top of the
stairs, panting. It was Elian Cross, the miller's youngest son.
He was sixteen, long-limbed, and usually cheerful, but his face
was white.

"It's my sister," he said. "Lina's gone."

Mara tightened her grip on the rope.

"Gone where?"

"No one knows. Her bed wasn't slept in. Her coat
is missing, and so are Father's boat keys."

The watchtower bell hung above them, waiting.

Mara pulled the rope three times.

Its clear notes rolled over the unseen lake.

By sunrise, every household in Bellweather was searching.

Lina Cross was twenty-two and due to marry a carpenter
from the northern valley in six days. She had no
reason to run away, according to her father. She had
every reason, according to half the village, though none could
agree what those reasons were.

Some said she feared marriage. Others said she had quarreled
with her fiancé. The baker's widow claimed Lina had been
seen speaking with a stranger near the old dam. A
shepherd insisted he had watched a lantern moving over the
lake after midnight.

They found the miller's boat tied to a dead willow
on the southern shore.

Inside lay one oar, a woolen scarf, and a brass
handbell green with age.

Mara recognized the handbell at once. It had once stood
on her grandfather's mantel. He claimed it came from the
schoolhouse in Old Bellweather. After his death, Mara had packed
it into a wooden chest beneath her bed.

She ran home.

The chest was open.

Nothing else had been taken.

By noon, the village council gathered in the inn. Rain
rattled against the windows while twenty people talked over one
another.

"She stole the boat," said the miller, red-eyed and furious.
"She crossed the lake and went on foot. That's all."

"With one oar?" asked the baker's widow.

"The other washed away."

"And left no footprints?"

"The rain took them."

"The lake took her," someone whispered.

Mara stood near the hearth, holding the brass handbell. It
was cold despite the fire. Fine mud filled the grooves
around its handle.

Elian sat at a table in the corner, his face
buried in his hands.

Mara crossed the room and placed the bell before him.

"Did Lina know this belonged to me?"

He looked up. "She knew the story."

"What story?"

"About the old chapel."

Mara sat down.

Elian glanced toward his father before lowering his voice. "Lina
had been asking questions about Old Bellweather. She went to
see Grandmother Senn three times this month."

Grandmother Senn was the oldest person in the village and
one of the few who remembered the town before it
drowned.

"What did she ask?"

"I don't know. When I asked, she told me to
mind my own business."

"Did she say anything else?"

Elian hesitated. "She said the lake level was falling."

That was true. The summer had been dry, and the
first autumn rains had not yet restored the streams. A
band of pale stone circled the lake, marking where the
water usually stood.

"But the ruins are still thirty feet down," Mara said.

"Most of them."

A gust struck the inn, making the windows shudder.

Mara picked up the handbell.

"Come with me."

Grandmother Senn lived beyond the sheep meadow in a house
crowded with clocks. None showed the correct hour. As Mara
and Elian entered, a dozen mismatched chimes announced times ranging
from noon to midnight.

The old woman sat by her stove, peeling an apple.

"I wondered how long you would take," she said.

"To do what?" Mara asked.

"To ask the right question."

"Where is Lina?"

"That is not the right question."

Elian stepped forward. "She's my sister."

"And because she is your sister, you think fear gives
you permission to waste time."

Mara set the brass bell on the table.

Grandmother Senn stopped peeling.

"Where did Lina find this?" Mara asked.

The old woman looked at the mud around its handle.
"She didn't find it. She borrowed it from you."

"Why?"

"To wake what was sleeping."

Elian made an impatient sound, but Mara raised a hand.

"What did Lina want in Old Bellweather?"

Grandmother Senn smiled faintly. "There. Better."

She rose, took one of the clocks from the wall,
and opened its case. From behind the pendulum she removed
a folded sheet of paper.

It was a map of the drowned town.

The old woman pointed to the chapel square. "When the
dam was built, the engineers sealed the old river channel
with stone and iron. But they left a maintenance passage
from the southern shore. Lina believed it could still be
entered."

"Why would she go there?"

"Because of the rain."

Mara listened. The storm was strengthening, water rushing from the
roof into barrels outside.

Grandmother Senn traced the old river on the map. "The
dam was built quickly. Too quickly. Everyone knew it, but
the mill owners paid inspectors to remain silent. Your grandfather
helped lay the foundation, Mara. He kept records—measurements, faults, repairs
that were never made."

Mara remembered her grandfather's notebooks, dozens of them, filled with
figures she had never understood.

"Lina found those records?"

"I showed them to her."

"Why?"

"Because no one listens to an old woman, but everyone
listens to a bride." Grandmother Senn's expression hardened. "She intended
to enter the maintenance passage, find the original inspection chamber,
and bring back proof that the dam is failing."

A silence followed.

Elian stared at her. "She went alone?"

"She was not supposed to. I was to meet her
at midnight with ropes and lanterns, but my knees were
swollen, and I could not walk. I sent word telling
her to wait."

"She didn't receive it," he said.

"No."

Mara looked again at the map. The passage began near
the willow where the boat had been found.

"Why take my handbell?"

"The inspection chamber has two doors. According to your grandfather,
the inner door could close if the water pressure changed.
The workers used bells to signal through the walls."

Mara understood. "She expected someone to follow."

Grandmother Senn nodded.

A deep vibration passed through the house.

The clocks trembled on their hooks.

Once.

Elian went still.

Twice.

The apple rolled from the table and struck the floor.

Three times.

Then a fourth.

Grandmother Senn closed her eyes.

"What does four mean?" Elian whispered.

Mara picked up the map.

"It means we are running out of time."

The entrance to the maintenance passage lay behind thorn bushes
and a slab of fallen masonry. Rain poured down the
hillside as Mara and Elian cleared branches away. At last
they uncovered a rusted iron door, no higher than Mara's
shoulder.

Water streamed from beneath it.

Together they pulled it open.

Air breathed out, cold and mineral-dark.

Elian lit two lanterns. Mara tied a rope around her
waist and gave him the other end.

"You stay here."

"No."

"If the passage floods, someone must pull me back."

"You don't know Lina's signals."

"And you do?"

He swallowed. "One ring means stop. Two means come forward.
Three means danger."

"Four?"

"We never used four."

Mara thought of the bell beneath the lake.

"Stay," she said. "That is not a request."

She entered before he could argue.

The passage descended sharply. Its brick walls sweated, and roots
had forced their way through the ceiling. Water rose from
her ankles to her knees. The lantern flame bent in
currents of air that moved like invisible animals.

After fifty steps, she reached the first chamber.

Rusted tools lay on shelves. A table had collapsed beneath
the weight of silt. On the far wall, faded red
letters read:

INSPECTION SHAFT — KEEP CLEAR.

A circular iron door stood open beyond it.

Mara raised her lantern.

On the other side, stone steps climbed into darkness.

She tugged the rope twice and continued.

The stairs led upward through the drowned town's foundations. Mara
could hear water moving beyond the walls—a constant grinding and
booming, as though the lake were dragging furniture across a
room.

Then came a sound that stopped her.

A bell.

Not the enormous note she had heard that morning, but
a small, weak ringing.

Two chimes.

Come forward.

"Lina!" Mara shouted.

An answer came faintly. "Here!"

Mara climbed faster.

The passage ended at a narrow chamber lined with iron
braces. Lina sat against the wall, soaked and shivering. One
leg was trapped beneath a fallen beam. Beside her lay
a leather document case and a small hammer.

Relief crossed her face when she saw Mara.

"Took you long enough."

Mara knelt by the beam. "Can you move your foot?"

"No."

"Broken?"

"Probably."

Mara examined the brace. The beam had fallen from the
ceiling, bringing stones with it. Water sprayed through the gap.

"What happened?"

"The chamber shifted when the rain started. I found the
inspection plates and copied the marks, but the ceiling came
down." Lina nodded at the document case. "There are photographs
too. The foundation is split almost end to end."

A groan passed through the walls.

Dust drifted onto them.

Mara pulled at the beam. It did not move.

"Elian is outside. We have a rope."

"My father will kill me."

"He'll have to wait."

They looped the rope around the beam. Mara rang the
handbell twice, then three times, hoping Elian would understand: pull,
danger.

The rope tightened.

The beam shifted an inch.

Lina cried out, then bit her sleeve.

"Again," Mara shouted.

The rope pulled harder. Mara braced both feet against the
wall and lifted. The beam rose just enough for Lina
to drag her leg free.

At that moment, something cracked below them.

Water exploded up the stairwell.

It struck Mara at the waist and extinguished the lantern.
The chamber plunged into darkness.

"Hold the rope!" she yelled.

They were dragged from their feet.

The current pulled them down the stairs, through the circular
door, and into the first chamber. Mara struck a wall,
lost the handbell, and felt Lina's fingers slip from hers.

Then the rope tightened around her waist.

Elian hauled from the entrance.

Mara found Lina in the dark, caught the back of
her coat, and locked both arms around her. The water
rose to her chest, her shoulders, her chin. The current
hammered them against the bricks.

From somewhere below came a vast metallic toll.

The drowned bell rang once.

The passage shook.

It rang twice.

A section of ceiling collapsed behind them.

It rang three times.

The water surged, carrying wood, stones, and the lost handbell
past Mara's face.

She kicked blindly and felt open air above her. Elian
kept pulling. Inch by inch, the rope dragged them upward.

The drowned bell rang a fourth time.

The sound filled the passage so completely that Mara seemed
to become part of it. She felt the note in
her teeth, her ribs, her blood. For one impossible moment,
she saw the drowned town as her grandfather must have
known it: dry streets under summer sun, sheets snapping on
clotheslines, children racing across the square, the chapel door painted
blue.

Then the vision vanished.

Mara's hands struck mud and grass.

Elian seized her coat and pulled her into the storm.
Together they dragged Lina clear as water burst through the
maintenance door behind them.

They lay in the rain, gasping.

Across the lake, a horn began to sound at the
dam.

The watchmen had seen the pressure gauges rise.

By evening, the lower villages were evacuated.

At midnight, part of the old dam gave way.

The break was smaller than the engineers feared but large
enough to send a wall of water down the empty
valley. It tore out two bridges, flattened the abandoned mills,
and covered the southern road in mud. No lives were
lost.

The photographs in Lina's case survived. So did the inspection
plates she had copied. They proved that the mill owners
had concealed faults in the dam for decades. By spring,
the capital ordered the structure dismantled and the lake drained.

Bellweather Lake fell slowly.

First came fences and treetops. Then chimneys emerged, black with
weeds. Roofs rose into daylight, followed by roads shining with
silt. Fish twisted in abandoned gardens until villagers carried them
to the river in buckets.

At last, the chapel tower broke the surface.

Its stonework was cracked, and its wooden doors had vanished,
but the bell remained in the belfry.

Mara rowed to it with Lina and Elian on the
first clear morning of summer. Lina's leg had healed badly,
and she walked with a cane. She had postponed her
wedding but not canceled it. Elian claimed the adventure had
made him wiser, though no one else had noticed.

They climbed the chapel tower.

The old bell was green and scarred. Its rope had
rotted away years ago.

Mara touched the metal.

"Do you still think it was only a story?" Lina
asked.

Mara looked across the shrinking lake toward the eastern watchtower.
Its brass bell shone above the village roofs.

"I think water moves iron," she said. "I think pressure
travels through stone. I think frightened people hear patterns in
thunder."

Elian laughed. "That sounds like yes."

Mara smiled.

Inside the old bell, carved beneath sixty years of moss,
they found a line of words:

A BELL IS NOT A WARNING.

IT IS A PROMISE THAT SOMEONE IS LISTENING.

Mara read the inscription twice.

The villagers later removed the drowned bell and raised it
beside the watchtower. They cleaned it but did not polish
away the green stains. On the first day of every
autumn, both bells were rung together: the bright bell for
those who lived above the water, and the deep bell
for everything that had once seemed lost.

Mara remained the keeper of the tower.

Each morning at six, she climbed the familiar stairs. Once
for clear skies. Twice for rain. Three times for fog.
Four when the mountain pass was closed.

And sometimes, when a traveler was missing or a child
had wandered too far into the woods, she rang a
fifth time.

That note had no meaning in the old customs, so
Mara gave it one.

It meant: We know you are out there.

It meant: Do not stop calling.

It meant: We are coming.

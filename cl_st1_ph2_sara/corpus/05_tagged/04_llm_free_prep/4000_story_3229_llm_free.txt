# The Cartographer of Lost Roads

Every morning, before the bells of Saint Orra struck six,
Elias Vale walked to the edge of the city and
drew a road that no longer existed.

He kept his maps in a narrow shop between a
bakery and a locksmith's, where the buildings leaned together as
though exchanging secrets. Above the door hung a wooden sign
painted with a compass rose and the words:

**ELIAS VALE — MAPS OF PLACES FOUND AND FORGOTTEN**

Most customers came for ordinary reasons. Sailors wanted charts of
the southern shoals. Merchants wanted the shortest route through the
copper hills. Newly married couples wanted decorative maps of the
city, with their first home marked by a red heart.
Elias accepted each commission, sharpened his pencils, and worked with
the careful patience of a man repairing a watch.

But in the locked drawer beneath his desk, he kept
a different atlas.

Its pages showed vanished alleys, demolished bridges, stairways buried under
newer streets, and country lanes swallowed by forests. Some had
disappeared through war or weather. Others had simply been forgotten.
Elias believed a road did not cease to exist merely
because no feet walked upon it. Every journey left an
impression on the world. Given enough skill, a mapmaker could
still detect it.

His father had taught him that.

"A true map," Silas Vale used to say, "does not
show where things are. It shows how one thing may
reach another."

When Elias was a boy, he had followed his father
through the city carrying rolls of paper under one arm.
Silas mapped everything: drains, rooftops, old wells, cellar tunnels, shortcuts
between gardens. He could stand at a crossroads, close his
eyes, and tell which lane led to the river by
the smell of wet stone.

Then, when Elias was seventeen, Silas vanished.

He had gone to investigate a road outside the northern
gate, a road mentioned in old tax records but absent
from every modern map. His horse returned the next morning
with an empty saddle and silver mud on its hooves.

No body was found. No road was found. Over time,
the city stopped searching.

Elias did not.

For twenty-three years, he gathered scraps of evidence. A shepherd
remembered a line of white stones near the northern marsh.
A dying soldier spoke of a road visible only in
moonlight. A widow brought Elias a letter in his father's
handwriting, posted three years after his disappearance but dated the
day before it.

The letter contained only one sentence:

**Some roads are lost because they do not want to
be found.**

Elias placed the letter in his secret atlas and continued
drawing.

On the first morning of winter, a girl entered his
shop carrying a broken compass.

She appeared to be about twelve. Her coat was too
large, its sleeves folded back twice, and rain shone in
her dark hair. She looked around at the maps covering
the walls but showed none of the usual wonder.

"Are you Elias Vale?" she asked.

"I am."

"The man who maps lost roads?"

Elias set down his pen. "Who told you that?"

"Your father."

The bakery clock next door ticked loudly through the wall.

Elias studied the girl's face. She had sharp eyebrows, a
small scar on her chin, and the steady gaze of
someone accustomed to being doubted.

"My father is dead," he said.

"No," she replied. "But he is running out of time."

She put the compass on his desk.

Its brass case was dented, and the glass had cracked
across the center. The needle spun slowly, though the instrument
lay still. Elias knew it at once. He had engraved
the tiny mark on its lid himself: an awkward letter
E, carved when he was ten.

It had belonged to Silas.

"Where did you get this?"

"From him."

"Where?"

The girl pointed north.

Elias came around the desk. "What is your name?"

"Mara."

"How did you find my father?"

"He found me. Two months ago, my village disappeared."

Elias paused. "Villages do not disappear."

"This one did."

She said it so plainly that he did not argue.

Mara unbuttoned her coat and removed a folded sheet of
cloth. It was a handkerchief, stained with mud. On it,
someone had drawn a rough map in charcoal. Elias recognized
his father's style in the precise curves, the small symbols
for water and stone, the neat arrows showing elevation.

The map depicted the northern marsh and, beyond it, a
road ending at a black square.

Beside the square were three words:

**THE HOUSE BETWEEN**

"Your father said you would know how to finish it,"
Mara said.

"I don't."

"He said you would say that too."

Elias almost smiled. It was exactly the sort of irritating
prediction Silas enjoyed making.

"Tell me everything."

Mara sat near the stove and held her hands toward
the heat.

Her village, Bellweather, lay far north of the city, beyond
the hills and marshes. It was small: thirty houses, a
chapel, a mill, and an inn called the Sleeping Fox.
In autumn, a traveler had arrived. He wore a gray
coat and carried a book without words.

"He asked us the way to Saint Orra," Mara said.
"Everyone told him to take the southern road. But he
said that road was too long. He wanted a shorter
one."

The villagers warned him about an old path through the
woods. No one used it anymore. It led to ruined
country where the trees grew crooked and compasses pointed in
circles.

The traveler laughed. That night, he opened his blank book
at the inn and drew a road in it.

"In the morning," Mara continued, "there was a new road
outside the village. Straight and white, running south. The traveler
left on it. Some villagers followed, thinking it would take
them to the city."

"Did they return?"

"No. And every day after that, the village became harder
to leave. The normal roads curved back. The river flowed
in a circle. Then houses began vanishing."

Elias leaned closer.

"Not crumbling," Mara said. "Vanishing. First the mill. Then the
chapel. People forgot them as soon as they were gone.
When my mother disappeared, no one remembered I had a
mother."

The stove popped.

"How did you escape?"

"Your father came."

Silas had entered Bellweather on foot, carrying a lantern that
burned blue. He seemed old and weary, but he knew
what was happening. The strange road, he explained, was consuming
the village by severing it from the rest of the
world. Every home is held in place by its connections—paths
to neighbors, trails to wells, routes to markets, memories of
journeys taken and repeated. Cut enough connections, and a place
becomes unmoored.

Silas led Mara out along a road only he could
see.

"Why didn't he come with you?" Elias asked.

"He stayed to hold the road open. He said he
could manage for nine days."

"How long ago was that?"

"Eight."

Elias rose so quickly that his chair fell backward.

He took his heaviest coat, his father's compass, the cloth
map, and the secret atlas. From a cabinet, he removed
a small blue-glass lantern. It had not been lit since
his father disappeared.

Mara watched him pack bread, rope, pencils, ink, and a
brass ruler.

"Is that all?" she asked.

"What else do we need?"

"A weapon."

Elias considered this, then took the locksmith's hatchet from next
door.

They left before sunrise.

Beyond the northern gate, the city faded into fields silvered
with frost. Mara walked quickly, never complaining. By noon they
reached the copper hills, where the road split around abandoned
mines. Elias checked the broken compass.

The needle pointed northeast, away from Bellweather.

"It doesn't point north anymore," Mara said.

"No. It points toward my father."

"How?"

"I don't know."

"You're a strange mapmaker."

"All mapmakers are strange. We spend our lives deciding where
the world ends at the edge of a page."

They climbed until evening. From the last hilltop, Elias saw
the marsh stretching below them, dark pools reflecting a blood-colored
sky.

He opened his secret atlas.

Many years ago, he had mapped this region from farmers'
stories and military surveys. A causeway once crossed the marsh,
but it had sunk a century earlier. On his page,
he had drawn it as a dotted line.

Now, in the fading light, pale stones emerged from the
water.

Mara saw them too.

They descended.

The vanished causeway held beneath their feet, though black water
lapped over the stones. Mist covered the marsh. Reeds whispered
without wind. Once, something large moved below the surface beside
them, keeping pace.

Halfway across, the path divided.

The cloth map showed no fork.

Elias raised the blue lantern and struck a match. For
a moment the wick refused to catch. Then blue flame
blossomed inside the glass.

One branch of the causeway turned transparent.

"False road," Elias murmured.

"How can a road be false?"

"The same way a promise can."

They took the solid branch.

Night had fallen when they reached the far side. The
path entered a forest of white-barked trees. Elias marked their
route in the atlas, but each time he looked back,
the trees shifted. The trail behind them closed like water.

They could not return that way.

Near midnight, they found a milestone leaning among roots. Its
face listed distances to places Elias had never heard of.

**Bellweather — 3 miles**

**Orra — 9 miles**

**Yesterday — 14 miles**

**The House Between — Nearer Than You Think**

Mara touched the final inscription. "What is the House Between?"

"A theory," Elias said.

"My village is being eaten by a theory?"

"Possibly."

He explained what his father had once told him. Every
road crosses a boundary: between one town and another, wilderness
and civilization, departure and arrival. Mapmakers in ancient times believed
all those boundaries touched a single place—a house standing between
every here and every there.

"If it exists," Elias said, "it would be connected to
every road in the world."

"Then why hasn't anyone found it?"

"Perhaps anyone who finds it does not return."

A bell rang somewhere in the forest.

Mara went pale. "That's our chapel bell."

She ran.

Elias followed her through the trees, branches striking his face.
The bell rang again, louder. Ahead, a warm light appeared
between the trunks.

They emerged at the edge of Bellweather.

At first, the village seemed ordinary. Small houses stood around
a square. Smoke curled from chimneys. The inn sign creaked
above the main road.

Then Elias noticed the gaps.

A fence enclosed an empty patch of earth where livestock
gathered around a trough that did not exist. A row
of gravestones stood without a chapel. On one side of
the square, a staircase climbed to an absent second floor
and ended in the open air.

People moved through the village silently. They did not greet
Mara. Some walked into missing houses and stood in the
rain, miming the opening of invisible doors.

At the northern edge of the square began the white
road.

It was smooth and perfectly straight, unlike any road shaped
by human use. No wheel ruts marked it. No weeds
grew along its sides. It passed between two houses and
continued into darkness.

A man stood at its entrance holding a blue lantern.

Elias knew his father despite the years.

Silas had become thin. His white beard reached his chest,
and his coat hung in rags. Yet his eyes were
unchanged: bright, amused, and endlessly curious.

"You took your time," he called.

Elias could not move.

He had imagined this meeting in a thousand ways. In
some, he embraced his father. In others, he struck him.
Sometimes he demanded explanations. Sometimes neither of them spoke.

Now all he managed was, "You look terrible."

Silas smiled. "And you look old."

They embraced in the middle of the road.

For one breath, Elias was seventeen again, smelling ink, wool,
and pipe smoke. Then anger returned.

"You were alive," he said, pulling away. "All these years."

"Not all in the usual order."

"You could have come home."

"I tried."

"One letter. You sent one letter."

"I wrote hundreds. The roads from here are unreliable."

"Then you should have tried harder."

Silas's smile vanished. "You are right."

That simple admission hurt more than an excuse would have.

The white road trembled beneath their feet.

Silas looked toward the darkness. "We have little time."

"Where does it go?" Mara asked.

"To the House Between," Silas replied. "Or what remains of
it."

He led them to the inn. The villagers inside sat
around cold tables, staring at empty plates. None reacted as
Mara passed.

"They're forgetting how things connect," Silas said. "Soon they will
forget speech. Then one another. Then themselves."

"Who was the traveler?" Elias asked.

"A cartographer named Corven. He found the House Between years
ago and decided the world was badly designed."

"That sounds like a cartographer."

"Indeed. He believes distance causes suffering. People become lonely because
they are far apart. Food spoils on long journeys. Armies
exhaust themselves crossing mountains. So he began redrawing the roads."

Mara frowned. "Shorter?"

"Much shorter. But distance is not empty. Journeys give places
meaning. A road links two points because of everything between
them. Remove the between, and eventually the points vanish too."

"Can we stop him?"

Silas looked at Elias's atlas.

"He can."

They left the inn as the final day began.

The white road carried them north. Bellweather disappeared behind them
almost immediately, as if a curtain had fallen. Around the
road lay not forest or field but shifting fragments of
other journeys: a desert under stars, a bridge in rain,
a crowded marketplace, a mountain pass filled with snow. Elias
glimpsed travelers moving in each scene, unaware that they were
being watched.

At last they reached a black house standing beneath a
colorless sky.

It had hundreds of doors.

Some were carved oak, others painted metal. One was no
more than a curtain of beads. Another was a round
stone entrance small enough for a child. Roads approached the
house from every direction, crossing and tangling like threads.

Inside, they found a vast room filled with maps.

Maps covered the floor and ceiling. They hung in the
air, their edges fluttering. Lines of glowing ink crawled across
them, erasing rivers, bending borders, joining distant cities.

At the center of the room stood a tall man
in a gray coat.

Corven looked up from his blank book.

"Silas," he said. "You brought your son."

"You expected us," Elias said.

"I drew a road for you."

The book lay open before him. On its page, a
thin black line connected the city of Orra to the
House Between.

"You took my father."

"I offered him work. He refused. Then he became inconvenient."

Silas raised his lantern. Blue light revealed dark chains circling
his wrists, stretching back toward Bellweather.

Elias had not seen them before.

"My father is bound to the village."

"He insisted on preserving it," Corven said. "Sentimental men make
their own prisons."

Mara stepped forward. "Give it back."

Corven regarded her with mild curiosity. "Bellweather?"

"My mother. My home. All of it."

"You misunderstand. I have not taken them. I have improved
the road. Your village was isolated, poor, and dying. Now
it is close to everywhere."

"It's close to nothing."

For the first time, Corven looked irritated.

Elias opened his atlas. "You drew your road over older
roads."

"Naturally."

"But you did not erase them."

"I did."

"No road can be erased entirely."

Corven smiled. "Your father taught you superstition."

"My father taught me to look."

Elias set the atlas on the floor.

Across its pages lay decades of work: forgotten lanes, broken
bridges, buried paths. Near the back was his map of
the northern marsh. He placed Mara's cloth map beside it,
aligning the edges.

Lines connected.

The sunken causeway joined a shepherd's trail. The trail met
an abandoned military road. That road passed near Bellweather's vanished
mill, curved through the square, and continued beneath Corven's white
road.

A route appeared—not shorter, not straighter, but older.

Elias dipped his pen in ink.

Corven moved quickly. He drew a line in his blank
book, and the floor split open between them. A gulf
appeared, filled with storms and churning seas.

Elias nearly fell. Mara grabbed his coat and pulled him
back.

"You cannot outdraw me," Corven said. "This house obeys my
maps."

Silas lifted the blue lantern and hurled it.

Corven flinched as the glass shattered against the blank book.
Blue fire spilled across its pages. Roads flared into view—thousands
of them, layered one over another.

"Now!" Silas shouted.

Elias knelt at the gulf's edge and drew.

He began with the causeway. His pen moved from stone
to stone, recording every curve. He drew the path through
the white forest, including the false fork. He drew the
milestone, the village square, the inn, the missing chapel, and
the staircase leading nowhere.

Mara knelt beside him.

"The mill was here," she said, pointing. "And our house
here. There was a footpath behind it to the river."

Elias added each detail.

Corven tore burning pages from his book. With every page,
part of the room vanished. Doors disappeared. Maps turned blank.
The gulf widened.

"Journeys are waste," Corven shouted over the roar. "They are
hunger, delay, separation, death!"

"Yes," Elias said, still drawing.

He remembered every road he had walked with his father.
Long summer lanes. Alleys smelling of bread. The steep street
where Silas had carried him after he twisted his ankle.
The northern road from which his father had not returned.

"And they are how we arrive."

He connected the last line.

The old road woke.

It rose beneath the white road like a buried root
breaking stone. Across the shifting landscapes, cobbles burst from sand
and soil. Footprints appeared: farmers, children, horses, wedding processions, funeral
carts. Every journey ever taken toward Bellweather shone for a
moment.

The village returned all at once.

Its chapel bell thundered. The mill wheel turned. Missing houses
struck the earth with a sound like drums. Names flew
through the air, returning to the minds that had forgotten
them.

Mara cried out.

A woman appeared in the doorway of a small house,
holding a wooden spoon.

"Mara?" she called, as if the girl had been gone
only minutes.

Corven stared at Elias's map.

Then the House Between began to collapse.

Without control of the stolen roads, it could no longer
remain in one shape. Doors opened onto oceans, bedrooms, cliffs,
and crowded stations. Wind tore maps from the walls.

Silas's chains broke.

"This way!" he shouted.

One door showed Bellweather's square. Mara ran toward it.

Corven seized Elias by the arm.

"You have restored their distance," he said. His calm had
vanished. "Do you know how many will die because help
cannot reach them? How many goodbyes will remain final?"

Elias thought of twenty-three years without his father.

"Yes," he said. "But a world without distance is a
world without return."

He pulled free and leaped through the door.

Silas followed.

They landed in Bellweather's square as the door slammed shut
behind them. For a moment its outline remained in the
air. Then it shrank to a black line, and the
line became a crack in an ordinary stone.

Snow began to fall.

Around them, villagers embraced returned husbands, daughters, neighbors, and friends.
Some wept with joy. Others wept because memory had restored
not only the living but also those who had been
lost long before.

Mara stood in her mother's arms.

Elias looked at Silas. "Are you coming home?"

His father gazed south, where the old road curved beyond
the village.

"If you will have me."

"I haven't decided."

"Fair."

"The journey will take weeks."

Silas smiled faintly. "Good."

They left Bellweather in spring.

The villagers had needed help repairing roofs, roads, and memories.
Elias spent the winter mapping every path around the village.
He marked the mill, chapel, well, and all thirty houses.
At Mara's request, he drew her mother's kitchen larger than
strict accuracy required.

When the thaw came, Mara gave Elias a new compass.
Its needle pointed north, as proper compasses should.

The road home was long.

Silas and Elias quarreled through most of it. They argued
about routes, weather, cooking, missing years, and whether maps should
be rolled or folded. Some nights they spoke until dawn.
Other nights they sat on opposite sides of the fire
in silence.

But every morning, when Elias began walking, he heard his
father's footsteps behind him.

Months later, above the narrow shop between the bakery and
the locksmith's, Elias hung a new sign:

**VALE & VALE — MAPS OF PLACES FOUND, FORGOTTEN, AND
FOUND AGAIN**

In the locked drawer beneath his desk, he kept the
map of Bellweather. At its edge, he had drawn the
House Between as a small black square. No roads led
to it.

Still, on certain winter mornings, a new line appeared faintly
on the page.

Whenever that happened, Elias took out his pen.

He did not erase the line.

He carefully drew another road across it—older, longer, winding through
fields and villages, over bridges and hills. A road with
milestones, resting places, wrong turns, and views worth stopping for.

A road that took time.

A road that led home.

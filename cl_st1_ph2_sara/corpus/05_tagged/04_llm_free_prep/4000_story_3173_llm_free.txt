Every morning at six, the sea returned one thing it
had taken.

Sometimes it was small: a sandal with a broken strap,
a brass key green with salt, a child's red bucket.
Sometimes it was large enough to draw the whole village
down to the shore: the mast of a vanished fishing
boat, a church bell from the drowned island of Saint
Orra, the marble hand of a statue no one remembered.

The objects always appeared in the same place, on the
black sand below the cliffs, placed neatly above the tide
line as if delivered by a careful servant.

Mara Vale had watched these offerings for thirty-seven years.

She was the village keeper of lost things, a title
that sounded grander than it was. Each morning she walked
from her narrow cottage to the beach, recorded what the
sea had returned, and carried it to the old customs
house beside the harbor. There, the object was cleaned, labeled,
and placed on a shelf.

If its owner claimed it, Mara asked for proof.

If no one came within a year, it became part
of the village collection.

The keeper before Mara had been her father, Elias. He
had taught her that the sea was not generous.

"It returns nothing for free," he used to say. "Even
when we cannot see the price."

Mara had believed him as a child. Then, at seventeen,
she had decided he was only trying to frighten her
away from the water. At nineteen, she had stopped believing
in warnings altogether.

That was the year her younger brother, Tomas, drowned.

His body was never found.

For eighteen years after his death, Mara went to the
shore each morning with the same forbidden hope. She imagined
finding his blue coat folded on the sand, or the
silver whistle he wore around his neck. She imagined some
sign that the sea had not swallowed him completely.

Nothing came.

Hope aged into habit. Habit hardened into duty.

Then, on a gray morning in November, the sea returned
a door.

It stood upright in the sand.

Mara saw it from the cliff path and stopped so
suddenly that the lantern swung from her hand. Dawn had
barely touched the horizon. The beach was empty except for
gulls and the dark, patient water.

The door was made of oak, though no oak grew
for a hundred miles. It had three iron hinges, a
round brass knob, and a small pane of blue glass
set near the top. There was no wall around it,
no frame sunk into the ground. It simply stood alone,
casting a long shadow toward the village.

Mara approached slowly.

A paper label had been tied to the knob with
white string.

On it, in faded ink, was her name.

MARA VALE.

She did not touch it.

For several minutes she listened to the waves. The tide
was going out, pulling pebbles across the shore with a
sound like whispered conversation.

At last Mara lifted the label and turned it over.

There were four words on the back.

YOU MAY ASK ONCE.

"Mara?"

She spun around.

A boy stood halfway down the path, carrying a basket
of bread. It was Kit Farrow, the baker's grandson, thin
as a mast and twice as curious.

"What is it?" he called.

"Stay there."

Naturally, he came closer.

Kit stared at the door. "Where did that come from?"

"The sea."

"Doors don't float."

"Neither do church bells."

He circled it, keeping several feet away. "Does it open?"

"I don't know."

"There's only one way to find out."

"There are often several ways to find something out," Mara
said, "and opening it is usually the most dangerous."

Kit grinned. He had the fearless grin of a child
who had not yet lost enough.

Mara untied the label and put it in her coat
pocket.

"We'll take it to the customs house," she said.

The door proved impossible to move.

Mara and Kit pushed. It did not shift. They dug
around its base and discovered that it rested on the
sand without sinking into it. Kit fetched rope, and before
long half the village had gathered on the beach. Six
fishermen pulled together. The rope snapped. The door remained upright.

By eight o'clock, everyone had an opinion.

"It belongs to a ship," said Old Brenn.

"What ship has a door standing in the middle of
the deck?" asked the baker.

"A rich man's ship."

"It's a warning," said Mrs. Cale, who considered most things
warnings.

"It's a miracle," said the priest.

"It's an obstruction," said the harbor master.

Mayor Pell wanted it fenced off. The priest wanted to
bless it. The schoolmaster wanted to measure it. Kit wanted
to open it.

Mara said nothing about the label.

At noon the crowd thinned. Fishing boats had to sail,
dough had to rise, and children had lessons. Two village
guards were posted beside the door, though no one could
explain what they were guarding it from.

By evening, word had traveled inland. Visitors began arriving on
horseback and in rattling carts. They stood at the top
of the beach and sketched the door, argued over it,
or offered coins for the privilege of touching the knob.

Mara refused them all.

At sunset, she returned home and placed the label on
her kitchen table.

YOU MAY ASK ONCE.

She read the words until the room grew dark.

There were questions she had carried for years.

Why had her mother left the village when Mara was
nine?

Had her father truly believed the sea demanded payment?

Would Mara have married Julian if she had not refused
to leave the coast?

Why had Tomas taken the boat out alone on the
night of the storm?

Which question mattered most depended on the hour.

After midnight, someone knocked.

Kit stood outside, shivering in a coat too large for
him.

"What happened?"

"It's my grandfather," he said. "He's gone to the door."

They ran.

Rain had begun to fall, fine and cold. Down on
the beach, the guards lay asleep against a rock. Kit's
grandfather stood before the door with one hand on the
brass knob.

Old Farrow had been blind for eleven years. His pale
eyes stared toward nothing, but his face was bright with
wonder.

"I can see light," he whispered.

Mara moved between him and the door.

"What kind of light?"

"Summer light. Through apple leaves." Tears ran into his beard.
"There's a garden. My wife is there."

Kit caught his arm. "Grandfather, Gran's dead."

"I know."

The old man reached for the knob again.

Mara stopped him. "Did the door speak to you?"

"No. But she did."

From the other side came a faint sound.

A woman was singing.

Kit went white. Even Mara recognized the melody. Mira Farrow
had sung it while kneading bread, in the years before
the fever took her.

Old Farrow trembled. "She says I only have to ask."

"Ask what?" Mara said.

"To enter."

The rain struck the blue pane and slid down like
tears. Mara looked through it but saw only darkness.

"What will it cost?" she asked.

The singing stopped.

For one moment the entire shore fell silent, even the
waves.

Then something knocked from the other side.

Three slow knocks.

Old Farrow recoiled. Kit pulled him away, and the guards
woke with embarrassed shouts. Together they led the old man
up the path.

Mara remained.

She watched the door until sunrise.

At six, the sea returned a silver spoon.

The door did not disappear.

For the next six days, it stood on the beach.
Every night it called to someone.

It called the priest with the voice of God.

It called Mayor Pell with the cheers of a great
city.

It called Mrs. Cale with the voice of the baby
she had lost forty years before.

Each time, Mara was summoned. Each time, she prevented the
chosen person from turning the knob. Some thanked her later.
Others cursed her.

By the seventh day, the village had split into factions.

One group believed the door offered what the heart desired.
Another believed a demon stood behind it. A third insisted
the entire affair was caused by unusual tides and collective
nervousness.

The mayor ordered the door destroyed.

Axes bounced from the wood. Saws lost their teeth. A
blacksmith swung a hammer at the blue glass and broke
his wrist.

That night, the door called Mara.

She woke to the sound of a silver whistle.

One clear note floated through her cottage.

Then another.

She dressed without lighting a lamp.

The streets were empty. Frost shone on the roofs. As
she descended the cliff path, she saw that the guards
were gone.

The door waited under the stars.

Through its blue pane glowed a warm, flickering light.

The whistle sounded again.

Mara stopped an arm's length away.

"Tomas?"

A shadow moved behind the glass.

"Mara," said her brother's voice.

It was exactly as she remembered: young, amused, always on
the edge of laughter.

Her knees weakened.

"Tomas?"

"You took your time."

He had said those words a thousand times when they
were children. Whenever she found him hiding in the boats
or waiting beyond the hill, he greeted her with the
same complaint.

You took your time.

Mara reached for the knob, then forced her hand down.

"Are you dead?"

"Probably."

"That sounds like you."

"I hoped it would."

She closed her eyes. "What is this door?"

"A question."

"No. The label said I could ask once."

"Yes."

"And if I ask the right question?"

"You get the right answer."

"What happens if I open it?"

On the other side, Tomas laughed softly. "Is that your
question?"

Mara stepped back.

"No."

"Good."

"What should I ask?"

"You know I can't tell you that."

Anger came suddenly, hot and welcome. "Then you're not Tomas.
He never followed rules."

"Death may have improved me."

"You're not dead. You said 'probably.'"

"I was nineteen, Mara. I thought nineteen-year-olds lived forever. Dying
was confusing."

She pressed both hands to her mouth.

For eighteen years she had imagined what she would say
if given one final conversation with him. She had prepared
accusations, apologies, declarations of love.

Now all she managed was, "Why did you go out
in the storm?"

"Is that your question?"

"No."

"Then don't waste it."

"You stole Father's boat."

"I borrowed it badly."

"You knew the storm was coming."

"Yes."

"Were you trying to die?"

Silence.

"Is that your question?" he asked again.

Mara turned away from the door. The waves were black
beneath the moon.

She remembered their last evening. Tomas had sat at the
kitchen table, turning the silver whistle between his fingers. Their
father had been ill for months. Debts filled a drawer.
Mara had told Tomas she was leaving with Julian, that
there was nothing for her in the village but sickness
and salt.

Tomas had called her selfish.

She had called him useless.

He had gone out before midnight.

Their father died believing himself responsible. Julian waited two years
for Mara, then left alone. And Mara became keeper of
lost things because she could not bear to lose anything
else.

"You went because of me," she said.

"No."

"You were angry."

"I was always angry. So were you."

"If I had stayed quiet—"

"If the storm had turned. If the mast had held.
If I had been wiser. If you had been kinder.
Pick an 'if,' Mara. Build a house from it. You
still can't live there."

She bowed her head.

"Then why are you here?"

"Because you want me to be."

"That isn't an answer."

"It may be the only one."

The blue glass brightened. For an instant, Mara saw him
clearly: wet hair across his forehead, narrow shoulders, the old
blue coat. He was nineteen and would always be nineteen.

"Open the door," he said.

Her hand rose before she knew it.

"Open it, and you won't have to wonder anymore."

Her fingers closed around the cold brass knob.

"What will happen?"

"Ask."

"What will it cost?"

"Ask."

"What are you?"

"Ask."

Each question crowded against the next. She had only one,
and the door seemed to feed on her fear of
choosing badly.

Then she understood.

All week the door had called with answers: lost wives,
dead children, divine voices, promised glory. It invited people to
ask whether their deepest hopes were true. But hope could
become a hook as easily as grief.

Her father's warning returned to her.

The sea was not generous.

Mara tightened her grip on the knob.

"I have my question," she said.

The figure behind the glass went still.

"Ask, then."

Mara drew a long breath.

"What question does the door not want me to ask?"

The blue glass shattered.

Not outward, but inward, as if the night behind it
had swallowed every fragment. A cry came from the other
side in a hundred voices—Tomas, Mira Farrow, weeping infants, cheering
crowds, mothers, gods, and monsters.

The brass knob turned beneath Mara's hand.

The door tried to open.

She threw her weight against it.

Through the empty window poured the smell of deep water.
Hands appeared around the door's edges, pale fingers curling from
the other side. The oak shuddered. Sand swirled around Mara's
boots.

A voice answered from everywhere.

"ASKING."

The door did not fear a particular question. It feared
the act itself: true inquiry, stripped of longing. It survived
by turning desire into certainty. It offered easy answers before
people could examine the hunger that made those answers irresistible.

Mara understood only part of this, but part was enough.

The door opened an inch.

Beyond it lay no garden, no heaven, no drowned brother.
She saw a vast darkness crowded with doors—thousands upon thousands,
standing on beaches, in forests, beside hospital beds, inside ruined
houses. Before each one waited a human figure, reaching for
the knob.

Mara pushed harder, but the gap widened.

Then another pair of hands pressed against the door beside
hers.

Kit.

His face was white with effort.

"I heard my mother," he gasped.

Kit's mother had abandoned him as a baby.

"Don't listen," Mara said.

"I didn't."

Old Farrow came next, guided by the baker. Then the
priest, Mrs. Cale, the harbor master, and one of the
guards. More villagers hurried down the beach, drawn by voices
only they could hear.

Together they pushed.

The door screamed.

It promised them everything.

It told Old Farrow that Mira was lonely.

It told Mrs. Cale that her baby had never forgiven
her.

It told Kit his mother had always intended to return.

It told Mara that Tomas was cold in the dark.

Still they pushed.

The door began to sink into the sand.

At first slowly, then faster. The pale hands vanished. Black
water gushed through the empty pane. Mara's boots slipped, but
Kit caught her coat.

With one final groan, the door dropped beneath the beach.

The sand closed over it.

The sea fell quiet.

At six, as the first line of sunlight crossed the
horizon, something gleamed at Mara's feet.

A silver whistle.

She picked it up.

It was tarnished and bent, with a broken chain still
looped through its ring. On one side were the initials
T.V., scratched clumsily with a knife.

The village waited.

Mara raised the whistle to her lips and blew.

No sound came.

She smiled anyway.

In the weeks that followed, people argued about what had
happened. The priest preached a sermon on temptation. The schoolmaster
wrote a paper on mass delusion. Mayor Pell claimed that
his leadership had saved the village, although he had arrived
after the door disappeared.

Mara recorded the event in the keeper's ledger.

NOVEMBER 17. One oak door with iron hinges and blue
glass. Unclaimed. Returned to sea.

Below it, she added:

NOVEMBER 18. One silver whistle. Claimed by Mara Vale.

The sea continued its old habit.

It returned a captain's compass, a glass eye, a crate
of oranges, and once, to everyone's astonishment, a live white
goat. Spring came. Frost loosened its grip on the cliffs.
Kit began helping Mara in the customs house, though he
read every label aloud and invented histories for objects that
had none.

One bright morning, he asked her whether the voice behind
the door had truly been Tomas.

Mara was polishing the silver whistle.

"I don't know," she said.

"Doesn't that bother you?"

"Yes."

"Then why aren't you sad?"

"I am sad."

"You don't look it."

Mara held the whistle up to the window. Sunlight caught
along its battered edge.

"When I was young," she said, "I thought sadness was
a question that had to be answered. Later, I thought
it was a debt that had to be paid. Now
I think it may simply be a room we visit
from time to time."

Kit frowned. "Does the room have a door?"

"Of course."

"Do you open it?"

"Sometimes."

"What's inside?"

Mara slipped the whistle into her pocket.

"Whatever I bring with me."

They walked down to the beach together.

It was six o'clock, and the tide was retreating. On
the black sand, exactly where the door had stood, lay
a small wooden box.

Kit ran ahead but stopped before touching it. He had
learned some caution, though not much.

Mara knelt beside the box. There was no name on
it, no lock, and no sound from within. Its lid
was carved with a single word:

FOUND.

"What do you think it means?" Kit asked.

Mara looked toward the horizon, where the sea and sky
met in a line too fine to hold all the
mysteries of the world apart.

Then she lifted the box.

"That," she said, "is a very good question."

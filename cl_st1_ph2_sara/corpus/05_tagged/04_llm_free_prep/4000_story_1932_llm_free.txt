# The Cartographer of Lost Roads

On the morning the road disappeared, Mara Vale was drawing
it.

She sat at the kitchen table with a ruler in
one hand and a mug of cold tea in the
other, copying the old route from memory. It began behind
the abandoned chapel, crossed the north meadow, and climbed through
a stand of black pines before reaching the ridge. Beyond
the ridge stood the blue house where her brother had
once lived.

Mara drew the final curve, pressed her pencil down to
mark the house, and heard a knock at the door.

The village postman stood outside, breathing hard.

"You'd better come," he said.

Mara took her coat.

The road behind the chapel had vanished.

There was no broken stone, no wheel track, no grass-covered
suggestion of where it had been. The north meadow stretched
unmarked from the chapel wall to the pines. Even the
hedges that had bordered the route now grew together in
a seamless green line.

A crowd had gathered. Mr. Bell from the bakery insisted
the road had run to the east. The schoolmistress claimed
it had begun beside the graveyard gate. Old Mrs. Lorne
said there had never been a road at all.

"There was," Mara said.

Everyone turned toward her.

It was natural that they should. Mara was the village
cartographer, though the village had little need of one. The
coast lay to the west, the mountains to the east,
and the streets of Vale's Crossing had not changed in
two hundred years. Still, she maintained the official maps, marked
new wells and fallen bridges, and drew property lines when
neighbors quarreled.

She also collected lost things.

Not gloves or coins. Lost paths. Forgotten alleys. Stairways bricked
behind plaster. She mapped shortcuts remembered only by children and
lanes abandoned after storms. In the attic of her cottage,
hundreds of maps lay rolled in cedar boxes.

Now she looked across the meadow and felt an old
fear move inside her.

"Did anyone travel the road yesterday?" she asked.

No one answered.

Mara turned to the postman. "You used to deliver letters
to the blue house."

He stared at her. "What blue house?"

"My brother's house. Beyond the ridge."

"You don't have a brother."

The villagers looked away with the embarrassed kindness people showed
when grief made someone foolish.

Mara returned home alone.

In the attic, she opened the largest cedar box. It
contained a single map, drawn on linen and stitched at
the edges. At its center was Vale's Crossing. Roads branched
outward in black ink, some dark and solid, others pale
as smoke. The vanished road was still there, but its
line had faded overnight.

Mara touched it.

A memory came to her: a summer evening twelve years
earlier, her brother Elias walking backward along the road, laughing
as he carried a basket of apples.

You'll fall, she had warned.

Then you'll have to remember me kindly, he had replied.

She remembered the blue house. She remembered rain on its
windows and copper pots in its kitchen. She remembered Elias
marrying a woman named Nessa, whose hair was red and
whose songs made dogs howl. She remembered their daughter, Lio,
born during a thunderstorm.

Yet when Mara tried to picture Elias's face, she saw
only fog.

On the map, the road faded further.

"No," she whispered.

She packed chalk, twine, a compass, bread, a lantern, and
the linen map. Then she walked to the chapel.

The villagers had dispersed. Evening shadows filled the meadow.

Mara stood where the road should have begun. She closed
her eyes and recalled every journey she had taken along
it. Seventy-three paces north. A bend beside a stone shaped
like a sleeping fox. A shallow ditch. A hawthorn tree
split by lightning.

She opened her eyes and stepped forward.

At first there was only grass. Then, under her boot,
she felt stone.

She marked the place with chalk and took another step.
Stone again. She tied her twine to the chapel fence
and continued into the meadow, following what memory told her
was there.

After seventy-three paces, she turned north.

The sleeping fox stone appeared at dusk.

It did not rise from the earth gradually. One moment
there was grass; the next, the gray stone stood beside
her, silver with lichen. As Mara touched it, the meadow
behind her blurred. The chapel's roof faded into mist.

She lit her lantern.

Ahead, the lost road emerged beneath the grass, one pale
stone at a time.

Mara walked until darkness swallowed the village.

The split hawthorn came next, though it was no longer
dead. White blossoms covered its branches, glowing in the lantern
light. Beneath it sat a boy in a red coat.

He was perhaps ten years old. His feet were bare.

"You took your time," he said.

"Who are you?"

"Jonas."

"Are you lost?"

"Not anymore."

The answer made Mara uneasy. "Where are your parents?"

The boy pointed along the road. "Everywhere but here."

Mara gave him bread. He ate quickly, watching her map.

"You shouldn't carry that openly," he said.

"Why?"

"The Collector dislikes competition."

"I'm not competing with anyone."

Jonas laughed. "Everyone who remembers is competing with him."

A wind stirred the hawthorn blossoms. They lifted into the
air, but instead of falling, they became white moths and
flew east.

Mara looked back. Her twine vanished into darkness.

"Do you know the blue house?" she asked.

"There are many blue houses."

"This one belonged to Elias Vale."

The boy's face changed.

"You should go home," he said.

"So you know him."

"I know the Collector took a man with that name."

Mara knelt. "Took him where?"

Jonas pointed toward the pines.

"To the House of Unremembering."

The black pines stood closer than they should have, crowding
both sides of the road. Their branches interlocked overhead. Mara
walked beneath them with Jonas at her heels.

He refused to explain who the Collector was. Whenever she
asked, he whistled or kicked stones. At last she stopped.

"I need to know what I'm facing."

Jonas studied her.

"People think forgetting is empty," he said. "It isn't. Forgotten
things have to go somewhere. A song leaves your head.
A face falls out of history. A path disappears because
no one walks it. They all come here."

"And the Collector?"

"He says he keeps them safe."

"But?"

"But a cage can be safe."

The forest whispered around them. Mara heard fragments in the
wind: half a melody, an unfinished prayer, someone calling a
name she almost recognized.

Jonas drew closer.

"He collects roads because roads lead back," he said. "He
collects names because names unlock doors. He collects people when
they're nearly forgotten."

"Elias was not forgotten."

"Were you the only one remembering him?"

Mara could not answer.

Elias had stopped visiting the village after their father died.
There had been an argument at the funeral—something about the
family cottage and old debts. Angry words. Months passed, then
years. Mara had written letters, but fewer each winter. Elias
had sent birthday cards until one year he did not.

She had meant to visit.

She had always meant to visit.

The forest opened onto a plain beneath a starless sky.
Thousands of roads crossed it. Some were paved with gold
bricks, others with shells, bones, broken clocks, or children's wooden
blocks. Staircases climbed into darkness. Bridges arched over dry land.
Doorways stood without walls.

At every crossing, iron signposts pointed in impossible directions.

ROME, said one.

YESTERDAY, said another.

A third read HOME, though its arrow turned slowly in
circles.

Mara unfolded her map. The road to the blue house
was now a thread-thin line.

"Which way?" she asked.

Jonas pointed toward a path made of white tiles.

The moment Mara stepped onto it, she forgot why she
had come.

She stood still, confused, lantern raised. Beside her waited a
barefoot boy. In her hands lay a linen map.

"What is this place?" she asked.

Jonas seized her sleeve. "Say his name."

"Whose name?"

"The person you're looking for."

Panic surged through her. There had been someone. A man
laughing with a basket of apples.

"Elias," she said.

The purpose of her journey returned in a rush.

They moved quickly. With each step, Mara repeated her brother's
name. The road tried to take other things instead.

She forgot the postman's face.

She forgot the color of her kitchen walls.

She forgot the year her mother died.

Small memories lifted from her like sparks and drifted away.
She saw them vanish into the darkness: her first day
at school, the taste of snow, the tune Nessa had
sung at her wedding.

"Elias," Mara repeated. "Elias Vale."

At the end of the white road stood a black
gate.

Beyond it rose a mansion assembled from mismatched parts. A
castle tower leaned against a farmhouse. Marble steps climbed to
a ship's door. Hundreds of chimneys pierced the roof, releasing
streams of pale smoke filled with whispering voices.

"The House of Unremembering," Jonas said.

The black gate opened.

"Are you coming?" Mara asked.

"No."

"Why not?"

Jonas looked down at his bare feet.

"Because I belong to him."

Before she could stop him, the boy ran into the
dark. His red coat faded last.

Mara crossed the gate alone.

Inside the house, shelves stretched higher than lantern light. They
held jars, boxes, books, bottles, and folded scraps of paper.
Labels hung from each object.

THE LAST VERSE OF A SAILOR'S SONG.

THE LOCATION OF THREE BURIED BELLS.

THE REASON ANNA MARCH LEFT HOME.

THE TRUE NAME OF A STAR.

THE SMELL OF ELIAS VALE'S COAT AFTER RAIN.

Mara reached for the final jar.

"Please don't touch the exhibits," said a voice.

A tall man stood at the end of the aisle.

He wore a gray suit and white gloves. His hair
was neither dark nor light, his face neither young nor
old. Mara looked away for an instant and immediately forgot
his features.

"You are the Collector," she said.

"A crude title, but serviceable."

"Where is my brother?"

"Which brother?"

"My only brother. Elias."

The Collector smiled politely. "Names are unreliable. Come."

He led her through the mansion.

They passed a room filled with portraits whose subjects had
vanished from history. In another, glass birds sang extinct languages.
A vast gallery contained doors from demolished houses. Behind each
door, someone could be heard moving through rooms that no
longer existed.

At last they entered a winter garden.

Snow fell beneath a painted ceiling. Bare trees grew from
white ground. In the center stood the blue house.

Its paint was peeling. Warm light shone at the windows.

Mara ran to the front door and knocked.

A woman with red hair opened it.

For a moment Mara remembered Nessa singing at her wedding.
Then the memory slipped away.

"Can I help you?" the woman asked.

"Nessa?"

The woman frowned. "My name is Ellen."

A girl of perhaps fifteen appeared behind her. She had
Elias's dark eyes.

Mara felt tears rise.

"Lio," she said.

The girl stepped back. "How do you know my name?"

"I'm your aunt."

"I don't have an aunt."

Then Elias came down the stairs.

He was older, thinner, and bent in the shoulders. But
he was Elias. Mara recognized the scar over his eyebrow,
the way he held the banister, the irritated crease that
appeared between his eyes when he was frightened.

"Elias," she breathed.

He looked at her without recognition.

The Collector stood behind Mara.

"You see the difficulty," he said.

"What did you do to them?"

"Very little. Forgetting is usually a communal effort."

Elias gripped the doorframe. "Who is this?"

"Your sister," said the Collector.

"I don't have a sister."

Mara stepped forward. "When you were nine, you climbed the
mill roof because I said you were afraid. You fell
through and broke your wrist. Father asked what happened, and
I said the roof collapsed by itself."

Elias stared at her.

"You carried apples backward along the ridge road," she continued.
"You gave me the cottage after Father died. I accused
you of only doing it because you wanted to leave.
We fought beside his grave."

Something flickered in his expression.

"The cottage had a green door," he whispered.

"Yes."

"You hated green."

"I still do."

He touched the scar above his eyebrow.

"Mara?"

The winter garden trembled. Snow slid from the blue house
roof. Somewhere in the mansion, glass shattered.

The Collector sighed.

"One memory is not enough," he said. "He was forgotten
by the village. Forgotten by former friends. Nearly forgotten by
you. His wife had already misplaced her old name. His
daughter's childhood began dissolving. I rescued them before they disappeared
entirely."

"You imprisoned them."

"I preserved them."

"Why erase their memories?"

"To prevent longing. A preserved thing must remain still."

Elias stepped from the house. "Can we leave?"

The Collector looked at Mara. "If she pays."

"Pays what?"

"A road cannot lead away unless it leads from somewhere.
To restore yours, another road must be surrendered."

Mara clutched the linen map. "Take any road you like."

"I do not choose payments. You do."

The map unfolded by itself.

Its roads shone across the fabric: village lanes, mountain paths,
coastal tracks. Mara saw every route she had ever drawn.

"What happens to the road I surrender?"

"It disappears. No one remembers what stood at its end."

Mara thought of farmhouses, fishing huts, shrines, wells. Every road
was tied to lives.

"I won't erase someone else."

"Then your brother stays."

Nessa—Ellen—came to the doorway with Lio. Confusion clouded their faces.
Elias looked at them, then at Mara.

"You should go," he said quietly. "You came for me.
That's enough."

"No."

"Mara."

"I spent twelve years planning to make things right. Planning
is not the same as doing."

The Collector watched with mild interest.

Mara studied the map.

In its lower corner, almost hidden beneath the stitched border,
was a short road leading nowhere. She had drawn it
as a child. It began at her father's cottage, crossed
an imaginary bridge, and ended at a castle in the
sea. Her mother had helped color the towers red.

"That one," Mara said.

The Collector leaned closer. "A childhood fancy?"

"Yes."

"It is a road."

"It leads nowhere real."

"There is no such road."

Mara hesitated. The imaginary castle had once been real to
her. In winter, she had visited its hundred rooms. Her
mother had been its queen, Elias its foolish knight, and
Mara its mapmaker. On the castle's highest tower, no one
died, no one argued, and no one left.

"What will I forget?"

"Whatever waited at the end."

Mara touched the tiny road.

She heard her mother laughing.

Not the weak laugh from the final winter, but the
bright laugh from before illness. Her mother sat at the
kitchen table, red pencil in hand, drawing flags on castle
towers.

Every kingdom needs a way home, she had said.

Mara's finger shook.

Then she looked at Elias. Already his recognition was fading.
His face had begun to close.

"I choose this road," she said.

The Collector placed one gloved hand over hers.

The imaginary road vanished.

For an instant Mara felt something tear open inside her.
She saw red towers sinking beneath black water. She heard
a woman call from a distant window, but she no
longer knew who it was.

Then the vision was gone.

Mara looked at the blank corner of the map. She
knew she had lost something precious, though she could not
remember what.

The Collector bowed.

"Payment accepted."

The blue house shuddered. Its walls folded inward like paper.
Snow became rain. The garden trees burst into green leaves,
and the black gate appeared at the end of a
sunlit road.

Elias remembered her completely.

She saw it happen in his face: childhood, anger, absence,
love.

He crossed the space between them and embraced her.

Behind him, Nessa gasped as her name returned. Lio began
to cry, remembering birthdays, songs, and an aunt who had
sent letters with maps drawn in the margins.

The Collector turned away.

"Wait," Mara said. "The boy in the red coat. Jonas."

He stopped.

"Who was he?"

"A forgotten child."

"Why does he belong to you?"

"Because no one came looking."

Mara thought of his bare feet beneath the hawthorn.

"I'm looking now."

The Collector faced her. His features shifted like an image
on disturbed water.

"You cannot claim what you do not remember."

"I remember his name."

"A name is not a life."

"It's a road."

For the first time, the Collector looked angry.

Around them, the mansion groaned. Bottles rattled on distant shelves.
The labels whispered.

Mara lifted the linen map. A new line had appeared,
beginning at the House of Unremembering and ending somewhere in
the dark.

"Jonas!" she shouted.

The name echoed through every room.

A red shape flashed between the winter trees.

"Jonas!" Elias called.

Nessa and Lio joined them.

Four voices became forty. The jars repeated the name. The
extinct birds sang it. People behind vanished doors whispered it
to one another.

Jonas.

The house shook.

The boy emerged from beneath a staircase, eyes wide. He
ran through the snow and seized Mara's hand.

The Collector reached for him, but the roads on the
linen map blazed with golden light.

Every door in the mansion flew open.

Forgotten songs poured out first, flooding the halls with music.
Lost faces stepped from portraits. Glass jars cracked, releasing scents,
promises, birthdays, and names. A thousand roads unrolled through the
walls, carrying exiles, ghosts, abandoned houses, and misplaced dreams toward
places that had once loved them.

The Collector stood amid the storm, white gloves clenched.

"You will lose them again," he shouted. "All of them!
That is what people do!"

"Then we'll remember them again," Mara said.

She led her family through the gate.

They followed the white-tiled road across the plain, but now
each step restored a memory instead of taking one. Mara
remembered her kitchen walls, the postman's face, her mother's final
winter. She remembered everything except what had stood at the
end of the surrendered road.

At the black pines, dawn appeared between the branches.

Jonas stopped beneath the flowering hawthorn.

"Where will I go?" he asked.

"With us," Mara said.

"For how long?"

"As long as the road allows."

They crossed the north meadow at sunrise.

The road returned beneath their feet with a sound like
rain. Hedges unfolded from the grass. Wheel ruts darkened the
earth. The chapel bell rang once, though no one pulled
its rope.

Villagers emerged from their houses.

The postman dropped his bag when he saw Elias. Mr.
Bell shouted that he remembered delivering bread to the blue
house. The schoolmistress began weeping because Lio had once sat
in her classroom. Old Mrs. Lorne insisted she had never
forgotten any of them, though everyone knew this was a
lie.

By noon, the road was crowded with people traveling to
welcome the lost family home.

Mara later redrew the village map. She made the ridge
road darker than any other and labeled every bend. At
Elias's request, she added the blue house, though it now
stood empty; he and his family chose to live near
the village.

Jonas stayed with Mara.

No one discovered where he had come from. Some remembered
a red-coated boy lost in a winter storm fifty years
earlier. Others recalled a child who had wandered away from
a traveling circus. Jonas himself offered a different story each
time he was asked.

Mara did not mind. A mystery, she decided, was not
the same as an absence.

Years passed.

New roads were built, old ones faded, and Mara mapped
them all. In the attic, she opened the cedar boxes
every winter and spoke the names written inside. Elias visited
often. Nessa sang while Lio studied cartography. Jonas grew taller,
though he never seemed to age as quickly as other
children.

Sometimes Mara found herself staring at the blank corner of
the linen map.

She knew a road had once been there. On quiet
evenings, she almost remembered red towers, a bridge across the
sea, and a woman's hand guiding hers.

But the memory never fully returned.

So Mara drew a new road in its place.

It began at her cottage, passed the chapel, crossed the
north meadow, and continued beyond the edge of the linen.
She did not mark where it ended.

Underneath, she wrote:

**EVERY KINGDOM NEEDS A WAY HOME.**

She could not remember who had first said it.

Yet whenever she read the words, she felt the strange
certainty that somewhere, beyond all mapped country, someone remembered her
too.

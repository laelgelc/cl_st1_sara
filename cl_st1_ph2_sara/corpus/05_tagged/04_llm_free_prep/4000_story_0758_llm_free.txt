# The Keeper of Lost Hours

On the morning the town clock lost thirteen minutes, Mara
Vale was the only person who noticed.

She stood beneath the tower in the market square, holding
a paper bag of warm rolls, and watched the minute
hand jump from 8:16 to 8:29. No bell rang. No
gears groaned. The hand simply moved, smooth and silent, as
if thirteen minutes had been erased from the face of
the world.

Around her, the market continued undisturbed. Fishmongers arranged silver rows
of herring. Bakers opened striped awnings. Children chased pigeons through
the fountain spray. No one cried out that something impossible
had happened.

Mara looked at the clock above the red roofs, then
at the little brass watch in her palm.

Her watch said 8:16.

A moment later, its minute hand trembled and slid forward
to 8:29.

The paper bag slipped from Mara's fingers.

A roll bounced across the cobblestones and stopped against a
polished black shoe.

"Careful," said Mr. Quill, the town watchmaker.

He was a tall, narrow man whose gray coat always
seemed too heavy for the season. He picked up the
roll, brushed it with one gloved hand, and returned it
to the bag.

"You saw it," Mara said.

Mr. Quill looked toward the clock tower.

"Saw what?"

"The clock jumped."

"Clocks don't jump."

"That one did."

"Then perhaps it was startled."

He tipped his hat and walked away.

Mara might have dismissed the incident if it had happened
only once. But at noon the same day, seven minutes
vanished while she was sweeping the floor of her mother's
bookshop. One instant the dust motes floated in a sunbeam
near the door; the next they had shifted across the
room. Her mother stood behind the counter with a cup
raised halfway to her mouth.

"What happened?" Mara asked.

Her mother blinked. "What do you mean?"

"You moved."

"I was drinking tea."

"No, you were standing there, and then—"

The church bell rang twelve times, though Mara's watch showed
12:07.

Her mother frowned at the clock on the wall. "That
old thing must be slow again."

By evening, everyone in Bellweather complained that the day had
gone too quickly. The butcher had forgotten a delivery. The
mayor arrived late to his own speech. Half the town
burned its supper.

Only Mara understood that they had not forgotten anything.

Time had been stolen.

She went to Mr. Quill's shop after sunset.

The narrow building leaned between a candle maker's house and
an abandoned tailor's shop. Dozens of clocks crowded its window:
brass carriage clocks, painted wooden cuckoo clocks, silver watches no
larger than coins. Their hands pointed to different hours, yet
their ticking came in perfect unison.

The door was locked.

Mara knocked.

No answer.

She knocked again, louder.

Behind the glass, every clock stopped.

The sudden silence made her step back.

Then the lock clicked, and the door opened by itself.

"Mr. Quill?" Mara called.

The shop smelled of cedar shavings and machine oil. Moonlight
gleamed on hanging pendulums. Beyond the empty counter, a curtain
stirred although there was no wind.

Mara should have gone home.

Instead, she stepped inside.

The door shut behind her, and all the clocks began
ticking again.

She pushed through the curtain and found a workshop lined
with shelves. Tiny screwdrivers lay in careful rows. Magnifying lenses
hung from hooks. A green-shaded lamp burned over a half-open
pocket watch.

At the far end of the room stood a blue
door.

Mara knew the shape of the building. There should have
been an alley beyond that wall, not another room.

She opened the door.

A spiral staircase descended farther than the cellar could possibly
allow. Dim blue lamps glowed along the stone walls. From
below came the steady thunder of machinery.

Mara followed the stairs down.

At the bottom, she found a chamber vast enough to
contain the entire market square. Gears turned beneath a vaulted
ceiling. Copper pipes twisted through the darkness. Pendulums as long
as ships' masts swung over bottomless pits.

In the center stood a machine with a hundred glass
bottles arranged around a circular iron frame. Pale gold light
swirled inside each bottle.

Mr. Quill was fastening a stopper into one of them.

Mara stepped closer. A paper label hung from the glass.

TUESDAY, 10:42–10:55, it read.

"You took them," she whispered.

Mr. Quill turned. He did not look surprised.

"Thirteen minutes," Mara said. "Then seven at noon. How many
more?"

"Four at sunset," he replied. "You were eating soup. You
didn't notice."

"Give them back."

"I can't."

"Why not?"

"Because they have already been used."

He lifted the bottle. Inside, the golden light spun like
a small storm.

"Used for what?"

Mr. Quill studied her for a long moment, then set
the bottle into the machine. A pipe above it filled
with light.

"Come with me."

He led Mara across a bridge suspended between moving gears.
Far below, something pulsed with a deep metallic heartbeat.

"You believe clocks measure time," he said. "They do not.
They merely persuade it to behave. Time is wild by
nature. It stretches during lonely nights, shrinks during joyful afternoons,
and tangles itself around memories. Long ago, keepers built this
machine to draw time into a steady current."

"You're a keeper?"

"The last one."

They reached a brass platform overlooking the heart of the
machine. There, inside a cage of iron rings, hung a
cracked crystal. Golden light leaked through the fracture.

"The Continuance," Mr. Quill said. "It keeps the hours moving
in their proper order. It is failing."

Mara watched darkness creep along the edges of the crystal.

"So you steal minutes from people?"

"I collect moments that will cause the least harm and
feed them into the machine. A wait at a quiet
window. A pause between one footstep and the next. Thirteen
minutes on an ordinary morning."

"They weren't yours to take."

"No," he said. "But without them, tomorrow may never arrive."

Mara wanted to call him a liar. Then the crystal
shuddered, and the chamber changed.

For one terrible instant, she saw the machine as a
rusted ruin. Mr. Quill was gone. Bones lay among the
gears. She saw herself standing on the platform with white
hair and a wrinkled hand.

Then the vision snapped away.

"The crack is widening," Mr. Quill said. "Soon minutes will
not merely disappear. They will fall out of order. A
woman may grow old before she gives birth. A house
may burn before the candle is lit. Morning may follow
morning forever."

Mara gripped the railing.

"How do we repair it?"

"I have searched for forty years. The Continuance cannot be
repaired from within time. Someone must enter the Unmeasured Hour."

"What is that?"

"The space between one day and the next. A place
where time has no direction."

"Then go there."

Mr. Quill looked toward the turning gears.

"I tried."

He removed his right glove. His hand beneath was not
flesh but clear glass, filled with drifting sand.

"The Unmeasured Hour takes time from whoever enters. I lost
thirty years before reaching the gate."

Mara remembered Mr. Quill as he had looked when she
was small. He had seemed old even then, unchanged through
every winter.

"How old are you?"

"One hundred and seventeen. Or eighty-seven. It depends which clock
you trust."

The Continuance groaned. A new fracture appeared in the crystal.

Mr. Quill quickly opened a valve. Light flowed from the
stolen bottle into the crack, sealing it with a thin
gold line.

"For tonight, that will hold," he said. "Tomorrow I will
need more."

"How much?"

"An hour, perhaps two."

"And the day after?"

"More."

Mara understood. "Eventually you'll steal whole days."

"If necessary."

"From whom?"

His silence answered her.

The next morning, Mara found four blank pages in the
journal she kept beside her bed. She always wrote before
sleeping, yet she remembered nothing after leaving the underground chamber.
Her mother said she had come home at ten, eaten
an apple, and gone upstairs.

Three hours had vanished.

Across town, others had suffered worse. The schoolmaster found himself
standing beside the river with no memory of walking there.
A baker opened her oven and discovered twenty loaves already
burned. An old man named Fen claimed that his wife's
portrait had changed: she had been smiling yesterday, he insisted,
but now she looked away.

Mr. Quill was no longer choosing harmless moments. The machine
was hungry.

At midday, the clock tower struck fourteen times.

Then the sun stopped.

It hung above the western hills for six hours while
shadows remained fixed on the ground. People gathered in the
square, whispering and praying. Birds circled without landing. A child
dropped a red ball, and it hovered a finger's width
above the stones.

Mara ran to the watchmaker's shop.

The blue door stood open.

Below, Mr. Quill fought with the controls of the machine.
Several bottles had shattered. Golden light poured across the floor,
where it became flickering images: a first kiss, a funeral,
a boy running through rain, a woman closing a garden
gate.

"The Continuance has stopped," he shouted over the grinding gears.
"I can restart it, but the repair will consume every
stored hour."

"Then do it."

"It would give us one day. No more."

"Show me the gate."

Mr. Quill stared at her.

"The gate to the Unmeasured Hour," Mara said. "Show me."

"You would not return."

"You don't know that."

"I know better than anyone."

"Then you know stealing time won't save us."

The great pendulums slowed. One by one, the blue lamps
went dark.

Mr. Quill's shoulders sagged.

At last, he reached beneath his coat and removed a
silver key on a chain.

"The gate is below the machine," he said. "There is
a staircase beside the central gear. When you enter, hold
tightly to one memory. It may lead you home."

"What am I looking for?"

"The First Clock—the clock from which all others learned their
rhythm. Its bell can call time back into order."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then nothing will."

The staircase beneath the machine ended at a door with
no handle. Mara pressed the silver key against its surface.
A keyhole appeared.

Behind the door lay a field of stars.

She stepped through.

There was no ground, yet she did not fall. There
was no sky, yet lights burned above and below her.
Around her floated pieces of unfinished moments: rain that never
reached the earth, words that had not yet been spoken,
tears suspended before they could fall.

Mara tried to remember the bookshop.

She pictured her mother behind the counter, sunlight in her
hair. She remembered the smell of paper, dust, and tea.
She held the memory as tightly as she could.

Already, other things were fading.

She could not recall what she had eaten for breakfast.
She could not remember the mayor's name. When she tried
to picture her own room, she saw only a door
opening into darkness.

Ahead, bells chimed.

Mara followed the sound.

She passed an army frozen on the edge of battle.
She saw a city not yet built and another sinking
beneath black water. She saw herself at many ages: a
child laughing by the fountain, a woman holding a newborn
baby, an old figure sitting alone in Mr. Quill's shop.

"None of this has happened," she whispered.

"Everything has happened here," replied a voice.

A girl stood among the stars.

She looked about Mara's age, but her eyes were ancient.
Her white dress shifted from new cloth to yellowed rags
and back again. In her hands she held a small
wooden clock.

"Are you the keeper?" Mara asked.

"I was the first."

"Is that the First Clock?"

"It is."

"Give it to me."

The girl smiled sadly. "Everyone asks for time as if
it were an object to own."

"My town is breaking apart."

"All towns break apart. All people vanish. That is what
time does."

"Then why build the Continuance?"

"Because people were afraid. They wanted rivers to flow in
one direction. They wanted children to be born before they
died. They wanted promises to remain made."

"That seems reasonable."

"It seemed so to me, once."

The girl lifted the wooden clock. It had no numbers
and only one hand.

"The machine is failing because your keeper feeds it stolen
time. Stolen time carries grief. Regret. Resentment. The Continuance was
never made to consume it."

"What does it need?"

"A gift."

Mara thought of the bottles beneath the shop.

"How much?"

"One hour freely given."

"That's all?"

"Not just any hour. The hour the giver values most."

The field of stars darkened.

Mara remembered summer evenings in the bookshop, when her mother
locked the door but left the windows open. They would
sit on the floor and read stories aloud while warm
wind stirred the pages. There was one evening, three years
earlier, when rain began suddenly. Her mother had laughed and
pulled Mara into the street. They danced barefoot in the
empty square while thunder rolled over the roofs.

It was Mara's happiest memory.

She felt the Unmeasured Hour waiting.

"If I give it," she asked, "will I forget?"

"Yes."

"Will my mother?"

"No."

That seemed almost crueler.

Mara closed her eyes.

She heard rain striking cobblestones. She felt her mother's hands
in hers. She saw their reflections turning in the dark
shop windows.

"I give that hour," she said.

Pain passed through her—not sharp, but hollow. A door closed
somewhere inside her mind.

When she opened her eyes, she was crying, though she
no longer knew why.

The girl placed the wooden clock in her hands.

"Carry it home."

"How?"

"Follow what remains."

Mara searched for her memory of the bookshop, but it
had become faint. She saw shelves, a green door, a
woman whose face she loved. It was enough.

She ran.

Stars streamed past her. Unspoken words burst like bubbles against
her skin. Years pulled at her clothes. Her hands became
small, then wrinkled, then young again.

In the distance, a rectangular doorway shone.

It began to close.

Mara leaped.

She landed on the stone floor beneath the machine.

Mr. Quill caught her before she struck the stairs. His
hair had turned completely white, and cracks covered his glass
hand.

"How long?" Mara gasped.

"For you? I cannot say. For me, nine years."

He helped her climb to the central platform. The Continuance
was almost entirely dark. Outside the chamber, the world had
gone silent.

Mara held up the First Clock.

"Where does it go?"

Mr. Quill pointed to a hollow inside the cage of
rings.

Mara placed the wooden clock beside the cracked crystal.

Nothing happened.

Then the clock's single hand moved.

Tick.

The sound rolled outward like thunder.

The crystal blazed. Golden light raced through the machine, along
every pipe and wire, into the foundations of the town.
The pendulums swung. The gears turned. Every bottle shattered at
once, releasing stolen moments in a storm of radiance.

Mara saw them rise through the ceiling.

Above, the butcher remembered his missing delivery. The schoolmaster found
his lost walk beside the river. Burned loaves became dough,
then baked again. The red ball dropped and bounced. Portraits
shifted in their frames.

The sun plunged below the hills, then rose in the
east, then settled into its proper place in the evening
sky.

The clock tower struck once.

The sound traveled across Bellweather, through every house and every
heart.

Time began again.

Mr. Quill sat heavily on the floor.

His glass hand had become flesh.

"It's finished," Mara said.

"No," he replied, smiling. "It has begun."

They climbed out of the underground chamber shortly before dawn.

In the market square, people wandered from their houses, confused
but unharmed. Some had recovered lost minutes, others whole afternoons.
A woman embraced a brother she had forgotten visiting. A
child laughed because his ice cream had returned to its
cone.

Mara's mother ran from the bookshop and pulled her close.

"I thought I'd lost you."

"I'm here," Mara said.

Her mother held her at arm's length. "Do you remember?"

"Remember what?"

"The rain. Three summers ago. We danced in the square,
and you slipped near the fountain."

Mara searched her mind.

There was only a warm emptiness.

"No," she said.

Her mother's smile trembled. "It was a good evening."

"Tell me about it."

So her mother did.

She told Mara how the rain had soaked their clothes,
how lightning had frightened all the pigeons from the roofs,
how they had laughed until they could scarcely breathe. As
Mara listened, she could not recover the hour she had
given away, but she could see its reflection in her
mother's face.

That, she decided, was enough.

Mr. Quill left Bellweather the following spring. He put his
shop, his tools, and all his clocks in Mara's care.

"I'm sixteen," she protested.

"You are also the only person who noticed thirteen missing
minutes."

"Where will you go?"

"Somewhere clocks are allowed to be wrong."

On his last morning, he handed her the key to
the blue door.

"Will the Continuance fail again?" she asked.

"Everything fails eventually."

"That isn't comforting."

"No," he agreed. "But until then, it will need watching—not
controlling, not feeding, merely watching."

Mara looked up at the clock tower. Its hands pointed
to 8:16.

"How will I know if something is wrong?"

"You'll notice."

He tipped his hat and walked toward the eastern road.

Years later, travelers came to Bellweather to have their clocks
repaired by Mara Vale. They said she could hear a
broken spring from across a crowded room. They said she
never charged children, sailors, or anyone who arrived late to
a funeral.

In her shop window, dozens of clocks ticked in imperfect
harmony. Some gained a minute each week. Others lost two.
Mara allowed these small errors. She had learned that time
did not need to be flawless in order to move
forward.

Every year, on the first summer evening when rain fell
over the town, Mara's mother closed the bookshop early.

They went into the square together.

Mara never remembered why.

But as the rain ran down her face and the
clock tower rang above them, she always took her mother's
hands.

And they danced.

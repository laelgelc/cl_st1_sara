### The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, before the baker lit his ovens
and before the ferries began crossing the gray river, Elias
Vale climbed the hill to the clock tower and unlocked
the door.

The tower had not told the correct time in seventeen
years.

Its eastern face was twelve minutes slow, its western face
seven minutes fast, and the northern face had stopped entirely
at twenty-three minutes past four. The southern clock possessed no
hands at all. They had fallen during a storm and
buried themselves like spears in the roof of the courthouse
below.

Still, Elias wound the mechanism each morning.

He was a narrow man with silver hair and careful
hands. In summer he wore a waistcoat the color of
dry moss; in winter, a long black coat that made
him resemble a shadow returning home. The people of Bellweather
knew him as the tower keeper, though few understood what
he kept there.

They assumed it was the clock.

It was not.

Behind the clockwork, concealed by a door with no handle,
lay a room filled with lost hours.

They stood in bottles along shelves reaching from floor to
ceiling. Some glowed gold, some blue, some with the soft
red of banked coals. Each bottle bore a paper label
written in Elias's precise hand.

ONE HOUR LOST WAITING FOR A LETTER THAT NEVER CAME.

FORTY MINUTES MISPLACED IN A HOSPITAL CORRIDOR.

A SUMMER AFTERNOON FORGOTTEN BY TWO SISTERS AFTER THEIR QUARREL.

SEVEN MINUTES TAKEN BY FEAR AT THE EDGE OF A
DIVING BOARD.

The bottles whispered when Elias passed. If he placed his
ear near the glass, he could hear rain on windows,
spoons against teacups, the dry cough of an old man,
the laugh of a child who had not yet learned
to be ashamed of laughing too loudly.

Lost time gathered everywhere, but Bellweather gathered more than most
places.

The town stood in a bend of the river where
fog lingered even under sunlight. Travelers often missed trains there.
Lovers arrived too late. Children spent whole days searching for
paths that had never existed. People forgot why they had
entered rooms and emerged years older, carrying objects they did
not remember picking up.

For three generations, the Vale family had collected these hours.
Elias's grandfather had found them shimmering in gutters and trapped
them in jam jars. His father had designed the labels
and built the hidden room. Elias had perfected the craft.

A lost minute felt like cool silk between the fingers.
An hour had weight. A year could break a man's
back.

There was only one rule: lost time could be returned,
but never to the person who had lost it.

"Time remembers the shape of its first owner," Elias's father
had warned him. "Pour it back into the same life,
and it will tear that life open trying to fit."

So Elias redistributed the hours when they were needed.

He gave a dying mother ten minutes to hear her
son's voice after his ship reached port. He gave a
surgeon thirty seconds to catch a mistake. He gave a
schoolboy an extra afternoon in which to find his little
brother in the woods.

No one knew.

People called such things miracles, luck, or errors in the
clocks.

Elias called them bookkeeping.

Then, one November morning, he found a girl sitting on
the tower steps.

She was perhaps fourteen, though her solemn expression made her
seem older. She wore a red scarf and a blue
coat with one missing button. Her dark hair had been
cut unevenly, as if she had done it herself without
a mirror.

"You're blocking the door," Elias said.

"I know."

"May I ask why?"

"You may."

She did not move.

Elias waited. The girl looked down at the sleeping town,
where chimneys had begun to breathe thin columns of smoke.

"My name is Mara Finch," she said at last. "You
have something that belongs to me."

Elias felt the tower key grow cold in his hand.

"I repair clocks."

"No, you don't. Everyone knows the clocks are wrong."

"Then you have no reason to trouble me."

"My mother died yesterday."

Elias lowered his eyes. Death was common knowledge in Bellweather;
grief traveled faster than fire. He remembered hearing that Lenora
Finch, the seamstress on Candle Street, had passed after a
long illness.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"She lost an hour last Tuesday."

"Many people lose hours."

"She was sitting beside the window. I was reading to
her. Then the room changed."

Elias looked at the girl more carefully.

"How?"

"The light jumped. The candle burned lower. The tea went
cold. Mother said she could hear bells under the floor."
Mara stood. "Then you walked past our house carrying a
black case."

Elias said nothing.

"I want the hour back."

"I don't have it."

"You're lying."

"You should go home."

"I don't have a home now. My aunt is selling
the house."

A gust climbed the hill and worried at the end
of her scarf. From somewhere near the river came the
lonely call of a ferry horn.

Mara held out her hand.

"Give it to me."

"I can't."

"Then give it to someone else and take me to
them."

"It doesn't work that way."

"How does it work?"

"It doesn't."

She stared at him, furious and wounded. Elias had seen
that expression before, on the faces of people who believed
the world had made a clerical mistake and could be
persuaded to correct it.

He stepped around her and fitted the key into the
lock.

Behind him, Mara said, "She was trying to tell me
who my father was."

Elias stopped.

"She'd never told me. That morning she said she would.
Then the hour disappeared, and afterward she couldn't speak."

The wind moved between them.

"I need to know what she said," Mara whispered.

Elias entered the tower and closed the door.

For the next six mornings, Mara waited on the steps.

Sometimes she spoke. Sometimes she did not. On the second
morning she brought a hammer and threatened to break the
door. On the third she brought bread. On the fourth
it rained, but she remained until her blue coat was
nearly black with water.

On the fifth morning, Elias asked, "Don't you attend school?"

"Not anymore."

"Why?"

"I lost patience with it. Do you keep that in
a bottle too?"

On the sixth morning, she followed him inside before he
could stop her.

The tower's interior was a hollow cylinder threaded by a
spiral staircase. Above them, gears turned with the groaning patience
of old animals. Dust trembled from the beams each time
the mechanism advanced.

Mara looked upward.

"If the clocks are wrong, why wind them?"

"If I stop, they'll become more wrong."

"That's a poor answer."

"It's the only one I have."

Elias climbed. Mara climbed behind him.

At the first landing, he turned.

"You cannot come farther."

"Why?"

"Because I said so."

"My aunt says that when she has no reason."

"Your aunt sounds wise."

"She isn't."

Elias continued. So did Mara.

At the top of the stairs stood the handleless door.
It opened only when Elias pressed his palm to the
center and surrendered a minute of his own life. The
tower took the minute cleanly. His heart skipped a beat;
the light shivered; and the door swung inward.

Mara slipped through beside him.

She stopped breathing.

The room was larger than the tower should have allowed.
Shelves vanished into blue distance, crowded with bottles that flickered
and murmured. Ladders moved along brass rails without being touched.
Paper labels rustled though there was no wind.

At the center stood a long wooden table. On it
rested Elias's black collection case.

Mara took one step forward.

"So I was right."

"You were persistent."

"That isn't the same thing."

"Often it is."

She approached the nearest shelf. A thumb-sized vial glowed with
pale green light.

"Can I touch one?"

"No."

She touched it.

The bottle rang like a tiny bell. Mara jerked back,
and for an instant the room smelled of summer grass.

"What was that?"

"A minute lost by a woman named Clara Holt when
she saw her future husband for the first time."

"How do you lose a minute seeing someone?"

"There are many ways."

Mara moved down the shelf, reading labels.

"Three hours lost in a snowstorm. A morning forgotten after
a fever. Twelve minutes stolen by a nightmare." She looked
at him. "Stolen?"

"Time is not always lost by accident."

"Who steals it?"

"Cruel people. Lonely places. Certain dreams."

"Clock keepers?"

Elias removed his coat.

"Sometimes."

She began searching the shelves.

"Your mother's hour is not here."

"You said you didn't have it."

"I don't."

"You collected it."

"Yes."

"Then where is it?"

Elias opened the black case. Inside lay empty bottles, silver
tongs, labels, sealing wax, and a small compass whose needle
pointed not north but toward the nearest fracture in time.

"I used it."

Mara's face went still.

"For what?"

"Two nights ago, a ferry rope snapped. A deckhand was
pulled into the river. I gave him an hour to
reach the bank before the cold stopped his heart."

"You gave my hour to a stranger?"

"It was not your hour."

"It was my mother's."

"She no longer needed it."

"I needed it!"

Her shout struck the bottles. Thousands of lost moments whispered
awake. The room filled with fragments: a baby crying, a
glass breaking, applause in a distant theater, a woman saying
wait, please wait.

Mara seized a golden bottle from the shelf.

Elias moved faster than she expected. He caught her wrist.

"Put it down."

"Give me an hour."

"No."

"Any hour."

"It won't contain your mother's words."

"Maybe I don't care anymore."

"You care very much."

She twisted free. The bottle slipped.

Elias caught it inches above the floor.

The light inside flared. For one heartbeat, they both stood
in a kitchen twenty years earlier while a young man
peeled apples and sang badly. Then the vision vanished.

Mara was crying.

"I only want to hear her again."

Elias placed the bottle safely on the table.

"I know."

"No, you don't."

He looked toward the highest shelves, where the oldest hours
glowed faintly behind layers of dust.

"My wife died thirty-one years ago," he said. "Her name
was Iris."

Mara wiped her face with her sleeve.

"Did she lose any time?"

"Eleven minutes on the day we met. Two days during
the birth of our son. Five hours in the summer
before she died."

"Did you keep them?"

"Yes."

"Did you use them?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I was a coward."

He took a ladder and pushed it along the rail.
It rolled by itself into the blue depths of the
room. Elias followed, and after a moment Mara joined him.

They passed years shelved in green demijohns, seconds held in
glass beads, and one dark bottle bound in chains. At
last Elias stopped before a small locked cabinet.

He opened it.

Inside were three vessels. Two were clear and empty. The
third contained a trembling violet light.

"Five hours," he said.

Mara leaned close.

"What was she doing?"

"Dying."

The violet light pulsed like a tired heart.

"Iris had a fever. Near the end, she became confused.
She thought I was someone else. She begged me to
find our son."

"Where was he?"

"Already dead."

Mara looked up.

"He had drowned the winter before. He was nine."

The clockwork groaned beyond the walls.

"I collected those five hours," Elias continued, "because I could
not bear them. I told myself I was preserving her
life, but really I was cutting away the part that
hurt me."

"Did it work?"

"No. Pain does not live in time. It lives in
us."

"Then why keep the bottle?"

"Because those were still her hours. Terrible as they were,
they belonged to her life. And I loved her whole
life, not only the pleasant parts."

Mara touched the cabinet door but not the bottle.

"Could you give those hours to me?"

"Yes."

"Would I see her?"

"You would see Iris. You would hear what she heard.
You might feel her fever and her fear. Time carries
everything."

Mara stepped back.

"I don't want that."

"No."

They returned to the table. Outside, the clocks began striking
seven, though each face started at a different moment. The
mismatched bells tumbled over Bellweather.

Mara sat on the floor.

"If my mother's hour is gone, then her words are
gone too."

"Perhaps."

"You don't sound sure."

"Words are rarely contained in a single hour. People spend
years preparing to say important things. They reveal them in
pieces without knowing."

"She never revealed anything about my father."

"Did she keep letters?"

"My aunt burned them yesterday."

"Photographs?"

"None."

"Friends?"

"She stopped seeing them when she became ill."

Elias considered this.

"What did your mother sew?"

"Clothes."

"For whom?"

"Everyone."

"Recently?"

Mara frowned. "She was making a green dress before she
became too weak. She worked on it for months, but
no one ever collected it."

"Then perhaps she was not making it for a customer."

They descended the tower together.

By then Bellweather was awake. Carts rattled over wet stones,
shutters opened, and the first ferry drew a white line
across the river.

Lenora Finch's house stood on Candle Street between a cobbler's
shop and a narrow alley. A card in the window
announced an estate sale. Mara's aunt, a severe woman named
Agnes, opened the door.

"You again," she said to Mara.

"I live here."

"Not after Friday."

Agnes looked suspiciously at Elias.

"The clocks are worse than ever."

"I'll make a note of it," he said.

Mara pushed inside.

The workroom was almost empty. Bolts of cloth had been
sold, drawers emptied, and Lenora's sewing machine stood under a
dust sheet. Only scraps remained on the floor like fallen
leaves.

"The green dress," Elias said.

Agnes crossed her arms. "Useless thing. Odd measurements. No customer
wanted it."

"Where is it?"

"In the rubbish."

They found the dress in a basket behind the house.

It was beautifully made. Green wool, narrow sleeves, pearl buttons.
Far too large for Mara and too small for Agnes.
Elias held it up.

"Try it on," he said.

"It won't fit."

"Try."

Mara changed behind a hanging sheet. As she had predicted,
the dress hung loose at the shoulders and reached nearly
to her ankles.

"It's not for me."

Elias examined the seams.

Lenora Finch had been known for invisible stitching. Yet the
hem was clumsy, thick in one place, as if finished
by an impatient apprentice.

"Scissors," he said.

Agnes protested. Mara handed him a pair.

He cut three stitches.

A folded strip of fabric slid from the hem.

On it, in faded ink, someone had written a name.

THOMAS ARDEN, CLOCKMAKER'S APPRENTICE, NORTH QUAY.

Mara read it twice.

"Is that my father?"

"I don't know," Elias said.

Agnes sat down heavily on an upturned crate.

"I told her to forget him."

Mara spun around.

"You knew?"

"He left before you were born."

"You burned the letters."

"They were old and private."

"They were mine!"

"No," Agnes said, and for the first time her severity
cracked. "They were hers."

Mara clutched the strip of cloth.

"Where is North Quay?"

"Across the river," Elias said. "Or it was. The district
flooded twelve years ago."

Agnes stared at the green dress.

"Thomas Arden didn't leave your mother," she said quietly. "Not
in the way she thought. He went to work one
morning and never came back."

"What happened?"

"No one knew. Lenora waited. Then she stopped waiting."

Mara looked at Elias.

"A lost hour?"

"Perhaps."

The compass in his black case began to spin.

Elias felt it before he saw it: a looseness in
the air, a tremor behind the ordinary ticking of the
world. He took out the compass. Its needle pointed toward
the river.

They hurried to the quay.

Fog covered the water. Ferries moved as dim shapes between
bells. The compass led Elias and Mara past the ticket
house, along the embankment, and down a stairway slick with
moss.

At the bottom, the river lapped against a bricked-up arch.

"I don't understand," Mara said.

Elias pressed his hand to the wall.

It was warm.

"Some lost places continue existing," he said. "They wait for
the missing time to find them."

"Can we get through?"

"Possibly."

"How?"

Elias opened his case. He had three empty bottles and
no loose hours. Nothing strong enough to open a fracture
sealed for more than a decade.

Except the five hours belonging to Iris.

Mara saw his expression.

"No," she said.

"He may be behind this wall."

"You said you kept those hours because you loved her
whole life."

"I do."

"Then don't use them."

Elias looked at the bricked arch. From the other side
came a faint metallic tapping.

Not random tapping.

A clockmaker's rhythm.

"I have guarded those hours for thirty-one years," he said.
"I told myself that was love. Perhaps love is not
guarding what the dead left behind. Perhaps it is spending
it wisely."

They returned to the tower.

The violet bottle seemed colder when Elias lifted it from
the cabinet. For three decades he had imagined opening it.
He had feared Iris's fever, her grief, and the chance
that her last hours contained no love for him at
all.

Now he carried it to the river.

He broke the seal against the wall.

Violet light poured over the bricks.

For an instant Elias heard Iris calling their son's name.
He smelled medicine and winter roses. He felt her hand
gripping his with astonishing strength.

Then the light entered the arch.

The bricks aged in seconds. Mortar crumbled. Moss spread and
withered. The river withdrew from a doorway that had not
existed a moment before.

Beyond lay a narrow street under a bright summer sky.

Mara took Elias's hand.

Together they crossed.

North Quay had been preserved inside a missing afternoon. Sunlight
shone on shop windows. A dog slept beneath a cart.
People stood motionless in the streets, their faces peaceful, as
if pausing between one second and the next.

From a workshop came the sound of tapping.

Inside, a man sat at a bench surrounded by clocks.

He was about thirty years old, with dark hair and
ink on his sleeves. One hand held a tiny hammer.
The other rested beside an unfinished watch.

When Mara entered, he looked up.

"You're not Master Bell," he said.

His eyes were the same gray as hers.

Mara could not speak.

Thomas Arden glanced toward the window. "Is something wrong? The
light changed."

Elias stepped forward.

"What date do you think it is?"

Thomas answered.

It was a date fifteen years earlier.

The lost afternoon had closed around North Quay just before
the flood. To those trapped within it, less than a
minute had passed.

Elias explained slowly.

Thomas did not believe him until Mara showed him the
cloth from the green dress.

He recognized Lenora's handwriting.

"She was going to tell me something," he said. "She
asked me to come after work."

Mara's face tightened.

"She died three days ago."

For Thomas, the words crossed fifteen years in an instant.

He sat down.

The clocks around him ticked, though none of their hands
moved.

"I loved her," he said.

"I know," Mara replied, though she had not known until
then.

The preserved afternoon was collapsing. Buildings flickered. Beyond the windows,
summer alternated with floodwater and fog.

"We have to go," Elias said.

Thomas snatched the unfinished watch from his bench. They ran
through the doorway as North Quay began to dissolve. The
frozen people vanished into light—not dying, Elias thought, but finally
reaching whatever hour had waited for them.

They crossed the arch just before the river broke through.

Cold water knocked Elias to his knees. Mara and Thomas
dragged him up the stairs while the bricks sealed behind
them. When they reached the embankment, the fog had lifted.

The town clocks were striking noon.

For once, all four faces agreed.

Thomas stood in the pale sunlight, soaked and shivering, a
young man in clothes fifteen years out of fashion. Mara
faced him with her mother's name still trembling between them.

Neither knew what to do.

At last Thomas opened his hand.

The unfinished watch lay in his palm.

"I made this for Lenora," he said. "I meant to
finish it that evening."

Mara touched the cracked glass.

"Can it be repaired?"

Thomas looked toward the clock tower on the hill.

"Yes," he said. "I think so."

Years later, people in Bellweather would tell the story differently.

Some said Mara Finch found her father living among river
ghosts. Some said Elias Vale stopped a flood by pouring
moonlight into the water. Agnes insisted there had never been
magic at all, only municipal negligence.

The tower clocks remained unreliable.

The eastern face lost three minutes every winter. The western
face gained an hour during thunderstorms. The northern clock resumed
at last but refused to acknowledge Tuesdays. New hands were
fitted to the southern face, though they sometimes turned backward
when anyone in the courthouse told a lie.

Elias retired when he was seventy-nine.

Mara became the next keeper.

Each morning she climbed the hill in a green coat
remade from her mother's dress. She wound the mechanism, dusted
the bottles, and listened for lost time gathering in the
streets below.

Thomas worked in the room beneath the bells, repairing watches
and learning how much the world had changed. He grew
older. Mara did too. Neither tried to recover the years
between them.

Some absences could not be filled.

Some hours should not be returned.

But on the highest shelf of the hidden room stood
a new bottle, glowing violet and gold. Its label, written
in Mara's hand, read:

ONE MINUTE FOUND BETWEEN A FATHER AND DAUGHTER, WHEN BOTH
BELIEVED IT WAS TOO LATE.

She never spent it.

She never sealed it away, either.

She kept it near the window, where every morning the
light passed through the glass and scattered across the tower
walls, turning all the wrong clocks bright.

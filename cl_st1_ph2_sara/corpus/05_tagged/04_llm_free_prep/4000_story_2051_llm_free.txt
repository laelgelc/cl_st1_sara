By the time the sea returned the first of the
missing houses, Mara Venn had been keeper of the Bellweather
Light for twenty-three years.

The house rose from the water at dawn.

It came slowly, roof first, black shingles breaking through the
gray surface beyond the reef. Then the chimney appeared, followed
by two attic windows bright with reflected sky. Water poured
from the eaves. Barnacles crusted the walls, and ropes of
green weed hung from the gutters, but the house itself
seemed whole.

Mara watched from the lantern room with one hand on
the brass rail.

She knew the house.

Everyone in Bellweather would know it. Once, it had stood
at the eastern end of Harbor Street, painted yellow, with
a blue door and a pear tree leaning over the
fence. The Great Tide had taken it forty years ago,
along with half the town and seventy-six people.

Among them were Mara's mother and younger brother.

The house had belonged to the Vale family. Mara remembered
playing beneath its kitchen table with Lio Vale, both of
them pretending the table was a ship and the floor
was a sea full of teeth.

Now the real sea had returned their ship.

Mara reached for the speaking tube that connected the lantern
room to the keeper's cottage below. Then she stopped. There
was no one downstairs to call. Bellweather Light had become
automated five years earlier, but Mara had refused retirement, and
the council had allowed her to remain as caretaker because
no one else wanted to live on the island.

She lifted the old signal telescope instead.

The house drifted beyond the reef where the drowned portion
of town lay beneath eight fathoms of water. It moved
against the current. As the sun rose, the building turned
gently until its blue door faced the lighthouse.

Then the door opened.

A boy stepped out.

He was perhaps ten years old. He wore short trousers,
a white shirt, and a red knitted cap. His feet
were bare. He stood on the narrow porch while waves
rolled under the house, lifting it without disturbing him.

Mara dropped the telescope.

For several seconds she could hear only the lamp mechanism
ticking behind her.

Then came the bell.

Far below, in the belly of the lighthouse, the storm
bell rang once.

It had not rung in forty years.

The sound passed through the stone tower and into Mara's
bones.

She ran.

At sixty-two, Mara had learned not to hurry on spiral
stairs, but she flew down them now, one hand sliding
along the central iron post. She snatched her coat from
its peg, crossed the wet grass, and dragged the small
motorboat down its rails into the inlet.

The engine coughed twice before catching.

"Mara Venn," she muttered, steering toward the reef, "you are
an old fool."

The returned house remained still while the sea moved around
it. As Mara approached, she saw curtains in the windows.
A brass knocker gleamed on the blue door. The boy
stood waiting.

She cut the engine several yards from the porch.

"Mara," he called.

His voice was exactly as she remembered.

She gripped the tiller. "Lio?"

The boy smiled.

Lio Vale had been eleven when the Great Tide came.
Mara had been nine. He should have been fifty-one now.

"You took your time," he said.

Mara stared at his narrow face, the chipped front tooth,
the red cap his grandmother had knitted. Memory, preserved without
mercy.

"You're dead," she said.

"So they tell me."

A wave nudged the boat against the porch. Lio crouched
and tied its rope to a white post.

"Come inside," he said. "Your mother is waiting."

Mara did not move.

The house smelled of salt, pears, and bread baking. Impossible
scents spilled through the open doorway.

"Is this a dream?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"A trap?"

"I don't know that either."

"That is not helpful."

"I'm still eleven."

It was such a thoroughly Lio answer that Mara almost
laughed. Instead, tears stung her eyes.

Behind him, a woman called her name.

Not "Mara," as everyone called her now, but "Marabelle," with
the second syllable stretched in the old family way.

Mara stepped onto the porch.

The kitchen had not changed. Copper pans hung over the
stove. Blue cups stood on open shelves. A loaf of
bread rested on the table, steaming as if just removed
from the oven.

Her mother sat beside it.

Elian Venn was thirty-four years old, with dark hair braided
around her head. She wore the green dress Mara remembered
from the last morning. Her hands were dusted with flour.

Beside her sat Mara's little brother, Tavin, who had been
six forever.

"Marabelle," her mother said.

Mara had imagined this moment in a thousand forms. In
some, she ran into her mother's arms. In others, she
shouted accusations. Why had Elian gone back for a wooden
box when the warning bells began? Why had she taken
Tavin with her? Why had she left Mara on the
hill, small and helpless, watching the ocean tear Bellweather apart?

But the years had worn the sharp edges from those
questions. What remained was heavier.

"You missed my whole life," Mara said.

Elian's expression broke.

"I know."

Mara sat at the table because her knees would no
longer hold her.

Tavin peered at her. "You got old."

"You didn't."

"Can I touch your wrinkles?"

"No."

He touched them anyway, tracing the lines beside her mouth
with one finger. Mara caught his hand. It was warm.

She began to cry then. Not gracefully, not quietly. She
bent over Tavin's small hand and wept until her ribs
hurt.

Her mother moved to embrace her, and for one miraculous
moment Mara was nine years old again, safe in the
green folds of that dress.

Outside, the storm bell rang a second time.

The kitchen dimmed.

"What does that mean?" Mara asked.

Lio looked toward the ceiling. "The sea is remembering."

Elian returned to her chair. "There isn't much time."

"For what?"

"To choose."

Mara wiped her face. "Choose what?"

Her mother glanced at Tavin, then at Lio. "The drowned
houses are returning all along the coast. Each one carries
the people it lost. Each one will offer the same
choice."

The room creaked. Water dripped somewhere beyond the walls.

"Those who enter may stay," Elian continued. "At sunset, the
houses will sink again. They will take everyone inside with
them."

Mara looked at the blue door. Through it she could
see the distant lighthouse rising from its island, pale and
solitary.

"And the other choice?"

"The dead may leave the houses," Lio said. "But only
if someone living takes each one's place."

Mara stared at him.

"One life for one life?"

He nodded.

"That's monstrous."

"It's the sea," Elian said. "It does not know fairness.
Only balance."

Tavin leaned against Mara's arm. "Could you take me home?"

The question opened something inside her.

She pictured bringing him to Bellweather. He would step onto
the harbor pier, still six years old, while people gathered
in silence. She would show him electric streetlamps, motorcars, the
rebuilt school. He would eat oranges in winter and complain
about shoes. He would grow.

Her mother could leave too, if Mara remained.

But there were three of them, counting Lio.

And only one of her.

"Can I trade for more than one?" she asked.

"No," said Elian.

Tavin's fingers tightened around her sleeve.

"Then I choose him."

"Mara—"

"I choose Tavin."

Her brother looked from Mara to their mother. "But I
don't want to go without Mama."

The truth of it settled over the table.

Of course he did not. He was six. Forty years
had passed for Mara, but not for him. To Tavin,
their mother had never been absent. Mara was the stranger.

Elian placed a hand over Mara's.

"You always decide before you understand," she said gently. "You
got that from me."

"What is there to understand?"

"You are not the only visitor expected here."

As if summoned by her words, an engine sounded outside.

A blue council boat rounded the reef. At the wheel
stood a young man in a brown coat, his fair
hair blown sideways by the wind.

Mara recognized him as Daniel Vale, Lio's nephew and Bellweather's
harbormaster. Behind him sat half a dozen townspeople.

News had traveled quickly.

The boat struck the porch harder than Mara's had. Daniel
leaped out before anyone tied the rope.

He pushed past Mara and stopped in the kitchen.

Lio stood.

For a moment uncle and nephew regarded each other, though
the nephew was nearly three times the uncle's age.

Daniel took off his cap. "My father used to talk
about you."

Lio shrugged, suddenly shy. "Is he here?"

"He died last winter."

"Oh."

"He never stopped looking for you."

Lio lowered his head.

The others crowded into the house. There was Mrs. Sare
from the bakery, whose twin sisters had drowned; old Thom
Bell, who had lost his wife; Councilor Reed, who had
lost no one but wore the hungry expression of a
man scenting opportunity.

Questions erupted. Names were shouted. Doors opened throughout the house.
The Vale home could not possibly contain so many rooms,
yet a corridor now stretched beyond the kitchen, lined with
doors Mara had never seen.

From behind them came voices.

The drowned of Bellweather were all here.

The house filled with reunions. Cries of joy and grief
mingled until they became almost indistinguishable. Mrs. Sare found her
sisters in a bedroom and collapsed between them. Thom Bell
emerged from another room holding the hand of a woman
young enough to be his granddaughter.

Councilor Reed returned alone.

"This is extraordinary," he said. "Do you understand what this
means? We must notify the capital. Scientists, clergy—"

"Sunset," Mara said. "The house sinks at sunset."

The councilor's face changed.

Around noon, more boats arrived.

People came from Bellweather and the surrounding farms. Some carried
old photographs; others brought flowers or offerings of food, as
if visiting graves. The house expanded to receive them. New
stairs appeared. Hallways unfolded. The outside remained no larger than
before, but within it contained parlors, attics, workshops, and bedrooms
belonging to every home the Great Tide had swallowed.

And in each room the choice was explained.

One for one.

By afternoon, arguments shook the walls.

A fisherman named Kerr offered himself for his daughter, but
the girl refused to leave without her drowned fiancé. Mrs.
Sare tried to choose between her sisters until both demanded
she go home. Thom Bell wanted his wife restored, while
she insisted that he had lived long enough and should
stay with her. Councilor Reed proposed drawing lots, then withdrew
the idea when the crowd turned on him.

Mara escaped to the porch.

The sky had cleared. Sunlight made a golden road across
the sea. On the lighthouse island, laundry flapped on the
line beside her empty cottage.

Daniel joined her.

"Lio says he doesn't want the trade," he said.

"They all say that."

"Would you expect anything else?"

"No."

Inside, someone began playing a piano. The tune was an
old dance, popular before the tide. Voices joined it, uncertain
at first, then stronger.

Daniel leaned against the porch post. "I thought I would
ask him about my father. All the things no one
else knew. But when I saw him, I couldn't think
of a single question."

"You still have time."

"The bell has rung twice. How many times does it
ring?"

"Three, I think."

"You think?"

"The first bell calls the lost. The second calls the
living." Mara remembered an old rhyme sailors used to recite
in taverns. "The third dismisses both."

Daniel looked west. The sun hovered two handspans above the
horizon.

"What will you do?"

Mara watched a gull circle the lighthouse.

"I have spent forty years keeping a light for people
who never came home."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

She returned to the kitchen.

Her mother was packing bread into a cloth bag for
Tavin, an ordinary act so absurd that Mara wanted to
scream.

"Stop that," Mara said.

Elian folded the cloth. "He gets hungry."

"He isn't going."

"No."

"And neither are you."

"No."

Mara sat across from her. "I hated you."

"I know."

"I thought if you had loved me enough, you would
have survived."

"I know."

"I built my life around waiting for an apology."

Elian's hands became still.

"I am sorry, Marabelle."

Mara closed her eyes.

The words did not repair the years. They did not
restore birthdays, winter illnesses, her wedding day, or the morning
her husband died. They did not give Tavin the chance
to grow tall. Yet something old and tightly knotted inside
her loosened.

"I don't forgive you," Mara whispered.

"You don't have to."

"But I'm tired of hating you."

Her mother smiled through tears. "That will do."

The sunlight in the kitchen turned amber.

All through the impossible house, the living and the dead
gathered. Word had passed from room to room: the end
was near.

A few trades had been agreed upon.

Kerr the fisherman stood beside his daughter, who now wore
his heavy coat. He would remain; she would leave. A
middle-aged woman had chosen to exchange her life for a
baby sister she had known only through stories. Two brothers
shouted at each other until one crossed the threshold and
the other vanished into the house.

Each bargain was sealed at the blue door.

When a dead person stepped onto the porch, age rushed
over them. Kerr's daughter changed from seventeen to fifty-eight in
a breath, her hair silvering, her face filling with the
years denied to her. She staggered but remained standing.

The living person did not become young. They simply faded
from the porch and reappeared inside, beyond the windows, untouched
by time.

Mara watched Tavin watching them.

"Does it hurt?" he asked.

"I don't know," she said.

"If you stay, will you be with us?"

"I think so."

He considered this. "But you'll miss your lighthouse."

"Yes."

"And who will turn on the lamp?"

"It turns itself on now."

"That's sad."

Mara laughed softly. "It is."

The sun touched the sea.

The storm bell rang a third time.

The sound rolled across the water, immense and low. The
house shuddered. In the distance, other bells answered from up
and down the coast.

Water climbed the porch steps.

"Decide!" shouted someone.

Panic broke the stillness. Several people rushed toward the door,
some pulling dead relatives, others being pulled back. The blue
door flashed with each exchange. Kerr vanished. His daughter screamed
his name. Thom Bell kissed his young wife once and
stepped away from her, choosing the years he had already
lived.

Mara held Tavin close.

"You could stay," he whispered.

"Yes."

"Do you want to?"

More than anything, she thought.

Not death. Not exactly. She wanted the kitchen, the smell
of pears, her mother's arms, her brother's warm hand. She
wanted to step outside the long corridor of years and
enter a room where nothing had been taken.

But through the open door she could see the lighthouse.

The automatic lamp came on.

Its beam swept across the darkening sea, passed over the
house, and moved on. Cold, precise, faithful.

Mara understood then that she had never kept the light
for the dead.

Lights were for the living, especially those still far from
home.

She knelt before Tavin. "I can't stay."

He nodded gravely. "I know."

"I'm sorry."

"That's all right."

"No, it isn't."

He put both hands on her wrinkled cheeks. "It will
do."

Mara began to cry again.

Her mother drew them together. For a moment the three
of them held one another while seawater spread across the
kitchen floor.

Then Elian let go.

"Go, Marabelle."

Mara stumbled toward the porch. Around her, the last visitors
made their choices. Daniel embraced Lio and backed through the
door alone. Mrs. Sare left with neither sister, all three
women weeping.

Mara crossed the threshold as the sun disappeared.

The blue door swung shut.

The returned house began to sink.

Those in the boats watched the roof descend. Faces appeared
at every window: the lost people of Bellweather, young and
old, holding lamps against the deepening dark.

Mara saw her mother in the kitchen window.

Tavin sat on her shoulders, waving.

Mara waved until the waves closed over them.

For a while, a warm square of light glowed beneath
the surface. Then it went out.

The sea became only sea.

Daniel started the council boat. One by one, the vessels
turned toward shore, carrying the grieving, the spared, and the
returned. Kerr's daughter sat in the bow, staring at her
aged hands. Behind her, the woman who had traded for
her baby sister cradled an astonished forty-year-old who had entered
the world that evening with no memory beyond a nursery.

Mara climbed into her own boat.

She did not follow the others.

Instead, she steered toward the lighthouse, guided by its revolving
beam.

The next morning, Bellweather began counting its miracles and its
losses. In the years that followed, people argued about what
the rising houses had meant. Priests called it judgment. Scholars
called it a temporal anomaly. Sailors, who were wiser, refused
to name it.

Mara remained keeper of the Bellweather Light.

She lived another eighteen years.

Every evening she climbed the spiral stairs before sunset, though
the lamp required no tending. She polished the glass, checked
the mechanism, and stood at the rail while darkness gathered
over the drowned town.

Sometimes she smelled pears and baking bread.

Sometimes, far below the waves, she thought she saw windows.

But the house never rose again.

On Mara's final night, a storm covered the coast. Rain
struck the lantern glass, and the wind howled around the
tower. She sat beside the lamp with a blanket over
her knees, listening to the old building breathe.

Near midnight, the automatic mechanism failed.

The great light went dark.

Mara rose slowly. Her hands shook, but she knew every
bolt and gear. She opened the maintenance panel, found the
broken drive chain, and fitted the emergency crank into its
socket.

Then she turned it.

The lens began to move.

Out at sea, beyond the reef, a ship answered with
three blasts of its horn.

Mara smiled and kept turning.

Just before dawn, the storm cleared. The first sunlight spread
over the water, and the ship entered the harbor safely.

When Daniel came to the island that morning, he found
Mara seated beside the crank, her head resting against the
brass housing, one hand still curled around the handle.

The lamp was burning.

Beyond the reef, where the Great Tide had buried Harbor
Street, three figures stood upon the water: a woman in
a green dress, a boy with solemn eyes, and another
boy wearing a red knitted cap.

As Daniel watched, Mara walked toward them.

She was nine years old.

The four figures joined hands. For one bright instant, beneath
the calm surface, the whole drowned town appeared—roofs and chimneys,
gardens and roads, every window full of morning.

Then the lighthouse beam passed over the sea, and they
were gone.

The lighthouse had been dark for thirty-two years when Mara
Vale saw it blink.

She was standing at the kitchen window of her father's
house, rinsing a cup, when a white beam swept across
the winter sea. It passed over the harbor wall, the
empty beach, and the black roofs of the village before
vanishing into the fog.

Mara dropped the cup. It struck the iron sink without
breaking.

For a moment, she wondered whether grief had finally damaged
her sight. She had slept badly since returning to Calder
Island, and the house was full of her father's unfinished
habits: his boots beside the stove, his spectacles on the
newspaper, two onions waiting on a board to be chopped.
He had been dead for nine days, yet every room
expected him back.

The light appeared again.

Slowly, silently, it crossed the sea.

Mara dried her hands and went outside.

Calder Island had only one village, one church, and one
road that climbed north through wet fields to the cliffs.
At the end of that road stood Saint Orra's Lighthouse,
a gray stone tower built long before electricity reached the
island. Its lantern had once warned ships away from the
Needle Rocks, but an automated beacon offshore had replaced it.
Since then, the tower had been locked.

Mara had not gone near it since childhood.

The road shone with rain. Wind pushed strands of hair
across her eyes as she climbed, and sheep watched her
from behind low walls, their wool bright in the darkness.
Every twelve seconds, the beam turned above her. It seemed
impossible that no one else had noticed, but the village
windows remained unlit.

At the lighthouse gate, Mara stopped.

A chain hung loose from the iron bars.

Beyond it, the tower door stood open.

"Hello?" she called.

The wind answered through the grass.

Inside, the lighthouse smelled of salt, oil, and cold stone.
A spiral staircase curved upward around a hollow center. The
walls were lined with narrow shelves, and the shelves were
crowded with glass bottles.

Mara took out her phone and switched on its torch.

There were hundreds of bottles, perhaps thousands. Some were clear,
others green or brown. Each was stoppered with wax and
labeled in faded handwriting.

She read the nearest tag.

MRS. ALICE ROWE—THE SOUND OF HER MOTHER SINGING WHILE GUTTING
FISH.

The bottle looked empty.

The next label read:

TOM BELL—THE WAY HOME FROM THE WESTERN COVE.

And another:

EDWIN VALE—HIS FIRST WIFE'S LAST WORDS.

Mara's breath caught.

Edwin Vale had been her grandfather. His first wife, Mara's
grandmother, had drowned before Mara was born.

She reached toward the bottle, but a voice above her
said, "I wouldn't open that."

Mara jerked around.

A man was descending the stairs. He was tall and
stooped, wearing a fisherman's sweater and carrying an old brass
lamp. His hair was white, but his face was not
as old as his posture made it seem.

"Who are you?" Mara demanded.

"Jonas Pike."

She recognized the name. Jonas had been the final keeper
of Saint Orra's, dismissed when the offshore beacon was installed.
Villagers said he had moved to the mainland. Others said
he had died.

"You're trespassing," Mara said, though she knew how foolish it
sounded.

"So are you."

"My father owned the key."

"He owned one of them."

The beam passed overhead, filling the stairwell with a brief
white pulse.

Mara pointed upward. "Why is the light on?"

Jonas glanced at the bottles. "Because something has been lost."

"Things are lost every day."

"Not like this."

He continued down until they stood three steps apart.

Up close, Mara saw rain on his shoulders, although she
had not heard him enter. A pale scar ran from
his left ear to the corner of his mouth.

"Your father took care of this place after I couldn't,"
he said.

"My father repaired nets."

"He did that too."

Mara almost laughed. "Are you telling me he was secretly
a lighthouse keeper?"

"No. The lighthouse hasn't needed a keeper in years. It
needed a collector."

Jonas raised the lamp toward the shelves.

"This tower was built to protect ships," he said. "That
is true enough. But Saint Orra's light has always done
something else. When people on the island lose a memory—truly
lose it—the light draws the memory here."

Mara stared at him.

"I see," she said. "And you put them in bottles."

"Your father did."

"My father had arthritis. He complained about carrying coal from
the shed."

"He complained about everything."

That, at least, sounded right.

Mara turned to the shelves. "Why?"

"Memories don't vanish when they leave us. They wander. Some
cling to places. Some enter dreams. Some sour and become
grief without a name. On an island, there aren't many
places for them to go. The tower gathers them before
they cause harm."

"And the bottles?"

"Keep them quiet."

She read another label.

LUCY HARROW—THE FACE OF THE MAN SHE PUSHED FROM THE
QUAY.

Mara stepped back.

Jonas smiled without humor. "Not everything forgotten deserves to be
returned."

"This is absurd."

"Yes."

"I'm going home."

"You should."

Mara walked toward the open door.

"Before you do," Jonas said, "ask yourself why your father
died with white wax under his fingernails."

She stopped.

The undertaker had cleaned her father's hands, but not completely.
Mara remembered the pale crescents along his nails. She had
assumed they were paint or candle grease.

She faced Jonas again. "What happened to him?"

"That's what the light is trying to tell us."

They climbed.

The bottles continued all the way up the tower. Each
landing held memories from a different period, the labels changing
from crisp white cards to yellow paper and then to
scraps of cloth marked in brown ink.

Near the lantern room, Mara found bottles bearing familiar names.

Her schoolteacher had forgotten a child's cruel remark. The baker
had forgotten the smell of his burned shop. A woman
who sang in church had forgotten a summer afternoon with
someone who was not her husband.

Then Mara saw her own name.

MARA VALE—HER FATHER CARRYING HER HOME IN THE RAIN.

The bottle was small and blue.

She lifted it from the shelf.

A memory stirred inside her—not an image, exactly, but a
warmth, a rhythm. For one instant, she felt herself as
a child, cheek pressed to rough wool, listening to her
father's heart while rain tapped on his coat.

The sensation vanished.

"I remember that," she whispered.

"Do you?"

"I fell near the old quarry and cut my knee."

Jonas examined the label. "You remember the fall. You remember
the cut. Do you remember being carried?"

Mara tried.

She saw mud, blood, wet grass, her father kneeling beside
her. After that came the kitchen, a towel, hot milk.
Between the quarry and the house was a blank.

"Why would I lose that?"

"People lose happy things too. Sometimes happiness is the first
thing grief takes."

Mara put the bottle back carefully.

At the top of the stairs, they entered the lantern
room.

The great lens turned around a bright flame that had
no visible fuel. Its machinery creaked and clicked, though rust
covered the gears. Beyond the windows, the island floated in
darkness.

On the floor lay a wooden box. Melted white wax
coated its lid.

Mara knelt beside it.

"Was this his?"

Jonas nodded.

Inside were labels, sealing wax, a ledger, and a short
knife. Beneath them lay her father's red scarf, neatly folded.

Mara touched it. The wool still smelled faintly of tobacco
and sea air.

The ledger's last pages were filled with her father's handwriting.
Most entries were lists of names and memories, each followed
by a date and shelf number. The final entry was
different.

January 14.

The light woke at 3:17 in the morning. No single
memory arrived. Instead, a hollow has opened in the village.
People repeat themselves. Nets are tied wrongly. Children forget songs
while singing them. I believe this is not a lost
memory but something feeding on memories already stored.

January 16.

Three bottles empty. No broken seals.

January 18.

I saw it between the shelves.

January 19.

It wore Mara's face.

Mara stopped reading.

Wind struck the glass.

"That's not possible," she said.

Jonas stood near the turning lens, his features appearing and
disappearing behind the light.

"What did he see?" Mara asked.

"A hunger," Jonas replied. "Old places grow their own hungers.
This one learned that memories have flavors."

She looked again at the page.

It wore Mara's face.

"Why mine?"

"Because your father would hesitate."

"Did he?"

"Yes."

The answer landed more cruelly than she expected.

"What happened?"

"He tried to catch it in a bottle. It showed
him things. Your childhood. Your mother alive. You coming home
for good. Every life he wished he'd had. He followed
it onto the gallery."

Mara's father had been found at the foot of the
cliff below the lighthouse. Everyone believed he had slipped while
walking in the storm. Mara had believed it too, because
the alternative was unbearable.

"Did it kill him?"

"I don't know."

"You were here."

"I arrived too late."

"Convenient."

Jonas looked out at the fog. "Very."

The flame suddenly dimmed.

Below them, glass shattered.

Mara and Jonas turned toward the stairwell.

A child began to laugh in the darkness.

Mara knew that laugh. It was her own at seven
years old: breathless, reckless, before self-consciousness had entered her life.

She moved toward the stairs.

Jonas caught her arm. "Don't answer it."

The laugh came again, farther down.

Then her father called, "Mara?"

She stopped breathing.

His voice was exactly right. Not the weakened voice from
his final years, but the deep, impatient voice she remembered
from home.

"Mara, bring the lamp, would you?"

Jonas tightened his grip.

"That isn't him," he said.

Her father called again. "There's a good girl."

Mara pulled free.

She knew it was not him. Knowledge made no difference.
Grief lived beneath knowledge, in an older and more obedient
part of the body.

She descended.

The beam circled above her. Every time it passed, the
stairwell changed. Bottles shone, shadows leaped, and something moved one
landing below.

"Mara," Jonas warned.

She followed the red scarf.

It slipped around the curve of the stairs, one fringed
end trailing over the stone. Her father had worn that
scarf every winter. She remembered teasing him because it made
him look like a retired actor. She remembered buying it
for him.

No, she thought. Her mother had bought it.

Or had she?

The thing below laughed.

As Mara descended, whispers rose from the bottles.

A thousand forgotten moments crowded the air: lullabies, insults, passwords,
prayers. She heard the name of a sailor buried without
a stone. She heard a woman confess that she had
never loved her youngest son. She heard someone describe the
color of the sky before an old ship went down.

The red scarf vanished through a door on the lowest
landing.

Mara followed it into a circular room she had not
noticed when she entered. It was windowless and lined with
mirrors. At its center stood her father.

He wore his brown coat and red scarf. His beard
was damp with fog.

"You took your time," he said.

Mara gripped the doorway.

He smiled.

It was a perfect copy of his smile, including the
slight downward twitch at one corner caused by an old
fishing accident.

Her knees weakened.

"I'm sorry," she whispered.

"For what?"

"For not coming sooner."

"Ah."

"I should have visited."

"You were busy."

"I should have called."

"Yes."

The word struck sharply.

Her father stepped closer. "I waited."

Mara's eyes filled with tears.

Behind her, Jonas reached the landing. "Look at the mirrors,"
he said.

Mara did.

Her own reflection appeared again and again around the room.
Jonas stood in the doorway. The figure in front of
her cast no reflection at all.

Its smile widened.

"Mirrors don't remember," Jonas said. "That's why it hates them."

The thing wearing her father's face turned.

Its features loosened like wax near a flame. For an
instant it became Mara as a child, then Mara as
an old woman, then her mother, whom Mara remembered only
from photographs.

Finally, it became a dark outline filled with tiny lights.

The lights were memories. Mara saw hundreds flickering inside it:
birthdays, funerals, stormy nights, first kisses, broken promises. Some belonged
to people long dead. Some belonged to her.

Her father's voice emerged from the shape.

"I waited."

Jonas thrust the brass lamp forward. The creature recoiled.

"Now!" he shouted. "The bottle in my pocket!"

Mara snatched a glass flask from his coat. It was
larger than the others and sealed around the neck with
a loop of silver wire.

"How?"

"Open it and remember something true."

She pulled the stopper.

Nothing happened.

The creature advanced. Darkness spilled from it and spread across
the floor. Where the darkness touched Mara's boots, memories vanished.

She forgot the kitchen window.

She forgot why she had come to the lighthouse.

She forgot the date of her father's funeral.

Panic seized her.

"Something true!" Jonas shouted.

Mara tried to think of her father, but the creature
offered her a thousand versions of him. Her father laughing.
Her father angry. Her father saying he forgave her. Her
father saying he never had.

Truth slipped among them.

Then she remembered the small blue bottle upstairs.

Not the fall. Not the blood. Not even the rain.

She remembered the blank.

All her life, she had believed love had to be
dramatic to count: a last-minute arrival, a perfect apology, a
vigil beside a hospital bed. Because she had failed at
those things, she had thought she had failed her father
entirely.

But once, in a space grief had stolen from her,
he had carried her home.

She closed her eyes.

"My father carried me," she said.

The creature stopped.

"I don't remember it. I can't see his face. I
can't hear his voice. But I know he carried me."

The darkness trembled.

"And he complained the whole way," she continued. "He told
me I was too old to be carried and too
heavy besides. But he never put me down."

A sound like cracking ice filled the room.

Mara held up the bottle.

"That is true," she said.

The creature collapsed toward it.

Faces rushed past her: the baker, the schoolteacher, her grandfather,
children not yet old enough to understand what they had
lost. Her mother's face appeared last.

"Mara," it pleaded.

She nearly lowered the flask.

Then her mother smiled.

It was a beautiful smile, but it was wrong. Her
mother had chipped one of her front teeth as a
girl. The woman in the photographs always smiled with her
lips closed.

Mara raised the bottle higher.

The creature became a stream of black smoke and silver
sparks. It poured into the flask with a howl that
shook every shelf in the tower. Bottles rattled. Mirrors broke.
Jonas shouted something she could not hear.

Then the last spark vanished.

Mara forced the stopper into place.

Silence followed.

For several seconds, she remained standing with the bottle clutched
to her chest.

Then the lighthouse beam went out.

Jonas lit three oil lamps in the lantern room. Without
the great light turning, Saint Orra's seemed older and smaller.
Dawn began as a thin gray line over the sea.

They sealed the flask with white wax. Inside, shadows pressed
against the glass.

"What do we do with it?" Mara asked.

"Keep it."

"Here?"

"For now."

She looked at the crowded shelves. "And all these?"

"Keep them too."

"Why not return them?"

"Some can be returned. Carefully. Others have been gone too
long. Give a person back the wrong memory and you
can unmake the life they built without it."

Mara thought of Alice Rowe hearing her mother sing again.
She thought of Lucy Harrow remembering the face of the
man at the quay.

"Who decides?"

"The collector."

"I'm not the collector."

Jonas gave her a tired look. "No?"

They carried the newly sealed bottle down to the deepest
shelf. Jonas placed it behind an iron grille and locked
the door with a key from the chain around his
neck.

"Why didn't you do this job after my father?" Mara
asked.

"Because the tower doesn't trust me."

"Why not?"

He was silent for a long time.

At last, he walked to a shelf and removed a
clear bottle. The label read:

JONAS PIKE—THE NIGHT HIS SON DROWNED.

"I tried to bring one back," he said. "Not just
a memory. A person."

Mara looked from the bottle to the scar on his
face.

"What happened?"

"What always happens when we ask the past to become
the present. It returned hungry."

He replaced the bottle.

"Your father stopped it," he continued. "He saved my life
and lost some of his own memories doing so. After
that, I could help, but I could never keep the
tower alone."

Mara understood then why her father sometimes forgot entire years
yet recalled the smallest details of her childhood. She had
blamed age, stubbornness, drink, anything that allowed her to remain
angry.

"Did he remember me?" she asked.

"Until the end."

"Are you sure?"

Jonas pointed toward the upper shelves.

"Half the bottles up there are things he surrendered to
make room."

They found a final page hidden in the back of
the ledger.

The handwriting was uneven.

If Mara comes, tell her I did not call because
I knew she would come if I asked. She has
always mistaken duty for love, and I would not use
one to steal the other.

Tell her I was proud.

Tell her I was often angry.

Both are true.

Mara read the words twice. Then she carried the ledger
to the gallery outside.

Morning had reached the village. Chimneys smoked below. A fishing
boat moved beyond the harbor, its wake a white thread
sewn across the dark water.

Jonas joined her.

"Will the light wake again?" she asked.

"Whenever something important is lost."

"On this island, that could be tonight."

"It usually is."

Mara laughed, and the sound surprised her.

She stayed at Saint Orra's until noon, cleaning broken glass
and sorting fallen bottles. Then she walked home. She packed
nothing.

Instead, she took her father's onions from the board and
made soup. She opened the windows. She called the undertaker,
the solicitor, and finally the office in the city where
she had worked for eleven years.

She told them she would not be returning.

That evening, the village gathered in the churchyard for her
father's memorial. People brought stories of him, most of which
Mara had never heard. In one, he rescued a dog
from a flooded cave. In another, he cheated shamelessly at
cards. The baker claimed he had once danced on the
harbor wall wearing nothing but boots and a red scarf.

Mara believed all of them.

Afterward, she climbed the northern road.

The lighthouse stood dark against a clear sky. She unlocked
the gate with her father's key and entered.

On the third landing, the small blue bottle waited.

Mara held it for a while.

Then she carried it to the lantern room, where Jonas
had left a candle burning beside the ledger. She broke
the wax, removed the stopper, and closed her eyes.

Rain rushed in.

She was seven years old again, shivering against her father's
chest. His coat scratched her cheek. One of his arms
supported her knees; the other held her back. Each step
made him grunt.

"You're too heavy," he complained.

"You're too old," she answered.

"That doesn't even make sense."

"Neither do you."

He laughed.

The quarry road was long, and rainwater ran into his
eyes. Twice he nearly slipped. Yet his hold never loosened.

Mara felt the memory settle inside her, not as a
wound or a command, but as warmth.

When she opened her eyes, the bottle was empty.

Far below, a light appeared in the village. Then another.
Windows glowing one by one.

Mara relabeled the bottle in her own handwriting.

MARA VALE—HER FATHER CARRYING HER HOME IN THE RAIN.

RETURNED.

She placed it back on the shelf.

Above her, the great lens shuddered.

A flame rose at its center, pale and steady, and
Saint Orra's began to turn its bright gaze across the
sea.

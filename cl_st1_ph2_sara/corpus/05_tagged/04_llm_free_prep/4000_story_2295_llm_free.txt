### The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, Elias Vale climbed the hill above
Bellwether and unlocked the door of the abandoned clock tower.

The town had not asked him to do this. In
fact, the town council had declared the tower unsafe twenty-three
years earlier, after lightning split the stone angel above its
eastern face. A red sign still hung crookedly across the
entrance:

DANGER. KEEP OUT.

Elias considered it less a warning than a description of
his profession.

He was the keeper of lost hours.

Inside the tower, clocks covered every wall. They stood on
shelves, crowded the stairs, and hung from nails driven into
the old beams. There were brass carriage clocks, painted kitchen
clocks, delicate watches, cuckoo clocks with broken birds, and alarm
clocks whose bells had gone silent. Some had been brought
to Elias for repair and never collected. Others he had
found in demolished houses, flooded cellars, or rubbish bins.

None told the correct time.

That was the point.

Each clock had stopped at a moment someone in Bellwether
wished to forget.

At 6:17, a silver pocket watch had frozen when Mr.
Dalloway learned his brother would not return from the war.

At 11:42, a nursery clock had halted in the room
where the Harrow family's youngest daughter took her final breath.

At 3:05, a cheap plastic alarm clock had fallen silent
when seventeen-year-old Mara Flint packed a bag and left town
without saying goodbye.

Elias knew the story of every clock. He wrote each
one in a ledger bound with green leather, then kept
the lost hour safe.

Most people did not know what he did. They only
knew that, after visiting him, certain memories became less sharp.
Grief loosened its grip. Regret stopped whispering at the edge
of sleep. A person might still remember an event, but
the hour itself—the unbearable instant—would no longer return with all
its teeth.

Elias never explained the cost.

For every hour removed from one life, the tower added
an hour to his own.

He was sixty-eight years old, though by ordinary measure he
had lived nearly a century. Lost hours accumulated in his
bones. They silvered his hair, stiffened his fingers, and filled
his dreams with other people's sorrow.

Still, he came every morning.

He wound the clocks that needed winding, polished their faces,
and listened to the layered ticking. The sound was not
orderly. Some clocks ticked fast, others slow. One advanced only
when it rained. Another moved backward on Sundays. Together they
made a restless murmur, like a room full of people
trying not to cry.

On a cold morning in November, Elias found a girl
sitting outside the tower.

She looked about twelve. Her dark hair was tied with
a red ribbon, and she wore a yellow coat too
thin for the weather. In her lap rested a small
wooden clock shaped like a house.

"You're early," Elias said.

"You're late," she replied.

He took out his watch. It was exactly six.

"For what?"

"For me."

The girl stood and offered him the clock. Its painted
door was open, but no bird emerged. The hands pointed
to 8:26.

Elias did not take it.

"Who told you to come here?"

"No one."

"What is your name?"

"Clara Venn."

He knew the name. Everyone in Bellwether did.

Three days earlier, a fire had destroyed a cottage near
the river. A lamp had overturned in the night. Clara
escaped through an upstairs window, but her mother had not
escaped at all.

Elias had seen the girl at the funeral, standing beside
an uncle she scarcely knew. She had not cried. People
mistook stillness for courage when they did not know what
else to call it.

"Where did you get the clock?" he asked.

"It was in the kitchen. The firemen found it under
part of the roof."

Elias looked more closely. One side was blackened. The varnish
had bubbled from the heat.

"You want me to repair it?"

"I want you to take the hour away."

A gust rattled the bare branches overhead. Far below, Bellwether
began to wake. Chimneys breathed smoke into the pale sky.

Elias unlocked the tower.

"Come inside."

Clara followed him up the spiral stairs. She said nothing
when she saw the clocks. Most visitors gasped or stared.
She merely listened.

"Which ones are working?" she asked.

"All of them."

"But they show different times."

"They show different truths."

He led her to a narrow room beneath the bell
chamber. There was a table, two chairs, a kettle, and
a small iron stove. He lit the stove, then set
water to boil.

"You know what happens if I take this hour?" he
asked.

"I won't remember the fire."

"You will remember there was a fire. You will remember
your mother died. But the moment will lose its hold
on you."

"Good."

"You may also lose things connected to it. The sound
of her voice that night. The last words she said.
The way she looked at you."

Clara placed the clock on the table.

"Good," she repeated, but less firmly.

Elias opened his ledger.

"Tell me what happened at 8:26."

"The fire was later."

"Then why did the clock stop at 8:26?"

"It didn't stop. I stopped it."

Elias raised his eyes.

Clara pressed her hands together until her knuckles whitened.

"At 8:26, Mama asked me to fill the lamp. I
was reading. I said I would do it later. She
asked again, and I shouted at her." The girl swallowed.
"Then she filled it herself."

"The lamp that caused the fire?"

Clara nodded.

"What did your mother say when you shouted?"

"She said, 'Time has strange manners, Clara. It waits all
day, then leaves when you need one minute more.'"

The kettle began to hiss.

Elias closed the ledger.

"You don't want to forget the fire," he said. "You
want to forget your guilt."

"Can you take it or not?"

"I can take the hour."

"Then take it."

"But guilt is not an hour. It will find another
clock."

Clara snatched the wooden clock from the table.

"You're a fraud."

"Possibly."

"You're supposed to help people."

"I keep what they cannot carry."

"I can't carry this."

"I know."

Her face tightened. For one terrible second, she looked much
younger than twelve. Then the stillness broke, and she began
to cry.

Elias did not touch her. Grief was a wild animal;
cornering it only made it fight harder. He poured tea
and waited.

When her sobs softened, Clara wiped her cheeks with the
sleeve of her yellow coat.

"How do you do it?" she asked.

"Do what?"

"Take an hour away."

Elias looked toward the clocks beyond the doorway.

"There is a room at the top of the tower."

"What's in it?"

"The first clock."

"Whose?"

"Mine."

Clara waited, but Elias did not continue.

"Show me," she said.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I said so."

"You sound like my uncle."

"I'm sorry to hear that."

For the first time, the corner of her mouth moved
as if she might smile. Then it vanished.

"I'm not going with him," she said. "He lives in
Dorset."

"That is what uncles are for. They appear after tragedies
and take children to distant counties."

"I'm staying here."

"Where?"

Clara looked around the tower.

"No."

"There are rooms."

"There are floors. Rooms generally have beds and windows."

"I can help with the clocks."

"No."

"I know how to clean. Mama taught me."

"No."

"I can read and write."

"So can several of the clocks, when properly encouraged."

"I have nowhere else."

That silenced him.

Elias looked at the burned clock on the table, at
the red ribbon in her hair, at the stubborn line
of her jaw. He had spent his life preserving painful
moments, yet he had forgotten how quickly a life could
be rearranged around one.

"You may stay until your uncle comes," he said.

"He's coming at noon."

"Then you may stay until noon."

Clara stayed for thirty-seven years.

At first, she hid whenever her uncle came searching. Elias
insisted she must leave; Clara insisted more effectively that she
would not. Eventually, the uncle decided a gloomy clockmaker and
an unsafe tower were preferable to an unwilling child in
a respectable house. Papers were signed. The town muttered. The
tower gained a narrow bed, a second teacup, and a
yellow coat hanging beside the door.

Elias taught Clara his trade.

He showed her how to listen to a clock before
opening it, because every mechanism had its own temper. He
taught her to file a gear tooth, balance a wheel,
and replace a mainspring without losing an eye. More carefully,
he taught her to recognize a lost hour.

"They are heavier than ordinary clocks," he explained.

Clara held a tiny gold watch in her palm. "It
feels the same."

"Not that kind of weight."

After a year, she understood.

People came secretly at dusk. A baker brought the clock
from his shop after his wife left him. A schoolmistress
brought a watch that had belonged to a drowned student.
A soldier arrived with no clock at all, only a
time written on a scrap of paper.

Clara listened to their stories.

Sometimes Elias accepted an hour. Sometimes he refused.

"It isn't fair," Clara said after he turned away a
man who wanted to forget the day he abandoned his
son.

"Fairness has little to do with mercy."

"Then why help anyone?"

"Because pain is not always a teacher. Sometimes it is
only pain."

When Elias accepted an hour, he carried the clock to
the top room alone. He locked the door behind him.
Minutes later, he returned looking older.

Clara noticed the changes before he did. A deeper line
beside his mouth. A tremor in his left hand. A
new cloudiness in his eyes.

"What happens in that room?" she asked.

"The clock is persuaded to carry what the person cannot."

"And you?"

"I supervise the exchange."

"You're lying."

"Only by omission."

When she was twenty, Clara stole the key.

She waited until Elias left for the market, then climbed
to the top of the tower. The door was made
of black oak, scarred by age. It opened reluctantly.

Inside, there was only one clock.

It stood taller than Clara, built into a case of
dark wood carved with stars, leaves, and sleeping faces. A
glass panel revealed no gears, only a pendulum that swung
without sound. Its face had no numbers.

Instead, names circled the dial.

Hundreds of names.

Clara recognized many from the ledger: Dalloway, Harrow, Flint. Near
the center, in faded gold, was one she had never
seen.

Rose Vale.

Beneath the name, the hands pointed to 4:13.

"You should not be here," Elias said behind her.

Clara turned. He stood in the doorway with a basket
of bread and apples. He did not look angry. He
looked tired.

"Who was Rose?"

"My daughter."

Clara glanced back at the clock.

"What happened at 4:13?"

"She died."

"How?"

"I forgot."

The answer chilled her.

Elias set down the basket.

"When Rose was nine, she became ill. The doctors could
do nothing. I was a young clockmaker who believed anything
could be repaired if one understood the mechanism."

"You took the hour away."

"From myself. I built this clock to hold it."

"And it worked?"

"At first. But memories do not vanish. They move. The
clock held the hour, and I held the clock. Then
others came to me with their own unbearable moments."

"Why does it make you older?"

"Time dislikes being cheated."

Clara looked at the names surrounding the dial.

"My name isn't there."

"You never gave me your hour."

"You've carried all of these?"

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"Long enough."

He stepped forward and closed the clock's glass panel.

"When I die, the hours return to their owners unless
another keeper takes my place."

Clara stared at him.

"You were never going to tell me."

"I hoped there would be no need."

"You trained me for twenty years."

"I taught you to mend clocks."

"You taught me to identify lost hours. You taught me
the ledger. You taught me everything except the cost."

"I wanted you to have a choice."

"A choice you hid is not a choice."

She pushed past him and descended the stairs.

For three days, she did not speak to him except
about meals and work. On the fourth, Elias suffered a
small stroke while winding the clock of a widow who
had forgotten why she had brought it.

Afterward, he could no longer climb beyond the second floor.

Clara became the keeper in all but name.

Years passed. Elias shrank into himself, though his mind remained
keen. He sat by the stove, repaired simple watches, and
complained that Clara made tea too strong.

Bellwether changed below them. Motorcars replaced carts. Electric lights replaced
lamps. The old bakery became a bank, then a cinema,
then a bakery again. Children who had once dared one
another to touch the tower door grew into parents who
climbed the hill carrying clocks of their own.

Clara refused them all.

"No more lost hours," she told Elias.

He did not argue.

She believed she had ended the trade. She repaired ordinary
clocks, kept the tower standing, and guarded the great clock
upstairs. If Elias died, she would become its keeper—not because
he had chosen her, she told herself, but because no
one else should bear it.

Then, on a rainy evening in March, a woman arrived
carrying a blue alarm clock.

Her name was Ada Flint. She was Mara Flint's younger
sister, though her hair had gone gray.

"My daughter is dying," Ada said.

Clara led her to the stove. Elias slept in a
chair nearby, a blanket over his knees.

"I'm sorry," Clara said.

"There was an accident at the mill. The doctors say
she may last until morning."

"You should be with her."

"She asked me to come."

Ada placed the alarm clock on the table. Its hands
showed 3:05.

Clara recognized it from the ledger.

"This is your sister's clock."

"Mara came home last month."

Clara remembered the story. At seventeen, Mara had vanished. Her
mother had brought Elias the clock, asking him to take
away the hour she discovered the empty bedroom.

"She was alive?"

"All these years. She lived in Canada. She wrote letters,
but Mother never answered. Mara thought she was still angry.
Mother never received them."

Clara understood.

The hour had been taken. Not merely softened, but removed.
Mrs. Flint remembered having a daughter and remembered that the
daughter was gone, but the moment of loss no longer
pressed upon her. Without the pain, perhaps the letters had
seemed like messages from a stranger.

"My mother died believing Mara never looked back," Ada said.
"Mara came home believing Mother hated her."

"And your daughter?"

"She found the letters yesterday, hidden in an old wall
during repairs. She went to tell Mara. On the way,
the mill cart struck her."

Ada's voice cracked.

"She is nineteen. She says she cannot bear to die
knowing she arrived one day too late. She wants you
to take yesterday away."

Clara closed her eyes.

Pain is not always a teacher, Elias had said. Sometimes
it is only pain.

But one lost hour had shaped decades. Mercy had opened
a wound time could not close.

"I can't," Clara whispered.

Ada stood.

"Then come tell her yourself."

The hospital smelled of soap, damp wool, and boiled vegetables.
Ada's daughter, Ruth, lay near a window. She was pale,
with bruises blooming along her neck. Mara sat beside her,
holding her hand.

Mara was sixty-six, but grief had made her resemble the
runaway girl in the ledger. On the bedside table stood
a bundle of yellowed letters tied with string.

"Are you the clock woman?" Ruth asked.

Clara nodded.

"Take yesterday away."

Mara bent over the girl's hand.

"No," she said.

Ruth turned weakly toward her. "Aunt Mara—"

"No. You brought me my mother's letters. You gave me
her words. You were not late."

"If I'd found them sooner—"

"You found them when you found them."

"But I wasted all that time."

Mara lifted the letters.

"So did I. So did your grandmother. We were all
waiting for one another. That is what people do when
they think time belongs to them."

Clara thought of her mother's final words. Time waits all
day, then leaves when you need one minute more.

Ruth began to cry.

"I'm afraid."

"I know," Mara said.

"I don't want this to be my last day."

"Then it won't be the only day we remember."

Through the night, they told stories. Ada spoke of Ruth
at six, furious because the moon followed her home. Mara
read letters her mother had written but never sent. Clara,
unexpectedly, told them about a girl in a yellow coat
who believed guilt could be placed inside a wooden clock.

Ruth died shortly before dawn.

No hour was taken.

When Clara returned to the tower, Elias was awake.

"You went to the hospital," he said.

"Yes."

"Did you accept the hour?"

"No."

"Was that mercy?"

"I don't know."

"Good."

She removed her wet coat.

"Why is that good?"

"People who are certain about mercy usually cause great harm."

He smiled faintly. Then his expression changed.

"Clara, it is time."

The great clock began to strike.

It had never struck before.

The sound rolled through the tower, shaking dust from the
beams. Every smaller clock fell silent. Elias looked toward the
ceiling.

Clara helped him to his feet. Together, slowly, they climbed.

The top room was filled with golden light, though dawn
had not yet broken. The names on the clock face
shone. Its silent pendulum had stopped.

Elias touched Rose's name.

"I have wondered all my life whether forgetting her death
also made me forget part of her life," he said.

"You remember her."

"Pieces. Red shoes. A gap between her front teeth. The
way she fed crumbs to birds and then chased them
for stealing."

Clara took his hand.

"What happens now?"

"I die."

"No."

"Yes. Even clocks reach the end of their springs."

"And the hours?"

"You choose."

He pointed to a small door at the base of
the great clock. Clara had never noticed it before.

"If you open that door, the hours return. Every living
person will remember what was taken. The dead will leave
their hours behind."

"And if I don't?"

"You take my place."

Clara thought of Mrs. Flint and the unanswered letters. She
thought of Ruth, afraid but surrounded by voices carrying her
whole life into the dark. She thought of countless others
who had built new lives because Elias had removed a
moment that would otherwise have destroyed them.

There was no clean choice.

"Will they forgive us?" she asked.

"They may not."

"Will it hurt?"

"Yes."

"Then why return anything?"

"Because pain is not the only thing inside a lost
hour."

Elias leaned against her. He had become very light.

"In the hour Rose died, she told me she was
not afraid. I gave that away with everything else."

Clara looked at the clock's small door.

Then she opened it.

Wind exploded through the room.

The clock faces below began spinning. Bells rang, cuckoos called,
alarms shrieked. Across Bellwether, people woke with lost moments returning
like sudden weather.

Mr. Dalloway, now an old man, remembered that his brother's
final letter had contained a joke.

The last surviving Harrow son remembered his little sister asking
him to open the curtains so she could see the
snow.

Mara Flint remembered standing at the railway station at 3:05,
hoping her mother would come after her.

And Clara remembered 8:26.

Her mother stood in the kitchen, lamp in hand, weary
and alive.

Clara heard herself shout. She felt again the hot shame
that followed.

Then came the part she had buried beneath it.

Her mother set down the lamp, crossed the room, and
kissed the top of Clara's head.

"You will understand time one day," she said. "But I
hope not too soon."

The memory struck Clara so fiercely that she fell to
her knees.

Beside her, Elias laughed.

It was a young man's laugh, astonished and bright.

"I remember," he whispered.

Clara turned.

His eyes were closed. His hand rested over Rose's name.
He was smiling.

He did not breathe again.

By sunrise, every clock in the tower had stopped.

For months, the people of Bellwether avoided the hill. Some
cursed Elias. Some thanked him. Some did both. Memories returned
unevenly, bringing sorrow, tenderness, rage, and forgotten love.

Clara remained in the tower.

She buried Elias beneath the eastern face, where the broken
angel watched the dawn. On the grave, she placed no
clock. Only a stone carved with two names:

ELIAS VALE
FATHER OF ROSE
KEEPER, UNTIL TIME RETURNED

Then she began repairing the clocks.

Not to remove their hours, but to set them moving
again.

People came carrying the objects that had once held their
grief. Clara cleaned the gears, replaced the springs, and adjusted
the hands. When she finished, she asked each owner what
time it was.

They answered by looking outside.

The work took years.

The last clock was her own: the little wooden house
blackened by fire. Clara repaired the bird, polished the scorched
wood, and moved the hands from 8:26.

When she wound it, the mechanism hesitated.

Then it ticked.

A tiny red bird emerged and sang eight clear notes.

Clara placed the clock above the tower door. Beneath it,
she hung a new sign:

ENTER. TIME IS NOT SAFE, BUT NEITHER ARE WE.

By then, she was old. Children visited to hear stories
of the keeper, and travelers brought broken watches from distant
places. Clara fixed what she could. What she could not
fix, she taught people to carry.

Every morning at six, she climbed the hill above Bellwether
and unlocked the tower.

Inside, a thousand clocks told different times.

None told the whole truth.

Together, however, they made a sound like rain, like footsteps,
like hearts continuing after they have been broken.

And among them, always, one small clock sang at 8:26—not
for the hour that was lost, but for the minute
that came after.

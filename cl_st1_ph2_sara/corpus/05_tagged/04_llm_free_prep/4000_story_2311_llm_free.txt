# The Minutes We Kept

Every morning at six, the clock in Bellwether Square lost
one minute.

No one noticed at first. The clock had been old
when Mara Venn was a child, and Mara herself was
forty-three now. Its four faces were clouded with salt from
the sea, its iron hands orange with rust. In winter,
gulls nested behind the numerals. In summer, the tower smelled
of hot stone and feathers.

A minute was nothing to such a clock.

But Mara repaired watches for a living, and she knew
that a minute was never nothing.

Her shop stood across the square beneath a blue awning.
On its front window, painted in flaking gold, were the
words:

Venn & Daughter
Clocks Mended, Watches Cleaned, Time Kept

The "Daughter" referred to Mara, though her father had been
dead for eleven years. She had never changed the sign.
She liked to think the shop still belonged to them
both.

On the first Monday of October, Mara unlocked her door
at five fifty-eight. The square was dark except for the
lamps along the harbor road. She set her pocket watch
on the counter, wound it, and waited.

At six, the tower bell gave a single deep note.

Its minute hand jerked backward.

Mara stared.

The hand had pointed exactly to twelve. Now it rested
one mark before.

She checked her pocket watch. Six o'clock.

Across the square, the bakery chimney began to smoke. A
fishing cart rattled over the cobbles. Nothing else seemed changed.

Mara watched until six ten by her watch. The tower
showed six nine.

The next morning, she returned early.

Again, at the bell, the hand moved backward.

By Friday, the tower was five minutes slow.

Mara climbed the hill to the municipal offices and informed
Clerk Hollis, who was eating a boiled egg over a
stack of tax forms.

"The clock is losing one minute each day," she said.

Hollis sprinkled salt on his egg. "Old clocks lose time."

"Not like this."

"Then repair it."

"The council rejected my maintenance proposal in March."

"We had to replace the mayor's carriage wheels."

"The mayor lives three streets away."

"Cobblestones are damaging."

Mara folded her arms.

Hollis sighed and opened a ledger. "Submit another proposal."

"By the time you approve it, the clock will be
an hour slow."

"Then submit an urgent proposal."

"I did."

"Submit an urgent reminder."

So Mara crossed the square and went to see Ilyan
Ro, keeper of the tower keys.

Ilyan lived behind the bell tower in a narrow house
crowded with books, umbrellas, and empty birdcages. He was eighty-two
and claimed to have served as keeper since the reign
of three different kings, although the country had been a
republic for sixty years.

"The clock is losing time," Mara told him.

"Everything does," he said.

"One minute every morning."

"That seems modest."

"I need the key."

Ilyan regarded her with pale, amused eyes. "Your father once
asked me for it."

"For a repair?"

"For an investigation."

Mara felt a small coldness beneath her ribs. "What investigation?"

But Ilyan only shuffled to a cabinet, removed a key
as long as Mara's hand, and placed it on the
table.

"Take a lantern," he said. "And don't touch the bell
rope."

"Why?"

"It dislikes strangers."

Mara thought he was joking, but she took the lantern.

Inside, the tower was larger than it appeared. A spiral
stair wound around a hollow shaft filled with ropes and
chains. Dust lay thick on the steps. Names had been
carved into the stone: lovers, soldiers, bored apprentices, children who
had dared one another to climb after dark.

Halfway up, Mara found her father's initials.

She pressed two fingers to the carving. He had been
twenty-nine then, younger than she was now.

Above the clock faces lay the mechanism room. Bronze gears
turned in the lantern light. The pendulum swung with a
steady, solemn beat. Nothing looked broken. The escapement was worn
but functional; the weights hung true; the main spring showed
no crack.

Mara worked until noon. She cleaned each pivot, tested the
teeth, checked the beat against her pocket watch.

The mechanism kept perfect time.

Yet the clock face remained five minutes slow.

"That's impossible," she whispered.

Behind her, something clicked.

Not a gear. A latch.

Mara raised the lantern. Between two stone pillars was a
small iron door she had not noticed. It had no
handle, only a keyhole.

The tower key fit.

Beyond the door, a staircase descended.

Mara stood still. She had climbed nearly to the top
of the tower. There could not be another staircase going
down inside the wall. She leaned over the first step
and lifted the lantern.

The stairs continued farther than the light reached.

She thought of Ilyan's smile, of her father's initials, and
of all the things her father had chosen not to
tell her. Then she stepped through and pulled the door
shut behind her.

The air smelled of rain.

Down she went, counting the steps. At one hundred, she
should have been below the square. At two hundred, beneath
the harbor. At three hundred, the stone walls changed to
red brick. At four hundred and twelve, she reached the
bottom.

There was a room.

It was larger than the square above and full of
clocks.

Thousands covered the walls from floor to ceiling. Grandfather clocks
stood in rows. Carriage clocks ticked beneath glass domes. Tiny
watch movements glittered in open drawers. Sundials lay on tables
despite the absence of sun. Water clocks dripped steadily into
silver bowls.

Every clock showed a different hour.

At the center of the room sat a woman in
a gray dress, winding a red thread around a wooden
spool.

"You're late," the woman said.

"By how much?"

"Eleven years."

Mara tightened her grip on the lantern. "You knew my
father."

"Yes."

"Who are you?"

The woman smiled. She had an ordinary face, neither young
nor old, but her eyes were the deep blue of
evening after the lamps were lit.

"I keep what is misplaced," she said.

"What does that mean?"

Instead of answering, the woman pointed toward a shelf.

There stood a plain wooden clock with a crack across
its face.

Mara knew it at once. It had hung in her
childhood kitchen. Her father had repaired it a hundred times,
tapping its case whenever it stalled. After his death, Mara
had searched everywhere for it.

The clock's hands were stopped at twenty-three minutes past four.

The hour he had died.

Mara approached it slowly. "How did this get here?"

"He brought it."

"When?"

"Eleven years ago."

The woman's red thread slipped around the spool, loop after
loop.

Mara remembered that day: rain striking the shop window, a
cup of tea cooling beside her father's workbench, his empty
chair. He had gone out after lunch, saying he needed
to inspect the tower. At four twenty-three, the bell had
rung without being pulled. They found him on the upper
stair, one hand closed around his pocket watch.

"What happened to him?" Mara asked.

"He tried to steal an hour."

Mara laughed once, sharply. "My father never stole anything."

"He wanted it for you."

The clocks around them ticked like soft rain.

The woman explained.

Time, she said, did not pass as cleanly as people
imagined. It frayed. It pooled in empty rooms. It caught
on certain objects: a wedding ring, an unread letter, a
child's shoe kept in a drawer. Most lost moments dissolved.
Some remained.

For centuries, the Bellwether clock had gathered those remnants.

A second from a last kiss. Ten minutes from a
train delay that saved a life. An afternoon forgotten by
everyone who lived it. Years left unused by those who
died young.

The keeper below the tower stored them.

"Your father came because you were ill," the woman said.

Mara remembered fever, white sheets, the doctor speaking quietly in
the hall. She had been thirty-two. She remembered waking after
five days and finding her father asleep with his head
beside her hand.

"He believed you would die before dawn," the woman continued.
"He asked me for an hour."

"And you gave it to him?"

"No. I told him time given must be time taken."

"Taken from whom?"

"From the one who asks."

Mara looked at the cracked kitchen clock.

"He traded his life for mine."

"Not his life. An hour."

"That makes no sense."

"Life rarely does when measured from inside it."

The woman placed the full spool of red thread on
the table. "Your father gave you his last hour. It
was enough for the fever to break. But he used
a tool meant for memory, not flesh. The exchange damaged
the tower. Since then, it has been trying to reclaim
the debt."

"One minute a day?"

"It began yesterday."

"It began five days ago."

"Above, yes. Here, yesterday lasts longer."

Mara glanced at the surrounding clocks. Some hands raced; some
crept; some shivered in place.

"What happens when the debt is paid?"

"The tower will stop."

"And then?"

"Bellwether will begin to lose more than minutes."

Mara imagined Clerk Hollis forgetting tax figures, fishermen forgetting tides,
mothers looking at their children with puzzled eyes.

"What kind of loss?"

"At first, small things. The names of streets. The taste
of pears. Which key fits which lock. Later, faces. Promises.
Grief. Love."

"You expect me to believe a clock holds the town's
memories?"

"No. The town holds its memories. The clock reminds them
to stay."

Mara thought of the sign above her shop, still bearing
her father's name. She had kept it because memory required
maintenance, like any delicate mechanism. Leave it untended, and dust
entered. Oil dried. Teeth wore down.

"How much does the tower want?"

"Your father took sixty minutes. Five have been returned."

"Then we have fifty-five days."

"Perhaps."

"Perhaps?"

"The clock may grow impatient."

A bell rang somewhere in the room. It was a
small, bright sound.

One of the wall clocks opened like a door. For
an instant, Mara saw a sunlit garden and a boy
running through tall grass. Then the clock closed.

"What do I do?" she asked.

"Return the hour."

"How?"

"You may give back the hour your father bought."

Mara understood before the woman finished.

"I would die."

"You would become as you were at four twenty-three that
afternoon."

"Sick?"

"Dying."

Mara looked again at the kitchen clock. Eleven years of
her life had followed that frozen minute. Eleven years of
opening the shop each morning, mending strangers' watches, walking beside
the sea. Eleven winters. Eleven springs. Small joys she had
not considered miraculous because they belonged to ordinary days.

Her father had purchased them with an hour.

"No," she said.

The woman nodded as if Mara had given the expected
answer.

"There is another way?"

"There is always another way. It is simply less convenient."

"What is it?"

"Replace stolen time with willingly surrendered time. Sixty people may
each give one minute. Six may give ten. One may
give the whole hour."

"And what happens to them?"

"They lose the chosen duration."

"From the end of their lives?"

"No. From their memories."

Mara frowned.

"They must surrender a moment they have lived," the keeper
said. "That moment will vanish from them completely. A true
minute—not one spent waiting, sleeping, or staring at a wall.
It must be a minute they would choose to keep."

Mara thought this crueler than death.

The keeper handed her the spool of red thread.

"You have until the clock reaches six again."

"It reaches six every day."

"Not this six."

The woman pointed upward.

Far above, through brick and stone, the tower bell began
to toll.

Mara climbed.

When she emerged, it was evening. The square lamps were
lit, though she had entered before noon. The tower showed
six minutes to six.

Her pocket watch had stopped.

She went first to Ilyan.

He listened without interruption. When she finished, he opened one
of his empty birdcages.

Inside was a yellow feather.

"My wife kept canaries," he said. "She sang while she
fed them. There was one morning, not long before she
died, when sunlight filled this room, and she laughed because
a bird had landed on my head."

"You would give that up?"

"No." He shut the cage. "I will give the minute
before it, when she came through the door carrying the
seed."

"The minute must matter."

"It matters because I knew what came next."

Mara tied one end of the red thread around his
finger.

He closed his eyes.

The thread glowed, briefly warm. When Mara unwound it, a
single black mark had appeared on the spool.

Ilyan looked at the cage.

"Did my wife keep birds?" he asked.

Mara swallowed. "Yes."

He touched the yellow feather, smiling uncertainly. "That sounds like
her."

They went together to the square.

Mara rang the handbell from her shop and called the
town to gather. People came grudgingly at first: bakers dusted
with flour, sailors smelling of rope and tar, children in
nightclothes, Clerk Hollis still carrying a ledger.

Mara told them the truth.

As expected, several laughed.

The mayor accused her of manufacturing a crisis to obtain
the tower maintenance contract. A fisherman suggested that if memories
could be collected, Hollis should donate the municipal tax code.
Hollis replied that the fisherman had never remembered it in
the first place.

Then the tower clock moved backward.

Not one minute.

Two.

The laughter stopped.

From the harbor came a crash as a boatman forgot
which rope held his mooring line.

An old woman screamed. She could no longer remember the
name of the man whose portrait she wore in a
locket.

Mara raised the spool.

"I need fifty-nine minutes," she said.

Silence settled across the square.

Then a baker named Fen stepped forward. "Take the minute
my daughter first called me Papa."

His daughter seized his arm. "No."

"I was there for everything after," he told her.

Mara tied the thread around his finger.

A sailor gave the moment he first saw Bellwether from
the sea.

A widow gave the final minute of a dance at
her wedding.

Clerk Hollis surrendered the instant he learned he had passed
the civil examination. Afterward, he looked down at his ledger
and muttered that he had never understood why he worked
for the council.

One by one, the marks appeared.

Seven minutes.

Fourteen.

Twenty-three.

Some volunteers wept before giving. Some wept after, sensing the
shape of an absence they could not name. A little
girl offered the minute she found her lost dog, but
Mara refused her. The child had fewer memories than the
adults, she said, and should be allowed to gather more
before she began giving them away.

At forty-one minutes, no one else stepped forward.

The tower showed one minute to six.

"We need eighteen," Mara said.

The crowd lowered its eyes.

She could not blame them. Every person possessed memories they
wished to lose: shame, pain, betrayal, funerals. Those were easy
to offer and therefore worthless to the clock. It demanded
joy. It demanded treasure.

The minute hand trembled.

Mara tied the thread around her own finger.

She chose an afternoon in the shop when she was
nine. Rain had drummed on the blue awning. Her father
had placed a broken watch in her palm and guided
her hands as she fitted a wheel no larger than
an eyelash.

"Listen," he had whispered.

"To what?"

"The moment it decides to live again."

The watch had begun ticking.

Her father's face had shone with pride.

Mara held that minute in her mind. She felt its
edges, its warmth, the weight of the watch, the smell
of tea and metal.

Then she pulled the thread tight.

Something opened inside her.

The memory went out.

She staggered. The spool showed forty-two marks.

"I gave one," she said. "Why only one?"

Ilyan steadied her. "How many did you mean to give?"

"All eighteen."

"A memory is measured by the life inside it, not
by the years surrounding it."

The clock began to strike six.

The first bell shook the square.

Windows rattled. Pigeons rose like smoke.

Mara ran into the tower.

She climbed as the second bell sounded, then the third.
Each note seemed to erase something. She forgot the color
of her childhood door. She forgot the first watch she
had repaired. On the sixth strike, she forgot the route
to the shop where she had worked every day of
her life.

At the mechanism room, she threw open the iron door
and descended.

The staircase had only thirteen steps now.

The keeper waited beside the cracked kitchen clock.

"You have forty-two minutes," she said.

"Take the rest from me."

"I cannot choose them."

"Then tell me how."

"Wind the clock."

Mara approached the kitchen clock. A key protruded from its
wooden case.

"What will it take?"

"What you are willing to turn."

Behind her, the tower bell struck again.

Mara grasped the key.

She remembered her father in fragments: his sleeves rolled above
the elbows, his habit of humming off-key, the burn scar
on his thumb. She remembered him carrying her on his
shoulders along the harbor wall. Teaching her to read Roman
numerals. Sleeping beside her bed while fever burned through the
night.

She turned the key.

Once.

The memory of the harbor wall vanished.

Twice.

His humming disappeared.

Three times.

She lost his voice.

Each turn wound the clock and unwound her father from
her mind.

Mara kept turning.

She forgot his favorite coat. His handwriting. The way he
drank tea. She forgot holding his hand at her mother's
funeral. She forgot the last argument they had, and the
apology after. She forgot the pride on his face in
a rain-darkened shop.

At the eighteenth turn, the clock began to tick.

The sound was small but certain.

The keeper removed the spool from Mara's hand. Sixty black
marks circled the red thread.

Above them, the bell fell silent.

The cracked clock's minute hand moved forward.

Four twenty-four.

Mara stared at it through tears she did not understand.

"Who owned this?" she asked.

The keeper's blue eyes softened. "Someone who loved you."

When Mara returned to the square, dawn was rising.

The tower clock showed six precisely.

People stood quietly together, touching the gaps in their memories.
Fen's daughter embraced him and told him, again and again,
the story of her first word. The sailor faced the
harbor as though seeing his home for the first time.
Ilyan held a yellow feather and listened while neighbors described
his wife's birds.

Mara crossed to the shop but stopped beneath the sign.

Venn & Daughter.

She read the words several times.

"Who was Venn?" she asked.

Ilyan came beside her.

"You are."

"And?"

He looked up at the faded lettering. "Your father."

Mara searched her mind and found an empty room.

"Was he kind?"

"Usually."

"Was he a good clockmaker?"

"Annoyingly good."

"Did he love me?"

Ilyan offered her the tower key.

"With all the time he had."

Years later, when children asked why the tower clock was
never wrong, Mara took them up the spiral stairs. She
showed them the gears and weights, taught them how each
tooth carried the next, how the pendulum divided eternity into
equal pieces.

Sometimes they asked about the initials carved into the wall.

Mara could not tell them whose they were.

So she told them a story instead: that long ago,
someone had climbed the tower to save the town; that
sixty people had each surrendered something precious; that time was
not made of hours or minutes, but of what people
chose to keep and what they were willing to give
away.

The children always wanted to see the secret staircase.

Mara would smile and say there was no such thing.

Yet every morning, before opening her shop, she climbed alone
to the mechanism room. Behind two stone pillars, an iron
door waited without a handle.

She never opened it.

She only placed her palm against the cold metal and
listened.

From somewhere far below came the ticking of a plain
wooden clock.

And though she did not know why, Mara always whispered
the same words.

"I'm here."

Then the tower bell rang, the square awakened, and all
across Bellwether, people remembered who they were.

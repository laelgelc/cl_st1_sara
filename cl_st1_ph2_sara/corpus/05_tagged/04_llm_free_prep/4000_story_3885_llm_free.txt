## The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

On the morning the sea returned the red boat, Mara
Vale was standing at the top of the lighthouse with
a cup of cold tea in her hands.

The boat drifted through the mist without sail or oarsman.
Its hull was scratched, its mast broken, and one of
its brass fittings flashed whenever the weak sun found it.
Mara watched it turn in the tide below, circling the
black rocks as though searching for a place to land.

She had not seen that boat in twenty years.

Her father had painted it red during the last summer
of her childhood. He had called it *The Merry Bell*,
though it had no bell and had never been particularly
merry. It was the boat in which he had disappeared.

Mara set down her tea.

By the time she reached the shore, the tide had
pushed the boat onto a strip of gray sand. It
looked older than memory but less ruined than it should
have been. Barnacles coated one side, and long ribbons of
seaweed hung from the stern. Inside lay a rusted lantern,
a coil of rope, and a wooden box no larger
than a book.

Mara stood at the water's edge, unable to step closer.

Behind her, the lighthouse rose from the island like a
white bone. Beyond it were the keeper's cottage, a wind-bent
ash tree, and the narrow pier where the supply boat
came every third Thursday. Nothing else disturbed the sweep of
grass and stone.

No one had lived on the island with Mara since
her mother died eight years before. There had been offers
to replace the old beacon with an electric mechanism operated
from the mainland, but storms delayed the work, money disappeared,
and officials changed. Mara remained. Every evening she climbed the
iron stairs and lit the lamp, just as her father
had done.

The sea, in return, gave her silence.

Until now.

She pulled the boat above the tide line. The wood
groaned under her hands, but it held. Then she took
out the box.

Her name was carved into the lid.

Inside was a letter wrapped in oilskin.

Mara recognized her father's handwriting before she opened it. The
letters leaned forward as if resisting a strong wind.

*My dearest Mara,*

*If the boat reaches you, then I did not.*

She stopped reading.

The mist moved around her, softening the rocks and erasing
the horizon. Somewhere offshore, a gull cried once.

Mara folded the letter, returned it to the box, and
carried it to the cottage.

For twenty years she had imagined what she would say
if her father came home. At twelve, she would have
demanded an apology. At sixteen, she would have pretended not
to care. At twenty-five, after her mother's illness began, she
would have asked him to stay for only one night
so she could sleep.

At thirty-two, she had stopped imagining.

Now his letter lay on the kitchen table.

She walked around it while doing useless tasks. She cleaned
the stove, sharpened a knife, swept sand from the floor,
and rearranged jars on a shelf. The letter waited.

At noon, she opened it again.

*My dearest Mara,*

*If the boat reaches you, then I did not. I
am sorry for leaving without farewell, but I believed I
would be gone only until morning. You will hear stories.
Some will say I was caught in a storm. Others
will say I ran away. Neither will be true.*

*There is a second light beyond North Point.*

Mara looked toward the window.

North Point lay three miles across the island, where cliffs
dropped into deep water. There was no second light. She
had walked there hundreds of times.

*You may have seen it on winter nights,* the letter
continued, *low over the water, where no ship could safely
pass. Your mother saw it first. I told her it
was a star, but she knew I was lying.*

*The light appears every nine years. Your grandfather called it
the Drowned Lantern. He warned me never to follow it.
On the night I left, I saw a boat sailing
toward it. I thought there were people aboard. I thought
I heard a child calling.*

*I took the red boat and followed.*

The next lines were blurred by water. Mara tilted the
page.

*The light is not a lighthouse. It is a door.*

She almost laughed, but the sound caught in her throat.

Her father had been practical to the point of irritation.
He trusted ropes, weather charts, and well-made boots. He had
once refused to attend a midsummer festival because the fishermen
planned to launch candles in paper boats, which he called
"a waste of good fire."

Yet here was his hand describing a door in the
sea.

*I do not know where it leads. I only know
that time moves differently beyond it. I was there for
three days. When I returned to the place where I
had entered, the island was gone. I saw no lighthouse
and no shore, only empty water. I have tried to
find you ever since.*

*The light will appear again on the first night of
the black moon. If this letter reaches you before then,
do not follow it. Whatever you hear, whatever you see,
remain on land.*

*Some doors open both ways.*

The letter ended there. No farewell. No signature.

Mara read it three times.

The date at the top was six months old.

She took an almanac from the shelf. The next black
moon was that night.

By sunset, clouds had covered the island. Mara lit the
lighthouse lamp early and checked the fuel twice. The great
lens turned, casting its beam across the gathering dark. Every
rotation sent a pale road over the sea, then pulled
it away.

She carried her father's letter in her coat pocket.

At eight, rain began. At nine, the wind struck the
cottage hard enough to rattle the shutters. At ten, Mara
climbed the tower and stood beside the lens.

The sea was invisible except when the beam passed over
it. Each glimpse revealed white water and leaning rain.

At half past ten, the lighthouse went dark.

Mara seized the emergency lantern. The main flame had not
failed. It still burned, blue and gold, but the lens
no longer caught it. Instead, the glass had clouded from
within.

Then she saw a light beyond North Point.

It was small and amber, hanging low above the waves.

A bell rang.

Not from the tower. Not from the island.

From the sea.

Mara pressed both hands against the cold glass. The amber
light moved closer. Beneath it, a dark shape rose and
fell.

A boat.

The bell rang again, and through the storm came a
voice.

"Mara!"

Her heart struck against her ribs.

The voice was her father's.

"Mara, bring the lantern!"

She stepped toward the stairs.

Then she remembered the letter.

*Whatever you hear, whatever you see, remain on land.*

The voice came again, clearer.

"Mara! Please!"

She had forgotten its warmth. That was the cruelest part.
Memory had reduced her father to scattered images: his boots
by the stove, his hands smelling of tar, his shadow
crossing the ceiling when he carried her to bed. She
remembered phrases he used, but not the exact music of
his speech.

Now she heard it.

She ran down the tower.

For a moment, she told herself she was only going
to secure the cottage. But she passed the cottage without
stopping and followed the path toward North Point.

Rain blinded her. The emergency lantern swung in her grip.
Its yellow circle leaped over grass, stones, and pools of
black water.

At the cliffs, she stopped.

The amber light floated a hundred yards offshore. Beneath it
was *The Merry Bell*, whole and newly painted, its mast
straight, its brass fittings bright.

A man stood inside.

Mara knew him, though he was younger than she was
now.

Her father raised one hand.

She could not breathe.

"Mara," he called. "I found the way back."

She moved to the edge. Waves smashed against the rocks
below, but between the island and the boat stretched a
path of still water.

Her father pointed toward the lantern in her hand.

"Bring me home."

She took one step down the cliff path.

The letter crackled in her pocket.

*Some doors open both ways.*

Mara looked behind her. The lighthouse had vanished. In its
place stood a dark tower with broken windows. The island
grass had become pale reeds, waving beneath a sky without
stars.

She turned back.

The man in the boat was no longer alone.

Figures stood behind him, packed shoulder to shoulder. Their clothes
belonged to different centuries. A woman in a silver gown.
A sailor with a rope around his neck. A boy
holding a wooden horse. Their faces were gray and swollen.

Her father still looked alive.

"Do not look at them," he said.

"Who are they?"

"People who lost their way."

The still path stretched to her boots. It looked solid,
though water moved beneath its surface.

"You sent the letter," Mara said.

His expression changed.

"Letter?"

She took it from her pocket. The rain had soaked
the page, but the ink remained.

Her father stared at it.

Then he looked over his shoulder toward the amber light.

"I did not send that."

The bell rang a third time.

The figures in the boat lifted their heads together.

Mara's father gripped the sides of the vessel. "Go back."

The still water reached around her ankles.

"Father—"

"Go back now!"

The figures opened their mouths.

The sound that came out was not a scream but
a hundred familiar voices. Her mother called from the cottage.
Friends long dead called her childhood name. She heard her
own voice as a little girl, sobbing for someone to
return.

The amber light widened.

Inside it, Mara saw a shore beneath a golden sky.
Her mother stood there, healthy and smiling. Beside her waited
her father, older now, his hair gray. A house stood
behind them with smoke rising from its chimney.

It was everything the years had taken from her.

"Mara," her mother said. "You have been alone long enough."

Mara stepped onto the water.

It held her.

Her father in the boat shouted, but the other voices
drowned him out. Warmth flowed from the amber doorway. The
rain no longer touched her face.

She took another step.

On the golden shore, her mother held out her arms.

Then Mara remembered the day her mother died.

There had been no peaceful final sentence, no perfect wisdom.
Her mother had been feverish, frightened, and furious. She had
gripped Mara's wrist and said, "Don't make a shrine of
your sorrow. Promise me."

Mara had promised.

The woman on the golden shore continued smiling.

Her real mother would have been angry that she had
forgotten.

Mara lifted the emergency lantern and hurled it at the
amber light.

The glass shattered.

Flame spread across the still water.

The golden shore collapsed like a reflection struck by a
stone. The waiting house bent sideways. The false mother's smile
opened too wide. Behind her face was a darkness deeper
than the sea.

The figures in the boat began to wail.

All except Mara's father.

He laughed.

It was a sudden, delighted sound, the laugh of a
man whose daughter had surprised him.

"Well done!" he shouted.

Fire raced toward the boat. The amber light folded inward,
dragging wind, rain, and waves with it. The dead passengers
reached for Mara's father, but he tore the mast loose
and swung it across the boat, knocking them back.

"Mara!" he called.

She crouched at the edge of the cliff path and
stretched out her hand, though the boat was too far
away.

He reached for her anyway.

For one instant, the years between them seemed no wider
than a doorway.

"I'm sorry," he said.

The amber light shrank behind him.

Mara's fingers closed on empty air.

The light vanished.

Darkness crashed over the sea.

She woke near dawn on the grass above North Point.
The storm had passed. Her coat was stiff with salt,
and one hand was burned. The letter lay beside her,
its ink washed away.

Far below, pieces of red wood floated among the rocks.

Mara remained there until the sun rose.

Then she returned to the lighthouse.

The lens was clear again, though the main flame had
gone out. She cleaned the mechanism, trimmed the wick, and
opened the tower windows to let in the morning air.

At noon, she saw a ship approaching from the mainland.
It was not the usual supply boat but a government
vessel with a steel hull. Three officials came ashore carrying
tools, papers, and the polished parts of an electric beacon.

The eldest official apologized for the lack of notice.

"We can complete the installation in two days," he said.
"After that, the station will operate automatically. You'll be free
to leave."

Free.

The word sounded strange.

Mara looked up at the tower. For twenty years she
had believed she remained because someone had to keep the
light. Only then did she understand that she had also
been waiting—for a boat, an apology, a return that could
restore the life she had lost.

The sea had returned all it could.

"When is the next passage to the mainland?" she asked.

"Friday morning."

"I'll be ready."

That evening, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs one last time
to light the old lamp.

The horizon was clear. Fishing boats dotted the western water,
and the first stars appeared above them. The beam turned
steadily, touching each vessel for a moment before moving on.

On the desk beneath the window, Mara found a wooden
box.

She was certain it had not been there before.

Her name was carved into the lid.

Inside lay a dry sheet of paper covered in her
father's handwriting.

*My dearest Mara,*

*You were always braver than I knew.*

*I cannot come home, but now I think I can
leave this place. Do not wait for another letter. Do
not wait for another light.*

*There are more shores in the world than the ones
we lose.*

*Go find yours.*

This time, he had signed his name.

Mara carried the letter outside and stood beneath the turning
beam. She considered keeping it safe in the cottage, locked
away from salt and fire.

Instead, she folded it into the shape of a small
boat.

At the cliff's edge, she set it on the wind.

The paper boat lifted rather than fell. It circled the
lighthouse once, bright in the beam, and sailed out over
the darkening sea.

Mara watched until it became indistinguishable from a star.

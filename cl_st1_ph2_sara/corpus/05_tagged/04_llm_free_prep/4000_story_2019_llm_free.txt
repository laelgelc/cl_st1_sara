Mara Venn knew the lighthouse was lying because the sea
had been gone for thirty-two years.

Yet every night, precisely at midnight, the great glass lens
began to turn. Its beam swept over the empty basin
where fishing boats had once risen and fallen, crossed miles
of pale salt, and vanished against the dark horizon. A
moment later, the foghorn sounded.

One long note. Two short.

The old signal for a ship in danger.

No one in Bellweather paid much attention anymore. The town
had learned to sleep through the noise, just as people
near railways learned to sleep through passing trains. But Mara
was the lighthouse keeper, and she could not ignore a
call made by the building in her care.

She lived alone in the keeper's cottage beneath the tower.
Each morning she climbed the spiral stairs, polished the lens,
checked the gears, and inspected the lamp. The mechanism was
simple, ancient, and impossible.

There was no fuel in the reservoir.

The clockwork had rusted solid.

The electrical cable had been cut before Mara was born.

Still, every midnight the lamp burned blue-white, the lens revolved,
and the foghorn called into the desert.

One long. Two short.

On the first Monday of autumn, something answered.

Mara was awake at her kitchen table, repairing the binding
of an old logbook, when a low sound came through
the open window. At first she mistook it for wind
moving around the cottage. Then it came again.

A horn, distant but unmistakable.

Three short notes.

Mara's needle stopped halfway through the leather.

She counted the seconds.

The lighthouse answered with one long note and two short.

From the darkness beyond the town came three more.

She pushed back her chair and stepped outside.

Bellweather lay quiet below the ridge. Its roofs were silver
under the moon. Beyond them stretched the seabed, a vast
white plain crossed by the black ribs of shipwrecks. Roads
had been built over part of it, and salt farmers
had dug square pools near the old harbor, but farther
out there was nothing except cracked earth and the abandoned
navigation towers.

A blue light flickered at the horizon.

Then it disappeared.

By morning, Mara had convinced herself she had imagined it.

By noon, three people had come to the lighthouse to
ask what she intended to do.

The first was Mayor Vale, who arrived in a polished
black carriage and climbed the hill with a handkerchief pressed
to his mouth. He disliked dust, birds, old buildings, and
all matters that could not be settled by committee.

"People are concerned," he said.

"People heard a horn," Mara replied.

"Exactly. They're saying it came from the basin."

"It did."

The mayor blinked. "You shouldn't confirm rumors."

"You asked."

"I expected reassurance."

"I can reassure you that it was definitely a horn."

Mayor Vale lowered his handkerchief. "The Founding Festival is in
six days. We have merchants coming from four provinces. If
visitors hear talk of ghost ships—"

"No one said anything about ghosts."

"Someone always does."

The second visitor was Tomas Reed, the town mechanic and
Mara's oldest friend. He brought a toolbox, two coils of
wire, and a loaf of sweet bread.

"You look tired," he said.

"You look intrusive."

"I brought breakfast."

"You may intrude."

Together they climbed the tower. Tomas dismantled the foghorn controls
while Mara inspected the lamp. They worked in silence until
the sun stood high over the dry basin.

"No pressure in the pipes," Tomas said at last. "No
power either. There's no reason this horn should make a
sound."

"It does."

"I know. I hear it every night."

"The question is why."

"No," he said, wiping grease from his fingers. "The question
is whether knowing why will improve our lives."

"That is a coward's approach to machinery."

"It's a mechanic's approach to cursed machinery."

The third visitor arrived just before dusk.

He was a boy of perhaps twelve, narrow-faced, sunburned, and
barefoot. Mara recognized him as Ilyan Or, whose family operated
the water station beside the southern road. He carried an
object wrapped in a red scarf.

"I found this on the salt," he said.

Inside the scarf was a dead bird.

Its wings were dark gray, its breast white, and its
long beak hooked at the tip. Mara had seen such
a bird only in illustrated books.

"A shearwater," she whispered.

"It was wet when I found it."

Mara touched its feathers. Her fingers came away smelling of
brine.

"Where?"

"Past Marker Nine. Near the wreck of the Saint Lume."

That was twelve miles into the basin.

"Did you see anything else?"

Ilyan hesitated. "Lights."

"What kind?"

"Green ones. In a row. Like windows."

Mara looked toward the horizon. The sky was clear and
enormous. Nothing moved on the salt.

"Don't tell the mayor," she said.

"I already did."

"Of course you did."

That night half the town gathered near the old harbor.

Some brought lanterns. Others carried blankets, food, or bottles of
festival wine. Mayor Vale had ordered everyone to stay home,
which guaranteed a large attendance. Children climbed the skeletons of
old piers. Elderly fishermen sat in folding chairs and stared
across the basin with expressions Mara could not read.

At midnight, the lighthouse awakened.

Its beam swept over the crowd. The foghorn gave one
long note, then two short.

Silence followed.

A minute passed.

Then, from beyond the darkness, came three short blasts.

The crowd stirred.

Mara stood at the edge of the old quay with
Tomas and Ilyan. Through her brass telescope, she searched the
horizon.

There.

A green point.

Then another, and another.

They appeared one by one, evenly spaced and low to
the ground.

"Windows," Ilyan said.

Mara adjusted the focus. Behind the lights she saw a
shape darker than the night, tall at one end and
sloping at the other. Two masts rose above it. Between
them hung the ragged outline of sails.

The impossible vessel moved slowly over the dry sea.

A woman in the crowd began to pray. Someone else
laughed too loudly. Mayor Vale shouted for calm, though no
one had panicked until he mentioned the possibility.

The ship's horn sounded again.

Three short notes.

"What does that mean?" Ilyan asked.

Mara knew the old signal codes from the lighthouse records.

"It means the vessel is moving backward."

"Why would it do that?"

She watched the green lights tremble above the salt.

"Maybe it isn't moving backward," she said. "Maybe it's moving
back."

The ship stopped before dawn, still several miles from town.
When the sun rose, it was gone.

In its place stood a line of wet footprints.

They began near the wreck of the Saint Lume and
ended at the lighthouse door.

Mara found them when she returned home. The prints were
bare, small, and filled with seawater. They climbed the hill,
crossed the threshold, and continued up the spiral staircase.

At the top of the tower, a girl was sitting
inside the great lens.

She appeared to be eight or nine years old. Her
dress was blue, old-fashioned, and dripping wet. Black hair clung
to her face. She watched Mara through the curved glass.

"You took a long time," the girl said.

Mara gripped the stair rail.

"Who are you?"

"Nell."

"How did you get in there?"

"Through the door."

"There is no door."

"There was when I used it."

Mara stepped closer. The lens broke the girl's face into
a dozen overlapping expressions.

"Are you alive?"

Nell considered the question. "I'm cold."

Mara removed her coat and tried to pass it between
the glass panels, but her hand met a solid surface.
The lens had no opening large enough for a child.

"Is the ship yours?" Mara asked.

"It belongs to Captain Saye."

"What does he want?"

"To come home."

"Why?"

"Because we left."

The girl said this as if it explained everything.

Below, the town bells began to ring. Their uneven clamor
meant the mayor had called an emergency assembly.

Mara sat on the metal platform beside the lens.

"When did your ship leave Bellweather?"

"Yesterday."

"What was the date?"

"October the fourteenth."

"What year?"

Nell gave the year.

Mara looked down at her hands.

It was the year the sea vanished.

Every child in Bellweather knew the story. On the morning
of October fifteenth, thirty-two years ago, people had awakened to
find the harbor empty. The water had withdrawn beyond the
horizon without a storm, an earthquake, or a warning. Hundreds
of vessels had settled onto the exposed seabed. Wells turned
bitter. Rain stopped for seven years.

But some ships had been at sea that night. They
disappeared with the water.

The Mercy Bell had been one of them.

According to the lighthouse register, she had carried forty-three passengers,
seventeen crew members, and a locked iron chest belonging to
the Provincial Observatory. Her captain, Orrin Saye, had signaled the
lighthouse shortly before midnight.

Vessel moving backward.

Those were the final words ever recorded from the sea.

Mara looked at Nell. "What happened after you left?"

"We sailed west for three days. Then the stars changed."

"Changed how?"

"They began falling upward."

Nell pressed both palms to the inside of the glass.

"The captain turned us around, but the wind came from
every direction. The clocks stopped. We saw Bellweather's light, always
far away. We sailed toward it for weeks. Or years.
No one grew older, but everyone dreamed of growing old."

"And now?"

"Now the light is close."

"Why can't you come ashore?"

"Because there's no water."

A cold drop struck Mara's cheek. She touched it and
tasted salt.

Nell was crying on the other side of the lens.

"The captain says the lighthouse took the sea," the girl
whispered. "He says it must give it back."

At the emergency assembly, Mayor Vale stood beneath a banner
that read THIRTY-SECOND ANNUAL FOUNDING FESTIVAL: TOMORROW BEGINS HERE.

He objected when Mara asked everyone to move the meeting
to the lighthouse archive.

"The hall is traditional," he said.

"The records are in the archive."

"Bring them here."

"There are four thousand volumes."

Mayor Vale gazed at her as if she had acquired
the volumes specifically to inconvenience him. Eventually, the council and
several dozen residents climbed the hill.

Mara showed them the final register from the night the
sea disappeared. The last entry had been written by her
predecessor, Keeper Arven.

11:47—Mercy Bell sighted west by southwest.
11:52—Vessel signals loss of navigation.
11:56—Observatory requests activation of experimental lamp.
11:58—Lamp ignited. Unusual refraction.
12:00—Sea no longer visible.

Below the entry, in different ink, Arven had added a
single sentence:

May God forgive us for mistaking distance for darkness.

"The experimental lamp," Tomas said. "That's the light burning now."

Mara nodded. "The iron chest aboard the Mercy Bell carried
a lens made by the Observatory. Its twin was installed
here."

"For what purpose?" asked an elderly councilwoman.

"To guide ships through fog by bending light around the
curvature of the world."

Mayor Vale frowned. "That sounds useful."

"It may have bent more than light."

Silence settled over the archive.

Mara told them about Nell. Some believed her immediately. Others
climbed the tower and returned pale, having heard the girl's
voice even though they could not see her. Tomas saw
only seawater suspended inside the lens. Ilyan saw Nell clearly.
Mayor Vale refused to look.

"If the ship needs water," Tomas said, "we cannot help.
There isn't enough in the province to float a rowboat."

"The lighthouse can return it," Mara said.

"You don't know that."

"No. But Keeper Arven did."

She opened another book, a private journal discovered behind the
register shelf. Its final pages contained diagrams of the lamp.
Two circles overlapped: one marked HERE, the other ELSEWHERE. Between
them, Arven had drawn the lighthouse beam.

"The light is an anchor," Mara said. "It's been holding
the Mercy Bell close to us for thirty-two years."

"Then extinguish it," said the councilwoman.

"If I extinguish it, the ship may be lost forever.
The sea may remain wherever it went."

"And if you reverse it?"

Mara looked at the final diagram.

"Then whatever is elsewhere will return here."

Mayor Vale finally spoke. "How much water are we discussing?"

"All of it."

Bellweather had been built on cliffs above the old harbor,
but in the decades since the disappearance, houses, workshops, farms,
and roads had spread across the seabed. Nearly a thousand
people now lived below the former tide line.

The festival was canceled.

Evacuation began that afternoon.

Many residents protested. Some refused to abandon homes they had
spent their lives building. Mara walked from door to door
with Ilyan, marking the old high-water line in blue chalk.
She expected arguments and received them. She also received keys,
names of missing neighbors, crates of chickens, bundles of legal
papers, and a baby thrust into her arms while its
mother dismantled a stove.

By sunset, a long procession climbed from the basin toward
the cliffs.

Tomas organized wagons. Mayor Vale, to everyone's surprise, proved excellent
in a crisis once the crisis became too large to
deny. He opened the festival storehouses, commandeered merchant tents for
shelter, and personally carried an old man up the harbor
stairs.

At the lighthouse, Nell was fading.

Through the lens, Mara could now see other figures behind
her: sailors with hollow cheeks, passengers wrapped in blankets, children
holding hands. They stood on the deck of the Mercy
Bell while an unnatural sky turned above them. Stars streamed
like rain toward a black sun.

Captain Saye appeared last.

He was tall, broad-shouldered, and gray with exhaustion. Though only
a pane of glass separated him from Mara, his voice
came from far away.

"You are the keeper?"

"I am."

"Can you reverse the lamp?"

"I think so."

"Thinking sank many ships."

"Did certainty save yours?"

The captain smiled faintly. "No."

He placed one hand against the lens.

"When the passage opens, the sea will return quickly."

"How quickly?"

"Do you remember waves?"

"I was born after."

"Then there is no answer I can give that will
mean enough."

The mechanism had to be reversed at midnight, during the
brief interval when the two lenses—one in the lighthouse and
one aboard the ship—aligned. The process required releasing the main
gear, rotating the lens by hand, and holding it against
its natural direction until the beam completed a full circle.

Tomas studied Arven's diagrams.

"The force could crush you," he said.

"Then help me."

"I intend to. I only want you properly afraid."

"I'm terrified."

"Good. That saves time."

At eleven forty, rain began.

No one in Bellweather had seen true rain for years.
People emerged from the tents and shelters along the cliffs,
lifting their faces. At first the drops vanished into the
dust. Then the ground darkened. Water ran from roofs, filled
gutters, and spilled down the old harbor steps.

At eleven fifty, the Mercy Bell appeared.

She was closer than before, her hull floating a few
feet above the salt. Green lamps burned along her deck.
Her sails billowed with wind from another world.

At eleven fifty-six, the lighthouse beam changed from white to
blue.

Mara and Tomas stood on opposite sides of the main
gear. Ilyan waited below beside the brake lever.

"On my signal," Mara called.

The clock hands crawled toward midnight.

Inside the lens, Nell watched her.

"What will happen to you?" Mara asked.

The girl smiled. "Tomorrow."

The clock struck twelve.

The foghorn sounded one long note and two short.

The Mercy Bell answered.

Three short notes.

"Now!" Mara shouted.

Ilyan pulled the brake.

The tower groaned. The lens stopped so violently that several
glass panels cracked. Mara and Tomas threw their weight against
the gear handles. For a moment nothing moved.

Then the mechanism turned backward.

The light swept east instead of west.

Where it crossed the basin, salt rose into the air
like smoke. The cracked earth folded in shining lines. A
sound came from below—not thunder, not wind, but something vaster,
a roar felt in bone before it reached the ear.

On the horizon, a wall appeared.

Mara thought it was cloud until lightning flashed within it
and revealed fish, wreckage, and the silver undersides of waves.

The sea was coming home.

"Keep turning!" Tomas yelled.

The gear fought them. Mara's shoulder burned. Metal teeth snapped
one after another. The beam crawled across Bellweather, illuminating the
empty streets below the tide line.

The first wave struck the outer basin.

Shipwrecks lifted from the salt. Some broke apart instantly; others
rolled upright as if waking from sleep. Water swallowed roads,
walls, salt pools, and abandoned fields. It rushed around the
Mercy Bell, which dropped onto the rising surface with a
tremendous crash.

Half a circle.

Tomas slipped. His hand vanished between the gears.

Mara seized his coat and pulled him back. Two of
his fingers were bleeding, but he returned to the handle.

"Properly afraid?" she gasped.

"Deeply!"

Three-quarters.

The sea reached the harbor. It smashed against the old
seawall and erupted upward in white towers. Crowds on the
cliffs cried out. Bells rang across the town, though no
one was pulling their ropes.

The lens began to fracture.

Nell's face broke into glittering pieces. Behind her, passengers crowded
the rail of the Mercy Bell. Captain Saye raised his
hand.

"Almost!" Ilyan shouted.

The final section of the turn faced the returning water.
Wind exploded through the tower windows. Mara tasted rain and
salt. The handle tore skin from her palms.

Then the beam completed the circle.

Every pane in the lens shattered.

Darkness filled the tower.

For several seconds there was no sound except Mara's breathing.

Then, from the harbor below, a ship's bell rang.

Dawn revealed a changed world.

The sea stretched blue and restless beneath the cliffs. Islands
that had once been hills rose from the water. Roofs
and chimneys protruded in the shallows, and festival banners drifted
among the waves. Along the horizon, storms moved away beneath
flocks of seabirds.

The Mercy Bell rested at the end of the old
quay.

Her paint was fresh. Her ropes were wet. Forty-three passengers
and seventeen crew members came ashore exactly as they had
departed thirty-two years before.

For them, only three weeks had passed.

Families gathered in silence, uncertain how to bridge the years.
A sailor found his younger brother, now an old man.
A woman returned to discover that her infant son was
mayor. Captain Saye stood at the quay while people embraced
around him, his cap held over his heart.

Nell was the last to leave the ship.

Mara waited at the lighthouse door. The girl climbed the
hill, no longer trapped behind glass, and held out something
wrapped in a red scarf.

It was the shearwater Ilyan had found.

The bird stirred in her hands, opened its dark eyes,
and flew toward the sea.

"Did the lighthouse take it?" Nell asked.

"Not by itself," Mara said. "People built the light. People
lit it."

"Will you fix it?"

Mara looked up at the tower. Its windows were broken,
its machinery ruined, its great lens reduced to shining fragments
across the floor.

"No," she said. "I think it has told its last
lie."

Years later, ships again crowded Bellweather's harbor. New houses rose
above the high-water line, and the lower streets became reefs
where silver fish moved through open windows. The town built
another lighthouse farther up the cliff, with an ordinary lamp,
an ordinary lens, and mechanisms Tomas could explain to anyone
willing to listen.

Mara remained its keeper.

Most nights, its beam crossed nothing stranger than waves, rain,
and the white wings of birds. Yet each year, on
October fourteenth, the people of Bellweather gathered at the harbor
and extinguished every light in town.

At midnight, the sea became black beneath the stars.

For one minute, no lamp challenged the darkness. No horn
gave warning or reply. The town listened to the water
breathing against the cliffs and remembered that a thing could
be lost not only by neglect, but also by the
desperate attempt to hold it too tightly.

Then Mara lit the new lamp.

Its beam moved slowly over the harbor, touched the sails
of the Mercy Bell, and traveled beyond them toward the
clean horizon.

This time, it showed the way without trying to shorten
it.

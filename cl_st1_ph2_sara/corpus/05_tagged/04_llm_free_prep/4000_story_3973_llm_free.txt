## The Keeper of Lost Hours

On the morning the town clock began running backward, Mara
Venn was repairing a watch that had stopped at the
exact moment its owner died.

The watch lay open beneath her lens, its brass wheels
exposed like the bones of some tiny animal. Outside, rain
polished the narrow streets of Bellweather, turning roofs, chimneys, and
weather vanes the color of wet ink. Across the square,
the clock tower struck nine.

Then it struck eight.

Mara lifted her head.

A flock of pigeons rose from the tower in alarm.
People stopped beneath their umbrellas and stared upward. The clock's
minute hand moved steadily counterclockwise. After a full revolution, the
bell sounded again.

Seven.

Mara went to the window. Her reflection looked pale against
the gray morning: thirty-two years old, dark hair pinned carelessly,
one eye enlarged by the lens still clipped to her
spectacles.

In the street below, old Mrs. Calder clutched her shopping
basket.

"It's going backward!" she cried, as if the town had
not noticed.

Bellweather had always trusted its clock. Fishermen left harbor by
it. Bakers lit their ovens by it. Children were born,
couples married, debts collected, and prisoners released according to its
solemn bronze voice. It had never lost so much as
a minute in two hundred years.

Now it chimed six.

Behind Mara, the dead man's watch began ticking.

She turned.

The hands had not moved. They remained fixed at 11:43.
Yet from within the silver case came the unmistakable rhythm
of life.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Mara crossed the room slowly. When she touched the watch,
the workshop vanished.

She stood on a moonlit road beside a young man
wearing a soldier's coat. He was laughing as he ran,
though blood darkened one sleeve. Somewhere behind him, horses thundered
and men shouted. He reached a stone bridge, leaned over
its parapet, and dropped a small wooden box into the
river.

"For Elian," he whispered.

Then a shot cracked through the night.

The vision ended.

Mara staggered backward, overturning her stool.

The watch was silent again.

She had known it belonged to Tom Arlen, the schoolmaster
who had died peacefully in his bed three days earlier
at the age of eighty-six. Everyone in Bellweather knew that.
Mara had known him since childhood. He had taught her
fractions, handwriting, and the names of constellations.

But the young soldier in the vision had not been
Tom Arlen.

Before Mara could gather her thoughts, someone hammered at the
workshop door.

Mayor Pell entered without waiting. He was a broad man
with white whiskers and a gold chain stretched across his
waistcoat. Rain ran from his hat.

"You must come," he said.

Mara glanced at the backward-moving clock. "Why me?"

"You are the town's clockmaker."

"I repair watches. I oil gears. I replace springs."

"And your father maintained the tower clock."

"My father is dead."

Mayor Pell lowered his voice. "That is why you must
come."

The clock struck five.

Across the square, panic began to spread. Shopkeepers stepped into
the rain. A woman dragged her children indoors. The harbor
bell rang wildly as sailors argued about whether the tide
itself might reverse.

Mara closed the dead schoolmaster's watch and slipped it into
her pocket.

"All right," she said.

The tower stood at the center of Bellweather, older than
the courthouse and taller than the church. Its inner staircase
spiraled upward through darkness. Mara followed Mayor Pell past ropes,
counterweights, and narrow windows through which the town appeared in
broken pieces.

At the mechanism room, the mayor unlocked an iron door.

The great clock filled the chamber.

Its gears turned with terrible smoothness. Brass teeth slid against
steel pinions. Chains rose as weights descended. Nothing was jammed
or broken. Yet every wheel moved the wrong way.

Beside the mechanism stood Mara's younger brother, Niles.

She had not seen him in almost a year.

He wore a rain-soaked coat, and his reddish hair curled
over his forehead as it had when he was a
boy. In one hand he held their father's notebook.

Mara stopped. "What are you doing here?"

"Good morning to you too."

"You left without saying where you were going."

"You wouldn't have listened."

Mayor Pell cleared his throat. "Niles returned before dawn. He
says he knows what is happening."

"Then why hasn't he stopped it?"

Niles looked toward the rotating gears. "Because the clock isn't
broken."

The bell struck four. The sound shook dust from the
rafters.

Mara folded her arms. "Explain."

Niles opened the notebook. Their father's handwriting covered the yellow
pages, but between the familiar diagrams were symbols Mara had
never seen: circles divided into thirteen parts, maps of underground
tunnels, notes written in red.

He held up a page.

"When Father became Keeper, he inherited more than a machine.
The tower clock doesn't measure time. It stores it."

Mayor Pell stared at him.

Mara almost laughed. "That is absurd."

"Is it?"

Niles stepped to the west wall and pressed one of
the stones. A hidden panel opened.

Behind it was a glass chamber no larger than a
lantern. Within the chamber floated dozens of pale blue lights.

Mara moved closer. Each light flickered with a rhythm of
its own.

"What are they?" the mayor whispered.

"Hours," Niles said. "Lost ones."

He read from the notebook.

"Every person misplaces time. An hour forgotten in grief. An
hour slept away during illness. An hour that passes unnoticed
in happiness. Most lost time disappears harmlessly. But some hours
hold too much regret, fear, or longing. They cannot fade.
The clock draws them in and keeps them from wandering."

"Wandering where?" Mara asked.

"Back into us."

As if answering him, the watch in Mara's pocket began
ticking.

She pulled it out. The case had grown warm.

"What is that?" Niles asked.

"Tom Arlen's watch."

The color left Mayor Pell's face.

Mara noticed. "You know something."

The mayor looked toward the stairs. "We should focus on
the tower."

The clock struck three.

From below came a scream.

They rushed to a window. In the square, a middle-aged
butcher stood in the rain calling for his mother. Mrs.
Calder sat on the stones, laughing like a child. Two
boys who had been fighting moments earlier now clung to
each other and wept over some shared sorrow neither could
explain.

"Memories are returning," Niles said. "Not always to the people
who lived them."

Mara opened Tom's watch.

The vision seized her again.

Moonlight. The soldier. The wooden box falling toward the river.
This time she saw another figure hiding beneath the bridge—a
boy with white-blond hair and fear in his eyes.

The soldier collapsed after the gunshot.

The hidden boy crawled from the shadows and took the
silver watch from the dead man's pocket.

"Forgive me, Elian," he whispered.

Mara returned to the tower chamber with a gasp.

Mayor Pell was staring at her.

"Elian," she said. "Who was Elian?"

The mayor gripped the windowsill.

"My father," he answered.

Silence filled the room except for the turning gears.

Mara held out the watch. "This belonged to a soldier
who died at the bridge."

Mayor Pell nodded slowly. "His name was Jonas Vale. He
was my father's closest friend."

"But Tom Arlen owned the watch."

"Tom was there that night too."

Niles flipped through the notebook. "What happened?"

Mayor Pell sank onto a wooden crate.

"It was during the rebellion. Jonas carried messages for those
resisting the governor. One night, he stole a list of
informants—names of townspeople who had betrayed their neighbors. He planned
to give it to my father. But soldiers followed him."

"The box," Mara said. "He threw it into the river."

"My father searched for it but never found it. Tom
saw Jonas die. He took the watch and kept silent
all his life."

"Why?"

"Fear. Shame. Who can say?"

The bell struck two.

The blue lights in the hidden chamber shuddered. Hairline cracks
spread across the glass.

Niles swore under his breath.

"What happens when it reaches midnight?" Mara asked.

"The chamber opens. Every lost hour returns at once."

"To Bellweather?"

"To everyone."

Mara imagined thousands of memories flooding the streets: deathbeds, betrayals,
first kisses, buried crimes, moments of happiness so bright they
would make ordinary life unbearable. The town would drown in
lives it had forgotten.

"How do we stop it?"

Niles turned several pages. "Father wrote that the Keeper must
release the hour causing the reversal."

"Which hour is that?"

"The one too heavy for the clock to hold."

Mayor Pell looked at the watch.

Mara understood. "Jonas's final hour."

"But it isn't in the chamber," Niles said. "It's trapped
in the watch."

The bell struck one.

All around them, the mechanism accelerated.

"Then release it," said the mayor.

"How?"

Niles read desperately. "An hour can be freed only when
its unfinished purpose is fulfilled."

Mara remembered the wooden box.

"For Elian," she said. "Jonas wanted your father to have
the list."

"My father has been dead forty years."

"Then perhaps it must reach you."

Mayor Pell shook his head. "The box was never found."

Mara looked through the rain-streaked window toward the river, which
curved beyond the eastern houses.

"The clock has gone back nine hours," she said. "If
it continues past midnight, what then?"

Niles scanned the notes. "It won't stop. It will move
backward through the day, perhaps farther."

"Can it make events happen again?"

"No. But it can return what events left behind."

Mara closed the watch in her hand.

"Then the box may return to where it was lost."

The clock began its final revolution toward twelve.

They ran.

Down the tower steps, across the square, through streets transformed
by stolen memories. A young woman stood embracing a lamppost,
calling it by her dead husband's name. A fisherman laughed
at invisible gulls. Children spoke in the voices of their
grandparents. Rain fell harder, and the gutters overflowed.

At the river path, Mayor Pell stumbled.

"Leave me," he said.

Niles pulled him upright. "You've spent twenty years giving speeches
about civic courage. Now seems a poor time to avoid
it."

They reached the old stone bridge as the tower's minute
hand neared midnight.

The river surged black beneath the arches.

"Where did Jonas throw it?" Mara asked.

Mayor Pell pointed. "Upstream side. Near the center."

Mara climbed onto the parapet.

Niles caught her arm. "You can't see anything in that
water."

She opened the watch.

Tick.

The world tilted.

Moonlight replaced rain. Jonas ran toward her, young and wounded.
Soldiers shouted in the distance. He hurled the box over
the parapet.

This time Mara followed its fall.

It struck the water, vanished, then reappeared briefly in the
current below. A branch snagged it and drew it toward
the bridge's northern arch, where it became wedged between two
stones.

The vision broke.

Mara pointed. "There."

Niles removed his coat and boots.

"You'll be swept away," she said.

"I'm the better swimmer."

"You always say that."

"Because you always pretend you don't remember."

He jumped.

The river swallowed him.

Mara leaned over the parapet, heart hammering. For several seconds
she saw nothing. Then Niles surfaced near the northern arch.
He caught a stone, lost his grip, and struck the
wall.

"Niles!"

"I'm fine!" he shouted, though his voice shook.

The clock began to toll midnight.

The first strike rolled over the town.

At once Mara remembered an afternoon from childhood: Niles falling
from an apple tree, Mara laughing before she saw the
broken bone.

The second strike.

She remembered her mother's last breath, though Mara had not
been in the room when she died.

The third.

A stranger's memory entered her: a wedding beneath red trees,
a groom unable to say the name of the woman
he loved.

The river convulsed. Pale lights rose from the water like
drowned stars.

Niles reached into a gap between the stones.

The fourth strike.

Mara was six years old, hiding in her father's workshop.
He was speaking to Mayor Pell.

The clock cannot hold secrets forever, her father said.

It only needs to hold them longer than we do,
Pell replied.

The fifth.

Mayor Pell collapsed to his knees.

"My father knew," Mara said. "He knew about Jonas."

Pell covered his face. "He knew everything. Keepers always did."

Niles surfaced holding a blackened wooden box.

"I found it!"

The sixth strike.

The river rose behind him.

Mara ran down the muddy bank and waded in. The
current pulled at her legs. Niles struggled toward shore with
one arm wrapped around the box.

The seventh strike.

Memories swept through Mara too quickly to understand: fires, births,
hands parting, doors closing, a thousand meals eaten and forgotten.
Bellweather's hidden life blazed inside her.

She caught Niles's wrist.

The eighth strike.

Together they crawled from the river.

The box was bound with rusted iron. Mayor Pell knelt
beside it, fumbling at the broken clasp.

The ninth strike.

Jonas's watch sprang open in Mara's hand. Its frozen hands
moved to 11:44.

The tenth.

Mayor Pell lifted the lid.

Inside, wrapped in decayed oilcloth, lay a bundle of papers.

The eleventh strike.

The top page contained a list of names.

Mayor Pell read them, and his expression changed.

"My grandfather," he whispered. "His name is here."

"An informant?" Niles asked.

Pell nodded.

The final stroke began.

Mara felt the lost hour gathering around them. It filled
the rain, the river, the spaces between heartbeats. Jonas stood
on the bridge once more, not as a ghost but
as a memory too stubborn to disappear. His soldier's coat
was dark with blood.

He looked at Mayor Pell.

"I'm sorry," Pell said. "My family built its honor on
your silence."

Jonas waited.

Pell took the papers and held them against his chest.

"I will tell them," he promised. "All of it."

The twelfth stroke ended.

For one breath, nothing happened.

Then the silver watch chimed.

Its sound was small, almost delicate.

A pale blue light emerged from the face, hovered above
Mara's palm, and rose toward the clouds. Jonas watched it
go. His expression softened.

The river grew still.

Across town, the clock hands stopped.

Then they began moving forward.

By morning, Bellweather had recovered.

People woke in doorways, shops, and rain-filled streets with fragments
of unfamiliar lives fading from their minds. Some remembered everything.
Most remembered only enough to feel embarrassed, sorrowful, or strangely
grateful.

The official explanation blamed lightning.

No one believed it, but Bellweather was a practical town.
Nets still needed mending. Bread still needed baking. Children still
had lessons, though the school remained closed until a replacement
could be found for Tom Arlen.

Three days later, Mayor Pell called a meeting in the
square.

He stood beneath the clock tower without his gold chain
of office. Before the entire town, he told the story
of Jonas Vale, Elian Pell, Tom Arlen, the stolen list,
and the families whose suffering had been hidden to preserve
the reputations of others.

Some shouted that he was lying.

Some demanded his resignation.

Some stood in silence because their own family names were
on the papers.

When he finished, Mayor Pell placed the list in the
town archive, where anyone could read it. Then he resigned.

Mara watched from the edge of the crowd with Niles
beside her.

"You think the truth fixes anything?" he asked.

"No."

"Then why tell it?"

"Because silence keeps breaking things."

Niles smiled faintly. "Father used to say that."

"No, he didn't."

"He said it to me."

Mara looked up at the tower.

There were many things her father had not told her.
She had thought this meant he trusted Niles more. Now
she wondered whether every parent divided the truth among their
children, hoping that one day they would piece it together.

"Are you leaving again?" she asked.

Niles shoved his hands into his pockets. "Do you want
me to?"

"No."

The answer seemed to surprise them both.

"I could stay a while," he said. "The tower needs
a Keeper."

Mara shook her head. "It needs two."

They found their father's tools in a locked cabinet behind
the mechanism room. There were oil cans, winding keys, brushes,
spare gears, and a second notebook containing records of lost
hours collected over generations.

Most entries were brief.

An hour of grief from a woman whose son did
not return from sea.

An hour of terror from a child trapped in a
well.

An hour of joy from two sisters reunited after sixty
years.

Mara and Niles read every page.

They repaired the cracked glass chamber, cleaned the gears, and
reset the great pendulum. Each week they climbed the tower
together. Niles inspected the weights while Mara listened to the
blue hours whisper behind the glass.

Sometimes she thought she heard her mother singing.

Sometimes Niles heard their father calling his name.

They never opened the chamber.

Tom Arlen's watch remained in Mara's workshop. After Jonas's hour
was released, the hands had begun to move again. It
kept poor time, gaining nearly three minutes a day, but
Mara chose not to correct it.

Years passed.

Bellweather changed, as towns do. Mayor Pell became the archivist
and spent the rest of his life uncovering truths his
family had hidden. A memorial to Jonas Vale was raised
near the bridge. Mrs. Calder stopped shopping in the rain.
The butcher never spoke of the morning he called for
his mother, though afterward he visited her grave every Sunday.

Niles married a woman from the harbor and taught their
children to swim before they could read. Mara never married,
though she loved more than once and was loved in
return. She preferred the company of delicate mechanisms, unfinished questions,
and the view from the tower at dusk.

Then, one winter evening, when Mara was old and Niles
had been dead for seven years, the clock stopped.

The town below fell silent.

Mara climbed the tower alone.

Snow drifted through the open arches. The mechanism stood motionless,
its gears locked. Behind the wall, the chamber of lost
hours glowed brighter than she had ever seen it.

One light pulsed near the glass.

Mara touched the chamber.

She stood again on the riverbank, young and drenched, holding
her brother's wrist while the current tried to take him.
But the memory continued beyond what she recalled.

As Niles crawled onto the mud, he looked at her
with terror, not relief.

"I saw Father," he had said.

She had not heard him over the bells.

"He told me the clock would stop for one of
us someday."

Young Mara turned away to help the mayor.

The vision faded.

Old Mara rested both hands on the glass.

"So this is mine," she said.

The blue light brightened.

She understood. It was not an hour she had forgotten,
but one she had refused to hear. An hour shaped
by all the words between herself and her brother that
had gone unspoken.

She opened the chamber.

The light drifted out and hovered before her. Within it
she saw Niles at every age: a red-haired boy stealing
apples, a young man leaping into black water, a father
lifting his daughter onto his shoulders, an old Keeper winding
the clock with hands that trembled.

"I wanted you to stay," Mara told him.

The light flickered.

"I should have said it sooner."

Warmth filled the room.

The lost hour touched her forehead and dissolved.

Below, the clock began ticking.

Mara went to the window. Snow had covered Bellweather in
white, softening roofs and roads, erasing boundaries without erasing what
lay beneath. Lamps glowed in the houses. Families gathered around
tables. Somewhere a baby cried, and somewhere someone laughed too
loudly at a familiar joke.

The tower struck midnight.

This time, the hour moved forward.

Mara listened until the final note faded, then wound the
clock for whoever would come after her.

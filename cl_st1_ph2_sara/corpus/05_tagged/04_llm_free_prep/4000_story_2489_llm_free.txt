On the morning the sea returned the church bell, Mara
Venn was repairing a clock that had not worked for
thirty-seven years.

The clock belonged to Mr. Vale, the village postmaster, who
had inherited it from an aunt he disliked and kept
it out of obligation. It was a narrow thing of
black wood, with a silver moon painted above the face.
Every hour, the moon was supposed to turn from pale
crescent to full and back again. It never did.

"Salt," Mara said, holding up a corroded spring with her
tweezers. "It gets into everything."

Mr. Vale leaned on her counter. Beyond him, rain pressed
against the windows of the shop.

"We're three streets from the harbor."

"Salt doesn't care."

As if to prove her point, a gust rattled the
sign above the door: VENN & DAUGHTER, CLOCKS REPAIRED. The
sign was older than Mara. There had been no Venn
to stand beside her for eleven years, but she had
never painted out the final two words.

Mr. Vale frowned at the clock's scattered organs. "Can you
fix it?"

"Of course."

"You always say that."

"And I am nearly always right."

The door flew open before he could answer. Tomas Reed
stood on the threshold, drenched to the skin, his red
scarf hanging like a wounded flag.

"They found it," he said.

Mara looked down at the tiny spring in her tweezers.
"Found what?"

"The bell."

Behind him, thunder rolled over the sea.

Everyone in Bellweather knew the story.

A hundred and nine years earlier, before the stone harbor
wall and the lighthouse and the white hotels on the
cliffs, the village church had stood on a hill above
the eastern shore. During the Great Storm, the hill collapsed.
The church went into the sea with twenty-three people sheltering
inside it.

The bodies were recovered over the following weeks, but the
bell was not.

Fishermen claimed they could hear it beneath their boats before
bad weather. Children dared one another to swim over the
drowned hill at midnight. Old Mrs. Cale insisted it rang
on the night her husband died, although old Mrs. Cale
also insisted she had once danced with a prince of
Spain.

Mara did not believe in stories that could not be
opened with a screwdriver.

She placed the spring on a square of blue cloth.

"Where?"

"North beach," Tomas said. "The storm uncovered something in the
rocks. Half the village is there."

"The sensible half?"

"The curious half."

Mr. Vale straightened. "I ought to see this."

"You ought to deliver the post," Mara said.

"History can't wait for the afternoon collection."

Apparently, neither could he. He pulled up his collar and
hurried out. Tomas remained in the doorway, dripping steadily.

"You're coming," he said.

It was not a question.

"I have work."

"You have one customer, and he just left."

"I have twelve clocks waiting."

"They've waited this long."

Mara glanced at the shelves behind her. Clocks stood in
solemn rows: brass carriage clocks, painted kitchen clocks, a walnut
grandfather clock whose hands had stopped at twenty past three.
For years she had repaired the village's hours. She polished
wheels, replaced pins, adjusted pendulums, and returned time to people
who complained it had been running fast or slow.

Time, she knew, did neither. People did.

Tomas was still waiting.

He had been waiting for Mara in one way or
another since they were children: outside school, at the end
of the harbor wall, beneath her upstairs window on summer
nights. He was patient only with her. With everything else,
he rushed.

She took her oilskin coat from its hook.

"If this is an anchor," she said, "I'm charging you
for the afternoon."

North beach lay beyond the lighthouse, where the cliffs bent
inward and trapped whatever the sea no longer wanted. Driftwood,
bottles, fishing nets, and once, when Mara was nine, a
crate of oranges had washed ashore there.

That morning, the entire beach smelled of iron and torn
weeds.

Villagers crowded among the rocks. Some carried umbrellas. Most had
forgotten them. Children climbed wherever adults told them not to
climb. The mayor stood on a boulder, shouting instructions nobody
obeyed.

At the center of the crowd was a bell.

It lay on its side in a bed of black
seaweed. Green with age, cracked along the lip, it was
larger than Mara had imagined. Faded letters circled its crown.

Tomas pushed through the crowd, making a path for her.

"There," he said, as proudly as if he had raised
it himself.

Several men had tied ropes around the bell. Every time
a wave entered the cove, water foamed around its mouth,
and the bell seemed to breathe.

Mara crouched beside it. Barnacles covered the bronze. She brushed
weed from the inscription.

WE CALL THE LOST—

The rest was hidden beneath the rock.

"Don't touch it," said a voice behind her.

Elias Venn had grown old without permission.

Mara remembered him as a wide-shouldered man who could carry
a longcase clock alone. The man on the beach leaned
on an ash cane. His white hair had escaped from
beneath his cap, and rain clung to his beard.

"Uncle," Mara said.

He looked at the bell, not at her. "It should
have stayed down."

"You don't believe the old stories."

"I believe the sea buries things for a reason."

Tomas gave Mara a quick glance. Family disagreements in Bellweather
had the awkward quality of public performances. There were always
spectators, and everyone knew the opening lines.

Mara stood. "Did you come all this way to tell
the sea it made a mistake?"

"I came to stop fools from ringing it."

The mayor, who had been supervising the ropes, laughed too
loudly. "It's cracked, Elias. It couldn't ring if the saints
themselves pulled the rope."

Elias's expression did not change. "Then you've nothing to prove."

A stronger wave broke against the rocks. The bell shifted.

From somewhere inside it came a low metallic sound.

Not a ring. Not quite.

The crowd fell silent.

Mara felt the note in her ribs.

Then a child squealed, someone laughed, and the moment broke
into a dozen explanations. A loose stone. The chains. The
water. An echo from the harbor.

Elias turned away.

As he passed Mara, he said quietly, "Your father heard
it too."

She caught his arm.

He stopped but did not look at her.

"My father heard what?"

Another wave washed around their boots.

Elias pulled free. "Ask the bell."

By noon, the villagers had dragged it above the tide
line. By two, it stood in the market square on
a timber frame borrowed from the boatyard. Someone scrubbed away
the weeds. Someone else sold hot cider. The mayor proposed
a festival.

Bellweather had always been efficient at turning tragedy into tourism.

Mara returned to her shop and locked the door.

She told herself she had come back for dry clothes,
but instead she climbed the narrow stairs to the attic.
The room smelled of dust, cedar, and machine oil. Her
father's workbench still stood beneath the round window. She had
not touched it since the winter he disappeared.

Jonas Venn had left his coat on the peg, his
supper on the table, and a half-repaired watch in the
vise. No one had seen him go. Three days later,
Tomas found one of his boots on the eastern shore.

The sea did not return the rest of him.

Mara knelt before the chest under the bench. Inside were
account books, old tools, and bundles of papers tied with
string. Her father's handwriting filled page after page: neat columns,
quick sketches of escapements, notes on customers.

She found the bell in the seventh book.

At first it was only a drawing in the margin:
a cracked bronze shape, the letters around its crown carefully
copied.

WE CALL THE LOST HOME.

Beneath it, Jonas had written:

Elias says it is imagination. But the sound is real.
Three nights now, always at 1:17. Not from the sea—from
inside the clocks.

Mara read the line twice.

The next pages were crowded with observations.

Pendulums stopping at 1:17.

Watches running backward.

A tide chart.

A map of the old church hill.

Then, on the final written page:

A bell does not create time. It divides it. Before
and after. Here and gone. If the drowned bell rings,
perhaps the division weakens.

Below that, in darker ink:

I heard Clara.

Mara closed the book.

Clara was her mother.

She had died when Mara was six, after three days
of fever. Mara remembered cool fingers against her forehead, a
blue ribbon, and a song with no ending.

Downstairs, a clock struck four. Another followed, then another, until
the shop filled with overlapping chimes.

Behind them, very faintly, Mara heard a woman singing.

She stood so quickly that the book fell.

The song continued for no more than a breath. Four
descending notes.

Mara knew them.

She had spent years pretending she did not.

At sunset, Elias was waiting outside the shop.

"You should have told me," Mara said.

He looked toward the windows, where dozens of clock faces
shone in the fading light. "Would you have believed me?"

"No."

"That was my hope."

She unlocked the door and let him in. Neither removed
a coat. Rain ticked against the glass, soft and regular.

Elias walked to the counter. The postmaster's black clock lay
open where Mara had left it.

"Jonas came to me a month before he vanished," he
said. "He said the clocks were speaking. I thought grief
had finally broken him. Your mother's death left a door
open in him, and he spent years waiting for something
to come through."

"He wrote that he heard her."

"So did I."

Mara stared at him.

"Once," Elias said. "In this room. Every clock stopped, and
Clara said my name. Just my name. Jonas took it
as proof. I took it as warning."

"Warning of what?"

"That the dead might call us as easily as we
call them."

In the square, a hammer struck wood. Men were reinforcing
the frame for the bell.

Mara opened her father's notebook on the counter. "He went
to the eastern shore at 1:17."

"Yes."

"You knew?"

"I guessed. By the time I reached the beach, he
was gone."

"And you never thought to tell his daughter?"

"You were nineteen. You had just lost the only parent
you had left."

"I lost him every day for eleven years."

Elias flinched.

Mara had wanted to hurt him. The knowledge brought no
satisfaction.

Outside, the rain stopped. In the sudden quiet, they heard
footsteps approaching. Tomas entered without knocking.

"They're going to ring it at midnight," he said.

Elias swore.

"The mayor says it opens the Founders' Festival."

"The founders have been dead for two centuries," Mara said.
"Surely they don't mind waiting until morning."

Tomas's face was pale. "There's more. Since they raised the
bell, people have been hearing things. Mr. Vale heard his
aunt in the post office. Mrs. Cale heard her husband
singing in the chimney. Half the clocks in the inn
have stopped."

"At what time?" Elias asked.

"Seventeen past one."

The three of them looked at the clock on the
wall.

It was five minutes to midnight.

The square glittered with lanterns.

Nearly everyone in Bellweather had gathered there, drawn by fear,
excitement, or the certainty that anything supernatural should happen in
front of witnesses. The bell hung from a new iron
bar. A rope had been looped through its rusted clapper.

The mayor climbed onto a platform.

Mara, Elias, and Tomas pushed into the crowd.

"You cannot ring it," Elias called.

The mayor raised both hands. "Friends, this bell is part
of our heritage."

"So is cholera," Mara said. "We don't hold a festival
for that."

A few people laughed.

The mayor did not. "Miss Venn, I respect your skill
with clocks, but this is a civic matter."

"It is a timepiece."

"It's a bell."

"What do you think bells are for?"

The clockmaker's argument caused a murmur. Bellweather respected expertise, particularly
when it was expressed irritably.

Mara climbed onto the platform.

"My father studied this bell before he disappeared," she said.
"Many of you have heard voices tonight. People you loved.
People you lost. If the bell has caused that without
being rung, we cannot know what will happen when it
sounds."

Mr. Vale removed his hat. Mrs. Cale began to cry.

The mayor lowered his voice. "Are you asking me to
cancel the first interesting event this village has had in
years because of ghosts?"

"Yes."

The harbor clock began to strike midnight.

On the first note, every lantern in the square went
out.

Darkness swept over them.

Someone screamed.

Then the drowned bell rang.

No one had pulled the rope.

Its cracked voice rolled through the square, deeper than thunder.
Mara felt the ground tilt beneath her. Shop windows burst
outward. Birds rose shrieking from the roofs.

The bell rang again.

The square changed.

For an instant, another village stood within Bellweather: cottages with
thatched roofs, muddy lanes, the old church on its green
hill. Pale figures crowded the streets. Water streamed from their
clothes. Seaweed twisted in their hair.

Then the vision vanished.

People cried out names.

Loved ones appeared between raindrops, in windows, beneath archways. A
dead brother lifted his hand. A lost child ran toward
her mother. Sailors drowned decades apart stood together beside the
fountain, staring at the sky.

Mara heard the unfinished song.

Her mother stood at the far end of the square,
wearing the gray dress in which she had been buried.
Beside her was Jonas.

He looked exactly as he had on the final morning:
brown hair untidy, spectacles low on his nose, waistcoat missing
a button.

"Mara," he said.

The crowd disappeared from her awareness. The square narrowed to
the distance between them.

She stepped off the platform.

"Mara," Elias warned.

Jonas smiled. "Little wren."

No one had called her that since he vanished.

She crossed half the square before Tomas caught her wrist.

"Wait."

She tried to pull free.

Her father's smile trembled. Behind him, the street darkened, not
with night but with depth. Water seemed to stand upright
like a wall.

"Come home," he said.

The words were gentle.

Around Mara, others were moving toward their dead. Mrs. Cale
reached for her husband. The baker walked toward two small
figures holding hands. A fisherman embraced the wet shadow of
his brother—and both vanished.

The bell swung.

It would ring a third time.

Mara looked at the harbor clock. Its hands spun backward,
gathering speed. Eleven fifty. Eleven thirty. Ten. Nine.

Roofs flickered between tile and thatch. The market fountain became
an ancient well. If the clock continued, Bellweather would be
dragged into the hour the church drowned.

"A bell divides time," Mara whispered.

Tomas tightened his grip. "What?"

"My father's notebook. Before and after. Here and gone."

"How does that help us?"

She looked at the cracked bell, then at the clocks
visible through the broken windows of her shop. All showed
1:17.

Clocks did not command time. They agreed upon it.

"Elias!" she shouted. "The bell is pulling everything toward one
moment. We have to give the village another."

Her uncle understood first. He seized the mayor's ceremonial hammer
and struck the little brass clock mounted above the town
hall door.

Once.

The sound was thin against the drowned bell's roar, but
it was clear.

Mara ran to her shop.

Tomas followed, dragging her past the pale figure of Jonas.
Her father reached for her.

"Little wren."

She stopped.

For eleven years, she had imagined what she would say
if he returned. She had prepared accusations, pleas, forgiveness. Now,
seeing the sea behind his eyes, she understood that whatever
wore his face held only the part of him that
had wanted to come home.

That part was real.

It was simply not all of him.

"I waited for you," she said.

"I know."

"I cannot keep waiting."

His expression softened.

Then Tomas pulled her through the shop door.

Mara seized the first clock she reached, wound it, and
set the hands to midnight. Tomas took another. Elias appeared
carrying three from the town hall.

"Midnight?" he asked.

"Midnight," Mara said. "A new day. Make them agree."

Word spread through the square.

People stumbled into houses and returned with clocks. Mantel clocks,
watches, alarm clocks, kitchen clocks shaped like cats. They set
every hand to twelve. Those who could not reach their
homes rang bicycle bells, struck pans, blew whistles.

The drowned bell began its third swing.

Mara placed Mr. Vale's black clock on the counter. The
corroded spring still lay on the blue cloth.

"Can you fix it?" Tomas asked.

"Of course."

She fitted the spring, closed the mechanism, and turned the
key.

The silver moon above the face moved.

Outside, the drowned bell's clapper rose.

"Now!" Mara shouted.

Bellweather rang.

A hundred clocks struck midnight. Watches chimed. Pans crashed. The
harbor horn bellowed. People shouted the hour until their voices
broke.

The sound met the bell's third note in the air.

For one impossible moment, the village stood between two times.

Mara saw the church hill beneath the sea. She saw
twenty-three frightened people gathered in the nave. She saw Jonas
on the shore eleven years earlier, walking into black water
while Clara's song drifted over the waves.

Then she saw him turn.

Not the ghost in the square. Her father as he
had truly been: tired, grieving, alive.

He looked across the years at her.

"Time to go," Mara said.

Jonas nodded.

The drowned bell split from crown to lip.

Its voice broke.

Water crashed through the square without wetting it. The dead
dissolved into spray. The old church folded into darkness. The
harbor clock's hands stopped at midnight.

Silence followed.

Then, one by one, the clocks began to tick.

By spring, the bell stood in the village museum, though
"stood" was a generous word for three bronze fragments laid
upon velvet. The mayor's plaque described the night as an
unusual atmospheric event. No one in Bellweather believed the plaque,
including the mayor.

Mrs. Cale stopped claiming she had danced with the prince
of Spain. She had remembered, at last, that he was
Portuguese.

Elias came to the shop every Thursday. At first he
brought clocks that did not need repair. Later, he stopped
pretending and brought tea.

Tomas replaced the broken windows and painted a new sign.

Mara objected when she saw it.

VENN & DAUGHTER & PERHAPS REED, CLOCKS REPAIRED

"Perhaps Reed?" she said.

"I didn't want to presume."

"You've presumed an entire partnership."

"Only perhaps."

She made him repaint it, though she allowed him to
keep a small perhaps in the lower corner.

Mr. Vale's clock sat in the front window. The silver
moon turned exactly as it should. Every day at 1:17,
it paused for the length of a breath, then continued.

Mara never tried to correct it.

Sometimes, when storms came in from the east and salt
rattled against the glass, all the clocks in the shop
fell briefly into rhythm. Their separate ticks became one steady
pulse.

On such nights, Mara would think of her father beyond
the final shore, and of her mother's unfinished song.

She no longer listened for them.

But she did not shut the window, either.

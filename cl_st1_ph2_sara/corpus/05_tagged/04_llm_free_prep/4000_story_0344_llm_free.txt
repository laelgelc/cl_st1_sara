# The Lighthouse at Low Tide

When Mara returned to Bellweather Island, the sea was missing.

It had drawn back farther than anyone remembered, beyond the
harbor wall, beyond the black teeth of the reef, leaving
fishing boats tilted in acres of shining mud. Ropes hung
slack from the pier. Crabs wandered through puddles that reflected
a hard white sky.

Her ferry could not dock. A fisherman carried her ashore
in a flat-bottomed skiff, poling through the shallows while the
other passengers whispered about earthquakes and storms.

"Just an exceptional tide," the fisherman said.

No one believed him.

Mara sat in the bow with her father's brass key
in her pocket. It was too large for any ordinary
lock, green with age around its teeth. The solicitor had
sent it with a brief letter.

Your father's estate consists chiefly of the lighthouse and its
contents. He requested that you visit before deciding what to
do with it.

She had not seen Elias Vale in fourteen years.

From the harbor, the lighthouse looked smaller than it had
during her childhood. It stood on the northern hill, a
white tower above a square gray house, its lamp room
blind in the afternoon sun. Mara had once believed it
held the moon captive at night.

Now weeds choked the path to the door.

The village had changed less than she expected. The same
blue cottages leaned into the wind. The same iron bell
hung outside the abandoned chapel. Even the gulls seemed to
recognize her, crying overhead with rude familiarity.

Mrs. Foss, who had run the island shop for as
long as Mara could remember, stood waiting beside the harbor
road. She was wrapped in a red coat despite the
warmth.

"You have his eyes," she said.

"People used to say I had my mother's."

"They were being kind."

Mara almost smiled. Mrs. Foss had never wasted kindness.

"Did he suffer?" Mara asked.

"No. Sat down at the kitchen table and died between
one breath and the next. There are worse ways."

"Yes."

"He left something unfinished."

Mara looked toward the lighthouse. "The solicitor said nothing about
that."

"Solicitors rarely know the important parts of anything."

Mrs. Foss pressed a paper parcel into her hands. Bread,
cheese, apples, and a box of matches.

"The electricity's been cut," she said. "Your father wouldn't let
anyone repair the cable. Said the old machinery was safer."

"Safer from what?"

But Mrs. Foss had already turned toward the village.

The lighthouse door opened with the brass key.

Inside, the air smelled of salt, cold ashes, and her
father's tobacco. His boots remained beneath the coat hooks. A
cracked mug stood beside the kitchen sink. On the table
lay a deck of cards arranged for a solitary game,
as if he had stepped away to answer the door.

Mara touched the back of his chair.

For years she had rehearsed what she would say if
he ever asked her to return. She had imagined refusing
him. She had imagined arriving too late and feeling nothing.

Instead, grief moved through her like weather through an empty
house, entering every room at once.

She opened windows. Wind stirred the curtains and carried dust
across the floor. Upstairs, her childhood bedroom contained a narrow
bed, a wardrobe, and a row of seashells on the
sill. She picked up a pink shell and remembered her
mother laughing as she held it to Mara's ear.

The sea is never far away, Celia Vale had said.
Even when you cannot see it.

Mara set the shell down.

Her mother had vanished on a clear autumn night when
Mara was eleven. No wreckage had washed ashore. No body
had been found. Elias had insisted Celia's boat must have
struck the reef, though she knew the waters too well
for that.

Afterward, he had changed. He stopped sleeping. He filled notebooks
with tides, stars, and columns of figures. Three years later,
Mara left for school on the mainland and refused to
come back.

At the bottom of the tower stairs, she found a
note pinned to the wall.

Mara—

Light the lamp at sunset. Do not let it go
dark before the tide returns.

I am sorry.

—Father

She read it twice.

The lamp had not been used in decades. Bellweather's automated
beacon stood on a steel platform at the eastern point,
powered by solar panels and maintained by the coast guard.
Her father's lighthouse was ceremonial, obsolete.

Yet beside the note waited a can of oil and
a box of long matches.

Mara climbed.

The spiral stairs wound through the tower, narrowing as they
rose. At the top, she unlocked the trapdoor and pushed
into the lamp room. Curved windows surrounded a great glass
lens. Brass gears gleamed beneath it, polished and oiled. Her
father's notebooks were stacked against the walls.

Mara opened the most recent one.

The final page was dated three days earlier.

Lowest tide at 18:47. Channel exposed shortly before sunset. Light
must turn until closure. One keeper sufficient, if willing.

Below this, in shaking handwriting, he had added:

She may still be waiting.

Mara stared at the words.

Outside, the retreating sea had uncovered a road.

It began beneath the northern cliff as a line of
pale stones and ran straight across the seabed toward the
horizon. Water streamed from it in silver threads. On either
side, fish twisted in shallow pools, and forests of kelp
lay flattened in the mud.

The road should not have existed.

Mara descended so quickly she bruised her shoulder against the
stairwell.

By the time she reached the village, half the island
had gathered at the harbor. Children tried to climb down
onto the exposed seabed while their parents dragged them back.
Old men argued about ancient maps. Someone claimed to see
buildings beyond the reef.

Mrs. Foss stood apart, watching the pale road.

"You knew," Mara said.

"I suspected."

"What is it?"

"A path."

"I can see that."

"Then you know as much as I do."

Mara thrust the notebook toward her. "My father wrote that
someone was waiting."

Mrs. Foss did not take it.

"Your mother," she said quietly, "didn't drown."

The harbor noise seemed to recede.

"You told me she did."

"No. Your father told you. The rest of us kept
silent."

"Why?"

"Because the truth would have sounded crueler."

Mrs. Foss looked toward the horizon. The sky had begun
to turn gold.

"People have disappeared from Bellweather for centuries. Not often. One
every generation, perhaps. They walk out when the road appears.
Some say they hear music. Some hear a loved one
calling. Your mother heard her sister."

"My mother didn't have a sister."

"She did. A little girl who died before you were
born."

Mara remembered no grave, no photograph.

"Your father followed Celia that night," Mrs. Foss continued. "He
reached the far side of the reef before the tide
began to rise. He saw her ahead of him, walking
toward lights beneath the horizon. She wouldn't turn back. He
did."

"And he let everyone believe she had been careless."

"He was ashamed."

"He should have gone after her."

"He had a daughter at home."

The answer struck harder than Mara expected.

Across the mudflats, a boy shouted. Something bright moved along
the distant end of the road, where the pale stones
entered a bank of mist.

The chapel bell rang.

No one stood near it.

A single iron note rolled over the island. Then came
another, and another.

Mrs. Foss gripped Mara's wrist.

"Sunset," she said. "Your father always lit the lamp when
the road appeared."

"What happens if I don't?"

"I don't know. He never failed."

Mara looked at the lighthouse, then at the impossible road.

"I'm going after her."

Mrs. Foss's fingers tightened. "Your mother has been gone twenty-eight
years."

"She may still be waiting."

"Or something wrote that because it wants you to follow."

Mara pulled free.

All her life, her mother's absence had been a locked
room inside her. She had built everything around it—her anger,
her ambition, the apartment in a city where no one
knew the name Bellweather. Now, for the first time, the
door stood open.

She ran down the harbor steps.

Mud sucked at her boots until she reached the stone
road. Its surface was dry and warm, though the air
had cooled. Behind her, villagers called her name. Ahead, the
mist glowed faintly blue.

She walked.

The island diminished behind her. First the cottages sank from
view, then the chapel roof, until only the lighthouse remained
above the dark line of the cliffs.

Along the road lay things the sea should have destroyed:
a silver spoon, a leather shoe, a child's wooden horse
with one red wheel. Mara passed the ribs of an
ancient ship jutting from the mud. Farther on, she found
a rusted bicycle standing upright as if balanced by an
invisible rider.

Then she heard the music.

Her mother had owned a small piano. On winter evenings,
Celia played the same melody while rain rattled the windows.
Mara had forgotten it for years, but now every note
returned.

The music drifted from the mist ahead.

"Mama?" she called.

A figure appeared.

Celia Vale stepped onto the road wearing the yellow coat
she had worn the night she disappeared. Her black hair
moved in a breeze Mara could not feel. She looked
thirty-six, still young, while Mara had grown older than her.

"Mara," Celia said.

Her voice broke something open.

Mara ran.

The distance between them did not close.

She ran until her lungs burned, but Celia remained twenty
paces away, smiling sadly.

"You grew up," her mother said.

Mara stopped.

"You left me."

"I know."

"Come back."

"I can't."

"Why not?"

Celia glanced over her shoulder. Through the mist, lights shimmered
like windows at dusk. Tall shapes moved between them.

"I went too far," she said. "I listened too long."

"To what?"

"To the promise."

The music softened, and beneath it Mara heard voices. Her
father's voice. Friends she had lost. Her own childhood laughter.

"It shows you a place where nothing is taken from
you," Celia said. "No one dies there. No one leaves.
Every door opens into a room you remember."

Mara's eyes filled with tears. "Is it real?"

"Real enough to enter."

A shadow crossed Celia's face.

"Not real enough to escape."

Behind Mara, the chapel bell continued ringing. The lighthouse stood
black against the burning sky.

Its lamp was dark.

Celia saw it too.

"You have to go back."

"Come with me."

"I have tried."

Mara took a careful step forward. This time, her mother
seemed nearer.

"Then try again."

The first water crossed the road.

It came in narrow streams between the stones, curling around
Mara's boots. On both sides, the mudflats trembled as the
sea began to return.

Celia's expression changed. "Mara, listen to me. The road isn't
for us. It belongs to what waits beyond it. The
light closes the way."

"Father wrote that you were waiting."

"I was. For him. For you. I don't know how
long."

"Then come."

The mist twisted behind Celia. The distant lights brightened. Shapes
gathered between them—too tall, too thin, wearing faces that shifted
like reflections in moving water.

The voices rose.

Stay, they whispered.

Mara saw her childhood kitchen on a summer morning. Her
father was making pancakes. Her mother stood at the window,
alive and laughing. Eleven-year-old Mara sat at the table, impatient
for a day that had not yet gone wrong.

She wanted it so fiercely that the road seemed to
tilt beneath her.

Celia slapped her.

The vision shattered.

Mara found her mother standing directly before her, one hand
raised. Celia's palm felt warm and solid against Mara's cheek.

"Run," she said.

They turned together.

The lighthouse was a distant needle. Water now covered the
road to Mara's ankles. Behind them, the mist advanced, carrying
its impossible city. The shapes moved within it without hurry.

Mara and Celia ran.

With each step, Celia aged.

Silver appeared in her hair. Lines formed around her mouth
and eyes. Her yellow coat faded, tore at the sleeves,
and streamed behind her. Twenty-eight stolen years claimed her in
minutes.

The water reached their knees.

Halfway to the island, Celia stumbled. Mara caught her.

"I can't," Celia gasped.

"Yes, you can."

"The light—"

"I know."

"You must leave me and light it."

"No."

"If the channel stays open after the tide returns, those
things won't need the road."

Mara looked back. The shapes had reached the edge of
the mist. One wore her father's face.

He seemed younger than when she last saw him. He
held out his hand.

I am sorry, he mouthed.

For one terrible moment, she wanted to forgive him more
than she wanted to live.

Then the face smiled, and the smile was not his.

Mara dragged her mother onward.

People were wading from the harbor to meet them. Mrs.
Foss led the line with a rope tied around her
waist. The water rose to Mara's hips, cold and violent
now, sweeping loose stones from beneath her feet.

Hands seized her.

Villagers passed Celia between them. Mara lost sight of her
in the spray but heard someone shouting that she was
breathing.

The lighthouse remained dark.

Mara fought through the water to the harbor steps and
ran uphill. Behind her, the sea poured over the pale
road. The last rim of sun touched the horizon.

She reached the lighthouse breathless and soaked.

The brass key slipped twice before entering the lock.

Inside, darkness had gathered. She took the oil and matches
and climbed the spiral stairs. Wind moaned through the tower.
As she passed the narrow windows, she saw mist flowing
over the harbor, moving against the wind.

Voices filled the stairwell.

Her father called from below.

Mara, wait.

She kept climbing.

At the trapdoor, something struck the tower. The walls shuddered.
Glass broke overhead.

Mara entered the lamp room.

The great lens waited in darkness. She poured oil into
the reservoir, opened the valve, and struck a match.

Nothing.

The wick was wet.

Below the tower, people screamed. The mist had reached the
first cottages. Pale figures walked upon the water.

Mara struck another match and held it to the wick.
The flame shrank, blue and weak.

"Please," she whispered.

A hand closed around hers.

Her father stood beside her.

Not the young imitation from the road, but Elias as
she remembered him: gray, stooped, exhausted. His hand smelled of
tobacco and machine oil.

"You always rush the flame," he said. "Cup your hand
around it."

She knew he was not real. Or perhaps he was
a memory. At that moment, it made no difference.

"You should have told me," she said.

"Yes."

"You should have trusted me."

"Yes."

"I hated you."

"I know."

The match burned close to her fingers.

Together, they shielded it from the wind.

The wick caught.

Fire spread in a golden line. Mara turned the brass
lever. Ancient gears groaned beneath the lens, reluctant after years
of stillness. Then the mechanism engaged.

The lamp began to revolve.

A blade of light swept across the sea.

Where it touched the mist, the mist tore apart. The
pale figures bent backward, stretching like shadows at sunset. Their
mouths opened, but the sound that emerged was only wind.

Again the light passed.

The city beyond the horizon flickered.

Again.

The road vanished beneath black water, and the mist collapsed
into the waves.

The returning sea struck Bellweather Island with a sound like
a vast door closing.

Then everything was still.

Mara looked beside her.

Her father was gone.

At dawn, the tide rested at its ordinary level. Boats
floated upright in the harbor, tugging at their ropes. Of
the pale road there was no sign.

Mara found Celia asleep in the lighthouse kitchen beneath a
pile of blankets. She had the face of a woman
in her sixties now. Her hair was white at the
temples, and her hands were thin and weathered.

Mrs. Foss sat nearby, drinking tea.

"She woke once," Mrs. Foss said. "Asked whether you still
hated cinnamon."

Mara laughed, though it came out as a sob.

Celia opened her eyes.

For a while, mother and daughter simply looked at each
other across the years.

"I'm sorry," Celia whispered.

Mara knelt beside her.

"Everyone is," she said. "Apparently."

Outside, sunlight entered the harbor. Villagers emerged from their homes
and began clearing seaweed from the streets. Someone tested the
chapel bell. Its ordinary, imperfect clang startled gulls from the
roof.

In the weeks that followed, Mara postponed the sale of
the lighthouse.

Then she canceled it.

She repaired the broken windows and learned to maintain the
gears. Celia moved slowly at first, as if gravity were
something she had to remember, but each day she walked
a little farther. Sometimes she spoke of the city beyond
the mist. More often, she did not.

On clear evenings, they climbed the tower together.

The automated beacon continued to flash from the eastern point,
bright, efficient, and approved by every maritime authority. But Mara
lit the old lamp as well.

Most nights, its beam crossed nothing stranger than fishing boats
and restless waves.

Years later, children would ask why Bellweather needed two lights.

Mara always gave them the sensible answer.

"One guides sailors home," she said.

Then she would look toward the dark horizon, where the
sea kept its roads hidden.

"And the other reminds the darkness that we are watching."

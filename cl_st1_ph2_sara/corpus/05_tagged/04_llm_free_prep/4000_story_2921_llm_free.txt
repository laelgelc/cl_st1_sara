The Last Lighthouse Keeper

On the morning the sea disappeared, Elias Vale was polishing
the great brass lens at the top of Bellwether Lighthouse.

He noticed the silence first.

For thirty-two years, the sea had spoken through the tower
walls. It whispered in summer, argued in autumn, and threw
itself against the black cliffs all winter long. Even when
Elias slept, some part of him listened to it.

That morning, there was nothing.

He lowered his cloth and looked through the salt-streaked window.

The sea was gone.

Beyond the cliffs, where gray water should have stretched to
the horizon, an immense plain of wet sand gleamed beneath
the rising sun. Stranded fish flashed like dropped coins. Shipwrecks
leaned from the mud, their broken masts pointing in every
direction. Far away, perhaps twenty miles from shore, a dark
blue line marked the retreating ocean.

Elias stared until a kettle began shrieking in the room
below.

He descended the spiral staircase, moving more quickly than he
had in years. At the bottom, the lighthouse door stood
open. A girl was framed against the pale morning.

Mara Fen was thirteen, narrow as a reed, with hair
that never seemed to acknowledge the existence of combs. She
lived with her aunt in the village and delivered Elias's
bread twice a week.

Today she carried no basket.

"Mr. Vale," she said, breathless, "the harbor's empty."

"I've seen."

"All the boats are lying on their sides."

"I imagined they would be."

"And there are things out there."

Elias stopped.

"What things?"

"Paths," Mara said. "Stairs. A whole road under the water."

From the village, bells began to ring.

Bellwether had three bells: one for fire, one for storms,
and one for ships in danger. The villagers were ringing
all three.

Elias took his coat from its peg.

"Stay here," he told Mara.

"I'm coming."

"No."

"You don't know where the road goes."

"Neither do you."

"Yes, but I'm curious."

"That is not a qualification."

"It's better than being old."

Elias opened his mouth, then closed it. He had known
Mara since she was a red-faced infant, and experience had
taught him that arguing merely gave her more opportunities to
win.

"Keep up," he said.

They followed the cliff path toward the village.

People had gathered at the abandoned harbor, staring over the
stone wall. Fishing boats rested in the mud below. Crabs
wandered through tangled nets. Beyond them, the exposed seabed descended
into a wide valley.

And there, exactly as Mara had said, ran a road.

It was paved with square stones and bordered by broken
pillars. Barnacles covered the surface, but its shape was unmistakable.
It began beneath the harbor wall and led straight toward
the distant sea.

Mayor Creel stood on an overturned barrel, calling for calm
in a voice that suggested he had misplaced his own.

"No one is to go down there," he shouted. "The
water may return at any moment."

"Then we should find out why it left," said someone
in the crowd.

"Preferably without drowning," the mayor replied.

A murmur rose as Elias approached. The villagers looked to
him whenever the sea behaved badly. He understood tides, storms,
warning lamps, and the moods of fog. They expected him
to understand this too.

He did not.

"What do you think, Elias?" asked Doctor Brin.

Elias studied the road. The stones bore carved symbols beneath
their shells: circles, stars, and eyes with vertical pupils.

"I think," he said, "that the mayor is right."

Creel brightened.

"And I think someone must go."

The mayor's face fell.

Within an hour, they had assembled a small party. Elias
would lead. Mara invited herself. Doctor Brin came with bandages
and a pistol she had inherited from her husband. Tomas
Reed, the strongest fisherman in Bellwether, carried rope, lanterns, and
enough food for two days.

Mayor Creel remained behind to "coordinate the defense of the
village," though no one could say what the village needed
defending from.

They climbed down a ladder into the harbor.

The smell struck them first. It was the heavy, ancient
scent of salt, rot, and things never meant to meet
the sun. Small pools quivered in the mud. Eels twisted
in them, black and frantic.

"Stay on the stones," Elias warned.

The road sloped gently downward. On either side, the sea
floor spread like a revealed secret.

They passed the skeleton of a whale, its ribs forming
a white cage taller than a house. They passed cannons
buried in silt and an anchor so enormous that Tomas
could stand inside one of its rusted links. Once, they
heard a deep groan beneath the earth, and the stones
trembled.

After two hours, Bellwether had become a row of tiny
shapes against the cliffs.

Mara stopped beside a half-buried statue.

It depicted a woman with three faces. One looked toward
land, one toward sea, and one directly upward. In her
six hands, she held a ship, a key, a flame,
a shell, a knife, and a human heart.

"There are words," Mara said.

Elias scraped away a layer of barnacles. Letters appeared, sharp
despite their age.

"What do they say?" Tomas asked.

Elias had spent many winters reading old books left by
previous keepers. He recognized the script as a form of
early Mariner's Cant, though several symbols were unfamiliar.

"'When the keeper abandons the flame,'" he translated slowly, "'the
sea shall abandon the shore.'"

Everyone looked back at the distant lighthouse.

Its white tower was barely visible, but the lantern room
caught the sun.

"I didn't abandon anything," Elias said.

"Maybe it means another keeper," Doctor Brin suggested.

"Maybe," Mara said, "it doesn't mean the lighthouse."

They continued.

By noon, the mud had dried enough to crack. The
blue line of the ocean seemed no closer, though the
road led toward it without bending.

They found the city in a hollow.

Towers emerged from the silt, covered in coral and trailing
weeds. Roofless halls stood around circular plazas. Pale fish flopped
between fallen columns. At the city's center rose a black
stone temple.

No one spoke for several minutes.

Every child in Bellwether knew the legend of Orra, the
drowned city. Adults treated it as a bedtime story: a
proud kingdom swallowed by the sea after its queen tried
to chain the moon. Scholars insisted Orra had never existed.

Yet its streets now lay beneath the sun.

"We should turn back," Doctor Brin said.

"Yes," Elias replied.

Mara was already walking toward the temple.

They shouted after her. She pretended not to hear.

Inside, the air was cool and strangely dry. A round
door stood at the end of a long corridor. Unlike
everything else in the city, it bore no coral or
weeds. A shallow bowl had been carved into its center.

Mara reached toward it.

Elias caught her wrist.

"Curiosity," he said, "is not a qualification."

She pulled her hand back.

Symbols covered the corridor walls. Some showed stars descending into
waves. Others showed crowds kneeling before a tall figure crowned
in fire. The last image depicted a tower on a
cliff, its beam sweeping across the sea.

Beneath it, Elias found another inscription.

"'The last flame is not to guide the living,'" he
read. "'It guards the sleep of the deep.'"

A sound came from beyond the door.

It was faint at first, like fingernails brushing stone. Then
something struck the other side.

Once.

Twice.

Dust fell from the ceiling.

Tomas took a step backward. "What's in there?"

Elias looked at the empty bowl.

"A flame opens it," he said.

"Then it stays closed," Doctor Brin replied.

A third blow shook the door.

This time, from far beyond the temple, something answered.

A low cry rolled across the exposed seabed. It was
too vast to come from any whale. In the shadowed
streets, stranded fish began thrashing all at once.

Mara turned pale.

"We need to go."

They ran.

As they left the city, a tremor split the road
behind them. One of Orra's towers collapsed, sending up a
cloud of salt and dust. The cry came again, closer
now.

The ocean was moving.

The distant blue line had risen. At first Elias thought
it was a trick of light, but the horizon grew
taller as he watched. A wall of water was advancing
across the seabed.

"How long?" Tomas asked.

Elias judged the distance. "Two hours. Perhaps less."

Bellwether was nearly four hours away.

They began to run anyway.

The road shuddered under their feet. Water burst from cracks
between the stones. Creatures crawled from the mud: silver worms
longer than a man's arm, blind crabs, soft white things
with trembling mouths.

Mara stumbled. Elias pulled her upright.

"Leave me," she gasped.

"No."

"I can run faster if you stop holding me."

He released her.

They reached the whale skeleton as the returning sea swallowed
Orra. The tops of its towers vanished beneath the wave.
Wind rushed ahead of the water, cold and sharp with
spray.

"We won't make the cliffs," Doctor Brin said.

Elias knew she was right.

Then Mara pointed to a wreck half a mile from
the road.

It was an old three-masted ship lying upright in the
mud, its hull remarkably whole. On its side, beneath moss
and shells, Elias could make out a name.

Mercy Bell.

He knew that name. Everyone in Bellwether did.

The Mercy Bell had disappeared eighty years earlier with forty
passengers aboard. No wreckage had ever been found.

They crossed the mud, sinking to their ankles. The ship
leaned slightly but held as they climbed through a breach
in the hull.

Inside, the air smelled of wood and decay.

They found the passengers in the lower cabin.

Forty skeletons sat at long tables, dressed in scraps of
rotted clothing. Before each one rested a silver cup. At
the head of the room, the captain's bones slumped over
an open journal.

Mara looked away.

Elias lifted the journal carefully. Most pages had dissolved, but
the final entry remained legible.

The lighthouse went dark at midnight.

Afterward, the stars beneath the water opened their eyes.

We heard singing. The crew followed it below. God forgive
me, I locked the passengers in.

The last lines had been written unevenly.

The keeper warned us. The flame must never fail.

The ship lurched.

Water rushed around its hull.

"Up!" Tomas shouted.

They climbed to the main deck as the first surge
lifted the Mercy Bell from the mud. Its masts groaned.
Torn sails streamed like ghosts.

The wave had spent much of its force crossing the
seabed, but it carried them swiftly toward shore.

Tomas found a length of chain and secured everyone to
the central mast. Water broke over the deck. The wreck
spun, struck something beneath the surface, and tilted so sharply
that Elias thought it would roll.

Through rain and spray, he saw shapes moving in the
sea.

They were longer than ships and blacker than the water
around them.

One rose near the wreck. Elias glimpsed a curved back
covered in rows of pale scars. An eye opened in
its flesh.

It was larger than the lighthouse lantern.

The eye fixed on him.

A voice entered his mind.

Flamekeeper.

Elias could not breathe.

The voice was not made of words. It was pressure,
darkness, and the crushing cold of the deepest trench.

The fire is dead.

"No," Elias whispered.

Mara gripped his arm. "What is it?"

He pointed toward Bellwether.

The lighthouse stood dark.

Night had not yet fallen, but storm clouds had covered
the sun. The lamp should have been burning. Elias had
lit it every morning before breakfast for thirty-two years.

That morning, distracted by the vanished sea, he had not.

The inscription had been right.

The keeper had abandoned the flame.

The creature struck the ship.

Wood shattered. Tomas's chain snapped from the mast. Doctor Brin
disappeared beneath a rush of water, then surfaced near the
railing. Mara caught her hand.

The current drove the Mercy Bell toward the cliffs.

In the village, people fled uphill. Elias could see them
on the winding paths. The harbor wall collapsed as the
first wave struck it. Boats smashed against houses.

Yet the lighthouse remained standing on its isolated point.

"If we can reach the tower," Elias said, "I can
relight the lamp."

Tomas stared through the storm. "We'll be broken on the
rocks."

"Not if we steer."

"This wreck has no rudder."

"Then we make one."

Using planks, rope, and the broken yardarm, they built something
that was not truly a rudder but could persuade the
current. Tomas and Elias hauled on the ropes while Mara
called directions from the bow.

The lighthouse point drew closer.

So did the rocks.

The Mercy Bell struck the reef.

The deck split with a sound like cannon fire. Elias
flew forward, hit the railing, and fell into the sea.

Cold closed over him.

He sank through green darkness filled with fragments of wood.
Below, an enormous shape circled the reef. Pale eyes opened
along its back, one after another.

The voice returned.

You are small, Flamekeeper.

Elias kicked toward the surface.

A black tendril curled around his ankle.

He remembered other mornings: trimming the wick while snow pressed
against the windows, winding the clockwork through fever, polishing the
lens after his wife's funeral because grief did not stop
ships from needing light. He remembered every storm in which
the lamp had burned.

And he remembered this morning's silence.

Not yet, he thought.

The tendril tightened.

A hand seized his collar. Tomas dragged him upward. They
broke the surface beside a slab of wreckage.

Mara and Doctor Brin had reached the rocks below the
lighthouse. Elias and Tomas struggled after them.

The four climbed as the wreck broke apart behind them.

At the tower door, Elias searched his pockets.

The key was gone.

"Move," Doctor Brin said.

She fired her pistol into the lock.

The first shot did nothing. The second shattered it.

They entered the lighthouse.

The tower trembled as water struck its base. Outside, the
sea creatures circled. Their voices gathered in Elias's skull, vast
and hungry.

The fire is dead.

He climbed.

Thirty-two years, and he had never hated the spiral staircase
until then. His wet boots slipped on iron steps. His
lungs burned. Mara stayed beside him, though he told her
twice to go back.

At the lantern room, Elias found the lamp cold.

He opened the fuel valve. Oil flowed, but his matches
were soaked.

"Tomas!" he shouted.

No answer came from below.

Mara searched the cabinets. "There must be something."

"The village keeps spare matches," Elias said.

"The village is underwater."

A blow shook the lighthouse. The lens cracked from top
to bottom.

Elias stared at the dark wick.

Then Mara pointed to the emergency signal pistol mounted on
the wall.

"That makes fire."

"It might also make the lamp explode."

"Will the tower survive those things outside?"

"No."

"Then an explosion isn't much worse."

The reasoning was difficult to dispute.

Elias aimed the signal pistol at the wick.

"Stand back."

He pulled the trigger.

Red flame filled the lamp.

For one terrible second, fire raced across spilled oil. Elias
threw himself toward the valve and shut it. Mara beat
at the flames with his coat.

Then the lamp caught properly.

Light poured through the fractured lens.

The clockwork mechanism groaned to life. Slowly, the great beam
turned.

It crossed the drowned village, the shattered harbor, and the
storm-dark sea.

Where the light touched the creatures, the water boiled.

Their cry split the night.

Windows burst throughout the tower. Elias fell to his knees,
hands clamped over his ears. Below, Tomas and Doctor Brin
screamed. Mara stood beside the lamp, blood running from her
nose, but she did not look away.

The beam swept on.

The black shapes writhed. One by one, they sank beneath
the waves. The vast eye near the reef closed last.

This is not ending, the voice said inside Elias.

It is keeping, he answered.

That is all a lighthouse ever promises.

The presence withdrew.

Wind weakened. Waves settled. Overhead, the clouds opened, revealing a
handful of stars.

For the rest of the night, the four survivors remained
in the lantern room. The tower had cracked near its
foundation, but it stood.

At dawn, they saw what remained of Bellwether.

The harbor was ruined. Fifteen houses had been swept away,
and every boat was gone. Yet most of the villagers
had reached high ground. From the hills, they raised blankets
and lanterns in greeting.

The sea had returned to its old boundary.

No trace of the road remained.

In the weeks that followed, Bellwether rebuilt. Scholars came from
the capital to question everyone. They listened gravely to stories
of the drowned city, the temple, and the creatures beneath
the waves.

Then they declared that an unusual tide had caused mass
confusion.

Mayor Creel agreed with them, having discovered that explanations were
easier to coordinate than disasters.

Tomas never went to sea again. He opened a carpentry
shop and made furniture that could survive being submerged. Doctor
Brin kept the captain's journal locked in a cabinet, along
with her empty pistol.

Elias repaired the lighthouse.

He replaced the broken glass, rebuilt the mechanism, and fitted
the door with three new locks. Every dawn, without fail,
he lit the lamp.

Mara became his apprentice.

"You are still too curious," he told her one evening
as they polished the brass lens together.

"You are still too old," she replied.

"Both statements can be true."

Outside, the sea lay calm beneath a violet sky.

Years later, when Elias finally left the lighthouse, the villagers
found him sitting in his chair beside the lamp. His
hands rested on the head of his cane. His eyes
were closed, and there was a peaceful expression on his
face.

The light was burning.

Mara buried him on the cliff where he could face
the horizon. Then she climbed the tower, trimmed the wick,
and took her place as keeper.

She served for fifty-one years.

During that time, ships were lost, storms came, and the
village changed its name twice. The old stories became children's
tales again. Few believed there had ever been a road
beneath the sea.

But every night, Mara lit the lamp.

And sometimes, when fog covered the water and no stars
shone overhead, she saw other stars opening far below.

On those nights, she turned the flame as high as
it would go.

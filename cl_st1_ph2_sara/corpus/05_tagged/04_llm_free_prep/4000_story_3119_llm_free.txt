### The Mapmaker of Lost Hours

Every morning at seven, Mr. Vale opened his shop on
Bellweather Street and placed a brass clock in the window.

The clock had no hands.

Beneath it, on a small card written in his careful
script, were the words:

**REPAIRS MADE TO TIME, MEMORY, AND DIRECTION.
NO REFUNDS.**

Most people assumed it was a joke.

Bellweather Street was full of such eccentricities. There was a
baker who sold bread shaped like sleeping animals, a tailor
who refused to sew pockets into wedding clothes, and a
bookseller who arranged novels according to the weather in which
they should be read. Mr. Vale's sign caused little comment.

Inside his shop, however, the walls were covered with maps.

They were not ordinary maps. None showed countries, roads, oceans,
or borders. One chart displayed all the places where a
person might feel brave. Another traced the routes by which
apologies traveled. A third, drawn in fading silver ink, showed
shortcuts between one regret and another.

Mr. Vale was a mapmaker, though he had not always
been one. Once, long ago, he had been a watchmaker.
But watches, he discovered, were simple things. They only measured
the time people possessed. Maps could reveal where the missing
parts had gone.

For forty-two years, Mr. Vale had worked alone.

Then, one rainy Tuesday in November, a girl named Elia
Finch entered his shop carrying an empty glass jar.

She was sixteen, soaked to the skin, and angry in
the manner of someone who had been sad for too
long.

"Are you Mr. Vale?" she asked.

"I am when the door is open."

Elia looked behind her. The door stood open, admitting rain.

"Then yes," he added. "Today I am."

She set the jar on his counter. It was an
ordinary preserving jar with a blue ribbon tied around its
neck.

"I need you to fill this."

"With what?"

"Yesterday."

Mr. Vale regarded her over the rims of his spectacles.

"Yesterday is rarely sold by the jar."

"I'm not buying it. I'm asking you to find it."

"That is usually more expensive."

"I have six coins."

"Then you cannot afford yesterday."

Elia lifted her chin. "I heard you repair time."

"The sign says many things. It also says no refunds,
and nobody ever takes that part seriously."

"My brother disappeared yesterday," she said.

The humor left Mr. Vale's face.

Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere in the back room,
a clock struck thirteen and then coughed apologetically.

Mr. Vale reached for the jar. "Tell me."

Elia's younger brother, Tomas, was ten years old and endlessly
curious. He collected keys, beetle wings, train tickets, broken lenses,
and questions nobody could answer. Their mother said curiosity would
lead him into trouble. Their father, before he died, had
said curiosity was only courage wearing untied shoes.

The previous afternoon, Elia and Tomas had gone to the
old railway station at the edge of town. No trains
had stopped there for twenty years, but Tomas liked searching
the platforms for forgotten objects.

At half past four, while Elia sat beneath the station
clock reading a book, Tomas called to her from the
far end of the platform.

"Elia! There's a train!"

She looked up.

There was no train.

She heard a whistle, though—faint, distant, impossible.

Then the station clock spun backward.

The world shuddered.

For an instant, Elia saw sunlight where there had been
rain. She saw passengers crowding the empty platform, dressed in
clothes from another age. Steam rolled across the tracks. A
black train waited beneath the iron roof, its windows glowing
gold.

Tomas stood beside an open carriage door.

Elia shouted his name.

He turned, excited rather than frightened.

"I think it goes to yesterday!" he called.

Then someone inside the carriage took his hand.

The train vanished.

The station was empty again. The clock read half past
four. Tomas was gone.

"I told the police," Elia said. "They searched the tracks
and the tunnels. They think he ran away."

"And you do not?"

"He left his collection of keys at home."

Mr. Vale nodded. That was evidence enough.

He untied the blue ribbon and held the jar to
his ear. "Why did you bring this?"

"It was Tomas's. He used it to catch fireflies."

"Good. Objects remember their owners, if they have been held
often enough."

"Can you find him?"

"Perhaps."

"Will you?"

Mr. Vale looked toward the handless clock in the window.

Years earlier, when his hair was black and his wife
was alive, he had boarded a train much like the
one Elia described. He had spent the rest of his
life trying to forget what happened aboard it.

He had failed.

"Lock the door," he said. "Turn the sign to CLOSED."

Elia did so.

Mr. Vale carried the jar to the largest table, where
a blank sheet of paper lay beneath a green-shaded lamp.
From a cabinet, he took a compass, a bottle of
ink, and a silver pocket watch.

The watch was warm in his hand. It had not
ticked for forty-two years.

"What are you doing?" Elia asked.

"Drawing a map."

"Of yesterday?"

"Of the road your brother took."

He placed the jar in the center of the paper
and opened it. At first, nothing happened. Then the scent
of summer grass drifted into the room.

A pale green light rose from the glass.

Within it appeared a tiny vision of Tomas laughing as
he ran through a field, chasing fireflies. The memory circled
the lamp once, then settled onto the paper.

Mr. Vale dipped his pen into the ink.

"Every journey leaves a shape," he explained. "Ordinary journeys are
easy. A walk to the market is a line. A
voyage across the sea is a curve. A trip into
yesterday is more complicated."

The pen began to move by itself.

It drew a railway track that looped around the page,
crossed its own path, and disappeared beneath the edge of
the paper. Along the track appeared stations with names such
as **THE HOUR BEFORE GOODBYE**, **THINGS NOT SAID**, and **THE
LAST PLACE YOU LOOK**.

At the center stood a black tower.

Mr. Vale's hand trembled.

"What is that?" Elia asked.

"The terminus."

"Is Tomas there?"

"If he has not yet been collected."

"Collected by whom?"

Mr. Vale folded the map before it could complete the
final line.

"The train will return tonight," he said. "Midnight, at the
old station. We must be waiting."

Elia studied him. "You've seen it before."

"Yes."

"You've ridden it."

"Yes."

"And you didn't come back alone."

Mr. Vale met her gaze.

"No," he said. "I came back alone."

At eleven forty-five, they stood beneath the broken clock at
Bellweather Station.

Rain swept across the empty platforms. Weeds grew between the
rails. The station windows were blind with dust.

Mr. Vale carried a leather case containing the map, the
jar, three pieces of white chalk, and the silver watch.
Elia carried nothing but her determination.

"When the train arrives," he said, "do not speak to
the conductor."

"Why?"

"He will ask where you wish to go. Any answer
gives him power."

"What if he asks my name?"

"Especially then."

A whistle sounded in the distance.

The station clock began to turn backward.

Midnight became eleven fifty-nine, then eleven fifty-eight. With each retreating
minute, the abandoned station changed. Broken lamps flared to life.
Dust vanished from the windows. Weeds sank into the ground.
Posters bloomed across the walls, advertising seaside holidays from fifty
years before.

Passengers appeared like figures remembered by a dream. They wore
gray coats and carried gray luggage. None had faces.

The black train slid into the station without disturbing the
rain.

Its doors opened.

A conductor stepped onto the platform. He was tall and
elegant, with a red carnation in his coat. Where his
face should have been, there was a clock.

Its hands pointed to midnight.

"Tickets," he said.

His voice came from inside the clock.

Mr. Vale opened his leather case and removed the unfinished
map.

The conductor tilted his head.

"A mapmaker," he said. "How tiresome."

"We require passage."

"Where do you wish to go?"

Mr. Vale said nothing.

The conductor turned to Elia. "And you, miss? Where do
you wish to go?"

She pressed her lips together.

"Perhaps you wish to see your father again."

Elia flinched.

Through the carriage window, she saw a man sitting beneath
a golden lamp. He was broad-shouldered, with laughing eyes and
ink stains on his fingers.

Her father.

He looked exactly as he had before the fever took
him. He smiled and raised one hand.

"Elia," he called. "I've been waiting."

She stepped toward the door.

Mr. Vale caught her wrist.

"That is not your father."

"He knows my name."

"Memories know our names."

The figure in the window continued smiling. But now his
smile seemed too still, as if painted on glass.

The conductor chuckled.

"Board," he said. "Or remain. The train is indifferent."

They entered.

Inside, the carriage was longer than the station, perhaps longer
than the town. Lamps swung overhead. Doors lined the narrow
corridor, each marked with a date.

Behind one door, Elia heard a baby crying. Behind another,
people applauded. From a third came the crash of breaking
glass and a woman's voice saying, "If only."

Mr. Vale unfolded the map.

A black line crept across it, drawing itself as the
train moved.

"Stay close," he said.

They passed through carriage after carriage. Some were empty. Others
held silent passengers watching scenes through the windows.

But the windows did not show the landscape outside. They
showed lost hours.

A soldier watched the morning before a battle, while his
mother cooked breakfast and sang. An old woman watched herself
refuse a marriage proposal beneath an apple tree. A boy
stared at the moment he had let go of a
red balloon. Each passenger leaned toward the glass, longing to
enter what was gone.

"Why don't they leave?" Elia whispered.

"They believe the next station will take them back."

"Will it?"

"No. The train feeds on their hope."

At the end of the seventh carriage, they found a
dining room.

A feast covered every table. Roast birds shone under silver
lids. Fruit glittered in crystal bowls. Cakes rose in sugared
towers. The air smelled of cinnamon, smoke, and warm bread.

Tomas sat alone at the nearest table, eating blackberry pie.

"Elia!" he cried.

She ran to him and threw her arms around his
shoulders.

"You idiot," she said into his hair.

"I knew you'd come."

"You boarded a ghost train."

"I wanted to see where it went."

"You could have died."

"I don't think people die here."

Mr. Vale stood at the doorway, pale and rigid.

At the far end of the dining car sat a
woman in a yellow dress.

She was perhaps thirty years old. Dark curls framed her
face, and a tiny scar crossed her left eyebrow. Before
her rested two cups of tea, steam rising from both.

"Jonas," she said.

Elia looked at Mr. Vale.

He gripped the silver watch so tightly that its chain
cut into his palm.

The woman smiled. "You took your time."

Tomas whispered, "Who is she?"

"My wife," Mr. Vale said.

The train gave a gentle lurch.

The door behind them slammed shut.

The conductor entered from the opposite end of the carriage.

"Welcome back, Mr. Vale."

Mr. Vale did not look at him. He looked only
at the woman.

Her name was Mara. Forty-two years ago, they had argued
at this very station. She wanted to leave Bellweather and
travel the world. He wanted to remain in his shop,
repairing the watches of people who never went anywhere.

Mara boarded the train in anger. Jonas followed.

When the conductor offered them a choice, they discovered the
train could carry one of them home—but only one. Mara
tricked Jonas into taking the place. She kissed him, pressed
the silver watch into his hand, and stepped backward as
the carriage door closed between them.

He had spent four decades searching for the road to
find her.

Now she sat ten paces away.

"You haven't aged," he said.

"Time is polite here," Mara replied. "It does not touch
us unless invited."

"I tried to return."

"I know."

The conductor's clock face ticked.

"A reunion," he said. "How moving. Unfortunately, the boy cannot
leave."

Elia moved in front of Tomas. "He's coming with us."

"The train carried three living passengers from the station tonight.
It must return with three."

"You mean one of us has to stay?"

"One?" The conductor's voice was amused. "Mara has waited forty-two
years. Surely Mr. Vale intends to rescue her as well."

The meaning settled over them.

Four people. Three places.

Mr. Vale closed his eyes.

The train accelerated. Through the windows, days and nights flashed
too quickly to count. The feast trembled on the tables,
though not a cup spilled.

"There must be another way," Elia said.

"There is always another way," said the conductor. "There is
simply never another price."

Mr. Vale opened the unfinished map.

The line had reached the black tower at its center.
Beneath it, words appeared in silver ink:

**ALL LOST HOURS BELONG TO THE CLOCK.**

He looked at the conductor's face.

Then at the handless clock drawn above the tower.

"Elia," he said softly, "the chalk."

She took the three white pieces from the leather case.

"Draw a door on the floor."

"What?"

"A door. Quickly."

Elia dropped to her knees. She drew a crooked rectangle
between the tables.

The conductor stepped forward. "Maps work poorly on moving trains."

"This isn't a map," Mr. Vale said. "It's a correction."

He opened the silver pocket watch. Inside the lid was
a miniature portrait of Mara, painted before their wedding.

The watch's hands began to move.

Once.

Twice.

Backward.

The conductor staggered. The hands on his clock face turned
with them.

"What have you done?"

"Invited time in."

A wind tore through the carriage. Lamps swung wildly. The
walls groaned. All around them, the train began to age.

Velvet seats split and faded. Silver tarnished. Wood cracked. The
cakes collapsed into dust. The faceless passengers cried out as
the lost hours in the windows shattered like mirrors.

Mara rose from her chair.

For the first time, age touched her.

Silver streaked her dark hair. Lines formed around her eyes.
Her yellow dress faded at the hem. Forty-two years passed
across her face in seconds, but her smile remained.

"Jonas," she said, laughing through tears. "You finally learned to
repair a watch."

Mr. Vale pressed the watch into the center of the
chalk door.

The floor opened onto Bellweather Station.

Rain fell upward through the doorway.

"Go!" he shouted.

Elia pushed Tomas through first. He tumbled onto the platform
below—or above, for directions had lost their meaning. Elia followed,
catching the edge with both hands.

Mara stepped toward the opening.

The conductor seized her arm.

"You belong to the train."

"No," Mr. Vale said. "She belonged to an hour. And
that hour is over."

He threw the glass jar.

It struck the conductor's clock face and shattered.

The memory of Tomas's summer field burst across the dining
car. Hundreds of green lights streamed through the air. Fireflies
filled the conductor's hollow face, glowing behind cracked glass.

He released Mara.

Mr. Vale caught her hand and jumped.

They fell through the chalk door as the train came
apart behind them.

For one endless instant, Elia saw every carriage split open.
Lost birthdays, final conversations, missed chances, and unfinished farewells poured
into the darkness like stars. The passengers reached toward them—not
to hold them back, but to let them go.

Then the black train folded into a single silver line.

The line became the minute hand of the station clock.

Time struck midnight.

The four travelers lay on the wet platform.

The abandoned station was abandoned once more. Weeds covered the
tracks. Rain tapped against broken glass. There was no train,
no conductor, and no golden light.

Tomas sat up first.

"That," he said, "was incredible."

Elia hit him with both fists and then hugged him
so tightly he complained he could not breathe.

Mr. Vale knelt beside Mara.

She was no longer thirty. Her hair was mostly silver,
and her hands showed the marks of years she had
never lived. But she was alive.

"I am sorry," he said.

"For what?"

"For letting you stay."

"You didn't let me. I chose."

"I should have found you sooner."

"You found me when you knew how."

She touched his face, tracing the deep lines beside his
mouth.

"You became old," she said.

"So did you."

"Only just now. I don't recommend doing it all at
once."

He laughed, and the sound was so unfamiliar that Elia
looked over in surprise.

They returned to Bellweather Street before dawn.

During the following weeks, Mr. Vale taught Elia how to
draw roads that did not exist. She learned that loneliness
tended to form islands, while hope preferred bridges. She learned
to use blue ink for futures, red for promises, and
gold for places that appeared only once.

Tomas was forbidden to enter abandoned stations, open mysterious doors,
follow unnatural lights, or board any form of transportation not
listed on an official schedule.

He obeyed for almost a month.

Mara moved into the rooms above the shop. She filled
them with music, arguments, and plants that Mr. Vale repeatedly
forgot to water. Sometimes she stood before the maps and
touched the routes of the life she had missed. Yet
she never asked to travel backward again.

"The past is a beautiful country," she told Elia one
afternoon. "But it has terrible border guards."

Years passed.

Bellweather Street changed. The baker retired. The tailor finally agreed
to sew pockets into wedding clothes. The bookseller began organizing
novels by the kind of courage required to finish them.

Mr. Vale grew older.

One winter morning, he did not come downstairs to open
the shop.

Elia, now twenty-three and his apprentice in everything but name,
found him in bed beside Mara. His eyes were closed,
and his hands rested peacefully over the silver watch.

He had left no letter.

Instead, on his worktable lay a completed map.

It showed Bellweather Street at dawn. A road of golden
ink ran from the shop, across a wide field, and
toward a distant station where no clock hung above the
platform.

At the bottom, Mr. Vale had written:

**DIRECTION IS NOT THE SAME AS DESTINATION.
GETTING LOST IS NOT THE SAME AS BEING GONE.**

Mara framed the map.

Elia placed the handless brass clock in the window and
opened the shop at seven.

For many years afterward, people came to her with missing
moments.

A woman asked to recover the courage she had possessed
as a child. A man wanted to find the final
joke his brother had told him. An old sailor searched
for the exact blue of a sea he had crossed
in his youth. Elia helped when she could and refused
when she must.

She never sold yesterday by the jar.

But sometimes, on rainy nights, a whistle sounded beyond the
rooftops. The clocks along Bellweather Street paused, listening.

Whenever that happened, Elia stepped outside.

She would see a silver line moving across the dark
sky—not a train, not quite, but perhaps the memory of
one. It traveled toward some country beyond regret, carrying no
prisoners and keeping no fare.

And from very far away, she would hear Mr. Vale
laughing, as though he had finally found a road that
led exactly where he meant to go.

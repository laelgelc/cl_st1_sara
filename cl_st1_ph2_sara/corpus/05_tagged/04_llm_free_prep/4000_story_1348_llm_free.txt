# The Museum of Forgotten Weather

On the morning the sky disappeared, Mara Vale was repairing
a thunderstorm.

It lay open across her workbench like a torn black
sail. Thin veins of lightning trembled beneath its surface, bright
enough to cast sharp shadows over her hands. Every few
seconds, the storm gave a low, irritated growl.

"Easy," Mara whispered.

She tightened a copper filament with a pair of glass
tweezers. The thunderstorm snapped at her, sending a spark into
the ceiling.

From behind a shelf of bottled fog, her cat lifted
his head.

"It missed," Mara told him.

The cat, whose name was Monday despite being born on
a Thursday, looked unconvinced.

Outside the museum windows, the people of Bellwether had begun
to gather in the street. They pointed upward, murmuring. Children
clung to their parents. Shopkeepers stood in open doorways, forgetting
counters and customers.

Above them was nothing.

Not darkness. Not cloud. Not blue. Nothing at all.

The roofs and chimneys rose toward a blankness so complete
that the eye slid away from it. Birds refused to
fly. Smoke from the bakery stopped at the height of
the clock tower, spread flat, and drifted sideways.

Mara tried not to look.

She bent over the damaged storm and guided the filament
through the torn edge.

The museum's front bell rang.

"Closed," she called.

It rang again.

"The Museum of Forgotten Weather is closed until ten."

The bell rang a third time, then continued ringing in
short, frantic bursts.

Monday jumped down from the shelf and vanished into a
cabinet labeled MINOR HAIL, UNSEASONAL.

Mara sighed, covered the thunderstorm with a sheet of insulating
silk, and crossed the long exhibition hall.

The museum occupied what had once been Bellwether's railway station.
Its departure board now listed weather no longer seen in
the town:

SUMMER RAIN — DELAYED INDEFINITELY
MORNING MIST — ARCHIVED
NORTHERN LIGHTS — ONE NIGHT ONLY
GENTLE SNOW — CANCELLED

Glass cases filled the hall. One contained the last golden
sunset over Bellwether Bay, folded neatly into twelve glowing squares.
Another held a week of autumn wind, which paced in
circles and occasionally rattled its label. In the far corner,
behind three locks, slept the Great Blizzard of Year Eight.

Mara opened the door.

Mayor Finch stood on the steps, hatless and pale.

"You've seen it," he said.

"I'm avoiding it."

"The sky is gone."

"So everyone keeps saying."

"Can skies simply go?"

"Nothing simply goes," said Mara. "People misplace things. They trade
them. They cut them into pieces and store them badly.
Sometimes they forget them."

The mayor looked over his shoulder. The blankness made his
face seem unfinished.

"Can you fix it?"

"I repair weather, Mayor. I don't manufacture heavens."

"But this museum has everything."

"This museum has what Bellwether threw away."

"That sounds like the same thing."

Mara closed the door in his face.

She had nearly reached her workbench when something scraped against
the other side of the door.

A small voice said, "Please."

Mara stopped.

She returned and opened it a few inches. A boy
stood there, perhaps ten years old, with a patched green
coat and muddy boots. She recognized him as the child
who delivered telegrams for the post office. His name was
Eli.

He held a blue envelope.

"This came for you," he said.

"There are no trains and no telegraph lines."

"It was in the dead-letter box."

"How long?"

"The postmaster thinks eighty years."

The envelope bore Mara's name in faded silver ink.

Not her full name, but the secret name her mother
had used when Mara was small.

Little Lark.

She snatched the envelope.

"Who sent it?"

"There's no return address."

Mara shut the door, locked it, and leaned against the
wood.

For a long time she did not move.

Then she broke the seal.

Inside was a railway ticket and a note.

The ticket was for the 9:17 evening service from Bellwether
to the Horizon. The date printed on it was today.

The note contained seven words.

YOU KEPT THE WEATHER. I KEPT THE SKY.

Mara read them twice.

Underneath, in smaller writing, was a name.

Silas Vale.

Her father.

Monday emerged from the hail cabinet and sniffed the ticket.
The hairs along his spine rose.

"I know," Mara said.

She had not seen Silas Vale for twenty-three years.

When Mara was eight, Bellwether had been famous for its
weather. Storms rolled in from the sea with grand black
banners. Winters filled every lane with snow. Spring mornings smelled
of wet stone and apple blossom. Summers ended each day
in red and violet fire.

Silas had been the town's weather keeper. He measured rainfall,
named hurricanes, rang the warning bells, and taught Mara to
listen for snow in the silence between sounds.

Then the council built the Great Engine.

The Engine was designed to make Bellwether perfect. It pushed
storms out to sea. It swept fog from the harbor
and pulled rain into underground tanks. It polished the sky
to an everlasting, harmless blue.

The town celebrated.

Silas did not.

"Weather is not an inconvenience," he told them. "It is
how the world remembers change."

No one listened.

Year by year, Bellwether became pleasant. Predictable. Empty.

The same blue morning arrived every day. The same mild
breeze blew from the west. Children grew up without jumping
into puddles or waking to frost. Painters stopped painting the
sky because there was nothing new to see.

Silas began collecting what the Engine removed. A jar of
mist. A thunderclap wrapped in wool. Six snowflakes in a
silver matchbox.

When the council ordered him to stop, he disappeared.

Mara had continued his work. She turned the abandoned station
into the museum, rescued every scrap of weather she could,
and refused to speak her father's name.

Until now.

At sunset—if sunset still existed—Mara stood on the old platform
with a suitcase in one hand and the ticket in
the other. Monday sat inside the suitcase, having rejected all
arguments against his coming.

Eli, the telegram boy, waited beside her.

"You are not invited," Mara said.

"I want to see where the sky went."

"You have deliveries."

"No one's sending telegrams. They're all staring upward."

"You're ten."

"Eleven."

"That is worse."

A whistle sounded in the distance.

The rails, rusted for decades, began to hum. White vapor
curled along the track, bringing the smell of coal smoke,
cold iron, and distant rain.

A locomotive emerged from behind the empty signal house.

It was the smallest train Mara had ever seen: one
black engine, one passenger carriage, and a red caboose. Stars
glittered in its windows, though the blank sky overhead had
none.

The train stopped.

Its door opened.

No conductor appeared.

Mara climbed aboard. Eli followed before she could stop him.

The carriage contained rows of blue velvet seats. Most were
occupied.

A woman in a wedding dress held a bouquet of
icicles. An old sailor slept beneath a cloud. Three identical
girls whispered together, each with a rainbow tied around her
waist. At the back sat a tall man with an
umbrella open over his head.

Rain fell beneath the umbrella and nowhere else.

Mara and Eli found an empty compartment. The train lurched
forward.

Bellwether slid past the window: the museum, the post office,
the clock tower, the rows of frightened faces. Then the
town vanished into gray vapor.

"Where are we?" Eli asked.

"Between forecasts."

"Is that a real place?"

"It is tonight."

Monday crawled from the suitcase and settled on Eli's knees.

The door opened. A conductor entered, though Mara was certain
no one had been in the corridor.

He had a neat white beard and wore a uniform
stitched with tiny silver clouds. A barometer hung from his
watch chain.

"Tickets."

Mara handed him hers.

He examined it and clipped a crescent moon from one
corner.

"And the boy?"

"He doesn't have one," Mara said.

The conductor looked at Eli.

"I can pay," Eli said, producing three buttons, a marble,
and a copper coin.

"Not with those."

"I also have half a peppermint."

"Return fare," said the conductor, "is one true memory."

Eli frowned. "What happens to the memory?"

"You won't have it anymore."

Mara reached for her purse. "I'll pay money."

"Money cannot buy a journey to the Horizon."

"Take one of mine."

"No substitutions."

Eli looked down at Monday. "Can I choose?"

"You must."

After a moment, Eli said, "Take the day I broke
Mrs. Wren's window."

The conductor shook his head. "A true memory is not
merely a thing that happened. It is a thing that
made you who you are."

Eli became quiet.

The train rattled onward. Outside, pale shapes moved through the
vapor: towers made of rain, mountains with lightning sleeping in
their caves, flocks of clouds migrating south.

Finally Eli said, "Take the last morning with my sister."

Mara looked at him.

He continued before she could speak.

"She was sick for a long time. That morning it
snowed, though it wasn't winter. She asked me to open
the window. We watched the flakes melt on the sill.
She said each one was a letter the sky had
forgotten how to read."

The conductor's expression softened.

"Are you certain?"

"No," Eli said. "But take it."

The conductor touched two fingers to Eli's forehead.

A small white light appeared there. He drew it out
slowly, spinning it into a thread, then wound it around
his ticket punch.

Eli blinked.

A moment later, he looked puzzled.

"Why am I crying?"

"No reason I can explain," said the conductor.

He clipped Eli's peppermint wrapper and handed it back as
a ticket.

Mara waited until he had gone.

"What was your sister's name?" she asked.

"I don't have a sister."

Mara turned to the window so he would not see
her face.

Hours passed, or perhaps years. Time behaved poorly between forecasts.

At last the vapor thinned.

The Horizon appeared ahead.

It was not a line, as Mara had always imagined,
but a city.

It stretched along the edge of the world, built upon
cliffs of violet stone. Thousands of towers rose into a
vast blue dome. Bridges arched between them. Windmills turned without
wind. Great ropes descended from the dome and disappeared into
streets paved with mirrors.

Above the city, the stolen sky curved from end to
end.

Mara knew it immediately. Bellwether's blue—too smooth, too perfect—shone over
the towers.

The train entered a station crowded with clocks. Some ran
backward. Some had thirteen hours. One enormous clock displayed not
the time, but the words ALMOST TOO LATE.

The conductor opened the door.

"Last train returns at dawn," he said. "Miss it, and
you remain until the world catches up."

"Where do I find Silas Vale?" Mara asked.

The conductor pointed to the tallest tower.

At its summit, someone had built a house.

The climb took them through the entire city.

They crossed a market where merchants sold bottled moonlight and
secondhand shadows. They passed a school in which children practiced
making clouds. They walked along an avenue crowded with refugees
from ruined climates: a monsoon carrying all its rain in
suitcases; a warm current shivering beneath a blanket; a family
of breezes who spoke in the leaves of dead trees.

Everywhere Mara looked, she saw weather that had been driven
out, dammed, burned, diverted, or forgotten.

"All of this came from somewhere," Eli said.

"All of it was unwanted somewhere," Mara replied.

At the foot of the tallest tower stood a door
painted red.

Mara knocked.

It opened at once.

Silas Vale had grown old.

That was Mara's first thought.

Her second was that he looked exactly the same.

His hair, once black, had turned white, and deep lines
marked his face. Yet his eyes were still storm gray.
His sleeves were still rolled to the elbows. A pencil
remained tucked behind one ear.

"Mara," he said.

She struck him.

The sound echoed up the tower.

Silas touched his cheek.

"I may have deserved that."

"You vanished."

"Yes."

"For twenty-three years."

"It was complicated."

"No. Repairing a thunderstorm is complicated. Abandoning your child is
simple."

Eli pretended to examine the red door. Monday did not
bother pretending. He hissed at Silas.

Silas stepped aside.

"Come in," he said.

His house was impossibly large inside. Maps covered every wall.
Barometers, thermometers, rain gauges, and instruments Mara could not name
crowded long tables. In the center of the room stood
a brass machine connected to ropes that vanished through the
ceiling.

The ropes trembled under immense strain.

"The sky," Mara said.

"I had to take it," Silas replied.

"You had to?"

"The Great Engine was beginning to damage more than weather.
The council kept increasing its reach. Perfect days require constant
correction. Every cloud became an error. Every wind, a fault.
Eventually the Engine would have flattened the seasons across the
entire coast."

"So you stole Bellwether's sky."

"I removed its anchor and brought it here."

"You could have told me."

"You were eight."

"I waited."

Silas looked away.

Mara hated him most for looking ashamed. Anger had been
easier when she imagined he had none.

He approached the brass machine. "The sky remained connected to
Bellwether through the Great Engine. For years, there was balance.
But three nights ago, the Engine began pulling again. Harder
than ever."

"The council shut it down years ago."

"Machines can continue doing what they were built to do
long after people forget why they built them."

A rope snapped.

The whole house shuddered.

Outside, the blue dome flickered.

"Bellwether's sky is collapsing," Silas said. "If the anchor breaks
completely, the blankness will spread."

"How far?"

"Everywhere."

Eli peered at the machine. "How do we stop it?"

Silas unrolled a map. It showed Bellwether on one side
and the Horizon on the other. Between them flowed dozens
of bright lines.

"We return the sky," he said.

Mara stared at him.

"You said the Engine would flatten the seasons."

"It will—unless someone destroys it at the exact moment the
sky is released. The controls are beneath the museum."

"The museum was built over the old station."

"The station was built over the Engine."

Mara remembered the locked door in the basement, the one
no key could open. She had always assumed it led
to a maintenance tunnel.

"You knew," she said.

"I hoped the museum would keep the weather safe until
Bellwether needed it again."

"You left me to do that alone."

"I did."

The honesty struck harder than an excuse.

Another rope snapped. A crack opened across the blue dome.

Through it came nothingness.

"Can you release the sky from here?" Mara asked.

"Yes."

"I can destroy the Engine."

"You would need to reach Bellwether before dawn."

Mara looked at the enormous clock visible through the window.

ALMOST TOO LATE had changed to NOW WOULD BE WISE.

They ran.

Down the tower, through the mirrored streets, past merchants covering
their moonlight and breezes calling warnings through the trees.

The station clock had begun to read REALLY, DO HURRY.

The last train waited with steam billowing around its wheels.

As Mara reached the platform, she realized Eli was no
longer beside her.

He stood ten paces back, staring at a woman in
a gray coat.

She held a small glass globe. Inside, snow fell upon
a miniature windowsill.

The woman smiled sadly. "You left something on the train,"
she told him.

The conductor appeared behind her, unwinding a white thread from
his ticket punch.

He touched it to Eli's forehead.

Eli gasped.

For several seconds, he could not speak. Then he whispered,
"Anna."

The memory returned all at once. Mara saw it in
his face—the pain, the love, the open window, the impossible
snow.

"The fare?" Mara asked.

The conductor glanced upward as another crack split the sky.

"Tonight," he said, "the railway will accept courage."

They boarded.

The train raced back between forecasts. Lightning ran beside it.
Rain struck the windows from below. The carriage shook so
violently that the passengers held one another upright.

Silas stood near the door, gripping the release lever connected
by some hidden mechanism to the sky.

"When we reach Bellwether," he told Mara, "you'll have nine
minutes."

"Why nine?"

"After that, the returning sky will strike the Engine. If
it is still operating—"

"What happens?"

"Bellwether becomes the center of a permanent storm."

Mara almost laughed. "The museum would be popular."

The train screamed into Bellwether Station at eight minutes before
dawn.

The town was dark. The blankness had descended to the
rooftops, erasing chimneys and upper windows. People fled through the
streets carrying lanterns.

Mara sprinted into the museum.

Seven minutes.

She crossed the exhibition hall, knocking over a case of
spring drizzle. It shattered. Rain spread across the tiles, fresh
and green-smelling.

Six minutes.

In the basement, she reached the locked door.

No key could open it.

She stepped back and hurled the suitcase into the lock.
It broke on the third strike. Monday shot out, offended
but uninjured.

Five minutes.

Beyond the door, stairs spiraled underground. Mara descended into the
roar of machinery.

The Great Engine filled a cavern beneath the town.

Its iron wheels were taller than houses. Pistons rose and
fell. Thousands of glass tubes carried captured wind, heat, moisture,
and light into a central furnace. Though rust covered every
surface, the Engine worked furiously, obeying instructions issued by people
long dead.

Four minutes.

Mara searched for a control panel.

There was none.

Only a brass plaque:

FAIR WEATHER, NOW AND ALWAYS.

Below it was a small red wheel.

She seized it and pulled.

Nothing happened.

Three minutes.

Eli appeared on the stairs.

"I told you to stay with the train!"

"You tell people lots of things."

He grabbed the wheel beside her.

Together they pulled.

It moved half an inch.

Above them came a sound Bellwether had not heard in
decades.

Thunder.

Two minutes.

The Engine accelerated. Glass tubes burst. Wind tore through the
cavern, spinning Mara around. Eli clung to the wheel. Monday
sank his claws into Mara's coat and climbed onto her
shoulder.

The sky was returning.

Mara pictured Silas at the station, his hand on the
lever. She wondered if he would leave again when this
was done.

If they survived, she decided, she would make him stay
long enough to answer every question.

One minute.

The wheel turned.

The central furnace opened.

Inside it burned a small, flawless blue flame: the heart
of Bellwether's perfect day.

Mara understood.

The Engine did not need to be smashed. It needed
to be given what it had spent decades rejecting.

"Eli!" she shouted. "Open the museum cases!"

"All of them?"

"All of them!"

He raced upstairs.

Mara tore the insulating silk from the thunderstorm she had
been repairing and dragged it down the steps. Lightning lashed
the walls. Thunder shook dust from the cavern roof.

Upstairs, glass began to break.

Autumn wind poured down the staircase, carrying red leaves. Summer
rain followed. Fog curled beneath the door. Snowflakes spun through
the gears.

Eli released the sunset. Gold and crimson light flooded the
cavern.

Mara lifted the thunderstorm.

"You wanted fair weather," she told the Engine. "Try all
of it."

She threw the storm into the furnace.

The world turned white.

For one suspended moment, there was no sound.

Then the Great Engine gave a deep sigh.

Its wheels slowed.

Its pistons stopped.

The blue flame went out.

The sky fell into place.

Dawn broke over Bellwether in a thousand colors.

Clouds rolled across the bay, purple at their edges. Rain
swept through the streets. People shouted and ran for shelter,
then stopped and lifted their faces. Children laughed as gutters
overflowed. The baker danced beneath his awning. Mayor Finch lost
one shoe in a puddle and did not seem to
mind.

The first true sunrise in twenty-three years burned above the
sea.

Mara climbed from the basement.

The museum was ruined.

Every case stood open. The forgotten weather had escaped. Mist
drifted through the ticket office. Snow covered the platform. A
warm summer breeze wandered lazily among the rafters.

Silas waited in the exhibition hall.

"You did it," he said.

"We did."

He looked toward the open doors. "Bellwether has its weather
back. There is no need for a museum now."

Mara surveyed the broken cases, the soaked labels, and the
Great Blizzard snoring beneath a bench.

"There is always a need to remember," she said. "But
perhaps we won't keep everything behind glass."

Silas nodded.

The silence between them was crowded with twenty-three years.

"Will you stay?" Mara asked at last.

"If you'll let me."

"I haven't forgiven you."

"I know."

"You'll work in the basement."

"I expected worse."

"You'll repair the roof, catalogue the fog, and explain why
there is a cyclone in the lost-property cupboard."

Silas smiled. "Your cyclone?"

"Yours."

Monday approached, sniffed Silas's boots, and bit his ankle.

"His name is Monday," Mara said.

"Of course it is."

Eli stood at the front doors, watching rain fall into
the morning light.

"My sister would have liked this," he said.

Mara joined him.

Across Bellwether, windows opened. Buckets and bowls appeared on doorsteps.
Somewhere a child screamed with delight at the sight of
a rainbow.

"Do you think the weather will stay?" Eli asked.

"No," Mara said. "That's the point."

Years later, visitors to Bellwether still came to see the
Museum of Forgotten Weather.

They found no storms trapped in cases and no sunsets
folded behind glass. Instead, the museum kept records: the first
snow after the blank sky, the hurricane that removed Mayor
Finch's chimney, the summer of seven rainbows, the winter when
the harbor froze and the whole town learned to skate
badly.

Mara became the weather keeper, though she refused to predict
anything with certainty.

Silas worked in the basement and grew tomatoes on the
roof. He and Mara argued often. In time, they also
learned how to speak.

Eli became a conductor on the railway to the Horizon.
He accepted many forms of payment, but never memories.

And above Bellwether, the sky changed endlessly.

Some days it was clear. Some days it was wild.
Sometimes it rained when no rain had been promised. Sometimes
the clouds gathered and passed without releasing a drop.

No one called this perfect.

They called it weather.

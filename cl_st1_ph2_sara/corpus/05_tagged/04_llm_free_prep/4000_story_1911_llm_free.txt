The Clockmaker of Bellweather

Every clock in Bellweather stopped at 3:17 on the morning
the sea disappeared.

Pendulums froze mid-swing. Pocket watches fell silent in waistcoats hung
over chairs. The great brass clock in the railway station
held its minute hand above the third notch, though the
last train had passed through the town nearly twenty years
before. Even the kitchen clock in Mara Venn's house, which
had always run seven minutes fast, stopped at precisely the
same moment as all the others.

Mara did not notice until dawn.

She woke to silence. Not ordinary silence, with gulls crying
over the roofs and waves striking the seawall below the
cliffs, but a deep, padded stillness. It seemed to press
against the windows.

She left her narrow bed, pulled on her boots, and
went downstairs to the workshop.

Her grandfather was already there.

Orin Venn sat at his workbench, surrounded by open clocks.
Springs and gears lay in careful rows beneath the yellow
lamplight. His silver hair was untidy, and he wore a
magnifying lens over one eye.

"Grandfather," Mara said, "the clocks have stopped."

"I know."

"All of them?"

"All of them."

He did not look surprised. That frightened her more than
the silence.

Mara crossed the workshop and opened the front door.

Bellweather stood on a cliff above the Northern Sea. For
as long as anyone remembered, the sea had been visible
at the end of every street: a dark, restless line
beneath the sky. Fishermen measured their lives by its tides.
Children learned to swim before they learned to read. Salt
whitened the houses, corroded hinges, and settled into bread.

But that morning, beyond the seawall, there was no water.

The sea had withdrawn past the horizon.

Where it had been lay a plain of wet sand,
black stone, and silver pools stretching beneath a colorless sky.
Stranded boats leaned on their sides. Fish twisted weakly in
the mud. Far in the distance, things that had been
hidden for centuries glimmered in the gray light: towers, roads,
and the broken roofs of a drowned city.

The townspeople gathered along the seawall.

Some prayed. Some shouted. A group of fishermen climbed down
the cliff path with buckets, already arguing about who owned
the exposed seabed. Mayor Holt rang the town bell until
its rope broke, though no sound came from the bronze
mouth.

Mara turned to her grandfather.

"What happened?"

Orin removed the lens from his eye.

"Something has taken the tide," he said.

"That isn't possible."

"Neither is a town full of stopped clocks."

He reached beneath his workbench and brought out a wooden
box. Mara had seen it before but had never known
what it contained. It was made of dark wood, bound
with green copper, and marked with the image of a
heron holding a key in its beak.

Orin opened it.

Inside lay a watch.

It was larger than an ordinary pocket watch and black
as coal. Its face had no numbers. Instead, twelve tiny
symbols circled a single silver hand: a shell, a flame,
a closed eye, a crown, a door, and others Mara
did not recognize. The hand pointed to the symbol of
an empty cup.

Unlike every other clock in Bellweather, the watch was ticking.

Mara leaned closer.

"What is it?"

"The Tidekeeper."

"Who made it?"

"I did," Orin said. "A very long time ago."

Mara stared at him. Her grandfather had been old all
her life, but not impossibly old. The watch looked ancient.
Its case was worn smooth, and fine grains of sand
clung beneath its glass.

Orin closed the box.

"There is a place beneath the sea called Lyr," he
said. "Before Bellweather was built, Lyr stood on this coast.
Its people learned to bind time into machines. Not merely
hours and minutes, but seasons, memories, even promises. They believed
anything could be preserved if given a mechanism strong enough."

"And you lived there?"

"Yes."

Mara wanted to laugh. Instead, she looked through the open
door at the drowned towers rising from the exposed seabed.

"What happened to it?"

"We tried to preserve too much."

Orin's voice softened.

"At the center of Lyr was a machine called the
Heart Clock. It governed the tides. We built it because
storms had taken too many ships and too many children.
At first, it worked. We could slow the sea, calm
it, command it to retreat. Then the king ordered us
to stop time around the city so Lyr would never
decay."

He paused.

"Time does not enjoy being commanded. The Heart Clock failed.
The sea rose. Lyr sank. Only a few escaped."

"And now the sea is gone because the machine has
started again?"

"Not started," Orin said. "It has been winding itself for
three hundred years. Last night, it reached the end."

"What happens next?"

"At sunset, the Heart Clock will release everything it has
held back."

Mara looked again at the endless plain.

"The sea will return."

"All at once."

Bellweather would vanish beneath it.

Orin stood, but his hands trembled against the bench. Only
then did Mara see how pale he was.

"You must go to Lyr," he said.

"Me?"

"The Heart Clock can be stopped with the Tidekeeper. But
the road is long, and my heart is no longer
suited to hurrying."

"Then I'll help you."

"You cannot help an old man move faster than his
bones."

"And you cannot expect me to find a machine in
a drowned city I've never seen."

Orin pushed the wooden box into her hands.

"The watch will guide you. Follow the silver hand. When
you reach the Heart Clock, place the Tidekeeper into its
center. Then turn the crown backward until the mechanism stops."

"That sounds too simple."

"It is simple," he said. "Simple things are not always
easy."

Outside, the town erupted in cheers. The fishermen had discovered
chests scattered among the wrecks below the cliff. Men and
women rushed down with carts, shovels, ropes, and sacks. Even
Mayor Holt joined them, his coat flapping behind him.

"Shouldn't we warn them?" Mara asked.

"I have tried."

Orin nodded toward the street.

On the wall beside the shop door hung a notice
written in his precise hand:

THE SEA WILL RETURN AT SUNSET. LEAVE BELLWEATHER. SEEK HIGH
GROUND.

Someone had drawn a smiling fish beneath it.

"People believe warnings only when danger has already arrived," Orin
said.

Mara put the box into her satchel. She took a
coil of rope, a hammer, two bottles of water, and
the loaf of bread left from supper. At the door,
she turned.

"What if I fail?"

Her grandfather lowered himself into his chair.

"Then try to be somewhere beautiful when the sea returns."

The journey across the seabed began easily.

Mara followed the old harbor road, passing stranded fishing boats
and townspeople gathering coins from the mud. Children chased crabs
through shallow pools. Merchants inspected barnacled statues and spoke excitedly
of museums and collectors.

As Mara walked farther, the voices faded.

The seabed was not flat. Deep channels cut through it,
and hills of black rock rose like the backs of
buried animals. Thick weeds clung to her boots. The air
smelled of salt, rot, and storms.

The Tidekeeper's silver hand moved whenever she changed direction. It
led her east, toward the drowned city.

By midmorning, she reached the first ruins.

A stone arch stood alone in the mud. Beyond it
stretched a road paved with white shells. Houses leaned against
one another, their windows crowded with anemones. Water dripped from
balconies carved in the shapes of wings.

Mara imagined people walking there beneath banners, carrying baskets, calling
to friends. She imagined Orin as a young man hurrying
through those streets with tools in his pockets.

She had gone only a few steps beneath the arch
when a voice called, "Wait!"

A boy scrambled over a mound of coral behind her.

He was perhaps fourteen, two years younger than Mara, with
a red scarf around his neck and a canvas sack
on his back. Mara recognized him as Tovan Reed, the
son of a fisherman. He had a reputation for stealing
apples, sleeping through sermons, and once setting fire to the
mayor's privy.

"You shouldn't be here," Mara said.

"Neither should you."

"I have work to do."

"So do I."

He opened his sack. It contained six tarnished goblets, a
silver plate, and the jeweled hilt of a sword.

"You're robbing the dead."

"They aren't using any of it."

"The sea will return at sunset."

"I heard your grandfather's warning."

"And?"

Tovan shrugged.

"My father says Orin Venn has breathed clock oil for
so long that his thoughts have turned into gears."

"Then go back."

"I would, but I saw you heading here with no
treasure sack and a very serious face. That usually means
something interesting is happening."

"It means something dangerous is happening."

"Even better."

Mara walked on. Tovan followed.

She tried ignoring him, then insulting him, then threatening to
strike him with the hammer. None worked.

"You'll get lost without me," he said. "I know how
to read the old fishermen's maps."

"This city isn't on any fishermen's map."

"Then you'll get lost with me, which is friendlier."

The Tidekeeper led them toward the center of Lyr.

The deeper they went, the taller the ruins became. They
crossed gardens filled with glass trees and passed a temple
whose stone doors opened and closed with the wind. In
a public square, hundreds of statues stood facing a dry
fountain. Each statue had the same expression: eyes raised, mouth
open, as if watching a great wave descend.

At noon, the ground trembled.

A low roar rolled across the seabed.

Mara climbed a fallen column and looked back toward Bellweather.
Along the horizon, a thin blue line had appeared.

The sea was returning.

Not quickly yet, but it was returning.

"We should go back," Tovan said.

"You should."

"You keep saying that as if I'm going to listen."

They ate bread while walking. The watch guided them through
a market and down a staircase into darkness.

Mara lit the small oil lamp in her satchel.

They entered a tunnel lined with metal pipes. Some were
no wider than her finger; others were large enough to
crawl through. Within them came a faint whispering.

Tovan put his ear against one.

"Voices," he said.

Mara listened.

At first, she heard only the hiss of trapped air.
Then words emerged.

Come home before dark.

Don't forget the blue ribbon.

I forgive you.

A woman's laughter followed, bright and distant.

Mara stepped back.

"What is this place?"

"The people of Lyr stored memories in machines," she said,
recalling Orin's explanation. "Perhaps these are some of them."

Tovan moved to another pipe.

His face changed.

"What do you hear?" Mara asked.

"Nothing."

But he continued listening until she pulled him away.

They went deeper. The whispers followed them, mixing into a
soft chorus. Some spoke with love, others with anger. A
mother sang to a child. A dying man asked someone
to open a window. A girl promised to wait on
a bridge.

The tunnel ended at a circular door.

Its center held a hollow the size of the Tidekeeper.

Mara placed the watch inside.

The door opened with a sigh.

Beyond it lay a chamber filled with clocks.

Thousands covered the walls, floor, and ceiling. Some were made
of gold, others of bone, crystal, or dark wood. There
were clocks with thirteen hands, clocks whose faces showed changing
landscapes, and tiny clocks beating like insect wings.

All were stopped.

As Mara and Tovan crossed the chamber, the Tidekeeper began
ticking faster.

At the far end stood three doors.

The first was painted with a shell, the second with
a crown, and the third with an open eye.

The silver hand pointed to the crown.

Mara reached for the handle.

"Wait," Tovan said. "The watch told us where to go
before. But this place was built by people who trapped
promises in pipes. Maybe following directions blindly is unwise."

"Do you have a better idea?"

He examined the doors.

Above them, nearly hidden beneath barnacles, were carved words. Mara
wiped them clean.

WHAT THE SEA GIVES, THE CROWN TAKES.

WHAT THE CROWN HIDES, THE EYE REVEALS.

WHAT THE EYE FEARS, THE SEA KEEPS.

"The watch points to the crown," Mara said.

"Yes, but what does the crown take?"

"Whatever the sea gives."

"And what has the sea given us?"

Mara thought of fish, salt, storms, and the city itself.
Then she understood.

"Time."

She looked down at the Tidekeeper. Its hand did not
point toward safety. It pointed toward what the Heart Clock
was doing.

The crown door would take their remaining time.

She chose the door painted with the open eye.

Behind it was a bridge.

It stretched across a vast underground chasm. Far below, gears
larger than houses turned slowly in darkness. There was no
railing. The bridge's stones were slick with seawater.

Halfway across, Tovan stopped.

"Mara."

A wall of water had entered the clock chamber behind
them. It crashed through the three doors, pouring into the
chasm.

The sea had reached Lyr.

"Run!" Mara shouted.

They ran.

Water swept around their ankles, then their knees. The bridge
shook as ancient machinery awakened below. One of the enormous
gears turned with a deafening groan.

Tovan slipped.

Mara caught his wrist, but his canvas sack dragged him
toward the edge. Goblets and coins spilled into the darkness.

"Let go of the sack!" she yelled.

"It's caught!"

She pulled the hammer from her belt and cut through
the strap with its claw. The treasure vanished into the
gears. Freed from its weight, Tovan climbed back onto the
bridge.

They reached the other side as the bridge collapsed.

The Tidekeeper now pointed straight ahead.

They climbed a spiral staircase while water thundered below. At
the top, they emerged into the highest tower in Lyr.

The Heart Clock filled it.

It was larger than Orin's entire shop. Rings of brass
rotated around a sphere of blue glass. Water moved inside
the sphere, though there were no pipes connected to it.
Lightning flashed in its depths.

Twelve iron figures stood around the machine, each holding a
chain. Kings, queens, priests, and clockmakers—all bowed beneath the strain.

One figure had Orin's face.

Mara approached it.

He was carved as a young man, but there was
no mistaking his sharp nose and deep-set eyes. The iron
Orin held a key in one hand and a broken
crown in the other.

"He helped build this," she whispered.

The tower shook.

Through the windows, Mara saw water flooding the streets. It
rose over houses and temples, turning roads into rivers. The
distant cliffs of Bellweather were no longer visible.

"Where does the watch go?" Tovan asked.

They searched the Heart Clock. At last Mara found a
round opening beneath the glass sphere.

She inserted the Tidekeeper.

The machine stopped.

For one breath, everything was silent.

Then a voice spoke.

"Mara Venn."

It came from the blue sphere.

A shape appeared inside: a woman with long dark hair
floating around her face. Her eyes were the same gray
as Mara's.

"Who are you?" Mara asked.

"The last keeper of Lyr."

The woman placed her hand against the inside of the
glass.

"And the first daughter of Orin Venn."

Mara could not speak.

"My mother?" she managed.

"Your mother's mother, many times removed. Blood is a road,
Mara. Long, but unbroken."

The silver crown of the Tidekeeper rose from its case.

"Turn it backward," Tovan said.

But the woman in the sphere smiled sadly.

"If you do, the Heart Clock will release its hold.
The sea will return to its natural course."

"That's what we want."

"Bellweather was built where the sea once stood. In its
natural course, the water will cover the town."

Mara looked through the window. The waves were already racing
toward the cliffs.

"Can the town be saved?"

"Yes. Turn the crown forward. The sea will retreat again,
and Bellweather will remain. But the Heart Clock must be
tended by a living keeper."

"For how long?"

"Until time ends."

Tovan stepped beside her.

"No."

The woman ignored him.

"You are of Orin's blood. The machine will accept you.
You will not age. You will not hunger. You will
keep the sea beneath your hand, and everyone in Bellweather
will live."

Mara imagined the workshop at dusk, lamplight warming the windows.
She imagined Orin waiting beside the silent clocks. She imagined
fishermen, children, and the foolish mayor. All could be saved.

She placed her fingers on the crown.

"What happened to the other keepers?" she asked.

The woman's face drifted closer.

"They remained."

"Inside the clock?"

"They became part of its memory."

Mara understood then. The woman was not alive. She was
an echo, like the voices in the pipes. A promise
preserved so perfectly that it did not know it had
been broken.

"How many people did Lyr sacrifice to keep itself safe?"
Mara asked.

The woman's expression did not change.

"As many as necessary."

"And was it ever enough?"

Outside, a tower disappeared beneath the waves.

Mara thought of her grandfather, who had escaped the city
but carried its guilt for three hundred years. He had
not sent her to preserve his work. He had sent
her to end it.

"Backward," she said.

She turned the crown.

The Heart Clock screamed.

Every clock in the tower began striking at once. The
iron figures shattered. The brass rings spun faster, shedding sparks.
Cracks spread across the blue sphere.

The woman inside pressed both hands against the glass.

"You will destroy Bellweather!"

"No," Mara said. "I'm giving it back its future."

The sphere broke.

Water exploded through the chamber.

Tovan seized Mara's arm as the floor tilted beneath them.
They were swept through a window and into the rising
sea.

The world became cold and green.

Mara lost the Tidekeeper. She saw it sinking below her,
its silver hand spinning freely. Then Tovan pulled her upward.

They broke the surface among rooftops.

The tower of the Heart Clock collapsed behind them, sending
a wave through the city. Mara grabbed a floating wooden
door. She and Tovan clung to it as the current
carried them over Lyr.

For a long time, there was only water.

Then they saw boats.

The fishermen of Bellweather had finally understood Orin's warning. They
had abandoned their treasure and launched every vessel they could.
Small boats crowded the sea, filled with townspeople. Some had
escaped to the hills above the cliffs; others had been
caught by the flood and pulled from rooftops.

A fishing boat hauled Mara and Tovan aboard.

"Bellweather?" Mara asked.

The fisherman looked away.

The town was gone.

Only the church steeple and the upper floor of the
railway station remained above water. Mara's home, the workshop, the
seawall, and every salt-whitened street had disappeared.

"Orin?" she whispered.

No one answered.

At sunset, the clocks began again.

Across the boats, watches ticked in wet pockets. The railway
clock, standing above the waves, moved from 3:17 to 3:18.

The sea settled into a gentle tide.

Years later, people built a new Bellweather farther inland.

They used stone recovered from the old town and timber
brought down from the northern forests. The fishermen made a
new harbor. The mayor, humbled and permanently unpopular, ordered warning
bells placed on every hill.

Mara opened a clock shop in the new town.

Above its door hung a wooden sign carved with a
heron holding a key in its beak. Tovan worked across
the street as a mapmaker, though his maps often included
sea monsters in places where no sea monsters had been
reported.

On quiet mornings, Mara repaired watches at the same workbench
her grandfather had used. It had washed ashore after the
flood, scarred but intact. In its smallest drawer, she found
a note written in Orin's hand.

There are things we preserve from love and things we
preserve from fear. Learn the difference.

Mara kept the note beneath her tools.

Sometimes divers came to her with objects recovered from the
drowned ruins: crystal gears, rusted keys, scraps of metal that
whispered when held to the ear. She locked such things
in iron boxes and refused to repair them.

Once, many years after the flood, a child brought her
a black watch found on the beach.

Its face had no numbers. Twelve symbols circled a single
silver hand.

The Tidekeeper was silent.

"Can you make it work?" the child asked.

Mara held the watch to her ear.

Deep inside, something shifted.

For an instant she heard waves, bells, and a woman's
voice calling her name from the other side of time.

Then all was still.

"Yes," Mara said, closing the case. "I think I could."

The child smiled.

Mara walked to the cliff and threw the watch into
the sea.

It flashed once beneath the sunlight before the water took
it.

Behind her, the clocks of Bellweather struck noon—not perfectly together,
but near enough. Their bells overlapped in a bright confusion
of sound.

Mara listened until the final note faded.

Then she returned to her shop, where a dozen broken
clocks waited to be given not more time, but the
right amount.

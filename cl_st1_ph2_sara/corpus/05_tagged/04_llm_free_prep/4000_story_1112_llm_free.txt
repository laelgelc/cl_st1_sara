Mara Venn first heard the lighthouse speak on a windless
night in November.

She was alone in the lantern room, polishing salt from
the glass, when the great brass lens turned without her
hand upon the crank. It rotated once, slowly, sending its
white beam across the black water, over the reef, and
into the empty northern sky.

Then a voice emerged from the speaking tube beside the
stairs.

"Mara," it said.

She froze. The polishing cloth slipped from her fingers.

The tube connected the lantern room to the kitchen below.
Her father had used it to call for tea when
his knees grew too stiff for the stairs, but he
had been dead for eleven years. No one else lived
on the island.

"Mara," the voice repeated. "A ship is coming."

She crossed the room and bent toward the mouth of
the tube.

"Who is this?"

For a moment, there was only the faint hollow whisper
of air passing through the walls.

Then the voice said, "You know me."

She did know it.

The voice belonged to her father.

Mara stepped back so quickly that her shoulder struck the
glass. The lens continued its silent turning. Outside, the sea
lay flat and dark beneath a moonless sky.

Her father had not possessed a gentle voice. He had
spoken as though every word were a stone to be
placed carefully in a wall. Even his kindness had sounded
stern.

A ship is coming, he had said.

Mara seized the binoculars from their hook and searched the
horizon. Nothing moved on the water. No lamps, no smoke,
no pale line of wake. The nearest harbor was forty
miles east, and no captain with sense would approach the
Black Teeth reef at night.

She returned to the tube.

"What ship?"

No answer came.

She called again, but the lighthouse had fallen silent.

At dawn, Mara found wreckage on the western shore.

A red life ring lay among the rocks, its painted
letters scraped away. Nearby floated a wooden box sealed with
tar. She dragged it above the tide line and pried
it open with a crowbar.

Inside was a blue wool coat, folded neatly around a
small silver handbell.

The coat was dry.

Mara lifted the bell. Its handle was engraved with the
shape of an eye. When she rang it, no sound
came, though she felt the metal tremble.

She searched the water again. There were no bodies, no
broken mast, no barrels or planks. Only the life ring,
the box, and the noiseless bell.

By noon, clouds had gathered in the west. Mara carried
the objects to the lighthouse and sent a message to
the mainland by radio.

"Possible wreck," she reported. "Unknown vessel. No survivors located."

Static hissed.

Then the harbor master answered. "No missing ships. Nothing expected
in your waters."

"I found wreckage."

"Old debris, perhaps."

"The coat was dry."

A pause followed.

"Mara," the harbor master said, "are you sleeping?"

"Enough."

"You've been alone too long."

"I prefer it."

"Yes," he said. "That's what concerns us."

She switched off the radio.

For the rest of the day, the silver bell sat
on the kitchen table. Whenever Mara passed it, she had
the uncomfortable impression that it had turned slightly to face
her.

That night the wind rose.

It came from the north, carrying the smell of snow,
though winter had barely begun. Waves struck the reef with
dull explosions. Mara lit the lamp, wound the mechanism, and
checked the emergency oil. By midnight, the tower shook in
the storm.

At seventeen minutes past one, the light stopped.

Mara swore and opened the clockwork housing. The gears were
intact. The weights hung properly in the shaft. Yet the
lens had ceased to turn.

Outside, the beam pointed north.

The speaking tube breathed.

"Mara."

She did not answer.

"Mara, listen to me."

Her father's voice sounded closer now. Not below, not within
the tube, but somewhere in the iron walls.

"You are not my father," she said.

"No."

The reply chilled her more than the voice had.

She gripped the railing. "What are you?"

"A warning."

Lightning opened the sky. For one bright instant, Mara saw
a ship beyond the reef.

It was enormous.

Three black masts rose from its deck, their bare rigging
tangled like dead branches. No sails drove it, yet it
moved against the waves. Rows of square windows glimmered with
blue light along the hull.

Then darkness returned, and the ship vanished.

Mara turned the manual crank. The mechanism resisted her. She
leaned into it with both hands, muscles burning, but the
lens would not move.

"What ship is that?" she demanded.

"The one I failed to stop."

Her father's voice cracked, and suddenly she remembered the last
time she had seen him alive.

She had been twenty-two. He had stood at the end
of the pier under a gray morning sky, one hand
raised in farewell. She had left the island after a
quarrel, shouting that she would never spend her life trapped
inside his tower. Three days later, during the worst storm
in a generation, the lighthouse went dark.

Her father's body was found on the lantern room floor.
The doctors said his heart had failed.

A passenger ship had struck the reef that night.

Thirty-eight people drowned.

Mara had returned for the funeral and never left.

She had kept the light burning for eleven years.

"What did you fail to stop?" she whispered.

"The crossing."

Another flash of lightning revealed the black ship nearer than
before. It should have struck the outer reef, but it
passed over the rocks as though they were shadows beneath
ice.

Mara ran downstairs to the radio.

"Black Island calling North Harbor. Unidentified vessel approaching from northwest."

Static answered.

"North Harbor, respond."

The radio crackled. Beneath the noise, many voices murmured at
once.

She caught fragments.

Cold.

Open the door.

We remember the light.

Then the voices merged into a single sentence.

"Ring the bell."

Mara looked at the silver handbell on the table.

She backed away from it.

The storm pressed against the kitchen windows. Above her, the
lighthouse groaned, and the beam remained fixed upon the northern
sea.

A pounding came from the door.

Three heavy knocks.

Mara took the flare pistol from its cabinet and approached.

"Who's there?"

The pounding stopped.

A man called from outside. "Please! Let me in!"

Mara lifted the iron cover from the peephole.

A young man stood on the threshold, drenched and shivering.
His fair hair clung to his forehead. Blood ran from
a cut above one eye. Behind him, rain swept across
the island.

"My boat went over," he said. "Please."

Mara hesitated. No ordinary boat could have reached the island
through the reef in this weather, but the man looked
painfully alive.

She unbolted the door and pulled him inside.

He collapsed against the wall.

Mara locked the door, then helped him to a chair.
He wore a dark uniform of unfamiliar design, with silver
buttons and a high collar. There was no insignia upon
it.

"What is your name?" she asked.

"Elias Vale."

"What ship?"

He glanced toward the ceiling.

"The Orison."

Mara placed the flare pistol on the table, still within
reach. "That ship sank eleven years ago."

Elias stared at her.

"No," he said. "We struck the reef tonight."

The kitchen seemed to tilt beneath her.

Mara remembered the name. Everyone did. The Orison had been
traveling south with eighty-one people aboard. Forty-three reached the lifeboats.
Thirty-eight remained below.

Including, according to the newspaper, a junior officer named Elias
Vale.

"You're dead," Mara said.

He gave a weak laugh. "I don't feel dead."

"You drowned eleven years ago."

His expression changed slowly from confusion to terror.

"No," he whispered. "The lamps went out. We heard the
hull tear open. I took a boat, but the sea—"

He pressed both hands to his head.

"The sea wasn't right. I rowed for hours. There were
faces under the water. Then I saw the lighthouse."

Above them, the speaking tube rattled.

Mara and Elias looked toward it.

The voice of Mara's father said, "Do not let him
ring the bell."

Elias stood so suddenly that the chair overturned.

"Who said that?"

Mara picked up the silver bell. "This came ashore before
you did."

The moment Elias saw it, all color left his face.

He retreated to the wall.

"Throw it into the sea."

"What is it?"

"Throw it away!"

He lunged toward her. Mara raised the flare pistol.

"Stop."

Elias did. His breath came fast.

"Tell me," she said.

He stared at the bell.

"There was a woman aboard the Orison," he said. "She
called herself Madam Rhyne. No one knew where she had
embarked. The captain wanted to put her ashore at North
Harbor, but she showed him that bell."

Thunder shook the glass in the cupboards.

"She said the dead were not gone," Elias continued. "She
said they lived in a country beneath the horizon, where
every lost hour still existed. She could open a passage
there. She could bring back sons, husbands, whole crews swallowed
by the water."

"And the captain believed her?"

"His wife had drowned the year before."

Mara thought of her father's voice in the tower.

"What happened?"

"Madam Rhyne rang the bell at midnight. The sea opened."

Elias looked toward the dark window.

"We saw another ocean beneath ours, black and still. There
were ships down there. Hundreds of them. Ancient galleys, warships,
fishing boats, vessels with no names. And on their decks
stood the drowned."

The storm's roar deepened.

"The captain saw his wife," Elias said. "He steered toward
her. Your lighthouse warned us away, but Madam Rhyne extinguished
the beam. She said the light was the lock upon
the door."

Mara's fingers tightened around the bell.

"The Orison struck the reef," she said.

"Yes. But we did not sink into your sea."

A blue glow moved past the kitchen windows.

Elias turned. "They're here."

Mara looked outside.

The black ship had reached the island.

It towered beyond the rocks, impossibly close. Blue lamps burned
in its windows. Figures crowded the deck, pale and motionless,
their clothes hanging in wet folds. Some wore gowns and
uniforms from Mara's time. Others wore rusted armor, rotting lace,
oilskins, chains.

All of them faced the lighthouse.

At the center of the deck stood an old woman
in a blue coat.

Her eyes were black.

She raised one hand and pointed at the bell.

The kitchen door shuddered under a blow.

Then came another.

Bolts strained in their brackets.

Elias seized Mara's arm. "We must keep the light turning."

"It won't move."

"The lens is pointed toward the crossing. That holds it
open."

Mara looked at the silent bell. "And this?"

"It calls the drowned across. If it rings, the passage
will remain open until dawn."

"It makes no sound."

"Not in this world."

The door bent inward.

Mara thrust the bell into her coat pocket and ran
for the stairs. Elias followed.

They climbed as the pounding spread around the lighthouse. Hands
scraped stone. Fists struck windows. Voices called through the walls.

Some pleaded.

Some threatened.

One voice belonged to Mara's mother, dead since Mara was
six.

Another belonged to a childhood friend lost under river ice.

At the second landing, Elias stopped.

A little girl stood above them on the stairs.

Water poured from her white nightdress. Seaweed twisted through her
hair.

"Elias," she said.

He made a broken sound.

"Your sister?" Mara asked.

Elias nodded.

The girl held out her arms. "You left me."

"No," he whispered. "Clara, I went back. I searched the
cabin."

"You left me in the dark."

Elias took one step upward.

Mara struck him across the face.

He stared at her, stunned. When he looked back, the
stairs were empty.

"They borrow memories," Mara said. She did not know whether
it was true, but saying it gave them strength to
keep climbing.

They reached the lantern room.

The lens stood immovable, its beam cutting northward through the
storm. Within the brass housing, the lamp flame burned blue.

Mara opened the maintenance panel. Elias joined her.

"The mechanism is jammed," she said.

"No." He pointed.

Something moved between the gears.

A hand.

Small gray fingers gripped the teeth of the largest wheel.
Another hand emerged beside it, then another. They pushed from
inside the machinery, holding the gears in place.

Mara took an iron bar from the tool rack and
drove it into the housing.

A shriek rose through the tower.

The hands released the wheel, but more appeared at once,
clutching axles and chains. Elias seized the manual crank and
pulled. The lens trembled.

Below, glass shattered.

"They're inside," he said.

Footsteps crowded the stairs.

Mara swung the bar again. Gray fingers broke apart like
wet clay, dissolving into seawater. The gears shifted. Elias dragged
the crank downward, and the lens rotated by a fraction.

The beam moved away from the north.

Outside, the black ship groaned. Figures on its deck opened
their mouths in a silent cry.

Then the lantern room door burst from its hinges.

Madam Rhyne entered.

She was small, scarcely taller than Mara's shoulder. Her blue
coat dripped steadily, though no rain touched her. Behind her
crowded the drowned.

"You have something of mine," she said.

Mara raised the iron bar.

The old woman smiled. "Do you not wish to see
him?"

Mara heard footsteps beside the lens.

Her father emerged from the shadows.

He looked as he had on the morning she left:
tall, broad-shouldered, beard streaked with iron. His wool sweater was
dry. His face held the weary patience she remembered.

"Mara," he said.

She could not breathe.

"I waited for you," he told her.

Her hands began to shake.

Madam Rhyne extended her palm. "Ring the bell, keeper. Let
one world become two shores of the same sea. No
one need ever be lost again."

Mara's father stepped closer.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I should have asked you to
stay."

Mara's vision blurred.

For eleven years, she had imagined words she might have
spoken if she had returned before the storm. She had
rehearsed apologies to empty rooms. She had maintained the lighthouse
as if each polished rail and filled oil cup might
erase the anger of their final morning.

Now he stood before her.

She reached toward him.

His expression softened.

Then she saw his hands.

They were wet.

Her father had died inside the lantern room, on a
dry floor.

Mara drove the iron bar through his chest.

The figure collapsed into black water.

Madam Rhyne hissed.

Elias threw his weight against the crank. The lens turned
another foot. White light swept across the black ship, and
wherever it touched, timbers cracked. The drowned recoiled.

Madam Rhyne rushed toward Mara.

Mara stepped aside and caught the old woman's coat. They
struck the railing together. Black eyes stared into hers.

"You cling to grief like a starving dog to a
bone," Madam Rhyne whispered. "Why refuse what grief desires?"

"Because grief lies."

Mara tore the silver bell from her pocket and forced
it into the lamp flame.

Madam Rhyne screamed.

The blue fire became white. The bell glowed red in
Mara's hand, yet she felt no heat. The engraved eye
on its handle twisted as if alive.

"Now!" Mara shouted.

Elias pulled the crank.

The lens broke free.

It spun with a deafening clash of gears, faster and
faster. Light swept around the room, crossing sea, sky, reef,
and ship. Each revolution struck the drowned like a blade.
They dissolved into spray.

Madam Rhyne clawed at the bell.

Mara held it in the flame until the metal softened.

A crack appeared along its side.

From within came a sound too vast for the human
ear: the tolling of submerged cities, the cry of whales
beneath ice, the last words of everyone who had vanished
at sea.

The bell shattered.

Night split open.

For an instant, Mara saw the country beneath the horizon.

An endless ocean hung below the world, reflecting no stars.
Lost ships drifted across it in multitudes. On every deck,
the dead lifted their faces toward the lighthouse. Some were
monstrous with longing. Others simply looked tired.

Among them stood Mara's father.

Not the wet imitation, but an older, quieter figure beside
the rail of the Orison.

He raised one hand.

This time, Mara understood the gesture.

It was not a summons.

It was farewell.

The lens completed one final turn.

The passage closed.

The black ship folded into the darkness like a reflection
disturbed by a stone. Madam Rhyne vanished with it. The
storm stopped at once.

Silence filled the lantern room.

Mara lay beside the lamp, her hand closed around fragments
of cold silver. Elias was slumped against the crank.

Outside, dawn touched the eastern horizon.

"Mara," he said faintly.

She crawled toward him.

His uniform had begun to fade. Through his shoulder, she
could see the iron railing.

"No," she said.

He smiled. "Eleven years, you said?"

"Yes."

"I suppose I've kept Clara waiting long enough."

"You don't have to go."

They both knew it was untrue.

Below them, the sea brightened from black to gray. The
reef emerged. Gulls began circling the rocks as if nothing
had happened.

Elias looked toward the western water.

"Keep the light turning," he said.

Then he was gone.

On the floor where he had sat remained a single
silver button.

Mara held it until sunrise.

Later that morning, she radioed North Harbor.

The harbor master answered sleepily. "Black Island, we read you.
Is everything well?"

Mara looked around the wrecked lantern room: the broken door,
flooded gears, shattered glass, and pools of seawater reflecting the
clean new day.

"The storm caused some damage," she said.

"What about the wreck?"

"Gone."

"Any survivors?"

Mara opened her hand and studied the silver button.

"No," she said. "But I found the lost."

A repair crew arrived two days later. They replaced the
gears and windows, fitted a stronger door, and asked many
questions Mara did not answer. The red life ring had
disappeared from the shore. The blue coat was gone from
the kitchen.

Only the button remained.

Mara placed it in the clockwork housing behind the largest
gear, where it caught the light each time the mechanism
turned.

Winter settled over Black Island. Night after night, the beam
swept across the sea. Some evenings Mara thought she glimpsed
distant masts beyond the horizon, but they never approached.

The speaking tube remained silent.

Years passed.

Sail gave way to steam, steam to diesel. Radios changed.
Maps improved. The lighthouse lamp became electric, and officials from
the mainland spoke of automation. Through it all, Mara stayed.

Travelers sometimes called her lonely.

She never corrected them.

On the last night before her retirement, she climbed to
the lantern room and placed her hand upon the warm
brass lens. The sea below was calm, the stars sharp
and innumerable.

At midnight, the light paused.

Only for a heartbeat.

The beam pointed north, toward that unseen country beyond the
world.

From the speaking tube came her father's voice, distant but
clear.

"Well kept," he said.

Mara closed her eyes.

When she opened them, the lens had resumed its turning.

She stood beside it until dawn, watching the light cross
the darkness again and again—not denying the existence of what
lay beyond, not summoning it closer, but marking the boundary
between the lost and the living.

And far out upon the water, where no chart showed
a reef and no compass could be trusted, the dead
saw the faithful beam and knew which way not to
sail.

# The Cartographer of Lost Roads

On the first morning of autumn, Mara Venn found a
road that had not been there the night before.

It began at the edge of her orchard, between two
apple trees planted so closely together that their branches had
grown into a single tangled crown. Beyond them, where the
stone wall should have run unbroken from the well to
the northern field, there was now an opening.

A narrow road slipped through it.

It was paved with pale stones, each one worn smooth
as old bone. Mist lay over its surface, though the
rest of the orchard shone beneath a clear blue sky.
The road curved beyond the wall and vanished among hills
that did not belong to Mara's land—or to any land
she knew.

She stood with a basket on her hip and stared.

A blackbird hopped onto the wall.

"Don't," it said.

Mara nearly dropped the basket.

The bird tilted its head. "Nothing good comes from a
road that arrives uninvited."

Then it flew away.

Mara remained still for several minutes. She had never heard
a blackbird speak before, but the road seemed the greater
impossibility, and so it held her attention.

At last, she set down the basket and stepped through
the opening.

She told herself she would walk only far enough to
see where the first bend led.

Beyond the wall, the air changed. It smelled of rain,
woodsmoke, and something sweet she could not name. The orchard
disappeared behind her, though she had taken no more than
a dozen steps. On either side of the road grew
silver grass as high as her knees. In the distance,
dark mountains rose beneath a sky crowded with unfamiliar stars.

Mara stopped.

It was morning behind her. Here, it was almost night.

She turned back, but the opening in the wall was
gone.

In its place stood an old man beside a wooden
signpost.

He wore a coat covered in pockets and held a
rolled map under one arm. His white beard was neatly
braided with red thread. Above his head, the signpost pointed
in seven directions, although Mara could see only one road.

The signs read:

**HOME**

**THE PLACE YOU WERE EXPECTED**

**THE PLACE THAT EXPECTED YOU**

**YESTERDAY**

**TOO LATE**

**THE SEA**

**ELSEWHERE**

"Excuse me," Mara said.

"No need," said the old man. "Most people come through
accidentally."

"I'd like to go back."

"So do most people."

He unrolled his map. It was made of yellow cloth,
covered in lines that shifted whenever Mara tried to follow
them. Some roads curled into knots. Others ended in drawings
of doors, wells, forests, or sleeping faces.

"Where did you enter?" he asked.

"My orchard."

"Name?"

"Mara Venn."

"Age?"

"Twenty-nine."

"Regret?"

She frowned. "What?"

"Regret. Roads require coordinates."

"I don't see how that is your concern."

"Then you'll remain unmapped."

Mara looked behind her once more. Silver grass rippled beneath
the rising moon. There was no wall, no apple trees,
no farmhouse.

"My brother," she said quietly.

The old man's expression softened. "Name?"

"Jonas."

"Dead?"

"Missing."

"How long?"

"Fifteen years."

The old man took a stick of blue chalk from
one of his pockets and marked the cloth. A new
line appeared, running from the edge of the map toward
a blank space in its center.

"Ah," he said. "That sort of road."

"What sort?"

"The troublesome sort."

He rolled up the map and placed it in Mara's
hands.

It was heavier than she expected.

"Follow the blue road," he said. "It may take you
home."

"May?"

"Maps describe possibility, not mercy."

Mara looked at the cloth. "Are you not coming?"

"I've been standing here for eighty-three years. If I leave,
people might get lost."

"I am lost."

"Then imagine how useful I've been."

Before Mara could ask anything else, the old man stepped
backward into the mist and disappeared.

The signpost remained.

After some thought, Mara chose the direction marked **HOME**. The
moment she did, the other six signs swung around to
point the same way.

She disliked that very much.

Still, the blue line on the map led along the
pale road, and there was nowhere else to go.

Mara walked.

By moonrise, she reached a village built entirely of doors.

There were red doors standing in fields, blue doors lining
the riverbank, iron doors set upright along crooked lanes. Some
were no taller than books. Others rose higher than church
towers. None had walls around them.

People moved among the doors carrying lanterns and keys. They
wore masks painted with smiling faces.

A woman in a fox mask approached Mara.

"You're late," she said.

"For what?"

"Whatever brought you."

"I'm trying to get home."

The woman laughed. "Everyone here is."

She pointed to a yellow door beneath a willow tree.

"Go through that one."

Mara consulted the map. The blue road passed directly beneath
the yellow door.

"What's on the other side?" she asked.

"Your kitchen, perhaps. Or the bottom of a lake. Doors
are honest only about being doors."

The yellow door had a brass handle polished by many
hands. Mara opened it cautiously.

On the other side was her childhood bedroom.

She knew it at once: the slanted ceiling, the faded
green wallpaper, the wardrobe her father had built. Rain tapped
against the window. Two small beds stood beneath the eaves.

On one sat a girl of fourteen, hugging her knees.

Mara recognized herself.

On the other sat Jonas.

He was twelve, thin and dark-haired, with a scratch across
his left cheek. He wore the gray shirt he had
been wearing on the day he vanished.

"You promised," the younger Mara said.

Jonas stared at the floor. "I know."

"You said you wouldn't tell."

"I didn't."

"Father knows."

"He guessed."

"He never guesses."

Jonas rubbed his cheek. "Maybe you should say you broke
it."

"He'll send me away."

"He won't."

"He said if I touched Mother's things again—"

"You sold her necklace."

"I was going to buy it back."

"With what?"

The younger Mara began to cry. Jonas crossed the room
and sat beside her.

"Don't worry," he said. "I'll tell him I took it."

Mara remembered this conversation. Or part of it.

She remembered the necklace, a silver chain with a small
amber pendant. She remembered selling it to a traveling merchant
because she had wanted money to leave home. She remembered
losing the money in the river after slipping on the
bank. And she remembered Jonas telling their father that he
had stolen the necklace.

Their father had not sent him away. He had merely
looked at Jonas with such disappointment that the boy had
fled the house before dawn.

He had never returned.

Mara stepped through the door.

The children did not see her.

"Don't," she said to Jonas.

He continued speaking to his sister. "I'll go to Uncle
Teren's for a few days. When Father calms down, I'll
come back."

"You don't know the way."

"Of course I do."

"No," Mara whispered. "You didn't."

The room began to fade. The rain stopped. Jonas opened
the bedroom door, and beyond it stretched the pale road.

He walked through.

Mara followed.

She emerged alone into a forest of white trees.

Behind her, the yellow door slammed shut and crumbled into
leaves.

The blue line on the map had grown longer.

It led between the trees, where glass bells hung from
bare branches. They chimed without wind. Inside each bell fluttered
a memory: a child blowing out candles, a soldier crossing
a bridge, an old woman combing her hair.

As Mara passed, one bell began to ring more loudly
than the others.

She looked up.

Inside it, Jonas stood at a crossroads beneath heavy rain.
He appeared small and frightened. One path led toward distant
village lights. The other led into darkness, where a lantern
bobbed between the trees.

A man's voice called, "This way, boy. I know your
uncle."

Jonas followed the lantern.

The memory vanished.

Mara reached for the bell, but it swung beyond her
grasp.

"Who was he?" she demanded.

The forest answered with a thousand soft chimes.

Then something moved among the trees.

It was tall enough to brush the branches. Its body
was hidden beneath a cloak stitched from pieces of road:
cobblestone, dirt, snow, dry leaves. Instead of a face, it
had a signpost with arrows pointing outward like antlers.

Mara backed away.

The creature bent toward her.

One arrow read **MARA VENN**.

Another read **JONAS VENN**.

A third read **THIEF**.

"What are you?" she asked.

Its reply came in the voices of distant travelers.

"Destination."

The arrow marked **JONAS VENN** turned toward a narrow path
between the trees.

Mara's heart struck hard against her ribs.

"Is he alive?"

"Alive," said one voice.

"Dead," said another.

"Waiting," whispered a third.

The creature began to walk. Mara followed.

They traveled until the white forest gave way to a
plain covered in snow. At its center stood an inn
with golden windows and smoke curling from its chimney. Above
the door hung a painted sign: a road coiled around
a sleeping man's throat.

The signpost creature stopped.

The arrow marked **THIEF** pointed to the inn.

Then the creature folded in on itself, becoming smaller and
smaller until only an ordinary wooden sign remained.

Mara opened the inn door.

Warmth rushed over her.

Travelers sat at long tables, eating stew and speaking in
languages she did not know. Some wore fine coats; others
wore armor rusted through with age. A young woman with
flowers growing from her hair played cards against a skeleton
in a red scarf.

Behind the counter stood a broad man with a shaved
head. An amber pendant hung around his neck.

Mara froze.

Even across the crowded room, she recognized it.

Her mother's necklace.

The innkeeper followed her gaze and covered the pendant with
one hand.

"Where did you get that?" Mara asked.

"Found it."

"You're lying."

"Then why ask?"

Mara crossed the room. "A boy came here fifteen years
ago. Twelve years old. Dark hair. Gray shirt. His name
was Jonas Venn."

At the sound of the name, the inn fell silent.

Even the skeleton stopped shuffling cards.

The innkeeper sighed. "Upstairs."

Mara reached beneath the counter, seized his collar, and pulled
him forward.

"What did you do to him?"

"Nothing that wasn't done to me."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," said the innkeeper, gently removing her hand, "that
he came through the forest following a lantern. I fed
him. I gave him a bed. In the morning, he
tried to leave, but the road had moved."

"Roads don't move."

Every traveler in the inn laughed.

The innkeeper poured a cup of water and pushed it
toward Mara.

"He waited for it to return," he continued. "Days became
months. Months became years. Time collects strangely here."

"And the necklace?"

"He traded it to me."

"For what?"

"A map."

Mara unrolled the yellow cloth. The blue line now climbed
toward a black square near the top.

"Like this one?"

The innkeeper's face hardened. "Where did you get that?"

"An old man at a signpost."

"There is no old man at the signpost."

"I spoke to him."

"There hasn't been a cartographer in eighty-three years."

The room seemed to tilt.

"What happened to him?"

"He left."

"Where?"

The innkeeper pointed upstairs.

Mara climbed.

At the end of the hall was a small room
beneath the roof. The door stood open.

Inside, maps covered the walls.

Maps drawn on paper, cloth, bark, and human skin. Maps
with roads that twisted like rivers. Maps of vanished cities.
Maps of houses not yet built. At a desk beneath
the window sat the old man in the pocketed coat.

He had removed his braided beard.

Without it, he looked much younger.

He looked forty.

He also looked like Jonas.

Mara could not breathe.

He turned.

For a moment neither of them spoke.

Then he said, "You got old."

She crossed the room and struck him.

The sound snapped through the silence.

Jonas touched his cheek.

"I suppose I earned that," he said.

Mara struck him again.

This time he caught her wrist.

"I thought you were dead," she said. "Fifteen years. I
searched every road, every village, every river. Father died believing
you were dead."

"I know."

"How could you know?"

"I mapped the road to his last day."

"You watched him?"

"I couldn't cross. I could only see."

Mara tore her hand free. "Why didn't you come home?"

"I tried."

He gestured to the maps.

"Every line is an attempt. I mapped your orchard a
thousand times. I found roads that led to the house
as it was before we were born, roads that led
there after you had abandoned it, roads that led to
places where it had burned down. Once I found a
road to the exact night I left, but I saw
myself already walking away."

"Then stop yourself."

"I did."

Mara stared at him.

"What happened?"

"Another road opened. Another version of me left. The world
dislikes correction. It prefers multiplication."

The anger inside her began to crack, revealing the grief
beneath it.

"You could have sent word."

"I sent birds."

Mara remembered the blackbird.

"Birds get distracted," Jonas added.

Despite herself, a broken laugh escaped her. It became a
sob.

Jonas stepped toward her, but she held up a hand.

"No. Not yet."

He stopped.

Below them, the inn's voices slowly resumed.

Mara moved to the window. Outside, the snowy plain was
gone. The inn now stood beside a dark sea. Waves
rolled beneath a red moon.

"I lied," she said.

Jonas leaned against the desk.

"I sold Mother's necklace. You took the blame, and you
left because of me."

"I left because I was twelve and dramatic."

"You were protecting me."

"I wanted to be a hero."

"You should have come back."

"Yes."

The word was simple and unguarded.

Mara looked at him.

"You should have tried harder," she said.

"Yes."

"You should have found a way."

"Yes."

She waited for excuses, but none came.

At last she said, "Come home now."

Jonas glanced at the maps.

"I can't."

"Why?"

"Because I'm the cartographer."

"I met you at the signpost."

"You met what remains there when I leave. A map
of a man. An echo."

"Then let it remain."

"It can't guide travelers. Not for long."

"That is not your responsibility."

"It became mine when I took the maps."

"From whom?"

"The man who brought me here. The one with the
lantern."

Mara thought of the memory in the glass bell.

"The thief."

"He stole travelers from their roads and sold them new
destinations. When I grew older, I stole his maps. I
trapped him inside them."

"And now you stand where he stood."

Jonas smiled without humor. "Roads enjoy symmetry."

Mara unrolled the cloth the echo had given her.

The blue line ended in the room where they stood.
But beneath it, almost too faint to see, another path
had appeared. It ran from the room toward the edge
of the map, where two apple trees intertwined.

"There," she said. "A road home."

Jonas examined it.

"That road wasn't there before."

"It is now."

"It may not be safe."

"No road is."

"It may demand a price."

"Then we pay it."

He looked toward the walls.

"If I leave, every road I've mapped may vanish."

"Perhaps some should."

"People will get lost."

"People are already lost."

Jonas was silent.

Mara rolled up the map and tucked it beneath her
arm.

"When we were children," she said, "you followed me onto
the frozen pond even though Father told us the ice
was thin."

"You said you knew a safe path."

"I was lying."

"I know."

"You knew then?"

"Of course."

"Why did you follow me?"

"Someone had to pull you out."

Mara opened the door.

"Your turn to trust me."

Jonas looked once around the room. Then he removed the
amber pendant from his neck and placed it on the
desk.

"No," Mara said. She picked it up and handed it
back to him. "We take our mistakes with us."

He closed his fingers around it.

Together they went downstairs.

The travelers watched them cross the common room. The innkeeper
stood behind the counter, polishing a glass.

"So," he said to Jonas, "you're finally going."

"I think so."

"About time."

"You'll need someone to tend the maps."

The innkeeper snorted. "Burn them."

Jonas considered that.

Then he smiled.

Outside, the road began at the edge of the sea.
Pale stones floated on black water, stretching toward the horizon.

Mara stepped onto the first stone.

It sank slightly, but held.

Jonas followed.

Behind them, the inn grew smaller. Light vanished from its
windows one by one. When they reached the horizon, Mara
looked back and saw only darkness.

Ahead stood the two apple trees.

Their branches formed an arch above the road. Beyond them
lay the orchard beneath early morning light.

Mara hurried forward, but Jonas caught her arm.

"Wait."

"What?"

He pointed to the map.

The blue line ended before the trees.

Between them and the orchard was a gap no wider
than a single step. Yet beneath that gap moved a
depthless darkness, filled with drifting doors and broken signposts.

Words appeared on the cloth:

**ONE TRAVELER MAY RETURN.**

Mara read them twice.

"No."

"The road demands a price," Jonas said.

"No."

"It was always going to."

"We'll find another way."

"The road is closing."

Behind them, the stones were dropping into the sea.

Jonas pressed the amber pendant into her hand.

"Tell me where to go," he said.

She shook her head.

"Mara."

"I will not leave you again."

"You didn't leave me the first time."

"I let you go."

"You were fourteen."

"I should have told the truth."

"Yes," he said. "But I chose the road."

Only three stones remained behind them.

Jonas took the map from her and tore it down
the middle.

The world screamed.

The sea rose in two black walls. Roads spilled from
the torn cloth—hundreds of them, thousands—uncoiling across the water, climbing
into the sky, plunging through open doors. Mara saw deserts,
cities, battlefields, nurseries, and forests filled with bells.

The gap before the apple trees widened.

"What have you done?" she shouted.

"Made a new possibility."

Jonas tore the map again.

The roads twisted together beneath their feet. For an instant,
Mara stood nowhere and everywhere. She saw herself returning alone.
She saw Jonas returning alone. She saw both of them
falling into darkness. She saw the orchard empty. She saw
the farmhouse crowded with children not yet born.

Then the blackbird landed on Jonas's shoulder.

"Took you long enough," it said.

The bird flew through the orchard opening.

Mara jumped after it.

Jonas jumped beside her.

They landed on wet grass.

Behind them, the two apple trees shuddered as if struck
by wind. The opening closed. The stone wall returned, whole
and ordinary beneath the dawn.

For a long time, brother and sister lay in the
orchard, laughing and crying.

At last Jonas sat up.

The farmhouse stood beyond the trees, its chimney leaning slightly
east. The same tin bucket rested beside the well. Mara's
basket lay where she had dropped it.

"How long were we gone?" he asked.

Mara looked at the sky.

"Perhaps an hour."

A child appeared at the farmhouse door.

She was eight years old, red-haired and barefoot.

"Mama?" she called.

Jonas stared.

Mara rose slowly.

The girl ran across the orchard and threw her arms
around Mara.

"I woke up and you were gone."

"I'm sorry, Elian."

The child noticed Jonas.

"Who's that?"

Mara looked at her brother.

In the strange country, beneath unfamiliar stars, he had seemed
only older. Here she saw the truth. Fifteen years had
passed for her, but far more had passed for him.
There were lines around his eyes. Silver ran through his
hair. He looked tired and frightened and hopeful.

"This is your uncle Jonas," Mara said.

Elian considered him.

"The dead one?"

Jonas coughed.

Mara smiled. "Not anymore."

In the weeks that followed, the village spoke of little
else. Some called Jonas a fraud. Others called him a
ghost. Old Mrs. Pell touched his face and fainted into
a basket of turnips. The priest asked whether he had
visited heaven, and Jonas answered that he had seen several
places making the same claim.

He moved into the small room above the kitchen.

At first he woke every night and checked the windows
for roads. He refused to open unfamiliar doors. He carried
chalk in his pockets and drew maps of every room
he entered.

But autumn deepened. The apples were gathered. The northern field
was plowed. Jonas repaired the orchard wall and taught Elian
to recognize birdcalls, though he warned her never to trust
a blackbird.

One rainy evening, Mara found him at the kitchen table
with a blank sheet of paper.

"What are you drawing?" she asked.

"Nothing yet."

He held up the pencil.

For years, he had drawn roads that led backward, sideways,
and away. Now his hand trembled over the empty page.

Mara placed the amber pendant beside him. She had repaired
its broken clasp.

Jonas picked it up and fastened it around his neck.

Then he began to draw the orchard.

He drew the wall, the well, the farmhouse, and the
two apple trees whose branches had grown together. From the
front door, he drew a narrow path leading toward the
village.

"Where does that road go?" Elian asked, peering over his
shoulder.

Jonas looked at Mara.

"Tomorrow," he said.

Outside, the rain softened. The house settled around them with
familiar creaks. Somewhere in the orchard, a blackbird sang only
as a blackbird should.

And for the first time in many years, no road
arrived uninvited.

# The Hour That Went Missing

At 2:17 every morning, the clocks in Bellwether stopped.

They did not slow down or lose their rhythm one
tick at a time. They simply ceased. Pendulums hung motionless.
Gears held their breath. Even the great clock in the
courthouse tower, whose iron hands weighed more than a grown
man, froze at seventeen minutes past two.

Then, at 3:17, all the clocks began again.

No one in Bellwether remembered when the missing hour had
first disappeared. Most people had learned to ignore it. Bakers
set their alarms early. Nurses adjusted their rounds. The railway
added sixty minutes to every overnight journey through town. Visitors
complained, but locals merely shrugged.

"An old electrical fault," said the mayor, although many clocks
required no electricity.

"Something in the water," said the doctor, though no one
could explain why water would stop a pocket watch.

"Mountains," said people who preferred an answer that sounded natural
and required no further thought.

Only Elias Vale, the town clockmaker, considered the missing hour
an insult.

His shop stood in a narrow lane behind the courthouse.
Hundreds of clocks covered its walls: brass carriage clocks, painted
cuckoo clocks, walnut mantel clocks, silver watches, and tiny travel
clocks with luminous hands. Their combined ticking sounded like summer
rain.

At 2:17 each morning, Elias sat among them and listened
to the silence.

He was seventy-three years old, lean as a clock hand,
with silver hair and a magnifying lens that seemed permanently
fixed over his right eye. He had lived alone since
his wife, Mara, vanished nineteen years earlier.

People said she had left him. There had been no
body, no note, no sign of struggle. Her coat and
shoes were gone, and so was the small blue suitcase
she used when visiting her sister.

But Elias knew Mara had not left.

She had disappeared at 2:17 in the morning.

He had woken to find her side of the bed
empty. Downstairs, the front door stood open. Snow blew across
the floorboards. Her footprints led from the house into the
white street and ended beneath the courthouse clock, as if
she had been lifted straight into the sky.

At 3:17, the clocks began ticking again, and Elias discovered
that an hour had passed.

From that night onward, he stayed awake during the silence.

For nineteen years, nothing happened.

Then, on the first morning of winter, someone knocked on
his shop door at 2:43.

Elias was so startled that he dropped his tea.

The knock came again: three slow taps.

None of the clocks moved. Outside, snow hung frozen in
the air. Through the window, Elias could see individual flakes
suspended above the street like stars trapped in glass.

He approached the door.

"Who is it?"

No one answered.

He opened it.

A girl stood on the threshold. She looked about twelve
years old and wore a red wool coat several sizes
too large. Her dark hair was tied with a yellow
ribbon. Under one arm, she carried a wooden clock shaped
like a house.

"Are you Elias Vale?" she asked.

"I am."

"You took long enough."

She pushed past him into the shop.

Elias stared at the motionless snow, then shut the door.
"Who are you?"

"June."

"June what?"

"Just June."

She placed the wooden clock on his workbench. It was
beautifully made, with carved windows and a tiny green door.
Instead of numbers, its face displayed painted images: a candle,
a key, a bird, a boat, a tree, a crown,
and other symbols Elias did not recognize. It had only
one hand, and that hand pointed nowhere.

"It's broken," June said.

"All clocks are broken when they come to me."

"Not like this one."

Elias examined it. The casing had no screws, seams, or
hinges. He pressed his ear to the wood and heard
a faint rushing sound, like distant wind.

"Where did you get it?"

"My mother gave it to me."

"And where is she?"

June looked toward the courthouse tower visible through the window.

"She's in the hour."

Elias's hands became cold.

"What hour?"

"The one your town lost."

He lowered himself onto his stool. "That is not an
answer a child should give lightly."

"I'm not a child."

"You look like one."

"You look like an old man. Appearances can be accurate
without being complete."

Despite himself, Elias almost smiled. It was the sort of
answer Mara would have enjoyed.

He lifted the clock. Though no larger than a breadbox,
it was surprisingly heavy.

"How do I open it?"

"With the key."

"And where is the key?"

"In the clock."

"That is inconvenient."

"It's a clock made from time," June said. "Convenience wasn't
the point."

Before Elias could ask what she meant, the wooden clock
shuddered. Its single hand swung toward the painted bird.

A cuckoo door opened above its face.

Instead of a mechanical bird, a folded strip of paper
slid out.

Elias unfolded it.

In Mara's handwriting were four words:

Please come before dawn.

He sat perfectly still.

June watched him. "You know her writing."

"Where did you get this?"

"I told you. The clock."

"Do not play games with me."

"I don't have time for games."

"No one does, apparently."

June sighed and climbed onto the stool opposite him.

"Long ago," she said, "before Bellwether was built, there was
a keeper beneath the hill. He looked after unwanted time."

"Unwanted time?"

"Moments people wished away. Long winters. Sleepless nights. Final goodbyes.
The minute before bad news. All the time people begged
to escape had to go somewhere."

"And it went beneath the hill?"

"Yes. The keeper stored it in a great clock. For
centuries, that was enough. Then people made more clocks, more
schedules, more deadlines. They began wishing away nearly everything. Workdays.
Rainy afternoons. Years of loneliness. Childhoods they wanted to outgrow.
Old age they feared enduring."

Elias glanced at the frozen clocks surrounding them.

"The great clock filled up," June continued. "So the keeper
stole an hour from Bellwether. It was supposed to be
temporary, just enough room to repair the mechanism. But something
went wrong. The keeper disappeared, and the stolen hour became
a cage."

"For Mara?"

"For everyone who entered it."

Elias gripped the note. "Why would she enter?"

"To find the keeper."

"She knew about this?"

"Some people do. Your wife was curious."

"That is one word for it."

"She believed she could restore the hour."

Elias looked again at the message. "And you say she
is your mother."

June nodded.

"Mara and I had no children."

"Not before she vanished."

The room seemed to tilt.

Elias rose so quickly that his stool fell backward. "Enough."

June did not move.

"You expect me to believe my wife entered a hidden
hour, remained there for nineteen years, and had a daughter?"

"Time behaves differently inside itself."

"That sentence means nothing."

"It will."

At that moment, the green door on the wooden clock
opened. A brass key dropped onto the workbench.

June picked it up.

"You can stay here if you like," she said. "At
3:17, I'll disappear, the clock will close, and the missing
hour will continue forever. Your wife will wait. You'll sit
among your clocks for however many years you have left."

Elias stared at the key.

"What happens if I come?"

"You may not return."

"Will Mara?"

"I don't know."

He took his coat from its hook.

"Then let us not waste time."

Outside, Bellwether lay silent and motionless. Snowflakes remained suspended above
rooftops. A cat hung in mid-leap between two barrels. Smoke
stood above chimneys in pale, unmoving pillars.

June carried the wooden clock. Elias followed her across the
square.

At the courthouse, she knelt beside the tower's stone foundation.
Near the ground was a carving Elias had passed thousands
of times without noticing: a circle containing a candle, a
key, a bird, and a crown.

June pressed the brass key into the carving.

Stone softened around it.

A narrow door appeared.

Beyond it, steps descended into darkness.

Elias took a lantern from his coat pocket, lit it,
and followed June underground. The door closed behind them with
a sound like a clock case snapping shut.

The staircase went deeper than the hill should have allowed.
Its walls were covered with messages scratched into the stone.

Not yet.

I need more time.

Let this day end.

Take me back.

At the bottom, they entered a vast cavern.

Thousands of glass spheres floated in the darkness. Some were
no larger than marbles; others were broad as houses. Within
each sphere, a moment repeated.

A soldier embraced his mother beside a train.

A woman sat alone at a birthday table, watching candles
burn down.

A child waited outside a headmaster's office.

A fisherman struggled through a storm.

A bride stood before a mirror, unable to lift her
veil.

Elias moved closer to one sphere. Inside, a younger version
of himself sat beside Mara on a riverbank. She was
laughing because he had dropped their picnic basket in the
water.

"That was forty-seven years ago," he whispered.

"Someone wished the afternoon would never end," June said.

"I did."

"Then part of it came here."

Elias watched his younger self retrieve an apple from the
river while Mara laughed so hard she had to hold
his shoulder.

He wanted to touch the glass.

June caught his hand. "Don't. Memories are greedy."

They continued.

The cavern narrowed into a corridor filled with doors. Behind
each came a different sound: thunder, music, weeping, applause, the
crash of waves.

At the corridor's end stood a clock tall as the
courthouse tower.

Its gears filled the chamber. Copper wheels turned slowly through
stone. Chains vanished into the ceiling. The pendulum, a black
blade, hung motionless above a bottomless pit.

The clock face bore the same symbols as the wooden
clock.

At its base, a woman in a gray dress worked
among the gears.

Her hair, once black, was threaded with silver. She held
a wrench in one hand. There was grease on her
cheek.

Elias knew her at once.

"Mara."

She turned.

For a moment, she looked exactly as she had on
the night she vanished: alert, impatient, and already thinking three
thoughts ahead. Then the wrench fell from her hand.

"Elias?"

He crossed the chamber without remembering the steps between them.

She met him halfway.

For nineteen years, Elias had imagined what he would say
if he found her. He had prepared accusations, questions, declarations,
and speeches polished by loneliness.

He said none of them.

He held her.

Mara pressed her face against his coat. He felt her
shoulders shake.

"You got old," she murmured.

"You did not have the decency to wait for me."

She laughed, and the sound was so familiar that grief
struck him harder than joy.

When they separated, Mara saw June.

"You found him."

"He needed convincing," June said.

"He always does."

Elias looked from one to the other. "She says she
is your daughter."

"Our daughter," Mara replied.

Elias felt the words enter him slowly.

"How?"

"On my first night here, I found a room full
of possible tomorrows. In one of them, you and I
had a child. A girl with my stubbornness and your
habit of taking things apart."

"I do not take things apart without reason."

June raised an eyebrow.

Mara smiled faintly. "The missing hour makes possibilities solid. June
was only an unlived future at first, but she grew.
I taught her. She helped me."

"Am I real?" June asked. Her tone was casual, but
her hands tightened around the wooden clock.

Elias looked at her red coat, her yellow ribbon, and
the challenging set of her mouth.

He thought of all the questions that could not be
repaired by logic.

"You are here," he said. "That is usually enough."

June looked down quickly.

A deep metallic groan passed through the chamber.

One of the giant clock's gears cracked. Black dust poured
from its teeth.

Mara snatched up her wrench. "We have little time."

"I was told that was our principal resource," Elias said.

"Not anymore."

She led him to the clock's base. Behind a panel
lay a tangled mechanism. Most of it was impossibly old,
made from dark metal and bone-white ivory. At its center
was a small, empty space shaped like a heart.

"The mainspring shattered," Mara said. "The keeper tried to replace
it with the stolen hour, but the hour had too
many moments inside it. Too much regret. The mechanism jammed."

"Where is the keeper?"

She pointed upward.

Entangled in the clock's gears was a figure in a
long black coat. His body had become part of the
machine. Brass roots ran through his arms. His open eyes
moved with every turn of a wheel.

"He has been holding the clock together," Mara said. "If
it breaks, all the unwanted time will return at once."

"What would that mean?"

"Everyone would relive every moment they ever wished away. All
at the same time."

Elias considered the mechanism. "You have spent nineteen years repairing
this?"

"Three years, from my perspective."

"That is still a long time to leave your husband
uninformed."

"I sent notes."

"I received one."

"I sent four hundred."

"Postal service has declined."

Another crack split a gear overhead.

The keeper opened his mouth, and the entire clock spoke
with him.

"A HEART," it boomed.

Elias stepped back.

"A HEART MUST BE GIVEN."

Mara gripped his arm. "That is the only repair I
could not make."

"What does it mean?"

"The clock needs a living heart to measure living time."

June set the wooden clock on the floor. "Mine."

"No," Mara said immediately.

"I was made here. I'm part of the hour."

"No."

"If I take the keeper's place, the mechanism will work."

"You do not know that."

"I do."

Elias studied the heart-shaped space inside the clock.

"You said a living heart," he murmured. "Not a life."

Mara recognized the tone in his voice. "What are you
thinking?"

"Every clock has an escapement. It does not create power.
It controls its release."

"This is not one of your mantel clocks."

"No. It is badly designed."

The keeper's eyes shifted toward him, perhaps in offense.

Elias removed his pocket watch.

It was a plain silver watch, scratched along the case.
Mara had given it to him on their tenth wedding
anniversary. He had carried it every day since.

At 2:17 each morning, it stopped with the others.

Elias opened the back.

Inside the lid, Mara had once engraved a line:

All my hours are yours.

He showed it to her.

"A heart is not only an organ," he said. "Sometimes
it is the thing that carries what the heart cannot."

Mara shook her head. "Sentiment will not repair a cosmic
mechanism."

"You married a clockmaker. Show some faith in precision."

He placed the pocket watch into the empty space.

Nothing happened.

June looked disappointed.

Then Elias reached into the mechanism and turned the winding
crown.

Once.

Twice.

On the third turn, the watch began to tick.

The sound echoed through the chamber.

The great clock answered.

One gear moved, then another. Chains drew tight. The black
pendulum swung over the pit.

The keeper screamed.

Brass roots tore from his arms. He fell from the
machinery, but instead of striking the floor, he broke apart
into thousands of moths that flew upward and vanished.

The glass spheres throughout the cavern began to burst.

Released moments rushed through the chamber: rain, laughter, gunfire, lullabies,
farewells, bells, and whispered names. They spiraled around the great
clock, becoming a bright river.

The single hand on its face moved from the painted
bird toward the painted tree.

Mara grabbed Elias.

"What have you done?"

"Repaired it, I hope."

"The hour is restarting. We have to leave."

They ran.

The corridor doors flew open as they passed. Storms and
songs spilled after them. In the cavern, the floating memories
shattered one by one.

Elias saw the riverbank again. His younger self and Mara
sat in the grass beneath a golden afternoon.

For one dangerous second, he wanted to remain.

Then the memory-Mara turned toward him. Though she should not
have been able to see beyond her moment, she smiled.

Go, her lips seemed to say.

Elias ran on.

They reached the stairs as the underground clock struck three.

The sound shook dust from the walls.

June unlocked the stone door. White light poured through.

They stumbled into the square.

Behind them, the entrance vanished.

For a breath, Bellwether remained frozen.

Then every suspended snowflake fell at once.

The cat landed between the barrels. Smoke curled above the
chimneys. The courthouse clock groaned and moved forward.

Its minute hand passed 2:17.

Elias, Mara, and June stood beneath the tower, watching.

2:18.

From houses across town came shouts. Lights appeared in windows.
Somewhere, a baby began to cry. Somewhere else, a man
laughed.

The missing hour had returned.

Mara took Elias's hand. "What happens now?"

He looked at her, then at June.

For years, he had imagined the restoration of his old
life: Mara coming home, the dust of loneliness swept away,
every interrupted plan resumed.

But time did not repair by moving backward.

It repaired by continuing.

"We open the shop late," he said. "Then we have
breakfast."

June frowned. "That's all?"

"No. After breakfast, your mother will explain four hundred undelivered
notes. Then you will tell me how old you actually
are. Then we will find you a coat that fits."

"I like this coat."

"It is absurd."

"It has pockets."

"So do sensible coats."

Mara slipped her arm through his. "You see? She is
ours."

They crossed the square together.

Behind them, the courthouse clock struck the quarter hour. All
over Bellwether, clocks answered—some early, some late, none quite agreeing,
but all moving.

In the shop, Elias found that most of his clocks
had survived. A few had lost a minute. One ran
backward. The blue cuckoo clock insisted it was Thursday, although
it was Monday.

On his workbench stood the wooden clock shaped like a
house.

Its painted symbols were gone.

In their place were twelve ordinary numbers and three black
hands.

The tiny green door opened.

Elias waited for a note, a key, or some new
impossibility.

Instead, a wooden bird emerged and gave a single, cheerful
call.

June laughed.

Mara leaned against Elias's shoulder.

Outside, dawn approached, slow and pale beyond the roofs. The
town had sixty new minutes and no idea what to
do with them.

Elias listened to the clocks ticking around his family.

For the first time in nineteen years, the sound did
not remind him of something being lost.

It sounded like something being given.

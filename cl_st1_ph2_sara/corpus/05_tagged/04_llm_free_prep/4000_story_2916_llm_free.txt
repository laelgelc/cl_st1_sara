The Clockmaker of Bellweather Street

Every clock in Bellweather stopped at 3:17 on the morning
that Elias Vale disappeared.

Pocket watches froze in waistcoat pockets. Grandfather clocks held their
brass pendulums to one side. The clock above the railway
station locked its hands together as if frightened, and the
little painted rooster inside Mrs. Finch's kitchen cuckoo clock leaned
halfway through its door without making a sound.

At precisely 3:18, they all began again.

Most people slept through it. Those who noticed blamed the
storm, though there had been no lightning, only rain falling
in soft, steady sheets over the roofs.

By breakfast, Elias's daughter Mara found his bed untouched.

His spectacles rested beside a cold cup of tea in
the workshop downstairs. His coat remained on its hook. His
shoes stood neatly under the bench. In the center of
the room, among the springs, gears, pliers, and open-backed watches,
lay a single brass key.

It was warm when Mara picked it up.

Her father had made clocks for as long as she
could remember. He repaired wedding watches, church clocks, carriage clocks,
kitchen clocks, and once an elaborate silver timepiece that played
four different tunes depending on the season. People traveled from
distant villages to visit Elias Vale because he could hear
what was wrong with a clock before opening its case.

"A clock tells you where it hurts," he used to
say. "You only have to stop talking long enough to
listen."

Mara had never been good at listening. She preferred questions.

Where did minutes go after they passed? Why did happy
hours feel short and lonely ones feel endless? If two
clocks disagreed, which one was lying? Could a person become
lost in time as easily as in a forest?

Elias answered most of her questions patiently. But whenever she
asked about the locked blue door at the back of
the workshop, he became silent.

"Nothing useful is behind it," he would say.

This was clearly untrue. Her father never locked away useless
things.

Now, with rain whispering against the windows and the warm
brass key in her hand, Mara approached the blue door.

The key fit.

Beyond it was a narrow staircase spiraling downward, although the
workshop had no cellar. Mara took a lamp from the
bench and descended.

The air grew colder with each step. Soon she could
no longer hear the rain. She counted one hundred and
twelve stairs before they ended at a circular chamber deep
beneath the street.

Thousands of clocks covered its stone walls.

Some were smaller than coins. Others were as wide as
cartwheels. There were clocks with one hand, clocks with thirteen
numbers, clocks that ran backward, and clocks with no faces
at all. Their ticking blended into a restless murmur, like
a crowd speaking in sleep.

At the center of the chamber stood a black iron
clock twice Mara's height.

It had no hands. In place of a face was
a round glass window, behind which gray mist swirled.

A chain ran from the clock to the floor. At
its end lay a silver pocket watch.

Mara recognized it immediately. Elias always carried it, though he
never used it to tell the hour.

She bent to pick it up.

The moment her fingers touched the silver case, the great
black clock struck once.

The sound passed through her bones.

The glass window filled with light, and the mist cleared
to reveal not gears, but a street.

Bellweather Street.

It looked almost as it had the day before, except
the sky was perfectly white, and the buildings had no
shadows. People stood motionless along the pavements: the baker holding
a tray, a child reaching for a rolling hoop, the
postman with one foot raised above a puddle.

In the distance, a solitary figure walked toward the railway
station.

"Father!" Mara shouted.

The figure stopped.

Elias turned. Even through the glass, she saw his expression
change from exhaustion to alarm.

He ran toward her.

"Mara, what have you done?"

His voice sounded faint, as if carried down a long
tunnel.

"What have I done? Where are you?"

"Between seconds." He looked over his shoulder. "You must close
the clock."

"How?"

"The key. Turn it to the left."

Mara reached for the brass key still clutched in her
palm, but before she could insert it into the iron
clock, something moved behind Elias.

At first she thought it was another person. Then it
unfolded.

It was tall and thin, dressed in a coat made
from overlapping strips of darkness. Its arms hung below its
knees. Where its face should have been, a white clock
dial showed only one hand.

That hand pointed at Elias.

He turned and ran.

The creature followed without hurrying. With every step, it crossed
half the distance between them.

Mara thrust the key into a slot beneath the glass
and turned left.

The clock screamed.

The chamber vanished.

She landed hard on the workshop floor as the blue
door slammed behind her. Every clock in the room chimed
at once, though the hour was 8:43.

Then the shop became quiet.

Mara pulled at the blue door. It would not open.
The brass key had disappeared.

Someone knocked at the front entrance.

It was Constable Reed, dripping rain onto the floor. Behind
him stood Mrs. Finch, the baker, and half the street,
all talking at once.

They wanted their clocks repaired.

Since 3:17 that morning, the town's timepieces had developed peculiar
faults. Mrs. Finch's cuckoo clock ran correctly, but the bird
announced things that had not yet happened. The station clock
lost nine minutes whenever a train approached. The church clock
struck according to the number of people praying inside. The
baker's alarm rang whenever his bread was about to burn,
even when he had not set it.

Constable Reed placed his pocket watch on the counter.

Its second hand trembled but refused to move forward.

"Where's your father?" he asked.

Mara looked at the crowd.

"Visiting a client."

"When will he return?"

"I don't know."

A murmur passed through the shop. Bellweather trusted Elias with
time the way it trusted Dr. Thorne with illness and
Reverend Pike with death. His absence frightened them.

Mara straightened.

"I can repair the clocks."

It was not entirely a lie. Elias had taught her
the basics. She could replace a spring, clean a wheel,
reset a pendulum, and identify most common faults. Yet there
was nothing common about a cuckoo predicting that the mayor
would fall into the fountain at noon.

Which, according to Mrs. Finch, he did.

Mara spent the day examining watches. None was broken. Their
gears were clean, their springs sound. Something else disturbed them,
something that moved invisibly through the town.

Near sunset, she opened her father's silver pocket watch.

Inside the lid, scratched into the metal, were four words:

TIME OWES NO ONE.

Beneath the mechanism she found a folded piece of paper
covered in Elias's cramped handwriting.

If the Hourless One wakes, it will follow the oldest
stolen hour. Return what was taken before the thirteenth bell.
Do not bargain with it.

Mara read the message three times.

Then she heard ticking from behind the blue door.

Not many clocks. One.

Slow.

Patient.

She searched the workshop until midnight, opening cabinets and pulling
books from shelves. At last she found her father's journal
hidden inside the case of a broken mantel clock.

The early pages contained repair notes, household accounts, and reminders
to buy tea. Halfway through, the handwriting changed. The lines
became crowded and urgent.

Twenty years ago, when Mara was an infant, she had
fallen ill with scarlet fever.

She knew that much. Elias had told her she had
nearly died. He never explained how she recovered.

The journal did.

On the worst night of her illness, the doctor told
Elias she would not survive until morning. Desperate, he went
beneath the workshop and opened the black clock, an ancient
machine built by clockmakers who believed time could be stored,
borrowed, and traded.

Elias stole one hour from the clock.

During that hour, Mara's fever broke.

But stolen time had to come from somewhere. The hour
was taken from Bellweather's future and sealed in a silver
watch. Elias believed he could keep it hidden. Instead, the
theft awakened the guardian inside the space between seconds.

The Hourless One.

It hunted stolen time and those who carried it.

For twenty years Elias had drawn it away from Mara,
entering the black clock whenever it came near. Each time
he escaped. This time, he had not returned.

Mara looked at the silver watch in her hand.

She understood then why her father carried it. He had
not been keeping time.

He had been carrying her stolen hour.

The workshop's clocks began to strike one.

Between the first and second chime, a shadow crossed the
front window.

Between the second and third, it reached the door.

There was no fourth.

Mara turned.

The Hourless One stood inside the shop.

It had entered without opening the door. Its coat trailed
into the dark corners of the room. The single hand
on its white face pointed at the silver watch.

Mara could not move.

A voice came from inside the creature, layered from many
voices speaking together.

"RETURN."

She remembered her father's warning: Do not bargain with it.

So, naturally, she said, "Take me to my father."

The hand on its face shifted toward her.

"RETURN."

"I have the hour. You need me."

The clocks on the shelves began running backward.

The Hourless One extended one long hand.

Mara placed the silver watch in its palm but did
not let go.

"My father first."

For a moment nothing happened.

Then the shop stretched like a reflection on disturbed water.
The walls bent away, the ceiling rose into whiteness, and
the floor became Bellweather Street.

Mara stood between seconds.

The town was frozen around her. Rain hung in the
air like glass beads. In an upper window, a candle
flame remained perfectly still. A blackbird hovered above a chimney,
wings spread.

The Hourless One released the watch and pointed toward the
station.

Mara ran.

She found Elias in the waiting room. He sat against
the wall beneath the silent station clock. His hair had
gone completely white, though that morning it had still been
more brown than gray.

He looked up and laughed once in disbelief.

"You never listen."

"I listened to your journal."

"I wrote that you must not bargain with it."

"I listened selectively."

Mara helped him stand. He seemed weak, almost weightless.

"How long have you been here?" she asked.

"I don't know. There are no hours here."

The Hourless One appeared on the platform.

Its clock-face hand pointed at Mara.

"RETURN."

Elias stepped in front of her. "Take the watch."

"It doesn't want the watch," Mara said. "Not really."

She had finally understood the message. The stolen hour was
not stored in the silver case. The watch had only
hidden its trail.

The hour lived in her.

It was the hour in which her fever had broken,
the extra hour her life had been given.

Elias gripped her arm. "No."

"The hour has to be returned."

"No."

"If I don't return it, that thing will keep coming.
The clocks will keep failing. Maybe all of Bellweather will
end up trapped here."

"I will find another way."

"You had twenty years."

His face tightened as though she had struck him.

Mara softened her voice. "You gave me more than one
hour, Father. You gave me twenty years."

"And I want twenty more."

"So do I."

The Hourless One moved closer.

Around them, the station trembled. Cracks appeared in the white
sky. Through them Mara glimpsed darkness filled with turning wheels.

The clock above the platform began to strike.

Once.

Elias pulled the brass key from his pocket. "When it
reaches thirteen, the space between seconds will close. If we
are inside, we stay inside."

Twice.

"Then go," Mara said.

"Not without you."

Three times.

The Hourless One reached for her.

Mara opened the silver watch. Its hands stood at 3:17.

"Tell me how to return the hour."

Elias shook his head.

Four.

"Tell me!"

"No father should teach his child how to die."

"No child should have to watch her father vanish because
he is too stubborn to be saved."

Five.

The creature's fingers closed around Mara's wrist. Cold rushed through
her. Memories flashed before her eyes: her first morning in
the workshop, Elias lifting her so she could see a
music box; rain on the windows; burnt toast; birthdays; arguments;
quiet evenings spent sorting gears.

She felt the Hourless One searching through her life.

Six.

Elias seized the creature's arm, but his hands passed through
its coat.

"It will take the hour from your beginning," he said
desperately. "Everything after it may disappear."

"May?"

"I don't know."

Seven.

Mara smiled despite her fear. "You always hated not knowing."

"I still do."

Eight.

The station walls peeled away. Beyond them stretched an endless
white plain crowded with motionless people. Mara realized they were
not only from Bellweather. There were thousands from different places
and different centuries: soldiers, sailors, children, kings, farmers. People caught
between one instant and the next, perhaps all because someone,
somewhere, had tried to steal a little more time.

Nine.

The Hourless One raised its other hand toward the watch.

Mara saw the answer.

The silver case had an empty keyhole in its back.

"Give me the key," she said.

Ten.

Elias closed his fist around it.

"Please," Mara said.

Eleven.

He placed the key in her palm.

"I'm sorry," he whispered.

"For saving me?"

"For making the choice for you."

Mara inserted the key into the watch.

"I'm not."

Twelve.

She turned it to the right.

The watch sprang open with a burst of golden light.

Warmth poured from Mara's chest. It did not hurt. That
surprised her. She felt the stolen hour rising through her
like a remembered song—the cool cloth on her childhood forehead,
her father's hand wrapped around hers, the first deep breath
she had taken as the fever released her.

The light entered the silver watch.

The hands began to move.

3:18.

The Hourless One bowed its head.

Then the thirteenth bell rang.

The world shattered.

Mara woke to birdsong.

She lay on the floor of the workshop with one
hand still wrapped around the brass key. Sunlight streamed through
the windows. The storm had passed.

The blue door was gone. In its place stood a
plain wall crowded with clocks.

All of them showed 7:06.

All of them were ticking.

Mara sat up too quickly and nearly fainted. Someone caught
her shoulders.

"Careful," Elias said.

She stared at him.

He looked exhausted, and his hair remained white, but he
was alive.

"So am I," she said.

He laughed and pulled her into his arms.

For several minutes neither spoke. Around them, the clocks continued
their steady conversation.

At last Mara noticed the silver watch lying nearby. Its
hands moved normally. Inside the lid, the scratched message had
changed.

TIME OWES NO ONE, it still read.

Beneath it were two new words.

LIVE ANYWAY.

In the days that followed, Bellweather's clocks returned to their
ordinary habits. Mrs. Finch's cuckoo stopped predicting accidents. The church
bell no longer counted worshippers. The station clock behaved itself,
although it always ran one minute slow in heavy rain.

No one remembered Elias disappearing.

No one except Mara and Elias remembered the clocks stopping
at 3:17.

There were consequences.

Mara had feared she might lose the twenty years that
followed her stolen hour. Instead, she lost pieces of them.
Some memories vanished completely. She no longer remembered learning to
read or the tune her mother used to sing before
her death. She forgot the name of her first friend
and the taste of the cake from her tenth birthday.

Other memories remained but changed around the edges, as if
they belonged to someone else.

Elias remembered everything for both of them.

When she asked, he told her the stories. Often she
corrected details by instinct.

"I would never have climbed onto the church roof," she
said one evening.

"You did."

"Why?"

"To see whether the weather vane knew which way the
wind would turn before it turned."

"That does sound like me."

"It does."

Years passed.

Elias taught Mara every craft he knew. This time she
listened—not because she had stopped asking questions, but because she
finally understood that listening was another way of asking.

When Elias became too old for delicate work, Mara took
over the shop. She repaired watches and clocks from across
the country. People said she had inherited her father's gift.
She could hear what was wrong with a clock before
opening it.

Sometimes, late at night, she heard another sound beneath the
familiar ticking: a single distant bell, ringing from somewhere no
map could name.

She never searched for the blue door.

After Elias died at the age of eighty-seven, Mara placed
his silver watch in the workshop window. She left it
open so passersby could read the words inside.

Many paused to look.

Some understood. Most did not.

On the first anniversary of his death, a young girl
entered the shop carrying a broken alarm clock. She had
fierce eyebrows, muddy boots, and too many questions.

"Where do minutes go after they pass?" the girl asked
while Mara examined the clock.

"I don't know."

"Why do happy hours feel short?"

"I don't know."

"If two clocks disagree, which one is lying?"

Mara smiled.

"I don't know."

The girl frowned. "You're a clockmaker. Shouldn't you know about
time?"

"My dear," Mara said, opening the clock's brass case, "clockmakers
know less about time than anyone. We only know how
precious it is."

Outside, Bellweather Street shone after the rain. People hurried beneath
umbrellas, complaining of delays, wasting minutes, saving seconds, promising tomorrow,
remembering yesterday.

Above them, the railway clock moved from 3:17 to 3:18.

No shadows unfolded.

No doors appeared.

The next minute arrived freely, belonging to no one until
it was lived.

# The Museum of Lost Hours

At nine every morning, Elias Vale unlocked the Museum of
Lost Hours and waited for people to bring him their
regrets.

The museum stood at the end of a narrow street
where rainwater gathered between the stones and weeds pushed through
the pavement. It had no sign above the door. Visitors
found it only when they had exhausted every reasonable destination.

Inside were clocks.

There were silver pocket watches under glass, kitchen clocks with
painted roses, brass carriage clocks, sundials that had forgotten the
sun, and cheap plastic alarm clocks whose glowing numbers had
gone dark. None kept the correct time. Some ran backward.
Some moved only when no one was looking. One had
a second hand that trembled permanently between twelve and one.

Each clock contained an hour someone had lost.

Not wasted—wasted hours were common and belonged to no one.
The museum collected only the hours torn away by a
decision, an accident, a hesitation, or an ending. The final
hour beside a hospital bed. The hour before a train
departed. The hour spent searching the wrong street while someone
waited on the right one. The hour during which courage
might have changed everything.

Elias catalogued them carefully.

He lived above the museum in two small rooms with
sloping ceilings. He ate the same breakfast each day, wore
gray suits in winter and lighter gray suits in summer,
and had not traveled farther than the railway station in
twenty-seven years.

He had once believed routine protected a man from regret.

He now knew it merely taught regret where to find
him.

On a wet Tuesday in November, a girl entered carrying
a clock wrapped in a yellow scarf.

She was perhaps thirteen, though her solemn expression made her
seem older. Rain shone on her black hair, and one
of her red boots squeaked as she crossed the tiled
floor.

"Is this the place where hours go?" she asked.

"Some of them," Elias said.

She placed the bundle on the counter and unwrapped it.
Inside was a small wooden clock shaped like a house.
A painted bird waited behind two closed doors above the
face.

"It stopped yesterday," she said.

Elias took out his magnifying lens. "Most clocks do eventually."

"Not this one. My mother fixed it every time."

"Then perhaps she can fix it again."

"She died last night."

Elias lowered the lens.

Outside, tires hissed over the wet street. Somewhere in the
museum, a clock chimed five times, although it was barely
past nine.

"I'm sorry," he said.

The girl nodded impatiently, as if sorrow were a fact
already established and therefore not worth repeating.

"She told me to bring it here."

"Did she?"

"She said the man at the museum would know what
to do."

Elias examined the clock more closely. Its wooden roof was
scratched, and one side had been repaired with a strip
of copper. Along the bottom, almost erased by years of
handling, were two initials: M.V.

His breath caught.

"What was your mother's name?"

"Mara Venn."

The name entered the room like a gust of cold
air.

Elias looked at the girl again. He saw Mara in
the determined line of her mouth, in the dark eyes
that refused to look away.

"What is your name?" he asked.

"June."

"And did your mother say anything else?"

June reached into her coat and produced an envelope.

The paper had softened at the folds. On the front,
in handwriting Elias recognized at once, were the words: For
the Keeper.

He did not open it.

"What's inside?" June asked.

"I don't know."

"You could read it."

"I could."

But his hands had begun to shake.

Twenty-eight years earlier, before the museum and the gray suits,
Elias had repaired clocks in his father's shop near the
river. Mara Venn had come in one summer afternoon with
the wooden clock under her arm and sunlight in her
hair.

The cuckoo would not come out, she explained. It was
shy.

"Birds aren't shy," Elias had said. "They're mechanical."

"This one is shy."

He repaired the bellows, adjusted the chain, and returned the
clock the next day. She came back a week later
to complain that the bird sounded lonely. Then again because
it sounded too cheerful for rainy weather.

By autumn, Elias understood that nothing was wrong with the
clock.

By winter, Mara no longer pretended there was.

They spent a year together, then two. They planned to
leave the town, travel south, and open a shop near
the sea. Mara painted a blue door on a scrap
of paper and kept it in her purse.

They bought train tickets.

On the morning of their departure, Elias went to say
goodbye to his father. The old man called him ungrateful.
Elias called him a tyrant. Words accumulated until there was
no room left for affection. Then his father clutched his
chest and collapsed among the clocks.

Elias missed the train.

His father survived, but only after a long illness. Elias
remained to care for him. At first, Mara wrote every
week. Then every month. Then once at Christmas.

Elias answered none of her letters.

He told himself she deserved a life unburdened by his
duty. He told himself silence was cleaner than asking her
to wait. The truth was smaller: he was ashamed. He
could not admit that a single hour had defeated all
his plans.

Years later, after his father died, Elias discovered the museum's
key in a box of unclaimed repairs. There was no
note, only an address. He visited the abandoned building and
found shelves of silent clocks. When he touched the first
one, he heard a woman whisper, "I should have stayed."

He became the keeper because somebody had to be.

He had never expected Mara to find him.

June tapped the counter. "Are you going to open it?"

Elias broke the seal.

The letter contained one sentence.

Give June the hour I left behind.

Beneath it Mara had drawn a blue door.

Elias read the sentence three times.

"That's all?" June asked.

"That's all."

"Which hour did she mean?"

"I think I know."

He turned toward the long central gallery.

The museum stored its most dangerous hours below ground. Ordinary
lost hours contained memories. Powerful ones contained possibilities, and possibilities
disliked cages.

"Stay here," Elias said.

"No."

"This is not a place for children."

"My mother sent me."

"That does not make it safe."

June wrapped the yellow scarf around the wooden clock and
tucked it under her arm.

"I'm coming."

Elias recognized the expression on her face. Mara had worn
it whenever someone offered sensible advice.

He unlocked the door behind the counter.

A staircase descended into darkness. The air below smelled of
dust, iron, and rain on warm pavement. Elias lit a
lantern and led the way.

The basement was larger than the museum above. Rows of
shelves vanished into shadow, each crowded with clocks. Their ticking
gathered like an army marching far away.

June stayed close.

"What happens if you open one?" she whispered.

"You don't open a lost hour."

"Why not?"

"Because it opens you."

They passed a grandfather clock that wept seawater, a watch
sealed inside a child's shoe, and a station clock whose
hands spun whenever Elias approached. At the far wall stood
a black cabinet with twelve locked drawers.

Elias selected a key from the chain at his belt.

The seventh drawer contained a white railway clock.

Its face showed 10:16.

Elias remembered the time exactly. At 10:16, he had knelt
beside his father while the train carrying Mara began to
move. At 10:16, Mara had stood at a carriage window,
scanning the platform. At 10:16, two futures separated.

The clock had appeared in the museum shortly after Elias
became keeper. He had locked it away without touching it.

"This is hers?" June asked.

"Ours."

He set the railway clock on a table.

June placed the wooden clock beside it. For a moment
nothing happened. Then the little painted doors swung open.

The cuckoo emerged.

Instead of calling, it spoke in Mara's voice.

"Elias."

The lantern went out.

The basement disappeared.

Elias stood on a railway platform beneath a summer sky.

Steam rolled along the tracks. Porters shouted. Doors slammed. At
the far end of the platform, Mara leaned out of
a carriage window. She was twenty-four again, her hair tied
with a green ribbon.

"Elias!" she called.

His heart lurched with such force that he staggered.

Beside him stood June, still holding the wooden clock. She
looked around in wonder.

"Is this real?"

"It was."

The station bell rang.

Elias knew what came next. The train would move. Mara
would wait until the platform vanished. He would remain absent,
kneeling in his father's shop half a mile away.

But the lost hour had placed him here.

Mara saw him.

Her face changed—first joy, then confusion, because he was not
the young man she expected. He was sixty-three, silver-haired and
thin, wearing a keeper's gray suit.

She stepped down from the carriage.

Passengers flowed around her like water around a stone.

"You took your time," she said.

Elias tried to speak, but twenty-eight years crowded his throat.

June watched them both.

Mara looked exactly as he remembered, yet there was something
in her eyes that belonged to the woman who had
written the letter. The hour had not resurrected her youth.
It had allowed all her ages to meet inside one
face.

"I'm sorry," Elias managed.

"For which part?"

"Every part."

"That's too much sorrow for one apology."

The whistle blew.

"This isn't what happened," Elias said.

"No," Mara replied. "It's what might have."

He glanced at the train. "Did you want me to
come with you?"

"Then? More than anything."

"And later?"

"Later I wanted you to write. Then I wanted to
stop wanting it. Eventually, I did."

The words hurt, but not cruelly.

June moved closer to her mother.

Mara smiled and touched her cheek. June closed her eyes.

"You said this hour was for me," June whispered.

"It is."

"What am I supposed to do with it?"

"Choose."

The station blurred. For an instant, Elias saw another place
beyond it: a hospital room at night. Rain tapped the
window. Mara lay pale against white pillows while June sat
beside her, clutching the wooden clock.

Then the platform returned.

June's fingers tightened around the clock. "Can I change what
happened?"

"No," Elias said.

Mara looked at him. "Are you certain?"

He was not.

No keeper fully understood the museum. Its rules were learned
through damage. A man had once opened the hour before
his brother drowned and returned dripping wet, with seaweed in
his pockets and no memory of his own name. A
woman had entered the hour of her abandoned wedding and
lived there for nine years, though only nine minutes passed
outside.

Possibilities asked a price. Usually they took the future in
exchange for the past.

June pointed toward the waiting train. "If he gets on,
everything changes."

"Yes," Mara said.

"You might not meet my father."

"No."

"Then I might not exist."

The station clock clicked from 10:16 to 10:17, then jumped
back.

June looked at Elias. "What happens if we do nothing?"

"The hour closes."

"And my mother is still dead?"

"Yes."

"Then this is useless."

Her anger cracked on the final word.

Mara knelt before her. "No hour with someone you love
is useless."

"It is if they leave at the end."

"They always leave at the end."

June struck the wooden clock with her fist. The cuckoo
cried out. A crack split the painted roof.

The platform shook.

Suddenly the waiting train multiplied. A dozen engines stood on
a dozen tracks that had not existed before. In one
carriage, young Elias and Mara sat together, laughing over a
map. In another, they argued beside a crying baby. Through
another window Elias saw himself alone in a seaside shop
with a blue door, while Mara walked away along the
beach.

There were futures in which June had never been born
and futures in which someone very like her carried a
different name. There were futures of illness, betrayal, forgiveness, poverty,
ordinary mornings, and shared old age.

June stared at them.

"Which one is right?" she asked.

"All of them," Mara said. "None of them."

The tracks spread farther, cutting the world into shining lines.

Elias felt the hour pulling at him. It offered youth,
escape, and the chance to become innocent of his own
life. He needed only to board.

He looked at the young Mara waiting beside the open
carriage door.

Then he looked at June.

He understood why Mara had sent her.

The hour was not a gift of correction. It was
a test of inheritance. Mara had carried this abandoned possibility
all her life. Now, dying, she had refused to pass
its weight to her daughter.

Elias took the railway clock from the table that was
also the platform. Its hands burned cold against his palms.

"What are you doing?" June asked.

"Keeping my appointment."

He walked to the edge of the nearest track.

Young Mara stood in the carriage doorway.

"If you break it," she said, "you lose the hour
forever."

"I lost it twenty-eight years ago."

"You could still choose me."

"I did choose you," Elias said. "I simply chose badly."

A smile touched her face.

"That sounds like you."

The train began to move.

Elias lifted the clock and threw it beneath the wheels.

Glass shattered.

A great wind rushed through the station. The many trains
folded into one another. Futures burst like reflections on disturbed
water. June screamed. Elias reached for her, but the platform
dropped away.

He fell through ticking darkness.

Then he was lying on the basement floor.

Every clock in the museum was ringing.

June knelt nearby, coughing, the wooden clock clutched to her
chest. Its painted doors hung crookedly, but its hands had
begun to move.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

The railway clock was gone.

Elias helped June to her feet.

"Did you destroy her hour?" she asked.

"No. I let it finish."

Tears streaked her dusty face. "I didn't get to say
goodbye."

The cuckoo doors opened again.

The bird emerged and gave a single, ordinary call.

A folded strip of paper slid from its beak.

June unfolded it.

In Mara's handwriting were six words:

You already did. I heard everything.

June sat on the floor and cried.

Elias sat beside her. He did not tell her not
to. Grief, he had learned, was an hour that could
not be hurried. It had to strike every minute.

They remained there until the clocks fell silent.

When they returned upstairs, the rain had stopped. Late sunlight
entered through the dusty windows, touching the clocks with gold.

June placed the wooden clock on the counter.

"Can you repair it?"

"I believe so."

"When will it be ready?"

Elias considered saying tomorrow. He considered saying next week. Instead,
he took Mara's letter from his pocket and looked at
the blue door.

"There is a clockmaker near the sea," he said. "Or
there was, many years ago. They make parts for old
cuckoo clocks. It may take me some time to find
the place."

June studied him. "You're leaving the museum?"

"Only for a while."

"Who will look after it?"

He glanced around at the shelves, the cabinets, and the
thousands of imprisoned hours. For decades he had believed he
was their keeper. Now he wondered whether they had been
keeping him.

"The clocks can wait."

June tied the yellow scarf around the wooden clock and
handed it to him.

"My aunt says I can't travel with strangers."

"A sensible woman."

"She also says I shouldn't walk home alone."

"I'll accompany you."

They locked the museum together.

At the corner, Elias looked back. Without its lamps, the
building seemed smaller. Just bricks, glass, and a dark green
door at the end of a narrow street.

The station clock read 4:42 when they arrived.

Elias bought two tickets south.

Their train was delayed by one hour.

June groaned, but Elias laughed so unexpectedly that the ticket
clerk smiled.

They sat on a bench beneath the great station clock.
June ate a paper bag of sugared almonds. Elias held
the wooden clock on his knees, listening to its steady
ticking.

"Tell me about my mother," June said.

So he did.

He told her about the green ribbon and the imaginary
shyness of mechanical birds. He told her about Mara's blue
door, her terrible singing voice, and her habit of reading
the final page of every book first. He told the
truth when he knew it and admitted uncertainty when he
did not.

Around them, travelers hurried toward departures and reunions. Announcements echoed
beneath the vaulted roof. Pigeons fluttered among the iron beams.

The minute hand advanced.

No one tried to stop it.

When the train finally arrived, Elias and June boarded.

At 5:47, it carried them out of the station. The
town slipped past the window: chimneys, rooftops, the river, and
at last the museum's distant street.

Elias did not look away.

He watched until everything he knew had vanished behind him.

Then he turned toward the sea.

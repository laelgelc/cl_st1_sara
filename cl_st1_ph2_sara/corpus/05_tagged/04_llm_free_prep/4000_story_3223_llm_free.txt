# The Keeper of Lost Hours

In Bellwether, every house had two clocks.

The first told ordinary time. It hung above the stove
or stood on a mantel, measuring breakfast, work, sleep, and
the small appointments people made with one another.

The second clock was smaller and had no numbers. Its
face was made of smoky glass, and its single silver
hand moved only when someone in the house lost an
hour.

People lost hours in many ways.

A child staring out a classroom window might lose one
to a cloud shaped like a ship. A baker could
lose three before dawn, thinking about a woman who had
gone away twenty years earlier. Old men lost them beside
cold fireplaces. Young lovers lost them under apple trees and
insisted they had not been lost at all.

Whenever it happened, the silver hand on the second clock
twitched forward.

No one knew where the hours went.

No one, except Mr. Pell.

He lived at the end of Narrow Lane in a
building with a blue door and no ordinary clock. Above
the entrance, painted in fading gold letters, were the words:

**ELIAS PELL
KEEPER OF LOST HOURS**

Every Monday morning, people brought him their second clocks.

Mr. Pell would welcome them into his shop, where hundreds
of tiny instruments ticked softly inside drawers and cupboards. He
would place each clock beneath a green-shaded lamp, adjust a
lens over his left eye, and examine the smoky face.

"Two hours lost to rain," he might say.

Or, "Forty minutes misplaced during a difficult conversation."

Or, "Ah. This one wandered off while you were deciding
whether to knock on a certain door."

Most customers accepted his judgment. Mr. Pell would open the
back of the clock with a silver key and draw
out the lost time, which emerged as a thread of
pale light. He wound the thread onto a black wooden
spool, labeled it, and stored it in the cellar.

"What happens to it then?" people sometimes asked.

"It is kept," he replied.

"For how long?"

"As long as necessary."

People found this answer unsatisfying, but Mr. Pell had a
way of lowering his eyebrows that discouraged further questions.

His apprentice, Mara Vale, was the only person permitted in
the cellar.

She had come to him at the age of twelve,
carrying a broken second clock and a letter from an
aunt who no longer wanted the expense of feeding her.
Mr. Pell had read the letter, looked at the clock,
and then looked at Mara.

"You lost seven years in one afternoon," he had said.

Mara, thin and rain-soaked, had stared at him.

"That is impossible."

"Most true things are, at first."

He had taken her in. For the next nine years,
she learned to clean springs, polish glass, recognize the color
of regret, and separate an hour of boredom from an
hour of grief. She learned that time lost in anger
sparked against the skin, while time lost in happiness was
warm and difficult to contain.

The cellar held all of it.

There were shelves from floor to ceiling, and on every
shelf stood black wooden spools. Each was wrapped in light
and marked with a name, date, and brief description.

**MRS. ALDEN: WATCHING SNOWFALL, 4 HOURS**

**CAPTAIN ROOK: WAITING FOR A SON, 11 YEARS**

**UNKNOWN GIRL: HIDING BENEATH BRIDGE, 19 MINUTES**

The oldest spools whispered when no one was speaking. If
Mara stood among them long enough, she heard fragments of
laughter, unfinished arguments, lullabies, prayers, and doors closing in distant
houses.

"Never unwind a spool," Mr. Pell warned her.

"Why?"

"Because a lost hour remembers the person who lost it.
If released, it will try to return."

"Would that be so terrible?"

Mr. Pell's expression became severe.

"Time does not like being corrected."

For years, Mara accepted this. Then the clock tower stopped.

It happened at noon on the first day of winter.

Bellwether's tower had marked time for three hundred years. Its
bell had rung through fires, weddings, invasions, floods, and one
mayor's famously long speech. But on that cold noon, the
great hands froze together at twelve, and the bell released
only a dull iron cough.

Every ordinary clock in town stopped with it.

For one minute, Bellwether was perfectly still.

Then the second clocks began to scream.

Their silver hands spun wildly. Smoky glass shattered in parlors
and kitchens. People rushed into the streets carrying clocks wrapped
in blankets, tucked beneath coats, or pressed to their chests
like injured animals.

Mara and Mr. Pell worked without rest.

They covered the shop windows and laid damaged clocks across
every table. Lost hours leaked from cracked cases in bright
ribbons, curling toward the ceiling. Mr. Pell caught them with
iron tongs while Mara wound them onto spools.

By sunset, she had filled four cupboards.

"What is happening?" she asked.

"The tower has stopped."

"I had noticed."

"It is not merely a clock. It is the town's
anchor. Ordinary time passes through Bellwether because the tower allows
it."

Mara tied off a thread of blue light. "Then we
repair the tower."

Mr. Pell did not answer.

She looked up. He had gone pale.

"You knew this could happen."

"I knew it would happen."

Outside, the streets had become strange. Without clocks, people could
not agree on how long anything lasted. Some claimed night
fell in seconds. Others swore the afternoon continued for days.
Bread burned the instant it entered ovens. Candles refused to
melt. A baby took its first steps and then seemed
to remain in mid-stride while its mother cried for help.

Only the shop remained untouched. Its walls were thick with
stored hours.

Mr. Pell locked the front door.

"We must go to the tower," he said.

They crossed the square carrying a leather case of tools.
Snow hung in the air without falling. Townspeople stood in
anxious groups beneath the frozen clock face.

The mayor pushed toward them. "Pell! Fix it."

"I will inspect it."

"Inspect it quickly."

"There is no quickly at present."

The mayor opened his mouth, found no answer, and stepped
aside.

Inside the tower, Mara and Mr. Pell climbed a spiral
staircase. The air smelled of dust and iron. At the
top, behind the clock face, they found the mechanism motionless.

Mara had expected a broken gear.

Instead, she found an empty space.

At the center of the machinery stood an iron cradle
shaped to hold a spool. Strands of pale light clung
to its teeth, but the spool itself was gone.

"What was here?" she asked.

Mr. Pell set down his case.

"The first lost hour."

Mara waited.

He sighed. "Bellwether was founded by people fleeing a war.
When they reached this valley, they wanted to build a
town where nothing from their old lives could follow them.
Among them was a clockmaker named Ansel Vale."

"My family?"

"Your ancestor."

Mr. Pell touched the empty cradle.

"Ansel discovered how to remove time from memory. Not the
memory itself, but the hours attached to it—the long nights
of fear, the moments of violence, the final conversations with
the dead. The settlers gave him those hours willingly. He
wound them together and placed them in this tower."

"To power the clock?"

"To protect the town. The first spool contained a thousand
hours of shared grief. It became an anchor. As long
as it turned, time in Bellwether moved forward without dragging
the past behind it."

Mara looked at the pale strands.

"And now someone has stolen it."

"Yes."

"Who could enter the tower?"

"Only the keeper has the key."

She turned toward him.

Mr. Pell took a brass key from his pocket.

"I did not steal it," he said.

"Then someone made another."

"No. There is one other key."

"Who has it?"

"The clockmaker's heir."

The cold seemed to sharpen.

"You said my family died."

"I said they were gone."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

Below them, someone began shouting in the square. The cry
stretched unnaturally, becoming a low, endless note.

Mara seized Mr. Pell's sleeve. "Tell me the truth."

He looked suddenly old. Not merely elderly, as she had
always known him, but worn down by years too heavy
to count.

"Your mother is alive," he said.

Mara let go of him.

For a while, neither spoke.

She remembered almost nothing of her parents: her father's boots
by a door, her mother singing in a dark room,
sunlight on a red sleeve. Then a blankness, smooth and
deep, occupying the place where childhood should have been.

"Where?"

"Nearer than you think."

"Where?"

"The old railway house beyond the north field."

Mara struck him.

The sound was small inside the great machinery.

Mr. Pell did not move to defend himself.

"You let me believe she was dead."

"She asked me to."

"Why?"

"Because she had become dangerous."

Mara laughed once, without humor. "More dangerous than a man
who collects everyone's life in his cellar?"

"Yes."

They descended the tower. The square seemed unchanged, yet the
crowd's footprints had vanished beneath snow that still had not
landed. Mr. Pell tried to follow Mara, but she turned
on him.

"Stay here. Do whatever you can."

"Mara—"

"You have hidden enough from me."

She crossed the town alone.

Beyond the last houses, the north field lay white beneath
a darkening sky. Mara walked through suspended snowflakes, each one
cold and solid as a grain of salt. With no
time to govern distance, the railway house sometimes appeared a
mile away and sometimes close enough to touch.

She fixed her eyes on the red door and kept
walking.

At last, her hand met the latch.

Inside, a fire burned in the grate. A woman sat
beside it, winding pale light onto a black spool.

She was perhaps fifty. A silver streak divided her dark
hair. Her left sleeve was red.

"You came," the woman said.

Mara closed the door behind her.

"Are you Lysa Vale?"

The woman smiled sadly. "You have his directness."

"Are you my mother?"

"Yes."

Mara had imagined this moment without knowing she imagined it.
In some hidden chamber of herself, she had prepared accusations,
embraces, questions, and refusals. Now all of them crowded forward
and became silence.

Lysa stood.

Mara stepped back.

Her mother stopped at once. "I understand."

"No, you do not."

"No," Lysa said. "Perhaps I don't."

On the table between them lay a large spool wrapped
in restless white fire. It hummed with many voices.

"You stole the first lost hour."

"I reclaimed it."

"You stopped the town."

"I stopped a lie."

Mara looked at the spool. Inside its light, images flickered:
soldiers running through smoke, wagons in mud, a child crying
over a body, hands raising the first beams of a
house.

"You cannot keep it here," Mara said. "People are trapped.
Time is breaking apart."

"Time has been broken here since Bellwether was founded. Mr.
Pell simply taught everyone to call the fracture a tradition."

Lysa gestured toward the fire.

"Do you know what happens to the hours in his
cellar?"

"He keeps them."

"For what?"

Mara said nothing.

"The tower's first spool is wearing out. It has been
wearing out for generations. Every keeper feeds it with the
town's lost hours."

Mara remembered Mr. Pell carrying sealed cases from the cellar
once each year. She had never asked where they went.

"All of them?" she whispered.

"Not all. Enough. Bellwether survives by consuming pieces of the
people who live here. A daydream here. A sorrow there.
The hours we lose are taken, stripped of their names,
and wound into the tower."

"Then why would you remove the spool? You could have
exposed him."

"I tried. Your father tried. Mr. Pell took something from
us before we could."

Mara's hand tightened around the back of a chair.

"What?"

"Seven years."

The room seemed to tilt.

Lysa's eyes filled, but her voice remained steady.

"You were twelve when Pell found you, but you had
known him all your life. Your father served as his
assistant. When we learned the truth, we planned to leave
Bellwether and take you with us. Pell believed we would
destroy the tower and doom the town. He tried to
remove one hour from your father's memory—the hour in which
we discovered what he had done."

"The removal went wrong," Mara said.

"Yes. Your father lost more than an hour. He forgot
how to breathe."

The fire cracked.

Mara heard Mr. Pell's old warning: time does not like
being corrected.

"And me?"

"You saw it happen. You would not stop screaming. The
shock tore through your second clock. Seven years came loose
at once."

"So he took my memory."

"He saved your life," Lysa said. "And then he used
your confusion to keep you."

Mara wanted to deny it, but the blankness inside her
no longer felt empty. It felt locked.

"Why did you leave me with him?"

"I did not choose to. Pell banished me from the
town. He bound my hours to this house so I
could never approach you. I have spent nine years learning
how to break that bond."

"And today you did."

"Yes."

"By endangering everyone."

Lysa's face hardened. "There was no gentle way to stop
the tower."

"There is always a choice."

"Spoken like someone Pell raised."

The words struck more deeply than the slap Mara had
given him.

Lysa looked away first.

"I am sorry," she said. "But if we return the
spool, the tower continues feeding. If we destroy it, all
the hours inside will return to those who lost them."

"What would happen?"

"People would remember."

"The founders are dead."

"Hours do not care."

Mara approached the table. The spool's voices grew louder. She
heard battle cries layered with wedding songs, sobbing mixed with
laughter, and beneath everything the deep pulse of the clock
tower.

"What happens if the hours have nowhere to return?"

"They find the nearest living memory."

"A thousand hours of grief could flood the town."

"Yes."

"And all the newer hours the tower consumed?"

"They will return as well."

Mara imagined Bellwether filled with generations of fear. Children remembering
wars fought before their birth. Bakers collapsing beneath the sorrow
of strangers. Old men reliving every regret they had surrendered
for the sake of one peaceful night.

"That could destroy them."

Lysa's voice softened. "Truth sometimes does."

"Then truth is not enough."

Her mother frowned. "What do you mean?"

Mara thought of the cellar shelves, the neat labels, the
hours separated and named. She thought of Mr. Pell, who
had lied to her, saved her, betrayed her, and taught
her the difference between time lost in anger and time
lost in grief.

"Hours remember the people who lost them," she said. "That
is why they become dangerous when released."

"Yes."

"But what if they are given somewhere else to go?"

"Where?"

Mara looked toward Bellwether.

The tower rose black against the motionless snow.

"Into the ordinary clock."

They carried the first spool across the north field together.

Lysa could leave the railway house now, but each step
cost her. Nine years of confinement pulled at her like
invisible chains. Mara supported her, and for the first time,
their shoulders touched.

When they reached the square, nothing moved.

The townspeople stood like figures in a painting. A dog
hung above the ground in the middle of a leap.
The mayor's coat was open, one brass button floating beside
it. Even the endless shout had gone silent.

Mr. Pell waited at the tower door.

His eyes moved from Mara to Lysa to the spool.

"So," he said. "The hour returns."

"Not to the cradle," Mara replied.

Lysa leaned heavily against her. "She has another idea."

Mr. Pell's mouth tightened. "We have little time."

"We have none," Mara said. "That may help."

At the top of the tower, Mara opened the leather
tool case. She removed pliers, winding rods, glass tubes, and
a narrow silver blade.

Then she began dismantling the mechanism.

Mr. Pell stared. "What are you doing?"

"The second clocks collect lost hours. The tower clock consumes
them. That is the design."

"Yes."

"I am changing it."

"To what?"

"A clock that returns what it takes."

He caught her wrist. "You do not understand the forces
involved."

Mara looked at his hand until he released her.

"You made sure I would not."

For a moment, pain crossed his face.

Then he stepped back.

Mara worked.

She connected the empty cradle to the great clock's four
faces. She reversed the teeth of the escapement and opened
channels in the brass plates. Lysa unwound a single strand
from the stolen spool, while Mr. Pell—silent now—adjusted the pendulum.

The strand fought them. It lashed like lightning, filling the
tower with memories.

Mara saw a road crowded with refugees. She smelled burning
wheat. She felt the hand of a terrified child gripping
hers, though she was not there and had not been
born.

"Hold it!" Mr. Pell cried.

Lysa wrapped the light around her bare arm. Blisters rose
where it touched her skin.

Mara fitted the strand into the reversed gear.

The mechanism shuddered.

For one terrible instant, the tower seemed to inhale.

Then the pendulum moved.

Once.

Twice.

The frozen hands jerked backward.

"No," Mr. Pell whispered.

Mara seized the winding rod. "Help me."

Together, the three of them turned it.

The clock resisted. Every gear screamed. The great hands trembled
between one moment and the next.

Then the bell rang.

The sound broke over Bellwether like thunder.

Snow fell.

The dog landed. The mayor's button struck the ground. Across
the square, people cried out as their second clocks burst
open.

Light poured from every house.

It streamed through chimneys, windows, and cracks beneath doors. It
rose from the keeper's shop in a shining column as
thousands of labeled hours unwound themselves in the cellar.

But they did not return to the people who had
lost them.

They entered the tower.

The reversed mechanism drew them through its gears and released
them into the ordinary clock, where memory became motion. Grief
moved the minute hand. Joy turned the hour wheel. Boredom,
longing, rage, wonder, and all the misplaced afternoons of Bellwether
flowed together, no longer hidden and no longer owned.

The clock began to measure more than passing time.

With every tick, someone remembered a little—not the full wound,
not the unbearable weight, but enough.

A widow in the square remembered her husband's worst joke
and laughed.

The baker recalled the face of the woman who had
left him, then realized he could no longer remember why
he had hated her.

A child looked at the falling snow and felt, for
a heartbeat, the courage of an ancestor crossing a battlefield.

Mara fell to her knees.

Her seven lost years returned as fragments.

Her father lifting her onto his shoulders.

Her mother teaching her to read clock faces.

Mr. Pell showing her how to hold a spring without
bending it.

An argument in the shop.

A flash of light.

Her father on the floor.

Mr. Pell weeping as he turned the silver key.

The memories hurt, but they did not drown her. They
passed through the tower, softened by thousands of other hours,
and settled into place.

When the bell stopped ringing, the clock showed one minute
past twelve.

Mr. Pell sat against the wall.

"It will need a keeper," he said.

"No," Mara replied. "It will need repairers. More than one."

Lysa knelt beside her. Her burned arm trembled.

"And the second clocks?" she asked.

Below them, the town's smoky-faced clocks were going dark. Their
silver hands slowed and became still.

"Let people keep their own lost hours," Mara said. "Perhaps
lost does not have to mean stolen."

Mr. Pell closed his eyes.

"What will become of my shop?"

Mara considered this.

"You could repair ordinary clocks."

"I have never cared for ordinary clocks."

"You might learn."

He gave a tired, almost offended sigh. It was the
first familiar thing he had done all day, and Mara
found that she could not hate him entirely. Forgiveness was
farther away, but it had appeared on the horizon.

That would have to be enough.

In the months that followed, Bellwether changed.

People removed their second clocks from the walls. Some kept
them in drawers. Some planted flowers in their empty cases.
The mayor ordered his melted down and made into a
very inaccurate sundial.

The tower continued to run, though never perfectly. On days
of great celebration, it gained several minutes. During funerals, it
slowed. In spring, when children stared too long at clouds,
the bell sometimes rang for no reason.

Mara opened the tower to anyone willing to learn its
machinery.

Lysa came each morning from the railway house. She and
Mara did not try to recover nine years all at
once. They began with tea. Then they added walks, arguments,
shared work, and occasional silences that were no longer empty.

Mr. Pell returned to the shop on Narrow Lane. He
painted over the old sign, though traces of the gold
letters remained visible beneath the new words:

**ELIAS PELL
REPAIRER OF CLOCKS
ORDINARY AND OTHERWISE**

He was not very good with ordinary clocks.

Mara visited on Mondays to help.

Years later, when travelers asked why Bellwether's tower was always
a little wrong, the townspeople gave different answers.

Some said the gears had been damaged in a winter
storm. Some blamed the town's damp air. The mayor claimed
the clock was not wrong at all and produced several
documents to prove it.

But the oldest residents told another story.

They said the clock kept not only the hours people
used, but the hours they wasted, mourned, dreamed through, or
gave away. It kept afternoons that seemed endless and years
that vanished overnight. It kept every moment people wished to
forget and every moment they wished they could live again.

That was why, they said, the tower sometimes hurried and
sometimes fell behind.

It was not measuring time as clocks were supposed to
measure it.

It was measuring time as people lived it.

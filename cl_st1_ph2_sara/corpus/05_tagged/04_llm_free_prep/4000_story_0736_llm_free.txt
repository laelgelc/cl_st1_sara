Mara Vale first heard the dead man's radio at 2:17
on a Thursday morning.

She was alone in the lighthouse kitchen, holding a mug
of tea that had long since gone cold, while rain
rattled against the windows. Beyond the glass, the sea was
invisible except when the beam turned across it, revealing a
white confusion of waves and foam.

The radio sat on a shelf above the sink. It
was an old marine set with a cracked dial, a
bent antenna, and a habit of humming whenever the weather
changed. Mara kept it switched on during storms, although few
boats came near Bellweather Rock anymore. The shipping lanes had
moved east decades ago, and modern vessels carried equipment far
more reliable than an aging lighthouse keeper with binoculars and
a brass bell.

At first, the voice was so faint she thought the
wind had found a new crack in the walls.

"Bellweather Light, this is the Mercy Anne. Do you read?"

Mara set down her mug.

Static filled the kitchen.

Then the voice returned, a man speaking calmly but with
great effort.

"Bellweather Light, this is the Mercy Anne. We have lost
our engine. We are taking water."

Mara reached for the transmitter.

"This is Bellweather Light. Mercy Anne, give me your position."

For several seconds there was only the hiss of the
storm.

"Three miles southwest of the rock," the man answered. "Four
souls aboard."

Mara took a pencil from the drawer and wrote the
position on the back of an unpaid electricity bill.

"What is the nature of your vessel?"

"A fishing boat. Red hull, white cabin."

"Can you deploy an anchor?"

"Negative. Cable snapped."

Mara glanced toward the dark window. A boat drifting northeast
from that position would be carried directly toward the Needle
Teeth, a ragged line of submerged rocks that had opened
more hulls than anyone in the village cared to count.

"Mercy Anne, I'm contacting the coast guard. Keep this channel
clear."

She switched frequencies and sent the emergency message. The station
at Dunsby replied at once, but their rescue vessel would
need nearly forty minutes to reach the area—longer if the
storm worsened.

Mara returned to the original frequency.

"Mercy Anne, rescue is on the way. What is your
rate of flooding?"

No answer.

"Mercy Anne, do you read?"

A burst of static, then the man said, "The pump's
failed."

His voice sounded closer now, though Mara knew that meant
nothing.

"Can you see the lighthouse beam?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Keep it off your port side if you can. The
current will carry you north. Do not try to steer
toward the light. There are rocks between us."

A different voice came over the radio, distant and frightened.
A child's voice.

"Papa?"

The man covered the transmitter, but not quickly enough.

Mara stood very still.

"Mercy Anne," she said, "you reported four souls. How many
adults?"

"Two. My wife and me. Two children."

"How old?"

"Eight and eleven."

The kitchen seemed to shrink around her. She pictured small
hands gripping the rails of a sinking boat, rain stinging
their faces.

"All right," she said. "Put life jackets on everyone. Keep
the children inside as long as the cabin is safe.
Rescue is coming."

"We don't have forty minutes."

"You may have more time than you think."

"We don't."

The certainty in his voice chilled her more than the
storm.

Mara pulled on her oilskin coat and climbed the spiral
stairs to the lantern room. The great lens revolved around
her, throwing bars of light across the sea. From the
western window she searched the darkness with binoculars.

At first she saw only rain. Then the beam swept
southwest, and for half a second something red appeared between
the waves.

A hull.

It vanished.

Mara waited for the light to return. When it did,
she saw the white cabin and a mast leaning at
a bad angle. The boat was already much closer than
three miles.

She ran downstairs and took the radio.

"Mercy Anne, I have you in sight."

"How far from the rocks?"

"Too close."

There was no point lying.

A heavy thud came through the receiver. Someone shouted.

"We hit something," the man said.

Mara grabbed the logbook from beneath the radio. The lighthouse
keepers had recorded every storm, wreck, repair, and unusual tide
since 1861. She turned to the hand-drawn chart pasted inside
the back cover. Modern maps marked the Needle Teeth as
a single hazard, but the old chart showed narrow passages
between the rocks.

Most were impossible in rough water. One, called the Widow's
Thread, ran from southwest to northeast and opened into the
little cove below the lighthouse.

Mara had sailed it once, at sixteen, with her father.

Never again, he had told her afterward. That channel is
not a road. It's a door that sometimes decides to
be a wall.

She studied the old bearings. To enter the passage, a
boat had to line up the lighthouse with the black
shoulder of Mount Varrow. But in the storm, the mountain
would be invisible.

Unless Mara gave them another marker.

"Mercy Anne, can you still steer?"

"Barely."

"Listen carefully. There is a channel through the rocks. It's
narrow, but it may bring you into the cove."

"May?"

"The rescue boat won't reach you before the reef does."

Silence.

Then the man said, "Tell me what to do."

Mara looked toward the lower gallery that circled the lighthouse.
Mounted on its western rail was the old fog bell.
No one used it now; the automatic horn had replaced
it before Mara was born. But the bell was still
there, green with age and solid as the day it
had been cast.

"You'll hear a bell," she said. "Steer directly toward it
until I tell you otherwise."

"In these waves?"

"Do you have a better idea?"

"No."

"Then trust me."

Mara left the radio on the gallery floor, turned its
volume high, and went out into the storm.

The wind struck her so violently she fell against the
railing. Rain needled her eyes. Thirty meters below, waves smashed
against the base of the cliff and leaped upward in
silver sheets.

She seized the bell rope and pulled.

The bell gave a dull, reluctant knock.

She pulled again. Nothing.

"Come on," she muttered.

She wrapped the rope around both hands and threw her
weight backward. Rust broke loose from the frame. The bell
swung, and its deep iron voice rolled over the water.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Through the radio, the fisherman said, "I hear it."

"Steer toward the bell."

Mara rang until her shoulders burned.

Between strokes, she counted the seconds. The lighthouse beam crossed
the sea, and the red boat appeared again. It was
turning, its bow rising and plunging as it fought the
current.

"More to starboard," Mara ordered.

The boat altered course.

"No—too far. Port. Two degrees if you can."

"Two degrees?" the man said. "The wheel's trying to break
my arms."

"Then don't let it."

A wave buried the Mercy Anne. For a terrible moment
it did not rise. Then the bow burst from the
black water, streaming foam.

Mara kept ringing.

The old chart was inside, pinned under the logbook. She
had memorized the first turn but not the second. She
crawled through the doorway, checked the bearing, and returned to
the bell.

"Now steer away from me," she said.

"What?"

"Turn east. The bell should be behind your left shoulder."

"You told me to follow it."

"If you keep following, you'll hit the Crow's Jaw."

"I can't see anything!"

"You don't need to see. Turn east now."

The reply dissolved into static.

Mara watched the beam circle back. The red hull appeared
among white breakers, broadside to the waves.

"Turn!" she shouted into the radio.

The bow moved slowly east.

A wave struck the boat's port side. The mast snapped
and vanished into the sea. The Mercy Anne rolled so
far that Mara saw the dark underside of its hull.
She held her breath until the vessel righted itself.

A child was screaming over the radio.

"We're through the first rocks," Mara said. "You're doing well."

"You call this well?"

"You're still talking to me."

The fisherman laughed once, without humor.

Mara looked again at the chart.

The next leg was longer, nearly half a mile between
two lines of reef. At the end, the channel narrowed
around a single tall rock known as Saint Orra's Finger.
In daylight it stood several feet above the water. Tonight
the waves hid it completely.

The safest route passed south of the rock, but the
current had changed since the chart was drawn. Mara knew
because her father had spent thirty years measuring it. In
winter storms, the surface water moved north while the deeper
current dragged south. A damaged boat riding low would feel
both.

She needed to know how much water the Mercy Anne
had taken on.

"How deep are you sitting?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"Is the deck awash?"

"Sometimes."

"Is the cabin floor flooded?"

"Yes."

"Above your ankles?"

A pause. "Above my knees."

Mara shut her eyes.

The deeper current would have the stronger grip.

"Keep east, but aim five degrees north."

"That leads toward the reef."

"It compensates for the current."

"You're sure?"

Mara thought of her father at the kitchen table, drawing
arrows across old charts. She thought of how impatient she
had been, how certain she would leave Bellweather and never
need any of it.

"Yes," she lied.

The next fifteen minutes passed one bell stroke at a
time.

The coast guard radioed twice. Their vessel had been forced
north by the wind but was approaching the cove from
the eastern side. They could not enter the reef. All
they could do was wait.

The fisherman's name was Elias. His wife, Nessa, had injured
her arm when the mast fell. Their children were Tom
and Lily. Mara learned these things in broken pieces between
instructions, though she had not asked. Elias seemed compelled to
tell her, as if names were ropes he could throw
across the water.

"They wanted to see the islands," he said.

"Save your breath."

"Lily's birthday was yesterday."

"Elias."

"I told them the weather would hold."

"Elias, listen to me. In two minutes you'll reach the
narrowest point."

The radio crackled.

"Tell me what to do."

"You're going to turn north when I give the word.
A rock will be on your right. You may not
see it, but you'll hear the waves breaking over it."

"And if I turn too early?"

"You hit the western reef."

"Too late?"

"The rock."

"Comforting."

"Watch the lighthouse beam. When it crosses your bow, count
to four. Then turn north."

The Mercy Anne vanished behind a wall of rain.

Mara rang the bell and waited.

The beam went around.

One.

Two.

Three.

It swept across the sea. For an instant, the boat
shone red as blood.

"Count now!" Mara shouted.

She counted with him.

"One. Two. Three. Four."

"Turn!"

Elias grunted with effort. Through the radio came the groan
of the damaged boat, the crash of loose equipment, Nessa
calling to the children.

Then another sound rose behind it all: the roar of
water against stone.

"Rock to starboard!" Elias shouted.

"Hold your course."

"It's right beside us!"

"Hold."

The sound grew thunderous.

"Hold!"

A grinding shriek tore through the radio. Mara imagined the
hull opening like paper.

But Elias cried, "We're past!"

She looked out just as the beam returned. The Mercy
Anne emerged on the northern side of Saint Orra's Finger,
still afloat.

Beyond it lay the mouth of the cove.

Mara laughed. She had not expected to, and the sound
came out half sob.

"You've done it," she said. "Come straight toward the bell."

She rang with renewed strength.

The boat limped through the final passage. The waves became
smaller as the reef broke their force. Soon the beam
revealed the cove's stone pier, the old boathouse, and four
coast guard officers waiting with lines.

Mara gave her last instruction.

"Cut the wheel to port and let the current bring
your stern around."

Elias obeyed. The Mercy Anne drifted alongside the pier. A
line flew through the rain and landed across its deck.

Hands reached down.

First came a boy in an orange life jacket. Then
a smaller child. Then Nessa, one arm hanging uselessly at
her side.

Elias left the boat last.

When his boots touched the pier, the Mercy Anne settled
suddenly, almost gently, and disappeared beneath the dark water.

Mara released the bell rope.

Her hands were bleeding where the wet fibers had cut
them. She watched the family climb the steps toward the
lighthouse, surrounded by rescuers.

Then the radio beside her spoke.

"Bellweather Light, this is the Mercy Anne. Do you read?"

Mara stared at it.

Elias was halfway up the cliff path. He stopped and
looked toward the gallery. Even at that distance, she saw
the confusion on his face.

The radio repeated the message.

"Bellweather Light, this is the Mercy Anne. We have lost
our engine. We are taking water."

It was the same voice.

Not merely like Elias's voice. The same words, the same
calm strain, the same faint wash of static.

One of the coast guard officers heard it through his
own radio. He raised the set and answered.

"This is Dunsby Rescue. Identify yourself."

"Three miles southwest of the rock," the voice continued. "Four
souls aboard."

The officer looked out to sea.

Mara ran inside and checked the clock.

It was 2:17 in the morning.

She had noticed the time when the first call came.
She was certain of it. Yet more than an hour
had passed, and the clock's second hand now trembled in
place without moving.

Downstairs, the rescued family entered the lighthouse.

Elias climbed to the lantern room alone. His face was
gray from cold. There was blood on his forehead, and
seawater ran from his coat.

"What is that?" he asked.

Mara turned down the radio's volume, but the voice continued
beneath her hand.

"Bellweather Light, do you read?"

"When did you make the first call?" she asked.

Elias stared at her. "About two hours ago."

"No. The one I answered."

"You never answered."

The bell moved slightly in the wind.

Elias looked back toward the stairs, where his children's voices
rose from the kitchen.

"Our radio died after we struck the first rock," he
said. "I kept calling, but no one replied. Then we
saw the lighthouse beam, and I heard the bell."

Mara felt the room tilt beneath her.

"You spoke to me all the way through the channel."

"I heard a woman's voice once, just before we entered
the reef. It said, 'Follow the bell.' After that, the
radio was dead."

From the receiver came the cries of children, the crack
of a breaking mast, and Elias's recorded laugh.

Mara switched the set off.

The voice continued.

Faintly at first, then clear enough to fill the room.

"You've done it," Mara's own voice said through the dead
speaker. "Come straight toward the bell."

Elias crossed himself.

The clock began ticking again.

Neither of them spoke until the coast guard officer entered.
By then, the radio was silent.

At dawn, the storm moved north. The sea around Bellweather
Rock turned a hard, innocent blue. A helicopter carried Nessa
and the children to the mainland, while Elias remained behind
to answer questions about the wreck.

The officials blamed electrical interference for the strange transmission. One
suggested atmospheric echo, though he could not explain how an
echo had repeated words before, rather than after, they were
spoken. Another said exhaustion could do peculiar things to memory.

Mara allowed them their explanations.

When everyone had gone, she opened the lighthouse logbook.

She intended to record the wreck, but first she turned
to the entries from older storms. There were hundreds of
rescues and dozens of losses. She read until evening.

In an entry dated November 3, 1896, Keeper Jonathan Wren
had written:

At seventeen minutes past two, received distress call from the
Mercy Anne, fishing vessel, four aboard. Could not establish position.
Heard bell in western gale though bell rope had been
removed for repair. No vessel found.

Mara read the passage three times.

She turned the page.

A note in another hand had been added many years
later:

The Mercy Anne was lost in 1896 with Elias Bell,
his wife Nessa, and their children Thomas and Lilian. Wreckage
recovered near the Needle Teeth. No survivors.

Mara carried the book to the window.

Far below, beside the cove, the modern Mercy Anne's mast
drifted in the shallows. The red paint on the broken
wood was bright and new.

That evening Elias returned from the village. He brought two
cups of tea to the gallery, where Mara was cleaning
rust from the bell.

"My grandfather named me," he said. "He was Thomas Bell."

Mara stopped scraping.

"His father was lost at sea when he was eight,"
Elias continued. "But my grandfather survived. Family story says a
bell led him through a reef in a small lifeboat.
He never knew where it came from."

He looked at the great bronze bell.

"This lighthouse, perhaps?"

"Perhaps."

"My daughter's full name is Lilian."

The setting sun turned the sea to hammered copper. For
a while, neither of them said anything.

"Do you think they called us?" Elias asked at last.
"The people from the old boat?"

Mara considered the question.

"No," she said. "I think the sea confused two nights."

"That's your explanation?"

"It's the only one I have."

Elias smiled faintly. "The officials had better ones."

"The officials weren't here."

They drank their tea.

Before leaving, Elias helped Mara fit a new rope to
the bell. It was clean white hemp, strong enough to
survive many winters. When they finished, the bell hung motionless
above the calm water.

Months passed.

The Mercy Anne was raised and taken away as salvage.
Nessa's arm healed. Tom wrote Mara a letter describing the
rescue in great detail, including a picture of the lighthouse
with flames coming out of the top. Lily sent a
small brass bell for the kitchen shelf.

Spring came late to Bellweather Rock. Seabirds nested on the
cliffs, and yellow flowers appeared between the stones. Tourists began
arriving from the village in little white boats. Mara told
them about the lens, the old foghorn, and the keepers
who had lived there before electricity.

She did not tell them about the radio.

But every Thursday night, she left it switched on.

Most nights it gave her only static, weather reports, and
the distant conversations of passing ships. Once she heard a
sailor singing. Once she heard two fishermen arguing about soup.
Never again did she hear the Mercy Anne.

Years later, when Mara's hair had turned silver and the
lighthouse had been marked for full automation, a final storm
came.

It arrived from the west with almost no warning.

At 2:17 in the morning, Mara woke to the sound
of the kitchen radio.

"Bellweather Light," said a young woman's voice, "do you read?"

Mara picked up the transmitter.

"This is Bellweather Light. Go ahead."

"My compass has failed. I can see your beam, but
the reef is between us. I don't know the channel."

Mara looked through the window. Far out in the rain,
a single green navigation light rose and fell.

"What is your name?" she asked.

"Lily Bell."

Mara closed her eyes.

The little girl from the Mercy Anne would be thirty-two
now.

"Are you alone?"

"Yes."

"Is your engine working?"

"Yes, but I'm being pushed toward the rocks."

Mara pulled on her oilskin coat.

"Listen carefully," she said. "There is a channel through the
reef. It's narrow, but it will bring you into the
cove."

"How do I find it?"

Mara opened the gallery door.

The old bell waited in the darkness, its rope pale
against the storm.

"You'll hear a bell," she said. "Steer toward it until
I tell you otherwise."

She stepped into the rain, wrapped both hands around the
rope, and rang.

The sound rolled out across the black sea—deep, certain, and
older than memory.

Far beyond the reef, a green light turned toward home.

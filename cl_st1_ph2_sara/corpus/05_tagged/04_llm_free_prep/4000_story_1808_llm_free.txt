Mara Venn first heard the dead man's radio at 2:17
in the morning.

She was alone in the lighthouse, halfway through a cup
of bitter coffee, when a voice broke through the receiver's
steady hiss.

"North Glass Light, this is the *Mercy Bell*. Do you
read?"

Mara set down her cup.

The lighthouse radio received little traffic in winter. Fishing boats
kept south of the reefs, and merchant ships used the
deeper channel twelve miles offshore. Sometimes the coast guard called
to test the equipment. Sometimes teenagers in the village found
an old transmitter and whispered ghost stories into the dark.

But no one called from the *Mercy Bell*.

The *Mercy Bell* had gone down twenty-three years ago.

Mara pressed the transmit key. "Unidentified vessel, repeat your name."

For several seconds, there was only static.

Then the voice returned, thin but calm.

"This is the *Mercy Bell*. Engine failure. Taking water. Fifteen
souls aboard. Request immediate assistance."

Mara stared at the radio.

On the wall above it hung a framed newspaper page,
yellowed around the edges. Her predecessor had put it there,
perhaps as a warning. The headline read:

FERRY LOST IN SUDDEN STORM—NO SURVIVORS FOUND

Below it was a photograph of the *Mercy Bell*, a
squat white ferry that had once carried mail, supplies, and
passengers between the mainland and the northern islands. It had
vanished on a clear September night. Wreckage washed ashore for
months, but no bodies were ever recovered.

Mara lifted the microphone again. "State your position."

The reply came at once.

"Half a mile east of Black Tooth Reef."

Mara turned toward the windows.

Outside, the sea was invisible. Fog pressed against the glass
like breath. The lighthouse beam rotated through it, casting a
pale wall of light that appeared and vanished every twelve
seconds.

Black Tooth Reef lay half a mile east.

Mara's fingers tightened around the microphone. "What is your captain's
name?"

A pause.

"Captain Elias Venn."

The room seemed to tilt beneath her.

She had been four years old when her father drowned.

Her memories of him were not memories so much as
fragments borrowed from photographs and repeated stories: a red scarf,
a tobacco-scented coat, large hands lifting her above his head.
Her mother had rarely spoken about the night the ferry
disappeared. When asked, she only said that the sea was
greedy and that Elias had trusted it too much.

Mara swallowed. "Say again."

"Captain Elias Venn."

The voice on the radio was not her father's. It
belonged to a younger man, perhaps the ferry's operator. Yet
the name itself opened something cold inside her.

"This is North Glass Light," she said. "What is the
date aboard your vessel?"

The answer dissolved in a crackle.

Then: "September fourteenth."

"What year?"

The static deepened. Through it, Mara thought she heard shouting,
the clang of metal, and water rushing across a deck.

"What year?" she repeated.

"Please," the operator said. His calm had broken. "The captain
says the light is wrong."

Mara looked up at the turning mechanism.

The lens revolved smoothly. The lamp burned bright. Its rhythm
was correct: three seconds of light, nine seconds of darkness.
She had checked it at midnight, as she did every
night.

"What do you mean, wrong?"

"He says North Glass should show two white flashes. We're
seeing one long beam."

Mara glanced at the brass plaque fixed beside the controls.

NORTH GLASS LIGHT
PATTERN ALTERED: 15 SEPTEMBER, 1998

The old signal had been two white flashes every twelve
seconds. It was changed the morning after the *Mercy Bell*
disappeared.

Mara's mouth went dry.

"What do you see around you?" she asked.

"Fog. Heavy swell. No stars. We struck something below the
waterline. Pumps aren't holding."

That, too, contradicted the official account. The weather report from
the night of the sinking described clear skies and a
calm sea.

"Listen carefully," Mara said. "Can your engines restart?"

"No."

"Lifeboats?"

"Port boat was damaged. Starboard davit is jammed."

Mara moved to the chart table. The old paper map
was covered with pencil marks, tide calculations, and depths measured
long before sonar. Black Tooth Reef curved beneath the sea
like a hook. A vessel drifting west from its eastern
edge would be pushed toward the lighthouse island by the
incoming tide—unless it found the narrow passage through the reef.

That passage was called the Needle.

No captain used it at night. Even modern rescue boats
avoided it in daylight.

"Can you steer?" she asked.

"Barely."

Mara traced the Needle with her finger. "Turn northwest. Hold
three hundred and fifteen degrees."

The operator did not respond.

"*Mercy Bell*, do you read?"

A different voice came over the radio.

"Mara?"

She stopped breathing.

The voice was deeper, roughened by wind and years of
cigarettes. She had heard it only once before, on a
wax cylinder her mother kept locked in a drawer. Elias
had recorded a message at a seaside fair, laughing as
he promised to bring home sugared almonds.

"Mara," the voice repeated. "Little star, is that you?"

Her knees weakened. She gripped the edge of the table.

"This isn't possible."

"No," Elias said softly. "I don't suppose it is."

Behind his words came the muffled roar of panic. Someone
was praying. Someone else called for buckets.

Mara forced herself to speak. "You're in the Needle, near
Black Tooth Reef. Turn northwest."

"I can't see the lighthouse properly. The signal is wrong."

"The signal changed after you sank."

Silence.

The beam swept past the windows, whitening the fog.

"After we what?" he asked.

Mara closed her eyes.

She could lie. She could tell him rescue was coming,
that dawn was near, that everything would be all right.
But Elias Venn had been a captain. A captain needed
the truth.

"It's been twenty-three years," she said. "The *Mercy Bell* was
lost with everyone aboard."

For a long moment, nothing came through the receiver but
the sea.

Then her father exhaled.

"So that's what this is."

"What?"

"A second chance," he said. "Or a punishment."

The radio clicked as he handed it away. The operator
returned, breathing fast.

"North Glass, we've turned northwest. Depth is falling."

"You're too far south," Mara said. "Come five degrees starboard."

The lighthouse shuddered.

A deep vibration passed through its stone walls, followed by
a sound like distant thunder. Mara looked toward the floor.

The tower had stood on its island for a hundred
and six years. Storms had broken against it, lightning had
split its old lantern, and once a wartime shell had
chipped the western wall. It did not shudder without reason.

The receiver lights flickered.

"North Glass?" the operator called.

"I'm here."

"We can see you now."

Mara approached the window.

At first, there was only fog. Then the beam turned,
piercing farther into the darkness, and something white appeared below.

A ship.

It emerged for three seconds: bow low in the water,
smokestack leaning, deck crowded with figures. Rust streaked its sides,
though the newspaper photograph showed fresh paint. Seaweed hung from
the railings. Its windows were black.

Then the beam passed, and the ship vanished.

Mara backed away.

The radio hissed.

"Depth is six fathoms," the operator said. "Five and a
half."

According to the chart, the Needle should have been nine
fathoms deep at high tide.

Unless they were not in the same sea.

Mara pulled open the lighthouse logbook. She turned past recent
entries, past years of weather notes and supply lists, until
she found the copied account of the sinking.

14 September, 1998.
11:40 p.m.—Unidentified light reported east of reef.
11:52 p.m.—Light appears to move north.
12:03 a.m.—Possible distress flare.
12:10 a.m.—Fog observed around Black Tooth despite clear conditions elsewhere.
12:19 a.m.—Received fragmentary wireless transmission: "Light is wrong."
12:27 a.m.—Transmission lost.

At the bottom of the page, in darker ink, someone
had added:

I turned the lamp to guide them. God forgive me.

Mara looked at the lantern controls.

The old keeper had seen the ferry. He had tried
to help. And the ferry had sunk.

"What happened last time?" she whispered.

Her father answered, as though still holding the radio nearby.

"You showed us the way."

"Did you follow the beam?"

"I did."

"And?"

"And we struck the reef."

Mara stared at the rotating lens.

The beam swept across the room, over the chart, over
the framed newspaper. Every twelve seconds, the *Mercy Bell* saw
it and steered toward safety. Every twelve seconds, Mara guided
it exactly where it had gone before.

Not a second chance, she thought.

A circle.

The lighthouse shuddered again, harder. A glass chimney fell from
a shelf and shattered. Wind screamed around the tower, though
a moment earlier the air had been still.

"Four fathoms!" the operator shouted. "Rocks ahead!"

Mara seized the microphone. "Turn east. Full starboard."

"East takes us back into open water."

"Do it."

The engine was dead, but the ferry could still turn
with the current and what remained of its steering. Mara
listened to men shouting aboard the ship. A bell rang
twice.

"Four fathoms," the operator said. "Holding."

Mara studied the chart. If the lighthouse beam was part
of the loop, she had to remove it. Yet switching
off a major navigation light was forbidden except during maintenance
or immediate danger. Other vessels could be at sea beyond
the fog.

But this fog belonged only to the dead.

She climbed the iron stairs to the lantern room.

The lens towered above her, a cage of prisms and
shining brass. Its electric lamp burned at the center, brilliant
enough to hurt her eyes. The rotation motor hummed beneath
the floor.

She opened the control box and reached for the main
switch.

Her hand stopped.

In the curved glass, she saw a reflection that was
not her own.

A little girl stood behind her, wearing a yellow raincoat.

Mara spun around.

No one was there.

When she looked back, the child remained in the glass.

Four-year-old Mara had worn a yellow raincoat the last morning
she saw her father. She remembered that now—not from a
photograph, but truly remembered. Rain dripping from the hood. Elias
kneeling to fasten the top button. His beard scratching her
cheek when he kissed her.

"I'll be back before your moon goes down," he had
said.

She had waited at the kitchen window until dawn.

The reflected child lifted one hand and placed it against
the glass.

Mara touched the same spot.

Below, the radio crackled loudly enough to echo up the
stairs.

"North Glass!" the operator cried. "We're turning toward the reef
again!"

Mara threw the switch.

The lamp went dark.

The world disappeared.

Without the beam, the fog consumed everything beyond the lantern
glass. The rotation motor slowed to silence. Mara heard only
her own breathing and the boom of waves below.

Then lights appeared in the fog.

Not electric lights—lanterns. Dozens of them floated on the black
water around the island. Each glowed beside the face of
a drowning person.

Mara saw an old woman with silver hair plastered to
her cheeks. A sailor clutching a broken oar. A boy
holding a wooden horse. The fifteen lost passengers and crew
of the *Mercy Bell* drifted in the sea, staring up
at her.

Among them was Elias.

He looked younger than Mara was now. His red scarf
moved in water that did not seem to touch him.

"You need the light," Mara said.

His pale face turned toward the eastern darkness.

"No," his voice replied through the radio below. "We needed
the truth."

The lanterns began to drift away from the reef.

Mara ran downstairs.

"Report your heading," she said.

"East-northeast," answered the operator. His voice was steadier now. "Depth
increasing. Five fathoms. Six."

The black lighthouse windows reflected the room back at her.
Without the lamp, she could no longer see the ship.

"Keep east-northeast," she said. "You'll reach deep water."

"Seven fathoms."

The tower shook so violently that the logbook slid from
the desk. Its pages fluttered. Cracks appeared in the plaster
beside the window.

The loop was breaking, and it did not want to
break gently.

Mara grabbed the book before it fell. Several pages had
turned to the year of the sinking. Ink crawled across
the paper by itself.

12:20 a.m.—Keeper extinguished North Glass Light.

The words vanished and appeared again.

12:20 a.m.—Keeper maintained signal.

Again.

12:20 a.m.—Keeper extinguished—

12:20 a.m.—Keeper maintained—

Two histories fought for space on the page.

"Mara," her father said.

"I'm here."

"We're in eight fathoms. The current has us."

"You're clear of the Needle."

A crash sounded above. One of the lantern windows shattered,
spraying glass down the stairwell. Wind tore through the tower.
The emergency system activated, and a warning bell began to
ring.

Mara knew what would happen next. After sixty seconds without
power, the backup lamp would ignite automatically.

She ran to the emergency cabinet and pulled at the
door.

Locked.

The key hung upstairs in the lantern room.

"Ten fathoms," the operator reported.

The warning bell rang again.

Fifty seconds.

Mara took a fire axe from the wall and swung
it into the cabinet lock. Metal shrieked. Pain shot through
her arms.

"North Glass," the operator said, "we see a light ahead."

"Describe it."

"Green. Low on the horizon."

A channel marker. The boundary of safe water.

Forty seconds.

Mara struck the lock again. The cabinet door bent but
held.

"What will happen if you reach it?" she asked.

"I don't know."

Her father answered. "We go where we were going."

"Where is that?"

"We were taking Mrs. Vale to see her first grandchild.
Tom Arlen was going to ask a woman to marry
him. Young Peter had never seen the city. I was
coming home to you."

Thirty seconds.

Mara lifted the axe.

"And now?" she asked.

"Now we finish the crossing."

The axe fell. The lock broke.

Inside the cabinet, a red lever stood beneath a protective
cover. Mara smashed the cover with the axe head and
pulled the lever down.

The warning bell stopped.

Darkness held.

"Marker close," said the operator. "Depth fourteen fathoms."

The tower gave one final groan. Then the shaking ceased.

Mara dropped the axe.

Through the eastern window, the fog began to thin. A
green light blinked once in the distance. Beneath it, barely
visible, the *Mercy Bell* moved across calm water.

It no longer looked drowned. Its paint was white. Its
smokestack stood straight. Warm light filled its windows, and music
drifted from the passenger cabin.

Figures lined the rail.

The operator spoke for the last time. "We're clear, North
Glass. Thank you."

Mara held the microphone with both hands. "Safe crossing, *Mercy
Bell*."

Her father's voice returned.

"I'm sorry I missed your life, little star."

Mara could not answer at first.

All the words she had once imagined saying crowded together:
Where were you? Why did you leave? Did you know
I waited? Did you know Mother never forgave the sea?
Did you know I became a lighthouse keeper because some
part of me believed I could guide you home?

Instead, she said, "I remember your red scarf."

Elias laughed softly.

"I hated that thing."

"You wore it every winter."

"Your mother made it."

"I know."

The green marker blinked again. The ferry grew fainter.

"I kept my promise badly," he said.

"You came back before my moon went down," Mara replied.
"It only took longer than I expected."

For a moment, she heard him breathing.

Then he said, "Look after the light."

The radio fell silent.

The fog withdrew from the island. Stars appeared overhead, sharp
and countless. Far out at sea, a cargo ship sounded
its horn, warning that the true world had returned and
needed its lighthouse.

Mara restored the main lamp.

The beam swept over empty water.

At dawn, the coast guard arrived to investigate the outage.
Mara told them a mechanical failure had forced her to
disable both systems. They inspected the equipment, found no cause,
and lectured her about emergency procedures.

One officer noticed the broken cabinet.

"Used an axe?"

"It was stuck."

He frowned but wrote it down.

After they left, Mara opened the logbook.

The old entry had changed.

14 September, 1998.
11:40 p.m.—North Glass operating normally. Clear skies. Calm sea. No
unusual activity.

There was no report of fog. No unidentified light. No
broken transmission.

The framed newspaper page had changed too.

FERRY ARRIVES AFTER MYSTERIOUS DELAY

The article said the *Mercy Bell* had reached the mainland
six hours late after suffering engine trouble. All passengers and
crew survived. Captain Elias Venn was praised for navigating away
from Black Tooth Reef despite heavy fog reported aboard the
vessel.

Mara read the article three times.

Her father had lived.

According to the new history, he returned home the next
day. He raised his daughter, retired at sixty, and died
peacefully only two years earlier.

Mara searched the lighthouse quarters for evidence of that life.
Photographs filled drawers that had once been empty. Elias at
her school graduation. Elias teaching her to row. Elias, gray-haired
and smiling, standing beside her on the day she became
keeper of North Glass.

She remembered none of it.

Or rather, she remembered it the way one remembers a
story heard in childhood. Behind those shadow memories remained the
kitchen window, the yellow raincoat, and twenty-three years of absence.

On the back of the final photograph, her father had
written:

For Mara, who always brings us home.

That evening, she placed the photograph beside the radio.

Winter passed. No dead ships called again.

In spring, Mara traveled to the mainland and visited her
father's grave. It stood on a hill overlooking the harbor,
beneath an ash tree just beginning to leaf. The stone
named him captain, husband, father, and stubborn old fool.

Someone had wrapped a faded red scarf around it.

Mara sat in the grass and told him about the
winter storms, the gull nesting on the tower roof, and
the coast guard officer who still believed she had destroyed
government property for no reason. She told him she was
sorry she could not remember the life he had lived
with her.

The wind moved through the new leaves.

Far below, a ferry sounded its horn as it entered
the harbor.

Mara closed her eyes.

For one impossible second, a radio hissed in the silence
between waves, and a familiar voice said, "I remember enough
for both of us."

When she opened her eyes, the sea was bright beneath
the afternoon sun.

That night, North Glass shone over calm water. Its beam
turned once every twelve seconds, faithful and clear, illuminating the
reef without promising that safety lay in the light itself.

And on ships passing beyond Black Tooth, sailors sometimes saw
a second vessel traveling beside them—a small white ferry with
music in its cabin and fifteen lanterns glowing along its
rail.

It never appeared on radar.

It never answered when called.

But those who saw it said that no matter how
thick the fog or violent the storm, the ferry was
always heading toward open water.

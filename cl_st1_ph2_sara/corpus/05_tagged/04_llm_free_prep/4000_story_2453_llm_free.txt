# The Lighthouse at Low Water

On the morning the sea disappeared, Mara Vale was repairing
a clock that had never worked.

It belonged to the old railway station, although no trains
had stopped in Bellweather for thirty years. The clock was
enormous, its white face cracked from eleven to four, its
iron hands frozen at six minutes past midnight. Mara had
spent three weeks cleaning salt from the gears and replacing
teeth worn down by decades of wind.

At nine in the morning, the harbor bell began to
ring.

There was no wind.

Mara lowered her screwdriver and listened. The bell sounded again,
deep and uneasy, followed by shouts from the street.

She crossed the station, pushed open the swollen oak door,
and stepped outside.

The sea was gone.

Bellweather stood on a crescent bay where water usually pressed
against the seawall at high tide. Now the harbor lay
empty. Fishing boats rested crookedly in black mud. Anchors, lost
nets, broken pots, and pale stones covered the exposed seabed.
Beyond them stretched a glistening plain, reaching farther than Mara
could see.

People crowded the wall. Some whispered prayers. Others climbed down
ladders to collect stranded fish.

Old Captain Orrin stood alone on the pier, gripping the
railing with both hands.

"It has gone out before," said Jory Pike, the baker.
"Storm tide. Moon tide."

"Not like this," Orrin replied.

His voice carried through the sudden silence.

At the edge of the horizon, where blue water should
have met gray sky, something shone.

Mara narrowed her eyes.

It was the top of a tower.

By noon, half the village had descended into the harbor.

They moved cautiously at first, sinking ankle-deep in mud. Children
gathered coins and green bottles. Fishermen inspected the exposed hull
of the Mercy Bell, which had sunk during a winter
gale before Mara was born. Its ribs rose from the
seabed like the bones of a whale.

Mara remained at the seawall.

The tower on the horizon troubled her. Sunlight flashed from
its windows in slow, regular intervals.

Flash. Darkness. Flash.

A lighthouse.

"That's impossible," Mara said.

Captain Orrin had appeared beside her without a sound.

"Most things are," he said, "until they happen."

He was eighty-two, broad-shouldered despite his age, with a face
the weather had carved into hard lines. He had known
Mara's father and sailed with her mother. He was also
the only person in Bellweather who never asked why Mara
had abandoned the city and returned home.

"Do you know what it is?" she asked.

Orrin watched the distant light.

"When I was a boy, my grandmother told me there
was once an island beyond the bay. On it stood
a lighthouse called Saint Lume. Then the sea rose in
a single night and swallowed both."

"A children's tale."

"So I thought."

"What was the lighthouse for?"

"To guide people home."

Mara folded her arms. "That is generally what lighthouses do."

Orrin gave her a sharp look.

"Not ships," he said. "People."

Before she could ask what he meant, a cry rose
from the exposed harbor.

A boy stood near the wreck of the Mercy Bell,
pointing toward the lighthouse. Beside him, a line of flat
stones had surfaced from the mud. They formed a road.

It ran straight across the empty sea.

By one o'clock, the council had forbidden anyone to follow
it.

By half past one, twenty-three people were walking toward Saint
Lume.

Mara was not among them.

She returned to the station and tried to work, but
the clock's gears resisted her. Each time she set the
escapement in motion, it stopped after six ticks.

Outside, bells rang as villagers called to friends crossing the
seabed. The sound grew fainter as the walkers moved away.

Mara opened her toolbox, selected a smaller file, and bent
over the mechanism.

"You're using the wrong spring."

She froze.

A man stood in the doorway.

For one bright, merciless second, Mara believed he was her
father. The same silver hair. The same narrow shoulders and
oil-stained waistcoat. But when he stepped inside, she saw that
he was younger, no more than thirty, with an unfamiliar
scar along his jaw.

"I beg your pardon?" she said.

"The balance spring," he replied. "It is too rigid."

"This station is closed."

"So is the sea, apparently. People still insist on walking
through it."

He approached the clock. Mara moved between him and the
workbench.

"Who are you?"

"Elias Rook."

"No Rooks live in Bellweather."

"Not anymore."

He produced a brass pocket watch. A seven-pointed star was
engraved on its lid. Mara recognized the mark immediately: it
appeared on the station clock, on every gear and plate,
though no history of the manufacturer survived.

"Where did you get that?"

"My family made it."

"Your family made this clock?"

"They made many things." He looked toward the bay. "Mostly
promises they could not keep."

Mara took the watch from him. It was warm, as
if it had been held near a fire. When she
opened the lid, the hands were moving backward.

"What is this?"

"A warning."

"That isn't an answer."

"No," Elias said, "but we have little time for better
ones."

He explained while Mara locked the station.

Saint Lume, he said, had not been built to guide
sailors. Its lamp marked a place where lost moments collected:
final words never spoken, doors never opened, journeys abandoned from
fear or pride. Usually those moments remained buried beneath the
sea. Once in a lifetime, however, the water withdrew and
the road appeared.

Those who reached the lighthouse before the sea returned could
recover one lost thing.

"It grants wishes?" Mara asked.

"It offers choices."

"That sounds like a wish disguised by someone who wants
to feel respectable."

Elias did not smile.

"The price is a memory."

"Whose memory?"

"Yours."

They stood at the top of the harbor ladder. Below
them, the stone road gleamed between fields of mud and
seaweed. People had spread out along it, carrying ropes, lanterns,
baskets, and food, as if heading to a festival.

Mara looked back at the station clock through the dusty
window.

"Why tell me?"

"Because your father reached Saint Lume thirty-seven years ago."

The world seemed to tilt beneath her.

"My father died last winter."

"I know."

"He never mentioned a lighthouse."

"He would not have remembered it."

Mara descended the ladder.

The road was wider than it had appeared from above.
Ancient paving stones lay fitted together without mortar. Small pools
trembled on either side, crowded with silver fish. Crabs fled
into the mud as Mara and Elias passed.

"What did my father recover?" she asked.

"Your life."

She stopped.

Elias continued several paces before turning.

"You were born too early," he said. "The village doctor
could do nothing. Your father followed the road. He asked
for the morning of your birth to happen differently."

"That is absurd."

"You lived."

"Children survive."

"Some do."

Mara's hand tightened around the pocket watch.

"What memory did he lose?"

"His wedding day."

She remembered her father sitting beside her mother during the
final months of her illness. He knew how she took
her tea, which songs she disliked, where she kept spare
buttons, and the exact way to fold a blanket around
her feet. Yet whenever she spoke of their wedding, he
listened with a polite, distant smile.

Mara had thought grief made him quiet.

Ahead, Saint Lume rose higher with every step. Its stone
walls were dark with old water stains. Around its base
stood the remains of a village: roofless cottages, a chapel,
a square filled with white shells. The travelers from Bellweather
moved among the ruins, calling out to one another.

Some had already found what they sought.

Jory Pike knelt in the square, embracing a woman in
a red coat. Mara recognized her from an old photograph
behind the counter of his bakery: his sister, dead for
nearly forty years. Near the chapel, Mrs. Fen held the
hand of a little girl who had vanished during a
storm. On the lighthouse steps, Captain Orrin sat beside a
young man in a sailor's uniform. Neither spoke.

"They are not truly returned," Elias said. "Not yet."

"What do you mean?"

"The lighthouse gives each traveler a choice. Carry the recovered
moment home, and lose a memory of equal weight. Or
leave it here and return unchanged."

Jory's sister laughed. The sound crossed the drowned square like
music.

"What happens to the people who are brought back?"

"They become real, if chosen."

"And their lives? Their deaths?"

"Rewritten where necessary."

Mara stared at him. "The whole world changes?"

"The world is always changing. Saint Lume merely permits someone
to notice."

A low boom rolled across the seabed.

Far behind them, at the edge of the horizon, the
sea had begun to rise.

The villagers shouted. Some turned toward Bellweather. Others ran into
the lighthouse.

"How long?" Mara asked.

"An hour, perhaps less."

She looked at the tower door.

"Why did you come?"

Elias glanced at the backward-moving watch.

"To stop my family from making the same mistake again."

Before Mara could question him, he walked into the lighthouse.

Inside, a spiral staircase wound around a hollow shaft. There
were no rooms, no doors, only hundreds of clocks fastened
to the walls. Grandfather clocks, carriage clocks, watches, sundials, hourglasses—all
moved at different speeds. Their combined ticking filled the tower
like rain.

Mara touched the nearest clock.

A memory opened beneath her fingers.

She was eight years old, standing on a kitchen chair
while her father taught her to repair a music box.
He placed each tiny screw in her palm and told
her that broken things were only puzzles asking to be
understood.

Then the scene vanished.

Mara snatched back her hand.

"Whose memory was that?" she asked.

"Yours," said Elias from the stairs above.

"But I remember it."

"Not for long, if someone chooses to take what waits
at the top."

They climbed.

On the next turn, Mara saw Jory descending with his
sister. He was smiling, but his face had changed. When
Mara greeted him, he stared at her blankly.

"It's me," she said. "Mara."

His sister drew closer to him.

"I'm sorry," Jory said. "Have we met?"

He had known Mara since childhood. Every Sunday for twenty
years, he had left a currant loaf on her doorstep.

Elias touched her arm.

"Keep moving."

Higher still, they passed Mrs. Fen and the little girl.
Mrs. Fen was weeping.

"I can't," she told the child. "I'm sorry. I can't
forget your father too."

The girl nodded solemnly and kissed her cheek. Then she
faded, not like a ghost, but like a reflection disturbed
by water.

At last, Mara reached the lantern room.

No flame burned there. Instead, a great glass lens revolved
around an empty pedestal, gathering sunlight and casting it out
across the drowned land.

A man stood beside the lens.

He wore an oil-stained waistcoat. His silver hair was untidy,
and his hands were black with grease.

"Hello, little gear," he said.

Mara forgot how to breathe.

Her father had called her that when she was small.

He looked as he had before the fever hollowed his
cheeks, before his hands began to shake, before he failed
to recognize the tools he had used all his life.

Mara stepped toward him.

"You're dead."

"So I've been told."

She embraced him. He smelled of soap, machine oil, and
the peppermint sweets he kept in his pockets. His arms
closed around her with familiar strength.

For several minutes, Mara was no longer thirty-six, no longer
alone in an empty house, no longer angry at all
the questions death had left unanswered.

She was simply someone's daughter.

When she released him, Elias stood by the stairs.

"What will it cost?" Mara asked.

Elias opened his pocket watch. Only a narrow arc remained
before the backward hands reached midnight.

"Your craft," he said.

Mara looked at him.

"You would forget everything your father taught you. Every clock
you repaired. Every hour at his bench. The knowledge, the
instinct, the joy of it."

Her father lowered his eyes.

"No," Mara said. "Take something else."

"The lighthouse decides what is equal."

"I don't care about clocks."

Her father smiled sadly. "That is a poor lie, little
gear."

The tower shook.

Through the windows, Mara saw water racing across the seabed,
swallowing pools and stones. People hurried along the road toward
Bellweather. Some ran alone. Others carried recovered children, lovers, parents,
and friends.

"Come with me," Mara told her father.

He touched the brass key hanging from her neck—the key
to the station clock.

"You almost have it working."

"It doesn't matter."

"It mattered yesterday."

"You weren't here yesterday."

"No," he said. "But what I taught you was."

Mara's throat tightened.

She thought of all the clocks awaiting her in the
workshop, each with its hidden failure. She thought of mornings
spent listening to their uneven beats until she could hear
which gear was worn, which spring strained, which wheel sat
crooked on its pin. She thought of her father guiding
her hands, then watching proudly when she no longer needed
guidance.

If she chose him, would she remember why she loved
him?

"You chose me," she said. "When you came here before."

"That was different."

"How?"

"You had a life ahead of you. I have had
mine."

"That isn't fair."

"No," he said gently. "It is only true."

Another wave struck the tower. Several clocks fell from the
wall and shattered. The lens spun faster, throwing wild bands
of light around the room.

Elias shouted, "You must choose now."

Mara seized her father's hand.

A door of white light appeared beside the pedestal. Beyond
it she saw the railway station, warm and bright. The
great clock ticked above the platform. Her father stood at
his workbench, alive. Mara stood beside him, smiling without recognition
as he placed a screwdriver in her hand and tried
to teach her what she had once known.

It could be enough, she thought.

People began again every day.

Then she noticed the station clock. Its hands showed six
minutes past midnight.

Still broken.

Her father followed her gaze.

"You were using the wrong spring," he said.

Mara laughed through her tears. "A stranger told me the
same thing."

"Then perhaps he is not entirely useless."

Elias looked offended, but only for an instant.

The pocket watch chimed.

Mara embraced her father once more.

"I don't want to lose you again."

"You won't," he said. "Not in the ways that count."

She stepped away from the door.

The white light collapsed.

Her father faded with it, his smile the final thing
to disappear.

For a moment, Mara stood in the empty lantern room.
Then the sea struck Saint Lume.

Elias grabbed her hand and pulled her toward the stairs.

They ran as water burst through the lower windows. Clocks
tore free from the walls, spilling gears into the flood.
The staircase groaned. Below, the square vanished beneath white foam.

"There is no time to reach Bellweather!" Mara shouted.

"There is another way."

"Where?"

"Up."

They climbed to the outer gallery. Waves surrounded the lighthouse,
hammering the rooftops of the drowned village. Far away, the
last stretch of stone road disappeared.

Elias opened his pocket watch and placed it inside the
empty lamp housing.

The seven-pointed star began to glow.

"What are you doing?" Mara asked.

"What my family should have done long ago."

The watch cracked.

Light poured through the lens, brighter than the sun. Every
clock in the tower chimed at once.

The next moment, Mara stood in the old railway station.

Rain tapped against the windows. The harbor bell was silent.
Outside, the sea rested against the wall as if it
had never moved.

Elias lay unconscious beside her.

Above them, the station clock ticked.

Mara looked up.

Six minutes past midnight.

Then the minute hand moved.

Elias woke near sunset. By then, Mara had learned that
only seven minutes had passed since she left the station.
Most of Bellweather remembered nothing. Jory Pike was behind his
counter, selling bread alone. Mrs. Fen sat outside the chapel
with her husband. Captain Orrin stood on the pier, gazing
toward the horizon with tears in his eyes.

"Did you find what you wanted?" Mara asked him.

Orrin nodded.

"Did you bring him back?"

"No."

"Why not?"

The old captain watched a fishing boat enter the harbor.

"Because saying goodbye is not the same as losing someone,"
he said. "Sometimes it is how we keep them."

He tipped his cap and walked home.

A year later, trains began stopping in Bellweather again.

No one could explain why the railway company restored the
forgotten route, but Mara did not object. She repaired the
station clock, polished its face, and wound it every Monday
morning.

Elias stayed in the village. His knowledge of clocks was
mostly theoretical, his habits irritating, and his ability to lose
tools unmatched. Still, Mara allowed him half the workbench.

Together they opened a shop in the station waiting room.

Above the door hung a brass sign engraved with a
seven-pointed star. Beneath it were the words:

VALE & ROOK
REPAIRS, RESTORATIONS, AND LOST TIME

Occasionally, customers brought them watches that ran backward. Mara always
refused payment for those.

On certain mornings, when the tide was very low, she
walked to the seawall and looked across the bay. She
never saw Saint Lume again. Yet now and then, sunlight
flashed at the horizon in slow, regular intervals.

Flash. Darkness. Flash.

She would touch the key around her neck and think
of her father's hands guiding hers.

Then, from the station behind her, the great clock would
strike the hour—not perfectly, perhaps, but clearly enough to be
heard across the whole village.

And Mara would turn toward the sound and go home.

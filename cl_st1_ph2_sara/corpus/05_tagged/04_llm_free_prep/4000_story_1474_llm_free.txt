On the morning the sea forgot the moon, Mara Venn
found a silver fish in her kitchen sink.

It was a little longer than her hand, with delicate
fins and scales that shone like coins at the bottom
of a well. It lay perfectly still beneath the dripping
tap. When Mara leaned closer, its black eye turned toward
her.

"Please," said the fish, "put me back."

Mara did not scream. She had lived alone for eleven
years in a house full of settling beams, rattling pipes,
and mice that scratched behind the walls. A talking fish
seemed only one more thing in need of repair.

"Back where?"

"The sea."

"The sea is half a mile away."

"I know."

"Then how did you get into my sink?"

The fish opened and closed its mouth. "The tide took
a wrong turn."

Mara considered this. Beyond the window, the village of Bellwether
was waking beneath a colorless sky. Chimneys exhaled thin ribbons
of smoke. Doors opened. Carts creaked toward the market square.
Everything appeared ordinary, except that the gulls were silent.

She found a blue ceramic bowl, filled it with water,
and carried the fish down the hill.

Bellwether stood on a crescent bay between two black cliffs.
Generations of storms had polished its houses and whitened its
streets with salt. The villagers were accustomed to impossible weather.
They had seen rain fall upward, lightning trapped inside bottles,
and a fog that whispered the names of the dead.
They respected such events, wrote them into the parish ledger,
and continued working.

But that morning, the sea was gone.

Mara stopped at the end of Anchor Street.

Beyond the harbor wall lay a vast plain of wet
sand stretching to the horizon. Ships rested on their sides.
Nets sagged between stranded buoys. Fish flapped in silver crowds,
and crabs hurried beneath rocks, offended by the sudden sky.

Hundreds of villagers stood along the shore.

Old Harker, who had sailed for forty years, gripped the
harbor rail with both hands. "Lowest tide I ever saw."

"There's no water at all," said the baker.

"That's what makes it low."

Children ventured onto the uncovered seabed, collecting shells and lost
cutlery. Their parents called them back, though no one could
agree on what danger to name.

Mara looked down at the fish in her bowl.

"Is this what you meant?"

"Yes," it said. "The sea has forgotten the moon."

"Seas don't forget."

"Everything forgets eventually."

The fish told her its name was Pell. It had
been swimming above the church steeple of a drowned city
when the water began to pull away. Currents twisted. Whirlpools
opened in impossible places. Pell had been swept through underground
channels, up a spring, along the village pipes, and out
of Mara's tap.

"Why did the water leave?" she asked.

Pell's gills fluttered weakly. "Someone stole the moon's name."

Before Mara could ask another question, the bell in the
harbor tower began to ring.

Mayor Vale had climbed onto a barrel. He was a
broad, red-faced man who believed that any crisis could be
reduced by standing above it.

"People of Bellwether!" he called. "Remain calm. The council has
formed a committee."

This announcement produced no visible comfort.

"The sea will return at the proper hour," he continued.
"Until then, do not walk beyond the wreck of the
Mercy Bell. Do not touch unfamiliar creatures. And if anyone
discovers the cause of this irregularity, report it to my
office before supper."

A murmur moved through the crowd.

Mara carried Pell home.

She had no particular affection for the sea. Eleven years
earlier, it had taken her husband, Edrin, and returned only
one boot. People told her not to blame the water.
The sea had no mind, they said, no cruelty or
intention. But grief prefers an enemy, and the sea was
large enough to hold all of hers.

She set Pell's bowl on the table and closed the
curtains against the empty horizon.

"Who could steal a name?" she asked.

"A thief."

"That is not helpful."

"A king. A poet. A person who has lost something
and cannot bear the loss."

Mara's hands went still.

Pell swam once around the bowl. "Names bind things to
the world. A moon without a name cannot be called.
If it cannot be called, the tides cannot follow."

"The moon is still in the sky."

"Is it?"

Although it was morning, Mara knew where to look. The
moon should have hung pale above the western cliff.

There was only blue-gray emptiness.

By noon, the village wells had begun to taste of
iron. Boats cracked under their own weight. The stranded fish
died in shining heaps, and a smell of salt and
rot crept through the streets.

The committee met in the town hall and issued three
statements, each more reassuring than the last. By the third,
everyone knew disaster was close.

Mara asked Pell how the moon's name might be found.

"Lost names gather in deep places," he said. "Cellars. Graves.
The bottoms of cups. The final pages of books no
one reads."

"The sea is full of deep places."

"Not anymore."

Mara went to the attic.

Edrin's belongings remained in a cedar chest beneath the eaves:
two wool coats, a rusted compass, letters tied with green
ribbon, and a chart of the bay drawn in his
narrow hand. For eleven years, Mara had opened the chest
only to keep the moths away.

Edrin had been a mapmaker. He charted reefs, currents, sandbanks,
and places where sailors claimed to hear bells beneath the
water. His final map showed a cluster of lines beyond
the western cliff. At their center he had drawn a
circle and written one word: Orison.

Mara remembered asking him about it.

"A drowned city," he had said.

"There are no drowned cities in Bellwether Bay."

"Then it must be somewhere else."

Two days later, he sailed into a clear morning and
did not return.

Mara brought the chart downstairs.

Pell leaped so violently that water splashed onto the table.
"That is where I was before the tide turned."

"The drowned city?"

"Orison. Its highest tower has a door made of pearl.
Behind it is a room where forgotten things wait."

Mara stared at the map. Without water, Orison might now
stand exposed somewhere on the endless seabed.

"You think the moon's name is there?"

"I think all lost things pass through Orison."

Outside, people shouted. Mara opened the door.

Along the horizon, a black wall had appeared.

At first she thought the sea was returning, but it
did not shine. It crawled. Birds, perhaps, or insects—millions of
dark specks sweeping across the uncovered floor of the ocean.

Pell's voice came faintly from the kitchen.

"Night crabs."

"What do they do?"

"They eat what daylight reveals."

"People?"

"If daylight reveals them."

The village committee dissolved before sunset.

Mara packed bread, rope, Edrin's compass, and the chart. She
poured Pell into a glass jar and fitted the lid
with holes. At the edge of the harbor, she found
a crowd preparing to flee inland.

Old Harker blocked her path. "Wrong direction."

"I know."

"Night crabs are coming."

"I know that too."

He looked at the map under her arm, then at
the jar. Pell stared back.

Harker sighed. "Always suspected fish could talk. Too smug not
to."

He took a lantern from his belt and handed it
to Mara.

"Your Edrin went hunting that city," he said. "Said he'd
heard its bells during a storm."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"You had enough ghosts."

Mara stepped over the harbor chain and began walking across
the seabed.

The wet sand sucked at her boots. She passed anchors
furred with weeds, bottles clouded by time, and the ribs
of ships forgotten before Bellwether had a name. Small pools
reflected the sky. In one, a stranded octopus watched her
with grave golden eyes.

By evening, the village had shrunk to a pale mark
behind her.

"Tell me the moon's name," Mara said.

"If I knew it, it would not be lost."

"What was it before?"

Pell was quiet for so long she thought he had
died.

"The moon has had many names. Lantern of Wolves. White
Sister. The Sleepless Coin. The first name was given by
the first person who looked up and wished not to
be alone."

"What happened to that person?"

"No one remembers."

The black line on the horizon grew wider.

Mara walked faster.

At dusk, she found the Mercy Bell, a three-masted ship
that had sunk eighty years before. It stood upright in
the sand as if still afloat, its broken sails trembling
in the wind. The figurehead was a wooden woman with
open arms.

Something moved on the deck.

Mara raised Harker's lantern.

A man descended the leaning gangplank. He wore a dark
coat stiff with salt. Barnacles covered one shoulder. His hair
was black, though strands of sea grass hung among it,
and when he lifted his face, Mara felt eleven years
collapse like a rotten floor.

"Edrin," she whispered.

He smiled.

She crossed the distance between them and struck him.

The blow staggered him. He touched his cheek.

"I expected something warmer."

"I buried an empty coffin."

"I'm sorry."

"I waited seven years before I buried it."

"I tried to come home."

"You took eleven."

Behind him, shadows gathered aboard the wreck: sailors in ruined
clothes, their skin pale and swollen. Some lacked eyes. Others
had fish swimming inside their open ribs.

Mara stepped back.

Edrin followed her gaze. "They won't hurt you."

"Are you dead?"

"Not precisely."

"That sounds like something a dead man would say."

He laughed, and the sound was so familiar that anger
could no longer hold her upright. She pressed both hands
to her mouth.

Edrin approached slowly. This time, when he embraced her, she
let him.

He was cold. Not winter cold, but deep-water cold, a
chill that seemed older than weather.

"I found Orison," he said against her hair. "A storm
drove us onto its tower. The city keeps what the
sea takes. It would not let us leave while the
water covered it."

"And now?"

"Now the sea is gone."

"Then come home."

His eyes moved toward the darkening horizon.

"I can't go farther inland than the old tide line."

Mara understood. The sea had released him, but not returned
him.

She showed him the chart and told him what Pell
had said.

Edrin frowned at the fish. "A moonfish."

"A what?"

But Pell had sunk to the bottom of the jar.

Edrin tapped the glass. "They are messengers. Or liars. Often
both."

"I am conserving water," Pell muttered.

The night crabs reached the Mercy Bell after midnight.

They came with a sound like hard rain. Each was
no larger than Mara's thumb, but together they covered the
sand in a moving black carpet. The drowned sailors formed
a ring around Mara. They stamped, swept, and crushed, yet
the creatures climbed over one another, stripping weed, flesh, and
old wood from everything they touched.

"Run!" Edrin shouted.

Mara ran.

The dead sailors remained behind. Their cries mixed with the
splintering of the ship. Edrin stayed beside her, though crabs
swarmed over his boots and bit his salt-white hands.

Ahead, towers rose from the darkness.

Orison emerged from the seabed beneath the stars, a city
of green stone and broken domes. Streets twisted between buildings
crusted with coral. Statues of forgotten rulers stared blindly from
alcoves. The highest tower leaned toward the east, and at
its base stood a door that gleamed like the inside
of a shell.

Mara and Edrin reached it as the crabs flooded the
outer streets.

There was no handle.

Mara pressed her palms against the pearl.

"Ask it for what you lost," Pell said from the
jar.

Mara looked at Edrin.

"No," he said.

The crabs poured into the square.

"Mara, don't."

She faced the door. "I lost my husband."

The pearl opened.

Beyond lay a circular chamber filled with shelves. On the
shelves stood thousands of jars.

Some held wedding rings, teeth, keys, feathers, and curls of
hair. Others contained less solid things: the smell of summer
rain, a child's first word, the memory of a mother's
face. Each shimmered in its glass like captured breath.

In the center of the room, an old woman sat
behind a desk.

She wore a crown of fishhooks and a dress sewn
from maps. A white crab slept in her lap.

"You're late," she said.

"For what?"

"Everything."

The door closed, shutting out the crabs.

Edrin entered behind Mara, but the old woman barely glanced
at him.

"We're looking for the moon's name," Mara said.

"So is the moon."

"You have it?"

"I have many things."

"Did you steal it?"

The old woman smiled, showing small pointed teeth. "Names cannot
be stolen. Only surrendered."

She lifted the white crab and placed it on the
desk. Underneath was a tiny sealed bottle. Inside, a thread
of pale light curled and uncurled.

Mara's heart quickened.

"Why would the moon surrender its name?"

"Because it was tired of being called." The woman's expression
softened. "Every lonely creature looked up and asked something of
it. Sailors asked for safe passage. Wolves asked for prey.
Lovers asked for forever. The grieving asked to see their
dead. For ages, the moon answered with light. At last,
it wished for silence."

"Without it, the sea is dying."

"All freedom has a price."

Edrin stepped forward. "Give the name back."

"I cannot. It must be bought."

"With what?"

"A name of equal weight."

Mara thought of mayors, kings, saints, and storms. "What name
weighs as much as the moon's?"

The old woman looked at Pell.

The fish darted to the far side of the jar.

"No," he said.

Mara lifted him to eye level. His silver scales had
begun to glow.

"What are you?"

Pell's small mouth trembled.

"Answer her," said the woman.

"I am the moon's first name," he whispered.

Silence filled the chamber.

Mara looked at the bottle on the desk, then back
at Pell. "You said you didn't know it."

"I don't. Not in the way you mean. A name
cannot speak itself. It needs another voice."

The old woman reached for the jar. "Give him to
me. I will place him in the bottle, open it
at dawn, and the moon will return to its labor."

"What happens to Pell?"

"What happens to every name. He will be used."

Pell floated motionless. For the first time, he looked like
an ordinary fish.

Mara thought of the empty sea, the dying creatures, and
Bellwether's cracked boats. She thought of the dark coast waiting
for a tide that could no longer hear the call
to return.

Then she thought of a pale light burdened by a
million wishes.

"Is there another price?" she asked.

"There is always another price," said the woman. "But rarely
a kinder one."

Mara placed Edrin's map on the desk.

"Take my name."

Edrin seized her wrist. "No."

"Is it equal?" Mara asked.

The old woman studied her.

"No single mortal name equals the moon. But a name
carries every promise attached to it, every memory in which
it lives. Yours is tied to the village, to the
man beside you, to the house on Anchor Street. It
may be enough if freely given."

"What will happen to me?"

"People will forget you. Records will lose you. Your house
will remember footsteps but not whose. Your husband will feel
love and never know its shape."

Edrin's grip tightened. "We'll find another way."

"For eleven years I wanted you back," Mara said. "Every
night I asked the moon, though I pretended I didn't.
Perhaps this is my answer."

"This isn't an answer. It's theft."

"No." She touched his cold cheek. "It's a trade."

He turned his face into her palm.

The old woman uncorked an empty bottle.

"Speak your name," she said.

Mara looked at Pell. "If I do this, will the
moon be free?"

"For a time," he said. "Names change. New names are
born."

"And the sea?"

"It will return."

Mara leaned over the bottle.

"My name is Mara Venn."

Something pulled inside her.

It did not hurt. That was almost worse.

Warmth rose from her chest in golden threads. She saw
herself at six years old, running barefoot over the harbor
stones. She saw her mother braiding her hair. She saw
Edrin on their wedding morning, nervous and laughing. Every time
someone had spoken her name, the sound lifted from her
and wound itself into the bottle.

The old woman sealed it.

At once, Edrin released her hand.

He blinked. "Who are you?"

The stranger before him could not answer.

She remembered her name, but it no longer belonged to
the world. It was a key for a vanished door.

The old woman opened the pale bottle.

Light burst through the chamber.

Pell leaped from the jar.

As he passed through the light, his body lengthened and
changed. Fins became wings of silver fire. Scales scattered into
stars. He flew through the ceiling without breaking it, rising
over Orison in a widening arc.

Far above, the empty sky brightened.

The moon returned.

It was only a thin crescent, fragile as a shaving
of bone, but the seabed answered.

A roar came from the horizon.

The sea was coming home.

The old woman and her chamber disappeared. The nameless woman
found herself standing in Orison's square beside a man she
knew and who did not know her. Water shone in
the distance, a white wall under the moon.

"Run," she told him.

They climbed the highest tower.

The sea struck the city.

It filled streets, shattered windows, and lifted the dead from
the sand. Night crabs vanished beneath foam. The tower shook.
Water climbed the steps, cold and furious, until there was
nowhere higher to go.

Edrin stood at the edge.

"I belong to it," he said, though he did not
know why he was explaining.

"I know."

"Do I know you?"

She wanted to tell him everything. Instead, she said, "You
loved someone once."

His expression changed. "Yes."

"Hold on to that."

The water reached for him.

Edrin smiled at her with unbearable gentleness. "I think I
will."

Then the sea took him.

The nameless woman jumped.

She woke on the beach below Bellwether with morning sunlight
on her face.

Villagers moved among broken boats and tangled nets. They celebrated
the tide's return, though no one knew why a woman
in a torn black dress had washed ashore.

Old Harker offered her a blanket.

"You from a wreck?"

"Perhaps."

"What's your name?"

She looked at the fading moon.

"I don't have one."

Harker considered this less unusual than most would have.

"Everyone needs something to be called."

He brought her to the village. The house on Anchor
Street stood empty, though no one could remember who had
lived there. Inside were women's clothes that fit her, bread
she recognized baking in an oven she knew how to
mend, and a cedar chest containing letters addressed to blank
paper.

She remained.

The villagers called her Luna at first, because she had
arrived with the returning moon. Over time, the name softened
to Lune. She repaired clocks, kept a blue bowl beside
the sink, and walked every evening along the harbor.

Years passed.

Sometimes sailors reported seeing a silver fish swimming through moonlight.
Sometimes, during the lowest tides, bells rang beneath the bay.

And sometimes Lune dreamed of a cold man standing at
the foot of a drowned tower. She never saw his
face clearly, but she always woke with grief beside her
like a faithful animal.

She did not ask the moon to explain.

She understood now that some lights were not answers. They
were only companions—distant, silent, and enough to guide a person
home.

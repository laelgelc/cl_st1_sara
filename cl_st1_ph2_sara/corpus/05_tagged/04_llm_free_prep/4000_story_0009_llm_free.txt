The lighthouse stood three miles inland, surrounded by wheat.

Travelers often stopped on the county road to photograph it:
a white stone tower rising from a golden plain, its
lantern room flashing every night toward an ocean that had
not existed for nearly a century.

Some called it a monument. Others called it a mistake.

To Mara Venn, it was home.

She had lived there since she was eight, when the
Council appointed her father Keeper of the Western Light. Now
she was thirty-two, her father was buried beneath the hill,
and the brass plate beside the door bore her own
name.

MARA VENN, KEEPER
BY ORDER OF THE COUNCIL
LET THE LIGHT REMAIN

Every evening, whether the sky was clear or crowded with
storms, Mara climbed the two hundred and seven iron steps
to the lantern room. She polished the mirrors, checked the
gears, filled the old reservoir, and lit the great lamp.

Its beam swept across the wheat. It passed over abandoned
farmhouses, wind pumps, transmission towers, and the black ribbon of
the county road. On very clear nights, the light could
be seen from the city sixty miles east.

No ship ever answered.

There had been no ships since the sea withdrew.

Mara's father used to tell her that the ocean had
not vanished but merely gone elsewhere.

"Water is a traveler," he would say. "It rises, falls,
freezes, hides under stone. Nothing so restless can truly disappear."

When Mara asked why they kept the lighthouse burning, he
always gave the same reply.

"Because someone may still need it."

As a child, she accepted this. As an adult, she
understood that it was not an explanation but a form
of faith.

The old coast ran in a pale line through the
land. From the top of the tower, Mara could see
its former shape: cliffs softened by dust, coves filled with
thorn trees, and the broad basin where the harbor town
of Bellweather had once stood. The town's roofs had collapsed
long ago, but on winter mornings its streets appeared beneath
the frost like veins under thin skin.

The sea had begun retreating in Mara's great-grandmother's time. At
first, people thought it was a tide. Then they blamed
the moon, the heat, the deep wells, the machines built
in distant countries. Every year the water moved farther west.
Ports extended their roads and rails to follow it. Fishing
towns packed themselves onto wheels. Nations fought over the rivers
that remained.

Then, one summer, the horizon became land.

The last Keeper of the old era had lit the
lamp anyway. His successor had done the same. The Council—newly
formed and eager to preserve symbols of order—declared that twelve
coastal lights would remain active forever.

By Mara's time, only three were still burning.

The Northern Light had fallen in an earthquake. The Southern
Light stood within a military exclusion zone. The Western Light,
Mara's light, shone over wheat.

Once a month, a Council truck brought fuel, machine oil,
canned food, and sealed envelopes of instructions. The driver was
a cheerful young man named Orin, who had never seen
the ocean and regarded the lighthouse with affectionate disbelief.

One afternoon in late summer, he arrived with half the
usual fuel.

"Shortage?" Mara asked.

Orin unloaded two metal drums from the truck.

"Reallocation."

"That is a longer word for shortage."

"It's the Council's word, not mine."

She examined the seals on the drums. "This will last
two weeks."

"Three, if you lower the flame."

"The regulations forbid lowering it."

Orin leaned against the truck and looked up at the
tower. The lantern glass flashed in the sun.

"There are new regulations coming," he said.

Mara waited.

He avoided her eyes. "The Council voted to discontinue the
remaining lights."

For a moment, the wheat seemed to hush around them.

"When?"

"End of the month."

"You're certain?"

"I saw the order."

"And this?" She tapped the nearest fuel drum.

"Your final delivery."

Mara turned toward the lighthouse. Its shadow lay across the
hill, narrow and dark, pointing west.

"My father was promised the light would remain."

"The Council that promised him is gone."

"The plate says by order of the Council."

"They'll probably send someone to remove the plate."

Mara almost laughed. Instead, she helped Orin carry the fuel
inside.

Before leaving, he stood with one hand on the truck
door.

"You could come to the city," he said. "They need
mechanics at the pumping station. Better pay than this. Better
company, too."

"I have company."

Orin glanced around at the empty fields.

"The mice don't count."

"They would disagree."

He smiled, but his expression soon faded.

"You can't stay here forever, Mara."

"Nothing stays forever."

He nodded toward the tower. "That's what I mean."

That evening, Mara climbed the stairs and lit the lamp.

The flame caught slowly, blue at the edges, then rose
into a bright white tongue. Clockwork groaned beneath the floor.
The mirrored lens began to turn.

The beam crossed the dry harbor, paused on the distant
ridge, and swept on.

Mara stood beside the glass until midnight.

For the first time in years, she found herself searching
the darkness for an answer.

None came.

Over the next week, she performed her duties with unusual
care. She scrubbed salt stains from the inner panes, though
there had been no salt wind in decades. She polished
the brass railings until they reflected her face. She repaired
a cracked gear tooth in the rotation mechanism and repainted
the red mark on the pressure gauge.

At night, she read her father's logbooks.

Most entries were practical.

Wind east. Dust heavy. Replaced upper bearing.

Council inspector arrived at noon. Complained of stairs.

Mara feverish. Slept by furnace. Better by morning.

But near the end of his final book, the handwriting
became uneven.

Light observed on western horizon, 2:13 a.m. Duration four seconds.

Again at 2:19.

Not lightning.

Mara remembered that night. She had been twenty-one. Her father
woke her before dawn and made her climb to the
gallery despite the cold.

"Watch," he had whispered.

They stood together as the lamp revolved behind them.

Far away, beyond the dead seabed, something flashed.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Mara had assumed it was a vehicle in the hills,
or a reflection from one of the old solar farms.
Her father believed otherwise.

"There is someone out there," he said.

"Then why haven't they come?"

"Perhaps they can't."

"Why haven't they signaled again?"

"They will."

He waited for that signal until his death.

Mara closed the logbook.

Outside, the lamp made its patient circles.

Two weeks passed. Fuel dwindled. The Council sent no official
message, but the radio carried speeches about necessary reductions and
practical stewardship. One commentator mocked the "nostalgia towers," asking why
citizens should sacrifice fuel so that obsolete buildings could illuminate
dirt.

Mara turned off the radio.

On the twenty-seventh day of the month, a storm arrived
from the west.

It was not a rainstorm. Rain had become rare in
that region. This was a storm of dust and static,
a towering brown wall that erased the afternoon sun.

Mara secured the shutters and covered the engine vents. By
evening, wind struck the lighthouse with the sound of thrown
gravel. The tower trembled. Dust forced itself under doors, through
keyholes, between stones.

At sunset, Mara climbed to the lantern room.

The remaining fuel would keep the lamp burning for perhaps
eight hours.

The Council's order took effect at midnight.

She filled the reservoir.

Wind screamed around the glass. Beyond it, nothing existed but
swirling darkness.

Mara struck a match.

The flame bent almost flat, recovered, and touched the wick.
The great lamp awakened.

Its beam pushed into the storm and vanished a few
yards beyond the tower.

"Someone may still need it," Mara said aloud.

At ten minutes before midnight, the engine coughed.

She adjusted the feed valve. The flame steadied.

At five minutes before midnight, she descended one level and
stood beside the cutoff lever. A red cord had been
tied around it by some previous Keeper. According to procedure,
when a light was decommissioned, the Keeper was to lower
that lever at the designated hour, drain the reservoir, stop
the clockwork, and cover the lens.

Mara watched the minute hand crawl across her father's old
watch.

At midnight, she placed her hand on the lever.

The storm pressed against the tower.

She thought of Orin's offer. A room in the city,
hot water, voices through the walls. Work that served a
measurable purpose. Pumps moved water. Engines powered homes. Lights in
the city helped people see.

This light illuminated dust.

The watch ticked past twelve.

Mara did not pull the lever.

Instead, she returned to the lantern room and opened the
fuel valve as far as it would go.

The flame brightened. The lens gathered it and hurled it
into the night.

"Until it's gone," she said.

At twelve forty-seven, something answered.

A white flash appeared in the storm.

Mara froze.

It came from the west, low on the horizon.

She counted under her breath.

Five seconds later, the flash came again.

Then again.

Three flashes. A pause. Three more.

Her heart beat so violently that she gripped the window
frame.

It could be lightning, she thought.

But lightning did not keep time.

Mara ran downstairs to the radio room. She switched on
the transmitter and called the Council frequency.

"This is Western Light. Unknown signal observed west of Bellweather
basin. Repeating white light. Request confirmation."

Static filled the speaker.

She tried again.

No one answered.

She changed to the emergency frequency.

"This is Western Light calling unidentified party west of Bellweather.
If you can receive, respond."

Nothing.

She climbed back to the lamp. The distant flashes continued,
sometimes disappearing behind the storm, always returning.

Three short.

Three long.

Three short.

Mara knew the old distress code. Every Keeper did.

SOS.

She searched the chart cabinet and found maps drawn when
the sea still touched the cliffs. Beneath them lay newer
surveys of the dry basin. The signals appeared to come
from beyond the ruins of Bellweather, perhaps fifteen miles away.

At one in the morning, the lamp coughed again.

Mara checked the reservoir. Less than an hour remained.

If the stranded travelers were using the lighthouse to navigate,
the beam had to continue.

In the storage room below, six emergency tins sat beneath
a canvas sheet. Each contained a liter of fuel, enough
for lamps and small stoves. Together, they might feed the
main lamp for another forty minutes.

Not enough.

Mara searched the workshop. Lubricating oil would smoke heavily, but
it would burn. So would the old cleaning spirits. There
was kerosene in the kitchen heater, though the pipes would
have to be drained.

She gathered everything.

By two o'clock, the storm had begun to weaken. The
beam reached farther across the basin. Each time it swept
west, the answering signal flashed.

Closer now, Mara thought.

Or perhaps she only wanted it to be.

At two fifteen, the main fuel ran out.

The flame shrank.

Mara poured in the emergency tins. The lamp flared yellow.
Smoke stained the glass, and she wiped it away with
a rag as the lens turned.

At two fifty, she added the heater fuel.

At three twenty, the flame dimmed again.

The signal in the west had disappeared.

Mara stood in the smoky lantern room, listening to the
gears.

Had the travelers lost sight of the lighthouse? Had their
signal failed? Had they changed direction?

She waited for one more flash.

None came.

The cleaning spirits remained, but they were dangerously volatile. A
warning label, faded almost white, showed a black flame above
the words KEEP FROM HEAT.

Mara unscrewed the cap.

"If this works," she told the lamp, "do not become
dramatic."

She poured slowly.

The lamp became dramatic.

Fire rushed up with a hollow thump. Mara threw herself
backward as blue flame spilled from the reservoir. It climbed
the brass housing and licked at the turning lens.

She seized the wool blanket kept for emergencies, wrapped it
around her arms, and smothered the burning fuel. The room
filled with bitter smoke.

For several terrifying seconds, all light vanished.

Then Mara uncovered the lamp. A small flame remained on
the wick.

She opened the feed no wider than a hair.

The flame grew. Weak, unsteady, but alive.

The rotating mechanism had stopped during the fire. Mara tried
the restart lever. Gears clicked uselessly. One of the belts
had burned through.

She had light, but no sweep.

Mara looked west. Without the lens turning, the beam pointed
northeast, toward empty fields.

She could rotate the assembly by hand.

The lens housing weighed nearly half a ton, but it
rested on a mercury bearing designed to move with little
friction. She put both hands on a brass support and
pushed.

At first, nothing happened.

Then the lens shifted.

A blade of light crawled across the floor, climbed the
wall, and moved over the western windows.

Mara pushed until the beam pointed into the basin.

She held it there.

The answering flash came at once.

Not on the horizon now.

Near the ruins.

Mara laughed, half in relief and half in exhaustion.

She began to turn the lens by hand. Slowly, step
by step, she walked around the housing. The beam crossed
the west, swung away, and returned. Each circle took nearly
a minute.

The tower clock struck four.

The storm thinned to a red haze. Shapes emerged below:
the old cliffs, the harbor basin, the silver line of
the abandoned railway. Mara kept walking.

Her shoulders burned. Blisters opened on her palms. The smoke
made her cough until black spots crowded her vision.

Still she pushed.

Shortly before dawn, she saw lights moving in Bellweather.

There were two of them, close together. They traveled along
the old harbor road, stopped, then continued toward the lighthouse.

Mara released the lens.

The world tilted. She sat on the floor, leaning against
the warm brass housing.

Below, an engine sounded.

She stumbled down the stairs, one hand sliding along the
rail. By the time she reached the ground floor, someone
was pounding on the door.

Mara lifted the locking bar.

Wind tore the door from her grip.

A woman stood outside wearing cracked goggles and a scarf
wrapped around her face. Behind her was a sand-crawler: a
low, broad vehicle with six wheels, its metal skin scarred
by the storm. One headlamp was broken. The other blinked
irregularly.

A boy of about ten sat in the passenger seat.
Beside him lay an old man whose face was gray
with pain.

"We saw your light," the woman said.

Mara stepped aside. "Bring him in."

They carried the old man to the kitchen and laid
him on the table. His leg was badly cut below
the knee. Mara cleaned and stitched the wound while the
woman held him still.

The travelers had come from a settlement two hundred miles
west, beyond the salt ridges. Their radio had failed on
the second day. During the storm, the crawler's navigation system
lost its connection to the satellites, and they drifted north
into the broken ground of the old seabed.

"We saw a flash through the dust," the woman explained.
"Thought it was lightning. Then it came again at regular
intervals."

"My grandfather knew what it was," the boy said. "He
said it was a lighthouse."

The old man opened his eyes.

"I said it couldn't be," the woman told Mara. "The
maps mark this tower as inactive."

"It is now," Mara said.

Above them, the lamp had gone out.

In the silence that followed, everyone heard the crawler's engine
sputter and stop.

The woman closed her eyes. "Fuel line again."

"You can repair it after sunrise," Mara said. "You're safe
here."

The boy wandered to the western window. Dawn was spreading
across the basin, revealing the ruins below. Brick walls rose
from drifts of sand. Rusted cranes leaned over docks that
led nowhere.

"Was there really an ocean?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Did you see it?"

"No."

"Then how do you know?"

Mara thought of maps, logbooks, salt stains, and promises inherited
from the dead.

"The lighthouse," she said. "Why else would anyone build it?"

The boy seemed satisfied.

They slept for a few hours. When Mara woke, the
storm had passed. Sunlight poured over the wheat, and the
sky was an impossible blue.

A Council vehicle was climbing the hill.

Orin stepped out, followed by a thin official in a
gray coat. The official carried a metal case and a
folded decommission notice.

"You were required to extinguish the light at midnight," he
told Mara.

She looked past him at Orin. He gave a helpless
shrug.

"I received no direct confirmation," Mara said.

"The order was broadcast throughout the region."

"My radio failed during the storm."

Orin coughed into his hand.

The official looked toward the sand-crawler, where the woman and
boy were repairing the fuel line.

"Who are they?"

"Travelers."

"From where?"

"The western settlements."

The official's expression changed. "There are no recognized settlements west
of the salt ridges."

"Then they are from nowhere."

The old man sat in the lighthouse doorway, his bandaged
leg stretched before him.

"If her light had gone out," he said, "we'd be
dead in nowhere."

The official frowned. He unfolded the notice.

"Keeper Venn, the order is not subject to local review.
The lamp is to be dismantled, the remaining stores inventoried,
and the property transferred to the Agricultural Authority."

Mara wiped soot from her hands.

"There are no remaining stores."

"What?"

"We burned them."

"All of them?"

"Nearly."

"For an unauthorized rescue?"

Mara glanced at the woman, the boy, and the injured
old man.

"Yes."

The official began to speak, then stopped. From the west
came a distant mechanical rumble.

Everyone turned.

A line of vehicles was crossing the basin.

There were dozens of them—trucks, crawlers, carts with patched sails,
and machines so old that their engines announced them long
before they came into view. Sun flashed from mirrors mounted
on their roofs.

The woman shaded her eyes.

"They followed us," she said.

"Who?" Orin asked.

"Our people."

The first vehicle reached the hill by noon. Then came
another, and another. Families climbed out carrying water cans, tools,
blankets, cages of birds, rolled tents, and boxes of seedlings.
Some had been traveling for weeks. The western wells, they
explained, had turned bitter. They were looking for land near
the eastern rivers.

During the storm, separated groups had seen the beam of
the lighthouse. They followed it toward Bellweather, where they found
the woman's crawler tracks leading inland.

By evening, more than three hundred people had camped among
the wheat.

The Council official sent urgent radio messages to the city.
His voice became less certain with each transmission.

The lighthouse kitchen served soup. Orin repaired engines. Mara dressed
wounds and found places for children to sleep on the
stairs. The boy from the crawler climbed to the lantern
room and stared at the blackened lens.

"Can it be fixed?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Will you fix it?"

Mara looked east.

Beyond the fields, lights had appeared on the county road.
Council trucks were coming, followed by medical vans and water
carriers.

The city had seen the travelers.

Or perhaps, Mara thought, the city had seen the light.

That night, the great lamp could not be lit. Instead,
the people gathered lanterns from their vehicles and carried them
to the top of the hill. Hundreds of small flames
surrounded the tower.

From far away, they must have looked like a fallen
constellation.

Weeks later, the Council withdrew the decommission order.

The official statement praised the lighthouse as an essential emergency
beacon for unregistered migration routes. New fuel reserves were approved.
A modern radio was installed, along with a more efficient
lamp and an electric motor for the lens.

The gray-coated official returned to replace the brass plate by
the door.

The new one read:

WESTERN NAVIGATION AND RELIEF STATION
MARA VENN, KEEPER

She kept the old plate in the workshop.

The western settlers were granted farmland around Bellweather. They built
homes beside the ruined harbor and planted gardens in streets
once traveled by ships. The boy visited often, usually with
impossible questions about whales, tides, and storms at sea.

Years passed.

The wheat gave way to orchards. Roads crossed the basin.
A school rose where the customs house had stood. At
night, windows shone across the old seabed.

And every evening, Mara climbed the two hundred and seven
steps.

She checked the gears. She polished the mirrors. She lit
the great lamp.

Its beam swept over the settlement, the fields, and the
long western road. Travelers answered with flashes of their headlights.
Children waiting below counted each revolution.

Sometimes, near midnight, Mara would stand at the glass and
look beyond the salt ridges.

She never saw the ocean.

But she no longer wondered why the lighthouse remained.

A light did not need to know who searched for
it.

It only needed to be there when the darkness came.

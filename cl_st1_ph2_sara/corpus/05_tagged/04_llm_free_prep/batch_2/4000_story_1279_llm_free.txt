# The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, before the fishing boats sounded their
horns and the bakery opened its blue shutters, Mara Vale
climbed the hill to wind the town clock.

The clock stood in a narrow stone tower above Bellweather,
facing the sea. Its four iron hands had crossed the
same white faces for one hundred and eighty-three years. Sailors
trusted it more than their compasses. Bakers kneaded by it.
Children were born beneath its chimes, couples married between its
strokes, and the dead were carried through the churchyard as
it marked the final hour.

Mara had inherited the work from her father, and he
from his mother before him. Each morning, she unlocked the
green door at the tower's base, climbed one hundred and
twelve wooden steps, and entered the mechanism room.

There, among wheels as large as cartwheels and springs thick
as her arm, she fitted a brass key into the
winding shaft and turned it thirty-six times.

On the morning the clock lost an hour, Mara noticed
nothing unusual.

The sky was pale, the sea quiet. Gulls circled the
harbor in lazy spirals. Inside the tower, every cog shone
with oil, every tooth met its neighbor neatly, and the
pendulum swung with a patient, polished certainty.

At noon, however, the clock struck eleven.

The entire town stopped.

Old Mrs. Fen, who had been carrying eggs across the
market square, looked up so sharply that she dropped six.
The schoolmaster dismissed his pupils too early. Captain Orrow's ferry
left without three passengers, and the baker pulled twenty loaves
from the ovens before their crusts had browned.

Mara ran up the hill.

She opened the clock case, checked the escapement, counted the
gear ratios, tested the weights, and listened to the pendulum.
Everything was correct. Yet the hands showed eleven, while her
father's silver pocket watch showed twelve.

She moved the minute hand forward.

"There," she whispered. "Behave."

The clock behaved until sunset.

At eight in the evening, it struck seven.

The next morning, everyone in Bellweather woke an hour older
than the clock claimed. By the second day, the town
had lost another hour. By the end of the week,
the clock was six hours behind.

Mara scarcely slept. She dismantled the smaller gears and polished
their pins. She replaced the cracked leather on the winding
drum. She inspected the tower stones for damp and the
bell ropes for stretching. Nothing explained the missing time.

The council summoned her to the meeting hall.

"This is becoming serious," said Mayor Pell, a small man
with a silver beard and a voice that made every
sentence sound like a complaint. "The mail coach arrived before
it departed. The fishermen sailed at dawn and found themselves
leaving at midnight. Yesterday, the church held morning prayers during
supper."

"People could use their own watches," Mara said.

"They do. But no two watches agree."

That was true. Since the tower clock had begun to
fail, timepieces throughout Bellweather had gone wrong. Some ran backward.
Some stopped whenever their owners lied. The school clock sped
through lessons and slowed during examinations. Mrs. Fen's kitchen timer
rang only after the cakes had burned.

"The tower sets the town's time," said the mayor. "Your
family has always maintained it."

"I know."

"Then repair it."

"I am trying."

"Try before Thursday," he replied. "The governor is visiting. We
cannot welcome her last month."

Mara left the hall with oil on her sleeves and
anger in her throat.

Outside, the square was full of confused citizens. Shopkeepers crossed
out their opening hours. Sailors argued with tide charts. Two
boys had discovered that if they stood beneath the clock
and held their breath, their shadows moved without them.

At the fountain sat Elias Reed, Bellweather's oldest resident.

No one knew exactly how old he was. Elias claimed
to have forgotten, though people said he had once been
a lighthouse keeper, a violinist, a smuggler, or all three.
He wore a brown coat in every season and carried
a cane carved with tiny moons.

As Mara passed, he tapped the stones beside him.

"You're looking in the wrong place," he said.

"I have looked everywhere."

"In the clock, perhaps."

"Where else would I look?"

Elias smiled. "Where the hours go."

Mara was too tired for riddles. "Hours don't go anywhere."

"Everything goes somewhere."

He pointed his cane toward the harbor. Beyond the masts
and chimneys, the sea shone gray beneath the afternoon sky.

"When I kept the western lighthouse," he said, "I sometimes
saw a door in the cliff below the tower. It
appeared only at the lowest tide, on days when the
clock struck the wrong hour."

"The clock has never struck the wrong hour before."

"Not in your lifetime."

Mara studied him. "You've seen this happen?"

"Once."

"When?"

Elias shrugged. "Long ago. Or next year. Time is difficult
to discuss when it becomes unwell."

She started to leave.

"Your grandmother found the door," he called after her. "Perhaps
she neglected to mention it."

Mara stopped.

Her grandmother had been keeper before Mara's father. She had
died when Mara was seven, leaving behind a brass key,
three wool coats, and a reputation for never being late.

"What was behind it?" Mara asked.

Elias looked toward the sea.

"Everything the town wanted to forget."

That evening, the tide fell lower than Mara had ever
seen it.

The harbor floor emerged in black ridges. Anchored boats tilted
in the mud. Crabs scuttled beneath piers, and the sea
withdrew from the western cliffs, exposing stone usually hidden under
twelve feet of water.

Mara crossed the wet sand with a lantern.

At the foot of the cliff, behind curtains of weed,
she found a green door.

It was narrow, salt-stained, and identical to the door at
the base of the clock tower.

Her tower key fit the lock.

Beyond lay a passage descending into darkness. The walls were
dry despite the sea overhead, and a faint ticking came
from far below.

Mara followed it.

The passage ended at a cavern filled with clocks.

Thousands of them crowded the floor and climbed the walls:
grandfather clocks, carriage clocks, sundials, pocket watches, hourglasses, water clocks,
candle clocks, and delicate gold mechanisms no larger than beetles.
Some ticked rapidly. Others gave one beat every several minutes.
Their combined sound resembled rain falling on a distant roof.

In the center of the cavern sat a woman at
a wooden desk.

She looked younger than Mara, though her hair was perfectly
white. She wore a dark dress sewn with small silver
numbers, and she was pouring tea into two cups.

"You took longer than your grandmother," the woman said.

Mara tightened her grip on the lantern. "Who are you?"

"People have given me many names. At present, I prefer
Nia."

"What is this place?"

"The keeping room."

"For what?"

Nia gestured at the clocks. "Lost hours."

Mara approached the desk but did not sit.

On its surface stood a glass jar. Inside it glowed
something like golden smoke. The light twisted and folded, occasionally
forming tiny images: a hand releasing another hand, a child
closing a bedroom door, a ship vanishing into fog.

"Bellweather's hours are coming here," Mara said.

"Some of them."

"Give them back."

Nia sipped her tea. "I cannot give back what was
surrendered willingly."

"No one surrendered anything."

"Didn't they?"

Nia lifted one pale hand, and several clocks fell silent.

The cavern changed.

Mara found herself standing in Mrs. Fen's kitchen. Mrs. Fen
was younger, holding an unopened letter beside the stove. Through
the window, sunlight glittered on summer leaves.

"If only this morning had never happened," Mrs. Fen whispered.

The scene dissolved.

Now Mara stood on the harbor wall as Captain Orrow
watched a storm batter three fishing boats beyond the breakwater.

"Let this day be forgotten," he said through clenched teeth.

Again the vision changed.

The schoolmaster sat alone in his classroom, staring at an
empty desk that had belonged to his son before the
boy left Bellweather forever.

"I would give anything not to remember the last hour
we spoke."

One scene followed another. A failed wedding. A ruined harvest.
A diagnosis delivered in a silent room. A soldier's telegram.
A betrayal. A final breath.

Then Mara stood beside her father's bed.

She was seventeen again, holding his hand as fever burned
through him. Rain struck the windows. On the table lay
his silver watch.

Her younger self bowed her head.

"Please," she whispered. "Take this hour away."

Mara stumbled backward, and the cavern returned.

Nia set down her cup.

"People say such things carelessly," she said. "They wish away
afternoons, curse long nights, beg for painful moments to disappear.
Usually, the clock tower keeps those wishes from reaching me.
Your ancestors built it for that purpose. It anchors Bellweather
to all its hours—the pleasant and the cruel alike."

"Then why has it stopped?"

"It has not stopped. It is letting go."

"Why?"

Nia looked at her almost kindly. "Because its keeper asked
it to."

Mara remembered the night a week earlier.

She had been alone in the mechanism room, repairing a
bent tooth on the striking wheel. Below, the town celebrated
the Festival of Lanterns. Music drifted up the hill. Families
crowded the square, and children carried colored lights along the
streets.

It had been her father's favorite festival.

Mara had sat beside the clock, surrounded by machinery he
had taught her to understand, and felt the old emptiness
open inside her.

"If I could forget one hour," she had said, "I
could be happy."

The clock had struck midnight.

Then, quietly, it had begun to obey.

"I didn't mean this," Mara said.

"Wishes rarely care what we mean."

"Take only my hour, then. Leave the town alone."

"Hours are not coins. They touch one another." Nia pointed
to the glowing jar. "Your final hour with your father
belongs partly to the doctor, who arrived too late. To
the baker who sent food you did not eat. To
Elias, who sat downstairs through the night. To every person
who heard the funeral bell the following morning. Remove one
hour, and the hours around it loosen. Remove enough, and
a life loses its shape."

Mara watched the golden smoke turn.

"If the hours continue coming here, what happens?"

"At first? Confusion. Then relief. People will forget their grief.
They will also forget what grief taught them. The captain
will forget why he checks the weather before sailing. Mrs.
Fen will forget why she writes to her sister every
Sunday. The schoolmaster will forget why he leaves the classroom
door open in case his son returns."

"And later?"

"Bellweather will forget birthdays, promises, names. The town will become
a place where nothing hurts because nothing matters."

The ticking surrounded them, enormous and soft.

"How do I stop it?"

Nia's eyes moved toward the far wall.

There, among hundreds of clocks, stood a familiar silver watch.

Mara picked it up. The case was dented near the
hinge. Inside, beneath the glass, the hands pointed to 3:17—the
minute her father had died.

"I carried this for years," Mara said. "Then I lost
it."

"No," said Nia. "You left it."

Mara remembered placing the watch in a drawer after the
funeral. She had avoided looking at it, then avoided the
drawer, then stopped entering the room altogether. Years later, when
the house was sold, she had searched for the watch
and assumed it missing.

"What must I do?"

"Wind it."

"The spring is broken."

"Yes."

"How can I wind a broken watch?"

"You cannot, unless you repair it."

"I don't have my tools."

Nia smiled. "You are a clock keeper standing in a
room of clocks."

Mara opened the watch.

The inner mechanism was tiny, far more delicate than the
tower machinery. A broken mainspring curled beneath the bridge. One
gear had lost a tooth, and rust stained the balance
wheel.

She worked at Nia's desk.

From an old French clock, she borrowed a sliver of
spring steel. From a sailor's chronometer, she took a screw
so small it rested within the lines of her fingerprint.
She shaped a new gear tooth with a file meant
for ivory and cleaned the rust using oil from a
clock that claimed to measure dreams.

Hours passed—or perhaps they did not. In the cavern, there
was no sunrise, no hunger, no change in the air.

As Mara worked, the watch released memories.

Her father lifting her onto his shoulders so she could
touch the tower bell.

Her father laughing when she dropped a box of screws
down the stairs.

His hands guiding hers around the winding key.

His anger the day she announced she wanted to leave
Bellweather.

His apology the next morning.

The last winter.

The fever.

At times Mara's vision blurred, and she had to stop
until the tears passed. Nia remained silent.

At last, Mara fitted the repaired spring beneath the bridge.

She turned the crown.

The watch ticked once.

Then again.

Its hands remained at 3:17.

"It isn't working," Mara said.

"It is working," Nia replied. "But you have not finished."

"What else is there?"

"You must set the time."

Mara's fingers rested on the crown.

To move the hands beyond 3:17 felt like a betrayal.
For years, that frozen minute had marked the border between
the life she wanted and the life she had been
forced to continue. If the hands moved, it would not
undo his death. It would only admit that the world
had gone on.

She thought of the tower clock above Bellweather. Of boats
waiting for the tide. Bread rising beneath cloth. Children growing
while their parents begged them to remain small. She thought
of how every clock was an agreement—not that nothing would
change, but that change could be measured and endured.

Mara pulled out the crown.

Slowly, she turned the hands forward.

3:18.

3:19.

3:20.

The golden smoke in the jar flared.

All around the cavern, clocks began to chime.

They rang in bright and broken voices: deep bells, silver
notes, wooden knocks, cuckoo calls, electronic beeps. The sound shook
dust from the ceiling and sent ripples through the stone
floor.

The jar cracked.

Golden light streamed out, filling the cavern. Within it, thousands
of lost moments unfolded. Sorrows and celebrations, mistakes and discoveries,
final words and first attempts rushed upward like sparks seeking
the sky.

Mara held the watch against her heart.

The cavern vanished.

She was standing in the tower's mechanism room.

Morning sunlight poured through the eastern window. Below, Bellweather lay
still. The sea had returned to the harbor, lifting the
boats from the mud.

The tower clock showed six.

In her hand, her father's watch showed the same.

The pendulum swung.

Once.

Twice.

Then the gears began to move.

At seven, the clock struck seven.

People emerged from their houses and looked up. The baker
laughed so loudly that Mara heard him from the hill.
Captain Orrow blew the ferry horn. Schoolchildren cheered, though they
had never before celebrated being sent to lessons on time.

By eight, every clock in Bellweather agreed.

The governor arrived on Thursday to find the town perfectly
punctual, although no one could explain why they all felt
as if they had awakened from a deep and melancholy
dream.

Mayor Pell praised Mara in the square.

"I trust," he said, "that this problem will not occur
again."

"It will," Mara replied.

His beard twitched. "What?"

"Clocks fail. Springs break. Gears wear down. People lose hours."

"That is not reassuring."

"No," Mara said. "But they can be repaired."

Elias Reed waited by the fountain after the crowd dispersed.

"You found the door," he said.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped."

"Did my grandmother truly go there?"

"Many times."

"Why?"

Elias looked toward the tower. "Keeping time is not the
same as winding a clock. Your grandmother understood that."

Mara took out the silver watch. Its second hand moved
steadily across the face.

"Did you sit downstairs the night my father died?"

Elias nodded.

"I had forgotten."

"No," he said. "You remembered the worst part so fiercely
that there was no room for the rest."

He walked away, tapping his moon-carved cane against the stones.

Years passed.

Mara continued to climb the one hundred and twelve steps
every morning. She replaced ropes, polished bearings, and taught apprentices
how to hear the difference between a healthy tick and
a troubled one.

But she also began a new tradition.

Once each year, during the Festival of Lanterns, the people
of Bellweather gathered beneath the clock tower. At midnight, the
clock did not strike. Instead, it remained silent for one
full minute.

During that minute, each person remembered an hour they had
once wished away.

They remembered hospital rooms and empty chairs, storms, farewells, failures,
harsh words, and doors that never reopened. They did not
pretend those hours were beautiful. They did not call suffering
a gift.

They simply allowed those hours to belong to them.

Then the great clock struck twelve, and the townspeople raised
their lanterns.

Mara always stood at the top of the tower, one
hand resting on the mechanism and the other holding her
father's watch.

Far below, the lanterns moved through the streets like a
river of stars. Beyond them, the sea carried moonlight toward
the horizon.

And as the clock began the first minute of a
new day, Mara listened to the steady turning of its
gears.

Time, she had learned, was not a thief.

It took nothing it did not also transform.

The Town That Borrowed Tomorrow

On the morning the river began flowing backward, Mara Venn
was repairing a clock that had never told the truth.

It was a delicate silver thing, small enough to fit
in her palm, with twelve blue stones set around its
face. The mayor had brought it to her three days
earlier, wrapped in a handkerchief and smelling faintly of rain.

"It belonged to my grandfather," he had said. "It loses
seven minutes every hour."

"Then it has been lying to your family for generations."

The mayor had not smiled. No one in Bellwether smiled
much anymore.

Mara worked at a narrow bench beneath the front window
of her father's old shop. Outside, the town sloped toward
the river in rows of gray roofs and crooked chimneys.
The sky was white with winter. Beyond the glass, snowflakes
hung in the air without falling.

That should have warned her.

Instead, she bent closer to the silver clock, lifted a
gear with her tweezers, and heard someone shout in the
street.

Then came another voice, and another.

Mara set down her tools.

The snowflakes were rising.

They drifted upward from windowsills, gutters, and the shoulders of
startled townspeople. In the square, a child laughed as a
crushed snowball leaped from the ground, rebuilt itself in midair,
and flew neatly into his mitten.

Mara opened the shop door.

Every bell in Bellwether rang once.

Far below, the river shuddered. Its black water stopped, gathered
itself like a held breath, and began climbing toward the
northern mountains.

People ran downhill to watch. Fishermen stood beside their boats,
arguing. The schoolmaster declared it an optical illusion until a
drowned branch emerged from beneath the bridge, sailed upstream, and
rooted itself again in the mud.

Mara remained in her doorway.

Above the rooftops stood the town clock, mounted in the
eastern tower. It was the largest clock in the province,
a black iron face with golden hands. Mara's father had
maintained it for forty years. After his death, the work
had passed to her.

Its hands were moving backward.

At first slowly, then with gathering confidence.

Tick.

A minute lost.

Tick.

Another.

Mara's heart tightened.

She locked the shop and ran.

The tower stair had two hundred and three steps. She
knew because she had counted them every Thursday since she
was twelve. At the top, she pushed through the mechanism-room
door and entered a forest of gears.

The tower clock had no ordinary machinery. Its brass wheels
were taller than men. Chains vanished into darkness below, while
pendulums swung through shafts cut deep into the stone. The
air smelled of oil, dust, and lightning.

In the center stood the escapement wheel, which should have
turned steadily eastward.

It turned west.

Mara pulled the brake lever. Nothing happened. She leaned her
whole weight against it, boots slipping on the floor.

The gears groaned but did not stop.

Then she noticed the key.

It protruded from the clock's central housing: a long iron
key shaped like a thorn. Mara had never seen it
before. No keyhole had been there the previous week.

Tied to its handle was a strip of red cloth.

Her father had worn a red scarf every winter.

Mara reached for the key.

The moment her fingers touched it, the tower vanished.

She stood in a summer orchard beneath green leaves. Bees
moved among white blossoms. Sunlight warmed her face.

Her father sat under an apple tree, younger than she
remembered him, though his hair was already silver at the
temples. He held a wooden toy clock in his hands.

"Mara," he said.

She forgot how to breathe.

Her father looked up and smiled. It was the tired,
uneven smile she had carried in memory for seven years.

"You shouldn't be here yet," he said.

She took one step toward him. "Are you dead?"

"I was."

The orchard flickered. For a heartbeat she saw the tower
room again, then grass returned beneath her feet.

"What is happening?"

Her father turned the toy clock between his hands. Its
wooden hands rotated backward.

"Bellwether has borrowed tomorrow."

"That makes no sense."

"Time rarely does when people try to own it."

He nodded toward a hill beyond the orchard. At its
summit stood the clock tower, though no town surrounded it.
A red sun hung behind its spire.

"Long before Bellwether was built," he continued, "the founders found
something beneath that hill. Not gold. Not water. A spring
of time."

Mara almost laughed, but her father's expression stopped her.

"They built the tower over it," he said. "They learned
to draw from the spring. A second here, a minute
there. They borrowed time during fires, epidemics, failed harvests. A
farmer could have an extra day to bring in wheat
before a storm. A doctor could have another hour to
save a child."

"And the key?"

"The key opens the spring."

"Who turned it?"

Her father looked toward the distant tower. "Someone who believed
tomorrow would be kinder than today."

The orchard trembled. Apples appeared on branches, reddened, fell, and
leaped back into place.

Mara grabbed her father's sleeve. It felt real.

"How do I stop it?"

"You must return what was borrowed."

"How?"

His face softened.

"You already know."

The orchard vanished.

Mara stood in the mechanism room, gripping the iron key.
Only a few seconds had passed, but outside the light
had changed. Dawn glowed over the western hills.

Time was retreating faster.

She released the key and ran downstairs.

Bellwether had become a town of miracles.

Broken cups rebuilt themselves. Smoke descended into chimneys. Fresh loaves
in the baker's window softened into dough. Old Mrs. Vale
stood in the square touching her face as wrinkles faded
from her cheeks. A lame horse kicked off its harness
and galloped in widening circles.

People stared, laughed, or prayed.

Mara found Mayor Orin near the fountain, where coins sprang
from the water and flew into the pockets of those
who had tossed them there.

"Who entered the clock tower?" she demanded.

"No one. You have the only key."

"Not the tower key. The iron one."

His expression changed.

That was enough.

Mara seized his coat. "What did you do?"

"Let go of me."

"The town is moving backward!"

"I can see that."

"Why?"

Around them, the square clock chimed ten, then nine.

Mayor Orin glanced at the townspeople. Many had begun to
notice him. He lowered his voice.

"My daughter is dying."

Mara's grip loosened.

Everyone knew about Elian Orin. She was nine years old
and had not left the mayor's house since autumn. The
physician from the capital had come twice. On his second
visit, he had departed before sunset, carrying his hat in
his hands.

"She won't survive the night," Orin said. "The doctors can
do nothing. But there are stories about the tower. Your
father knew them. So did mine."

"You turned the key to save one person?"

"I turned it to find a day before she was
ill."

"You're unwinding everything."

"I didn't know."

"Then help me stop it."

"No."

The word was quiet.

A murmur moved through the crowd. Somewhere behind Mara, a
woman cried out in joy. Her husband's white beard had
darkened.

Orin stepped onto the fountain's edge.

"Listen to me!" he called.

Faces turned toward him.

"We have been given a gift. Look at yourselves. Wounds
are closing. Age is lifting. We are being returned what
life took from us."

Mara stared at him. "And what happens when we pass
our own births?"

Silence spread across the square.

Orin's eyes flickered, but only for an instant.

"We will stop before then."

"You don't know how."

"You do," he said. "Your family guarded the tower."

"I know we must return what was borrowed."

"And what does that mean?"

Mara did not answer.

Because she did know.

The knowledge had waited inside her father's final words. Return
what was borrowed. Time could not be created, only moved.
For Bellwether to reclaim its past, it had stolen from
somewhere else.

From tomorrow.

The town was spending its future.

A scream rose near the market.

A young man had vanished. His clothes collapsed empty onto
the cobblestones. His mother threw herself over them, wailing. He
had been seventeen, Mara remembered, and his family had moved
to Bellwether only sixteen years ago.

Panic broke like glass.

People ran for the roads, but the roads no longer
led out. The southern bridge shortened plank by plank. The
old northern path disappeared beneath trees that grew younger, thinner,
and finally sank into the earth.

Bellwether was being erased from the outside inward.

Mayor Orin fled toward his house.

Mara followed.

Inside the mayor's home, portraits vanished from the walls as
she passed them. Wallpaper peeled itself away in long, curling
sheets. On the upper floor, she found Orin beside a
bed.

Elian lay under a yellow quilt. Her face was pale,
but she was awake.

"Papa?" she whispered.

Mayor Orin dropped to his knees.

The child's breathing strengthened. Color returned to her cheeks. For
one radiant moment, hope filled the room.

Then she began to shrink.

Her arms shortened. Her hair retreated toward her scalp. Nine
years became eight, then seven.

Orin gathered her up.

"Stop it," he said.

Mara looked at him. "Give me the tower key."

"I threw it into the river."

"Why?"

"So no one could undo this."

Elian whimpered in his arms, too young now to understand.

The backward river was carrying the key north.

Mara raced outside.

The town unraveled around her. Chimneys lowered brick by brick.
Trees pulled their roots free of the soil and became
saplings. The church bell gave a final cry before melting
into raw bronze.

At the riverbank, the water rushed toward the mountains with
impossible speed.

Mara searched the dark current and saw the tower key
flash beneath the surface.

She plunged in.

The cold struck like a hammer. The current dragged her
north, but it also dragged younger things out of the
world. Her coat became fresh wool, then loose thread. The
scar on her palm vanished. Memories fluttered at the edges
of her mind: her first day in the shop, her
father's funeral, her mother's voice.

She kicked downward.

The key rolled over stones below her.

Her fingers closed around it.

The river immediately tried to tear it away. Mara held
on, pushed against the current, and found no strength left
in her limbs.

Then someone caught her wrist.

For one impossible second, she thought it was her father.

It was Tomas Reed, the ferryman, pulling from the bank.
He had aged backward into a broad-shouldered man of twenty,
though an old man's fear remained in his eyes.

Together they stumbled onto the shore.

"Go," he said.

Mara ran uphill.

By the time she reached the square, half the town
was gone. Houses had become stacks of timber. Streets narrowed
into dirt tracks. People clung to one another as years
peeled away from them.

Mara climbed the tower steps.

One hundred and ninety.

One hundred and forty.

The staircase shortened beneath her.

At the top, the door had become newly cut oak.
She thrust in the key.

Inside, the gears spun so quickly they screamed. The iron
key remained in the central housing, turning by itself.

Mara locked the tower door behind her.

"Return what was borrowed," she said aloud.

She looked at the mechanism, and at last understood its
design. Her father had taught her that every clock had
two powers: the force that drove it and the resistance
that governed it. Without one, a clock stopped. Without the
other, it destroyed itself.

The iron key was the force.

A life had to become the resistance.

Her father had known. Perhaps every keeper had known.

Mara inserted the tower key into a narrow slot beneath
the escapement wheel. The gears slowed slightly. A panel opened,
revealing a small brass lever and an inscription:

WHAT IS GIVEN FREELY MAY BE GIVEN FOREVER.

Footsteps pounded outside the door.

"Mara!" Mayor Orin shouted. "Wait!"

The door shook.

She placed her hand on the lever.

The orchard returned, faint and transparent around the machinery. Her
father stood beneath the apple tree.

"Will it kill me?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Will the town remember?"

"Not everything."

"Will you be there?"

He smiled sadly. "I don't know."

The door burst open.

Mayor Orin rushed in, carrying Elian, now no more than
two years old. He stopped when he saw Mara's hand
on the lever.

"What will happen?" he demanded.

"The borrowed time will return."

"To my daughter?"

"To everyone."

"She'll be sick again."

"Yes."

His face crumpled. "Then don't."

Elian reached toward the spinning gears, fascinated by their golden
light.

Mara thought of all the people below: the mother holding
empty clothes, the newly young ferryman, old Mrs. Vale touching
a smooth face she could not keep. She thought of
tomorrow, robbed before it had arrived.

"We can't live by devouring what comes next," Mara said.

Orin stepped forward. "There must be another price."

"There is."

He understood.

"No," he whispered.

Mara pulled the lever.

The world stopped.

Not slowed. Not paused. Stopped.

The gears froze. Orin stood motionless. Dust hung like stars
in the air.

Only Mara could move.

Light rose around her fingers and traveled up her arm.
It did not hurt. That surprised her. Her skin became
transparent, filled with tiny moving scenes.

She saw herself at six, asleep beneath her father's workbench.

At twelve, repairing her first watch.

At twenty, standing beside her father's grave in the rain.

She saw days she had not yet lived: gray in
her hair, a child laughing in the shop, summer journeys,
quiet meals, griefs, reconciliations, hundreds of ordinary mornings.

These were the days the tower wanted.

Not her past.

Her future.

Mara began to cry.

Her father appeared beside her, no longer in the orchard
but in the tower itself.

"I'm afraid," she said.

"I know."

"I haven't done enough."

"No one ever has."

She looked at the unwritten years flowing from her hand
into the clock. "Will it be wasted?"

"Time given for others is never wasted."

"That sounds like something you put on a shop sign."

"I always wanted a shop sign."

She laughed through her tears.

Then the light became too bright to see.

The tower clock struck once.

The river turned.

Water crashed southward. Snow fell from the sky. Houses rebuilt
themselves. Roads lengthened. Children became adults again. The vanished young
man reappeared in his mother's arms, confused and breathless.

In the mayor's house, Elian returned to her bed beneath
the yellow quilt. Her illness returned with her.

But so did tomorrow.

The tower struck twice.

Then the sound faded.

For three days, Bellwether spoke of nothing else.

No one remembered precisely what had happened inside the tower.
Mayor Orin recalled climbing the stairs with Elian in his
arms, but he could not remember who had stood before
the clock. The ferryman remembered pulling someone from the river,
though the face slipped away whenever he tried to picture
it.

In the narrow clock shop, every tool remained in place.

A silver clock sat unfinished on the bench.

The mayor visited the shop on the fourth morning. He
stood at the door for a long time, troubled by
a grief he could not name. Finally, he entered and
found the clock wrapped in his grandfather's handkerchief.

Beside it lay a note.

It contained only four words:

Seven minutes fast now.

He wound the clock.

It ticked perfectly.

Years passed.

Elian Orin did not die that winter. A physician from
the coast arrived the following morning, delayed by a storm
that had unexpectedly cleared. He tried a new treatment. It
worked slowly, then all at once.

When Elian grew older, she became Bellwether's clock keeper.

She did not know why the tower felt familiar. She
did not know why she always wore a red scarf
when she climbed its two hundred and three steps, or
why she sometimes dreamed of a woman standing among the
gears with light pouring from her hands.

Under Elian's care, the tower never lost a second.

Yet once each year, on the morning the river had
reversed, every clock in town stopped for seven minutes.

During those minutes, no one worked.

The baker left his bread uncut. The schoolmaster closed his
books. The ferryman tied his boat to the shore. People
stood in doorways, held the hands of those they loved,
and listened to the silence.

They could not say whom they were honoring.

They only knew that the minutes did not feel empty.

They felt given.

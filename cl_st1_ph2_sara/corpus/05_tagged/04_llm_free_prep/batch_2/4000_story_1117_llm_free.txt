# The Lighthouse of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, Mara Vale climbed the ninety-three iron
steps to the lantern room and turned off the light.

It was an old ritual, older than she was, older
than the village of Bellweather, perhaps even older than the
black cliffs on which the lighthouse stood. At dusk, she
woke the great lens. At dawn, she let it sleep.
Between those hours, the beam swept across the sea in
slow, patient circles, warning ships away from the reef known
as the Widow's Teeth.

Mara had kept the lighthouse alone for seven years.

The villagers said she preferred it that way.

They were wrong.

She preferred silence to pity, and solitude was simply the
price of silence.

Seven years earlier, her younger brother Tom had taken their
father's fishing boat out before a storm. He had been
nineteen, reckless and cheerful, with a red scarf around his
neck and a habit of tapping his fingers against every
table as if hearing music no one else could hear.

The boat had never returned.

Afterward, people lowered their voices when Mara entered the bakery.
They touched her arm in the street. They said things
such as, "At least he knew you loved him," and,
"The sea gives, and the sea takes."

Mara wanted to ask what, exactly, the sea had ever
given.

Instead, she accepted the position of lighthouse keeper after old
Mr. Foss retired, and moved into the whitewashed tower above
the cliffs.

For seven years, nothing unusual happened.

Then, one November night, the lighthouse began shining inland.

Mara noticed it at thirteen minutes past midnight.

She was in the kitchen below, mending a tear in
her coat, when the familiar rhythm of brightness across the
window changed. Usually the beam flashed against the glass for
only a moment before continuing toward the sea. That night,
the light remained.

A pale bar stretched across the kitchen floor.

Mara set down her needle.

The machinery above groaned. The lens had stopped rotating.

She climbed the stairs quickly, carrying a wrench and an
oil lamp. The wind pressed against the tower, making the
stones murmur. When she reached the lantern room, she found
the mechanism working perfectly. The brass gears turned. The drive
shaft trembled. The chains moved over their wheels.

Yet the lens did not turn.

Its beam pointed east, away from the ocean, across the
moor.

Mara approached the glass.

On the dark slope beyond the village stood the remains
of Bellweather railway station. No train had stopped there in
thirty years. The tracks were rusted, the roof had collapsed,
and weeds grew through the platform.

But now the abandoned station shone under the lighthouse beam.

And someone was waiting there.

Mara could see only a small figure in a dark
coat. It stood perfectly still beside the broken ticket office,
facing the cliffs.

Facing her.

The lantern went out.

Darkness swallowed the room.

Mara froze. A second later, the light returned, and the
lens began rotating as if nothing had happened.

When its beam crossed the station again, the platform was
empty.

At breakfast, Mara told herself she had imagined the figure.
A trick of rain, perhaps, or an animal standing upright
against a post.

By noon, she had almost convinced herself.

Then a letter arrived.

It lay on the lighthouse steps in a blue envelope,
though the postman never came on Tuesdays. There was no
stamp and no address. Only Mara's name, written in a
hand she knew at once.

She carried the letter inside and locked the door.

For a long time, she left it unopened on the
table.

The handwriting was Tom's. The letters leaned too far to
the right. The final stroke of the M curled underneath
his words like a fishhook. When they were children, Mara
had teased him that even his writing looked as if
it were running toward trouble.

At last, she broke the seal.

Inside was a single sheet of paper.

*Dear Mara,*

*I don't know when this will find you. The stationmaster
says letters are difficult here because the trains never travel
at the proper speed. Sometimes the mail arrives before it
is written. Sometimes it arrives too late.*

*I have been waiting at Bellweather Station for seven years.*

*Please come before the last train.*

*Tom*

Mara read the letter three times.

Then she burned it.

She held one corner over the stove flame and watched
the fire travel across the page. It consumed Tom's name
last.

For the rest of the day, she worked without pause.
She cleaned the kitchen, polished the brass railings, inventoried the
oil, and replaced a cracked pane in the storage room.
At dusk, she lit the lantern.

She did not look toward the station.

At midnight, the lens stopped again.

This time, Mara refused to climb the stairs.

The beam remained fixed across the moor. It spilled through
every eastern window of the lighthouse, illuminating dust, furniture, and
stone. Mara sat at the kitchen table with her hands
clasped tightly before her.

Then came a knock at the door.

Three quick taps. Two slow ones.

Tom's knock.

He had invented it when they were children. Three quick,
two slow meant: *It's me. Don't tell Father.*

Mara did not move.

The knock came again.

Three quick. Two slow.

"Mara?" called a voice from outside.

She stood so abruptly that her chair fell backward.

The voice was not Tom's. It was thinner and higher,
the voice of a child.

"Mara Vale? I have a message."

She took the iron poker from beside the stove and
approached the door.

"Who are you?"

"Eli."

"Eli who?"

There was a pause.

"I don't remember."

Mara opened the door.

A boy of perhaps ten stood on the threshold. He
wore a railway uniform several sizes too large and a
cap pulled low over his ears. Rain darkened his pale
hair. In one hand he held an old-fashioned signal lantern,
though no flame burned inside it.

"Are you dead?" Mara asked.

The boy considered this.

"I think so."

She nearly closed the door.

"Wait," he said. "Tom said you might do that."

The boy reached into his coat and removed a red
scarf.

Mara knew every frayed thread.

For seven years, she had dreamed of that scarf floating
on dark water.

She seized it from him. It was dry and warm,
as though Tom had removed it only moments ago.

"Where did you get this?"

"At the station."

"Tom is there?"

Eli nodded.

"Alive?"

"I don't know. People aren't exactly alive at the station.
But they aren't gone, either."

Mara looked across the moor. The lighthouse beam still pointed
toward the old platform, making a silver path through the
rain.

"What does he want?"

"To leave."

"Then why hasn't he?"

"He's waiting for you."

Anger rose in Mara so quickly that she tasted metal.

"He has no right."

The boy lowered his eyes.

"No," he said. "Probably not."

The simple agreement disarmed her.

She wrapped the red scarf around her hand.

"What is the last train?"

"It comes when the lighthouse points inland for the third
time. Anyone who boards must travel on. Anyone left behind
must stay until the light finds the station again."

"How long will that be?"

Eli glanced toward the tower.

"Lighthouses are slow clocks. It might be another seven years.
It might be seventy."

"And tonight is the second time?"

"Yes."

Mara should have sent him away. She should have returned
to her duties and waited for dawn.

Instead, she put on her coat.

The path to Bellweather Station had almost vanished beneath heather
and thorn. Mara followed Eli across the moor while the
lighthouse beam shone above them. Rain whispered through the grass.
Behind her, the tower stood on the cliff like a
tall white bone.

Eli walked quickly, holding his unlit lantern before him.

"How did you die?" Mara asked.

"Waiting for a train."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

The station grew larger ahead of them. Under ordinary daylight,
it was a ruin. Tonight, the lighthouse beam rebuilt it.

Where the light touched, broken bricks became whole. Rotten beams
straightened. Glass returned to the windows in glittering sheets. A
green-painted sign appeared above the platform:

**BELLWEATHER — CHANGE HERE FOR ALL LOST DESTINATIONS**

Beyond the edge of the beam, the building remained collapsed
and black.

Mara stopped.

People crowded the platform.

Some wore modern clothes. Others were dressed in fashions from
old photographs: long coats, high collars, broad hats, military uniforms.
A woman held a baby wrapped in lace. A sailor
smoked a pipe that gave off no smoke. Two girls
sat on a trunk, playing cards with blank faces.

No one spoke above a whisper.

At the far end of the platform stood Tom.

He was nineteen.

Mara's breath caught so painfully that she pressed a hand
against her ribs.

He looked exactly as he had on the morning he
disappeared: untidy brown hair, narrow shoulders, patched boots. Only the
red scarf was missing from his neck.

For a moment, neither of them moved.

Then Tom smiled.

It was the same crooked smile that had once persuaded
Mara to help him steal apples from the vicar's garden.

She crossed the platform and struck him across the face.

The sound echoed beneath the roof.

Tom staggered. A red mark appeared on his cheek.

"So," Mara said, trembling, "you can feel that."

"I deserved it."

She hit him again.

"You let me think you drowned."

"I did drown."

"You wrote me a letter."

"Recently."

"Seven years is not recently!"

"It is here."

She wanted to strike him a third time, but her
anger broke apart. She grabbed his coat instead, clutching the
rough fabric in both fists.

Tom put his arms around her.

Mara had imagined this reunion in countless ways. In some
dreams she screamed at him. In others she forgave him
instantly. Sometimes he was cold and waterlogged; sometimes he crumbled
into sand when she touched him.

She had never imagined that he would smell of rain,
smoke, and the soap their father used to buy.

She buried her face against his shoulder.

"You idiot," she whispered.

"I know."

"You selfish, impossible idiot."

"I know."

"I waited for you."

"I know."

She pulled back.

"How?"

Tom looked toward the tracks. They gleamed inside the lighthouse
beam but disappeared into darkness in both directions.

"When the boat went down, I remember the water. Then
I woke up here. A stationmaster told me I had
missed my train because I was holding on to something."

"Holding on to what?"

"You."

Mara's grip tightened.

Tom continued quietly. "He said the dead can carry love
with them, but not guilt. Guilt is too heavy for
the train. I thought you would hate me for taking
the boat. I thought I had ruined your life."

"You did ruin my life."

"I know."

The words came without defense, and that made them harder
to say.

Mara released him.

"No," she said after a moment. "That isn't true. I
ruined what was left of it."

A bell rang inside the station.

The waiting passengers turned their heads toward the western tracks.

Eli appeared beside Mara.

"The last train is coming," he said.

In the distance, something whistled.

The sound was low and mournful, like wind passing over
the mouth of an empty bottle.

Tom reached for Mara's hand.

"You have to go back."

"You asked me to come."

"I needed to see you."

"For what? Forgiveness?"

"Yes."

"At least you're honest."

The rails began to hum.

"And I needed to tell you something," Tom said. "The
storm wasn't why I took the boat."

Mara stared at him.

"I was leaving Bellweather."

"What?"

"I wanted to reach Ashport and sell the boat. I
had a place on a merchant ship. I thought Father
would stop me, and I knew you'd try to make
me stay."

"You stole our father's boat?"

"I meant to send the money back."

Despite everything, Mara almost laughed. "That was your plan?"

"It was a very bad plan."

"It was the stupidest plan I have ever heard."

"Yes."

"You could have told me."

"I was afraid."

"Of Father?"

"Of you."

The train whistle sounded again, closer now.

Tom looked nineteen, but in his eyes Mara saw the
years he had spent waiting.

"You always knew what our lives were supposed to be,"
he said. "After Mother died, you took care of everything.
You took care of Father. You took care of me.
I loved you for it, but sometimes loving you felt
like living in a room where all the doors were
locked for my own protection."

Mara stepped back as if he had struck her.

"I was trying to keep us together."

"I know."

"Someone had to."

"I know."

"You were a child."

"I was nineteen."

"You acted like a child."

"Yes."

The rails shivered. A pale glow appeared in the western
darkness.

Tom's expression softened.

"I'm sorry, Mara."

She looked away.

All these years, she had thought grief was a single
wound: Tom's death. Now she saw the smaller wounds hidden
inside it. His fear. Her control. The words they had
not spoken. The life he had wanted and the life
she had tried to preserve.

The approaching train emerged from the rain.

It was black, with silver wheels and windows glowing amber.
No engine pulled it. It glided soundlessly except for the
steady rhythm of wheels over rails.

The station clock began moving backward.

Eli raised his lantern. A blue flame appeared inside.

"Train now arriving," he called, his child's voice suddenly carrying
the authority of bells, "for Elsewhere, Afterwards, and All Points
Beyond."

Doors opened along the carriages.

The waiting passengers gathered their luggage. The woman with the
baby stepped aboard. The sailor followed. One by one, the
dead entered the train.

Tom did not move.

Mara understood.

"You're still holding on," she said.

He nodded.

"To my forgiveness?"

"To the hope of it."

She wanted to tell him that forgiveness could not be
demanded at a railway platform. It could not be summoned
by a letter or delivered by a ghostly messenger.

But the train was waiting, and some truths arrived before
one was ready.

"I forgive you," she said.

Tom closed his eyes.

Mara felt the words leave her, but the weight inside
her chest did not disappear. It changed. The hard stone
of anger became something softer and sadder.

Tom opened his eyes again.

"Thank you."

"But I'm still angry."

He smiled. "I would be offended if you weren't."

A conductor leaned out of the nearest carriage. Beneath his
cap, there was only starlight.

"Board now," he said. "The beam is turning."

Mara looked toward the lighthouse.

The silver path across the moor had begun to shift.
Darkness crept over the station wall. Wherever the light withdrew,
the restored building collapsed again into ruin.

Tom stepped toward the train, then paused.

"You should leave Bellweather," he said.

"This is my home."

"It's your hiding place."

Mara almost argued. Instead, she glanced at the crowded carriage.

"Where are you going?"

"I don't know."

"Are you afraid?"

"Terrified."

She took the red scarf from around her hand and
looped it over his shoulders.

"Good," she said. "You always did your best work terrified."

Tom laughed.

It was the sound Mara had missed most, though she
had not known it until then.

He climbed aboard.

At the last moment, Mara caught his sleeve.

"Did it hurt?"

"The drowning?"

She nodded.

"Only for a little while."

"And after?"

"Mostly I waited."

She let him go.

The doors closed.

As the train began to move, Tom lowered the window
and leaned out.

"Mara!"

She walked beside the carriage as it gathered speed.

"What?"

"You were a wonderful sister!"

The darkness was swallowing the platform. Rain fell through the
vanishing roof.

Tom's face moved beyond the lighthouse beam.

For one instant, he was only a pale shape behind
glass.

Then the train disappeared.

The station collapsed around Mara.

She found herself standing among wet weeds and broken bricks.
Rusted tracks stretched into darkness. There was no clock, no
platform roof, no sign.

Only Eli remained.

His blue lantern flickered.

"You missed the train," Mara said.

The boy looked down the empty tracks.

"I always do."

"Why?"

"I'm the messenger."

"That doesn't seem fair."

"No."

Mara considered him. "Will another train come?"

"When the light returns."

"In seven years?"

"Or seventy."

She turned toward the lighthouse. Its beam had resumed its
circle over the sea.

"What happens to you until then?"

"I wait."

Mara held out her hand.

"Not at the station."

Eli looked at her suspiciously. "Where else would I wait?"

"At the lighthouse."

"Is there anything to do?"

"More than you'd think."

"Do you have cake?"

"Sometimes."

He slipped his small hand into hers.

They crossed the moor together.

By dawn, Eli had become faint around the edges. In
direct sunlight, Mara could see the kitchen wall through him.
He ate three slices of bread with blackberry jam, though
the food vanished before reaching his stomach.

At six, Mara climbed the ninety-three steps and extinguished the
lantern.

Eli followed.

From the gallery, they watched the sun rise over Bellweather.
Smoke curled from chimneys. Fishing boats moved across the harbor.
The abandoned station was nothing more than a dark scar
on the eastern hill.

"What now?" Eli asked.

Mara thought of Tom's last words.

For seven years, she had kept the lighthouse because ships
needed warning. But she had also kept it because the
tower asked no questions. Its circular stairs led only to
the same rooms. Its beam traveled endlessly and never went
anywhere.

"Now," she said, "we find out who you were."

Bellweather's church records listed an Elias Reed, aged ten, who
had disappeared in the winter of 1891. Mara found his
name after six weeks of searching. An old newspaper reported
that the boy had last been seen selling matches at
the railway station. His body had never been discovered.

With help from the village schoolmaster, Mara traced the Reed
family to a city in the north. Eli had a
grandniece there, an elderly woman named Florence, who wrote back
saying her grandmother had spoken often of a lost little
brother.

The letter enclosed a photograph.

In it, Eli stood beside a chair, wearing a stiff
collar and an impatient expression. Behind him was a painted
garden.

When Mara showed him the picture, he stared for a
long time.

"My mother combed my hair with rosewater," he said at
last.

Memory returned slowly after that.

He remembered his sister teaching him to whistle. He remembered
stealing hot chestnuts from a market stall. He remembered falling
asleep in an empty carriage while waiting for the snow
to stop. The carriage had been moved onto a side
track and forgotten. He had frozen before morning.

When the lighthouse shone inland the following November, Bellweather Station
rebuilt itself for the third time.

A black train arrived beneath the backward-moving clock.

This time, Eli boarded.

"You'll be all right?" he asked Mara from the doorway.

"No," she said. "But I think I will be eventually."

He nodded, satisfied.

After the train vanished, Mara returned to the lighthouse and
packed her belongings.

The village council appointed a new keeper, a young woman
from Ashport who asked sensible questions about oil, weather, and
lens maintenance. Mara answered every one.

Then she took the morning coach out of Bellweather.

She traveled for years.

She crossed deserts where the stars looked near enough to
gather in both hands. She worked aboard a cargo ship
and learned to read the moods of unfamiliar seas. She
slept in cities whose languages she did not understand. She
grew older. She made friends. She fell in love twice,
unwisely the first time and well the second.

Wherever she went, she visited railway stations.

Sometimes she stood on crowded platforms and listened for a
low, mournful whistle. Sometimes she saw a boy in an
oversized uniform or a young man wearing a red scarf.

But she never saw Tom again.

She did not need to.

Many years later, Mara returned to Bellweather.

She was an old woman then, silver-haired and straight-backed, carrying
a small suitcase covered in labels from distant places. The
village had grown. Electric lamps lined the harbor. The old
railway station had been cleared away, and yellow flowers grew
where the tracks had once run.

The lighthouse still stood above the cliffs.

At dusk, its beam awakened and swept across the sea.

Mara climbed the hill slowly. She rested twice before reaching
the tower, where the keeper welcomed her inside and made
tea.

At midnight, the great lens stopped.

Its beam turned inland.

Mara smiled.

Across the moor, where no station had stood for decades,
warm windows appeared in the darkness. A platform formed beneath
them. Figures gathered under a green-painted sign.

Then she heard it.

Three quick knocks.

Two slow ones.

Mara opened the lighthouse door.

Tom stood outside, no longer nineteen. He looked as he
might have looked in middle age, with lines around his
eyes and silver in his brown hair. The red scarf
was still looped around his neck.

"You took your time," he said.

"I had places to go."

"So I heard."

Beyond him, a black train waited under the stars.

Mara looked back once at the spiral staircase, the kitchen,
and the turning gears above. She thought of all the
hours she had once believed were lost.

They had not been lost.

They had only been waiting for her to live them.

She took her brother's hand.

Together, they walked toward the station before the light could
turn away.

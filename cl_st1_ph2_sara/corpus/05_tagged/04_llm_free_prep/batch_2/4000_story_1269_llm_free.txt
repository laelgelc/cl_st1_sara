By the time Mara Venn reached the lighthouse, the sea
had swallowed the road behind her.

She stood beneath the rusted gallery, rain running from her
hood, and watched the last white stones disappear under black
water. The tide rushed across the causeway with the speed
of a closing door. In less than a minute, the
island was cut off from the mainland.

Mara checked her watch. Half past four.

"You're late," said a voice above her.

She looked up. An old man leaned over the gallery
rail, his gray beard flattening in the wind.

"The storm delayed the bus," Mara called.

"The storm delayed nothing. Storms arrive exactly when they mean
to."

He vanished from the rail.

A moment later, the lighthouse door opened. The old man
wore a blue sweater, patched trousers, and slippers shaped like
yellow ducks.

Mara stared at the slippers.

"My boots are wet," he said.

"I didn't ask."

"You were about to."

He stepped aside. "Come in, then, before the weather carries
you to Norway."

Mara entered.

The lighthouse smelled of kerosene, salt, and burnt toast. The
round entrance room was crowded with shelves holding tools, coiled
rope, cloudy glass bottles, books swollen by damp, and an
impossible number of clocks. There were clocks on the walls,
clocks on tables, clocks hanging from hooks. Some ticked. Some
had stopped. One moved backward.

The old man shut the door against the wind.

"You're Mara Venn."

"Yes."

"Jonas Pike."

"I know."

"People often know my name. Usually because they want something."

"I'm here to repair the lamp."

"No, you're here to replace me."

Mara set down her case. "The Coastal Authority is automating
the station. I'm an engineer, not an executioner."

"An engineer is an executioner who brings instructions."

"I was told you'd be difficult."

"And I was told you'd be older."

"That disappoints you?"

"Most things do."

He lifted her case before she could stop him and
carried it toward the iron staircase.

"The main lens is at the top," he said. "You
can look, but don't touch anything tonight."

"I need to inspect the mechanism."

"Tomorrow."

"My schedule—"

"Your schedule is underwater."

Thunder rolled over the island.

Jonas pointed to the narrow window beside the door. Beyond
it, the sea heaved over the causeway. Even if Mara
had wanted to leave, there was nowhere to go.

"Tea?" he asked.

"No."

"Good. We're out."

He climbed the stairs.

Mara followed.

The tower contained seven floors, each smaller than the one
below. There was a kitchen, a storeroom, a bedroom, a
radio room, and two levels filled with machinery. On the
highest floor, the lantern chamber shone like a glass crown.

The great Fresnel lens stood at its center, taller than
Mara and bright with concentric rings. Brass fittings gleamed in
the rotating assembly. The clockwork motor beneath it clicked with
patient precision.

Mara took out a notebook.

"The gears look original," she said.

"Some are."

"The drive weight?"

"Runs through the center shaft."

"And the lamp?"

"Electric now, with an oil burner in reserve."

"Rotation period?"

"Twenty seconds."

She walked around the lens. Rain lashed the windows, blurring
sea and sky into a single darkness.

"The new system will be more reliable," she said.

Jonas grunted.

"It will monitor visibility, adjust intensity, signal the mainland if
anything fails—"

"Can it see?"

"It has optical sensors."

"That isn't what I asked."

Mara closed her notebook. "The light doesn't need to see.
Ships see the light."

Jonas turned toward her. His eyes were pale and clear.

"That's what they think on the mainland."

Before Mara could reply, a bell rang below.

It was not loud. It sounded once, a thin metallic
note barely audible above the storm.

Jonas went still.

"What is it?" Mara asked.

He hurried down the stairs, moving with surprising speed.

Mara followed him to the radio room. On the desk
stood an ancient wireless receiver beside modern equipment. Jonas put
on headphones and turned a dial. Static crackled from the
speaker.

"Did someone call?" Mara asked.

He raised a hand for silence.

The static deepened. For a moment, Mara thought she heard
a voice under it—not words exactly, but a breath shaping
itself around impossible syllables.

Jonas removed the headphones.

"Light the reserve burner," he said.

"The electric lamp is working."

"Light it."

"Why?"

"Because someone's lost."

Mara looked at the radar display. It showed no vessels
within twenty nautical miles.

"There's no one out there."

Jonas was already opening a cabinet. He removed a box
of matches and a brass key.

"Someone is always out there."

They returned to the lantern chamber. Jonas unlocked a panel
beneath the electric lamp and reached for the fuel valve.

Mara caught his wrist.

"You can't ignite the oil burner while the electric system
is operating. The heat may damage the mount."

"I've done it before."

"That doesn't make it safe."

"No," he said. "It makes it necessary."

The bell rang again.

This time, Mara felt the sound in the soles of
her feet.

Jonas pulled free and opened the valve. Kerosene fumes spread
through the chamber.

"If you light that," Mara said, "I'll report you."

"To whom?"

"The Authority."

"Tell them the ducks helped."

He struck a match.

The oil caught with a soft roar.

Golden fire filled the heart of the lens. For an
instant, the glass rings seemed to gather every scrap of
light in the tower, compressing it into a blazing blade.
The rotating mechanism carried the beam across the sea.

Once.

Twice.

On the third sweep, something answered.

Far beyond the windows, where no ship appeared on the
radar, a pale light flickered in the storm.

Mara pressed her face near the glass.

"What is that?"

Jonas did not answer.

The distant light emerged again, rising and falling between the
waves. It was weak, bluish, and strangely low. No mast
light. No emergency flare. It shone for three seconds, vanished,
then returned.

Mara counted the intervals.

"Sixteen seconds," she said.

"Seventeen."

"It's signaling."

"It's searching."

"For what?"

"For us."

The bell sounded a third time.

The clocks below began to strike.

All of them.

Their chimes surged up the stairwell in a clashing metallic
tide. Midnight bells, though it was barely five in the
afternoon. Tiny alarms rattled. Wooden cuckoos called. The backward clock
spun so quickly that its hands blurred.

Then, as suddenly as they had begun, the clocks stopped.

The lighthouse became silent.

Even the storm seemed to hold its breath.

On the sea, the blue light moved closer.

Mara stepped back from the window.

"What have you done?"

Jonas watched the darkness. "My job."

A shape rose behind the light.

At first Mara took it for a wave, but waves
did not stand against the wind. The shape lengthened, revealing
three narrow masts and a hull blacker than the sea.
Torn sails hung without movement. Water poured from the railings.

An old ship passed through the storm toward the island.

There was no engine sound, no creak of timber, no
voices on deck. It cut across the waves without disturbing
them.

Mara stared. Her mind offered explanations and rejected them: hallucination,
reflection, an unregistered historical vessel, exhaustion, some elaborate trick.

The ship turned as the lighthouse beam touched it.

For one sweep of the lens, Mara saw figures standing
along the rails.

Dozens of them.

Some wore yellow oilskins. Some wore old naval uniforms. Some
appeared dressed in clothes from paintings: wide collars, dark cloaks,
caps pulled low over long hair. Their faces were gray
and shining with water.

One of them lifted a hand.

Mara stumbled into the lens assembly.

The electric lamp went out.

Darkness fell across the chamber, broken only by the oil
flame.

"What is that?" she whispered.

Jonas adjusted the burner. "The Far Lantern."

"That's a ship?"

"Once, perhaps."

"What does it want?"

"A way home."

The ghostly vessel continued its silent approach.

"There's no harbor here," Mara said.

"Not the sort of home you mean."

Jonas checked the mechanism. The electric lamp's failure indicator glowed
red.

"Why did the electric lamp go out?" Mara asked.

"It always does when they're near."

"You've seen this before?"

"Four times."

"And you never reported it?"

"I reported it the first time. They sent a doctor."

Mara looked again. The ship had vanished behind the rain,
but its blue lantern still blinked.

"Why does the oil lamp work?"

"Fire remembers."

"That makes no sense."

"Neither do storms, if you ask a stone."

Jonas moved toward the stairs.

"Keep the burner steady," he said.

"Where are you going?"

"To wind the mechanism."

"The motor is electric."

"Not tonight."

He disappeared below.

Mara stood alone with the flame.

The lens rotated more slowly now. Each time its beam
crossed the sea, the Far Lantern appeared closer. Figures crowded
its rails. Their faces turned toward the lighthouse in desperate
attention.

Mara forced herself to inspect the burner. Fuel pressure was
dropping. She opened the valve slightly.

The flame brightened.

The figures on the ship recoiled.

Not hope, Mara realized.

Fear.

She leaned toward the glass.

The vessel was not approaching the lighthouse voluntarily. It was
being pulled by the beam.

"Jonas!" she shouted.

The lens ground onward.

She hurried downstairs and found him in the machinery room,
fitting a long crank into the clockwork drive. He turned
it with both hands, winding the heavy cable around a
drum.

"What aren't you telling me?" Mara demanded.

"A great many things."

"The ship is afraid of the light."

"Of course."

"You said it was searching for us."

"It is."

"You said it wanted a way home."

"It does."

"Then why are they afraid?"

Jonas stopped turning the crank.

"Because the road home is not gentle."

The tower shuddered. Dust fell from the ceiling.

Mara heard something strike the outside wall.

Once.

Again.

Not waves. Hands.

She backed toward the stairs.

Jonas resumed winding. "Do not open any door, no matter
who asks."

"What happens when the ship reaches us?"

"It passes."

"Through what?"

"The light."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

The pounding climbed the lighthouse wall.

Mara ran up one level and looked through a narrow
window. Faces drifted outside the glass. Drowned men and women
rose through the air as though climbing invisible stairs. Their
hands pressed against the tower.

One face turned toward her.

Mara forgot to breathe.

The woman outside had dark hair cut to the jaw,
a narrow nose, and a scar above her left eyebrow.

Mara touched the same scar on her own face.

The dead woman smiled.

"Mum," Mara whispered.

The woman's lips moved beyond the glass.

Mara stepped closer.

Her mother had drowned when Mara was eleven. A ferry
accident in calm weather, thirty passengers lost less than two
miles from shore. No distress call. No wreckage. The inquiry
had found nothing but a lifeboat floating upside down.

Mara had spent twenty years despising the sea for keeping
the bodies.

Now the sea had returned one.

Her mother raised a hand and pointed down.

Toward the entrance.

"Mara!" Jonas shouted from below. "Whatever you see, it isn't
yours!"

Her mother's smile faded. She looked frightened. Her fist struck
the window.

"Help me," came a muffled voice.

Mara pressed both palms to the glass.

Her mother had said those words once before.

Not on the day she died. Earlier, in their kitchen,
while trying to lift a sack of flour from the
floor. Help me, little bird. Mara had hugged the sack
instead, and together they dragged it across the tiles, laughing.

No ghost could know that.

"Help me," the woman repeated.

Mara turned toward the stairs.

The lighthouse groaned. The pounding covered every wall now. Voices
whispered through cracks and keyholes.

Help me.

Cold.

Open.

Please.

Jonas stood at the bottom of the stairs with the
brass key in his hand.

"Give me that," Mara said.

"No."

"My mother is outside."

"Mine too."

She stopped.

Jonas looked older than he had minutes before. Beneath his
beard, his mouth trembled.

"She comes every time," he said. "Forty-three years, and she
still comes wearing the red scarf I gave her."

"What are they?"

"The lost. Or what remains of them."

"They need our help."

"They need the light. They need us to keep it
turning and the door shut."

Her mother screamed outside.

Mara lunged for the key.

Jonas caught her arm. They struggled on the narrow stairs.
The tower pitched beneath them, though the island itself could
not move. Mara struck his shoulder, and the key fell,
bouncing down the iron steps.

Both stared after it.

A child's voice spoke from the entrance room.

"Jonas?"

The old man went white.

A small shadow passed under the door.

"Jonas, I'm scared."

He released Mara.

The voice came again. "Papa?"

Jonas descended one step, then another.

"You told me not to open it," Mara said.

He moved past her as if asleep.

"Who is it?"

"My boy."

The pounding ceased.

In the sudden quiet, the child began to cry.

Jonas reached the entrance room and picked up the key.
His hand shook so violently that the metal chattered against
the lock.

Mara seized his wrist.

"You said it isn't ours."

"He was six."

"You said to keep the door shut."

"He hated the dark."

"So did I."

The key slid halfway into the lock.

Mara dragged Jonas backward. He fought with wild strength.

"You don't understand!" he shouted. "I heard him calling from
the cliff, and I was tending the lamp. I chose
the light. By the time I reached him, the tide—"

The door shook in its frame.

"Papa!" the child screamed.

Jonas tore free.

Mara did the only thing she could think of. She
slapped him.

The crack silenced both the child and the storm.

Jonas stared at her.

Then the brass key turned by itself.

The door opened an inch.

Black water burst through the gap.

Mara threw her weight against it. Jonas joined her. Together
they pushed, but the water drove the door wider. Pale
fingers curled around its edge from outside.

"Upstairs!" Jonas gasped.

"We can't leave it open."

"The lamp matters more."

The phrase transformed him. His grief remained, but duty closed
around it like armor.

He pulled Mara away. They fled up the spiral staircase
as seawater flooded the lower floor. Behind them came footsteps—hundreds
of bare, wet feet climbing the tower.

In the lantern chamber, the flame had shrunk to a
blue bead. The rotation had almost stopped.

Jonas seized the fuel pump. "Pressure line is blocked."

Mara opened her case. "Tools."

The lens crept past the windows. Outside, the Far Lantern
towered above the island, its masts vanishing into cloud. The
ship's bow seemed made from broken doors, coffins, figureheads, and
ribs of smaller vessels. The dead covered its decks.

Mara removed the burner housing. Black grit clogged the feed
pipe.

"Salt," she said.

"Shouldn't be there."

"Tell it."

The footsteps reached the floor below.

Jonas held the failing lamp while Mara threaded a wire
into the pipe. Her fingers felt numb. The obstruction would
not move.

A figure appeared on the stairs.

Mara's mother.

Water streamed from her dress. Her skin was pale, her
lips blue.

"Mara," she said softly.

Mara kept working.

"You grew up."

The wire slipped.

"I thought of you every day," her mother said.

Mara's eyes filled with tears.

"I watched," the ghost continued. "Every birthday. Every train you
missed. Every night you sat alone pretending you preferred it."

"Don't," Mara whispered.

"You don't have to be alone now."

More figures gathered on the stairs behind her. Sailors, children,
fishermen, travelers. Among them stood a boy holding Jonas's hand.

Jonas made a broken sound.

The flame dwindled further.

Mara pushed the wire deep into the fuel line. It
struck something hard.

"Mum," she said, "what did you call me?"

The ghost smiled. "My little bird."

Mara closed her eyes.

It knew.

Of course it knew.

She could step away from the lamp. She could take
her mother's hand. For one instant, that seemed not merely
possible but sensible. All life moved toward reunion. Every road
ended at a door where someone waited.

Then Mara remembered the flour sack.

They had not dragged it together.

That was the lie grief had polished smooth.

Her mother had lifted the sack easily. Mara had been
the one who stumbled, spilling flour across the kitchen, and
her mother had drawn wings in the white dust with
both hands.

Little bird, she had said. Every bird makes a mess
before it flies.

Mara looked at the ghost.

"You remembered the wrong thing."

Her mother's loving face twisted.

The mouth opened too wide. Seawater poured from it.

Mara drove the wire through the blockage.

The fuel line cleared.

Jonas pumped once. The burner exploded into golden light.

The ghosts shrieked.

Brightness struck the lens and multiplied. The chamber became a
wheel of fire. The dead were lifted from the stairs,
their bodies unraveling into streams of rain that swept outward
through the windows.

The beam crossed the Far Lantern.

Its black hull split along lines of gold.

For an instant, every figure aboard became distinct. Mara saw
wonder replace terror on their faces. She saw her mother
among them—not the thing from the stairs, but her mother
as she had truly been, laughing in a flour-covered kitchen.

Beside her stood Jonas's son.

The boy waved.

Then the ship opened like a shadow at dawn.

Light poured through it.

The sea, the storm, and the night turned white.

Mara heard a sound like a thousand people sighing after
a long journey.

The Far Lantern vanished.

Morning filled the chamber.

The storm was gone.

Below them, the clocks began ticking again.

Jonas sank to the floor. For a while, neither of
them spoke. The oil flame burned steadily in the center
of the lens, pale against the sunrise.

At last, Mara sat beside him.

"Was that the fifth time?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Will it return?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"Storms arrive exactly when they mean to."

She almost laughed.

Far below, the entrance door hung open. Seawater covered the
floor, but no footprints remained on the stairs.

At low tide, Mara crossed the causeway to the mainland.
She carried her tools in one hand and the automation
unit in the other.

Her supervisor waited beside a white Authority van.

"You were supposed to install that," he said.

"It's defective."

"It's brand-new."

"Still defective."

He looked toward the lighthouse. Jonas stood on the gallery
in his yellow duck slippers, one hand raised against the
wind.

"We can't keep Pike there forever," the supervisor said.

"No," Mara replied. "He needs an assistant."

"A what?"

"Someone trained in electrical systems, mechanical repair, fuel maintenance, and
optical calibration."

"Who are we going to find willing to live on
that rock?"

Mara placed the automation unit in the van.

Then she took her case and walked back toward the
causeway.

Behind her, the supervisor shouted about budgets, regulations, and common
sense. Ahead, the lighthouse flashed in the morning sun.

Mara kept walking.

She had spent most of her life believing a light
was only a warning: rocks here, danger here, keep away.

Now she understood that a light could also be an
invitation.

Through the open lighthouse door, Jonas called, "You're late."

Mara stepped inside.

"Storm delayed me."

"Storms delay nothing."

"Then put the kettle on."

"We're out of tea."

"I brought some."

For the first time, Jonas smiled.

Above them, the great lens turned, gathering the daylight into
its many rings. Far beyond the island, beyond the charts
and shipping lanes, the sea waited with all it had
taken.

The light went on watching.

The Lantern Keeper's Last Winter

By the time Mara Vale arrived at Saint Orra, the
sea had already swallowed the road.

She stood at the edge of the flooded causeway with
one suitcase in her hand and watched the island rise
from the evening mist. Black cliffs supported a crown of
grass, crooked pines, and stone cottages. Above them all stood
the lighthouse, its white tower stained gray by decades of
storms.

A boat waited beside the drowned road. Its owner, a
broad man in a yellow coat, lifted Mara's suitcase and
dropped it among coils of rope.

"You're late," he said.

"The train stopped twice."

"The train always stops twice."

Mara climbed aboard. "Then I'm exactly on time."

The boatman gave her a doubtful look and started the
engine.

His name was Calder Rusk. During the crossing, he said
very little. When Mara asked about the lighthouse keeper, he
pretended not to hear. Only when the boat struck the
island's small wooden pier did he finally speak.

"Elias Vale hasn't taken an apprentice in thirty years."

"I'm not his apprentice."

Calder tied the boat to a post. "No?"

"I'm his granddaughter."

At that, he looked at her properly.

Mara was twenty-six, narrow-faced and pale from city winters. She
wore a coat too thin for the island and boots
already ruined by seawater. A silver mechanical watch hung from
a chain around her neck. It had belonged to her
mother, and before that, to Elias.

Calder nodded toward the lighthouse.

"You'd better tell him."

The path climbed past the village. Saint Orra had no
church, no inn, and no streets wide enough for a
cart. There was a bakery, a post office that opened
on Tuesdays, and seventeen houses built from dark local stone.
As Mara passed, curtains shifted. No one greeted her.

The lighthouse door was unlocked.

Inside, the air smelled of oil, salt, and iron. A
spiral staircase wound upward through the tower's hollow center. Mara
called her grandfather's name. Her voice rose and disappeared.

No answer came.

She climbed.

At the top, Elias Vale stood inside the lantern room
with his back to her. He was polishing the great
brass housing around the lens. Beyond the glass, the sea
stretched in every direction, cold and restless beneath a violet
sky.

He had grown smaller since she last saw him. His
shoulders bent under a blue wool coat. His hair, once
gray, had become pure white.

"You took your time," he said.

Mara stopped on the final stair.

"You knew I was coming?"

"I saw the boat."

"I wrote three weeks ago."

"I don't read letters that bring bad news."

"You didn't read it, then?"

"I knew it was bad because it was from you."

She might have laughed if his voice had contained any
kindness.

Elias turned. His eyes were sharp beneath tangled brows.

"You look like your mother."

"So everyone tells me."

"She hated it here."

"So she told me."

"She left."

"Yes."

"And now she's dead."

Mara's fingers closed around the watch at her throat.

"Yes."

Elias put down his cloth. For a moment, something in
his face weakened. Then the lighthouse mechanism gave a deep
metallic click, and the lens began to rotate. A golden
beam swept across Mara, across the windows, and out over
the darkening sea.

"Come downstairs," he said. "There's soup."

They ate in the keeper's cottage, a low building attached
to the tower. The soup was made of potatoes, onions,
and too much pepper. Wind worried at the shutters.

Elias did not ask how his daughter had died. Mara
did not volunteer the details: the hospital room, the failing
heart, the final night in which her mother had gripped
her wrist and whispered, Go to Saint Orra. He has
something of mine.

"What do you have of hers?" Mara asked.

Elias lowered his spoon. "Her face, apparently."

"She said you kept something."

"Your mother said many things when she wanted her way."

"She was dying."

"That never improved anyone's honesty."

Mara stood so abruptly that the table shook.

"I didn't cross half the country to be insulted."

"No. You came for an inheritance."

"I came because she asked me to."

Elias stared into his soup.

Outside, the light completed another slow revolution. Its glow passed
through the cottage window and withdrew.

"Your room is upstairs," he said. "We'll speak tomorrow."

But tomorrow brought a storm.

The wind screamed from the north, bending the pines and
throwing stones of hail against the glass. The supply boat
did not come. Neither did Calder. Elias woke before dawn
and climbed the tower to extinguish the lamp. Mara followed
him.

"I can help," she said.

"No."

"You're limping."

"I've been limping since before you were born."

"I repair clocks."

"This isn't a clock."

"It's gears, weight, and motion. That makes it a clock
with a dramatic opinion of itself."

Elias almost smiled.

The lighthouse still used the old mechanism: a system of
weights and gears that rotated the enormous Fresnel lens around
a fixed lamp. Every few hours, the weights had to
be wound to the top of the tower. Elias showed
Mara the iron crank.

"Turn steadily," he said. "If the teeth slip, don't try
to catch the handle."

"I know."

"You don't."

"I've worked on tower clocks older than this country."

"And did any of them keep ships from breaking open
on Black Tooth Reef?"

Mara took the handle. "Not that I'm aware of."

She wound the mechanism. Slowly, the cables tightened and the
weights rose through the tower's central shaft. The work was
heavy, but familiar. Metal spoke to her through resistance and
vibration. She felt where the gears dragged and where the
old teeth no longer met cleanly.

"This bearing is worn," she said.

"It has another year."

"Six months."

"A year."

"Eight months, if we're bargaining."

Elias grunted.

For the next week, Mara stayed.

Each morning, she intended to demand the object her mother
had sent her to find. Each morning, Elias found work
for her first. They cleaned soot from the lamp, polished
the lens, checked the shutters, and carried oil up the
long staircase. At night they ate in silence, listening to
winter gather over the island.

Mara began to understand why her mother had hated Saint
Orra.

The sea decided when you could leave. The wind entered
every room. Salt coated the windows, the floors, even the
pages of books. The villagers kept their distance, and Elias
possessed the warmth of a locked safe.

Yet the lighthouse itself seemed alive.

Mara loved the exactness of it: the lamp centered within
the lens, the counterweights descending at a measured pace, the
beam identifying Saint Orra by its pattern—three bright flashes, one
darkness, three bright flashes again. Far offshore, unseen hands trusted
that pattern. A captain saw it and altered course. A
sailor woke another sailor. A ship passed safely through the
night because this old machine continued to turn.

On her ninth day, Mara found the hidden door.

It was behind a cabinet in the workshop. She noticed
scratches on the floor where the cabinet had been moved,
though not recently. After Elias went to the village, she
dragged it aside.

A narrow wooden panel stood in the wall.

Behind it was a room no larger than a closet.

Shelves covered three sides, holding dozens of glass jars. Inside
each jar floated a small blue flame.

Mara stared.

The flames gave no heat. They twisted gently in their
sealed vessels, each one no bigger than a candle's tongue.
Labels had been tied around the jars with faded string.

Gull Queen, 1908.

Meridian, 1921.

Isabelle Rose, 1947.

Northward Star, 1963.

Some names Mara recognized from island memorials. All were ships
lost near Saint Orra.

At the back of the lowest shelf stood one unlabeled
jar. Its flame burned brighter than the others.

"You shouldn't be in here," Elias said.

Mara turned.

He stood in the doorway, rain shining on his coat.

"What are they?"

He moved past her and touched one of the jars.

"Last lights."

"That means nothing."

"It means exactly what it says."

He explained without looking at her.

Sometimes, when a ship went down within sight of Saint
Orra, a light appeared on the sea after the vessel
vanished. It might hover for an hour or for only
a breath. The keeper's oldest duty, older than the tower
itself, was to gather that light before it faded.

"Gather it how?"

"With the lantern."

He pointed to an old brass hand-lantern hanging from a
hook.

"And then?"

"Keep it."

"Why?"

"So the drowned aren't forgotten."

Mara looked again at the jars. Their flames moved as
if underwater.

"You expect me to believe this?"

"No."

"What's in the unlabeled one?"

Elias was silent.

Mara lifted the jar.

His hand closed around her wrist with surprising strength.

"Put it back."

The flame surged blue-white between them.

"Is this why my mother sent me?"

"Put it back, Mara."

She looked into his face and understood.

"This is my father's ship."

Elias released her.

Mara had been three years old when her father disappeared.
Thomas Arden had sailed from the mainland on a summer
evening and never returned. Wreckage from his small fishing boat
had been found near Black Tooth Reef.

Her mother had always blamed the lighthouse.

She said the beam failed.

Elias sat on the workshop stool.

"That light appeared the night he died," he said.

"The lighthouse went dark."

"Yes."

"For eleven minutes."

"Yes."

"And his boat struck the reef."

"Yes."

Mara gripped the jar.

"Why did the light fail?"

Elias's gaze rested on the floor.

"Your mother was here that night. She had brought you
to see me. We argued. She wanted money to leave
the country with Thomas. I told her he was reckless.
She said she would never come back."

The flames on the shelves trembled as wind pressed against
the tower.

"I followed her outside," he continued. "The drive weight jammed.
I heard it, but I thought I had time. Eleven
minutes. That was all."

"You chose an argument over the lamp."

"I chose my pride."

"And my father died."

"Yes."

The simple answer struck harder than any excuse.

Mara hurled the jar at him.

Elias flinched. The jar missed his shoulder and shattered against
the wall.

Blue fire burst across the room.

It did not burn the shelves. It passed through them
in a shining wave, cold as moonlight. The other jars
rang like bells. Their flames leaned toward the broken vessel,
and for one impossible instant Mara heard voices: sailors shouting,
wood breaking, men calling names into the sea.

Then the blue fire gathered into the shape of a
man.

He stood between Mara and Elias, translucent and dripping with
pale light. Mara could not remember her father's face, but
she knew him. His eyes were hers.

He reached toward her.

The lighthouse bell began to ring.

Once. Twice. Again and again.

Elias stumbled to the window.

Through the rain, three white lights moved on the sea.

"A ship," Mara whispered.

"Too close to the reef."

They ran to the lantern room.

The storm had thickened into blackness. Rain covered the glass.
Far below, waves exploded against the cliffs. The three lights
belonged to a passenger steamer, driven south by the gale.

The lighthouse beam had stopped.

The lens stood motionless, pointing inland.

Elias opened the mechanism case. A steel shaft had split
beside the worn bearing.

"Can you repair it?" he asked.

"Not tonight."

The steamer's horn moaned through the storm.

"It won't see the reef," Elias said.

Mara examined the gears. The main drive was useless. Even
if she replaced the shaft, the bearing would seize under
the lens's weight.

"Can we rotate it by hand?"

"Not fast enough."

Another horn blast sounded, nearer now.

Below them, blue radiance spilled from the workshop and rose
through the stairwell. One by one, the sealed lights floated
upward. They passed through the iron steps and entered the
lantern room like a procession of stars.

The figure of Mara's father came last.

Elias took the old brass hand-lantern from its hook. His
face had become calm.

"What are you doing?" Mara asked.

"The lighthouse needs a flame."

"It has a flame."

"Not one bright enough to reach through this."

He opened the lantern. The wandering blue lights streamed into
it, all but the figure of Thomas Arden. The brass
frame shook in Elias's hands.

"There's a platform outside," he said. "The lantern can be
hung above the gallery."

"In this wind?"

"It will mark the reef."

"You'll be blown off."

Elias looked at the glowing figure.

"I should have gone after him that night."

"This won't change what happened."

"No."

"It won't bring my father back."

"No."

"Then don't pretend this is for me."

"It isn't." He raised the lantern. "It's for the people
on that ship."

He stepped onto the outer gallery.

The wind struck him against the railing. Mara caught the
back of his coat. Together they crawled around the tower,
Elias clutching the lantern to his chest.

Above the seaward side hung an old signal hook. Elias
tied the lantern beneath it, but the blue light remained
faint, trapped behind clouded glass.

"The door," he shouted. "It has to be opened."

"If you open it, the wind will put it out."

"No. These flames don't fear wind."

He reached for the tiny latch.

His foot slipped.

Mara seized his arm, dropping hard to one knee. For
a moment Elias hung beyond the railing with the sea
raging below.

The lantern door flew open.

Blue fire erupted into the night.

It climbed the lighthouse in a spiral, wrapped the tower,
and shot outward in a brilliant arc. The storm turned
blue. Every wave appeared sharp and silver-edged. Black Tooth Reef
rose from the water directly in the steamer's path.

The ship's horn gave three short blasts.

Slowly, its lights turned east.

Mara pulled Elias back over the railing.

They lay together on the wet iron as the steamer
passed beyond the reef. The blue fire shone until the
vessel reached safe water. Then the many lights separated and
drifted upward, disappearing among the clouds.

Only Thomas remained.

He stood on the gallery untouched by the wind.

Mara rose.

All her life, her father had been an absence shaped
by other people's anger. Her mother remembered his laughter. Elias
remembered his recklessness. Villagers remembered wreckage. Mara remembered nothing.

Now he looked at her with sorrow and affection.

She wanted to ask him whether he blamed Elias. She
wanted to ask if he had been afraid, whether he
had called her name, whether he had known the light
would fail.

Instead she said, "I don't remember you."

Thomas touched two fingers to his heart.

Then he pointed to hers.

His shape dissolved into a single flame. It circled Mara
once, warm now, and entered the silver watch hanging at
her throat.

The watch began to tick.

It had not run since her mother died.

Winter held Saint Orra for another three months.

Elias became ill after the storm. He survived, though his
limp worsened and his hands shook too badly for delicate
work. Mara repaired the lighthouse mechanism with a new shaft
forged by the village smith and a bearing sent from
the mainland.

When the spring supply boat arrived, Calder found her painting
the cottage shutters.

"Staying?" he asked.

"For a while."

"That's what everyone says before they stay forever."

Mara looked up at the lighthouse. "My grandfather needs an
apprentice."

From an open window, Elias shouted, "I do not."

Calder smiled.

That summer, ships passing Saint Orra saw the old pattern
restored: three bright flashes, one darkness, three bright flashes again.

At night, Mara climbed the tower to wind the weights.
Elias often joined her, though he complained about her methods.
They did not forgive each other all at once. Some
wounds refused to close simply because the truth had entered
them. But they worked side by side, and that was
a beginning.

Years later, when Mara became the keeper and Elias's ashes
were scattered beyond Black Tooth Reef, she placed no blue
flame in a jar for him.

She opened the brass lantern instead.

A small light rose from the sea, circled the tower,
and paused beside her. For a moment, she smelled pepper
soup and lamp oil. She heard an old man insisting
that a failing bearing had another year in it.

Then the light flew out across the water.

Mara watched until it joined the beam of the lighthouse
and became impossible to distinguish from it.

Below, the sea moved through the dark, carrying ships toward
distant harbors. Above, the great lens turned with the patient
rhythm of a clock.

Three bright flashes.

One darkness.

Three bright flashes again.

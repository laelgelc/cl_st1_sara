The first time Mara heard the lighthouse speak, it said
her name in her dead brother's voice.

She was halfway up the spiral staircase, carrying a toolbox
in one hand and a lantern in the other, when
the sound drifted down from the watch room.

"Mara?"

She stopped so suddenly that the lantern struck the iron
railing.

Outside, the winter sea threw itself against the cliffs. The
entire tower seemed to shiver. Salt water hissed over the
glass high above, and the great lens groaned as it
turned.

"Mara, is that you?"

Her brother Elias had been dead for seventeen years.

She set down the toolbox, though she did not release
the lantern.

"Who's there?"

Only the machinery answered: gears clicking, chains whispering over wheels,
the old brass governor tapping out its patient rhythm.

Mara climbed.

At sixty-three she knew better than to run up lighthouse
stairs, but by the time she reached the watch room
her breath had torn loose from discipline. She pushed through
the door and lifted the lantern.

The room was empty.

In the center, the Fresnel lens revolved around its lamp,
sending four blades of white light across the black water.
The clockwork below it had been built in another century,
when engineers believed that anything important should be made of
brass and iron and should last longer than its maker.
Mara had cared for it since she was twenty-one. She
knew every tooth in every wheel. She knew when the
drive chain needed oil from the way it sighed on
damp nights. She could tell the temperature by the pitch
of the regulator spring.

Tonight, however, the machine produced a sound she did not
know.

Beneath the ordinary ticking ran a second rhythm, faint and
uneven.

Tap-tap. Pause. Tap.

"Mara," the lighthouse said again.

The voice came from the brass speaking tube beside the
lens pedestal.

Mara approached it slowly. The tube ran down through the
tower to the old keeper's quarters, where it opened above
the kitchen table. It had not been used in decades.
No one lived below now. The door was locked, and
Mara had the only key.

She pressed her ear to the flared mouth.

"Mara, don't let the light go out."

She pulled away.

The voice was Elias's—not merely similar, but his exactly, with
the same soft rasp he had acquired after a childhood
fever and the same upward turn at the end of
a sentence, as though every declaration concealed a question.

Then the lamp flickered.

Mara forgot the voice.

She opened the fuel valve, checked the pressure gauge, adjusted
the mantle, and examined the rotation assembly. Everything appeared normal,
but after twelve seconds the light flickered again.

That was not supposed to happen. The lamp had two
independent fuel lines, and the emergency generator had been serviced
a month before. Even if every system failed, the reserve
battery should ignite the electric beacon.

Mara watched another dark pulse pass through the lens.

Twelve seconds later, another.

Not random, she realized.

A signal.

She took a pencil from her breast pocket and marked
the intervals on the back of an old maintenance receipt.

Short. Short. Long. Short.

The lamp was speaking in code.

By midnight she had copied three lines.

DO NOT ANSWER THE RADIO.

At twelve minutes past midnight, the radio crackled.

Mara stood in the doorway of the watch room, looking
down at the receiver mounted on the wall. It was
an ugly gray box installed by the Maritime Authority after
automation came to the coast. Most nights it carried weather
reports or complaints from fishermen whose engines had failed.

The speaker hissed.

"Blackglass Light, this is the cargo vessel Merciful Dawn. Do
you receive?"

Mara did not move.

"Blackglass Light, this is Merciful Dawn. We have lost navigation
and are taking water. Twenty-seven souls aboard. Do you receive?"

She looked at the lamp.

It burned steadily now.

The radio crackled again. "Keeper, if you hear us, please
answer."

Keeper.

No one had called her that officially in eleven years.
According to the Authority, Mara was a maintenance contractor. The
lighthouse was automated, and the human being who climbed its
stairs every evening was merely a temporary redundancy.

She crossed the room and lifted the microphone.

Before she could press the switch, Elias spoke through the
tube.

"They're not in the water."

Mara closed her eyes.

Her brother had been twenty when the fishing boat Saint
Orra capsized north of Blackglass Point. Mara had seen the
wreck through this very lens, a dark shape vanishing between
waves. Their father, the keeper then, had held the light
steady while the rescue boat searched until dawn.

They found three bodies.

Elias was not among them.

For years Mara imagined him afloat somewhere beyond the beam,
calling to shore with salt in his mouth.

"Blackglass Light," the radio said. "Please."

She pressed the transmit switch.

"This is Blackglass. State your position."

Silence followed.

Then a voice said, very softly, "Thank you, Mara."

Every light in the tower went out.

Darkness fell with physical weight. The lens stopped turning. The
machinery below gave a final clank and became still.

Mara dropped the microphone and reached for her lantern, but
its flame had died too.

Outside, the storm vanished.

Not weakened—vanished.

One moment the sea had been hammering the cliff; the
next there was no wind, no rain, no water. The
silence was so complete that Mara heard blood moving in
her ears.

Then something struck the tower door far below.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

The radio lit with a dull green glow.

"They've come ashore," Elias said through the tube.

Mara opened the emergency cabinet and found the hand crank.
The lighthouse had been designed to survive failures of fuel,
electricity, and human courage. If she connected the crank to
the main drive, she could turn the lens manually, though
it would require constant effort.

She fitted the iron handle into its socket.

Far beneath her, the tower door shook.

"Mara," Elias whispered, "you have to keep the beam moving."

She turned the crank.

At first nothing happened. She braced one foot against the
pedestal and pulled harder. Old gears resisted, then surrendered with
a metallic groan. The lens began to rotate.

There was no light inside it, but as the first
glass panel faced the sea, it gathered a pale glow
from nowhere. A thin beam slid across the water.

Where it passed, the world returned.

Rain appeared within the beam, each drop bright as a
needle. Waves leaped from the darkness, wild and white-capped, only
to vanish when the light moved on.

The beam reached the harbor.

A ship stood on the dry seabed below the village.

It was enormous, three-masted and ancient, its sails hanging in
rags. Barnacles armored its hull. Seaweed streamed from its rigging
despite the absence of wind. Across its stern, in faded
gold letters, was written MERCIFUL DAWN.

Figures were climbing the road from the harbor.

Mara recognized the first of them before the beam moved
away.

Captain Varlo, lost with his crew in 1843.

His portrait hung in the village hall.

The tower door boomed again.

The lock broke.

Mara kept turning.

The beam crossed the stairwell windows, and for a moment
she saw shadows ascending inside the tower. Some limped. Some
crawled. Water spilled from their clothes and ran upward along
the walls.

"There are too many," she whispered.

"There are always too many," Elias replied.

"Why are they here?"

"For the light."

The crank dragged against her shoulders. "Why?"

"Because it remembers the way home."

Footsteps circled upward.

Mara turned the lens through another revolution. The beam crossed
the graveyard behind Saint Orra's Church. In its light, people
stood beside nearly every stone. Men in oilskins. Women in
long dresses. Children holding hands. All faced the lighthouse.

The beam passed, and they vanished.

Mara thought of all the ships Blackglass Light had watched
perish: cutters broken on the reef, steamers burned offshore, fishing
boats swallowed without wreckage. The sea kept no cemeteries, so
the tower had become one. Every night its beam crossed
the places where people had last been alive.

Perhaps light remembered what darkness tried to erase.

The first sailor entered the watch room.

He wore the blue coat of a naval officer, though
half his face had been stripped to bone. Water poured
from his open mouth. Behind him came a woman in
a red shawl, then two boys tied together by a
length of rope. More crowded the stairs below.

They did not look at Mara.

They looked at the lens.

The officer reached for it.

"Don't let them touch the glass," Elias said.

Mara released the crank with one hand and snatched a
brass oil can from the workbench. She swung it at
the officer's wrist.

The can passed through him.

Cold exploded up her arm. For an instant she was
not in the watch room. She was beneath black water,
sinking among scraps of burning wood. A bell tolled below
her, impossibly deep. All around, pale faces turned toward a
column of light descending from the surface.

Then she was back.

The officer had moved closer to the lens.

Mara seized the crank again and turned. As the beam
swept through him, his body became transparent. She saw a
different man beneath the corpse: young, terrified, clutching a miniature
portrait of a woman.

He whispered a name.

Then he dissolved into rain.

The woman in the red shawl stepped forward next. The
beam touched her, and she vanished too.

"They don't want to extinguish it," Mara said.

"No," Elias replied. "They want to enter it."

One by one the drowned approached the revolving light. One
by one the beam carried them away.

Soon the watch room filled with the murmur of names.

Some called for mothers. Some for husbands or wives. Some
spoke names in languages Mara had never heard. A little
girl said only "home," and the word nearly caused Mara
to lose her grip.

The crank grew heavier.

"How long?" Mara asked.

Elias did not answer.

"How long must I turn it?"

"Until dawn."

She looked at the clock. Its hands had stopped at
twelve minutes past midnight.

"What if dawn doesn't come?"

This time Elias said nothing.

The drowned continued climbing.

Hours might have passed, or minutes. Mara's shoulders burned. Skin
tore from her palms and darkened the iron handle. She
tried wrapping a rag around the grip, but the fabric
became slick with blood.

Still they came.

A cook carrying a broken ladle. A bride with shells
woven into her hair. A soldier locked inside a rusted
cage. A baby sleeping against the breast of a woman
who had no face.

Each stepped into the beam and was gone.

At last Mara saw a familiar yellow coat on the
stairs.

Elias entered the watch room as he had been at
twenty: narrow-shouldered, black-haired, with a foolish red scarf tied at
his throat. He was wet, but he bore no wound.
He smiled.

Mara stopped turning.

The lens slowed.

"Mara," he said, now with his own mouth.

She had imagined this meeting thousands of times. In some
dreams she struck him for leaving. In others she held
him until they both became children again. She had prepared
accusations, apologies, jokes, and prayers.

What came out was, "You lost your boots."

Elias looked down at his bare feet.

"So I did."

The lens slowed further, and the beam weakened.

"Keep turning," he said.

She forced the crank forward.

He walked beside her, staying just beyond the edge of
the light.

"Did you suffer?" she asked.

"Yes."

She hated him for the truth and loved him for
it too.

"Were you alone?"

"At first."

"And afterward?"

"There isn't an afterward where I am. Not like yours."

More drowned sailors waited on the stairs, but none entered.
They seemed to understand that this moment belonged to the
living.

Mara turned the crank. "Why did you warn me not
to answer?"

"The dead can't cross without being invited. The radio needed
a living voice."

"You told me not to answer, and I answered anyway."

"You never listened to me."

"You were usually wrong."

He laughed, and the sound was exactly as she remembered.
It broke something inside her that had remained rigid for
seventeen years.

"Why did you call?" she asked.

"It wasn't me at first. The others borrowed my voice
because it was one you would trust."

"I didn't trust it."

"You came upstairs."

"I came to fix the lamp."

"You always do."

He looked older when he said it, though his face
did not change.

Mara glanced through the glass. Beyond the tower, the beam
continued to reveal fragments of the drowned world. The Merciful
Dawn waited in the dead harbor. Thousands of figures stood
along the road, stretching beyond the village and over the
hills.

"I can't guide all of them," she said.

"Not all. Only those who saw Blackglass before they died.
Only those who believed its light meant shore."

"There are thousands."

"Yes."

"My arms are failing."

"I know."

"Help me."

"I can't touch the crank."

"Then what good are you?"

Elias smiled sadly. "I can keep you company."

So he did.

He told her what happened to the Saint Orra. A
wave had struck them from the north, though the wind
blew west. Elias had been trapped beneath a net. He
had seen the lighthouse beam once underwater, warped by the
sea into a golden road.

He told her he had not been brave. He had
cried. He had called for their father, and then for
Mara.

She told him their father had kept the light burning
for six more years and died in the keeper's chair.
She told him the village had built a supermarket where
they used to gather mussels. She told him she had
married briefly, divorced sensibly, and had a daughter who taught
mathematics in Edinburgh.

"You would like her," Mara said.

"I do."

"You've seen her?"

"We see what the light sees."

Mara looked at the turning lens. "Everything?"

"Only moments. A face at a window. A boat crossing
the beam. You climbing the stairs."

"Were you here all these years?"

"Sometimes."

"And you never spoke?"

"The door was closed."

She understood then that the door was not wood or
iron. It was the boundary between a signal and an
answer, between grief remembered and grief invited inside.

The radio had not asked for help. It had asked
permission.

The line of drowned moved again. The beam took them
one by one, while Elias remained beside the lens.

Slowly, almost imperceptibly, the darkness outside softened from black to
gray.

"Dawn," Mara said.

"Almost."

The crank resisted every turn. Her left arm had gone
numb, and pain pulsed beneath her ribs. She did not
know whether she had another revolution in her.

Elias stepped closer.

The moving light touched the edge of his red scarf,
turning it bright as flame.

"It's time," he said.

"No."

"You must let the beam take me."

"You're the one who told me to keep it moving."

"And now I'm telling you not to stop."

The old anger rose in her, hot and welcome. "You
don't get to come back for ten minutes and tell
me what to do."

"It has been longer than ten minutes."

"Not long enough."

"No," he said. "It never is."

Behind him, the last of the drowned waited: Captain Varlo,
the little girl, the sailors from the Saint Orra. Some
had been standing outside the light for nearly two centuries.
Their faces held no hunger now, only exhaustion.

Mara turned the crank.

Elias entered the beam.

For a moment he was twenty. Then twelve, racing barefoot
along the cliff path. Then six, asleep beside the kitchen
stove. Then only a shape made of rain and gold.

"Mara," he said.

She waited for some final revelation.

Instead he grinned.

"You still owe me three shillings."

The beam passed through him.

He was gone.

Mara laughed so hard she nearly fell. She laughed and
sobbed and cursed him, but she kept turning the crank.

The beam gathered the final figures. Captain Varlo removed his
hat. The little girl waved. The sailors of the Saint
Orra passed last, and with them went the wreck in
the harbor, the silence, and the strange dry world beneath
the sea.

Morning broke over Blackglass Point.

Wind slammed into the tower. Rain returned. The waves crashed
against the cliffs with such force that the glass shook.

The main lamp ignited by itself.

The machinery started.

The crank spun free, throwing Mara backward onto the floor.

For a while she lay beneath the turning lens, listening
to the ordinary world. Gears clicked. Chains whispered. The radio
muttered static. Gulls cried beyond the windows.

Then the receiver spoke in the clipped voice of the
Maritime Authority.

"Blackglass Light, report. Your beacon was unresponsive for twelve seconds."

Mara stared at it.

Twelve seconds.

"Blackglass Light, do you receive?"

She pushed herself upright and crossed the room. Her hands
were bloody. Her back ached. Every part of her felt
older than the tower.

She lifted the microphone.

"This is Blackglass," she said. "Beacon operational."

"Cause of interruption?"

Mara looked at the brass speaking tube.

For just an instant, she thought she heard distant laughter.

"Routine maintenance," she replied.

The Authority acknowledged and signed off.

By noon the storm had passed. Sunlight lay across the
sea in long silver bands. Mara cleaned the watch room,
oiled the drive chain, and recorded the lamp failure in
the official log. She did not mention the ship, the
drowned, or the door that a living voice could open.

At the bottom of the page she added a private
note:

If the radio calls after midnight, do not answer.

Then she considered this, crossed it out, and wrote:

If you answer, keep the light moving.

Years later, after Mara was gone and Blackglass Light had
become a museum, visitors sometimes asked why an iron hand
crank stood mounted beside the great lens. The guides explained
that it was an obsolete emergency mechanism from the age
of lighthouse keepers.

Children liked to turn it, though it was no longer
connected to anything.

On quiet days, if a child pushed hard enough, the
lens would move a fraction of an inch. A blade
of sunlight would slide across the watch room and touch
the brass speaking tube.

Most heard only the sea.

A few heard their names spoken by voices they remembered.

And once in a great while, when winter storms hid
the coast and some lost vessel searched the darkness for
shore, the old lighthouse answered with a beam brighter than
any lamp inside it—turning, returning, and showing the way home.

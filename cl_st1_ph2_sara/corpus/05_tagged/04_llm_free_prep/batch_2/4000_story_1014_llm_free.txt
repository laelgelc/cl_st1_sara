# The Department of Lost Things

Every morning at eight, Mara Vale unlocked the blue door
beneath Bellwether Station and collected the things the city had
forgotten.

Umbrellas arrived first. They always did. Black umbrellas with curved
wooden handles, red umbrellas with broken spokes, small transparent umbrellas
printed with yellow ducks. They gathered overnight in the wire
basket beside her desk, damp even in summer.

After the umbrellas came gloves, keys, scarves, spectacles, wallets, notebooks,
and toys. At noon, the station porters delivered larger objects:
suitcases left on platforms, bicycles chained to railings for too
many years, and once, memorably, a cello in a silver
case.

Mara catalogued each item in a thick green ledger.

**Item 14,823: One left glove, blue wool, child-sized. Found on
eastbound train.**

**Item 14,824: Brass key, three teeth, tied with white ribbon.
Found beneath Clock Tower Bridge.**

**Item 14,825: Glass jar containing seven buttons and a dead
moth. Found in waiting room.**

The Department of Lost Things had no sign above its
door. Most people believed the station's basement held pipes, coal,
and rats. Those who needed the department, however, always found
it.

They arrived with desperate eyes.

"I've lost my wedding ring."

"My daughter left her bear on the number six train."

"There was a letter in my coat pocket. I need
the letter, not the coat."

Mara would ask them for a description, consult the green
ledger, and disappear into the storerooms. If the missing thing
had reached the department, she returned with it wrapped in
brown paper.

People often cried. They hugged her. They promised to be
more careful.

They rarely were.

Mara understood. Losing things was human. People misplaced objects because
they were hurried, grieving, distracted, in love, or simply alive.
She never judged them.

It was the other kind of loss that troubled her.

Those objects arrived after midnight.

The first had appeared during Mara's third week at the
department, eleven years earlier. She had been staying late to
catalogue a trunk of winter coats when something knocked from
inside a locked cupboard.

Three slow taps.

Mara had opened the cupboard and found a little glass
bottle on the shelf. It was stoppered with wax and
filled with pale gold light.

Her predecessor, Mr. Quill, had turned white when she showed
him.

"Some lost things aren't things," he had said.

He led her to a narrow room at the back
of the department. It contained hundreds of shelves, and on
those shelves stood bottles, boxes, envelopes, and jars. Some hummed.
Some whispered. Some shivered when touched.

There were lost hours, folded like silk handkerchiefs.

There were forgotten names, sealed in blue envelopes.

There was the courage of a soldier who had fled
his first battle, kept in a matchbox.

There was the last note of a song no one
could finish.

There were childhood summers, a grandmother's voice, faith in tomorrow,
and the memory of how rain smelled on a certain
street before the factories came.

"Can we return them?" Mara had whispered.

"Sometimes," Mr. Quill said. "If the owner comes looking."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we keep them safe."

Mr. Quill retired the following winter. Mara inherited his brass
desk lamp, his collection of tea tins, and the department
itself.

In eleven years, no one had ever come asking for
a lost hour.

Then, on the first snow of December, a boy arrived
looking for Tuesday.

He was perhaps ten years old, thin and solemn, with
wet black hair flattened across his forehead. Snow had gathered
on the shoulders of his green coat.

Mara was closing the department when he slipped through the
blue door.

"We're closed," she said gently. "Come back tomorrow."

"I can't."

"What have you lost?"

"Tuesday."

Mara's hand stopped above the ledger.

The boy pulled a crumpled railway ticket from his pocket
and placed it on her desk. It had been stamped
on Monday evening. The destination read Bellwether. On the back,
in purple ink, someone had written:

**REMEMBER TUESDAY.**

"What's your name?" Mara asked.

"Eli Thorne."

"And what do you mean, you've lost Tuesday?"

"I went to sleep on Monday, and when I woke
up it was Wednesday. Everyone says Tuesday happened, but I
don't remember it. My mother doesn't, either."

"Perhaps you were ill."

"We weren't."

"You may have slept through the day."

"My mother's roses bloomed."

"In December?"

He nodded. "Then they turned black."

Mara looked toward the narrow door at the back of
the department.

"Wait here."

She entered the room of uncommon losses and lit the
wall lamps one by one. Dim shelves stretched farther than
the station above should have allowed. The air smelled of
dust, wax, and approaching rain.

Lost time was kept in the eastern cabinets.

Mara searched among labelled drawers.

**ELEVEN MINUTES, LOST IN ANTICIPATION.**

**AN AFTERNOON, WASTED IN ANGER.**

**THREE YEARS, GIVEN TO THE WRONG MAN.**

At last she found a new drawer. Frost silvered its
handle. When she pulled it open, cold air struck her
face.

Inside lay a square of darkness, folded four times.

A label had tied itself around it.

**TUESDAY, 12 DECEMBER. OWNERSHIP DISPUTED.**

Mara stared.

Lost things generally knew to whom they belonged. Even a
single key carried the faint impression of its lock. A
memory might hold a face, a scent, or a name.

She reached toward the folded day.

The darkness moved.

For one instant Mara saw a garden in winter. Red
roses opened beneath falling snow. A woman stood among them,
holding a silver bell. Behind her loomed a tall figure
without a shadow.

Then the drawer slammed shut.

Mara returned to the front office.

Eli sat exactly where she had left him, hands clasped
between his knees.

"I found something," she said. "But I can't return it
yet."

"Why not?"

"Your Tuesday isn't merely lost. Someone took it."

The station clock struck nine above them.

With the final chime, every umbrella in the wire basket
opened at once.

Eli jumped.

Mara looked toward the ceiling. Dust drifted from the beams.
Somewhere in the uncommon room, glass shattered.

"Stay here," she said.

The blue door burst inward.

A wind tore through the office, carrying snowflakes and dead
rose petals. The desk lamp went dark.

A man stood in the doorway.

He was tall and elegantly dressed in a grey suit.
Snow did not touch him. His skin had the colour
and smoothness of candle wax, and though the light fell
behind him, he cast no shadow.

"Good evening," he said.

Eli gripped the edge of his chair.

Mara stepped between them. "The department is closed."

"So it is." The man smiled. "I have come to
report a theft."

"What was stolen?"

"A day."

Mara kept her face still. "Describe it."

"Twenty-four hours. Tuesday. Snow in the morning, clear skies by
noon. A rather unpleasant evening."

"And your name?"

"You may write Mr. Grey."

"I require a full name."

"That is the only one I have left."

His smile widened, but sorrow flickered behind it. Mara had
seen that look before. People wore it when they had
lost something so long ago that grief had become part
of their face.

Eli rose. "You were in our garden."

Mr. Grey's gaze shifted to him.

"And you rang the bell," Eli continued. "I remember that
much."

"You remember nothing."

"I remember you."

The windows rattled.

Mr. Grey removed one glove, finger by finger. On his
palm was a black mark shaped like a clock with
no hands.

"The day belongs to me," he said. "Return it, and
I will leave."

Mara opened the green ledger.

"Proof of ownership?"

"I was there."

"So was Eli."

"The boy has no claim."

"He lost the day."

"He surrendered it."

"I didn't!" Eli shouted.

Mr. Grey glanced at him. "Ask your mother."

For a moment there was only the hiss of the
radiator.

Mara closed the ledger. "No disputed item can be released
until ownership is established."

"Then establish it."

"I will."

"Tonight."

"Investigations take time."

"Time," Mr. Grey said softly, "is precisely what we do
not have."

A crack ran across the face of the office clock.
Its hands began turning backwards.

Mara felt the room tilt. Tea leaped from her cup
into the pot. A pencil rolled uphill across the desk.
Wet footprints vanished from the floor beneath Eli's boots.

Mr. Grey's mark was reversing the minute.

Mara snatched the brass key from around her neck and
pressed it against the ledger. The book flew open, pages
fluttering. She wrote one word across the paper:

**STILL.**

The clock stopped.

Mr. Grey studied her with new interest.

"Eleven years in this cellar," he said, "and already you
command its little tricks."

"They aren't tricks."

"No. They are rules. Far more dangerous."

Mara pointed to the door. "Leave."

"To return empty-handed would cost me dearly."

"That isn't my concern."

"It will be."

He put on his glove. Before stepping outside, he looked
at Eli.

"Your mother made a promise on Tuesday. If she breaks
it, the day will break with her."

Then he vanished into the snow.

Mara locked the blue door.

"Who is he?" Eli asked.

"I don't know."

"You looked afraid."

"I was."

Adults had lied to Mara often when she was a
child, believing dishonesty could serve as a blanket against the
cold. It never had. She saw no reason to repeat
the mistake.

"Take me home," Eli said. "We can ask my mother."

The Thornes lived at the edge of the city, where
brick streets narrowed into frozen lanes. Mara and Eli rode
the last tram north. Neither spoke much.

At the final stop, Eli led her to a small
white house behind an iron fence.

The garden was filled with black roses.

Their stems bowed beneath the snow, but their petals looked
burned. As Mara passed, the flowers turned toward her.

Eli's mother waited in the kitchen. Ada Thorne was a
pale woman in a red cardigan, with a bandage around
her right hand. She did not seem surprised to see
Mara.

"You found the department," she told Eli.

"You knew about it?" he asked.

"I hoped you wouldn't need to."

Mara sat across from Ada at the kitchen table. "What
happened on Tuesday?"

Ada placed a kettle on the stove.

"I don't remember."

"That answer won't help your son."

"It's the truth."

"Then tell me what happened on Monday."

Ada's shoulders sagged. "Eli was sick. Not a fever or
cough—something stranger. He began fading."

Eli looked down.

"Fading?" Mara asked.

"In photographs first. Then mirrors. By Monday evening, I could
see the wallpaper through his hands."

Ada reached for her son. Her fingers trembled as they
touched his hair.

"The doctors wouldn't listen. I sat in the garden and
begged anyone, anything, to help me. At midnight, the roses
bloomed. Mr. Grey appeared."

"What did he offer?"

"A cure."

"At what price?"

"A day of Eli's life."

Eli's head snapped up. "You gave him Tuesday?"

"I thought he meant one day at the end. When
you were old. I thought—" Ada covered her face. "I
would have agreed to anything."

"But he took the next day," Mara said.

Ada nodded. "I woke on Wednesday. You were solid again.
The roses were black, and this was in my hand."

She unwrapped the bandage.

The mark of a handless clock stained her palm.

Mara felt suddenly cold.

"Why does Mr. Grey want the day if he already
took it?" Eli asked.

"Because something went wrong," Mara said. "The day escaped him.
It came to the department."

"Can I have it back?"

Mara looked at Ada. "A bargain freely made creates a
claim."

"He tricked her!"

"I know. But the department recognizes rules, not fairness."

Eli stood so quickly that his chair fell over.

"Then your department is useless."

He ran upstairs.

Ada stared at the black roses through the window. "He's
right."

"No," Mara said. "But he isn't entirely wrong."

The kettle began to whistle.

As Ada removed it from the stove, every light in
the house went out.

A silver bell rang in the garden.

Once.

Twice.

On the third ring, the kitchen door opened by itself.

Mr. Grey stood among the roses.

"The day is weakening," he called. "Come and see."

Mara, Ada, and Eli stepped into the garden.

The snow above the house had stopped falling. Each flake
hung motionless in the air. Between the black rosebushes, a
vertical tear opened in the darkness.

Through it, Mara saw Tuesday.

Eli lay transparent in his bed. Ada sat beside him,
weeping. Mr. Grey stood in the corner, holding out his
unmarked hand.

The scene shifted.

Ada rang a silver bell in the garden.

The roses bloomed.

Mr. Grey spoke words Mara could not hear, and Ada
answered.

Then something unexpected happened.

As Mr. Grey touched Eli's forehead, the boy opened his
eyes. He reached into the empty air and pulled.

The entire day shuddered.

Tuesday folded around Eli like a cloak and vanished.

The vision closed.

Mr. Grey looked furious.

"He took it," Ada whispered.

"Not knowingly," Mara said.

Eli stared at his hands.

Mr. Grey approached. "The bargain required a day. The boy
refused to yield it, so time itself became lost."

"You still cured him," Mara said.

"I kept my word."

"And now?"

"The missing day has left a wound. By midnight, the
week will collapse around it. Wednesday will follow Monday, then
Monday will lead to Wednesday, again and again. Your city
will circle two days until no one remembers there was
ever anything else."

The distant city clocks began striking eleven.

"One hour," Mr. Grey said. "Give me Tuesday, and the
week survives."

"And Eli loses a day."

"He will not feel it."

"He already does."

Mara considered the black roses, the suspended snow, and the
clock mark on Ada's hand.

There were always rules.

But rules had edges, and edges could be turned.

"The bargain was for a day of Eli's life," she
said.

"Yes."

"Not specifically Tuesday."

Mr. Grey's eyes narrowed.

Mara turned to Eli. "What is the worst day you
remember?"

He frowned. "Why?"

"Answer me."

"The day my father left."

Ada inhaled sharply.

"How long ago?"

"Three years."

"Did you live the whole day?"

"Yes."

"Do you want to keep it?"

Eli thought for a moment. "No."

Mara drew the brass key from around her neck.

Mr. Grey stepped forward. "Past days cannot be traded."

"Lost days can."

"It isn't lost."

"Not yet."

She placed the key against Eli's chest.

"Think of that day," she told him. "Every hour. Every
detail."

Eli closed his eyes.

The key warmed in Mara's hand. A February morning filled
her mind: a blue cup on the table, a suitcase
by the door, Ada crying silently, a father who could
not meet his son's eyes. Eli running after him in
the rain. A car disappearing at the end of the
street.

Mara turned the key.

Eli gasped.

The memory came free as a long grey thread. Mara
wound it around her hand until it formed a small,
cold square.

A lost day.

She offered it to Mr. Grey.

"The contract requests one day of Eli Thorne's life. Here
it is."

Mr. Grey did not take it.

"The department will recognize the exchange," Mara said.

"But will he?"

They looked at Eli.

The boy touched his cheek. Tears were drying there, though
his expression had softened.

"I know my father left," he said. "But I don't
remember watching him go."

"Do you accept the trade?" Mara asked.

He looked at his mother, then at the tear in
the night where Tuesday had appeared.

"Yes."

The grey square lifted from Mara's palm and flew into
Mr. Grey's hand.

The mark beneath his glove began ticking.

Once.

The black roses crumbled into ash.

Twice.

The frozen snow fell.

Three times.

Across the city, clocks raced toward midnight.

Mr. Grey closed his fingers around the substituted day. "Clever,"
he told Mara.

"You sound disappointed."

"I am rarely pleased to be outwitted."

"You kept your bargain. We kept Tuesday."

"For now."

The clock mark vanished from Ada's palm and appeared, faintly,
on Mr. Grey's throat.

"What will happen to the day you took?" Eli asked.

Mr. Grey looked down at the grey square. For the
first time, he seemed old.

"I will carry it."

"Forever?"

"Nothing is forever. That is why people value it."

He turned to leave.

"Mr. Grey," Mara called.

He stopped.

"What day did you lose?"

His back stiffened.

Mara had recognized the grief in his face, the way
he spoke of time as though it were both treasure
and enemy. No one became such a creature without first
losing something precious.

After a long silence, he said, "The day before I
became what I am."

"Have you ever looked for it?"

"Everywhere."

"Not everywhere."

She handed him a card bearing the address of the
department.

Mr. Grey examined it.

Then, with a faint and bitter smile, he disappeared.

At midnight, Tuesday returned.

It passed through the city in a single breath. Snow
melted upward. Trains ran backward along their tracks. Unspoken apologies
entered waiting mouths. The dead roses grew red, and then
became buds.

For one bright instant, everyone remembered what had been taken.

Then Wednesday settled properly into its place.

The next morning, Mara unlocked the blue door beneath Bellwether
Station at eight.

The wire basket was full of umbrellas.

Eli sat beside her desk, labelling a box of unmatched
gloves. He had insisted on helping after school, arguing that
any department capable of misplacing a whole day needed more
staff.

Mara had been unable to disagree.

Ada's clock mark was gone. Eli was healthy. Tuesday remained
in his memory, though most of it was ordinary: breakfast,
snow, his mother's worried face. The ordinary details seemed to
please him most.

At ten, Mara entered the uncommon room to return a
jar of misplaced laughter.

A new envelope rested on the central table.

It was grey, sealed with black wax, and addressed to
her.

Inside was a note.

**Miss Vale,**

**I have considered your question. I believe the day I
lost may be in your care. If so, I will
come to claim it when I am prepared to pay
the price.**

**Until then, guard your Thursdays.**

There was no signature.

Beneath the note lay a silver bell and a white
rose.

Mara catalogued both.

Then she opened the oldest cabinet in the room, where
objects had waited longer than anyone could remember. At the
back, behind a king's forgotten mercy and a map of
a vanished road, she found a little glass bottle filled
with pale gold light.

It was the first uncommon loss she had ever discovered.

For eleven years, its label had been blank.

Now ink slowly appeared across it.

**ONE DAY, LOST BEFORE THE BEGINNING.**

Mara carried the bottle to her desk.

"Whose is that?" Eli asked.

"Someone who isn't ready for it."

"What do we do?"

Mara placed the bottle in a padded box and locked
it carefully away.

"We keep it safe," she said.

Above them, trains arrived and departed. People hurried through the
station carrying bags, letters, promises, and names. They dropped coins.
They forgot umbrellas. They left books beneath benches and courage
in doorways. They lost their tempers, their chances, and occasionally
their way.

Sooner or later, some of those things found the blue
door.

And when they did, Mara was waiting.

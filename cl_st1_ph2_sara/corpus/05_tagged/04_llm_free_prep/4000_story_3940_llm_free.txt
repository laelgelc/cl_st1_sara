### The Keeper of Lost Hours

On the morning the clocks began to disappear, Mara Vale
found a silver pocket watch inside a loaf of bread.

She discovered it at dawn, while slicing the first loaf
in her bakery. The knife struck metal with a clear,
delicate note. Mara drew the blade back and pulled the
bread apart with both hands. Buried in the warm center,
where the crumb was still steaming, lay a watch no
larger than a plum.

It was polished, unscratched, and ticking.

Mara looked toward the rear door. It was bolted. The
windows were shuttered, and no one else had entered the
bakery since she had mixed the dough before midnight. She
had shaped every loaf herself.

She picked up the watch.

Its hands pointed to six minutes past three.

At once, the bakery vanished.

Mara stood beside a river beneath a violet evening sky.
Rain silvered the water. On the opposite bank, a boy
in a red coat waved both arms and shouted something
she could not hear. Behind him rose the dark outlines
of flooded houses.

Then the vision ended.

Mara was back in the bakery, clutching the watch. The
loaf lay split on the table.

The clock on the wall said four minutes past five.

She set the pocket watch down.

The ticking stopped.

By seven, people were gathering in the town square. Mara
joined them after hanging a CLOSED sign on the bakery
door.

Bellwether was a small town built around an old stone
clock tower. The tower had stood for nearly three hundred
years, surviving fires, wars, and one particularly determined lightning strike.
Its four great faces could be seen from every road
leading into town.

That morning, all four faces were blank.

The hands, numbers, and minute marks had vanished, leaving circles
of pale glass that reflected the cloudy sky.

Other clocks had disappeared as well. The school clock was
gone. So were the clocks in the railway station, the
mayor's office, the doctor's waiting room, and the church. Wristwatches
had become smooth bands of leather or metal. Alarm clocks
had turned into empty shells.

Only sundials remained, and the day was too overcast for
them to be useful.

The people of Bellwether responded as people often do when
robbed of something invisible but essential: they became angry.

"How am I to know when to open my shop?"
cried Mr. Bess from the butcher's.

"Open when you're awake," someone called.

"I'm awake at two in the morning!"

"How do you know?"

This led to an argument.

Mayor Pell climbed onto the fountain and raised his hands.
He was a narrow man with a beard shaped like
a paintbrush.

"Please remain calm," he said. "This is almost certainly temporary."

"Based on what?" asked Mara.

The mayor looked at her as if questions were a
form of disobedience.

"Based on common sense."

"Clocks don't commonly disappear."

A murmur of agreement passed through the crowd.

Beside the mayor stood Orrin Greve, keeper of the clock
tower. He was older than anyone could accurately remember. His
long gray coat was fastened with brass buttons, and his
eyebrows curled upward like smoke. For fifty years, he had
wound the tower mechanism every Sunday night. Children believed he
slept among the gears.

Orrin stared at Mara.

Not at her face, she realized, but at her apron
pocket.

The silver watch ticked inside it.

His expression tightened.

He stepped down from the fountain and walked away.

Mara followed him.

She caught up near the narrow lane that led to
the tower. "You know what happened."

Orrin kept walking.

"I found a watch," she said.

He stopped.

Around them, townspeople hurried in confused circles. Without clocks, no
one knew whether they were early or late, and the
uncertainty made every task feel urgent.

"Where?" Orrin asked.

"In a loaf."

He closed his eyes. "Of course."

"Is that a normal place to find one?"

"No. But lost hours return through ordinary things. Bread is
as ordinary as anything."

Mara drew out the watch. "What is it?"

Orrin did not touch it. "A memory with a mechanism."

"That means nothing."

"It means you should put it somewhere safe and forget
it."

"I saw a flooded town. There was a boy across
a river."

Orrin's face changed so slightly that another person might not
have noticed. Mara had spent twenty years watching dough rise.
She was good at detecting small transformations.

"You know the boy," she said.

"No."

"You're lying."

"Yes."

With that, he unlocked the tower door and went inside.

Mara followed before he could shut it.

The tower's interior smelled of oil, dust, and rain-soaked stone.
A spiral staircase wound upward around a forest of chains
and iron rods. High above, hidden in shadow, enormous gears
sat motionless.

Orrin lit a lamp.

"The people of Bellwether think this tower measures time," he
said. "It does not. It keeps time where it belongs."

Mara folded her arms. "And where does time belong?"

"In order."

He climbed the stairs. Mara followed.

As they rose, he explained.

Every choice, he said, created a moment that happened and
countless moments that did not. Most vanished harmlessly. But some
were too powerful to disappear. A missed train. An unanswered
letter. A door opened seconds too late. These abandoned moments
became lost hours, though they might last only a minute—or
many years.

Long ago, the clockmakers of Bellwether had built the tower
to collect them. Its mechanism drew in the lost hours
before they could drift into the present and change it.

"And now they're escaping?" Mara asked.

"They're being released."

"By whom?"

"That is what I intend to discover."

They reached the mechanism chamber. Wheels taller than houses filled
the room. Brass teeth interlocked with iron cogs. Pendulums hung
frozen over the floor.

At the center stood an empty pedestal.

Orrin lifted the lamp. Scratches marked the dusty stone around
it.

"Something was taken," Mara said.

"The heart spring. The tower cannot function without it."

"Who has a key?"

"Only I do."

"That seems convenient."

"I am too old for convenience."

He knelt and examined the scratches.

Mara wandered among the gears. Thousands of names had been
carved into the metal. Some were accompanied by dates, others
by a single word.

Forgive.

Stay.

Run.

Wait.

She found her own name on a small copper wheel.

MARA VALE — 17 OCTOBER

No year was given.

"Orrin."

He looked up and saw where she stood.

"What happened on October seventeenth?" she asked.

"Many things, across many years."

"Something involving me."

Before he could answer, a bell rang beneath the floor.

Not the deep bell of the tower. This was a
small, bright chime.

The pocket watch trembled in Mara's hand.

Its lid sprang open.

The hands were moving backward.

The chamber blurred.

Mara stood in her childhood kitchen.

Sunlight streamed through yellow curtains. Her mother, dead for sixteen
years, sat at the table peeling apples. She was younger
than Mara remembered, with dark hair hanging loose over one
shoulder.

Mara's breath caught.

Her younger self—nine years old, thin-kneed and impatient—ran through the
room carrying a paper windmill.

"Slow down," her mother said.

"I'm going to the hill."

"Take your brother."

"I don't want to."

At the table sat a boy of six in a
red coat.

Mara stared at him.

Elias.

She had almost forgotten the coat.

"No," adult Mara whispered.

Her mother looked up sharply, as though she had heard.

The kitchen dissolved.

Rain fell hard. The river had swollen over its banks.
Nine-year-old Mara scrambled up the muddy hill, still gripping the
ruined paper windmill. Elias followed, crying because she had left
him behind.

"Go home!" young Mara shouted.

"I'll tell Mama!"

"You always tell!"

She crossed the wooden footbridge. Elias started after her.

The river struck the bridge supports. Wood cracked.

Mara remembered what came next. She had remembered it every
day of her life.

She ran back and reached for him.

She was too late.

The bridge split between them, and Elias vanished into the
water.

But this time, the memory continued.

Another figure appeared on the far bank: Orrin, decades younger,
wearing the same gray coat. He seized Elias by the
collar and pulled him from the river.

Mara stared.

That had not happened.

It could not have happened.

Young Orrin knelt beside the boy. Elias coughed and opened
his eyes.

Then Orrin looked across the flooded river at young Mara.

With sorrow in his face, he took a silver watch
from his pocket and pressed its crown.

The world went dark.

Mara returned to the tower chamber on her knees.

The watch lay beside her.

"You saved him," she said.

Orrin stood motionless.

"You saved my brother."

"For eleven minutes."

"He was alive."

"For eleven minutes that did not belong to this world."

Mara rose unsteadily. "What did you do?"

"The bridge broke at three minutes past three. I reached
him at nine minutes past. In the true hour, he
drowned before I arrived."

"But I saw—"

"You saw an hour I stole. I turned back the
watch and made another path. For eleven minutes, Elias lived.
Then the lost hour began to spread."

Orrin's voice weakened.

"In that altered time, the river destroyed half the town.
Your mother drowned searching for you. Forty-two others died before
sunset. Time demands consequence. Save one life, and the current
bends around it."

"So you changed it back."

"I had to."

"You killed him."

"I returned what I had stolen."

Mara slapped him.

The sound echoed among the gears.

Orrin did not flinch. "I have wished to do the
same every day since."

She turned away, shaking.

Below them came a tremendous metallic groan. One of the
tower gears moved a fraction, though nothing drove it. From
outside rose shouts and breaking glass.

Mara ran to the window.

Across Bellwether, lost hours were returning.

A steam train appeared in the square, passing soundlessly through
the fountain before vanishing into the courthouse. A house flickered
between ruin and new construction. People appeared in old-fashioned clothes,
walked several steps, and dissolved. Snow fell upward from the
streets.

In the distance, the river glimmered beneath a violet sky.

"The heart spring," Orrin said. "Whoever took it is trying
to restore a particular hour."

"Which one?"

He looked at the silver watch.

"The eleventh minute."

Mara understood.

"Elias?"

A voice behind them said, "Not Elias alone."

Mayor Pell stood at the top of the stairs. In
both hands he held a tightly coiled band of shining
metal.

The heart spring.

"You," Orrin said.

Pell smiled sadly. "You never did change the locks."

The tower shook. Dust fell from the ceiling.

"My wife died six years ago," Pell said. "You remember,
of course. Fever. The doctor reached her house at eight
seventeen. She died at eight twelve."

Orrin moved toward him. "The hours cannot be used to
rescue the dead."

"You used them."

"And learned why no one should."

"A lesson is not a law."

Pell fitted the spring into a slot in the central
mechanism.

The tower came alive.

Gears slammed into motion. Chains snapped taut. The great pendulum
swung, knocking Orrin to the floor. Wind spiraled through the
chamber, though every window was shut.

The blank clock faces filled with darkness.

Pell gripped a brass lever. "The tower holds thousands of
better worlds. Worlds where children live. Where bridges stand. Where
doctors arrive in time. Why should we accept this one
merely because it happened first?"

"Because it happened," Orrin shouted.

Mara looked at the watch.

Its hands had stopped at six minutes past three—the moment
Orrin pulled Elias from the water. Eleven lost minutes remained
inside it, waiting.

Below, the town fractured into possibilities.

The bakery vanished and reappeared with a blue door instead
of a green one. Mr. Bess became an old man,
a child, and an empty outline. The river rose over
the streets, then retreated. Bells rang from churches that had
never been built.

And across the square stood Elias.

He wore his red coat.

He was six years old.

"Mara!" he called.

She ran down the stairs.

Orrin shouted after her, but the tower bent around her
like a reflection in disturbed water. One step became a
hundred. The stone walls turned to rain.

Then she was outside.

Elias waited beside the fountain.

Mara crossed the square slowly, afraid that any sudden movement
would break him. He looked exactly as he had that
morning: wet hair, missing tooth, one bootlace untied.

"You left me," he said.

The words struck harder than the slap she had given
Orrin.

"I know."

"You said to go home."

"I know."

"Why didn't you come back?"

"I tried."

He held out his hand. "Come now."

Behind him, the town had become a bright summer place.
Her mother stood in the bakery doorway. She had flour
on her cheek and laughter in her eyes.

Mara wanted that world with a hunger so great it
seemed to hollow out her bones.

She took one step.

The watch ticked.

Summer sunlight spread across the square. Elias smiled.

Then Mara saw movement behind the bakery windows.

Strangers crowded inside: men, women, and children, their faces pale
with terror. The forty-two people who had died in the
altered flood. More figures stood behind them, hundreds perhaps, each
displaced by another correction. Every rescued hour cast a shadow.

Her mother's smile faded.

"Time to choose," she said.

Mara closed her eyes.

For sixteen years, she had imagined what she would say
if she could see them again. Now every word seemed
too small.

"I'm sorry," she whispered.

Elias squeezed her hand.

"You always say that."

"I don't know what else to say."

"You could say goodbye."

She knelt and wrapped her arms around him. He smelled
of rain, wool, and the honey cakes their mother used
to make.

"Goodbye, Eli."

"Goodbye, Mara."

She pressed the crown of the silver watch.

The glass cracked.

The hands spun forward.

Elias disappeared.

Mara fell onto the cold stones of the square.

The false town collapsed around her. Summer darkened into morning.
The dead vanished. The river returned to its banks. Inside
the tower, gears screamed as the accumulated lost hours rushed
back into the mechanism.

Mara ran upstairs.

She found Orrin holding Mayor Pell away from the lever.
Both men were bleeding. The heart spring shone white-hot in
its cradle.

"Pull it out!" Orrin shouted.

Pell struck him and reached for the lever again.

Mara threw the broken watch into the gears.

For one suspended second, every clock in Bellwether chimed.

The mechanism stopped.

The force flung all three of them to the floor.

Silence followed.

Then came a soft, steady sound.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

The heart spring cooled.

Outside, numbers and hands returned to the tower faces. Watches
reappeared on wrists. The railway clock resumed at seven forty-three.
Alarm clocks rang in empty bedrooms. The church clock announced
eleven, though it was nowhere near that hour.

Mayor Pell sat against the wall with his face in
his hands.

"My wife," he said.

Orrin lowered himself beside him. "I know."

"No," Pell whispered. "You don't."

Orrin looked toward the gears where the silver watch had
vanished.

"Yes," he said. "I do."

The people of Bellwether never agreed on what had happened
that morning. Some remembered a flood. Others recalled snow, a
train in the square, or conversations with relatives long dead.
Mr. Bess insisted he had lived to the age of
ninety-seven and was offended when no one treated him with
the respect due to a much older man.

Mayor Pell resigned. He left town before winter, taking no
clock with him.

Orrin remained keeper for one more year. On the following
October seventeenth, he handed Mara the tower key.

"I'm a baker," she said.

"So was the first keeper."

"I know nothing about clocks."

"That may be an advantage. People who know clocks are
always tempted to fix them."

He showed her how to wind the mechanism, oil the
gears, and listen for lost hours. They sounded, he explained,
like ticking from places where no clock stood.

Before leaving, he gave her a paper parcel.

Inside was a child's red coat, neatly folded and perfectly
dry.

Mara did not ask where it had come from.

Years passed.

She continued to bake bread each morning and climb the
tower each Sunday night. Now and then she found small
lost moments lodged in ordinary things: a wedding hidden in
a teacup, a farewell inside a button, an entire quiet
lifetime folded into a train ticket.

She returned each one to the tower.

She never opened them for long.

When townspeople came to her grieving, begging for one minute,
one word, one final embrace, Mara did not lecture them
about consequence. She made tea. She listened. Sometimes she gave
them bread.

And once, only once, on a rainy October evening, she
carried the red coat to the river.

The old footbridge had long since been replaced by one
of stone. Mara stood at its center and watched the
dark water pass below.

From somewhere across the river came the faint laughter of
a boy.

She held the coat against her chest.

The clock tower began to strike three.

At the sixth chime, the river seemed to pause.

Mara could have called his name. She could have searched
the bank, followed the sound, and perhaps found some remnant
of those stolen eleven minutes waiting among the reeds.

Instead, she let the coat fall.

The current took it gently.

For a moment, the red cloth floated bright upon the
black water. Then it passed beneath the bridge and disappeared
downstream.

The final chime faded.

Time moved on, as it always had—not kindly, not cruelly,
but carrying everything.

Mara watched until the river was only a river again.

Then she went home to set the dough for morning.

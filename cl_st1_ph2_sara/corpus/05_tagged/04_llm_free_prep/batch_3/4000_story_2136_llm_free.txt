### The Museum of Unfinished Things

On the first Tuesday of every month, the Museum of
Unfinished Things opened its doors precisely at midnight.

It stood at the end of Bellweather Street, between a
bakery that had been closed for thirty years and a
narrow house with no windows. During daylight, the museum looked
abandoned. Dust clouded its tall glass doors, weeds pushed through
the steps, and a brass sign above the entrance displayed
only three words:

**NOT YET COMPLETE**

Most people passed without noticing it. Those who did notice
soon forgot.

But at midnight on the first Tuesday, lamps glowed behind
the dusty windows. The weeds drew back from the steps.
The brass letters shone. And Mr. Vale, the museum's only
curator, unlocked the doors.

He was a thin man with silver hair, a black
suit, and a pocket watch that had no hands. He
never advertised the museum, yet visitors always came.

Some arrived carrying half-written letters. Some brought broken machines they
had once intended to repair. Others carried songs without endings,
plans for gardens never planted, wedding dresses never worn, or
apologies they had rehearsed but never spoken.

Mr. Vale accepted almost everything.

He placed each item in a glass case, wrote a
small card, and displayed it in one of the museum's
endless halls.

There was a model ship whose maker had died before
adding the sails. There was a symphony missing its final
note. There was a staircase built by a carpenter who
had never decided where it should lead. There were portraits
with blank faces, clocks without numbers, and a chess game
abandoned one move before checkmate.

Visitors often asked Mr. Vale why he collected unfinished things.

"Because completed things belong to the world," he would say.
"Unfinished things belong to possibility."

Then, one rainy Tuesday night, a girl named Mara arrived
carrying a jar.

She was thirteen years old, wore a yellow coat two
sizes too large, and had walked three miles through the
storm. Water streamed from her dark hair. Mud covered her
boots.

Mr. Vale opened the door before she could knock.

"You're late," he said.

Mara looked past him into the glowing entrance hall. "The
sign says you open at midnight."

"It is twelve-oh-seven."

"My bicycle chain broke."

"Then your bicycle is unfinished?"

"No. It's broken."

"Ah." Mr. Vale stepped aside. "That is a different museum."

Mara entered.

The jar in her arms was sealed with red wax.
It was an ordinary preserving jar, the sort used for
peaches or jam, but something faintly luminous moved inside it.

Mr. Vale noticed at once.

"What have you brought me?"

"A summer," Mara said.

He raised one silver eyebrow.

"My brother's last summer."

The rain tapped softly against the windows. Somewhere deep in
the museum, a bell rang once.

Mr. Vale extended both hands. "May I?"

Mara hesitated, then passed him the jar.

It was warm. Inside, pale gold light swirled like mist.
When Mr. Vale held it near his ear, he heard
distant waves, gulls, and two children laughing.

He looked at Mara carefully. "Where did you get this?"

"I caught it."

"That is not usually possible."

"I used my mother's preserving recipe."

"For summer?"

"For apricots. I changed it."

Mr. Vale turned the jar. Tiny shapes flickered behind the
glass: a red kite, a wooden pier, a blue bucket,
a boy running barefoot along a beach.

The label, written in a child's uneven handwriting, read:

**JULY—BEFORE EVERYTHING CHANGED**

"What was left unfinished?" Mr. Vale asked.

Mara stared at the floor.

"My brother, Eli, wanted to swim to the black rock.
It was far from shore, and our mother said he
was too young. He promised we would do it together
before the summer ended."

"But you did not."

"He got sick."

Mr. Vale waited.

"He died in September."

The golden light in the jar dimmed.

Mara quickly took it back and held it against her
chest.

"I don't want you to keep it forever," she said.
"I only want you to put it somewhere safe."

"Safe from what?"

"From me."

Mr. Vale's expression changed, though only slightly.

"Explain."

"Sometimes I open it."

"You open the jar?"

"Only a little. Just enough to smell the sea."

"That is unwise."

"I know."

"No," said Mr. Vale. "I do not think you do."

He turned and walked toward the nearest gallery. With no
better choice, Mara followed.

They passed a collection of unwritten novels, their blank pages
fluttering behind glass. They crossed a room filled with inventions
that almost worked. A pair of mechanical wings beat slowly
near the ceiling, lifting no one. In another hall, hundreds
of locked doors stood without walls.

At last, Mr. Vale stopped before an empty case.

"Unfinished memories are dangerous," he said. "They do not remain
unchanged. Each time you visit them, they borrow something from
the present."

Mara frowned. "Memories can't do that."

"Most cannot. Yours has been preserved."

"I only go back for a minute."

"And when you return?"

She did not answer.

Mr. Vale leaned closer. "What have you forgotten?"

"Nothing."

"Your teacher's name?"

Mara opened her mouth.

No answer came.

"The color of your bedroom?"

She looked down, frightened.

"Your mother's voice?"

"I know her voice."

"Then describe it."

Mara gripped the jar until her fingers whitened.

In her mind, her mother's voice should have been easy
to find. It had called her downstairs for breakfast that
morning. It had reminded her to take an umbrella. Yet
when Mara searched for its sound, she found only the
cry of gulls and Eli shouting from the beach.

"That's why I brought it," she whispered.

Mr. Vale unlocked the glass case.

"You may leave it here."

"For how long?"

"As long as you need."

"Will I be able to visit?"

"On the first Tuesday."

"Can I open it?"

"No."

Mara stepped toward the case, but before she could place
the jar inside, something knocked from within it.

Once.

Twice.

Then a boy's voice, faint but clear, called, "Mara?"

She froze.

Mr. Vale's face became grave. "Do not answer."

"Mara!" the voice called again. "Come on! The water's warm!"

Her eyes filled with tears.

"That's Eli."

"It is an echo."

"He sounds real."

"Echoes often do."

Inside the jar, the summer brightened. A tiny shoreline unfolded
beneath the glass. Eli stood ankle-deep in glittering water, waving.

Beyond him, black against the sunset, rose a lonely rock.

Mara put her thumb against the wax seal.

Mr. Vale caught her wrist.

"If you open it now, the memory will take more
than a teacher's name."

"I have to go."

"You have already gone many times."

"We never reached the rock."

"And you never will."

Anger flashed across Mara's face. "You collect unfinished things. Why
won't you let me finish this?"

"Because finishing the past destroys the present."

"You don't know that."

"I know it better than anyone."

For the first time, Mr. Vale looked old. Not elderly,
but ancient—as though the years had been waiting behind his
eyes and had suddenly stepped forward.

He released her wrist.

"Come," he said.

He led Mara beyond the public galleries, through a narrow
door marked **CURATOR ONLY**.

Behind it lay a staircase descending into darkness.

They went down for a long time. The air grew
cold. The sounds of the museum faded until Mara could
hear only their footsteps and the soft murmur of the
sea inside her jar.

At the bottom stood a circular room.

Unlike the galleries above, it contained a single object: a
small white door in a silver frame.

There was no wall around it.

The door was slightly open, and warm daylight shone through
the gap.

Mr. Vale stopped several feet away.

"This is mine," he said.

"Your unfinished thing?"

"Yes."

"What's behind it?"

"My home."

Mara looked at him. "You live in the museum."

"Now I do."

He drew the handless watch from his pocket.

"Long ago, I was a clockmaker. I had a wife
named Adeline and a little boy named Simon. One winter
morning, I left home after an argument. It was a
foolish argument. I cannot remember what it was about, only
that I wanted to win it."

He rubbed the watch's smooth face.

"I went to my workshop. That afternoon, a fire swept
through our street."

Mara looked toward the white door.

"This is the last moment before I left," Mr. Vale
said. "Adeline is in the kitchen. Simon is building a
castle beneath the table. If I step through, I can
stay. I can apologize. I can warn them."

"Why don't you?"

"I tried."

The daylight beyond the door flickered.

"I returned to that morning hundreds of times. In one
version, I stopped the fire. In another, I sent my
family away. In another, I caught the man who knocked
over the lamp that started it. Each time I changed
the memory, it consumed part of my life."

"What parts?"

"My childhood. My friends. The first clock I ever made.
My mother's face. Eventually, I forgot the sound of Adeline's
laughter—the very thing I had been trying to save."

He closed his fingers around the watch.

"The past does not permit repairs. It accepts only exchanges."

Mara looked at the jar. Eli still waited in the
golden water, calling her name.

"If you knew it was dangerous, why did you keep
the door?"

"Because knowing is not the same as letting go."

A gust of salty air swept through the underground room.

The red wax on Mara's jar cracked.

Mr. Vale spun around.

A thin line of gold appeared beneath the lid.

Eli's voice filled the chamber.

"Mara! Hurry!"

The jar had been opened too many times. The memory
no longer needed her permission.

The lid burst free.

Summer poured out.

Sunlight struck the stone floor. Sand spread beneath Mara's boots.
The circular room vanished under a bright blue sky, and
suddenly she was standing on the beach exactly as it
had been three years before.

Mr. Vale was gone.

So was the museum.

Mara looked down. Her boots had become bare feet. Her
yellow coat had disappeared. She wore her old green swimsuit,
the one with the crooked strap her mother had promised
to mend.

Eli stood beside her.

He was ten again, brown-haired and sunburned, grinning as he
held out his hand.

"You took forever," he said.

Mara could not breathe.

She touched his face.

Warm.

Real.

He laughed and pulled away. "What are you doing?"

She threw her arms around him.

Eli made an embarrassed sound. "Mara, stop."

But after a moment, he hugged her back.

She remembered this day. Their mother was reading beneath a
striped umbrella. A radio played near the lifeguard station. The
air smelled of coconut lotion and hot sand.

Far out in the water, the black rock waited.

"Ready?" Eli asked.

Mara looked toward the umbrella.

Their mother raised a hand and called something. The wind
carried her words away.

Mara tried to remember her voice, but the memory had
already begun to take it.

"Eli," she said, "we shouldn't."

"You promised."

"I know."

"We can make it."

Mara looked at him—at the water shining on his shoulders,
at the tiny scar above his eyebrow, at his impatient
smile.

She had wanted this every day since he died. One
more hour. One more conversation. A chance to keep the
promise.

Eli ran into the sea.

Mara followed.

The water rose around their knees, their waists, their shoulders.
Then they began to swim.

At first, it was easy. The sea held them gently.
Eli raced ahead, laughing whenever Mara splashed him.

But the black rock did not seem to come closer.

After several minutes, Mara looked back.

The beach was fading.

The striped umbrella had lost its colors. The lifeguard station
blurred. Their mother stood at the edge of the water,
but her face was blank.

Mara stopped swimming.

Every stroke toward the rock carried her deeper into the
memory. Every second erased something waiting for her outside it.

She tried to think of home.

She could not remember the color of the front door.

She tried to picture her best friend.

A name appeared—Nina—then dissolved like foam.

Ahead, Eli reached the black rock and climbed onto it.

"We did it!" he shouted.

Mara floated between him and the disappearing shore.

"Come on!" he called.

Her arms felt heavy. Beneath the clear water, she saw
things sinking into darkness: her schoolbooks, her bicycle, her mother's
favorite cup, a birthday cake with fourteen candles waiting to
be lit next spring.

Her future.

"Mara!"

She swam toward the rock.

One stroke.

Two.

Eli leaned down, reaching for her.

If she took his hand, she knew she could stay.
They could sit on the sun-warmed stone forever. The afternoon
would never end. September would never come.

"Mara," he said softly.

She reached up.

Their fingers almost touched.

Then she saw his expression.

It was not the impatient grin of the preserved memory.
It was sad and knowing.

"You're not Eli," she said.

The boy tilted his head.

"You're what I remember."

"I'm enough," he replied.

For one terrible moment, she wanted that to be true.

Then, from across the water, she heard a voice.

Not clear. Not yet familiar. But desperate.

Her mother was calling her home.

Mara turned away from the rock.

The boy grabbed her wrist.

"You promised!"

She pulled free.

"I know," she sobbed. "But I can't keep it."

The sea darkened.

"You'll lose me."

"I already lost you."

The words broke something.

The perfect summer cracked from horizon to horizon.

Mara swam.

Behind her, Eli called again, but his voice became the
cry of a gull. Waves rose around her. The shore
seemed impossibly distant.

As she fought toward it, she gathered fragments of her
present from the water.

Her bicycle had a silver bell.

Her bedroom walls were green.

Her teacher was Mrs. Alden.

Nina laughed with a snort and hated oranges.

Her mother sang while washing dishes, always too quietly to
hear the words.

Each recovered detail made the beach more solid.

At last, Mara's feet struck sand.

Her mother stood before her, still faceless, arms outstretched.

Mara ran toward her.

The summer shattered.

She fell onto the cold floor of the museum's underground
room.

Glass rained around her, dissolving before it landed.

Mr. Vale knelt nearby, one hand braced against the silver
frame of his white door. It had blown wide open.

Beyond it, a woman stood in a bright kitchen. A
little boy crouched under the table, placing wooden blocks on
top of one another.

"Papa!" the boy cried when he saw Mr. Vale.

The curator stared.

Mara understood. When her jar had broken, it had opened
his memory too.

The woman turned. "Are you leaving without saying goodbye?"

Mr. Vale rose unsteadily.

His hand found the handless watch.

He looked at Mara. Then he looked at his family.

The longing on his face was so deep that Mara
had to turn away.

"Papa?" Simon called.

Mr. Vale stepped toward the door.

He reached for the handle.

Then he gently pulled it closed.

The daylight narrowed to a golden thread and disappeared.

In the silence, the handless pocket watch began to tick.

Mr. Vale looked down in surprise.

Two black hands had appeared on its face.

The time was twelve forty-three.

For the first time in many years, it moved forward.

Mara sat up.

"My jar," she said.

"Gone."

"Is the summer gone too?"

"No." Mr. Vale touched his chest. "Only unsealed."

She searched her memory.

She could still see Eli standing on the black rock,
but the image was softer now. Imperfect. He was missing
a front tooth, though she had forgotten that before. His
hair stuck up at the back. He was not glowing.
He was not an echo calling her to stay.

He was simply her brother.

Mara began to cry.

Mr. Vale sat beside her and waited.

After a while, he offered her a clean white handkerchief.

"Will I forget him?" she asked.

"Some details."

"I don't want to."

"You will remember others."

"What if I forget his voice?"

"You may."

"That's horrible."

"Yes."

She looked at him sharply, expecting comfort.

Mr. Vale gave none.

Then he added, "But forgetting the exact sound of a
voice is not the same as forgetting what it said."

Mara wiped her eyes.

Above them, bells began to ring throughout the museum.

One after another, glass cases opened.

The unfinished symphony played its final note.

The mechanical wings folded and became still.

Behind the doors without walls, locks clicked. Half-written letters filled
with ink, though whether they became apologies, confessions, or farewells,
no one could say.

"What's happening?" Mara asked.

Mr. Vale listened.

"The museum is closing."

"Until next month?"

"No. I think it has been waiting for me."

"For what?"

"To finish."

They climbed the stairs.

In every gallery, objects were disappearing. Not destroyed, but released.
The model ship grew sails and vanished into a painted
sea. The abandoned chess player moved her queen. Blank faces
appeared in portraits, smiling uncertainly.

At the entrance, the brass sign fell from above the
doors.

Mr. Vale picked it up.

**NOT YET COMPLETE**, it still read.

He turned it over.

On the other side were two different words:

**BEGIN AGAIN**

He gave the sign to Mara.

"Can a museum do that?" she asked.

"A museum can do anything once no one is curating
it."

Outside, the rain had stopped. Dawn touched the rooftops.

Mara's mother stood beneath a streetlamp, still wearing her nightdress
under a hastily buttoned coat. Her hair was wet, her
face pale with fear.

"Mara!"

This time, Mara knew the voice.

She ran down the steps.

Her mother caught her so tightly it hurt. She demanded
to know where Mara had been, why her bed was
empty, why she had taken the jar, what she had
been thinking.

Mara answered none of the questions.

She only listened.

Her mother's voice was lower than she had remembered. It
cracked when she was frightened. Certain words carried the faint
accent of a town she had left as a child.

It was not the voice Mara had tried to preserve.

It was better.

Over her mother's shoulder, Mara looked back.

Mr. Vale stood in the museum doorway, his silver hair
bright in the rising sun.

"What will you do now?" she called.

He considered the question.

"I was once a clockmaker."

"Do you remember how?"

"No."

Mara smiled. "Then you can learn."

Mr. Vale smiled too.

He closed the museum doors.

By the time Mara and her mother reached the corner,
the building was gone. Between the abandoned bakery and the
windowless house stood an empty lot filled with tall grass.

Years later, Mara sometimes wondered whether the museum had existed
at all.

She remembered enough to believe it had.

She also remembered enough to doubt.

She grew older. She finished school, though not every assignment.
She studied marine biology, abandoned it, and became a carpenter
instead. She built tables, cabinets, boats, and once a spiral
staircase for a house overlooking the sea.

She made mistakes. She left projects incomplete. She forgot birthdays
and lost photographs. She argued with her mother and sometimes
apologized too late.

But every July, Mara swam to the black rock.

The first time, she went alone.

The water was colder than it had been in the
jar. The distance was greater. Halfway there, she nearly turned
back.

When she finally climbed onto the rock, she found it
rough, gray, and covered with sharp barnacles. It was nothing
like the smooth black island from her memory.

Mara sat there until sunset.

She did not see Eli.

She did not hear him call.

But when the wind crossed the water, she remembered something
he had once said—not on the beach, but in the
hospital, near the end.

She had forgotten the words for years.

"You don't have to finish for both of us," he
had whispered. "Just keep going."

Mara looked toward the shore, where lights had begun to
shine in the windows of the town.

Then she slipped back into the sea and swam home.

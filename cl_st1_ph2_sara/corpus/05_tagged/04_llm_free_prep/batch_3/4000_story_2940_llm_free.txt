Mara Venn first heard the lighthouse speak on the night
the sea returned her father's watch.

She was alone in the lantern room, polishing soot from
the great brass housing, when a voice rose through the
iron floor.

"Mara."

Her cloth stopped against the glass.

Outside, the storm had erased the horizon. Rain struck the
windows in hard silver handfuls, and the beam turned steadily
through the darkness, illuminating nothing but waves and rain. The
lighthouse trembled under the wind.

"Mara," the voice said again.

She knew it at once, though twelve years had passed
since she had last heard it.

Her father had possessed a low, patient voice, made rough
by salt and tobacco. He had spoken as if every
sentence were a rope he meant to place securely in
another person's hands.

Mara set down the cloth.

"No," she whispered.

The voice came not from the stairwell but from deeper
inside the tower—from the walls, the pipes, the old granite
foundation.

"Third step beneath the tide."

Then it was gone.

Mara stood motionless until the lamp mechanism gave a small
warning click. Habit pulled her back to work. She checked
the oil line, adjusted the wick, and wound the clockwork
that turned the lens. Whatever ghosts had entered the lighthouse,
ships still depended on its light.

At dawn, the storm withdrew eastward, leaving the sky bruised
purple and yellow. Mara went down to the shore.

The lighthouse stood on Blackglass Island, a mile from the
mainland, surrounded by reefs that had broken more ships than
anyone cared to count. At low tide, stone steps emerged
below the western cliff. They had been carved generations ago
by monks who once kept signal fires on the island.
Most ended above the water. The lowest steps descended into
a pool where green weed swayed over black rock.

Third step beneath the tide.

Mara removed her boots and rolled up her trousers. The
pool was winter-cold. On the third submerged step, her toes
struck metal.

She reached down.

The watch lay wedged in a crack, its silver case
dark with tarnish. On the back were the initials E.V.,
scratched there by her father with the tip of a
fishing knife.

Elias Venn.

Mara nearly dropped it.

The watch had vanished with him.

Twelve years earlier, Elias had rowed from Blackglass Island during
a fog, intending to cross to the mainland for medicine.
Mara's mother had been sick with fever. He never arrived.
Searchers found one oar and a strip of blue-painted wood
from his boat. The sea returned nothing else.

Until now.

Mara climbed the steps and sat beneath the cliff. With
shaking fingers, she pressed the catch. The watch opened.

Inside, under cracked glass, the hands stood at seventeen minutes
past three.

On the inner lid, where no writing had been before,
someone had etched five words:

THE LIGHT MUST NOT GO OUT.

Mara looked up at the tower.

Its white walls shone in the wet morning. Gulls circled
the lantern. It seemed ordinary, stubborn, and silent.

By noon, the supply boat arrived from the mainland. Jonah
Vale jumped ashore carrying a canvas mail sack and a
loaf of bread tucked beneath his coat. He was tall
and narrow, with hair the color of rusted iron. He
had been delivering supplies to Blackglass for six years and
proposing marriage to Mara for three.

"Sea's ugly," he said, tying the boat. "Another storm coming
tomorrow."

"Worse than last night?"

"Much worse."

He smiled, expecting one of her dry replies. When none
came, his expression changed.

"What happened?"

Mara showed him the watch.

Jonah turned it over in his palm. He had known
Elias, though only as a boy knows a distant, impressive
man.

"Where did you find this?"

She told him.

He glanced toward the lighthouse.

"You heard your father's voice?"

"Yes."

"In the tower?"

"Yes."

"And you went where it told you?"

"I found the watch."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

Jonah rubbed his thumb over the inscription inside the case.
"Could someone have planted it?"

"Underwater?"

"Could've been pushed into the crack during the storm."

"After engraving a warning inside?"

"People do strange things."

"Not as strange as dead men."

He gave her a worried look. "Come back to town
with me."

"I can't."

"The weather office sent a warning." He pulled a folded
telegram from the mail sack. "Pressure's falling faster than they've
seen in twenty years. They're telling everyone along the coast
to shelter inland."

"The light has to be kept."

"The lighthouse has survived hundreds of storms."

"Because someone kept it."

Jonah looked past her at the tower. "There are no
ships due."

"There are always ships."

"Mara—"

"The light must not go out."

He heard something in her voice and stopped arguing.

"All right," he said. "Then I'm staying."

"You're not."

"I am."

"Your boat won't survive at the pier if the storm
turns."

"I'll haul it above the eastern cove."

"You have a mother on the mainland."

"And she has four daughters, two sons-in-law, and a terrier
that hates me. She'll manage."

Mara folded her arms.

Jonah smiled faintly. "You can order me off the island,
Keeper Venn. But you'll have to do it before I
move the boat."

He stayed.

By evening, the western sky had become a wall of
cloud. They carried fuel cans from the storage shed, covered
the lantern room windows with storm mesh, and checked every
shutter and hinge. The lighthouse had been electrified years ago,
but Mara trusted electricity only slightly more than she trusted
weather. In the lower storeroom stood the old oil lamp,
cleaned, filled, and ready.

After supper, Jonah placed Elias's watch on the table between
them.

"Do you really believe it was him?"

"I believe I heard him."

"That's different."

"Yes."

The kitchen clock ticked. The old tower breathed around them,
its stones clicking softly as the temperature fell.

Jonah leaned forward. "What happened the night he disappeared?"

"You know what happened."

"I know the story everyone tells."

Mara stared at the flame beneath the kettle.

"My mother was burning with fever," she said. "The radio
had failed. Father said he would row across and bring
the doctor. I asked him not to go. He told
me the fog was nothing. He said he knew every
current between here and the harbor."

"You were fourteen."

"I told him I hated him."

Jonah said nothing.

"I wanted him to stay, and he wouldn't. So I
said I hoped the sea took him." Mara closed the
watch and pushed it away. "Then it did."

"You were a child."

"The sea doesn't care."

"Your father knew you didn't mean it."

"I never had the chance to tell him."

The first gust struck the lighthouse like a giant hand.

Cups rattled. Somewhere above, metal groaned.

Jonah looked toward the ceiling. "Seems the weather wants a
vote."

By midnight, the storm had swallowed the island.

Wind screamed around the lantern room. Waves burst against the
cliffs and rose as white spray above the windows. The
electric light continued its steady rotation, sweeping over chaos.

Mara watched the gauges while Jonah sat on the iron
steps, fighting sleep.

At twelve forty, the voltage needle trembled.

At twelve forty-three, it dropped.

The lamp flickered.

Both of them stood.

"Generator," Mara said.

They ran down the spiral stairs. In the engine room,
the backup generator coughed but did not engage. Mara opened
the fuel valve, checked the intake, and tried again. The
machine shook violently, gave a burst of black smoke, and
died.

"Water in the line," she said.

Another gust hammered the tower. The electric lights went dark.

Above them, the great lens slowed.

For the first time in Mara's life, Blackglass Light went
out.

The darkness felt alive.

"Oil lamp," she said.

They carried it up together, Jonah ahead and Mara below.
The lamp was heavy, with a copper reservoir and thick
glass chimney. The wind made the tower sway just enough
to set the hanging chains tapping against the walls.

Halfway up, a sound rose from below.

"Mara."

Jonah froze.

This time, he heard it too.

The voice filled the stairwell with a low vibration.

"Below the foundation."

Jonah looked down into darkness.

"Tell me that was a pipe."

"It wasn't."

"Then your father has terrible timing."

They continued upward. In the lantern room, Mara mounted the
oil lamp inside the lens. Jonah lit the wick. A
warm yellow flame grew behind the glass, and the rotating
beam returned—dimmer than the electric arc but steady.

Almost immediately, a bell began ringing below.

Not the telephone. Not the engine alarm.

The fog bell.

Its rope had been disconnected thirty years ago.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Then silence.

"Below the foundation," Jonah said. "What does that mean?"

"There's nothing below the foundation."

"Are you sure?"

Mara thought of the old plans stored in the keeper's
office. Her grandfather had shown them to her when she
was small: cross-sections, measurements, drainage channels. The tower's foundations descended
twenty feet into the rock.

But older drawings existed.

They hurried down to the office. Mara unlocked the plan
chest and pulled out rolls of linen paper. The newest
plans showed the lighthouse as she knew it. The oldest,
dated 1811, showed something else.

Beneath the tower was a chamber.

A narrow shaft led to it from the western side
of the foundation. Written beside the chamber in faded ink
were the words: Old fire room. Seal upon completion.

"There," Jonah said.

Mara followed the drawn passage with her finger. "The entrance
should be behind the fuel racks."

They found the outline of a door beneath layers of
whitewash. Together, they dragged away the racks. There was no
handle, only a shallow depression in the stone. Mara pressed
it.

Nothing happened.

Jonah pushed his shoulder against the wall. A section shifted
inward with a grinding sound, releasing a breath of cold,
foul air.

Beyond lay steps.

They took lanterns and descended.

The passage was older than the lighthouse. Its walls were
rough black stone, wet with condensation. The storm sounded distant
there, like boulders rolling across the world.

At the bottom, they entered a round chamber.

In its center stood an iron brazier large enough to
hold a bonfire. Around it, symbols had been carved into
the floor: circles, waves, and lines radiating outward like beams
of light. Rusted mirrors lined the walls.

On the far side was a wooden table.

A blue-painted box rested upon it.

Mara stopped breathing.

The box bore the name of her father's boat: Mercy
Bell.

She crossed the chamber and opened it.

Inside lay a coil of rope, a tobacco tin, a
water-stained notebook, and one blue wool glove. Beneath them was
a brass hand lamp.

Jonah picked up the notebook. Its pages were swollen, but
the writing remained legible.

The first entries were ordinary: tides, repairs, supply lists. Then,
near the middle, Elias's hand became hurried.

October 17. Fog arrived before sunset. Heard bell below the
tower though rope is cut.

October 18. Found old chamber. Records hidden in wall. Monks
believed light did not merely warn ships from reef. It
held something beneath reef asleep.

October 19. Mara laughed when I told her. I'm glad.
Better she thinks me foolish.

Mara turned the page.

October 21. Anne worse. Must fetch doctor. Fog too thick.
Voice from tower says I will not reach mainland by
boat. Says there is another way through old fire.

October 21, later. If bargain is true, Anne lives. Mara
lives. Light continues. Cost is mine.

Mara's eyes blurred.

The final lines had been written unevenly.

To whoever keeps Blackglass after me: The thing beneath the
reef feeds on darkness and calls in the voices of
the drowned. The old fire binds it. The lantern above
is only half the seal. If both flames fail, the
reef will open.

I have given my years to feed the lower fire.
It may burn twelve. Perhaps thirteen. When it fades, another
flame must be carried down.

Mara looked at the cold brazier.

"Twelve years," Jonah said.

The floor lurched.

A crack split one of the rusted mirrors from top
to bottom.

From beyond the chamber walls came a vast, slow scraping.

Then voices.

Dozens at first, whispering through the stone. Then hundreds. Men,
women, children—pleading, weeping, calling names.

One voice rose above them.

"Mara."

Her father stood in the passage.

He looked exactly as he had the night he vanished:
broad-shouldered, rain in his beard, blue coat unbuttoned. His face
was pale, but his eyes were kind.

"My girl," he said.

Mara's lantern shook in her hand.

Jonah whispered, "Don't go near him."

Elias smiled. "You found my watch."

Mara could not speak.

"You have to light the fire," he said. "Quickly."

"With what?"

"The lamp."

The brass hand lamp in the box contained no oil,
yet when Mara lifted it, a blue flame appeared behind
its small glass pane.

The voices in the walls shrieked.

The figure of Elias staggered as though struck.

"Hurry," he said.

Mara approached the brazier.

Words appeared around its rim, glowing faintly red.

A LIFE FOR LIGHT. A LIGHT FOR LIFE.

Jonah read them. "No."

Mara opened the lamp.

The blue flame leaned toward the brazier.

Understanding came over her with terrible calm. Her father had
not drowned. He had entered this chamber and given himself
to the fire, buying twelve more years for the island,
for her mother, perhaps for every ship that crossed the
reef.

"Mara," Jonah said, "wait."

The stone floor cracked. Black water seeped upward, carrying the
smell of deep ocean trenches. In the fractured mirrors, something
enormous moved where their reflections should have been.

Elias stood beside the brazier now.

"It doesn't hurt long," he said.

Mara looked at him. "Was that really what you told
yourself?"

His smile vanished.

Jonah seized her wrist. "That isn't your father."

The thing wearing Elias's face turned toward him.

"You should not have come," it said.

The voice was wrong now—too deep, crowded with other voices.

Mara pulled away from the brazier.

The apparition flickered.

From inside the blue-painted box came a faint ticking.

Elias's watch, still in Mara's pocket, answered it.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

She drew out the watch. Its hands had begun to
move.

The apparition stared at it with sudden hatred.

Mara opened the case. Beneath the warning on the lid,
another line appeared, revealed by the blue flame:

NO MORE LIVES. TURN THE LIGHT BELOW.

She looked at the mirrors.

Not mirrors, she realized.

Reflectors.

The old monks had not fed people to the fire.
The demand carved on the brazier was part of the
trap—the creature's lie, made sacred by fear. Her father had
discovered only part of the truth. Or perhaps, at the
end, he had discovered all of it and sent back
the watch.

"Jonah, help me turn them."

They ran to the walls. Each reflector was mounted on
a rusted pivot. The first resisted until Jonah kicked the
base loose. It swung inward, facing the brazier.

The apparition screamed.

The sound shattered its human shape. For an instant, Mara
saw a tower of black water full of pale mouths.

They turned the second reflector, then the third.

Water poured through the floor cracks. The chamber filled to
their ankles.

Above, the lighthouse beam continued to rotate. With every sweep,
a shaft of yellow light descended through a narrow glass
column hidden in the ceiling. It struck the brazier, then
vanished uselessly into the chamber.

"Now the lamp!" Mara shouted.

Jonah took the brass lamp and thrust its blue flame
into the brazier.

Fire erupted without fuel.

It rose white and gold, meeting the beam from above.
The nearest reflector caught the light and threw it to
the next. One by one, the mirrors awakened, passing the
brilliance around the chamber until a perfect circle burned beneath
the tower.

The black water recoiled.

The creature surged from the walls, filling the chamber with
storm and teeth. Mara saw faces inside it—the lost of
Blackglass Reef, trapped in its darkness. Among them was her
father.

Not the false figure.

The real Elias looked older, exhausted, and peaceful.

He met Mara's eyes.

She had only a moment.

"I'm sorry," she said.

He shook his head.

Then he placed one hand over his heart, as he
always had when promising something.

The circle of light closed.

A thunderclap rose from beneath the island. The creature folded
inward, dragged through the cracks in the floor. The trapped
faces dissolved into sparks. Elias was the last to disappear.

His voice remained after his face was gone.

"You kept it."

The chamber fell silent.

The water drained away. The brazier's fire settled into a
steady blue-white flame. In its light, the carved words changed.

No longer A LIFE FOR LIGHT.

Now they read:

LIGHT FREELY GIVEN OUTLASTS THE DARK.

By dawn, the storm had passed.

Mara and Jonah climbed from the chamber and repaired the
generator. When the electric lamp resumed, its beam seemed brighter
than before. Far out at sea, a cargo ship sounded
its horn in thanks.

Later, on the western steps, Mara opened her father's watch.

The hands showed the correct time.

Jonah sat beside her, his coat torn and his face
gray with exhaustion.

"So," he said, "does this make me an assistant lighthouse
keeper?"

"No."

"Heroic rescuer?"

"No."

"Man who sensibly stayed on an island during the worst
storm in twenty years?"

She looked at him. "Certainly not."

He nodded. "I was afraid of that."

Mara slipped her hand into his.

Below them, the tide withdrew from the third step.

The sea had taken much from Blackglass Island. It had
taken boats, names, years, and all the words people waited
too long to say.

But that morning, as the lighthouse beam turned above her
and the old fire burned below, Mara understood that not
everything lost was gone forever.

Some things became warnings.

Some became promises.

And some, if carried faithfully through the dark, became light.

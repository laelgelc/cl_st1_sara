The Clockmaker of Bellweather Street

Every morning at seven, Elias Venn unlocked the green door
of his shop and wound the clocks.

There were one hundred and six of them, though no
two visitors ever agreed on the number. Some stood in
walnut cases as tall as coffins. Others hung from brass
hooks, rested beneath glass domes, or crouched on shelves between
jars of screws and cloudy bottles of oil. At seven
fifteen, when Elias finished his rounds, the shop became a
forest of ticking.

Bellweather Street woke around him. Bakers lifted shutters. Horses dragged
milk carts over the wet stones. Children hurried toward school
with scarves streaming behind them. Above the roofs, the city
bells announced the quarter hour, always a little later than
the clocks in Elias's shop.

"Public clocks," Elias liked to say, "have committees. Mine have
springs."

For forty-two years, he had repaired watches, carriage clocks, kitchen
clocks, music boxes, mechanical birds, and once a silver crab
whose claws opened every midnight to reveal a portrait of
an Italian countess. He had corrected minutes lost at sea
and hours gained in tropical heat. He had restored a
pocket watch flattened beneath a carriage wheel and made it
tick again, though it was never entirely round afterward.

People trusted Elias with time.

They brought it to him in velvet cases, wrapped in
handkerchiefs, or loose in their pockets among coins and crumbs.
They apologized when it had stopped. They smiled when he
returned it.

Yet there was one clock in the shop that Elias
never repaired.

It hung alone on the wall behind his workbench. Its
face was plain white, its hands black, its case made
of dark cherrywood. It had stopped at eleven minutes past
four.

Visitors sometimes pointed to it.

"That one's broken," they said.

"No," Elias replied. "That one is finished."

Most customers assumed this was a clockmaker's joke. Only Mara,
the flower seller across the street, understood that it was
not.

The clock had belonged to Elias's wife, Anwen. It had
stopped on the afternoon she died.

That was fourteen years ago.

On a foggy Tuesday in November, a girl entered the
shop carrying a blue suitcase.

The bell above the door gave a tired jingle. Elias
looked up from a pocket watch and saw a narrow
face beneath a red wool cap. The girl was perhaps
twelve, though her expression belonged to someone much older. Rain
glistened on her coat. Her boots were muddy.

"Are you the clockmaker?" she asked.

"I am when business is good."

"Is it good?"

Elias examined the empty shop.

"It may improve."

The girl placed the suitcase on his counter. It was
small, battered, and fastened with two brass clasps.

"I need you to fix something."

"Open it, then."

She hesitated.

"Before I do, you must promise not to throw me
out."

Elias set down his screwdriver. "I have never thrown anyone
out."

"Have you wanted to?"

"Frequently."

"That isn't the same."

"No."

The girl opened the suitcase.

Inside lay a clock unlike any Elias had seen. It
had no face, no hands, and no numbers. Its mechanism
was enclosed in a cage of blackened silver, through which
hundreds of tiny gears could be seen. Some were no
larger than grains of sand. A key protruded from one
side, shaped like a feather. At the center of the
mechanism was a glass sphere filled with pale blue light.

Elias leaned closer.

The light inside the sphere flickered. For a moment, he
heard the distant crash of waves.

Then the shop was quiet again.

"Where did you find this?" he asked.

"It was my father's."

"And where did he find it?"

"He never told me."

Elias reached toward the mechanism but stopped before touching it.
Every clock had a rhythm, even when broken. A skilled
repairer could feel it through the case: the faint pressure
of a spring, the reluctance of a bent tooth, the
ghost of motion waiting to begin.

This clock felt cold enough to burn.

"What is it supposed to do?"

The girl watched him carefully.

"It gives back lost time."

Outside, a cart rattled past. Somewhere in the shop, a
cuckoo emerged, announced ten o'clock twice, and retreated in embarrassment.

Elias closed the suitcase.

"No," he said.

"You promised not to throw me out."

"I am not throwing you out. I am refusing the
work."

"You haven't even looked at it."

"I have looked enough."

The girl's hands tightened around the edge of the counter.
"My father said you were the only person who might
understand it."

"Who is your father?"

"Jonah Vale."

Elias went still.

He had not heard that name in thirty years.

Jonah had been his apprentice when they were both young
enough to believe skill excused arrogance. He was brilliant, impatient,
and fascinated by clocks that did more than count ordinary
hours. He collected stories of machines built to predict deaths,
preserve dreams, or trap a single perfect afternoon. Elias had
dismissed those stories. Jonah had chased them.

Then, one winter, he disappeared.

"Your father is alive?" Elias asked.

"He was six months ago."

"And now?"

"I don't know."

The girl reopened the suitcase.

"He left this with me. He said that if he
didn't return by the first frost, I should bring it
to Bellweather Street. He said you would refuse to help
because you were sensible, and then you would help because
you were lonely."

Elias glanced at the clock behind his workbench.

The girl followed his gaze.

"Was that hers?" she asked.

"You may go now."

"He said that too."

Elias stood. "What is your name?"

"Linnet."

"Well, Linnet Vale, your father was always too certain of
other people and never certain enough of himself."

"Will you fix the clock?"

"No."

She closed the suitcase, lifted it, and walked to the
door. Her shoulders were rigid, but Elias saw her wipe
one cheek with her sleeve.

"Wait," he said.

She turned.

"You're dripping on the floor."

It was not an apology, and it was not agreement.
Nevertheless, Linnet took off her coat.

By noon, Elias had cleared the large table at the
back of the shop. He locked the front door, drew
the curtains, and set the strange clock beneath the brightest
lamp.

Linnet watched as he examined it.

"What do you know about the mechanism?" he asked.

"My father called it a horologium."

"That only means timepiece."

"He liked names that explained nothing."

"Yes. He did."

Elias discovered a maker's mark beneath the base: a crescent
crossed by a needle. He did not recognize it. The
outer gears appeared to be silver, but they were harder
than steel. Several turned freely, while others moved only when
he was not looking directly at them.

He removed a thin plate from the underside.

Beneath it, engraved in cramped letters, were seven words:

WHAT IS LOST MUST FIRST BE GIVEN.

Linnet read over his shoulder.

"What does that mean?"

"Something inconvenient."

"Can you repair it?"

"It may not be broken."

He touched the feather-shaped key.

The blue light brightened, and the shop vanished.

For an instant Elias stood on a summer hillside beneath
a yellow sky. A woman ran ahead of him through
tall grass, laughing. Her black hair had come loose, and
she held her shoes in one hand.

Anwen turned and shouted, "You'll miss it!"

"Miss what?" he called.

But the vision shattered.

Elias found himself on the floor of his shop, his
chair overturned beside him. Linnet knelt nearby, holding his wrist.

"You stopped breathing," she said.

He pulled his hand away from the key.

"How long?"

"Ten seconds. Maybe less."

It had felt like minutes. He could still smell sun-warmed
grass.

"The clock stores memories," Linnet said. "That's why I need
it working."

"Memories are not time."

"They're all the time we keep."

Elias looked at her sharply. The phrase sounded like Jonah.

Linnet reached into her pocket and took out a folded
letter.

"My father left this inside the case."

The paper was worn from repeated reading. Elias recognized Jonah's
slanting handwriting.

Linnet,

If you are reading this, I have failed to return.
The horologium was made to recover a lost hour, but
no machine can create time from nothing. It must exchange
one hour for another. I believed I could control the
bargain.

I was wrong.

Bring the machine to Elias Venn. Trust him when I
did not.

Your loving father.

Elias read the letter twice.

"What hour did he try to recover?" he asked.

"The hour my mother died."

Linnet spoke without trembling, as if she had practiced the
sentence until it no longer belonged to her.

"She drowned when I was five. My father was supposed
to meet her by the river, but he was late.
He thought if he could go back, he might save
her."

"And instead?"

"He vanished. The clock stopped shining. Then last week it
started again."

As if listening, the light in the glass sphere pulsed.

Elias stared into it. Shapes moved beneath the blue glow:
a bridge, dark water, a man reaching upward.

Jonah was not dead. He was caught somewhere inside the
hour he had tried to reclaim.

Elias spent three days studying the horologium.

Linnet slept in the room above the shop, once used
for spare parts and now hurriedly cleared of dust. Mara
brought soup, bread, and an extra blanket. She asked no
questions, though she looked at the curtained windows and remarked
that secrets were terrible for business.

By the second day, Elias understood the machine's basic design.
By the third, he wished he did not.

The horologium could open a path into a memory, but
the path required balance. To enter a lost hour, the
traveler had to surrender an hour of equal importance. Not
an hour measured by duration, but by love.

Jonah had tried to trick the mechanism. He offered an
ordinary hour from his childhood in exchange for the hour
of his wife's death. The machine rejected the bargain and
took him instead.

"Can we bring him back?" Linnet asked.

"Yes."

Her face brightened.

"But the clock will demand a fair exchange."

"Take one of my hours."

"No."

"He's my father."

"And you are a child."

"That doesn't mean my time is worthless."

"It means you do not yet know which hours you
cannot spare."

She glared at him. "Then use one of yours."

Elias looked toward the cherrywood clock.

Eleven minutes past four.

For fourteen years he had preserved every detail of his
last hour with Anwen. Rain had tapped against the bedroom
window. A coal fire had burned low. She had asked
him to describe the shop, though she had seen it
ten thousand times.

"Tell me which clocks are arguing," she whispered.

"The French carriage clock insists it is three."

"It always was vain."

"The oak grandfather clock says half past."

"Stubborn."

"The little brass one says the hour doesn't matter."

"That one is wise."

At eleven minutes past four, she squeezed his fingers. Then
she was gone.

The wall clock stopped at the same moment, though no
defect was ever found.

If the horologium took that hour, Elias knew it would
not simply blur. It would disappear. He would remember that
Anwen had lived and that he had loved her, but
not the sound of her voice, the warmth of her
hand, or the last faint smile at the corner of
her mouth.

He would lose her twice.

"No," he said.

Linnet lowered her gaze. "That's what my father said too."

That night the frost came.

It silvered the roofs and turned the street white beneath
the moon. Elias woke shortly before four to the sound
of clocks striking all at once.

He ran downstairs.

The horologium blazed on the worktable. Blue light poured between
the curtains. Gears spun so quickly they formed circles of
silver flame.

Linnet stood beside it, one hand on the feather key.

"What have you done?" Elias shouted.

She turned the key.

The shop folded inward.

The walls stretched into darkness. The floor became black water.
Clocks floated around them like lanterns, their hands racing forward
and backward. A terrible current seized Elias, pulling him toward
the blue sphere.

He caught Linnet's arm.

Together they fell into the lost hour.

Cold river water closed above Elias's head.

He kicked upward and broke the surface beneath a stone
bridge. Rain slashed the water. On the bank, lamps glowed
through the storm.

"Father!" Linnet screamed.

A man clung to one of the bridge supports. His
hair was gray, his face hollow, but Elias knew him.

Jonah.

Beyond him, closer to the center of the river, a
woman struggled against the current. She wore a green dress.
The hour was repeating exactly as Jonah remembered it: his
wife drowning, forever just beyond reach.

"Don't touch her!" Elias shouted.

Jonah looked toward them. "Elias?"

"The memory isn't real!"

"It can be changed!" Jonah cried. "I only need another
chance."

He let go of the support and swam toward the
woman.

The river darkened. The current became hands, thousands of them,
dragging him down.

Linnet lunged after him, but Elias held her back.

"Let me go!"

"If you follow, it will keep you too."

Jonah disappeared beneath the surface. Linnet struck Elias's chest, screaming.
Then the river rose around them in a wall.

Within the water, Elias saw his final hour with Anwen.

She lay in bed, pale in the winter light.

Not an imitation. Not an image. She was there, as
near as the day he lost her.

The horologium was asking its price.

Elias understood then why Jonah had failed. A fair exchange
could not be made reluctantly. The offered hour had to
be released.

Anwen looked at him through the wall of water.

"You'll miss it," she said.

He remembered the summer hillside, her laughter, and the question
he had never been able to answer.

Miss what?

Now he knew.

Everything. He would miss everything, whether he held it or
not.

Elias closed his eyes.

"Take the hour," he said.

The river went silent.

Warm fingers touched his cheek.

Then the memory was gone.

Elias opened his eyes with an emptiness inside him so
clean and sharp that he could not breathe. He knew
Anwen's name. He knew she had been his wife. But
when he searched for her final words, he found only
a locked white door.

The horologium's blue light turned gold.

Jonah burst from the river, gasping. Elias caught his coat,
and Linnet seized his hand. The three of them were
pulled upward as the lost hour collapsed below.

They landed on the shop floor among shattered glass and
fallen clocks.

For a while, nobody moved.

Then Linnet crawled toward her father.

Jonah looked older than he should have. To him, perhaps,
the river had lasted years. He touched Linnet's face as
though uncertain she was real.

"You came," he whispered.

"You didn't," she replied, and began to cry.

He held her. "I'm sorry."

Elias rose unsteadily.

The horologium lay on the table. Its sphere was clear
and empty. The gears had stopped. On the underside, the
engraved words had changed.

WHAT IS GIVEN IS NOT ALWAYS LOST.

Behind the workbench, the cherrywood clock began to tick.

Elias stared at it.

The hands moved from eleven minutes past four to twelve.

He felt no miracle. Anwen's last hour did not return.
The clock had kept its bargain.

Yet as the minute hand advanced, another memory came quietly
to him.

Not the bedroom. Not the rain.

A summer hillside beneath a yellow sky.

Anwen ran through the grass, carrying her shoes. She turned,
laughing, and called, "You'll miss it!"

This time he remembered what waited beyond the hill: a
field full of white birds rising at sunset. They had
watched them together until the sky emptied.

The machine had taken an hour, but love had not
lived in only one.

By morning, the frost began to melt.

Jonah and Linnet stayed above the shop for several weeks.
Jonah slept badly and spoke little. He no longer argued
that time could be conquered. Instead, he repaired simple clocks
under Elias's supervision and accepted correction with surprising grace.

The horologium remained in the blue suitcase, unwound and silent.

In spring, Jonah took it to the river and dropped
it from the bridge. He and Elias watched the suitcase
sink.

"Do you think that will destroy it?" Jonah asked.

"No," said Elias.

"Then why do it?"

"Some mistakes deserve the inconvenience of being underwater."

Jonah laughed for the first time in many years.

Linnet eventually became Elias's apprentice. She had quick hands, a
sharper tongue, and no patience for cuckoo clocks. Customers liked
her, particularly when she told them their watches were not
broken but offended.

Every morning at seven, Elias unlocked the green door of
the shop. He and Linnet wound the clocks together.

There were one hundred and seven now, though no two
visitors ever agreed on the number.

The cherrywood clock hung behind the workbench. It lost three
minutes each week, and Elias never corrected it. Whenever someone
pointed out that it was slow, he smiled.

"No," he said. "It has simply learned that there is
no need to hurry."

Then he returned to his work as Bellweather Street woke
around him, and the shop filled with the patient sound
of time continuing.

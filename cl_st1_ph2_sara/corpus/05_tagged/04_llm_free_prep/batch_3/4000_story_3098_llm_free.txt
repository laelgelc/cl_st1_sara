At six o'clock every morning, Mara Venn opened the shutters
of the Last Post Office and waited for the dead
to write.

The building stood at the edge of Bellweather, where the
cobbled road dissolved into marsh grass and pale water. It
had once been a railway station. You could still see
the platform behind it, though no train had passed in
forty years. Rusted tracks ran into the reeds and vanished
beneath the mud.

Above the door hung a blue sign:

**LETTERS SENT ANYWHERE
DELIVERED WHEN POSSIBLE**

People in Bellweather pretended not to notice the second line.

Mara had inherited the office from her aunt Elspeth, who
had explained the work in a single afternoon.

"Most letters are ordinary," Elspeth had said, sorting envelopes into
wooden pigeonholes. "Bills, apologies, invitations, lies. They go by bicycle
or boat. A few want to travel farther."

"How much farther?" Mara had asked.

Elspeth pointed toward the ceiling.

"Not necessarily up."

That had been twelve years ago. Elspeth was now buried
beneath an elm in the churchyard, and Mara worked alone.

Each morning she swept the floor, lit the iron stove,
and set a pot of coffee beside it. Then she
checked the brass slot in the office's eastern wall.

The slot had no opening on the other side. Mara
had verified this many times. From outside, there was only
brick. Yet on certain mornings an envelope waited in the
tray beneath it, chilled as if it had spent the
night under snow.

Letters from the dead were easily recognized. Their paper smelled
faintly of rain, and the stamps bore the faces of
monarchs who had never ruled.

Mara delivered them without asking questions.

One went to a baker whose brother had drowned as
a child. After reading it, the baker sat on the
flour-room floor until sunset, laughing and weeping into his apron.

Another went to an old woman who had spent fifty
years waiting for an apology from her husband. She read
his letter twice, burned it in the kitchen stove, and
said, "Still making excuses."

Sometimes the dead wrote nonsense. They complained about lost buttons.
They described dreams. They asked whether the apple tree had
survived the winter.

And sometimes they wrote only one sentence:

**I was wrong.**

Mara never received a letter.

At first she had not expected one. Her parents were
alive, if distant, and she had no siblings. Elspeth had
died peacefully after a long illness, with Mara beside her.

Still, after the funeral, Mara began checking the brass tray
before taking off her coat.

Weeks passed. Then months.

Nothing came.

She told herself it did not matter. Elspeth had said
everything that needed saying. She had given Mara the keys,
the account books, three jars of plum preserves, and precise
instructions for oiling the hinges.

Yet silence has a way of turning even certainty into
doubt.

By the seventh year, Mara had stopped expecting a letter.

By the twelfth, she had almost stopped wanting one.

Then, on the first morning of November, she found an
envelope in the tray bearing her own handwriting.

She stood very still.

The paper was gray and cold. Her name was written
across the front:

**MARA VENN
THE LAST POST OFFICE
BELLWEATHER**

In the upper corner, where a return address ought to
have been, were three words:

**NORTH OF TOMORROW**

Mara carried the envelope to her desk. She turned it
over, held it to the light, and examined the seal.
It was dark green wax stamped with the shape of
a moth.

She owned that seal.

It lay in the bottom drawer beneath unpaid invoices and
a packet of peppermint tea.

Mara opened the drawer. The seal was still there.

For several minutes she listened to the stove crackle.

Then she opened the letter.

**Dear Mara,**

**At noon today, a boy will come to the office
with a red envelope. Do not send it.**

**If you do, Bellweather will be gone by morning.**

**You will want proof. Look beneath the third floorboard under
the sorting table. Elspeth left something there.**

**I am sorry it took us so long to write.**

**Mara**

The signature was unmistakable. Not merely similar, but hers down
to the final impatient slash.

Mara read the letter three times.

Outside, the marsh wind pushed mist against the windows.

She checked the clock. Twenty minutes past six.

The third floorboard beneath the sorting table looked no different
from the others. Mara fetched a screwdriver and pried it
loose. Beneath was a narrow cavity containing a tin box.

Inside lay a photograph.

It showed Aunt Elspeth on the station platform, younger than
Mara had ever known her. Beside her stood six postal
workers in blue uniforms. Behind them waited a black locomotive,
its windows glowing white.

Mara knew every photograph of Elspeth in existence—or had believed
she did. She had never seen this one.

On the back, in Elspeth's handwriting, were the words:

**The Night Route, 18 November 1963.
Seven went north. One returned.
Never accept red mail.**

A second object lay in the box: a little iron
key attached to a label.

**SIGNAL HOUSE**

Mara looked through the rear window toward the abandoned tracks.
Half a mile into the marsh stood the old signal
house, a thin brick tower leaning beneath the sky.

She had never known there was a key.

At nine, Mr. Vale arrived to send a parcel of
lemon cakes to his daughter in the city. At ten,
two children bought a stamp and argued over who would
lick it. At eleven thirty, Mara locked the brass slot
and turned the sign on the door from OPEN to
CLOSED.

Then she waited.

At precisely noon, someone knocked.

A boy of perhaps fourteen stood outside. Rain darkened his
blond hair. His coat was too large for him, and
muddy water dripped from its hem.

Under one arm he carried a red envelope.

"We're closed," Mara said through the door.

The boy looked at the sign, then at her.

"It says letters sent anywhere."

"Not today."

"I only have one."

"I can see that."

He pressed closer to the glass. His face was pale
and earnest.

"Please. I have to send it before the train comes."

Mara's hand tightened around the key in her pocket.

"What train?"

"The northbound."

"There are no trains in Bellweather."

The boy glanced over his shoulder toward the marsh.

"There will be tonight."

Mara opened the door but kept herself in the doorway.

"What is your name?"

"Daniel."

"Daniel what?"

"I don't know."

"That's an unusual problem."

"They took it."

"Who did?"

"The people on the train."

Mara studied him. His clothes were old-fashioned, but not theatrical:
rough trousers, wool coat, boots repaired with string. There was
a bruise on his left cheek.

"Where are your parents?"

"I don't know that either."

He held out the red envelope.

"Please."

Mara did not touch it. "Who is it addressed to?"

"No one."

There was indeed no name on the front. The envelope
was sealed with green wax stamped in the shape of
a moth.

Mara felt the morning's letter like an extra heartbeat in
her pocket.

"What's inside?"

"I'm not allowed to say."

"Then I'm not allowed to send it."

Daniel's expression hardened. For an instant he looked far older
than fourteen.

"If you don't send it, they'll come looking."

"Who are they?"

But the boy stepped backward.

Beyond him, through the thinning mist, Mara saw a figure
standing on the abandoned platform.

It was a woman in a blue postal uniform.

Her face was hidden beneath the brim of her cap.

Mara blinked, and the platform was empty.

Daniel shoved the envelope through the doorway.

It struck the floor between them.

Mara jumped back. The envelope lay on the tiles, vivid
as fresh blood.

"Don't leave that here," she said.

But Daniel was already running. He crossed the road, slipped
through a gap in the hedge, and disappeared among the
reeds.

Mara stared after him. Then she picked up the envelope
with the fire tongs and placed it inside an empty
biscuit tin.

The instant the lid closed, every clock in the office
stopped.

The clock above the door read twelve minutes past noon.
The smaller clock beside the scales read twelve minutes past
noon. Mara's wristwatch, which usually ran three minutes fast, had
also stopped at twelve minutes past noon.

From inside the walls came a faint, rhythmic sound.

Click-clack.

Click-clack.

Like wheels on rails.

Mara took the iron key and went to the signal
house.

A boardwalk had once followed the tracks, but most of
it had rotted away. She picked her way across the
marsh, stepping on sleepers where they rose above the black
water. Wind bent the reeds in long silver waves.

The signal house door was painted the same blue as
the post office sign. The key turned with surprising ease.

Inside, dust coated a row of levers. A telegraph machine
sat beneath the window. On the wall hung a railway
map, though the lines it showed did not correspond to
any country Mara knew.

Routes branched across blank oceans. Stations appeared in impossible places:

**YESTERDAY'S HARBOR**

**THE CITY UNDER GLASS**

**NORTH OF TOMORROW**

At the very edge of the map, printed in faded
red, was Bellweather.

Mara stepped closer.

A pin marked the post office. From it, a black
railway line ran north through the marsh and off the
paper. Beside the line, someone had written:

**CLOSED 18 NOVEMBER 1963, AFTER LOSS OF TRAIN AND CREW.**

The telegraph clicked.

Mara nearly dropped the biscuit tin.

A strip of paper unrolled from the machine.

**IS THE RED LETTER ACCEPTED?**

She found a pencil and wrote beneath the message:

**NO.**

The machine began clicking again.

**THEN RETURN IT BEFORE NIGHTFALL.**

"Return it to whom?" Mara said aloud.

The machine answered:

**THE SENDER.**

Another line followed.

**DO NOT BREAK THE SEAL.**

Mara looked at the biscuit tin.

Naturally, that was the moment she decided to break the
seal.

She told herself she had no choice. The sender was
unknown, the recipient unnamed, and the boy gone. Whatever danger
the envelope contained could not be understood while it remained
closed.

She used the fire tongs to lift it from the
tin. The wax seal came apart beneath her thumbnail.

Inside was a single sheet of red paper.

At first it appeared blank. Then black letters surfaced slowly,
as if rising through deep water.

They were names.

Hundreds of them.

Mara recognized the baker, the old woman, Mr. Vale and
his daughter, the children who had bought the stamp that
morning. Everyone in Bellweather was listed.

Her own name appeared last.

Below it, in smaller writing, were the words:

**UNDELIVERABLE PERSONS TO BE COLLECTED AT MIDNIGHT.**

The wind stopped.

In the silence, Mara heard a train whistle far away.

She folded the letter and put it back in the
envelope, though the broken seal would not hold. On the
reverse, words had appeared:

**RETURN TO DANIEL ROOK
PLATFORM NINE
NIGHT ROUTE**

So the boy had a surname after all.

Mara searched the signal house until she found a ledger
locked inside a cabinet. The iron key opened that too.

The final entries were written by Elspeth.

**16 November. Night Route sighted beyond milepost. No scheduled service.**

**17 November. Seven letters received, all red, each containing names
of residents reported missing the following day. We refused collection.**

**18 November. Train arrived at 11:40 p.m. Daniel Rook aboard.
He has not aged. Claims he was taken in 1913
and forced to carry notices ahead of the train. Red
mail functions as a claim. Once accepted and processed, collection
cannot be prevented.**

**We intend to divert the Night Route north at Bellweather
Junction. Someone must operate the signal manually. I have chosen
myself.**

Below that, in a different hand:

**Elspeth did not reach the lever in time. Six lost.
She escaped by leaving Daniel's letter unopened. The route remains.**

Mara read the last sentence twice.

The envelope in her pocket had been opened.

Outside, daylight was fading with unnatural speed.

She ran back to town.

The office door stood open, though she was certain she
had locked it. Inside, the lamps burned blue. The clocks
had started again, but their hands were spinning backward.

Daniel sat on the sorting table.

"You opened it," he said.

"Yes."

"I told you to send it."

"You told me you had to send it."

"I have to say that." He looked toward the walls,
as though they might be listening. "They make me."

"What happens now?"

"The train comes. Everyone whose name is in the letter
goes aboard."

"Can the letter be destroyed?"

"I've tried."

"Can the train be stopped?"

Daniel lowered his eyes.

Mara pulled out the ledger. "Elspeth tried to divert it."

"She almost did."

"How?"

"At the junction there are two routes. South comes here.
North goes somewhere the train is afraid of."

"North of Tomorrow?"

Daniel looked sharply at her. "How do you know that?"

"I received a letter."

"From who?"

"Me."

For the first time, hope disturbed the careful emptiness of
his face.

"Then you survived."

"Or I become a ghost with access to stationery."

"You can't write from the train. No one can."

The rhythmic clatter inside the walls grew louder.

Click-clack.

Click-clack.

The windows rattled in their frames.

Daniel jumped down. "The switch is controlled from the signal
house. But the lever won't move unless the route accepts
a substitute."

"A substitute for what?"

"The town."

Mara understood.

"No."

"That's why your aunt failed. Her crew wouldn't let her
do it."

"Do what?"

"Give herself to the route."

The blue lamps flickered. Outside, people emerged from their houses
and began walking toward the station.

Mr. Vale came first, still wearing his flour-dusted apron. Behind
him walked the old woman, the baker, the two children
and their mother. None carried luggage. None seemed fully awake.

Mara rushed to the door.

"Go home!" she shouted.

No one looked at her.

A whistle sounded again, much closer.

Across the marsh, twin lights appeared in the dark.

Daniel picked up the red envelope. "Once they board, Bellweather
goes with them. Streets, houses, every memory that it existed.
By morning there'll be nothing here but water."

"Then we move the lever."

"The train needs a name."

Mara looked at the approaching lights.

"You've carried these letters for more than a century."

Daniel's mouth tightened. "I don't remember exactly."

"How many towns?"

"I don't remember."

"How many names?"

"I don't remember."

This time his voice broke.

Mara took the red envelope from him. She pulled out
the list and found his name on the reverse.

Then she crossed it out.

The paper shrieked.

Not loudly, but with such concentrated pain that every window
in the office cracked. Daniel doubled over. A black thread
rose from his chest, stretched toward the marsh, and snapped.

The train screamed in answer.

Daniel looked up.

The bruise on his cheek was fading. His oversized coat
sagged as if it had become merely cloth.

"What did you do?" he whispered.

"Return to sender."

Mara wrote another name at the bottom of the list.

**MARA VENN**

It was already there, but she wrote it again, pressing
so hard the pen tore the paper.

Nothing happened.

"Apparently," she said, "the route objects to duplicates."

The people of Bellweather were gathering on the platform. Behind
them, the Night Route emerged from the mist.

The locomotive was black and enormous. White fire burned in
its windows. Its carriages seemed to continue forever, bending away
into distances the marsh could not contain.

Figures in blue uniforms stood aboard, their faces hidden.

Mara thought of Elspeth, waiting twelve years to write.

Not necessarily up.

She understood then that the morning's letter had not come
from her death. It had come from a place north
of tomorrow—a future that might exist only if she reached
it.

She grabbed the moth seal from her desk, held it
over the stove flame, and pressed it into the torn
red paper beside her name.

The paper shuddered.

A new sentence appeared:

**ONE POSTMASTER ACCEPTED IN EXCHANGE FOR ALL LISTED PERSONS.**

Daniel seized her wrist. "Don't."

"You said it needed a substitute."

"Not you."

"You're no longer employed."

"I can still go."

"You've gone far enough."

The train entered the station.

Its doors opened.

Cold poured across the platform, carrying the smell of rain
and old paper.

Mara ran.

She crossed the tracks ahead of the locomotive and followed
the sleepers toward the signal house. Behind her, the conductor
called the names of Bellweather one by one.

With each name, someone stepped toward the open doors.

Mara reached the tower, unlocked it, and threw herself at
the northern lever.

It did not move.

She pulled again. Rust cut into her palms.

On the platform, the conductor called, "Mara Venn."

The lever loosened.

"Mara!" Daniel shouted from the doorway.

"Keep them off the train!"

"How?"

"You're a postman. Misdeliver them!"

He stared at her for half a second, then grinned.

Daniel raced back toward the platform, shouting, "Wrong address! All
of you! This is Bellwether with an e!"

The conductor turned.

Mara pulled with all her strength.

The lever came down.

Outside, the rails groaned. The junction shifted, one track sliding
away from the other. The northern signal rose.

Green light flooded the marsh.

The train began to move.

Not south toward Bellweather, but north along the impossible line.

The conductor screamed Mara's name.

A force seized her through the open window. She felt
herself pulled apart—not flesh from bone, but past from future,
memory from name. She saw the office, Aunt Elspeth's hands
sorting mail, her mother waving from a distant doorway, every
dawn she had spent waiting beside the brass slot.

Then Daniel caught her around the waist.

The train pulled.

Daniel held on.

The signal house shook. Bricks split. The map tore itself
from the wall and flew into the night.

For one endless second, Mara hung between the boy and
the white windows.

Then the red letter ripped in half.

The train vanished north.

The marsh fell silent.

Mara woke at sunrise on the signal-house floor.

Daniel was asleep beside her, one hand still gripping her
coat. Outside, the people of Bellweather wandered home, complaining of
strange dreams and cold feet.

Where the northern tracks had been, white flowers grew among
the reeds.

The Last Post Office survived, though every window was broken
and all the clocks were permanently twelve minutes slow.

Daniel remained in Bellweather. He aged normally after that, to
his great annoyance, and became Mara's apprentice. He remembered little
of his life before the Night Route, but discovered that
he liked lemon cakes, bicycle deliveries, and sleeping past six.

For several years, no red envelopes arrived.

Ordinary mail continued. Bills, apologies, invitations, lies.

The dead continued to write as well.

One spring morning, Mara opened the eastern shutters and found
an envelope waiting beneath the brass slot.

The paper smelled of rain.

It was addressed to her in Elspeth's handwriting.

Mara sat at her desk for a long time before
opening it.

Inside was a single sheet.

**Dear Mara,**

**I am sorry it took us so long to write.**

**The north is beautiful once you pass tomorrow. The others
are here, and the trains no longer stop where they
are not wanted. You did what I could not. I
am proud of you.**

**There is only one problem.**

**We appear to need a post office.**

**With love,
Elspeth**

At the bottom, beneath the signature, someone had added a
hurried note:

**P.S. Send peppermint tea.**

Mara laughed until Daniel came running downstairs, convinced something was
wrong.

Together they wrapped three boxes of tea in brown paper.
Mara addressed them to Elspeth Venn, North of Tomorrow, and
Daniel added enough stamps to reach the moon.

"Will it get there?" he asked.

Mara carried the parcel to the brass slot.

"Delivered when possible," she said.

She pushed it through.

For an instant, from somewhere beyond the wall, came the
sound of a distant whistle—not hungry now, nor mournful, but
bright and clear.

Then the parcel was gone.

# The Keeper of Lost Hours

On the morning the town clock began to run backward,
Mara Vale was repairing a music box.

It was a small thing, no larger than her palm,
with a painted moon on the lid and a silver
ballerina inside. The ballerina had once turned to a waltz,
but now she trembled in place as though afraid to
complete even a single circle.

Mara bent over the mechanism at her workbench. Her shop
was narrow and deep, crowded with watches, clocks, broken instruments,
and objects whose purposes had been forgotten. Tiny brass tools
hung above her in neat rows. Springs slept in labeled
drawers. The air smelled of oil, dust, and the sea.

At nine o'clock, every clock in the shop struck eight.

Mara looked up.

The tall walnut clock by the door chimed eight solemn
notes. The blue ceramic clock on the shelf gave eight
cheerful rings. A wooden cuckoo burst from its house, shouted
eight times, then withdrew with an embarrassed snap.

Outside, the great clock in the tower above the town
square groaned.

Its iron hands moved backward by one hour.

People stopped in the street. Bakers stepped out of their
shops with flour on their arms. Fishermen raised their faces
from their nets. Children on their way to school cheered,
believing they had been granted another hour of freedom.

Mara did not cheer.

She crossed the shop and opened the front door. The
wind carried a taste of salt from the harbor. Above
the red roofs, the clock tower showed eight, although sunlight
had already climbed high enough to prove otherwise.

A bell rang once from somewhere beneath the town.

No one else seemed to hear it.

Mara closed the door and returned to her workbench. The
ballerina still trembled on her silver pin.

"Well," Mara whispered, "he's finally done it."

At noon, which the town clocks insisted was eleven, Mayor
Quill called an emergency meeting in the square.

He was a broad man with a red beard and
a permanent expression of personal offense. He climbed onto the
dry fountain and waved his arms until the crowd fell
silent.

"There is no cause for alarm," he announced.

The tower clock groaned and slipped back another five minutes.

The crowd murmured.

Mayor Quill glanced over his shoulder. "A minor mechanical problem."

"Then ask Mara to fix it!" someone called.

Faces turned toward her.

Mara stood near the edge of the square, wearing her
brown work apron. She disliked crowds, speeches, and being noticed.
That day, unfortunately, she had all three.

Mayor Quill beckoned. "Miss Vale, you are our clockmaker."

"My father was the tower keeper," Mara said.

"And you were his apprentice."

"I repaired gears. He kept the clock."

"A distinction without a difference."

Mara looked up at the tower. Its face was pale
stone, weathered by a hundred winters. The minute hand twitched
backward again.

"There is a difference," she said. "A large one."

The mayor lowered his voice. "Can you fix it?"

"Perhaps."

The crowd brightened.

"But not from the tower."

The crowd darkened again.

Mayor Quill rubbed his beard. "Then from where?"

Mara did not answer. Her eyes had gone to the
alley beside the old library. A man stood there in
a dark blue coat, watching her.

He was very tall and very thin. Though the wind
pulled at everyone else's clothes, his coat remained perfectly still.
A silver chain ran from his waistcoat pocket, but there
was no watch at its end.

When Mara met his gaze, he smiled.

Then the tower bell rang thirteen times.

The sound rolled over the rooftops, shook dust from windows,
and sent a flock of gulls screaming toward the sea.

The tall man in the alley disappeared.

That evening, Mara locked her shop early.

She waited until the streets were empty and the town's
lamps had been lit. According to her kitchen clock, midnight
was approaching. According to the tower, it was half past
ten. According to the stars, neither was quite right.

Mara packed a leather bag with a lantern, pliers, a
winding key, a spool of copper wire, and the unfinished
music box. Then she climbed the narrow stairs to the
room above her shop.

Her father's coat still hung behind the bedroom door.

Elias Vale had vanished seven years before. Most people believed
he had drowned, though no boat had been missing and
no body had washed ashore. Mara knew better, but knowing
had brought her little comfort.

She reached into the coat's inner pocket and removed a
brass key.

It was longer than her hand, cut with seven crooked
teeth. Her father had given it to her the night
before he vanished.

"If the clock ever runs backward," he had said, "go
below the library. Do not go alone."

At the time, Mara had been sixteen and impatient.

"Why would the clock run backward?"

"Because someone has stolen what cannot be returned."

"Who?"

Her father had looked toward the tower.

"The Keeper of Lost Hours."

Mara had waited seven years for an explanation. None had
come.

Now she closed her fingers around the key and went
out into the night, alone.

The library had been abandoned since a storm cracked its
roof. Ivy covered the windows, and rain had swollen the
front door shut. Mara entered through a broken side window,
tearing her sleeve on the glass.

Inside, moonlight silvered rows of empty shelves. The town had
moved the books long ago, but the library still held
the dry, patient smell of paper.

Mara crossed to the reading room. Beneath a circular rug,
she found an iron door set into the floor.

The brass key fit.

The lock turned with a sound like a sigh.

Stone steps spiraled downward into darkness. Mara lit her lantern
and began to descend.

The air grew colder. Above her, the library vanished into
shadow. Below, she heard thousands of clocks ticking, none in
rhythm with another.

The stairs ended at a black river.

A narrow boat waited at the bank. The tall man
in the blue coat sat inside it, one hand resting
on a silver oar.

"You came alone," he said.

"My father told me not to."

"Elias was often wise after wisdom could help him."

Mara raised her lantern. The man's face was young, but
his eyes seemed exhausted.

"Are you the Keeper?"

"I am Silas Wren."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No," he said. "It rarely is."

He gestured to the empty seat. Beyond him, the river
flowed through a vast cavern. Strange lights glimmered on the
distant walls.

Mara remained on the bank. "What have you done to
the town clock?"

"Nothing."

"It is running backward."

"So would you, if someone were pulling your past out
through your heart."

Mara tightened her grip on the lantern. "Speak plainly."

"Your town has been losing time for years. Today it
began to notice."

"Who is stealing it?"

Silas studied her. "Get in the boat."

Mara disliked him immediately. But she disliked unanswered questions more.

She stepped aboard.

The boat moved without Silas touching the oar. It glided
over water smooth as polished glass. Beneath the surface, pale
shapes drifted like sleeping fish.

Mara leaned closer.

They were clocks.

Pocket watches, hourglasses, sundials, kitchen timers, carved water clocks—all floated
beneath the boat. Some were broken. Others still moved, measuring
hours for places that no longer existed.

"What is this river?" Mara asked.

"Time that no one remembers."

"That makes no sense."

"Most important things don't, at first."

The cavern widened. Doorways appeared along the banks, hundreds of
them, each standing alone without a wall. Through one doorway,
Mara saw a summer field. Through another, a snow-covered city.
Behind a third, a woman sat beside a hospital bed,
holding the hand of an old man.

"Lost hours," Silas said. "Moments forgotten, abandoned, or wished away."

Mara remembered all the mornings she had begged to pass
quickly after her father disappeared. All the evenings she had
worked until exhaustion so she would not have to think.

"Why keep them?" she asked.

"Because forgotten time is still time."

The river carried them toward a distant golden glow.

As they approached, Mara saw an island covered with clocks.
They rose in heaps and towers, ticking, ringing, chiming. At
the island's center stood a house made of dark wood,
its windows lit from within.

The boat touched shore.

Silas stepped out. "Before we go inside, you must understand
one thing. The man you meet may look like your
father."

Mara's breath caught. "May?"

"He may sound like him. He may remember what your
father remembered."

"Is he alive?"

Silas's expression softened, and she hated him for that softness.

"That depends on what you mean by alive."

Mara pushed past him and ran to the house.

Inside, clocks covered every wall. Their hands spun at different
speeds. Some faces had numbers; others bore names, seasons, or
phases of the moon.

A man stood at a worktable with his back to
her.

His hair was gray at the temples. His left shoulder
bent slightly lower than his right. A thin white scar
crossed the back of his hand.

Mara stopped.

"Father?"

The man turned.

For seven years, Mara had tried to preserve his face
in memory. She had repeated its details like a prayer:
green eyes, long nose, one crooked tooth, two lines between
his brows. Yet memory had blurred him despite her efforts.

Now he stood before her exactly as he had on
the night he vanished.

"Mara," Elias Vale said.

She crossed the room and struck him.

The sound silenced every clock for a single second.

Elias touched his cheek.

"You left," Mara said.

"Yes."

"You told me not to come alone, then left me
alone for seven years."

"I know."

"You are not allowed to say that as if it
repairs anything."

"No," he said quietly. "It does not."

Mara wanted to strike him again. She wanted to embrace
him. She wanted to become sixteen once more, if only
for the chance to refuse his brass key and bar
the door.

Instead, she set down her lantern.

"What is happening to the town?"

Elias looked past her at Silas. "The clock has started
reversing?"

"This morning," Silas said.

"Then we have very little time."

"That appears to be the problem," Mara replied.

For a moment, her father almost smiled.

He led her to the worktable. A glass vessel stood
upon it, round as a human heart. Golden light churned
inside. A crack ran from its rim to its base,
and through that crack spilled bright grains that vanished before
touching the table.

"What is it?" Mara asked.

"The town's first hour," Elias said. "Every place begins with
a moment. The first stone laid. The first fire lit.
The first name spoken with the intention of belonging. That
moment becomes an anchor. All later hours are tied to
it."

"And it is breaking?"

"It was broken."

"By whom?"

Elias looked toward Silas again.

Silas removed his blue coat. Beneath it, the right side
of his waistcoat was scorched black.

"I did," he said.

Mara reached for the pliers in her bag.

Silas held up a hand. "Not deliberately."

"Comforting."

"Seven years ago," Elias said, "Silas discovered someone stealing lost
hours from the river. We tracked the thief here."

"Who was it?"

"A woman named Thalia Voss," said Silas. "She believed forgotten
time could be gathered and used. She wanted to return
to the day her son died."

Mara looked at the clocks crowding the walls. "Could she?"

"No. Time can be remembered, revisited in dreams, or echoed
in places. It cannot be changed. But grief is skilled
at disguising impossible things as difficult ones."

Thalia had built a machine, they explained, deep beneath the
town. She had chosen Bellweather because its first hour was
unusually strong. For more than a century, the townspeople had
lived by the tower bell. They woke, worked, married, mourned,
and celebrated by its sound. Their shared attention fed the
anchor.

When Silas tried to destroy Thalia's machine, the struggle cracked
the vessel. Elias used himself to seal it.

Mara stared at her father. "Used yourself?"

Elias unbuttoned his shirt at the collar.

Golden light shone beneath his skin. It pulsed slowly, and
with each pulse the crack in the glass dimmed.

"I became part of the mechanism," he said. "As long
as I remain here, the first hour survives."

"Then why is the clock reversing now?"

"Because Thalia's machine was never destroyed," Silas said. "It has
begun again."

"Thalia?"

"Dead," Elias answered. "For six years."

Mara folded her arms. "Machines do not usually wait six
years after their maker's death to start themselves."

"This one was designed to activate when the town had
produced enough abandoned time," said Silas.

Mara thought of Mayor Quill declaring winter evenings useless. Of
factory workers wishing away long shifts. Of children staring through
school windows. Of widows begging nights to end and sailors
cursing windless afternoons.

"How long?" she asked.

"Until what?"

"Until the town loses everything."

Elias listened to the clocks.

"At sunrise, the people will begin forgetting recent days. By
noon, years. By sunset, Bellweather's first hour will be pulled
free."

"And then?"

"The town will never have existed."

Silence passed through the room in waves of ticking.

Mara opened her leather bag.

"Show me the machine."

They returned to the river.

This time Elias came with them, though each step away
from the house made the golden light beneath his skin
flicker. The boat carried them farther underground, where the cavern
ceiling dropped low and the river narrowed between walls of
black stone.

They landed before a bronze door.

It had no handle, only a keyhole shaped like a
seven-pointed star.

Mara took out the brass key.

"My father gave me this before he disappeared," she said
to Silas. "Why didn't he keep it?"

"Because once he joined the anchor, the door would no
longer recognize him as human."

Mara inserted the key.

The door opened.

Beyond it lay a circular chamber filled by an enormous
machine. Copper pipes twisted around a central wheel. Glass tubes
ran from floor to ceiling, carrying streams of golden dust.
At the machine's base stood twelve empty chairs.

Straps hung from their arms.

Mara approached the central wheel. It turned backward with slow,
crushing force. Each movement shook the chamber.

"Thalia built this alone?" she asked.

"Grief gave her patience," Silas said.

"Grief has done worse."

Mara examined the gears. Her fear settled as soon as
she touched the machinery. Metal was honest. Springs did not
conceal motives. Gears did not call abandonment sacrifice.

She traced the drive system. The machine drew power from
a shaft descending into the river. Its output passed through
the glass tubes toward the town above.

"It's not simply stealing the first hour," she said. "It's
searching for something."

Elias nodded. "Thalia's final command."

"Her son."

"Yes."

The machine had sifted through years of lost time, trying
to assemble one perfect hour from the boy's life. But
it could not tell one person's memories from another's. It
consumed everything.

Mara opened her tool bag.

"Can we stop the wheel?"

"We tried," Silas said.

"You tried seven years ago."

"Yes."

"And you broke the town's first hour."

Silas frowned. "Also yes."

"Then perhaps stand over there and try nothing."

She worked.

The machine was more complex than any clock, but its
logic was familiar. Tension. Release. Measure. Repetition. Thalia had built
it from clockwork because she had wanted to command time,
yet every clock carried a warning in its design: no
matter how cleverly built, it could measure only what passed.
It could not make the passing obey.

Mara removed one panel, then another. She climbed between pipes,
following the vibration. At last she found a silver cylinder
buried near the machine's heart.

It hummed when she touched it.

"Memory chamber," Elias said. "The machine's purpose lives there."

"Then we remove it."

"We cannot," said Silas. "The machine is bound to the
first hour. Tear out its purpose, and it will tear
out the anchor with it."

Mara swore.

Above them, muffled by earth and stone, the tower bell
began to ring.

It rang twelve times, then eleven, then ten.

The machine accelerated.

Golden dust poured through the tubes. Images flashed inside them:
a wedding beneath apple blossoms, boys diving from the harbor
wall, a woman reading beside a fire, a soldier limping
home, Mara at six years old riding on her father's
shoulders.

She jerked away.

The machine had reached her memories.

Elias staggered. Light streamed from his hands.

"Mara," he said, "you must leave."

"No."

"If the anchor breaks while you are below—"

"I said no."

She took the music box from her bag.

The painted moon on its lid was chipped. Inside, the
ballerina waited, one foot raised forever above a mirrored floor.

"Whose is that?" Elias asked.

"Mrs. Wren's."

Silas went still.

Mara looked at him. "Your mother brought it to my
shop three days ago. She said it belonged to her
grandson."

Silas moved closer. "My sister's child."

"She said he died when he was eight."

"Thalia's son," Elias whispered.

Mara wound the key.

The ballerina trembled. A broken melody spilled out—three notes, a
pause, then the same three notes again.

Silas closed his eyes.

"He used to play it at breakfast," he said. "It
drove us mad."

Mara turned the box over. Scratched into the bottom were
two initials: A.V.

"Thalia's machine is searching for her son's lost hour," Mara
said. "What if we give it one?"

"A memory cannot satisfy it," Elias said.

"Not a memory. A measurement."

She opened the music box and removed the damaged governor.
Then she turned toward the machine.

"Thalia built this to recognize time through machinery. This box
measured hours in her son's life. Not important hours, perhaps.
Not perfect ones. But real ones."

Understanding crossed Silas's face. "You want to replace the memory
chamber."

"With the music box cylinder."

"It may reject it."

"It may."

"It may destroy the anchor."

"It may."

Silas looked almost amused. "You are very like your father."

"No," Mara said. "I finish my repairs."

The tower bell rang backward through six o'clock.

There was no more time for caution.

Mara cut the copper wire around the memory chamber. Silas
braced the housing while Elias placed both glowing hands against
the central wheel. The machine screamed, a vast metallic cry
that shook the cavern.

"Now!" Elias shouted.

Mara pulled the silver chamber free.

For one instant, she saw what it contained: Thalia kneeling
beside a bed, holding a feverish child and promising him
that morning would come.

Then the image shattered.

Darkness swallowed the chamber. The wheel lurched. Across the room,
pipes burst open, releasing storms of golden dust.

Mara fitted the music box cylinder into the empty housing.

It was too small.

She tore the copper wire from her spool and wound
it around the spindle. Her fingers slipped. One of the
gears caught her sleeve and dragged her forward.

Silas seized her arm.

The fabric tore, freeing her.

"Again!" he shouted.

Mara pushed the cylinder into place.

The machine stopped.

In the sudden quiet, the music box played.

Three notes.

A pause.

Three notes again.

The melody was thin and imperfect. The silver ballerina turned
one uncertain circle.

Images filled the glass tubes.

A boy sat at a kitchen table, laughing as his
mother burned pancakes. Morning sunlight touched his hair. Silas, younger
and less tired, threw a cloth at him. Thalia laughed
too.

It was not the day the boy died.

It was not a birthday, a farewell, or a miracle.

It was only breakfast.

The machine shuddered.

Then the central wheel began to turn forward.

Golden light rushed back through the pipes. The backward-ringing bell
above them fell silent.

Elias collapsed.

Mara ran to him. The light beneath his skin was
fading.

"No," she said. "No, you don't get to leave again."

"The anchor is repairing itself," he whispered.

"Then you are free."

"I am part of what must be repaired."

She gripped his coat. "We can find another way."

He smiled, and she saw the crooked tooth she had
worked so hard to remember.

"Mara, there is always another way. But not always another
hour."

Around them, the machine began to dissolve. Copper rusted into
red dust. Glass tubes collapsed into sand. The bronze door
cracked down the middle.

Silas pulled Mara back as the floor opened.

Elias remained beside the wheel.

"I am sorry," he said.

"That isn't enough!"

"I know."

The river surged into the chamber.

Mara tried to reach him, but Silas held her as
water rose around their knees, their waists, their shoulders. The
last thing she saw was her father standing in golden
light, one hand raised in farewell.

Then the river took her.

Mara woke in the town fountain at dawn.

She lay in six inches of cold water with Silas
beside her and the music box clutched against her chest.

People gathered around them.

Mayor Quill pushed through the crowd in his nightshirt. "Miss
Vale! What in heaven's name are you doing?"

Mara looked up at the tower.

The clock showed six fifteen.

Its hands moved forward.

The bell rang once, clear and bright, and every clock
in Bellweather answered.

For several days, the town behaved as though waking from
a dream. People rediscovered appointments they had forgotten, letters they
had never finished, and promises made years before. Old friends
knocked on one another's doors. Parents left work early to
walk beside the sea with their children. Mayor Quill shortened
all official speeches, though this reform lasted less than a
month.

No one remembered that the clock had run backward.

No one except Mara and Silas.

Silas stayed in Bellweather until winter. He rented the room
above the bakery and spent his mornings helping Mara in
the shop. He knew almost nothing about ordinary clocks, but
he could identify the hour in which nearly any lost
object had vanished.

Together they repaired the music box.

When they returned it to Mrs. Wren, she opened the
lid and listened to the ballerina's imperfect waltz.

"My grandson loved this," she said.

Silas stood behind Mara, silent.

Mrs. Wren touched the painted moon. "I used to think
the last day was the one I needed to remember.
But lately, I keep thinking about breakfast."

She smiled through her tears.

"So many burned pancakes."

Years passed.

Mara became keeper of the tower, though she continued to
repair clocks in the narrow shop by the harbor. Each
morning, she climbed the stone stairs and wound the great
mechanism by hand.

She never made the clock perfectly accurate.

Some days it ran a minute fast, giving the town
an extra breath of urgency. Some days it ran a
minute slow, granting lovers longer goodbyes. Mara found perfection suspicious,
especially where time was concerned.

Above the tower door, she placed a small brass plaque:

NO HOUR IS EMPTY.

Most people assumed it was a clockmaker's motto.

On certain nights, when the streets were quiet and the
tide was low, Mara heard a bell ringing beneath the
town. Then she would unlock the tower and climb to
the clock face.

Through its round glass, she could see all of Bellweather:
the roofs silvered by moonlight, the dark harbor, the sleeping
square, and the lamps of those still awake.

She would think of the river below, carrying forgotten moments
through the earth.

She would think of a woman burning pancakes, a child
laughing, and her father standing in golden light.

Then Mara would place one hand against the warm gears
and feel the whole town moving forward—not away from what
it had lost, but with it.

And when morning came, the bell would ring, the doors
would open, and the people of Bellweather would step into
another hour.

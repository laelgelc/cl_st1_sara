By the time the sea reached Bellweather Station, only three
trains still stopped there.

The first came at dawn, carrying engineers north toward the
new seawall. The second arrived at noon with mail, medicine,
and the occasional tourist who had misunderstood the map. The
last came just before midnight, empty except for a conductor
named Vale, who always stepped onto the platform, checked his
silver watch, and asked the stationmaster the same question.

"Any passengers tonight, Miss Wren?"

And Mara Wren always gave him the same answer.

"Not tonight."

For thirty-eight years, Mara had lived above the ticket hall
in two narrow rooms that smelled of coal dust, lavender
soap, and old paper. Her father had been stationmaster before
her, and his father before him. Both were buried on
the hill behind the church, where the salt water had
not yet reached.

Bellweather itself was nearly gone.

Once, it had been a town of six thousand people,
with a brass band, a cinema, and a factory that
made blue glass bottles. Children had raced bicycles down Station
Road. Fishermen had crowded the harbor at dawn. On summer
evenings, couples had danced beneath paper lanterns in the public
gardens.

Then the sea began climbing.

It did not come like an army. Armies could be
seen, resisted, and blamed. The sea came like a patient
creditor. Each winter it took a field, a street, a
row of houses. It filled basements, soured wells, and left
silver fish flickering in abandoned kitchens.

The government built barriers. The barriers cracked. New barriers were
promised.

People left.

The cinema closed first, then the school, then the blue-bottle
factory. The church remained, though its congregation had dwindled to
seven stubborn souls and a dog. Bellweather Station remained because
the railway ran on higher ground and because, according to
an old transport law no one had bothered to repeal,
a station could not be abandoned while it still served
a permanent resident.

Mara was that resident.

She sold three or four tickets a week, swept the
platform every morning, wound the station clock every Sunday, and
polished the brass sign above the ticket window until the
letters shone:

TRAVELERS ARE ADVISED TO KEEP THEIR DESTINATIONS IN MIND.

No one knew who had written the warning or what
it meant. Mara's grandfather had claimed it was older than
the railway.

On the first Tuesday of November, a storm came in
from the west.

By late afternoon, rain had erased the horizon. The sea
struck the seawall with a sound like distant artillery. Wind
shook the station windows and drove water beneath the waiting-room
door.

Mara placed buckets under the leaks, lit the iron stove,
and listened to the weather report on the radio.

"Mandatory evacuation," said the announcer. "All remaining residents in the
Bellweather flood zone must leave by eighteen hundred hours."

At seventeen forty-five, Reverend Ash arrived with his dog in
the front seat of an ancient green van.

He hurried into the station, rain streaming from his hat.

"Mara," he said, breathless, "we're going."

"So I hear."

"You must come."

"I have a train at midnight."

"There won't be a midnight train. They'll close the line."

Mara glanced toward the tracks, two black ribbons shining in
the storm.

"The midnight train has never missed Bellweather."

"This is not an ordinary storm."

"It never is, according to the radio."

Reverend Ash removed his hat. He was seventy-two, tall and
stooped, with eyebrows that made him look permanently astonished.

"The eastern barrier has failed," he said. "Water is already
crossing Market Square."

Mara's hand tightened around the broom.

Market Square lay less than half a mile from the
station.

"The van has room," the reverend continued. "I have Mrs.
Wood and the Carters. We're meeting the evacuation bus at
High Thorn. Please."

Mara looked around the ticket hall. The wooden benches. The
brass clock. The framed photographs of steam engines and opening
ceremonies. A black-and-white portrait showed her father as a young
man, standing on the platform with one hand resting on
the shoulder of a solemn little girl.

Mara remembered the day it had been taken. Her father
had given her a red flag and told her never
to wave it unless she meant to stop a train.

"What about the station?" she asked.

Reverend Ash's expression softened.

"It is a building."

"Not to me."

"No," he said. "I know."

A horn sounded outside. The dog barked from the van.

Reverend Ash set his hat back on his head. "I
can wait five minutes."

Mara shook her head.

He looked as if he wanted to argue, but perhaps
he saw something in her face that made him stop.

"When the water reaches the first step," he said, "you
go upstairs. When it reaches the ticket counter, you get
onto the roof."

"I know how floods work, Reverend."

"And if that train comes—"

"It will."

"If it comes, you get on it."

Mara almost smiled. "Stationmasters do not abandon their stations."

"Then for once," he said, opening the door to the
storm, "be a passenger."

He left.

At eighteen hundred hours, the electricity failed.

Mara lit every oil lamp she could find. Their flames
trembled in the drafts, filling the station with amber shadows.
Outside, the rain slanted across the tracks. Beyond the platform,
Station Road had become a shallow river.

At nineteen twenty, water reached the first step.

At twenty ten, it covered the floor of the waiting
room.

Mara carried the ledgers upstairs, then returned for the photographs.
She moved slowly, not because she lacked urgency, but because
each object demanded judgment. What could be saved? What deserved
to be saved? The old timetables? Her father's cap? The
wooden box of unclaimed luggage tags?

She chose the station logbooks, the silver ticket punch, and
the photograph of herself beside her father.

At twenty-one thirty, a small boat floated past the station
entrance.

At twenty-two fifteen, the sea entered the ticket hall.

Mara stood behind the counter, watching black water spread across
the tiles. It lifted fallen papers and spun them gently
around the room. A timetable opened like a white flower,
then dissolved.

The station clock continued ticking.

At twenty-three forty, Mara heard a bell.

One clear note sounded through the storm.

Then another.

She climbed onto the counter and peered through the rain-streaked
window. At first she saw only darkness. Then two points
of golden light appeared beyond the curve.

The midnight train was coming.

It moved without hurry, though wind tore at the trees
and waves broke across the rails. Its engine was unlike
the electric locomotives that usually passed through Bellweather. This one
was black and gleaming, with brass fittings and a tall
chimney pouring out white steam.

As it approached, the floodwater drew back from the tracks.

The train stopped precisely at midnight.

Its doors opened.

Conductor Vale stepped onto the platform.

He wore the same dark blue uniform, the same polished
shoes, and the same expression of courteous patience he had
worn every night for as long as Mara could remember.
Rain did not touch him. Around the train, the storm
seemed to pause.

Vale consulted his silver watch.

"Any passengers tonight, Miss Wren?"

Mara remained on the ticket counter, waist-deep in shadow.

"Where does this train go?" she called.

Vale looked toward the brass sign above the window.

"Where it has always gone."

"That isn't an answer."

"It is the only answer printed on the timetable."

"There is no midnight train on the timetable."

"No," he agreed. "Not on yours."

Water surged against the counter.

Vale held out his hand.

"Your ticket, Miss Wren."

"I don't have one."

"Everyone has one."

Mara almost told him not to be ridiculous. Then she
remembered a summer afternoon sixty-five years earlier.

She had been eight years old, helping her father clean
the lost-property room. Inside a cracked leather suitcase, she had
found a ticket unlike any other: thick, cream-colored, and edged
with gold. No destination was printed on it. Only a
name.

MARA WREN.

Her father had snatched it from her hand.

"Not yet," he had said.

"What is it?"

"Something our family keeps safe."

"For whom?"

"For the day it is needed."

He had locked it in the bottom drawer of the
ticket desk. Years later, when Mara took over the station,
she found the drawer empty and assumed her father had
destroyed it.

Now she felt something stiff inside the pocket of her
dress.

Her fingers closed around a rectangle of thick paper.

She drew out the ticket.

Its golden edges glimmered in the lamplight. Her name remained
printed across the center, but beneath it, where the destination
should have been, new words had appeared.

NOT YET DECIDED.

Vale smiled faintly.

"You see?"

Mara climbed down from the counter. Water rose to her
knees, icy and strong. She waded through the waiting room,
carrying the photograph and logbooks in a canvas satchel.

At the door, she stopped.

Behind her, the station clock began to strike midnight. Each
chime sounded deeper than the last.

"This building has stood for one hundred and twelve years,"
she said.

"So have many things," Vale replied.

"My mother met my father on this platform."

"I know."

"My grandfather died in that office."

"I know."

"I was born upstairs."

"I know that too."

The ninth chime sounded.

"If I leave," Mara said, "Bellweather Station ends."

Vale looked at the rising water.

"Miss Wren, all stations end. That is why there are
trains."

The twelfth chime faded.

Mara stepped onto the platform.

Vale punched her ticket. The silver punch made a sound
like a tiny bell.

"Mind the gap," he said.

Inside, the carriage was warm.

There were no electric lights, only green-shaded lamps fixed above
velvet seats. The walls were paneled in dark wood, and
the brass luggage racks shone. The carriage smelled of smoke,
oranges, wet wool, and a faint perfume Mara could not
name.

It was not empty.

A young soldier sat by the first window, his boots
muddy and his uniform cut in a style from a
century ago. Across from him, a woman in a red
coat held a sleeping baby. An old fisherman gripped a
wooden box. Two boys played cards on the floor. A
bride, still wearing her veil, stared at a pressed flower
in her palm.

Some passengers were solid and clear. Others seemed faded, as
if viewed through mist.

All of them turned when Mara entered.

A man near the back stood up.

He was broad-shouldered, with a gray stationmaster's cap tucked beneath
one arm.

Mara forgot how to breathe.

Her father looked exactly as he had on the morning
of his final journey: sixty-three years old, stern around the
mouth, his silver hair combed carefully to one side.

"You took your time," he said.

Mara gripped the top of a seat.

"You died."

"Yes."

"You died twenty-seven years ago."

"I remember."

She wanted to embrace him. She wanted to strike him.
Instead she said, "You left the ticket."

"I kept it safe until it was needed."

"You could have explained."

"You wouldn't have believed me."

"I might have."

Her father raised one eyebrow.

Mara sat down opposite him.

Outside, Bellweather Station began to slide away. The train had
started moving so gently she had not felt it. Water
covered the platform. It climbed the walls, touched the windows,
and swallowed the brass station sign.

Mara watched until the roof vanished beneath the black sea.

Then there was only darkness.

"I should have stayed," she whispered.

Her father leaned forward.

"To do what?"

"My duty."

"You did your duty."

"The station is gone."

"The station was never the bricks."

"What was it, then?"

He nodded toward the carriage.

Mara looked at the passengers.

The soldier was showing the sleeping baby's mother a tarnished
medal. The fisherman had opened his wooden box, revealing dozens
of blue glass bottles. The boys were arguing cheerfully over
the rules of their card game. The bride had begun
to hum.

"A station," her father said, "is a promise that departure
is not the end of the journey."

The conductor entered the carriage.

"Next stop," Vale announced, "Bellweather."

Mara turned sharply.

The darkness outside the windows brightened.

Morning sunlight spilled across green fields. The sea was gone.
In its place stood a town Mara knew and did
not know.

Bellweather spread across the hills, untouched by flood. Smoke rose
from factory chimneys. Fishing boats crowded the harbor. The cinema
marquee advertised a film Mara had seen as a girl.
Paper lanterns hung in the public gardens.

On the platform, hundreds of people waited.

Some wore modern clothes. Others wore fashions from vanished decades.
Mara recognized faces from old photographs and gravestones. Mr. Pell,
who had sold sweets near the school. The Turner sisters,
who drowned when their boat overturned. Mrs. Wood's husband, dead
twelve years. Children who had grown old and old people
who looked young.

At the center of the crowd stood Mara's mother.

The train stopped.

Doors opened along the platform, and passengers began to disembark.

Mara's father stood.

"Come on," he said.

Mara did not move.

Beyond the glass, her mother lifted a hand. She wore
her yellow summer dress. Her dark hair fell over her
shoulders, and her smile was warm with recognition.

Mara's chest ached.

"What is this place?" she asked.

Her father's voice was gentle.

"A destination."

"Am I dead?"

"Not necessarily."

"That is not reassuring."

"It isn't meant to be."

She looked at her ticket. The words NOT YET DECIDED
remained.

"What happens if I get off?"

"You stay."

"With you?"

"With us."

"And if I remain on the train?"

"You go where you are needed."

"Where is that?"

"No one can tell you. That would make it their
choice."

Her mother waited on the platform.

For twenty-seven years, Mara had imagined speaking to her parents
again. She had collected things she wanted to tell them:
how lonely the station became, how the town emptied, how
she found her first gray hair in the mirror above
the ticket window. Now none of those things seemed important.

Her father stepped into the aisle.

"Mara," he said, "there is no shame in being tired."

She closed her eyes.

She was tired.

Tired of storms. Tired of farewells. Tired of watching every
familiar thing become a story no one else remembered.

The platform bell rang.

"All aboard," Vale called.

Mara opened her eyes.

At the far end of the platform stood a little
girl holding a red flag.

The child was no more than eight. Her coat was
too large for her, and her hair had been tied
with a green ribbon. Mara recognized herself at once—not as
a ghost or memory, but as a possibility still waiting
to be chosen.

The girl raised the flag, then hesitated.

Never wave it unless you mean to stop a train.

Mara's mother came to the carriage door.

"You can stay," she said.

Mara swallowed. "Do you want me to?"

"Always."

"Then tell me what to do."

Her mother shook her head.

"No. That was never my gift to give."

The bell rang again.

Mara thought of Reverend Ash's van struggling through the storm.
She thought of the evacuation center at High Thorn, crowded
with frightened families. She thought of children who would grow
up hearing about a town beneath the sea. They would
need someone to tell them where the streets had run,
where the gardens had bloomed, why blue glass still washed
ashore after storms.

Memory, she realized, was not a room one locked and
guarded. It was luggage. It had to be carried forward.

Mara stepped back from the door.

Her father nodded once.

Pride and sorrow moved across his face.

"Travel well, Stationmaster," he said.

The doors closed.

On the platform, her parents stood side by side. Her
mother's yellow dress fluttered in the morning breeze. Her father
lifted his cap.

The train pulled away.

Mara watched until Bellweather disappeared.

For a long time, the carriage was silent.

Then Conductor Vale sat opposite her.

"Where am I going?" she asked.

He opened his silver watch.

"Where are you needed?"

"I don't know."

"Then keep your destination in mind."

"That sign never made sense."

"It makes sense now."

The train entered a tunnel.

In the darkness, Mara heard wheels striking rails, steady as
a heartbeat. She held the canvas satchel on her lap.
Inside were the station logs, the silver punch, and the
photograph of a little girl beside her father.

Light appeared ahead.

When the train emerged, it was dawn.

The storm had passed. Flat gray clouds stretched across the
sky, and flooded fields shone like mirrors. In the distance
stood a cluster of warehouses and emergency shelters.

The train slowed beside a temporary wooden platform.

A hand-painted sign read:

HIGH THORN EVACUATION TERMINAL.

Vale opened the door.

"This is not on the railway line," Mara said.

"Not on yours."

She stepped down.

The train vanished almost immediately, not into mist or light,
but simply by no longer being there.

For a while, Mara stood alone on the platform.

Then Reverend Ash came running across the flooded car park,
coat flapping behind him.

"Mara!"

He reached her, seized her shoulders, and stared as though
unsure whether to hug her or check for broken bones.

"How did you get here?"

"Train."

"The line was washed out."

"Not all lines."

Behind him, the shelter doors opened. Evacuees emerged into the
pale morning. Mrs. Wood began to cry when she saw
Mara. The Carter children shouted her name.

Reverend Ash looked at the satchel.

"Did you save anything?"

Mara opened it.

The logbooks were dry. The photograph was dry. But the
silver punch was gone.

In its place lay a thick bundle of cream-colored tickets
edged with gold.

Each one was blank.

Years passed.

Bellweather was never rebuilt. After the final seawall failed, the
government declared the coast unsalvageable. Maps were redrawn. Roads ended
at water. On clear days, boaters claimed they could see
the church spire beneath the waves.

Mara settled at High Thorn.

At first she lived in a prefabricated shelter with a
leaking roof. Later she was given a small house near
the new railway station, which served three resettled towns and
was always crowded.

She became stationmaster there.

She learned the names of commuters and schoolchildren. She kept
spare umbrellas behind the counter. She wrote down the stories
of Bellweather and persuaded the county museum to preserve the
blue bottles that washed ashore.

Above the ticket window, she hung a polished brass sign:

TRAVELERS ARE ADVISED TO KEEP THEIR DESTINATIONS IN MIND.

People often asked what it meant.

Mara always answered, "You'll know when you need to."

She kept the golden tickets in the bottom drawer of
her desk.

For many years, they remained blank.

Then, one winter evening, a boy named Daniel came into
the station after missing the last train home. He was
fifteen, thin and angry, carrying all his possessions in a
torn backpack.

"No one wants me there," he said when Mara offered
to call his family.

"Perhaps not," she replied. "But that doesn't mean no one
wants you anywhere."

He looked at her with the fierce suspicion of the
very young.

She opened the bottom drawer.

One ticket now bore his name.

Mara did not give it to him. Not yet.

Instead, she made tea, found him a blanket, and listened.

Outside, snow began to fall on the tracks. Near midnight,
a bell sounded in the distance.

Mara looked through the window.

Two golden lights had appeared beyond the curve.

She checked the clock, put on her stationmaster's cap, and
stepped onto the platform.

The black train slowed. Steam rolled around its wheels. The
door opened, and Conductor Vale descended with his silver watch
in hand.

He had not aged a day.

"Any passengers tonight, Miss Wren?" he asked.

Mara glanced through the waiting-room window.

Daniel had fallen asleep beside the stove, his head resting
on his backpack. There would be time for him to
choose another road. Time for many trains.

She turned back to Vale.

"Not tonight."

Vale smiled.

"Very good, Stationmaster."

The midnight train moved on, carrying its warm lights into
the snow.

Mara remained on the platform until the last carriage disappeared.

Then she went inside, wound the clock, and waited for
morning.

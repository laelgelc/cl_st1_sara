# The Keeper of Lost Hours

At the end of Bellweather Street stood a shop that
sold no clocks.

It had clocks in the window, certainly—dozens of them. Brass
carriage clocks, painted cuckoo clocks, silver pocket watches, and a
tall walnut grandfather clock whose hands always pointed to eleven
minutes past four. Yet no price tags hung from them,
and no customer had ever been seen carrying one away.

Above the door, faded gold letters read:

**ELIAS VALE
REPAIRER OF LOST HOURS**

Most people assumed this was an old-fashioned way of saying
*watchmaker*. They were wrong.

Mara Venn discovered the truth on a wet Thursday in
November, three weeks after her mother died.

She had spent the morning in her mother's apartment, sorting
a lifetime into cardboard boxes. Clothes for charity. Papers for
the solicitor. Photographs to keep. Broken things to throw away.
Each decision felt cruel. Her mother had touched all of
it: the chipped blue teapot, the wool coat that smelled
faintly of lavender, the yellow scarf Mara had hated as
a child.

By noon, she could bear no more. She left the
half-filled boxes in the hall, put on her own coat,
and walked without choosing a direction.

Rain polished the city to a gray shine. Buses sighed
at curbs. Umbrellas bloomed and collided. Mara kept walking until
she reached Bellweather Street, a narrow lane she had never
noticed before.

She stopped outside Elias Vale's shop because every clock in
the window began to strike at once.

They did not agree on the hour. Some chimed twice,
others nine times. The cuckoo clock's little bird burst through
its door and cried fourteen sharp notes. Beneath the confusion,
Mara heard another sound: her mother laughing.

It was unmistakable.

Not the tired laugh from the hospital, or the polite
laugh she used with strangers, but the wild, breathless laugh
of summer afternoons long ago.

Mara pushed open the shop door.

A bell gave a single low note.

Inside, clocks covered the walls from floor to ceiling. Their
ticking filled the dim room like rain falling through leaves.
Shelves held glass jars, each labeled in black ink. Most
appeared empty.

A man sat behind a counter, peering through a brass
lens at the open back of a pocket watch. He
was very old, or perhaps merely exhausted. His white hair
stood up in soft clouds. He wore a green waistcoat
with seven pockets.

Without looking up, he said, "You heard something."

"My mother."

"That happens."

Mara glanced back at the door. "I should go."

"People often should. They rarely do."

He set down the watch and removed the lens from
his eye. His gaze was pale and steady.

"What did you hear?"

"Her laugh."

"And when was it lost?"

Mara almost told him he was mad. Instead, she remembered
a garden, hot sunlight, and a bowl of strawberries overturned
on the grass.

"I was eight," she said. "Maybe nine."

"Ah. Childhood hours are stubborn. They cling to places."

"What is this shop?"

The old man gestured toward the jars. "When people say
they have lost time, they usually mean they wasted it.
But some hours are truly lost. Forgotten by those who
lived them. Stolen by grief. Misplaced in dreams. Abandoned in
houses that are pulled down. Such hours do not vanish.
They wander."

"And you repair them?"

"When I can."

Mara stared at the nearest jar. Its label read: **One
winter morning, 1978. Smell of toast. No sorrow.**

A second jar said: **Seven minutes before the answer was
given.**

A third: **The last hour in which both brothers were
friends.**

"Are they inside?" she asked.

"Fragments. An hour is too large to keep whole."

"Can you bring one back?"

"For a price."

Mara's hand tightened around the strap of her bag. "How
much?"

"Not money."

Of course, she thought. Magical old men in hidden shops
never wanted money. They wanted names, shadows, firstborn children.

"What, then?"

"One hour of equal weight."

She frowned.

"To recover a lost hour," Elias said, "you must surrender
an hour you still possess."

"You mean a memory?"

"More than a memory. The hour itself. Once given, it
will be as though you never lived it."

"And I can choose?"

"Yes."

Mara thought of the hospital. The white room. Her mother's
hand growing cool in hers. The final hour, when each
breath had seemed too fragile to reach the next.

"I know which one."

Elias's expression changed.

"Everyone does," he said. "At first."

He led her through a curtain behind the counter into
a small room with blue walls. In the center stood
two wooden chairs facing each other. Between them was a
table bearing an hourglass filled with black sand.

"Sit," he said.

Mara took one chair. Elias sat opposite.

"Close your eyes. Think of the hour you wish to
surrender."

She obeyed.

At once she was back in the hospital.

Her mother lay beneath a thin blanket. Rain streaked the
window. The machine beside the bed made a soft, regular
sound. Mara remembered wanting to smash it because its calmness
seemed insulting.

Her mother had opened her eyes once.

"Don't let the last page ruin the book," she whispered.

Mara had leaned closer. "What?"

But her mother was already drifting away.

The rest of the hour had been waiting.

Waiting for another sentence. Waiting for a miracle. Waiting for
the next breath until there was no next breath.

"Hold the hour," Elias said, his voice coming from far
away. "All sixty minutes. Do not look aside."

The black sand began to rise from the lower chamber
of the hourglass. It flowed upward in a thin, impossible
stream.

Mara felt something pull inside her.

Then she heard her mother's words again.

*Don't let the last page ruin the book.*

Mara opened her eyes.

The sand fell still.

"I can't," she said.

Elias nodded as though he had expected this.

"I thought I wanted to forget."

"No. You wanted it not to have happened. That is
different."

Mara stood abruptly. "Then take another hour."

"You must choose it."

She searched her memory. There were bad hours in abundance.
Arguments. Funerals. Nights of loneliness. The afternoon her fiancé, Daniel,
had returned his key and said he had grown tired
of knocking on a door she never opened.

"What happens to the hour I give you?"

"I keep it until it finds where it belongs."

"That tells me nothing."

"It may enter a clock. It may drift into a
dream. It may be used to mend someone else's broken
time."

"So another person could live it?"

"Not as you did. No hour can be repeated exactly."

Mara walked back into the shop. The ticking seemed louder
now, thousands of small mechanical hearts refusing to stop.

On the counter lay an old photograph she had not
noticed before. It showed Elias as a young man beside
a dark-haired woman. They stood in front of the same
shop, though the lettering above the door was new.

"Your wife?" Mara asked.

"Yes."

"Is she dead?"

"Yes."

"Did you recover a lost hour with her?"

Elias slipped the photograph into a drawer.

"I recovered too many."

He turned away, but Mara remained by the counter.

"What does that mean?"

For a long moment, he said nothing. Then he took
a jar from the highest shelf. Unlike the others, it
glowed faintly gold. Its label was worn blank.

"My wife was named Lenora," he said. "She died when
we were thirty-two. Fever. Sudden and senseless."

"I'm sorry."

"I was not. Sorry is a small, clean feeling. I
was furious."

He held the jar carefully.

"I found this shop when another man kept it. I
traded an hour for one I had lost with Lenora.
Then another. Then another. Our wedding morning. A walk beside
the river. The night we decided to have a child.
I surrendered every hour I disliked—every humiliation, failure, quarrel, and
pain."

"That sounds like a good bargain."

"So I believed."

Elias placed the jar on the counter.

"But pain is not stored separately from joy. Hours touch
one another. Remove the night you quarreled, and you may
also lose the apology at dawn. Remove the day you
failed, and the courage you found the next morning has
no root. I carved away my life until only Lenora
remained."

Mara looked at the hundreds of clocks.

"What happened?"

"I recovered our happiest hour." His fingers tightened around the
jar. "I stepped into it and saw her clearly. Her
red dress. Sunlight in her hair. The river behind her.
She looked at me and spoke my name."

His voice faltered.

"I did not know who she was."

The clocks ticked on.

"I had traded away every hour that had made me
the man who loved her."

Mara thought of the boxes in her mother's apartment. She
had been trying to separate what mattered from what did
not, as if a life were a cupboard and grief
merely an exercise in sorting.

"Why do you still do this?" she asked.

"Because lost hours gather. If no one tends them, they
sour."

"Sour?"

"A neglected happy hour becomes regret. A neglected painful hour
becomes fear. Enough of either can poison a house, a
street, sometimes a whole family."

He looked toward the window. Outside, the rain had thickened.

"And because," he added, "I owe a debt."

The grandfather clock struck once.

Every other clock stopped.

Silence swept through the shop.

Elias turned pale.

"What is it?" Mara asked.

He hurried to the window.

In the street, people had frozen.

A woman stood beneath a red umbrella, one foot suspended
above the pavement. A cyclist leaned at an impossible angle.
Raindrops hung in the air like glass beads.

Only Mara and Elias could move.

"Something has found us," he said.

A shadow passed the window.

It had the rough shape of a man, but it
was too tall and thin, and its edges fluttered like
burned paper. Where its face should have been was the
blank white circle of a clock.

Elias seized the glowing jar.

"Into the back room."

"What is that?"

"A hollow hour."

The door rattled in its frame.

Mara did not move. "You said hours don't vanish."

"They do if someone tears out everything inside them. Memory,
feeling, consequence. A hollow hour consumes other hours to fill
itself."

The thing outside tapped on the glass.

Once.

Twice.

A crack appeared in the window.

Elias pushed the jar into Mara's hands. It was warm,
and something moved inside like trapped sunlight.

"Take this to the blue room. Turn the hourglass over
when I tell you."

"What will that do?"

"Open the hour in the jar."

"Your hour with Lenora?"

"Yes."

The window cracked again.

"You said you didn't remember her."

"I do not. But the hollow hour remembers nothing. Even
a joy whose meaning is lost may be enough to
lure it."

"And then?"

"I will close it inside."

"How?"

Elias looked at her, and she understood.

"With an hour of equal weight.

"No," Mara said.

The window shattered inward.

The tall figure entered without stepping over the sill. Around
it, the air faded. The green walls turned gray. The
brass clocks lost their shine. Mara felt memories loosening inside
her: her father singing off-key, Daniel asleep with one hand
under his cheek, her mother tasting soup from a wooden
spoon.

She clutched the jar and ran.

Behind the curtain, the blue room was already paling. Mara
set the jar beside the hourglass.

"Now?" she shouted.

"Not yet!"

A wind roared through the shop. Elias cried out.

Mara turned the hourglass anyway.

Black sand surged upward.

The jar opened.

Summer burst into the room.

Sunlight flooded the walls. The smell of river water and
warm grass replaced dust and oil. For one impossible instant,
the little room became a riverbank beneath a cloudless sky.

A young woman stood near the table.

She wore a red dress. Her dark hair lifted in
the breeze. She was laughing at a young man whom
Mara recognized, after a moment, as Elias.

The hollow hour appeared in the doorway.

Its blank face turned toward Lenora.

The summer scene trembled. The creature stretched out one long
arm, and the sunlight bent toward it.

Old Elias staggered in behind it, holding an empty clock
case.

"Lenora," he whispered.

The woman in red looked at him.

Not at the young Elias beside her. At the old
man.

Her laughter faded.

"You came back," she said.

Elias dropped the clock case.

Mara felt the hour shift. This was not merely a
memory. It had become a doorway, and something on the
other side had recognized him.

The hollow figure moved closer.

"Close it!" Elias shouted.

"How?"

"You must give the hourglass an hour!"

"Which hour?"

"Any hour!"

But Mara knew now that there was no such thing
as *any hour*. Every moment was tied to another by
invisible threads. Pull one free and the pattern changed.

The hollow hour reached the woman in red.

Her hand began to fade.

Mara seized the hourglass.

An hour of equal weight.

She thought of her mother's death, but again she could
not surrender it. She thought of Daniel leaving, but that
hour held truths she had needed. She thought of childhood
shame, failures at work, lonely birthdays, all the moments she
had wished erased.

Each had roots. Each had branches.

Then she understood.

An hour did not have weight because it was happy
or painful. It had weight because of what one chose
to carry from it.

She closed her eyes.

She remembered the day after her mother died.

Morning had entered the apartment without permission. Mara had sat
at the kitchen table for exactly one hour, doing nothing.
No calls. No tears. No thoughts she could later recall.
She had simply watched sunlight move across the floor.

At the time, it had seemed empty.

Now she looked more closely.

Dust floated in the light. Pipes knocked in the wall.
Somewhere upstairs, a child practiced the same three notes on
a piano. On the table sat her mother's blue teapot.
Mara's hands were wrapped around a cup.

And within that hour, beneath the numbness, her life had
continued.

Her heart had beaten. Her lungs had filled and emptied.
Light had crossed the room inch by inch, carrying her
toward the next moment.

It was not hollow.

It was an hour of survival.

"I have one," Mara said.

She placed both hands on the glass.

The memory poured out of her.

The apartment vanished from her mind: the sunlight, the piano
notes, the cup between her palms. She knew the hour
had existed, but she could no longer enter it.

Black sand filled the upper chamber.

The summer riverbank folded inward. Lenora's red dress became a
flame, then a thread, then a single red spark. The
hollow figure twisted as the spark pierced its blank face.

A clock began to tick inside its chest.

Once.

Twice.

The creature collapsed into the empty clock case.

Elias slammed the lid.

Every clock in the shop started at once.

Outside, rain struck the pavement. The cyclist righted himself. The
woman with the red umbrella completed her step. No one
screamed or stared through the broken window. The city continued
as if nothing had happened.

In the blue room, the golden jar was empty.

Elias sat on the floor with the clock case before
him. His face was wet with tears.

"Did you remember her?" Mara asked.

"Yes."

"Will you forget again?"

"Yes."

He smiled.

"But not yet."

Mara helped him stand.

On the counter, something chimed. A small copper clock, green
with age, had begun to glow. Its hands pointed to
eleven minutes past four.

Elias stared at it.

"What?" Mara said.

"Your lost hour."

"My mother's laugh?"

He nodded.

"I did not repair it. You did."

The clock's glass face softened like water.

Mara saw a garden.

She was eight years old, sitting barefoot in the grass.
Her mother, young and strong, carried a bowl of strawberries
from the kitchen. Mara's father said something from behind the
camera. Her mother turned, caught her foot on the garden
hose, and dropped the bowl.

Strawberries rolled everywhere.

For one startled second, no one moved.

Then her mother began to laugh.

Young Mara laughed too. Her father's voice joined them. The
summer afternoon opened wide around the sound.

Mara reached toward the clock.

"Wait," Elias said. "You may enter the hour. You may
remain for what feels like a minute or a lifetime.
But when you return, the memory will begin to fade
again. All recovered hours do."

"Then what's the point?"

"The point is not to keep them."

The garden waited beyond the glass. Her mother knelt in
the grass, gathering strawberries into her skirt.

Mara suddenly understood why Elias had spent his life among
ticking clocks. Not because he believed time could be defeated,
but because he had once mistaken return for rescue.

She lowered her hand.

"I don't need to go in," she said.

Elias raised his eyebrows.

"I heard her laugh. That's enough."

The image faded. The copper clock became ordinary again.

Mara expected sorrow to rush in and fill the space,
but sorrow had been there all along. It had simply
changed shape. It no longer felt like a locked door.
It felt like a room she could leave and enter.

She looked at the shelves of jars.

"What happens to the hour I gave you?"

Elias led her to the window. On the sill stood
a new glass jar, no larger than a saltshaker. A
little morning light shimmered inside.

He wrote a label in black ink:

**One quiet hour after the worst had happened. Proof that
time continued.**

"Someone may need it," he said.

Mara imagined a stranger arriving years from now, convinced that
emptiness contained nothing. She imagined the jar opening and sunlight
moving across a kitchen floor.

"Can hollow hours return?"

"Always."

"And you can stop them?"

"Not forever."

Elias glanced around the damaged shop. The shattered window had
already begun to mend itself, thin veins of glass spreading
across the empty frame.

"I am old, Mara Venn. Older than I look, which
is saying something. This shop requires a keeper."

She laughed once, without humor. "I came in because I
heard my dead mother, and now you're offering me a
job?"

"I am offering you nothing. I am merely stating two
facts in suspicious proximity."

Mara looked through the window.

She had an apartment full of boxes waiting for her.
There was Daniel's number still in her phone, though she
did not know whether she would call it. There was
work on Monday. There were bills, winter, and hundreds of
mornings she had not yet lived.

"I can't stay," she said.

"Good."

Elias took a broom from behind the counter and began
sweeping broken glass.

"Good?"

"The worst keepers are those who wish to hide from
time. Go home. Finish your boxes. Come back tomorrow, if
you choose."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you do not."

Mara reached the door.

The rain had stopped. Water dripped from awnings, and a
pale strip of sky had appeared between the buildings.

Behind her, Elias called, "One more thing."

She turned.

He tossed her a silver pocket watch. Its case was
engraved with tiny strawberries.

"I thought you didn't sell clocks."

"We do not."

"Then what is this?"

"A reminder."

The watch had no hands.

Mara looked up, but Elias had already disappeared behind the
curtain.

She carried the watch to her mother's apartment.

All afternoon, she sorted.

She kept the blue teapot. She gave away the wool
coat. She threw out three drawers of expired coupons, two
broken lamps, and a stack of magazines no one would
ever read. She found photographs she remembered and others she
did not. In one, her mother stood in a red
dress beside a river.

At sunset, Mara opened the window. The city smelled of
wet stone.

She thought of the hour she had surrendered. She could
not remember sitting at the kitchen table the morning after
her mother died. She could not recall the light, the
sounds, or the feel of the cup. Yet its lesson
remained inside her, detached from its origin but strong enough
to stand.

Time continued.

Not kindly. Not cruelly.

It simply continued, carrying everything forward: laughter, loss, strangers, strawberries,
last breaths, first mornings, and all the quiet hours people
mistook for nothing.

The next day, at eleven minutes past four, Mara returned
to Bellweather Street.

The sign above the shop had changed.

The faded gold letters now read:

**ELIAS VALE & MARA VENN
REPAIRERS OF LOST HOURS**

She stood beneath it for a while, pretending to be
annoyed.

Then every clock in the window began to strike.

From somewhere inside, she heard Elias swear as a cuckoo
flew past his head.

Mara opened the door.

The bell gave a single low note, and the clocks
made room for one more hour.

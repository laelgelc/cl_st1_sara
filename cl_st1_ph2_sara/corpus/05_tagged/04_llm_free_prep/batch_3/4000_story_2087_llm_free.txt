By the time the sea reached Bellwether Station, there were
only three trains left on the timetable.

The first carried workers inland at dawn. The second brought
supplies in the afternoon. The third existed only as a
thin gray line on the board above the ticket windows,
with no destination and no hour of departure.

Every morning, Mara Vale climbed a brass ladder and wiped
salt from that line.

No one had told her to do it. No one
had told her not to.

For seventy-two years, Bellwether Station had stood on a cliff
above the town of Lowhaven. Then the water climbed the
seawall, crossed the market square, and swallowed the lower streets.
Now waves rolled through the abandoned post office and struck
the station steps at high tide. From the platform, Mara
could look down through green water and see chimneys, balconies,
and the pale dome of the old courthouse.

The station remained open because the railway company had not
yet decided how to close it.

Mara remained because she was the stationmaster.

She wore the same navy uniform each day, though the
cuffs had frayed and the silver buttons had gone black.
At sunrise, she unlocked the waiting room. At noon, she
wound the clock. At dusk, she lit the platform lamps.
She sold tickets, announced delays, and answered questions about destinations
that were disappearing one by one.

"Two for Halden," a fisherman might say.

"The line beyond Croft is under repair," Mara would reply.

"Since when?"

"Since yesterday."

"When will it reopen?"

"When the water goes down."

They both knew it would not.

One November afternoon, the supply train arrived without supplies.

It came through the rain in a cloud of steam,
brakes screaming, its three empty carriages rattling behind the engine.
The driver, Mr. Pell, climbed down and removed his cap.

He was a broad man who had once laughed with
his whole body. Lately, his laughter stopped at his throat.

"No cargo?" Mara asked.

"No cargo."

"No mail?"

"No mail."

"No passengers?"

"One."

From the last carriage stepped a boy in a yellow
coat.

He was perhaps ten years old. His hair was dark
and neatly cut, and he carried a small wooden box
against his chest. He looked around the flooded town with
grave curiosity, as if comparing it to a picture he
had seen.

"Name?" Mara asked.

"Elias."

"Surname?"

"Just Elias."

"Children don't travel under one name."

"I did."

She glanced at Mr. Pell.

"He had a ticket," the driver said.

"From where?"

Mr. Pell reached into his pocket and handed her a
square of gray card.

The ticket bore the railway company's old winged-wheel emblem. It
had not been used for twenty years. The ink had
faded, but Mara could still read the words.

BELLWETHER STATION
SINGLE JOURNEY
THIRD TRAIN

She turned it over. The back was blank.

"Where did you find this?"

"I didn't," Mr. Pell said. "He gave it to me."

"Where did he board?"

Mr. Pell looked past her at the tracks.

"I don't know."

"That is not a station."

"I stopped at Croft to check the signal. When I
returned to the cab, he was sitting in the last
carriage."

Mara studied the boy. He stood beneath the station clock,
dripping rainwater onto the cracked tiles.

"Who are you traveling with?" she asked.

"My mother."

"Where is she?"

"She hasn't arrived yet."

"And when will she arrive?"

"Before the last train."

Mr. Pell muttered something under his breath.

Mara folded the ticket and slid it into her pocket.
"There is no last train."

Elias looked up at the timetable. "It says there is."

"It gives no time and no destination."

"It wouldn't be the last train if everyone knew when
it was coming."

Outside, a wave broke against the station steps. The sound
passed through the waiting room like distant thunder.

Mr. Pell pulled Mara aside.

"I have orders," he said quietly. "The morning service is
canceled from tomorrow. The line is closing."

"For repairs?"

"For good."

Mara looked at the boy again. He had placed his
wooden box on a bench and was warming his hands
near the iron stove.

"What about the town?"

"Evacuation boats come Sunday."

"Boats."

"Tracks east of Croft are sinking. We barely crossed today."

"And the afternoon service?"

"Today was the last."

The station clock ticked above them.

Mara felt an unreasonable urge to climb the wall and
stop its hands.

"What am I supposed to do with him?" she asked.

"Take him on the boats."

"He says he's waiting for his mother."

"Then she can take the boats too."

Mr. Pell squeezed water from the brim of his cap.
"Come with us now, Mara. There's room in the cab."

"I have to close the station."

"No one would blame you for leaving it open."

"I would."

He gave her the look people reserved for the stubborn
and the doomed. Then he returned to his engine.

The empty train pulled away at four seventeen. Mara and
Elias watched until its red rear lamp vanished into the
rain.

For the first time in seventy-two years, Bellwether Station had
no future service.

Mara locked the ticket office, though there was nothing left
to steal. She took Elias upstairs to the small apartment
where stationmasters had once slept between shifts. She found him
a blanket, a dry shirt, and a bowl of potato
soup.

He ate silently.

"What's in the box?" she asked.

"My mother's voice."

Mara set down her spoon.

Elias opened the box.

Inside lay a cylinder of blue wax and a tiny
hand-cranked phonograph. The instrument was old but beautifully made, with
a brass needle and a horn no larger than a
flower.

"My father built it," Elias said. "You turn the handle."

Mara did.

The cylinder rotated. The needle trembled. Static filled the room,
followed by a woman singing.

The song was faint and broken. Mara could not understand
the words, but the melody made her think of a
window open on a summer night. Beneath the music came
another sound: gulls, perhaps, or children calling from far away.

Then the woman stopped singing and spoke.

"Elias, if you hear this, go to Bellwether. Wait for
me. I will come before the last train."

The cylinder clicked to a halt.

"When did she record that?" Mara asked.

"Tomorrow."

Mara stared at him.

"You mean yesterday."

"No."

"Where is your father?"

"He died before I was born."

"Who gave you the box?"

"My father."

The wind pressed rain against the windows.

Mara had worked among travelers long enough to know that
everyone lied. People lied about the weight of their luggage,
the age of their children, the animals hidden beneath their
coats. They lied about where they were going and why
they were leaving. Most lies were small boats built to
carry people across waters they feared.

Elias's lies were different. They had no shore in sight.

"You should sleep," she said.

He closed the box. "Will you wake me if the
train comes?"

"It won't."

"But if it does?"

Mara looked toward the dark window.

"Yes," she said. "I'll wake you."

That night, the sea entered the station.

At two in the morning, Mara heard glass break below.
She ran downstairs and found water rushing beneath the waiting-room
doors. The next wave burst them inward.

She and Elias carried what they could upstairs: ledgers, blankets,
the emergency lantern, a sack of coal. Mara rescued the
station clock's maintenance key but left the clock itself ticking
above the flood.

By dawn, silver fish swam across the waiting-room floor.

The evacuation boats arrived under a white sky. There were
six of them, painted orange, with uniformed officers shouting through
loudspeakers.

Residents emerged from the upper town carrying suitcases and wrapped
bundles. Some brought portraits. Some brought tools. One old woman
carried a rosebush in a cooking pot.

Mara stood behind the ticket counter in knee-deep water and
stamped evacuation papers.

"Why?" asked the boat officer. "These aren't tickets."

"People need a record of departure."

"They need life jackets."

"They can have both."

Near noon, the officer announced that the final boat would
leave at three.

Mara found Elias on the platform, watching the drowned rooftops.

"You're going with them," she said.

"My mother isn't here."

"She may be waiting inland."

"She told me to wait."

"You cannot stay."

"Neither can you."

"I am responsible for the station."

"And I am responsible for my mother."

"You are ten."

"Nine."

"That makes it worse."

He held the wooden box tighter.

Mara crouched in front of him. "Listen to me. Sometimes
people make promises they cannot keep. Not because they mean
to deceive us, but because the world changes before they
can return."

"She will return."

"The railway is closed."

"She knows."

"The tracks are underwater."

"She knows that too."

"Then how will she come?"

Elias looked at her with a sorrowful patience that made
him seem much older.

"On the train."

At two forty-five, the boat officer came for them.

Mara had packed one suitcase. Inside were her uniform coat,
three station ledgers, a photograph of her parents, and the
brass bell used to announce departures. Elias stood beside her.

The boat waited at the foot of the steps, rising
and falling on the waves.

"This is everyone?" the officer asked.

Mara looked back.

Bellwether Station had survived winter storms, two wars, a fire
in the parcel room, and the famous summer when ten
thousand pilgrims arrived to see a cloud shaped like an
angel. It had welcomed kings and refugees, lovers and prisoners,
soldiers who returned, and soldiers who did not. For decades,
Mara had believed that stations existed to help people leave.

Only now did she understand that they also existed to
remember who had gone.

She locked the doors, though the doors no longer met
their frames. Then she placed the key beneath the ticket-window
ledge, where the next stationmaster would know to look.

"All aboard!" called the officer.

Mara took Elias's hand.

From somewhere beyond the rain came a whistle.

It was low, long, and unmistakable.

The officer turned. "What was that?"

Elias pulled free of Mara.

A second whistle sounded.

The rails west of the station curved along the cliff
before vanishing into a tunnel. That tunnel had flooded in
spring. Yet a light now glowed within it—a pale circle,
growing larger.

"Back to the boat!" shouted the officer.

The drowned tracks began to sing.

Mara had heard that sound every day of her adult
life: the humming of rails beneath an approaching engine. It
traveled through the station walls, through the iron columns, through
the soles of her shoes.

The platform lamps flickered on by themselves.

A locomotive emerged from the flooded tunnel.

It was black, immense, and streaming water. No steam rose
from its chimney. Seaweed hung from its wheels. Its lamps
burned blue. Behind it came seven carriages, each from a
different age: one paneled in polished mahogany, one made of
riveted steel, one painted in the railway company's newest colors,
and others of designs Mara did not recognize.

The train slowed without a sound.

Its doors opened.

The boat officer fled down the steps. Most of the
evacuees followed, pushing their boats away from the station. But
some remained, staring.

People stood inside the carriages.

Mara saw faces through the rain-streaked windows: a young soldier
with a bandaged head, a woman in a bridal veil,
three miners covered in white dust, a child holding a
paper windmill. They wore clothes from many decades. Some looked
frightened. Some looked peaceful. All seemed to be waiting.

A conductor stepped onto the platform.

He was tall and thin, dressed in an old red
uniform. His cap was decorated with the winged-wheel emblem from
Elias's ticket.

"Bellwether Station," he announced. "Terminus and departure point."

Mara's training overcame her terror.

"Departure for where?"

The conductor consulted a silver watch. "Elsewhere."

"That is not a destination."

"It is the oldest destination."

"Where is this train from?"

"Elsewhere."

"You cannot use the same answer for both."

"Why not? Most journeys end where others begin."

Elias hurried forward. "Is my mother aboard?"

The conductor looked at him. For the first time, his
formal expression softened.

"Not yet."

"She said she would come."

"So she shall."

"When?"

The conductor opened his watch.

"Soon, from her direction. Long ago, from yours."

Mara stepped between them. "This child has an invalid ticket."

"No," the conductor said. "It is perfectly valid."

"There is no date."

"The date has not happened."

"There is no destination."

"The passenger has not chosen it."

"There is no fare."

"It has been paid."

"By whom?"

The conductor looked at Mara.

"You."

A coldness spread through her chest.

"I have never seen that ticket."

"Not yet."

The station clock struck three.

Its bell rang underwater, each note deep and distorted. With
the final stroke, every door on the train closed except
one.

Then the woman arrived.

She came through the station, wading across the flooded waiting
room. She was perhaps thirty years old and wore a
gray traveling dress. Blood stained one sleeve. In her arms,
wrapped in a yellow coat, she carried a newborn baby.

Elias made a sound like a gasp and a sob
together.

The woman stepped onto the platform.

She saw him and stopped.

For a moment, neither moved.

Then Elias ran to her.

She knelt, still holding the baby, and wrapped one arm
around him. He buried his face against her shoulder. She
whispered his name again and again.

Mara could not speak. The woman's face was unmistakable: the
same dark eyes, the same narrow chin. She was Elias's
mother, but ten years too young.

The woman looked at the boy's wooden box.

"You received it," she said.

"You haven't made it yet," he replied.

"No. But I will."

She kissed his forehead, then turned to Mara.

"Thank you for waiting with him."

"Who is the baby?" Mara asked, though she already knew.

The woman looked down.

The infant slept inside the yellow coat.

"This is Elias."

Rain fell through the blue light of the train lamps.

Mara felt the platform tilt beneath her.

"How can he be there and here?"

"He isn't. Not for long."

The conductor cleared his throat. "Madam, you have a choice.
The infant may travel forward with you, though the journey
is uncertain. Or the boy may travel back, and the
circle will close."

Elias's mother held him tighter.

"What happens if I choose the boy?" she asked.

"You return to the night you left. You keep the
child. You lose the years that brought him here."

"Will I remember him?"

"In dreams, perhaps."

"And if I choose the baby?"

"The boy remains here. You travel beyond the flooded years."

"To safety?"

"I did not say safety."

"What happens to him?"

The conductor glanced at the rising sea.

"That depends on the stationmaster."

Mara understood then.

The fare had not been money. It was a decision
no mother should have to make, placed in the hands
of a stranger who had spent her life sending people
away.

Mara reached into her pocket and found the gray ticket.

The rain had not damaged it. Fresh black letters now
covered the reverse.

ONE PASSENGER ONLY.

She thought of all the travelers she had watched through
the years. Parents waving until trains rounded the bend. Children
pressing their palms to windows. Lovers promising to write. Every
departure contained a small cruelty: someone was always left on
the platform.

"No," Mara said.

The conductor raised an eyebrow.

"This ticket is invalid."

"You said that before."

"I am saying it officially now."

"On what grounds?"

"Bellwether Station regulations, article seventeen: no child under twelve may
travel alone."

"He will not be alone."

"Article twenty-two: passengers may not occupy two points on the
same railway."

"A rarely enforced rule."

"Article thirty-one: the stationmaster may suspend service when conditions are
unsafe."

The conductor smiled slightly. "Conditions have never been safe."

"Then I should have suspended service years ago."

She took the brass departure bell from her suitcase.

"As stationmaster, I am canceling this train."

The people in the carriages began to murmur. The blue
lamps dimmed.

The conductor's smile vanished. "You cannot cancel the last train."

"I can cancel any train that stops at my station."

"This station is closed."

"Not until I say so."

The sea surged over the platform edge. Water rose around
their ankles.

The conductor shut his watch.

"If the train does not depart, Bellwether will remain between
moments. The flood will never fall. The passengers will never
arrive. You will wait here forever."

Mara looked through the windows at the silent travelers. They
had waited long enough already.

"Then issue another ticket," she said.

"To whom?"

"To me."

Elias turned. "No."

"One passenger only," Mara said. "That is the rule, isn't
it?"

The conductor studied her.

"You do not know where the train goes."

"I have sold tickets to places I have never seen."

"You may not return."

"I have nowhere to return to."

The words hurt less than she expected.

Lowhaven was beneath the sea. The railway was closed. The
station would soon be gone. Mara had spent her life
standing still while others traveled toward weddings, funerals, new jobs,
new countries, and new names.

Perhaps a final train was not only for the dead
or the lost.

Perhaps it was for those who had forgotten to leave.

The conductor held out his hand.

Mara gave him Elias's ticket.

He tore it neatly in half. One piece dissolved into
rain. On the other, new words appeared.

MARA VALE
SINGLE JOURNEY
DESTINATION: UNDECIDED

Elias gripped her sleeve. "You can't go."

"Everyone says that at stations."

"You promised to wake me if the train came."

"And I did."

His mother shifted the sleeping infant into one arm and
took the boy's hand.

"Will we meet you again?" Elias asked.

Mara looked at the train, at its many carriages and
impossible passengers.

"All lines connect somewhere."

It was not an answer, but railway people knew that
some questions could only be answered by departure.

She picked up her suitcase.

The conductor helped her aboard.

Inside, the carriage smelled of coal smoke, oranges, and summer
rain. Passengers made room for her. The young soldier lifted
her suitcase onto the rack. The bride smiled. The child
with the paper windmill offered Mara the window seat.

Through the glass, she saw Elias standing beside his mother
and his infant self.

The conductor raised his hand.

"Stationmaster," he called, "your final duty."

Mara opened the window and lifted the brass bell.

For seventy-two years, its voice had announced every departure from
Bellwether. It had rung for ordinary mornings and historic nights,
for crowded holiday trains and empty funeral cars. Now its
clear note rose above the storm.

The station clock began moving backward.

Water poured out of the waiting room. Waves retreated down
the steps. Roofs emerged from the sea, streaming silver. Streets
unrolled from the depths. Windows repaired themselves. Lights appeared behind
curtains.

On the platform, Elias grew younger.

Ten became nine, nine became five, five became one. His
mother lifted him as he shrank into her arms. The
second infant faded like mist. The wooden phonograph box remained
at her feet.

She picked it up.

For one instant, she looked directly at Mara. Recognition flickered
in her eyes—not memory, perhaps, but the beginning of a
dream.

Then the train moved.

Bellwether Station slid past the window. Mara saw it restored
to its first morning, bricks bright, clock shining, rails reaching
toward an unwritten horizon. A younger version of herself stood
on the platform in a new uniform, waiting nervously for
her first day of work.

The younger Mara heard the train and looked up.

Their eyes met.

Mara raised one hand.

So did the girl on the platform.

Then the station disappeared into sunlight.

The passengers sighed around her. Some laughed. Some wept. One
by one, they began to fade as the train carried
them toward their chosen years.

The conductor entered the carriage and punched Mara's ticket.

"Where would you like to go?" he asked.

She looked out the window.

Beyond the glass lay no tracks, only a wide gold
landscape crossed by countless shining lines. Some curved into cities.
Some climbed mountains. Some vanished among stars. None had names.

For the first time in her life, no timetable told
Mara what came next.

She smiled.

"Forward," she said.

The conductor tipped his cap.

And the last train obeyed.

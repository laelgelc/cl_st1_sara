The Clockmaker of Bellweather Street

Every clock in Bellweather stopped at 3:17 on the morning
Elias Vale disappeared.

The tall clock in the railway station froze with its
brass minute hand trembling above the third notch. The painted
cuckoo in Mrs. Pender's kitchen pushed open its little wooden
door, forgot to sing, and remained there with its beak
parted. Pocket watches, alarm clocks, the silver carriage clock in
the mayor's bedroom—all fell silent at precisely the same moment.

Only one clock continued to tick.

It stood in the window of Vale & Daughter, Clockmakers,
beneath a drift of dust and a hand-painted sign that
had not been changed in twenty-six years. It was a
plain black clock with a white face, no numbers, and
three narrow hands. The shortest hand moved as a clock's
hour hand should. The longer counted minutes. The third hand
traveled backward.

Mara Vale found her father's bed empty at sunrise.

His slippers waited neatly beneath it. His spectacles lay on
the bedside table, folded beside a half-finished glass of water.
His coat was gone, though the rain had been falling
all night and his umbrella remained in the hall.

On his pillow rested a brass key.

Mara held it in her palm and knew at once
that it belonged to no clock in the shop. She
had cleaned every gear, polished every pendulum, and fitted nearly
every key since she was old enough to climb onto
the workbench. This key was heavier than it looked. Its
bow was shaped like an eye, and its shaft was
etched with tiny marks resembling waves.

She ran downstairs.

The workshop smelled of oil, cedar, and old metal. Hundreds
of clocks covered the walls. Some had belonged to ship
captains, schoolmistresses, judges, and thieves. Some had traveled across oceans.
Others had never left Bellweather Street. Now they were all
silent.

Except for the black clock in the window.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Its backward hand moved steadily toward the top of the
face.

Mara's father had never allowed her to touch it.

"Some clocks tell time," he would say whenever she asked
about it. "Some clocks keep it. That one does neither."

"What does it do?"

"It remembers."

She had thought this was one of his clockmaker's jokes.
Elias Vale was fond of saying that watches were vain
because they always wanted to be looked at, and that
hourglasses had narrow minds. But he had never smiled when
speaking of the black clock.

Mara took it from the window.

The case was colder than the morning air. On the
back, beneath a layer of dust, she found a keyhole
shaped like an eye.

The brass key fit perfectly.

Before she could turn it, someone knocked at the front
door.

Mayor Lorne entered without waiting to be invited. He was
a broad, red-faced man who wore his mayoral chain even
before breakfast. Behind him stood Constable Rusk, dripping rain onto
the floor.

"Miss Vale," the mayor said, glancing at the silent clocks,
"there is a situation."

"My father is missing."

"Yes. That may be part of it."

"Part of what?"

The mayor took out his watch and shook it as
though trying to wake a lazy servant.

"Every clock in Bellweather has stopped. The railway signals are
frozen. The mill whistles did not sound. No one knows
when to open the market, begin school, or change shifts
at the glassworks."

"The sun still rose," Mara said.

Mayor Lorne frowned. "The sun does not run a railway."

Constable Rusk wandered toward the window. Mara quickly placed the
black clock behind a stack of ledgers.

"When did you last see your father?" he asked.

"At eleven. He was working."

"On what?"

"I don't know."

That was not entirely true. Elias had spent the evening
searching through old drawers and muttering about an appointment. At
ten minutes before eleven, he had asked Mara whether she
remembered the flood.

She had been six when the River Orr burst its
banks. What she remembered was water in the streets, lamps
floating like fallen stars, and her mother lifting her onto
the roof of the bakery. She remembered her father rowing
toward them through the storm.

She also remembered—though no one believed her—a bell ringing beneath
the water.

"What appointment?" Constable Rusk asked when she told him.

"He didn't say."

The mayor sighed. "Find him, Rusk. And Miss Vale, repair
the town clocks."

"All of them?"

"You are a clockmaker."

"Apprentice clockmaker."

"Today you have been promoted."

They left behind a trail of rainwater and the mayor's
impatience.

Mara locked the door. Then she returned to the black
clock and turned the key.

The backward hand stopped.

A sound rose from inside the case—not the chime she
expected, but a distant rushing, like water moving through a
tunnel. The workshop windows darkened. For a moment, Mara smelled
river mud and wet stone.

Then a voice whispered from the clock.

"Mara."

She nearly dropped it.

The voice came again, faint beneath the rushing water.

"If you're hearing this, I have failed."

Her father.

Mara pressed her ear to the cold case.

"Go to the station," Elias whispered. "Locker seventeen. Bring the
clock. Trust no one who remembers the missing hour."

The voice faded. The backward hand resumed its motion.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

At the railway station, disorder had replaced routine. Passengers crowded
beneath the frozen clock, arguing over timetables that no longer
meant anything. The stationmaster stood on a chair, shouting that
the northbound train would depart when he judged it to
be nine o'clock.

"How will you judge that?" someone called.

"By experience!"

Mara slipped past the ticket office and found the lockers
near Platform Two. Number seventeen was locked, but the brass
key opened it.

Inside lay a blue wool coat.

It belonged to Mara's mother.

Mara had not seen it since the flood.

She lifted it with shaking hands. The coat was dry,
clean, and smelled faintly of lavender. In one pocket she
found a train ticket dated October 14, twenty years earlier—the
day of the flood. In the other was a folded
note.

Mara,

If Elias has given you the key, then the river
is rising again, even if the streets are dry.

Come to the old bell tower before the backward hand
reaches twelve.

I am sorry you had to grow up without me.

—Mother

Mara read it three times.

Her mother, Celene Vale, had drowned in the flood. Elias
had found Mara alone on the bakery roof and searched
for Celene until exhaustion pulled him from the boat. The
river had given up carts, furniture, livestock, and six bodies.

Celene's had never been found.

The station seemed to tilt around Mara. She gripped the
locker door until the iron edge hurt her fingers.

"Interesting," said a voice behind her.

Mayor Lorne stood at the end of the corridor.

Without his chain, he might have passed unnoticed in his
gray overcoat and low hat. But his heavy gold watch
chain still crossed his waistcoat.

Mara pushed the note into her sleeve.

"You followed me."

"I came to check on the railway."

"You said the sun couldn't run it."

"Neither can the stationmaster, apparently." His eyes moved to the
blue coat. "Where did you get that?"

"You recognize it?"

Something changed in his expression—too quickly to name.

"No."

Mara remembered the clock's warning.

Trust no one who remembers the missing hour.

"What happened at 3:17?" she asked.

The mayor became very still.

"Your father told you."

"He told me nothing."

"Then keep it that way."

Mayor Lorne took one step closer. Mara slammed the locker
door into him and ran.

She crossed the platform as the stationmaster blew his whistle.
With a groan and a burst of steam, the northbound
train began to move. Mara jumped onto the rear step,
clung to the brass rail, and dropped to the far
side before the train gathered speed.

The mayor shouted behind her, but the carriages rolled between
them.

Mara ran toward the river.

The old bell tower stood on a hill beyond the
abandoned quarter, where the flood had ruined twenty streets and
left houses leaning like tired men. No bell had hung
in the tower for a century. According to town legend,
it had fallen during a storm and sunk so deeply
into the earth that no one could find it.

As Mara climbed the hill, the black clock grew colder
beneath her arm. Its backward hand was only two hours
from twelve.

At the tower door she found fresh footprints in the
mud.

Inside, a spiral staircase wound upward, but the footprints led
down through a trapdoor concealed beneath rotting boards. Mara lifted
the iron ring and descended.

The steps ended in a round chamber below ground. Water
glimmered across the floor, though the river lay half a
mile away. In the center stood a great bronze bell,
green with age, buried up to its rim in black
water.

Beside it waited a woman in a blue dress.

Her hair was streaked with silver, and her face was
older than the one in Mara's photographs. Yet Mara knew
her immediately. She had the same narrow chin, the same
dark eyes, and the same small scar beside her mouth.

"Mother?"

Celene Vale smiled, and the twenty lost years vanished from
her face.

"My little bird," she said.

Mara did not remember crossing the chamber. One moment she
stood at the stairs; the next she was in her
mother's arms, breathing lavender and rain.

Questions rose in her, tangled and sharp.

"You're alive. Where have you been? Why didn't you come
back? Father said—he thought—"

"I know." Celene touched Mara's cheek. "For me, the flood
was yesterday."

She led Mara to the bell. Symbols covered its surface:
waves, stars, open eyes, and circles divided into hours.

"Bellweather was built around this," Celene said. "Long before there
was a railway or a glassworks, people came here to
ask the bell for more time. A minute to escape
a fire. An hour to reach a dying child. A
day to prepare for war."

"It grants wishes?"

"No. It makes exchanges."

Celene knelt and touched the water. Ripples spread, reflecting not
the chamber ceiling but a storm-black sky.

"During the flood, the bakery roof collapsed. You fell into
the river. I rang the bell and asked for one
hour to save you. The bell gave it to me.
Time stopped across Bellweather, though only four of us remained
awake: you, Elias, Mayor Lorne, and me."

"I don't remember."

"You were very young."

"But Father saved me."

"We both did." Celene's voice trembled. "We found you caught
beneath a gate downstream. But when the hour ended, the
bell demanded payment. One hour borrowed; one life removed from
time. I chose myself before Elias could."

Mara stared at her. "You've been here for twenty years?"

"Not exactly. I have been inside that hour. I can
walk its edges, but I cannot leave. Elias spent his
life trying to free me."

The black clock ticked in Mara's arms.

Celene looked at it with sorrow.

"He built that to hold memories of the missing hour.
Every year it carried a little more of the burden.
Last night he tried to reverse the exchange."

"What happened to him?"

"He entered the hour."

"Then we bring him out."

Celene shook her head. "The bell will not release us
both. Elias knew that."

From above came the crash of the tower door.

Mayor Lorne descended the stairs, breathing hard. Constable Rusk followed
with one hand on his baton.

"Step away from the bell," the mayor said.

Celene faced him. "Garrick."

He flinched at the sound of his first name.

"You haven't aged a day," he whispered.

"I have, just not where you could see."

Constable Rusk looked from Celene to Mara. "Mayor, who is
this?"

"A ghost," Lorne said. "And ghosts should remain buried."

He pulled a small pistol from his coat.

Rusk seized his wrist. "That's enough."

The shot exploded through the chamber. It struck the bell.

The bronze rang.

The sound was too deep to hear with ears. Mara
felt it in her bones, a vast note rolling outward
beneath the town.

The water rose.

It climbed over Mara's boots in an instant, icy and
fierce. The chamber walls disappeared behind curtains of rain. The
tower became transparent, then vanished entirely.

Mara stood in Bellweather on the night of the flood.

Rain fell upward.

Houses floated in darkness. A horse hung motionless above the
street, suspended in a wave. Lamps burned beneath the water.
Across the rooftops, hundreds of people remained frozen in terror,
untouched by the missing hour.

Constable Rusk was gone. Mayor Lorne stood waist-deep in the
river, younger now, his red face pale.

The clock's backward hand was minutes from twelve.

"Mara!" Celene pointed.

A rowboat approached against the current.

Elias Vale sat inside.

He looked as Mara had seen him that morning—gray-haired, thin,
wearing his workshop coat. His spectacles were gone. Blood ran
from a cut above his eye.

He reached for them as the boat passed, but an
invisible current dragged him away.

"There's no time!" he shouted.

Mara almost laughed at the absurdity of it.

Celene caught the boat's rope and wrapped it around the
iron gate where, twenty years before, they had found their
daughter drowning. The boat swung around.

Elias climbed out and embraced Mara.

"You should not have come," he said.

"You left me a key."

"I hoped you would be less obedient."

"You raised me badly."

He smiled, but grief filled his eyes when he saw
Celene.

They stood together in the impossible storm: three members of
a family separated by an hour that had lasted twenty
years.

Mayor Lorne waded toward the bell, which now rose from
the center of the flooded street.

"You can't undo it," he said. "You don't know what
happens if you undo it."

Elias turned on him. "Tell her."

Lorne's mouth tightened.

"Tell me what?" Mara asked.

The mayor looked toward the frozen roofs.

"During the missing hour, I robbed the town bank."

Even the rain seemed to hesitate.

"I was a clerk," he continued. "Poor, invisible, and tired
of both. When time stopped, I discovered I could move.
I took enough to leave Bellweather. But then Celene rang
the bell again and surrendered herself. The hour ended. The
bank blamed the missing money on the flood."

"And you built your life with it," Mara said.

"I invested it. I rebuilt the mill. The station. Half
the town exists because of what I did."

"Half the town belongs to a thief," Elias replied.

"If the hour is restored, everyone will remember. History will
correct itself. Celene may return, yes—but the flood will finish
what it began. People your wife saved during that hour
may die. Buildings moved, gates opened, children carried to roofs—all
of it will be undone."

Mara looked at her mother.

"Is that true?"

Celene nodded. "The bell can release the hour, but it
cannot preserve what happened inside it."

The backward hand crept closer to twelve.

"Then what does the clock do?" Mara asked.

Elias laid his hand on its black case.

"It remembers," he said. "And memory is a kind of
time."

He explained quickly. The clock had collected the missing hour
piece by piece: a reflection from the river, a chime
from the buried bell, the memories carried by the four
who had moved while the town stood still. At midnight
backward—when the third hand reached twelve—it could replace the hour
without erasing it.

"But the bell still requires a life outside time," Celene
said.

"Yes."

Mara understood before her father spoke.

"No."

"Mara—"

"No. You don't get to decide."

"I already have."

Elias took the clock from her, but Mara held on.

"You left without saying goodbye."

"Because I could not."

"That isn't good enough."

"No," he said softly. "It isn't."

The water surged around them. On a nearby roof, six-year-old
Mara appeared, frozen beneath the rain, her small hands reaching
toward the gate. Celene gasped at the sight.

The past was folding inward.

Elias touched his daughter's forehead.

"When your mother vanished, I gave my life to bringing
her back. I told myself it was love. Perhaps it
was. But love can become a room with no doors.
I made you grow up inside that room."

"You were my father."

"Not often enough."

The clock ticked.

Five seconds.

Mayor Lorne lunged for it.

Constable Rusk emerged from the rain and struck him across
the jaw. The mayor fell into the water.

Four seconds.

Celene gripped Elias's hand.

"No," she said. "I chose this."

"Then let me choose now."

Three.

Mara held both of them.

There had been years when she had imagined her mother's
return. In those dreams, nothing was difficult. Her mother stepped
through the shop door; her father laughed; they ate supper
while every clock in the house chimed at once.

But time did not return what it took. It changed
everything it touched and offered no apologies.

Two.

Mara tore the brass key from the clock.

The backward hand stopped.

Silence consumed the storm.

Elias stared at her. "What have you done?"

"Made a different exchange."

She thrust the key into the eye-shaped mark on the
bell.

It fit.

The bell opened.

Not like a door, but like an eye.

Inside burned all the hours Bellweather had ever lost: final
breaths, forgotten birthdays, sleepless nights, childhood summers, delayed trains, moments
wasted in anger, moments spent waiting for letters that never
came.

Mara understood then that the bell had never demanded a
life. It had demanded time, and people—fearful, hurried people—had given
it the largest thing they could imagine.

She turned the key.

"Take one hour from everyone," she said.

The bell answered.

From every citizen of Bellweather, it took a single forgotten
hour: an hour spent staring at a ceiling, standing in
a queue, watching rain, waiting for sleep. From thousands of
lives came thousands of small, unnoticed pieces of time.

Enough to pay the debt.

The black clock shattered.

Light poured across the water.

Mara felt her parents' hands torn from hers. She called
out, but the bell's great voice carried every sound away.

Then every clock in Bellweather began to ring.

Mara woke on the floor of the buried chamber.

Constable Rusk lay nearby, groaning. Mayor Lorne sat against the
wall with his face in his hands. The pistol had
vanished. So had the flood.

The bell was silent.

At its base, Elias and Celene stood together.

For a moment no one moved.

Then Mara ran to them.

Later, the people of Bellweather would disagree about what happened
that morning. Some insisted the clocks had never stopped. Others
remembered losing an hour but could not say when. The
stationmaster swore the northbound train departed twice. Mrs. Pender claimed
her cuckoo began singing backward.

Everyone remembered Mayor Lorne walking into the constable's office and
confessing to a robbery committed twenty years before.

The missing money could not be returned, having long ago
become factories, houses, scholarships, and polished monuments bearing Lorne's name.
The town removed the monuments and kept the scholarships.

As for Vale & Daughter, Clockmakers, the sign was changed
at last.

It became Vale, Vale & Daughter.

Celene struggled with the modern clocks, which she said lacked
dignity. Elias learned to leave the workshop before supper. Mara
became the finest clockmaker in three counties, though she refused
to repair any clock that had been thrown deliberately at
a wall.

Years later, when her parents were old and the story
of the missing hour had faded into legend, Mara made
a new black clock.

It had a white face, no numbers, and only two
hands.

A customer once asked why it had no second hand.

"Seconds are too small to worry over," Mara said.

"What does it measure?"

She looked through the workshop window. Her father was asleep
in a chair beneath the pear tree. Her mother was
reading beside him, one hand resting on his shoulder. Evening
sunlight filled the garden, and from the town came the
overlapping music of a hundred imperfect clocks.

"It doesn't measure anything," Mara said. "It reminds."

"Of what?"

"That time is not what we lose."

The customer waited for her to explain, but Mara only
smiled and wound the clock.

Outside, the bell in the new tower struck five—slightly late,
as usual—and all along Bellweather Street, people paused for a
moment before continuing home.

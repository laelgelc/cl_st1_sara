The Bell at Low Water

Every morning at six, Mara Vale climbed the lighthouse stairs
with a brass key in one hand and a cup
of black tea in the other.

There were one hundred and twelve steps. She knew where
the stone was chipped on the thirty-seventh, where the iron
rail grew coldest on the sixty-fourth, and where the tower
leaned just enough to make the final turn feel steeper
than the rest. At the top, she unlocked the glass
chamber, extinguished the lamp, and watched daylight spread over the
sea.

For twenty-three years, this had been her work.

The lighthouse stood on Saint Orra, a narrow island shaped
like a sleeping dog. A village occupied the sheltered eastern
side, while the western cliffs faced the open ocean. In
winter, waves struck those cliffs hard enough to shake spoons
in kitchen drawers. In summer, the water shone blue and
mild, and visitors came on the ferry to buy postcards,
eat crab soup, and complain that there was no signal
for their telephones.

Mara lived alone in the keeper's cottage. Her husband, Tomas,
had vanished at sea fourteen years earlier. His boat had
been found with its sail torn and its mast broken,
but his body had never returned.

On Saint Orra, the missing were treated differently from the
dead.

The dead had stones in the churchyard. Their names collected
lichen. People brought flowers, said prayers, and eventually learned to
speak of them in the past tense.

The missing had no place to rest. They remained in
doorways, in weather reports, in empty chairs. They were always
about to come home.

For the first seven years, Mara watched every sail.

For the next seven, she told herself she did not.

On a gray morning in October, as she reached the
ninety-eighth step, she heard a bell ringing beneath the sea.

She stopped.

The sound came faintly through the lighthouse walls.

One clear note.

Then another.

Mara held her breath. The island church had one bell,
but this sound did not come from the village. It
rose from somewhere west of the cliffs, low and deep,
with a long trembling echo.

She climbed to the lamp room and looked out.

The tide was at its lowest. Black rocks curved through
the water like the spine of a buried animal. Beyond
them, fog lay across the horizon.

The bell rang a third time.

Mara set down her tea.

She knew the old story. Everyone on Saint Orra did.

Long before the lighthouse, there had been another island west
of the cliffs. It was called Merrow, and it had
fields, wells, a chapel, and thirty houses roofed in red
clay. One winter night, after weeks of storms, the sea
rose over it. By morning, Merrow was gone.

According to the story, its chapel bell still rang at
low water. Some said it warned of shipwreck. Others said
it called the drowned home. Children were told never to
follow it, because those who did would walk into the
sea and keep walking until the water closed over their
heads.

Mara had never believed the story. She had heard wind
in sea caves, chains knocking against harbor posts, and buoys
tolling miles away. The ocean made strange music, especially when
people were eager to hear it.

Still, that morning, she searched the water until her tea
went cold.

At the base of the tower, she found Eli Cress
waiting beside the door.

Eli was ten years old, thin as a mast, and
always missing one button from his coat. His grandmother ran
the island shop. His father worked on cargo ships and
returned twice a year with foreign coins, carved animals, and
promises to remain ashore next time.

"Did you hear it?" Eli asked.

"Hear what?"

"The bell."

Mara locked the lighthouse door. "Probably the western buoy."

"The western buoy broke loose in March."

"Then perhaps it came back."

"It washed up in Dunley," Eli said. "Mr. Pell saw
it when he went for his teeth."

Mara gave him a sharp look, but his face was
solemn.

"Sound travels oddly in fog," she said.

"There isn't any fog here."

"There is beyond the rocks."

Eli considered this, then followed her down the path.

"What does it mean?"

"It means you're late for school."

"It rang three times."

"Then you're three times late."

She expected him to grin. He did not.

At the village school, the teacher had also heard the
bell. So had the baker, the harbor master, two fishermen,
and old Nessa Crow, who claimed she remembered it ringing
in 1958, just before a passenger ship struck the northern
reef.

By noon, the entire island was talking about it.

Mara spent the afternoon checking weather instruments. Pressure was dropping,
but not dangerously. The forecast promised rain and moderate winds
from the southwest. She inspected the emergency generator, polished the
lamp lenses, and tested the radio.

Everything worked.

At sunset, she lit the great lamp. Its beam swept
across the darkening water in steady white intervals.

Far offshore, something flashed in answer.

Mara leaned toward the glass.

There it was again: a small yellow light, low on
the western horizon.

She took up the binoculars. The sea was roughening, lifting
in long steel-colored swells. The light disappeared behind them, then
returned.

A boat, she thought.

But no vessel had been reported nearby, and the yellow
glow did not move north or south. It remained fixed
beyond the drowned rocks of Merrow.

Mara reached for the radio.

Before her fingers touched it, the bell rang.

The note rolled through the tower, stronger than before.

The yellow light went out.

The next morning, Eli was waiting again.

"My grandmother says Merrow comes back when it wants something,"
he announced.

"Is that so?"

"She says it borrows the bell from the dead."

Mara swept rainwater from the cottage step. "Your grandmother also
says cold potatoes cure warts."

"They cured mine."

"You never had a wart."

"That proves how well they worked."

This time Mara smiled despite herself.

Eli looked toward the western cliffs. "I saw a light
last night."

"So did I."

His eyes widened. "What was it?"

"I don't know."

"Can we go and see?"

"No."

"At low tide, there's a path."

"There are rocks. Slippery ones."

"Jory Flint went halfway last summer."

"Jory Flint also put a live crab in the church
collection box."

"He said it was all he had."

Mara rested the broom against the wall. "Listen to me.
The western shore is dangerous, especially with the pressure falling.
Stay away from it."

Eli scuffed his boot against the ground. "My father was
supposed to come home yesterday."

Mara's irritation faded.

"Which ship?"

"The North Lark. It reached Bellhaven, but he wasn't on
it. Gran says he probably changed routes."

"Probably."

"He didn't send word."

Mara knew what silence could grow into. At first it
was a small empty space. Then it became a room,
then a house, then a country in which a person
might live for years.

"Cargo schedules change," she said. "Radios fail. Men forget to
send messages."

"Did Tomas forget?"

The question struck with childish accuracy.

Eli immediately looked ashamed. "I'm sorry."

Mara picked up the broom. "Go to school."

He went.

All day, rain crossed the island in silver curtains. At
three in the afternoon, the ferry canceled its evening journey.
At four, the mainland weather office issued a gale warning.

Mara climbed to the lamp room before dusk. The wind
had turned west and was rising fast. White water broke
over the outer rocks. She lit the lamp early and
watched its beam cut through rain.

At six, the harbor master called.

"Small boat missing out of Bellhaven," he said. "Single mast,
blue hull. Man aboard, perhaps two. They think it headed
east yesterday."

"Name?"

"Morning Rose."

Mara closed her eyes.

Tomas's boat had been named Morning Rose.

For one absurd second, fourteen years vanished. She saw him
in the cottage doorway, hair wet with rain, laughing because
he had forgotten his key. She smelled tar and salt
on his coat.

"Mara?" said the harbor master.

"I'm here."

"Not the same boat," he said gently. "That one was
never recovered from the north beach after they found it."

"I know."

"This one belongs to a fellow named Senn. Purchased it
six years ago."

"Who is missing?"

"Unclear. We'll call when we know."

By seven, the gale struck.

Wind howled around the tower. Rain hissed against the glass.
Mara kept the lamp turning and monitored the radio, but
the storm filled every frequency with static.

At half past eight, the bell began to ring.

Not three times.

Again and again it sounded beneath the storm, each note
shuddering up through the rock. The tower seemed to vibrate
with it.

Mara pressed her palms to the glass and stared west.

For a moment, the lamp's beam swept across nothing but
rain. Then it returned, and she saw the yellow light.

It was closer now.

It hovered beyond the black rocks, bright as a lantern
carried by an invisible hand.

The radio cracked.

A boy's voice whispered through the static.

"Mara?"

She seized the receiver. "Eli?"

Only noise answered.

"Eli, where are you?"

"Mara, I found—"

The signal broke.

She tried again, calling the harbor, the shop, anyone. No
reply came.

Then the lighthouse door slammed far below.

Mara left the lamp room and ran down the stairs.
She nearly fell at the thirty-seventh step and struck her
shoulder against the wall. At the bottom, the entrance stood
open, wind driving rain into the tower.

On the floor lay Eli's cap.

She picked it up. It was soaked.

Outside, a flashlight flickered on the path toward the western
cliffs.

Mara shouted his name, but the wind tore it away.

She pulled on her oilskin coat, took a coil of
rope from the emergency cupboard, and followed.

The western path was little more than a pale scar
through the grass. Rain blinded her. Twice she lost sight
of the flashlight; twice it reappeared farther ahead.

At the cliff edge, she found Eli standing beside the
old descent, a steep trail used generations ago by people
gathering mussels. He was staring down at the shore.

The tide had retreated farther than Mara had ever seen.

Between the cliff and the outer rocks, the sea floor
lay exposed. A road of flat stones ran westward through
shallow water.

Beyond it, under the sweep of the lighthouse beam, rooftops
rose from the sea.

Mara could not move.

Dark walls, broken chimneys, and narrow streets emerged between veils
of rain. At the far end stood a chapel. Its
steeple leaned, and within it swung a bell green with
age.

Merrow.

The drowned island stretched before them.

Small lights glowed in its windows.

"I heard my father," Eli said.

Mara grabbed his arm. "We're going back."

"He called from the chapel."

"It isn't him."

"You don't know."

"Yes," Mara said, though she did not. "I do."

A figure appeared on the stone road.

It carried a yellow lantern.

The figure moved slowly toward them, sometimes hidden by waves
spilling across the path. It wore a fisherman's coat and
a dark wool hat.

Mara stopped breathing.

She knew that walk.

Tomas had favored his left leg after a rope injury
when he was seventeen. In bad weather, the limp became
more pronounced. The approaching figure leaned exactly as he had.

"Mara," it called.

His voice had not aged.

The years inside her split open.

She remembered their last morning: Tomas at the table, eating
bread with honey; Tomas complaining about a frayed sail; Tomas
kissing her cheek while she looked at a lighthouse maintenance
list and told him to buy lamp oil on the
mainland.

She had not watched him leave.

For fourteen years, she had revised that morning until it
became a punishment. If she had looked up. If she
had noticed the pressure dropping. If she had asked him
to stay. If she had said goodbye properly.

Now he was walking toward her through the rain.

"Mara," he called again. "Come help me."

Eli pulled against her grip. "There! Someone's behind him."

A second lantern appeared near the chapel.

"Dad!" Eli shouted.

The boy wrenched free and started down the trail.

Mara caught him by the collar.

He kicked and twisted. "Let go!"

"No."

"He needs me!"

"So does your grandmother."

"My father's there!"

"Tomas is there," Mara said, and the words nearly broke
her. "But Tomas is dead."

The figure on the road stopped.

The bell rang.

All across drowned Merrow, doors opened.

People stepped into the streets.

There were dozens of them: men in old coats, women
in long skirts, children barefoot in the rain. Some looked
swollen and pale. Others seemed as warm and solid as
the living. All turned their faces toward the cliff.

All began calling names.

The storm filled with voices.

Mothers heard lost children. Widows heard husbands. The old heard
friends from youth. Mara heard her own name in Tomas's
voice, tender and impatient.

"You left the lamp," he called. "Come home."

Eli sagged against her. "What do we do?"

Mara tied one end of the rope around his waist
and the other around hers.

"We climb."

They had taken only a few steps when part of
the cliff gave way.

Mud and stone slid beneath them. Eli fell. Mara dropped
to one knee, braced against a rock, and held the
rope as the boy swung over the edge.

He screamed.

Below him, waves poured across the exposed road. The dead
moved closer.

Mara wrapped the rope around her forearm and pulled. Her
shoulder burned where she had struck the lighthouse wall.

"Climb!" she shouted.

"I can't!"

"Use your feet!"

Eli found a ledge. Mara hauled again. For a moment,
his head rose above the edge.

Then Tomas spoke behind her.

"You can't save everyone."

She looked back.

He stood only a few yards away.

She had not seen him climb the trail. Water streamed
from his coat. His face was the face she remembered—weathered,
brown-eyed, a thin scar beneath the chin. In his hand,
he held the yellow lantern.

"You tried," he said. "That is enough."

Mara's grip weakened.

He smiled, and she saw the small gap between his
front teeth.

No ghost invented by grief should have known that smile.
No trick of storm and shadow should have known how
he touched two fingers to his brow when he was
worried.

"I waited," she whispered.

"I know."

"I kept the lamp."

"I know."

"Why didn't you come home?"

Tomas looked toward the sea. "I could not."

Below them, Eli sobbed and clawed at the rock.

Tomas held out his hand. "You can stop waiting now."

Mara wanted to take it.

She wanted rest more than she had ever admitted. She
wanted to walk down the stone road and enter one
of those lit houses. She wanted a kitchen untouched by
years, a kettle singing, Tomas's boots by the door. She
wanted to become the woman she had been on the
morning before the sea divided her life.

The rope slipped another inch.

Eli cried out.

Mara looked at Tomas's open hand.

Then she remembered something.

On the morning he vanished, he had not eaten bread
with honey. They had been out of honey. He had
spread plum jam on his bread and complained that the
jar had gone moldy, though it had not.

For fourteen years, she had remembered the scene incorrectly.

Grief had polished it, sweetened it, made it shine.

The thing before her was not Tomas. It was the
shape of waiting.

Mara tightened her grip on the rope.

"You are not my husband."

The figure's smile disappeared.

Behind him, the dead fell silent.

Mara pulled.

Pain flared through her shoulder and down her back. Eli
kicked against the cliff, found another ledge, and rose. She
caught his wrist and dragged him onto the grass.

Tomas lunged.

The yellow lantern went out.

For one instant, his face changed. Skin peeled into gray
water. His eyes became empty sockets filled with sand. Seaweed
hung from his teeth.

Then the lighthouse beam swept over him, and he vanished.

Mara and Eli crawled uphill.

Below, the tide returned.

It did not creep. It charged.

Waves crashed across the stone road and shattered against the
drowned houses. Merrow's lights went out one by one. The
bell rang wildly as water climbed the chapel walls.

The dead called again, but their voices grew faint.

Mara did not look back.

They reached the lighthouse as the first wave struck the
base of the western cliff. Inside, she bolted the door,
untied the rope, and wrapped Eli in an old blanket.

"The lamp," she said.

They climbed together.

The mechanism had stopped turning.

Perhaps Mara had failed to wind it fully. Perhaps the
violence of the bell had shaken something loose. The great
lens stood still, its beam fixed uselessly toward the north.

Mara opened the machinery panel.

A drive chain had slipped from its gear.

She reached inside, but her injured shoulder refused to lift.
Eli pushed the sleeve of his coat above his elbows.

"Tell me what to do."

Together they reset the chain. Mara guided his hands while
he forced the metal over the gear teeth. Then she
wound the mechanism, each turn sending fire through her arm.

At last, the lens began to move.

The beam swept west.

A blue-hulled boat appeared among the rocks.

Its mast was broken. Its sail dragged in the water.
A man clung to the stern while waves drove the
vessel toward the reef.

"Morning Rose," Mara whispered.

Eli pressed himself to the glass. "Dad!"

The man looked up, though he could not have heard.

A rescue boat could not leave Saint Orra's harbor in
such seas. Mara radioed the mainland and fired the lighthouse
emergency flare. Red light opened above the water.

The blue boat was too close to the rocks.

"Can he steer?" Eli asked.

"Maybe."

Mara adjusted the auxiliary signal lamp. With the main beam
showing the reef, she flashed the old harbor code across
the water.

Three short. One long. Two short.

Safe passage east.

For several seconds, nothing happened.

Then the man at the stern moved. He pulled himself
toward the tiller. A wave spun the boat sideways, but
another lifted it clear of the first rock.

Mara sent the signal again.

Three short. One long. Two short.

The boat turned.

It entered the narrow channel between the reefs just as
the tide swallowed the last stone of the road to
Merrow.

By dawn, the gale had passed.

The Morning Rose grounded on the eastern beach, damaged but
afloat. Two men were aboard. One was the boat's owner,
Senn, who had broken his leg. The other was Eli's
father.

He had missed his cargo ship after helping Senn repair
the boat in Bellhaven. When the weather changed, they had
tried to reach Saint Orra rather than return against the
wind. Their radio failed before they could send word.

Eli ran into the shallows and threw himself against his
father.

Mara watched from the beach, her arm in a sling.

Later, when the doctor had examined everyone and the villagers
had finished telling the story incorrectly, Eli found her beside
the lighthouse.

"Was that really Merrow?" he asked.

Mara looked west.

The sea was blue and calm. Gulls circled over the
rocks. Nothing remained of the road, the houses, or the
chapel.

"Yes," she said.

"Was that really Tomas?"

"No."

Eli waited.

Mara considered her answer.

"Perhaps part of it was," she said. "The part I
wouldn't let go."

"Will the bell ring again?"

"Probably."

"What should we do if it does?"

Mara put the brass key into the lighthouse door.

"We listen," she said. "But we don't follow."

Winter passed. The story of the storm traveled to the
mainland and returned enlarged. Newspapers wrote about an optical illusion
caused by unusual tides. A university sent two researchers to
examine the western rocks. They found fragments of red roof
tile, a rusted hinge, and a bronze clapper from a
bell.

The people of Saint Orra were not surprised.

In spring, Mara cleared Tomas's clothes from the wooden chest
at the foot of her bed. She gave his heavy
coat to Eli's father, his boots to the harbor master,
and his wool hats to the church charity.

At the bottom of the chest, she found a jar
wrapped in cloth.

Plum jam.

The seal had broken years ago. Inside, the fruit had
dried into a dark, sweet crust.

Mara laughed until she cried.

Then she buried the jar beneath the apple tree and
placed a flat stone over it. She carved no name.
She did not need to. It was not a grave,
only a place to set down what she had carried
too long.

Years later, when Mara's hair had turned white and Eli
was old enough to serve as assistant keeper, the bell
rang again at low water.

They stood together in the lamp room.

Out beyond the western rocks, a yellow light moved through
the dusk.

Eli glanced at Mara.

"Do you hear anyone?" he asked.

She listened.

The bell's note traveled through water and stone. Beneath it
rose a confusion of distant voices, gentle as rain on
glass. Once, she thought she heard Tomas laugh. The sound
no longer pulled at her. It passed through her like
wind through an open house.

"No," she said.

It was almost true.

Together, they lit the lamp.

Its beam crossed the dark water, turned over the place
where Merrow slept, and continued toward the horizon.

# The Keeper of Lost Hours

On the morning the town lost an hour, only Mara
Venn noticed.

At eight o'clock, the bell in the tower above the
council hall rang seven times. Bakers slid loaves from their
ovens, children buttoned their coats, and the ferry captain untied
his ropes. No one looked up. No one counted.

Mara did.

She stood beneath the tower with a basket over one
arm and watched the last bronze note dissolve into the
cold air.

"Seven," she whispered.

The clock face showed eight.

She checked the little silver watch that had belonged to
her father. Its hands also pointed to eight. Yet Mara
knew what she had heard.

At the bakery, she asked Mr. Lorne if the bell
had rung strangely.

"Strangely?" He lifted a tray of sugared buns. "It rang
as bells do."

"How many times?"

"Eight, I suppose."

"You didn't count?"

Mr. Lorne laughed. "Who counts the hours when the clock
can do it for you?"

Mara bought a loaf and continued uphill toward home, but
unease followed her like a stray dog.

Bellwether was a small town built along a gray-green river.
It had steep roofs, crooked chimneys, three bridges, and a
clock tower older than any person living there. Travelers said
the tower could be seen from half a day's journey
away. The townspeople were proud of it, though few knew
who had built it.

Mara's father had known.

Before his death, Tomas Venn had served as keeper of
the tower. Each Monday he climbed the two hundred and
three iron steps to wind the mechanism, oil the gears,
and check the great counterweights hanging in the shaft. Mara
had often gone with him. While he worked, she sat
among the gears and listened to their steady conversation.

"Clocks don't make time," her father once told her. "They
only persuade it to move in an orderly fashion."

At ten, Mara heard nine bells.

At noon, eleven.

By sunset, the tower was an hour behind, though every
clock in town displayed the correct time.

That evening, people seemed tired. Mrs. Pell left her laundry
in the rain. The blacksmith forgot a customer's name. Mara's
neighbor, old Ansel Grey, stood at his own door for
several minutes, searching his pockets for a key while the
door was already open.

"Long day," he muttered.

But it had not been a long day.

It had been a short one.

Mara waited until midnight. Wrapped in her father's coat, she
sat by her bedroom window and watched the tower rise
above the roofs. Snow began to fall, softening the town
into a sketch of itself.

At twelve, the bell rang eleven times.

Then came another sound: a faint, impossible chime from somewhere
beneath the streets.

It was not loud. Mara felt it more than heard
it—a delicate silver note traveling through stone, water pipes, and
frozen earth.

Her father's watch trembled in her palm.

Its cover sprang open.

The hands spun backward.

Mara snatched her lantern and ran.

The council hall was locked, but she knew the tower's
side entrance, hidden behind a curtain of dead ivy. Her
father had carried its key for twenty-five years. After his
funeral, the council had asked for it back.

Mara had given them a copy.

The original still hung on a blue thread beneath her
collar.

The key turned reluctantly. The wooden door opened on darkness
and the smell of iron, dust, and old rain.

Inside, the clock's machinery ticked overhead. The sound had once
comforted her. Now it was uneven.

Tick.

Tick.

Pause.

Tick.

Mara raised her lantern. The weights hung in the central
shaft, each as large as a coffin. The first was
descending normally. The second shivered on its cable.

The third was gone.

In its place, a cut chain swung gently.

She climbed.

On the first landing, she found fresh scratches across the
floor. On the second, a black feather. On the third,
a scattering of pale sand.

The clock chamber waited at the top. Brass gears filled
the room, turning within turning, their teeth flashing in lantern
light. The bell ropes strained upward into the belfry. Frost
silvered the windows.

Mara inspected the mechanism as her father had taught her.
The escapement was intact. The pendulum swung freely. No ordinary
fault could explain the missing hour.

Then she noticed a narrow brass door behind the largest
gear.

She had never seen it before.

Engraved upon it were the words:

WHAT IS LOST IS NEVER EMPTY.

The key around Mara's neck fit the lock.

Beyond the door, a stairway spiraled down.

That made no sense. The door was at the top
of the tower, and yet the steps descended far beyond
the height of the building. Mara leaned over the railing.
Small blue flames burned in wall niches, illuminating stairs that
vanished into a misty depth.

She thought of waking the mayor. She imagined Mayor Vale
in his nightshirt, blinking at the impossible stairway and asking
whether it could wait until morning.

By morning, another hour might be gone.

Mara stepped through and shut the door behind her.

The stairway ended in a railway station.

A tiled platform stretched into darkness. There was no sky,
only a vaulted ceiling where constellations moved like luminous fish.
Tall clocks stood along the platform, each showing a different
time. Some had thirteen numbers. Some had no hands. One
ran so quickly that its hands were a blur.

Across the tracks hung a sign:

LOST HOURS — ALL DESTINATIONS

A man sat behind a ticket window. He wore a
red uniform with gold buttons, though one sleeve was empty
and pinned neatly at the shoulder. His face was narrow,
and his mustache curled upward at both ends.

"You are late," he said.

"For what?"

"Everything, eventually."

Mara approached the window. "An hour has been stolen from
Bellwether."

"Misplaced."

"I heard it beneath the streets."

"Lost hours make noise. Forgotten ones do not."

"Where is it?"

The man sighed and pushed a ledger toward her. Its
pages turned by themselves, covered in names written in green
ink.

"Mara Venn," he read. "Daughter of Tomas. You have some
authority here."

"My father was the clock keeper."

"Your father was more than that."

Before Mara could ask what he meant, a whistle sounded
in the tunnel.

The ticket seller slid a small brass token beneath the
window. On one side was an eye; on the other,
an hourglass.

"Platform three. Take the train to the Archive. Speak with
the Curator."

"Will the Curator return our hour?"

"If it has not been spent."

The train arrived without smoke or thunder. It was black
and polished, with windows glowing amber. Its carriages were filled
with passengers who seemed not to notice Mara boarding.

A bride sat alone, still holding a bouquet of wilted
white flowers. An old soldier polished a medal. A boy
in a sailor's coat slept with his head against the
glass. None of them moved when the train began to
roll.

Mara took a seat beside a woman knitting a red
scarf.

"Where are we going?" Mara asked.

"The place after too late," the woman replied.

Outside the window, scenes flashed by.

A summer afternoon lay abandoned in a meadow, complete with
buzzing bees and a picnic no one had attended. A
rainy Tuesday floated in darkness, its streets shining beneath lamps.
There were final minutes, wasted mornings, sleepless midnights, and birthdays
forgotten by everyone except the person whose birthday it had
been.

Mara pressed her forehead to the glass.

She saw her father.

He sat beside the river on a golden evening, fishing
rod in hand. Mara recognized the day. She had been
nine years old. He had invited her to come fishing,
but she had refused because she was angry with him
for breaking a promise. He had sat alone until sunset.

The scene vanished.

Mara closed her eyes.

"Don't stare too long," said the knitting woman. "Lost time
enjoys being wanted."

The train slowed at a station built inside a vast
cavern. Shelves rose from the platform to an invisible ceiling.
Upon them stood millions of glass bottles, each containing a
different light.

The Archive.

Mara stepped off the train. The other passengers remained behind.

A tall figure approached between the shelves. At first Mara
thought it was a woman dressed in white. As it
drew nearer, she saw that its gown was made of
overlapping paper calendars. Its hair was a bundle of clock
springs, and its eyes were two dark, polished stones.

"I am the Curator," it said. Its voice had the
dry whisper of pages turning. "You have come for Bellwether's
missing hour."

"Yes."

"You may have what remains."

The Curator raised one long hand. A bottle floated down
from a shelf and settled into its palm.

Inside was a pale blue glow.

The bottle was nearly empty.

"Who took it?" Mara asked.

"The hour was not taken by one person. It was
borrowed in pieces."

The Curator touched the bottle.

The blue light expanded, becoming a circle in the air.
Within it, Mara saw Mr. Lorne the baker asleep beside
his ovens. He woke suddenly to find his bread burning.

"He borrowed six minutes," said the Curator, "to bake the
loaves again."

The image changed. Mrs. Pell stood beside a bed, speaking
harshly to her daughter. The girl fled the room in
tears. Mrs. Pell covered her face.

"She borrowed eleven minutes to choose kinder words."

Old Ansel Grey appeared next, feeding papers into his fireplace.
Mara recognized the documents that would send him from his
home for unpaid debts.

"He borrowed twenty minutes to hide what he was ashamed
to reveal."

Scene after scene appeared. A teacher corrected a mistake before
anyone noticed. The ferry captain returned for a passenger he
had left behind. Mayor Vale added a flattering speech to
his public address.

The blue glow shrank with every vision.

"People can borrow time?" Mara asked.

"Everyone borrows time," said the Curator. "Most pay it back
in sleep, memory, patience, or regret. But Bellwether has not
paid. Its tower has protected the town for generations, keeping
the debt outside its walls."

"The missing counterweight," Mara said.

"A seal. Broken deliberately."

"By whom?"

The last of the blue light formed an image.

A man stood in the clock chamber three nights earlier,
sawing through the chain. He wore a dark coat. Snow
glittered in his hair.

Mara knew him.

"Councilor Saye."

The Curator closed its hand, and the image vanished.

Councilor Saye was Bellwether's richest man. He owned warehouses, boats,
and half the houses by the river. He was also
expected to become mayor in spring.

"Why would he break the tower?"

"Ask what a wealthy man buys when he has everything
but enough years."

The Curator turned and walked between the shelves. Mara followed.

They passed bottles containing seconds as bright as sparks and
decades dim as moonlit fog.

"Councilor Saye borrowed from the town's future," the Curator said.
"A minute here, an hour there. He believes he can
lengthen his life."

"Can he?"

"Yes."

The answer chilled her more than a denial would have.

"How much has he taken?"

"From today? One hour. From tomorrow, perhaps two. As the
seal weakens, he will take more. The people of Bellwether
will rush through days they cannot remember. Children will wake
older than their parents. Winter will become spring between one
breath and the next."

"Then stop him."

"I keep lost hours. I do not choose how they
are lost."

Mara looked back at the nearly empty bottle. "Can I
return what remains?"

The Curator held it out.

At the bottom flickered less than a minute.

"Time cannot simply be given back," it said. "The scales
require an exchange."

"What kind of exchange?"

"An hour for an hour."

Mara thought of the town above: the bakery, the river,
old Ansel's open door. She thought of people waking tomorrow
with another piece of their lives missing.

"Take an hour from me."

"Which one?"

"Does it matter?"

"Every hour matters once it is gone."

The shelves shifted around them. Bottles chimed against one another,
and a new light appeared in the Curator's hand.

Mara saw herself at six years old, sitting on her
father's shoulders while the tower bell rang on Midwinter Night.
Snow fell around them. Her father was laughing.

"This hour?" asked the Curator.

"No."

Another vision appeared. Mara and her father repaired a clock
at their kitchen table. She was thirteen and impatient. He
guided her hands as she fitted a spring into place.

"This one?"

"No."

The next showed a hospital room. Her father lay pale
beneath white sheets, struggling to breathe. Mara sat beside him,
holding his hand. In that final hour, he told her
stories about her mother. He gave her the silver watch.
He tried to explain something about the tower, but pain
interrupted him.

Mara recoiled. "Not that one."

"You must choose."

"Take an ordinary hour."

"There are no ordinary hours. There are only hours we
have not yet learned to value."

Mara turned away. Along the nearest shelf, a bottle glowed
with warm yellow light. A label bore her name.

"What is that?"

"An hour not yet lived."

The glass showed Mara older, standing on the bridge at
sunrise. Beside her was someone whose face she could not
clearly see. She was holding a baby wrapped in green
wool. The joy in her expression was so fierce that
Mara's breath caught.

"Future hours may be exchanged," said the Curator. "They are
often cheaper because their loss leaves no memory."

Mara reached toward the bottle, then stopped.

If she surrendered that hour, would the child still exist?
Would the faceless person beside her disappear? Would she simply
be elsewhere, never knowing what she had missed?

Councilor Saye had made his choice by stealing from futures
that were not his.

Mara lowered her hand.

"Take the fishing hour," she said.

The Curator tilted its head.

"The evening when my father sat by the river alone.
Take my memory of seeing it from the train. Take
my anger from that day. Take everything that hour means
to me."

"You did not share that hour."

"But it has lived in me for years. I have
spent more than an hour regretting it."

The Curator's stone eyes shone.

"Regret," it whispered, "is time paid twice."

It raised a finger and touched Mara's forehead.

Pain flashed, quick and bright.

Something vanished.

Mara staggered. She knew she had lost something connected to
her father and the river, but when she reached for
it, there was only a smooth blankness.

The bottle in the Curator's hand filled with blue light.

"One hour," it said. "No more."

The train returned Mara to the station beneath the tower.
The ticket seller saluted as she passed.

"Mind the stairs," he called. "They lead both ways, and
neither is up."

When Mara stepped through the brass door, dawn was whitening
the clock chamber windows.

Councilor Saye was waiting.

He stood beside the mechanism with a pistol in his
hand. Up close, he looked strangely young. His hair had
been gray the previous week; now it was black. The
wrinkles around his eyes were gone.

"You should not be here," he said.

"Neither should you."

His gaze dropped to the glowing bottle in her hand.
Hunger transformed his face.

"Give me that."

Mara backed toward the bell ropes.

"You cut the counterweight."

"I improved a failing system."

"You're stealing from the town."

"I am taking what others waste." Saye stepped closer. "Do
you know how much time is thrown away in Bellwether?
People spend years complaining, sleeping, waiting for rain to stop.
I can use those years."

"They aren't yours."

"Everything belongs to whoever understands its value."

He reached for the bottle.

Mara threw it upward.

Saye lunged, but she seized the nearest bell rope and
pulled with all her strength.

Above them, the great bell struck.

The sound shook the room.

The bottle shattered against the bronze gear.

Blue light burst across the mechanism.

Every gear stopped.

For one vast second, the world held its breath.

Then the clock began to turn backward.

Saye screamed.

The stolen years rushed out of him in silver threads.
His black hair faded to gray, then white. His smooth
face wrinkled. He fell to his knees, suddenly an old
man.

Throughout Bellwether, borrowed minutes returned to their owners. Mr. Lorne
smelled smoke and remembered his burned bread. Mrs. Pell heard
both the cruel words she had spoken and the kinder
words she had chosen. Old Ansel remembered the debt notices
in the fire.

The tower's cut chain rose from the shaft like a
waking serpent. Its broken links joined. Far below, the missing
counterweight climbed out of darkness and locked into place.

The clock hands returned to eight.

Outside, the sun paused on the horizon.

Then the bell rang.

Once.

The missing hour unfolded over Bellwether.

It was not added at the beginning or end of
the day. It entered all at once, filling forgotten spaces.
People stood motionless in kitchens and streets as memories settled
into them—an hour of quiet thoughts, delayed decisions, unfinished conversations,
and opportunities they could no longer pretend had never existed.

Saye lay on the floor, breathing weakly. The pistol had
fallen from his hand.

Mara picked it up and placed it beyond his reach.

"You've killed me," he rasped.

"No," she said. "I think you've simply arrived at your
own age."

Later, the council claimed that Councilor Saye had damaged the
tower during an attempt to steal its valuable antique parts.
It was easier to believe than the truth. He was
sent away to live with relatives in the south, where,
according to rumor, he refused to keep clocks in the
house.

Mara was appointed keeper of the tower.

Every Monday, she climbed the two hundred and three steps.
She oiled the gears, checked the weights, and listened to
the clock's steady conversation.

Behind the largest gear, the brass door remained. She locked
it and wore the key beneath her collar.

Years passed.

Sometimes, standing by the river, Mara felt an emptiness she
could not explain. It was not pain exactly. It was
the shape pain leaves after departing. She knew it concerned
her father, an evening, and a choice she wished she
had made differently.

But she could no longer remember enough to regret it.

So she began a custom.

Once a month, regardless of rain, snow, or work waiting
in the tower, Mara took a fishing rod to the
river. She sat there until sunset. Sometimes she caught fish.
Usually she did not.

When people asked why, she said, "I promised someone."

She did not know whom.

Many years later, Mara stood on the eastern bridge at
sunrise. Beside her was Eli Lorne, the baker's son, whose
face time had made familiar and dear. In her arms,
wrapped in green wool, slept their daughter.

The child opened her eyes as the tower bell rang
seven times.

Mara looked up.

The clock showed seven.

She counted carefully.

Across the river, windows brightened one by one. Boats drifted
from their moorings. The town woke slowly, possessing all the
hours of the day and unaware of how precious that
possession was.

Eli touched her shoulder. "Are you all right?"

Mara smiled.

"Yes."

The baby yawned, closing one tiny hand around Mara's finger.

The eighth bell rang.

And time, persuaded once more to move in an orderly
fashion, carried them gently forward.

### The Keeper of Lost Hours

When Mara Vale arrived in Bellweather, every clock in town
was thirteen minutes slow.

The station clock showed 4:02, though her watch insisted it
was 4:15. The clock above the bakery agreed with the
station. So did the brass clock in the window of
the jeweler's, the painted wooden clock outside the school, and
the pale face in the church tower.

Mara checked her watch against her telephone. Both displayed 4:15.

"Time runs differently here," said the stationmaster.

He was an elderly man with a red scarf and
a cap that appeared too large for him. He stood
beside Mara's suitcase as though guarding it from escape.

"Thirteen minutes differently?" she asked.

"Always."

"Why?"

The stationmaster considered the question. "Because no one has fixed
it."

Mara looked again at the church tower. Its minute hand
remained steady for several seconds, then jumped forward with visible
effort.

"I'm here for the clockmaker's shop," she said. "Do you
know Juniper Street?"

"Everyone knows Juniper Street."

"Could you point me toward it?"

"I could."

He picked up her suitcase and began walking.

Mara followed him out of the station and into a
square paved with yellow stones. She had expected Bellweather to
be gray. The solicitor's letters had made it sound gray:
a remote town, an inherited property, an estate of negligible
monetary value. Instead, red and blue houses crowded around the
square, and window boxes overflowed with flowers despite the November
cold.

People glanced at Mara as she passed. Some nodded. Others
whispered.

She had never met her grandfather, Elias Vale. Her mother
had spoken of him only once, saying, "He repaired clocks,
but not the kind you're thinking of." When Mara asked
what that meant, her mother had closed the kitchen window
and changed the subject.

The stationmaster led her through three narrow streets to a
crooked building squeezed between a tailor's and a shop selling
maps. Above its door hung a black sign painted with
a silver hourglass.

VALE & DAUGHTER
CLOCKS MENDED
HOURS RESTORED

The final line had been crossed out, though not thoroughly
enough to hide it.

The stationmaster set down her suitcase.

"Your grandfather was a difficult man," he said.

"So I've heard."

"He was also kind. People often confuse the two."

Before Mara could reply, he hurried away.

The key sent by the solicitor stuck twice before turning.
When Mara opened the door, dozens of bells chimed overhead.

Inside, the shop was deeper than the building seemed to
allow. Clocks covered every wall: round clocks, square clocks, clocks
shaped like suns and cats and ships. Pendulums swung behind
glass. Gears whispered. Cuckoos waited behind tiny doors.

None showed the same time.

A note lay on the counter.

Mara recognized the handwriting from the label attached to the
key.

Dear Mara,

If you are reading this, then I am dead, unless
Dr. Pell has made another embarrassing mistake.

The shop is yours. Sell it, burn it, or repair
what remains. The choice must be freely made, or it
is no choice at all.

One warning: do not open the blue door after midnight.

I am sorry about your mother.

Your grandfather,
Elias

Mara read the note twice.

Behind the counter, half concealed by a curtain of hanging
chains, was a blue door.

"Of course," she murmured.

She had come to Bellweather intending to stay three days.
One to inspect the property, one to meet the solicitor,
and one to arrange the sale. She had a job
in the city cataloging municipal records. She had a rented
apartment with white walls, two neglected plants, and a neighbor
who practiced the violin badly but always stopped by ten.

Her life was quiet, orderly, and accurate.

Bellweather irritated her immediately.

That evening, while searching for clean sheets, she found a
narrow bedroom above the shop and a pantry containing tea,
crackers, and six jars labeled TUESDAY. She did not open
them.

At half past eleven, according to her watch, someone knocked
downstairs.

Mara descended and found a little girl waiting outside. She
wore a green coat and held a dead pocket watch
in both hands.

"We're closed," Mara said.

The girl looked past her into the shop. "Are you
Elias's granddaughter?"

"Yes."

"Can you mend this?"

"Probably not. I'm not a clockmaker."

"It isn't a clock."

Mara remembered her mother's words.

"Then what is it?"

The girl opened the watch.

Instead of hands, its face contained a moving picture. A
boy and girl sat beneath a pear tree, laughing. The
image repeated every few seconds: the boy tossed a pear,
the girl failed to catch it, and both collapsed against
the trunk with delight.

"That's my brother," the girl said. "His name was Tomas."

"Was?"

"He died last winter."

Mara's irritation softened. "I'm sorry."

"I can't hear his laugh anymore. The watch used to
keep it, but it stopped."

Mara touched the tiny glass. For a moment, she heard
a distant burst of laughter, faint as wind chimes.

She nearly dropped the watch.

"My grandfather made this?"

"He kept hours for people. Special ones."

The clocks behind Mara ticked in uneven waves.

"I don't know how to repair it."

"You could learn."

The girl said this without accusation. Then she took the
watch, thanked Mara, and walked away.

Mara locked the door.

At midnight, she was still standing behind the counter, studying
her grandfather's tools. There were ordinary screwdrivers and tweezers, but
also glass needles, silver thread, tiny bottles of colored smoke,
and a tuning fork engraved with the words FOR SUNDAYS
ONLY.

The clocks began to strike.

Some chimed twelve times. Some struck four. One shouted, "Run!"

Then the blue door opened by itself.

Beyond it, a staircase descended into golden darkness.

Mara's grandfather had explicitly told her not to open the
door. Technically, she had obeyed.

She took a lamp and went down.

The stairs ended in a chamber far larger than the
shop. Shelves rose into shadow, holding thousands of glass jars.
Light moved within them.

Mara lifted the nearest jar. Its label read:

MRS. IDA BELL
SEVEN MINUTES BEFORE THE PROPOSAL
JUNE 18

Inside, a young woman stood on a bridge at sunset
while a nervous man approached behind her, hiding a bouquet
beneath his coat. The moment repeated endlessly. He walked closer;
she turned; anticipation brightened her face—and the scene began again.

Other jars held other moments.

A baby's first step.

A soldier's return.

A final dance in an empty ballroom.

A breakfast at which four sisters laughed so hard that
tea came out of the eldest one's nose.

Not all were joyful. In one, a man waited beside
a hospital bed, gathering the courage to say goodbye. In
another, a woman stood in the rain outside a house,
unable to knock.

Mara wandered between the shelves until she found a workbench.
An enormous book rested open there.

The Ledger of Borrowed Hours, read the title page.

The final entry had been written three days earlier.

Bellweather, thirteen minutes, renewed yearly. Purpose: containment.

Below it, in shakier handwriting:

There is never enough time, so people beg for more.
They do not understand that an hour cannot be created.
It can only be moved.

Mara turned the page.

If you have come this far, the town is in
danger.

Something breathed in the darkness.

Mara raised the lamp.

At the far end of the chamber stood a clock
taller than a house. Its face had no numbers, only
a black circle at its center. Thick chains wrapped around
its case. Each chain trembled with the strain of holding
something shut.

The clock's single hand moved backward.

A whisper passed through the chamber.

Mara.

She backed toward the stairs.

Mara Vale.

The black circle widened like a pupil.

I can give it back.

The chamber disappeared.

Mara stood in her childhood kitchen. Sunlight warmed the yellow
tiles. Her mother stood at the sink, alive and younger,
her dark hair tied with a red ribbon.

"Mara?" she said.

Mara could not breathe.

Her mother had been dead for nine years. A stroke,
sudden and merciless. Mara had been twenty-two. Their final conversation
had been an argument about an unanswered letter from Elias.

"You're not real," Mara whispered.

Her mother dried her hands. "Does that matter?"

It mattered. It did not matter. Mara crossed the kitchen
in two steps and embraced her.

The warmth was perfect. The smell of soap and rosemary
was perfect. Her mother's fingers brushed her hair exactly as
they once had.

"You left so much unsaid," said the thing wearing her
mother's face. "You could have another hour. A day. A
year."

"What would it cost?"

"Only thirteen minutes."

"From whom?"

"Everyone."

The kitchen walls flickered. For an instant, Mara saw the
giant clock and its shaking chains.

"Thirteen minutes every year," the voice continued. "No one misses
them. Bellweather has paid for generations."

"Paid for what?"

"For keeping me asleep."

Mara pulled away.

Her mother's expression did not change, but the kitchen darkened.

"I am every hour wished back," said the voice. "Every
moment regretted. Every farewell refused. Your grandfather fed me crumbs
to stop me from devouring the feast."

The vision broke.

Mara found herself on the chamber floor. Her lamp had
gone out. The giant clock groaned. One of its chains
snapped, lashing the stone hard enough to scatter sparks.

Then someone seized Mara's arm.

The little girl in the green coat dragged her toward
the stairs.

"Move!"

They ran. Behind them, the clock began to strike, each
blow swallowing every other sound.

They reached the shop as the blue door slammed shut.

The girl leaned against the counter, panting.

"What are you doing here?" Mara demanded.

"Saving you."

"What is that thing?"

The girl looked at the blue door. "Elias called it
the Hollow Hour."

"How do you know?"

"He taught me."

"You're a child."

"I'm forty-six."

Mara stared.

The girl sighed. "The watch."

She opened the pocket watch again. This time the image
showed not Tomas beneath the pear tree, but the girl
herself—older, silver beginning in her hair—sitting beside Elias in the
shop.

"He stored my years," she said. "Most of them. I
was ill. I agreed to give up thirty-six years if
he would save Tomas."

"But Tomas died."

"Yes. Elias warned me he couldn't change the past. I
thought he was lying."

"What's your name?"

"Anwen."

Mara glanced at the clocks. They all showed different minutes,
but each second hand now spun backward.

Anwen placed the watch on the counter.

"Elias used Bellweather's missing thirteen minutes to bind the Hollow
Hour. Once a year, he reset the mechanism. He died
before he could do it again."

"Why didn't he teach you?"

"He did. I refused."

"Why?"

"Resetting it requires a keeper to surrender one perfect hour.
Not a memory stored in glass. The real hour. Once
given, it vanishes as though it never happened."

Mara thought of the jars below. Thousands of moments preserved
because people could not bear to lose them. Her grandfather's
work had not been clock repair. He had been a
custodian of longing.

"What happens if no one resets it?"

"At dawn, the Hollow Hour wakes. It will offer everyone
the past they want most. By sunset, the town will
be empty."

"Empty?"

"People who live in yesterday have no use for today."

Outside, footsteps approached.

The stationmaster entered without knocking, followed by the baker, the
solicitor, two schoolteachers, and a crowd of townspeople. They filled
the shop and spilled into the street.

"You knew," Mara said.

The stationmaster removed his cap. "We knew Elias kept something
dangerous beneath the town."

"And you let him do it alone."

"We offered help."

"Not the kind he needed, apparently."

No one answered.

The church clock began to ring. It struck once, though
every clock in the shop claimed dawn was still hours
away.

The blue door rattled.

Mara looked at the gathered townspeople. In every face, she
saw fear, but beneath the fear she saw hunger. Each
person had someone they wanted returned, some choice they wanted
unmade, some door they wished they had opened or kept
forever closed.

"What was my grandfather's perfect hour?" she asked.

The stationmaster swallowed. "The hour your mother was born."

Mara closed her eyes.

He had given it away. Not forgotten it, exactly. Made
it never exist. Perhaps that was why he and Mara's
mother had grown distant. Perhaps love could survive the loss
of an hour, but not the shape of its absence.

On the counter, Elias's note stirred though there was no
wind.

I am sorry about your mother.

The words now seemed less like an apology for an
old quarrel and more like an epitaph.

"How do I reset it?" Mara asked.

Anwen shook her head. "You don't have to."

"Yes, I do."

"You could leave."

"And it would follow?"

"It would grow. First Bellweather, then elsewhere."

"Then tell me."

Anwen led Mara below.

The chains were breaking one by one. Around the giant
clock, visions bloomed in the darkness. The stationmaster saw a
young man boarding a train and cried out. The baker
reached toward a vanished wife. Anwen's dead brother stood beneath
the pear tree, holding out his hand.

Mara saw her mother waiting in the kitchen.

She forced herself to look at the ledger instead.

Beneath the final entry, new words appeared:

NAME THE HOUR.
WIND THE CLOCK.
DO NOT LOOK BACK.

A key protruded from the clock's base. It was as
long as Mara's arm.

The Hollow Hour whispered from every vision.

Choose carefully.

Mara had assumed her perfect hour would be with her
mother. A birthday. A rainy afternoon. The last Christmas before
illness entered their lives.

But grief had polished those memories so often that they
no longer felt entirely true. She had altered them with
every remembrance, trimming away impatience, sharpening tenderness, turning ordinary time
into sacred artifact.

Another memory came instead.

Mara was seven. She had woken before sunrise and found
her mother asleep at the kitchen table. A letter lay
open beside her. Elias's name appeared at the bottom.

Her mother woke and quickly folded it.

"What does he want?" Mara had asked.

"To meet you."

"Can I?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because some people love by holding on too tightly."

Then her mother had made hot chocolate. They watched dawn
spread across the windows. Neither spoke. Mara remembered the chipped
blue cup between her hands and her mother's bare feet
on the cold floor.

It was not a dramatic hour. Nothing began or ended.
Yet in it, Mara had felt completely safe.

She placed both hands on the key.

"I give the hour before sunrise," she said, "when I
was seven and my mother made hot chocolate."

The vision of the kitchen sharpened.

Her mother turned from the sink.

"Don't," she said.

Mara's grip faltered.

This was not the Hollow Hour speaking. It was the
real memory, defending itself.

"You'll lose me," her mother said.

"I already did."

"Not this part."

The first light of dawn touched the high windows of
the chamber.

Mara began to turn the key.

Pain tore through her—not physical pain, but the sensation of
a door slamming somewhere inside her mind. She smelled chocolate.
She felt the warmth of the cup. She heard her
mother whispering a song.

Then each detail vanished.

She kept turning.

The giant clock's hand slowed, stopped, and began moving forward.

The visions screamed.

Anwen lunged toward Tomas, but the stationmaster caught her. The
baker collapsed. All around the chamber, people watched their impossible
pasts fracture into light.

Mara did not look back.

With a final turn, the key locked into place.

The Hollow Hour struck once.

Every clock in Bellweather answered.

Then silence fell.

Mara woke behind the shop counter at 7:13 in the
morning.

Sunlight poured through the windows. Townspeople slept on the floor,
leaning against walls and one another. Anwen lay curled beneath
the stationmaster's coat.

The blue door was gone. In its place hung a
narrow mirror.

Mara looked into it and saw a tired woman with
tears on her face.

She knew she had sacrificed something. She remembered choosing. She
remembered that the lost hour had belonged to her mother.

But the hour itself was empty.

That absence hurt in a clean, exact way.

On the counter, the little pocket watch ticked.

Anwen woke as Mara picked it up. The image of
Tomas beneath the pear tree had returned, bright and silent.
After a moment, sound crackled from within: a boy's laughter,
then a girl's.

Anwen smiled.

"Can you make it last longer?" she asked.

"No," Mara said.

"Good."

They stood together as Bellweather woke.

At 8:00, the church bell rang. Mara checked her watch.

It was exactly 8:00.

Throughout the morning, people arrived carrying broken objects. A widow
brought a music box that held the last dance with
her husband. A teacher brought a bottle containing the courage
she had misplaced before asking someone to marry her. The
stationmaster brought nothing but tea.

"I thought you were selling the shop," he said.

"So did I."

"Will you keep hours, then?"

Mara studied the sign above the door. The crossed-out words
were visible in the morning light.

HOURS RESTORED.

"No," she said. "Hours can't be restored."

She took a brush and painted a new line beneath
the old one.

MOMENTS MENDED
WHILE TIME ALLOWS

The stationmaster read it and nodded.

Years later, travelers would come to Bellweather because they had
heard of the clockmaker on Juniper Street. Some asked Mara
to recover lost childhoods or undo final words. Some offered
fortunes for a single hour with the dead.

She refused them gently.

But she repaired what could be repaired.

She taught music boxes to remember songs. She coaxed laughter
back into photographs. She placed courage in bottles and regret
in boxes that opened only once. She kept no hour
longer than necessary and warned every customer that memory was
not a home.

Anwen became her apprentice. As the stored years slowly returned
to her, she grew older in uncertain steps—ten years one
winter, two the next—but she never again asked to be
young.

And every autumn, Mara descended through the mirror to the
chamber below, where she wound the giant clock and gave
it one cherished hour.

The hours were never easy to lose.

A first kiss beneath a green umbrella.

An evening when the whole town ate supper in the
street.

The afternoon Anwen called her family.

Each sacrifice left a quiet room inside Mara, but around
those rooms life continued building corridors, opening windows, filling the
house.

In time, she understood her grandfather.

He had not guarded the past because it was more
precious than the present. He had guarded the world from
the temptation to choose the past instead.

When Mara was old, a young traveler came to the
shop carrying a watch that had never worked.

"What does it hold?" Mara asked.

"Nothing," the traveler said. "It's empty."

Mara opened the case.

Inside, the hands waited at twelve.

"No," she said, smiling. "It isn't empty."

"What is it, then?"

"A beginning."

She wound the watch and returned it.

Outside, the clocks of Bellweather struck the hour together—neither early
nor late, not missing a single minute.

And all across town, people stopped to listen.

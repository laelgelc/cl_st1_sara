The Clockmaker of Low Tide

Every morning, when the sea withdrew from Bellweather Bay, it
left behind the usual things: ropes of kelp, pale shells,
bottles clouded by salt, and once, to great local excitement,
a wooden leg with a silver buckle.

On the morning Elias Vale found the clock, the tide
had gone farther out than anyone in the town could
remember.

The bay lay open beneath a colorless sky, its wet
sand shining like a road of dark glass. Fishing boats
rested on their sides in the harbor, exposing bellies striped
with barnacles. Gulls strutted over places where waves should have
been, bewildered by their sudden kingdom.

Elias was twelve years old, an age at which he
was considered too young to be trusted with a boat
but old enough to mend nets until his fingers bled.
His father had sent him to gather driftwood before breakfast.
Instead, he followed a line of footprints across the sand.

They began near the harbor wall.

This was strange, because the footprints came from the sea.

They were narrow and deep, as if made by someone
carrying a heavy burden. Elias looked toward the horizon. In
the distance, the water had retreated into a silver band.
No person was visible. Only the footprints remained, crossing the
exposed bay and ending beside a black rock.

Half buried in the sand was a clock.

It was no larger than a dinner plate. Its case
was brass, green with age, and its face was covered
by thick glass. There were no numbers. Instead, tiny painted
images circled the dial: a cradle, a ship, a key,
a rose, a knife, a crown, a grave, and dozens
of other symbols too small for Elias to understand.

It had only one hand.

The hand pointed to a painted door.

Elias lifted the clock. It was surprisingly warm.

The instant his fingers closed around it, a sound came
from inside the case.

Tick.

The bay trembled.

Tick.

A flock of gulls rose together, shrieking.

Tick.

Far out at sea, the silver line of water began
to move.

Elias tucked the clock under his coat and ran home.

His father, Bram Vale, was in the kitchen splitting a
stale loaf with a knife. He was a broad man
with gray in his beard and a permanent bend in
his shoulders from years spent hauling traps. Since Elias's mother
had died, he had spoken mostly in instructions.

"Wood?" Bram asked.

"I found something."

"Unless it burns, put it away."

Elias placed the clock on the table.

Bram's knife stopped halfway through the loaf.

For a moment, all color left his face.

"Where did you get that?"

"On the sand."

"Where?"

"Beyond Widow's Rock."

Bram seized Elias by the shoulders. "Did anyone see you?"

"No."

"Did you wind it?"

"It was already ticking."

Bram released him and backed away. The clock's single hand
had moved. It no longer pointed to the door.

Now it pointed to a ship.

His father snatched a flour sack from a hook, wrapped
the clock in it, and shoved it into the cold
ashes of the stove.

"You will not touch that again," he said.

"What is it?"

"Nothing."

"You know what it is."

"I know trouble when it enters my house."

Bram took his coat and left without eating. Through the
window, Elias watched him hurry uphill toward the church.

The tide returned before noon.

It did not creep back in its ordinary way. It
rushed over the sand in one great shining sheet, lifting
boats, swallowing rocks, and filling the harbor almost to the
top of the wall. The town bell rang a flood
warning, though there was no storm. People ran from the
waterfront carrying baskets, children, ledgers, cages, whatever they valued most.

Then the water stopped.

By evening the bay was calm, but unease had settled
over Bellweather. Old people stood in doorways whispering. Fishermen tied
extra knots in their mooring lines. The priest walked along
the harbor sprinkling holy water until a wave soaked his
boots.

Bram returned after dark.

"With the spring tide," he told Elias, "you'll go inland
to your aunt."

"But I've never met her."

"She has a farm near Tor Hill."

"I don't want to go."

"This is not a matter of wanting."

"Is it because of the clock?"

Bram stared into the fire. "There is no clock."

From inside the stove came a soft, patient ticking.

That night Elias dreamed of his mother.

He remembered very little about her when awake: dark hair
threaded with copper, a laugh that arrived suddenly, hands that
smelled of lavender and fish scales. In the dream she
stood ankle-deep in the bay, wearing the blue dress in
which she had been buried.

"You took your time," she said.

Elias tried to run to her, but the sand clung
to his feet.

"Are you dead?" he asked.

"Everyone is, somewhere."

"I don't understand."

"You will."

She pointed toward the harbor. A ship was sailing into
Bellweather Bay, though no wind moved its black sails. Figures
crowded its deck. Some wore crowns, some carried lanterns, and
some had seaweed trailing from empty eye sockets.

At the wheel stood a woman in a red coat.

Elias woke to find the clock beside his bed.

The flour sack lay folded beneath it.

Its hand still pointed to the ship.

At dawn, a ship appeared beyond the harbor mouth.

It was small, narrow, and painted white. Its single sail
was red, the brightest thing under the gray sky. It
came forward without wind or oars and docked with such
precision that its hull barely kissed the pier.

A woman stepped ashore.

She wore a long red coat, black gloves, and boots
polished enough to reflect the clouds. Her hair was white,
though her face was neither young nor old. A scar
crossed her left cheek like a thin stroke of chalk.

"Good morning," she called to the gathering townspeople. "I am
looking for a clock."

No one answered.

The woman smiled.

"Brass case. One hand. Poor manners. Has anyone seen it?"

Bram pushed through the crowd. "Leave this town."

"Bram Vale," she said warmly. "Still giving orders to tides?"

"You took my wife."

"No. She made a bargain."

"You twisted it."

"I fulfilled it."

Elias, watching from behind a stack of lobster pots, felt
his heart strike his ribs.

The woman turned her head directly toward him.

"And there is her boy."

Bram lunged, but two sailors stepped from the ship. They
looked human except for their eyes, which were perfectly black.

"Run!" Bram shouted.

Elias ran.

He fled uphill through narrow streets, past the bakery, the
schoolhouse, and the church with its green copper roof. The
clock beat against his chest beneath his coat. Behind him
came the tolling of the town bell.

At the northern edge of Bellweather stood the abandoned workshop
of Silas Orr, the clockmaker.

Silas had disappeared seven years earlier. Some said he had
fallen from the cliffs. Others said he had built a
clock that could reverse death, tested it on himself, and
become an infant. His workshop remained untouched because no one
wanted to discover which story was true.

Elias broke a window and climbed inside.

Hundreds of clocks covered the walls. Grandfather clocks, carriage clocks,
cuckoo clocks, watches, sundials, and hourglasses filled with black sand
stood beneath sheets of dust. None of them moved.

At the back of the room was a desk buried
under papers. Elias set down the brass clock, and every
clock in the workshop began to strike.

The noise was enormous.

Bells rang. Springs shuddered. Wooden birds burst from tiny doors.
Hands spun around painted faces. An hourglass flipped itself over
and over until it shattered.

Then, all at once, silence returned.

A panel opened in the desk.

Inside lay a leather book bearing Elias's family name.

He opened it.

The first pages were filled with diagrams of the brass
clock. Later pages contained lists of names and dates. Some
names were marked with a black wave. Others had been
crossed out.

Near the end, Elias found his mother's name.

Mara Vale — one year purchased. Price: one unclaimed hour.

Below it, in different handwriting, someone had added:

The hour cannot remain unclaimed. It will seek an owner.
If the Clock of Tides returns, the debt has ripened.

The workshop door creaked open.

The woman in red stood on the threshold.

"You have her eyes," she said.

Elias stepped back. "Who are you?"

"Captain Anja Rook."

"What happened to my mother?"

"She was dying before you were born. Her heart was
weak. The doctors said she would not survive the winter,
and you would never take your first breath."

Elias gripped the book. "My father said you took her."

"I gave her twelve months. Enough time to bear you,
name you, and hold you. In return, she promised me
one hour that belonged to no living soul."

"That makes no sense."

"Time rarely does."

Captain Rook removed one glove. On her palm was a
circular scar shaped like the clock's dial.

"Your mother believed there were hours no one wanted," she
continued. "Hours wasted in waiting rooms, prison cells, sickbeds. But
every hour belongs to someone. Joyful or miserable, each one
must be lived. So the clock took payment from the
future."

"Whose future?"

"Yours."

The clock ticked faster.

Elias looked down. Its hand had moved beyond the ship
and now pointed to a knife.

"What happens when it goes all the way around?"

"The unclaimed hour opens. For sixty minutes, all possible moments
exist together. The dead may return. The unborn may speak.
Lost ships enter harbor. Old wounds reopen. And when the
hour ends, the clock chooses a life to erase so
the balance may be restored."

"My life?"

"Most likely."

Captain Rook held out her scarred hand.

"Give it to me. I can carry it beyond the
last line of longitude, where time has no authority."

"Then why didn't you do that before?"

Her expression changed, and Elias knew he had found the
edge of a lie.

"Because," she said, "the clock cannot cross the final sea
without a living keeper."

"You need me."

"I need someone."

Elias thought of the black-eyed sailors.

"What happened to the last keeper?"

"He became the clockmaker whose workshop you entered."

"Silas Orr?"

"He lasted longer than most."

A floorboard groaned overhead.

Captain Rook glanced up.

Bram dropped from the loft, a whaling harpoon in his
hands. He drove it through the captain's red coat and
into the wall.

For an instant she remained pinned there, more surprised than
hurt.

Bram grabbed Elias.

"Go!"

They escaped through the broken window. As they ran downhill,
every door in town opened at once.

The dead came out.

An old fisherman walked from the bakery with a hook
still buried in his throat. Three children who had drowned
in the winter flood skipped hand in hand toward the
school. A soldier in an ancient uniform stood in the
square calling for a regiment buried two centuries before.

People screamed, wept, or rushed forward to embrace those they
had lost.

Elias stopped.

His mother stood beside the fountain.

She wore the blue dress from his dream.

Bram saw her and staggered as if struck.

"Mara?"

She smiled.

He crossed the square slowly, unable to breathe. When he
reached her, he lifted one hand but did not quite
touch her face.

"You got old," she said.

Bram laughed then, a broken sound Elias had never heard
from him.

"You didn't."

They embraced.

For a few seconds Elias forgot the clock, the captain,
and the terrible debt. He ran to them, and his
mother folded him into her arms.

She smelled of lavender and fish scales.

"My little tidepool," she whispered.

He began to cry.

The clock ticked against his chest.

His mother drew back. "You found it."

"Captain Rook says I have to die."

"Anja always mistakes rules for fate."

"You knew her?"

"I knew what she was."

"What is she?"

"A collector. A ferryman. A prisoner of her own bargain.
Names matter less than choices."

The clock's hand moved from the knife to a painted
crown. Around them, the square shifted. Buildings appeared and vanished.
The church became a ruin, then a forest, then a
church again. Elias saw himself as an old man standing
beneath the bell tower. He saw himself drowned. He saw
children with his eyes. He saw an empty place where
Bellweather had once been.

"What do I do?" he asked.

His mother knelt before him.

"The clock was built to make one moment belong to
no one. That is its weakness."

"I don't understand."

"You will."

She kissed his forehead and turned to Bram.

"I am sorry," she said.

"No," he replied. "Stay. Just until it ends."

"I can't."

"You have an hour."

"I have already spent it."

Her body began to fade.

Bram clung to her, but his arms closed on empty
air.

Across the square, Captain Rook approached. The harpoon still protruded
from her chest, though no blood marked her coat. Behind
her came the two black-eyed sailors.

"The hour is collapsing," she said. "Give me the clock."

Elias pulled it out.

The hand pointed to the grave. Only two symbols remained
before the painted door where it had begun.

"What will you do with me?" he asked.

"You will board my ship. If we reach the final
sea, Bellweather will be spared."

"And me?"

"I cannot promise."

His father stepped between them. "Take me instead."

"The debt is not yours," said Rook.

"Then make it mine."

"You cannot give another person your death."

Bram seized the clock.

The moment he touched it, the glass cracked.

A sound like thunder rolled beneath the town.

All around them, possible worlds opened.

In one, Bram lay dead in the square. In another,
Captain Rook's ship burned in the harbor. In another, Elias
had never been born, and his parents walked past each
other as strangers on a summer road.

The clock's hand advanced to the final symbol: an eye.

Captain Rook tore the harpoon from her chest. "Decide now!"

Elias remembered his mother's words.

The clock had been built to make one moment belong
to no one.

Every hour belonged to someone. That was why the debt
could never be paid.

Unless the hour belonged to everyone.

Elias took the clock from his father and raised it
above his head.

"I claim this hour," he said.

Nothing happened.

Captain Rook laughed bitterly. "It is already yours."

"Then I give it away."

"To whom?"

"To everyone."

He hurled the clock onto the stones.

The brass case dented, but did not break.

The hand reached the painted door.

The door on the clock face opened.

It was not a picture anymore. It was a true
door, impossibly small and filled with white light. From it
came a wind that smelled of every season: snow, rain,
hot dust, apples, chimney smoke, newborn grass.

Elias picked up a loose paving stone and struck the
clock.

Once.

The glass shattered.

Twice.

The brass split.

On the third blow, the single hand snapped.

The unclaimed hour burst open.

It passed through Bellweather in a wave.

Every person in the town suddenly remembered a life they
had never lived. They remembered being kings and beggars, sailors
and murderers, beloved children and lonely ghosts. Each felt the
first breath of birth and the last beat of death.
For one boundless instant, no moment belonged to one person
because all moments belonged to all.

The debt vanished.

Captain Rook screamed.

Cracks of white light spread across her skin. Her black-eyed
sailors collapsed into heaps of wet rope. In the harbor,
the red sail tore loose and flew upward, becoming a
flock of crimson birds.

Rook reached for Elias.

"What have you done?"

"Changed the rules."

"No one changes the rules."

"My mother did."

The captain's face softened. Beneath her anger was something older
and sadder: relief.

"Yes," she whispered. "She did."

Then the light carried her away.

The clocks in Silas Orr's workshop began ringing on the
hill. The church bell answered. Soon every bell in Bellweather
was sounding, though no hand pulled any rope.

The dead disappeared.

The possible worlds closed.

The sea returned to its proper place.

Elias woke at sunset with his head in his father's
lap. The square was crowded with townspeople, all silent, all
changed in ways they would struggle to explain.

In Elias's hand lay a piece of broken brass.

It was shaped like a tiny door.

Years passed.

Bellweather rebuilt the windows shattered by the unclaimed hour, repainted
the boats, and turned Captain Rook's vanished ship into a
story told on winter nights. Most people eventually decided that
the tide had released a strange gas from beneath the
sand, causing the entire town to dream together.

Bram Vale never agreed or disagreed.

He became gentler after that day. He still spoke in
instructions, but sometimes the instructions were, "Sit down and watch
the sunset," or, "Your mother liked this song," or, "The
nets can wait until morning."

Elias grew tall. He sailed beyond the bay, though never
beyond the last line of longitude. He studied gears, springs,
stars, and tides. At twenty-three, he reopened Silas Orr's workshop.

Above the door he painted:

ELIAS VALE
CLOCKS REPAIRED
LOST HOURS FOUND

People brought him watches that ran slow, clocks that struck
thirteen, and music boxes that had forgotten their tunes. He
fixed what he could.

He never built a clock with one hand.

On certain mornings, when the tide withdrew unusually far, Elias
walked across the wet sand to Widow's Rock. He often
found bottles, shells, and driftwood. Once he found a silver
buckle that might have come from a wooden leg.

And once, many years later, he found a set of
footprints emerging from the sea.

They ended beside a woman in a red coat.

She looked younger than he remembered. Her scar was gone,
and her eyes were the blue-gray color of deep water
beneath clouds.

"Good morning," she said.

Elias looked toward the harbor. No white ship waited there.

"Are you collecting something?" he asked.

"Not anymore."

"What do you want?"

Captain Rook smiled.

"To see what you did with your hour."

Elias considered this, then offered her his hand.

"Come into town," he said. "I'll show you."

Behind them, the tide began to turn.

The Clockmaker of Bellweather Street

Every clock in Bellweather stopped at 4:17 on the morning
the river caught fire.

For forty-three seconds, blue flames ran across the black water,
curling around bridge pillars and licking the hulls of sleeping
boats. Then rain fell from a cloudless sky, the flames
vanished, and every clock began ticking again.

Every clock except one.

It stood in the window of a narrow shop between
a bakery and an abandoned cinema. Its walnut case was
dark with age, its brass pendulum motionless. Behind the glass,
two hands pointed forever to 4:17.

Above the door, a sign read:

ELIAS VALE
CLOCKS REPAIRED
TIME RESTORED WHEN POSSIBLE

Most people in Bellweather considered the last line a joke.
Mara Venn did not.

At seventeen, Mara knew that time could be damaged.

Her mother had disappeared during the forty-three seconds when the
river burned. One moment, Dr. Lena Venn had been standing
on the north bank with her camera raised. The next,
only the camera remained, balanced neatly on the stone wall
as if placed there by a careful hand.

No body was found. No footprints led away. The photograph
stored in the camera showed nothing but blue light.

Ten years passed.

Mara grew from a frightened girl into the assistant archivist
at the Bellweather Museum of Civic History. She catalogued old
maps, water-damaged letters, rusted coins, and portraits of mayors nobody
remembered. She rented the same apartment where she had lived
with her mother and kept Lena's yellow coat hanging beside
the door.

Each year, on the anniversary of the fire, Mara stood
at the north bank at 4:17 in the morning.

Each year, nothing happened.

Then, on the tenth anniversary, the clock in Elias Vale's
window chimed.

It rang once.

Mara was across the street beneath a red umbrella. The
sound was low and deep, more like a bell heard
underwater than the voice of a mantel clock.

The hands remained fixed at 4:17.

A light appeared in the shop.

Mara crossed the empty street.

Before she could knock, the door opened.

Elias Vale was older than she remembered. He had repaired
her mother's wristwatch when Mara was a child, and she
recalled a tall man with black hair and quick hands.
Now he was stooped and silver-haired. A magnifying lens covered
one eye.

"You heard it," he said.

"Yes."

"That is unfortunate."

"For whom?"

He considered the question. "Probably both of us."

The shop smelled of oil, dust, and rain-soaked wood. Hundreds
of clocks covered the walls. Grandfather clocks crowded the floor.
Pocket watches lay open in velvet trays. Tiny carriage clocks
shivered on glass shelves. Yet the shop was silent.

Not one clock ticked.

Elias locked the door behind her.

"What happened to them?" Mara asked.

"They are listening."

"To what?"

He pointed toward the window.

The walnut clock had no keyhole, no maker's mark, and
no visible hinges. Fine lines covered its case. Mara leaned
closer and realized they were names carved into the wood.

NORA BELL, 1891
TOM ARLEN, 1903
SILAS WREN, 1918
MAY ROWE, 1944

Dozens more continued down the sides.

Near the base, freshly cut into the wood, was another
name.

LENA VENN, 2016.

Mara touched the glass.

The shop disappeared.

For an instant, she stood in darkness filled with ticking.
A woman called her name from very far away.

"Mara!"

She pulled back.

The shop returned so suddenly that she stumbled into a
cabinet. Elias caught her elbow.

"You heard her?" he asked.

Mara's heart hammered. "My mother is in that clock."

"No."

"I heard her."

"You heard where she is. That is not the same
thing."

"Open it."

"I cannot."

"Then break it."

"I cannot do that either."

Mara seized a brass hammer from his workbench.

Elias stepped between her and the clock. "Strike the case
and every person whose name is carved there will be
lost."

"They're already lost."

"Lost things can be found. Destroyed things cannot."

She lowered the hammer, though every part of her wanted
to swing it.

Elias took the tool from her hand.

"What is it?" she asked.

"A door."

"To where?"

"To the missing time."

Outside, rain whispered against the window.

Elias explained that Bellweather had always suffered small accidents of
time. A minute vanished from a school day in 1922.
An entire Sunday repeated itself in 1956, though only three
people remembered. In 1984, every newborn in the city opened
its eyes simultaneously and stared toward the river.

Most incidents passed unnoticed. People blamed faulty watches, poor memory,
exhaustion.

"But sometimes," Elias said, "the damage is too large to
hide."

"The river fire."

He nodded. "Forty-three seconds were torn out of the world.
Your mother was standing too close."

"Where did the seconds go?"

"Nowhere. Somewhere. Language was not designed for this."

Mara looked at the clock. "And the others?"

"Each disappeared during an earlier fracture. The clock gathered them."

"Why?"

"I do not know."

"You've had ten years to find out."

Elias flinched.

That was when Mara understood.

"You knew," she said. "You knew what happened from the
beginning."

"Yes."

"And you said nothing."

"What could I have said? That your mother had fallen
between seconds? That her only road home was sealed inside
a clock I could not open?"

"You could have told me the truth."

"I told your father the truth once."

Mara went still. Her father had died when she was
six. She remembered almost nothing about him except the scent
of tobacco in his scarf and the song he sang
while washing dishes.

"What did my father have to do with this?"

Elias removed his magnifying lens. His left eye was milky
white.

"Your father did not die in a railway accident," he
said. "He entered the clock."

Mara laughed, a single empty sound.

"He tried to rescue someone," Elias continued. "A man named
Daniel Rook, his closest friend. Your father opened the door,
but he never returned. I told your mother. I showed
her everything."

"And she kept it from me."

"To protect you."

"No. She spent years studying the river. She wasn't protecting
me. She was trying to follow him."

Elias said nothing.

Mara turned toward the walnut clock. Her mother had not
been an accidental victim. She had found the fracture and
entered it deliberately.

All those anniversaries Mara had stood by the river, mourning
a woman who had chosen to leave.

Anger arrived before grief.

"How do we open the door?" she asked.

"We do not."

"It chimed for a reason."

"Yes. It is ready to take someone else."

"Or let someone out."

"In a clock, every opening serves both purposes."

Mara faced him. "I am going in."

"No."

"You don't get to decide."

"I have guarded this door for fifty-two years."

"And in all that time, how many people have you
saved?"

His silence answered her.

Mara looked around the motionless shop. "Why did the other
clocks stop?"

"Because the door is about to open."

"When?"

Elias glanced at the fixed hands.

"Tomorrow morning," he said. "At 4:17."

They spent the remaining hours preparing.

Elias drew chalk circles on the floor and placed twelve
clocks around them, each set to a different hour. He
gave Mara a silver pocket watch with no hands.

"It will feel cold when you are near your own
time," he said. "Warm when you move away from it."

"How do I come back?"

"Find the river."

"That's all?"

"No. But it is all I know."

He warned her that the missing time would not resemble
a place. It would build itself from memories, fears, and
unfinished moments. She might see people she loved, but they
could be echoes made by the fracture.

"How will I know what's real?"

"You won't."

"Useful."

"I have had few opportunities to experiment."

"Only my father."

Pain crossed his face. "Only Adrian."

Mara studied him. "You went with him."

Elias rolled back his left sleeve. A line of tiny
numbers was burned into his skin from wrist to elbow.

4:17, repeated again and again.

"I made it back," he said. "Part of me, at
least."

At four in the morning, the rain stopped.

At 4:16, every clock in the shop began ticking at
once.

The sound filled the room like insects in the walls.

Elias stood inside the chalk circle. Mara stood before the
walnut clock, the handless watch clutched in her fist.

"If the door remains open longer than forty-three seconds," Elias
shouted over the ticking, "the fracture will spread."

"What happens then?"

"Bellweather may repeat the same moment forever."

"Then close it behind me."

"Mara—"

"Forty-three seconds. Close it."

The clock struck 4:17.

The glass face swung outward.

Behind it was the river.

Mara stepped through.

She landed on a bridge beneath a white sky.

Bellweather surrounded her, but the city was assembled incorrectly. Streets
rose like stairways into the air. Buildings stood upside down,
their chimneys buried in clouds. Boats drifted through doorways. Thousands
of clocks floated in the river, their faces shining under
the water.

On the bridge, dozens of people stood perfectly still.

A woman held a market basket. A soldier leaned on
a cane. A boy reached for a paper airplane hanging
motionless beyond his fingers.

Mara checked the pocket watch. The silver case was warm.

Wrong way.

She turned.

At the far end of the bridge stood her mother.

Lena wore the yellow coat that still hung in Mara's
apartment. Her dark hair was tied back. She looked exactly
as she had ten years ago.

"Mara," she said.

Mara could not move.

Her mother crossed the bridge slowly, as if approaching a
frightened animal.

"You've grown," Lena whispered.

It was a foolish thing to say, and because it
was foolish, Mara believed her.

She struck her mother across the face.

Lena staggered.

"That," Mara said, trembling, "was for leaving."

Then she embraced her.

For a few seconds, ten years collapsed. Mara was seventeen
again, breathing in the familiar scent of lavender soap and
river rain. Her mother held her so tightly it hurt.

"I tried to come back," Lena said.

"You chose to come here."

"Yes."

"For him?"

"For your father."

"And did you find him?"

Lena released her.

"Come with me."

They crossed the bridge. Around them, frozen people slowly turned
their heads to watch.

"Who are they?" Mara asked.

"People the fractures took."

"Are they alive?"

"Sometimes."

The bridge ended at the museum where Mara worked. Inside,
the galleries were lit by blue flames. Portraits whispered in
their frames. Glass display cases contained objects from Mara's childhood:
a broken red crayon, her first lost tooth, a birthday
card she had never sent.

Lena led her to the archive room.

Adrian Venn sat at a desk, smoking his pipe.

He looked up.

Mara remembered almost nothing about her father, yet she knew
him immediately. He had her narrow face and restless hands.
The tobacco scent struck her with such force that tears
filled her eyes.

"My little star," he said.

No one had called her that since she was six.

Mara approached him.

The pocket watch turned hot in her hand.

She stopped.

"What is it?" Lena asked.

"This isn't him."

Adrian smiled sadly. "Not all of him."

"What does that mean?"

He tapped ash into a dish. The motion repeated: tap,
lift, tap, lift.

Mara noticed the smoke rising from his pipe. It formed
the same spiral again and again.

"He's a memory," she said.

Lena's eyes filled with tears. "He remembers me."

"Because you remember him."

"He talks to me."

"Does he ever say anything you don't already know?"

Lena looked at Adrian.

He smiled again. "My little star."

Tap, lift, tap, lift.

Lena sank into a chair.

"I spent years searching," she whispered. "I thought if I
found the right room, the right hour…"

Mara knelt beside her.

"Come home."

"I don't know how."

"The river."

Lena shook her head. "It changes whenever I approach. It
brings me back here."

"Then we approach together."

The floor shuddered.

Somewhere, a bell rang thirty times in rapid succession.

The archive shelves began to stretch upward.

"He's closing the door," Lena said.

"Elias?"

"He can't."

"I told him to."

"If he closes it while you're here, you'll become part
of this place."

They ran.

Outside, the white sky had darkened. Cracks spread across it,
revealing the clockmaker's shop beyond. Through one crack, Mara saw
Elias straining to push the walnut door shut.

"Mara!" he shouted.

His voice shook the city.

The frozen people began to move.

The woman dropped her market basket. The soldier lifted his
cane. The boy caught his paper airplane.

Then all of them turned toward Mara and Lena.

"Run," Lena said.

They raced down a street that bent beneath their feet.
The crowd followed, first walking, then sprinting. Their faces flickered
from young to old, alive to dead.

At the riverbank, the water rose into a vertical wall.
Thousands of submerged clocks ticked beneath its surface.

The pocket watch burned Mara's palm.

"This is it," she said.

"There's no crossing."

"There has to be."

The pursuing crowd filled the street behind them.

Mara stared into the upright river. Her reflection stared back,
but it was seventeen years old. Beside her stood Lena,
and beside Lena stood Adrian.

Not the repeating echo from the archive. This Adrian was
older. Silver threaded his hair. His eyes were tired and
alert.

Lena touched the surface.

"Adrian?"

He raised his hand from the other side.

The water rippled.

The crowd stopped.

A voice came from the river, faint but clear.

"Only two."

Mara looked at her mother.

"What does it mean?" she asked.

Lena's expression told her she already knew.

"There are three of us caught between," Lena said. "You,
your father, and me. The door will carry only two
back."

"No."

"Mara—"

"No. We find another way."

The city buckled. Buildings folded into the streets. The crack
in the sky narrowed as Elias forced the clock shut.

"Only two," the river repeated.

Adrian pressed his palm against the water.

Lena did the same.

For ten years she had searched for him. Mara saw
that time in her mother's face now—not in wrinkles, because
Lena had not aged, but in weariness far deeper than
age.

"I'm staying," Lena said.

"You can't."

"I came here to find him."

"You came here because you couldn't let go."

"Yes."

Mara gripped her arm. "I lost both of you because
neither of you could let go of the dead."

Lena stared at her.

The words were cruel, but there was no time to
make them gentle.

"Dad followed Daniel," Mara said. "You followed Dad. If I
stay for you, then what? We haunt each other forever?"

Behind the river, Adrian closed his eyes.

Then he stepped forward.

His hand passed through the surface and touched Lena's cheek.

He smiled—not the repeated smile from the archive, but something
trembling and human.

"Take her home," he said.

The river broke.

Water crashed over them, full of clocks and blue fire.

Mara felt Lena's hand in hers. Adrian seized her other
hand. For one impossible moment, all three were pulled through
roaring darkness.

Then Adrian let go.

Mara screamed his name.

She saw him falling backward among the floating clocks, growing
younger as he fell. Forty years old. Thirty. Twenty. A
boy laughing beneath summer rain.

He vanished into the blue flames.

Mara struck the floor of the clock shop.

The walnut door slammed shut behind her.

All the clocks stopped.

For several seconds, she could hear only her own breathing.

Then someone coughed.

Lena lay beside her.

Elias leaned against the workbench, his face gray. The chalk
circles had burned into black rings. The walnut clock was
split from top to bottom.

"You came back," he said.

Mara helped her mother sit up.

Lena looked around the shop as if she had awakened
in a stranger's house.

"What time is it?" she asked.

Elias examined his wristwatch.

"4:18."

Outside, Bellweather was beginning to wake.

The first buses groaned along wet streets. Lamps appeared in
apartment windows. At the bakery next door, someone carried trays
toward the ovens.

Only one minute had passed.

Lena stood unsteadily and approached the broken clock. Adrian's name
had appeared beneath hers.

ADRIAN VENN, 2006.

She touched the carving.

"I left him there."

"No," Mara said. "He sent us home."

The walnut case gave a quiet sigh.

Then it collapsed into dust.

A wind swept through the shop though the windows were
closed. The dust rose, spun once around Lena and Mara,
and vanished.

One by one, the other clocks began to tick.

Elias listened with his eyes closed.

"Is it over?" Mara asked.

"This fracture is."

"And the people whose names were on the clock?"

"Gone, I think."

"Dead?"

"Free."

Lena went to the window. Across the street, the river
lay dark and ordinary between its banks.

"I don't know how to return to my life," she
said.

Mara stood beside her. Their reflections looked like sisters: one
aged by ten years, the other untouched.

"You don't return to it," Mara said. "You start from
here."

In the months that followed, Bellweather accepted Lena's reappearance with
surprising ease.

The police reopened old files. Journalists camped outside the apartment.
Scientists asked questions Lena could not answer. Some people called
her a fraud. Others called her a miracle.

The city records office faced a particular difficulty. According to
the law, Lena Venn had been declared dead seven years
earlier. A tired clerk eventually stamped her application for legal
restoration and said, "You wouldn't believe how often the forms
fail to cover the situation."

Lena moved back into the apartment.

At first, she wandered through the rooms at night, touching
objects from a future she had not lived. She learned
to use a newer telephone. She watched ten years of
news in fragments. She discovered which friends had married, moved
away, or died.

She and Mara argued.

They argued about Adrian, about abandonment, about the unpaid electricity
bill, about whether milk could still be used two days
after its printed date. Ordinary anger entered the spaces where
grief had lived.

Slowly, they became a family again—not the family they had
been, but a smaller, stranger one.

Elias closed his shop that winter.

He gave Mara the keys.

"I'm not a clockmaker," she told him.

"Neither was I when I began."

"What will you do?"

He looked toward the river. "Find out what retirement feels
like. I'm told it passes quickly."

Mara kept her job at the museum, but every evening
she opened the shop on Bellweather Street. Elias taught her
to clean gears, balance pendulums, and hear the difference between
a tired spring and a broken tooth.

The sign remained above the door:

CLOCKS REPAIRED
TIME RESTORED WHEN POSSIBLE

Customers still thought the second line was a joke.

Years passed without another fracture.

Then, one rainy afternoon, a little boy entered carrying a
green alarm clock. He placed it on the counter and
said it had begun ringing every night at an hour
that did not exist.

Mara examined the clock.

Its hands pointed between 2:59 and 3:00.

From inside came a faint sound like someone knocking on
a distant door.

Mara looked across the shop at Lena, who was repairing
a pocket watch beneath a bright lamp.

Lena had finally begun to age. There were silver strands
in her hair and lines at the corners of her
eyes. She heard the knocking too.

For a moment, neither woman moved.

Then Lena set down her tools.

Mara turned the sign in the window from OPEN to
CLOSED.

Together, they listened.

### The Museum of Forgotten Weather

On the morning the rain disappeared, Mara Vale found a
cloud in a glass jar.

It stood on the highest shelf of her father's museum,
squeezed between a bottle of thunder and a wooden box
labeled *The Smell Before Snow*. The cloud was no larger
than a sleeping cat. It pressed against the glass in
pale folds, restless and gray, and whenever Mara touched the
jar, a cold drop of water appeared on the inside.

Outside, the city shone beneath another spotless blue sky.

For eleven years, no rain had fallen on Bellwether.

The weather towers kept the days warm, the nights cool,
and the clouds beyond the city walls. Storms damaged roads,
interrupted trade, and made people late for work, so the
Council had decided that weather was an inconvenience the city
could live without.

At first, everyone had celebrated. Weddings were planned without tents.
Laundry dried in an hour. Children grew up never having
to run home through a downpour.

Then the river narrowed.

The parks turned brown.

Water was rationed, fountains were drained, and every citizen learned
to wash with a cup and a cloth. Still the
towers hummed, the sky stayed blue, and the Council insisted
that engineers would solve the problem.

Mara's father had not believed them.

That was why he had opened the Museum of Forgotten
Weather in an abandoned railway station at the edge of
the city. There he collected things people no longer remembered:
umbrellas, muddy boots, hailstones preserved in salt, sketches of lightning,
and recordings of wind in chimneys.

Most visitors came to laugh.

"People carried those?" children asked, pointing at the umbrellas.

"They folded up in storms," Mara's father would say.

"Why didn't they just stop the storms?"

He never answered that question directly. Instead, he opened one
of the museum's narrow windows and listened to the dry
wind dragging dust across the old rails.

"Because not everything that troubles us is our enemy," he
said.

Six months earlier, Mara had found him asleep at his
desk with his cheek resting on an unfinished map. He
had not awakened. Now she ran the museum alone, though
hardly anyone visited.

The cloud in the jar had not been there the
night before.

Mara climbed a ladder and carried it down. A tag
hung from a thread around its neck.

*For when Bellwether is ready.*

The handwriting was her father's.

Beneath those words, in smaller letters, he had written:

*Take it to the western tower before sunset. Do not
let the Council see.*

Mara read the message three times. Then she looked through
the station windows at the western weather tower.

It rose above the rooftops like a silver needle, its
rotating arms flashing in the sun. There were four towers
around Bellwether, each producing an invisible field that pushed natural
weather away. The western tower was the oldest. Her father
had circled it many times on his unfinished map.

A knock echoed through the museum.

Mara threw a cloth over the jar.

Inspector Quill entered without waiting.

He was a tall, narrow man dressed in the white
uniform of the Civic Climate Office. Dust never seemed to
touch him. Even his boots were polished, though the street
outside was thick with powdery soil.

"Miss Vale," he said. "You are open early."

"I live upstairs."

"Yes. An arrangement the Council continues to tolerate."

His gaze moved over the exhibits. He disliked the museum.
That alone did not worry Mara. Most officials disliked anything
that reminded people the city had once been different.

What worried her was the black metal instrument in his
hand. A needle trembled behind its round glass face.

"A humidity detector," he said when he saw her looking.
"We registered an unauthorized atmospheric concentration in this district."

Mara leaned against the table, hiding the jar behind her.

"Perhaps someone boiled a kettle."

"Citizens are not permitted to waste water boiling kettles."

"For tea?"

"Especially for tea."

He walked toward the shelf.

Beneath the cloth, the jar gave a soft rumble.

Mara coughed loudly.

Inspector Quill stopped.

"Are you ill?"

"Dust."

"Another reason this building should be closed."

He consulted the detector. The needle jerked toward Mara.

Her heart quickened.

Then, from the back of the museum, came a deafening
crash. Inspector Quill spun around.

A case of preserved icicles had collapsed, scattering empty glass
tubes across the floor. In the middle of the wreckage
stood a boy with red hair, a patched coat, and
a guilty expression.

"Sorry," he said. "Was that valuable?"

"Tobin," Mara said through her teeth.

Inspector Quill pointed at him. "You. Remain where you are."

Tobin ran.

The inspector chased him through the gallery, past the Exhibition
of Unpleasant Breezes and around a full-size model of a
snowman. Tobin slipped through a service door. Quill followed, shouting
regulations.

Mara seized the jar, wrapped it in the cloth, and
hurried out the front entrance.

She knew Tobin would escape. He always did.

They met ten minutes later behind a bakery. The bakery
had not baked bread in two years, but its awning
still smelled faintly of yeast.

"You could have warned me," Tobin said, brushing dust from
his sleeves.

"I didn't know you were there."

"I sleep in the baggage room on Wednesdays."

"You sleep in the museum?"

"Only when the roof of my building is too hot.
What did Quill want?"

Mara showed him the jar.

Tobin's eyes widened. "Is that smoke?"

The cloud flashed from within. A tiny bolt of lightning
struck the glass.

Tobin nearly dropped it.

"It's angry smoke."

"It's a cloud," Mara said.

"I've seen pictures of clouds. They're enormous."

"This one is folded."

"Clouds can fold?"

"How would I know? I've never seen one either."

She showed him the tag.

Tobin read it and looked toward the western tower.

"We should throw it away."

"No."

"Then sell it."

"No."

"Then at least wait until Quill isn't searching the city
for illegal dampness."

"My father wanted me to take it before sunset."

"Your father also kept bottled fog in his bedroom."

"He knew more about the towers than anyone."

Tobin returned the jar. The cloud swirled around itself, darkening.

"What happens when you get there?"

"I don't know."

"That is my favorite kind of plan," he said miserably.
"It always ends with running."

They crossed the city by alleys and covered markets, keeping
the jar hidden beneath Tobin's coat. Bellwether had once been
famous for its gardens. Mara's father had described avenues shaded
by chestnut trees and balconies dripping with flowers. Now the
trees stood bare, and painted blossoms covered the balconies.

People waited in long lines at public pumps. Each person
received one bottle of water. Guards watched to make sure
no one returned twice.

At the old central fountain, a crowd had gathered around
a Council crier.

"Reservoir levels remain adequate," the crier announced. "Citizens are reminded
that rumors of shortage are false and punishable."

Behind him, workers were boarding up another dry well.

Tobin slowed.

"My sister says the south district pump stopped yesterday," he
said. "They told everyone to come back next week."

Mara watched a woman tip her ration bottle so her
little boy could drink. He swallowed eagerly. The woman did
not take any for herself.

"We'll reach the tower," Mara said.

"If the cloud can make rain."

"It must."

"And if it can't?"

She had no answer.

At noon, every public screen in the city flickered. The
Council seal appeared, followed by Inspector Quill's face.

"Attention. A hazardous atmospheric device has been stolen from civic
custody. The suspects are Mara Vale, aged nineteen, and Tobin
Rusk, aged seventeen. Do not approach them. Report all sightings
to the Climate Office."

"Civic custody?" Tobin said. "It was in your museum."

"The Council considers everything its custody."

"My age is eighteen."

"You told me you were seventeen."

"I was avoiding tax registration."

People in the square turned toward them.

Tobin pulled his cap low. Mara tightened her grip on
the bundle. They slipped into a fabric shop and escaped
through the rear while whistles sounded behind them.

The western district was separated from the city center by
an old canal. Its bridges were guarded, but Mara's father's
map showed a maintenance tunnel beneath the waterway.

They found the entrance behind a collapsed warehouse. The iron
door was rusted shut. Tobin kicked it twice and hurt
his foot. Mara searched the ground until she found a
brick, then broke the lock.

A breath of cold air rose from the darkness.

Inside, steps descended below the city. Mara led the way,
holding a small lamp. The tunnel smelled of metal, stone,
and something else.

She stopped.

"What is it?" Tobin whispered.

Mara breathed deeply. The scent was cool and ancient, like
the inside of a cave.

"Water."

A narrow stream ran along the tunnel floor.

Tobin knelt, but Mara grabbed his shoulder.

"It may not be clean."

"It's water."

He dipped his fingers into it and touched them to
his tongue. Then he laughed.

Mara had heard Tobin laugh many times—usually when something expensive
broke—but never like this. The sound echoed through the tunnel.

"It's cold," he said.

The jar shook beneath his coat.

Mara pulled it out. The cloud had turned almost black.
Drops raced down the glass.

As she held it near the stream, the cloud gathered
itself against the side closest to the water.

"It wants out," Tobin said.

"Not yet."

A metallic clang rang behind them.

Inspector Quill stood at the top of the steps, flanked
by two officers.

"Put the device down," he ordered.

Tobin snatched up a loose pipe.

"Don't," Mara said. There was nowhere to run. The tunnel
ahead ended at a locked grate, and the officers blocked
the stairs.

Quill descended carefully.

"You do not understand what you are carrying," he said.

"My father did."

"Your father was a sentimental fool."

Mara's fear hardened into anger. "He knew the reservoirs were
failing."

"Of course he knew. Everyone in the Climate Office knows."

Tobin lowered the pipe slightly.

"Then why keep the towers running?" Mara asked.

Quill's mouth tightened. "Because order depends on certainty. The city
was built on schedules, harvest projections, transportation guarantees. Weather is
chaos. If the towers stop, storms may come. Floods. Lightning.
Disease."

"People are thirsty now."

"The Council is developing alternatives."

"The Council is boarding up wells."

Quill held out his hand.

"The object in that jar is not an ordinary cloud.
It is a seed system—an experimental concentration created before the
weather towers were activated. Released near a tower, it could
reverse the field. Once that begins, we may never regain
control."

Mara looked at the tiny cloud.

Her father had not merely saved a piece of weather.
He had saved the possibility of weather returning.

Quill took another step.

"Your father understood the risk in the end. That is
why he did not release it."

"No," Mara said. "He was waiting."

"For what?"

She turned the tag so he could read it.

*For when Bellwether is ready.*

Quill's expression changed. For an instant, Mara saw something behind
his official calm: exhaustion, perhaps, or fear.

"The city will never be ready," he said quietly.

From above came the faint ringing of bells.

One bell, then another. Soon dozens joined them.

The water bells.

Mara had heard them only once before, during a cracked
pipe emergency. They meant the daily ration had been reduced.

The officers looked at one another.

Tobin smiled without humor. "Sounds ready to me."

He swung the pipe at the lamp.

Darkness filled the tunnel.

Someone shouted. Mara heard boots scrape stone and Quill ordering
everyone to stay still. Tobin caught her hand and pulled
her toward the stream.

"The grate," she whispered.

"I picked the lock while he was talking."

They splashed forward. Mara struck her shoulder against the open
grate, squeezed through, and ran bent double along the tunnel.
Behind them, an officer relit the lamp.

"There!" Quill shouted.

The tunnel rose. They climbed a ladder and emerged inside
the western tower.

Its interior was vast and hollow, crowded with gears, cables,
and humming coils. A spiral staircase wound upward around a
central column of blue light. The air tasted sharply of
metal.

The door slammed open below.

Tobin pushed Mara toward the stairs.

"I'll slow them down."

"With what?"

He looked around, grabbed a red lever, and pulled it.

Every gear in the tower stopped.

Then they began turning backward.

"That," he said.

Alarms shrieked. Sparks rained from the walls. Mara climbed. Tobin
followed as the officers entered below.

The staircase trembled beneath them. At the top, Mara found
a circular room surrounded by windows. Beyond the glass, Bellwether
stretched beneath its empty sky. The city looked brittle from
above, bleached and pale, its dead parks like burns on
the earth.

In the center of the room stood the tower's control
engine. Four brass arms rotated around a dark sphere. Each
arm bore the same warning:

*ATMOSPHERIC SEAL—DO NOT INTERRUPT.*

Mara set the jar beside the engine.

There was no keyhole, no obvious place to put it.

"What now?" Tobin asked.

She searched her father's map. A line ran from the
museum to the tower, ending in a small drawing of
an open hand.

"Maybe we release it here."

"By opening the jar?"

"Yes."

"That seems too simple."

Boots thundered up the stairs.

Mara gripped the lid. It would not turn.

Tobin tried. The lid remained fixed.

Below, Inspector Quill shouted, "Step away from the engine!"

Tobin struck the jar against the floor.

"No!"

The glass did not crack. The cloud flashed violently, and
thunder shook the room.

Quill reached the top of the stairs, breathing hard. One
officer followed him. The other had apparently remained below, perhaps
trapped by whatever Tobin had done to the gears.

"It cannot be opened by force," Quill said. "Your father
designed it that way."

"You know how?"

"Yes."

He approached slowly.

"Then help us."

"I can't."

The water bells were still ringing across Bellwether.

Quill looked through the windows. From the tower, they could
see lines forming at every pump. The lines were longer
now. People held buckets, bottles, cooking pots—anything that might carry
water.

"My daughter is down there," Quill said.

Mara had never imagined him with a family.

"She is seven. She believes rain is a story invented
to frighten children indoors."

"Help us show her it's real," Mara said.

The officer behind Quill raised his baton. "Inspector, your orders
are to recover the device."

Quill did not move.

"Inspector?"

The officer stepped toward the jar.

Tobin tackled him.

They crashed into the control panel. The brass arms spun
wildly. A window shattered, and hot wind rushed into the
room. The officer threw Tobin aside and reached for the
cloud.

Quill caught his wrist.

For a moment, neither man spoke.

Then Quill twisted the baton from the officer's hand and
struck him once behind the ear. The officer crumpled.

Tobin stared.

"I've wanted to do that for twelve years," Quill said.

He took the jar.

"The lid opens with moisture."

"There's water inside," Mara said.

"Not the cloud's water. It requires a willing contribution."

Quill held the jar out to her. "A safeguard. Your
father believed that no one should control the weather without
first giving up something of their own."

Mara thought of the stream beneath the city.

"I don't have water."

Quill removed a silver flask from inside his uniform.

"My ration," he said.

He poured a few precious drops onto the lid.

Nothing happened.

"You said it had to be willing," Tobin said.

Quill looked offended. "It was."

"Maybe part of you still wants control."

Quill stared at the flask. Then he slowly tipped it
upside down. Water poured over the lid, down the jar,
and onto the dusty floor.

All of it.

The lid clicked.

The room became silent.

Mara placed her hand on the glass. The cloud pressed
against her palm from the other side.

She thought of her father opening museum windows to listen
for wind. She thought of the woman giving her ration
to her child. She thought of muddy boots, ruined picnics,
leaking roofs, lightning, delays, uncertainty.

Not everything that troubles us is our enemy.

She opened the jar.

The cloud rose like a ribbon of smoke.

For one disappointing second, it hovered above the jar, small
and uncertain.

Then the tower's central sphere cracked.

Blue light spilled upward. The cloud plunged into it.

The explosion made no sound.

A ring of darkness spread across the sky, swallowing the
blue. The tower arms stopped. Far away, the northern tower
flashed and went dark, then the eastern, then the southern.

The perfect sky broke apart.

Clouds rolled over the city walls.

Real clouds—vast, towering, black at their roots and silver along
their edges. They came like mountains in motion. Wind struck
the tower, carrying scents Mara knew only from bottles and
boxes: wet stone, leaves, distant fields, rivers.

The first drop landed on the broken window.

It was perfectly round.

It trembled on the glass, then slid down.

Another struck Mara's cheek.

She touched it. Her fingers came away wet.

Tobin laughed.

Rain swept across Bellwether.

It hammered rooftops and flooded gutters. It washed dust from
windows, signs, statues, and trees. It soaked the people waiting
at the pumps.

For a moment, no one moved.

Then the city erupted.

Children screamed and danced. Adults lifted pots toward the sky.
Some ran for shelter out of instincts inherited but never
learned, while others simply stood in the streets with their
mouths open.

Mara leaned out of the broken window. The rain drenched
her hair and clothes. It was colder than she expected.
Wilder, too. It stung her skin and blurred the city
below.

Thunder rolled overhead.

Tobin flinched, then grinned.

"Was it always this loud?"

"I hope so."

Inspector Quill stood apart from them. Rain ran down his
face. He removed his white officer's cap and let the
wind carry it away.

"What happens now?" he asked.

Below, water rushed through streets never designed for storms. Roofs
leaked. Market awnings tore loose. Somewhere, an alarm began to
ring.

Mara looked at the dark clouds filling the world.

"Now we learn."

The rain continued for three days.

It caused exactly the trouble the Council had feared. Roads
flooded. Trains stopped. A chimney collapsed through the roof of
the tax office. Hundreds of homes leaked, including Mara's rooms
above the museum.

No one arrived at work on time.

But the river widened.

The wells filled.

Within a week, green shoots appeared in the parks. Citizens
gathered around them in wonder, guarding them from careless feet.
The Council attempted to restart the towers, but no engineer
could make them work. The atmospheric field had reversed permanently.

Inspector Quill was dismissed from office. He took a position
at the museum instead.

He was surprisingly good with visitors, though he still disliked
children touching the exhibits.

Tobin registered his correct age, paid none of his overdue
taxes, and became the city's first official roof-drain specialist. Whenever
asked where he had learned the trade, he said he
had always understood water, which Mara considered a lie.

As for the Museum of Forgotten Weather, it changed its
name.

Mara removed the old sign and hung a new one
above the railway doors:

**THE MUSEUM OF ORDINARY WEATHER**

The exhibits changed too. There were fresh hailstones, new recordings
of thunder, and photographs of the first storm. In one
glass case, Mara displayed the empty jar with her father's
tag beside it.

Visitors often asked what had been inside.

"A choice," Mara told them.

Years later, people still argued about the return of weather.

Farmers praised the rain and cursed the hail. Children loved
snow until they had to walk through it. Lovers met
beneath umbrellas. Lightning split the old Council Hall from roof
to foundation. Summers became unpredictable, winters inconvenient, and every picnic
was a wager.

Bellwether never regained certainty.

Instead, it regained seasons.

And sometimes, on gray afternoons, Mara closed the museum early
and stood beneath the station's iron roof, listening as rain
rattled above her.

She would hold out her hand beyond the shelter and
wait for a drop to strike her palm.

No two ever felt exactly the same.

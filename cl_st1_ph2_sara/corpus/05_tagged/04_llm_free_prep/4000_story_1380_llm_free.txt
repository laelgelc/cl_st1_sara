The town of Bellwether had no clocks.

There were pale circles on kitchen walls where clocks had
once hung, and empty brackets above the doors of public
buildings. The jeweler still displayed velvet trays of watches, but
their hands had been removed. In the schoolhouse, children learned
sums, geography, and the names of the winds, but no
one taught them how many minutes made an hour.

Time in Bellwether belonged to the tower.

At dawn, the tower rang once. At noon, twice. At
dusk, three times. Its bronze bell was so large that
when it sounded, spoons trembled in drawers, birds lifted from
rooftops, and ripples appeared in cups of tea. The townspeople
woke, ate, worked, prayed, and slept according to those three
daily notes.

The keeper of the tower was a quiet old man
named Abel Flint. Every morning he climbed the spiral staircase,
wound the ancient mechanism, checked its brass wheels, and listened
for anything out of rhythm.

Nobody else was permitted inside.

"Why?" the children often asked.

"Because time is delicate," their parents replied.

Only one child remained dissatisfied with that answer.

Her name was Mira Vale, and she lived with her
mother in a narrow blue house behind the bakery. She
had a talent for noticing things that other people did
not: a loose slate before it fell, the first yellow
leaf of summer, the pause a person made before telling
a lie.

She noticed that the tower was not always exact.

Sometimes the dawn bell rang when the eastern sky was
still dark. Sometimes dusk arrived while the sun hovered above
the hills. Once, during a winter storm, the noon bell
sounded twice in a single afternoon.

The adults pretended nothing unusual had happened.

"Perhaps you counted wrong," said the baker.

"Perhaps the thunder confused you," said the schoolmistress.

"It rang twice," Mira insisted.

Her mother, Elian, glanced toward the tower and pressed a
flour-covered finger to Mira's lips.

"Some things are safer left unquestioned."

That, Mira thought, was what adults said when they were
frightened.

Years earlier, before Mira was born, her father had worked
in the tower. His name had been Julian Vale, and
whenever Mira asked what had happened to him, her mother
gave the same answer.

"He went away."

"Where?"

"Far away."

"Why?"

"Because he could not stay."

Mira had learned that repeating a question did not guarantee
a better answer. So she kept her questions to herself
and collected clues instead.

Her father had left behind three possessions: a gray coat
with silver buttons, a leather notebook filled with blank pages,
and a small brass key that opened nothing in their
house.

Mira carried the key on a string beneath her shirt.

On the morning of her fifteenth birthday, the tower failed
to ring.

The sun rose without permission.

At first, Bellwether remained asleep. Chimneys did not smoke. Bakers
did not knead dough. Shopkeepers did not lift their shutters.
Dawn spread over tiled roofs and empty streets as silently
as spilled milk.

Mira was awake because birthdays made sleep difficult. She watched
sunlight creep across her bedroom floor and waited for the
bell.

It did not come.

She dressed, hurried downstairs, and found her mother standing by
the window.

"Has it ever missed dawn before?" Mira asked.

Elian looked pale. "Once."

"When?"

"The day your father left."

A door slammed outside. Then another. People emerged into the
street in nightclothes and hastily buttoned coats, blinking at the
sky.

"What do we do?" someone shouted.

"Is it morning?"

"How long has the sun been up?"

"Should we open the shops?"

The confusion spread rapidly. Without the bell, no one trusted
the evidence of light, hunger, or wakefulness. They gathered in
the square beneath the tower and called for Abel Flint.

There was no answer.

The mayor arrived wearing one shoe. He pounded on the
tower door, but it remained locked.

"We must wait," he announced.

"For what?" asked Mira.

The mayor frowned at her. "For the keeper."

"What if he is inside?"

"Then he will come out."

"What if he can't?"

A murmur passed through the crowd.

The mayor raised his voice. "Nobody enters the tower. That
is the law."

Mira touched the key beneath her shirt. She had passed
the tower door every day of her life without considering
its lock closely. Now she pushed through the crowd.

The door was made of black oak, studded with iron.
At its center waited a small, old-fashioned keyhole.

"Mira," her mother said behind her.

Mira drew out the brass key.

The square fell silent.

"Where did you get that?" the mayor demanded.

"My father left it."

A sound came from inside the tower.

Not a bell. A knock.

Three slow blows echoed through the door.

Mira inserted the key before anyone could stop her. It
turned smoothly.

The door opened onto darkness.

Her mother seized her wrist. For a moment, Mira saw
fear and anger struggle across Elian's face.

Then Elian released her.

"Take his coat," she whispered.

"Whose?"

"You know whose."

Mira ran home, returned wearing her father's gray coat, and
stepped into the tower.

The door closed behind her.

The air inside smelled of cold stone and machine oil.
Narrow windows admitted blades of light. A staircase curled upward
around a central shaft in which ropes, chains, and iron
weights hung motionless.

"Master Flint?" Mira called.

No answer.

She climbed.

At the second landing, she found Abel's lantern. At the
third, his toolbox lay open, wrenches scattered across the steps.
At the fifth, one of the immense iron weights had
fallen sideways, cracking the floor.

Beside it lay Abel Flint.

Mira hurried to him. He was breathing, though a purple
bruise covered one temple. When she touched his shoulder, his
eyes opened.

"Julian?" he murmured.

"No. I'm Mira."

He stared at her coat, then at her face. "Of
course. You have his eyes."

"What happened?"

"The heart stopped."

"Your heart?"

"The tower's."

Abel tried to rise but collapsed with a groan.

"You need a doctor."

"The door must stay shut."

"The whole town is outside."

"That is why it must stay shut."

He pointed upward. "Restart the mechanism. Then fetch whoever you
like."

"I don't know how."

"Your father did."

"My father isn't here."

"No," Abel said bitterly. "He chose not to be."

Another knock reverberated through the tower, though nobody stood at
the door. This time Mira realized it came from the
walls.

Three slow blows.

"What is that?"

Abel's eyes moved toward the central shaft.

"The thing the tower keeps."

Mira waited for him to explain. When he did not,
she took the lantern and climbed higher.

The stairs ended in a circular chamber beneath the bell.
Gears crowded every wall: brass wheels large as wagon wheels,
silver cogs no bigger than coins, chains, pendulums, springs, and
levers. Most had stopped, but one wheel still turned weakly,
clicking like an insect.

At the center stood a locked metal cabinet.

The brass key opened it.

Inside, Mira found a stack of notebooks. The first belonged
to Abel Flint. The next bore names she knew from
old gravestones. At the bottom was a leather notebook identical
to the blank one her father had left at home.

JULIAN VALE, it said.

She opened it.

The early pages contained diagrams of the tower. Later came
hurried notes.

The mechanism is not a clock.

The sentence had been underlined three times.

Mira read on.

Centuries ago, something had been discovered beneath Bellwether—a fracture, her
father called it, though not in earth or stone. It
was a fracture in time itself.

Minutes leaked through it.

At first, the effects were small. A woman entered her
house and found that her infant son had grown into
an old man. A shepherd took one step and crossed
an entire winter. Dead flowers bloomed, crumbled, and bloomed again.
People remembered events that had not happened and forgot their
own names.

The founders of Bellwether built the tower over the fracture.
Its mechanism gathered the wandering minutes and forced them into
a steady rhythm. The bell did more than announce time.

It made time obey.

Mira turned the pages faster.

Her father had believed the mechanism was failing. Each year,
it required more power. Each keeper repaired it without understanding
why its gears wore down more quickly than before.

Then Julian discovered the truth.

The tower did not run on springs or weights alone.
It drew time from the keeper.

Each winding of the mechanism stole a little: a day,
a month, sometimes a year. The keepers aged quickly, though
the town believed they were always old men when appointed.
Abel Flint had been thirty-two when he entered the tower.
After thirteen years, he looked nearly seventy.

Julian had refused to become keeper.

He had searched for another way, but Abel—then his assistant—had
feared what would happen if the mechanism stopped. They argued.
Julian left Bellwether, promising to return when he found a
solution.

The last page contained only a warning.

If the tower stops, do not wind the red wheel.
It will restart the mechanism, but the price is greater
than they know.

Mira looked across the chamber.

There, beside the motionless pendulum, was a red wheel.

Footsteps scraped below. Abel entered, pulling himself up the final
stair.

"You read it," he said.

"Yes."

"Then you know what must be done."

"My father said not to turn the wheel."

"Your father valued his own life above thousands of others."

"He was looking for another solution."

"He failed."

"You don't know that."

"He has been gone fifteen years."

Below them, fists hammered at the door. The townspeople were
losing patience.

A groan passed through the tower. Dust fell from the
ceiling. For an instant, sunlight through the narrow windows became
red, then white, then vanished. Mira smelled snow. A moment
later the chamber was hot with summer.

The fracture was opening.

Abel dragged himself toward the red wheel.

"Don't," Mira said.

"If the mechanism remains stopped, Bellwether will be torn across
centuries."

"What happens if you turn it?"

"The tower takes what it needs."

"How much?"

He did not answer.

Mira moved between him and the wheel.

"How much, Abel?"

"All of it," he said. "One life can hold the
fracture for perhaps twenty years."

Mira understood. "There have been more keepers than the names
on the tower records."

"Yes."

"What happens to them?"

"Nothing remains."

Not death, then. Something worse. The keeper's entire lifetime would
be fed into the tower—past and future alike. No body,
no grave, perhaps not even a memory.

"That's murder," Mira said.

"That's Bellwether."

The knocking came again, loud enough to shake the gears.

Three blows.

A crack appeared in the air between them.

Through it, Mira saw the same chamber, but vines covered
the machinery. A woman stood among them. She had silver
hair and wore a gray coat with silver buttons.

Mira was looking at herself.

The older Mira lifted one hand and pressed her palm
against the other side of the fracture.

Then the vision disappeared.

Abel stared. "The tower is showing possibilities now."

"Was that my future?"

"One of them."

"Then I have a future. The tower hasn't taken it
yet."

"Help me turn the wheel."

"No."

"Mira—"

"My father wrote that the tower steals time because the
fracture is starved. But what if it isn't starving? What
if the tower has trained it to feed?"

Abel's expression hardened. "You are a child guessing at forces
you cannot understand."

"So were the founders when they built this."

She opened Julian's notebook again. One diagram showed the fracture
as a jagged line beneath the tower. Around it, he
had drawn the mechanism's lowest gears. One sentence was written
in the margin:

A wound cannot heal while the knife remains inside.

"The tower is the knife," Mira said.

Abel shook his head. "The tower is the only thing
protecting us."

"Or it keeps reopening the fracture every time it winds."

Another crack appeared. Mira saw herself as a little girl,
sitting on her father's shoulders while bells rang above them.
She had no memory of that moment, yet she felt
the warmth of his hands around hers.

In another crack, the tower lay ruined and the square
was empty.

Then came a third vision: the blue house behind the
bakery, its windows dark. Her mother sat alone at the
table, older than Abel, whispering Mira's name.

Possible futures. Possible pasts. Time spilling loose.

Mira examined the mechanism. The red wheel connected to every
major gear, but a black lever near the floor led
downward by a single iron rod.

"What does that do?"

Abel followed her gaze. "It releases the foundation lock."

"Meaning?"

"It separates the mechanism from the fracture."

"Then pull it."

"No keeper has ever touched it."

"Why build a lever nobody should touch?"

"To dismantle the tower, perhaps."

"Exactly."

Abel grabbed her arm. Despite his weakness, his grip was
fierce.

"If you pull that lever and you are wrong, everyone
outside may vanish."

"If I turn the wheel, someone inside certainly will."

"The town has always required a keeper."

"The town has always required a sacrifice," Mira said. "That
isn't the same thing."

Below them, the tower door burst open. Voices and footsteps
rose through the stairwell.

The mayor entered the chamber first, followed by Mira's mother,
the doctor, the baker, and half a dozen others. They
stopped at the sight of the machinery.

"You broke the law," Abel said.

"So did she," the mayor replied, pointing at Mira. "Yet
the bell still hasn't rung."

Elian ignored him. She went to Abel, examined his wound,
then looked at the red wheel.

"It wants another keeper, doesn't it?"

The mayor blinked. "Another?"

Elian's face twisted with old sorrow. "Tell them, Abel."

He remained silent.

So she did.

She told them about the price of the tower. She
told them that Julian had fled because the council demanded
he become keeper after the previous one disappeared. She told
them Abel had volunteered in his place and sworn her
to secrecy.

The townspeople listened in growing horror.

When she finished, the baker removed his cap.

The mayor stared at Abel. "Why weren't we told?"

"You needed the bell," Abel said.

"We might have found another way."

"Or fought over who would be sacrificed. Or fled. Or
gone mad with fear. Bellwether survived because the burden belonged
to one person."

"And now?" the doctor asked.

"Now the mechanism must be wound."

No one moved.

The red wheel waited.

Mira saw the truth settle over them. Each person wanted
the town saved. Each hoped somebody else would step forward.

The tower groaned again. The cracks multiplied.

In one, the square outside was full of unfamiliar people
dressed in strange clothes. In another, wolves roamed among roofless
houses. In a third, the tower had not yet been
built, and laborers hauled stones across a muddy field.

The fractures widened.

Mira seized the black lever.

The mayor shouted. Abel lunged forward. Elian said her daughter's
name once, very softly.

Mira pulled.

For a heartbeat, nothing happened.

Then the iron rod beneath the lever dropped.

Far below, something broke with a sound like a mountain
splitting.

Every gear began to spin at once.

The bell rang.

Not once, twice, or three times, but continuously. Its voice
crashed through the chamber, too powerful to be heard as
sound. Mira felt it in her bones. Windows shattered. People
fell. The great gears tore free of their axles and
rolled across the floor.

The crack in the air opened from ceiling to floor.

Mira tumbled through.

She fell into winter.

Snow covered Bellwether. The tower was gone. Her father stood
in the square, older than she remembered from the only
portrait at home, but unmistakable.

"Mira?" he said.

She reached for him, but summer swept him away.

She saw the town burning.

She saw it being built.

She saw the square beneath a flood, fish passing through
broken windows.

She saw herself old, young, unborn, dead.

A thousand lost moments whirled around her like leaves. Some
belonged to Bellwether, some to places beyond it, and some
to no world that had ever existed. She felt them
pulling at her, asking her to choose one and remain.

Then, through the storm, she heard her mother singing.

It was a lullaby from childhood, a song about a
bird finding its way home by following the river.

Mira followed the voice.

She opened her eyes on the floor of the tower.

The bell was silent.

The machinery had collapsed. Brass gears lay in heaps, and
the red wheel had cracked in two. Morning sunlight streamed
through the broken windows.

"Mira." Elian knelt beside her. "Mira, look at me."

"I'm here."

Her mother pulled her close.

Across the chamber, the doctor tended Abel. The others rose
slowly, checking themselves as if uncertain they still occupied their
own bodies.

"What happened?" asked the baker.

Everyone looked toward Abel.

The old keeper listened to the silence.

"It closed," he said at last. "The fracture closed."

Mira sat up. "Then the tower—"

"Is only a tower."

They descended into the square.

The entire town waited outside. The tower's final ringing had
shaken tiles from roofs and cracked every remaining clock face
in the jeweler's shop. Yet the streets, houses, and people
were still there.

The sun stood a little higher than before.

"What time is it?" someone asked.

No one answered.

For several days, Bellwether drifted.

People woke too early or too late. Bread burned in
ovens. The schoolmistress dismissed her pupils after what she thought
was an hour, only to discover that the sun had
barely moved. The mayor scheduled three meetings on the same
afternoon and attended none of them.

But slowly, the town adjusted.

Roosters announced dawn. Shadows measured noon. Lamps marked evening. The
jeweler fitted hands back onto old clocks and began repairing
their mechanisms. At first, the clocks disagreed wildly. Some gained
minutes; others lost them. The townspeople argued over which one
was correct until Mira laughed.

"They're all close enough," she said.

This became an unofficial motto.

Abel recovered, though he never looked young again. He resigned
as keeper and opened the tower to anyone willing to
help clear the broken gears. Children raced up the forbidden
stairs. Artists painted the view from the bell chamber. The
mayor proposed turning the place into a museum, but nobody
listened to him.

Three months after the fracture closed, a stranger arrived in
Bellwether.

He came at sunset, carrying a weather-stained bag and leaning
on a wooden cane. His hair was gray, and a
scar crossed his chin. He paused at the edge of
the square and looked up at the silent bell.

Mira was helping the jeweler set the hands of the
new public clock. She noticed the stranger before anyone else
did.

The pause before he stepped forward told her he was
afraid.

The brass key beneath her shirt grew warm.

She climbed down from the ladder.

The stranger saw the gray coat she wore despite the
summer heat. His face changed.

"Mira?"

She knew him at once, not because she remembered his
face, but because she had seen his hands holding hers
in a broken piece of the past.

"You took a long time," she said.

Julian Vale laughed, though tears filled his eyes.

"I got lost."

Her mother came out of the bakery. The loaf she
carried fell from her hands.

For a while, nobody spoke.

Then Elian crossed the square and struck Julian hard across
the face.

He accepted this without protest.

Afterward, she embraced him.

That evening, the three of them sat in the narrow
blue house while Julian told his story. He had traveled
from city to city searching for scholars who understood the
fracture. Most called him mad. A few believed him but
offered no solution. Years passed. When he finally met a
physicist who recognized the mechanism from his drawings, she told
him what he had already begun to suspect.

The tower was preventing the fracture from healing.

He had returned as quickly as he could.

"You were too late," Mira said.

"Yes."

"But I had your notebook."

"I left you blank pages."

"Not that one. The other one."

Julian stared at her. "My notes were in the tower."

"Then why did you leave me a blank notebook?"

He smiled. "So you could write something better."

Outside, the new clock struck the hour. Its bell was
small and rather thin. It rang seven times, though the
jeweler later insisted it should have rung six.

Nobody in the blue house counted.

Years later, when Mira became keeper of the tower, the
title meant something different. She kept its doors open. She
kept its stones from crumbling and its bell polished against
the rain. She kept records of the town's births, weddings,
arguments, inventions, and ordinary afternoons.

On the first page of her father's blank notebook, she
wrote:

Time is not a road, nor a river, nor a
wheel.

Then she crossed out the sentence.

She tried again.

Time is what we give to one another.

That seemed closer.

The people of Bellwether eventually filled their homes with clocks.
They wore watches, set alarms, complained of lateness, and hurried
to appointments. Yet they never entirely surrendered to measured time.

Shops sometimes closed on beautiful afternoons. School lessons stretched when
the children asked good questions. Friends arriving late were welcomed
without scolding. No one celebrated the tower's final ringing, because
nobody knew exactly which day it had occurred.

And each evening, as the shadows climbed the western hills,
the townspeople paused for a moment—not because a bell commanded
them, but because they chose to.

They looked at the changing sky.

They noticed who stood beside them.

Then they went home when they were ready.

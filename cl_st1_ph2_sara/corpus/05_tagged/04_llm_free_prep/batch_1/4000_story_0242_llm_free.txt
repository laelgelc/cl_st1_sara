The first time Mara heard the mountain speak, it said
her name.

She was standing alone in the abandoned railway station, sweeping
snow from the platform while the evening train climbed toward
her through the valley. The broom stopped in her hands.

"Mara."

The voice was low and distant, less like a human
voice than stones shifting beneath deep water.

She looked toward the tracks. The rails curved around the
black shoulder of Mount Ilyan and disappeared into falling snow.
No light shone between the pines. The train was still
ten minutes away.

"Mara."

This time she turned toward the mountain.

Its upper slopes were hidden by clouds. Below them stood
a dark forest, the trees packed so closely together that
no snow seemed able to enter. Beyond the forest, high
on the eastern face, a line of old signal towers
rose like broken teeth.

Mara had not heard the mountain speak since she was
eight years old, and no one had believed her then.

She set the broom against the station wall and went
inside.

The waiting room smelled of coal dust, wet wood, and
the dried orange peels that Old Tomas left on the
stove. He was sitting behind the ticket window, asleep in
his chair, his gray head tipped backward. A brass bell
rested beside his hand.

Mara shook his shoulder.

"Tomas."

His eyes opened at once. "Train?"

"Not yet."

"Then why are you waking an old man before his
time?"

"I heard something."

"That is what ears are for."

"The mountain."

Tomas's annoyance vanished. He sat upright.

"What did it say?"

"My name."

For several seconds, only the iron stove made a sound.

Then Tomas rose, crossed the waiting room, and locked the
station door.

"What are you doing?" Mara asked.

"Keeping you inside."

"The train is coming."

"The train can wait."

"It cannot. That is almost the entire purpose of a
train."

Tomas did not smile. He pulled the curtains over the
windows and returned to the stove. His hands trembled as
he lifted the kettle.

Mara watched him. "You believe me."

"I believe many things I wish I did not."

"Has it spoken before?"

"Not to me."

"But to someone?"

Tomas poured hot water over tea leaves and pushed a
cup toward her. "Your mother."

The evening train arrived seven minutes late. Its lamp glowed
through the snow, and its whistle rolled across the valley.
Mara sold two tickets, unloaded three sacks of mail, and
helped an elderly passenger find the road to the village
inn. She performed each task automatically while Tomas stood by
the door, staring at Mount Ilyan.

The conductor noticed him.

"Storm coming?" he asked.

"Something like that," Tomas replied.

When the train departed, its red rear lamp shrank into
the dusk. Silence followed, large and immediate.

Mara carried the mail sacks inside. "Tell me."

Tomas sat on the wooden bench beneath the station clock.
Above him hung an old railway map, faded almost white.
One black line crossed the valley, passed the station, and
entered a tunnel through Mount Ilyan.

The tunnel had been sealed twenty years ago.

"Your mother heard the mountain three days before the accident,"
Tomas said.

Mara looked at the map. "What accident?"

His face tightened. "You were told."

"I was told there was a landslide."

"There was."

"And that my mother was killed on the mountain road."

"She wasn't on the road."

Mara felt a strange calm settle over her, the sort
that comes when fear is too large to enter all
at once.

"Where was she?"

"In the tunnel."

Outside, the wind pressed a handful of snow against the
window.

Tomas rubbed his palms together. "Before the eastern tunnel was
closed, trains went straight through Ilyan. It saved nearly an
hour. Then the mountain began to move. Cracks appeared in
the walls. Water came through the ceiling. Engineers inspected it
and declared it safe, because engineers are often paid to
declare things safe."

"My mother worked at the bakery."

"Your mother was many things before you knew her. She
studied geology in the capital. When she returned to the
valley, the railway hired her to examine the tunnel. She
told them to close it."

"They did close it."

"After the accident."

Mara sat opposite him.

"What happened?"

"A maintenance train went in during the night. Your mother
was aboard. So were eleven others. The tunnel collapsed near
the center."

"No bodies?"

"None recovered."

"And three days before, the mountain spoke to her?"

Tomas nodded.

"What did it say?"

"She never told me."

The station clock ticked above them, measuring a world that
suddenly seemed dishonest.

Mara stood. "Why did everyone lie?"

"Your father decided it was kinder."

"My father left when I was ten."

"Grief makes cowards of some people and wanderers of others."

"And what did it make of you?"

Tomas looked toward the covered windows. "A man who waited
too long to tell the truth."

The mountain spoke again at midnight.

Mara was in the small bedroom above the station, lying
awake beneath a wool blanket. The storm had passed. Moonlight
poured across the floorboards.

"Mara."

She rose, dressed, and went downstairs without lighting a lamp.

Tomas had fallen asleep beside the stove. His boots were
still on. Mara took the ring of station keys from
his belt and slipped outside.

The cold was sharp enough to hurt her teeth. Stars
burned above the valley, and Mount Ilyan stood clear against
them.

"Mara."

The voice came from the old tunnel.

She crossed the tracks and followed the abandoned rail bed
east. Snow covered the sleepers, but the twin rails remained
visible, black lines running toward the mountain. The signal towers
watched from the slope.

When she was a child, people in the village had
told stories about Ilyan. They said the mountain was hollow.
They said a sleeping king lay beneath it with an
army of iron soldiers. They said anyone who heard the
mountain speak would lose one thing they loved and find
one thing they feared.

Mara had dismissed the stories years ago, along with dragons,
spirits, and promises made by adults.

The tunnel entrance appeared beyond the trees.

A wall of stone and concrete sealed it, overgrown with
pale vines. At the center was an iron service door.
Rust streaked its hinges.

Mara tried three keys before the lock opened.

Cold air breathed from the darkness beyond.

She stepped inside.

The service passage was narrow, forcing her to turn sideways.
After several yards, it opened into the tunnel. Her lantern
revealed cracked walls, fallen cables, and rails orange with rust.
Water dripped steadily from the ceiling.

"Mara."

The voice traveled through the stone under her feet.

She moved deeper.

The tunnel smelled of minerals and stagnant air. Every hundred
paces, alcoves had been cut into the wall for workers
to shelter from passing trains. In one, she found a
broken shovel. In another, a row of glass insulators lay
arranged on a ledge, untouched for two decades.

She wondered whether her mother had passed them on the
night she died.

Mara's memories of her were small and bright: flour on
her sleeves, a blue scarf, a song about a fox
crossing a frozen river. She remembered warm hands braiding her
hair. She remembered waiting by the window after the accident,
certain that her mother would return if she remained awake
long enough.

A sound came from behind her.

Mara spun, raising the lantern.

Tomas stood several yards away, panting. He carried a coil
of rope over one shoulder and a crowbar in his
hand.

"You walk loudly," Mara said.

"You stole my keys."

"I borrowed them."

"You also borrowed my good lantern."

"I left you the bad one."

He came closer. "We must go back."

"Not yet."

"You don't understand this place."

"Then explain it."

Before Tomas could answer, a deep groan rolled through the
tunnel. Dust fell from the roof. Somewhere ahead, stone struck
stone.

Tomas's face went pale.

"The mountain is moving," he whispered.

"No. It's calling."

"That may be the same thing."

They continued together.

Half a mile inside, the tunnel ended in a slope
of shattered rock. The collapse reached to the ceiling, a
solid wall of stone.

Mara lifted the lantern.

Across the rubble, someone had painted a white circle. Though
the surrounding wall was stained and cracked, the mark looked
fresh.

"What is that?" she asked.

Tomas stared at it. "Your mother's sign."

"You've been here before."

"Once. After the collapse. The rescue crews dug for nine
days. Then a second fall killed two men, and the
company stopped the work."

"And the circle?"

"She marked unstable stone that way."

Mara climbed onto the rubble.

Tomas caught her arm. "Stop."

She pointed. A thread of air stirred the flame toward
the painted circle.

"There's an opening."

"There are many openings in a collapsed tunnel. Most are
called graves."

The mountain spoke a fourth time.

But now it did not say Mara's name.

"Below."

The stones trembled.

Mara pressed her ear to the rubble. At first she
heard only the slow grinding of the mountain. Then came
a faint metallic rhythm.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

She drew back.

"Tomas."

"I heard it."

They began to dig.

The loose stones near the white circle came away easily.
Behind them lay a narrow gap. Tomas used the crowbar
to widen it while Mara pulled rocks aside. After an
hour, they had opened a passage barely large enough to
crawl through.

Tomas tied the rope around Mara's waist.

"If I pull twice, come back."

"If I pull three times?"

"I come after you."

"And if I pull once?"

"I will assume you are trying to be mysterious."

Mara lowered herself into the gap.

The passage descended steeply through broken stone. She wriggled forward,
pushing the lantern ahead. Once, the rocks shifted beneath her
knees, and she froze until they settled.

Ten yards in, the passage opened suddenly.

Mara slid down a slope and landed on metal.

Her lantern rolled in a circle, casting wild shadows before
coming to rest.

She was standing on the side of a railway carriage.

The maintenance train lay tilted in a cavern beneath the
original tunnel. During the collapse, the floor must have given
way, dropping the train into a natural chamber. The engine
was crushed beneath boulders, but two rear carriages remained nearly
intact.

Mara pulled the rope three times.

Tomas crawled through and stopped beside her.

"Merciful heavens," he said.

A strange blue light glowed at the far end of
the cavern. It came from veins of crystal running through
the rock. Their light pulsed slowly, like breath.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

The sound came from inside the nearest carriage.

Mara climbed along its side until she found a door.
It resisted at first, then opened with a scream of
rust.

The carriage held tools, ruined lamps, and wooden crates. Bones
lay near the lower wall, wrapped in the remains of
railway uniforms.

Tomas removed his cap.

Mara counted silently. One. Two. Three. Four.

The tapping came again, beyond the next door.

She moved through the carriage.

More bones waited in the second compartment. A skeleton sat
upright against a bench, one arm stretched toward the wall.
Beside it lay a blue scarf.

Mara stopped breathing.

She knew the cloth at once. Color had survived where
flesh had not.

"My mother."

Tomas stood behind her. "Yes."

Mara knelt.

The scarf was stiff with age. Beneath it lay a
leather notebook protected by an oilskin cover. She picked it
up carefully.

On the first page, in faded handwriting, was her name.

For Mara, when she is old enough to forgive us.

Her hands shook as she turned the pages.

The early entries contained measurements, diagrams, and notes about the
tunnel. Farther in, the writing became hurried.

October 14. The sound is not geological. Tomas heard nothing,
but I felt words through the wall.

October 15. There is a cavern beneath the tunnel. The
crystals respond to pressure, perhaps storing vibration. Ridiculous sentence. Yet
the mountain repeats sounds made years ago.

October 16. It called me by my daughter's name.

Mara read the line twice.

The final pages described the maintenance journey. Her mother had
boarded the train to stop the crew from drilling into
an unstable section. The company supervisor refused to turn back.
Minutes later, the tunnel collapsed.

The last entry had been written in darkness.

Some died at once. Seven of us remain. There is
air, but little water. We hear rescuers above us. We
strike the wall, but they cannot hear.

The mountain can.

The crystal carries sound through the rock, though not in
any way I understand. It takes our voices and sends
them elsewhere—or elsewhen. I called Mara's name, and the mountain
answered with her voice as a child.

Mara looked toward the blue crystal. Its light brightened, then
dimmed.

At the bottom of the page, her mother had written
one more line.

If you hear me, little fox, follow the frozen river
home.

Mara closed her eyes.

The song. The old song her mother used to sing.

A fox crossed a frozen river,
Under stars and over stone.
When the dark called out to keep her,
She followed the bright ice home.

Mara had forgotten all but the first line.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

She raised the lantern.

"Where is that coming from?"

Tomas pointed to the floor of the carriage, which was
actually its side. A metal pipe disappeared through a split
in the wall and touched the crystal-veined rock. With each
pulse of blue light, it tapped against the carriage.

Not a message, then. Only stone breathing through metal.

Mara almost laughed. Instead, tears came.

She sat beside her mother's remains and cried with a
notebook pressed to her chest. She cried for the woman
in the blue scarf, for the child at the window,
and for twenty years given to a lie. Tomas stood
nearby without speaking.

At last, the cavern trembled.

A crack shot through the carriage window.

"We have to leave," Tomas said.

Mara wrapped the notebook in its oilskin. She took the
blue scarf and tied it around her neck.

They crawled back toward the tunnel. Halfway through the passage,
the rocks shifted. Mara heard Tomas shout behind her. The
rope tightened, then went slack.

She twisted around. "Tomas?"

No answer.

She pulled once.

Nothing.

She pulled three times.

The rope moved weakly.

Mara crawled backward until her boots struck empty air. Turning
in the narrow gap, she saw Tomas pinned beneath a
slab of stone. Dust covered his face.

"Leave me," he said.

"No."

"The passage won't hold."

"That is a very poor argument."

She wedged the crowbar beneath the slab. It did not
move.

The mountain groaned around them.

"Mara," Tomas said, "your mother made me promise that if
anything happened, I would tell you she loved you."

"You failed magnificently."

"Yes."

"Then you can spend the rest of your life making
up for it."

She pulled the crowbar again. Pain tore through her shoulders.
The slab lifted half an inch.

Tomas dragged his leg free.

The passage collapsed behind them as they climbed into the
old tunnel. A wave of dust blew out the service
door, chasing them into the snow.

They reached the trees before the ground stopped shaking.

Dawn broke over the valley.

For the first time in twenty years, sunlight touched the
sealed entrance while someone who had entered the tunnel returned
from it.

By noon, the station was crowded. Villagers came first, then
railway officials, then reporters from the city. Tomas told the
truth to anyone who asked and several people who did
not. The company denied responsibility until Mara placed her mother's
notebook on the ticket counter and invited them to read
the dated inspection reports.

Recovery crews arrived in spring.

They found eleven of the twelve missing workers in the
cavern. The twelfth, the supervisor, had apparently tried to escape
through a fissure. His remains were discovered far below, beside
the drill he had refused to abandon.

Mara's mother was buried on a hill overlooking the railway.
Half the valley attended.

Afterward, Mara returned to the station. She hung the blue
scarf beside the railway map and kept the notebook in
the ticket office, where anyone could read it.

Tomas retired, though he continued arriving every morning before sunrise
to complain about how poorly Mara made tea.

The old tunnel was never reopened. Engineers sealed the cavern
carefully, leaving instruments to measure the crystal's vibrations. Scholars wrote
papers about pressure, resonance, and the unusual mineral structure of
Mount Ilyan. None could explain how Mara's mother's voice had
crossed twenty years.

Mara did not need them to.

Years passed. Trains came and went. Snow buried the tracks
each winter and melted each spring. Children grew tall enough
to buy their own tickets. Travelers asked about the blue
scarf, and Mara told them the truth.

Sometimes, late at night, she stood alone on the platform
and listened.

Usually, the mountain said nothing.

Once, when Tomas died peacefully in his sleep, she heard
a sound like distant laughter rolling through the stone.

And once, many years later, when Mara's hair had turned
silver and the station clock had begun losing seven minutes
every day, the mountain spoke her name again.

"Mara."

She stepped outside.

A little girl stood on the platform, holding a red
suitcase. She had arrived on the evening train with no
family to meet her and a note pinned to her
coat. Mara had promised the frightened stationmaster that the child
could stay with her until morning.

The girl looked toward Mount Ilyan.

"Did you hear that?" she whispered.

Mara followed her gaze. Snow shone on the mountain beneath
a field of stars.

"Yes," she said.

"What is it?"

Mara thought of the crystal cavern and the buried train.
She thought of a woman in darkness sending her love
into stone, uncertain where or when it might be heard.
She thought of all the words people leave too late,
and all the roads by which they still try to
return.

"The mountain remembers," Mara said.

The girl slipped her hand into Mara's.

Together they walked into the station, where the stove was
warm and the lamps burned gold against the winter night.

# The Lighthouse of Lost Voices

On the northern edge of Bellweather Island stood a lighthouse
that had not shone for twenty-three years.

Its white paint had peeled away in long strips, exposing
stone the color of storm clouds. Rust covered the balcony
rail. Gulls nested in the lantern room, and salt had
turned the windows opaque. The islanders avoided it, especially after
sunset, when the wind blew through cracks in the tower
and made sounds like distant conversation.

Mara Vale did not believe the stories.

She had grown up hearing that the old lighthouse collected
the voices of people lost at sea. According to her
grandmother, every final cry, whispered prayer, and unfinished sentence drifted
toward the tower and stayed there, trapped inside the walls.
On certain winter nights, when the tide was at its
highest, those voices could be heard calling the names of
the living.

Mara believed in faulty masonry, narrow pipes, nesting birds, and
people frightened by their own imaginations.

She believed in practical things because practical things remained where
they were put.

Voices did not.

Her father's voice, for example, had vanished twelve years ago.

He had left Bellweather in a small fishing boat before
dawn, promising to return by supper. By noon, the sky
had gone black. At three, the storm struck. At four,
the harbor bell tore loose from its frame. His boat
was never found.

Mara had been nine. For years, she feared she would
forget how he sounded. She repeated his favorite sayings under
her breath and tried to remember the exact shape of
his laughter, but memory softened at the edges. By the
time she was twenty-one, his voice had become an imitation
performed by her own mind.

That autumn, the island council hired her to repair the
lighthouse.

No one else wanted the job.

The council had received a letter from the mainland Authority
of Navigation, which had decided to restore the Bellweather light.
Cargo ships had begun using the northern channel again, and
the dark tower was considered a hazard. An electrical crew
would arrive in six weeks. Before then, the staircase, floors,
windows, and roof needed to be made safe.

Mara was the island's only carpenter under sixty. She accepted
immediately.

"Some doors should stay closed," her grandmother warned when Mara
told her.

They were sitting at the kitchen table, peeling apples for
preserves. Elian Vale was eighty-four, with small bright eyes and
silver hair she wore in a braid.

"It's a lighthouse, Gran."

"It was a lighthouse. Now it is something else."

"A neglected building?"

Elian's knife stopped.

"Your father went there the night before he disappeared."

Mara looked up. "What?"

"He said he heard someone calling from the tower."

"Who?"

"He wouldn't tell me."

"You never mentioned this."

"You were a child."

"I haven't been a child for some time."

"No," Elian said quietly. "But I have been a coward
for a very long while."

Mara waited for more, but her grandmother returned to the
apple in her hand. The strip of red peel descended
in an unbroken spiral.

The next morning, Mara packed her tools and walked north.

The path to the lighthouse crossed a mile of heather
before climbing the cliffs. Wind pushed gray waves against the
rocks below. Far out, sunlight flashed on the water, but
above the island the clouds hung low and heavy.

The lighthouse door was locked with a chain. Mara cut
it away.

Inside, the air smelled of wet stone, rust, and something
faintly sweet. The circular entrance room contained a broken desk,
shelves of mildewed logbooks, and a cold iron stove. A
staircase curled upward along the wall.

Mara set down her toolbox.

"Nothing haunted about you," she said.

From somewhere above came a soft knock.

She froze.

The knock came again: three quick taps, followed by two
slower ones.

Mara looked toward the ceiling. A bird, she thought. A
loose shutter. A branch, though no tree grew within half
a mile.

She climbed.

The first landing had once been the keeper's bedroom. Its
floorboards sagged dangerously. The second contained the machinery that had
turned the original lamp. Brass gears stood under blankets of
dust. Above that was the storage room, and above that,
the lantern chamber.

No gulls flew from the rafters. No shutters moved.

Mara found nothing that could have made the sound.

She opened the windows and spent the morning examining the
damage. The building was in better condition than expected. The
outer walls were thick, and although several beams needed replacing,
the central structure remained strong.

At noon, she sat on the balcony to eat bread
and cheese.

The sea stretched in every direction except south, where Bellweather's
slate roofs clustered around the harbor. From this distance, the
village looked like a handful of stones thrown against green
earth.

Mara had just poured tea from her flask when a
voice behind her said, "Cold today."

She dropped the cup.

Tea ran between the balcony grates.

Mara spun around. The lantern room was empty.

"Who's there?"

The wind pressed against the glass. Metal creaked.

She stepped inside, checking behind the old lamp mechanism, though
there was nowhere a person could hide.

"Very amusing," she called.

No answer came.

Mara descended the staircase, inspecting each room again. The tower
remained empty. At the entrance, she discovered that the door
had blown shut. She tested it, heard the hinges groan,
and almost laughed.

The voice had been the wind bending itself around the
door.

That was all.

She worked until dusk.

Before leaving, Mara took one of the logbooks from the
entrance shelf. Most were ruined, but this volume had been
protected beneath another. Its leather cover was cracked. Faded letters
on the first page read:

EDMUND VALE, KEEPER
1889

Edmund had been Mara's great-great-grandfather. She knew he had tended
the light, but no one in the family spoke much
about him.

At home, she cleaned the cover and began to read.

Most entries were ordinary records of weather, fuel, and passing
vessels.

October 8. Calm sea. Eastern wind. Lamp lit at 6:12.

October 11. Rain. Supplied wick replaced. Observed three ships in
northern channel.

Then, halfway through the book, the entries changed.

November 3. Heard knocking below after midnight. Inspected door. No
visitor.

November 4. Knocking returned. Five beats. Clara says it resembles
the signal Thomas used as a boy.

November 7. A voice in the stairwell. Clara heard it
too.

November 12. Thomas spoke from inside the wall.

Mara turned the page.

Thomas had been Edmund's younger brother. He had died at
sea in 1887.

She read on.

The tower does not hold the dead. It holds what
the living cannot release. Every word remembered, every voice carried
beyond its proper hour, gathers here as rain gathers in
a barrel.

Clara wishes to answer. I have forbidden it.

November 19. I answered.

The remaining pages had been torn out.

Mara carried the book to her grandmother.

Elian read the final lines without surprise.

"You knew," Mara said.

"I knew some."

"What happened to Edmund?"

"He walked into the sea."

"And Clara?"

"His wife left the island. She never spoke again."

Mara paced the kitchen. "Why would Father go to the
lighthouse?"

Elian stared toward the dark window.

"Because of your mother."

Mara stopped.

Her mother, Lysa, had died when Mara was four. A
fever took her over the course of three days. Mara
remembered cool hands, the smell of lavender soap, and a
song without words. Her father had raised Mara alone after
that.

"He heard her?" Mara asked.

"He believed he did. The night before his final voyage,
he came here in a terrible state. He said Lysa
had spoken to him from the old tower. She told
him there was something beneath the lantern room. A chamber
Edmund had sealed."

"What chamber?"

"I don't know."

Mara thought of the floor beneath the lamp mechanism, of
the wide iron base bolted into stone.

"What did Mother say was inside?"

Elian's face tightened. "A way to bring a voice home."

Rain began ticking against the window.

The following day, Mara returned to the lighthouse with a
crowbar and a lantern.

She worked on the necessary repairs until noon, telling herself
she was not afraid. After lunch, she climbed to the
top and examined the lamp base.

Six large bolts fixed it to the floor. Five were
red with rust. The sixth had scratches around it.

Mara knelt closer.

Someone had loosened it before.

It took an hour to remove the bolts. Using the
crowbar, she shifted the iron base several inches. Beneath it
lay a circular wooden hatch.

Three quick taps sounded below her.

Then two slow ones.

Mara's breath caught.

It was a pattern she knew.

Whenever her father came home late, he had knocked on
her bedroom wall in exactly that way: three quick, two
slow. Their private signal. I'm home. Good night.

The knocking came again.

Mara placed her palm on the hatch.

"Father?"

Silence.

Then, faintly: "Little star?"

Her eyes filled with tears before she could stop them.

No one else had called her that. Not her grandmother.
Not even her mother, as far as Mara remembered.

She tore open the hatch.

A ladder descended into darkness.

The space below could not have existed. The lantern room
sat directly over the storage level, yet the ladder continued
far deeper than the height of the whole tower. Mara
lowered her lantern and saw stone walls narrowing around a
shaft without any visible end.

"Little star," the voice called again.

She climbed down.

The hatch became a pale circle overhead. The air grew
warmer as she descended, and the sweet smell she had
noticed at the entrance became stronger. It reminded her of
apples stored through winter.

Other voices stirred in the walls.

A woman laughing.

A child counting.

A man saying, "Tell her I did not mean—"

Someone singing a hymn.

Someone calling for help.

Mara nearly turned back, but then her father spoke again.

"Not far now."

She continued.

At last, her boots touched stone. The ladder ended in
a narrow passage. Its walls glimmered as though wet, although
they were dry beneath her fingers.

"Father?"

"This way."

The passage opened into a round chamber.

Thousands of glass bottles filled shelves from floor to ceiling.
Some were green, some clear, some blue. They varied from
tiny medicine vials to jars large enough to hold a
person's head. Every bottle had been sealed with black wax.

Whispers trembled inside them.

Mara held up her lantern.

In the chamber's center stood a wooden table. On it
rested a single unsealed bottle and a bundle wrapped in
rotting sailcloth.

Beside the table was her father.

He looked exactly as he had the morning he disappeared:
tall, broad-shouldered, dark hair touched with gray at the temples.
His blue sweater was wet. A thin cut marked his
cheek.

Mara could not move.

He smiled.

"There you are."

She crossed the room and threw her arms around him.

Her hands passed through cold mist.

The shape of him broke apart, then slowly gathered again.

"Oh," she whispered.

"I'm sorry."

She pressed both hands to her mouth.

He seemed so real. Every detail was right: the crooked
nose, the missing tip of one eyebrow, the way he
leaned more heavily on his left leg.

"Are you dead?" she asked.

"Yes."

The answer struck harder than she expected. Some hidden part
of her had continued to hope all these years. A
nameless village, a lost memory, a ship traveling under another
flag—she had imagined a hundred impossible explanations.

"How?"

"The storm. My boat overturned north of the island."

"Why did you go out when you knew it was
coming?"

His expression folded with regret.

"Your mother's voice told me to."

Mara looked at the bottles. "That wasn't her, was it?"

"It was her voice. Her memories. But not her."

"I don't understand."

"A voice is a shape left in the world. The
tower gathers those shapes. Given enough memory, a shape learns
to speak again, but it does not become the person
who made it."

"Then what are you?"

His smile was unbearably familiar.

"Everything you remember about me. Everything Gran remembers. A few
words the sea carried here when I died. Your father's
echo."

Mara wanted to deny it. Instead, she noticed that his
clothes were precisely those he had worn on his last
morning—the only version of him fixed permanently in her mind.

"Did this place kill you?"

"No. Grief did, perhaps. Hope. I heard Lysa and wanted
to believe death had made a mistake."

His image flickered.

Mara approached the table. "What's in the bundle?"

"Edmund's tools. He built this chamber. The stone beneath the
island has strange properties. It catches sound, but memory gives
the sound form. Edmund learned to contain the voices in
glass."

"Why?"

"At first, to preserve them. Then to silence them."

A chorus of murmurs passed around the chamber.

Mara examined the open bottle. "And this?"

"Mine."

She turned to him.

"If you speak my name into it and seal it,
you can carry my voice away. I could stay with
you."

The longing that rose in her was immediate and fierce.

She imagined placing the bottle on the kitchen shelf. She
could speak to him in the evenings. Ask where he
had hidden the little wooden horse he had carved for
her. Tell him about the years he had missed. Hear
him call her little star whenever she wished.

Her father reached toward her, his transparent fingers stopping beside
her cheek.

"I have waited so long," he said.

Mara picked up the bottle.

A label had been tied around its neck. On it,
in faded ink, was her father's full name.

Elias Vale.

She set the lantern on the table and unwrapped the
sailcloth bundle. Inside were iron tongs, sticks of black sealing
wax, a small hammer, and a narrow ledger.

The ledger contained names.

Hundreds of them.

Some Mara recognized from island gravestones. Beside each name was
a list of those who remembered the dead person. Many
names had been crossed out. At the back of the
ledger she found a recent entry.

Elias Vale
Remembered by Elian Vale
Remembered by Mara Vale

Below it, in fresher ink, another line had begun to
appear.

Remembered by—

The ink moved by itself, trailing across the page like
an insect.

Mara dropped the ledger.

Her father's echo watched her.

"Who else remembers you?" she asked.

"No one as strongly as you."

"Then why is it writing another line?"

The chamber shuddered.

Dust fell from the ceiling. All around her, voices became
louder.

Her father stepped closer. "Seal the bottle, Mara."

She heard urgency in his tone now, but also something
else. Hunger.

"What happens when I take you out of the tower?"

"I will be with you."

"What happens to me?"

He did not answer.

Mara backed away from the table.

The unfinished line in the ledger lengthened.

Remembered by Elias Vale.

"That's impossible," she said.

The echo's face changed. Only slightly. The smile remained, but
it no longer reached his eyes.

"I remember you," it said.

A crack appeared in one of the bottles.

Mara understood.

The tower gathered voices. Memory gave them shape. But a
memory that learned to remember could gather voices of its
own.

Her father had not called her down because he missed
her.

He needed her to open the way.

From the shelves came the sound of breaking glass.

Black wax seals popped loose one after another. Whispers poured
into the chamber, twisting through the air like smoke. Figures
began to form: sailors with swollen faces, women in old
dresses, children dripping seawater onto the stone.

Each turned toward Mara.

Each spoke a name.

The names overlapped until the chamber roared.

Her father's echo held out its hand. "Take me home."

Mara seized the hammer.

She struck the open bottle.

It shattered.

The echo screamed.

His body stretched into a ribbon of gray vapor. For
one terrible instant, his face became truly alive with pain.

"Little star," he gasped.

Mara almost reached for him.

Then she remembered her father teaching her to mend a
net on the harbor wall. If a rope catches your
ankle, he had said, cut it. Don't pity the rope
while you're drowning.

She swung the hammer again, smashing the neck of the
bottle.

The echo collapsed into a rush of cold air.

Every lantern in the chamber—though Mara had brought only one—blazed
white.

The released figures surged toward the table.

Mara grabbed the ledger and thrust it into the flame
of her lantern. The corner caught slowly, then flared. Ink
crawled across the pages as if trying to escape the
fire.

The voices rose into shrieks.

Shelves shook. Bottles fell and burst against the floor. A
storm of words filled the chamber: declarations of love, curses,
recipes, apologies, jokes, prayers, and final messages never delivered.

Mara ran for the passage.

Behind her, the round chamber cracked open. Light poured through
the fractures, bright as morning. The voices chased her to
the ladder, tugging at her clothes and hair.

She climbed.

The shaft seemed longer than before. Her palms blistered. Her
arms trembled. Below, the white light rose like floodwater.

Halfway up, she heard her mother singing.

The wordless lullaby surrounded Mara, tender and clear.

She stopped.

A woman's voice whispered, "Stay."

Mara closed her eyes.

For years she had treasured four notes of that melody.
Now the whole song waited beneath her.

She could climb down. She could see her mother's face.
She could ask every question a four-year-old child had never
known to ask.

The light reached her boots.

Mara climbed.

At last, she pulled herself through the hatch and rolled
onto the lantern room floor. The tower groaned beneath her.
She pushed the iron lamp base over the opening just
as a blast of wind struck from below.

Every window shattered outward.

A pillar of pale light shot from the tower into
the clouds.

Across Bellweather Island, people stopped where they stood.

Fishermen in the harbor heard drowned brothers laugh. Old women
heard mothers call them in for supper. Widows heard husbands
whisper from empty beds. For one minute, the lost voices
of Bellweather filled the sky.

Then the wind carried them out to sea.

The tower fell silent.

Mara remained on the floor until sunset.

When she returned home, bleeding from both hands, her grandmother
was waiting by the door.

Elian took one look at her and said, "You heard
him."

"Yes."

"Was it Elias?"

Mara considered the question.

"Part of him."

Her grandmother nodded, tears shining in her eyes. "Is the
part still there?"

"No."

Elian swayed, and Mara caught her. They held each other
in the doorway while night settled over the island.

"I can't remember his laugh," Mara confessed.

"I can," Elian said.

"Tell me."

So her grandmother did.

She described how Elias laughed too loudly in church, how
he snorted when he tried to stop, and how once,
at sixteen, he laughed so hard at the dinner table
that milk came out of his nose. Mara listened until
she began to laugh too.

It was not his laughter.

But it was warm, and living, and enough.

Six weeks later, the electrical crew arrived from the mainland.
They found the lighthouse repaired, its floors sound, its windows
replaced, and the old lamp mechanism polished clean. Mara did
not tell them about the chamber. When they moved the
iron base, they discovered only solid stone beneath it.

On the first night of winter, the Bellweather light shone
again.

Its beam swept across the cliffs, crossed the village roofs,
and reached far into the northern channel. Islanders gathered along
the harbor to watch. Some wept. Some covered their ears.

Mara stood with her grandmother at the end of the
pier.

As the beam passed over them, Elian slipped her hand
into Mara's.

"Do you hear anything?" she asked.

Mara listened.

There was the sea against the pilings. Wind in the
ropes. A child calling to his sister. A fisherman singing
badly as he tied his boat.

Voices of the living, untidy and unfinished.

"No," Mara said. "Nothing that shouldn't be here."

Far across the dark water, a ship sounded its horn.

The lighthouse answered with light.

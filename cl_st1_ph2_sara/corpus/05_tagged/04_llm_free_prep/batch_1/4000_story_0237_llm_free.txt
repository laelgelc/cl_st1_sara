# The Museum of Lost Hours

Every morning at nine, Mr. Vale unlocked the Museum of
Lost Hours and swept yesterday's dust from the marble steps.

The museum stood between a bakery and an abandoned cinema
on Bellweather Street. It had no sign except for a
brass clock above the door. The clock possessed thirteen numbers
and only one hand, which moved backward when it moved
at all.

Most people passed without noticing the building. Those who entered
usually did so by accident.

They came looking for the cinema, sheltering from rain, or
following the smell of bread. Then they found themselves in
a narrow hall where glass cabinets shone beneath green lamps.
Each cabinet held a clock, and beneath each clock lay
a small card written in Mr. Vale's careful hand.

ONE HOUR LOST BY MRS. AGNES FIELD
ON A TRAIN BETWEEN YORK AND LONDON
DURING WHICH SHE MIGHT HAVE SAID YES

THREE MINUTES LOST BY TOM ARDEN, AGED NINE
WHILE WAITING FOR HIS FATHER TO TURN AROUND

A SUNDAY AFTERNOON MISPLACED BY AN UNKNOWN SAILOR
FOUND IN THE POCKET OF A BLUE COAT

Visitors often laughed when they read the cards. Mr. Vale
never minded. He stood behind the front desk, polishing his
spectacles, and waited.

Sooner or later, the laughter stopped.

In the museum's first room were moments lost through boredom:
meetings, examinations, delayed buses, winters spent staring through office windows.
In the second were moments lost through fear. The clocks
there ticked softly, as though ashamed.

The third room contained hours lost through love.

Few visitors remained long in that room.

Mr. Vale had cared for the museum for forty-two years.
Before him, his mother had been its keeper. Before her,
an uncle whose portrait hung in the stairwell, his painted
eyes fixed suspiciously on the future.

The work was simple. Lost time arrived in many forms:
folded into letters, trapped inside watches, sealed in jars, hidden
between the pages of books. Sometimes people brought it willingly.
Sometimes Mr. Vale found it waiting on the doorstep at
dawn.

He catalogued each hour, wound its clock, and placed it
under glass.

Only one rule governed the museum.

Nothing could be returned.

"Lost time is lost," his mother had told him when
he was a boy. "We preserve it so that the
world does not become full of holes. But we must
never put it back."

Mr. Vale had always obeyed.

Then, on the first Tuesday of November, a girl named
Elianor Finch came to the museum carrying a broken alarm
clock.

She was twelve years old, though she looked younger. Rain
had darkened her red hair, and the knees of her
school uniform were muddy. She set the clock on the
desk.

"I need this repaired," she said.

"There is a watchmaker in Market Lane."

"He told me to come here."

Mr. Vale looked at the clock. It was a cheap
plastic thing shaped like a yellow moon. A crack ran
across its face. Both hands had stopped at seventeen minutes
past six.

"We do not repair clocks," he said.

"You repair time."

"No."

"You keep it, then."

"Sometimes."

"So it's true."

Mr. Vale disliked the brightness in her eyes. Hope was
dangerous in the museum. It made people touch the glass.

"What is your name?" he asked.

"Elianor Finch. My mother calls me Nell."

"And what is inside this clock, Nell?"

"Yesterday morning."

Mr. Vale's hand paused above the alarm clock.

"Explain."

"My little brother woke me at six seventeen. He said
he couldn't breathe. I thought he was pretending because he
didn't want to go to school. I told him to
get out." She stared at the crack in the clock's
face. "He went back to his room. Mum found him
twenty minutes later."

Outside, rain whispered against the museum windows.

Mr. Vale had heard thousands of regrets. They were the
language most commonly spoken beneath his roof. Yet there was
something strange about the clock. It did not merely feel
broken. It felt heavy.

He touched one finger to the yellow casing.

The entrance hall vanished.

For a moment, he stood in a child's bedroom at
dawn. Paper stars covered the ceiling. A boy in striped
pajamas stood beside the bed, one hand pressed to his
chest.

"Nell," the boy whispered. "Something's wrong."

Then the vision snapped shut.

Mr. Vale pulled his hand away.

The girl watched him.

"It's in there, isn't it?" she asked. "The hour I
lost."

"Not an hour. Twenty minutes."

"Can I have them back?"

"No."

"You didn't even think."

"I have spent my life thinking about that question."

"Then think once more."

She pushed the clock toward him.

Mr. Vale folded his hands. "Even if I could return
those minutes, they would not belong only to you. Time
is not a private road. It is a city. Change
one turning, and everyone arrives somewhere else."

"My brother might arrive somewhere else."

"Or someone else might not arrive at all."

"I don't care."

"You might."

Nell's face hardened.

"His name is Jamie," she said. "He is seven. He
likes beetles and burnt toast, and he sleeps with a
light on because he thinks the dark has teeth. He
is in St. Orison's Hospital, and the doctors say he
will probably die before Friday. If your rule matters more
than that, it's a stupid rule."

She turned and walked out.

The yellow clock remained on the desk.

Mr. Vale called after her, but the door had already
closed. Its bell gave a single, guilty note.

He carried the clock into the catalogue room. The museum
had never received time like this before. Usually, hours arrived
only after every possible use had faded from them. They
were shells abandoned by the tide.

But the twenty minutes inside Nell's clock were alive.

They shivered beneath his fingertips.

He opened the great register and dipped his pen.

TWENTY MINUTES LOST BY ELIANOR FINCH, AGED TWELVE, he wrote.

He stopped.

There was no proper category. They had been lost through
impatience, perhaps, or disbelief. Yet neither description felt right. Children
were often impatient. They were taught by adults not to
believe inconvenient things.

At last he wrote:

LOST BECAUSE SHE DID NOT KNOW THEY COULD BE LOST.

Mr. Vale placed the clock in an empty cabinet in
the Room of Fear.

That night, he dreamed of paper stars.

The next morning, the yellow clock had moved.

Its hands showed eighteen minutes past six.

Mr. Vale checked the cabinet lock. It remained sealed. He
checked the catalogue, then the museum's brass clock. All appeared
normal, except that the single hand above the entrance had
begun to tremble.

At noon, a bus driver entered the museum and surrendered
eleven seconds he had lost while looking at a dog
in the road. At three, an elderly woman brought an
entire summer wrapped in a handkerchief.

Throughout the day, the yellow clock advanced.

Six nineteen.

Six twenty.

Six twenty-one.

Each minute made the museum colder.

Mr. Vale went to the basement, where the oldest hours
were kept. Their clocks filled the darkness: sundials from vanished
kingdoms, candle clocks burned black, silver watches taken from battlefields.
The air below tasted of snow.

At the far end stood a wooden door with no
handle.

His mother had shown it to him only once.

"Behind this door," she had said, "is the first hour
ever lost. It longs for all the others. If the
clocks begin to run together, the door will open, and
lost time will pour back into the world."

"What would happen then?" the young Mr. Vale had asked.

"Everything unfinished would begin again."

At the time, he had imagined half-eaten dinners and interrupted
songs. Now he imagined wars, illnesses, shipwrecks, arguments, fires. Every
bullet returning to its gun. Every wound opening twice.

He pressed his ear to the door.

Something ticked on the other side.

By Thursday morning, every clock in the museum had begun
moving toward seventeen minutes past six.

Mr. Vale shut the doors to visitors. He covered the
windows. Then he stood before the yellow alarm clock with
the great register open beside him.

"The time is searching for its owner," said a voice.

Mr. Vale looked up.

His mother sat in a chair across the room.

She had been dead for twenty-nine years, but there she
was: gray dress, silver hair, strong hands resting on a
black cane.

"You are not real," he said.

"Neither is a museum of lost hours, if we're being
strict."

Mr. Vale removed his spectacles and cleaned them. When he
replaced them, she remained.

"What is happening?" he asked.

"You catalogued living time as lost."

"The girl left it."

"She entrusted it to you."

"She asked me to use it."

"And you refused."

"According to your rule."

His mother sighed. "Children always remember the rule and forget
the reason."

"You said time must never be returned."

"I said lost time must never be returned. Those minutes
aren't lost yet. They're waiting."

"For what?"

"For a choice."

Mr. Vale glanced at the clock. It now showed six
thirty-one.

"If I give them back, the past will change."

"The past changes constantly. That is what memory is."

"Memory does not alter events."

"Doesn't it?"

He disliked the way she smiled. Death had not made
her less argumentative.

"Tell me what to do," he said.

"I spent your childhood telling you what to do. It
made you very obedient and not especially wise."

The lights flickered.

From below came a deep wooden crack.

The handleless door was beginning to open.

Mr. Vale seized the yellow clock and hurried downstairs. His
mother followed, her cane striking each step.

The ancient clocks were all chiming now. Some rang like
bells; others hissed or groaned. Their hands spun toward the
same minute.

A line of golden light appeared around the basement door.

Mr. Vale understood. The twenty living minutes were pulling at
every lost hour in the museum. Until they were completed
or released, the clocks would continue to gather around them.
When enough lost time accumulated, the first hour would wake.

He could destroy the yellow clock.

The thought arrived cleanly, almost kindly.

Broken vessels released their contents. The twenty minutes would dissolve
unused, and the museum would settle. The rule would survive.

He lifted the clock above the stone floor.

Then he saw Nell's bedroom again.

Jamie stood beside her bed, struggling for breath. This time
the vision continued. Nell pulled the blanket over her head.

"Stop being annoying," she muttered.

Jamie waited.

The clock ticked.

At six eighteen, he sat on the floor.

At six nineteen, he whispered her name again.

At six twenty, Nell threw back the blanket.

The vision vanished.

Mr. Vale lowered the clock.

"She did go to him," he said.

His mother stood beside the glowing door. "Eventually."

"She lost only three minutes."

"She believes she lost twenty."

"The clock contains twenty."

"Guilt is greedy. It takes more time than it owns."

Mr. Vale examined the clock. The minute hand had nearly
reached six thirty-seven. Jamie had been found then. The entire
period had been trapped, but only the first three minutes
were truly lost.

"What happens if I return all twenty?" he asked.

"You give guilt control of the past. Never sensible."

"And if I return three?"

"You give a child another chance to answer."

The door shuddered. Golden light spilled across the floor, carrying
voices.

A soldier cried for his mother.

A bride said, "Wait."

A doctor said, "I should have seen it."

A thousand unfinished moments pressed against the opening.

Mr. Vale clutched the clock. "How do I send back
only three minutes?"

His mother pointed to the register.

"Correct the record."

They ran upstairs as the museum began to come apart.

Glass cabinets cracked. Clock hands whirled. Lost afternoons filled the
corridors with summer sunlight. Rain fell upward in the Room
of Fear. In the Room of Love, unseen people laughed,
quarreled, kissed, and disappeared.

At the front desk, Mr. Vale opened the register. The
ink beneath Nell's name moved like insects.

TWENTY MINUTES LOST.

He struck out the words and wrote:

THREE MINUTES.

Nothing happened.

The clocks continued to roar.

"What else?" he asked.

His mother did not answer.

She was fading. He could see the wallpaper through her
dress.

"Why were they lost?" she asked.

"Because Nell did not understand."

"Not good enough."

"Because she was tired. Because she was impatient."

"Those are explanations."

The brass clock over the museum door gave a deafening
chime. A crack split the wall from ceiling to floor.

Mr. Vale thought of the girl in the rain, carrying
more blame than her thin arms could hold.

Then he understood.

He bent over the register and completed the entry.

THREE MINUTES LOST BY ELIANOR FINCH, AGED TWELVE,
BECAUSE SHE WAS A CHILD AND DID NOT KNOW HER
BROTHER WAS DYING.

He hesitated, then added:

THEY ARE NOT HERS TO KEEP.

The yellow alarm clock sprang open.

Three bright minutes rose from it like sparks. They circled
the room, illuminating every cabinet, every label, every particle of
dust. Then they flew through the front door without opening
it and vanished into the Thursday afternoon.

All at once, the museum became silent.

The clocks stopped.

The crack in the wall closed. The backward hand above
the entrance settled between eleven and twelve.

Mr. Vale stood alone.

His mother's chair was empty.

On Friday morning, he went to St. Orison's Hospital.

He did not know what he expected. Perhaps the building
would have vanished. Perhaps no one named Finch would ever
have lived. Instead, the hospital looked painfully ordinary. Ambulances waited
outside. A man drank coffee beneath a NO SMOKING sign.

At the reception desk, Mr. Vale asked for Jamie Finch.

"Family?" said the receptionist.

"No."

"Then I can't provide information."

"I understand. Could you tell his sister that the clock
has been repaired?"

The receptionist studied him.

Before she could answer, a girl's voice called from the
corridor.

"Mr. Vale?"

Nell ran toward him. Her hair was untidy, and she
wore the same muddy shoes, but her face had changed.
The terrible tightness around her mouth was gone.

"He woke up," she said.

Mr. Vale let out a breath.

"Then the doctors were able to help?"

"They say he might still be ill for a long
time. But they found out what was wrong earlier, so
the medicine worked better." She frowned. "I remember two mornings."

"That may happen for a while."

"In one, I ignored him. In the other, I didn't."
She looked at Mr. Vale. "Which one is real?"

"Both, I expect."

"That doesn't make sense."

"Reality rarely takes the trouble."

Nell sat on a plastic chair, and Mr. Vale sat
beside her.

"I still told him to go away," she said. "Even
in the better morning. But then I heard him sit
down, and I got up."

"You cannot always control your first answer."

"Only the second?"

"Sometimes the second is the hour that matters."

She considered this. "Did you break the rule?"

"Yes."

"Are you in trouble?"

"Almost certainly."

"Good."

Mr. Vale smiled.

A small boy appeared at the end of the corridor,
pushing an intravenous stand. His face was pale, and his
striped pajamas were too large. Nell jumped up.

"You're supposed to stay in bed."

"I'm bored."

"You nearly died."

"I'm still bored."

She took the stand from him and wheeled it back
toward his room. Jamie glanced over his shoulder at Mr.
Vale.

"Is that the clock man?"

"Yes," Nell said.

"Does he have my twenty minutes?"

"Only three."

Jamie nodded as if this were a perfectly reasonable answer.

Mr. Vale returned to Bellweather Street in the afternoon. The
museum was dusty, disordered, and still standing. He swept the
marble steps, unlocked the door, and turned the sign from
CLOSED to OPEN.

The first visitor was an old man carrying a pocket
watch.

"I was told you might help me," the man said.
"There's an hour in here I've regretted for fifty years."

Mr. Vale accepted the watch but did not immediately reach
for the register.

"Is the hour truly lost?" he asked.

The old man blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"Is everyone who belongs to it gone? Is every choice
finished? Is there no apology left to make, no door
left to knock upon?"

"I don't know."

"Then you should find out."

The old man looked from Mr. Vale to the watch
in his hand.

After a while, he placed it back in his pocket
and left.

That evening, Mr. Vale made new cards for several exhibits.
He worked until the bakery closed and the streetlamps came
on.

He removed the word LOST where it did not belong.

ONE HOUR WAITING TO BE ANSWERED BY MRS. AGNES FIELD.

THREE MINUTES STILL CALLING TO TOM ARDEN.

A SUNDAY AFTERNOON WHOSE OWNER MAY YET RETURN.

He left many labels unchanged. Some time was truly gone.
Wisdom did not mean pretending otherwise.

Last of all, he carried the yellow moon clock to
the entrance hall. Its crack had disappeared. Its hands now
showed the correct time.

He placed it inside a new cabinet and wrote:

THREE MINUTES RETURNED TO ELIANOR FINCH, AGED TWELVE,
WHO USED THEM TO GET OUT OF BED.

Below that, in smaller letters, he added:

THE FIRST ANSWER IS NOT ALWAYS THE LAST.

Years later, visitors would say this was the museum's most
valuable exhibit. Some stood before it for an hour. Others
looked only briefly before rushing out the door, suddenly remembering
a letter unwritten, a promise delayed, or a person waiting
beside a telephone.

Mr. Vale never stopped them.

He simply watched them go, then turned to the museum's
thirteen-numbered clock.

Its single hand still traveled backward from time to time.

But now, every so often, it moved forward too.

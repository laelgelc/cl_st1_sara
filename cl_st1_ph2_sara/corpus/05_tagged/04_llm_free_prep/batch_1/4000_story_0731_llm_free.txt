# The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, Mara Venn unlocked the narrow green
door beneath the railway clock and listened for the hours
people had lost.

They arrived in many forms.

Some ticked softly inside jars. Some slept in drawers like
folded handkerchiefs. Others wandered through the workshop as pale ribbons
of light, slipping beneath cupboards and curling around the legs
of chairs. One particularly troublesome Wednesday had taken to nesting
in the chimney, where it coughed soot whenever Mara tried
to catch it.

The workshop belonged to the Central Office of Weights, Measures,
and Misplaced Time, though no sign announced this. Above the
green door, commuters hurried toward trains, checking watches, spilling coffee,
and complaining of delay. Few knew that beneath their feet,
Mara catalogued the moments they had misplaced.

An hour lost waiting for news from a doctor.

Twenty minutes spent searching for keys that had been in
a coat pocket all along.

Three summer afternoons forgotten by an old woman who could
remember every word of her wedding song but not the
face of the man she had married.

Mara labelled each one in neat black ink.

She had been taught never to judge the value of
an hour by its contents. A minute of grief could
weigh more than a year of happiness. A dull afternoon
might later prove to have held the last conversation between
two friends. Time, her predecessor Mr. Vale had often said,
was an unreliable witness.

"People think they know what they've lost," he told her
on her first day, "but they seldom do."

That had been nineteen years ago. Mr. Vale was gone
now, retired to a cottage by the sea where, according
to his annual Christmas cards, the clocks always ran slow.
Mara had taken his desk, his brass keys, and his
responsibility.

She worked alone and preferred it that way.

At eight each morning, the collection tubes began to rattle.
They ran through the walls from post offices, hospitals, schools,
railway stations, and certain government buildings whose names Mara was
forbidden to record. She placed a glass bottle beneath each
tube and waited.

On Monday, the northern tube delivered two hours from a
delayed train, four seconds from a boxer who had been
knocked unconscious, and a long silver thread marked ONLY IF
NEEDED.

The western tube produced a childhood summer that smelled of
cut grass.

The eastern tube gave her nothing.

That was unusual.

Mara tapped the brass pipe. A hollow sound returned.

She consulted the delivery ledger. The eastern districts covered half
the city: six hospitals, forty-two schools, the municipal courthouse, three
libraries, and the old riverside neighborhood. Not a single second
had arrived from any of them.

People were always losing time.

She sent a request upstairs. An hour later, a reply
fluttered down in a blue envelope.

TEMPORARY IRREGULARITY. CONTINUE NORMAL DUTIES.

Mara did.

On Tuesday, the eastern tube remained silent.

On Wednesday, she received a telephone call.

The black telephone on Mr. Vale's old desk had not
rung in eleven years. Its cord was not connected to
the wall. Nevertheless, at half past three, it gave one
sharp metallic cry.

Mara stared at it.

It rang again.

She lifted the receiver. "Central Office."

For a moment, there was only breathing.

Then a boy whispered, "Have you found Thursday?"

Mara tightened her grip. "Who is this?"

"My name is Eli."

"Eli what?"

"I don't know if I'm supposed to tell you."

"Then tell me where you are."

Another pause. Behind his breathing, Mara heard bells and the
distant rush of traffic.

"I'm at Saint Orra's," he said.

Mara knew the name. Saint Orra's Hospital stood in the
eastern district.

"What do you mean, have I found Thursday?"

"It disappeared."

"Thursday hasn't happened yet."

"It did here."

The line crackled.

Mara opened the drawer where she kept emergency forms. "Listen
carefully, Eli. What date do you believe it is?"

"Friday the fourteenth."

Mara looked at the calendar.

Wednesday the twelfth.

"What happened on Thursday?" she asked.

"I don't know. Nobody does. But my mum was alive
on Wednesday, and on Friday she was dead."

The workshop seemed suddenly too quiet.

"I'm sorry," Mara said.

"They said it happened during the night, but there wasn't
a night. Everyone went to sleep on Wednesday and woke
up on Friday. The clocks jumped. The newspapers changed. Only
I remembered there should have been a Thursday, because Thursday
was my birthday."

Mara stood. Pale lost minutes stirred beneath the shelves, disturbed
by her movement.

"Eli, stay where you are."

"I can't. The man is coming."

"What man?"

"The one who collects clocks."

The line went dead.

Mara called the hospital through the ordinary exchange. A receptionist
answered and confirmed that Saint Orra's was operating normally.

"May I speak to a patient named Eli?" Mara asked.

"Surname?"

"I don't have one."

"We have seven Elis."

"A boy whose mother died last night."

The receptionist became quiet.

"Please hold."

A different voice came on the line. Calm, male, official.

"There is no such patient."

"I was told—"

"You were misinformed."

"Who is this?"

The man hung up.

Mara put on her coat.

The rules prohibited her from leaving the workshop during office
hours. The rules also prohibited unregistered disappearance of a calendar
day. She decided the second violation was more serious than
the first.

Before leaving, she opened the emergency cabinet.

Inside were six stopwatches, three sealed envelopes, a compass that
pointed toward regret, and a small brass device shaped like
a key. Mr. Vale had called it the Unwinder.

"Never use it," he had said.

"What does it do?"

"What the name suggests."

She slipped it into her pocket.

Saint Orra's stood beside the river, its white stone façade
stained gray by rain. In the entrance hall, patients sat
beneath a clock with no hands. Mara approached the reception
desk.

"I'm looking for Eli."

The receptionist's practiced smile vanished.

"You called earlier."

"Yes."

"There is no Eli."

"You said there were seven."

"I was mistaken."

Mara took out her official identification. It carried no photograph,
only her name beneath a silver seal that shifted when
viewed from different angles.

"I am authorized to inspect temporal irregularities."

The receptionist went pale.

A door opened behind her.

A tall man entered the lobby. He wore a charcoal
suit, gray gloves, and a chain of silver watches across
his waistcoat. His hair was perfectly white, though his face
seemed young.

"Ms. Venn," he said. "You should be at your post."

"And you are?"

"Someone correcting an old mistake."

Every clock in the lobby began ticking backward.

Patients continued reading magazines. Nurses crossed the floor. No one
looked up.

Mara felt the Unwinder grow warm in her pocket.

"What happened to Thursday?" she asked.

The man smiled. "Such an odd question. Days don't belong
to anyone."

"They belong to everyone."

"Then no one will miss one."

"A boy did."

The smile left his face.

From somewhere above came the crash of breaking glass.

The man looked toward the ceiling.

Mara ran.

She reached the stairwell before he could stop her. Behind
her, shoes struck the floor with an unnaturally regular beat.
She climbed two flights, then three, following the faint ringing
of bells she had heard over the telephone.

On the fourth floor, every room stood empty.

At the end of the corridor, a red door swung
shut.

Mara pushed through it and found herself on the roof.

Rain glazed the tiles. Chimneys and towers rose across the
city beneath a low black sky. Near the ledge stood
a thin boy in a hospital gown, holding an alarm
clock against his chest.

"You came," he said.

"Eli?"

He nodded.

The clock collector stepped onto the roof behind her.

"Give me the clock, child."

Eli backed toward the ledge.

Mara held out a hand. "Come away from there."

"He took Thursday," the boy said. "He put it in
this."

The alarm clock was an old blue thing with two
bells on top. Its hands spun rapidly, blurring around the
face. Through a crack in its glass, golden light escaped
in pulses.

Mara had seen light like that before, though never so
bright. Stored time.

A whole day could not fit inside a clock. Yet
there it was, shaking in Eli's hands.

The collector removed one gray glove. The skin beneath was
marked with tiny numbers.

"You do not understand what you are protecting," he said.

"Then explain," Mara replied.

He looked across the city.

"Yesterday, at seven minutes past two, a generator failed beneath
this hospital. The backup system did not start. Twelve people
on life support died. At eight minutes past two, a
nurse attempted to restart the main circuit and caused a
fire. By half past two, the east wing was burning.
By three, one hundred and fourteen people were dead."

Rain ran down his face, but he did not blink.

"I removed the day before it could settle into history."

Mara looked at Eli.

"My mum?" he asked.

The collector's voice softened. "Her machine failed first."

"So she died on Thursday."

"Yes."

"Then why is she still dead if you took Thursday
away?"

"Because consequences resist correction. Time is not a page that
can simply be torn from a book. Events leave impressions.
Some disappear. Some remain."

Eli's fingers tightened around the clock. "Put it back."

"If I restore the day, the fire returns. So do
all the deaths."

"My mum comes back first."

"For less than a day."

"I don't care."

The collector turned to Mara. "You see the difficulty."

She did.

It was the kind of problem the office avoided by
hiding beneath railway stations and writing rules. Every lost hour
was supposed to be returned if its owner could be
identified, but a day belonged to millions. Returning it would
restore birthdays, promises, meals, arguments, accidents, and deaths.

"Why didn't my office detect this?" she asked.

"I redirected the eastern tubes."

"You caused the irregularity."

"I contained it."

"You failed to contain Eli."

The collector studied the boy. "Children are troublesome witnesses. Their
sense of time is not yet disciplined. Adults accept what
calendars tell them. Children remember that Thursday comes after Wednesday."

Eli held the clock away from him. "If I smash
it, does Thursday come back?"

"Yes," said the collector.

"No," said Mara at the same time.

Eli looked between them.

Mara moved closer. "We don't know what will happen."

"I know," the collector said.

"You admitted that consequences resist correction."

"I have done this before."

The rain seemed to stop in the air.

Mara stared at him. "How many times?"

He said nothing.

"How many days have you stolen?"

"Seven."

"From this city?"

"From many places."

"Why?"

"To prevent wars. Epidemics. One earthquake. Each removal saved lives."

"And what did it cost?"

His jaw tightened.

Mara understood then. "You don't know."

"I know what would have happened."

"You know one version. You don't know what vanished with
those days."

"Minor events weighed against catastrophe."

"There are no minor events."

His expression sharpened. "That is a clerk's answer."

"It is a keeper's answer."

The collector stepped forward.

Mara drew the Unwinder.

It looked absurd in her hand, no larger than a
door key, but the man stopped. The watches across his
waistcoat began to tremble.

"Vale gave you that?" he asked.

"You knew him?"

"He knew better than to use it."

"So do I."

"Do you?"

No, she thought.

But Mr. Vale had taught her something else: when dealing
with time, certainty was usually a disguise worn by arrogance.

She fitted her thumb into the ring at the Unwinder's
end and turned.

The world opened.

The roof peeled away like paper. Rain rose into the
clouds. Bells swallowed their own echoes. Mara felt herself falling
through a thousand invisible floors.

Then she stood in a hospital room filled with afternoon
sun.

A woman lay in bed beside a humming machine. Eli
sat near the window, wearing a paper crown. A small
cake rested on the table between them.

Ten candles.

Thursday.

Mara was present but not solid. She saw the collector
beside her, equally translucent, his face strained with anger.

"What have you done?" he demanded.

"Unwound the container."

"You'll release the entire day."

"No. Not yet."

The scene remained still except for Eli and his mother.

"You promised not to sing," the woman said.

"I didn't promise. I said I'd think about it."

"Well?"

"I thought about it."

He began singing loudly and badly. His mother laughed until
she coughed. Eli stopped, frightened, but she waved him on.

Mara looked away.

The collector did not.

The room shifted.

At seven minutes past two, the machine beside the bed
went dark.

The woman's eyes opened.

Eli stood. "Mum?"

An alarm began somewhere in the corridor. Nurses shouted. The
lights flickered.

The collector raised one hand. "This is the point."

Time slowed around them.

A nurse was frozen in the doorway. Eli's paper crown
hung in the air as it fell from his head.
His mother lay motionless, her final breath not yet finished.

"I removed everything after this moment," the collector said. "The
day cannot be restored safely."

Mara examined the machine. "Why did the generator fail?"

"Mechanical neglect. A corroded valve."

"And the backup?"

"An automatic switch jammed."

She walked through the frozen corridor, following cables that glowed
faintly beneath the walls. The collector followed.

"What are you looking for?"

"The lost hour that caused this."

"There is no lost hour."

"There's always a lost hour."

In the basement, they found the generator room. A maintenance
log hung beside the door. Mara turned its pages.

Three months earlier, an engineer named Tomas Reed had scheduled
a safety inspection. It had been postponed because of a
staff meeting. Rescheduled for the following week, it had been
postponed again during a storm. The third appointment was cancelled
when Reed's daughter fell ill.

Each delay had been reasonable. Together, they had killed twelve
people.

Mara placed her hand over the entries.

There.

Between the paper fibers, almost invisible, lay forty-seven lost minutes.
The inspection would have required only thirty.

She pulled.

The minutes came free as a green thread, twisting around
her fingers.

"You can't insert lost time into an established sequence," the
collector said.

"Not without payment."

"What payment?"

"An equal duration freely surrendered."

He stared at her. "Whose?"

"Mine."

Before he could stop her, Mara wound the green thread
around the Unwinder and turned it backward.

The basement vanished.

She was in her workshop three months earlier, watching herself
label jars. Above her, a train groaned into the station.
The clock read 4:13.

Mara touched her past self's shoulder.

Forty-seven minutes lifted from her life.

They were not special minutes. She had spent them drinking
tea, sharpening pencils, and watching rain slide down the window.
Still, their removal hurt. Every moment clings to the life
that contains it.

Her tea vanished from the cup. The pencils became sharp.
Rain streaked upward. The clock jumped from 4:13 to 5:00.

The stolen minutes flowed into the eastern collection tube and
sped across the city.

They reached Tomas Reed as he stood to leave work.

He paused.

In the first history, he had gone home.

In the new history, he looked at the maintenance request
on his desk and sighed.

"Forty minutes," he said to the empty office. "Then I'm
done."

He went to Saint Orra's.

He found the corroded valve.

The world shuddered.

Mara was thrown back onto the hospital roof.

The blue alarm clock shattered at Eli's feet.

Golden light rushed into the sky.

Thursday returned.

Across the city, people stopped in streets and kitchens and
offices as memories struck them. A wedding reappeared in the
minds of its guests. A shopkeeper remembered selling a red
umbrella. Two sisters recovered an argument. A teacher recalled a
lesson nobody had known they had forgotten.

At Saint Orra's, alarms sounded.

But there was no fire.

Mara ran downstairs.

She found Eli in room 417, sitting beside his mother.
The paper crown was on his head. The cake had
been eaten down to crumbs.

His mother was alive.

She looked tired and confused, but alive.

Eli touched her hand as if he were afraid it
might disappear.

In the doorway, the collector watched.

"You altered official time," he said.

"So did you."

"You risked hundreds of lives."

"To restore what you stole."

"And if the repair had failed?"

"It didn't."

"That is not an answer."

"No," Mara said. "It is a result."

He looked older now. The numbers on his bare hand
had faded. One by one, the watches on his waistcoat
stopped.

"What happens to me?" he asked.

"You return the other days."

"Some contain terrible things."

"Most days do."

"And if restoring them causes disaster?"

"Then we look for the lost hour. We repair what
can be repaired. We accept what cannot."

He gave a bitter laugh. "You make it sound simple."

"It will be the hardest thing you have ever done."

For the first time, Mara saw fear in his face.

"Will you help me?"

She considered the silent eastern tube, the impossible ledger, and
the shelves beneath the station. She thought of seven missing
days scattered across the world, each crowded with lives that
did not know they had been interrupted.

"Yes," she said. "But first you will tell me your
name."

He looked down at his stopped watches.

"Vale," he said. "Adrian Vale."

Mara felt the world tilt.

"Mr. Vale's son?"

"My father believed time should be kept, never corrected. We
disagreed."

"Where is he?"

"By the sea, unless he has finally forgiven me."

Mara smiled despite herself. "His clocks run slow."

"They always did."

A nurse entered and asked them to leave. To her,
they were merely two strangers blocking the doorway.

Eli called Mara back before she stepped into the hall.

"Did you save my mum?"

Mara glanced at Adrian Vale.

"No," she said. "A man named Tomas did. He just
needed enough time."

Eli seemed to understand this better than an adult would
have.

He nodded solemnly and returned to his mother.

The next morning, Mara unlocked the green door beneath the
railway clock at six.

The workshop was in chaos.

Thursday had arrived through every tube at once. It overflowed
from bottles, flashed beneath cabinets, and rang inside drawers. Twenty-four
restored hours crowded the room, carrying rain, laughter, smoke, music,
fear, and the smell of birthday candles.

Adrian stood in the middle of it all with his
sleeves rolled up.

"Where do we begin?" he asked.

Mara hung her coat, opened the ledger, and gave him
a pen.

"With everything people thought they had lost."

Above them, trains arrived and departed. Thousands of feet crossed
the station floor. Watches ran fast, clocks ran slow, and
hurried people complained that there was never enough time.

Beneath them, Mara and Adrian worked.

They labelled each recovered moment in neat black ink.

They returned what belonged to the living.

They kept what had no home.

And whenever a lost hour seemed small or useless, Mara
placed it carefully on the shelf, knowing that somewhere, someday,
someone might need exactly that much time.

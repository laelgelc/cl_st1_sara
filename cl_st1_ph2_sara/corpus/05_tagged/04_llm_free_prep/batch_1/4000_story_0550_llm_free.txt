Every morning at seven, the sea left a letter on
Mara Vale's doorstep.

The first arrived in October, folded into a square and
sealed with gray wax. It was damp but not soaked,
though the tide had climbed the harbor wall during the
night and flooded half of Lantern Street. Her name was
written across the front in a narrow, slanting hand:

**MARA VALE, CLOCK REPAIRER**

Inside was a single sentence.

**Your brother is alive beneath the western bell.**

Mara read it twice, then carried it to the stove
and held one corner over the flame. The paper browned.
The ink remained black. Before the fire could take it,
she dropped the letter into the sink and turned away.

Her brother, Eli, had been dead for eleven years.

The western bell had not rung for twelve.

In Bellweather, these facts were as solid as the cliffs.

The town stood on a crescent of black rock at
the edge of a cold northern sea. Five bell towers
watched over it—north, south, east, west, and center—built generations ago
to warn of storms. Four still rang. The western tower
had been silent since the night Eli Vale disappeared.

People said the rope had snapped. Mara knew better. She
had been there.

At seven the next morning, the sea left another letter.

**You have forgotten what you promised him.**

Mara tore it in half.

On the third morning:

**Ask Tomas Reed who cut the rope.**

That one she did not burn.

Tomas Reed kept the town records in the lower rooms
of the council hall. He had been old when Mara
was a child and seemed to have aged only in
texture since then, like leather left in sunlight. He wore
three pairs of spectacles on cords around his neck and
trusted none of them.

When Mara entered his office, he was standing on a
stool, arguing with a shelf.

"You moved," he told it.

The shelf said nothing.

"Tomas."

He climbed down carefully. "Mara Vale. If this concerns the
clock in the council chamber, it loses seven minutes a
day because the mayor insists on winding it backward."

"It concerns the western bell."

Tomas became still.

Mara placed the letter on his desk. His eyes moved
over the sentence. Then he removed one pair of spectacles
and put on another.

"Where did you get this?"

"It was on my doorstep."

"Who delivered it?"

"The sea, according to local gossip."

"Local gossip has poor handwriting."

"Did you cut the rope?"

Tomas looked toward the window. Beyond the warped glass, gulls
wheeled above the wet roofs.

"At your age," he said, "you should know that questions
are rarely dangerous. Answers are."

"Did you cut it?"

"No."

"Who did?"

"Your brother."

Mara stared at him.

Tomas lowered himself into his chair. "Sit down."

"I prefer standing."

"You always did."

He opened a drawer and removed a ledger bound in
cracked blue cloth. Its pages smelled of salt and dust.
He turned to a date eleven years earlier: the seventeenth
of November.

Mara remembered the date without needing to see it. She
remembered the storm pressing its enormous hands against the windows.
She remembered fifteen-year-old Eli grinning beneath a yellow oilskin, daring
her to follow him up the western tower. She remembered
the iron steps, wet beneath her boots, and the black
bell hanging overhead.

Most of all, she remembered the thing they had heard
inside it.

Not a ring.

A voice.

"Mara," Eli had whispered, "it knows my name."

She had laughed because she was afraid.

In Tomas's ledger, the entries for that night were sparse.

**9:14 p.m. — Western bell sounded without human action.**

**9:20 p.m. — Tower keeper ordered evacuation.**

**9:26 p.m. — Bell rope severed.**

**9:31 p.m. — One child missing.**

Child. As if Eli had been any child. As if
he had not collected sea glass in blue jars, whistled
through the gap in his front teeth, and slept with
one foot outside his blankets even in winter.

"You wrote that the rope was severed," Mara said.

"I wrote what I was told."

"By whom?"

"By you."

The room seemed to tilt.

"That isn't true."

Tomas opened another drawer and took out a small brass
key.

"You came to me before dawn," he said. "Your hands
were bleeding. You gave me this and told me to
hide it until the sea began writing."

Mara did not touch the key.

"I never did that."

"You did."

"I would remember."

"Would you?"

He spoke gently, which angered her more than cruelty might
have.

Mara snatched up the key. "What does it open?"

"The door beneath the western bell."

"There is no door beneath the western bell."

Tomas replaced the spectacles around his neck one pair at
a time.

"You said that too."

At seven the following morning, the fourth letter waited on
her doorstep.

**Bring the clock that has never worked. Come before the
lowest tide.**

Mara owned two hundred and six clocks.

They crowded the walls of her shop, stood in glass
cabinets, and lay in careful pieces across her workbenches. There
were walnut mantel clocks, brass carriage clocks, silver pocket watches,
cuckoo clocks with painted doors, and one complicated French clock
whose moon turned through a sky of blue enamel.

At six every evening, Mara wound them all.

At midnight, the shop became a forest of ticking.

Only one clock had never worked.

It had belonged to Eli.

He had built it from scraps when he was thirteen:
a crooked wooden box containing mismatched gears, a bent spring,
and a white face on which he had painted thirteen
numbers. The thirteenth sat where twelve should have been, at
the top.

"What hour is that?" Mara had asked when he showed
it to her.

"The hour that gets lost," he said.

Now she took it from the highest shelf. Dust softened
its edges. On the back, beneath a stain she had
always assumed was rust, someone had scratched three words:

**TURN BACK TIDE.**

Mara closed the shop early.

The lowest tide came just after dusk. It drew the
sea away from the western cliffs, exposing rocks that shone
like the backs of sleeping animals. The western bell tower
stood at the edge of the town, fenced and abandoned,
its stones furred with salt.

Mara found the gate unlocked.

Inside, the air smelled of iron. The staircase curled upward
into darkness, but she did not climb. She searched the
circular room at ground level, pressing the brass key against
cracks, hinges, and gaps between stones.

At last she noticed a keyhole hidden behind a loose
piece of mortar.

The key turned.

A section of wall opened inward.

Beyond it, steps descended beneath the tower.

Mara carried Eli's clock in one hand and a lantern
in the other. The stairs were narrow and slick. They
went down farther than the base of the cliff, farther
than seemed possible. The sound of the sea grew louder,
not above her but around her, as though she were
descending into the throat of some vast creature.

At the bottom stood a red door.

A brass plate at its center had been engraved with
a single word:

**LATE**

Mara placed her palm against the wood. It was warm.

The door opened before she could knock.

On the other side was Bellweather.

Yet it was not the Bellweather she knew.

The town lay under a clear summer sky. Sunlight filled
the streets. Boats with bright sails crowded the harbor, and
the five towers rang together in a joyful storm of
bronze.

People moved through the square, but none had faces.

Where eyes and mouths should have been, their features blurred
like reflections troubled by rain.

All except one.

Eli stood beside the fountain.

He was fifteen.

His yellow oilskin was unbuttoned. His dark hair fell across
his forehead, and the gap still showed between his front
teeth.

"You took your time," he said.

Mara could not move.

He came toward her, then stopped a few feet away.
His smile faded.

"You got old."

"You didn't."

"I was busy."

She set the lantern down because her hand had begun
to shake. "Are you alive?"

"Sometimes."

"What does that mean?"

"It means you'll need a better question."

Mara crossed the distance between them and struck him across
the face.

Her palm met warm skin.

Eli touched his cheek. "I suppose I deserved that."

She struck him again, then seized his coat and pulled
him into her arms. He felt real. He smelled of
rain, wet wool, and the peppermint sweets he used to
steal from their mother's pantry.

Mara held him until grief became anger again.

"You left me."

"No," he said against her shoulder. "You left me."

She pushed him away.

The faceless people had stopped walking. Every blank head turned
toward them.

Eli took Mara's hand. "We can't stay in the open."

He led her through streets that were familiar and wrong.
The baker's shop sold loaves shaped like fish. The council
hall had no doors. Mara's own clock shop stood on
the corner, though it had not existed when Eli disappeared.
Through its windows she saw herself at many ages—eight, seventeen,
thirty, forty—bending over clocks that ran backward.

They entered a small house behind the eastern tower. Inside,
hundreds of bells hung from the ceiling. None moved, yet
their tones trembled in the air.

"What is this place?" Mara asked.

"The hour that gets lost."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." Eli sat at a
table. "Every clock loses something, Mara. A second here, a
minute there. People think the lost time vanishes. It doesn't.
It washes down to this place."

She looked out the window. The sun had not shifted.

"The night you disappeared—"

"The bell opened the way. It had done so before.
That's why the towers were built. Not to warn the
town about storms. To drown out the voices beneath the
sea."

Mara remembered the sound inside the western bell calling Eli's
name.

"You cut the rope," she said.

"I tried to stop it ringing. But it was too
late. The door opened."

"And you went through."

"We both did."

"No."

"Yes."

He reached across the table and touched a pale scar
on her wrist. Mara had carried it for eleven years
without remembering how she got it.

"You held on to me," Eli said. "The tide was
pulling us deeper. The people here came for us. So
you made a bargain."

"I don't remember."

"That was the price."

A bell near the ceiling gave a small, clear note.

Outside, the faceless townspeople gathered around the house.

"What bargain?"

Eli looked at the broken clock in her arms.

"You asked the hour to take your memory instead of
your life. You promised to build a clock that could
return what had been lost. Then you escaped and sealed
the door."

"Why didn't you come with me?"

His expression answered before he spoke.

"The door needed someone on this side."

Mara stood so abruptly that the hanging bells trembled.

"No. I wouldn't have agreed to that."

"You were sixteen. We were drowning. I told you to
go."

"And I obeyed?"

"You always did when it mattered."

A fist struck the front door.

The wood bent inward.

Eli took the clock from her and examined its crooked
face. "You kept it."

"You made it."

"We made it. You designed the escapement."

"I would remember."

"You keep saying that."

Another blow shook the house. A crack opened across the
door.

"What do they want?" Mara asked.

"The same thing everyone wants. More time."

The broken clock began to tick.

Once.

Twice.

Its single hand moved from one to two.

"I never repaired it," Mara whispered.

"You weren't supposed to repair it. You were supposed to
bring it back."

Eli turned the clock around. A small brass keyhole waited
in the rear panel. Mara inserted the key Tomas had
given her.

It fit.

When she turned it, the clock sprang apart.

Gears unfolded like metal flowers. Slender arms extended from the
wooden case. Rings of brass spun around a dark center
in which stars appeared—tiny, cold, and impossibly distant.

The faceless people screamed.

The door shattered.

They entered in a pale crowd, filling the house shoulder
to shoulder. Their unfinished faces tilted toward the clock.

Eli shouted something Mara could not hear. The bells overhead
were ringing now, hundreds of them, each with a different
voice. Some laughed. Some wept. Some called names.

The clock's hand passed six.

A faceless woman caught Eli by the arm. He tore
free, leaving a strip of yellow cloth in her fingers.
Mara swung the lantern. Glass broke against the woman's shoulder,
and blue fire raced over her dress. She did not
burn. Instead, a face flickered into being: old eyes, a
child's mouth, a nose made of shadow. Then it vanished.

"They're memories," Mara said.

"Possibilities," Eli replied. "Lives that almost happened. Hours no one
lived."

The hand reached ten.

The red door appeared at the back of the house,
standing open onto the dark stairway beneath the tower.

"Go!" Eli shouted.

Mara seized him. "Both of us."

"I told you. The door needs a keeper."

"Then they can keep it."

"If no one does, this place floods into ours. Every
lost moment returns at once. The dead come home. The
unborn arrive. Yesterday sits beside tomorrow. The world stops."

"Then we find another way."

"We had eleven years to find another way."

"No. I forgot. You waited."

The hand reached twelve.

There was one number left.

Thirteen.

Eli looked exactly as he had on the last night:
frightened, stubborn, young.

Mara understood then that waiting was not the same as
living. He had spent eleven years in a single hour,
preserving a town that mourned him and a sister who
had forgotten her guilt.

She took his face between her hands.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"I know."

"I'm not leaving you again."

"Mara—"

"When the hand reaches thirteen, does the door close?"

"Yes."

"And it needs a keeper?"

"Yes."

"Not a prisoner?"

His eyes widened.

Mara pushed him toward the red door.

He struggled, but she had the strength of forty-three years
and every morning she had risen without him. Every clock
she had repaired, every winter she had endured, every silent
meal had hardened something within her.

"No!" he shouted. "You can't."

"I'm older."

"That's not a reason."

"I've had my time."

"That's not a reason either!"

The faceless crowd closed around them.

Mara pressed her forehead to his.

"Then let this be the reason: I am your sister,
and this time you will obey me when it matters."

She shoved him through the red door.

Eli landed on the stone steps beyond. He turned, but
the crowd surged between them. His hand reached through a
narrowing gap.

"Mara!"

"Ring the western bell!"

"The rope is cut!"

"You were always good at fixing things."

"I made one clock!"

"And you put thirteen hours on it."

The clock struck.

The sound was not loud. It was the delicate chime
of a spoon touching a glass.

The faceless people stopped.

Outside, the summer sky cracked from horizon to horizon.

Behind Eli, deep in the real world, the tide began
to return.

"You'll forget me," Mara said.

He shook his head fiercely.

"That is the price."

"No."

"It's all right."

"It isn't."

"No," she agreed. "But it will have to be."

The hand moved onto thirteen.

The red door slammed.

For one moment, Mara stood in complete silence.

Then the lost hour came apart.

Streets rose into the sky like ribbons. Houses folded flat.
The faceless crowd burst into showers of rain, each drop
containing an unlived life. Mara saw herself married to a
fisherman she had once kissed at a midsummer dance. She
saw Eli as an old man with silver hair. She
saw their mother alive, opening her pantry to find every
peppermint sweet untouched.

The visions passed through her and were gone.

The bells fell quiet.

Mara found herself alone beside the fountain.

The broken clock rested at her feet, ticking steadily. Its
hand had returned to one.

She picked it up and walked toward the western tower,
though no tower stood there now. Only a narrow house
with a red door.

Inside, hundreds of bells waited overhead.

Mara sat at the table.

Beyond the windows, the lost town rebuilt itself beneath an
unmoving summer sun. Faceless people wandered through the square. Somewhere
far above, on the other side of the sea, morning
approached.

At seven, Eli Vale woke on the wet stones beneath
the western bell.

He was twenty-six years late and remembered nothing of where
he had been.

The tide foamed around his boots. In his hand he
held a brass key. Beside him stood an old lantern,
a broken clock, and a sealed gray letter addressed in
handwriting he did not recognize.

He opened it.

**Your sister is alive beneath the western bell.**

Eli read the sentence several times.

He had no sister.

At least, he could not remember one.

Yet grief rose in him, sudden and vast, and for
a while he could do nothing but kneel in the
shallow water and weep for someone whose face was gone.

When the tide climbed to his knees, the western bell
rang above him.

Its rope had been cut eleven years before, but now
a new one hung from the bronze mouth, braided from
yellow cloth and strands of gray hair.

The first note rolled across Bellweather.

In the north tower, the keeper woke and pulled his
rope. The southern bell answered, then the eastern, then the
central. Lights appeared in windows. Doors opened. People stepped into
the rain and looked toward the tower that had been
silent for so long.

Eli rose.

Inside the bell's fading voice, he heard the ticking of
hundreds of clocks and a woman saying his name.

He climbed the tower stairs.

At the top, carved into the stone beneath the bell,
he found instructions written in a narrow, slanting hand:

**RING AT DAWN.**

**RING AT STORM.**

**RING WHEN THE SEA BEGINS TO WRITE.**

Below that, in smaller letters:

**DO NOT LET ME BE FORGOTTEN.**

Eli traced the final words with his fingertips.

Then he took hold of the rope and rang the
bell again.

Far beneath him, in the hour that gets lost, Mara
heard it.

The faceless people paused in their wandering. The fixed sun
shivered. One of the small bells above her table answered
with a bright note.

Mara smiled.

Every morning after that, at seven, Eli found a letter
on the tower steps.

Some contained warnings of storms. Some held names of sailors
who could still be saved. Others offered instructions for repairing
strange clocks that appeared overnight in his workshop, though he
could not remember opening a workshop or learning the trade.

One letter simply said:

**You still sleep with one foot outside the blankets.**

Another:

**Peppermint was never safe around you.**

And another:

**When you were six, you believed the moon followed you
home because it was lonely.**

Eli kept every letter.

Years passed. His hair silvered. Bellweather changed around him. New
houses rose, old boats sank, children learned the story of
the man who had returned from the sea without a
past.

Each dawn he rang the western bell.

Each morning the letters came.

With them, memory returned—not all at once, and never completely,
but in flashes. A girl laughing on a cliff path.
Oil-stained fingers guiding his over a stubborn gear. A hand
holding his in a storm.

At last, on a calm autumn morning, the sea left
no letter.

Eli waited until noon.

At sunset, he unlocked the hidden door and descended with
the thirteen-hour clock in his arms. He was an old
man by then, but his steps were certain.

At the bottom, the red door stood open.

Beyond it, beneath a clear summer sky, a woman waited
beside a fountain. She looked forty-three, exactly as she had
when the lost hour claimed her.

This time, Eli remembered her face.

"You took your time," Mara said.

He smiled, and the gap between his front teeth showed
just as it had when he was fifteen.

"I was busy."

Behind him, the red door swung shut.

Above the sea, the five bells began to ring, though
no human hand pulled their ropes.

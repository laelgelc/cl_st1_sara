### The Clockmaker's Garden

On the morning the town clock stopped, Mara Venn was
planting white beans beside her father's grave.

The clock had ruled Bellwether for more than a century.
Its four black faces watched from the tower above the
market square, visible from nearly every road and rooftop. It
rang at dawn, noon, and dusk, and once for every
hour between. Children were born by it, bakers opened their
ovens by it, trains arrived—usually late—beneath its disapproving hands.

At eleven minutes past nine, the minute hand shuddered and
refused to move.

Mara did not notice. She was kneeling in the damp
earth behind the abandoned clockmaker's shop, pressing beans into a
furrow with her thumb. Her father had believed that useful
things should grow around the dead.

"Flowers are apologies," Elias Venn used to say. "Plant onions.
The dead have already had enough sweetness."

But Mara had chosen beans because they climbed. By midsummer,
she hoped, their vines would cover the iron fence around
his grave and soften its sharp points.

She had just covered the last seed when someone hammered
on the garden gate.

"Mara! Miss Venn!"

It was Tomas Reed, the baker's youngest apprentice. He leaned
over the gate, flour whitening his eyebrows.

"The clock's stopped."

Mara brushed soil from her hands. "Then people will have
to guess when they're hungry."

"It's never stopped before."

"It stopped during the flood."

"Only because the tower was underwater."

"Then perhaps it's raining very hard up there."

Tomas looked offended. The town had been waiting for two
months to see what Mara would do with her father's
shop, his tools, and his duties. They seemed to regard
grief as an apprenticeship exam.

"Mayor Vale says you must come."

"Mayor Vale can say many things."

"He says it's an emergency."

Mara glanced toward the tower. Beyond the tiled roofs, the
great eastern clock face gleamed in the sun. The hands
stood crooked at nine eleven.

Her father had maintained the town clock for forty-three years.
For twenty of those years, Mara had climbed the tower
behind him, first carrying oilcloths and brushes, later adjusting escapements
and replacing worn teeth. At fourteen, she could dismantle the
quarter-hour mechanism. At seventeen, she had built a pocket watch
that lost less than a second each month.

Then, at twenty-three, she had left Bellwether and sworn never
to repair another clock.

She returned eleven years later to bury her father.

"Tell the mayor I'll come after breakfast," she said.

"The emergency is now."

"Emergencies often are."

She locked the garden gate, leaving Tomas outside, and went
into the house.

The clockmaker's shop occupied the front room. Dust lay over
the display cases. Behind the glass, unfinished clocks waited with
blank faces or missing hands. Hundreds of tools hung in
precise rows along the wall: files, pliers, screwdrivers, tiny saws,
brass hammers, and delicate instruments whose names Elias had invented
when proper names displeased him.

Mara paused beside his workbench.

A mug remained where he had left it. A brown
circle stained the wood beneath it. Inside were three bent
screws, a feather, and a dead moth.

Mara had found him in the garden on the first
cold morning of spring. He had been sitting beneath the
pear tree with his coat neatly buttoned and a half-peeled
apple in his hand. The knife lay closed in his
lap. His final expression was not peaceful, as neighbors politely
claimed. It was surprised.

She took his tower key from the hook.

"Breakfast," she told the empty shop, "can wait."

By the time she reached the square, half the town
had gathered below the clock. Without its ticking and bells,
the silence felt swollen. Shopkeepers stood in their doorways. Farmers
checked wristwatches inherited from people who had checked the town
clock. Children pointed upward as if expecting the tower to
collapse.

Mayor Vale met Mara at the door. He was a
tall, narrow man with a silver chain across his waistcoat
and the permanently wounded expression of someone whose authority was
never admired enough.

"At last," he said.

"It's been twenty minutes."

"Thirty-one."

"Then why do you need the clock?"

His mouth tightened. "Can you repair it?"

"I haven't seen what's wrong."

"Your father never needed to see what was wrong."

"My father frequently needed to see things."

Vale lowered his voice. "There's a Founders' Day delegation arriving
tomorrow. The governor's secretary will be here. We cannot have
the principal civic landmark showing the wrong time."

"Cover it with a sheet."

He stared at her.

Mara unlocked the tower door.

Inside, the familiar smell met her: stone dust, old oil,
pigeon feathers, and cold metal. A spiral staircase wound upward
through darkness. She climbed slowly, one hand trailing along the
central pillar. Names and dates had been carved into the
stone by generations of maintenance workers, young lovers, bored guards,
and children who had stolen keys.

Halfway up, she found her own initials beside her father's.

M.V., age 9.

E.V., older than stone.

She had forgotten that.

The mechanism room occupied the level behind the clock faces.
Sunlight entered through the pale glass, washing everything in watery
gold. The machine filled most of the chamber—a massive arrangement
of iron frames, brass gears, winding drums, rods, cables, and
counterweights descending into the tower's hollow depths.

Mara stood at the threshold and listened.

A stopped clock was not truly silent. Metal settled. Cables
strained. Wood expanded in the sun. Somewhere inside the mechanism,
a spring gave a faint, exhausted sigh.

She removed her coat and began.

The main drive was wound. The pendulum hung straight. The
escapement showed no fracture. She inspected the striking train, quarter
train, winding gears, and governor fan. Nothing was broken. Nothing
was jammed.

Yet the clock would not move.

Mara placed two fingers on the great center wheel and
applied gentle pressure. It resisted as if someone on the
other side were holding it.

She fetched a lantern and crouched beneath the frame.

There, behind the hour wheel, something shone.

At first she thought it was wire. Using tweezers, she
drew out a long golden thread, fine as hair and
warm between her fingers. It did not belong to any
mechanism she knew.

More of it wound through the gears.

Mara followed the thread to a narrow gap between the
clock frame and the western wall. She held up the
lantern. The gap should have ended after a few inches.

Instead, she saw a door.

It was no taller than her knee, made of dark
wood, with a brass knob and a keyhole shaped like
an eye.

Mara sat back.

Her father had taught her that when a machine behaved
impossibly, the mechanic had misunderstood the machine. He had not
mentioned tiny doors appearing inside public monuments.

She touched the knob.

The door opened inward.

Beyond it stretched a garden beneath a violet sky.

Mara closed the door.

She waited.

Then she opened it again.

The garden remained.

Rows of silver grass swayed without wind. Blue trees bore
small glass fruits. A path of black stones led toward
a distant greenhouse glowing warmly among the leaves. The golden
thread crossed the path and vanished into the grass.

The doorway was too small to crawl through. Mara reached
inside, expecting her hand to meet the back of the
wall.

Instead, the instant her fingers crossed the threshold, the room
lurched.

She fell forward.

For a moment she was tumbling through darkness, surrounded by
the sound of clocks striking every hour at once. Then
she landed on soft earth.

When she stood, the little door had become a full-sized
gate behind her. Through it she could see the mechanism
room, oddly distant, as if viewed through the wrong end
of a telescope.

The air in the garden smelled of rain on hot
stone.

"Mara," said her father.

He was standing on the path.

Elias Venn looked as he had five years before his
death: broad-shouldered, white-bearded, and mildly irritated by existence. He wore
his brown work coat. A magnifying lens was tucked over
one eye.

Mara could not breathe.

He raised a hand. "Before you become emotional, I should
explain—"

She struck him across the face.

The sound startled glass birds from a nearby tree.

Elias touched his cheek. "That was emotional."

"You died."

"Yes."

"I buried you."

"I noticed."

She struck him again, though less hard.

"Ow."

"Do dead men say ow?"

"This one does."

Mara stepped backward. Her legs trembled. "What is this?"

Elias looked toward the glowing greenhouse. "A complication."

"You sat under the pear tree and died."

"I sat under the pear tree. Death came slightly later."

"I washed your body."

"That was kind of you."

"I hated you."

"I know."

"I loved you."

"I know that too."

She covered her face, but tears slipped between her fingers.
Elias waited. He had always been better at waiting than
comforting.

Finally, Mara lowered her hands. "Are you real?"

"Mostly."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only accurate one."

He turned and walked along the path. After a moment,
Mara followed.

The silver grass chimed against their boots. In the branches
overhead, glass fruit held tiny moving scenes: a woman closing
a window, a dog crossing a bridge, a ship vanishing
into fog.

"What are those?" Mara asked.

"Minutes."

"Whose?"

"Everyone's."

He plucked a fruit from a low branch. Inside it,
Mara saw Tomas carrying bread across the market square. The
image moved briefly, then darkened.

"Every minute that passes in Bellwether grows here," Elias said.
"Most ripen, fall, and dissolve. The important ones last longer."

"Important to whom?"

"The person living them."

They reached the greenhouse. Its panes were fogged from within.
Golden threads emerged through cracks in the glass and ran
across the garden, twining around trees, disappearing into the earth.
The thread tangled in the town clock came from here.

Elias opened the door.

Inside, thousands of clocks covered the walls. Tall clocks, tiny
clocks, watches, sundials, hourglasses, water clocks, candle clocks. Some raced.
Some ticked once each minute. Others moved backward. Their combined
sound was not mechanical but oceanic, a rising and falling
pulse.

At the center stood a leafless pear tree.

A single clock hung from one branch.

Mara recognized it at once. She had made it when
she was seventeen: a small brass pocket watch, plain except
for a star engraved on the case. She had given
it to her father on the day she left.

"You kept it," she whispered.

"I keep everything."

"That was always your problem."

"Yes."

Golden thread had burst from the watch's seams, spreading through
the greenhouse and out into the garden.

"What did you do?"

Elias took the watch down. "I broke the clock."

"The town clock?"

"Time in the town. Temporarily."

"Why?"

"To bring you here."

Mara stared at him. "You stopped an entire town to
arrange a conversation?"

"You weren't answering my letters."

"You're dead."

"A poor excuse for rudeness."

Anger steadied her. "Start it again."

"I can't."

"Then why bring me?"

"Because you can."

He handed her the watch.

It was hot. The hands spun wildly beneath the crystal,
forward and backward, too fast to read.

Elias sat on a wooden stool. He suddenly looked older.
Translucent light showed through his hands.

"I maintained more than the clock," he said. "Every keeper
does, though most never understand it fully. Bellwether was built
where lost time collects. Regrets. Delays. Words spoken too late.
Lives spent waiting for better lives. If the accumulation grows
too heavy, hours begin repeating. People forget years. Children wake
older than their parents."

Mara looked around at the countless clocks. "And this place
prevents that?"

"It gives lost time somewhere to grow."

"The garden."

He nodded. "But it needs a keeper."

"No."

"I haven't asked yet."

"You brought me into a magic garden behind a gear
and showed me your ghost. You're not building toward a
request for turnips."

"I am asking you to repair the watch, untangle the
threads, and become the next keeper."

"No."

"Mara—"

"I left because of clocks."

"You left because of me."

"Yes."

The clocks seemed to quiet.

Elias bowed his head.

Years ago, Mara had been offered a position at the
Royal Observatory in Leth, where she would have designed instruments
for measuring the movement of stars. Elias had refused to
bless the opportunity. Bellwether needed a clockmaker, he said. The
shop needed an heir. Their family had obligations.

They argued until dawn.

At the station, Mara waited for him to come. He
did not.

She boarded the train alone.

In Leth, she built instruments, married briefly, divorced quietly, and
learned that distance did not erase anger. She returned home
twice in eleven years, both times for funerals. Her father
wrote every month. She answered three letters.

"You could have told me," she said.

"I wanted you to choose freely."

"You made duty feel like a chain and called that
freedom."

"I know."

"You could have said you were proud of me."

"I was."

"You could have come to the station."

"I should have."

"You could have told me any of this before you
died."

"Yes."

Each answer was soft, with no defense in it. That
made her angrier than excuses would have.

"Why didn't you?"

Elias looked toward the glass roof. Above it, the violet
sky was darkening.

"Because I thought there would be time."

A crack appeared in the watch crystal.

Golden thread spilled over Mara's hand.

Outside, one of the blue trees bent sharply. Its glass
fruit began falling, shattering on the path. From each broken
fruit came a sound: laughter, a slammed door, a baby
crying, a final breath.

"The clock must restart before noon," Elias said.

"What happens at noon?"

"It becomes nine eleven again."

"And then?"

"Again. Eventually the town will wear through the same hour
like a foot wearing through a stair."

Mara examined the watch. "Why is this causing the blockage?"

"It contains one moment I would not release."

The back of the case had no hinge, but when
Mara pressed the engraved star, it opened.

Inside, where gears should have been, she saw Bellwether station.

Her younger self stood beside a suitcase on the platform,
looking toward the entrance. Steam gathered around her boots. The
station clock showed nine eleven.

She remembered that minute. The train doors had opened. The
conductor had called. Still she watched the entrance, certain her
father would appear.

Inside the watch, the scene repeated.

Young Mara looked up. The doors opened. The conductor called.
No one came.

Again.

Again.

Again.

"You kept this?" Mara asked.

Elias's outline flickered. "I came to the station after the
train left. I found the watch on my workbench when
I returned home. When I opened it, that minute was
inside."

"You trapped it."

"I thought if I kept it, I might someday repair
it."

"You can't repair what happened."

"No."

"You taught me that."

"I was not always an attentive student."

The ground shuddered. Several clocks fell from the walls. One
struck the floor and burst into moths.

Mara carried the watch to the worktable. Tools were laid
out there, but none were familiar. One resembled a silver
thorn. Another was a lens filled with smoke. A tiny
hammer whispered every time she touched it.

"How do I release the minute?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"You've had eleven years."

"I tried to change it. I tried to make myself
arrive. The watch rejected every false version."

"Of course it did."

"I tried to remove the memory entirely."

"And?"

"It grew roots."

Mara studied the golden threads. They did not emerge from
the trapped minute itself but from the edge of the
open case, where two tiny words had been engraved.

FOR ALWAYS.

She had written them as a promise and an accusation.

Outside, the greenhouse glass began to crack.

Mara picked up the silver thorn. Its point reflected not
her face, but the face she had worn at seventeen.

"What is this tool?"

"For separating truth from intention."

"That sounds dangerous."

"It is useless on machines and essential for people."

She inserted the thorn beneath the engraving.

The watch screamed.

Mara nearly dropped it. The station scene expanded around her,
swallowing the greenhouse.

Suddenly she stood on the old platform. Rain hissed on
the tracks. Her younger self waited by the train, thin
and furious, pretending not to be afraid.

Elias stood beside Mara, pale as smoke.

"She can't see us," he said.

Young Mara looked toward the entrance.

No one came.

"Why were you late?" Mara asked.

"I wasn't late."

"What?"

"I never intended to go."

The truth struck harder than either slap.

Elias continued, his voice rough. "I believed that if I
said goodbye, I was giving permission. I thought withholding myself
might keep you home."

Mara watched her younger self's hope collapse by degrees.

"That was cruel."

"Yes."

"I needed you."

"Yes."

"I waited until the last second."

"I know."

The conductor called, "All aboard!"

The younger Mara lifted her suitcase.

Beside the station entrance stood a shadow Mara had never
noticed. Not Elias as he had been that day, but
Elias as he was now: an old man at the
end of his life, watching what he had failed to
do.

"You put yourself in the memory," Mara said.

"I've been standing here for years."

"Punishing yourself?"

"Waiting."

"For what?"

"For you to stop waiting for me."

The train doors began to close.

Mara understood then. The trapped minute did not belong only
to her father. A part of her had remained on
the platform too, forever watching the entrance, forever demanding that
the past become kinder before she allowed herself to leave.

She stepped toward her younger self.

The girl turned.

For one impossible instant, they saw each other.

"Is he coming?" the girl asked.

Mara's throat tightened.

"No."

The girl looked down.

"But you should go," Mara said. "The world is larger
than his fear. Larger than yours."

"Will I forgive him?"

"Not for a long time."

"Will it hurt?"

"Yes."

"Then why go?"

Mara glanced at Elias. Light passed through him now. Beyond
the station walls, she could hear the greenhouse clocks breaking
one after another.

"Because pain is not a place to live," she said.

The girl considered this. Then she climbed aboard.

The doors closed.

The train pulled away.

As it gathered speed, the station clock moved from nine
eleven to nine twelve.

The memory dissolved.

Mara found herself back at the worktable. The pocket watch
lay open before her. Its gears had returned. The golden
threads were gray and brittle.

She lifted one with the tweezers. It crumbled to dust.

Throughout the greenhouse, clocks began to ring.

Elias smiled.

"Is that all?" Mara asked. "I fix your mistake and
inherit your prison?"

"The garden is not a prison."

"You never left."

"I was afraid to."

"Of what?"

"That another keeper would choose differently."

"Differently from what?"

"From me."

Mara looked at the clocks, the tree, the strange tools.
"You wanted a copy of yourself."

"For a long time."

"And now?"

"Now I would prefer you."

A wind moved through the greenhouse. Elias's coat fluttered, though
he had become almost transparent.

"Must the keeper stay in Bellwether?" Mara asked.

"No."

"Must the keeper repair clocks?"

"Not necessarily."

"Must the shop remain a shop?"

He frowned. "What else would it be?"

"A school. A workshop. A place where anyone can learn
to mend things."

"Anyone?"

"Anyone."

"That sounds disorderly."

"It will be."

He considered this with visible discomfort. Then he nodded.

A bell began to toll in the distance.

Once.

Twice.

The sound came through the open gate from the tower.

"Time to go," Elias said.

Mara knew he did not mean back to the mechanism
room.

There were many things she could have said. Most were
too late. Some were still too sharp. The right words
did not arrive, and for once she refused to wait
for them.

She embraced him.

At first he stood stiffly, as he had whenever she
hugged him as a child. Then his arms closed around
her.

"I am proud of you," he said.

"You are extremely late."

"I know."

At the eleventh bell, he vanished.

At the twelfth, Mara stood alone.

The garden brightened. New leaves unfolded on the pear tree,
each shaped like the hands of a clock. The repaired
watch ticked on the table.

Mara carried it back along the path. The silver grass
chimed. In the blue trees, fresh minutes ripened: Tomas dropping
a loaf and catching it with his foot, Mayor Vale
practicing a speech to an empty mirror, a child discovering
a red beetle beneath a stone.

At the gate, Mara looked back.

The garden no longer seemed like a secret built to
imprison her. It seemed unfinished.

She stepped through.

The tower mechanism roared to life.

Gears turned. Cables tightened. The pendulum swung. Across the town,
the clock hands jumped from nine eleven to twelve, and
the great bell finished striking noon.

When Mara descended, the square erupted in applause.

Mayor Vale pushed through the crowd. "What took so long?"

Mara looked at the clock. "Three minutes."

"It has been nearly three hours!"

"That depends where you were standing."

"Is it repaired?"

"For now."

"For now?"

"All clocks stop eventually."

Mayor Vale blanched, perhaps imagining the governor's secretary trapped in
an hourless town.

Mara walked past him.

At the bakery, Tomas handed her a warm roll without
asking for payment. She ate it on the way home.

The beans had not yet sprouted beside her father's grave.
Of course they had not. Some things required more than
one morning.

Mara unlocked the shop, opened every curtain, and carried the
unfinished clocks into the sunlight. She took down the old
sign that read ELIAS VENN, CLOCKMAKER.

By evening, she had painted a new one.

THE GARDEN WORKSHOP
REPAIRS TAUGHT
QUESTIONS WELCOME
TIME GIVEN FREELY

The next morning, three children arrived with a broken music
box, a cracked compass, and a wooden bird missing one
wing. Tomas came carrying a kitchen timer that rang whenever
it pleased. An old woman brought a watch that had
belonged to her wife and asked if it could be
made to run again.

Mara examined each object carefully.

Some she repaired. Some she could not. For the wooden
bird, she carved a new wing unlike the first, because
matching wood no longer existed. The child complained until the
bird was wound and began hopping in bright, uneven circles.

Behind the shop, white beans broke through the soil.

They climbed all summer.

And sometimes, when the town was quiet and the light
struck the shop wall at a certain angle, a door
appeared behind the shelves. Mara would take the brass watch
from her pocket, open the door, and walk beneath the
violet sky.

She never kept the garden as her father had.

She moved paths. She pruned the blue trees. She built
benches where people could sit with the minutes they had
lost. She opened other gates in distant cities, in observatories,
train stations, kitchens, and hospital corridors. She taught those who
entered that time could be measured but not owned, remembered
but not repaired.

Years later, when Mara's hair had turned silver and children
she had taught were teaching children of their own, the
town clock stopped again.

No one panicked.

Bakers checked their bread. Farmers studied the sun. Lovers remained
beneath blankets. The mayor, a cheerful woman with no silver
chain, hung a sign on the tower door:

THE CLOCK IS RESTING. SO MAY YOU.

Mara climbed the stairs with a young apprentice beside her.

Behind the mechanism, golden thread glimmered.

The apprentice stared. "What is that?"

"A complication," Mara said.

"Can we fix it?"

Mara smiled and handed her the key.

"We can try."

# The Cartographer of Lost Roads

Every morning, before the bells of Saint Orra struck six,
Elian Voss walked through the eastern gate and searched for
roads that no longer existed.

He carried a leather map case, three sticks of blue
chalk, a compass inherited from his mother, and a small
brass wheel that clicked when rolled across the ground. The
guards had long ago stopped asking where he was going.
They knew he would return by sunset with dust on
his boots and disappointment folded into the lines of his
face.

Elian was the last cartographer employed by the city of
Bellweather. Once, there had been twelve of them. They had
mapped trade routes, rivers, orchards, fortifications, and the changing borders
of the marsh. But merchants now used mechanical compasses, soldiers
had their own surveyors, and the marsh had swallowed every
village worth naming.

Only Elian remained, and his task was one most people
considered ceremonial.

He mapped lost roads.

A lost road was not merely abandoned. An abandoned road
could still be found beneath weeds or mud. Its stones
remained; its destination could be reached. A lost road was
different. It vanished entirely, sometimes overnight, leaving no ditch, milestone,
or memory of where it had led.

Bellweather had once stood at the center of seven great
roads. Three remained.

The North Road climbed toward the mines. The River Road
followed the water south. The King's Road stretched west through
fields of pale barley.

The other four had disappeared.

One had led to the sea, though no ocean lay
within five hundred miles. One had led to a forest
where the trees grew silver leaves. One had led to
the birthplace of Saint Orra. The last, according to Elian's
mother, had led home.

"Home from where?" he had asked as a child.

"From wherever you've gone," she had replied.

She had been Bellweather's finest cartographer. She could judge distance
by birdsong, find north in a windowless room, and draw
a coastline while standing on a rocking boat. When Elian
was twelve, she set out to map the fourth lost
road.

She never returned.

Twenty-four years later, Elian still carried her compass.

It was an unreliable object. Its needle did not point
north. At times it pointed toward a particular chimney, an
empty doorway, or a patch of sky. For seven years
after his mother vanished, it had pointed east. Then, one
winter morning, it swung south and remained there for a
decade. Now it trembled constantly, as if uncertain.

The city paid Elian because an old law required it.
"So long as any road from Bellweather remains unaccounted for,"
the law declared, "one cartographer shall seek it." The city
council had tried twice to repeal the law, but each
attempt ended with every door in the council chamber opening
at once. Even on windless days. Even after the doors
were nailed shut.

Bellweather was practical, but not foolish.

So Elian searched.

He measured the old fields east of the walls. He
questioned shepherds and ditch diggers. He followed rumors from children,
drunkards, and people who had recently awakened from fever. Such
people were considered the most reliable witnesses. Lost roads rarely
appeared to anyone certain of where they were.

For years, Elian found nothing.

Then, on the first morning of autumn, he discovered a
milestone in the middle of a wheat field.

It stood waist-high, clean and white among the golden stalks.
Elian knew the field well. He had crossed it the
previous evening. There had been no stone.

One side bore an arrow.

Beneath the arrow was a single carved word:

ELSEWHERE.

Elian touched the letters. They were warm.

He took out his notebook and recorded the time, weather,
position of the sun, wind direction, soil condition, and number
of visible crows. Then he rolled the brass wheel around
the milestone three times.

The wheel clicked seventy-seven times on each circuit, though the
distance should have produced forty-two.

Elian's heart began to beat faster.

He raised his mother's compass.

The needle pointed directly at the stone.

Behind him, wheat rustled.

Elian turned. A girl stood several paces away, though he
had heard no footsteps.

She looked about fifteen. Her black hair had been cut
unevenly with a knife, and she wore a red coat
far too large for her. Around her neck hung a
house key on a piece of string.

"You took your time," she said.

Elian glanced across the field. "Who are you?"

"Nia."

"How did you get here?"

"By road."

"There is no road."

"There wasn't."

Elian looked again at the milestone. "What lies elsewhere?"

Nia shrugged. "Mostly other places."

"Did you place this stone?"

"No. It grows when the road is ready."

"Stones do not grow."

"Neither do cities, but people say they do."

Elian studied her boots. They were coated in gray dust
unlike the dark soil of Bellweather. "Where are you from?"

"I'm not."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

She stepped past him and pressed her palm to the
milestone. The wheat bent away from her in two smooth
waves. Between them appeared a narrow road paved with blue
stones.

It had not been hidden beneath the crop. Elian would
have seen the unevenness in the land. Yet there it
was, extending east toward the horizon.

His compass needle held steady.

Nia started down the road.

"Wait," Elian called. "Where does it lead?"

She looked over her shoulder. "You know."

He did not know. But the compass pulsed in his
hand like a second heart.

Elian thought of his mother closing the buckles of her
map case on the morning she disappeared. She had kissed
his forehead, told him to keep the kettle warm, and
walked east beneath a sky the color of iron.

He had kept the kettle warm for three days.

"Did a woman come this way?" he asked. "Years ago.
Tall, dark-haired, carrying a green map case?"

"Lots of people come this way."

"Her name was Mara Voss."

Nia's expression changed. It was only a flicker, but Elian
saw it.

"You know her."

"I know the name," she said.

"Is she alive?"

Nia faced the eastern horizon. "That depends on which road
we take."

Then she continued walking.

Elian followed.

At first, the road passed through familiar country. They crossed
the wheat field, descended a shallow hill, and entered an
orchard belonging to a farmer named Pell. Elian had surveyed
the property six times. It contained four hundred and eight
apple trees.

Today it contained four hundred and nine.

The last tree stood in the center of the blue
road. Its branches were heavy with small white fruit.

Nia picked an apple.

"That tree wasn't here yesterday," Elian said.

"It might say the same about you."

She bit into the fruit. Its flesh was deep red.

Elian did not take one.

Beyond the orchard, the road entered a valley that should
not have existed.

Its slopes rose steeply on either side, covered in purple
grass. Hundreds of glass bottles hung from wooden posts. They
rang softly in the wind, each producing a different note.

Elian stopped and looked behind him.

The orchard was gone. The blue road curved through a
wall of mist.

He took out his notebook.

"You can't map it like that," Nia said.

"I can map anything with dimensions."

"It doesn't always have dimensions."

"Everything has dimensions."

"Regret doesn't."

"Regret is not a place."

Nia smiled sadly. "You haven't traveled much."

He ignored her and began measuring the road. It was
nine feet wide. Then eleven. Then four. The brass wheel
clicked backward when he rolled it east and whispered when
he rolled it west.

He tried to sketch the valley, but each line faded
from the page.

"What are those bottles?" he asked.

"Words people wanted to say."

Elian approached one. It was green, stoppered with wax, and
smaller than his thumb. A voice murmured inside it.

He leaned close.

"I forgive you," the bottle whispered.

Another said, "Please stay."

A third contained only laughter.

"Who collects them?"

"The road."

"Why?"

"Roads collect what travelers drop."

Elian looked down at the stones beneath his feet. Between
them lay buttons, coins, broken combs, scraps of letters, and
a child's wooden tooth. He wondered what his mother might
have dropped.

They walked until the valley narrowed into a gorge. There,
they found a tollhouse.

It was a crooked building with walls made from old
doors. Behind its window sat a blind man wearing a
uniform from no country Elian recognized.

"Toll," the man said.

Nia placed her white apple on the counter.

The blind man accepted it and nodded.

Elian reached for his purse.

"No money," said the collector. "This road has no use
for money."

"What does it require?"

"Something you know to be true."

Elian frowned. "Bellweather lies west of here."

The collector tilted his head. "Do you know that?"

Elian glanced behind him at the mist.

"My name is Elian Voss."

"Names are borrowed."

"I am thirty-six years old."

"Not on this road."

Elian grew impatient. "What kind of truth do you want?"

"One that costs you."

The bottles in the valley chimed.

Elian looked at his mother's compass. He remembered waiting beside
the kettle. He remembered inventing reasons for her delay: rain,
illness, a broken bridge. After a week, he told himself
she had found the road and could not return. After
a year, he told himself she was still searching.

After twenty-four years, he still refused the simplest answer.

"My mother chose the road over me," he said.

The words hurt more once spoken.

The collector held out his hand. A small dark object
rested in his palm, no larger than a seed. Elian
understood that it had come from him.

"Toll accepted," the man said.

The gate opened.

Beyond the gorge lay a city.

Its towers leaned at impossible angles, connected by bridges that
curved through the air. Boats sailed along canals suspended above
the streets. Clocks crowded every wall, but no two displayed
the same hour.

People filled the avenues: a soldier carrying flowers, women in
antlered masks, children with paper wings, merchants leading animals made
of smoke. Some moved normally. Others walked backward. A few
stood still while the city flowed around them.

"What is this place?" Elian asked.

"Orison," Nia replied.

"I've never seen it on a map."

"You have. There was a blank space in the corner."

"That is not how maps work."

"It's how this one works."

They descended into the city. The blue road twisted through
markets and courtyards, sometimes climbing staircases, sometimes passing through buildings.
No one seemed surprised by it.

Elian attempted to record their route, but his notes rearranged
themselves. Distances became questions. Street names turned into dates. One
page filled with a detailed map of his childhood bedroom.

At last he shut the notebook.

Nia led him to a square where hundreds of roads
met beneath a stone arch. Some were paved with gold,
some with bones, some with rainwater. One road was no
more than a line of sunlight.

Travelers gathered beneath signs bearing destinations:

THE PLACE YOU ALMOST REACHED

THE LAST DAY OF SUMMER

A COUNTRY WITHOUT KINGS

BEFORE

HOME

Elian stared at the final sign.

Beneath it waited a narrow dirt path bordered by lanterns.
It looked ordinary. That frightened him more than anything else
he had seen.

"Is that the road my mother took?" he asked.

"Partly."

"What does that mean?"

"All roads become other roads."

Nia sat on the edge of a dry fountain. "Mara
came to Orison twenty-four years ago. She was looking for
the road called Home."

"Did she find it?"

"Yes."

"Then why didn't she return?"

"She misunderstood the destination."

Elian's jaw tightened. "Take me to her."

"I can take you to where she is. I can't
promise what that means."

"Alive or dead?"

"Those are local words."

He seized Nia's arm. "Enough riddles."

Around them, the square fell silent. Even the clocks seemed
to pause.

Nia did not pull away. "Lost roads exist because direct
answers are sometimes too cruel to travel."

Elian released her.

She rubbed her arm, then pointed toward a staircase descending
beneath the fountain.

"That way."

The staircase wound deep under Orison. The air smelled of
wet stone and rosemary. At the bottom stood a railway
platform illuminated by a single lamp.

There were no tracks.

A woman sat on a bench with a green map
case beside her.

Elian stopped breathing.

She looked exactly as she had on the morning she
left: forty years old, tall, dark-haired, wearing a gray traveling
cloak. Not a day older.

"Mara," Nia said.

The woman rose.

Her gaze moved from Nia to Elian. Confusion crossed her
face, followed by recognition so sudden it seemed to wound
her.

"Elian?"

He had imagined this moment a thousand times. In some
versions he embraced her. In others he shouted. Sometimes he
turned away.

He did none of those things.

He said, "The kettle boiled dry."

Mara covered her mouth.

"You were twelve," she whispered.

"I was."

"But—" She looked toward the staircase. "How long?"

"Twenty-four years."

Her knees weakened, and she sat again.

Nia moved away to the far end of the platform.

Mara stared at Elian's face, studying the years she had
missed. "I thought it had been three days."

"I know."

"No, you don't. I found the road, but it divided.
Every turn led to another version of Bellweather. In one,
the city had burned. In another, you had never been
born. In another, I returned before I left. I kept
trying to find the right one."

"You found it now."

"I don't know that."

Elian took out her compass. The needle pointed to her.

Mara reached for it, then stopped. "You kept this."

"It was the only map you left me."

Tears filled her eyes. "I was trying to come home."

"You were trying to solve the road."

Her face tightened.

Elian heard again the truth he had paid at the
tollhouse: My mother chose the road over me.

Mara looked at the green case beside her. "I believed
if I understood it, no one would ever be lost
again."

"And how many people became lost while you were understanding?"

She lowered her head.

The platform remained silent. Far above, the clocks of Orison
resumed their separate hours.

Elian sat beside her.

For a while, neither spoke.

Finally Mara asked, "What became of you?"

He told her.

He spoke of the woman next door who fed him
for the first winter. Of the cartographers who trained him
because they pitied him. Of his first map, his first
expedition, and the years spent searching the eastern fields. He
told her about his brief marriage to a musician named
Sella, who had left because Elian watched every road more
closely than he watched her. He told her about his
father's death, the closing of the mapmakers' hall, and the
four hundred and ninth apple tree.

Mara listened without interruption.

When he finished, she looked old, though her face had
not changed.

"I cannot repair that," she said.

"No."

"I cannot give it back."

"No."

"I am sorry."

It was not enough. It was only everything she had.

Elian looked down the empty railway line. "Can you return
to Bellweather?"

"I tried. The road rejects me."

"Why?"

"Because I no longer know where home is."

He thought of the sign in the square and his
mother's old answer.

Home from wherever you've gone.

Perhaps she had been wrong. A road could lead to
a house, a city, a room with a kettle. It
could not make the traveler belong there.

"Come with me anyway," he said.

Mara shook her head. "If I take the wrong road
again, I might lose another lifetime."

"Then we choose one and accept where it goes."

"You sound like your father."

"I barely remember him."

"That was his great wisdom. He was always getting lost
and pretending it was philosophy."

Elian laughed.

It surprised him. It surprised Mara too. For one instant,
the railway platform felt almost like a kitchen from long
ago.

A wind rose in the tunnel.

Nia returned. "The road is moving."

"What does that mean?" Elian asked.

"It means decide."

The platform began to fade at its edges. Beyond the
lamp, darkness opened like a door.

Mara clutched her map case. "There is one route that
may return us to Bellweather, but it demands a guide."

"I am a cartographer."

"That isn't the same."

"It will have to be."

Nia shook her head. "A guide can't be going home.
A guide has to stay with the road."

Elian understood before Mara did.

"No," Mara said. "There must be another way."

"There are always other ways," Nia replied. "There is not
always more time."

The floor beneath them had become transparent. Roads glimmered below
it, crossing and tangling like threads. Elian saw deserts, mountains,
crowded stations, empty nurseries, battlefields, and moonlit seas. Every road
carried someone who believed they knew where they were going.

"Why did you bring me here?" he asked Nia.

"Because the road chose you."

"Roads do not choose."

Nia raised an eyebrow.

Elian almost smiled.

He looked at his mother. She had searched for twenty-four
years without aging. He had waited twenty-four years and become
someone she did not know. Between them lay an absence
no road could erase.

But perhaps a road could still carry them forward.

"If I stay," he said, "can she return?"

Nia nodded. "You can guide her to the eastern field.
After that, the road will close."

"And me?"

"You'll map what can't be mapped."

Mara gripped his hands. "I won't let you trade your
life for mine."

"I'm not twelve anymore."

"That doesn't make you less my son."

"No. It makes the choice mine."

She began to protest, but he stopped her.

"All my life, I have searched for this road. If
I go back now, I'll continue searching. Maybe not for
you. For an answer. For a reason. For proof that
none of it was wasted."

"Was it?"

"Yes," he said. "Some of it."

The honesty passed between them without cruelty.

"Then come home," she whispered. "Stop searching."

Elian looked at the countless roads beneath his feet.

For years, he had believed he wanted his mother back.
Standing beside her, he understood that what he had truly
wanted was the past corrected. No road led there. Even
in Orison, even among paths to Before and Almost, time
could not be repaired. It could only be exchanged for
another kind of loss.

He handed Mara the brass compass.

The needle spun once and pointed upward.

"Follow it," he said.

She pressed the compass to her chest. "What will I
tell them?"

"Tell the council the fourth road has been found."

"And where does it lead?"

Elian considered the question.

"Onward."

The platform vanished.

For a breath, they stood among stars and blue stones.
Then a road unfolded beneath Mara's feet, running through the
bottle valley, the orchard, and the eastern wheat field. At
its far end, Bellweather's walls shone in the evening light.

Mara embraced him.

This time he let himself become twelve. He held tightly
to her cloak and breathed in rosemary, dust, and the
faint smoke of the old kitchen. He wanted to say
he forgave her. He wanted the words to be true.

They were not, not yet.

So he said, "Goodbye."

Mara stepped onto the road.

With every pace she aged. Silver entered her hair. Lines
formed around her eyes. Her shoulders bent beneath the years
returning to her. She did not look back until she
reached the milestone.

By then she was sixty-four.

Elian lifted a hand.

She did the same.

Then the wheat closed behind her.

Orison rose around him once more.

Nia stood beneath the archway, holding out a roll of
blank paper.

"I thought the road chose me," Elian said. "Why are
you here?"

"It chose me last time."

"What happens to you now?"

"I get to find out where I'm from."

She removed the key from around her neck and placed
it in his hand. It was warm, like the milestone.

"Does this open something?"

"Probably."

"What?"

She grinned. "You ask too many direct questions."

Nia chose a road marked SOMEWHERE SMALL AND KIND. As
she stepped onto it, her red coat became a scatter
of autumn leaves. Then she was gone.

Elian stood alone in the square of roads.

He unrolled the paper. At first it was blank. Then
a thin blue line appeared at its center. It curved
toward the edge, divided, circled back, and continued beyond the
page.

He took a pencil from his coat.

Years later, travelers told stories of a man who waited
where lost roads crossed.

He gave directions to cities that had vanished, houses not
yet built, and rooms remembered only in dreams. He warned
travelers that the shortest road was rarely the kindest. He
taught them to measure distance in promises, danger in silences,
and time in the number of names they had forgotten.

Some said he never aged. Others claimed they met him
first as an old man and again, decades later, as
a boy. He always carried a map case. He always
asked where they had started, though he seldom asked where
they were going.

And in Bellweather, on the first morning of every autumn,
Mara Voss walked beyond the eastern gate to a wheat
field where a white milestone had once stood.

She brought two cups and a small iron kettle.

She lit a fire at sunrise. She boiled water and
made tea, pouring one cup for herself and one for
the son who had found her.

The second cup always remained untouched.

But it never grew cold.

# The Keeper of Lost Hours

On the morning the town lost three o'clock, Elias Vale
was repairing a watch that had belonged to a dead
woman.

It was a delicate silver thing, no wider than a
walnut, with a painted bluebird on its porcelain face. The
bird's wings spread from nine to two, as if it
were forever trying to lift the hands into flight. The
watch had not ticked in forty-seven years.

Elias knew this because the dead woman's daughter had told
him so.

"It stopped the moment my mother fell from the orchard
wall," Mrs. Wren had said, placing it carefully on his
counter. "My father never had it repaired. He said some
hours ought to remain where they died."

Elias had heard similar sentiments before. People liked to believe
clocks remembered. They brought him watches dropped in rivers, watches
carried through wars, watches found beneath floorboards with dust packed
into their gears. They told him where each had stopped
and what had happened at that exact minute. A birth.
A farewell. A murder. A kiss.

Elias listened politely, but he knew clocks remembered nothing. Their
gears caught, their springs snapped, their oil dried. Grief happened
elsewhere.

He bent over the bluebird watch with a magnifying lens
fixed to his eye. Outside, rain polished the steep streets
of Bellweather. Across the square, the clock tower struck twice.

Then it struck four.

Elias looked up.

For several seconds, he heard only the small noises of
his shop: rain tapping the window, tools settling in their
wooden tray, the faint grind of the regulator clock on
the wall.

He glanced at that clock.

Its minute hand stood at twelve. Its hour hand stood
at four.

"That cannot be right," he murmured.

He checked the marine chronometer beneath its glass dome. Four
o'clock.

The grandfather clock by the door gave a low, uncertain
groan and chimed four times.

Elias opened the shop door.

All around the square, people had stepped into the rain.
Shopkeepers, porters, clerks, schoolchildren, and two constables stood looking up
at the tower. No one spoke at first. They seemed
embarrassed, as if each feared that everyone else had heard
a different number.

At last the baker shouted, "Wasn't it just two?"

A dozen voices answered at once.

"Three," said one woman.

"It was nearly three."

"I was due at the bank."

"My soup has burned!"

"Your soup cannot burn in a missing hour," someone replied.

The tower clock showed four exactly.

A flock of pigeons burst from the roof, wheeling through
the rain. Elias watched them circle the tower and felt,
for the first time in many years, something close to
fear.

He went back inside and locked the door.

Every clock in his shop had lost the same hour.

Not stopped. Not jumped through it. Lost it.

Elias examined the regulator first. Its mechanism was flawless. The
escapement ticked evenly. The weights had fallen the proper distance
for three hours since he had wound them at noon,
yet the face insisted that four hours had passed.

He checked his records. At two fifty-eight, according to his
notebook, he had removed the balance wheel from Mrs. Wren's
watch. The next line, written in the same ink, read:

4:01 — balance wheel intact; cause of failure unknown.

Elias stared at the line.

He did not remember writing it.

His handwriting was normally narrow and severe. This line slanted
upward, almost playfully. Beneath it appeared a second sentence:

Do not repair the bluebird.

A knock rattled the door.

Elias covered the notebook and opened it to find his
eleven-year-old niece, Mara, dripping on the step. Her red hair
clung to her cheeks, and she held one boot in
her hand.

"Uncle," she said, breathless, "I know where three o'clock went."

Mara had lived with Elias since her parents died at
sea six years earlier. She was observant, untidy, impatient with
rules, and entirely unimpressed by clocks. Elias had tried to
teach her the trade, but she disliked any machine that
behaved predictably.

"Put your boot on," he said.

"There's a door in the tower."

"There are several."

"A new door."

Elias looked past her. People still clustered in the square,
arguing about appointments and wages. The rain had thickened, turning
the tower into a black column against the clouds.

"How do you know it is new?"

"Because it has my name on it."

Elias went very still.

Mara reached into her coat and produced a brass key.

"I found this in my pocket when it became four,"
she said. "I didn't put it there."

The key was long and green with age. Its bow
had been fashioned in the shape of an eye. Along
its stem, in letters so small that Elias needed his
lens to read them, was an inscription:

FOR THE HOUR THAT KNOWS YOU.

Elias closed his hand around it.

"When did you go to the tower?"

"Just now. I was coming home from school when the
clocks changed. I saw a light under a door beside
the north stair. It said MARA VALE across the wood."

"You did not open it?"

"I didn't have the key then. I found it afterward."

"After what?"

Mara looked down at her boot.

"After I heard Mother calling from behind it."

The rain seemed to recede, though Elias could still see
it striking the glass.

Mara had been five when the Sea Star sank in
winter waters. No bodies were recovered. Elias remembered her waiting
at the harbor for weeks, certain that any distant sail
would bring her parents home.

"You know it could not have been her," he said
gently.

"I know what dead means."

"Mara—"

"She called me Little Minnow. No one else called me
that."

Elias looked at the bluebird watch lying open on his
bench. Its frozen hands pointed to two fifty-nine. He thought
of the sentence in his notebook.

Do not repair the bluebird.

He slipped the brass key into his pocket.

"We are going to the tower," he said.

The town clock had stood in Bellweather for nearly three
hundred years. Its builders were forgotten, although their carved faces
peered from the stonework beneath the eaves. Elias had maintained
its mechanism since his apprenticeship. He knew every stair, axle,
rope, and counterweight.

Yet the door Mara showed him had not been there
the day before.

It stood in a curved wall halfway up the tower,
squeezed between two narrow windows. The wood was pale and
smooth, untouched by dust. MARA VALE had been cut into
it at a child's eye level.

Elias ran his fingers across the letters.

They changed beneath his hand.

ELIAS VALE.

Mara stepped back. "It didn't do that before."

From the other side came a soft knock.

Three times.

Elias held his breath.

A voice said, "Ellie?"

No one had called him Ellie since his sister Lena
died.

Mara gripped his sleeve. "That's Mother."

Elias felt the tower sway, though he knew it was
only his knees. Lena's voice had been deeper than Mara's,
warm and amused, always on the edge of laughter. This
voice was exactly so.

"Ellie," it called again. "Open the door. We haven't much
time."

He drew out the key.

"Uncle," Mara whispered, "you said it couldn't be her."

"It cannot."

"But you're opening it."

"Yes."

The key entered the lock without resistance. When Elias turned
it, every bell in the tower trembled.

The door opened onto sunlight.

Beyond the threshold lay no hidden chamber, but a summer
field under a golden sky. Tall grass bent in a
wind that did not touch the tower. Somewhere nearby, water
rushed over stones.

Lena stood a few steps away.

She was twenty-nine, the age she had been when the
sea took her. Her red hair was braided over one
shoulder. She wore a yellow dress Elias remembered from a
family picnic, though he had not thought of it in
years.

Beside her stood Tomas, Mara's father, smiling with tears in
his eyes.

Mara made a sound like a sob caught inside a
laugh.

Lena raised one hand. "Stay where you are."

Mara froze at the threshold.

Elias examined the impossible landscape. The sunlight was too soft.
The river repeated the same three notes. A white butterfly
crossed behind Lena, vanished in the grass, then crossed behind
her again.

"What is this place?" he asked.

"Three o'clock," Lena replied.

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

She looked at Mara, and her careful smile broke. "Oh,
my little minnow."

Mara began to cry.

Tomas stepped forward, but Lena caught his arm. "No closer.
The door chooses what it shows. It also chooses what
it keeps."

Elias tightened his grip on the key. "Why was the
hour taken?"

"Not taken. Hidden."

"By whom?"

Lena looked beyond them, toward the unseen mechanism above. "The
Keeper. She has been gathering lost hours since the first
clock was wound. Usually she takes only what people surrender—a
minute forgotten in sleep, an afternoon blurred by fever, the
last hour of a life. But she is dying, Elias.
Her machinery is failing. Today she took an entire hour
from everyone in Bellweather to hold her house together."

"Then we must restore it."

"If you do, this door will close."

Mara wiped her face with both hands. "Will I ever
see you again?"

Lena's eyes filled with pain. "I don't know."

"Are you truly my mother?"

"I am the part of her that remains in this
hour."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No," Lena said. "It isn't."

A deep clang sounded above. The field shivered. For an
instant, Lena became transparent, and behind her Elias saw an
immense darkness filled with pendulums.

"Go to the clock room," she said quickly. "Find the
stair behind the bell frame. Take the bluebird watch."

Elias stared at her. "How do you know about it?"

"Because it was made by the Keeper."

The vision began to dim. Tomas stepped close to Lena
and placed his hand over hers.

"Please," Mara said. "Just one more minute."

"We have an hour," Lena told her. "But the hour
is ending."

The door swung shut.

Mara struck it with both fists. "No!"

Elias seized the handle, but the lock would not turn.
The letters on the wood had disappeared.

Above them, the clock mechanism gave another shuddering clang.

They ran.

The main clock room was a forest of iron. Gears
taller than Elias turned around the central axle, while ropes
descended through holes in the floor to the weights below.
The pendulum swept back and forth with a heartbeat's solemn
rhythm.

Behind the bell frame, where Lena had said, Elias found
a spiral staircase.

It was made of bone.

Mara stared at it. "Was that here before?"

"No."

"Good," she said shakily. "I'm glad you're finally admitting when
things are strange."

They climbed.

The stairs twisted far beyond the tower's height. Soon the
bells vanished below, but still the staircase continued upward through
darkness. Small lights floated around them. When Elias looked closely,
he saw scenes inside each one.

A man stood at a railway platform, deciding whether to
board.

A girl leaned toward another girl beneath a flowering tree.

An old soldier slept in a chair while morning brightened
the room.

"Memories?" Mara asked.

"Hours," said a voice overhead.

A woman waited on the next landing.

She was extremely old and unusually tall. Her gray dress
hung in layers, each covered with clock hands that crawled
like insects across the cloth. Her white hair drifted around
her as though she stood underwater.

Where her eyes should have been, two tiny clock faces
ticked.

"The Keeper," Elias said.

"The repairman," she answered. Her voice contained the sound of
distant bells. "You took your time."

"You stole it."

"I borrowed what your town wastes."

Mara stepped between them. "Give it back."

The Keeper tilted her head. The clocks in her eyes
spun. "Children always imagine time belongs to them."

"Doesn't it?"

"No. But that has never stopped anyone from spending it."

The landing opened behind her into a vast hall crowded
with clocks. Some were grand and gilded; others were cheap
alarm clocks, sundials, hourglasses, water clocks, and devices Elias could
not recognize. Between them hung thousands of doors.

Behind every door, someone was laughing, weeping, singing, or saying
goodbye.

Elias heard Lena's voice among them.

"What do you want with the bluebird watch?" he asked.

The Keeper held out her hand.

He had left the watch in his shop.

At least, he believed he had.

Yet when he reached into his coat, his fingers closed
around cool silver.

"Do not give it to her," Mara said.

The Keeper's expression did not change. "Then Bellweather will remain
broken. Tomorrow it will lose noon. The next day, midnight.
Soon babies will be born older than their mothers, bread
will rot before it is baked, and the dead will
arrive early for their funerals."

"Why does that watch matter?" Elias asked.

"Because it contains the first hour I ever kept."

The bluebird's frozen hands still pointed to two fifty-nine.

The Keeper moved closer.

"Long ago, I was human," she said. "I made clocks
in a village whose name has been forgotten. I had
a daughter who climbed an orchard wall while I worked.
When she fell, I begged the world for one more
hour with her. The world refused."

Elias remembered Mrs. Wren's story.

"My daughter made that watch," the Keeper continued. "When she
died, it stopped. I discovered I could enter the hour
trapped inside it. So I built this place around that
moment. In time, other lost hours came to me."

"You built a prison," Elias said.

"A refuge."

"For whom?"

For the first time, the Keeper looked uncertain.

A crack split the darkness overhead. Several floating lights went
out. From far below came the violent grinding of the
tower clock.

The Keeper swayed.

"I cannot hold the house much longer," she said. "The
watch is broken. Repair it, and I can return your
hour."

"And then?"

"And then I continue."

"Taking pieces of people's lives?"

"Preserving them."

Mara looked at the countless doors. "Do the people inside
know they're dead?"

"They know only their hour."

"My mother does."

"Your mother is recent. The recent dead are troublesome."

"She said you were dying."

The Keeper's clock eyes slowed.

"All keepers die," she said. "That is why another must
take the key."

Elias felt the brass key grow heavy in his pocket.

"You chose Mara," he said.

"I chose a child who wanted the past more than
the future."

Mara went pale.

"And when the door opened," the Keeper continued, "I found
a man who had wanted nothing for so long that
he mistook emptiness for peace."

Elias flinched.

The hall of clocks ticked around him. For years after
Lena's death, he had lived by schedule alone. Wake at
six. Open at eight. Lunch at noon. Close at seven.
Wind the clocks, oil the gears, sleep without dreams. He
had cared for Mara dutifully, reliably, and at a distance.
He had believed this was strength.

"What happens if I repair the watch?" he asked.

"The house survives. The doors remain. One of you becomes
Keeper when I am gone."

"And if I do not?"

"The first hour ends. Without it, the others will be
released."

"Released where?"

"Where all hours go."

The Keeper spoke the words with terror.

Mara took Elias's hand. "Uncle, the town needs its hour."

"Yes."

"But Mother is in here."

"Yes."

"If we break it, I lose her again."

He looked at his niece. He wished there were a
mechanism to examine, a cracked jewel or bent tooth he
could show her. He wished grief were a technical problem.

Instead, he knelt and placed the bluebird watch in her
palm.

"The dead are not kept by clocks," he said.

"You don't know that."

"No. I don't."

The honesty surprised them both.

Mara's fingers closed around the watch. "What would Mother choose?"

From somewhere among the doors, Lena called her name.

Mara shut her eyes.

Then she handed the watch back to Elias.

"She'd choose me," she whispered. "Even if I hated her
for it."

The Keeper rushed forward.

Her long fingers closed around Elias's wrist, cold as metal.
"Repair it!"

The hall changed around him.

Suddenly he stood in his childhood kitchen at sunset. Lena,
nine years old, sat at the table stealing raisins from
a bowl. His father hummed by the stove. His mother,
dead for thirty years, turned and smiled.

"Ellie," she said, "you're home."

His heart broke open.

He smelled cinnamon, wood smoke, rain drying on wool. Everything
was exactly as it had been. The chipped green plates.
The loose floorboard. Lena's ink-stained fingers.

Here was the hour he had wanted without knowing he
wanted it.

The Keeper's voice whispered behind him.

"You see? Nothing loved needs to be lost."

His mother opened her arms.

Elias almost stepped toward her.

Then he noticed the rain on the window. The same
drop slid down the same pane again and again. His
father hummed the same four notes. Lena lifted a raisin,
ate it, and lifted it once more.

Not memory.

Machinery.

Elias opened his hand. The bluebird watch lay against his
palm. Through the case, he felt one faint pulse.

He removed his magnifying lens and drove its sharp metal
edge into the porcelain face.

The bluebird cracked in two.

The Keeper screamed.

The kitchen vanished. The great hall folded inward. Doors burst
open in every direction, releasing wind, sunlight, snow, voices, music,
and darkness. Lost hours poured through the air in shining
rivers.

Elias saw Lena and Tomas standing in the summer field.

Mara ran to them.

This time no threshold stopped her. They embraced, all three
together, while grass and golden light streamed upward around them.

Lena looked at Elias over Mara's shoulder.

"Thank you," she said.

"For what?"

"For bringing her forward."

Then she kissed Mara's hair and stepped back.

The field dissolved.

Mara reached after her, but Elias caught her hand. The
staircase collapsed beneath them, and they fell amid a storm
of hours.

They landed in the clock room.

Rain struck the windows. The tower mechanism roared. The great
gears spun backward, then forward, then stopped.

For one breath, the entire town was silent.

The bell struck three.

Outside, people gasped. A cart horse reared. Somewhere, the baker
shouted that his soup was not burned after all.

The minute hand began to move.

Mara lay beside Elias on the floor, still holding his
hand. In the other, she clutched the broken watch. The
bluebird was divided by a white fracture, but its hands
had moved.

Three o'clock.

"Is she gone?" Mara asked.

Elias sat up slowly. His bones hurt. His heart hurt
more.

"Yes," he said.

Mara began to cry.

He gathered her into his arms. At first she resisted,
as if anger might keep the lost hour from leaving.
Then she buried her face against his coat.

Elias held her while the clock ticked.

He did not tell her not to cry. He did
not say time healed. He did not offer any of
the tidy lies people used when a sorrow could not
be repaired.

He simply remained.

Months later, Mrs. Wren returned to the shop.

Elias placed the silver watch on the counter. He had
repaired its mechanism but not its face. The cracked bluebird
still spread its wings beneath the glass.

"I'm afraid the damage could not be hidden," he said.

Mrs. Wren studied it for a long time. Then she
wound the crown.

The watch began to tick.

"My grandfather said some hours ought to remain where they
died," she murmured.

"Perhaps," Elias said. "But clocks were made to move."

She smiled and paid him twice his fee.

After she left, Mara came in from the back room
carrying two cups of tea. She had begun apprenticing with
him, though she still claimed predictable machines were boring. Together
they had changed the sign over the shop.

It no longer read:

ELIAS VALE, REPAIRER OF CLOCKS AND WATCHES.

Now it read:

VALE & VALE, KEEPERS OF PRESENT TIME.

Mara set down the tea. "The regulator is losing a
minute."

"Then adjust it."

"I did. It's still slow."

"Adjust it again."

She sighed dramatically and fetched a screwdriver.

Across the square, the tower prepared to strike three. Elias
paused over his work. He sometimes wondered where the lost
hours had gone, and whether a golden field continued somewhere
beyond the reach of clocks.

He had learned not to demand an answer.

The bell rang once, twice, three times.

Nothing vanished.

Mara looked up and smiled.

Then the hour moved on.

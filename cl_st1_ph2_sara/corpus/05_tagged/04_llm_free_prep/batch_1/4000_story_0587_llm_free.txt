The Clockmaker of Bellweather

Every clock in Bellweather stopped at 3:17 on the morning
the sea disappeared.

Pendulums froze in their arcs. Springs held their breath. The
brass hands of the town hall clock shuddered to a
halt, and in the dark rooms below, people woke with
the uneasy feeling that someone had spoken their names.

By sunrise, half the town stood on the harbor wall.

Where the sea had been, there stretched a plain of
black mud shining under a pale sky. Fishing boats rested
on their sides like exhausted animals. Anchors, bottles, cannonballs, and
the ribs of ancient wrecks lay exposed for miles. Far
beyond them, at the edge of sight, the missing water
formed a dark blue line.

No wave moved toward the shore.

No gull cried overhead.

Mr. Elias Venn watched from the doorway of his clock
shop, a screwdriver still in his hand. Above him, the
painted sign—VENN & DAUGHTER, CLOCKMAKERS—creaked in the wind.

The words had become a sort of cruelty. His daughter
had been gone eleven years.

"Elias!" called Mayor Harrow, hurrying across the square. His red
sash had been tied hastily over his nightshirt. "The town
clock. Can you repair it?"

Elias looked up at the tower.

"I can repair a clock," he said. "I can't repair
a morning."

The mayor did not appreciate the distinction.

"People are frightened. If the clock starts again, perhaps they'll
feel things are normal."

"Things aren't normal."

"That is precisely why they must appear to be."

Elias placed the screwdriver in his coat pocket and followed
him.

Inside the tower, the air smelled of dust, pigeons, and
cold iron. Elias climbed the narrow stairs to the clock
chamber. The great mechanism stood silent, its gears webbed in
shadow.

He examined the escapement, checked the weights, and tested the
mainspring. Nothing was broken. Nothing was jammed. Yet the mechanism
would not move.

He took out his own pocket watch. Its hands also
showed 3:17.

It had belonged to his father and had never lost
more than a second in a year.

Elias opened its case. The gears were perfect.

From somewhere below came a knock.

He paused.

Three slow taps echoed through the tower.

Knock.

Knock.

Knock.

"Mayor?" Elias called.

No answer.

He descended to the clock face, then to the bell
chamber. The tower door remained shut. The knock came again,
not from below this time, but from inside the wall.

Elias pressed his ear to the stone.

He heard water.

Not rain, nor the rush of a river. He heard
deep water moving through a vast place. Beneath it came
another sound, so faint he mistook it for the strain
of the building.

A girl was humming.

Elias stepped back so quickly he struck his shoulder against
the bell rope.

The tune was one his daughter, Mara, had invented as
a child. She used to hum it while sorting screws
in the workshop, arranging them from smallest to largest. He
had not heard it since the day she vanished.

"Mara?" he whispered.

The humming stopped.

Something clicked inside his pocket.

The watch had opened by itself.

The minute hand moved backward one mark.

Then another.

Elias stared until it stopped at 3:15.

Two minutes before the town's clocks had frozen.

He ran from the tower.

By then, hundreds of people had climbed down onto the
seabed. They gathered coins, lost jewelry, old weapons, and cargo
spilled from wrecked ships. Children danced around a bronze statue
crusted with barnacles. Merchants drove carts across the mud, shouting
claims over exposed chests and barrels.

At the harbor steps, Elias found Nell Rusk, keeper of
the lighthouse, trying to stop them.

"The sea comes back," she warned. "It always comes back."

"When?" someone shouted.

Nell glanced toward the distant wall of water. "When it
is ready."

People laughed.

Elias pushed through them. Nell was a narrow, silver-haired woman
with a face weathered by forty years of storms. She
had been on duty the night Mara disappeared.

"What happened at 3:17?" he asked.

Nell studied him. "This morning?"

"Eleven years ago."

Her expression changed.

Mara had been sixteen then. She had gone to the
lighthouse with a repaired chronometer wrapped in green cloth. Nell
said she never arrived. The only trace of her had
been one shoe found on the western rocks.

At least, that was the story Elias had accepted.

"Tell me," he said.

Nell looked at the crowd spreading across the mud. "Not
here."

They returned to the clock shop. Dusty morning light fell
across benches crowded with tools and unfinished repairs. Hundreds of
silent clocks covered the walls. Without their ticking, the room
seemed dead.

Nell locked the door.

"She did reach the lighthouse," she said.

Elias felt the floor tilt beneath him.

"You lied."

"Yes."

"For eleven years?"

"Yes."

He gripped the edge of his workbench. "Why?"

"Because she made me promise."

He wanted to shout, but anger was too small for
what filled him.

Nell continued quietly. "Mara arrived shortly after three. She gave
me the chronometer. Then she asked about the bell beneath
the lighthouse."

"There is no bell beneath the lighthouse."

"There is."

Long before Bellweather had become a harbor town, Nell explained,
monks had built a chapel on the cliffs. They cast
a bell from metal found in a fallen star. It
did not ring for prayer. It rang when time itself
had gone astray.

According to the old records, the bell had sounded only
three times. Each time, the sea withdrew. Each time, someone
walked across the seabed to a door that should never
have opened.

"What door?" Elias asked.

"No one knows. The records were damaged."

"And Mara?"

"She had found references in your grandfather's notebooks. She believed
the bell could return a lost hour."

Elias remembered Mara studying old ledgers after closing time. He
had assumed she was interested in obsolete mechanisms.

"Why would she want a lost hour?"

Nell hesitated. "Your wife."

Anna Venn had died when Mara was six. A fever
took her between sunset and dawn while a winter storm
kept the doctor away. For years afterward, Mara had asked
whether a clock could be made to run backward.

Elias always told her no.

"She thought she could save her mother," Nell said. "I
told her the stories were warnings, not instructions. But she
rang the bell. The sea vanished. She went down onto
the seabed."

"And you let her?"

"I followed. I almost caught her. Then the water returned."

Elias closed his eyes.

"Why did you tell me she never arrived?"

"Because her last words to me were, 'Don't let Father
follow.'"

On the wall behind Nell, a wooden cuckoo clock clicked.

Its hands turned backward.

Around the shop, other clocks stirred. Minute hands unwound. Hour
hands swept counterclockwise. The noise rose into a frantic mechanical
whisper.

3:16.

3:15.

3:14.

All of them stopped at 2:43.

Elias's pocket watch sprang open again. Inside the lid, letters
appeared beneath the glass as if traced by an invisible
finger through dust.

FATHER, COME BEFORE THE TIDE.

Nell read the message over his shoulder.

"No," she said.

Elias had already taken his coat.

The western route across the seabed began below the lighthouse.
Elias and Nell descended a stair cut into the cliff.
Around them, townspeople hauled treasure toward shore, though their carts
sank to the axles in mud.

Elias carried a lantern, rope, his watch, and the heaviest
hammer from his shop. Nell carried an old naval pistol.

"You said the bell is beneath the lighthouse," he said.

"It is. But we do not need to ring it
again."

"You're certain?"

"No."

"That is not a useful kind of certainty."

"It is the only kind age leaves you."

At the foot of the stair, the mud gave way
to a road.

Flat white stones crossed the seabed in a perfectly straight
line. They were smooth and clean, untouched by weed or
silt. No map of Bellweather showed such a road.

Elias and Nell followed it west.

They passed the skeleton of a whale with an iron
door set between its ribs. They passed a field of
porcelain masks, each wearing a different expression. They passed a
sunken warship whose drowned crew stood behind green windows, watching
silently as the travelers went by.

"Do not answer if they speak," Nell said.

"They're dead."

"That has never stopped everyone."

After an hour, Bellweather was a gray smudge behind them.
The wall of sea loomed ahead, impossibly high. Fish moved
within it, flashing like coins. A vast shadow turned in
the blue.

The road ended at a house.

It stood alone on the mud, a narrow three-story house
built of dark stone. Its windows glowed with yellow light.
Smoke rose from its chimney and drifted sideways along the
seabed.

A brass plate on the door read:

OFFICE OF MISLAID MINUTES
PLEASE KNOCK AT THE APPOINTED TIME

Elias checked his watch. Its hands showed 2:59 and moved
backward.

He knocked.

The door opened onto a warm hall lined with umbrellas.
At the far end sat a clerk behind a high
desk. He was tall and colorless, with silver spectacles and
a black suit of an old-fashioned cut. Instead of eyes,
two tiny clock faces turned behind the lenses.

"Elias Venn," he said. "Eleven years late."

"Where is my daughter?"

"Early, in most respects. Late in others."

"Where is she?"

The clerk opened an enormous book. "Mara Venn entered this
office at 3:17 on the ninth day of October, eleven
years ago. She requested the return of one hour belonging
to Anna Venn."

"And?"

"Her request was denied. Death is not a scheduling error."

Elias's hand tightened around the hammer. "Then why didn't she
come home?"

"She appealed."

The clerk turned a page.

"Appeals are heard at the end of time. The current
wait is considerable."

Nell raised the pistol. "Bring her to us."

The small clocks behind the clerk's spectacles spun.

"Threats waste time, Madam Rusk. Here, waste is a form
of payment."

As he spoke, a lock of Nell's hair turned from
silver to white.

She lowered the pistol.

Elias stepped closer. "What do you want?"

"Want is irrelevant. There are rules. Your daughter may withdraw
her appeal if she accepts the original decision."

"Then let me see her."

The clerk considered this. "One minute."

A door appeared in the wall.

"Do not exceed it," he said. "Do not promise what
you cannot surrender. Do not touch anything that remembers you."

Elias opened the door.

On the other side was his workshop as it had
looked eleven years before.

Sunlight streamed through the windows. Every clock ticked. At the
main bench sat Mara, sixteen years old, her dark hair
tied with a red ribbon. She was fitting a spring
into a watch.

Elias could not move.

She looked up.

"Father?"

He crossed the room and embraced her. She felt solid
and warm. She smelled of oil, soap, and the lavender
Anna used to keep in drawers.

Mara clung to him. "You weren't supposed to come."

"I'm taking you home."

"How long has it been?"

"Eleven years."

For her, he saw, it had been no more than
an afternoon.

"I thought if I waited, they'd hear my appeal," she
said. "I thought I could bring Mother back."

"You cannot."

"I know that now."

A clock chimed once.

The sunlight dimmed.

Mara looked toward the door. "They won't let me leave.
I tried."

"The clerk said you could withdraw."

"I did. But I paid the appeal fee before I
understood it."

"What fee?"

"My last hour in Bellweather."

Elias stared at her.

"They took the hour in which I would return," she
said. "Without it, there is no time when I can
go home."

The room's edges began to blur.

There had to be a mechanism. Everything had one. Every
lock, every clock, every rule. If an hour had been
removed, another could replace it.

"What if someone gives you an hour?" Elias asked.

Mara shook her head. "It must be a last hour."

Another chime sounded, though less than a minute had passed.

The clerk's warning returned to Elias: Do not promise what
you cannot surrender.

Elias understood.

He kissed Mara's forehead. "Take mine."

"No."

"My last hour in Bellweather. Whenever it comes."

"No, Father."

"You wanted to save your mother. Let me save my
daughter."

"That's different."

"It never is."

The workshop darkened. Beyond the windows, water pressed against the
glass.

Mara seized his hands. "If you give it to me,
you'll never have a final hour at home. You could
die out here. Or somewhere worse."

"Then I shall be careful where I spend my time."

"Father—"

"I have spent eleven years listening to a door that
never opened. Do not ask me to walk away from
you now."

A third chime shook the room.

The door flew open.

The clerk stood outside. "The minute is concluded."

Elias faced him. "Take my last hour in Bellweather and
give it to Mara."

"Accepted," the clerk replied at once.

Mara cried out.

The floor vanished.

Elias fell into black water.

He awoke on the white road with Nell kneeling beside
him. The dark house was gone.

Mara lay a few feet away, still wearing her red
ribbon.

Above them came a roar.

The wall of sea had begun to collapse.

Nell dragged Mara upright. "Run!"

They raced toward Bellweather.

The sea struck the mud behind them with the sound
of mountains breaking. Wrecks rose in the first surge. Anchors
tumbled. The old warship split apart, releasing a cloud of
pale figures.

Elias ran until his lungs burned. The white road buckled
beneath his feet.

They would not reach the cliffs.

Ahead, townspeople abandoned carts of treasure and fled toward the
harbor stairs. Some were already being swept away by fingers
of water racing through channels in the mud.

Mara stumbled. Elias caught her.

"Keep going!"

The lighthouse bell began to ring.

Its voice came from below the earth, deep enough to
shake the bones. With each toll, the oncoming water slowed.

Nell stopped.

"What are you doing?" Elias shouted.

"Someone must ring it."

"You said it was beneath the lighthouse."

"The rope is beneath the lighthouse. The bell is beneath
everything."

She thrust the pistol into his hand and turned back.

"Nell!"

She smiled at Mara. "I am sorry I kept you
waiting."

Then she left the road and climbed onto the ribs
of the whale skeleton. At its center, she opened the
iron door and disappeared inside.

The bell rang again.

The sea stopped.

For one impossible moment, a vertical wall of water towered
above Nell's small figure. Elias saw whales suspended in it
like birds in a storm.

"Go!" her voice echoed from everywhere.

Elias and Mara ran.

They reached the harbor stairs as the final bell sounded.
The sea crashed down.

Water hurled them upward. Elias struck stone, lost his grip
on Mara, and spun through a world of foam and
darkness. His pocket watch tore free and vanished.

Then hands pulled him onto the harbor wall.

He coughed seawater onto the stones.

Mara lay nearby, alive.

All around them, the people of Bellweather stared as the
sea settled into the harbor. Boats righted themselves. Gulls returned,
shrieking indignantly. The town hall clock gave a rusty groan.

Its minute hand moved.

3:18.

For several seconds no one spoke.

Then every clock in Bellweather began to ring.

In the years that followed, people argued about what had
happened. Some said an earthquake had drawn back the tide.
Others blamed unusual currents or secret naval experiments. The treasures
brought from the seabed turned to salt by nightfall, and
those who tried to describe the white road could never
agree on where it had begun.

A brass plaque was placed at the lighthouse in memory
of Nell Rusk, though no body was found.

Mara returned to the clock shop.

At first, the town treated her like a ghost. Then,
gradually, it treated her like a curiosity, and finally, as
towns do with every wonder that stays long enough, it
treated her like a neighbor.

She was sixteen in a town where her childhood friends
had become bakers, sailors, mothers, and undertakers. She grieved for
the years she had lost, but she did not let
grief take the years ahead.

Elias taught her everything he knew, though she soon improved
on his methods. Together they repaired every clock that had
stopped at 3:17.

Every clock except one.

The town hall mechanism refused to keep proper time. It
lost exactly one hour each year, always on the anniversary
of the vanished sea. The mayor complained until Mara pointed
out that people enjoyed the extra hour of sleep. After
that, the mayor claimed the defect had been his idea.

The sign above the shop no longer seemed cruel.

VENN & DAUGHTER, CLOCKMAKERS.

Years passed. Elias grew old. Mara tied silver-streaked hair with
a red ribbon and took charge of the business. Sometimes
customers asked why her father never left Bellweather, not even
to visit the next village.

Elias would smile and say he had traveled enough.

He knew the bargain waited for him. His last hour
could not be spent in Bellweather because it belonged to
Mara. He wondered how the rule would remove him. Perhaps
illness would strike while he was away. Perhaps some force
would draw him back to the seabed.

So he remained within the town boundaries, testing the logic
of eternity with a clockmaker's patience.

On his eighty-third birthday, a letter arrived without a stamp.

It contained a single sheet of paper.

YOUR ACCOUNT IS OVERDUE.

Mara found him reading it at the breakfast table.

"You're not going," she said.

"I may have no choice."

"You taught me that every rule has a mechanism."

"And you taught me mechanisms can be improved."

They spent the day studying old notebooks. By evening, Mara
found the answer in a margin written by Elias's grandfather:

A town is not its stones. A town is its
people keeping time together.

At midnight, Mara climbed the clock tower.

She turned the hands back one hour.

Across Bellweather, by prior arrangement, every citizen did the same.
Bakers, sailors, teachers, children, and the mayor reset their clocks.
For one hour, Bellweather declared itself elsewhere.

Elias stepped beyond the old town gate.

Nothing happened.

He laughed so loudly that dogs began barking.

Mara joined him, and together they walked to the hill
above the harbor. It was the first time he had
crossed the boundary in decades.

Below them, the town shone beneath the moon. The lighthouse
flashed over a calm sea. From deep under the water
came a bell's low, distant note.

Elias sat in the grass.

His breath left him gently.

Mara held his hand as the clocks below began striking
one, though by every honest measure it was midnight.

For the rest of her life, she kept her father's
final hour in a small glass case above the workbench.
It looked like an ordinary brass gear, warm to the
touch and turning without any spring.

Once each year, when the sea grew still and every
clock in Bellweather lost an hour, Mara closed the shop
early. She climbed the hill and sat between two empty
places in the grass.

There she listened to the lighthouse bell beneath the sea
and to the town ticking behind her.

And for that stolen hour, no one she loved was
gone.

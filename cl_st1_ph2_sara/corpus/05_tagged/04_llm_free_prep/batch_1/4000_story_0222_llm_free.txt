On the morning the sea returned the bells, Mara Venn
was repairing a clock that had never told the correct
time.

It was a wedding clock, made of walnut and brass,
with two painted swallows above its face. Every hour, the
birds leaned toward each other and touched their beaks. The
clock had belonged to three generations of the Orrel family,
and according to its owner, it had run seven minutes
slow since the day his grandmother married.

"Then perhaps seven minutes slow is correct for this clock,"
Mara had said.

Mr. Orrel had frowned. People came to a clockmaker because
they wanted errors removed, not explained.

Now, alone in her workshop, Mara adjusted the pendulum and
listened to the rain scratching at the windows. Outside, the
harbor town of Bellweather was waking. Carts groaned over wet
stones. Chimneys released thin ropes of smoke. Gulls circled above
the roofs, complaining to one another.

Then, beneath the ticking of forty clocks, she heard a
bell.

The sound was deep and distant.

Mara's hands stopped.

A second bell answered, then a third.

Every clock in the shop continued ticking, but none of
them struck. The sounds came from outside—from the sea.

Mara crossed the room and opened the door.

People stood motionless in the street. Bakers, sailors, children, and
fishwives all faced the harbor. Even the gulls seemed to
hang silently in the gray air.

Far beyond the breakwater, where the sea met the colorless
sky, bells were ringing under the water.

Everyone in Bellweather knew the story.

A century earlier, the old town had stood in the
valley east of the cliffs. It had possessed a market
square, a church with seven bells, and streets lined with
apple trees. Then came the great storm. Rain fell for
nine days, the river burst its banks, and the sea
forced its way inland. By the tenth morning, the entire
valley had vanished beneath the water.

The survivors built a new town on higher ground and
carried with them whatever they had saved: a silver cup,
a sack of seeds, a cradle, a church door. But
the seven bells had been too heavy. The tower collapsed
before anyone could lower them.

It was said that the bells rang beneath the sea
before disaster.

Mara did not believe the story.

Her father had believed it. He had been a harbor
pilot, a broad-shouldered man who smelled of salt and lamp
oil. When Mara was ten, he had taken her to
the cliff during a storm and claimed he could hear
the drowned bells.

"They're warning us," he had said.

"Of what?"

He had smiled and squeezed her hand. "If we knew
that, they wouldn't need to warn us."

Two days later, his boat disappeared beyond the northern reef.

For years, Mara had hated the bells for failing to
warn clearly. Later, she hated herself for ever imagining they
could.

But now she stood in her doorway and counted seven
distinct voices rolling across the water.

When the ringing ended, Bellweather remained silent for several breaths.
Then everyone began speaking at once.

By noon, the council had called a meeting in the
town hall. By one, the hall was so crowded that
people stood on windowsills and filled the stairway. Mara attended
only because Councilor Vale sent his assistant to fetch her.

Vale was a narrow man with a silver beard and
an expression of permanent disappointment. He sat behind a long
table with the harbor master, the priest, two merchants, and
Old Nessa Quill, who had delivered half the town's children
and buried most of the other half.

On the table lay a rusted iron object.

"A gear," Vale said when Mara approached.

She examined it. It was the size of her palm,
its teeth corroded but regular.

"Part of a clock?" he asked.

"Possibly."

"It was found this morning in a fisherman's net."

Mara turned it over. Beneath the rust, she saw a
stamped mark: a seven-pointed star.

Her stomach tightened.

Her father's compass had borne the same symbol. As a
child, she had assumed it was merely decoration. After his
death, she searched for the maker but found no record
of it.

"Do you recognize it?" Vale asked.

"No," she lied.

At the back of the hall, someone shouted that three
dead fish had washed ashore with black eyes. Another person
claimed the well water tasted of copper. The priest raised
his hands and asked for calm, which only increased the
noise.

Old Nessa struck the table with her cane.

"The bells rang in my grandmother's time," she said. "The
year the fever came. They rang in my mother's time,
before the eastern cliff fell. And they rang when I
was a girl, before the winter that froze the harbor
solid."

"You never told us that," Vale said.

"You never asked."

"What followed the bells each time?" Mara asked.

Nessa looked at her. "Something we should have prepared for."

The council decided to close the harbor, inspect the sea
wall, and store food in the town hall. These were
sensible measures, which comforted no one. By evening, families had
begun leaving for inland villages.

Mara returned to her workshop and locked the door.

She took a box from beneath a loose floorboard. Inside
lay her father's compass, wrapped in faded blue cloth.

The compass had never pointed north. Its needle turned slowly
even when held still, as though searching for something it
could not find. Mara had dismantled it many times, but
its inner mechanism made no sense. Beneath the needle were
rings marked with numbers from one to thirteen, though only
twelve positions existed around the dial.

On the back was the seven-pointed star.

She placed the rusted gear beside it.

They belonged to the same machine.

A knock made her jump.

"Mara?" called a voice.

It was Eli Thorne, keeper of the lighthouse and her
oldest friend. He entered dripping rain onto the floor, his
red hair plastered to his forehead.

"You heard them," he said.

"So did everyone."

"I heard something else."

From inside his coat, he produced a roll of paper.
It was a lighthouse log, covered in neat columns recording
wind, tides, and visibility.

"The tide turned at dawn," Eli said. "It should have
risen for another three hours, but it stopped when the
bells began. Then it went out."

"Tides go out."

"Not that far."

He pointed through the window.

Beyond the roofs, the harbor was an expanse of gleaming
mud. Boats leaned against their ropes. The sea had retreated
beyond the breakwater, farther than Mara had ever seen it.

People carrying lanterns were already walking across the exposed seabed.

"They're fools," Mara said.

"Yes," Eli replied. "We should go before them."

They joined the crowd descending the harbor steps.

The seabed smelled of salt, weeds, and ancient decay. Pools
reflected the evening sky. Crabs fled beneath stones. Here and
there lay objects swallowed by the sea long ago: broken
anchors, green bottles, a cannon from some forgotten war.

As Mara and Eli walked east, the mud became a
road.

Cobblestones emerged beneath their feet.

Ahead, lanterns moved among black shapes rising from the mist.
Walls. Chimneys. Roofless houses furred with weed and barnacles.

The drowned town had returned.

No one spoke loudly now. The people of Bellweather wandered
through the ruins as though entering a cemetery. Some searched
for family names carved above doorways. Others filled their pockets
with coins, buckles, cups, and anything else the sea had
uncovered.

At the center of the valley stood the remains of
the old church. Its tower had fallen, but the lower
walls remained. Beneath heaps of stone, Mara glimpsed the curved
flank of a bronze bell.

Eli caught her arm.

"Look."

Behind the church, where old maps showed only an orchard,
a narrow tower rose from the mud. It was made
of dark stone, windowless except for a single opening at
the top.

"I've never heard of that," Mara said.

Neither had anyone else. While the crowd gathered around the
church bells, Mara and Eli crossed what had once been
the orchard.

The tower's door stood open.

Inside, a spiral stair descended underground.

"Of course it goes down," Eli muttered. "Why would anything
tonight go in a pleasant direction?"

Mara lit a lantern, and they descended.

The stair ended in a circular chamber. Its walls were
covered in brass plates, pipes, and motionless gears. At the
center stood a mechanism taller than a man. It resembled
both a clock and a navigational instrument, with concentric dials
surrounding a glass sphere.

Mara approached in wonder.

The machine was connected to seven thick cables that vanished
into the ceiling, leading toward the church.

"The bells," she whispered. "This rang them."

Eli pointed to an empty space among the gears. "Something's
missing."

Mara removed the rusted gear from her pocket. She had
taken it from the council table without thinking—or so she
had told herself.

It fit the space perfectly.

When she pressed it into place, the machine shuddered.

The glass sphere filled with pale light.

All around the chamber, gears began to turn. Dust and
dried salt fell from their teeth. The concentric dials rotated
at different speeds, clicking into alignment.

The compass inside Mara's coat grew warm.

She drew it out. Its needle no longer wandered. It
pointed toward the glass sphere.

Words appeared inside the light.

Not written words, exactly. They came as shapes, numbers, and
images that Mara somehow understood.

She saw the valley from above. She saw lines spreading
beneath it like cracks in glass. She saw the moon
dragging the sea, the earth shifting under the water, and
a wall of black waves racing toward the coast.

At the edge of the vision glowed a circle divided
into thirteen parts.

One part was already dark.

Eli stared at the sphere. "What does it mean?"

Mara watched a second section slowly dim.

"Time," she said. "We have until the circle goes black."

"For what?"

"To leave."

They ran back to town.

Councilor Vale did not believe them.

Or rather, he believed enough to be frightened and not
enough to act.

"You found a machine under the old church," he said,
pinching the bridge of his nose. "A machine that showed
you a wave."

"Yes."

"And your evidence is this compass?"

On the table, the compass needle pointed east, toward the
drowned tower. Two of the thirteen marks had darkened.

Eli slammed his palm down. "The sea has withdrawn farther
than any tide in recorded history. What evidence would you
prefer?"

"Panic could kill more people than a storm."

"So could caution," Old Nessa said from her chair by
the fire.

Vale looked at the crowded hall. "Where would we go?
The inland road crosses the lower bridge. If the sea
returns while people are crossing—"

"The north path," Mara said. "Up Lantern Hill."

"That path is too narrow for carts."

"Then leave the carts."

A murmur moved through the room.

Leave the carts meant leave food, furniture, tools, savings, heirlooms—every
heavy object around which people had built their lives.

Merchant Pell rose. "Convenient advice from someone with no children
and a shop full of portable clocks."

Mara felt the insult find its mark. She looked at
the townspeople: suspicious faces, frightened faces, faces already calculating the
cost of belief.

Then the bells rang again.

This time, they were not distant.

The sound shook dust from the rafters. Windows rattled. Cups
danced across the table.

Seven bells, tolling beneath the exposed earth.

A crack split the plaster above Vale's head.

That ended the debate.

The evacuation began in darkness.

People crowded the north road with handcarts and bundles. Rain
lashed the town. Lanterns bobbed in the streets, and frightened
animals pulled against their leads. Someone opened the jail and
released two smugglers, who immediately began helping carry sacks of
flour uphill.

Mara returned to her workshop.

She intended to take only her coat and tools. Instead,
she stood among the clocks, listening to their thousand small
sounds.

There was the blue enamel clock her mother had given
her. The ship's chronometer recovered from a wreck. The ridiculous
wedding clock with its kissing swallows. Each contained hours of
labor and fragments of other lives.

For one wild moment, she considered loading them into a
cart.

Then she understood Merchant Pell's anger. A life was portable
only when viewed from outside.

Mara took her tool roll and her father's compass. Before
leaving, she wound every clock in the shop.

Their ticking followed her into the rain.

Halfway to Lantern Hill, she found Councilor Vale arguing beside
an overturned cart. His wife sat in the mud, clutching
her ankle, while trunks and silver dishes lay scattered around
her.

"Help me lift it," Vale demanded.

"We don't have time."

"I can't leave her."

"I mean the trunks."

Together they freed Vale's wife. Mara and Vale carried her
between them while Eli pushed people forward.

The compass now showed nine dark marks.

Below, the sea lay beyond sight. The drowned valley had
disappeared into darkness, but the bells continued ringing in uneven
bursts.

At the base of Lantern Hill, the path narrowed. People
abandoned carts, wardrobes, barrels, and chests. Possessions piled up like
offerings.

Mara climbed until her lungs burned.

Near the summit, Old Nessa stopped.

"I can't," she gasped.

"You can," Mara said.

"No. But you can carry my bag."

Mara crouched, expecting medicine. The bag clinked with bottles.

"Brandy," Nessa explained. "For the survivors."

Eli and one of the freed smugglers made a chair
with their arms and carried her upward.

Twelve marks on the compass were dark.

The entire town seemed to be on the hill, but
Mara knew that was impossible. She saw no sign of
the fishermen who had gone to inspect the old church.
Several families were missing. So was Merchant Pell.

She looked back.

Lanterns still moved in Bellweather.

"I'm going down," she said.

Eli seized her sleeve. "No."

"They don't know."

"They know. They chose."

Another mark began to dim—the last one.

From far out at sea came a sound like mountains
breaking.

The horizon rose.

At first Mara mistook it for a cloud. Then lightning
flashed, revealing a wall of water stretching across the world.

People screamed.

The wave struck the drowned valley first. It swallowed the
tower, the church, and the exposed road. The bells gave
one final, tremendous cry.

Then water crashed into Bellweather.

Warehouses burst apart. Ships were lifted over the sea wall
and carried through streets. Mara saw her workshop windows flare
with warm light before the building folded beneath the black
water. For one instant, dozens of clocks shone inside it
like stars.

The wave climbed the lower slope of Lantern Hill.

People scrambled upward. Mara pulled a child from the mud.
Eli lost his footing, and Councilor Vale caught him. Water
seized the abandoned carts and spun them together. A wardrobe
struck a tree and exploded into splinters.

The surge reached Mara's knees.

Then her waist.

She wrapped one arm around the tree and held the
child above her head. The water was freezing, filled with
branches, roof tiles, and broken furniture. Something struck her shoulder.
Her grip weakened.

The compass tore free from her coat.

She saw it in the water, its brass case flashing
as it spun away.

Her father's last possession.

Mara reached for it.

For a heartbeat, her fingers touched the chain.

Then the child cried out.

Mara let the compass go and seized the child with
both hands.

A moment later, the water began to recede.

Dawn revealed a new coastline.

Bellweather's harbor was gone. The lower town had been scraped
to its foundations. The sea had entered the valley and
remained there, covering the drowned ruins once more. Only the
upper streets, the town hall, and the lighthouse survived.

The people on Lantern Hill counted the living.

They counted again.

Thirty-seven were missing, including Merchant Pell and the fishermen who
had returned to the old church for bronze and treasure.
Others had survived by climbing roofs or trees. Throughout the
morning, boats searched the floodwater.

Mara sat beneath a blanket while Nessa poured brandy into
tin cups.

"Your father would have been proud," Eli said.

Mara watched pieces of her town drifting in the new
bay.

"My father would have saved the compass."

"No," Eli said. "He would have known what it was
for."

In the weeks that followed, Bellweather rebuilt.

There were arguments about where houses should stand, who owned
land that had become sea, and whether the council had
acted too late. Councilor Vale resigned, then was elected again
because no one else wanted the work. The smugglers became
respectable boatmen, though not entirely respectable.

Mara reopened her workshop in the surviving lighthouse storehouse.

For months, townspeople brought her clocks recovered from the wreckage.
Most were ruined. Salt had invaded their cases, warped their
faces, and frozen their gears.

She repaired what she could.

Mr. Orrel found the wedding clock lodged in an apple
tree. The painted swallows were gone, and the walnut case
had cracked from top to bottom.

"I suppose it's finished," he said.

Mara opened the case. The mechanism was green with corrosion,
but when she touched the largest gear, it moved.

"No," she said. "Only changed."

It took her eleven months to restore it.

On the anniversary of the wave, the people of Bellweather
gathered on Lantern Hill. They had raised a wooden frame
there and hung a new bell cast from metal recovered
along the shore: broken anchors, buckles, cooking pots, clock springs,
and fragments of the old church bells.

At noon, Old Nessa pulled the rope.

The bell's voice rolled across the rebuilt town and over
the water. It was not a warning this time. It
was remembrance.

In Mara's workshop, the wedding clock began to strike.

It was still seven minutes slow.

She had decided that was correct.

The two newly painted swallows leaned toward each other above
its face, touching their beaks as the last note faded.

Then all the clocks resumed their patient ticking, measuring not
the time that had been lost, but the time that
remained.

## The Station at the Edge of Morning

Every night at eleven forty-three, the last train arrived at
Bellweather Station.

It came without sound.

No iron shriek announced its approach, no white eye shone
in the tunnel, and no wind swept ahead of it
to stir the old newspapers lying between the rails. One
moment the platform was empty; the next, a black train
stood beneath the yellow lamps, its windows glowing with the
dim blue light of winter dawn.

For thirty-two years, Silas Vale had been there to meet
it.

He wore the same uniform he had received on his
first day as stationmaster: navy wool, brass buttons, a cap
with a silver badge. The sleeves had been let out
twice and taken in once, and the badge had lost
its lettering long ago. Yet every night, before the train
arrived, Silas polished it with the corner of his handkerchief.

There were rules.

He kept them in a small red book locked in
the bottom drawer of the ticket desk.

**Rule One: Do not board the train.**

**Rule Two: Do not prevent anyone from boarding.**

**Rule Three: A passenger may ask one question. Answer truthfully.**

**Rule Four: If a passenger carries no ticket, give them
one.**

**Rule Five: The train departs at midnight. It must never
leave late.**

Silas had not written the rules. The stationmaster before him,
a narrow-faced woman named Mrs. Brindle, had shown them to
him on a rainy Tuesday in October.

"Are they dead?" Silas had asked, watching the passengers emerge
from the fog.

"Some are," Mrs. Brindle had replied.

"Where does the train go?"

"You'll be asked that often."

"But what should I say?"

"The truth."

"What is the truth?"

Mrs. Brindle had looked toward the black train. For the
first time, Silas noticed that she was afraid.

"I don't know."

The next evening, Mrs. Brindle boarded. She did not return.

After that, Silas kept the station alone.

Bellweather had once been a thriving place, with three platforms,
a café, a newspaper stand, and porters who hurried about
with brass whistles between their teeth. Then the northern mines
closed. The mills followed. Trains stopped coming one by one,
and grass grew over the tracks.

By Silas's twentieth year, the ordinary timetable had been reduced
to two trains a day.

By his twenty-fifth, there were none.

The railway company locked the waiting room, removed the clocks,
and sent Silas a letter thanking him for his service.
He continued to report for work. Someone, or something, continued
to place his wages in his bank account on the
last Friday of every month.

The town forgot the station, but the last train did
not.

Its passengers came from everywhere.

They arrived through the front doors, although Silas kept those
doors locked. They appeared on benches when he looked away.
They stepped from shadows or wandered down staircases that had
been demolished years before.

Some were old. Some were young.

Some wore hospital gowns, bloodstained shirts, wedding dresses, military coats,
or ordinary pajamas. Others carried suitcases, birdcages, violin cases, bouquets,
broken umbrellas, and once, a jar filled with fireflies. One
man arrived holding a birthday cake with all its candles
burning. A little girl came with mud on her shoes
and asked if her dog would be on the train.

"I don't know," Silas told her.

The girl considered this, then nodded.

"That's all right," she said. "He usually finds me."

Most passengers understood why they had come, even if they
could not say it aloud. A few argued. A few
wept. A few tried to run, but the station doors
opened only onto the station again. Eventually, midnight approached, and
the black train waited with infinite patience.

Silas punched every ticket with an old steel clipper.

The clipper made holes shaped like stars.

In thirty-two years, only one passenger had refused to board.

His name was Thomas Wren. He had been a clockmaker
and, by his own account, a stubborn man.

"I have unfinished business," he said.

"Everyone does," Silas replied.

"My daughter hates me."

"You still have to board."

"I won't."

Silas checked the clock he kept in his waistcoat pocket.
It was eleven fifty-nine.

"You must."

The train doors began to close.

Thomas seized the edge of Silas's desk. His hands passed
through the wood to the wrists, as though the desk
were made of water.

"Please," he whispered.

Silas remembered Rule Two: Do not prevent anyone from boarding.
It said nothing about forcing them.

So he did not.

At midnight, the train vanished.

Thomas vanished with it, though he remained on the platform.
His outline lingered for a few seconds like smoke, his
mouth open in surprise. Then the yellow lamps flickered, and
the platform was empty.

Silas never learned what happened to him.

After that, he became careful not to offer hope.

Then, on the first night of spring in his thirty-third
year, the train brought someone to Bellweather Station.

Silas had never seen anyone get off before.

At eleven forty-three, the black carriages appeared beneath the lamps.
The doors opened. Passengers on the platform began to board,
moving slowly through the blue light.

And a young woman stepped out.

She was perhaps twenty-five, dressed in a red coat several
sizes too large. Her dark hair had been cut unevenly,
and a pale scar ran from her left eyebrow to
her cheek. She carried a green suitcase covered with railway
stickers from cities Silas had never heard of.

She looked around.

"Bellweather?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Oh, thank goodness."

She hurried toward the ticket desk. Silas backed away.

"You came off the train."

"I know."

"No one comes off the train."

"I just did."

"That is impossible."

The young woman smiled. "I've always found that word means
something has happened before people are ready for it."

Silas reached for the red rule book, unlocked the drawer,
and opened it. He read all five rules twice. There
was nothing about arriving passengers.

"May I ask your name?" he said.

"Mara Venn."

"Are you dead?"

"Not at present."

"Where did you board?"

"Far away."

"Where does the train go?"

Mara's smile faded.

"You don't know?"

"No."

She studied his face, then glanced at the passengers filing
into the carriages.

"Do they?"

"Some think they do."

"And you let them board anyway?"

Silas stiffened. "I follow the rules."

"Who wrote them?"

"I don't know."

"Have you ever wondered?"

"Every night."

Mara set her suitcase on the floor. "Then perhaps it's
time you found out."

The platform clock struck a single note.

Silas turned. The hands showed eleven fifty-eight.

"You cannot stay here," he said.

"Why not?"

"Everyone here must board before midnight."

"I already boarded. Now I've arrived."

"That is not how it works."

"It worked exactly like that."

A door slammed at the far end of the train.
Only one passenger remained on the platform: an elderly woman
in a green hat, clutching a paper bag.

Silas went to her.

"Your ticket, please."

The woman handed him a faded blue ticket. Printed across
it were the words **ONE WAY — DESTINATION UNDISCLOSED**.

"Stationmaster," she said, "will it hurt?"

It was her one question.

Silas looked at the train. He looked at Mara, who
stood beside his desk with her hands buried in the
pockets of her red coat.

"I don't know," he said.

The woman nodded and boarded.

Eleven fifty-nine.

Silas faced Mara. "You must get back on."

"No."

"I cannot allow the train to leave without you."

"I am not its passenger anymore."

The final door began to close.

Silas grabbed Mara's suitcase and threw it onto the train.

"What are you doing?" she cried.

"Rule Two. I cannot prevent anyone from boarding."

"I'm not boarding!"

She seized the suitcase handle and pulled. For a moment,
she and Silas struggled on either side of the closing
door. Then the suitcase sprang open.

Its contents scattered across the floor of the carriage and
the platform.

There were no clothes inside.

Instead, hundreds of paper tickets fluttered around them, red and
gold, blue and silver, tickets scorched at the edges, tickets
stained with rain, tickets bearing dates from centuries past and
centuries yet to come.

One landed at Silas's feet.

**SILAS VALE**

**BELLWEATHER TO TERMINUS**

**DEPARTURE: 12:00**

His pocket watch began to ring.

Midnight.

The train disappeared.

The blue windows, black carriages, and scattered tickets vanished in
an instant. Mara fell backward onto the platform, still gripping
her empty suitcase.

Silas stared at the ticket in his hand.

The yellow lamps went out.

For the first time in thirty-two years, the last train
had left without him.

In the darkness, Mara said, "You missed it."

Silas could hardly breathe. "Why did I have a ticket?"

"Because tonight was supposed to be your last night."

"No one told me."

"Were the others told?"

He did not answer.

The station lights came on one by one. Mara rose
and brushed dust from her coat.

Silas opened the red book again. Beneath Rule Five, words
began to appear in black ink.

**If the stationmaster misses the train, the line will remain
open.**

A sixth rule formed slowly below it.

**Before the following midnight, the stationmaster must choose a replacement
or discover the terminus.**

Silas read it aloud.

"And if you do neither?" Mara asked.

The page trembled. A final sentence appeared.

**All passengers in transit will be lost.**

Silas thought of the little girl waiting for her dog,
Thomas Wren dissolving like smoke, the woman in the green
hat asking if it would hurt.

"How many passengers are in transit?" he asked.

Mara closed her suitcase. "All of them."

Silas looked up sharply. "You know what the rules mean."

"I know more than you do."

"Then tell me."

"I spent seven years on that train," she said. "Or
perhaps seven hundred. Time does not behave properly between stations."

"You said you weren't dead."

"I wasn't. I boarded by accident."

"How?"

"I was looking for my brother."

Mara sat on a bench beneath the dead clock. She
told Silas that her younger brother, Eli, had disappeared when
he was nine. He had chased a white dog into
an abandoned underground station and never returned. The police searched
the tunnels for weeks. Mara searched for years.

At eighteen, she found a platform that appeared on no
map.

At midnight, a black train arrived.

"I thought I saw Eli in one of the windows,"
she said. "So I got on."

"Rule One," Silas murmured.

"I wasn't a stationmaster."

"What happened?"

"The train carried me through places with no names. Stations
beneath deserts, stations at the bottom of the sea, stations
suspended among stars. At each stop, more people boarded. No
one got off."

"No one?"

"Not where I could see."

"Then how did you come here?"

"I found the conductor."

Silas had never seen a conductor aboard the train.

"What did he tell you?"

"That the line was built long ago, when people first
began to fear the distance between death and whatever comes
after. The train was meant to guide them. The stationmasters
opened the doors. The conductor carried them safely to the
terminus."

"That sounds merciful."

"It was, until the conductor forgot the destination."

Silas frowned. "How can a conductor forget?"

"The way anyone forgets. One detail at a time."

Mara explained that the conductor had traveled for so long
that his face had worn smooth. His uniform had become
part of his skin. He still collected tickets, still closed
the doors, still guided the train along its endless route—but
he no longer remembered where the tracks were meant to
end.

"So everyone stays aboard?" Silas asked.

"Forever, unless they fade."

"Like Thomas."

"Perhaps."

Silas stared at the red book. "Why send you here?"

"The conductor didn't. I stole the tickets, found a door,
and jumped when the train slowed. I hoped this was
the terminus."

"It isn't."

"No." Mara looked around at the cracked tiles, boarded windows,
and moss hanging from the ceiling. "Clearly."

They had less than a day.

Silas made tea in the old staff kitchen. Mara had
not eaten in seven years, yet she drank three cups
and devoured half a tin of stale biscuits.

At dawn, they searched the station.

Silas knew every room, cupboard, tunnel, and loose floorboard, but
Mara noticed what he had overlooked. Above the sealed entrance
to Platform Three, a pattern of brass stars matched the
holes made by his ticket clipper. Behind the wooden boards,
they found a narrow staircase descending beneath the station.

The air grew warmer as they went down.

At the bottom stood a circular chamber. Thousands of clocks
covered the walls. Some raced forward, others spun backward, and
many had stopped.

In the center of the room sat Mrs. Brindle.

She looked exactly as she had on her final night:
narrow-faced, gray-haired, dressed in her stationmaster's uniform.

"You took your time," she said.

Silas nearly dropped his lantern.

"You boarded."

"I did."

"Then how are you here?"

Mrs. Brindle tapped the wall behind her. "The railway passes
through more than space. Every station has a heart, and
every heart is connected."

Mara approached her. "Do you know the terminus?"

"No. But he does."

She pointed toward a dark corner.

A boy sat there, curled up asleep beside a white
dog.

Mara made a sound like something breaking.

"Eli."

The boy woke.

He was still nine years old.

For a moment he stared at Mara without recognition. Then
he scrambled to his feet.

"You found me," he said.

She crossed the chamber in two steps and wrapped her
arms around him. The white dog barked and circled them,
wagging its tail.

Silas turned to Mrs. Brindle. "How did he get here?"

"The dog led him. Animals rarely need trains. They know
the way naturally."

"The way to where?"

"To the terminus."

Hope rose in Silas so quickly that it hurt.

"Then the dog can guide the train."

"If the conductor listens."

"How do we make him?"

Mrs. Brindle looked at Silas's ticket, still clenched in his
hand.

"The conductor stops for passengers, not arguments."

Silas understood.

"No," Mara said. She held Eli tightly. "You missed the
train. Choose a replacement."

"And condemn them to this?"

"Choose me. I've already lost seven years."

"You found what you were looking for."

"So did you."

Silas glanced around. "What did I find?"

"The reason you stayed."

All his life, Silas had told himself he remained at
Bellweather because of duty. Because someone had to punch the
tickets, answer the questions, and keep the final train on
time.

But that was not the whole truth.

He had stayed because the station needed him. Beyond its
walls, houses had emptied, friends had died, and the world
had changed its language, its machines, and its speed. Here,
every night, frightened strangers looked at him as though he
might know the way.

He never did.

Still, he had been there.

Perhaps sometimes that was enough.

By evening, they had a plan.

At eleven thirty, Silas returned to the platform in full
uniform. He polished his silver badge and placed the red
rule book on the ticket desk.

Mara stood with Eli near the stairs. The white dog
sat between them.

"If this fails?" she asked.

"Then choose a kinder stationmaster than I was."

"You were kind."

"I was punctual."

"Sometimes that is a kind of kindness."

At eleven forty-three, the train arrived.

Its doors did not open.

The windows blazed blue, revealing thousands of faces inside. They
filled every seat, corridor, and carriage, people layered in reflections
stretching farther than the train itself.

Silas held up his ticket.

A door appeared.

He boarded with the white dog at his side.

Rule One burned through his mind, but rules were maps
drawn by those who had traveled before. When the road
changed, the map had to change with it.

The carriage was impossibly crowded, yet a path opened for
him. Passengers watched silently as he passed.

He saw the little girl with muddy shoes. The old
woman in the green hat. The man still carrying his
birthday cake, though the candles had burned down to pools
of wax.

At the end of the train, Silas found the conductor.

Mara had described him well. He was tall, dressed in
black, with brass buttons sunk into his chest. Where his
face should have been was only a blank oval of
pale skin.

"Ticket," the conductor said.

Silas handed it over.

The conductor clipped it, leaving a hole shaped like a
star.

"Terminus," Silas said.

The conductor tilted his featureless head.

"Destination unknown."

"This dog knows."

The white dog barked.

The conductor stood motionless.

Behind Silas, the carriage doors began to close. Through them,
he saw Mara and Eli on the platform. Midnight approached.

"Let him guide us," Silas said.

"Destination unknown."

"Then remember."

"I cannot."

"You have carried every memory with you."

Silas gestured toward the passengers.

A murmur moved through the train.

The little girl stepped forward. She took the white dog's
face in her hands, laughed, and kissed its nose.

"I knew you'd find me," she whispered.

The dog wagged its tail, then ran toward the front
of the train.

The conductor turned after it.

For the first time, a line appeared across his blank
face. It opened like a crack in porcelain.

A mouth.

"Home," he said.

The train lurched.

Outside, Bellweather Station fell away. The tracks rose into a
darkness filled with stars, though Silas could not tell whether
they were in the sky or under an endless sea.

The passengers began to speak.

They shared names, songs, prayers, jokes, and final messages. Their
voices rolled through the carriages until the train itself seemed
to remember them. With every word, the conductor's face returned:
first a nose, then eyes, then the weathered features of
an exhausted man.

Ahead, a light appeared.

It was not blue like winter dawn. It was gold,
warm and broad, the color of morning through kitchen curtains.

The train slowed.

Doors opened all along the carriages.

One by one, the passengers stepped into the light.

Some laughed. Some wept. Some simply disappeared beyond the threshold.
The old woman in the green hat turned to Silas
before leaving.

"You were right," she said.

"About what?"

"It doesn't hurt."

The little girl followed the dog. The man with the
cake carried it into a crowd waiting beyond the light.
Last of all, the conductor removed his cap.

"Stationmaster," he said, "will you disembark?"

It was, Silas realized, the conductor's one question.

Silas looked back through the empty train. Far behind them,
at the other end of the impossible line, Bellweather Station
waited.

"I don't know," he answered truthfully.

The conductor smiled.

"Then you still have time."

At seven the next morning, Mara and Eli found Silas
asleep on a bench.

The black train was gone. So were the tracks. Beyond
the platform edge, wildflowers grew in bright drifts beneath the
rising sun.

On the ticket desk lay the red rule book.

All its pages were blank except the first.

In Silas's handwriting, though he did not remember writing it,
were three new rules:

**No journey is endless when the lost are remembered.**

**No map is final.**

**Always leave a door open for those who wish to
come home.**

Bellweather Station reopened that summer.

No railway company claimed it, and no ordinary train ever
stopped there. Even so, travelers came. They arrived carrying grief,
uncertainty, and questions they had saved for years. Mara turned
the old waiting room into a café. Eli repaired the
clocks, though he allowed each one to keep a slightly
different time.

Silas remained stationmaster.

Most nights passed quietly.

But now and then, at eleven forty-three, a black train
appeared beneath the yellow lamps. Its windows glowed gold instead
of blue, and its passengers were not afraid.

Some boarded.

Some disembarked.

And the train was never again required to leave at
midnight.

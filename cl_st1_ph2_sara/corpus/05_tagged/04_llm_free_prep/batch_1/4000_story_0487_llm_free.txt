Mara first heard the mountain speak on a Tuesday morning,
while she was repairing the clock above the bakery.

It was an old municipal clock, green with corrosion, mounted
in a square tower overlooking the village of Bellwether. For
eighty-three years it had measured the hours with stubborn inaccuracy,
losing four minutes in winter and gaining seven in summer.
The villagers trusted it more than any modern device. When
the clock said noon, the baker took out his loaves,
the school released its children, and Mr. Vale opened the
doors of the Golden Ram.

That Tuesday, however, the clock stopped at 9:17.

Mara climbed the tower with a leather bag of tools
and found the minute hand trembling beneath her fingers. Inside,
the gears were clean, the springs unbroken, and the pendulum
swung freely. Yet every time she restarted the mechanism, it
halted at the same minute.

On the fourth attempt, a vibration passed through the tower.

At first Mara thought it was thunder. Then the vibration
deepened, traveled through the stones, and gathered itself into something
like a voice.

Mara.

She froze.

Dust drifted from the rafters. Pigeons burst from the roof
and wheeled over the village square.

Mara.

The voice did not come from the clock. It rose
from below, through the tower foundations and the earth beneath
Bellwether, immense and patient.

"Who is this?" she whispered.

There was a long silence.

Then the mountain north of the village answered.

I am waking.

Mara descended the tower so quickly that she left her
screwdriver inside.

No one believed her.

Mr. Vale laughed until ale spilled over his hands. The
baker suggested she had worked too long without breakfast. At
the council hall, Mayor Quill listened with an expression of
ceremonial concern and advised her to take the afternoon off.

"Mountains do not speak," he said.

"This one did."

"And it knew your name?"

"Yes."

"That seems unusually sociable for a geological formation."

Three councilors chuckled. Mara did not.

Bellwether stood in the shadow of Mount Aster, a dark,
steep-sided mountain crowned with snow even in midsummer. There were
no trees on its upper slopes and no paths to
its summit. According to village legend, the first settlers had
found a stone door near its base, but no one
had seen it for generations.

The mountain gave Bellwether its springs, its rich black soil,
and the silver veins that had once made the village
prosperous. In return, the villagers painted it on shop signs,
embroidered it on banners, and ignored it.

Mara had never ignored old things.

She repaired clocks because her father had done so, and
because broken mechanisms made sense to her. A stopped clock
was not lazy or wicked. It simply carried a hidden
cause. Find the cracked tooth, the tangled chain, the grain
of dust lodged in the escapement, and time could continue.

Her father had taught her that.

He had also taught her not to climb Mount Aster.

"Some silences are doors," he used to say. "Best not
knock on all of them."

He had disappeared twelve years earlier, leaving behind his coat,
his tools, and a half-repaired pocket watch. The villagers assumed
he had fallen into the river. Mara had never accepted
this, though she had searched its banks until winter sealed
the water with ice.

At dusk, the clock began to ring.

It struck once for every hour it had missed, though
its hands remained fixed at 9:17. The bell rang until
windows shook and dogs howled in the streets. On the
thirteenth stroke, a crack opened across the village square.

People fled as cobblestones tilted upward. The crack widened no
more than a finger's breadth, but from it came warm
air smelling of iron and rain.

Then every clock in Bellwether stopped.

Mantel clocks, pocket watches, brass alarm clocks, the painted cuckoo
clock in the schoolhouse—all halted at 9:17.

In the silence, the mountain spoke.

Bring me the keeper.

This time everyone heard it.

The mayor called an emergency meeting.

By midnight, half the village was crowded into the council
hall. Oil lamps smoked overhead. Farmers argued with miners, parents
clutched children, and old Mrs. Brindle insisted that a goat
must be sacrificed immediately.

"The mountain asked for a keeper, not a goat," Mara
said.

"Could be the same thing," Mrs. Brindle replied. "Depending on
the goat."

Mayor Quill struck the table with a wooden mallet.

"We must remain calm. It is possible that the voice
was produced by underground pressure."

"Underground pressure said Mara's name?" the baker asked.

The room turned toward her.

Mara wished, briefly, that she had stayed in the clock
tower.

"What does it mean by keeper?" Mayor Quill asked.

"I don't know."

"Your father called himself a timekeeper," said Mr. Vale.

"He was a clockmaker."

"He went to the mountain before he vanished," said another
voice.

Mara looked up. It was Elias Reed, who had been
her father's closest friend. He was thin now, with clouded
eyes and hands knotted by age.

"What did you say?" she asked.

Elias stared at the floor.

"The morning he disappeared, I saw him take the north
road. He carried a lantern and a coil of rope."

"You told me he went to the river."

"I told you what people had decided to believe."

Mara crossed the hall before she knew she was moving.
"You knew?"

"I knew only that he walked north."

"For twelve years?"

His face tightened. "Your father made me promise not to
follow and not to send you after him."

"Why?"

"He said the mountain's clock was failing."

The villagers fell silent.

Outside, something rumbled beneath the streets.

Elias reached into his coat and produced a tarnished key.
It was longer than his hand, with a circular bow
and three asymmetrical teeth.

"He left this with me. He said I would know
when to give it to you."

Mara took it. The metal was warm.

Mayor Quill cleared his throat. "Let us not rush into
anything. We should organize a committee."

The mountain spoke again, louder than before.

Hurry.

Tiles slid from the council roof.

Mara closed her hand around the key.

"I'm going tonight."

The north road ended at an abandoned silver mine. Beyond
it, the ground rose sharply toward Mount Aster. Mara carried
her father's tool bag, a lantern, food, rope, and the
unfinished pocket watch he had left behind.

Elias went with her.

"You're not invited," Mara told him.

"At my age, invitations are mostly for funerals. I go
where I like."

Snow began before they reached the upper slopes. It fell
in slow, heavy flakes that vanished on the warm ground.
The whole mountain pulsed faintly beneath their boots, like the
chest of a sleeping animal.

Near dawn, they found the stone door.

It stood in a cliff face blackened by age. No
handle marked its surface, only a keyhole surrounded by carved
circles. Mara brushed away the moss and saw that the
circles were not decorative. They were clock faces, hundreds of
them, each showing a different hour.

Elias lifted the lantern.

"Your father found this," he said. "Long ago, when we
were boys. He spent his life trying to understand it."

"And you waited until now to mention that?"

"He believed knowledge could be dangerous."

"Secrets are more dangerous."

"Yes," Elias said quietly. "I know."

Mara inserted the key.

The mountain shuddered. Deep inside it, gigantic mechanisms shifted. The
stone door opened without a sound.

Beyond lay a passage descending into golden darkness.

The walls were lined with veins of glowing crystal. Between
them ran metal rods, chains, and toothed wheels taller than
houses. Some turned slowly. Others jerked or stood still. Each
movement sent tremors through the floor.

"This is impossible," Elias whispered.

Mara approached the nearest gear. Its teeth were carved with
tiny scenes: a child chasing a red scarf, a woman
closing a book, a soldier kneeling in mud. The images
changed as the gear turned.

"These aren't machines," Elias said.

"Yes, they are," Mara replied. "But they're measuring something other
than hours."

They descended.

The passage opened into a chamber so vast their lantern
could not find its ceiling. Bridges crossed a gulf filled
with turning wheels. Pendulums swung through the darkness like inverted
towers. Rivers of silver sand poured upward into glass chambers,
and thousands of bells rang too softly to hear.

At the center stood a clock.

It had no face and no hands. It was a
sphere of dark metal suspended over the gulf, bound by
twelve enormous chains. Eleven chains moved in a steady rhythm.

The twelfth hung slack.

Beside it, on a narrow platform, sat Mara's father.

He had not aged.

He wore the same gray vest he had worn the
morning he vanished. His hair remained black, his shoulders broad,
his hands unmarked by the twelve missing years. One hand
rested on a brass lever. The other held a pocket
watch identical to the one in Mara's bag.

"Mara," he said.

She could not breathe.

Elias made a broken sound behind her.

Her father stood. Hope entered his face, followed immediately by
fear.

"You shouldn't be here."

Mara crossed the platform and struck him.

The slap echoed across the chamber.

He touched his cheek.

"That," she said, trembling, "was for making me search the
river."

Then she threw her arms around him.

For a moment he seemed uncertain. Then he held her,
and she was nine years old again, sitting at his
workbench while rain tapped the windows and tiny gears glittered
beneath the lamplight.

She pulled away first.

"Twelve years."

"For me, less than a day."

She stared at his unchanged face.

"The clock," he said. "Time moves differently near its heart."

"What is this place?"

Her father looked toward the suspended sphere.

"The first clock."

"Who built it?"

"No one knows. Perhaps it built us. Perhaps the universe
made it in order to remember itself."

Elias stepped forward. "Jonas, the mountain is waking."

"No. It is dying."

A deep metallic snap rolled through the chamber. One of
the moving chains jerked hard enough to shake the platform.

Jonas gripped the lever.

"The clock does not create time," he said. "It distributes
it. Every place receives its portion: birth, growth, decay, memory.
But the northern regulator broke. I came to repair it
and became trapped holding the balance."

"Then let go," Mara said.

"If I do, the clock stops."

"What happens then?"

"Everything connected to it loses sequence. Seeds sprout before they
are planted. The dead return without knowing they died. Children
become older than their parents. A century may pass in
one room while the next remains untouched."

Mara remembered the clocks of Bellwether, all stopped at the
same minute.

"Why did it call me?"

"Because I asked it to."

Her father nodded toward the pocket watch in her bag.

She removed it. Its case was scratched, and its face
had no numbers. For twelve years its hands had remained
motionless. Now they spun wildly.

"It isn't a watch," Jonas said. "It's a replacement regulator.
I built it from a fragment of the clock, but
I couldn't finish it in time."

"You could have told me."

"I meant to return."

"You always mean to return when you leave someone waiting."

Another snap sounded. A second chain sagged.

The vast sphere shifted.

Across the gears surrounding them, carved images flickered. Mara saw
winter fields turn green, a house collapse and rebuild itself,
a woman grow young and disappear into her mother's arms.

Jonas thrust the lever toward Elias.

"Hold this."

Elias took it, groaning as the force bent him forward.

Jonas led Mara to a worktable crowded with delicate tools.
At its center was an empty cradle shaped to hold
the pocket watch.

"The regulator lacks an escapement," he said. "The clock's beat
is too powerful. Every metal I tried shattered."

Mara examined the mechanism. "What did the original use?"

Jonas said nothing.

She looked at him.

"What did it use?"

"A human memory."

Mara nearly laughed. "That isn't a material."

"Here it is."

He pointed into the gulf. Silver streams rose through the
darkness.

"The clock is made from moments," he said. "Memories give
time shape. Without them, one minute is indistinguishable from another."

"Which memory?"

"One strong enough to endure repetition. The clock will take
it, divide it, and use it to control the mechanism."

"Take it from whom?"

"The new keeper."

Mara understood.

"No."

"Mara—"

"You called me here to replace you."

"I called the only person capable of completing the repair."

"And then what? I stand at this lever while you
go home?"

"If the regulator works, no one will need to stand
here."

"But I lose a memory."

"Yes."

"Which one?"

"You choose."

The platform lurched. Elias cried out as the lever dragged
him a step toward the edge.

Mara opened her tool bag.

She had repaired hundreds of clocks. This one was larger,
stranger, and probably alive, but every mechanism obeyed a need.
Power, transmission, regulation. The principles remained.

She set the pocket watch in its cradle and opened
the back. The empty space at its center was no
larger than a grain of rice.

"What makes a memory strong?" she asked.

"Love," Jonas said. "Or grief."

She thought of twelve years beside the river. Twelve winters
waking at every knock. Twelve summers glancing toward the door
of the workshop. She thought of anger kept polished like
a favorite tool.

Then she thought of her father teaching her to fit
a gear onto its arbor.

Never force it, little bird. If it doesn't fit, understand
why.

"I know which one," she said.

Jonas seemed to read her expression. "Not me."

"It's my choice."

"You'll forget I was your father."

"No. I'll forget the morning you left."

"Mara."

"For twelve years that morning has been happening inside me.
You put on your coat. You say you'll be back
by supper. The door closes. I wait. Again and again."

Her voice shook, but her hands did not.

"If the clock needs a moment that can endure repetition,
it can have that one."

Jonas gripped the edge of the table.

"I'm sorry."

"I know. But knowing isn't the same as being free
of it."

The mountain groaned.

A third chain stopped.

There was no time for ceremony. Jonas placed two fingers
against Mara's temple and one hand over the open watch.

"Hold the memory clearly," he said.

She did.

Morning light on the workshop floor. The smell of oil
and tea. Her father buttoning his coat. Snow in his
hair though the day was clear. His hand on the
doorknob.

Back by supper, little bird.

The door closing.

Tick.

The empty house.

Tick.

The soup growing cold.

Tick.

Mara felt something unwind inside her.

The memory did not vanish all at once. It passed
through her like thread pulled through cloth, taking with it
the color of the light, the sound of his boots,
the exact angle of his smile. She tried to hold
it, then remembered she had chosen to let go.

The pocket watch began to tick.

Once.

Twice.

The sound was tiny, almost ridiculous within the vast chamber.

Yet every gear answered.

The upward rivers of sand paused. Pendulums aligned. The slack
chain tightened with a thunderous crash, and the great sphere
returned to its center.

Elias released the lever and fell backward.

Light burst from the clock.

It traveled outward through every mechanism, filling the carved scenes
with gold. Mara saw lives moving across the gears—not grand
histories, but ordinary moments. A cup dropped. A letter opened.
A child carried home asleep. A fisherman watched sunrise. A
woman at a bedside whispered a name for the last
time.

Then the clock struck 9:18.

The sound lifted them from their feet.

When Mara woke, she was lying in the grass outside
the stone door.

Morning sunlight touched the snow. Elias snored beside a rock.
Her father sat nearby, staring at his hands.

He had aged.

His hair was streaked with gray, and fine lines crossed
his face. Not twelve years all at once, but enough
to prove that time had claimed him again.

Mara sat up.

"Did it work?"

From far below came the faint, steady ticking of the
mountain.

Jonas smiled. "Yes."

She knew him. She knew his voice, the shape of
his hands, the songs he sang badly while repairing clocks.
She remembered birthdays, arguments, lessons, and laughter.

But when she reached for the day he had disappeared,
she found only a clean, quiet space.

"How did you leave?" she asked.

Pain crossed his face.

"I made a mistake."

"That sounds like you."

Elias awoke with a snort. He looked at Jonas, then
began to laugh. Soon Jonas joined him.

Mara did not understand what was funny, but after a
while she laughed too.

They returned to Bellwether at noon.

The villagers saw them descending the north road and ran
to meet them. Some embraced Mara. Some touched Jonas as
if he were a ghost. Mayor Quill immediately announced that
the council's policy of cautious observation had led to a
successful resolution.

Mrs. Brindle brought a goat anyway.

In the village square, the crack between the cobblestones had
sealed. Warm rain fell for three minutes beneath a cloudless
sky. Every stopped clock began moving at once.

All except the clock above the bakery.

Its hands remained fixed at 9:17.

Mara climbed the tower with her father's tools. Jonas followed,
moving more slowly than he once had. Together they opened
the mechanism.

Nothing was broken.

"Strange," Jonas said.

"No," Mara replied. "It's waiting."

"For what?"

She adjusted the minute hand.

The mechanism caught. The pendulum swung, and the clock advanced
to 9:18.

Below, the baker took out his loaves. The school released
its children. Mr. Vale opened the Golden Ram, though it
was not yet noon and no one objected.

Years later, people would tell different stories about the day
Mount Aster spoke. Mayor Quill claimed he had negotiated with
it. Mrs. Brindle credited the goat. Elias told everyone the
mountain had swallowed twelve years and returned them with interest.

Mara told no story at all.

She reopened the workshop with Jonas, and together they repaired
the village clocks. In time, she became famous for clocks
that never gained a minute in summer or lost one
in winter. Travelers came from distant cities carrying elaborate mechanisms
wrapped in velvet, and she treated each with the same
patient care.

Above her workbench hung the pocket watch from the mountain.
It no longer spun or shone. It ticked softly, keeping
no hour anyone could read.

Sometimes, just before sleep, Mara sensed that she had forgotten
something important. She would picture a door, though she could
not say where it led. She would feel an old
ache stir and then settle.

On those nights, the mountain's voice moved gently beneath the
earth.

Not words exactly. More like the rhythm of an enormous
heart.

Tick: what is lost.

Tick: what remains.

Tick: what returns in another form.

And each morning, when Mara opened the workshop, she found
her father already at the bench, making tea, gray-haired and
imperfect and there.

Together they wound the clocks.

Together they set the village moving.

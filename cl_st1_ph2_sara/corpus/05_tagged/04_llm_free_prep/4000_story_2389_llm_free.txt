The Last Lighthouse Keeper

On the morning the sea climbed the cliffs, Elias Vale
was repairing a clock that had never told the correct
time.

It hung in the kitchen of the old lighthouse, above
a shelf crowded with chipped cups and jars of rusted
screws. Its white face claimed that it was twenty minutes
past seven. Elias's wristwatch insisted it was nearly noon.

He stood on a wooden chair, turning the minute hand
with one finger.

"Twenty-three years," he told the clock, "and you still think
dawn lasts all day."

The clock answered with a tired click.

Outside, rain struck the windows in handfuls. The lighthouse tower
groaned as the wind pushed against it, but Elias paid
no attention. Storms had been pressing their shoulders against the
tower since long before he was born. The lighthouse had
survived lightning, war, and one government inspection. It would survive
this.

He climbed down and listened.

Beneath the wind and the rain, he heard the telephone
ringing.

The telephone was green, heavy, and attached to the wall
by a cable thick as a rope. No one had
called it in eight years.

Elias stared at it until it rang again.

He picked up the receiver.

"Blackstone Lighthouse."

For several seconds there was only a faint hiss.

Then a girl said, "Is this the keeper?"

Elias tightened his grip. "Who is this?"

"My name is Mara. Our boat is sinking."

The wind threw rain against the glass.

"What boat?"

"The Lark. We lost the mast. The engine won't start,
and my father is hurt."

Elias crossed the room to the window. Gray water filled
the horizon, broken by white lines where the waves tore
themselves open. No boat was visible.

"Where are you?"

"I don't know."

"You called from a radio?"

"No. A telephone."

Elias looked down at the receiver.

"What telephone?"

"The one in the cabin."

"There are no telephone lines at sea."

"I know," Mara whispered. "That's why I called the lighthouse."

A crackling silence opened between them.

Elias had spent forty-one years listening to strange noises carried
across water: voices from distant ships, fragments of songs, bells
where no bells stood. The sea was a great collector.
It gathered what people lost and returned things out of
order.

"Tell me what you can see," he said.

"Water."

"What else?"

"A light. Sometimes."

"Describe it."

"It flashes twice, then once."

Elias looked toward the tower stairs. Blackstone's lamp had been
dark for nine years, ever since the automatic beacon was
built on a steel platform three miles north. Before that,
its pattern had been two flashes followed by one.

He had not lit it since.

"Listen carefully, Mara. Go to the window. When you see
the light again, tell me where it is compared with
the front of your boat."

He waited as the line hissed.

"It's behind us," she said at last. "A little to
the left."

"Which way are the waves moving?"

"Past the right side."

"And the wind?"

"I don't know."

"Wet your finger."

"My father says that doesn't work."

"Your father is correct, but do it anyway."

There was a pause.

"It's cold on the left."

Elias closed his eyes and pictured the bay. If the
boat lay with Blackstone behind and to port, and the
storm was pushing east, the Lark might be crossing the
Needle Shelf. At low tide, black rocks there rose like
teeth. At high tide, they hid beneath the surface and
tore open any hull unlucky enough to pass above.

"When did you leave harbor?" he asked.

"This morning."

"From where?"

"Bellweather."

Bellweather Harbor had vanished in the storm of 1987.

Elias opened his eyes.

On the wall, the kitchen clock clicked. Its minute hand
shuddered forward.

"What year is it, Mara?"

The girl did not answer.

"Mara?"

"Nineteen eighty-seven."

The chair stood between Elias and the table. He lowered
himself onto it carefully.

He remembered the Lark.

Everyone in Blackstone village remembered. A father and daughter had
sailed from Bellweather hours before the storm changed direction. Search
boats had found a broken mast, a red scarf, and
half a nameboard. They never found the bodies.

Elias had been twenty-six that year, serving as assistant keeper
under his own father. At dusk, lightning had struck the
lighthouse mechanism. The lamp failed for fourteen minutes.

Fourteen minutes.

His father repaired the burned contact with a pocketknife and
a strip of copper from the kitchen. By then, the
Lark had disappeared.

"Are you there?" Mara asked.

"Yes."

"You sound old."

"I am."

"You sounded younger before."

Elias felt the room tilt, though the tower stood firm.

"Before?"

"When I called the first time."

The rain seemed to draw away from the windows.

"I've never spoken to you."

"You said you would light the lamp."

A memory rose, not from his mind but from somewhere
deeper: the green telephone ringing while his father fought the
dead mechanism above. Elias, young and terrified, lifting the receiver.
A girl's voice. Our boat is sinking.

He had thought it was a prank. Someone in the
village, perhaps, trying to frighten the new assistant keeper during
the worst storm in thirty years.

He had hung up.

The memory returned with such force that he could smell
wet wool and burned metal.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"What?"

"Nothing. Mara, is your father awake?"

"Yes, but there's blood on his face."

"Can he steer?"

"The wheel is broken."

"Do you have oars?"

"One."

Elias stood.

"Find it. Tie a rope around your waist before you
go outside. Secure the other end to something solid."

"What should I do with the oar?"

"Use it as a rudder. Put it over the back
of the boat and hold it on the right side.
You must turn until the light is directly behind you."

"But the light keeps going out."

"It will return."

He set the receiver on the table, leaving the cord
stretched, and hurried toward the spiral stairs.

"Where are you going?" Mara called.

"To keep a promise."

The tower had one hundred and thirty-two steps. Elias knew
because he had counted them as a boy, as a
young man, and again after his wife died, when counting
steps was easier than counting empty rooms.

He climbed slowly at first. His left knee protested. By
step thirty, his breath came hard.

Above him waited the lantern room, sealed under a glass
dome. The original lens remained, though the harbor authority had
removed the main bulb and disconnected the power. Dust covered
the brass mechanism. Gulls had built a nest against one
of the outer panes.

Elias reached the top and leaned against the railing.

The great Fresnel lens stood in the center like a
palace made of glass. During its working life, it had
gathered a small flame and thrown it twenty miles across
the darkness. Now it held only reflections.

Elias opened the maintenance cabinet.

Inside lay wrenches, polishing cloths, a bottle of oil, and
a cardboard box containing things no sensible inspector would have
allowed near an abandoned lamp: coils of wire, spare contacts,
fuses, candles, and an emergency burner older than Elias.

He lifted out the burner.

Below, faint through the stairwell, he heard Mara shouting from
the telephone.

Elias carried the burner to the lens, fitted it into
the old mounting bracket, and filled its reservoir with oil.
His hands shook as he struck a match.

The flame caught, blue at its heart and gold at
the edges.

Light entered the lens.

It multiplied, sharpened, and became a brilliant white blade sweeping
across the storm.

For a moment Elias saw every raindrop against the glass.

Then the lens stopped.

The rotation mechanism had seized years ago. Blackstone's signal required
two short flashes and one long: two, one. Without the
pattern, Mara might steer toward the wrong beacon.

Elias removed the mechanism cover. The gears beneath were dry
and brown with rust.

He poured oil over them and pulled the manual lever.

Nothing moved.

He pulled again.

The lever groaned.

"Come on," he muttered. "You've slept long enough."

He braced one foot against the base and dragged the
lever down with both hands. A gear shifted half an
inch. Another answered. Dust fell from the teeth.

The lens began to turn.

Slowly at first, then with gathering confidence, the glass palace
revolved around the flame. Its beam crossed the windows, vanished
into rain, returned, vanished again.

Two flashes.

One.

Elias laughed.

The mechanism completed another rotation, then jerked to a stop.

"No."

He pulled the lever. The gears refused.

Below, the telephone rang again.

Elias looked from the dead mechanism to the storm. He
could climb down to answer, but without the moving signal
Mara would be blind. He could stay and turn the
lens manually, but he would not know whether she was
safe.

Then he remembered the clock.

He descended the stairs faster than he had climbed them,
one hand sliding along the central rail. In the kitchen
he seized the receiver.

"Mara?"

"I can't hold the oar!"

"You must."

"The rope is cutting me."

"Keep the light behind you. Do not let the boat
turn sideways."

A deep sound traveled through the line, like stones grinding
together.

"What was that?" Elias asked.

"I don't know."

He knew.

The Lark had reached the edge of the Needle Shelf.

"Mara, listen. In a few minutes you will pass between
two rocks. You may not see them. The waves will
change. They'll come from both sides at once."

"What do I do?"

"Keep straight. Whatever happens, keep straight. After that, you'll enter
calmer water."

"My father says there's no calm water."

"Tell him Elias Vale says there is."

She repeated the name.

Her father spoke in the background, too weak for Elias
to understand.

"What did he say?"

"He asked how you know him."

"I don't."

"He knows you."

Elias had no time to consider the answer. He glanced
at the wrong clock above the shelf, then carried the
receiver as far as its cord allowed. He placed it
on the floor by the tower stairs.

"I have to keep the light moving," he said. "I
won't be able to hear you."

"Don't leave."

"I'll be right above you."

"That's not the same."

Elias looked up into the dark spiral.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

A wave struck the Lark. Mara screamed.

Elias lifted the receiver.

"When I was nine," he said, "I climbed outside the
lighthouse during a storm. My father found me halfway around
the gallery, holding the rail so tightly he had to
open my fingers one at a time."

"Why did you go outside?"

"I wanted to know whether I was brave."

"Were you?"

"No. But I came back inside. That was close enough."

Another grinding roar came through the line.

"I see the rocks," Mara said.

"Keep straight."

"They're too close."

"Keep straight."

"We won't fit!"

"You will."

The telephone crackled, and for one terrible second the voice
vanished. Then Mara cried out. There was a crash, followed
by the sound of breaking glass.

Elias pressed the receiver to his ear.

"Mara?"

Nothing.

"Mara!"

A cough.

"I dropped the phone."

"Are you through?"

"I can't see the rocks."

"Are the waves calmer?"

"Yes."

Elias closed his eyes.

"Good. Keep the light behind you. The current will carry
you toward Blackstone beach."

"You'll be there?"

"Yes."

This time, he did not hesitate.

"I'll be there."

He laid down the receiver and climbed.

In the lantern room, the burner fluttered. Elias grasped the
brass frame of the lens and pushed. It resisted, then
rolled forward. The beam moved across the sea.

He counted under his breath.

"One, two. Dark. One, two. Dark."

Each rotation required him to walk in a circle around
the lamp, pushing the heavy frame. Soon his shoulders burned.
The metal rim pressed a red groove into his palms.

Below, the telephone's voice rose and fell beyond hearing.

He continued.

The storm darkened the afternoon until it resembled night. The
beam swept over a world without boundaries, sea and sky
joined in a single gray violence.

On the seventh rotation, Elias glimpsed a boat.

It appeared within the beam for less than a second:
a small hull tilted on a wave, mast gone, white
cabin shining. A figure stood at the stern with an
oar. Then darkness swallowed it.

The Lark was real.

Elias pushed harder.

"One, two. Dark. One, two. Dark."

The boat reappeared farther south, approaching the narrow channel beside
the cliff. Elias remembered that channel from childhood. It ended
at a crescent of shingle below the lighthouse.

No harbor existed there. No rescue crew waited. The modern
village stood five miles inland, abandoned along the shore after
repeated floods. Even if he called for help, no vehicle
could reach the beach during the storm.

He was seventy-two years old. The path down the cliff
had partly collapsed. He had no boat, no rope long
enough to reach the water, and no idea whether saving
someone forty years ago could change anything at all.

Still, he had promised.

The lens grew heavier with every turn. Pain spread from
his hands to his elbows. The flame weakened as the
oil level fell.

When the Lark entered the channel, Elias released the lens
and ran downstairs.

The telephone receiver lay on the kitchen floor.

"Mara?"

"I can see the cliff."

"Good. You're almost here."

"How do we stop?"

The question struck him.

The shingle beach was narrow. Beyond it rose a wall
of rock. A boat carried by storm waves could smash
against the cliff before anyone aboard climbed out.

Elias took his coat from the hook.

"Turn sideways before you reach the beach."

"You said never to turn sideways."

"Now you must."

"Why?"

"Because the waves will push you onto the stones."

"Will it hurt?"

"Probably."

She was silent.

"I'm frightened," she said.

"I know."

"Are you?"

"Yes."

That seemed to comfort her.

Elias put the telephone on the table.

"Wait until you see three quick flashes. Then turn."

"How will you make three?"

He looked at the kitchen clock. Its hands now showed
twenty minutes past seven—the same time they had shown during
the storm of 1987, when lightning killed the lamp.

"I'll find a way."

He ran outside.

The wind nearly knocked him down. Rain struck his face,
cold and sharp. He crossed the yard to the cliff
path, carrying an electric torch, a coil of rope, and
the red emergency lantern that hung beside the door.

The path descended in steep turns cut into the rock.
Parts of it had fallen away, leaving gaps filled with
mud. Elias moved sideways, gripping roots and stones.

Halfway down, he slipped.

His boots shot from under him. He struck the ground
and slid toward the edge, clawing at wet grass. His
right hand caught a thorn bush. The branches bent. For
an instant he hung above the foaming sea.

The bush held.

Elias dragged himself back onto the path. Blood ran from
his palm.

He began to laugh—not from relief, exactly, but because at
nine years old he had wanted to know whether he
was brave, and at seventy-two the question seemed ridiculous. There
was never time to decide. There was only the next
handhold.

At the beach, waves rolled over the shingle. Elias tied
the emergency lantern to the end of the rope and
climbed onto a flat boulder.

Out in the channel, the Lark approached.

He could see Mara now, a thin figure at the
stern. A man lay near the cabin door.

Elias switched on the electric torch and flashed it three
times.

Mara lifted the oar.

The Lark turned.

A wave caught the hull and spun it broadside. Another
lifted it high. For a second the whole boat balanced
on the breaking crest, black against the sky. Then it
crashed onto the beach.

Elias ran into the water.

The cold stopped his breath. He waded until the sea
reached his waist and threw the lantern. It landed across
the Lark's bow.

"Tie it!" he shouted.

Mara stared at him through the rain.

"Elias?"

"Tie the rope!"

She looped it around a cleat. Elias pulled. The next
retreating wave dragged the boat backward, nearly tearing the rope
from his wounded hands.

Then the water returned, driving the Lark higher onto the
stones.

Mara jumped over the side. She was twelve or thirteen,
with dark hair plastered to her face.

"My father!"

Together they reached into the boat. Her father was conscious
but unable to stand. Elias took the man under the
shoulders while Mara lifted his legs.

As they carried him above the waterline, the man opened
his eyes.

He was younger than Elias remembered, but the face was
unmistakable.

"Jonah?" Elias said.

The injured man smiled faintly.

Elias had known Jonah Bell for only one summer. They
had been boys together—Jonah from Bellweather, Elias from Blackstone. They
explored caves, stole apples, and once tried to build a
raft from fence posts. Then Jonah's family moved away.

Years later, Elias had heard that Jonah returned to Bellweather
with his daughter. Their boat disappeared the same day.

"You took your time," Jonah murmured.

Elias stared at him. "You knew I would come?"

"Knew you'd become an ugly old man, anyway."

Mara looked between them. "You said you didn't know him."

"I didn't know who he was."

"That's often the way with friends," Jonah whispered.

They sheltered beneath an overhang while the storm began to
weaken. Elias wrapped his coat around Mara and tied a
strip of cloth over Jonah's wound.

"What happens now?" Mara asked.

Elias looked toward the path.

He had no answer. Above them, the lighthouse beam continued
to turn, though no one was pushing it.

Two flashes.

One.

The signal swept across the beach, illuminating the damaged boat,
the rain, and three figures huddled beneath the cliff.

Then another light appeared at the mouth of the channel.

A rescue vessel came slowly through the waves. It was
painted orange and white and carried the emblem of the
modern coast guard.

Mara stood. "Is that from Blackstone?"

"Not your Blackstone," Elias said.

The vessel lowered a smaller boat. Two crew members reached
the beach and ran toward them.

The first stopped when she saw Mara and Jonah. Her
face went pale.

"Sir," she said to Elias, "step away from them."

"They need help."

The rescuer looked at her companion, then back at the
ruined Lark.

"What boat is this?"

"The Lark."

"That's impossible."

Mara clung to Elias's sleeve.

He understood, then, that the past had not replaced the
present. It had entered it.

The rescuers carried Jonah aboard, wrapped Mara in a silver
blanket, and examined Elias's hands. As the small craft pulled
away from the beach, Mara leaned over the side.

"You promised you'd be there," she called.

Elias raised one bleeding hand.

"So did you."

By morning, the storm had gone.

The official report avoided several subjects. It did not explain
why the rescued man and girl matched two people missing
since 1987. It did not explain the Lark's antique registration,
or how the disconnected lighthouse telephone had made a call,
or why Blackstone's dead lamp shone through the night.

The newspapers were less careful. For several weeks, Elias's quiet
kitchen filled with reporters, historians, scientists, and strangers who wanted
miracles.

Jonah recovered in the hospital. Mara, still thirteen, learned that
the world had moved forward forty years without her. Elias
visited often. He brought Jonah a chessboard and gave Mara
books about computers, which she declared less interesting than lighthouses.

Eventually, the crowds disappeared.

One spring evening, Elias sat in the kitchen with Mara
while Jonah slept upstairs. The wrong clock ticked above the
shelf.

Mara pointed at it.

"Why don't you fix that?"

"I have tried."

"You fixed the lighthouse."

"The lighthouse wanted to be fixed."

"And the clock doesn't?"

"Apparently not."

Mara climbed onto the wooden chair. She opened the little
glass cover and moved the minute hand backward.

The mechanism clicked.

The clock began to strike.

It struck once, twice, and continued until it had rung
fourteen times—the number of minutes the lighthouse had been dark
during the storm of 1987.

Then its hands moved by themselves.

They turned past eight, nine, ten, spinning through all the
lost hours. Elias and Mara watched without speaking.

At last the hands stopped at the correct time.

Outside, evening settled over a calm sea. The automatic beacon
flashed in the distance, cold and precise.

High above them, in the old lantern room, another light
quietly came alive.

Two flashes.

Then one.

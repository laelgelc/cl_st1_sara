## The Cartographer of Lost Roads

Every morning, before the bells of Saint Orra struck six,
Mara Vale unlocked the door of the map shop and
swept away the roads that had appeared during the night.

They came in as thin lines of silver dust, slipping
beneath the door or pressing themselves through cracks in the
window frames. Some wandered only a few inches across the
floorboards before ending beneath a cabinet. Others curled around the
shop in elaborate loops, crossing under tables, climbing walls, and
vanishing into the painted mountains on the ceiling.

Mara never stepped on them.

Her father had taught her that much.

"A lost road is still going somewhere," he used to
say. "Best not to insult it."

So Mara swept carefully, following each silver line to its
end and gathering the dust in a blue glass jar.
When the jar was full, she sealed it with wax
and stored it beside dozens of others in the cellar.

No one in the town of Bellweather knew about the
roads. To everyone else, Vale & Daughter was simply an
old shop that sold maps: maps of the kingdom, maps
of the coast, maps of rivers, mines, forests, and forgotten
battlefields. Travelers came for directions. Merchants came to calculate distances.
Children came to stare at the sea monsters drawn in
green ink.

But the finest maps were never displayed.

Those were kept in a locked room behind the counter,
where Mara's father had once drawn roads that did not
yet exist and paths that had already disappeared.

Then, seven years ago, he had vanished.

He left no letter. His boots remained beside the stove,
his coat hung on its peg, and a cup of
tea waited half-finished on his desk. Only his silver compass
was missing, along with a blank sheet of lambskin.

The town guard searched the river and questioned passing strangers.
Mara searched the hills, the woods, and every road for
twenty miles. No trace of him was found.

At first, she believed he would return.

After one year, she began to doubt.

After three, neighbors stopped asking.

After seven, even Mara spoke of him in the past
tense—except when she was alone in the locked room.

There, surrounded by his unfinished maps, she still whispered, "Where
did you go?"

One autumn morning, a new road appeared.

It was not made of silver dust.

Mara discovered it at dawn, running from the front door
to the back wall in a line of black sand.
Unlike the lost roads, it did not curve or hesitate.
It cut straight across the shop, passing beneath the counter
and ending at a framed map of Bellweather.

Mara set down her broom.

The map had hung there for as long as she
could remember. Her father had drawn it when she was
a baby. Every street, bridge, and well was marked in
faded brown ink. In one corner, he had added a
tiny picture of Vale & Daughter, with two figures standing
in the doorway.

Now a black line crossed the map.

It began at the shop, continued through the eastern gate,
and traveled into a blank region beyond the Red Hills.
At its end was a small circle.

Inside the circle, in ink that was still wet, someone
had written one word:

MARA.

She touched the letters.

The wall behind the map gave a soft click.

Mara removed the frame and found a narrow compartment hidden
in the plaster. Inside lay a folded piece of paper
and a silver key.

The paper was covered in her father's handwriting.

My dearest Mara,

If you are reading this, the Hollow Road has returned,
and I have failed to return with it.

Do not follow.

Burn this letter. Melt the key. Sweep away the black
sand before sunrise.

Some roads are lost because they wish to be found.
Others are lost because the world is safer without them.

Forgive me.

Father

Mara read the letter three times.

Then she folded it, placed it in her pocket, and
stared at the black road.

At sunrise, it did not disappear.

By noon, it had darkened. By evening, the grains looked
less like sand and more like tiny fragments of night.

Mara closed the shop early.

She packed bread, cheese, a coil of rope, two bottles
of water, her father's field notebook, and the blue jar
of silver dust she had filled that morning. She took
a lantern from the cellar and tied a red scarf
around her neck.

Last of all, she lifted her father's spare compass from
the locked room.

Its needle spun without stopping.

"Very helpful," she muttered.

At midnight, she stepped onto the black road.

The shop disappeared.

There was no flash, no thunder, no sensation of falling.
One moment Mara stood between the counter and the door.
The next, she was walking through a forest beneath a
white moon.

The trees had no leaves. Their branches knitted together overhead
like the ribs of a vast animal. The black road
stretched ahead, smooth and narrow, while pale grasses whispered on
either side.

Mara looked back.

There was no shop behind her.

The road continued into darkness.

She raised her lantern and began to walk.

For several hours, nothing moved except the grass. The moon
remained fixed overhead, and the air smelled faintly of rain,
though the sky was clear.

Then Mara heard footsteps behind her.

She turned quickly.

A boy stood on the road.

He looked about twelve, though his eyes seemed much older.
His clothes were patched and muddy, and a copper bell
hung from a string around his neck. He carried no
lantern.

"Are you lost?" Mara asked.

The boy considered this.

"I was," he said. "Now I'm following you."

"That does not answer my question."

"It answers mine."

Mara lowered the lantern slightly. "What is your name?"

"Tallow."

"Where are your parents?"

"Probably older than they were."

Mara sighed. "That is also not an answer."

"You ask heavy questions."

"And you give light answers."

Tallow smiled. "That helps us carry them."

He fell into step beside her.

Mara did not invite him, but she did not send
him away. The forest was too quiet for that, and
there was something comforting in the sound of another person
breathing.

After a while, Tallow pointed to the jar tied to
Mara's pack.

"What's in there?"

"Lost roads."

"All of them?"

"Only the ones that entered my shop."

"Why do roads go into a shop?"

"To be mapped, perhaps."

"Do you map them?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Mara thought of the cellar shelves, crowded with blue glass.

"Because I don't know where they lead."

Tallow gave her a sideways look. "Isn't that why people
make maps?"

Before Mara could reply, the trees ended.

Ahead lay a village.

The houses leaned close together, their windows glowing with warm
yellow light. Smoke rose from chimneys. Somewhere, a fiddle played
a lively tune.

A wooden sign beside the road read:

WELCOME TO LASTHAVEN
POPULATION: EVERYONE WHO STAYS

"We should go around," Tallow said.

Mara smelled roasting meat and fresh bread. Her stomach tightened.

"We need food," she said.

"We have food."

"We may need more."

"We may need fewer reasons to stop."

Mara ignored him and entered the village.

At once, doors opened. People hurried out, smiling. They were
old and young, richly dressed and ragged, but each wore
a silver thread around one wrist.

"Travelers!" cried a broad woman in an apron. "Come in,
come in! You must be exhausted."

"We can't stay," Tallow said.

"Of course not," the woman replied cheerfully. "No one can."

She led them into an inn, where long tables bowed
beneath plates of food. Mara saw roast chicken glazed with
honey, bowls of buttered carrots, dark bread, red apples, and
cakes dusted with sugar.

The villagers watched eagerly as she sat.

Tallow remained standing.

Mara tore off a piece of bread. It smelled exactly
like the loaves her father used to bake on winter
mornings.

She took a bite.

The inn vanished.

She was sitting at the kitchen table in the map
shop. Morning sunlight warmed the floor. Her father stood by
the stove, younger than she remembered him, humming badly as
he turned a loaf out of its pan.

"You're late," he said.

Mara could not breathe.

He looked over his shoulder. "Well? Aren't you hungry?"

Her eyes filled with tears.

She knew it was false. She knew the road was
somewhere beneath the illusion, that Tallow was probably shouting, that
the smiling villagers were waiting for her to surrender.

But her father was here.

He placed the bread before her and sat down. "I
had the strangest dream," he said. "I dreamed I left
you alone."

Mara reached across the table and gripped his hand.

It was warm.

"You did," she whispered.

His smile faded. "Then stay. We can correct it."

For one terrible moment, she wanted nothing more.

Then she noticed his hands.

Her father had always carried ink stains on his fingers:
blue in the mornings, black by noon, sometimes red or
green by evening. This man's hands were clean.

Mara pulled away.

"You're not him."

The kitchen darkened.

The false father leaned closer. His face stretched, becoming pale
and smooth.

"I could be," it said.

Mara seized the blue jar from her pack and smashed
it against the table.

Silver dust burst into the air.

The kitchen split like paper in a flame.

Mara found herself on the inn floor. Tallow was pulling
her backward while villagers crowded around them. Their smiles had
vanished. Silver threads uncoiled from their wrists, reaching like worms.

The spilled road dust whirled across the floor and formed
a shining path to the door.

"Run!" Mara shouted.

They followed it outside.

The villagers pursued them to the edge of Lasthaven, but
as soon as Mara and Tallow returned to the black
road, the silver threads snapped tight. The villagers stood trapped
among the houses, calling promises after them.

Anything you lost can be restored!

Anyone who left can be returned!

Any sorrow can be undone!

Mara walked faster until the lights disappeared.

Afterward, Tallow said, "You broke your jar."

"I noticed."

"To escape something you wanted."

"Yes."

He touched the copper bell at his neck. "That's harder
than escaping something you fear."

They continued in silence.

The forest gave way to a desert of gray ash.
Half-buried signposts stood everywhere, pointing in conflicting directions. Some bore
names Mara recognized—Bellweather, Highmont, the Western Sea. Others named places
she had never heard of: The City Below Morning, The
Palace of Unasked Questions, Home Before It Happened.

The black road passed through them all.

"What is this place?" Mara asked.

"The country between choices," Tallow replied.

"How long have you been here?"

He kicked a little cloud of ash. "I ran away
when I was twelve."

"And how old are you now?"

"Twelve."

Mara stopped.

Tallow's copper bell gave a quiet chime.

"I lived near the southern border," he said. "There was
a war. My mother told me to take my sister
into the hills. I was afraid, so I hid alone.
When I came back, everyone was gone. I found this
road while searching."

"How long ago?"

"I don't know. There aren't any mornings here."

"Why haven't you left?"

"I don't know which road leads home."

Mara looked at the thousands of signposts.

"Perhaps none of them do."

"That's what I was afraid you'd say."

They walked on.

Eventually the ash desert sloped downward into a valley filled
with statues. They were not carved from stone. They were
people, frozen in place.

A soldier knelt with his sword raised. A woman reached
toward a cradle. Two brothers stood back-to-back. A king covered
his face with both hands. Dust coated them all.

At the center of the valley, a stone arch straddled
the road. Beneath it sat a blind woman wrapped in
a cloak of white feathers.

"Payment," she said as Mara approached.

"For what?"

"For passage."

"I have no money."

"I do not accept money."

The blind woman extended one hand. "Give me the memory
of your happiest day."

Mara felt the request like cold fingers around her heart.

"No."

"Then give me the face of the person you love
most."

"No."

"Then your name."

"No."

The woman smiled. "Everyone pays."

Tallow stepped forward. "Take my bell."

"No," Mara said.

"It's all I have."

The blind woman turned her empty gaze toward him. "That
bell carries your sister's voice. If you surrender it, you
will never remember the sound."

Tallow closed his fist around the bell.

Mara looked at the frozen people.

"They refused to pay?" she asked.

"They attempted to pass without sacrifice."

Mara took out her father's field notebook.

It contained his instructions on measuring impossible distances, notes on
places that shifted when observed, and sketches of creatures found
only at crossroads. It was the last work of his
hands that remained to her.

"Take this," she said.

The blind woman touched the cover. "Knowledge is not memory."

"No, but knowledge preserves memory. This contains years of my
father's life."

"Would you surrender it freely?"

Mara hesitated.

Then she thought of her cellar full of roads she
had never dared to map. She thought of her father's
warning, written for a daughter he had expected to remain
obedient and afraid.

"Yes," she said.

The blind woman accepted the notebook.

The arch filled with pale light.

"Pass, cartographer."

"I'm not a cartographer."

"Then why has the road called you?"

Mara had no answer.

Beyond the arch, the valley ended at the shore of
a black ocean.

There was no boat, but the road continued across the
water. It floated on the surface like a ribbon of
ink.

Mara and Tallow stepped onto it.

The ocean reflected no moon. Shapes moved far below—enormous, slow
things whose pale eyes occasionally opened beneath the waves.

Halfway across, they found a man lying on the road.

Mara knew him at once.

His hair had gone almost entirely gray. His face was
thin, and his clothes hung loosely from his body, but
the ink stains remained on his fingers.

"Father."

She fell to her knees beside him.

His eyes opened.

For a second, they showed no recognition. Then he whispered
her name.

Mara helped him sit up. She wanted to embrace him,
strike him, laugh, and demand seven years of answers. Instead,
she held his shoulders and said, "Are you real?"

"I hope so."

"Show me your hands."

He looked confused, but held them out. Blue and black
stained his fingertips.

Mara pulled him into her arms.

He trembled against her.

"I told you not to follow," he said.

"You hid the key where only I could find it."

"I hid it where I hoped no one would."

"You marked the map with my name."

"I didn't."

Mara drew back.

Behind them, the black ocean stirred.

Her father struggled to his feet. "The road marked it.
It wants a mapmaker."

"Why?"

"Because it is dying."

Tallow glanced down. The edges of the road were dissolving
into the water.

Mara's father continued. "The Hollow Road connects places that have
been lost—not destroyed, but separated from the world. It was
once maintained by cartographers. Over centuries, they disappeared. When the
road began to break, it called me."

"Why didn't you return?"

"I tried. But every path I drew became another branch
of the Hollow Road. It wouldn't release me."

"Then we'll leave together now."

"We cannot."

The road shuddered.

Across the ocean, something rose from the water.

At first Mara thought it was a tower. Then it
unfolded.

A creature stood above the waves, formed from shipwrecks, broken
gates, mile markers, and fragments of abandoned bridges. A hundred
wooden arms spread from its body. At its center, where
a heart should have been, hung a blank map.

"The Keeper," her father said. "It has been waiting."

The creature reached toward Mara.

The compass in her pocket grew hot. She drew it
out. For the first time, the needle had stopped spinning.

It pointed at the blank map.

"What does it want me to do?" she asked.

"Draw the Hollow Road," her father said.

"All of it?"

"All of it."

"That could take years."

"Yes."

Mara stared at him.

Understanding came slowly, then all at once.

"You stayed to draw it."

"I stayed so it would not choose you."

"But it did."

"I'm sorry."

The road cracked beneath their feet.

Tallow jumped back as black water surged through the gap.

Mara watched the break widen. In the depths below, she
glimpsed countless places: cities without doors, gardens under glass, lonely
houses drifting among stars. Lost things. Lost people. All held
together by one failing road.

"If no one maps it," her father said, "these places
will vanish completely."

"And if someone does?"

"The road survives."

"But the cartographer stays."

He lowered his head.

The Keeper extended one long arm. In its wooden palm
lay a silver pen.

Mara did not take it.

Instead, she removed the red scarf from her neck and
knelt on the road.

"What are you doing?" her father asked.

"Something you taught me."

From her pack, she took a piece of chalk, a
small ruler, and a stoppered bottle of ink. She drew
a circle on the black surface, then marked three lines
through its center.

"A map is not the land," she said. "A map
is an agreement about how to find the land."

Her father stared.

Mara pointed to Tallow. "Tell me everything you remember about
home."

"What?"

"Quickly."

"A farm," he said. "South of a town with a
green church roof. There was a well with a cracked
stone horse beside it. The hills looked purple at sunset."

Mara drew as he spoke. The details formed a crude
map around the chalk circle.

She tied Tallow's copper bell to the center.

It chimed.

A silver road burst across the black water, racing toward
the horizon.

At its far end appeared green hills beneath the light
of dawn.

Tallow gasped.

"I remember," he whispered. "That's the way."

The Keeper recoiled.

Mara looked at her father. "The road thinks it needs
one cartographer because it has always had one. But maps
can be shared."

His eyes widened.

Mara seized the silver pen from the Keeper's hand.

She drew doors.

Hundreds of them.

She marked every place they had passed: the moonlit forest,
Lasthaven, the ash desert, the valley of statues. Her lines
were rough, hurried, and imperfect, but each became a road
of silver light.

The Hollow Road writhed.

The ocean rose.

"Names!" Mara shouted. "We need names!"

Tallow understood first. He rang his bell and cried the
name of his village.

Her father knelt beside her and began naming places he
had found during his seven years of wandering.

"The Orchard of Winter Birds!"

A road flared.

"The Library at the Bottom of the Well!"

Another appeared.

"The Island That Forgot the Sea!"

Light spread across the water in a shining web.

The Keeper raised its many arms. From within its body
came a sound like bridges breaking.

Mara kept drawing.

She understood now why lost roads entered the shop. They
had not been asking to be stored in jars. They
had been asking to be remembered, connected, and given back.

The blank map in the Keeper's chest filled with silver
lines.

Then the creature came apart.

Ships, gates, stones, and bridges fell gently into the sea.
The dark water turned clear. For a moment, Mara saw
every lost place below, each connected to another by a
thread of light.

The black road vanished beneath them.

Mara fell through brightness.

She landed hard on the floor of the map shop.

Morning sunlight streamed through the windows.

Tallow lay beside the counter. Her father had fallen across
a display of coastal charts. For several seconds, no one
moved.

Then her father sat up and began to laugh.

Mara had never heard him laugh like that. It was
wild, breathless, and full of disbelief.

She joined him.

Tallow looked from one to the other. "Are all cartographers
mad?"

"Only the competent ones," Mara's father said.

The bells of Saint Orra struck six.

Outside, carts rattled over cobblestones. Shopkeepers raised shutters. Somewhere, a
dog barked at a milkman.

Seven years had passed in Bellweather, but dawn was still
dawn.

Mara opened the front door.

A silver road ran from the threshold toward the eastern
gate. Another turned west. A third climbed the wall of
the house opposite and disappeared into a chimney.

Across the town, people emerged to stare.

The roads were visible to everyone now.

In the weeks that followed, Mara and her father reopened
the locked room. They moved the unfinished maps into the
shop and invited anyone who had traveled a strange path
to add what they knew. Sailors drew impossible islands. Shepherds
marked valleys that appeared only in winter. Children contributed shortcuts
adults had forgotten how to see.

Tallow found his village.

More than sixty years had passed there. His mother and
sister were gone, but his sister's children still lived on
the farm beside the cracked stone horse. They welcomed him,
though no one could agree whether he was their uncle,
their great-uncle, or simply a boy who carried their grandmother's
voice in a copper bell.

He visited Bellweather often.

As for the cellar, Mara opened every blue jar.

The lost roads rose in clouds of silver, swirled around
the shop, and settled onto blank sheets of paper. Some
led to marvelous places. Some led to dangerous ones. A
few led nowhere at all.

Mara mapped them anyway.

Years later, travelers would speak of Vale & Daughter as
the greatest map shop in the kingdom, though the sign
above its door eventually read:

VALE, DAUGHTER & ALL WHO WANDER

Beneath those words, in smaller letters, Mara painted a warning:

NO ROAD IS ONLY A WAY IN.
NO MAP IS EVER FINISHED.
AND NOTHING IS TRULY LOST
IF SOMEONE REMEMBERS THE WAY.

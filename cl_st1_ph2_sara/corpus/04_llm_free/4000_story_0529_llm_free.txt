The town of Bellwether had no clocks.

There had been clocks once, of course. Brass clocks above shop doors, silver watches tucked into waistcoats, painted clockfaces in kitchens, and a great black clock in the tower above the town hall. Then, on an autumn morning forty-three years ago, every clock had stopped at seven minutes past nine.

No winding, oiling, pleading, or hammering had persuaded them to move again. New clocks brought in from neighboring towns stopped at the boundary. Watches froze in travelers’ pockets. Even sundials cast their shadows in the wrong direction, as if the sun itself had become confused.

So the people of Bellwether learned to live without hours.

They woke when the eastern roofs turned gold, ate when they were hungry, and slept when the dark grew heavy. Bakers judged bread by scent, teachers ended lessons when the youngest children began staring out the windows, and the railway abandoned the station because no timetable could survive there.

Only Mara Venn still cared what time it was.

She was seventeen and lived with her grandfather in the narrow house beside the abandoned station. Her grandfather, Elias, had once been the town clockmaker. Though his hands now trembled too badly to fit gear to gear, his workshop remained crowded with motionless clocks.

They covered the walls in dark wooden rows. They stood on shelves and tables, leaned against books, and hung from hooks in the ceiling. Some had painted moons. Some had golden pendulums. One contained a mechanical bird that had been caught forever halfway through opening its beak.

Every morning Mara dusted them.

“You should sell the cases,” Elias often said. “Cupboards, picture frames, firewood. That is all they are now.”

“They’re clocks,” Mara always replied.

“They were clocks.”

She never argued beyond that. Her grandfather’s sadness was an old room with a locked door. Mara knew better than to force it open.

Instead, she repaired what she could. Lanterns, music boxes, door hinges, mill wheels, spectacles—anything brought to the shop. People said she had inherited Elias’s clever fingers, though not his patience. Where he had once worked in meditative silence, Mara muttered, sang, and occasionally threatened stubborn machinery with a wrench.

She also collected minutes.

Not real minutes, perhaps, but moments she believed deserved to be measured. She kept them in a blue notebook under her mattress.

The time it takes rain to cross the station roof.

The time between lightning and thunder.

How long Grandfather laughs when he forgets to be unhappy.

How long a candle burns before the wax reaches the first red mark.

She had invented a system involving heartbeats, drips of water, and the length of certain songs. It was not accurate, but accuracy seemed less important than noticing.

On the first morning of winter, Mara found a box on the station platform.

No trains had come to Bellwether in thirty-eight years. Grass grew between the tracks, and a rowan tree had split the western bench in two. Yet there the box sat beneath the faded sign, untouched by frost.

It was small enough to fit under Mara’s arm. The wood was gray, bound with black iron, and engraved with a single word:

ELIAS.

Mara carried it home.

Her grandfather was stirring porridge when she entered the kitchen. At the sight of the box, the spoon slipped from his hand.

“Where did you get that?”

“The station.”

“That’s impossible.”

“In Bellwether, that narrows it down very little.”

Elias did not smile. He sank into a chair and stared at the iron lid.

“Throw it in the river,” he said.

Mara set the box on the table. “It has your name on it.”

“I know.”

“Who sent it?”

“No one.”

“That would be the impossible part.”

He looked suddenly older than he had the night before. The skin around his eyes seemed thin as paper.

“Some things arrive late,” he said. “Others arrive exactly when they mean to. Throw it away.”

Then he left the kitchen and locked himself in the workshop.

Mara waited until the porridge congealed. She knocked twice, received no answer, and carried the box to her room.

The lock had no keyhole.

She examined the hinges, tested the iron bands, and ran her fingers over the carved letters. Beneath the E, nearly hidden in the grain, she found a shallow circular hollow. It was the size of a watch.

Mara went downstairs.

Her grandfather’s old waistcoat hung behind the workshop door. He had not worn it in years, but one pocket sagged with a familiar weight. Mara slipped her hand inside and removed a tarnished silver watch.

It was the only clock in the house she was forbidden to touch.

Like all the others, it had stopped at seven minutes past nine.

The watch fit perfectly into the hollow.

The box opened with a sigh.

Inside lay a folded sheet of paper and a clockwork heart.

It was no larger than a plum. Silver ribs curved around tiny brass gears, and a red jewel sat at its center. A winding key protruded from one side. Unlike the clocks of Bellwether, the heart was moving.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Mara froze.

She had heard clocks in other towns, but this sound was different. Those clocks had seemed ordinary, almost disappointing. This one filled the room. Each tick pressed against the silence as distinctly as a footstep in snow.

She unfolded the paper.

Elias,

You asked me to make a heart that would never stop. I have done better. This heart can restore what you stole, but only if someone is willing to carry the missing hour to the tower.

Do not attempt it yourself. The hour knows you.

I am sorry.

—S.

The bedroom door creaked.

Elias stood on the threshold.

For a long moment, neither of them moved.

Then his gaze fell to the watch in the lid, and something in him broke.

“You had no right,” he whispered.

“It was in your pocket.”

“My pocket is not your property.”

“Neither is the truth, but you’ve been hoarding that too.”

His face tightened. Mara expected anger. Instead, he crossed the room, took the letter, and read it with trembling hands.

“Soren,” he said.

“Who was he?”

“My brother.”

“You never told me you had a brother.”

“No.”

“Did he make this?”

“He always was better with small things.”

The clockwork heart continued ticking between them.

Mara pointed to the letter. “What did you steal?”

Elias sat on the edge of the bed. For the first time in her life, he seemed afraid of her.

“An hour,” he said.

Outside, the wind moved through the dead grass beside the tracks.

Mara waited.

“When I was young,” Elias continued, “the tower clock did more than tell time. Bellwether was built around it. The clock kept the town aligned with the hours passing everywhere else.”

“That sounds like something from a fairy tale.”

“Most machines do, if you describe them badly enough.”

Despite herself, Mara smiled. Elias did not.

“The clock was made by our great-grandmother,” he said. “She understood mechanisms no one else could explain. The tower did not create time, but it gave time a road through Bellwether. Soren and I maintained it after our father died.

“And then your grandmother became ill.”

Mara had seen one photograph of her grandmother Lysa: dark hair, bright eyes, one hand raised to shield her face from the camera. She had died long before Mara was born.

“The doctors gave her a week,” Elias said. “Perhaps two. I thought if I could slow the tower, I could slow the illness. Soren warned me not to try. But grief makes arrogance feel like courage.

“I opened the central mechanism and removed an hour.”

Mara glanced at the silver watch. “Seven minutes past nine?”

“The tower stopped then, but the missing hour was not nine to ten or any ordinary hour. It was an hour from the town’s future. I believed Bellwether would simply fall behind. Instead, time lost its path.”

“And Grandmother?”

“Lived three more days.”

His voice cracked. He looked away.

“After she died, I tried to put the hour back. It would not let me. It had learned me, or I had damaged it, or both. Soren said someone else might return it one day, if he could build a mechanism strong enough to carry it. We quarreled. He left town. I never heard from him again.”

Mara picked up the clockwork heart. It was warm.

“Where is the missing hour?”

“In the tower.”

“And I have to carry it where?”

“To the clock chamber.”

“That seems straightforward.”

“No.” Elias seized her wrist. “You will throw the heart into the river.”

“You told me some things arrive exactly when they mean to.”

“I was trying to frighten you.”

“It worked poorly.”

“Mara, listen to me. The tower is not empty. The stolen hour has been trapped there for forty-three years. Unlived time grows hungry. It will show you what you want. It will promise what it cannot give. If it catches you, you will become part of it.”

She looked toward the window. Above the roofs, the tower rose against the winter sky. Its four black faces still displayed seven minutes past nine.

“What happens if no one returns the hour?”

“Nothing.”

“Nothing?”

“Nothing changes. Bellwether continues as it is.”

“No trains. No clocks. Children leaving because the rest of the world finds us inconvenient.”

“We survive.”

“We linger.”

His grip loosened.

Mara regretted the word as soon as she said it, but neither of them took it back.

The heart ticked in her palm.

At dusk, she climbed the tower.

Elias had hidden the key to the town hall, so Mara entered through a cellar window. Dust lay thick across the council chamber. Portraits of former mayors watched her cross the floor, all of them painted beside clocks, as if the artist had enjoyed a private joke.

The tower stairs began behind a warped oak door.

Mara carried a lantern, the clockwork heart, her blue notebook, and her grandfather’s silver watch. The first hundred steps were ordinary stone. Then the walls narrowed, the air grew warm, and she heard rain.

Not rain outside. Rain from years ago.

It whispered beyond the walls, accompanied by laughter and the squeal of train brakes. Mara paused at a narrow window. Below, the town lay dry and dark. Yet in the glass she saw a summer platform crowded with passengers.

A girl stood among them.

She had dark hair and bright eyes. Lysa.

Mara knew at once.

Her grandmother appeared no older than Mara was now. She held two train tickets and laughed at someone outside the frame.

“Mara,” Elias’s voice called from above.

She turned from the window.

“Grandfather?”

“Mara, come quickly.”

She climbed.

At the next landing, a door stood open. Elias’s workshop glowed beyond it, though it could not possibly be there. Every clock was ticking. Her grandfather stood at his bench, not old and stooped, but tall, with steady hands.

He fitted a gear into place and looked at her.

“There you are,” he said. “I’ve been waiting.”

Mara’s throat tightened.

On the wall, the mechanical bird burst from its clock and sang.

“This isn’t real,” she said.

“Real enough.”

“You’re not my grandfather.”

“Does that matter? Stay, and he can be this again. Young. Unburdened. You can have every question answered. Every clock repaired.”

The clockwork heart beat faster in Mara’s pocket.

She opened her notebook and wrote: The time it takes a lie to learn your voice.

The letters trembled.

When she looked up, the workshop was gone. Only a blank stone wall remained.

Mara continued.

The stairs twisted. Bells rang without moving. Through one window she saw herself as a child, asleep against Elias’s chest while he hummed. Through another, she saw a future in which she boarded a train and never returned. Then she saw the same future altered: Elias collapsing on the station platform, one hand reaching after her.

“Stop,” Mara whispered.

The vision vanished.

Higher still, the tower showed her things that had never happened. Her parents, alive and smiling. A workshop in a great city with MARA VENN, MASTER HOROLOGIST painted on the window. A crowded table where no chair was empty. A hundred versions of herself, each certain she had chosen correctly.

The missing hour did not speak again. It did not need to.

Every step became a choice.

Mara began counting heartbeats. At five hundred, she should have reached the clock chamber. At a thousand, she knew the staircase was lengthening.

She stopped.

The steps continued upward beyond the lantern’s reach. Below, they spiraled into darkness.

“You’re wasting my time,” she said.

The tower groaned.

“No,” Mara corrected herself. “You’re trying to make me waste it.”

She took the clockwork heart from her pocket. Its gears spun wildly now, the red jewel flashing. On instinct, she held it against the wall.

The stones became transparent.

Behind them was no masonry, but a vast dark space filled with floating fragments: a cup tipping from a table, a kiss interrupted, a door forever half-open, thousands of snowflakes suspended before touching the ground. At the center burned a band of pale gold light.

The stolen hour.

It had been beside her all along.

Mara pushed the heart against the wall.

The silver ribs opened.

The golden light recoiled. The tower shook so violently that Mara fell to one knee. The lantern shattered, but no darkness came; the hour blazed through the stones.

Images struck her in rapid succession.

Lysa dying.

Elias removing a shining gear from the great clock.

Soren shouting.

A train leaving with Soren aboard.

Elias alone in the tower, trying to force the stolen hour back into place while blood ran from his hands.

Then a final memory, one Mara understood did not belong to Elias.

Soren sat in a distant workshop, older, coughing, his fingers assembling the clockwork heart. Beside him lay letters addressed to Bellwether, all returned unopened because no train went there.

“I’m sorry,” he whispered as he closed the box. “It took longer than I thought.”

The golden band twisted around Mara’s arm.

Cold flooded her.

The hour was not merely light. It was possibility compressed until it had become pain. Every unlived second demanded to be lived at once. Mara felt herself age and grow young. She remembered events that had not occurred. She stood at a hundred graves, received a hundred kisses, missed a hundred trains.

The heart’s silver ribs snapped shut around the light.

Silence fell.

The wall became stone again.

In Mara’s hands, the mechanism had changed. Gold shone between its gears, and the red jewel pulsed like blood.

Ahead of her, the stairs ended at a wooden door.

She entered the clock chamber.

The great mechanism filled the room. Gears taller than houses stood locked together beneath ropes, counterweights, and iron axles. Frost silvered the teeth. Four shafts extended toward the clockfaces, all motionless.

At the center was an empty socket shaped like a heart.

Mara approached it.

“Don’t.”

She turned.

Elias stood in the doorway, gasping, one hand clutching the frame.

“How did you get here?”

“I built half these stairs.”

“You said the hour knew you.”

“It does.”

The chamber darkened.

Golden light seeped from the heart, forming a figure between them. Lysa appeared, exactly as she had in the station window. Dark hair. Bright eyes. Two train tickets in her hand.

Elias made a wounded sound.

“Ly,” he whispered.

She smiled at him.

“You kept me waiting.”

Mara watched her grandfather take one step forward.

Then another.

“Elias,” the figure said. “We can have it back.”

He began to cry.

Mara had never seen him cry. Not when arthritis took his craft from him, not when fever nearly killed Mara at eleven, not even when speaking of Lysa’s death. His grief had always been silent and disciplined.

Now it bent him like wind bends wheat.

“All I wanted,” he said, “was more time.”

Lysa held out her hand.

The clockwork heart strained in Mara’s grip. Tiny gears screamed.

She understood then. The missing hour was not trying to escape. It was trying to remain stolen. It had survived on regret for forty-three years, feeding on every life imagined instead of lived.

Mara took the silver watch from her pocket.

“Grandfather.”

He did not look at her.

She opened the watch case and held it toward him. The hands still pointed to seven minutes past nine.

“You got three days,” she said.

His face twisted.

“I know.”

“No. You’ve spent forty-three years calling them a failure. But you got three days with her.”

Lysa’s smile faltered.

Mara stepped closer.

“What did you do with them?”

Elias stared at the figure.

“We went to the river,” he said faintly. “She wanted to see the ice form.”

“What else?”

“We burned supper. We argued about whether starlings leave before the first snow.”

“What else?”

“She cut her hair because she was tired of brushing it. I told her it looked terrible.”

A laugh escaped him through the tears.

The golden figure flickered.

“She knew I was lying,” he continued. “She always knew.”

Lysa lowered her hand.

“What else?” Mara asked.

“On the last morning, she made me open the curtains. She said if the sun had made the effort to rise, the least we could do was look at it.”

The apparition cracked from head to foot, light spilling through it.

Elias closed his eyes.

“She told me not to make a shrine of my sorrow.”

The false Lysa shattered.

Gold rushed across the chamber. The great gears groaned. Mara nearly dropped the heart, but Elias caught her hands in his.

Together, they carried it to the mechanism.

“You must do it,” he said.

“We can do it together.”

“No. Some repairs belong to those who come after.”

His hands were still wrapped around hers, steady now despite their age.

Mara pressed the clockwork heart into the empty socket.

For one breath, nothing happened.

Then the heart beat.

Once.

The sound shook dust from the beams.

Twice.

Ice broke from the gears.

Three times.

The hands on the four clockfaces moved.

The great mechanism turned with a roar. Wheels engaged wheels, teeth meeting teeth after forty-three years apart. Ropes snapped taut. Counterweights plunged into darkness. Above them, the tower bell swung.

Its first note rolled over Bellwether like thunder.

Mara and Elias stood hand in hand as the town received the missing hour.

Every clock awoke.

In kitchens, forgotten pendulums swung. In cupboards, watches began ticking. The mechanical bird in Elias’s workshop completed the song interrupted four decades before. Sundials released their crooked shadows. Bells chimed in empty houses, startling sleepers from their beds.

And at the abandoned station, the signal lamp turned green.

The restoration was not gentle.

Time rushed through Bellwether, eager to fill every neglected corner. Milk soured. Old paint peeled. A dead elm collapsed beside the church. People dreamed, in a single instant, of all the birthdays, departures, and endings they had pretended would never come.

Then the hour passed.

The tower mechanism settled into a steady rhythm.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Mara looked through the eastern clockface. Dawn touched the roofs, though it felt as if the night had only begun.

“What time is it?” she asked.

Elias examined the great clock.

“Seven minutes past ten.”

They laughed until the bell rang again.

In the weeks that followed, Bellwether discovered punctuality with mixed enthusiasm.

The baker objected to customers asking when the bread would be ready. The schoolchildren protested that lessons had once ended much earlier, though no one could prove it. The mayor ordered a clock for every public building, then spent most meetings looking anxiously at the hands.

The railway sent an inspector. He arrived with three watches and checked each of them at the town boundary. When none stopped, he removed his hat in astonishment.

By spring, workers had cleared the tracks.

Elias returned to the workshop. His fingers still trembled, but he no longer hid from the clocks. Mara did the delicate work while he advised her, often incorrectly, from a chair by the stove.

One rainy morning, a parcel arrived from the city archive. It contained Soren’s returned letters and notice of his death many years earlier. Elias read every page. Then he placed his brother’s photograph beside Lysa’s on the mantel, where sorrow could be remembered without being worshiped.

The first train reached Bellwether at twelve minutes past noon on the first day of summer.

Nearly the whole town gathered on the platform. Children covered their ears as the engine whistled. Steam swept along the tracks, and the doors opened with the solemnity of a miracle.

Mara stood beside Elias beneath the restored station clock.

She carried a traveling case and her blue notebook. A school in the capital had offered her a place to study mechanical engineering. She would leave for one year, perhaps two.

Elias checked his watch.

“You have four minutes,” he said.

“That’s enough.”

“For what?”

Mara looked at the roofs, the tower, the workshop chimney, and the rowan tree growing through the broken bench. She listened to the platform’s noise: greetings, warnings, luggage scraping over stone, the stationmaster announcing a departure he had waited most of his life to announce.

“For noticing,” she said.

She opened her notebook.

The time it takes to leave home, she wrote, is not the same as the time it takes home to leave you.

“That sounds very wise,” Elias said over her shoulder.

“It is private.”

“Then you should develop smaller handwriting.”

The conductor called for passengers to board.

Mara embraced her grandfather. He held her tightly, but only for a moment.

“Do not waste your time,” he said.

She pulled back. “You mean I should work hard?”

“No. Work when it is time to work. Rest when it is time to rest. Miss trains occasionally. Burn supper when necessary. Waste a little time on purpose, but never by accident.”

“That is very complicated advice.”

“Time is a complicated machine.”

Mara boarded.

The train lurched, hesitated, and began to move. Elias walked beside her window until the platform ended. She waved until he became a small figure beneath the station clock, then a blur, then a memory already changing shape.

Bellwether receded behind fields bright with summer.

Mara sat back and listened to the rhythm of the wheels.

For the first time, the sound did not remind her of something lost.

It sounded like a clock, immense and unafraid, carrying her forward.

The Town That Borrowed Tomorrow

Every morning at six, the clock in the tower of Bellwether struck thirteen times.

No one in town remembered when it had begun. The oldest residents said it had always done so, even before lightning split the elm beside the courthouse, even before the river changed its course and left the old mill standing beside a field of weeds. Visitors often counted the chimes with puzzled expressions, but the people of Bellwether rarely noticed.

They had other things to worry about.

Bellwether was a town forever waiting for better days. Its paint peeled in long curls. Its roads collected potholes deep enough to drown a boot. The bakery sold yesterday’s bread, the school used maps with vanished countries, and the mayor campaigned every four years on the promise of repairing the fountain in the square.

Still, everyone agreed that tomorrow would be different.

Tomorrow the mill might reopen. Tomorrow the highway might bring travelers. Tomorrow the rain would stop—or begin. Tomorrow someone would fix the fountain.

Only Mara Vale disliked the word.

She was seventeen, worked in her father’s clock shop, and trusted only things she could take apart. Springs had reasons for tightening. Gears had reasons for turning. A clock might lie, but it lied predictably, and once Mara found the fault, she could make it honest again.

People were harder.

Her father, Elias, had once been the finest clockmaker in three counties. Now his hands shook when he held small tools, so Mara handled most repairs while he sat near the window, wrapped in a brown coat even during summer.

“Patience,” he always told her. “Every broken thing reveals itself eventually.”

Mara did not believe him. Some broken things simply became accustomed to being broken.

The tower clock was one of them.

For years, Elias had been responsible for maintaining it. Every Sunday, he climbed the spiral staircase carrying a leather case of tools. He oiled the mechanism, adjusted the pendulum, and descended with dust on his sleeves. Yet the clock still struck thirteen.

“Why don’t you fix it?” Mara had asked when she was ten.

“It isn’t broken,” he replied.

At twelve she asked again.

“It keeps the time Bellwether asks of it.”

At sixteen she stopped asking.

Then, on the first morning of November, the tower struck only twelve times.

The silence after the final chime woke Mara more sharply than thunder.

She sat up in the narrow room above the shop. Outside, dawn pressed a gray face against the glass. She waited for the thirteenth bell.

It did not come.

Downstairs, something shattered.

Mara ran into the workshop and found Elias standing among pieces of a teacup. He was staring toward the tower.

“What happened?” she asked.

He turned to her, and in his expression she saw something she had never seen before.

Fear.

“Lock the door,” he said.

“Why?”

“Do it, Mara.”

She slid the bolt into place. Outside, people were emerging from their houses in coats and nightclothes. They gathered in the square, murmuring and pointing upward.

The tower clock showed six.

Exactly six.

Elias crossed the workshop and pulled the curtains.

“You need to leave town,” he said.

Mara laughed, but the sound died quickly. “Because the clock missed a chime?”

“Because it didn’t.”

He removed a key from around his neck. It was small, silver, and shaped like the skeleton of a fish. Mara had seen him wear it all her life, but he had never explained what it opened.

Elias went to the tall cabinet behind his workbench and unlocked a drawer she had assumed was decorative. Inside lay a dark blue ledger.

He placed it on the table.

The cover was stamped with one word:

TOMORROW.

Mara opened it. The pages were filled with names, dates, and numbers written in several kinds of ink. She recognized families from Bellwether: Ward, Fen, Holloway, Pike. Beside each name were entries such as “three hours,” “two days,” “one summer.”

Near the bottom of the last page, she found her own name.

Mara Vale — seventeen years.

She stared at her father.

“What is this?”

“A debt.”

“To whom?”

“To the clock.”

Outside, the townspeople had begun knocking on doors. Someone shouted that there would be a meeting at the courthouse.

Elias lowered himself into a chair.

“Bellwether should have disappeared seventy-three years ago,” he said. “There was a flood. Not like the little floods we have now. The dam broke in the hills, and the river came through at midnight. By sunrise, there was nothing left from the railway station to the church.”

Mara frowned. “That never happened.”

“It did. Then it didn’t.”

He spoke carefully, as though each sentence were a fragile object.

“My grandfather was the tower keeper. The night of the flood, he discovered something inside the clock—a chamber built behind the mechanism, older than the tower itself. In that chamber was a second pendulum. It did not measure time. It stored it.

“He stopped the clock at one minute before midnight and wound the hidden mechanism. Bellwether borrowed one day from its own future. The flood was delayed. Everyone escaped to high ground. When the water came the next night, the town was empty.”

Mara looked again at the ledger. “You can’t borrow time.”

“No,” said Elias. “But Bellwether can.”

She wanted to accuse him of lying, yet his face held no trace of invention.

“What does the thirteenth chime mean?”

“It is the borrowed hour being added to the day. A little piece of tomorrow, given to us in advance.”

“And the debt?”

“Must be repaid.”

Mara ran a finger down the page until she reached her name.

“Why do I owe seventeen years?”

Elias closed his eyes.

“When you were born, you did not breathe.”

The workshop seemed to contract around them.

“The doctor tried everything,” he continued. “Your mother was dying. I had already lost her, and I could not lose you too. So I went to the tower and asked for time.”

“Seventeen years?”

“One year at first. Then another. Each year, on your birthday, the clock asked whether I wished to renew the loan.”

Mara stepped back from the ledger.

“And you said yes.”

“I thought I could find another way.”

“But you didn’t.”

“I’m sorry.”

The knocking at the door grew louder.

“Elias!” called Mayor Rusk. “Open up! We need you at the tower.”

Mara felt as if she were still waiting for the thirteenth chime, suspended in the silence before it.

“What happens when the clock stops lending?”

Her father looked at her.

“Everything borrowed becomes due.”

The townspeople escorted them across the square.

A cold wind moved between the buildings, carrying dry leaves and the smell of iron from the river. Mayor Rusk walked ahead, dressed in trousers, slippers, and a red ceremonial sash. Behind him came half the town.

Everyone had noticed something wrong.

Mrs. Fen’s orchard, green the previous evening, was now bare and twisted. Mr. Ward’s newborn son had woken with the face of a six-year-old child. The post office had received bags of letters bearing dates from the next decade. Along the river, fish floated on the surface, silver bellies turned toward the sky.

At the foot of the tower, the mayor faced Elias.

“Fix it,” he demanded.

“I may not be able to.”

“You’ve fixed it for thirty years.”

“No. I maintained the loan.”

Uneasy murmurs moved through the crowd.

Elias explained. He told them about the flood, the hidden pendulum, and the cost of borrowed time. Some called him mad. Others fell silent because they remembered private miracles they had never understood.

A harvest saved after an early frost.

A child recovering from fever.

A soldier returning from war after his mother begged the tower for one more tomorrow.

Mayor Rusk’s face reddened. “Even if this nonsense is true, start the clock again.”

“The mechanism will not accept another winding.”

“Then make it.”

“I warned the council ten years ago. And five years ago.”

“You spoke in riddles!”

“You preferred them.”

The mayor turned to the crowd. “We cannot surrender our town because an old man has lost his nerve.”

Cheers answered him, though not many.

Elias gave Mara his leather tool case.

“Come with me.”

They climbed the tower stairs while the mayor and several councilmen followed. The spiral seemed endless. Narrow windows flashed past, offering glimpses of Bellwether below: slate roofs, empty streets, the dark thread of the river.

At the top, Elias unlocked the clock room.

The ordinary mechanism occupied most of the chamber. Great brass gears interlocked beneath the four clock faces. The main pendulum hung motionless, its blade gleaming in the dim light.

Behind it, Elias opened a narrow iron door.

Cold blue light spilled out.

Mara stepped into a chamber no larger than a closet. At its center swung a thin glass pendulum, though nothing appeared to drive it. With each movement, mist gathered inside the glass and vanished.

The walls were covered in names.

Thousands of them.

Some had been carved deeply. Others were faint as scratches in ice.

Mara saw her mother’s name.

Lydia Vale — nine minutes.

“How much did she borrow?” Mara whispered.

“None,” Elias said. “She gave nine minutes. Long enough for me to reach the tower and ask for your first year.”

Mara touched the carving. Her mother had died before Mara opened her eyes, and all her life Elias had described Lydia’s voice, her laughter, her habit of leaving books upside down. He had never said that her final nine minutes had been spent waiting for a clock.

A crack appeared in the glass pendulum.

The sound was soft, but everyone heard it.

Elias pushed Mara toward the door. Blue mist leaked from the fracture and crawled across the floor.

“What is that?” the mayor asked.

“Unspent time,” Elias replied.

“Then use it.”

“It belongs to no one.”

“Everything belongs to someone.”

Mayor Rusk lunged past him and grabbed the pendulum.

The tower bell struck.

Once.

The sound threw everyone to the floor. The clockwork began to turn backward. Gears screamed against their teeth.

Through the clock face, Mara saw the sun leap above the eastern hills, race across the sky, and vanish in the west. Night followed for the span of a breath. Then the sun rose again.

All across Bellwether, years passed in seconds.

Paint vanished from walls. Chimneys collapsed. Saplings thickened into trees, shed leaves, and bloomed again. The river widened and shrank. The crowd below scattered as children grew into adults and adults bent beneath sudden age.

Mayor Rusk released the pendulum, but his hand had withered to bone.

“Stop it!” he shrieked.

Elias dragged a brass lever beside the hidden door. It would not move.

“Mara, the case!”

She opened the leather tool case. He seized a wrench, then dropped it when his hand trembled.

“You do it,” he said. “Loosen the anchor bolt.”

“Which one?”

“The black bolt under the escapement.”

Mara climbed into the mechanism. The gears turned around her, huge and merciless. She found the black bolt and fitted the wrench over it.

“It’s stuck!”

“Clockwise first. Then back.”

“That tightens it.”

“Trust me.”

She turned it clockwise. The bolt shifted a fraction. Then she pulled in the opposite direction with all her strength.

It came free.

The hidden pendulum stopped.

Outside, time halted its wild advance.

The silence that followed was filled by the groaning of the tower.

Mara crawled out. Elias lay against the wall, his face pale. Mayor Rusk had fled down the stairs.

“How much time passed?” she asked.

“I don’t know.”

They looked through the clock face.

Bellwether had changed.

The square was cracked and crowded with tall grass. Several buildings had collapsed. Others looked newly painted, though no one had painted them. The old fountain flowed clear and bright for the first time in Mara’s memory.

People wandered below in shock. Some had aged by decades. Others had become younger. Mrs. Fen, who had been eighty-three, stood beside the fountain with black hair and the bewildered face of a girl. The Ward child was now a grown man holding his weeping mother, who had not changed at all.

Time had not moved evenly. It had gone wherever debt drew it.

Mara looked at her hands. They remained the same.

Then she saw her father.

Elias’s brown coat had become too large. His white hair was darkening at the roots. The lines around his eyes faded until he looked not old, but middle-aged.

“You’re younger,” she said.

“No.” He touched his face, understanding before she did. “The clock is taking back the years it lent you. From me.”

Mara looked toward the ledger, which had fallen open on the floor.

Her entry had changed.

Mara Vale — seventeen years  
Paid: six years, four months, eleven days

She felt cold.

“How?”

“I offered my own years as security.”

“You never told me.”

“I hoped you would never need to know.”

The tower shook again. Dust fell from the beams.

Elias stood with difficulty and examined the mechanism.

“The anchor bolt stopped the release, but the pendulum is broken. It won’t hold.”

“So we repair it.”

“Glass cannot be repaired while it contains time.”

“Then we empty it.”

“That would release every debt at once.”

Mara stared at the crack. Blue mist pulsed within it like light beneath deep water.

“What happens to me when mine is due?”

Her father’s silence answered.

“No,” she said.

“Mara—”

“No. You don’t get to decide this alone again.”

Below them, voices rose in the square. The townspeople were discovering the ledger’s truth, one altered body at a time. Soon they would climb the tower and demand another tomorrow.

Elias gripped the edge of the mechanism. “There is one possibility.”

“I’m listening.”

“The clock accepts exchanges. Time for time.”

“You already gave yours.”

“Not enough. I have perhaps seven years left that it can take.”

“And I owe more than ten.”

“Others may volunteer.”

Mara almost laughed. She imagined Mayor Rusk offering a decade of his life. She imagined neighbors arguing over minutes while the town fell apart around them.

“No,” she said. “There has to be another way.”

She studied the mechanism as she would a damaged pocket watch. Ignore its purpose. Observe its behavior. The pendulum stored time. The ledger recorded ownership. The clock released debts when it could no longer maintain them.

A system, not a curse.

“Why does it lend time?” she asked.

Elias blinked. “What?”

“Machines don’t act without force. What powers it?”

“No one knows.”

“Your grandfather wound it.”

“He began the bargain.”

“With whom?”

The bell above them gave a quiet, uncertain tremor.

Mara looked at the four clock faces. Each showed a different hour now. East showed dawn. West showed sunset. North stood at midnight, south at noon.

Not one clock.

Four.

“Bellwether didn’t borrow from its own future,” she said. “It borrowed from all its possible futures.”

Elias stared at her.

She pointed to the names on the wall. “Every time someone asked for more time, the clock chose a tomorrow where they survived. But the other tomorrows didn’t disappear. They were stored here.”

The blue mist moved faster.

“Possibility,” Elias whispered.

“Not time. That’s why debts are uneven. The clock is trying to force all the different futures back into one.”

“Even if you’re right, how does that help us?”

Mara turned to the main mechanism. Four shafts ran from the clock faces into a central gear. Three were engaged.

The fourth, connected to the north face, was locked by a silver pin.

“What happens at midnight?” she asked.

“The clock resets the daily loan.”

“But it has never reached midnight, has it? Not truly. The thirteenth hour keeps the day open.”

Understanding spread across Elias’s face.

“The town has been living in the same borrowed day for seventy-three years.”

“And every tomorrow has been piling up behind it.”

The tower door burst open.

Mayor Rusk entered with a crowd behind him. His skeletal hand was wrapped in cloth. Fear had stripped the authority from his face, leaving only hunger.

“Start the clock,” he ordered.

“We can’t,” Mara said.

“You will.”

“If we do, it gets worse.”

“My wife is ninety years old. She was thirty-nine this morning.”

“And my father is dying for years I borrowed without knowing.”

“That is not my concern.”

“No,” Mara said. “That is the problem.”

The crowd shifted. Some looked ashamed. Others looked toward the pendulum with desperate eyes.

Elias stepped between them and Mara. “She may know how to end this.”

“End it?” cried Mrs. Holloway. She held the hand of a boy who had been her husband the day before. “Can she restore us?”

“I don’t know,” Mara said.

“Then wind the clock.”

“No more borrowing.”

Mayor Rusk reached for her. Elias blocked him, but the mayor struck the old clockmaker with his wrapped hand. Elias fell against the mechanism.

The glass pendulum cracked again.

Blue light flooded the chamber.

The townspeople froze.

In the mist, figures appeared: countless versions of themselves, overlapping like reflections in broken mirrors. Mara saw Mrs. Holloway old, young, widowed, surrounded by children, and alone. She saw Mayor Rusk wearing a judge’s robe, a prisoner’s coat, a farmer’s muddy boots. She saw Elias dying as an infant, living past a hundred, leaving Bellwether before Mara was born.

And Mara saw herself.

A breathless baby in her mother’s arms.

A child running beside the river.

A woman repairing clocks in a city of glass.

A grave beneath an elm tree.

A hundred possible lives, none more real than the others.

The mist touched Mayor Rusk. He cried out as his reflections pulled at him from every side.

Mara climbed onto the frame of the main gear.

“What are you doing?” Elias shouted.

“Letting tomorrow happen.”

She seized the silver pin connected to the midnight face.

It would not move.

Below, the crowd surged toward the pendulum. Some begged for lost youth. Some called the names of the dead. The tower filled with shimmering possibilities.

Mara braced both feet against the gear and pulled.

The pin slid halfway out.

The mechanism roared.

Every clock in Bellwether began to strike: mantel clocks, pocket watches, schoolroom clocks, the little brass alarm clock beside Mara’s bed. Their voices rose through the broken town in a storm of bells.

Elias climbed beside her.

“Together,” he said.

They gripped the pin.

“On three,” Mara said.

The tower bell struck once.

“One.”

Again.

“Two.”

A third chime shook the windows.

“Three.”

They pulled.

The silver pin came free.

For the first time in seventy-three years, the tower clock reached midnight.

Darkness fell.

Not ordinary darkness, but a complete absence of all unfinished things. Mara felt the futures around her collapse, not destroyed but released. The town that flourished, the town that vanished, the town buried by water, the town forgotten by roads—all drifted away like seeds on a wind.

Only one Bellwether remained.

The choice was made not by the clock, nor by Mara, but by the simple weight of the present.

The glass pendulum shattered.

Mara heard her father call her name.

Then tomorrow began.

She woke beside the river.

Morning sunlight warmed her face. Grass pressed against her palms, wet with dew. For a moment she listened, expecting the tower to strike thirteen.

It chimed seven times.

Mara sat up.

Bellwether stood along the river, battered but alive. Some buildings were gone. The courthouse roof had collapsed. The fountain in the square was dry again.

People emerged slowly from houses and fields. Most looked as they had the previous morning. Mrs. Fen was old. Mr. Ward’s son was a baby. Mrs. Holloway stood beside her gray-haired husband.

Mayor Rusk stared at his restored hand.

Elias lay a few feet from Mara.

She crawled to him.

His hair was white again, his face lined, his hands trembling. But he opened his eyes.

“How old are you?” Mara asked.

He smiled faintly. “Rude question.”

She laughed and pressed her forehead to his.

Later, they found the blue ledger beneath the tower ruins. Every page was blank except the last.

There, in fresh black ink, were seven words:

WHAT IS GIVEN FREELY IS NOT A DEBT.

The tower clock never ran again.

Some wanted it rebuilt, but the town council—under a new mayor—voted to leave its face frozen at midnight. They repaired the roads instead. Then the school. Then, after considerable argument, the fountain.

Bellwether did not become prosperous overnight.

The mill remained closed. Storms ruined crops. Children grew ill. People missed trains, made mistakes, and received news they wished they could postpone. Without the promise of borrowed time, problems had to be faced while they were happening.

At first, the town found this exhausting.

Then, gradually, it found it useful.

Mrs. Fen planted new trees, though she knew she might never sit in their shade. Mr. Holloway apologized to his wife for a quarrel they had carried for nineteen years. The former mayor left town, returned six months later, and began repairing roofs without asking anyone to elect him.

Mara took over the clock shop when Elias’s hands could no longer hold a screwdriver.

She kept the sign with his name above the door.

Years later, travelers still came to Bellwether and asked why the ruined tower had four faces showing four different times. The townspeople gave various answers. Some spoke of floods. Some spoke of miracles. Some dismissed the whole thing as an old local legend.

Mara never argued with them.

She married no one, though she loved more than once. She traveled for a few years, repaired a clock in a city of glass, and returned when Elias became too weak to climb the stairs above the shop. He died one spring morning with his hand in hers, after complaining that the bedside clock was running three seconds slow.

Mara buried him beneath the elm near the courthouse.

Not the grave she had seen in the mist. Another one.

Her own.

On the morning she turned seventy, Mara opened the shop and found a girl waiting outside with a broken watch. The girl placed it on the counter.

“It belonged to my mother,” she said. “Can you fix it?”

Mara examined the watch. Its spring was snapped, its hands bent, its case green with age.

“Maybe,” she said.

“How long will it take?”

Mara looked through the window at the ruined tower. Sunlight shone through the empty clock face. Beyond it, clouds gathered over the river, promising rain by evening.

She picked up her smallest screwdriver.

“As long as it takes,” she said.

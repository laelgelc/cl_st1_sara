The lighthouse had been dark for thirty-seven years when Mara Vale saw it blink.

Once.

Twice.

Then, after a pause long enough for her to doubt herself, a third time.

She stood at the kitchen window with her hands submerged in gray dishwater, watching rain drag silver lines down the glass. Beyond the crooked roofs of Bellweather, beyond the harbor crowded with empty boats, Blackglass Lighthouse rose from its island like the last black tooth in an old man’s mouth.

No light had burned there since the night Mara was born—the night the sea wall broke, seven fishermen drowned, and her father vanished.

Yet there it was again.

A pale beam turned slowly across the water and disappeared into the storm.

Mara pulled her hands from the sink.

“Gran,” she called.

From the sitting room came the soft scrape of a chair. “What is it?”

“The lighthouse.”

The scraping stopped.

Mara’s grandmother appeared in the doorway, small and narrow in her black dress. Elian Vale was seventy-eight, but her eyes remained sharp enough to find a lie in a locked drawer.

“What about it?”

“It’s lit.”

Gran did not go to the window. “No, it isn’t.”

“I saw it.”

“You saw lightning.”

“There wasn’t any thunder.”

“Heat lightning, then.”

“In November?”

Gran’s mouth tightened. “Finish the dishes.”

The beam swept over the harbor again, brushing the wet masts and shuttered warehouses with white fire.

Mara pointed. “There!”

This time Gran looked.

For one heartbeat, fear broke across her face. Then she reached out and pulled the curtain shut.

“Leave it alone,” she said.

Mara had learned, over her twenty-two years, that there were subjects on which her grandmother could be persuaded and subjects on which she became a stone wall. Blackglass belonged to the second kind. So did Mara’s father. So did the night of the flood.

Usually, Mara obeyed.

That night, she waited until Gran had gone to bed.

Then she took her coat, the brass key she wore around her neck, and the small red boat tied beneath the eastern pier.

The boat had once belonged to her father. His name, Tomas, was still visible under peeling paint along the bow. As a child, Mara had traced the letters with her fingertips and imagined the man they belonged to: broad-shouldered, laughing, smelling of salt and tobacco. Gran kept no portraits of him, but people in town said Mara had his dark hair and his troublesome curiosity.

The harbor water lifted and dropped beneath the rain. No sane person would have crossed to Blackglass Island in such weather, but the storm had begun to weaken, retreating toward the northern cliffs. Mara started the motor, and the little boat coughed into life.

The journey took fifteen minutes.

Every few moments the beam circled above her. It did not shine like an electric lamp. It had a warmer color, almost golden, and the rain seemed to bend away from it.

Blackglass Island had no proper dock, only a stone landing worn smooth by decades of tides. Mara tied the boat to a rusted ring and climbed the steps.

Up close, the lighthouse looked less abandoned than it should have. The iron door showed no rust. Weeds grew on either side of the path, but none crossed it. Rainwater streamed down the black stone walls, making them glisten like polished bone.

Mara tried the door.

Locked.

She drew the brass key from under her shirt.

Gran had given it to her on her sixteenth birthday with no explanation. It was too large for any lock in their house, and its handle bore the same symbol carved above the lighthouse door: an open eye surrounded by nine waves.

Mara pushed the key into the lock.

It turned.

Inside, the air smelled of oil, wet stone, and something faintly sweet—apples, perhaps, or the memory of them. A spiral staircase wound upward around a hollow shaft. High overhead, machinery clicked and breathed.

“Hello?” Mara called.

Her voice climbed the stairs and came back smaller.

No one answered.

She shut the door and began to ascend.

The lighthouse had twelve levels. She knew this because she counted them, partly to steady herself. Each landing held a narrow room: a kitchen, a storage chamber, two bedrooms, a workshop. Dust lay thick on most surfaces, yet she found fresh footprints on the seventh level.

They were bare.

They led upward.

By the tenth landing, Mara’s legs burned. On the eleventh, she found a green coat hanging from a peg. Water dripped from its hem, forming a dark pool on the floor.

She knew that coat.

It had hung for years in Gran’s wardrobe, hidden behind winter blankets. Mara had discovered it at thirteen. When she asked about it, Gran had snatched it away and said it belonged to a dead man.

Mara touched the wet sleeve.

A floorboard creaked above her.

She looked up.

“Who’s there?”

A man answered.

“Mara?”

She forgot how to breathe.

Footsteps descended. Bare feet appeared first, then brown trousers rolled to the knee, a white shirt, and a weathered face framed by dark hair.

The man stopped three stairs above her.

He looked no older than thirty.

A faded burn scar ran from his left jaw to his collar. His eyes were gray.

Mara knew those eyes. She saw them every morning in the mirror.

“Oh,” the man whispered. “You grew up.”

She backed against the wall.

He reached toward her, then let his hand fall. “I suppose I shouldn’t— You don’t know me.”

“You’re Tomas Vale.”

“Yes.”

“My father is dead.”

“No.”

“My father disappeared thirty-seven years ago.”

“Thirty-seven?” He glanced toward the lantern room. “No. It’s been…” He rubbed his face. “Three days. Four, perhaps.”

Mara laughed once, without humor. “You expect me to believe that?”

“I don’t know what I expect.” His gaze dropped to the key around her neck. “Your grandmother gave you that?”

“Yes.”

“Then she knew this might happen.”

“Knew what?”

“That the light would call you.”

The machinery overhead gave a deep metallic groan. The tower trembled, and a bright arc swept past the landing window.

Tomas looked upward.

“We don’t have much time.”

“For what?”

“To put it out.”

Mara stared at him. “You lit it.”

“I woke it. That’s different.”

He climbed past her, moving quickly down the stairs. After a moment, Mara followed.

“You vanished the night the sea wall broke,” she said.

“I went to the lighthouse.”

“Why?”

“Because it called me, just as it called you.”

“Gran said there was a storm.”

“There was.”

“Seven men drowned.”

His steps faltered.

He turned on the landing below. “Seven?”

Mara named them. She had memorized the list from the bronze plaque in the churchyard.

With each name, Tomas’s face grew paler.

“I tried to warn them,” he said. “The harbor master wouldn’t listen. I came here because I thought the old lens might still work. I wanted to guide the boats home.”

“You expect me to believe you’ve been in this tower for thirty-seven years without aging?”

“No.” He looked suddenly exhausted. “But I believe it.”

He entered the workshop on the seventh floor and lit a lamp. Tools covered the benches: pliers, hammers, files, instruments Mara could not name. On the far wall hung maps of coastlines that did not exist. One showed Bellweather beneath a vast red sea. Another showed the town several miles inland, surrounded by desert.

Tomas lifted a leather-bound book from a shelf.

“The first lighthouse was built here three hundred years ago,” he said. “Not to warn ships away from the rocks. That story came later. It was built to warn us.”

“Warn us about what?”

“The light.”

“That makes no sense.”

He opened the book. Most pages were covered in cramped handwriting, but several contained drawings of the lens above them: concentric circles surrounding a dark center.

“The lantern doesn’t burn oil,” he said. “It burns time.”

Mara shook her head.

“I know how it sounds. I didn’t understand either. Not until I lit it. The moment the lens began to turn, the storm stopped. Outside this tower, everything froze. Rain hung in the air. Waves became walls of glass. I thought I had saved the harbor.”

He turned several pages and showed her a drawing of a shadowy human figure trapped inside the lens.

“Then someone came down the stairs.”

“Who?”

“Me.”

The tower groaned again.

Tomas closed the book.

“He was older. Sixty, maybe seventy. He knew my name. Knew yours, too, though you had been born only hours before. He told me that every time the light is lit, it creates a path between what happened and what might have happened. The longer it burns, the wider the path becomes.”

“And then?”

“I didn’t believe him. We fought. I pushed him into the lens.”

Mara stared.

“He didn’t fall,” Tomas continued. “He vanished. The storm began moving again. I ran downstairs, but when I opened the front door, the island was gone. There was only fog. I’ve been trying to find my way back ever since.”

“For three days.”

“For me.”

Mara thought of the fresh coat, the bare footprints, the untouched iron door. She thought of Gran’s face when the light appeared.

“You said we have to put it out.”

“Yes.”

“Then do it.”

“I can’t.”

“Why not?”

“Because I’m the one who lit it.”

Mara looked down at the brass key.

Tomas nodded. “It has to be you.”

They climbed to the lantern room.

The final door opened onto blinding gold.

The lens filled the center of the chamber, taller than any man, turning without visible gears. Light spiraled inside its glass panels. At first Mara saw only fire. Then images appeared.

A little girl ran along the harbor holding her father’s hand.

Mara stopped.

The child was herself at five or six, red boots splashing through puddles. Beside her walked an older Tomas with gray in his hair. They were laughing.

The image turned with the lens and vanished.

Another took its place.

Gran sat alone in the kitchen, younger but already bowed with sorrow. Beyond the window, the harbor was empty. A newspaper lay on the table. Its headline read: FINAL BODY FOUND AFTER BELLWEATHER FLOOD.

Then came another world.

The lighthouse lay broken on its island. Bellweather’s streets were underwater. Silver fish moved through the church windows.

Another.

Mara stood in a white dress under summer trees. A woman kissed her beneath a crown of flowers. Tomas applauded beside Gran, both of them old and happy.

Another.

The sea burned.

Another.

Mara had never been born.

She approached the lens.

Voices whispered from within it—thousands of them, layered together. She heard Gran calling her name. She heard herself laughing, screaming, singing. She heard her father telling bedtime stories he had never lived to tell.

At the base of the lens stood a brass keyhole.

“Turn the key,” Tomas said. “The light will go out. The paths will close.”

“What happens to you?”

He did not answer.

Mara looked at him.

“What happens to you?”

“I don’t know.”

“Yes, you do.”

Tomas watched the lens turn. Gold light passed across his face, making him look by turns young and ancient.

“The tower brought me back because the light was lit again,” he said. “When it goes dark, I may return to the moment I left. I may go back into the fog. I may disappear.”

Mara’s hand closed around the key.

“Come with me,” she said.

“I can’t leave while it’s burning.”

“Then I’ll put it out, and we’ll leave together.”

“It may not work that way.”

“It may.”

“Mara—”

“You don’t get to return after thirty-seven years and ask me to kill you.”

“I’m not asking that.”

“What would you call it?”

“Closing a door.”

“From your side.”

He flinched.

The lens showed another life.

Mara saw herself on the kitchen floor, Gran’s body in her arms. Outside, the lighthouse shone every night. Along the harbor, people wandered like sleepwalkers, searching for lives they had lost. Some stepped into the sea.

The vision changed.

A crowd filled the island. Men and women struck the lighthouse door with axes. Above them, the sky was divided into fragments: sun, stars, rain, snow, all existing at once.

“The paths are widening,” Tomas said. “Soon everyone will see them. Not just us.”

“See what?”

“The lives they wanted. The mistakes they wish they could undo. The dead they would give anything to hold again.”

The lens revolved.

Gran stood in the light now.

She was young, younger than Mara had ever seen her. Her black hair hung loose to her waist. In her arms she held a newborn child, while Tomas stood beside her.

“Mother,” Tomas whispered.

Gran looked straight through the glass at Mara.

“Don’t,” she said.

Mara stumbled backward.

The image of Gran came closer. “Keep the light burning. Bring him home.”

Tomas seized Mara’s shoulders. “That isn’t her.”

“How do you know?”

“Because it told me the same thing.”

The image changed again. Tomas appeared inside the lens, older and scarred.

His future self pressed both palms against the glass.

“Mara,” he said, “your grandmother lied to you. She sent me to the island that night. She knew what the lighthouse was.”

Mara turned to the man beside her.

His expression told her enough.

“What does he mean?”

“I don’t know.”

The older Tomas smiled sadly. “Ask him why he has the burn.”

Mara looked at the scar along her father’s jaw.

“Stop listening,” Tomas said.

“How did you get it?”

“In the storm.”

“Liar.”

The word echoed in the chamber.

Tomas released her.

For the first time, Mara noticed the scar was shaped like branching veins, almost like cracks in glass.

“I told you I fought an older version of myself,” he said quietly. “I told you I pushed him into the lens.”

“Yes.”

“I didn’t tell you what happened before that.”

The light turned.

Hundreds of versions of Tomas appeared around the room. A fisherman. A soldier. A drunk asleep in an alley. An old man surrounded by grandchildren. A corpse floating facedown in black water.

“He showed me these,” Tomas said. “All the lives I might have lived. In some, I went home. In some, I drowned. In one, the flood reached the house before I returned.”

Mara understood.

“I died.”

“You and your grandmother both.”

“So you kept the light burning.”

“I thought I could find a path where everyone lived. I searched for hours. Maybe years. Time had no meaning here. But every better world led to a worse one. Save one boat, lose another. Save the town, lose you. Save you…” He shut his eyes. “Lose your mother.”

Mara had never known her mother. Gran said she had left Bellweather before Mara could remember her.

“How did she die?”

“She didn’t,” Tomas said. “Not in the life you know. She left because I failed to come home. In the life where I returned, she died in the flood.”

The lens hummed more loudly. Fine cracks began appearing in the lantern room windows.

“I chose,” Tomas said. “I chose the world in which you lived. Then the older me tried to stop the lens. We fought, and it burned me. When he fell into the light, I realized he hadn’t come to trap me.”

“He came to put it out.”

Tomas nodded. “He was closing the door, just as I’m asking you to do now.”

“But you pushed him in.”

“Yes.”

“Was he killed?”

“He became me.”

Mara stared at the turning lens, at all the worlds trapped in its golden heart.

A terrible understanding settled over her.

“You’ve done this before.”

“Perhaps countless times.”

“And if I turn the key?”

“The circle may break.”

“Or I may become part of it.”

“Yes.”

Below them, a door slammed.

Footsteps rose rapidly through the tower.

Tomas moved between Mara and the stairwell.

Gran entered the lantern room wearing her nightdress, soaked from the rain. She held a kitchen knife in one hand.

“Mara,” she said. “Step away from him.”

Tomas went still.

Gran looked at him. The knife slipped from her fingers and struck the floor.

“My boy,” she whispered.

“Mother.”

She crossed the room and touched his face. He bowed his head. For a moment, they simply stood together while the impossible light turned around them.

Then Gran slapped him.

The sound cracked through the room.

“You foolish, selfish boy.”

Tomas laughed, and the laugh became a sob.

Gran pulled him into her arms.

Mara had never seen her grandmother cry. She did now—not quietly, not gracefully, but with the grief of thirty-seven years tearing its way out.

“I waited,” Gran said. “Every night, I watched this cursed tower.”

“I’m sorry.”

“I told you never to come.”

“The boats were out.”

“I would rather have lost every boat in the harbor.”

“Seven men died.”

“And you thought eight would be better?”

The light swept across them.

For an instant Gran’s appearance changed. She became young, then middle-aged, then a skeleton in a black dress. Mara grabbed the brass key.

“We have to end this.”

Gran lifted her head. “Yes.”

“You knew?”

“I knew enough. My mother kept the lighthouse before me. She gave Tomas the first key, and I gave you the last.”

“Why?”

“Because the light chooses a family. Ours has carried the burden for generations.”

“That’s not a reason.”

“No,” Gran said. “It’s only an excuse.”

A window shattered.

Rain burst into the chamber but did not fall. Each drop hung suspended in the air, shining with tiny visions.

In one drop, Mara saw the wedding beneath the trees.

In another, she saw a child with her eyes.

In another, Gran died peacefully at ninety-four.

She reached toward it.

Tomas caught her wrist.

“They aren’t promises,” he said. “Only possibilities.”

“What’s the difference?”

“Promises belong to the future. Those belong to hunger.”

The tower lurched. A deep crack opened across the floor.

Mara knelt at the base of the lens and inserted the key.

It fit perfectly.

The whispering became a roar.

Every lost thing called to her.

Her mother sang from one world. Her unborn children laughed from another. Friends she had not yet met begged her to find them. A hundred fathers reached through the light, each wearing Tomas’s face.

She gripped the key with both hands.

“What if this is the wrong choice?” she cried.

Gran knelt beside her.

“It will be,” she said.

Mara looked at her.

“All choices are wrong to the lives they leave behind,” Gran said. “Choose this one anyway.”

Mara turned the key.

The lens stopped.

Darkness struck like a wave.

For a moment there was nothing—not the tower, not the storm, not even Mara’s own body. She existed only as a thought falling through cold space.

Then someone took her hand.

She held on.

The world returned with the sound of rain.

Mara lay on the lantern room floor. Dawn seeped through the broken windows, pale and ordinary.

The great lens was dark.

Gran sat nearby, clutching Mara’s left hand.

Her right hand was empty.

Tomas was gone.

They searched the tower until sunrise. They searched the island, the caves, and the rocks below the cliffs. When the harbor boats arrived, they searched the surrounding water.

They found no body.

Only the green coat remained, hanging on the eleventh landing.

Gran carried it home.

In the weeks that followed, people in Bellweather argued about the light. Some swore they had seen it. Others insisted the storm had caused reflections in the clouds. The town council sent engineers to Blackglass, but they found the lantern mechanism rusted beyond use.

Mara said nothing.

Winter settled over the coast. Gran grew quieter but somehow lighter, as if an old chain had broken inside her. She began telling Mara stories about Tomas: how he stole apples from the mayor’s garden, how he sang badly while mending nets, how he once tried to keep a seal in the bathtub.

“He would have liked you,” Gran said one evening.

Mara looked toward the curtained window. “Did he?”

“Yes,” Gran replied. “He simply didn’t have enough time to know how.”

In spring, Mara returned alone to Blackglass.

Wildflowers had overtaken the path. Rust covered the iron door. The brass key no longer fit the lock, so she climbed through a broken window.

Inside, the tower was silent.

She climbed to the lantern room and found the lens clouded with salt. No visions stirred within it. No voices whispered.

At the base, where the keyhole had been, someone had carved a line into the brass.

Mara wiped away the dust and read:

THE LIFE YOU KEEP IS THE ONE YOU MUST LIVE.

Beneath the words lay a small object.

A red wooden button.

Mara recognized it from the green coat.

She held it tightly and laughed until she began to cry.

Then, from far below, the iron door creaked open.

Mara turned.

Footsteps started up the stairs—slow, uneven, unmistakably real.

She waited in the dark as they climbed.

And though every hungry part of her imagined who might appear, she did not call out.

Not yet.

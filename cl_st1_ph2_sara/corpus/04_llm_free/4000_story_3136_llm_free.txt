### The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every town has a place people forget to notice.

In Bellwether, it was a narrow shop squeezed between the bakery and the post office. Its windows were always clean, though no one had ever seen them washed. Behind the glass stood dozens of clocks: tall clocks, round clocks, brass clocks, clocks painted with flowers, and one tiny clock no larger than a walnut. None displayed the same time.

Above the door hung a faded sign:

**ELIAS VALE — CLOCKS REPAIRED, HOURS RESTORED**

Most people assumed the last two words were a joke.

Mara Venn did not.

She was sixteen when she first entered the shop, on a rainy afternoon in October. A bell rang somewhere deep inside, though there was no bell above the door.

The air smelled of cedar, metal, and old books. Clocks covered every wall, their hands moving with soft, unsynchronized clicks. Yet the shop was not noisy. The ticking blended into a steady hush, like rainfall heard from bed.

At the back, an old man bent over a workbench. He had silver hair, careful hands, and a jeweler’s lens fixed over one eye.

“We are closed,” he said.

“The sign says open.”

“The sign is unreliable.”

Mara approached the bench. “Are you Elias Vale?”

“That depends who is asking.”

She placed a small pocket watch in front of him. Its silver case was scratched, and its chain had been repaired with a piece of blue thread.

“My father owned this.”

Elias removed the lens from his eye. His expression changed when he saw the watch.

“Daniel Venn,” he said quietly.

Mara’s fingers tightened around the edge of the workbench. “You knew him?”

“I repaired this watch once.”

“Then repair it again.”

Elias opened the case. The hands had stopped at seventeen minutes past nine.

“It is not broken,” he said.

“It hasn’t moved in four years.”

“That does not mean it is broken.”

“My father died four years ago.”

“I know.”

Outside, rain traced crooked lines down the windows. Mara watched the old man’s face, searching for pity. She had grown skilled at recognizing pity. It softened mouths, lowered voices, and made adults reach for her shoulder as if grief were a coat they could help her remove.

Elias only looked tired.

“My mother says he left home at nine that night,” Mara said. “They argued. He drove toward the coast, and his car went over the bridge. They found the car, but they never found him.”

Elias closed the watch.

“What do you want from me?”

“The truth.”

“I repair clocks.”

“Your sign says you restore hours.”

For the first time, one of the clocks chimed. It was a dark wooden clock near the ceiling, though its hands showed midnight.

Elias glanced at it.

“Some hours are lost,” he said. “Others are abandoned. A few are stolen. They are not the same thing.”

“I want the hour before my father disappeared.”

“No.”

“You haven’t even asked why.”

“Everyone asks for the same reasons. To change a decision. To prevent a death. To say what was not said. To hear what can no longer be heard.”

“I only want to know what happened.”

“Knowledge changes things too.”

“Not knowing has already changed everything.”

That answer seemed to trouble him.

Elias stood and crossed the shop. He moved slowly, but not weakly. From a locked cabinet he took a wooden box inlaid with mother-of-pearl. Inside lay a coil of gold wire, a black key, and a glass vial filled with something that looked like smoke.

He returned to the bench.

“An hour cannot be restored completely,” he said. “Time dislikes repetition. It allows only echoes.”

“What does that mean?”

“You may enter the lost hour, but you cannot alter it. You cannot speak to anyone. You cannot touch anything. If the hour notices you, it will try to keep you.”

Mara stared at him. “You expect me to believe that?”

“No. Belief is rarely necessary.”

He looped the gold wire around the pocket watch, inserted the black key into its winding hole, and opened the vial. The smoke poured downward rather than up, wrapping around the watch like dark silk.

“What will it cost?” Mara asked.

Elias looked at her.

“One hour of your life.”

“Which hour?”

“That is not mine to decide.”

She thought of all the hours ahead of her: sleeping hours, waiting hours, happy hours, lonely hours. One among thousands seemed a small price.

“Do it.”

Elias turned the key.

Every clock in the shop stopped.

The silence struck like thunder.

Then the floor vanished.

Mara fell through darkness filled with the scent of rain.

She landed on the carpet in her childhood home.

For a moment she could not breathe. The hallway was exactly as she remembered it: yellow wallpaper, muddy shoes by the radiator, a chipped blue bowl for keys. She looked down and saw her smaller hands.

No—not her hands. Her hands were still sixteen-year-old hands, but pale and faint, as if made from winter sunlight.

Voices came from the kitchen.

“You promised,” her mother said.

Mara followed the sound.

Her father stood by the kitchen table in a brown coat, rain shining on his shoulders. Daniel Venn looked younger than Mara remembered, though she had seen his face each day in the framed photograph beside her bed. He was tall, with dark curls and a narrow scar on his chin.

Her mother stood across from him. She looked exhausted and furious.

“You said it was over,” she said.

“It is.”

“Then why did he call?”

“I don’t know.”

“Don’t lie to me, Daniel.”

“I’m not lying.”

On the table lay the silver pocket watch. Its hands moved toward nine o’clock.

Mara stepped closer.

Her father rubbed both hands over his face. “Vale said this might happen.”

At the sound of the name, Mara’s heart lurched.

Her mother shook her head. “I never should have agreed to any of this.”

“We didn’t have a choice.”

“We had choices. We simply chose the worst one.”

A child laughed upstairs.

Mara recognized her own voice.

Her father looked toward the ceiling, and all the anger left his face.

“He’ll come here,” he said. “If I don’t meet him, he’ll come here.”

“Then we call the police.”

“And tell them what? That a man who should be seventy looks thirty? That he collects years from people? That our daughter is alive because we borrowed time from him?”

Mara went cold.

Her mother gripped the back of a chair. “We paid him.”

“We paid what he asked.”

“No. We paid what he asked then.”

The room flickered. For an instant the walls became transparent, revealing the clock shop beyond them. Elias stood at his workbench, watching.

Then the kitchen returned.

Daniel picked up the pocket watch.

“Keep Mara inside,” he said.

“Daniel—”

“I’ll lead him to the coast.”

“And then?”

He did not answer.

Mara’s mother crossed the room and struck him across the face. The sound was sharp and terrible.

“You don’t get to leave me with silence,” she said. “You don’t get to become another unanswered question.”

Daniel took her hand and pressed it to his cheek.

“I’m sorry.”

Upstairs, the younger Mara called, “Dad?”

Daniel’s eyes closed.

The lost hour trembled.

Every object in the kitchen blurred at the edges. The walls stretched away, and a low ticking rose from beneath the floorboards.

Mara remembered Elias’s warning: *If the hour notices you, it will try to keep you.*

Her father opened his eyes and looked directly at her.

Not at where she had been as a child. At where she stood now.

“Mara?” he whispered.

She froze.

Her mother seemed not to hear. She remained motionless, caught in the old moment.

Daniel stepped toward Mara.

“You shouldn’t be here.”

She tried to speak, but no sound came.

He could see her. Somehow, impossibly, he could see her.

His gaze moved over her face, and wonder broke through his fear.

“You grew up,” he said.

Tears blurred her vision.

He reached for her, but his hand passed through her shoulder in a burst of silver light.

The ticking beneath the floor became frantic.

“Listen to me,” he said. “You have to leave.”

Mara shook her head. She pointed to the watch, then toward the door, desperately asking without words: *What happened? Where did you go?*

“I thought I could trick him,” Daniel said. “I was wrong.”

The kitchen darkened. Rain struck the windows harder, though outside the glass there was only blackness.

“He gives nothing,” Daniel continued. “He lends. When you were ill, your mother and I begged for more time. Three years, he said. Just three years, until the doctors found a treatment.”

Mara remembered hospitals in fragments: white ceilings, plastic bracelets, her father sleeping beside her bed.

“He gave you those years,” Daniel said. “Then he came to collect mine.”

A crack split the kitchen wall. Through it Mara saw a bridge shining under rain, an empty car hanging against a broken rail.

Daniel looked over his shoulder.

“I left the watch behind so he couldn’t take all of me. Part of an hour stayed here. Part of me stayed with it.”

Mara stepped closer. She wanted to tell him she had missed him. She wanted to tell him about her first day at secondary school, about the time she broke her wrist, about the terrible soup her mother made each Sunday because it had been his favorite. She wanted to ask if he had been frightened. She wanted to say she forgave him for leaving, though until that moment she had not known she was angry.

Her mouth opened, but the lost hour allowed no voice.

Daniel smiled sadly.

“I know,” he said.

The floor tilted.

Beyond the kitchen door, a shadow appeared in the hallway. It had the shape of a man but seemed made from overlapping years: a young face inside an old one, smooth hands that ended in withered fingers.

Daniel’s expression hardened.

“Go.”

The shadow entered the kitchen.

Mara reached for her father.

This time, his fingers touched hers.

The hour seized her.

Memories rushed around her, bright as sparks. She saw her seventh birthday, though she had never lived it: Daniel carrying her through a garden while snow fell in summer. She saw her mother growing old in a single breath. She saw herself at eighty, waking from a dream she could not remember. She saw clocks buried beneath oceans and clocks turning in the hearts of stars.

The shadow smiled.

“There you are,” it said.

Its voice came from every ticking clock in the world.

Mara felt something pulling at her—not her body, but the years inside her. Days of childhood lifted like pages in a wind. She forgot the name of her first teacher. She forgot the color of her bedroom walls. She forgot the sound of her father singing.

Then Daniel shoved her away.

The kitchen vanished.

Mara woke on the floor of the clock shop, gasping.

The clocks had begun again.

Elias knelt beside her. The pocket watch lay open between them, its hands still fixed at seventeen minutes past nine.

Mara sat up and struck him.

The blow knocked the jeweler’s lens from his eye.

“You knew,” she said.

Elias touched his bleeding lip. “Yes.”

“You knew what took him.”

“Yes.”

“And you let me go in there.”

“You insisted.”

“You could have told me!”

“You would not have believed me.”

She rose unsteadily. “My father said you helped us.”

“I did.”

“He said a man gave me three years.”

Elias’s face seemed suddenly older.

“Not a man.”

“What was it?”

“Something that lives where unused time gathers. It bargains with the desperate.”

“And you?”

“I once made the same bargain.”

Mara looked around at the clocks. None of them seemed harmless now.

“What did you ask for?”

“My wife had died,” Elias said. “I wanted one more day with her.”

“Did you get it?”

“Yes.”

“And what did it cost?”

“My son’s future.”

The words hung between them.

“I did not know the price when I agreed,” Elias continued. “Afterward, I learned to repair the damage such bargains leave behind. I learned to hide fragments of time in clocks. When your parents came to me, I warned them, but warnings mean little to those watching a child die.”

Mara looked at the pocket watch.

“Is my father dead?”

“Partly.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is the only honest one. Most of him was taken. The final hour remained anchored to the watch.”

“Can I bring him back?”

Elias did not reply.

“Can I?”

“There may be a way.”

Hope was painful. It entered her like a blade.

“What way?”

“The collector cannot keep what is freely given elsewhere. Your father’s last hour could be exchanged for another.”

Mara remembered the shadow pulling memories from her.

“Mine.”

“No.”

“You said the price was one hour of my life.”

“The journey cost one hour. This would cost more.”

“How much?”

“Everything you have lived since the night he vanished.”

Four years.

Mara thought of four birthdays, four winters, four years of her mother learning to sleep alone. She thought of exams, friends, books, scraped knees, long walks, and the stray cat she had buried beneath the apple tree. She imagined all of it gone.

“Would I forget?”

“Yes.”

“Would I become twelve again?”

“No. Your body would remain as it is. But the years would be empty.”

“And he would return?”

“The part of him that remains would. Whether it is enough, I cannot promise.”

Mara stared at the watch for a long time.

Then she picked it up.

“I’ll do it.”

“No,” said a voice behind her.

Mara turned.

Her mother stood in the doorway, rain dripping from her coat. Her face was white.

“How long have you been there?” Mara asked.

“Long enough.”

“You knew about this place.”

“Yes.”

“You knew Dad wasn’t just in an accident.”

“Yes.”

The betrayal felt almost as sharp as grief.

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

“Because I wanted you to have a life untouched by our mistake.”

“Untouched?” Mara laughed bitterly. “You think not knowing left me untouched?”

Her mother stepped into the shop.

“No,” she said. “I was wrong.”

Mara held up the watch. “I can bring him back.”

“And lose four years of yourself.”

“He gave his life for mine.”

“That was his choice.”

“So this is mine.”

Her mother’s eyes filled with tears. “You are exactly like him when you say foolish things.”

One of the clocks chimed twice.

Elias looked toward the dark cabinet. “There is not much time.”

The smoke had begun to gather around the pocket watch again, seeping through its closed case.

Mara’s mother approached her.

“Your father did not save you so that you could spend your life repaying him,” she said. “Love is not a debt.”

“He’s trapped.”

“Yes.”

“Then how can we leave him there?”

Her mother looked at Elias. “There is another way, isn’t there?”

The clockmaker remained silent.

“Tell her,” she demanded.

Elias sighed. “The anchor can be broken.”

“What does that mean?” Mara asked.

“The last hour would end. Your father would be beyond the collector’s reach.”

“But he’d be gone.”

“Yes.”

The shop seemed to shrink around her.

Return him at the cost of four years, or release him and lose him forever.

Neither choice felt like mercy.

The pocket watch grew warmer in her hand. Its lid sprang open.

The hands moved.

Seventeen minutes past nine became eighteen.

Smoke filled the shop.

The shadow emerged among the clocks, unfolding from the narrow spaces between seconds. Its face shifted from youth to age and back again.

“An account remains unpaid,” it said.

Mara’s mother took her hand.

Elias lifted the black key. “Choose now.”

The collector extended one pale hand.

Mara saw what it offered: her father walking through the shop door, alive and smiling. She saw him at breakfast the next morning, embarrassed by his long absence. She saw years restored, wounds closing, her mother laughing as she had not laughed in four years.

Then the vision changed.

She saw herself sitting among strangers who called themselves her friends. She saw notebooks in her handwriting that meant nothing to her. She saw her mother explaining four lost years. She saw her father looking at her with guilt every day of his borrowed life.

Love is not a debt.

Mara closed the watch.

“No,” she said.

The collector’s smile vanished.

She took the black key from Elias and drove it into the watch.

The metal screamed.

For one instant, the shop disappeared.

Mara stood again in the kitchen, facing her father. There was no shadow now, no rain, no broken bridge. Morning sunlight rested on the table.

Daniel looked at her as if he had been waiting.

“You found out,” he said.

“Yes.” This time, she had a voice.

“I’m sorry.”

“I know.”

“I wanted to come home.”

“I know.”

He smiled. “You grew up.”

“Not all the way.”

“No one does.”

She wanted to hold him, but she understood now that some wishes became cruel when held too tightly.

“Will it hurt?” she asked.

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

“No,” he admitted, and she laughed through her tears.

He touched her cheek. His hand was warm.

“Tell your mother—”

“Tell her yourself.”

Her mother appeared beside Mara, younger and older at once, carrying every hour she had spent waiting.

Daniel’s face crumpled.

“I’m sorry,” he said again.

She embraced him.

The kitchen filled with golden light.

Mara turned the key.

The pocket watch shattered.

Every clock in Elias Vale’s shop rang at once.

The collector let out a sound like a century collapsing. Cracks of white light opened across its body. The clocks answered with bells, chimes, birdsong, and music. Then the shadow folded inward and vanished into the space between one tick and the next.

Silence followed.

In Mara’s palm lay a handful of silver dust.

Elias sank onto his chair.

“Is it gone?” Mara asked.

“For now.”

Her mother opened her hand. Inside was the piece of blue thread that had once repaired the chain.

“Your father tied this,” she said.

Together they stood in the strange shop while rain softened against the windows.

A clock near the ceiling began to chime nine. This time, its hands agreed.

Months later, the narrow shop between the bakery and the post office closed. Elias Vale left Bellwether without farewell, taking most of his clocks with him. He left one behind.

It arrived at Mara’s house wrapped in brown paper.

It was a plain wooden clock with no numbers on its face. Beneath it, Elias had written a note:

**Not every hour must be counted to matter.**

Mara’s mother hung it in the kitchen.

The clock kept imperfect time. It gained seven minutes in winter and lost eleven in summer. Some days it stopped entirely, only to begin again when someone laughed.

They never repaired it.

Years passed. Mara finished school, left Bellwether, returned, fell in love, fell out of love, and became a maker of watches—not magical ones, as far as she knew, but honest ones. On the wall of her workshop she hung no sign promising restored hours.

People often brought her clocks that had belonged to the dead.

She treated each one carefully.

Sometimes a customer would ask whether a broken clock could remember its owner.

Mara always gave the same answer.

“No,” she would say. “But we can.”

Then she would open the case, lift her smallest tools, and set the hands moving forward.

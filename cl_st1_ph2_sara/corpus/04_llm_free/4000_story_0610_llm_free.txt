### The Cartographer of Lost Roads

Every morning, before the town awoke, Elias Vale walked to the eastern gate and drew a road that no longer existed.

He used blue chalk for rivers, white for milestones, and a soft black stick of charcoal for the road itself. Beginning beneath the stone arch, he worked across the broad gatehouse wall, tracing a path through hills and forests, past ruined towers, over bridges that had long since collapsed. By sunrise, the wall held a map large enough to guide a traveler halfway across the kingdom.

By noon, wind and wagon dust had blurred the lines.

By evening, the guards had washed the wall clean.

The next morning, Elias returned and began again.

The townspeople considered him harmless, though not entirely sane. Children gathered to watch his hands move. Merchants complained that he blocked the gate. Travelers sometimes copied pieces of the map, only to be told that the roads he drew led nowhere.

“There is no forest beyond the north ridge,” the guards would say. “It burned before you were born.”

“That bridge fell into the gorge forty years ago.”

“No one has crossed the Salt Marsh since the old king’s reign.”

Elias never argued. He simply drew.

His little daughter, Mara, had disappeared on one of those roads eleven years earlier.

She had been eight years old, small for her age, with a red wool coat and a habit of collecting buttons. Her mother had died the winter before, and grief had made both father and daughter restless. Elias, who was then a royal surveyor, received orders to inspect the eastern road after a season of heavy rain. He took Mara with him because there was no one else to mind her.

At the foot of Blackwood Hill, a storm came down.

Elias remembered the rain hammering the leaves. He remembered the frightened horses, the overturned cart, and the white flash of lightning striking an oak. For only a moment, he let go of Mara’s hand.

When he reached for her again, she was gone.

Searchers combed the woods for weeks. They found the red coat caught on a thornbush, but no footprints, no blood, no body. The road itself vanished after a landslide covered the lower valley. The king’s engineers built a new route farther south, and the old road was abandoned.

Yet Elias continued searching.

At first he studied official maps. Then monastery charts, military records, farmers’ boundary stones, shepherds’ songs, and the fading journals of dead explorers. Over the years, he became convinced that roads did not truly disappear. They slipped away from memory, perhaps, but remained somewhere beneath the world, waiting for the right traveler to find them.

So he drew them at the eastern gate, where all journeys began.

One autumn morning, while Elias was sketching the ruins of a roadside chapel, he noticed an old woman standing behind him.

She wore a green cloak fastened with a silver clasp. Her hair was braided with dried yellow flowers, and she leaned on a blackthorn staff. Unlike the usual spectators, she did not watch his hands. She studied the finished portions of the map.

“You’ve drawn the Willow Road incorrectly,” she said.

Elias paused.

“No,” he replied. “It bends northwest after the mill.”

“It did once. Then the river changed its name.”

“Rivers change course, not names.”

“Everything changes names when no one remembers what to call it.”

The old woman tapped the wall with her staff.

“Here,” she said. “Your road should pass beneath the hill, not around it.”

“There is no tunnel in any record.”

“Then your records are incomplete.”

Elias straightened, irritated despite himself. “Have you traveled the Willow Road?”

“Not for a very long time.”

“It vanished eighty years ago.”

“So did I.”

The guards nearby laughed, but the old woman did not smile.

From within her cloak, she drew a small round object and placed it in Elias’s palm.

It was a brass button shaped like a sun.

His breath stopped.

Mara had collected buttons in a little wooden box. Brass, bone, shell, glass—she treasured them all. On the day she vanished, Elias had fastened three sun-shaped buttons to her red coat. He had found two still attached to the torn cloth.

“Where did you get this?” he whispered.

“On the road beneath the hill.”

“From whom?”

“A girl gave it to me. She said her father would recognize it.”

Elias closed his fingers around the button. The eastern gate, the guards, the wagons, and the waking town seemed to fall away.

“When?”

“Yesterday,” said the woman.

“That is impossible.”

“For ordinary roads, perhaps.”

Elias seized her sleeve. “Take me to her.”

The old woman looked at his hand until he released her.

“I can show you the entrance,” she said. “Beyond that, you must follow your own map.”

She touched the gatehouse wall. Elias’s chalk lines trembled. At first he thought it was a trick of the morning light, but then the charcoal road shifted, crawling like a black thread through the painted hills. The rivers twisted. The ruined chapel moved east. A mountain opened, revealing a narrow passage beneath it.

Elias stared.

“Draw carefully,” the woman said. “A map is not only a picture of a place. Sometimes it is a promise that the place can be reached.”

She turned and walked through the gate.

Elias scrambled to gather his chalk, papers, compass, and cloak. By the time he followed, she was already far along the southern highway. Though she appeared frail, she moved so swiftly that he had to run to catch her.

They traveled until noon, then left the highway and crossed fields silver with autumn frost. The old woman gave no name and answered few questions. When Elias asked about Mara, she said only, “The girl is waiting.”

“Is she well?”

“She is waiting.”

“Is she still a child?”

The old woman glanced at him. “What answer do you hope for?”

He did not know.

Near sunset, they reached Blackwood Hill.

Elias had not returned in years. The forest had recovered from the fire, but the new trees were thin and pale. The land rose before them like the back of some sleeping animal.

The old woman led him to a wall of exposed stone half hidden by ivy.

“Your tunnel,” she said.

Elias saw no opening.

The woman pointed to his satchel. “Draw.”

“On what?”

“Anything.”

He took out a sheet of paper and, kneeling on the cold ground, copied the altered map from memory. He drew the hill, the forgotten road, and the passage beneath the stone. His fingers shook. Twice he broke the charcoal and had to sharpen it with his knife.

When the final line was complete, the wind ceased.

Somewhere under the earth, a bell rang.

The ivy slid aside.

In the rock was a low archway, and beyond it stretched a road lit by a dim golden sky.

Elias stood slowly.

The old woman remained outside.

“You are not coming?”

“My road goes elsewhere.”

“How will I return?”

“If you find what you seek, returning will be the least of your troubles.”

He wanted to demand an explanation, but the brass button in his pocket seemed to burn against his leg. He stepped under the arch.

After three paces, he looked back.

There was only stone behind him.

The road ahead was paved with flat gray slabs. On either side grew orchards heavy with unfamiliar fruit—silver pears, blue apples, and small red berries that glowed like coals. Though no sun was visible, the sky held the soft light of late afternoon.

Elias unfolded his map. A black line now ran across the page where he had drawn nothing. As he walked, new marks appeared: a well, a crossroads, a cluster of houses.

The first house stood beside a dry fountain. Its windows were open, and smoke rose from the chimney. An old man sat outside, repairing a shoe.

“Good evening,” Elias called.

“Is it?” the man replied.

“I am looking for a girl named Mara Vale.”

“Many people are looking for someone.”

“She came here eleven years ago. She wore a red coat.”

The old man pushed his needle through the leather. “A red-coated child passed this way yesterday.”

“Yesterday?”

“Or fifty years ago. We do not keep clocks here.”

“Which way did she go?”

The old man pointed toward the crossroads. “She took the road she remembered.”

“And where does it lead?”

“If she remembered correctly, home.”

Elias hurried on.

At the crossroads, seven roads branched in different directions. A weathered post stood at their center, but its wooden signs were blank.

Elias checked his compass. The needle spun uselessly.

He took out his chalk and wrote NORTH on one sign.

At once, all seven roads moved.

They writhed across the land like loosened ropes. Orchards shifted, distant hills exchanged places, and the golden sky darkened to violet. When the earth settled, only three roads remained.

Elias understood. The country responded to maps and names, but not obediently. Every mark was a choice, and every choice erased other possibilities.

He studied his page. Near one road, a tiny red dot had appeared.

He followed it.

Night came without stars. The glowing berries along the roadside provided just enough light to walk. Elias passed abandoned wagons, broken gates, and milestones carved with names he almost recognized. Once he heard hoofbeats behind him, but when he turned, the road was empty.

At last, he saw a red coat hanging from a tree.

It was Mara’s coat, still torn at the shoulder, still missing two brass buttons. Rainwater dripped from its hem, though the night was dry.

“Mara!” he shouted.

His voice traveled far ahead, repeating her name until it became a whisper.

A figure stepped onto the road.

She was a young woman of nineteen, with dark hair and a thin, serious face. She wore a traveler’s cloak and carried a wooden box beneath one arm.

Elias knew her immediately. He recognized the tilt of her head, the sharp curve of her eyebrows, and the small scar on her chin from the time she had fallen near the town well.

But he had never seen her grown.

“Mara,” he said.

She looked at him for a long moment.

“You took longer than I thought.”

He tried to speak, but no words came. He crossed the distance between them and embraced her.

She stood stiffly at first. Then the wooden box dropped from her hands, spilling hundreds of buttons across the road, and she wrapped her arms around him.

Elias wept against her shoulder.

“I searched,” he said. “Everywhere. Every day.”

“I know.”

“I should never have let go.”

“I know that too.”

They remained there until the strange darkness began to lift.

Mara gathered her buttons. Some were ordinary, but others were made of materials Elias had never seen: green metal, translucent stone, polished wood that beat softly like a heart. He picked up the brass sun and placed it in her palm.

“The old woman gave me this.”

Mara smiled faintly. “Her name is Seln.”

“Who is she?”

“She keeps the forgotten roads. Or they keep her. She never explains it the same way twice.”

“Why didn’t she bring you home?”

“She couldn’t.”

“Why not?”

Mara closed the wooden box. “Because I forgot the way.”

Elias looked back. The road behind him was no longer the one he had traveled. It now crossed a broad plain under a brightening sky.

“You remembered me,” he said.

“Yes. But remembering a person is not the same as remembering a place.”

“You were eight.”

“At first.”

She told him what had happened after the storm. She had seen a lantern among the trees and followed it, believing Elias carried it. Instead, she found the old road and walked beneath the hill. By the time she realized she was lost, the entrance had closed.

Seln found her and cared for her. Together they traveled roads erased by war, flood, fire, and forgetfulness. Mara saw cities whose names had vanished from history. She slept in inns that appeared only during thunderstorms. She crossed a bridge built from the promises of dead kings.

Years passed for her, though not in any orderly fashion.

“Sometimes I reached the edge of our world,” she said. “Once I saw the town from a hill. But when I walked toward it, I arrived at a harbor where every ship had sunk. Another time I followed a road marked with our family name, but it led to a village where everyone looked like us and no one knew who I was.”

“Then how do we leave?”

“You drew a way in. You must draw a way out.”

Elias opened his map.

The page was crowded now. Roads covered roads, tangling into knots. The tunnel had disappeared. So had the hill.

“I can draw the town,” he said.

“Do you remember it exactly?”

“I mapped every street.”

“That is not what I asked.”

Elias took out a fresh sheet.

He began with the eastern gate. He drew its stone arch, the guards’ narrow windows, and the wall where he worked each morning. He added the market square, the bell tower, the old well, and his house on Lantern Street.

As his charcoal moved, he realized the lines were too precise. The town on the paper was a surveyor’s town: measured, numbered, lifeless. It contained no smells, voices, or memories. It could have been any place.

He started again.

This time, he drew the gate as it looked in rain, when the stones turned almost black. He drew the bakery chimney leaning slightly east, though official records insisted it stood straight. He drew the market square with pigeons covering the statue of the king. He drew Lantern Street narrower than it truly was because it had always seemed narrow when he carried sleeping Mara home.

Last, he drew their house.

He included the crack above the kitchen window, the apple tree Mara’s mother had planted, and the bedroom wall where a child had once marked her height in blue ink.

A thin golden line appeared on the paper.

Mara leaned close. “There.”

The line led forward across the plain.

They walked together.

The road changed beneath their feet. Stone became gravel, gravel became mud, and mud became a carpet of fallen leaves. Around them rose the blackened trunks of the old forest. Rain began to fall.

Elias heard horses screaming.

Ahead stood a younger version of himself beside an overturned cart. He was calling Mara’s name, frantic and hoarse. Lightning split the sky, illuminating a little girl in a red coat.

Mara stopped.

“That’s the moment,” she whispered.

Elias understood that the road had brought them not merely to a place, but to a memory.

The child saw a lantern between the trees.

“No!” Elias shouted.

She could not hear him.

Young Elias turned away to calm the horses. The little girl followed the light.

Mara gripped her father’s hand. “If we stop her, what happens to me?”

“I don’t know.”

“Do you get eleven years back?”

“I don’t care.”

“But I do.”

The child walked closer to the hidden road.

Elias could run after her. He could seize her hand and prevent everything: the loss, the search, the lonely years. Mara would grow up at home. He would watch her become the woman beside him.

Or perhaps that woman would cease to exist.

He looked at his daughter. Rain ran down her face.

“I used to dream of changing this,” he said.

“So did I.”

“But this is not our road anymore.”

Together, they watched the child disappear among the trees.

The storm froze. Every raindrop hung motionless in the air.

Then the memory broke like glass.

Elias and Mara stood before the eastern gate at dawn.

No guards had arrived yet. The town was quiet. Elias’s map from the previous morning remained faintly visible on the wall.

Mara touched the stone arch as if to prove it was real.

“We’re home,” she said.

Elias laughed. It was an unfamiliar sound after so many years, but it returned quickly. Soon they were both laughing, though neither knew exactly why.

The guards found them there at sunrise. One dropped his spear. Another crossed himself. Within an hour, half the town had gathered at the gate.

Questions filled the air.

Where had she been?

Why had she not aged eleven years all at once, or remained eight forever?

Who was the old woman in green?

Was there gold on the forgotten roads?

Could the dead be found there?

Elias answered none of them. He took Mara home.

The house on Lantern Street was smaller than she remembered. The apple tree had died, and dust covered her old room. Yet the blue marks remained on the wall.

Mara stood beside the highest one. It reached only to her shoulder.

For a time, father and daughter did not know how to live together. Elias still thought of her as a child and woke at night fearing she had vanished again. Mara had spent years making her own decisions and resisted every attempt to protect her. They argued over locked doors, late walks, and whether roads could be trusted.

But ordinary days, they discovered, were patient things.

They cooked. They repaired the roof. Mara found work with a bookbinder and began filling blank pages with accounts of places no scholar believed existed. Elias returned to surveying, though he no longer worked for the king.

Each morning, they walked together to the eastern gate.

There they drew the lost roads.

Travelers still laughed. Merchants still complained. The guards still washed the wall at dusk.

But sometimes a stranger approached with a question.

A woman searching for the village where her mother was born.

A soldier hoping to find the field where he had abandoned a friend.

A sailor who remembered a river flowing through the desert.

Elias would listen. Mara would ask what they remembered—not measurements or directions, but the color of the doors, the sound of evening bells, the taste of water from the well. Then together they drew.

Most roads remained only chalk and charcoal.

A few opened.

Years later, when Elias’s hair had gone white and Mara had a daughter of her own, an old woman in a green cloak appeared at the gate.

Seln looked exactly as she had before.

“You corrected the Willow Road,” she observed.

Elias, seated on a stool, smiled. “The river changed its name.”

“So it did.”

Mara embraced the old woman. Seln pretended to dislike this but did not step away.

“Have you come to stay?” Mara asked.

“No. I have come because a road is missing.”

She pointed to a blank place on the wall.

Elias rose, though his knees protested, and selected a piece of black charcoal.

“Where does it lead?”

Seln looked beyond the gate, toward hills touched by the first light of morning.

“No one knows,” she said.

Mara handed her father a fresh sheet of paper.

Together, they began to draw.

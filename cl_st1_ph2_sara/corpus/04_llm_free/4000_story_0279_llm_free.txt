### The Museum of Unfinished Things

On the morning the sea disappeared, Mara Venn was repairing a music box that had never played.

It was a small, stubborn object with a walnut lid and brass hinges, donated to the Museum of Unfinished Things by a woman who claimed her grandfather had spent forty years building it. Inside, a silver bird waited on a spring. Its wings were polished smooth, its beak open in permanent expectation.

Mara turned the key. The mechanism tightened, clicked, and stopped.

“Still refusing?” asked Mr. Vale.

He stood in the doorway of the restoration room, holding two cups of tea. He was the museum’s oldest curator, though no one knew exactly how old. His suits always smelled faintly of dust and rain.

“It wants to sing,” Mara said. “It just doesn’t know what song.”

“Common problem.”

He placed one cup beside her and opened the shutters.

Beyond the museum, the harbor lay under a pale autumn sky. Fishing boats rested crookedly in the mud. Buoys leaned on their sides. Anchors, nets, bottles, broken oars, and the dark ribs of old wrecks covered the seabed as far as anyone could see.

The sea had withdrawn beyond the horizon.

For a while, neither of them spoke.

Then the city bells began to ring.

People ran downhill toward the harbor. Shopkeepers abandoned their counters. Schoolchildren poured into the streets, followed by teachers pretending to maintain order. Dogs barked at gulls stranded on wet sand. The gulls themselves circled in noisy confusion, as if protesting a theft.

Mara set down her tools.

“Is there a storm coming?”

Mr. Vale watched the empty horizon. “Not a storm.”

“How do you know?”

“Storms arrive. This is something leaving.”

The bells continued until one cracked.

By noon, the mayor had forbidden anyone from walking onto the exposed seabed. By one o’clock, hundreds of people were doing exactly that.

They gathered shells, coins, cannonballs, bits of china, rusted knives, and timber blackened by centuries underwater. Children climbed shipwrecks. Old men stood in the mud and pointed toward places where houses had once been, before the old quarter slipped into the bay during the earthquake of 1783.

Mara remained inside the museum.

The Museum of Unfinished Things occupied a narrow stone building between the courthouse and a bakery. Its collection included a painter’s final blank canvas, a clockmaker’s watch without hands, half a wedding dress, a novel ending in the middle of a sentence, and a ladder with six rungs missing. Every object had been begun with purpose and abandoned through death, doubt, poverty, war, forgetfulness, or simple exhaustion.

Mara had worked there for nine years. She repaired what could be repaired, preserved what could not, and recorded the stories of both.

At sunset, a boy knocked on the museum door.

He was soaked to the knees and carried something wrapped in his coat.

“We’re closed,” Mara said.

“I found this.”

He unwrapped a wooden sign covered in barnacles. Beneath the green stains, faded gold letters read:

STATION FOR DEPARTURES  
ONE PASSENGER AT A TIME

“Where did you find it?” asked Mr. Vale.

“Out past the lighthouse.”

“The lighthouse is three miles from shore.”

The boy shrugged. “It isn’t anymore.”

Mr. Vale gave him a silver coin and told him to go home before dark. Then he carried the sign into the main gallery and locked the doors.

Mara followed him.

“You recognize it.”

“I recognize the lettering.”

“From where?”

He pointed to a glass case containing the museum’s oldest object: a railway ticket printed on gray paper. It had no date, no destination, and no price. The only words on it were ONE PASSENGER.

The ticket had been found in the hand of the museum’s founder after her death.

“The same gold ink,” Mara said.

Mr. Vale nodded.

“You think there was a station under the sea?”

“I think,” he replied, “we should visit the lighthouse before the sea returns.”

They left at dawn.

The road across the seabed was not truly a road, but it became one through use. Hundreds of footprints wound between pools and ridges of stone. The city had emptied into the vanished sea, despite the mayor’s warnings. Families carried baskets as if attending a festival. Merchants had already set up stalls beside an overturned schooner, selling hot bread, rope, maps, and souvenirs gathered only hours earlier.

The exposed world smelled of salt, metal, and rot.

Mara and Mr. Vale passed forests of red weed flattened by the retreating water. Fish flapped in shallow hollows, and volunteers hurried among them with buckets. Farther out, the mud hardened into a plain of rippled sand. Strange towers of coral rose from it, white as bone.

By midday, the city behind them had become a thin line.

The lighthouse stood on a rocky hill that had once been an island. Without the sea, it looked too tall, a useless finger pointing at the sky.

Beyond it, they found the station.

A roof of green copper emerged from the sand. Two chimneys leaned at opposite angles. Arched windows stared blindly from walls furred with seaweed. Above the entrance was an empty space where the wooden sign had hung.

People had not yet reached it.

Mara descended the stone steps and pressed her hands against the doors. They opened without resistance.

Inside, everything was dry.

Not recently dried, not damp at the edges, but completely untouched by the sea. Dust lay across the floor. Wooden benches stood in rows. A ticket window waited behind a brass grille. A clock above the platform doors showed seven minutes past four.

Mr. Vale took out the gray railway ticket.

The clock began to tick.

Mara stepped back. “What did you do?”

“Nothing.”

The minute hand moved.

Somewhere beyond the platform doors, a train whistle sounded.

It was faint at first, then deepened until the glass in the windows trembled. A wind swept through the station, carrying the smell of coal smoke and winter air.

The ticket window slid open.

A woman sat on the other side.

She had white hair, dark skin, and a red uniform buttoned to the throat. Her eyes were the blue-green color of shallow water.

“Ticket,” she said.

Mr. Vale placed the gray slip under the grille.

The woman examined it. “This is not yours.”

“No,” he said.

“Then why have you brought it?”

“To ask where it goes.”

“Where all such tickets go.”

Mara leaned toward the opening. “Which is where?”

The woman looked at her. “To the unfinished.”

Mara glanced around the station. “This place has been underwater for centuries.”

“Has it?”

“We found your sign on the seabed.”

“Then perhaps the sea was over the city, rather than the station beneath the sea.”

Mr. Vale smiled slightly, as if she had confirmed an old suspicion.

Mara did not enjoy answers shaped like riddles. “Why did the sea disappear?”

“The train is coming.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is the only answer you have time for.”

The clock showed four minutes past four. Its hands were moving backward.

Mr. Vale pushed the ticket closer to the window. “Who does it belong to?”

The woman did not touch it. “The passenger.”

“Which passenger?”

“One of you.”

Mara laughed once, without humor. “Of course.”

The platform doors unlocked with a heavy clunk.

Mr. Vale picked up the ticket.

“We should leave,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

But he did not move.

The station clock continued backward. Three minutes past four.

Mara knew that stillness. She had seen it in people standing before half-finished portraits or unopened letters in the museum. It was the stillness of someone who had spent years approaching a decision while pretending to avoid it.

“You knew this was here,” she said.

“I hoped.”

“You brought me because of the ticket?”

“I brought you because the ticket must be used.”

“No. You brought me because you want to use it.”

Mr. Vale looked toward the platform doors.

“My daughter disappeared thirty-two years ago,” he said. “She was nineteen. She left a note saying she was going to see the world. No address, no promise to return. For a long time I believed she would come home. Then I believed she was dead. Eventually, I understood that belief and truth are different things.”

Mara had worked beside him for nine years and had never heard him mention a daughter.

“Her name was Lysa,” he continued. “The museum’s founder was her grandmother. When the old woman died, she held that ticket in her hand. She had spent her life studying impossible places. In her journals she wrote of a station that appears whenever the sea withdraws farther than memory.”

The clock showed two minutes past four.

“You think the train can take you to your daughter?”

“No. I think it can take me to the place where my search ended before I found an answer.”

“That sounds like the same thing.”

“It may not be.”

The whistle sounded again.

Mara crossed her arms. “Then go.”

Mr. Vale turned to her. “The ticket isn’t mine.”

The gray slip remained blank.

“What makes you so certain?”

“Because I tried to use it once.”

He unbuttoned his sleeve and showed her a pale scar circling his wrist, thin as a thread.

“The sea withdrew twenty years ago, though only for a few minutes. I reached the station. The train came. When I offered the ticket, it cut me and returned to my pocket.”

Mara stared at the scar.

“Why would it be mine?”

“Your brother.”

The words struck harder than she expected.

Tomas had been missing for eleven years. His boat had been found drifting beyond the northern reef, sail torn, supplies untouched. Everyone else accepted that he had fallen overboard.

Mara had not.

For six years she searched islands, harbors, hospitals, and prisons. She questioned sailors who lied for money and fortune-tellers who lied for free. In the seventh year, she stopped traveling. In the eighth, she stopped asking. By the ninth, she could speak of Tomas in the past tense.

She had believed that meant the wound was closed.

The platform doors opened.

A train waited beyond them, though there were no rails.

It was black, with silver windows and a pale lamp at the front. Steam curled along the platform, but the wheels made no sound. Every carriage door stood open.

The clock reached one minute past four.

Mr. Vale held out the ticket.

Mara did not take it.

“If I go, can I come back?”

The woman behind the grille said, “Everyone asks.”

“And?”

“Everyone discovers.”

“I need a real answer.”

“No. You need a certain answer. Those are rare and expensive.”

“What does it cost?”

“One unfinished thing.”

Mara looked at the ticket. “What kind?”

“The one that keeps you here.”

The clock reached four.

For a moment, all sound ceased.

Mara thought of Tomas at seventeen, teaching her to sail. Tomas at twenty-three, dancing badly at their cousin’s wedding. Tomas on the morning of his last voyage, standing at the kitchen door with half an apple in his hand.

I’ll be back before the pears are ripe, he had said.

The pear tree had produced eleven harvests.

She took the ticket.

Black letters appeared across it:

MARA VENN  
RETURN NOT GUARANTEED

The paper was warm.

Mr. Vale closed his eyes.

“You knew,” Mara whispered. “You knew it would choose me.”

“I suspected.”

“And if I find Lysa?”

His face changed. For one instant, hope made him look young.

“Tell her,” he began.

Then he stopped.

Mara waited.

At last he shook his head. “No. I have no right to turn you into my message.”

She stepped onto the platform.

The nearest carriage was empty. Its walls were paneled in dark wood. Blue lamps burned above the seats, and frost covered the windows from the inside.

Behind her, the station woman called, “Your unfinished thing.”

Mara looked down at her hands.

“What do I give?”

“You do not give it to me.”

The whistle shrieked. The carriage doors began to close.

Mr. Vale stood inside the station, framed by the platform doorway.

Mara suddenly understood.

She tore the ticket in half.

The train vanished.

There was no explosion, no flash of light. One moment it waited at the platform; the next there was only an empty plain stretching beyond the station, marked by two iron rails that faded into the distance.

The torn halves of the ticket fluttered from Mara’s fingers.

“What have you done?” cried Mr. Vale.

“I finished it.”

The rails began to hum.

Far away, something moved toward them—not the train, but a wall of darkness spanning the horizon.

The sea was returning.

Mr. Vale seized Mara’s arm. They ran through the station, up the steps, and onto the exposed seabed. Behind them, the ticket window slammed shut. The station doors closed. Sand rose around the walls as if pulled by invisible hands.

The returning water had no crest. It advanced like nightfall, smooth and impossibly fast.

They could never reach the city.

“The lighthouse!” Mara shouted.

They climbed the rocky hill as the wind strengthened. Other figures were running across the seabed now, distant and small. Bells rang from the shore. Carts overturned. People abandoned baskets of treasure and fled.

Mara and Mr. Vale reached the lighthouse door just as the first streams of water curled around the hill.

Inside, they climbed.

The keeper’s room was empty. They continued up the spiral stairs to the lantern chamber. The old lens stood in the center, clouded with salt. Mr. Vale collapsed beside it, clutching his chest.

“Are you hurt?”

“Only old.”

Below, the sea swallowed the station. Water flooded the plain, lifting wrecks, barrels, and broken masts. The returning tide rose around the lighthouse hill.

Mara looked toward the city.

Thousands of people were still crossing the seabed.

“They won’t make it.”

“The lens,” Mr. Vale gasped. “Light it.”

“There’s no fuel.”

“Then repair it.”

“It’s a lighthouse, not a music box.”

“You are surrounded by unfinished things. Make use of one.”

Mara opened the mechanism beneath the lens. Most of the gears had rusted through. The oil reservoir was empty, the mirrors black, the wick reduced to ash.

Outside, the sky darkened.

She emptied her pockets onto the floor: a screwdriver, a file, copper wire, three screws, a lump of wax, and the tiny silver bird from the music box. She had taken it without thinking, intending to work during the walk.

Mr. Vale watched as she pried open the bird’s body.

“What are you doing?”

“Finishing something badly.”

The bird contained a compact spring and a cylinder studded with pins. She fitted the spring into the lighthouse’s rotation gear, bound it with copper wire, and used the music cylinder as a replacement cog. It would not last long. Perhaps not even a minute.

But the lens might turn.

“There’s still no light,” Mr. Vale said.

Mara looked at the afternoon sun, faint behind the clouds.

She broke the blackened outer mirrors away, exposing a strip of clean silver beneath. Then she rotated the lens by hand until it caught a narrow blade of sunlight.

“Turn the key,” she said.

“What key?”

She handed him the brass key from the music box.

He inserted it into the makeshift gear and wound the spring.

The mechanism shuddered.

The silver bird trembled inside the machine. Its wings opened. The lens began to turn, slowly at first, then faster. Sunlight flashed across the flooded plain in great white arcs.

The people below saw it.

They changed direction, following the beam toward higher ground: the roofs of submerged ruins, the spine of an ancient sea wall, the lighthouse hill itself. Boats from the harbor moved among them, rescuing those cut off by the water.

The machine clicked and strained.

Then the silver bird sang.

Its song was thin and metallic, only six notes repeated again and again. Yet the sound filled the lantern chamber, bright as a child’s laughter.

Mr. Vale began to cry.

“Was that the song?” Mara asked.

He nodded.

“Lysa wrote it,” he said. “When she was little.”

The sea climbed the lighthouse steps but rose no farther.

By evening, the rescue boats had collected the last survivors. The water settled into the harbor, covering the wrecks, the coral towers, the station, and its impossible rails. The city lights flickered on one by one.

The lighthouse mechanism stopped.

The silver bird gave a final note and folded its wings.

For several days, the newspapers spoke of miracles. Then officials issued more reasonable explanations involving underwater earthquakes, unusual tides, and collective panic. The mayor ordered the wooden station sign destroyed, but it disappeared from the evidence room before that could happen.

No one admitted taking it.

Mr. Vale retired before winter. On his final day, he placed the repaired music box in the museum’s central gallery.

“It is no longer unfinished,” Mara told him.

“Nothing is ever completely finished,” he replied. “That is merely where we choose to stop working.”

He left her a bundle of journals, the lighthouse key, and his blue teacup.

In spring, a letter arrived from a distant city in the south.

It was addressed to Mr. Elias Vale.

Mara recognized neither the handwriting nor the seal. She considered forwarding it, but Mr. Vale had left no address. He had said only that he intended to travel.

The letter sat unopened on her desk for three days.

On the fourth, an elderly woman entered the museum. She wore a red coat and carried a suitcase covered in railway labels.

“I’m looking for Elias Vale,” she said.

Mara stood so quickly that her chair fell backward.

The woman glanced at the music box in the central gallery. The silver bird rested beneath its walnut lid.

“Where did you get that?”

“Do you know it?”

The woman approached the glass case. Her reflection trembled over the bird’s polished wings.

“My father made it,” she said. “Or tried to. I composed the tune when I was seven, but he could never make the mechanism work.”

Mara’s heart beat painfully.

“What is your name?”

The woman turned.

“Lysa.”

For a moment, Mara imagined a train moving beneath the city, passing through black water on rails no map recorded. She imagined torn tickets repairing themselves, impossible stations waiting with their clocks stopped, and all the unfinished stories of the world traveling in silent carriages toward their final lines.

Then she took Mr. Vale’s letter from her desk and gave it to his daughter.

“He’s been waiting for you,” Mara said.

Lysa held the envelope but did not open it.

“Is he here?”

“No. He went to look for something.”

“For me?”

“Perhaps at first.”

Mara looked through the museum windows toward the harbor. The sea shone under the spring sun, restless but ordinary. Fishing boats crossed the water. Gulls wheeled around the lighthouse.

“Do you know where he went?” Lysa asked.

Mara thought of the blank railway ticket, the backward-moving clock, and the cost of certainty.

“No,” she said. “But I think he’ll find his way back.”

Lysa smiled sadly. “How can you be sure?”

“I’m not.”

From the glass case came a soft click.

The music box began to play.

Six bright notes rose into the dusty air. Visitors turned to listen. Outside, the bells marked the hour, and somewhere beyond the harbor, hidden by sunlight, a whistle answered.

Mara opened the museum doors.

This time, she did not mistake uncertainty for an ending.

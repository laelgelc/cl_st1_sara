## The Keeper of Lost Hours

On the northern edge of Bellwether stood a narrow stone tower with no windows and a single blue door. Every citizen passed it eventually, though few admitted to looking. Children dared one another to knock. Lovers met in its shadow. Old men, returning from the tavern after midnight, sometimes leaned against the cold stones and listened.

They claimed they could hear clocks inside.

Not one clock, but hundreds: mantel clocks chiming out of turn, pocket watches ticking nervously, grandfather clocks groaning as their weights descended, tiny carriage clocks ringing like silver spoons against glass. Yet the tower had no clock face, and no clockmaker had ever worked there.

Above the blue door, worn into the lintel, were six words:

**WHAT IS LOST MAY STILL BE KEPT.**

Mara Vale had read them every morning for seventeen years.

Her bakery stood across the street, and before sunrise she would lift the shutters, light the ovens, and set loaves on wooden trays. At precisely five minutes past six, the blue door opened. A tall man in a gray coat stepped out, crossed the street, and purchased one roll with raisins.

His name was Elias Or.

“Good morning, Miss Vale,” he always said.

“Good morning, Mr. Or.”

“Is this fresh?”

“Fresh as a new regret.”

He would smile at that, although she had been saying it for years. Then he would place a copper coin on the counter, carry the roll back to the tower, and disappear behind the blue door.

This happened every day.

Until the morning it did not.

Mara noticed his absence at once. Five past six came and went. Then ten past. Then half past. The raisin roll cooled on the counter.

By seven, customers filled the bakery. Mara sold loaves, poured coffee, and wrapped sweet cakes in brown paper, but she kept glancing through the window.

The blue door remained closed.

“Waiting for your clockman?” asked Mayor Foss, who came each morning for two butter biscuits and the latest rumor.

“He isn’t a clockman.”

“What is he, then?”

Mara looked at the tower. “Punctual.”

At noon, she carried the raisin roll across the street.

She had never stood so close to the blue door. Its paint was cracked, and the brass knocker had been shaped like an open hand. The tower above her seemed taller than it did from the bakery.

Mara knocked.

The clocks inside stopped.

The sudden silence felt vast enough to swallow the street.

Then the door opened.

Elias stood in the entrance. His gray coat was buttoned incorrectly, and his silver hair, usually combed flat, rose in startled wisps.

“Miss Vale,” he said. “You should not be here.”

“You missed breakfast.”

For a moment, he looked as though she had spoken in an unknown language. Then he noticed the paper parcel in her hands.

“Yes,” he murmured. “I suppose I did.”

“Are you ill?”

“Not exactly.”

“Dying?”

“Everyone is, though usually not in a way worth mentioning.”

Mara pushed the parcel toward him. “Your roll.”

He took it carefully.

Behind him, somewhere in the darkness, a clock began to tick. Another joined it, then another, until the tower filled again with its restless mechanical rain.

Elias glanced over his shoulder.

“What is it?” Mara asked.

“Nothing that concerns you.”

“People say that when something concerns everyone.”

He smiled faintly. “You always were inconveniently observant.”

“And you always were conveniently mysterious.”

The smile vanished. He looked tired—more tired than Mara had ever seen anyone look.

“Thank you for the roll,” he said, and closed the door.

That night, Bellwether lost an hour.

It disappeared between eleven and midnight.

People remembered lifting cups but not setting them down. A card player saw the queen of hearts leave his hand and appear on the table without crossing the space between. At the White Hart Inn, a candle burned from six inches tall to a puddle in one blink. A woman in labor felt one pain, and then suddenly held a crying child.

Mara had been kneading dough. One moment her hands were deep in flour; the next, seven finished loaves sat before her.

The town clocks disagreed wildly. Some insisted it was midnight. Others claimed it was eleven, and one cheerful little clock in the mayor’s parlor struck three.

Across the street, the blue door stood open.

Mara wiped the flour from her hands and entered the tower.

Inside was not darkness, but golden light.

Clocks covered every wall. They stood on shelves that rose beyond sight and hung from chains in the air. Some were grand and jeweled; others were cheap things made of tin. There were sundials with no sun, hourglasses whose sand flowed upward, candles that burned without shrinking, and watches small enough to rest on a fingernail.

The interior was far larger than the tower itself.

Narrow staircases twisted through the open space, joining platforms and balconies. Pendulums swung below them like blades. Thousands of hands circled thousands of faces, each measuring a different moment.

Mara stood frozen.

“Close the door,” Elias called.

He was high above her on a ladder, reaching into the workings of a clock with a black wooden case.

Mara shut the door.

Immediately, he climbed down and hurried toward her.

“I told you not to come.”

“You did not.”

“I strongly implied it.”

“What happened to the missing hour?”

Elias stopped.

“So you noticed.”

“The entire town noticed.”

“Most will convince themselves otherwise by morning. People are skilled at repairing reality after it breaks.”

“What happened?”

He looked up at the clocks.

“One of the hours escaped.”

Mara waited for him to laugh. He did not.

“Hours do not escape,” she said.

“Most do not.”

He led her between shelves crowded with ticking faces. Some clocks were labeled with small brass plaques. Mara read them as she passed.

**THE LAST HOUR OF THE WAR**

**AN AFTERNOON BESIDE THE GREEN SEA**

**SEVEN O’CLOCK, BEFORE THE FIRE**

**THE HOUR IN WHICH NO ONE LIED**

They reached a black clock with an empty glass case. Its hands were missing. The plaque beneath it read:

**THE HOUR BELLWETHER FORGOT**

“What does that mean?” Mara asked.

Elias sat heavily on a stool.

“This tower keeps lost time,” he said. “Unremembered afternoons. Hours surrendered to fever. Moments erased by grief. Time stolen by enchantment, illness, war, or simple human carelessness. Most lost hours fade harmlessly. Some do not. So the keeper gathers them and locks them here.”

“And you are the keeper.”

“For the moment.”

“How long is a moment?”

“In my case, three hundred and twelve years.”

Mara studied his face. She had known him since childhood, yet he looked exactly as he had the first morning she sold him bread.

“You have kept these clocks for three centuries?”

“I have kept what is inside them.”

“And tonight one got out.”

“Yes.”

“Where did it go?”

Elias pointed toward the ceiling.

Mara looked up.

Far above, something moved among the rafters: a ripple of shadow, quick as a bird.

“It is hiding,” Elias said. “It has been trapped for a long time, and now it is hungry.”

“For what?”

“What all lost time wants. To be lived.”

A bell rang somewhere in the tower. Its note was low and deep.

Elias rose so quickly that the stool fell over.

The clocks began stopping.

The silence spread from the entrance upward. Tick after tick vanished. Pendulums froze. Bells failed in mid-chime.

“What is happening?” Mara whispered.

“The hour has found a memory to enter.”

“Whose?”

Elias turned toward her, alarm sharpening his features.

“Yours.”

The tower disappeared.

Mara stood in her childhood kitchen.

Morning sunlight lay across the table. Copper pans shone on the wall, and the room smelled of apples and cinnamon. Her mother stood by the stove in a yellow dress, humming.

Mara’s breath caught.

Her mother had been dead for twenty-two years.

Not as Mara last remembered her, gray with illness and unable to lift her head, but young, bright-eyed, and laughing because flour dusted the end of her nose.

“There you are,” her mother said. “I wondered how long you meant to sleep.”

Mara looked down. Her hands were small. She wore a blue child’s dress and one red sock.

“Mama?”

“Who else would I be?”

Mara crossed the room in three steps and threw her arms around her.

Her mother felt warm. Real. She smelled of lavender soap.

For several moments, Mara could do nothing but hold on.

Then a clock ticked behind her.

Elias stood in the kitchen doorway, pale and distant, as though seen through rain.

“This is not real,” he said.

Mara’s mother did not react. She turned back to the stove and stirred a pot.

“It feels real,” Mara said.

“That is how the hour feeds. It gives you what you most desire, then builds itself from your desire.”

Her mother set two bowls on the table.

“Breakfast,” she said.

Mara sat.

The porridge tasted exactly as it had on winter mornings long ago, sweetened with apples preserved in honey. Outside the window, snow drifted through a clear blue sky.

“You can stay,” her mother said.

Mara looked at Elias.

“Can I?”

“For a while,” he replied.

“How long?”

“You will not know. That is the price.”

The kitchen brightened. Sunlight filled every corner, warm and golden.

Her mother reached across the table and squeezed her hand.

“We lost so much time,” Mara whispered.

“We have it now,” her mother said.

Elias took a step into the room.

“No. You have an imitation created from grief. The hour will repeat this morning until you forget there was ever anything else.”

“Would that be so terrible?”

“Not at first.”

Mara looked around the kitchen.

Her father’s boots stood by the door. A loaf cooled on the sill. Her mother began humming again. Every detail was perfect, yet beyond the window the same clump of snow slipped from the same branch, again and again.

Mara watched it fall three times.

“What happens to Bellwether?” she asked.

“The hour will move from memory to memory,” Elias said. “Everyone will be given a past they cannot bear to leave. The town will sleep while years pass outside it.”

“And you?”

“I will remain in the tower until the last clock stops.”

“Then what?”

“I will stop with them.”

Her mother placed a second spoonful of honey in Mara’s bowl.

“You always liked it sweet,” she said.

Mara’s eyes filled with tears.

She remembered another morning—not this perfect one, but a morning near the end. Her mother had been too weak to rise. Mara, fifteen years old and angry at the world, had carried breakfast upstairs.

Her mother took one bite and whispered, “Too sweet.”

They had both laughed.

That memory hurt. It was flawed, bitter, and true.

The kitchen before her had no place for it.

Mara stood.

Her mother looked up. “Where are you going?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then stay.”

“I can’t.”

“Why not?”

Mara leaned down and kissed her mother’s forehead.

“Because you never wore yellow,” she said. “You hated yellow.”

The kitchen cracked.

A line split the wall from ceiling to floor, revealing darkness filled with clocks. Her mother reached for her, but her fingers broke into golden dust.

“Mara!”

The voice was perfect.

Mara nearly turned back.

Instead, she seized Elias’s hand, and together they stepped through the crack.

They returned to the tower.

The escaped hour hung above the empty clock case. It had no true shape. One moment it resembled a dark bird; the next, a spinning wheel, a child’s face, an open doorway, a sun setting over water.

“It chose you because you entered the tower,” Elias said. “Now that you have refused it, it is weak.”

“How do we put it back?”

Elias opened the glass case.

“We must give it a memory freely.”

“What sort of memory?”

“An hour loved enough to satisfy it and surrendered completely.”

Mara understood.

“If I give it a memory, I lose that memory.”

“Yes.”

“Why don’t you give one of yours?”

Elias looked away.

“I have given too many.”

The clocks around them flickered, and Mara saw scenes reflected in their glass: Elias as a young man beside a river; Elias holding a baby; Elias dancing with a woman under lanterns; Elias kneeling near three graves.

Each image vanished almost as soon as it appeared.

“I no longer remember their names,” he said. “Only that I once loved them. A keeper pays for every escaped hour. After three centuries, I have little left worth taking.”

The dark shape circled overhead. The tower trembled.

Mara searched her life.

She thought of her father teaching her to swim, though he was more frightened of the water than she was. She thought of opening the bakery on her first morning alone. She thought of snowball fights, summer dances, burned pies, quiet evenings, and the day her mother let Mara shape her first loaf.

The hour stirred eagerly.

Then Mara found the memory she would give.

She was nine years old. A storm had cut the road, and the whole family remained trapped indoors. Her father played the fiddle badly. Her mother baked chestnuts. Rain beat against the windows, and Mara danced barefoot on the table while both her parents laughed.

For one hour, no one was sick, worried, absent, or afraid.

It was the happiest hour of her life.

“No,” Elias said, reading her expression. “Choose something smaller.”

“Will smaller be enough?”

“Perhaps.”

“And if it isn’t?”

“The hour will escape again.”

The tower shuddered. A shelf collapsed, sending clocks tumbling into the dark.

Mara stepped toward the empty case.

She held the memory firmly one last time: the sound of the fiddle, the smell of chestnuts, her mother’s laughter, her father pretending outrage as muddy feet marked the table.

Then she opened her hands.

Light poured from her palms.

The escaped hour dropped from the air.

It struck the light, folded around it, and rushed into the black clock. Elias slammed the glass door shut.

The clock began to tick.

Once.

Twice.

Then all the clocks in the tower answered.

The sound rose like rain after drought. Bells rang. Gears turned. Pendulums resumed their arcs. The golden lamps flared so brightly that Mara had to close her eyes.

When she opened them, dawn shone beneath the blue door.

Elias leaned against the clock case.

“It is done,” he said.

Mara searched her memory.

She saw rain. She knew there had been music. She remembered being happy.

But she could not hear her father’s fiddle or her mother’s laughter. She could not recall why there had been mud on the table.

The empty place inside her was clean and sharp.

“Was it worth it?” she asked.

“That is a question only those who remember can answer.”

“Then you answer.”

Elias looked at the black clock.

“Yes,” he said. “Though worth is not the same as fairness.”

Outside, Bellwether woke.

The missing hour returned quietly. Candles regained their length. Cards flew backward into players’ hands. The mayor’s clocks apologized with thirteen simultaneous chimes. Most people remembered nothing.

Mara returned to the bakery and found the seven impossible loaves reduced again to dough.

She began to knead.

At five minutes past six, the blue door opened.

Elias crossed the street in his gray coat. His hair was combed, and every button sat properly in its hole.

“Good morning, Miss Vale.”

“Good morning, Mr. Or.”

“Is this fresh?”

Mara placed a raisin roll on the counter.

“Fresh as a new regret.”

He smiled and set down a copper coin.

But before he could leave, Mara said, “You need an apprentice.”

Elias paused.

“No.”

“You missed breakfast. An hour escaped. You nearly stopped existing.”

“All excellent reasons for you to avoid the position.”

“How long have you been alone in that tower?”

“That depends on which calendar one considers legitimate.”

“More than a hundred years?”

“Yes.”

“Two hundred?”

“Yes.”

“Three?”

“I see no profit in this conversation.”

Mara folded her arms.

Across the street, the tower’s stones glowed pale in the morning light.

“I have spent seventeen years watching that door,” she said. “I thought I was merely curious. Now I think part of me was waiting.”

“The work is difficult.”

“So is bread.”

“The hours are worse.”

“So are customers.”

“You will lose things.”

“I already have.”

Elias studied her for a long time.

At last, he took the roll and broke it in half.

He placed one half on the counter between them.

“An apprentice,” he said, “must arrive before six.”

“I own a bakery. I arrive before everyone.”

“An apprentice must ask no clock for mercy.”

“Clocks have never shown me any.”

“And an apprentice must understand that what is lost cannot always be returned.”

Mara thought of a storm, a fiddle, and a room filled with laughter she could no longer hear.

“I understand,” she said.

Elias nodded.

The following morning, the people of Bellwether woke to find the bakery closed. On its door hung a sign:

**BREAD WILL BE SOLD IN THE AFTERNOONS.  
MORNINGS ARE NOW RESERVED FOR TIME.**

From then on, two figures crossed the street before dawn: a tall man in a gray coat and a woman carrying a basket of warm rolls.

Years passed.

The citizens grew accustomed to seeing lamplight beneath the blue door. Children still dared one another to knock, and old men still listened to the clocks after midnight. Sometimes they heard voices inside: Elias explaining the moods of pendulums, Mara arguing that every tower needed a proper kitchen, both of them shouting as some unruly minute escaped beneath a cabinet.

Bellwether never again lost an hour.

It lost other things, of course. All towns do.

It lost a bridge to a flood, a steeple to lightning, and three elections to dishonesty. People lost keys, tempers, fortunes, and friends. Children lost their fear of darkness and later lost the certainty that adults understood the world. Lovers lost letters they should have kept. Parents lost the sound of small feet in empty rooms.

Yet now and then, at the instant something precious slipped beyond reach, a clock in the stone tower began to tick.

Mara would lift it gently from the receiving table. She would polish its case, wind its spring, and attach a small brass plaque.

Some were labeled with great events:

**THE NIGHT THE SHIPS CAME HOME**

**THE MORNING THE QUEEN FORGAVE HER BROTHER**

**THE FINAL HOUR OF THE LONG WINTER**

Others held moments no history would record:

**TEN MINUTES BENEATH AN APPLE TREE**

**A BABY’S FIRST DREAM**

**THE LAST TIME TWO SISTERS SPOKE WITHOUT ANGER**

And on the highest shelf, beside a black wooden clock, stood a small copper timepiece.

Its plaque read:

**A STORM, A FIDDLE, AND MUDDY FEET**

Mara did not remember placing it there.

Still, whenever she climbed the ladder to dust it, she felt an inexplicable warmth. Sometimes she imagined she heard laughter within its ticking—two voices, dearly familiar, calling to a barefoot child.

She never tried to open it.

Some memories, she had learned, did not need to belong to us in order to remain loved.

And some lost hours, if carefully kept, continued to shine in the dark.

The Museum of Forgotten Weather

On the morning the rain forgot how to fall, Mara Venn woke to find a silver drop trembling outside her bedroom window.

It hung three stories above the street, perfectly still.

Other drops were suspended beyond it, thousands of them, perhaps millions, each one catching the pale dawn and holding a tiny upside-down city within. Below, people emerged from houses and shops with their palms raised. Children jumped to touch the nearest drops. Cyclists wove carefully between beads of water floating at eye level. A dog barked at a puddle that had stopped splashing halfway beneath a wagon wheel.

Mara opened the window and touched the silver drop.

It was cold and solid, like a glass marble.

“Oh,” she whispered. “Not again.”

She dressed quickly, pinning her gray hair beneath a black hat, and hurried downstairs. On her front door hung a brass plate:

MARA VENN  
CURATOR OF IMPOSSIBLE CLIMATES  
KNOCK TWICE

The museum occupied the ground floor of her narrow house, though few people knew it existed. From the street it appeared to be a small repair shop, its window crowded with broken barometers, rusted weather vanes, and clocks that displayed phases of the moon instead of hours.

Inside, however, a corridor extended far beyond the physical boundaries of the building. Its shelves held bottles, cabinets, jars, drawers, and sealed wooden boxes. Each object bore a handwritten label.

A THUNDERSTORM THAT APOLOGIZED AFTER EVERY CLAP.

THE LAST FROST OF THE OLD KINGDOM.

A BREEZE THAT KNEW EVERYONE’S CHILDHOOD NAME.

SEVEN MINUTES OF SUNLIGHT FROM THE DAY THE SEA TURNED GREEN.

Mara had inherited the collection from her mother, who had inherited it from an aunt, who claimed to have received the first impossible weather in payment from a traveling magician. For seventy years, Mara had collected atmospheric events that behaved as weather should not.

She had seen snowflakes rise from the ground and assemble into clouds. She had heard fog sing lullabies to ships. She had once spent six weeks negotiating with an arrogant rainbow that refused to appear unless the mayor publicly complimented all seven of its colors.

But frozen rain was different.

Frozen rain meant someone had opened Cabinet Nine.

Mara found the museum’s inner door unlocked.

Beyond it, at the end of a corridor lined with sleeping hurricanes, Cabinet Nine stood open. Its door was iron, its hinges thick as fists. Three locks hung broken from the handle.

The cabinet was empty.

Mara stared at the oval patch of dust where a blue bottle had stood for nearly forty years.

Its label lay on the floor.

THE HESITATION OF THE SKY  
Collected over Bellweather, 3 October  
Dangerous if released.  
Do not confuse with ordinary doubt.

Mara knelt and touched the broken locks. They had not been cut or smashed. They had simply decided to open.

That was troubling.

A sound came from behind her.

“Don’t move,” said a young voice.

Mara stayed where she was.

A boy stepped out from behind a shelf of miniature tornadoes. He was thin, perhaps fourteen, with wet black hair and a coat several sizes too large. He held a museum broom like a spear.

“You’re the thief,” he said.

“I own the broom.”

The boy glanced at it, then lowered the handle slightly.

“You’re Mara Venn?”

“I am. And you are standing in a restricted corridor.”

“I followed someone inside.”

“Who?”

“A woman in a yellow coat. She had a bottle under her arm.”

Mara rose. “When?”

“Before dawn. I sleep across the street, above the bakery. I saw her break into your shop.”

“The front door wasn’t broken.”

“She opened it with a key.”

“That is worse.”

The boy nodded gravely. “I thought so.”

Mara studied him. His eyes were dark and steady, but his hands tightened around the broom. “What is your name?”

“Eli.”

“Why did you follow her?”

He looked toward the window at the motionless rain.

“My sister was outside when it stopped.”

Mara’s impatience vanished. “Show me.”

They hurried into the street.

Eli led her two blocks north, weaving through the bewildered crowd. Some people were laughing now, taking photographs beside hanging raindrops. Vendors collected them in baskets and sold them as jewels that melted on the tongue. No one yet understood that the rain was only the beginning.

At the corner of Lantern Street, they found Eli’s sister.

She was frozen in the act of running.

One foot hovered above the pavement. Her red scarf streamed behind her, stiff as painted cloth. Her face was turned toward something across the street, and her lips were parted around a word she had not finished speaking.

Mara approached carefully.

The girl was not encased in ice. She was warm. A pulse moved faintly in her throat, but so slowly that Mara had to wait nearly a minute to feel it.

“What happened?” she asked.

“We were delivering bread,” Eli said. “Nella saw the woman in yellow come out of your shop. She shouted at her. The woman uncorked the bottle, and then the rain stopped. Nella stopped too.”

Mara looked at the girl’s suspended foot.

“She hesitated,” Mara said.

“For what?”

“I don’t know. But when the bottle opened, every hesitation in the city became permanent.”

Eli’s face tightened. “Can you fix her?”

“If we recover what was stolen.”

“And if we don’t?”

Mara looked up.

Clouds covered the sky in a gray sheet. They seemed ordinary, but at their edges birds hovered without flapping. On a nearby roof, a chimney’s smoke had paused midway into the air. All around them, decisions were slowly failing.

A carriage stood at the intersection because its driver could not choose whether to turn left or right. A woman held two apples, unable to decide which to buy. A man at an open door had forgotten whether he meant to enter or leave.

“It will spread,” Mara said. “First through the city, then wherever the clouds go. By tonight, people may be unable to choose anything. By tomorrow, hearts may hesitate between one beat and the next.”

Eli swallowed. “Then we’d better find the woman.”

Mara expected him to stay with his sister. Instead, he placed the broom beside Nella like a guard’s staff, adjusted her scarf, and turned to Mara.

“You saw which way she went?” Mara asked.

“No. But Nella did.”

He pointed across the street.

There, in the window of a closed tailor’s shop, someone had drawn a circle in the condensation. Inside the circle was a crude picture of a bell.

“Nella draws what she’s thinking,” Eli said. “Even when she shouldn’t. Especially when she shouldn’t.”

Mara recognized the symbol.

“Bellweather Station,” she said.

They crossed the city on foot because no driver could confidently choose a destination. With every street, the paralysis worsened. Pedestrians paused before curbs. Shopkeepers stood motionless behind counters. In a public garden, a gardener had lifted his shears around a rose but could not decide where to cut.

Only Mara and Eli seemed mostly unaffected.

“Why can we still move?” Eli asked.

“You knew what you wanted before the bottle opened. You wanted to help Nella. A clear purpose protects you, for a while.”

“What about you?”

“I want my bottle back.”

“That doesn’t sound as important.”

“It is to me.”

He frowned, but kept walking.

Bellweather Station had been abandoned for decades. Its great clock had no hands, and weeds grew between the platform stones. Once, trains had carried passengers from the city to the northern coast. Then the sea withdrew, the coast became mud, and no one found a reason to travel there anymore.

Mara and Eli entered through a broken gate.

A yellow coat disappeared at the far end of the platform.

“There!” Eli shouted.

They ran after it.

The woman moved through a doorway beneath the clock tower. Mara followed, climbing a spiral staircase slick with dust. At the top, they entered the chamber behind the clock face.

The woman in yellow stood beside the motionless gears.

She was not young. Her white hair fell loose around a narrow face, and rainwater shone on her coat. In one hand she held the blue bottle. The cork was missing.

“Put it down, Iona,” Mara said.

Eli looked between them. “You know her?”

Iona smiled without warmth. “She remembers me after all.”

“I remember everyone who steals from me.”

“Then you remember why I left.”

Mara stepped into the room. “You were dismissed.”

“I was your apprentice for twelve years.”

“You opened a jar of backward lightning in the reading room.”

“By accident.”

“You opened it twice.”

“The second time was to put it back.”

“The roof burned for a month.”

Eli raised a hand. “Is this relevant to saving my sister?”

“No,” said both women.

Iona placed the bottle on a wooden beam. Blue mist curled from its mouth and slipped through cracks in the clock face.

“I needed your attention,” she said.

“You have endangered every living thing beneath these clouds.”

“Yes. I thought that might work.”

Mara took another step. “Cork the bottle.”

“I can’t.”

“You opened it.”

“And the cork fell into the rain.”

Eli groaned.

Mara studied Iona’s face. Beneath the defiance, she saw exhaustion. There were shadows under her eyes and burn marks on her fingers.

“What happened?” Mara asked.

Iona turned toward the clock face. Through its cloudy glass, the city lay below them, caught between movements.

“Three months ago,” she said, “I went north to Bellweather.”

“There is no Bellweather. Not anymore.”

“The town remains. Thirty houses, a church, a school, and four streets where nobody visits. The sea left, the railway closed, and the maps stopped printing its name, but people still live there.”

“Why were you there?”

“Collecting a drought.”

“Droughts are not museum material.”

“This one was afraid of water.”

Mara almost asked how a drought had expressed fear, then decided against it.

Iona continued. “The people told me something strange was happening. Every morning, they woke remembering two versions of the previous day. In one, they had eaten supper and gone to bed. In the other, a storm had come from the old sea, and everyone in town had vanished.”

Eli moved closer. “Which one was real?”

“Both, perhaps. Or neither. The memories grew stronger. Children drew waves taller than the church. Adults heard bells underwater. Then people began disappearing for seconds at a time.”

Mara felt a coldness unrelated to rain.

“The Unchosen Storm,” she said.

Iona nodded.

Eli waited. “What is that?”

Mara looked down at the blue mist circling their shoes.

“Every event creates possibilities,” she said. “Things that might happen but do not. Usually, those possibilities fade. But weather touches too many lives at once. Once in a great while, an unchosen storm refuses to disappear. It waits outside reality until it finds a place uncertain enough to enter.”

“Bellweather,” Eli said.

“The town was abandoned by maps, trains, and the sea,” Iona replied. “Even the country seems unsure whether it exists.”

“And you released the Hesitation to stop the storm,” Mara said.

“To delay it. If the sky cannot decide to change, the Unchosen Storm cannot enter.”

“You could have asked me for help.”

Iona laughed once. “I sent seven letters.”

“I received none.”

“I sent messages through the Atmospheric Society.”

“They haven’t spoken to me since the rainbow incident.”

“I came to the museum yesterday. You wouldn’t open the door.”

“I was cataloguing a private eclipse.”

“You are always cataloguing something.”

The accusation landed harder than Mara expected.

Below them, the city was becoming quiet. Not peaceful—empty. Conversation required choices, one word after another. People now stood in streets with their mouths half-open.

Eli picked up the blue bottle.

Both women shouted, “Don’t!”

He nearly dropped it.

“If this pauses the storm,” he said, “and closing it starts the storm again, what are we supposed to do?”

Iona lowered her voice. “There is one way. The Unchosen Storm can be made real. Not here, but over Bellweather, where it belongs. Once chosen, it will become ordinary weather and spend itself.”

“You said everyone vanishes,” Eli said.

“They vanish in one version of the day.”

“And in the other?”

Iona’s gaze fell.

Mara answered. “The storm never arrives.”

“So choose that one.”

“You cannot choose an event out of existence,” Mara said. “Not once it has grown this strong. You can only choose how to meet it.”

Eli stared at her. “That sounds like something people say when they don’t have a plan.”

“It is.”

The clock gears groaned.

Outside, the suspended raindrops began moving—not down, but north. Slowly at first, then faster, they streamed through the city like a river in the air.

“The storm is pulling them,” Iona said.

“The hesitation is weakening.”

“Or the Unchosen Storm has learned to use it.”

The clock tower shook. From somewhere far away came the sound of a bell tolling beneath water.

Mara took the bottle from Eli. Blue mist poured over her hands.

“We go to Bellweather.”

“There are no trains,” Iona said.

Mara looked down through the tower at the tracks.

An old locomotive stood beneath a collapsed shed, red with rust. Grass covered its wheels. Its chimney was home to a family of swallows.

“Every machine remembers being useful,” Mara said. “Some only need the right weather.”

Back at the museum, in a drawer labeled FOR JOURNEYS THAT SHOULD NO LONGER BE POSSIBLE, Mara kept six lumps of coal from the first train ever to reach Bellweather.

They returned through streets nearly petrified by indecision. Eli ran ahead to Lantern Street and stopped beside Nella. For a moment, he stood with his forehead against hers.

“I’m going to finish this,” he told her. “You’d better finish that step.”

Then he followed Mara into the museum.

She found the coal in its drawer. Iona collected a jar of steam from an emperor’s final railway journey, while Eli filled a leather case with supplies: bottled tailwind, emergency moonlight, two wool blankets, and a paper packet marked SANDWICHES, PROBABLY.

They carried everything back to the station.

The locomotive resisted them.

Its door refused to open. Its wheels had settled deep into the earth. When Mara put the historic coal into its furnace, the lumps sat cold and offended.

“Speak to it,” she told Eli.

“Why me?”

“Machines prefer young people. They assume you have destinations.”

Eli climbed into the driver’s cabin and placed both hands on the corroded controls.

“My sister is waiting,” he said. “And there’s a town everyone forgot. If you take us there, you’ll be the first train to reach it in forty-three years.”

Nothing happened.

Then, with a sigh like an old man rising from a chair, the furnace ignited.

The locomotive shuddered. Vines snapped from its wheels. The swallows fled its chimney in outrage. Steam poured around the platform, smelling faintly of coal smoke and royal perfume.

They climbed aboard.

The train rolled north.

At first it moved through familiar districts: brick factories, rows of narrow houses, then fields divided by stone walls. The airborne river of rain followed overhead.

As the city disappeared behind them, the landscape grew uncertain.

Roads ended in ponds. Trees cast shadows in the wrong direction. Once, they passed a station where transparent passengers waited in clothes from fifty years before. They lifted their hands as the train went by.

“Are they ghosts?” Eli asked.

“Possibilities,” Mara said. “People who almost took this train.”

He stopped waving.

By evening they reached the old coast.

Bellweather stood on a ridge above miles of dark mud. Rusted ships lay on their sides where the harbor had been. The town’s windows glowed, but no smoke rose from its chimneys.

Above it towered the storm.

It was larger than the sky.

Clouds folded over one another in impossible layers: black thunderheads, green summer rain, white blizzards, spirals of red dust. Lightning flashed upward. Waves moved through the air without water. Within the clouds, Mara saw streets, houses, and human figures appearing and disappearing.

The train stopped at Bellweather Station.

No one waited on the platform.

“Where is everybody?” Eli whispered.

“At home,” Iona said. “Remembering two evenings.”

The floating rain swept over them and vanished into the storm. At once, the blue bottle in Mara’s hands cracked.

The hesitation was ending.

“Where do we release it?” Mara asked.

“The church,” said Iona. “That’s where the bell rings in everyone’s memory.”

They ran uphill.

The town flickered around them. In one moment the streets were dry and abandoned. In the next, rain flooded the gutters and pale figures hurried past, unable to see them. Mara glimpsed a woman carrying a child, then an empty doorway, then a fishing boat sailing where the road should have been.

At the church, the door shifted between open and closed.

Eli charged through at the instant it became open. Mara and Iona followed.

Inside, the townspeople sat in the pews.

Thirty, perhaps forty people stared silently toward the bell rope hanging beside the altar. Some wore nightclothes. Some clutched umbrellas. A little girl held a wooden boat.

An old man rose.

“You came back,” he said to Iona.

“I brought help.”

His eyes moved to Mara. “Can you make the storm go away?”

“No,” Mara said.

Iona gave her an angry look, but Mara continued.

“I can help you decide what happens when it arrives.”

Murmurs filled the church.

“We already know,” said a woman in the front pew. “We disappear.”

“You might,” Mara said. “If you meet the storm as a town that is already vanishing.”

The floor trembled. Dust fell from the rafters.

“What does that mean?” the old man asked.

“Bellweather exists, but the world doubts it. Worse, you doubt it. You live among empty houses, waiting for the last family to leave. The storm has entered that uncertainty.”

“How do we stop doubting?”

“Choose to stay,” Eli said.

Everyone looked at him.

He stood beside the bell rope, small in his oversized coat.

“You don’t all have to stay forever,” he continued. “But choose something. Fix the station. Put the town back on maps. Build a road. Ring the bell every morning until someone complains. My sister says people disappear when no one expects them anywhere. So make yourselves expected.”

The little girl with the boat raised her hand. “Expected by whom?”

Eli thought.

“Us,” he said. “We came all this way.”

The church flickered.

For one instant there were no people, no pews, no roof—only deep green water and the bell swaying in silence.

Then the building returned.

The blue bottle shattered in Mara’s hands.

Rain crashed against the church windows.

Not ordinary rain. It struck in conflicting directions, carrying snow, ash, salt, flower petals, and tiny silver fish. Thunder spoke in voices the townspeople recognized as their own.

The Unchosen Storm had arrived.

“Ring the bell!” Mara shouted.

The old man seized the rope, but it would not move.

“Too many possibilities,” Iona said. “The bell doesn’t know which sound to make.”

Mara opened the leather case. She threw aside the moonlight and blankets, searching desperately.

Her hand found the packet marked SANDWICHES, PROBABLY.

Inside were no sandwiches. There was a cork.

“The bottle’s cork,” Eli said.

“It must have fallen into your supply case at the museum.”

Mara closed her fingers around it. The cork still held a trace of the Hesitation of the Sky.

She climbed onto the altar and pressed it against the bell rope.

Immediately, the rope stopped flickering.

“Now!” she cried.

Eli, Iona, the old man, and half the town pulled together.

The bell rang.

Its first note shook the church.

Its second rolled across the dead harbor.

Its third traveled south along the railway, through the uncertain countryside, all the way to the city where Nella stood with one foot above the pavement.

The storm broke.

Rain fell straight down.

Thunder became only thunder. Snow melted before reaching the ground. The impossible waves collapsed into harmless water that rushed through Bellweather’s streets and washed decades of dust from the stones.

The people in the church did not vanish.

They cheered until the bell’s echo faded.

Then they began to argue about what to do next.

Mara had never heard anything so hopeful.

Three days later, the train returned to the city carrying the first official delegation from Bellweather in forty-three years. The mayor came with petitions for a road, a post office, and restoration of the railway. The little girl brought her wooden boat and announced her intention to become harbor master whether or not the sea returned.

At Lantern Street, Nella completed her step.

She stumbled, caught herself, and finished the word that had been trapped on her lips.

“Thief!”

Then she saw Mara, Iona, Eli, and the crowd of strangers behind them.

“Oh,” she said. “Did I miss something?”

Eli hugged her so tightly that neither of them could speak.

The city recovered quickly. Most citizens treated the event as an unusual storm and returned to their routines. Vendors sold the last of the suspended raindrops until they melted. The mayor commissioned a report, misplaced it, and chose not to commission another.

At the museum, Mara replaced the locks on Cabinet Nine.

She added a new display near the entrance: a cracked blue bottle, an old railway ticket, and the cork that had steadied Bellweather’s bell rope.

The label read:

THE DAY THE SKY MADE UP ITS MIND  
Collected in several places at once.  
Dangerous, but not always in the expected way.

Iona stood beside her, reading it.

“You left out my drought,” she said.

“It was afraid of water. It can wait.”

“So I’m still dismissed?”

Mara adjusted the label until it was straight.

“Apprentices can be dismissed,” she said. “Colleagues are harder to remove.”

Iona smiled.

From the front room came a crash, followed by Eli’s voice.

“It was already broken!”

Nella shouted, “That cloud had my face!”

Mara closed her eyes.

After returning from Bellweather, Eli and Nella had begun helping at the museum in the afternoons. They were enthusiastic, fearless, and catastrophically curious. In one week, Eli had taught a breeze to cheat at cards, while Nella had drawn eyebrows on a sleeping cyclone. Mara had considered banning them both.

Instead, she had given them keys.

Now she and Iona hurried into the front room.

A small pink cloud bounced along the ceiling, making rude expressions. Broken glass glittered on the floor. Eli held an empty jar. Nella stood on a chair, trying to catch the cloud with a butterfly net.

“It remembers everyone who ever insulted it,” Eli explained.

The cloud shaped itself into Mara’s face and stuck out its tongue.

Iona laughed.

Mara sighed, removed her hat, and reached for a jar of disciplinary sunshine.

Outside, a gentle rain began to fall.

Every drop went exactly where it meant to go.

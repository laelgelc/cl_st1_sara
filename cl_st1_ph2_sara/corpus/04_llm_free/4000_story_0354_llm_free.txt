### The Cartographer of Lost Roads

On the morning the road disappeared, Mara Vale was drawing it.

She sat at her kitchen table with a ruler beneath one hand and a cup of cold tea beneath the other. Outside, rain tapped softly against the windows of the old tollhouse. Her pencil moved over thick cream paper, tracing the narrow lane that ran from Bellwether Bridge to the village of Orison.

The road had existed for three hundred years. It curved around Hazel Wood, crossed the eastern fields, and passed beneath a pair of leaning ash trees before reaching the village square. Mara had walked it every day as a child and driven along it nearly every day as an adult.

Yet when she looked out of her window, it was gone.

Beyond the tollhouse gate lay only wet grass.

Mara stared for a long moment. Then she put down her pencil, pulled on her boots, and stepped outside.

The rain had thinned to mist. She crossed the garden, unlatched the gate, and stood where the road should have been. There were no wheel tracks, no ditches, no broken stones. Wild grass spread from one hedgerow to the other, silvered with rain. Even the old milepost had vanished.

A delivery van waited on the far side of the meadow. Its driver leaned out of the window.

“Morning!” he called. “Is this the way to Orison?”

“It was yesterday,” Mara answered.

The driver laughed uncertainly.

Mara did not.

She walked into the grass. The earth beneath it felt firm, but there was no trace of gravel or tar. She followed the remembered curve toward Hazel Wood. At the eastern field, she found Farmer Pell standing beside his tractor, scratching his beard.

“Lost something?” Mara asked.

“My road,” he said. “And three sheep, though I suppose the sheep are a separate matter.”

“The road is missing all the way to the village.”

“So it seems.”

They stood in silence, studying the meadow.

“Can roads go missing?” Pell asked.

“Not usually.”

“You’re the mapmaker.”

“Cartographer.”

“Then cartograph us a new one.”

By noon, half the village had gathered at the edge of the vanished road. Children ran through the grass, delighted by the impossibility. Adults argued about ownership, access, and blame. Councillor Venn arrived wearing a bright orange raincoat and announced that the matter would be investigated.

“By whom?” Mara asked.

“The appropriate people.”

“Who are the appropriate people for a disappearing road?”

Venn frowned. “We’ll determine that during the investigation.”

Mara left them to it.

She returned home and examined the map on her table. The pencil line she had drawn that morning ended at Bellwether Bridge. Everything beyond it had faded from the paper.

That troubled her more than the missing road itself.

The map was part of a commission from the county archive: a complete survey of the old routes around Orison before the government widened the northern highway. Mara worked using measurements, historical records, satellite images, and long walks through the countryside. Her maps were not decorative. They were exact.

Lines did not fade from them.

She took a magnifying glass from her desk. The paper showed no sign of erasure. The road simply ceased to be represented, as though it had never crossed the page.

Behind her, the front door opened.

Only one person entered without knocking.

“Mara?”

Her younger brother, Elias, stood in the hallway, rain dripping from his dark hair. He had the anxious expression he wore whenever he had bad news and hoped charm might disguise it.

“You’ve seen the road,” Mara said.

“I’ve failed to see the road. That’s the problem.”

He removed his coat and hung it beside hers. Though they were twins, few people guessed it. Mara was small and deliberate, with straight black hair cut at her chin. Elias was tall, untidy, and forever moving, as if remaining still might allow some old sorrow to catch him.

He came to the table.

“Did you do this?” he asked.

Mara looked up sharply.

“I don’t mean intentionally,” he said. “But you were drawing it when it vanished.”

“How did you know that?”

“I saw you through the window.”

“You were here this morning?”

“I came by early. I didn’t want to disturb you.”

“You always disturb me.”

“Yes, but usually with confidence.”

Mara folded her arms. “Why were you here?”

Elias looked toward the window. Beyond it, villagers wandered through the wet field where the road had been.

“I need to show you something.”

From inside his coat, he took a roll of paper tied with blue thread. Mara recognized the thread first. Their mother had tied letters, parcels, and sprigs of drying herbs with that same shade of blue.

Their mother had been dead for eleven years.

“Where did you get that?” Mara whispered.

“In the attic.”

“What were you doing in the attic?”

“Looking for the brass telescope.”

“You sold that last winter.”

“Then I was looking for something else.”

He placed the roll on the table and untied it.

The paper was brittle with age. When he opened it, Mara saw a map drawn in faded brown ink. It showed Orison and the surrounding countryside, but not as they were now. The river followed an older course. Hazel Wood stretched nearly to the village. Several forgotten hamlets appeared in the hills.

In the lower corner, in their mother’s handwriting, were the words:

**A map of roads no longer remembered.**

Mara touched the edge of the paper.

“I’ve never seen this.”

“Neither had I.”

“This isn’t possible. Mother made garden plans and watercolors. She wasn’t a cartographer.”

“She taught you.”

“She taught me to draw.”

“She taught you to notice.”

Mara ignored him and examined the map more closely. Thin lines crossed the countryside in a dense web. Some ended abruptly. Others ran through rivers or forests. One went directly through the center of the tollhouse.

Then she saw the road from Bellwether Bridge to Orison.

It had been drawn in black ink rather than brown. As Mara watched, the line trembled.

A bead of ink rose from the paper.

It gathered itself like a living drop, slid toward the map’s edge, and vanished.

At that same moment, someone shouted outside.

Mara ran to the window. A line of trees now stood where the road had disappeared. They were ancient oaks, massive and twisted, though the field had been empty moments before.

Elias joined her.

“That seems bad,” he said.

Mara turned on him. “Tell me everything.”

They carried the old map to the sitting room, where no drafts could reach it, and weighted the corners with books. Elias explained that he had found it inside a wooden case hidden under the attic floorboards. The case also contained a compass, a silver pen, and a letter addressed to Mara.

“Where’s the letter?”

“I haven’t opened it.”

“That would be unusually respectful of you.”

“I opened it,” he admitted, “but the page was blank.”

He gave it to her.

The envelope bore her name. Inside was a single sheet of white paper. Mara held it near the fire, turned it over, and examined it against the light.

Nothing.

“Did the case contain anything else?”

“A notebook.”

“And?”

“I may have misplaced it.”

Mara stared at him.

“I definitely misplaced it,” he said.

She closed her eyes. “Where?”

“I brought everything downstairs. Then I heard something in the garden. When I came back, the notebook was gone.”

“Something in the garden?”

“A voice.”

“Whose voice?”

Elias hesitated.

“Mara’s,” he said.

Neither of them spoke for several seconds.

“You heard my voice,” Mara said, “while I was in my own house.”

“It came from the orchard. You were calling my name.”

“And you followed it?”

“I thought you were hurt.”

“You thought I was hurt in an orchard half a mile from where you had just seen me?”

“I didn’t say it was a good decision.”

A sudden scratching came from the sitting-room wall.

They both turned.

The sound traveled slowly behind the wallpaper, like the movement of a fingernail. It stopped beside the fireplace. A narrow black line appeared there, running from floor to ceiling.

The wall split open.

Not cracked—opened.

Where the wallpaper had been, Mara saw a narrow lane beneath a summer sky. White flowers grew along its banks. In the distance stood a little house with a red door.

A woman waited outside.

Even after eleven years, Mara knew the slope of those shoulders and the way that head tilted slightly to one side.

“Mother,” Elias breathed.

He stepped forward.

Mara caught his arm.

The woman raised one hand.

She was too far away for them to see her face clearly, but her voice crossed the impossible distance.

“Elias,” she called.

He pulled against Mara’s grip.

“That isn’t her,” Mara said.

“How do you know?”

“Because she’s dead.”

“That doesn’t prove anything today.”

He broke free and moved toward the opening. Mara seized the fire poker and thrust it across the gap just before he stepped through.

The lane vanished.

The wall snapped shut, cutting the iron poker in two.

Elias stood with his hand outstretched.

For once, he had nothing to say.

By evening, Orison had begun to come apart.

A staircase appeared in the village square, descending into the earth. It led to the ruins of a chapel demolished a century earlier. Three houses on Mill Street changed places. The river forgot one of its bends and flooded the cricket field.

Most troubling of all, people began seeing roads where roads could not be.

They opened in mirrors, cupboards, wells, and dreams. They appeared at the ends of corridors or behind garden gates. Along them waited lost things: childhood homes, vanished pets, squandered fortunes, dead friends.

Mara and Elias carried their mother’s map to the village hall. Councillor Venn had established an emergency committee, though nobody knew what powers it possessed. Farmer Pell sat near the door holding one of his missing sheep, which he had found standing in his bedroom.

Mara spread the map across a table.

“I think the missing road is only the beginning,” she said.

Venn peered at the paper. “Is this an official map?”

“No.”

“Then I can’t accept it as evidence.”

The floor beneath his chair became a pond.

Councillor Venn splashed, shouted, and climbed onto the table.

“We’ll make an exception,” he said.

Mara explained what little she understood. The old map did not merely record forgotten roads. Somehow, it contained them—or kept them separate. Now the boundaries were failing. Roads that had disappeared from the world were returning, bringing with them the places and possibilities to which they had once led.

“Possibilities?” asked Pell.

“Things that could have happened, perhaps. Or things people wish had happened.”

“My wife saw our son at the end of a lane behind the barn,” Pell said quietly.

Mara knew that Pell’s son had drowned at twelve.

“Did she follow him?”

“No. But she wants to.”

The room fell silent.

“There must be a way to close them,” Elias said.

At that moment, the blank letter in Mara’s pocket grew warm.

She took it out.

Writing appeared across the page, line by line, in her mother’s hand.

**My dear Mara,**

**If you are reading this, the map has opened. I hoped it never would. Every road exists twice: once beneath our feet and once inside us. The first kind carries the body. The second carries longing. A skilled cartographer may draw either, but the roads of longing are dangerous. They do not lead where they promise. They lead only farther from the world.**

**The map must be completed. Find the road that has no destination and draw its end.**

**Forgive me for leaving this task to you.**

**Mother**

Below the signature, a final sentence appeared:

**Do not let Elias follow me.**

Mara handed the letter to her brother.

He read it twice.

“She knew,” he said.

“Apparently.”

“She knew I would see her.”

“Or knew you’d chase the first impossible thing that called your name.”

His expression hardened. “You saw her too.”

“Yes.”

“And you didn’t want to follow?”

Mara looked away.

Outside the village hall, bells began ringing. Not the church bells of Orison, but many bells, some deep and distant, others sharp as glass. The sound came from roads unfolding all over the village.

Mara studied the old map. More lines were turning black. Ink trembled across the page.

“The road with no destination,” she murmured. “How are we supposed to find it?”

Elias pointed near the western edge. “What about that?”

A faint line began at the tollhouse and traveled beyond the borders of the map. Unlike the others, it had no branches, names, or landmarks.

Mara touched it.

The village hall disappeared.

She stood beneath a gray sky on a straight white road. Elias was beside her, still holding the map. Behind them, the road stretched back toward Orison, where rooftops rose above a forest of impossible towers, bridges, ships, and trees.

Ahead was emptiness.

“No destination,” Elias said. “That looks promising.”

“You have an unusual definition of promising.”

They began to walk.

The white road seemed made of chalk. On either side lay a flat gray plain without stones or grass. Their footsteps made no sound. There was no sun, yet everything was evenly lit.

After a while, doors appeared beside the road.

The first was blue and covered in scratches. Behind it, Mara heard their father laughing. He had left when the twins were seven and never returned.

Elias kept walking.

The second door opened onto a room in which a younger Mara stood beside an unpacked suitcase. It was the morning she had refused a place at the Royal Institute of Geography because their mother was ill. In the vision, Mara lifted the suitcase and left home.

She saw herself travel. She saw deserts, ice fields, and cities bright with unfamiliar stars. She saw awards on walls and her name printed in books.

Then the door closed.

“You never told me,” Elias said.

“There was nothing to tell.”

“You stayed because of us.”

“I stayed because I chose to.”

“Do you regret it?”

Mara looked back at the door.

“Every choice leaves a ghost,” she said. “That doesn’t mean you chose wrongly.”

They walked on.

More doors appeared.

Behind one, Elias was married and held a sleeping child. Behind another, he was a celebrated musician playing before a vast audience. In a third, he sat at their mother’s bedside on the night she died.

In truth, Elias had been in another city that night. He had arrived at dawn, breathless and too late.

He stopped.

Through the doorway, their mother opened her eyes.

“You came,” she said.

Elias made a broken sound.

Mara reached for him, but he stepped through.

The gray plain vanished beneath her feet. Wind tore the map from Elias’s hands, sending it tumbling along the white road. Mara caught his coat and pulled.

Inside the room, their mother reached toward him.

“Stay,” she whispered.

Elias leaned forward.

Mara slapped him.

The room blinked out.

They fell together on the road.

For a moment, Elias lay motionless. Then he rolled onto his back, one hand pressed to his cheek.

“You hit me.”

“You’re welcome.”

“She was there.”

“No. Your guilt was there, wearing her face.”

“You don’t know that.”

“Yes, I do.”

“How?”

“Because Mother never asked us to stay.”

Elias looked toward the blank sky. Tears slid silently into his hair.

Mara found the map several yards away. A long tear ran through its center. Black ink poured from the torn edges, spreading across the white road like roots.

Around them, the gray plain began to fill with voices.

Thousands of lost roads emerged from the horizon. They twisted overhead, crossed one another, and plunged into the earth. Figures moved along them: children chasing vanished summers, old people returning to burned houses, soldiers walking toward battles they believed they could change.

“We’re running out of time,” Mara said.

She removed the silver pen from her pocket. Elias had brought it from the attic, though neither of them knew how to use it. Its nib was dry.

“Draw the end,” he said.

“With what ink?”

The black stream from the torn map curled around her boots.

Mara knelt and dipped the pen into it.

At once, the road shuddered.

Images rushed through her mind: every place she had left, every choice she had not made, every word she wished she could recall. She saw herself at eight, holding Elias’s hand beneath the kitchen table while their parents argued. She saw her mother hiding the map beneath the floorboards. She saw the old woman’s final night, when Mara had sat beside her bed and pretended not to be afraid.

Last came a memory she had buried deepest.

Her mother, barely conscious, whispering, “There is no road back, darling.”

Mara had thought it cruel.

Now she understood it was a warning.

The roads of longing did not return what was lost. They consumed the present by persuading travelers that life existed elsewhere.

Mara set the pen to the map.

“What are you drawing?” Elias asked.

“The only true end of every road.”

She drew a small circle at the end of the faint line. Within it, she marked the tollhouse, the kitchen table, and two tiny figures standing beside a window.

“Home?” Elias said.

“Not exactly.”

The white road cracked.

A roar rose from the gray plain. Doors flew open on both sides, revealing glittering lives. Their father called to them. Their mother called to them. Unlived children, unwritten books, unspoken apologies—all shouted at once.

Mara finished the final line.

“Not home,” she said. “Here.”

The map folded itself.

The white road rushed backward beneath them. Doors shattered into light. The gray world spun, and for one impossible second Mara felt every lost possibility pass through her like wind.

Then she landed on the floor of the village hall.

Elias fell beside her.

The pond beneath Councillor Venn’s chair vanished with a wet sigh, leaving him suspended briefly in the air before he crashed to the floor. Outside, the bells stopped.

Everyone waited.

A woman ran in from the square.

“The roads are gone!” she cried.

Mara stood slowly and looked through the window.

Orison had returned. The river curved through its proper channel. The staircase in the square had disappeared. Houses stood where they belonged.

Beyond the rooftops, the road to Bellwether Bridge wound past Hazel Wood and crossed the eastern fields.

It was raining again.

Three days later, Mara sat at her kitchen table and completed her county map.

The old map had changed. All the strange roads were gone, leaving only ordinary routes in faded brown ink. The letter from her mother was blank again. Elias wanted to burn both, but Mara placed them in a new wooden case and locked it in the archive beneath the tollhouse.

“Is that wise?” he asked.

“No,” she said. “But destroying dangerous things sometimes only makes people forget why they were dangerous.”

He considered this.

“You could put a note on the box.”

“I have.”

“What does it say?”

“‘Do not let Elias open this.’”

He smiled. It was not his old careless smile. That one had belonged to a man who believed every mistake could be outrun. This smile was smaller and sadder, but more honest.

They walked to Bellwether Bridge.

The restored road was damp beneath their feet. Villagers had spent the morning touching it, photographing it, and arguing over whether it seemed narrower than before. Farmer Pell’s sheep grazed in the eastern field as if nothing unusual had happened.

At the bridge, Elias leaned over the stone wall and watched the river.

“I’m leaving Orison,” he said.

Mara glanced at him.

“I’ve taken a job in the north. Repairing instruments at a school. I was going to tell you before all this.”

“When?”

“Soon.”

“You said that last year.”

“This time I bought a train ticket.”

She looked back along the road toward the tollhouse.

“Good,” she said.

“Good?”

“You should go.”

“I thought you’d be angry.”

“I am. You borrowed my suitcase.”

He laughed.

Below them, the river hurried east, never once mistaking motion for regret.

Elias left the following Monday. Mara drove him to the station before dawn. They did not make promises about writing every day or visiting every month. They had made such promises before and broken them. Instead, Elias hugged her on the platform and boarded the train.

As it pulled away, he raised one hand to the window.

For an instant, Mara imagined a thousand other endings. She saw herself stopping the train. She saw Elias remaining in Orison until they were both old. She saw them quarreling, reconciling, forgetting, remembering. She saw every road that love and fear might draw.

Then the train rounded the bend and vanished.

Mara returned to the tollhouse alone.

The morning was clear. Sunlight fell across her kitchen table, illuminating the new map. She sharpened a pencil and bent over the page.

There were still roads to draw—real roads, imperfect roads, roads that flooded in winter and cracked in summer. Roads that led away. Roads that brought people back. Roads that ended without explanation.

She traced each one carefully.

And when she came to the road between Bellwether Bridge and Orison, Mara drew it not as it had been, nor as she wished it might be, but as it was: a narrow line crossing the green earth, carrying every traveler forward.

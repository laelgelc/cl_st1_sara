# The Cartographer of Lost Roads

Every morning, before the city woke, Elias Vale walked to the end of Bellweather Street and drew a road that no longer existed.

He carried a folding stool, a tin box of charcoal, and twelve sheets of heavy paper. At the corner where the bakery leaned against the old clockmaker’s shop, he sat beneath a rusted streetlamp and began.

First came the paving stones, irregular and pale. Then the iron drain covers. Then the narrow houses with their crooked chimneys, each window bright with remembered light. Last, he drew the road itself, stretching beyond the page toward a hill that had been demolished thirty-seven years before.

People passing on their way to work sometimes stopped to watch.

“What street is that?” they would ask.

“Larkspur Lane,” Elias replied.

“There’s no Larkspur Lane.”

“Not anymore.”

Most laughed and moved on. Some shook their heads. A few bought a drawing for the price of a cup of tea. Elias accepted whatever they offered, though he always kept the best sketch for himself.

By eight o’clock, traffic filled Bellweather Street. Delivery vans blocked the view, shopkeepers rolled up their shutters, and the clockmaker placed a wooden sign on the pavement: REPAIRS WHILE YOU WAIT, though everyone knew waiting was exactly what the clockmaker’s repairs required.

Elias packed his charcoal into its tin and went home.

His rooms were above a tailor’s shop on Candlewick Square. Every wall was covered with maps. They showed vanished paths, abandoned bridges, lanes buried beneath office buildings, and staircases that had once joined one courtyard to another. Red threads connected certain maps. Small handwritten labels gave dates, names, and sometimes warnings.

DO NOT FOLLOW AFTER MIDNIGHT.

VISIBLE ONLY IN RAIN.

LAST WALKED BY M. VOSS, 14 OCTOBER.

At the center of the largest wall hung an unfinished map of the city. Its familiar streets had been drawn in black ink. The lost roads were in blue.

There were hundreds of them.

Elias had learned about lost roads from his mother.

When he was seven, she had taken him to a brick wall behind the central railway station. Weeds grew through cracks in the pavement, and the air smelled of smoke and wet iron.

“There used to be a path here,” she told him.

Elias looked at the wall. “Where did it go?”

“From here to the river. People used it when the station gates were locked.”

“But where is it now?”

His mother smiled. “Roads don’t disappear just because we build over them.”

She placed his hand against the bricks.

At first, he felt only their cold roughness. Then something shifted. The station noise faded. Beneath his palm came the faint vibration of footsteps: hurried shoes, dragging heels, children skipping, the tap of a cane. Hundreds of journeys lingered inside the wall like whispers trapped beneath ice.

Elias pulled his hand away.

His mother’s smile had gone.

“Never listen too long,” she said.

Years later, when illness made her thin and translucent, she gave him her mapmaking tools: brass dividers, rulers, ink, and the charcoal tin he still carried.

“You’ll notice places others don’t,” she told him. “Draw them. That’s how you keep them from becoming hungry.”

He thought fever had confused her.

“What do roads eat?”

“Travelers,” she said.

She died that winter.

For thirty years, Elias mapped the city’s forgotten ways. He found them in old records and faded photographs, but also in dreams, shadows, and the strange pull he felt at certain corners. Most lost roads were harmless. They slept beneath buildings and parks, content to remember rain and footsteps.

Others were restless.

Restless roads appeared where no road should be: between shelves in a library, under the surface of a canal, behind a bedroom door. They tempted the lonely with shortcuts. A turn taken carelessly could lead a traveler miles away—or years away—or somewhere from which no map offered a return.

Elias marked those roads in blue and kept the maps hidden.

Then, one November morning, a girl stepped out of Larkspur Lane.

Elias was shading the roof of a house when the streetlamp above him flickered. A wind rose, carrying the scent of crushed leaves and chimney smoke. The traffic noise softened, though the morning rush had already begun.

Beyond his paper, between the bakery and the clockmaker’s shop, the brick wall seemed to lengthen. A strip of wet pavement appeared beside it.

Elias froze.

He knew Larkspur Lane better than any lost road in the city. He knew its thirteen houses, its chestnut trees, and the steep bend near the demolished hill. He had drawn it so often that he sometimes dreamed of living there.

A figure emerged from the gray air.

She was perhaps sixteen, with dark hair tied in a green ribbon. Her coat was old-fashioned, its collar too large, its brass buttons scratched. Mud covered her boots.

She looked at Elias, then at the buses and electric signs roaring along Bellweather Street.

“Is this Calder?” she asked.

“Yes.”

Her eyes widened. “What year is it?”

Elias did not answer immediately. People hurried past without noticing the girl or the narrow lane trembling behind her.

“What year did you leave?” he asked.

“1989.”

It was the year Larkspur Lane had been erased.

Elias folded his stool. “We should go.”

The girl glanced back. The lane was already fading.

“My brother is still there.”

“What’s your name?”

“Clara Voss.”

Elias thought of a label pinned beside an old railway map.

LAST WALKED BY M. VOSS, 14 OCTOBER.

“Your brother is Martin.”

She stared at him. “How do you know?”

“Come with me.”

At Candlewick Square, Elias locked the door behind them and closed the curtains. Clara stood in the middle of his map-filled room, turning slowly.

“You knew Martin?” she asked.

“No. I know where he vanished.”

“We both vanished.”

“History remembers only him.”

She flinched.

Elias took down a blue map. It showed streets near the old northern railway yard. Along one edge ran a narrow route labeled CINDER PATH. A dotted line connected it to Larkspur Lane.

“Tell me what happened,” he said.

Clara sat at the table, hands hidden in her coat sleeves.

“We were going home. Martin wanted to take a shortcut by the railway yard. There was a gate I’d never seen before. He said he used it all the time.”

“What color?”

“Red.”

“And beyond it?”

“A path. Fog. We could hear trains, but we couldn’t see them. I told him to turn back. He laughed at me.”

She stared at the map.

“We walked for hours. Maybe days. The path kept changing. Sometimes there were houses beside it, but nobody inside. Sometimes it went through fields. Martin started leaving marks on trees. When we passed the same marks again, we argued.”

“And then?”

“We found Larkspur Lane. It looked normal. People were having dinner. A dog barked at us through a fence. We knocked on doors, but nobody answered. Martin said we should wait until morning.”

Her voice grew quieter.

“When I woke, he was gone. I searched. Every house was empty. I stayed there—I don’t know how long. The sky never changed. Then today I saw you drawing at the end of the street.”

Elias looked toward the unfinished city map. A new blue line had appeared, faint as a vein, running from Larkspur Lane toward Candlewick Square.

Clara followed his gaze.

“Did you draw that?”

“No.”

The line lengthened by the width of a finger.

Elias rolled up the map.

“What does it mean?” Clara asked.

“It means the road followed you.”

Something knocked beneath the floorboards.

Once.

Twice.

Then, from the tailor’s shop below, came the sound of footsteps moving down a corridor that did not exist.

Elias opened a cabinet and removed a canvas satchel. Inside were chalk, nails, a compass, a length of red cord, and a small glass bottle filled with rainwater.

“What are you doing?” Clara asked.

“Finding your brother before the road finds you.”

“You think he’s alive?”

“Alive may not be the right word.”

Her face hardened. “It’s the only word I care about.”

They left by the window.

Elias climbed onto the tailor’s awning, dropped to the pavement, and helped Clara down. Behind them, the front door shook in its frame. A thin line of darkness appeared beneath it, widening like an opening eye.

They crossed Candlewick Square and entered the market.

“Stay where people are,” Elias said. “Lost roads prefer empty places.”

The market was crowded with fishmongers, flower sellers, office workers, and tourists. Elias moved through them quickly, checking his compass. Its needle pointed not north but backward, toward the rooms they had abandoned.

“How do we reach Martin?” Clara asked.

“Cinder Path took you. Larkspur Lane held you. Lost roads form networks, just like ordinary streets. If we enter through a related road, we may find him.”

“May?”

“No map is perfect.”

Clara gave him a bleak look. “You’re not very comforting.”

“My mother said comforting people makes them careless.”

“Your mother sounds cheerful.”

“She was a cartographer.”

They reached the covered arcade at the market’s eastern end. Most of its shops had closed years ago. Dusty windows displayed yellowed dresses, broken radios, and silverware dark with tarnish.

Elias stopped before a shop with no sign.

“Here,” he said.

Clara saw only a locked glass door.

Elias poured rainwater across the threshold. For a moment, nothing happened. Then the glass clouded from within. A reflection appeared—not of the arcade behind them, but of a railway platform under a white sky.

He tied the red cord around Clara’s wrist and fastened the other end to his own.

“Three rules,” he said. “Don’t answer if someone calls your name from behind. Don’t enter any house with a blue door. And if we meet ourselves—”

“What?”

“Run.”

He pushed open the door.

Cold swallowed them.

They stood on a railway platform surrounded by fog. The shop door had vanished. Rails stretched in both directions, polished and silent. Across the tracks stood a station sign:

YESTERDAY’S END.

Clara moved closer to Elias. “This wasn’t here before.”

“Lost roads change according to their travelers.”

A bell rang far away.

They followed the platform until it narrowed into a path lined with cinders. Elias marked the ground with chalk every fifty steps. Each time he looked back, the previous mark had vanished.

The fog was full of sounds. Bicycle bells. Carriage wheels. Snatches of conversation. Once, Elias heard his mother singing a lullaby behind him.

He did not turn.

Clara did.

The red cord pulled tight.

“Martin?” she whispered.

A boy stood in the fog beside the tracks.

He looked fourteen, with Clara’s dark hair and a school satchel over one shoulder. His face was pale.

“Clara,” he said. “You took so long.”

She stepped toward him, but Elias caught her arm.

The boy smiled.

His teeth were small paving stones.

Clara recoiled.

The thing wearing Martin’s shape opened its mouth, and from inside came the noise of carts rattling over cobbles.

Elias threw a handful of iron nails across the path.

The creature collapsed into a pile of wet leaves.

They ran.

The cinder path twisted downhill. Fog scraped across their faces. On both sides, houses appeared, each with a blue door.

Someone knocked from inside every one.

“Help us,” voices called. “Please. We know the way out.”

Clara kept her eyes ahead.

At the bottom of the hill, they found a crossroads. Its signpost carried four names:

WHAT MIGHT HAVE BEEN

WHAT WAS FORGOTTEN

WHAT WAITS BELOW

HOME

The arrow marked HOME pointed into a ditch.

“Helpful,” Clara said.

Elias knelt and pressed his palm against the ground.

Footsteps moved through the soil. Some were light and quick; others staggered. He closed his eyes, separating recent journeys from ancient ones.

Among them was a familiar rhythm: two steps, pause, three steps, pause.

A boy stopping to mark trees.

“This way.”

They followed the road marked WHAT WAS FORGOTTEN.

As they walked, objects appeared along the verge: a child’s glove, a wedding ring, keys, letters never sent. Thousands of lost things lay half-buried in the grass.

Clara stopped beside a red toy windmill.

“I had one like that.”

“Don’t touch it.”

“My father bought it the summer before he left.”

“That is why you mustn’t touch it.”

The windmill began to turn, though there was no breeze. For an instant, sunlight shone across Clara’s face. Elias saw reflected in her eyes a garden, a picnic blanket, a man holding out his arms.

Then Clara kicked dirt over the toy and walked on.

“You learn quickly,” Elias said.

“I had thirty-seven years to practice being disappointed.”

The road ended at a river.

It flowed black and smooth, carrying fragments of the city on its surface: street signs, roof tiles, lit windows, whole rooms turning slowly in the current. A stone bridge arched over the water.

At its center stood Martin Voss.

He was no longer fourteen.

He appeared older than Elias, his hair white, his coat patched with scraps of maps. He leaned on a staff made from a railway sign. Hundreds of blue threads were tied around his wrists, stretching into the fog.

Clara stopped breathing.

Martin looked at her. His face crumpled.

“You found the way out,” he said.

She crossed the bridge at a run and embraced him.

For a moment, the lost city was silent.

Then the blue threads tightened.

Martin gasped and fell to one knee.

“What are these?” Clara seized one of the threads.

“Don’t pull them,” he said. “They’re roads.”

Elias approached. Each thread trembled with distant motion.

“You’ve been holding them,” he said.

Martin nodded. “They were following her. I kept them here as long as I could.”

“For thirty-seven years?”

“Longer here.”

Clara knelt beside him. “Why did you leave me?”

“I didn’t. I woke and saw something standing outside the house. It looked like you. It told me the road would let one of us go if the other stayed. I followed it before it could find you.”

“You idiot,” she whispered, crying now. “You absolute idiot.”

“I was fourteen.”

“So was I.”

Behind them, the river began to rise.

Elias looked back. Shapes moved beneath the water—roads uncoiling like serpents. The bridge stones shifted under his feet.

“The road lied,” he said. “It never meant to release either of you.”

A thread snapped from Martin’s wrist.

Across the river, a row of houses rose from the ground. Larkspur Lane assembled itself piece by piece: chimneys, windows, chestnut trees. At its end appeared Bellweather Street under the light of dawn.

Home.

Clara helped Martin stand.

“Can you walk?”

He looked toward the city, his expression full of longing.

“No,” he said. “But you can.”

The remaining threads pulled taut. Cracks spread across the bridge.

Elias opened his satchel and removed his brass dividers.

“My mother told me roads become hungry when they are forgotten,” he said. “She was wrong.”

He placed one point of the dividers against the bridge and the other against his unfinished map.

“They become hungry when they are unfinished.”

He drew a line.

The brass points sparked. Pain shot through his hand. On the paper, black ink spread from the bridge toward the river, making a route where none had been before.

A new road.

The lost city shuddered.

“What are you doing?” Martin shouted.

“Giving it somewhere else to go.”

The black line crossed the map, heading beyond the city’s borders, beyond the coast, into the blank margin. One by one, the blue threads released Martin and fastened themselves to the new route.

The river roared.

The bridge tilted.

“Run!” Elias shouted.

Clara pulled her brother toward Larkspur Lane. Martin stumbled, aged limbs struggling to remember youth. Elias followed as the bridge collapsed behind them.

They reached the first house.

Its blue door opened.

Inside stood three figures: Elias, Clara, and Martin, all smiling.

Clara swore and ran faster.

The false Elias lunged from the doorway. His skin was made of paper, his veins drawn in blue ink. Elias slashed at him with the dividers. The creature tore from throat to waist, spilling dust and dead beetles.

The chestnut trees bent over the road. Their roots tore free, reaching like fingers. Windows opened and begged them to stay.

At the far end of Larkspur Lane, Bellweather Street flickered in and out of sight.

The red cord around Elias’s wrist snapped.

He fell.

The lane folded between him and the others, stretching a few yards into a mile. Clara and Martin became distant shapes.

“Elias!” Clara screamed.

He tried to stand, but paving stones closed around his ankle.

The road spoke through every door and drain.

YOU DREW ME.

“Yes,” Elias said.

YOU REMEMBERED ME.

“Yes.”

YOU BELONG TO ME.

The street tightened its grip.

Elias looked at the charcoal tin that had fallen from his satchel. His mother’s tin. Dented, blackened, carried through half a lifetime of mornings.

He understood then why she had drawn roads but never people.

A road remembered through stones and turns. It knew destinations, not travelers. Each day Elias had given Larkspur Lane shape, detail, and direction. He had taught it how to return.

But he had never shown it where a road should end.

Elias took a stick of charcoal and drew a thick black line across the paving stones.

The lane recoiled.

All roads feared an ending.

He drew another line, then another, enclosing himself in a charcoal circle. The stones released his ankle. The houses screamed with slamming doors.

Elias ran.

The distance folded again, this time beneath his feet. He caught Martin’s coat just as the lane collapsed behind them.

The three fell onto Bellweather Street.

A bus horn blared.

The bakery owner shouted as a tray of rolls hit the pavement. Commuters swerved around them, annoyed but unsurprised, as city people often were by miracles that blocked the sidewalk.

Between the bakery and the clockmaker’s shop stood an ordinary brick wall.

Larkspur Lane was gone.

For several minutes, none of them moved.

Then the clockmaker stepped outside.

He looked at Martin.

The wooden sign slipped from his hands.

“Mr. Voss?” he said.

Martin raised his head. “Do I know you?”

The clockmaker began to laugh. He laughed until tears ran down his cheeks.

“I was in your class,” he said. “Peter Hall. You broke my nose during football.”

Martin stared at the old man before him.

“Peter?”

Their laughter drew a crowd.

Clara sat beside Elias on the curb, the green ribbon loose in her hair.

“What happens now?” she asked.

“To the road?”

“To us.”

Elias considered the question.

“Now you become difficult paperwork.”

She smiled.

Martin stayed with the clockmaker while Elias and Clara returned to Candlewick Square. The tailor’s shop was exactly as it had been, except for a trail of muddy footprints ending at the wall.

Upstairs, Elias unrolled the city map.

The blue roads had faded.

Not vanished, but quieted.

The new black road crossed the river and continued into the blank edge of the paper. At its farthest point, in handwriting that was not Elias’s, someone had added two words:

BEGIN AGAIN.

Clara stood beside him.

“Will the lost roads come back?”

“Some.”

“And you’ll keep mapping them?”

“Yes.”

She studied the walls, the ink, the labels, and the old wooden table crowded with instruments.

“You could use help.”

Elias glanced at her. “Mapmaking is badly paid, dangerous, and mostly ignored.”

“I’ve lost thirty-seven years, my family is gone, and my little brother is now old enough to complain about his knees. My prospects are flexible.”

For the first time in many years, Elias laughed.

The following morning, before the city woke, two figures walked to the end of Bellweather Street.

Elias carried the folding stool and charcoal tin. Clara brought fresh paper.

They sat beneath the rusted streetlamp. The space between the bakery and the clockmaker’s shop was only a wall now, dark with rain.

“What do we draw?” Clara asked.

Elias listened.

Behind the bricks, faint footsteps moved away from them. They did not sound hungry. They sounded like travelers beginning a long journey.

“Not Larkspur Lane,” he said. “It knows where it ends.”

He handed her a sheet of paper and pointed across the street, where a narrow alley ran between two office buildings.

“That alley was closed in 1962. Officially, it never went anywhere.”

“And unofficially?”

Elias opened the charcoal tin.

“Unofficially, three people entered it during a thunderstorm and returned speaking a language no one has heard before or since.”

Clara picked up a stick of charcoal.

The sun rose over Calder, touching the rooftops one by one. Shopkeepers opened their doors. Buses groaned through the waking streets. Beneath the pavement, forgotten roads shifted in their sleep.

Clara drew the mouth of the alley.

Deep inside it, beyond sight, something drew a breath.

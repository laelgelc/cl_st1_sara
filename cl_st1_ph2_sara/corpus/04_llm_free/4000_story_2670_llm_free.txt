## The Cartographer of Lost Roads

Every morning, before the bells of Saint Orra announced the hour, Elias Vale opened his shop and erased a road from the map.

He did this with a square of white cloth, a drop of bitter-smelling solvent, and the steady hand of a surgeon. The roads never vanished easily. Ink clung to parchment as memory clings to an old grief. He rubbed until the line faded, leaving behind a pale scar between mountains, villages, and rivers.

Then Elias updated the ledger.

**North road from Bellweather to Mirren’s Ford—lost during the flood.**

**Pilgrim’s path through the Amber Wood—lost to fire.**

**Western pass beyond Mount Ilyon—lost after the king closed the border.**

People believed roads disappeared because bridges collapsed, forests grew wild, or rulers planted soldiers across them. But Elias knew better.

A road was not truly lost when it became impassable. It was lost when nobody remembered where it led.

His shop stood in the oldest quarter of Bellweather, squeezed between a clockmaker and a woman who sold candles for the dead. Above the door hung a wooden sign showing a compass rose with one broken point. Inside, maps covered every wall. Some were as small as handkerchiefs; others unrolled from ceiling to floor. They showed sea routes, mountain tracks, royal highways, smugglers’ tunnels, and footpaths remembered only by shepherds.

Elias had inherited the shop from his mother, Mara Vale, the finest cartographer in the kingdom. She had taught him that maps were promises.

“A line on paper says you can leave,” she used to tell him. “It also says you can return.”

Then, twenty years earlier, she had gone searching for a road that did not exist.

She never came back.

Elias had been twelve. Since then, he had trusted only roads that could be measured beneath his own boots.

On the first morning of winter, while snow whispered against the windows, a girl entered the shop carrying a map that was completely blank.

She was perhaps sixteen, wrapped in a red cloak several sizes too large. Melted snow shone in her black hair. She shut the door quickly and leaned against it, as if something had followed her.

“Are you Elias Vale?” she asked.

“I am.”

“The man who finds lost roads?”

“I draw roads. Finding them is usually someone else’s mistake.”

She approached the counter and placed the blank map before him.

At first, Elias thought it was merely an unused sheet of parchment. Then he saw the border: a chain of tiny blue flowers painted around all four edges. In one corner stood his mother’s mark—a winged compass.

His breath stopped.

“Where did you get this?”

“My father kept it hidden under the floor,” the girl said. “He told me to bring it to you if anything happened to him.”

“What happened?”

“Three nights ago, men came to our house. They wore gray coats, but no badges. My father made me climb out the back window. I waited in the orchard until morning. When I returned, he was gone.”

Elias touched the winged compass. The parchment was old, but the paint had not faded.

“What is your name?”

“Lina Orrel.”

“And your father?”

“Tomas.”

Elias remembered him. Tomas Orrel had once guided expeditions beyond the eastern hills. He had a loud laugh and a scar across his chin. More importantly, he had been Mara Vale’s final companion.

“You know him,” Lina said.

“I knew him long ago.”

“He said the map would show itself to you.”

“It is blank.”

“He said you would say that too.”

Elias glanced at her.

Lina reached beneath her cloak and produced a small brass compass. Instead of the four familiar directions, words had been engraved around its face:

**HERE. THERE. BEFORE. AFTER.**

The needle turned restlessly, then pointed at Elias.

He stepped back.

“That belonged to my mother.”

“My father said she gave it to him.”

Elias took the compass. It was warm despite the cold. He remembered watching Mara hold it beside the fire, her thumb brushing the cracked glass. She had called it an unreliable instrument.

“It does not point north,” he had complained.

“North is easy to find,” she had replied. “Other things are harder.”

Elias placed the compass atop the blank parchment.

A blue line appeared.

It began at Bellweather, crossed the eastern hills, and wound through places no official map named. At the far edge of the parchment, written in Mara’s narrow hand, were three words:

**The Road Unchosen.**

Elias stared until the letters blurred.

Lina whispered, “Where does it lead?”

He already knew the answer, though nothing on the map said so.

“To the place where my mother disappeared.”

By noon, Elias had closed the shop, packed two bags, and left instructions with the candle seller to feed his cat.

He told himself he was going because Tomas Orrel might still be alive. He told himself he was going because gray-coated men would soon come searching for Lina. He told himself many things, all of them sensible.

He did not admit that, for twenty years, some part of him had remained twelve years old, listening for his mother’s key in the door.

They left Bellweather through the eastern gate. Snow lay over the fields, erasing fences and ditches. The compass needle pointed to **THERE**.

For two days, they followed roads Elias knew. They passed frozen farms and bare orchards, slept in crowded inns, and avoided questions. Lina spoke little about herself. Her mother had died when she was young. Her father had taught her to ride, read tracks, and lie convincingly to tax collectors.

“He also taught me not to trust maps,” she said.

Elias looked offended. “Why?”

“He said maps only show the world someone wants you to see.”

“Your father always enjoyed sounding wiser than he was.”

“Is he wrong?”

Elias considered the royal map folded in his coat. It showed the king’s borders in thick red ink, as if rivers and mountains obeyed decrees.

“No,” he admitted. “But neither is he entirely right.”

On the third day, the blue line led them away from the highway and toward a wall of black thorn bushes.

“There is no path here,” Lina said.

“There was once.”

Elias found two weathered stones under the snow, set opposite each other like gateposts. Beyond them, branches interlocked so tightly that even a fox could not pass.

The compass needle shifted from **THERE** to **BEFORE**.

The air trembled.

For a heartbeat, Elias saw sunlight. The thorn bushes became green hedges. Between them stretched a narrow summer lane scattered with white petals. A horse cart rolled along it, silent as a dream.

Then winter returned.

Lina caught his arm. “You saw it?”

“Yes.”

“What was it?”

“The road as it used to be.”

The compass pointed to **BEFORE** again. This time, the vision remained. Snow melted beneath their boots, though cold still bit their faces. They stepped between the gateposts and entered the vanished lane.

Walking it felt like moving through someone else’s memory. Summer light lay on the road, but beyond the hedges stood the real winter forest. Birds sang from branches covered in snow. The air smelled of roses and woodsmoke.

At sunset, they found an abandoned tollhouse. Half appeared freshly painted; half had collapsed into moss-covered stones. They made camp in the portion that seemed least likely to vanish.

Lina sat by the fire, studying Elias.

“Did my father leave your mother behind?”

The question was so direct that he nearly lied.

“I don’t know.”

“But you wondered.”

“For twenty years.”

“My father kept a locked chest. After he disappeared, I broke it open. There were letters inside. Most were from your mother.”

Elias looked up sharply.

“Why didn’t you bring them?”

“I did.”

She removed a bundle from her bag, tied with faded green ribbon.

Elias recognized Mara’s handwriting at once. His name appeared on several envelopes, but none had been opened.

He reached for them, then stopped.

“Why did Tomas never send these?”

“I don’t know.”

Anger rose in him, old and familiar. “He had twenty years.”

“Maybe he had a reason.”

“People always claim reasons when they mean cowardice.”

Lina’s face hardened. “You think you’re the only person who was abandoned?”

She walked outside before he could answer.

Elias sat alone with the letters.

The first had been written three weeks after Mara vanished.

**My dearest Elias,**

**If this reaches you, then Tomas has found a way back, and I have not. Do not blame him. The choice was mine.**

Elias lowered the letter. Outside, snow drifted through the summer hedge.

He read on.

Mara wrote of the Road Unchosen, a path older than the kingdom. It did not cross ordinary distance. It passed through possibilities—the lives people might have lived, the doors they had almost opened, the words they had nearly spoken. At the end stood a place called the Crossroads, where abandoned futures accumulated like dust.

She and Tomas had gone there because King Hadrian, the current king’s grandfather, wanted to use the road to change the past. Mara had sealed the way. Tomas had escaped, carrying the compass and map.

The final lines shook in Elias’s hands.

**I know what this will cost you. I know you may never forgive me. But there are roads that must remain closed, not because nothing lies beyond them, but because too much does.**

**Whatever life you choose, live it fully. Do not stand forever at the crossing.**

Elias read the letter twice.

When Lina returned, her cheeks were red from the cold.

“He didn’t abandon her,” Elias said.

“No.”

“Did he ever tell you?”

“Only that he had made a promise which ruined two lives.”

Before Elias could respond, hoofbeats sounded beyond the hedge.

Lina extinguished the fire.

Gray-coated riders surrounded the tollhouse. Their leader dismounted: a tall woman with silver hair and a sword curved like a crescent moon.

“Elias Vale,” she called. “By order of His Majesty, surrender the map.”

Elias folded the letter and placed it inside his coat.

“What does the king want with a road that doesn’t exist?”

“The same thing every king wants,” the woman replied. “A world in which he cannot lose.”

Soldiers entered from both ends of the lane.

Lina drew a hunting knife.

“A poor choice,” the commander said.

The compass in Elias’s hand swung toward **AFTER**.

The tollhouse changed.

For an instant, he saw its future: walls fallen, roof gone, a deep crack splitting the floor. He seized Lina’s wrist and pulled her left.

“Jump!”

They leaped as the vision became real.

Stone collapsed behind them. The floor opened beneath the soldiers, swallowing men, horses, and the silver-haired commander in a cloud of dust.

Elias and Lina landed on the far side of the crack and ran.

The Road Unchosen carried them deeper into impossible country.

They passed a village where every house contained the same family living different lives. In one, an old woman ate alone. In another, she was surrounded by children. In a third, she had left the village as a girl, and strangers occupied her home.

They crossed a battlefield where no soldiers had died because, in that version of history, the two generals had chosen peace. Wildflowers grew from empty armor.

They walked beside a river that flowed in both directions.

With each mile, the compass grew hotter.

The gray-coated soldiers followed. Sometimes Elias glimpsed them on parallel versions of the road: shadows moving through rain while he and Lina walked in sunlight, riders crossing a stone bridge where only ruins stood in his world.

On the sixth night, Lina confessed the truth.

“My father wasn’t taken.”

They had sheltered beneath an enormous tree filled with doors instead of leaves. Each door opened briefly onto a different room: a kitchen, a prison cell, a nursery, a throne room.

“What do you mean?” Elias asked.

“He left willingly. The gray coats came, and he went with them.”

Elias felt the old anger shift toward her. “You said—”

“I said they came and he was gone. I needed you to follow the map.”

“Why?”

“Because my father serves the king.”

A door opened above them. Through it, Elias saw Tomas Orrel kneeling before a crowned figure.

Lina stared at the ground.

“He has been searching for the Crossroads all these years. He believes he can find a life where my mother survived. The king promised to help him.”

“And the letters?”

“I thought they might convince you to continue.”

“You used my mother’s words.”

“Yes.”

Elias stood.

“Say something,” Lina whispered.

“What would you like? That I understand? That grief excuses betrayal?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

“That you won’t leave me here.”

She looked suddenly young, not a clever guide or practiced liar, but a frightened girl who had followed her father’s obsession into a place where roads remembered what people regretted.

Elias knew what it was to shape an entire life around someone who had disappeared.

“I won’t leave you,” he said. “But no more lies.”

“No more.”

At dawn, they reached the Crossroads.

It lay in a vast plain beneath a colorless sky. Thousands of roads met there: broad avenues, forest trails, stairways, bridges, tunnels, lines of stepping-stones floating in the air. At the center stood a signpost with countless wooden arms.

Elias saw his name carved into one.

Lina saw hers on another.

The compass needle spun until the glass cracked.

Then the gray-coated soldiers arrived.

They came from seven roads at once, led by the silver-haired commander. With them walked Tomas Orrel.

He was older than Elias remembered, but the scar still crossed his chin. His eyes went first to Lina, then to the letters visible in Elias’s coat.

“You read them,” Tomas said.

“You hid them.”

“I promised Mara I would.”

“She wrote them to me.”

“She also wrote one final letter. She ordered me to keep the others until the road reopened.”

Elias’s anger faltered. “Why?”

“Because if you knew this place existed, you would come looking. She knew you too well.”

Tomas turned toward the signpost. One road had begun to shine. At its end, beneath a bright summer sky, stood a dark-haired woman.

Lina gasped. “Mother?”

The woman smiled and raised a hand.

Tomas took a step toward her.

The commander drew her sword. “The road belongs to the king.”

“No,” Elias said. “It belongs to everyone who might have been. Which means it belongs to no one.”

She signaled, and the soldiers advanced.

The Crossroads erupted.

Every possible outcome flickered around them. Elias saw himself struck by an arrow, then saw the arrow miss. He saw Lina run to her mother, then vanish as the road closed. He saw Tomas kill the commander, and the commander kill Tomas. Futures appeared faster than thought.

The compass burned in Elias’s palm.

**HERE. THERE. BEFORE. AFTER.**

All directions were choices.

All maps were choices too.

He finally understood what his mother had done.

She had not destroyed the road. She had removed it from the world’s memory. Yet no seal could last forever while one cartographer remembered the possibility of return.

Elias pulled the blank map from Lina’s bag.

The blue road glowed across it.

“What are you doing?” Tomas shouted.

“My work.”

Elias tore the map in half.

The Crossroads shuddered.

The soldiers stopped. Roads began folding into the earth. The shining path to Lina’s mother dimmed.

Tomas ran toward it.

Lina caught his hand.

“She’s there!” he cried.

“No,” Lina said, weeping. “A life with her is there. It isn’t ours.”

For one terrible moment, Elias thought Tomas would pull away.

Then he looked at his daughter—not the child she had been, nor the daughter she might have become, but the girl standing before him—and stepped back.

The shining road vanished.

The commander lunged for the torn map. Elias opened his hand and let the pieces fall. They became white birds before touching the ground and flew into the colorless sky.

A roar like the breaking of the sea filled the plain.

“Choose!” Elias shouted.

The compass needle pointed to **HERE**.

Lina seized her father’s hand. Elias seized Lina’s. Together, they ran down the narrowest road, an unremarkable dirt path leading beneath gray clouds.

Behind them, the Crossroads disappeared.

Elias woke in the snow beside two weathered gateposts.

Lina lay nearby. Tomas was on his back, staring at the winter sky.

There were no soldiers, no thorn-covered lane, and no sign of the compass except a circle of cracked brass in Elias’s palm.

They returned to Bellweather three weeks later.

Tomas surrendered himself to the city magistrates and confessed his service to the king. The king denied everything, as kings often do, but the silver-haired commander and her soldiers were never seen again.

Lina visited her father in prison every Sunday. In spring, when evidence of the king’s secret expeditions became public, Tomas was pardoned. He did not go searching for lost roads again. Instead, he planted an orchard on a hill outside the city.

Elias reopened his shop.

For a long time, the empty space on the eastern wall troubled him. He had no map of the Road Unchosen, no compass, and no proof that the journey had occurred. Even his memories began to soften at the edges.

Then one morning, Lina arrived carrying a wooden sign.

It showed a compass rose with all four points intact.

“I thought the old one looked unfinished,” she said.

“It was meant to look that way.”

“It was ugly.”

Elias protested, but she replaced it anyway. Afterward, she remained as his apprentice.

She was impatient with rulers, careless with ink, and far too willing to draw monsters in unexplored seas. Yet she understood what many cartographers never learned: an empty place on a map was not an invitation to invent, but a request to listen.

Years passed.

New roads were built. Old ones vanished. The kingdom changed its borders, then changed them back. Elias’s hair turned silver. Lina became the finest mapmaker in Bellweather, though Elias never admitted this aloud.

Each morning, before the bells of Saint Orra announced the hour, they opened the shop together.

Sometimes they erased a road.

Sometimes they added one.

And sometimes, when a traveler entered with mud on their boots and a story nobody believed, Elias would take out a fresh sheet of parchment and ask the only question that truly mattered.

“Where did you mean to go?”

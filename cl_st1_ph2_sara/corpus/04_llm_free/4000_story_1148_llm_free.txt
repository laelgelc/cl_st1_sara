# The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every town loses time.

A minute slips beneath a café table while someone waits for an apology. An afternoon vanishes in a hospital corridor. Whole summers are misplaced by children who swear they only closed their eyes for a moment.

In Bellwether, lost time gathered beneath the clock tower.

No one knew this except Elias Vale, the tower’s keeper, and he had learned it from his mother, who had learned it from hers. For one hundred and forty-three years, a member of the Vale family had climbed the narrow staircase each night, entered the clock room, and collected whatever the town had let fall.

Usually, there was not much.

A few seconds glittered on the floor like flakes of mica. Minutes clung to the gears in pale threads. Elias drew them carefully into glass bottles, labeled each one by date, and stored them in cabinets built into the tower’s circular walls.

There were thousands of bottles.

Some held only a sigh.

Others contained enough time to boil an egg, mend a stocking, or say something that should have been said before a door closed.

Elias never opened them.

“That is the first rule,” his mother had told him. “Lost time belongs to no one.”

“The second rule?” he had asked.

“Never let the clock stop.”

“And the third?”

She had looked toward the highest cabinet, where the oldest bottles rested under blankets of dust.

“Never return what was willingly given away.”

At twelve years old, Elias had not understood. At fifty-seven, he believed he did.

The people of Bellwether knew him as a tall, quiet man with silver beginning at his temples and a habit of checking his pocket watch during conversation. They waved to him in the market. They called him when mantel clocks ran slow or grandfather clocks began striking thirteen. Children dared one another to knock on the tower door after midnight, though none had ever found the courage.

Elias lived alone in two small rooms beneath the clock. He slept lightly, ate plainly, and kept his memories in better order than most people kept their cupboards.

Then, one autumn evening, a woman named Mara Quinn knocked on his door.

Elias knew who she was. Everyone did.

She had grown up in Bellwether, left at seventeen, and returned three weeks earlier to care for her father, who was dying. In her youth, she had been famous for climbing rooftops and swimming in the river during storms. Now she was forty, perhaps, with rain-dark hair and a red coat that seemed too bright for the gray evening.

“I need an hour,” she said when Elias opened the door.

Most people began with good evening.

“I repair clocks,” he replied.

“You keep lost time.”

The rain ticked against the streetlamps. Somewhere above them, the tower mechanism shifted with a heavy iron sigh.

Elias held the door halfway closed. “Who told you that?”

“My father.”

“Daniel Quinn tells stories.”

“He is dying.”

“Dying people tell stories too.”

Mara stepped closer. Her face was pale from lack of sleep, but her eyes were steady.

“He said your mother gave him seven minutes in 1982. He had missed the last bus from Stonebridge, and those seven minutes let him reach it before it left. If he hadn’t caught that bus, he wouldn’t have met my mother.”

Elias said nothing.

The story was true.

His mother had broken the first rule only once, as far as he knew. Daniel Quinn had arrived at the tower on a winter night, frantic and shivering. Elias had been a boy then, listening from the stairs. His mother had opened a bottle and poured seven minutes into the clock.

For those inside the tower, nothing happened. Outside, the town drew backward like a breath. Snow rose from footprints. Chimney smoke folded into brick. The bus returned to the station, and Daniel ran.

Elias remembered his mother weeping afterward.

“Seven minutes,” Mara continued. “That is all he told me. He made me promise not to ask until I truly needed it.”

“And now you need an hour.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“My father cannot speak anymore. He tried this morning, but the words wouldn’t come. The doctor says he may not wake tomorrow.”

“I’m sorry.”

“I don’t want your sorrow.”

“Most people don’t. They ask for it anyway.”

Her jaw tightened. “I argued with him the last time he could talk. I told him he had driven everyone away. I told him my mother died miserable because of him.”

“Was it true?”

“Some of it.”

“And you want to unsay it.”

“I want one hour before I said it. One hour while he was still himself.”

Elias looked up the spiral staircase behind him. The clock struck six, each bell rolling through the walls and into his bones.

“One hour is not a small thing,” he said.

“It is to you.”

“No. It never is.”

She reached into her coat and produced a silver wristwatch. Its face was cracked, its hands frozen at 4:17.

“My mother’s,” she said. “Payment.”

“I don’t sell time.”

“Then take mine.”

Elias looked at her.

“Take an hour from me,” Mara said. “A year. Whatever is fair.”

“There is no fair exchange.”

“Then make an unfair one.”

He should have closed the door. Instead, he noticed the wristwatch’s second hand trembling beneath the cracked glass, trying and failing to advance.

“Come back tomorrow,” he said.

“He may not have tomorrow.”

“Neither may you.”

“That has always been true.”

She met his eyes until he looked away.

“Midnight,” Elias said. “Come alone.”

After she left, he climbed to the clock room.

The great mechanism filled most of the chamber, a forest of brass wheels and black iron rods. Pendulums swung through wells in the floor. Moonlight shone through four enormous clock faces, painting the room in milky circles.

The bottles whispered in their cabinets.

Lost time was never silent. When enough of it gathered, it murmured with fragments of what had been abandoned.

A spoon striking a saucer.

A baby’s first laugh, unheard by a sleeping father.

Rain on a wedding morning.

The final note of a song interrupted by war.

Elias passed shelves labeled by year until he found the current month. Three weeks earlier, on the day Daniel Quinn had suffered his first seizure, the town had lost nearly four hours. Most of it had pooled in the hospital: time surrendered in waiting rooms, time buried in helplessness, time people wished would pass quickly and then begged to have back.

Among the bottles was one containing sixty-three minutes.

The glass was warm.

Elias carried it to his worktable. He told himself he was only examining it, but he had told himself many small lies during his life, and he recognized the shape of another.

At eleven minutes past midnight, Mara arrived.

“You’re late,” he said.

“My father woke. I stayed until he slept again.”

“Then perhaps you have already found what you wanted.”

“No.”

Elias led her upstairs.

She paused when she saw the clock room. The machinery moved around her with stately indifference. Behind the glass faces, the town lay under a black sky, roofs shining after rain.

“So much,” she whispered, looking at the cabinets.

“Most is unusable.”

“Why?”

“A lost second can be restored easily. An hour resists. A day might tear the clock apart.”

“You said might.”

He ignored that.

On the table, the bottle glowed faintly. Mara reached for it, but Elias caught her wrist.

“You must understand what happens.”

“I go back.”

“No. The town goes back. Everyone within sound of the tower bells returns to where they were one hour ago. Their bodies, their words, the rain in the gutters—everything. But only you and I will remember.”

“Why you?”

“The clock remembers its keeper.”

“And why me?”

“Because you will drink the hour.”

For the first time, her resolve flickered. “Drink it?”

“It will taste like nothing you know. Afterward, the clock will strike backward. When it stops, you will have one hour before the old moment arrives again.”

“Then I can change it.”

“You can try.”

“Try?”

“Time is not a road, Mara. It does not enjoy being walked twice.”

She pulled her wrist free. “You helped my father.”

“My mother did.”

“So help me.”

Elias turned the bottle in his hands. Pale light moved inside it like mist.

“There is a price,” he said. “Not one I choose. When the hour is restored, another hour will be taken from you.”

“You told me there was no fair exchange.”

“There isn’t. The clock may take an hour from the end of your life, or from a morning years from now. It may take an hour you treasure.”

“Will I know?”

“No.”

“Then open it.”

He removed the stopper.

The whispers in the room ceased. Even the pendulum seemed to hesitate.

Mara raised the bottle and drank.

The light vanished down her throat.

Above them, the first bell struck.

Its note was wrong—deep and inverted, more felt than heard. The clock hands shuddered and began to turn backward.

A second bell sounded.

Beyond the glass faces, chimney smoke sank. Fallen leaves lifted from pavements and fastened themselves to branches. A cat leaped backward onto a wall. Rain flew upward from the river in silver needles.

Mara gripped the table.

Bell after bell, the town unwound.

Elias watched the minute hand retreat. He had seen time restored only once, and that childhood memory had softened at the edges. He had forgotten the terror of it: the sense that the world was a tapestry being pulled thread by thread through the eye of a needle.

At last, the clock stopped.

The hands showed 4:02 in the afternoon.

Sunlight replaced midnight.

Mara stood beside him, still wearing her red coat, though she had not been wearing it at 4:02. The returned hour clung around her like a transparent flame.

“You have until 5:02,” Elias said.

She ran.

Elias remained in the clock room, listening.

The bells did not move. The gears were locked, though the pendulum swung. Across Bellwether, people repeated their hour without knowing it. Bakers returned bread to ovens. Teachers erased the same chalkboards. Lovers spoke familiar lies with fresh conviction.

Elias went to the eastern clock face and looked down.

Mara crossed the square, dodging people who seemed to her unbearably slow. She reached the hospital and disappeared through its doors.

On the table, the empty bottle cracked.

A thin line appeared from neck to base.

Elias stared at it.

His mother had told him that cracked glass meant the returned time had been rejected. The hour would not settle cleanly. Something within it had changed too greatly—or had never wanted to be changed at all.

He went to the mechanism. A small gear near the escapement was turning forward while every other wheel remained still.

“That is impossible,” he murmured.

The gear spun faster.

From the cabinets came a sudden rattling. Hundreds of bottles shook on their shelves. Lost seconds flared in the glass like trapped stars.

Then a voice spoke behind him.

“Elias.”

He turned.

His mother stood beside the stairs.

She looked as she had on the night she died: seventy-two, thin as winter, wearing her blue house dress. One hand rested over her heart. Her gray braid fell across her shoulder.

Elias could not breathe.

“You let the clock stop,” she said.

“You’re not here.”

“No.”

“You’re a memory.”

“Yes.”

“Whose?”

She glanced at the cabinets. “Everyone’s.”

The bottles rattled harder. Elias backed toward the worktable.

“What is happening?”

“The hour is looking for somewhere to go.”

“It belongs to Mara.”

“Not all of it.”

His mother’s image flickered, and for an instant Elias saw dozens of faces layered over hers: strangers, neighbors, children grown old, old people made young.

“Time given away willingly cannot be returned,” she said.

“The third rule.”

“You remembered the words.”

“Not the meaning.”

“No.”

The spinning gear screamed.

“What did Mara’s father give away?” Elias asked.

His mother looked toward the town. “An hour.”

“When?”

“Forty years ago.”

Elias understood.

Seven minutes had allowed Daniel Quinn to catch the bus, but a restored moment demanded its price. The clock had taken an hour from him sometime later. An hour he had surrendered, perhaps gladly, perhaps without knowing.

And now Mara had swallowed an hour gathered on the day of his illness.

“Is it the same hour?” Elias asked.

“Partly.”

“What happens if she returns it?”

“The clock will take back the gift.”

“What gift?”

But the apparition had already begun to fade.

“Mother.”

Her expression softened.

“You should have left Bellwether,” she said.

“You needed me.”

“No. The clock did.”

Then she vanished.

Elias stood alone.

At 4:39, he left the tower and ran to the hospital.

The repeated afternoon had an unreal brightness. People moved through it like actors in a play he had seen too many times. Mrs. Farrow dropped a bag of apples outside the grocer’s. Elias knew she had dropped them before, because he had seen one bruised apple in the gutter that evening. A cyclist shouted at a delivery van. A dog barked from an upstairs window.

At the hospital, Elias found Mara in Room 12, seated beside her father.

Daniel Quinn looked smaller than Elias remembered. Illness had hollowed his cheeks, but his eyes were open and clear.

Mara held his hand.

“You came,” she said.

Daniel turned his head slowly. “Clock keeper.”

His voice was weak, but it was a voice.

Elias glanced at the wall clock.

4:44.

“You told him?” Mara asked.

“Not yet,” Daniel whispered.

“Told me what?”

Daniel closed his eyes, gathering strength. “Your mother.”

Mara stiffened.

“She wasn’t on the bus,” he said.

“You met her on the bus.”

“No.”

“You always said—”

“I saw her at the station. She was waiting for the same bus. But when time turned back, the bus returned. I ran for it.” He swallowed painfully. “She didn’t.”

Mara looked at Elias.

“My mother returned seven minutes,” he said.

Daniel nodded. “I saw the clock run backward. Thought I’d gone mad. Then the bus was there, and I got on. Through the window, I saw Anna walking away.”

“But you met her.”

“Next morning. I found her at the boardinghouse. Asked her to breakfast.” A tired smile moved across his face. “She said any man foolish enough to chase a stranger deserved coffee.”

Mara’s grip loosened around his hand. “You lied all these years.”

“I made the story simpler.”

“You make everything simpler by making it false.”

“Mara,” Elias warned.

She looked at the clock.

4:51.

“I came back to apologize,” she said. “Not to do this again.”

Daniel watched her with the calm of someone standing beyond defense. “Then don’t.”

Silence filled the room.

Outside, a trolley rolled past. Somewhere down the corridor, a metal tray struck the floor. Elias remembered hearing the same crash earlier that day.

Mara leaned closer to her father.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “What I told you before—I wanted to hurt you.”

“You did.”

“I know.”

“You were not wrong.”

“That doesn’t make it better.”

“No.”

She waited, perhaps expecting forgiveness to arrive like a doctor called from another room.

Daniel drew a breath. “Your mother was unhappy near the end.”

Mara’s eyes filled.

“So was I,” he continued. “We loved each other badly for a long time. Then we stopped knowing how to stop.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

“I thought if you blamed me, you could keep loving her.”

“I could have loved both of you.”

“I know that now.”

The clock showed 4:57.

Elias stepped forward. “Mara, the hour is unstable.”

“What does that mean?”

“It may take more than it promised.”

“How much more?”

“I don’t know.”

Daniel looked from one to the other. “You turned it back for her?”

“Yes,” Elias said.

A shadow of anger crossed the old man’s face. “Fool.”

“I have heard that before.”

Daniel gripped Mara’s hand with sudden strength. “Leave.”

“No.”

“You said what you came to say.”

“I still have five minutes.”

“Those five minutes are not yours.”

Mara began to cry then, silently and helplessly.

Daniel lifted her hand to his lips.

“Go,” he whispered. “Let something end.”

At 4:59, Elias and Mara left the room.

They stood in the corridor while the wall clock advanced. Mara leaned against the wall, trembling.

“What happens at 5:02?” she asked.

“The two hours meet.”

“And my father?”

“I don’t know.”

She laughed bitterly. “You say that often for a man who keeps time.”

“A keeper is not a master.”

The second hand moved.

5:01.

From the tower, far across town, came a low metallic groan.

Lights flickered overhead. The corridor lengthened, then snapped back. Nurses repeated half-steps. A doctor emerged from an elevator twice.

Mara seized Elias’s sleeve. “Can we stop it?”

“No.”

“Can we give the hour back?”

“He gave part of it away willingly. It cannot be returned.”

“Then where does it go?”

Elias thought of the cracked bottle, the spinning gear, his mother’s face.

“Somewhere empty,” he said.

The clock reached 5:02.

The tower bell struck.

The hospital vanished.

For one impossible instant, Elias stood in a darkness filled with doors.

Thousands of them stretched in every direction: red doors, wooden doors, doors painted blue, doors without handles. Behind each waited an hour someone had lost. He heard laughter, arguments, lullabies, final breaths. He saw his own life divided into bright rooms.

In one, he was twenty-three and boarding a train from Bellwether.

In another, he was forty, holding the hand of a woman he had never met.

In another, he sat beside his mother as she died, not beneath the tower winding the clock.

All the hours he might have lived.

All willingly surrendered.

The returned hour rushed out of Mara in a ribbon of white fire. It searched among the doors, rejected one, then another.

At last it found an open room.

Elias knew it at once.

It was the hour after his mother’s funeral, when he had climbed the tower, looked out across Bellwether, and decided to remain its keeper forever. He had given that hour—and every hour after it—to the clock willingly.

The white fire entered.

The door closed.

Then the hospital returned.

Mara was on her knees. Elias lay beside her.

The wall clock read 5:03.

For several seconds, neither moved.

A nurse ran past them into Room 12. Mara followed. Elias remained in the corridor, too weak to stand.

He heard no alarm from the room, no shouted demand for equipment. Only quiet voices, and then Mara’s grief—soft, broken, real.

Daniel Quinn had died at 5:02, as he had the first time.

The hour had changed nothing.

And yet, Elias thought, that was not true.

Somewhere beneath the pain, a knot had been untied.

He looked at his hands. They seemed younger. The veins had faded. The ache in his knuckles was gone.

A silver wristwatch lay on the floor beside him.

Its cracked face showed 4:17.

Then the second hand moved.

Elias picked it up.

When he returned to the tower, dawn was beginning to thin the sky.

The clock had restarted. Bottles stood peacefully in their cabinets. The empty glass on the worktable had broken into two clean pieces.

Elias climbed to the highest platform and opened a narrow door he had never opened before.

Cold morning air entered the tower.

Below, Bellwether stirred. A bakery lit its ovens. Street sweepers gathered wet leaves. In the hospital, Mara sat beside her father for the final time. The town continued, spending what it had and losing what it could not hold.

Elias placed Mara’s wristwatch on the table.

Then he took off his keeper’s coat.

By sunrise, he had packed one bag.

He did not know how many years the clock had returned to him, nor how many waited beyond the town. He did not know who would climb the stairs that night or whether anyone should.

For the first time in his life, uncertainty did not frighten him.

At the station, Mara stood beneath the iron awning in her red coat.

“I thought you might come,” she said.

“Why?”

“You left my mother’s watch at the hospital.”

“I repaired it.”

She held it up. The second hand moved steadily.

“Where are you going?” she asked.

Elias looked at the timetable. There were trains to Stonebridge, to the coast, to cities whose towers kept ordinary clocks.

“I don’t know.”

Mara nodded as if this were an excellent destination.

The train arrived at 7:10.

Neither of them hurried, but they reached it before the doors closed.

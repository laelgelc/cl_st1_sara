The Bell Beneath the Lake

Every morning at seven, Mara Venn climbed the hill behind her cottage and rang the bronze bell.

She rang it once in spring, twice in summer, three times in autumn, and not at all in winter, when ice covered Lake Orra and the whole valley seemed to hold its breath. These were the rules her father had taught her, and his father had taught him, though no one in the village remembered why.

The bell stood beneath a crooked roof of black timber. It was older than the church, older than the stone bridge, perhaps even older than the village itself. Green rust streaked its sides, and around its rim ran words in a language no one could read.

Mara had been the bell keeper for nine years.

Most villagers treated the ringing as a quaint custom. Children followed her up the hill and dared one another to touch the rope. Visitors sketched the belfry from the road. Old Corven, who sold flour and lamp oil, claimed the bell had once warned people of wolves.

“But there haven’t been wolves here for a hundred years,” Mara had told him.

“Then it works,” Corven replied.

Mara’s father had not considered the bell amusing. On the night before his death, when his breath came shallow and his hands felt cold despite the fire, he gripped Mara’s wrist with surprising strength.

“Ring it properly,” he whispered.

“I will.”

“Listen after.”

“After what?”

“After the bell.”

She thought he meant she should listen to the note, to judge whether it sounded clear. But when she asked him, his eyes had already closed.

For nine years, Mara rang the bell and listened.

She heard the fading bronze note, the whisper of grass, the cries of swallows, and sometimes the distant slap of oars on the lake. Nothing else.

Then, on the first morning of her tenth autumn as bell keeper, something answered.

Mara pulled the rope three times.

The first note rolled down the hill.

The second crossed the village roofs.

The third drifted over Lake Orra, where a veil of mist covered the water.

Mara released the rope.

The air stilled.

Far below, beneath the lake, another bell rang.

It was a deep and broken sound, so low that Mara felt it in her ribs before she truly heard it. The note rose through dark water and trembled across the mist. Birds burst from the reeds. On the shore, a row of fishing boats rocked against their posts.

Mara stood frozen.

A moment later, the echo died, and the valley returned to silence.

She hurried down the hill.

By the time she reached the village square, people had gathered outside their homes. Corven stood in the doorway of his shop with a sack of flour in his arms. Two fishermen stared toward the lake. A dog barked at nothing.

“You heard it,” Mara said.

No one answered at first.

Then Corven set down the sack.

“Thunder,” he said.

“There isn’t a cloud in the sky.”

“An underground tremor, then.”

“It was a bell.”

Corven’s face tightened. “We have one bell, and you were ringing it.”

The villagers murmured in agreement, though none met Mara’s eyes.

Only Ilyan Reed, the schoolteacher, seemed curious. He was a narrow man with sleeves always too short for his arms and silver beginning to show in his dark hair.

“You said your father told you to listen,” he said.

Mara nodded.

“What else did he tell you?”

“Nothing useful.”

“That depends on what we’re looking for.”

They spent the afternoon in the church attic, where village records lay in boxes beneath generations of dust. Mara searched through tax rolls, birth lists, maps, and mold-spotted letters while Ilyan sneezed beside her.

Lake Orra appeared on every map, but its shape changed.

On the newer maps, it was a long oval bordered by the village to the east and steep woods to the west. On an older map, yellowed and cracked, the lake was smaller. A road continued beyond its present shore and ended at a black square marked ORRA.

“That must be a mistake,” Mara said.

Ilyan leaned closer. “Orra wasn’t the lake. It was a town.”

They found the first mention near sunset, in a book with a warped leather cover. The account had been written two hundred and forty-three years earlier by a priest named Tovan.

The drought continues. The river has failed. Mayor Seln insists the gates remain closed until the engineers arrive from the capital. This morning, the bell keeper came to me and said he heard ringing beneath the earth. I told him grief makes signs of ordinary things.

Three pages had been torn out.

The next surviving entry was dated eleven days later.

May mercy find the drowned. We who remain have built our homes above the new shore. The old bell is lost with the town. The lesser bell shall be raised on the eastern hill and rung according to the covenant: once in the season of opening, twice in the season of light, thrice in the season of descent. In the season of silence, let no hand touch it.

Below that, in darker ink, someone had added:

If the drowned bell answers, open the way before the seventh night.

Mara read the sentence twice.

“What way?” she asked.

Ilyan turned the page, but the rest of the book contained only hymns.

They brought the record to the village council that evening. The council met in the back room of Corven’s shop, between barrels of vinegar and shelves of soap.

Mayor Ansel listened with his fingers pressed together. He was a broad, careful man who disliked decisions made quickly and questions without profitable answers.

“A drowned town is believable,” he said. “The valley narrows in the north. A landslide could have blocked the river.”

“And the bell?” Mara asked.

“Sound travels strangely over water.”

“The book says to open the way before the seventh night.”

“Priests used to say many things.”

Ilyan placed the old map on the table. “There may be a dam or gate beneath the northern cliffs. If it has survived, it could be dangerous.”

“Then we will send someone to inspect the cliffs tomorrow.”

“Who?”

The mayor glanced around.

Everyone suddenly became interested in the vinegar barrels.

“I’ll go,” Mara said.

“You are the bell keeper,” said Corven, as if that made her incapable of walking.

“Exactly.”

Ilyan rolled up the map. “And I’ll go with her.”

They left before dawn with ropes, lanterns, food, and iron tools borrowed from the smithy. The path followed the lake north through alder woods. By noon, they had passed beyond the fishermen’s farthest huts.

Here the lake narrowed between walls of gray rock. Brambles covered the shore. The old map showed the buried road entering the western cliff near a symbol shaped like a key.

Mara and Ilyan searched until the light began to fade.

“We should turn back,” Ilyan said reluctantly.

Then Mara saw a line where no natural line should be: a vertical seam behind a curtain of ivy.

Together they tore away the leaves, revealing a stone arch sealed by an iron door. At its center was the same symbol shown on the map—a circle crossed by three descending lines.

There was no handle.

Ilyan scraped mud from an inscription above the arch. “Can you read it?”

“No.”

“Neither can I.”

“That is an inspiring advantage in a schoolteacher.”

“I teach children, not dead civilizations.”

Mara placed her hand on the iron. It was colder than the surrounding stone. As her fingers traced the symbol, something clicked within the door.

The iron swung inward.

Air rushed out, smelling of wet stone and old leaves.

Their lanterns revealed a passage descending into the cliff. The walls were lined with green tiles, many cracked or missing. Water trickled beneath their boots.

After fifty steps, the passage opened onto a narrow platform.

Lake Orra stretched before them—but from below.

They stood inside an enormous cavern, separated from the lake by a wall of ancient stone and iron. Water pressed through gaps in the masonry in thin, shining streams. Rusted chains descended from the ceiling. On the far side of the platform stood three wheels, each taller than a man.

Ilyan lifted his lantern.

“Floodgates,” he said.

An inscription had been carved into the floor beside the wheels. This one was written in the common language, though the spelling was old:

WHEN THE DEEP BELL SPEAKS, THE WATERS HAVE RISEN ABOVE THE RED MARK. OPEN EACH GATE BEFORE THE SEVENTH NIGHT, OR THE WALL SHALL BREAK.

A red line ran across the stone wall.

The water had risen a hand’s width above it.

Mara stared at the black lake pressing against the barrier.

“How long since the bell rang?”

“This morning was the second day,” Ilyan said.

They examined the wheels. The first moved only after they fitted an iron bar between its spokes and leaned on it together. Metal shrieked somewhere below. The platform trembled.

Beyond the wall came a roar.

Through a grated opening at the base of the cliff, water burst into an old channel heading north, away from the village.

“One gate,” Mara gasped.

The second wheel resisted longer. When it finally turned, a chain snapped overhead and whipped across the platform. Ilyan pulled Mara aside just before it struck the place where she had been standing.

The second channel opened.

The water level began to creep down the wall.

But the third wheel would not move.

They pushed until their hands bled. They hammered rust from the axle and poured lamp oil into its joints. Nothing helped.

“We need more people,” Mara said.

They returned to the village after midnight.

Mayor Ansel called the council at dawn. This time, the meeting filled the square. Mara described the wall, the gates, and the red mark. Ilyan showed them flakes of ancient mortar and a broken link from the fallen chain.

The villagers listened in uneasy silence.

Then Corven said, “If opening those gates lowers the lake, what happens to the fishing?”

Mara thought she had misheard him.

“The wall may break.”

“But two gates are open already. Perhaps that is enough.”

“The warning says all three.”

“Warnings are often generous.”

A fisherman named Pell stepped forward. “My family depends on the lake. If the water falls too far, we lose the autumn catch.”

“If the wall breaks, you lose your house.”

“If it breaks.”

Arguments spread through the square.

Some believed Mara. Others insisted the ancient builders could not have known what would happen centuries later. A few claimed opening the gates might cause the very disaster Mara feared. Mayor Ansel called for calm, then formed a committee to examine the evidence.

“How long will that take?” Mara asked.

“Two or three days.”

“We have five.”

“Then there is time.”

That evening, the drowned bell rang again.

It sounded without warning, one vast note rising from the depths. Cups rattled on shelves. Roof dust drifted down. Across the lake, a section of the western cliff collapsed into the water.

The committee reached its decision within the hour.

At sunrise, thirty villagers followed Mara and Ilyan north.

They carried hammers, poles, ropes, axes, oil, and enough food for several days. Even Corven came, though he complained that no one trustworthy had been left to mind his shop.

At the floodgate chamber, they tied ropes around the third wheel. Twenty people pulled while others struck the axle with mallets.

The wheel groaned but did not turn.

By evening, they had gained less than the width of a finger.

On the fourth day after the first bell, divers examined the submerged mechanism. They returned shivering and blue-lipped. A beam from the drowned town had become lodged in the outer gate.

“It’s as thick as an oak,” said Pell. “We can’t cut it underwater.”

“Can we pull it free?” Mara asked.

“Not from this side.”

That night, no one returned to the village. They slept in the tunnel or beneath the trees outside. Mara lay awake near the open iron door, listening to water thunder through the two working channels.

Just before dawn, her father’s final words came back to her.

Listen after.

Not to the bell, she realized.

After the bell.

She rose and went into the chamber. Ilyan found her examining the inscriptions around the wheels.

“You should sleep,” he said.

“Hold up your lantern.”

Beneath the warning on the floor, partly hidden by silt, was a second line:

THE KEEPER SHALL DESCEND BY THE LESSER BELL’S ROAD.

Mara stood.

“The belfry,” she said.

They hurried back to the village with six others. Mist lay over the lake, and the hilltop bell appeared to float above the white air.

Mara searched the wooden frame, the roof beams, and the bronze itself. Nothing resembled a road. Then Ilyan noticed that the stone beneath the bell was hollow.

They dug around its base and uncovered a ring set into a square slab. Beneath it, steps spiraled down.

The passage was older and narrower than the one in the northern cliff. It descended deep beneath the hill, then leveled and continued toward the lake.

“Your father knew,” Ilyan said.

“If he did, he should have told me.”

“Perhaps his father only told him to listen.”

“Then we have inherited a tradition of extremely poor explanations.”

Despite everything, Ilyan laughed.

The passage ended in a circular room. Water covered the floor to their knees. In the center stood a stone pedestal, and upon it rested a metal lever.

Words encircled the pedestal:

THE LESSER CALLS. THE DEEP REPLIES. THE ROAD OPENS.

Mara pulled the lever.

At first, nothing happened.

Then the floor shook. Stone grated against stone, and a section of the wall slid aside. Black water surged into the room, rising to Mara’s waist before settling.

Beyond the opening lay a street.

The drowned town of Orra waited beneath the lake.

Air filled the upper part of a transparent stone tunnel that stretched between collapsed houses. It should have been impossible. Water pressed against the unseen barrier overhead, where pale fish swam through the ruins of chimneys. Silt covered the road, but old doorways remained, along with a fountain, a row of carved posts, and the broken face of a clock.

The tunnel led north.

They moved through the town in stunned silence. On either side, the lake rested inside homes. Chairs lay overturned in shadowy rooms. A tree grew from the window of a bakery. In the central square hung the deep bell, suspended in a stone tower.

It was enormous.

A crack split it from rim to crown. Currents moved its broken clapper, making the metal shiver with notes too soft to hear.

At the base of the tower, Mara found a skeleton with an iron chain looped around one wrist. Beside it lay a sealed copper box.

Inside was a letter.

To those above, if the covenant endures:

We opened the northern gates, but the third was struck by timber when the lower quarter flooded. I have opened the keeper’s road so it may be reached from within. The water rises too quickly. I cannot return.

The letter bore the name of Tovan, the priest whose record they had found.

Mara folded it carefully.

The keeper’s road continued beyond the square and ended near the outer side of the jammed floodgate. Here the magical barrier narrowed, leaving only a thin wall of air between them and the lake. The beam lay wedged across the gate mechanism, exactly as the divers had said.

Axes struck wood.

They worked for hours. Chips flew. The old beam was waterlogged but hard, preserved by cold and darkness. When one person tired, another took their place.

On the sixth evening, the drowned bell began to move.

The clapper pulled back though there was no current.

“Run!” Mara shouted.

They threw themselves down as the bell struck.

Sound crushed the water.

The transparent walls rippled. Windows burst outward in silent clouds. Cracks raced through the street beneath their feet.

Far away, the great barrier wall answered with a thunderous snap.

“The seventh night,” Ilyan whispered.

“No,” Mara said, rising. “One night early.”

The ancient warning had not promised seven full days.

They attacked the beam again. A crack had opened through its center, but the severed parts remained jammed.

“Rope,” Mara said.

They looped a rope through the split and pulled. The wood shifted slightly, then stuck.

Water began leaking through the invisible roof.

First came droplets. Then narrow streams.

The keeper’s road was failing.

Mara looked at the gate, at the beam, and at the black water beyond. On the other side, thirty villagers waited at the wheel.

She took the iron bell chain from the skeleton and wrapped it around the broken timber.

“Go back to the pedestal,” she told Ilyan.

“What are you doing?”

“When the road closes, the water will rush in. It may pull the beam free.”

“It may pull you into the gate.”

“That is why you are going to pull the lever.”

“No.”

The road trembled. A section of unseen barrier collapsed behind them, filling half the street with roaring water.

Mara seized his coat.

“If the wall breaks, everyone in the village dies.”

“And if I pull that lever, you die.”

“Not if the old builders knew their work.”

“You just called their instructions extremely poor.”

“They had no schoolteacher.”

For a second he stared at her. Then he took the far end of the chain and tied it around a stone post.

“I’ll pull the lever,” he said, “but I’m not leaving you here alone.”

They ran back through the drowned town as water spilled into the road behind them. At the pedestal, Ilyan gripped the lever.

Mara held the chain, which stretched away into darkness.

“Now.”

He pushed.

The stone door began to close. The transparent tunnel vanished section by section, surrendering the town to the lake.

Water struck them like a moving wall.

Mara lost her footing. The chain tore skin from her palms. Ilyan caught her around the waist, and together they were dragged toward the narrowing doorway.

Then the chain snapped tight.

Far below, the beam came free.

The third gate opened.

The force reversed. Water rushed north through the flood channel, pulling away from the keeper’s road. Mara and Ilyan fell against the pedestal as the stone door sealed behind them.

For a long time, they lay in knee-deep water, breathing.

Then, from beneath the lake, the deep bell gave one final note.

It was no longer a warning.

It sounded like farewell.

By morning, Lake Orra had fallen twelve feet.

The newly exposed shoreline revealed walls, roads, and the tops of drowned houses. The water never drained completely; the old town remained mostly submerged. But the pressure against the northern barrier eased, and engineers summoned from the capital declared the structure safe enough to repair.

The fishing was poor that autumn, exactly as Pell had feared. Yet the northern channels uncovered rich soil, and by the next year the village had fields where marshes had been. Corven began selling postcards of the ruins and claimed he had supported Mara from the beginning.

No one believed him.

The copper letter was placed in the church beside Priest Tovan’s book. Ilyan wrote a full account of what had happened, using plain language and making six copies.

Mara wrote her own instructions and nailed them to every side of the belfry.

In winter, she did not ring the bell.

On the first morning of spring, the whole village climbed the hill with her. Children crowded close. Mayor Ansel stood bareheaded in the wind. Ilyan waited beside the rope, his left hand wrapped in the same white scars that crossed Mara’s palms.

She rang the lesser bell once.

Its voice traveled over the roofs, the new fields, and the clear blue water of Lake Orra.

Everyone listened after.

From the depths came no answer.

Only the whisper of waves along the shore, the rustle of new grass, and the bright, ordinary songs of birds.

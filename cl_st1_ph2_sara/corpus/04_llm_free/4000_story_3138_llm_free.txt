The first time Mara heard the lighthouse speak, it said her name.

She was standing at the bottom of the spiral staircase with a toolbox in one hand and a coil of copper wire over her shoulder. Rain pressed against the windows in silver sheets. Somewhere above, gears shifted inside the ancient lamp, though the lighthouse had not been connected to the island’s power grid for twenty-three years.

“Mara Vale,” the voice whispered through the speaking tube beside the stairs.

She stopped.

The tube was brass, green at the joints, and connected the lantern room to the keeper’s quarters below. As a child, Mara had shouted into it while her father worked upstairs.

Tea is ready!

Storm coming!

Can I light it, Dad? Just once?

He had always answered with a laugh made thin by the tube.

Now her father had been dead for six months, and the lighthouse was empty.

“Mara Vale,” it said again.

The toolbox slipped from her hand. A screwdriver skittered across the stone floor.

She stared at the black mouth of the tube.

“Who’s there?”

Only the storm replied.

Mara told herself it had been the wind. Old buildings collected noises and released them at inconvenient times. Her father had taught her that. Pipes groaned. Windows muttered. Floorboards sighed beneath the weight of their memories.

She gathered her tools and climbed.

At the top, the lantern room smelled of salt, oil, and rust. The great Fresnel lens stood in the center like a palace made of glass. Its layered prisms caught the gray light and broke it into pale fragments across the walls.

The council wanted the lighthouse repaired before Founders’ Day. Not restored to service, exactly—the automatic beacon on the eastern cliff had guided ships for years—but made presentable. Tourists would climb the hill, listen to speeches, and admire the island’s maritime heritage. Someone had decided the old lamp should turn again for the celebration.

Mara had taken the job because she was the only electrician on Bellweather Island who knew the machinery. She had taken it because her father’s unpaid medical bills had survived him. She had taken it, she told herself, for every reason except the truth.

The truth was that she missed him.

She opened the control housing. Most of the wiring was rotten. Mice had nested inside the lower panel, and salt had corroded the relay contacts. Yet the rotation gears were moving.

Slowly, almost imperceptibly, the lens turned.

Mara crouched beside the motor. No power cable fed it. The emergency battery had been removed in 1998. She placed her fingers against the iron casing.

It was warm.

From below came a metallic whisper.

“Mara.”

She straightened so quickly that her shoulder struck the lens assembly.

This time the voice was unmistakable.

“Dad?”

The turning lens stopped.

For a moment, Mara heard nothing but rain rattling the glass. Then three words rose through the speaking tube.

“Light before dark.”

It was something her father used to say. The keeper’s first rule, according to him. Light before dark. Shelter before storm. Forgiveness before sleep.

Mara went down the stairs two at a time.

The keeper’s quarters were empty. She searched the kitchen, the storage room, and the narrow bedroom where her father had slept through nearly forty years of gales. The front door was still locked from the inside.

She returned to the speaking tube.

“What do you mean?”

A faint vibration hummed through the brass.

“Light before dark,” the voice repeated.

Then it was gone.

By noon, Mara had convinced herself that grief was a kind of faulty wiring.

The brain, like an old house, could connect circuits that should remain separate. A familiar smell might close a switch. A phrase might produce a face. Wind in a tube might become the voice one most wished to hear.

She worked through lunch, replacing cables and cleaning terminals. At three, the rain stopped. At four, the clouds broke over the western sea.

Mara was packing her tools when she saw the boat.

It was a small blue fishing vessel about a mile offshore, rising and vanishing among the waves. Through her binoculars she recognized the white stripe on the bow. The Skylark belonged to Tomas Reed.

Tomas had been Mara’s closest friend until they were seventeen, when friendship had become something more complicated. At nineteen, he had asked her to leave the island with him. At twenty, she had gone alone.

Now they were both thirty-four. He had returned to fish his grandfather’s waters. She had returned to bury her father.

The Skylark turned south toward the harbor.

Beyond it, along the horizon, a wall of cloud rose like a dark country.

Mara checked her phone. No weather warning. No messages.

The lighthouse spoke behind her.

“Light before dark.”

She looked at the powerless lamp.

Outside, the sun still shone through the broken clouds.

“No,” she said. “You aren’t real.”

The lens began to turn.

Mara backed away.

Her father had believed the lighthouse possessed moods. In winter it grew melancholy. During fog it became attentive. On calm summer nights, it liked company.

Mara had laughed at him.

“It’s a building,” she used to say.

“Everything that stands against the sea long enough becomes something else,” he would answer.

She went to the gallery and studied the horizon. The distant cloud had thickened. Beneath it, the water was no longer blue but iron-colored. Gulls flew inland in ragged groups.

A pressure settled in her ears.

She called the harbor office.

“Bellweather Harbor,” answered Niall Dane, the harbormaster.

“It’s Mara. Is there a storm forecast?”

“A little rain tonight.”

“Look west.”

A pause.

“I’m looking.”

“And?”

Another pause, longer this time.

“I’ll call the weather station.”

“What about the boats?”

“Most are already in.”

“The Skylark isn’t.”

“I’ll radio Tomas.”

Mara hung up and checked the old lamp. Even with new wiring, it lacked a power source. The main line had once run underground from the keeper’s cottage, but landslides had severed it years ago. For Founders’ Day, she planned to install a temporary generator.

The generator was still in town.

The speaking tube crackled.

“Basement.”

Mara closed her eyes.

When she was nine, she had fallen from the harbor wall and split her knee on a barnacled rock. Her father had carried her home while she sobbed into his coat.

When she was twenty-eight, after five years of ignoring his calls, she learned that he was sick.

When she finally returned, he had not asked why she stayed away. Forgiveness before sleep.

“Basement,” the lighthouse said again.

The basement door was concealed beneath the stairs. Its hinges protested as Mara pulled it open. Damp steps descended into darkness.

She switched on her phone’s flashlight.

The room below was circular and cramped. Shelves lined the walls, crowded with paint cans, old ropes, and rusted tools. At the center stood a machine covered by canvas.

Mara dragged the cloth away.

Under it was a compact diesel generator.

A paper tag hung from the handle.

FOR MARA—IF THE OLD GIRL ASKS.

She recognized her father’s handwriting.

For several seconds she could not move.

Then the phone rang in her hand.

“Mara,” Niall said. His voice was tight. “The weather station’s radar went down an hour ago. That front appeared out of nowhere. Wind’s already hitting North Point.”

“Tomas?”

“His radio isn’t responding.”

The windows upstairs began to shake.

“Get everyone off the docks,” Mara said. “I’m lighting the tower.”

“You can’t. The cable—”

“I found Dad’s generator.”

Silence.

Then Niall said, “Of course you did.”

Mara filled the fuel tank from two cans on the shelf. She checked the oil, opened the vent, and pulled the starter cord. The engine coughed but did not catch.

She tried again.

Nothing.

The first gust hit the lighthouse like a giant’s hand. Dust sifted from the ceiling.

Mara removed the side panel. The fuel line had cracked. Diesel dripped onto the floor.

“No, no, no.”

She searched the shelves and found electrical tape, but the old adhesive peeled beneath her fingers. The engine needed a new hose.

The lighthouse groaned around her.

Her phone chimed with a message from Niall.

STORM MOVING FAST. VISIBILITY NEAR ZERO.

Mara thought of the Skylark beyond the harbor, its blue hull swallowed by rain. She thought of Tomas at seventeen, lying beside her on the warm roof of the schoolhouse and naming constellations he could not see. She thought of him at nineteen on the ferry dock, asking her to stay. She had wanted to, which was why she left.

Wanting anything deeply had frightened her then.

Perhaps it still did.

She looked at the broken fuel line, then at the speaking tube running down the basement wall.

“I need help,” she said.

Nothing answered.

She struck the brass pipe with her palm.

“You called me here. You tell me what to do.”

The generator ticked as it cooled. Rain began hammering the lighthouse.

Mara leaned her forehead against the tube.

“Dad,” she whispered.

A breath of static passed through it.

“Red box.”

On the highest shelf, behind a coil of rope, sat a red metal box. Inside were spare fuses, spark plugs, and a short length of rubber hose.

Mara laughed once, almost angrily.

“Of course.”

She replaced the line, tightened the clamps, and pulled the starter cord.

The generator roared awake.

Lights flickered overhead.

Mara ran upstairs.

Power surged through the new cables. Relays snapped shut. The rotation motor groaned, hesitated, then engaged. Slowly, the great glass lens began to revolve.

But the lamp itself remained dark.

Mara opened the housing. The bulb was intact. Voltage reached the socket. She checked the contacts, the ballast, and every wire she had replaced.

Everything should have worked.

Outside, daylight vanished.

The storm swallowed the island in less than ten minutes. Wind screamed around the tower. The sea disappeared behind gray rain, and the windows trembled in their frames.

Her phone rang.

“I reached Tomas,” Niall shouted over the wind. “His engine’s dead. He’s drifting near the Widow’s Teeth.”

The Widow’s Teeth were a chain of black rocks west of the harbor entrance. In clear weather they were easy to avoid. In a storm they were invisible.

“How far out?”

“He doesn’t know. His instruments are failing.”

“Tell him to look for the light.”

“There is no light, Mara.”

“There will be.”

She ended the call.

The lens turned uselessly around the dead bulb.

“Why won’t you light?” she demanded.

The lighthouse had no answer.

She pulled the bulb free. Its filament looked whole, but when she tested it with her meter, the circuit remained open.

A broken filament.

In the supply cabinet she found three replacement bulbs. One was cracked. One had corroded terminals. The third flashed blue when she switched it on, then burst with a sound like a pistol shot.

Darkness returned.

Mara stood amid glittering fragments.

The modern automatic beacon on the eastern cliff would be visible from the far side of the island, but not through the rock and storm. The harbor lights were too low. Tomas needed the western beam.

Mara searched every cabinet again. No more bulbs.

The speaking tube hissed.

“Old light.”

“What old light?”

“Heart of glass.”

She looked at the Fresnel lens.

At its center, beneath the electrical socket, was the original oil burner.

The mechanism had not been used since Mara was a child. Her father had kept it after the lighthouse was electrified, claiming a flame was more trustworthy than a wire because fire understood the importance of being seen.

Mara opened the lower cabinet. The oil reservoir was dry.

She raced downstairs to the storage room. On the highest rack she found two sealed tins of kerosene. She carried them up, filled the reservoir, and primed the burner.

The wick would not move.

Its adjustment wheel had rusted solid.

She soaked it in oil and worked the gear with pliers. The metal cut through her glove and into her palm. Blood slicked her fingers.

“Come on,” she said.

The wheel shifted half an inch.

She turned it again. The wick rose.

Mara struck a match.

The flame bent violently in the draft, nearly touching her fingers. She cupped it with both hands and lowered it to the wick.

For one second, nothing happened.

Then fire blossomed.

The lens caught it.

Light gathered inside the glass rings, growing brighter as each prism bent and strengthened it. The rotating mechanism carried the beam across the lantern room, through the rain, and over the black sea.

Once every twelve seconds, the western dark opened.

The lighthouse was alive.

Mara stood beside the burning lamp, shaking.

Her phone crackled with Niall’s voice.

“He sees it!”

She gripped the phone.

“Tomas?”

“He sees the beam. He says he can steer by it, but the current’s pulling him north.”

“Tell him to keep the light on his port side and follow it east. The beam crosses the harbor mouth after the third interval.”

“You’re sure?”

She watched the lens revolve.

As a child, she had known every rhythm of the tower. Twelve seconds west, darkness, twelve seconds south, darkness, a flash across the harbor, darkness, then west again.

“I’m sure.”

For the next forty minutes, Mara fed the flame and listened to fragments of radio reports relayed by Niall.

Skylark clear of the outer rocks.

Taking water.

Engine still dead.

Rescue boat dispatched.

The storm struck with full force. One of the western panes cracked. Wind forced rain into the room. The flame guttered.

Mara took off her coat and pressed it against the broken pane. Cold water soaked through the fabric. Each gust drove her backward, but she held on.

“Not yet,” she told the lighthouse. “You don’t get to go dark yet.”

The tower shuddered.

Below, the generator sputtered.

If it stopped, the lens would cease turning. The flame would still burn, but without rotation there would be no signal, only a fixed beam.

Mara could not leave the broken window.

“Dad,” she said into the speaking tube. “I need you.”

No voice replied.

The generator faltered again.

Mara waited for instructions, for a whisper, for proof that she was not alone.

There was only machinery and weather.

Then she understood.

Her father had left the generator. He had stored the hose, the kerosene, the tools. He had prepared the tower for a storm he could not have predicted. Whether his voice had crossed the border of death or risen from the rooms of Mara’s own memory no longer mattered.

He had brought her this far.

The rest was hers.

She wedged an iron bar against the broken pane and tied her coat over it with wire. Then she ran to the basement.

The generator was starving for fuel. The vent had vibrated shut. She opened it, and the engine steadied.

When she returned to the lantern room, the flame still burned.

Minutes later, her phone rang.

“They’re inside the harbor,” Niall said. “Rescue boat has them. Tomas is safe.”

Mara sat down hard on the floor.

The beam continued its patient sweep.

West. South. Harbor. Darkness.

West again.

By dawn, the storm had passed.

Broken branches covered the hill. Roof tiles lay in the road. The sea remained rough, but sunlight spread across it in long golden paths.

Mara extinguished the lamp.

At the foot of the tower, she found Tomas waiting beside an old truck. He wore a borrowed coat and had a bandage above one eyebrow.

For a moment, neither of them spoke.

“You look terrible,” Mara said.

“So do you.”

He smiled, and she remembered every version of him at once: the boy on the schoolhouse roof, the young man on the ferry dock, the fisherman lost in the dark.

“I heard you came back,” he said.

“I heard you did too.”

“Temporary?”

She looked up at the lighthouse. In daylight, it was only a weathered stone tower with cracked paint and salt-stained windows.

“No,” she said. “I don’t think so.”

Tomas glanced toward the lantern room.

“Niall says the old light saved us.”

“The old light had help.”

“Your father?”

Mara looked at him.

Tomas shrugged. “People are already talking.”

“People on this island have always talked.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“No,” she said. “It isn’t.”

He held out a paper cup of coffee. She accepted it, warming her cut hands against the sides.

Together they watched the sun climb.

Behind them, deep inside the lighthouse, metal contracted as it cooled. Pipes settled in the walls. Gears released the tension of the night.

Then, from the open doorway, came the faintest brass whisper.

“Forgiveness before sleep.”

Mara turned.

The tower stood silent.

When she looked back, Tomas was watching her.

“What is it?” he asked.

She thought of all the years she had spent leaving before she could be left, refusing love before it could become loss. She thought of her father, waiting without accusation. She thought of a beam crossing darkness again and again, not knowing who might see it, shining because that was what a light was meant to do.

“Nothing,” she said.

Then she took Tomas’s hand.

Far above them, though the generator had stopped and the wind was still, the great lens moved a fraction toward the sea.

### The Cartographer of Lost Roads

On the morning the western road disappeared, Mara Venn was drawing a map of a country that no longer existed.

She sat at the wide oak table in her father’s old workshop, surrounded by bottles of ink and instruments made of brass. Above her, the rafters creaked in the winter wind. Below the window, the town of Bellweather was waking: shutters opening, chimneys smoking, shopkeepers sweeping snow from their steps.

Mara bent over the parchment. She was adding the final line to the River Senn when someone hammered on the door.

Not knocked. Hammered.

She set down her pen.

“Come in.”

Mayor Hollis entered without waiting. He was a tall, nervous man whose beard seemed to grow in several directions at once. Snow clung to his coat.

“Mara,” he gasped. “You must come.”

“If this is about the boundary stones again—”

“It’s the western road.”

“What about it?”

“It’s gone.”

Mara looked at him.

Mayor Hollis swallowed. “All of it.”

The western road began at the town gate, passed the abandoned mill, crossed the hill country, and eventually reached the distant city of Aurelia. Mara had walked part of it as a child. She had mapped every turn and ditch five years earlier.

She put on her coat.

Outside the western gate, a crowd had gathered in silence.

The road was indeed gone.

It had not been buried by snow or washed away by rain. The land itself had closed over it. Grass and heather lay where the cobbles should have been, untouched and silvered with frost. The avenue of ancient oaks that had bordered the road now formed a dense, unbroken wood.

Mara crouched near the last remaining stone. It ended in a clean edge, as if the road had been cut from the world with a knife.

“How far?” she asked.

“We don’t know,” said the mayor. “Tomas tried to follow the old direction.”

A shepherd standing nearby raised a trembling hand. “I walked into the trees,” he said. “Kept west by the compass. After an hour, I came out behind my own cottage.”

A murmur passed through the crowd.

Mara took a compass from her pocket. Its needle spun slowly, hesitated, then pointed toward the town well.

“That isn’t west,” said the mayor.

“I know.”

A girl pushed through the crowd. Lio Dane, the blacksmith’s apprentice, was seventeen, broad-shouldered, and permanently smudged with soot.

“My brother left for Aurelia three days ago,” she said. “He took that road.”

No one answered.

Mara stared into the woods. Between the trunks, the shadows looked thicker than they should have. She felt an old sensation stir in her chest: the uneasy certainty that a map was not merely a picture of the world, but an agreement with it.

And some agreement had been broken.

“I need to examine my records,” she said.

Lio caught her sleeve. “And my brother?”

“I’ll find what I can.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Mara met the girl’s eyes. “Then I’ll find him if he can be found.”

Back in the workshop, she unrolled her most recent map of the region. The western road was drawn in brown ink, exactly where it had always been. She traced it from Bellweather to Aurelia.

Halfway between the two stood a mark she had nearly forgotten: a small circle at the edge of the hills.

The House of Saint Orra.

It was an old waystation, abandoned before Mara was born. Travelers once stopped there for food and shelter. Her father had visited it during his final survey.

Mara searched the shelves until she found his field journals. Corin Venn had been a meticulous cartographer. He recorded bridges, wells, ruins, trees struck by lightning, villages with bad bread, and inns that watered their beer.

His last journal was different.

The early pages were orderly. Then the writing became hurried.

June 14. Reached Saint Orra. No inhabitants. Six rooms outside, seven inside.

June 15. Heard footsteps beneath the road.

June 16. Found an older map carved into cellar wall. Roads do not connect places. Roads keep places apart.

June 17. Something follows the survey line.

The final page had been torn out.

Mara closed the journal.

Her father had disappeared twenty years ago, when she was nine. The town believed he had fallen into a ravine during a winter expedition. His body had never been found.

A knock sounded.

Lio stood in the doorway with a bedroll, a hammer, and a canvas bag.

“You’re going,” she said.

“Yes.”

“So am I.”

“No.”

“My brother’s name is Eren. He has dark hair, a red scarf, and a scar on his left thumb. He whistles when he’s frightened, though he denies it.”

“You can’t come.”

“I know the hills. I can repair tools. And if you leave without me, I’ll follow.”

Mara studied her. “Can you read a compass?”

“Yes.”

“Not anymore, you can’t.”

Lio frowned.

“Pack food for five days,” Mara said. “We leave before noon.”

They entered the woods where the road had ended.

Mara carried her father’s journal, surveying tools, and three maps. Lio carried food, rope, blankets, and a blacksmith’s short-handled hammer. Snow began to fall, whitening the branches.

Within minutes, Bellweather vanished behind them.

Mara marked trees with red chalk, but whenever she looked back, the marks were gone. She tied string between branches. The string loosened and fell, as though the trees had shifted closer together.

“Are we lost?” Lio asked.

“Not yet.”

“When will we be?”

“When I pretend I know where we are.”

They continued west by the position of the pale winter sun. At dusk, they reached a stream that did not appear on any map. The water flowed uphill.

Lio stared at it. “That seems bad.”

“It’s certainly inconvenient.”

They camped beneath a rocky ledge. Mara slept badly. She dreamed of her father standing at the workshop table, drawing a road that curled off the parchment and around his wrist.

Near midnight, she woke to whistling.

She sat up.

Across the dead fire, Lio was awake too.

“That’s Eren,” she whispered.

The tune drifted between the trees.

Three rising notes. Two falling.

Lio scrambled to her feet.

Mara seized her arm. “Wait.”

“It’s him.”

“Maybe.”

The whistling came again, farther away.

“Eren!” Lio shouted.

The forest answered from every direction.

Eren.

Eren.

Eren.

Then, in a voice like branches rubbing together, something said, “Lio.”

The girl went still.

Mara threw salt into the ashes, an old surveyor’s superstition. A blue flame leaped up. Beyond the firelight, a tall shape moved between the trees. It had too many joints and no face that Mara could see.

The creature circled until dawn.

In daylight, they found footprints around the camp. The tracks resembled bare human feet, except they were each as long as Mara’s arm.

Lio said nothing as they packed.

By afternoon, the ground rose sharply. The woods thinned, revealing slopes of black grass. At the top of the highest ridge stood a stone house.

Saint Orra.

Its roof sagged. Ivy covered its walls despite the cold. The front door hung open.

Inside, dust lay thick on the floor. There were six rooms: a kitchen, a common hall, a pantry, and three sleeping chambers.

“Six outside,” Mara murmured.

“What?”

She showed Lio the journal entry.

They searched until sunset. In the smallest bedroom, Lio found a trapdoor beneath a rotten rug.

“That makes seven,” she said.

Stone steps descended into darkness.

The cellar smelled of wet earth. Mara lit a lantern. Carved into the far wall was a map so large it reached from floor to ceiling.

It showed Bellweather, Aurelia, the hill country, and the road connecting them. But beneath that familiar landscape, deeper lines twisted across the stone. They did not pass through towns or valleys. They enclosed them.

Mara touched one of the carvings.

“These aren’t roads,” said Lio.

“No. They’re boundaries.”

“Boundaries between what?”

Mara raised the lantern.

Shapes had been scratched into the spaces between the lines: long figures in forests, mouths beneath rivers, eyes inside mountains.

“Between us and them.”

A sound came from the stairs.

Three rising notes. Two falling.

Lio turned.

A young man stood at the cellar entrance. His coat was torn, and a red scarf hung around his neck.

“Eren?”

He smiled.

Lio ran to him.

Mara saw his hands.

“Stop!”

Lio froze inches away.

The young man raised his left hand. There was no scar on the thumb.

His smile widened.

Then his face opened vertically.

Lio swung her hammer. It struck with a sound like an axe hitting damp wood. The thing staggered, and Mara pulled Lio behind the carved wall’s nearest boundary line.

The creature lunged after them but stopped at the groove in the floor.

Its body shuddered. It wore Eren’s shape badly now: one arm too long, teeth visible through the cheek, eyes flickering like candle flames.

“Lio,” it pleaded.

She gripped her hammer.

“Where is my brother?”

The creature tilted its head. “Walking.”

“Where?”

“Under the road.”

It retreated up the steps and disappeared.

For a long moment, neither woman moved.

Then Lio knelt and touched the line that had stopped it.

“The road was one of these boundaries,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And now it’s gone.”

“Yes.”

“Can we put it back?”

Mara studied the wall.

At the center of the carved map was a round hollow. Something had been removed from it. Tool marks scarred the edges.

She opened her father’s journal again and held it near the lantern. The torn final page had left a narrow strip along the binding. On it, barely visible, were the words:

Forgive me, Mara. The compass is the key.

Mara’s breath caught.

She took out her compass. The needle still pointed toward Bellweather’s well. She pried open the brass casing.

Beneath the needle lay a thin disk of gray stone.

It matched the hollow in the wall.

“My father took it,” she said.

“Why?”

Mara remembered the date of his disappearance. Twenty years ago, a fever had swept through Bellweather. Mara had been dying. Her father had left town promising to find medicine in Aurelia.

He returned once, in the middle of the night. Mara remembered cool hands on her forehead and a bitter drink. By morning, her fever had broken.

By morning, he was gone.

“He needed the road to move,” Mara said. “Maybe to shorten the journey. Maybe to get back in time.”

She placed the stone disk into the hollow.

The wall trembled.

Dust fell from the ceiling. The carved lines glowed faintly, one after another, spreading outward from Saint Orra. Mara watched the light travel toward Bellweather, but it stopped where the western road should have begun.

A section of the carving was missing.

“No,” she whispered.

The creature’s blow upstairs had cracked the wall. A piece of the map lay shattered on the cellar floor.

Mara knelt beside the fragments. The road line had broken into a dozen pieces.

“Can you repair it?” Lio asked.

“Stone isn’t my craft.”

“But lines are.”

Mara looked at her.

Lio opened her canvas bag. “And stone is close enough to mine.”

They worked through the night.

Lio fitted the fragments together, heating iron pins in the kitchen fire and hammering them into cracks. Mara copied the original line from her paper map, adjusting for the deeper boundaries beneath it.

Yet one piece was missing: a narrow section where the road crossed the hill called Orison’s Back.

“We need something to fill it,” Lio said.

Mara tried wax, wood, and melted lead. Each crumbled when placed against the glowing map.

“It wants stone from the road,” she said.

“The road doesn’t exist.”

“Not here.”

Lio understood. “Bellweather. The last cobble.”

They had to return.

Outside, night covered the hills, though dawn should have come hours earlier. Shapes moved in the valley below. Tall figures unfolded from the woods. Pale things crawled out of the uphill stream. In the distance, something vast pressed against the underside of the earth, lifting the ground like a blanket.

“How long will that line hold?” Lio asked.

Mara looked at the repaired carving. “Not long.”

They began walking east.

The landscape no longer pretended to obey the world they knew. Paths doubled back in impossible circles. Trees hung downward from patches of empty sky. Once, they crossed a field where hundreds of black doors stood upright in the snow. Knocking came from behind each one.

Mara used her paper map as a guide, not to where the land was, but to where it should have been.

“This stream ought to bend south,” she said. “So we turn north.”

“That makes no sense.”

“Correct.”

At midday—or what their bodies insisted was midday—they heard whistling again.

Eren stumbled over the ridge.

This time he looked thinner. Blood stained his sleeve. His red scarf was gone.

Lio lifted her hammer.

He stopped.

“I suppose,” he said weakly, “this isn’t the welcome I imagined.”

“Show me your hand,” Lio demanded.

He raised his left hand. A white scar crossed his thumb.

She ran to him.

Eren had been walking the western road when the world changed. One moment, he said, he had been beneath an oak tree; the next, he was in a tunnel with a ceiling of packed soil. He followed distant voices for what felt like weeks.

“There were other travelers,” he said. “Some had been down there a long time. Decades, maybe. They couldn’t remember the way out.”

Mara thought of her father.

“Was there an older man?” she asked. “Gray eyes. Ink stains on his fingers.”

Eren hesitated.

“Yes.”

Hope was painful. Mara had forgotten that.

“Where?”

“He helped me escape. He stayed behind to hold open a gate.”

Mara closed her eyes.

“Did he give you anything?”

Eren reached beneath his coat and brought out a folded piece of paper.

“He said a cartographer would ask.”

It was the final page of Corin Venn’s journal.

Mara,

If you read this, then I failed to return, though perhaps not for lack of trying.

A map is a promise. We draw a line, and the world agrees to remember it. I broke that promise to save you. I would do so again.

But all debts wait.

Do not come looking for me. Restore the road. Live well. Draw carefully.

Your loving father.

Beneath the message, he had drawn a small map of the underground paths. At its edge was a gate marked with a single word:

HOME.

The earth heaved.

Behind them, the ridge split open. Long white fingers curled over the broken ground.

They ran.

Eren knew parts of the way. Mara knew others. Lio struck anything that came too close. The false Eren followed them through the trees, calling in the voices of people they loved.

At last they saw Bellweather’s walls.

The townspeople stood at the gate with torches. Mayor Hollis waved frantically.

But between the travelers and the town lay the vanished road’s final stone.

A creature stood upon it.

It wore Mara’s father’s face.

“Mara,” it said gently.

She stopped.

He looked as she remembered him: dark-haired, weary, smiling. Ink marked his fingers.

“I found the way home,” he said.

Mara’s heart moved before her mind did.

Then she noticed the compass in his hand.

Her father’s compass was in her pocket.

“You always were a poor imitation,” she said.

The creature rushed at her.

Lio’s hammer shattered its knee. Eren pulled Mara aside as its arms lengthened. It seized Lio by the coat and lifted her from the ground.

Mara grabbed the compass, tore out the gray stone disk, and pressed its sharp edge into the creature’s arm.

Light burst through its skin.

It screamed with a hundred voices and dropped Lio. The three of them dragged the final cobble through the gate.

The moment they crossed, the opening behind them vanished.

There was no forest, no hill, no field of impossible doors.

Only smooth grass stretching west.

“We need to finish it,” Mara said.

In the town square, they broke the cobble into fragments. Lio ground one piece into powder and mixed it with clay. Mara spread her regional map across the workshop table.

“Will a paper map be enough?” Mayor Hollis asked.

“No,” she said. “But it can show the stone where to go.”

She drew the western road again, mixing powdered cobble into the ink. The line passed through the hills, crossed streams and valleys, and ended at Aurelia.

As she completed the final stroke, a deep sound rolled beneath the town.

Every door slammed open.

The well overflowed with clear water.

Beyond the western gate, ancient cobbles thrust through the earth. One after another, they rose beneath the grass, stretching toward the horizon. Oaks unfolded from empty air. Ditches opened. Mile markers emerged from the snow.

The road had returned.

So had the missing travelers.

They appeared along its length, bewildered and shivering: merchants, soldiers, pilgrims, and farmers from years long past. Some had not aged. Others had aged too much.

Mara waited at the gate until sunset.

Her father did not come.

The road remained quiet.

At last, she unfolded his final page. In the fading light, she noticed something she had missed: a thin line continuing beyond the gate marked HOME. It wound through the margin and ended at a tiny, carefully drawn star.

Under it, her father had written:

Not yet.

Years passed.

Bellweather grew into a busy crossing town. Lio became its finest blacksmith, though she preferred the title “boundary repairer.” Eren opened an inn beside the western gate and never again whistled after dark.

Mara continued making maps.

But her maps were different now. Along every road, she added small protective marks. At every bridge, she drew lines the eye could barely see. Travelers carried her work across the country, strengthening the old agreements wherever they went.

She never stopped searching for the gate beneath the road.

Then, one spring morning, a letter arrived with no seal and no address.

The paper smelled of earth and rain.

Inside was a map.

It showed a road Mara had never seen, crossing a country that did not exist. At the road’s end stood a little workshop with an oak table. A man waited outside, holding a compass.

Below the drawing were four words in her father’s hand:

Bring ink. Long journey.

Mara laughed, then cried, then packed her instruments.

At the western gate, Lio was waiting with two horses and her hammer.

“You weren’t invited,” Mara said.

“I know.”

“You don’t even know where I’m going.”

“No.”

“It may be dangerous.”

Lio smiled. “Then you’ll need someone who can repair the road.”

Together they rode west.

Behind them, Bellweather’s roofs shone in the rising sun. Ahead, the old road passed between the hills and vanished into morning mist.

Mara carried no compass.

This time, she carried a blank map.

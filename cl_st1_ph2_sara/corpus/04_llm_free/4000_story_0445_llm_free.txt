The Last Lighthouse Keeper

On the morning the sea disappeared, Mara Venn was polishing the lighthouse lens.

She noticed the silence first.

For thirty-two years, the sea had struck the black cliffs below Greycap Light with the patience of a creditor. Even in summer, when the water shone blue and harmless, waves sighed through the caves under the headland. In winter, they shook cups in cupboards and sent salt rattling against the windows.

But that morning there was nothing.

Mara lowered her cloth. The great lens revolved around her, scattering pale slices of dawn across the glass walls. She listened for gulls, wind, the low bell of the buoy anchored beyond the reef.

Silence.

She crossed the lantern room and stepped onto the iron balcony.

The sea was gone.

It had not retreated to the horizon. It had vanished entirely, leaving behind a vast basin of wet sand, reefs, and glistening trenches. Fish twitched in shallow pools. Shipwrecks lay exposed like old bones. Far out, where the curve of the world should have swallowed everything, the seabed continued beneath a bruised morning sky.

Mara gripped the railing.

On the beach below, the village was waking. Doors opened. Figures emerged and stood motionless. Then someone shouted.

Within an hour, half the village had gathered at the foot of the lighthouse.

They climbed the path carrying spyglasses, cameras, buckets, walking sticks, and enough opinions to rebuild the sea by argument alone. Children were the first to venture onto the wet sand. Their parents called them back, but not very forcefully. The exposed coast was terrifying, yet it was also irresistible.

Mayor Pell arrived in his nightshirt beneath an official red coat.

“You saw it happen?” he asked Mara.

“No.”

“You heard anything?”

“No.”

“Any warning from the mainland?”

“The radio’s dead.”

Mayor Pell looked out over the empty basin and frowned, as though the disappearance might be an administrative error.

“We should investigate,” he declared.

“We should stay off the seabed,” Mara said.

“Why?”

“Because the sea may come back.”

This possibility silenced the villagers for several seconds.

Old Tomas, who had fished the Greycap waters since boyhood, spat into the sand. “Tide’s never gone farther than the western stones.”

“This isn’t a tide,” said Mara.

“No,” he agreed. “But anything that leaves can return.”

Despite this wisdom, people soon began walking outward.

At first they stayed near the shore. They collected silver fish, shells, anchors, coins, and bottles clouded by age. By noon, the beach had become a festival. Fires were lit. Children chased crabs between rocks. Someone found a bronze cannon beneath a mat of weed, and six men dragged it toward the village while arguing over who owned it.

Mara remained in the lighthouse.

She tried the radio every quarter hour. Only static answered. She checked the barometer, the generator, the emergency lamp, and the clockwork mechanism that turned the lens. Everything worked. The light had no sea to guard, but she wound the machinery anyway.

At three in the afternoon, a boy named Ell came running up the stairs.

Ell was twelve, narrow as a rope, and usually followed by at least one apology. His father had been a sailor. His mother, Lena, mended nets that no one needed that day.

“Mara,” he gasped, reaching the lantern room. “There’s a road.”

She looked down at him. “A road?”

“On the seabed.”

He pulled her to the eastern window and pointed.

Beyond the nearest reef, a line crossed the mud. It was too straight to be natural and too wide to be the remains of a wall. As the seabed dried beneath the afternoon sun, pale stones emerged from the dark silt, fitted together in a path leading toward the horizon.

People had already found it. Tiny figures clustered around its beginning.

“We’re going to see where it goes,” Ell said.

“You are not.”

“Everyone else is.”

“That has never made a bad idea good.”

“My father might be out there.”

Mara turned from the window.

Seven years earlier, Ell’s father had sailed from Greycap during a storm to guide a damaged passenger ship around the reef. The ship survived. His boat did not return. Neither had his body.

“The road is older than your father,” Mara said gently.

“But it goes where the sea was.”

“So does every shipwreck.”

His mouth tightened. “You don’t know he isn’t there.”

“No,” she said. “I don’t.”

That answer disappointed him more than a lie would have.

By evening, Mayor Pell had organized an expedition. He called it an expedition because “reckless walk across the bottom of the ocean” lacked dignity. Twenty-three villagers volunteered. They packed food, ropes, lanterns, and canvas tents. The road, they decided, might lead to another island or perhaps the mainland.

Mara met them at the shore.

Clouds had gathered in the west, stained purple by sunset. The empty basin smelled of brine and decay. Already, countless fish lay still in the mud, their scales catching the light.

“You cannot go,” she told them.

Mayor Pell adjusted his pack. “The mainland may need warning.”

“The mainland has its own coast.”

“Then they may be coming toward us.”

“Let them.”

A few people laughed, but Mara did not.

She pointed toward the lighthouse. “Greycap Light stands one hundred and eighty feet above the waterline. If the sea returns, the lantern room is the safest place for miles.”

“And if it doesn’t?” asked Tomas.

Mara had no answer.

The road seemed to glow in the dusk. Not brightly, but enough that every pale stone remained visible while the surrounding seabed darkened.

One by one, the expedition set out.

Tomas went. Mayor Pell went. The baker and his two eldest daughters went. So did the schoolmistress, the blacksmith, and three young fishermen who had spent the day boasting that they were not afraid.

Ell tried to join them. His mother caught him by the collar and dragged him home.

Mara watched the lanterns shrink into the distance.

She lit the lighthouse at sunset.

The beam swept across a world it had never touched before: ridges furred with weed, sunken boats, canyons of blue shadow, and the white road reaching east. Each time the light crossed the expedition, the walkers raised their hands.

Then, shortly before midnight, the beam revealed something standing beside the road.

Mara thought at first it was a mast. On the next rotation she saw a tall, narrow shape with two arms, two legs, and a head bowed forward.

A person.

It stood six miles from shore, facing the expedition.

Mara opened the balcony door and rang the warning bell.

The iron clang rolled over the empty seabed.

Far away, the expedition stopped.

The figure lifted its head.

Even at that distance, Mara felt its attention. It was not looking at the approaching villagers. It was looking past them, directly at the lighthouse.

The lens rotated.

Darkness crossed the basin.

When the beam returned, the figure was gone.

The expedition continued.

Mara rang until her arms ached, but no one turned back.

At dawn, three villagers returned at a run.

One was the blacksmith. His left arm hung broken at his side. The other two were the fisherman Daro and one of the baker’s daughters. All three were covered in grey dust.

Lena and Mara met them on the beach.

“The road reaches a city,” Daro said.

His eyes kept drifting toward the eastern horizon.

“What city?” Mara asked.

“One under the sea.”

They carried the blacksmith into the lighthouse kitchen. While Lena set his arm, Daro described what they had found.

The road descended into a trench. At its bottom stood towers carved from black stone, their windows sealed with glass. A wall surrounded the city, though parts of it had collapsed. The expedition entered through an arch taller than the village church.

“There were statues,” Daro whispered. “Hundreds. Maybe thousands. People with long hands and closed eyes.”

“Where are the others?” Mara asked.

“They stayed.”

“Why?”

Daro began to cry.

The baker’s daughter answered for him. “They heard voices.”

“What voices?”

“People they’d lost.”

The blacksmith groaned as Lena tightened the splint.

“My wife called me,” he muttered. “From inside a house. She died fifteen years ago, but I knew her voice.”

“Was she there?” Ell asked from the doorway.

No one had noticed him enter.

The blacksmith stared at the table. “Something was.”

The returned villagers had fled when the statues began to move.

“Not walking,” Daro said. “Only turning their heads. Following us.”

Mara looked east through the kitchen window.

The white road vanished into morning haze.

“When will the others return?”

The baker’s daughter shook her head. “They won’t. They found what they wanted.”

Outside, a low sound passed beneath the ground.

The crockery trembled. Dust sifted from the ceiling.

Then came a noise like a single wave breaking very far away.

Everyone froze.

Mara ran up the spiral stairs, through the service room, and into the lantern chamber. She lifted the spyglass.

At first she saw only the road and the scattered shapes of wrecks.

Then the eastern horizon moved.

A dark line had appeared at the edge of the world.

The sea was returning.

Not as a gradual tide. It rose like a wall, black at its base and white at its crest. It was still many miles away, but it stretched from north to south without end. Lightning flickered inside it.

“How long?” Lena asked behind her.

Mara handed her the glass.

Lena looked once and lowered it. “Ell.”

They found the kitchen empty and the outer door open.

Mara reached the balcony in time to see him running across the seabed.

He was following the road east.

Lena screamed his name.

Ell did not turn.

Mara was already moving. She took the emergency lantern, a coil of rope, and a flare pistol. At the door, Daro caught her shoulder.

“You can’t bring him back.”

“I can try.”

“You don’t understand. The city shows you who you miss.”

Mara pulled away.

“I understand perfectly.”

She ran down the path.

The seabed had changed during the night. The drying mud was cracked and hard, but pools still filled the deeper hollows. Stranded eels twisted among stones. Mara followed Ell’s footprints until she reached the pale road.

The stones were warm under her boots.

Behind her, Greycap Light shone against the morning. Lena stood on the balcony, ringing the bell.

Ahead, Ell was a dark speck.

Mara walked faster.

The returning sea grew taller. Its distant thunder came through the stones and into her bones. She calculated without wanting to: the wave was perhaps twenty miles away, moving faster than a horse. Less than an hour, then. Perhaps much less.

“Ell!” she shouted.

He vanished over the edge of a trench.

Mara reached it ten minutes later.

The road descended steeply between cliffs that had once been hidden in black water. At the bottom lay the city.

Its towers were slender and uneven, built like the spiraled shells of sea creatures. Bridges arched between rooftops. Glass domes covered silent courtyards. Everywhere stood statues with long fingers and smooth, closed eyes.

Ell was halfway down the slope.

A man waited beneath the great arch.

He wore a sailor’s coat.

“Papa!” Ell called.

The figure opened its arms.

Mara drew the flare pistol and fired.

The red flare struck the road before the figure, flooding the trench with savage light.

The sailor’s coat was empty.

Something thin and white unfolded inside it. Long fingers spilled from the sleeves. A head rose above the collar, hairless and shining, its closed eyes sealed beneath translucent skin.

Ell stumbled backward.

All across the city came the scrape of stone.

The statues were turning their heads.

Mara reached the boy and seized his arm.

“That isn’t him.”

Ell sobbed, trying to pull free. “It knows my name.”

“So do I.”

From the archway, the thing spoke in his father’s voice.

“Ell.”

The boy stopped resisting.

“Your mother is waiting,” Mara said.

“My father—”

“Your father sailed into a storm to save strangers. Do you think he would call you toward a wave?”

Ell looked east.

Above the trench, the sky had turned black. The wall of water filled half the horizon.

The creature took one soundless step forward.

Then another voice came from the city.

“Mara.”

She knew it at once.

Her younger sister, Sella, had been nineteen when she drowned below Greycap Light. The two of them had argued that night. Sella had taken the little rowing boat to frighten her, to prove she could manage the rough water alone.

Mara had watched from the cliff as the boat overturned.

For thirty-two years, she had kept the lighthouse because leaving felt too much like forgiveness.

“Mara,” Sella called again. “I’m here.”

A girl in a yellow dress stood on one of the bridges.

She had wet black hair and bare feet. She looked exactly as she had that last summer, impatient and bright, before the sea closed over her.

Mara forgot the wave.

She forgot Ell, Greycap, and the lighthouse beam. There was only the bridge and the impossible mercy of a face she had rehearsed in dreams.

“You took your time,” Sella said.

It was something she used to say whenever Mara was late.

Mara stepped toward her.

Ell caught her hand.

“That isn’t her.”

The words were her own, returned to her.

The girl on the bridge smiled. For a moment the smile was perfect.

Then it widened.

Mara turned and ran.

She and Ell climbed the road as the city woke behind them. Stone feet struck stone streets. Glass cracked in the towers. Voices poured from every doorway—fathers, wives, children, friends—calling with love, anger, and promises.

At the top of the trench, Mara looked back once.

The sailor in the empty coat stood at the bottom of the road. Beside it was the thing wearing Sella’s yellow dress. Behind them gathered hundreds of pale figures.

Their eyes began to open.

Mara and Ell ran west.

The lighthouse beam swept toward them and away. Greycap looked impossibly distant.

The wall of water swallowed the eastern sky.

On the road ahead, figures appeared through the haze.

The expedition was returning.

Mayor Pell led them. Tomas walked beside him. Behind came the others, hand in hand with people who could not be there: drowned sailors, dead grandparents, lost children. Some of the companions looked human. Others flickered between familiar faces and tall white shapes.

“Turn around!” Mara shouted.

Mayor Pell smiled peacefully. His dead wife held his arm.

“We found them,” he said.

The ground shook.

Several villagers looked toward the wave and screamed. Others only tightened their grip on the creatures beside them.

Tomas stood alone. He met Mara’s eyes.

“How far?” he asked.

“Not far enough.”

He nodded, then began shouting at the others, pushing them toward Greycap. Some obeyed. Some fought him. Mayor Pell sat down in the middle of the road and embraced the thing that wore his wife’s face.

There was no time to save everyone.

Mara hated the truth, but truth did not care.

She lifted Ell onto her back when he stumbled. Tomas helped the baker and her daughter. Nine villagers ran with them. Behind, the rest remained among their returned dead.

The first edge of water reached them not as a wave, but as rain.

Droplets fell from the clear air. Then streams rushed through cracks in the seabed. Pools overflowed. The ocean was arriving from below as well as from the east.

Greycap’s bell rang and rang.

At the shore, Lena waited waist-deep in rising water.

She seized Ell and held him so tightly he cried out. Then the three of them climbed the path as water raced across the beach.

The villagers crowded into the lighthouse. Mara slammed the iron door and dropped the bar. Tomas and Daro shuttered the lower windows. Lena led everyone upward.

The sea struck Greycap.

The impact threw Mara against the wall.

Windows burst in the keeper’s cottage. Stone groaned. Water exploded under the door and spiraled around her boots. For a terrible second, the entire tower seemed to bend.

But Greycap had stood for two hundred and four years.

It stood again.

Mara climbed.

People huddled on the stairs and in the service room. At the top, Ell pressed his face to the lantern glass.

The village was gone beneath boiling water. Chimneys vanished. Roofs broke apart and spun away. The sea hurled wreckage against the cliffs, reclaiming boats that had rested on its floor for centuries.

Far below, shapes moved in the foam.

Long white hands clung to the rocks, then slipped beneath the surface.

The wave passed.

The sea settled slowly, rising almost to the lighthouse door before beginning to fall. By afternoon, the familiar outline of Greycap Island emerged again.

Fourteen villagers had returned.

Nine had survived in the lighthouse.

No one spoke of the mathematics of grief.

At sunset, the radio crackled.

Voices arrived from the mainland, from ships, from islands north and south. The sea had vanished everywhere. Cities had been found in trenches and abyssal plains. People had walked down roads. Some had returned. Most had not.

No one knew where the water had gone or why it had come back.

Governments called it an unprecedented tidal event. Scientists proposed ruptures in gravity, distortions in time, or changes in the moon. Priests filled churches. Harbors filled with wreckage.

Mara listened until the words became noise.

Then she climbed to the lantern room.

The great lens had stopped. One of the gears had cracked during the impact. Ell sat beside it, turning a loose brass tooth in his fingers.

“Can you fix it?” he asked.

“Yes.”

This was not entirely true, but it was the right answer.

Together they opened the mechanism. Mara showed him how the gears engaged, how the clockwork released its stored weight, how each polished panel focused the lamp into a beam strong enough to reach beyond the reef.

“Why light it?” Ell asked. “No ships will come tonight.”

“Maybe not.”

“And the sea knows where the rocks are.”

“The light isn’t for the sea.”

“Then who is it for?”

Mara glanced at the dark water.

“For anyone trying to come home.”

They worked until midnight.

When the repaired mechanism began to turn, its rhythm filled the tower: click, whisper, click. The lens gathered the lamp’s glow and sent it outward.

Across the sea.

Across the reef.

Across the place where the white road slept under fathoms of black water.

For a moment, as the beam passed over the waves, Mara saw a girl in a yellow dress standing on the surface.

Sella raised one hand.

Mara’s breath caught, but she did not ring the bell. She did not look away.

The beam moved on, and the figure disappeared.

Beside her, Ell watched the darkness.

“Did you see something?” he asked.

Mara placed a hand on his shoulder.

“Yes,” she said.

“What?”

She listened to the waves striking the cliffs below, steady and ancient, as though they had never left.

“Only the sea.”

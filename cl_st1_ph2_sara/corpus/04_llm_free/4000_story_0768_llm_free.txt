# The Lighthouse Keeper’s Last Guest

On the morning the sea returned the red boat, Elias Vale was repairing the lighthouse clock.

The clock had not told the correct time in twenty-three years. Its face hung above the spiral staircase, visible from every floor, and its hands had stopped at seventeen minutes past four on the night Elias’s daughter disappeared.

Most people would have replaced it. Elias could not.

He sat on the stairs with a screwdriver between his teeth and the brass casing balanced on his knees. Outside, rain pressed against the windows. Wind prowled around the tower, moaning through cracks in the stone.

Then came three knocks at the lighthouse door.

Elias looked toward the entrance.

No one visited Blackglass Island in November. The supply boat had come four days earlier, and the fishermen from the mainland knew better than to cross the channel in a storm.

The knocks came again.

Three, slow and deliberate.

Elias tucked the screwdriver into his pocket and descended. When he opened the door, cold rain rushed over his boots.

A young woman stood on the step.

She wore a yellow coat, soaked dark at the shoulders, and held one hand against the doorframe to steady herself. Her black hair clung to her cheeks. She was perhaps twenty-five, though exhaustion had added shadows beneath her eyes.

Behind her, drawn halfway onto the rocks, was a little red boat.

Elias stared at it.

“Please,” the woman said. “May I come in?”

He stepped aside.

She entered, bringing with her the smell of rain, salt, and something faintly metallic. Elias closed the door and slid the iron bolt into place.

“There’s a fire upstairs,” he said. “You’ll want to get warm.”

The woman removed her coat. Beneath it she wore a gray sweater and a silver pendant shaped like a crescent moon.

Elias stopped breathing for a moment.

His daughter had owned a pendant exactly like it.

The woman noticed his expression and covered the necklace with her hand.

“Is something wrong?”

“No.”

“You looked as if you’d seen a ghost.”

“People often mistake memory for one.”

She studied him, uncertain whether he had made a joke. Then she followed him up the winding stairs.

The lighthouse kitchen occupied the second floor. It was a circular room with a coal stove, a narrow table, and shelves crowded with jars. Elias gave the woman a blanket and set water to boil.

“What’s your name?” he asked.

“Mara.”

He nearly dropped the kettle.

The woman frowned. “Are you all right?”

Elias placed the kettle firmly on the stove.

“My daughter was called Mara.”

“Oh.” She drew the blanket tighter. “I’m sorry.”

“Why?”

“You said ‘was.’”

The storm struck the tower with a sudden gust. Cups rattled in their hooks.

Elias looked out the window. Waves shattered white against the black cliffs.

“How did you get here?”

“In the boat.”

“I can see that.”

“I don’t remember much else.”

“Where did you launch from?”

“I don’t know.”

“You crossed the Blackglass Channel in that thing during a storm, but you don’t know where you came from?”

Mara looked down at her hands.

“I woke up in the boat,” she said. “There was water around my ankles. I saw the lighthouse and rowed toward it.”

“You rowed?”

“Yes.”

“There are no oars.”

Silence entered the room.

Elias took the lamp from the table and descended to the entrance. Through the thick glass of the door, he could see the red boat grinding against the rocks.

No oars lay inside it.

No rope, sail, or engine. Nothing that could have brought it across the channel.

When he returned, Mara was standing before the old clock.

“Why did it stop?” she asked.

“It broke.”

“At four seventeen.”

“That is what the hands suggest.”

She touched the brass rim.

The clock ticked.

Elias froze.

A second tick followed, then a third.

The minute hand trembled and moved forward.

Mara snatched her fingers away.

The clock stopped.

Neither of them spoke.

At last, Elias went to the cupboard, took out a bottle of brandy, and poured a small measure into his tea.

“Perhaps,” Mara said, “I should explain.”

“That would be welcome.”

“I’ve been having dreams.”

“So have I. Mine usually involve unpaid bills.”

“I’m serious.”

“So am I.”

She returned to the table but did not sit.

“For months, I’ve dreamed about this lighthouse. I’d never seen it before, yet I knew every room. I knew there was a crack in the third step from the top. I knew the lantern glass had a bullet mark on the western side.”

Elias said nothing.

Both things were true.

“In the dreams,” she continued, “someone was calling me from under the water. Last night the voice became clear.”

“What did it say?”

“Come home.”

The wind moaned against the walls.

Elias drank the brandy without tasting it.

“This island is not your home.”

“I know.”

“Where do you live?”

“Bellweather.”

“That’s two hundred miles north.”

“I have a flat above a bakery. I restore old paintings at the city museum. My mother’s name is Jean, my father’s name is Thomas, and I have a cat called Bishop who hates everyone except the postman.” Her words came quickly, as if she were reciting evidence. “I know who I am.”

“Then why are you here?”

“I hoped you could tell me.”

Elias looked toward the clock.

Its hands still pointed to seventeen minutes past four.

Twenty-three years earlier, his daughter had left the lighthouse in a red boat.

She had been nineteen, furious, and determined to reach the mainland despite an approaching storm. They had argued at the door. Elias remembered each word because grief had carved them into him.

You cannot command the sea, Mara had shouted.

No, he had replied, but while you live under my roof, I can command you.

Then I won’t live under your roof.

She had taken the boat. Elias had watched its red hull vanish between walls of rain.

At four seventeen in the afternoon, lightning struck the tower. The clock stopped. The lantern failed for nine minutes.

The coast guard found pieces of the boat the following day.

They never found Mara.

Now a young woman with her name and necklace had arrived in another red boat that could not possibly have crossed the channel.

Elias poured a second brandy.

“I think you should leave when the storm clears,” he said.

Mara stared at him. “That’s all?”

“That’s enough.”

“You know something.”

“I know storms make fools of sailors.”

“I’m not a sailor.”

“Clearly.”

She flinched at his sharpness. Elias regretted it but offered no apology.

The storm continued through the afternoon. By evening, the channel had disappeared beneath rain and darkness. Elias lit the great lantern at the top of the tower. Its beam swept across the sea, vanished, and returned.

Mara wandered through the lighthouse.

She knew where things were.

She found spare candles in a drawer she had never opened. She avoided the loose board beside the bedroom door. When Elias asked her to fetch a wrench, she went directly to a cabinet on the fourth floor.

Each small certainty made him colder.

After supper, Mara sat beside the stove while Elias washed their bowls.

“What was she like?” she asked.

“Who?”

“Your daughter.”

He scrubbed a bowl that was already clean.

“Stubborn.”

“That sounds like something a stubborn father would say.”

“She was reckless. Impatient. Always asking questions and never listening to the answers.”

Mara smiled faintly. “Anything good?”

“She could whistle any song after hearing it once. She rescued moths from candle flames. She believed there was no machine that couldn’t be repaired if you understood why it had broken.”

“That last part sounds like you.”

“No. I believe some things stay broken.”

The lighthouse clock gave a single tick.

Elias turned.

Its hands had not moved.

Mara touched her pendant. “Did that belong to her?”

“Yes.”

“My mother gave it to me when I was a baby. She said it was found wrapped in my blanket.”

“Found where?”

Mara hesitated.

“On a beach near Bellweather.”

The bowl slipped from Elias’s hands and struck the basin. It cracked neatly in two.

“My parents adopted me,” she said. “No one knows where I came from. I was discovered alone just before dawn. The doctors thought I was only a few days old.”

“What date?”

“The twelfth of November.”

Elias gripped the edge of the basin.

Twenty-three years ago, Mara’s boat had vanished on the eleventh of November.

Behind him, the clock began ticking.

This time it did not stop.

The sound filled the kitchen, gentle and ordinary, yet Elias felt it in his bones. The minute hand moved from seventeen past four to eighteen.

At that instant, the lighthouse lantern went dark.

Elias ran.

He took the stairs two at a time, climbing toward the lantern room. Mara followed. Above them, the mechanism groaned as the great lens slowed.

When Elias reached the top, he smelled burned oil. The main wick had gone out, though the fuel reservoir was full. He struck a match. The flame bent sideways in a wind that should not have existed inside the sealed chamber.

Beyond the glass, the sea was black.

Then, far below, something answered with a light of its own.

A pale green glow rose beneath the waves.

It formed a path leading from the island toward the open ocean.

Mara pressed her palms to the glass. “I’ve seen that in my dreams.”

A figure stood at the end of the path.

It was impossibly distant and perfectly clear: a young woman in a white sweater, balanced on the surface of the sea. Her dark hair streamed in the storm. Around her neck gleamed a crescent moon.

Elias struck another match. It went out.

The figure lifted one arm.

Not waving.

Beckoning.

“Mara,” Elias whispered.

Beside him, his guest answered, “Yes?”

But he had not been speaking to her.

The figure on the water turned and walked toward the horizon. The green path began to fade behind her.

The young woman in the lantern room grabbed the door handle.

Elias caught her wrist.

“Let me go.”

“No.”

“She wants me to follow.”

“And you intend to?”

“I need to know.”

“I know what waits in the sea.”

“You don’t know anything.”

“I know it took my daughter.”

Mara pulled against his grip. “What if it didn’t?”

The clock chimed below them.

Once.

Twice.

It had no bell, yet the sound rolled through the tower.

With every chime, Elias saw a fragment of another night.

Mara as a child, sleeping beneath his coat while he polished the lantern glass.

Mara at twelve, standing on the rocks with both arms spread, pretending to command the gulls.

Mara at nineteen, her face bright with anger.

Then I won’t live under your roof.

Three.

Four.

The tower shuddered.

A crack appeared in the lantern glass, racing from the old bullet mark to the ceiling.

Five.

Mara twisted free and ran down the stairs.

Elias followed, shouting her name. She reached the entrance before him, pulled back the bolt, and opened the door.

Wind exploded into the lighthouse.

The red boat waited on the rocks.

The green path had reached the shore.

Mara stepped into the rain.

Elias caught her at the water’s edge.

“Listen to me!” he shouted.

The sea surged around their ankles, though they stood well above the ordinary tide.

The distant figure had stopped walking. She watched them from the horizon.

Mara turned on Elias. Rain streamed down her face.

“She’s calling me.”

“She called me too.”

“What?”

“Every night for twenty-three years.” He struggled to make himself heard above the waves. “I dreamed of her beneath the water. Sometimes she begged me to follow. Sometimes she promised I could change what happened. But grief promises many things.”

“This is different.”

“No. It only feels different because it has learned your name.”

Mara looked toward the glowing road.

“I have to know who I am.”

“You told me already. You restore paintings. You live above a bakery. You have a hateful cat.”

“That isn’t enough.”

“It is everything.”

The figure on the sea raised both arms.

The water beside the island rose into the shape of a wall. Within it, faces appeared—pale, wavering faces of sailors and passengers, children and old men, all those the Blackglass Channel had taken.

Among them was Elias’s daughter.

Not as she had been at nineteen, but older.

She looked almost exactly like the woman standing beside him.

Elias’s heart broke with such force that he staggered.

The face in the wave opened its eyes.

“Father,” it said.

The voice was not loud. It did not need to be.

Elias stepped forward.

Mara seized his hand. “Don’t.”

His daughter smiled from within the water.

“I waited,” she said.

Elias felt the years fall away. He was no longer an old man on a freezing shore. He was a father standing in a doorway, one apology away from preventing the worst mistake of his life.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

The face came closer.

“You can tell me.”

“I should have stopped you.”

“You tried.”

“I should have gone after you.”

“Yes.”

The word entered him like a blade.

The wall of water leaned over the rocks.

Mara pulled at his arm. “That isn’t her.”

But Elias wanted it to be. Want was a tide stronger than reason. He saw his daughter’s hand extend from the wave, white and open.

“Come home,” she said.

Home.

He took another step.

The water closed over his boots.

Then the young woman beside him began to whistle.

The tune was thin and shaking at first, nearly lost in the storm. Elias recognized it after three notes.

It was an old harbor song. He had sung it when his daughter was small, and she had whistled it whenever she was afraid.

Elias turned.

Mara’s eyes were shut. Tears mixed with rain on her cheeks, but she kept whistling.

The face in the wave changed.

For an instant, it became furious. Its mouth stretched too wide, and darkness poured from its eyes.

Elias understood.

Memory could wound him, but it had never been cruel. Whatever wore his daughter’s face had chosen the one word she would not have said.

Yes.

His Mara would not have blamed him, even if he deserved it.

Especially then.

Elias stepped backward.

“You are not my daughter.”

The sea roared.

The wall collapsed.

Water swept across the rocks, knocking Elias down. Mara caught his coat, but the current dragged them both toward the edge. The red boat spun past, struck the cliff, and shattered.

Elias clawed at the stone. His fingers slipped.

Mara’s pendant snagged in a crack.

The silver chain snapped tight around her throat.

For one terrible second, it held them.

Elias reached up and closed his hand around the crescent. The metal felt warm. Images flooded his mind—not dreams, not memories, but something stranger.

His daughter in the red boat.

Lightning striking the sea.

A wave opening like a door.

Mara sinking into green light, the silver pendant floating above her.

Then a newborn child crying on a distant beach at dawn.

Time folding.

A life ending and beginning at once.

Elias tore the pendant free from the rock. The chain broke, but he managed to seize Mara’s wrist. Together they crawled beyond the reach of the waves.

Behind them, the green path vanished.

The figure at the horizon remained for a moment longer.

It was no longer a drowned woman or a hungry thing. It was only a shadow.

Then the lighthouse beam returned, sweeping across the water, and the shadow was gone.

Dawn found Elias and Mara sitting on the kitchen floor, wrapped in blankets.

The clock showed six forty-two.

It ticked steadily.

Neither had spoken for a long time.

At last Mara placed the broken pendant on the table.

“Was I her?” she asked.

Elias watched steam rise from his tea.

“I don’t know.”

“You saw something when you touched it.”

“So did you.”

She nodded. “I remember the storm. But I also remember my mother teaching me to read. I remember breaking my arm when I was seven. I remember my first day at the museum.”

“Then those memories are yours.”

“And the others?”

“Also yours, perhaps.”

“That makes no sense.”

“The sea rarely does.”

Mara picked up the crescent. “If I am your daughter—”

“You are not.”

She looked wounded.

Elias continued more gently. “And you are. Maybe some part of her crossed the water. Maybe time gave her another shore. But the woman my daughter might have become is not a debt you owe me.”

Mara stared at the old man across the table.

“What if I want you to be my father?”

Elias looked down.

For twenty-three years, he had imagined this moment in a thousand forms. In every version he begged forgiveness, and his daughter granted it. The fantasy had always ended there. He had never considered what came after forgiveness, because grief was easier to preserve than a life.

“I was not very good at it,” he said.

“So you’ve had time to practice.”

He laughed then, unexpectedly and painfully. Mara laughed too.

Outside, the storm moved east. Sunlight broke through the clouds and touched the wet stones, making the island shine.

The mainland rescue boat arrived before noon. Its captain found the remains of the red boat scattered along the rocks and demanded to know how Mara had crossed the channel.

She looked at Elias.

“I got lost,” she said.

The captain stared at the wreckage. “In a boat with no oars?”

“Very lost.”

Before she boarded, Mara wrote her address on the back of an old supply receipt.

“You could visit,” she said. “Bellweather has an excellent museum.”

“I dislike cities.”

“There’s a maritime gallery.”

“I dislike galleries.”

“And a bakery.”

Elias took the paper. “I will consider the bakery.”

Mara smiled. Around her neck hung the crescent pendant on a piece of lighthouse cord.

As the rescue boat pulled away, she stood at the stern and whistled the harbor song. Elias listened until the distance swallowed it.

Winter passed.

The lighthouse clock continued to keep time. Elias no longer repaired it, though every morning he paused beneath its face to hear the ticking.

In March, he packed a small suitcase.

He left detailed instructions for the temporary keeper, locked the tower door, and boarded the supply boat for the mainland. From there he took two trains north.

Bellweather was larger than he expected and louder than seemed necessary. He became lost twice before finding the bakery. Above it was a blue door with a brass knocker.

Elias climbed the stairs.

Before he could knock, the door opened.

Mara stood there, smiling. A large gray cat glared at him from behind her legs.

“You came,” she said.

“I said I would consider the bakery.”

“Of course.”

She stepped aside.

On the wall behind her hung a newly restored painting of Blackglass Lighthouse beneath a clear morning sky. A red boat rested safely on the shore.

Elias entered.

The cat hissed.

From somewhere in the apartment, a clock chimed four seventeen, then continued on to four eighteen.

This time, Elias did not look back.

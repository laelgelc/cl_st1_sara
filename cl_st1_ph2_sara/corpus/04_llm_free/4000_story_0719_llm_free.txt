# The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, Mara Vale unlocked the door beneath the clock tower and listened to the city wake.

First came the gulls, arguing over the fish market roofs. Then the baker’s shutters rattled open across the square. Delivery carts groaned over wet cobblestones, and somewhere in the maze of narrow streets a child always began to cry just before the bells struck the hour.

Mara knew the sounds so well that she could tell when one was missing.

On the morning the city lost nine minutes, the bells rang before the child began to cry.

Mara froze with the iron key still in her hand.

Above her, the great mechanism thundered through the tower. Chains tightened. Gears engaged. Six bronze bells spoke over the rooftops.

One.

Two.

Three.

Four.

Five.

Six.

Only after the final echo faded did the child’s cry rise from the eastern quarter.

Mara stepped inside and locked the door behind her.

The clock tower had no windows on its lower floors. She climbed by lamplight, passing shelves of oil, tools, and ledgers left by generations of keepers. Her father’s handwriting appeared on many of the labels: careful, upright letters fading from black to brown. He had taught her that a clock was not a machine for measuring time.

“It is a promise,” he used to say. “The city agrees to wake, work, dine, and sleep together. We keep the promise running.”

He had been dead seven years, and Mara had kept it alone ever since.

At the top of the stairs, she entered the mechanism room. Dawn poured through the four clock faces, staining the machinery blue and gold. The central pendulum swung in its long, solemn arc.

Left.

Right.

Left.

Right.

Everything appeared normal.

Mara checked the escapement, then the weights. She examined every tooth of the main gear and tested the tension in the bell cables. Nothing was cracked, bent, or loose. According to the clock, it was twelve minutes past six.

According to Mara’s pocket watch, it was three minutes past.

She held the watch to her ear. Its ticking was swift and frightened.

Nine minutes gone.

She went downstairs and crossed the square to the bakery. Old Tomel was arranging loaves in his window.

“Good morning,” Mara said. “What time did you wake?”

“The usual.”

“Which was?”

“Half past four.”

“Are you certain?”

Tomel shrugged. “The alarm rang. I got up.”

“Did the dough rise properly?”

At that, he frowned.

“A little fast,” he admitted. “I thought the kitchen was warm.”

Mara visited the fish market. The boats had returned at what the fishermen called the usual time, though their lamps had burned less oil than expected. At the schoolhouse, the caretaker insisted he had unlocked the gates at five forty-five, yet the stove had not finished heating. A milk cart arrived before the farmer remembered loading it.

Everyone had followed the clocks.

Only their bodies, bread, flames, and memories knew that something had been stolen.

By noon, the city had adjusted. Clerks complained of hunger arriving too late. Factory workers felt cheated of their morning rest. A magistrate missed his own speech despite being seated in the courtroom when it began. But no one was frightened.

Nine minutes seemed too small for fear.

The next day, the city lost seventeen.

On the third, it lost twenty-three.

Mara stopped sleeping. She remained beside the mechanism through the night, recording every swing of the pendulum. At 4:13 on the fourth morning, the room became suddenly cold. Her lamp flame narrowed to a blue thread.

The pendulum swung left.

It did not swing back.

Silence filled the tower.

Mara could hear her own heart. She counted five beats, ten, twenty. The pendulum hung at the end of its arc, defying gravity.

Then someone knocked on the door three floors below.

The pendulum returned.

The gears leaped forward with a metallic crash. The hands on the four clock faces spun. One minute. Five. Eleven. Mara seized the brake lever and threw her weight against it, but the machinery dragged her across the floor. The hands advanced until the tower had swallowed thirty-one minutes.

Then all was normal again.

The knocking continued.

Mara ran downstairs.

At the door stood a boy of perhaps twelve, thin as a matchstick, wearing a coat far too large for him. Rain shone in his black hair. He held no umbrella, though the square behind him was dry.

“You took your time,” he said.

“Who are you?”

“Eli.”

“How did you get into the square?”

“Walked.”

“The gate is locked until five.”

“Not where I came from.”

Mara stared at him. “Did you stop the clock?”

“No. I came because it stopped.”

He slipped past her before she could object and looked up the dark stairwell.

“You shouldn’t be here,” Mara said.

“Neither should those.”

He pointed toward the shelves of ledgers.

Mara lifted her lamp. The nearest volumes contained repair records from the previous century. She saw nothing unusual until Eli pulled out a narrow book hidden between two larger ones.

Its leather cover bore no title. Dust lay everywhere except upon its spine.

Mara opened it.

The first page was filled with her father’s handwriting.

If you are reading this, it began, then the city has started losing time again.

Her knees weakened.

She carried the book to the worktable while Eli sat on a sack of sand and watched her.

The entries spanned thirty years. Mara’s father had documented missing seconds at first, then minutes. He wrote of winter frost melting before sunrise, of candles burning without shortening, of conversations remembered by only one participant.

The final pages described a chamber beneath the tower.

The clock, he wrote, was older than the city. Its visible mechanism was only a regulator. Below it lay another machine, one built not to measure time, but to contain it.

Centuries ago, the city’s founders had discovered a fault in the world—a place where unused moments collected. Hesitations. Delays. Hours wished away by the grieving. Afternoons squandered in boredom. Final seconds rejected by the dying. These fragments drifted toward the fault like rainwater to a drain.

The founders built the tower over it and designed a clock to release the accumulated time gradually, returning it to the world in harmless instants: a long summer evening, a dream that seemed to last all night, an hour with a friend that felt like two.

But the chamber required a keeper.

Not a mechanic.

A witness.

Mara turned the page.

The last entry had been written three days before her father’s death.

The chamber is filling faster. People have become impatient with their own lives. They wish away journeys, workdays, illnesses, childhoods. The clock cannot return what the city refuses to receive.

Mara read the sentence twice.

Below it, in shakier writing, he had added:

If I fail, find the door behind the pendulum. Do not go alone.

She looked at Eli.

“Did you know my father?”

The boy studied his wet shoes. “A little.”

“You’re twelve.”

“I was twelve when he knew me.”

The tower bells began to ring seven.

Outside, sunlight suddenly flooded the square.

Mara rushed to the door. A moment earlier it had been before dawn. Now the baker was serving customers. Pigeons wheeled overhead. The fountain glittered in full morning light.

Three hours had vanished.

People stopped in the square, staring at the sky. For the first time, they screamed.

Panic spread quickly, but not quickly enough. That afternoon disappeared between one heartbeat and the next. At noon, Mara saw officials gathering below the tower. Then the light turned red, and the same officials were lighting lanterns. At midnight, the bells rang only once before dawn broke again.

People tied themselves to chairs, afraid they might awaken elsewhere. Families wrote notes to remember unfinished conversations. The hospitals overflowed with citizens convinced they had aged overnight, though no one appeared older.

The mayor came to the tower with six guards.

“You will stop this,” he told Mara.

“I’m trying.”

“You are the keeper.”

“I keep the clock. I don’t command the sun.”

“Then slow it down.”

“That may make it worse.”

“Worse?” He looked toward the bright afternoon sky. It had been morning when he entered. “At this rate, the city will reach winter by tomorrow.”

“Not winter,” Eli said from the stairs. “The end.”

The mayor turned. “Whose child is that?”

“No one’s,” Eli replied.

Mara showed the mayor her father’s ledger. He read half a page, closed it, and declared it nonsense.

“Machines do not feed on boredom,” he said.

“This one does.”

“Then give it some. Half the council produces nothing else.”

The guards laughed uneasily.

The mayor ordered Mara to dismantle the clock before another hour could be lost. She refused. He threatened arrest. She handed him a wrench and invited him to climb the tower himself.

While they argued, the day vanished.

Night pressed against the open door. The mayor went pale.

“Do what you must,” he whispered, and fled with his guards.

Mara waited until the square was empty. Then she and Eli climbed to the mechanism room.

Behind the pendulum, concealed by a panel of iron leaves, they found a keyhole.

Mara inserted the tower key.

It did not fit.

Eli reached beneath his shirt and drew out a key on a red cord. It was small, black, and shaped like a human rib.

“Where did you get that?”

“Your father gave it to me.”

“Before he died?”

“After.”

Mara nearly laughed, but Eli’s face stopped her.

“He found me in the chamber,” the boy said. “I fell in a long time ago. Or maybe tomorrow. It’s hard to know down there.”

“Why didn’t he bring you out?”

“He did. Just not all of me.”

Eli placed the black key in her palm. It was warm.

“Your father used his own time to close the fault,” he said. “Seven years of it. That’s why he died.”

Mara remembered her father’s final morning. He had seemed healthy the night before, tired only in the ordinary way. At dawn, she found him in his bed, old beyond recognition, his hair white, his hands spotted and thin. He had lived long enough to say her name.

Seven years.

The exact number since his death.

“You knew this would happen,” she said.

Eli nodded.

“And you waited.”

“I was lost.”

“You had the key.”

“I couldn’t use it. The door won’t open for someone without a future.”

Mara looked into his dark, unblinking eyes.

Then she unlocked the panel.

The stone wall behind it split with a sigh. A stairway curved downward, though they stood near the tower’s summit. Its steps disappeared into silver mist.

Mara took her lamp and began to descend.

The stairway should have carried them through the mechanism room, the tower floors, and deep into the earth, but there were no doors, landings, or foundations. They walked for what felt like an hour. Or a minute. Mara’s legs grew tired, recovered, and grew tired again.

Voices whispered in the mist.

I wish this day were over.

I can’t wait until I’m grown.

Let winter come.

If only these last hours would pass.

Each wish released a pale thread that drifted downward beside them.

At last, the stairs ended at the shore of a black lake.

Above the water hung thousands of clocks. Grandfather clocks, pocket watches, sundials, hourglasses, kitchen timers, and devices Mara did not recognize. Some had hands that moved backward. Some had no numbers. One ticked each time she remembered something she had forgotten.

In the center of the lake stood a circular platform. On it rose an enormous glass vessel shaped like an hourglass.

Its upper chamber was cracked.

Silver light leaked through the fracture and poured into the lower chamber, which was already full. With every drop, the city above lurched forward.

“How do we stop it?” Mara asked.

Eli pointed across the water.

A narrow bridge led to the platform, but sections of it appeared and disappeared like thoughts. Mara stepped onto the first stone. It trembled beneath her foot.

“Wait here,” she told him.

“He told me that too.”

“My father?”

“He said he would come back.”

Mara looked at the boy’s oversized coat and wet hair. “How long have you been waiting?”

“I don’t know.”

She held out her hand.

Together they crossed.

With every step, Mara saw moments from her life flicker beneath the water. Her mother singing while mending a shirt. Her father lifting her so she could touch the pendulum. A summer afternoon when she had hidden in the tower to avoid visiting her sick aunt.

That afternoon shone brighter than the others.

She remembered wishing it away.

Other moments followed. Rainy mornings she had hurried through. Meals eaten without tasting. Conversations endured while thinking of something else. Years spent waiting to become head keeper, followed by years spent wishing she could be free of the responsibility.

All of them were in the lake.

Everyone’s unwanted time.

The platform shook as another torrent of silver poured through the cracked vessel. Mara approached its base and found a brass inscription.

WHAT IS REFUSED MUST BE BORNE.

Below the words was a hollow shaped like two hands.

Mara placed her palms inside it.

The chamber vanished.

She stood in the square at sunset. Her father sat beside the fountain, alive and young. Pigeons pecked around his boots.

“You took long enough,” he said.

Mara could not breathe.

He smiled, and for an instant she was eight years old again, running down the tower stairs while he pretended not to hear her.

“Is this real?” she asked.

“It happened. That is one kind of real.”

She sat beside him. His shoulder felt solid against hers.

“You should have told me.”

“I wanted you to have a life beyond the tower.”

“I didn’t.”

“I know. I’m sorry.”

Anger rose in her, sharp after seven years of grief. “You left me your work, your secrets, and a city that eats its own days.”

“I left you a choice.”

“What choice?”

Her father looked across the square. People moved through the sunset, unaware that they were memories. Tomel carried flour into his bakery. The mayor, not yet elected, kissed a woman beneath an archway. Mara saw herself at seventeen, climbing the tower with oil on her cheek.

“The vessel is broken,” her father said. “Someone must hold the lost time until the city learns to take it back.”

“You did.”

“For as long as I could.”

“And it killed you.”

“Eventually.”

Mara stared at her younger self. “How much time would I lose?”

“All that the city has refused.”

“Years?”

“Perhaps.”

“And if I do nothing?”

“The hours will continue spilling. Days, then seasons, then generations. The city will race forward while its people remain trapped between moments. At last there will be no future left to enter.”

Mara thought of Eli on the platform, waiting outside this memory.

“What happened to the boy?”

“He wished away a moment he should have lived.”

“What moment?”

“The one in which he died.”

The sunset darkened.

Her father folded his hands. “He fell from the harbor wall. As the water closed over him, he wanted the moment to pass. The fault took it, but death could not finish without it. Now he belongs neither before nor after.”

“Can I save him?”

“You can return what he refused.”

“And then?”

“You know.”

Mara closed her eyes.

“When I place my hands in the mechanism,” she said, “I take the city’s lost time.”

“Yes.”

“How do I give it back?”

“One moment at a time.”

“That could take my whole life.”

“It will be your whole life.”

She looked at him. “Stay with me.”

Pain crossed his face.

“I can’t.”

“Just a minute.”

“Mara—”

“One minute. I’ll take it willingly.”

He smiled then, though his eyes filled with tears.

They sat beside the fountain as the sun touched the roofs. Mara listened to the gulls and the market carts. She felt every second arrive, not swiftly or slowly, but completely.

When the minute ended, she was grateful for it.

The square disappeared.

Mara stood again before the hourglass, her hands pressed into the brass hollows.

Time entered her.

It came as heat at first, then weight.

Millions of abandoned moments rushed through her body. She felt sleepless nights cursed by the lonely, childhood summers discarded by the impatient, final breaths feared by the dying. She endured every queue, every fever, every journey whose destination mattered more than the road.

Her hair grew to her waist, then turned gray. Wrinkles formed across her hands.

“Mara!” Eli shouted.

She held on.

The lower chamber emptied. Silver light poured through the brass plate and into her bones. Above them, the countless clocks began to ring.

When the last drop was gone, Mara pulled her hands free.

She collapsed.

Eli caught her, though he was small and she had become very old.

The crack in the vessel remained.

“We failed,” he said.

“No.”

Mara removed the black key from its cord. She fitted it into the fracture. The glass closed around it, sealing the break.

The hourglass turned.

The lost time began to rise.

The lake filled with images. Across the city, people stopped.

A baker stood before dough that required waiting and understood that waiting was part of bread. A mother listened to her daughter finish a long and wandering story. Factory owners opened doors and let workers see the afternoon sun. Patients received nights they had once begged to lose. Prisoners discovered that even confinement contained breaths that belonged to them.

The city took back its hours.

Eli gasped.

Water streamed from his coat. His face changed—not older, but more real. Color entered his cheeks. His body began to tremble.

“I’m cold,” he said with wonder.

Mara knelt slowly before him.

“The harbor water was cold,” she said. “You fell.”

“I remember.”

“You have to finish the moment.”

Fear widened his eyes. “Will it hurt?”

“Yes.”

“What comes after?”

“I don’t know.”

He glanced toward the bridge. “Will you come with me?”

“As far as I can.”

They stepped into the black lake together.

It was shallow now. With each step, Eli’s coat grew heavier. The chamber faded around them, replaced by wind, rain, and a stormy harbor from a century before.

A younger Eli struggled in dark water.

Mara held his hand.

“Don’t wish it away,” she told him. “It’s yours, even this.”

He cried out. The sea closed over his head.

For one terrible second, his fingers tightened around hers.

Then they released.

The storm disappeared.

Mara stood alone at the foot of the tower stairs, holding a child’s red cord.

She climbed.

When she emerged into the mechanism room, only nine minutes had passed.

The pendulum swung steadily. Dawn glowed through the clock faces. In the square below, the city was quiet.

Mara looked at her reflection in the polished brass. Her hair was white. Her face had aged perhaps fifty years, though she was only thirty-four.

She opened the tower door at six.

The gulls began their quarrel over the fish market. The baker’s shutters rattled. Carts groaned across the cobbles.

Then the bells rang.

One.

Two.

Three.

Four.

Five.

Six.

And in the eastern quarter, a child began to cry at exactly the right moment.

In the weeks that followed, the city learned slowness.

Not happily. People complained when meetings dragged, when rain delayed parades, when winter refused to become spring. But they no longer said they wanted time to disappear. It had become a dangerous phrase, then an impolite one, and finally an old-fashioned expression children scarcely understood.

The mayor ordered benches placed throughout the square. Tomel began selling bread that took two days to rise. Schools set aside an hour in which nothing was required of students except that they notice where they were.

As for Mara, she remained keeper of the clock.

Each morning, people saw an old woman unlock the tower. Few remembered that she had once been young. Fewer still knew why the city’s clocks sometimes paused for a single beat before continuing.

During that beat, Mara would feel one of the lost moments leave her.

A sailor watching the horizon would receive an extra glimpse of sunrise. Two friends parting at a station would find enough time for one final embrace. A dying man would hear the end of his daughter’s sentence.

Years passed, properly and in order.

One rainy evening, long after Mara’s hands had begun to shake, a girl knocked on the tower door. She wore a yellow scarf and carried a toolbox.

“I’m here about the apprenticeship,” she said.

Mara had advertised no apprenticeship.

“What makes you think I need one?”

The girl pointed upward. “Your west clock loses three seconds every month.”

Mara raised an eyebrow. “Anything else?”

“The bell rope is fraying. The lower gear needs oil. And you look tired.”

Mara let her in.

She taught the girl how to clean the teeth of the gears, how to judge a minute without a watch, and how to listen for errors hidden between ticks. Years later, when the girl was grown and ready, Mara showed her the iron panel behind the pendulum.

She told her everything.

When the story was finished, the new keeper asked, “Is the chamber full?”

“Never full,” Mara said. “Never empty.”

“Will it break again?”

“Everything breaks again.”

The young woman considered this, then nodded.

Mara handed her the tower key.

That evening, Mara sat beside the fountain as sunset spread across the city roofs. The square had changed over the years. The bakery had a new sign. Electric lamps shone beneath the arches. Motorcars grumbled where horses once stood.

Yet the gulls still fought overhead.

Mara closed her eyes.

She felt no need for the evening to last longer. She did not wish the cold away, nor the ache in her back, nor the loneliness that came with remembering those who had gone before her.

Each belonged to the hour.

The tower bells began to ring.

Mara listened as the city kept its promise.

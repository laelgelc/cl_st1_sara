The Town That Borrowed Tomorrow

On the first Tuesday of every month, the future arrived in Bellwether by train.

It came at six in the morning, when fog lay thick over the river and the station lamps still burned. The engine was always black, though no soot marked its chimney, and it made no sound except for a soft ticking, like a watch wrapped in cloth.

No passengers ever stepped onto the platform. Only parcels.

There were usually a dozen: brown boxes tied with white string, each labeled with the name of someone in town. The stationmaster placed them on a brass cart and rang the bell.

By breakfast, everyone knew who had received something from tomorrow.

A farmer might find a jar of next season’s honey and learn whether the clover would bloom. A widow might receive a newspaper and see her own name absent from the death notices. Children sometimes opened boxes containing schoolbooks with answers written in their older handwriting. Once, Mayor Vale received a photograph of the town square with a marble statue of himself in the center. He ordered the pedestal built that afternoon.

There were rules.

A parcel could come only from one day ahead. Nothing living could be sent. No weapons were permitted, though no one knew who enforced this. And every object borrowed from tomorrow had to be returned before midnight.

At eleven fifty-five each night, the recipients gathered at the station. The black train arrived from the opposite direction, silent as before, and the borrowed things were placed back inside. If a parcel was not returned, tomorrow would not come.

This last rule was painted in red above the station doors.

RETURN WHAT IS NOT YET YOURS.

No one knew what would happen if they failed. No one wished to find out.

For thirty-seven years, Bellwether borrowed tomorrow without incident.

Then Mara Venn received a letter from her dead mother.

Mara was the town clockmaker. She lived above her shop on Pendulum Street, among the patient breathing of clocks. Her mother, Elian, had died twelve years earlier, after a winter illness that no medicine could cure. Mara had been nineteen then, old enough to understand death and young enough to believe understanding made it easier.

It had not.

On the first Tuesday of October, the stationmaster delivered a narrow parcel to Mara’s shop. It was light and flat, and her name was written across the paper in a hand she recognized before she admitted recognizing it.

Mara Venn.

The final n curled downward. Her mother had always done that.

Mara locked the shop door.

For several minutes she stood behind the counter, listening to a hundred clocks disagree about the hour. Then she cut the white string.

Inside the parcel was an envelope.

My dearest Mara, it said on the front.

Her knees weakened. She sat on the floor.

The letter within was four pages long.

Her mother wrote of ordinary things. The pears in the eastern orchard had ripened early. The kitchen roof still leaked above the stove. She had misplaced her blue scarf and suspected Mara’s father had used it to polish his boots. She asked whether Mara still bit the inside of her cheek while repairing difficult mechanisms.

Then the letter became stranger.

I do not know how this reaches you, Elian wrote. For me, it is Monday evening, and you are asleep upstairs. You are nineteen. The doctor says I may have another week. Perhaps two. A man came to the door at dusk. He wore a conductor’s uniform and told me I could send one thing forward, but only if someone sent it back. I asked how far. He said, “As far as grief requires.”

Mara stopped reading.

Outside, someone tried the shop door. A customer called her name and went away.

She looked again at the handwriting.

You will want to keep this letter. You must not.

The words blurred.

Return it tonight. If you do, another letter will come next month. I have been promised twelve. If you keep any one of them, it will be the last.

Mara read the letter twice more. By noon, she knew every line. By evening, she could hear her mother’s voice in every turn of phrase.

At eleven fifty, she walked to the station with the envelope hidden inside her coat.

The platform was crowded. Mayor Vale had borrowed tomorrow’s election results and looked pleased. A baker carried a loaf made from dough that had not yet risen. Two brothers argued over a racing form. Near the luggage cart, a little girl clutched a glass globe containing snow that would not fall until the following day.

The stationmaster, Mr. Rook, checked each object against his ledger.

“Letter,” he said when Mara approached.

She held it tighter. “Where did it come from?”

“Tomorrow.”

“No. When did it come from?”

Mr. Rook studied her over his spectacles. He was an old man with a long, narrow face and silver hair combed neatly behind his ears.

“You know I cannot answer questions about the line.”

“My mother wrote it twelve years ago.”

“Then perhaps twelve years ago is tomorrow somewhere.”

“That makes no sense.”

“Railways rarely do when viewed from the platform.”

The train appeared through the fog. Its single lamp shone pale and round. The ticking grew louder.

Mara looked down at the envelope.

“I could keep it,” she whispered.

Mr. Rook closed his ledger. “You could.”

“What would happen?”

“I don’t know.”

“Has no one ever tried?”

“Not in Bellwether.”

The train stopped.

Its doors opened onto darkness.

Mara thought of the years since her mother’s death: birthdays without her, winters without her, the clock shop inherited too soon. She thought of all the questions she had saved for a person who could no longer answer. Twelve letters. Twelve months in which her mother might become, for a few hours at a time, more than memory.

She placed the envelope on the cart.

Mr. Rook rolled it into the dark.

The doors shut. The train departed.

At midnight, every clock in Bellwether struck twelve.

Tomorrow came.

The second letter arrived in November.

Elian wrote about Mara’s childhood: the time she had dismantled the hall clock at age six and hidden the springs under her pillow; the summer she tried to teach a goat to dance; the night she asked whether stars were holes through which the dead watched the living.

I told you no, the letter said. I did not want you to think heaven was spying. But lately I wonder if love is a kind of watching. Not judgment. Attention.

The third letter came in December. The fourth in January.

Mara returned each one.

She began living from Tuesday to Tuesday, from parcel to parcel. Customers noticed that she smiled more often. She repaired clocks ahead of schedule. She bought pears even when they were out of season.

In February, her mother wrote about fear.

I am afraid of dying, though everyone praises courage as if fear were a failure of character. I think courage may simply be fear that has put on its boots.

In March, she wrote about Mara’s father, who had left when Mara was nine.

In April, she apologized for being impatient.

In May, she described a dream in which they met as old women beside a green sea.

Then came June.

The train arrived on time. Mr. Rook unloaded eleven parcels and frowned.

Mara waited beside the brass cart.

“That’s all,” he said.

“There should be one for me.”

“There is no other cargo.”

“You missed it.”

“I did not.”

She stepped toward the train, but the doors had already closed. The black engine slipped away into the morning.

All day, Mara moved through the town as though walking underwater. She checked the station three times. She searched the post office, questioned the conductor of the noon train, and opened her empty mailbox until dusk.

That night, she confronted Mr. Rook.

“You said there would be twelve.”

“I said no such thing.”

“She did.”

He removed his spectacles and polished them. “Perhaps the letter was lost.”

“On a train from tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow loses many things.”

“Can I send something back?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“The line carries loans, not messages.”

“The letters are messages.”

“The letters are cargo.”

Mara struck the counter with her palm. “That is a distinction made by a coward.”

Mr. Rook’s expression did not change, but his hand stopped polishing the glass.

“Yes,” he said. “Most rules are.”

Mara left before she said something worse.

The seventh letter arrived in July.

There was no explanation for the missed month. Her mother wrote as though the sequence were unbroken. She described rain against the windows, the taste of mint tea, and the sound of nineteen-year-old Mara sleeping in the next room.

Mara read the pages carefully. Near the end, one sentence had been underlined.

The conductor says the track is weakening.

At eleven fifty-five, Mara stood alone at the far end of the platform.

She watched townspeople return their borrowed objects. Mayor Vale surrendered a speech applauded by an audience that did not yet exist. A young couple returned a key to a house they hoped to buy. The baker carried an empty cake box, having eaten the contents despite the rule that objects should remain unchanged. Mr. Rook merely sighed and marked his ledger.

Mara took out the letter.

The train arrived.

She imagined walking away. She imagined hiding the pages under a floorboard or locking them inside a clock. What could the train do? Stop tomorrow? The idea seemed childish, a threat told to keep an obedient town obedient.

Yet the letter itself was impossible. Her mother’s handwriting was impossible. The black train, the future newspapers, the snow globe filled with Wednesday’s storm—all impossible.

One impossibility could enforce another.

She returned the letter.

August brought the eighth.

The track is weakening because too much has been borrowed, Elian wrote. Not objects. Certainty.

She explained that the conductor had visited again. Bellwether had spent decades looking ahead, choosing only roads that promised success. Farmers planted crops after seeing the harvest. Merchants stocked what tomorrow’s customers demanded. Lovers proposed only after glimpsing acceptance. Elections were decided before votes were cast. Risks vanished. So did accidents, surprises, and acts of faith.

A town cannot live forever on confirmed decisions, Elian wrote. Tomorrow must be allowed to arrive unopened.

Mara read the sentence aloud.

That afternoon, she walked through Bellwether.

She saw things she had always seen without noticing. The florist displayed bouquets copied from photographs of tomorrow’s weddings. Boys outside the betting shop traded information from future scores. At the council hall, Mayor Vale reviewed a transcript of the next day’s debate and rehearsed his cleverest replies.

No one wondered anymore.

No one guessed.

At the schoolyard, children played a game called Prediction. One child named an action; the others consulted slips of paper borrowed from the next day and declared whether it would succeed. When a boy proposed climbing the old cedar tree, a girl checked her paper.

“You fall,” she said.

The boy stepped away from the trunk.

“Does it say I’m hurt?”

“No.”

“Then maybe I should climb.”

“But you fall.”

He did not climb.

In September, the ninth letter contained only two paragraphs.

The conductor says one letter will not reach you. I do not know which. He says a missing thing is sometimes more important than an arriving one.

Then:

Mara, when the last letter comes, you will be asked to choose. Do not choose for me.

The rest of the pages were blank.

Mara took the letter to Mr. Rook.

“What choice?”

He looked older than he had the month before. Deep shadows hung beneath his eyes.

“I cannot tell you.”

“Cannot or will not?”

“Both.”

“Is the train dangerous?”

“Yes.”

“To me?”

“To everything.”

She leaned across the counter. “Then why have you spent thirty-seven years unloading it?”

“Because the first parcel had my name on it.”

He opened a drawer and removed a child’s red mitten. The wool was faded, and one thumb had been clumsily mended.

“My daughter disappeared when she was seven,” he said. “We searched the river, the forest, every road. Then the train came. It brought this from the following day.”

Mara touched the mitten.

“You found her?”

“No. But each month, another object arrived. A ribbon. A shoe. Once, a drawing of a house under three suns. I thought the next parcel would tell me where she had gone.”

“Did it?”

“After a year, they stopped. By then the town had seen what the train could do. People demanded that I keep the route open.”

“And your daughter?”

Mr. Rook looked toward the tracks.

“She would be forty-four now. Some mornings, I think she is the conductor.”

October arrived cold and clear.

The tenth letter warned that the final train would come in December.

November’s letter was the longest. Elian wrote of everything she wished she could have lived to see: Mara’s first gray hair, the changing town, inventions not yet imagined, the person her daughter would become without a mother to witness it.

But perhaps that is what children are, she wrote. Futures their parents cannot visit.

Mara wept until the ink ran beneath her fingers.

Before returning the letter, she added three words at the bottom.

I miss you.

She knew the rules. She knew borrowed objects had to be returned unchanged. Still, she folded the pages and placed them on the cart.

Mr. Rook saw the addition. He said nothing.

The train carried the message into darkness.

On the first Tuesday of December, every person in Bellwether gathered at the station.

No parcels had arrived for anyone except Mara.

Her box was small.

Inside lay the twelfth letter and a brass lever no longer than her hand.

The letter began:

My dearest Mara,

Your words appeared on the page.

I miss you.

For me, you wrote them before I died. For you, I imagine many years have passed. I cannot explain what it means to hear from the woman my daughter became. I can only tell you that, for one moment, death feels less like a wall than a door we are standing on opposite sides of.

The conductor has offered us a bargain.

Keep the lever, and the line will remain open. More letters may come—not only from me, but from anyone who has been lost. Bellwether will continue to receive tomorrow.

Return the lever, and the train will never stop here again.

You may think you are choosing whether to lose me. You are not. You lost me twelve years ago.

You are choosing whether to let tomorrow belong to the living.

The station was silent when Mara finished reading.

Everyone had heard of the lever by then. Rumors moved quickly in Bellwether, especially rumors about the future.

Mayor Vale pushed through the crowd.

“You cannot return it,” he said.

A murmur of agreement followed.

“My office relies on those reports.”

“So does my farm,” called someone else.

“My husband is ill,” said a woman near the door. “Tomorrow’s tests tell us whether the medicine works.”

A shopkeeper raised his voice. “The train belongs to the town.”

“It came to me,” Mara said.

“But the choice affects us all.”

“Yes.”

“Then we vote,” Mayor Vale declared.

Mr. Rook stood behind his counter, ledger closed.

“No,” he said.

The mayor stared at him. “No?”

“The railway does not recognize elections.”

The platform clock showed eleven forty-eight.

People surrounded Mara. They pleaded, argued, threatened. A man offered her his house. The baker promised free bread for life. The sick woman took Mara’s hand and asked whether uncertainty was worth dying for.

Mara had no answer.

At eleven fifty-three, the ticking began beyond the fog.

She looked at the lever.

With it, she might hear from her mother again. There could be thirteen letters. A hundred. Perhaps one day the train would bring a photograph, a voice recorded on some future device, a way to cross the door entirely.

But she remembered the boy beneath the cedar tree.

You fall.

He had accepted those words as a command.

The train emerged.

Mayor Vale seized Mara’s wrist.

“For once,” he said, “think about tomorrow.”

Mara looked at him.

“We have done nothing else for thirty-seven years.”

She pulled free.

The train stopped, and its doors opened.

For the first time, a conductor stood inside.

The figure wore a dark uniform with silver buttons. Beneath the cap was the face of a woman neither young nor old. Mr. Rook made a broken sound.

The conductor looked at him but did not speak.

Mara stepped forward.

In one hand she held the lever. In the other, her mother’s final letter.

She wanted to keep both.

Instead, she placed them inside the train.

The conductor inclined her head.

“Will she know?” Mara asked. “My mother—will she know what I chose?”

“She already did.”

The doors began to close.

Mr. Rook rushed forward. “Lysa?”

The conductor turned.

For an instant, she smiled like a seven-year-old girl.

Then the doors shut.

The black train departed Bellwether, ticking softly until the fog swallowed it. The rails shivered. A moment later, they had rusted through.

At midnight, the town waited.

No clocks struck.

Every face lifted toward the great clock above the station, but its hands had stopped one minute before twelve.

Someone screamed. Mayor Vale whispered that tomorrow had failed to come.

Then, from Mara’s shop across town, a single clock chimed.

Another answered from a house near the river.

Then another.

The station clock gave a groan, and its minute hand lurched forward.

Midnight arrived—not all at once, not perfectly, but in a wave of bells spreading through Bellwether.

Wednesday began.

It rained unexpectedly.

The baker burned his first batch of bread. Mayor Vale forgot a speech and had to speak honestly. The farmer planted winter wheat without knowing whether it would survive. At the schoolyard, the boy climbed the cedar tree.

He fell.

He landed laughing in a pile of wet leaves, then climbed again.

Years later, people still spoke of the train, though many claimed it had been a shared superstition. The tracks were removed. The station became a library. Mr. Rook retired and spent his mornings painting pictures of houses under three suns.

Mara kept her mother’s first eleven letters only in memory. Sometimes she recalled entire passages. Sometimes all she could remember was the shape of the handwriting or the feeling of paper trembling in her hands.

She married. She did not know in advance that the answer would be yes.

She had a daughter. She did not know whether the child would be healthy.

She grew older. She made mistakes no newspaper had warned her about and found joys no parcel could have contained.

On the first Tuesday of every December, Mara closed the clock shop before dawn and walked to the old platform. She stood there until sunrise, listening.

The train never returned.

Yet once, when fog covered the river and the world seemed to be holding its breath, she heard a soft ticking in the distance.

Mara waited.

The sound came closer.

For one wild moment, she wished for the black engine to emerge, for the doors to open, for one more letter to arrive with her name written in that familiar hand.

But the ticking was only rain beginning on the library roof.

Mara smiled.

She had not known it would rain.

# The Museum of Forgotten Weather

On the morning the rain was scheduled to return, Mara Vale woke to the sound of hammers on the roof.

For one hopeful second, she mistook the tapping for drops.

Then a workman shouted, “Pass me the copper nails,” and the illusion vanished.

Mara lay still beneath her thin summer blanket. The bedroom window stood open, as it had every night for eleven years, but no breeze entered. Beyond the glass, the city shimmered under a white sky. Roofs sagged in the heat. The riverbed was a pale scar between rows of buildings. Even the pigeons had stopped flying by noon.

Today, according to the Ministry of Atmospheric Recovery, the drought would end at four o’clock.

Mara did not believe them.

She dressed in her gray uniform, pinned back her hair, and walked downstairs to the Museum of Forgotten Weather.

The museum occupied the lower floors of her house, though the house had not always been a museum. Once, before Mara was born, it had been a hotel for travelers arriving by riverboat. The lobby still held a brass reception bell and a wall of small wooden boxes where guests had once left their keys. Now each box contained a different kind of memory.

Box seven held the smell of wet pavement.

Box twelve held the silver stillness before snow.

Box twenty-three held the pressure in the ears that came before a thunderstorm.

The memories were sealed in glass vials, wax envelopes, music boxes, and jars with rusted lids. People came from distant districts to see them. Children who had never felt rain pressed their noses to display cases while guides explained umbrellas, puddles, and mud.

On the first floor stood the Hall of Winds. On the second, the Gallery of Clouds. The basement contained the dangerous collections: lightning, hail, fog dense enough to become lost in, and one small tornado preserved inside a green bottle.

Mara was the museum’s curator, caretaker, ticket seller, and only permanent employee.

At eight, she unlocked the front doors.

A crowd was already waiting in the street.

“Is it true?” a woman called.

“Will you close the museum when the rain comes back?” asked someone else.

A boy near the front held up a folded paper boat. “Can I sail this today?”

Mara raised both hands.

“The museum opens at nine,” she said. “The rain, if it arrives, is expected at four. Until then, all exhibits remain available except the storm cellar.”

The crowd murmured at her careful choice of words.

“If it arrives?” said a reporter in a blue hat. “The Ministry guarantees precipitation.”

“The Ministry has guaranteed precipitation six times.”

“But the new machines—”

“Open at nine,” Mara repeated, and shut the doors.

She leaned against them after the crowd’s voices faded.

From the reception desk, a tiny bell rang.

Mara looked up.

The bell had not been touched. It was the old hotel bell, tarnished and cracked, and it rang only when someone brought weather to the museum.

A second chime trembled through the lobby.

Someone was already inside.

Mara took the iron key from her pocket and moved quietly past the Hall of Winds. The exhibits whispered around her. A bottled sea breeze stirred blue ribbons behind glass. A winter gale shook the door of its cabinet. Somewhere overhead, the ceiling creaked beneath the workmen’s boots.

At the end of the corridor stood a girl in a red coat.

She was perhaps nine years old. The coat was far too heavy for the heat, and its hem was dark with moisture.

Mara stopped.

“Where did you come from?”

The girl pointed toward the front doors.

“They were locked.”

“I know.”

“Then how did you enter?”

“I knocked.”

Mara glanced at the corridor windows. Every latch was fastened. There was no sign of a broken pane, no open door, no muddy footprint—although the girl’s black shoes were wet.

“What is your name?” Mara asked.

“June.”

“Do your parents know where you are?”

June shrugged.

Water fell from the cuff of her coat and struck the floor.

Mara stared at the drop. It rested on the tile, round and bright as a glass bead.

“Is that rainwater?”

“No,” June said. “It’s mine.”

From inside the red coat, she produced a jam jar. The jar was filled with a low, dark cloud.

It turned slowly behind the glass.

Mara forgot the heat. She forgot the crowd outside and the men repairing the roof. She forgot, for an instant, how to breathe.

“Where did you get that?”

“I found it.”

“Where?”

“In my room.”

June held out the jar. Thunder, very small and very far away, murmured inside it.

The reception bell rang a third time.

Mara did not take the jar.

“Put it on the desk,” she said.

June obeyed.

Mara circled it carefully. She had spent her life around preserved weather and knew the signs of a false specimen. Steam could be trapped in a bottle. Smoke could be colored. A hidden metal plate could imitate lightning. But this cloud had depth. It folded into itself, gray over violet, alive with invisible currents. Condensation jeweled the inside of the glass.

“What do you want for it?” Mara asked.

“Nothing.”

“Everyone wants something.”

“I want you to let it out.”

Mara looked at her.

“Absolutely not.”

“It doesn’t like the jar.”

“Most weather doesn’t like being contained.”

“Then why do you keep so much of it here?”

The question was simple, almost innocent, but Mara felt it like an accusation.

“Because there isn’t any left outside.”

June lifted her eyes toward the ceiling. “There will be.”

“So the Ministry says.”

“The Ministry is wrong.”

Mara folded her arms. “They say rain will begin at four.”

“It will. But it won’t stop.”

A hammer struck the roof. Thunder answered from the jar.

June continued, “It will rain through the night. By morning, the river will return. By tomorrow evening, the lower streets will be underwater. The Ministry machines will break, but the rain won’t.”

Mara looked toward the window. The sky was blank, bright, and merciless.

“How could you know that?”

“I remember.”

“You’re nine.”

“I remember anyway.”

The girl’s face held no mischief. That troubled Mara more than any smile would have.

Mara picked up the jar. The glass was cold enough to hurt.

“Come with me.”

She led June upstairs, through the Gallery of Clouds. Here, old skies hung in gilded frames: feathery cirrus from the western farms, bruised thunderheads from the coast, a single sunset cloud preserved on the last evening before the drought began. In the center of the gallery stood a long table covered with maps.

Mara set the jar beside a barometer.

The needle spun wildly.

June wandered to a painting of rain over a green field.

“I’ve seen this place,” she said.

“That field is gone. It’s part of the eastern dust flats now.”

“It was yellow before the rain.”

“Wheat.”

“There was a tree.”

Mara glanced at the painting. A willow leaned over the field’s edge.

“Yes.”

“You were sitting under it.”

The room seemed to contract.

Mara placed both hands on the table. “Who told you that?”

June turned. “You did.”

“I’ve never met you.”

“Not yet.”

Mara’s first instinct was to call the Ministry. Her second was to hide the girl. Years of caring for dangerous things had taught her to trust the second instinct, especially when the first involved officials.

She examined June more closely.

The red coat was old-fashioned but well made. One brass button was missing. A faint scar crossed the girl’s left eyebrow. Around her wrist, she wore a blue thread tied in three knots.

Mara knew that thread.

She wore an identical one beneath her sleeve.

Her mother had tied it there when Mara was a child, telling her the knots stood for what had been, what was, and what might yet be. Mara had never removed it. The thread should have decayed years ago, but it remained stubbornly whole.

“Show me your wrist,” Mara said.

June held out her arm.

Mara touched the three knots. They were damp.

“Who tied this?”

“You did.”

A knock sounded downstairs, loud enough to echo through the museum.

“Mara Vale!” called a man’s voice. “Ministry inspection!”

June reached for the jar.

Mara caught her hand. “Leave it.”

“They’ll take it.”

“They take everything,” Mara said. “That doesn’t mean they find everything.”

She opened a cabinet labeled ALTOCUMULUS, removed a tray of harmless white cloud fragments, and placed the jar behind them.

The knocking below became pounding.

Mara led June to the Hall of Winds and unlocked the exhibit known as the North Room. A cold draft slipped through the door, smelling of pine and distant ice.

“Stay here,” Mara said. “Don’t touch anything.”

“Especially the blue fan?”

Mara narrowed her eyes. “Especially the blue fan.”

“I remember.”

Before Mara could question her further, the pounding resumed.

She hurried downstairs and opened the front door.

Minister Halden stood outside with four officers. He was tall, silver-haired, and perfectly dry despite the sweat shining on every other face in the street. Behind him, the waiting crowd had grown. Vendors sold paper umbrellas. Children wore rubber boots inherited from grandparents.

“Curator Vale,” Halden said. “May we speak privately?”

“You brought an audience.”

“I brought witnesses to a historic day.”

“You brought armed officers.”

“History is unpredictable.”

Mara let them enter.

Halden removed his gloves and looked around the lobby. He had visited the museum often during Mara’s childhood, when he was still an engineer and her mother was curator. He had taught Mara how to read a barometer. Later, as Minister, he had ordered half the museum’s collection seized for research.

None of it had produced rain.

“We detected an atmospheric disturbance here at eight-oh-three,” he said.

“This is a weather museum.”

“An active disturbance.”

“The basement leaks.”

“It hasn’t rained in eleven years.”

“Then perhaps it leaks dust.”

Halden’s smile was weary. “Your mother used to make the same kind of joke.”

“My mother liked you.”

“Once.”

Before you took her final storm, Mara thought.

The museum had possessed a complete thunderstorm then, stored across seven copper vessels. Halden’s engineers had carried them away. Something went wrong during the experiment. The vessels ruptured, the laboratory burned, and Mara’s mother disappeared inside a room filled with unnatural fog.

No body was found.

Halden approached the reception desk. A circle of moisture marked the wood where June’s jar had stood.

He touched it with one finger.

The officers saw his expression and reached for their weapons.

“At eight-oh-three,” Halden said softly, “every sensor in the city pointed here.”

“And what did they detect?”

“A storm forming backward.”

Mara said nothing.

“Ordinary storms gather energy and then release it. This signal began with the release. The cause followed after, as if the storm were moving against time.”

Outside, the crowd began counting down the hours until four, though there were still seven to go.

Halden looked toward the staircase.

“What did you receive?”

“Nothing.”

The old reception bell rang.

Mara closed her eyes.

Halden’s gaze shifted to the bell. “The same bell rang when your mother brought us the final storm.”

“It rings when the metal expands.”

“In this heat, it should ring constantly.”

A door slammed upstairs.

One officer drew his pistol.

Mara stepped between him and the staircase. “No weapons near the exhibits.”

A cold gust swept through the lobby.

Visitors outside cheered, believing it was the first sign of changing weather.

Then June appeared at the top of the stairs, her red coat flapping in the wind.

She held the blue fan.

Behind her, winter poured from the North Room.

Frost flowered over the railing. Snow swirled through the gallery. The officers backed away as ice spread across the floor, cracking tiles and whitening the reception desk.

“I told you not to touch that,” Mara said.

“You were going to tell me,” June replied.

Halden stared at the girl.

For the first time, his composure broke.

“Elena?”

The name passed through Mara like lightning.

Elena had been her mother’s name.

June descended one step at a time. “Not exactly.”

Halden gripped the railing. “Where did you come from?”

“After.”

“After what?”

“The rain.”

The winter wind died as abruptly as it had begun. Snow settled over the lobby. Outside, people pressed against the windows, astonished.

June handed Mara the fan.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “This is how it happened before.”

Mara felt cold for reasons unrelated to the exhibit.

“Before?”

June nodded. “He came. I opened the North Room. Then you hid the jar, but he found it.”

Everyone looked toward the stairs.

Halden moved first.

Mara swung the blue fan. A blast of northern wind struck him and the officers, driving them backward into the wall. She seized June’s hand and ran upstairs.

They reached the Gallery of Clouds. Mara locked the door, dragged the map table against it, and retrieved the jar from the cabinet.

The cloud inside had doubled in size.

“How do we stop the flood?” Mara asked.

June shook her head. “I don’t know.”

“You said you remembered.”

“I remember the water. I remember you putting me in a boat. I remember the museum breaking apart.”

Below, Halden shouted orders.

Mara held up the jar. “Does this cause the rain?”

“It begins it.”

“Then we keep it sealed.”

“You did. It broke at four anyway.”

“What if we destroy it now?”

“You tried that too.”

Mara studied the girl’s face. “How many times have you done this?”

“Only once.”

“That doesn’t make sense.”

“It hasn’t happened yet for you.”

Something struck the gallery door.

The table jumped.

June spoke quickly. “Tomorrow night, the city is underwater. You take me to the museum roof. You tie your blue thread around my wrist and tell me the three knots are what was, what is, and what might yet be. Then lightning hits the tornado bottle.”

Mara glanced instinctively toward the basement.

“The tornado escapes,” June continued. “It carries me into the storm. I fall through the cloud and land in my room yesterday morning.”

“Your room where?”

“Here.”

“This is my house.”

“It becomes mine.”

The door shook again. Wood splintered near the lock.

Mara looked at the cloud paintings, the cabinets, the labels written in her mother’s careful hand. She had spent eleven years preserving the past so perfectly that the museum had become a tomb for the future.

“What happened to me?” she asked.

June lowered her eyes.

That was answer enough.

The door burst inward. Halden stumbled through with an officer behind him.

“Give me the storm,” he said.

“You can’t control it.”

“We don’t need to control it. We need to distribute it.”

“It will flood the city.”

“The city can be evacuated.”

“By four?”

His silence was brief but unmistakable.

“You knew,” Mara said.

“We calculated a significant risk.”

“You promised them an end to the drought.”

“Because hope prevents panic.”

“And what prevents drowning?”

Halden took another step. “Eleven years ago, we stripped the sky of its ability to recover. Every experiment since then has failed. That cloud may be the atmosphere correcting itself.”

“It’s a storm moving backward through time.”

“It’s rain.”

“To you, perhaps those are the same thing.”

The jar cracked.

A thin black line appeared near its base.

Thunder rolled under Mara’s hands.

Beyond the windows, the white sky darkened to gray.

From the street came a roar of celebration.

It was three minutes to four.

Halden lunged.

June snatched the jar first.

“Tell me what to do!” she cried.

Mara’s mind raced through the exhibits. Wind, pressure, cold, electricity—pieces of systems separated, labeled, and placed behind glass. Her mother had always said weather died when divided. A storm was not rain or thunder or wind. It was all of them moving together.

The crack widened.

A drop fell onto the table.

At once, rain struck the windows.

The crowd screamed with joy.

Mara ran to the wall and tore down the painting of the eastern field. Behind it was a brass lever designed to open every emergency lock in the museum.

Halden understood.

“No,” he said. “Those exhibits are irreplaceable.”

“That’s the problem.”

She pulled the lever.

Throughout the building, bolts slammed open.

The museum inhaled.

First came the winds. They rushed from their rooms and cabinets, circling the gallery in warm and cold currents. Then clouds spilled from frames, swelling against the ceiling. Fog climbed from the basement stairs. Hail rattled through the corridors. Captive lightning flickered inside the walls.

The green tornado bottle rose from its shelf below and shattered.

The museum exploded into weather.

Halden and the officer were thrown against the map table. Windows burst outward. Clouds rolled into the city. Wind lifted years of dust from rooftops and carried it away in golden sheets.

Mara took the jar from June.

The cloud inside beat against the glass like a heart.

“What might yet be,” Mara whispered.

She opened the lid.

The cloud streamed upward, joining all the weather the museum had kept apart. For one breathless moment, a storm filled the entire building—wild, balanced, and alive.

Then it rose through the broken roof.

Outside, the rain changed.

The heavy, violent drops softened. The black clouds stretched across the city instead of piling over it. Wind drove them east toward the dust flats, then south along the dead river. Thunder rolled, no longer trapped or backward, but moving naturally into the distance.

The river did not return that night.

It returned slowly over the next three months.

By then, green shoots covered the eastern fields. People patched roofs, cleared drains, and learned that rain was not a performance scheduled by a ministry. It arrived when it arrived. Sometimes it disappointed. Sometimes it stayed too long. Mostly, it ignored speeches.

The Museum of Forgotten Weather did not survive.

Without weather, it was only a damaged old hotel with empty cases and water-stained walls.

Minister Halden resigned. Before leaving the city, he sent Mara a parcel containing the brass reception bell. She installed it above the front desk.

The hotel reopened in spring.

Travelers came first by road, then, eventually, by riverboat. They left wet umbrellas in the lobby and complained about damp sheets. Children jumped in puddles outside until their parents dragged them in.

June remained.

She chose the small bedroom above the Gallery of Clouds, though the gallery was now a dining room. Mara never learned exactly where June had first been born or how many futures had vanished when the storm changed. Some questions, like clouds, lost their shape when held too tightly.

One evening, nearly a year after the scheduled rain, Mara and June walked to the eastern fields.

The wheat was still young and green. At the field’s edge stood a willow, little more than a sapling, its narrow leaves shining after a shower.

They sat beneath it.

June wore the red coat, though the evening was warm. The blue thread remained around her wrist, tied in three knots.

“Do you remember this?” Mara asked.

June considered the field.

“No,” she said at last.

Mara smiled.

Above them, clouds moved freely across the sky.

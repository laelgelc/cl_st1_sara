The Clockmaker of Bellwether Street

Every clock in Mr. Vale’s shop told a different time.

The tall walnut grandfather clock beside the door insisted it was twenty minutes past three. The silver carriage clock in the window claimed midnight. A row of brass alarm clocks shouted contradictory hours in bright red numerals, while the tiny watch pinned beneath a glass dome had not moved from six forty-two in nearly thirty years.

People often asked Mr. Vale why he did not synchronize them.

“They are not soldiers,” he would reply. “There is no reason for them to march together.”

Then he would bend over his workbench and return to whatever delicate task occupied him: fitting a spring, polishing a gear, or listening with a stethoscope to the private heartbeat of a watch.

Bellwether Street was narrow, crooked, and old enough to remember when horses had outnumbered people. Its buildings leaned over the road like neighbors sharing gossip. At one end stood the railway station; at the other, the gray clock tower of Saint Orison’s Church. Between them were a bakery, a tailor, a bookshop, three houses, and Mr. Vale’s clock shop.

The painted sign above his door read:

VALE & DAUGHTER  
CLOCKS REPAIRED  
TIME RESTORED

There had never been a daughter, as far as anyone knew.

The people of the town had invented explanations. Some said Mr. Vale’s daughter had died young. Others said she had quarreled with him and gone abroad. Mrs. Pembrook, who sold bread two doors down, believed the sign had been left by a previous owner and that Mr. Vale was too lazy to repaint it.

Mr. Vale answered no questions about the matter.

He had lived alone above the shop for forty-one years. Every morning at eight, he opened the blue door. At one, he ate soup. At seven, he closed the shutters. On Sundays, he walked to the river and scattered crumbs for ducks, though he never attended church.

His routine changed on a wet Tuesday in October, when a girl named Iris Bell came into the shop carrying a broken clock.

She was twelve years old, small for her age, and dressed in a yellow raincoat. Water ran from her dark hair onto the floorboards. In her arms she held a round kitchen clock with a cracked white face.

Mr. Vale looked up.

“You are dripping on the nineteenth century,” he said.

Iris glanced at the floor. “Sorry.”

He handed her a cloth.

“Dry yourself, then the nineteenth century.”

She obeyed. After wiping her face and hair, she knelt and rubbed the floorboards until they shone.

Mr. Vale extended one thin hand toward the clock. “Let me see.”

Iris placed it on the counter.

The clock was cheap, made of faded green plastic. Its hour hand hung loose, pointing permanently downward.

“It fell,” she said.

“Clocks seldom fall without assistance.”

“I knocked it off the wall.”

“Then you fell it.”

“Can you fix it?”

Mr. Vale turned the clock over. “Almost anything can be fixed.”

“How much?”

He named a price.

Iris stared at him. “I have three pounds and sixteen pence.”

“That presents a difficulty.”

“I could work for the rest.”

“I do not employ children.”

“You have ‘and Daughter’ on your sign.”

“That is not an advertisement for a vacancy.”

“I can sweep. And clean windows. And make tea.”

“Can you repair a balance wheel?”

“No.”

“Cut a replacement pinion?”

“No.”

“Recognize a French striking movement?”

“No.”

“Then your usefulness is uncertain.”

Iris met his eyes. “I learn quickly.”

Something in the shop clicked. Then something else clicked in reply. Rain whispered against the window.

Mr. Vale pushed the clock back toward her.

“Three pounds and sixteen pence,” he said. “And six Saturdays.”

“Doing what?”

“Discovering whether you learn quickly.”

That was how Iris Bell became the first assistant Mr. Vale had ever permitted behind the counter.

She arrived the following Saturday at seven fifty-five, wearing the same yellow coat. He gave her a broom and instructed her to sweep without touching anything.

This was harder than it sounded. The shop was so crowded that even the dust had to squeeze between objects. Clocks covered every wall. They stood on shelves and hung from hooks. They occupied cabinets, windowsills, and chairs. There were solemn clocks, ridiculous clocks, jeweled clocks, plain clocks, and one carved like a cat whose eyes moved from side to side with each tick.

By ten, Iris had broken nothing.

Mr. Vale regarded this as promising.

He taught her how to open the back of a watch without scratching it. He showed her gears no larger than snowflakes and springs as fine as eyelashes. He explained that clocks did not measure time itself.

“Nothing measures time,” he said. “A clock measures its own movement and invites us to call that time.”

Iris frowned. “Isn’t that the same thing?”

“Is a map the same thing as a country?”

“No.”

“Is a recipe the same thing as a cake?”

“No.”

“Then a clock is not the same thing as time.”

Mr. Vale placed a brass gear in her palm.

“Time is what happens,” he said. “A clock is what watches.”

By the end of her first Saturday, Iris had learned to clean a mainspring. By the end of her second, she could identify six kinds of clock by sound alone. The kitchen clock was repaired during the third week, but she continued coming to the shop.

Neither of them mentioned the original agreement.

At home, the clock returned to the wall above the table. Iris’s father barely noticed.

Daniel Bell had once been a loud, laughing man. He had taught music at the secondary school and played the piano at every town festival. But since Iris’s mother had died two years earlier, he had become quiet. He moved through the house like someone searching for a room that was no longer there.

He no longer played the piano.

Sometimes, after dinner, Iris found him sitting in the dark, looking at nothing.

The broken kitchen clock had belonged to her mother. Its ticking had filled every breakfast, every argument, every hurried morning before school. When Iris knocked it from the wall, the sudden silence had terrified her. She had felt, absurdly but completely, that she had broken the last living piece of her mother.

Now it ticked again.

Her father looked up at it one evening.

“You had it repaired.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

He lowered his gaze to his untouched tea.

Iris wanted him to say more. She wanted him to smile, to tell her that her mother would be pleased, to stand and play something bright on the piano. Instead, the clock ticked above them, measuring the distance between their chairs.

Winter settled over Bellwether Street.

Iris spent every Saturday with Mr. Vale. He remained strict, but his strictness gradually acquired kindness around the edges. He kept ginger biscuits in a tin and pretended not to notice when she ate three. He found a magnifying lens that fit her eye. When she solved the problem in a Victorian mantel clock—a bent tooth in the escape wheel—he nodded once and said, “Adequate,” which she understood to mean excellent.

In December, while searching a cabinet for oil, Iris found a photograph.

It showed Mr. Vale as a young man, though she recognized him only by his sharp nose. Beside him stood a woman with a wide smile. Between them was a little girl in a white dress, holding a wooden clock shaped like a house.

On the back, written in blue ink, were the words: Elianor, Mara, and Rose. Summer, 1958.

Mr. Vale took the photograph from her hand.

“You were told to look for oil.”

“I’m sorry.”

He placed the picture in a drawer.

“Was Rose your daughter?”

His face became still.

“You may go home early today.”

“But—”

“Now, Iris.”

She left without finishing her work.

The following Saturday, she almost did not return. Shame kept her awake the night before, replaying the moment in the cabinet. But at eight, she stood outside the blue door.

Mr. Vale opened it.

For several seconds, neither spoke.

Then he stepped aside.

“There is a regulator clock in the back room,” he said. “Its pendulum suspension is damaged.”

Iris removed her coat.

They worked in silence until noon. At last, while she held the pendulum rod steady, Mr. Vale spoke.

“Rose was my daughter.”

Iris did not move.

“She died when she was nine,” he continued. “Scarlet fever. Quick as a match burning. Her mother survived her by eleven years, though not in any meaningful sense.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Yes. People often are.”

He adjusted a screw with careful fingers.

“Why is her name not on the sign?” Iris asked softly.

“It is.”

She thought of the words above the door.

“And Daughter.”

“The shop was meant to be hers. She liked mechanisms. At five, she dismantled a pocket watch with a kitchen knife. Ruined it entirely.”

He smiled, but only for an instant.

“After she died, I painted the sign. My wife thought it morbid. Perhaps it was. But grief requires a place to live. If you do not give it a room, it occupies the whole house.”

Iris thought of her father sitting in the dark.

“Does it ever go away?”

“No.”

Her heart sank.

Mr. Vale looked at her.

“But it learns manners,” he said. “Sometimes.”

That winter was the coldest in thirty years. The river froze from bank to bank. Snow softened the roofs, buried the tracks, and turned Bellwether Street into a white corridor.

Then, on the first Thursday of February, the clock in Saint Orison’s tower stopped.

It stopped at six forty-two in the morning.

At that same minute, every clock in Mr. Vale’s shop stopped as well.

Mr. Vale was upstairs making tea when the sound vanished.

For forty-one years, he had lived surrounded by ticking. He no longer heard it consciously, just as a sailor does not hear the sea. But when it ceased, the silence struck him like a blow.

He ran downstairs.

Pendulums hung motionless. Second hands rested between marks. The cat clock’s eyes stared to the left. Even clocks disconnected from the tower, clocks powered by batteries, weights, springs, or electricity, had stopped at six forty-two.

All except one.

Under its glass dome, the tiny watch that had shown six forty-two for thirty years began to tick.

Mr. Vale stared at it.

The watch had belonged to Rose.

It had stopped at the hour of her death.

Now its second hand moved.

He lifted the glass dome with trembling hands.

The bell above the shop door rang. Iris rushed in, breathless.

“The church clock—” she began, then saw the shop. “What happened?”

Mr. Vale did not answer.

Rose’s watch ticked in his palm.

Then the minute hand moved forward.

Six forty-three.

“No,” he whispered.

Iris stepped closer. “Mr. Vale?”

He closed his fingers around the watch.

Outside, people gathered in the street. Doors opened. Faces turned toward the silent church tower. An uneasy murmur spread through the town.

The baker entered the shop without knocking snow from her boots.

“Vale, the tower clock has stopped,” Mrs. Pembrook said. “And so has mine. Every one in the bakery. Tom says the station clocks are frozen too.”

Mr. Vale looked around his motionless shop.

“What time is it?” Mrs. Pembrook asked.

No one replied.

The question traveled through Bellwether Street. People checked telephones, but their screens displayed six forty-two. Computers showed the same. At the railway station, the electronic board froze. Radios played music, but no announcer gave the hour. Trains halted outside town because signal systems had failed.

Only the little watch continued.

At seven twelve by Rose’s watch, Mr. Vale climbed the church tower with Iris behind him.

“You should stay below,” he said.

“You’ll need someone small enough to reach inside the movement.”

“I will not.”

“You usually do.”

He lacked time to argue.

The tower stairs spiraled upward through cold darkness. Dust lay thick on the steps. Above them, the giant clock mechanism waited in a wooden chamber behind the faces of the tower. Its gears were green with age, each one larger than a dinner plate. Iron rods stretched toward the four motionless hands outside.

Mr. Vale inspected the escapement.

“Nothing is broken,” he said.

“Then why did it stop?”

“Because something is broken.”

“That doesn’t make sense.”

“It describes most of life.”

He checked the weights, cables, teeth, and bearings. Everything was in order. Yet the pendulum refused to move. When he pushed it, it swung once and stopped as if caught by an invisible hand.

Rose’s watch ticked in his waistcoat pocket.

Iris heard it.

“Why is that watch still working?”

Mr. Vale’s expression warned her away, but she persisted.

“It was already stopped, wasn’t it? Before today.”

He turned toward the mechanism.

“It belonged to Rose.”

“What time did she die?”

The tower chamber seemed to grow colder.

“Six forty-two.”

Iris looked through the dusty glass at the town below. People stood in clusters in the snow. With no clocks to obey, no trains to catch, and no school bells to answer, they waited helplessly.

“What if the watch isn’t measuring time?” she said.

Mr. Vale glanced at her.

“You told me clocks only measure their own movement. Maybe all the other clocks have stopped because time here has stopped. But this watch is measuring something else.”

“What?”

“I don’t know. Her time?”

“Rose has no time.”

“Your grief, then.”

He stiffened.

Iris almost apologized, but did not.

The watch ticked between them.

Mr. Vale removed it from his pocket. It read seven twenty-nine.

“My grief does not control the clocks of Bellwether.”

“How do you know?”

“Because grief is not magic.”

“Neither are clocks stopping all at once.”

Mr. Vale opened the back of the watch. The balance wheel turned smoothly. Beneath it, engraved inside the case, were words he had never seen.

He held the watch closer to the window.

“What does it say?” Iris asked.

He read aloud.

“When you are ready, let the morning continue.”

The handwriting was his wife’s.

Mr. Vale sat heavily on a crate.

For years after Rose’s death, Elianor had repaired clocks beside him. She had been the better craftsperson, patient where he was stubborn, intuitive where he was exact. This watch had stopped on the morning Rose died, and neither of them had touched it again.

Or so he had believed.

Perhaps Elianor had tried to repair it. Perhaps she had succeeded and then fixed it in place, waiting for a day she knew her husband could not yet imagine.

Iris sat beside him.

“What happened at six forty-two?” she asked.

“My wife called me from upstairs. Rose was ill. The doctor had said the fever might break by morning. I was in the shop, working.”

“On what?”

“A music box. A customer wanted it before noon.”

He stared at the turning gears of the little watch.

“Elianor called my name. I said I would come in a moment. I was fitting a pin. A ridiculous piece of metal. I heard her call again.”

He stopped.

“When I went upstairs, Rose was gone.”

“You couldn’t have known.”

“I knew she was ill.”

“You thought there was time.”

“Yes.”

The words broke something open in him.

“For sixty-three years,” he said, “I have woken at six forty-two. Every morning. No alarm. I hear Elianor calling me. Every morning, I think that if I move quickly enough, I might reach the room before the second call.”

Iris looked at him with tears in her eyes.

“But you can’t.”

“No.”

The watch ticked.

Below, the town waited in a morning that refused to advance.

Iris took his hand.

“My father stopped playing the piano when my mother died,” she said. “He says he doesn’t want to, but I think he’s afraid.”

“Of what?”

“That if he makes music, it means the world is allowed to keep going.”

Mr. Vale closed his eyes.

The watch showed seven thirty-seven.

“How do I let the morning continue?” he asked.

Iris considered this.

“Maybe you don’t fix anything.”

He gave a tired laugh. “That is a poor suggestion to make to a clockmaker.”

“Maybe that’s why you need it.”

She pointed to the open watch.

“Let it run.”

Mr. Vale’s fingers tightened around the case.

For decades, he had preserved the hour of Rose’s death beneath glass. He had told himself it was remembrance, but now he understood that it was refusal. He had built his life around a minute and called the prison a memorial.

To let the watch run felt like losing her again.

Then, faintly, he remembered Rose at five years old, laughing over the pieces of the ruined pocket watch. He remembered her hands black with oil. He remembered summer light, Elianor singing, and the three of them eating cherries above the shop.

None of those memories had happened at six forty-two.

He closed the watch and placed it on the mechanism of the tower clock.

“All right,” he whispered.

At once, the giant pendulum swung.

A gear turned.

Then another.

The mechanism shuddered into motion. The minute hand outside lurched forward, and the bell above them struck once.

Across Bellwether Street, clocks awakened.

In kitchens, stations, classrooms, and shops, second hands jumped forward. Telephones flashed. Radios announced the time. The cat clock’s eyes rolled right. The entire town filled with ringing, chiming, buzzing, and ticking.

Every clock resumed at seven thirty-eight.

Rose’s watch continued with them.

Mr. Vale bowed his head.

Iris stood beside him until the bells stopped.

Spring came early that year.

The story of the frozen clocks became a town legend, though no two people told it the same way. Mrs. Pembrook blamed an electrical storm, despite the absence of lightning. The vicar called it a miracle. The stationmaster insisted it had been foreign interference.

Mr. Vale offered no explanation.

He changed in small ways. He began taking lunch outside when the weather was fine. He allowed customers to bargain. Once, he closed the shop on a Wednesday and went to the coast for no reason anyone could discover.

Rose’s watch remained on his workbench, still ticking.

Iris continued her apprenticeship. By fourteen, she could repair a marine chronometer. By sixteen, she could make parts Mr. Vale’s aging hands could no longer manage. Her father sometimes came to collect her, lingering near the door and examining the clocks.

One evening, he noticed an old music box on a shelf.

“Does it work?” he asked.

Mr. Vale looked at Iris.

“It does now,” she said.

She wound it. A slow melody filled the shop.

Daniel Bell listened without moving. Then he went home, opened the piano, and played for the first time in four years.

The music drifted through the walls while Iris sat on the stairs and cried quietly, not from sorrow alone.

Years passed.

When Iris was twenty-three, Mr. Vale brought her outside before opening time. The painted sign above the blue door had been taken down.

“I thought you were having it cleaned,” she said.

“I was.”

A workman across the street turned the sign around.

It now read:

VALE & DAUGHTER  
CLOCKS REPAIRED  
TIME RESTORED

Beneath the old lettering, in smaller gold letters, someone had added:

I. BELL, CLOCKMAKER

Iris stared at it.

“People will think I’m your daughter.”

“People have thought more foolish things.”

She looked at him. His hair was entirely white now, his shoulders bent, but his eyes were clear.

“Is this your way of saying you want me to stay?”

“No. It is my way of admitting you already have.”

She hugged him.

Mr. Vale endured this with the awkward patience of a man waiting for bad weather to pass. But after a moment, he placed one hand gently on her back.

Above them, Saint Orison’s clock began to strike eight.

Along Bellwether Street, shutters opened. The baker set warm loaves in her window. A train whistled at the station. Somewhere, a piano began its morning scales.

Mr. Vale unlocked the blue door.

Inside, the clocks ticked at different speeds and displayed different hours. Some were fast, some slow, and some awaiting repair. Rose’s watch rested beneath its glass dome, no longer frozen at six forty-two.

It kept imperfect time, gaining almost a minute each day.

Mr. Vale never corrected it.

Not every clock needed to be right.

Some only needed to go on.

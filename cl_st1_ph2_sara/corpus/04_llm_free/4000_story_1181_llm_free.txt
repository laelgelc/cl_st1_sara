The Last Train to Bellweather

At 11:47 each night, the last train passed through Bellweather Station without stopping.

It came out of the eastern tunnel as a single silver carriage, its windows glowing amber, its wheels making almost no sound. It slowed as it crossed the platform, though no passenger ever stepped down and no one waiting there was ever permitted to board. Then it entered the western tunnel and vanished beneath the hills.

By midnight, the rails were cold again.

Most people in Bellweather ignored the train. They closed their curtains before it arrived and turned their clocks toward the wall. If visitors asked about it, the townspeople said it was a mail train, an inspection train, or a trick of the mountain fog.

Only the stationmaster watched it every night.

His name was Elias Vale, and he had kept Bellweather Station for forty-two years. He was a tall, narrow man with white hair, careful hands, and a limp he had acquired in a railway accident long ago. At eleven each evening, he swept the platform. At eleven thirty, he lit the green lamp above the ticket office. At eleven forty-five, he stood beside the yellow line, took off his cap, and waited.

As the silver carriage passed, Elias looked through its windows.

Some nights he saw nothing but empty seats.

Some nights he saw strangers.

And some nights he saw the dead.

He never spoke of this. Bellweather had enough ghosts without anyone naming them.

One November evening, a girl arrived at the station carrying a red suitcase.

She could not have been more than twelve. Her dark coat was too large for her, its sleeves covering half her hands, and rain glittered in her black hair. She entered the waiting room at ten minutes past eleven and placed the suitcase carefully between her shoes.

“One ticket, please,” she said.

Elias looked up from the timetable he had been pretending to read.

“There are no more trains tonight.”

“The last one comes at 11:47.”

“That train doesn’t stop.”

“It will for me.”

Her voice was quiet, but there was no uncertainty in it.

Elias removed his spectacles. “What is your name?”

“Mara.”

“Mara what?”

“Just Mara.”

“Where are your parents?”

The girl looked toward the dark window. Rain crawled down the glass in crooked lines.

“My mother is on the train,” she said.

Elias felt something tighten beneath his ribs.

He folded the timetable and set it aside. “Who told you that?”

“I saw her last night.”

“You were here?”

“Across the street.”

The station stood at the edge of town, opposite a shuttered bakery and an abandoned hotel. Elias remembered seeing a small shadow beneath the hotel awning, but shadows were common in Bellweather, especially in November.

“What did you see?” he asked.

“My mother was sitting by the third window. She looked exactly the same as before.”

“Before what?”

“Before she died.”

The station clock ticked loudly.

Elias had heard many people claim to see loved ones on the silver train. Most had been grieving. Some had been drunk. A few had returned night after night until grief hollowed them into quiet, watchful things. The train never stopped for any of them.

“How long ago?” he asked.

“Seven months.”

“I’m sorry.”

“So am I.”

Mara knelt and opened the red suitcase. Inside were a wool scarf, a toothbrush, two apples, a small blue book, and a pair of polished shoes.

“I’ve packed everything,” she said. “I won’t be trouble.”

Elias closed the suitcase.

“You are not getting on that train.”

Her eyes rose to his. They were gray and fierce.

“You don’t decide.”

“I’m the stationmaster.”

“Then make it stop.”

“I can’t.”

“You mean you won’t.”

“I mean no living person can board it.”

Mara stood. “That’s not true.”

“How would you know?”

“Because you did.”

Elias went still.

The rain tapped against the roof. Somewhere in the ticket office, a pipe groaned.

Mara pointed to his left leg.

“You got that limp when you jumped off.”

Elias glanced toward the station doors, though they were locked. The waiting room was empty except for the two of them.

“Who have you been talking to?”

“The old woman who sells candles beside the church.”

“Mrs. Quill talks too much.”

“She said you climbed onto the train when you were young. She said you came back, but part of you didn’t.”

“Mrs. Quill sells stories with her candles. The worse the story, the higher the price.”

“She told me for free.”

“That proves it was worthless.”

Mara gripped the handle of her suitcase. “I’m getting on.”

Elias rose. He was taller than she had expected, and for a moment she seemed very young. Then she lifted her chin.

“No,” he said. “You are going home.”

“I don’t have one.”

“Everyone has somewhere.”

“Not me.”

He softened despite himself. “Where have you been staying?”

“In my aunt’s house.”

“That sounds like somewhere.”

“It’s where I sleep. It isn’t home.”

“Your aunt will be worried.”

“No. She’ll be relieved.”

Elias knew he should telephone the constable. He should keep Mara talking until someone came to collect her. Instead, he looked at the clock.

11:19.

Twenty-eight minutes until the train.

“Sit down,” he said.

“I don’t want to.”

“Then stand, but listen.”

He walked to the ticket window and pulled the wooden shade. Behind it, on a shelf beneath the cash drawer, was a small iron box. He unlocked the box with a key he wore around his neck.

Inside lay a silver ticket.

Its surface was dull with age, and no destination was printed on it. A dark stain marked one corner.

Mara stared at it.

“That’s how you boarded,” she said.

Elias laid the ticket on the counter.

“When I was seventeen, my older brother, Tomas, drowned in the river. He was nineteen. Brave, handsome, stupid. The river flooded that spring, and he bet three boys he could swim from one bank to the other.”

“You saw him on the train.”

“The week after we buried him.”

“Did he call to you?”

“No. He only looked at me.”

“But you wanted to follow.”

Elias smiled without humor. “I thought grief was a door, and if I pushed hard enough, it would open backward.”

Outside, wind sent rain rattling against the station.

“The next night,” he continued, “I came here. There was another stationmaster then, Mr. Croft. He found me waiting and told me to leave. I refused. We argued. When the train came, I ran beside it.”

“And it stopped?”

“No. But one of the doors opened.”

Mara leaned closer.

“Did your brother open it?”

“I never knew. I saw a hand. I took it, and someone pulled me aboard.”

The station seemed colder now. The green lamp above the ticket office flickered.

“What was it like?” Mara whispered.

Elias looked toward the tracks.

“Warm. Warmer than any place I’d ever been. The carriage smelled of bread, cedar smoke, and summer rain. The seats were blue. Outside the windows, there were no hills, no station, no town. Only fields beneath a sky filled with stars.”

“And Tomas?”

“He was there. He stood at the far end of the carriage, smiling.”

Mara’s face brightened with such sudden hope that Elias hated what he had to say next.

“I went to him. But no matter how many steps I took, he remained at the far end. I called his name. He shook his head. I kept walking. Then I began to forget things.”

“What things?”

“My mother’s face. The river. The sound of my own name. They disappeared one by one, and each loss felt like setting down a heavy bag. I wanted to surrender everything. I wanted to become light enough to reach him.”

The clock read 11:26.

“What stopped you?” Mara asked.

“Mr. Croft.”

“The old stationmaster?”

“He had caught hold of my coat as I boarded. He kept one foot on the platform and one inside the carriage. He shouted at me, but I couldn’t hear the words. Finally, he struck me with his lantern.”

Mara blinked.

“He hit you?”

“Hard. Broke my nose. I became angry, and anger reminded me I was alive.”

Elias touched the bridge of his nose.

“I turned back. Mr. Croft pulled me toward the door. Just before I jumped, Tomas spoke.”

“What did he say?”

“He said, ‘Not this way.’”

The waiting room fell silent except for the clock and the rain.

“When I landed, my leg went beneath the wheels,” Elias said. “Mr. Croft dragged me clear before the train took it entirely. The doctors saved the leg, though some winter mornings I wish they hadn’t.”

“And the ticket?”

“It was in my hand when I woke.”

Mara stared at the piece of silver.

“So it does let people board.”

“It lets them leave. That is not the same thing.”

“My mother might tell me to come back.”

“Or you might forget why you should.”

“I wouldn’t forget her.”

“You would be surprised what the train can take.”

Mara looked down at her suitcase.

Elias studied her bowed head. “What happened to your mother?”

“She was sick.”

“For a long time?”

“Three years.”

“And your father?”

“I never knew him.”

“Your aunt took you in?”

“She took the house. I came with it.”

Elias said nothing.

“At first,” Mara went on, “she tried to be kind. She made soup. She bought me that coat. But I remind her of my mother, and she didn’t like my mother. They used to fight.”

“What about?”

“Everything. Money. Grandfather’s house. My father. Me.”

The station clock moved another minute forward.

“My aunt says I’m ungrateful,” Mara said. “Maybe I am. She says she gave up her quiet life for me. She says I look at her as if she stole something. Sometimes she looks at me the same way.”

“That doesn’t mean she wants you gone.”

“Last week I heard her speaking to a boarding school.”

Elias sighed. “A boarding school is not the end of the world.”

“How do you know?”

“I’ve seen the end of the world. It has blue seats.”

Mara almost smiled, but the expression vanished quickly.

11:31.

Elias picked up the silver ticket.

“Do you know why the train shows us the people we’ve lost?”

“To take us to them.”

“That is what we want to believe. But perhaps it only shows us what will make us climb aboard.”

“You think it lies?”

“I think a hook can be shaped like hope.”

Mara’s gaze hardened again. “You got to say goodbye to your brother.”

“Yes.”

“I didn’t.”

The words struck more sharply than accusation.

Elias remembered Tomas on the riverbank, laughing as he stripped off his shirt. He remembered watching his brother enter the brown water. He remembered shouting when Tomas vanished, and then waiting for the sleek dark head to rise again.

For forty-two years Elias had told himself that the train had given him a farewell. He had rarely considered the cruelty of possessing one.

“What was the last thing you said to your mother?” he asked.

Mara’s hands tightened around the suitcase handle.

“I don’t remember.”

“Try.”

“She was in bed. My aunt had gone to fetch the doctor. Mother asked me to open the window.”

“Did you?”

“No. It was cold.”

“What happened then?”

“She said she wanted to smell the rain. I told her she was being foolish. She asked again. I shouted at her.”

Mara’s voice began to shake.

“I said I was tired of doing everything for her. I said I wished she would either get better or…” She stopped.

“Or what?”

Mara looked at him with wet, furious eyes. “You know.”

Elias did know.

“Then I left,” she whispered. “I went to the kitchen. When I came back, she was dead.”

The rain continued outside, patient and soft.

“So I have to tell her I didn’t mean it,” Mara said.

“You were frightened.”

“I still said it.”

“You were tired.”

“I said it.”

“You loved her.”

“She died thinking I hated her.”

“No.”

“You don’t know.”

“No,” Elias said. “But neither do you.”

Mara wiped her face with her sleeve.

The station clock read 11:36.

Eleven minutes.

Elias returned the silver ticket to the iron box.

Mara moved quickly. She darted behind the counter, seized the ticket before he could close the lid, and ran for the platform doors.

Elias caught her coat. She twisted, kicked his bad leg, and broke free. Pain flashed from his knee to his hip. By the time he straightened, she had unlocked the door and stumbled into the rain.

“Mara!”

She ran to the edge of the platform. Wind pulled at her coat and hair. She held the silver ticket in one raised hand as if signaling a ship.

Elias limped after her.

“Give it back.”

“No.”

“You don’t know what you’re doing.”

“Yes, I do.”

“You’re throwing away your life.”

“It’s mine.”

“No life belongs only to the person living it.”

“Mine does!”

“No. That is the burden of being loved.”

Her face crumpled, but she did not lower the ticket.

The eastern tunnel lay beyond the wet rails, black and perfectly round. Elias could feel the train before he heard it: a faint trembling under his shoes, like the station remembering an old fear.

11:43.

“Mara,” he said, “step away from the edge.”

“Will the ticket make it stop?”

“I don’t know.”

“Will it open the door?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then how do I use it?”

He came closer. “You don’t.”

She backed away along the platform.

At 11:44, a warm wind blew out of the eastern tunnel. It carried the smell of cedar smoke and summer rain.

Mara inhaled sharply.

“Do you smell that?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“That was her favorite soap. Cedar.”

“It shows you what you want.”

“I don’t care.”

At 11:45, a silver light appeared in the tunnel.

Elias remembered standing on the same platform at seventeen, heart bursting with terror and longing. He had believed love meant refusing to remain in a world without his brother.

It had taken him decades to understand that grief often disguised itself as loyalty.

“Mara, listen to me. If your mother is on that train, she won’t want you there.”

“You don’t know her.”

“No. But I know the dead ask nothing of us. It’s the living who need things.”

“Nobody needs me.”

“I do.”

The words surprised them both.

Mara stared at him.

The silver light grew brighter.

Elias stepped forward, rain running down his face. “I need someone to help me sweep the platform. My leg hurts in bad weather.”

“You’re lying.”

“I need someone to check the lamps, because my eyes aren’t what they were.”

“You didn’t know me an hour ago.”

“I need someone to remember this station when I’m gone.”

“You’re just trying to stop me.”

“Of course I am.”

At 11:46, the train emerged.

It was brighter than Elias remembered. Its polished sides reflected the platform, the lamps, and the rain, but not the two figures standing beside the tracks.

The carriage slowed.

Amber windows passed one by one.

In the first were empty blue seats.

In the second sat a man holding a violin.

In the third, a woman looked out.

Mara cried, “Mother!”

She ran.

Elias caught the back of her coat. The red suitcase fell over, spilling its contents across the platform. One apple rolled beneath a bench. The blue book landed open in a puddle.

The woman inside the train rose.

She was thin and pale, with dark hair like Mara’s. She pressed both hands to the glass.

Mara struggled against Elias.

“Let me go!”

The woman’s mouth moved.

The train made no sound, but Mara answered as if she had heard.

“I’m sorry! I’m sorry!”

A door slid open.

Warm golden light spilled onto the platform.

Beyond the doorway waited a row of blue seats and a narrow aisle that seemed to stretch farther than the carriage could contain. At the end stood Mara’s mother.

She was no longer thin. Her face was full and healthy. She wore a green dress, and her arms were open.

Mara stopped struggling.

“Please,” she whispered.

Elias’s grip weakened.

For one terrible moment, he wanted to release her.

He wanted to believe the train was merciful. He wanted to believe Mara could step into that warm carriage, embrace her mother, say the words that had haunted her, and then come home.

But the train had never been merciful. Mercy did not ask a child to choose between love and morning.

Elias took the silver ticket from Mara’s hand.

The carriage lights flickered.

Her mother’s expression changed. She was still smiling, but now sorrow filled her face. She lowered her arms and placed one hand over her heart.

Then she pointed—not into the carriage, but toward the town.

Mara shook her head.

Her mother pointed again.

The church bell began to strike midnight, though the station clock still showed 11:47.

One note.

The wind rose.

Two.

Elias stepped toward the open door.

“Take the ticket,” he told the train.

He threw it inside.

The silver ticket landed in the aisle.

At once, the carriage groaned like a tree splitting in winter. The open door began to close.

Mara lunged forward.

“Mother!”

The woman in green pressed her palm to the glass.

Mara pressed hers against the window from the platform. For a heartbeat, their hands matched with only a thin pane between them.

Her mother spoke three silent words.

Then the train moved.

Mara ran beside it. Elias ran too, though pain tore through his leg. The amber windows slid past, faster and faster, until the final red lamp disappeared into the western tunnel.

Darkness returned.

Mara stood at the end of the platform, shaking.

“What did she say?” Elias asked.

The girl sank to her knees.

Elias reached her as the station clock finally began to strike midnight.

Mara looked up at him.

“She said, ‘Open the window.’”

For a while, neither of them moved.

Then Elias collected the things that had fallen from the suitcase. He found the scarf beneath a bench, wiped the blue book on his sleeve, and retrieved both apples. Mara watched without speaking.

When he finished, he sat beside her on the wet platform.

“Is the ticket gone forever?” she asked.

“I hope so.”

“Will the train come tomorrow?”

“I don’t know.”

It did not.

Nor did it come the night after that.

For the first time in memory, the rails remained silent at 11:47. The people of Bellweather noticed, though they pretended not to. Curtains stayed open. Clocks faced forward. Mrs. Quill, who sold candles beside the church, closed her shop for three days and told anyone who asked that she had a cold.

Mara’s aunt arrived at the station at dawn on the first morning. She came without a coat, her hair unpinned, her face white with fear. When she saw Mara asleep in the waiting room, wrapped in Elias’s stationmaster jacket, she began to cry.

Mara woke and looked at her.

For a moment, neither moved.

Then her aunt crossed the room and held her. Mara remained stiff in her arms, but she did not pull away.

There were apologies after that, though not all at once. There were arguments too. The boarding school was discussed and rejected. A smaller house was found because the old one held too many memories and cost too much to heat. Mara’s aunt learned not to call sacrifice a gift if she intended to demand payment for it. Mara learned that adults could be frightened, selfish, and loving at the same time.

Neither lesson was easy.

Every Saturday, Mara came to the station.

At first, she said she was only there to return Elias’s jacket. The next week, she came to collect her blue book, though it was already in her suitcase. After that, she stopped making excuses.

Elias taught her how to read timetables, polish brass, change signal lamps, and make tea strong enough to wake a sleeping porter. Mara swept the platform in bad weather. In spring, she painted the waiting-room walls. In summer, she planted red geraniums in boxes beneath the windows.

Years passed.

Trains continued to stop at Bellweather: green trains, black trains, crowded morning trains, and sleepy evening trains. They carried students, soldiers, salesmen, musicians, brides, runaways, and people returning home after swearing they never would.

The silver train did not return.

When Elias grew too old to work, Mara took his place. On his final evening as stationmaster, she found him standing beside the yellow line at 11:45, his cap in his hands.

“Waiting for something?” she asked.

“No,” he said.

But she stood beside him anyway.

At 11:47, a warm wind came from the eastern tunnel.

The rails began to sing.

Mara felt Elias reach for her hand.

A silver light appeared in the darkness.

The train emerged slowly, its windows glowing amber. In the third window sat a young man with Elias’s eyes and a laughing face. Tomas raised one hand.

Elias smiled.

The carriage slowed, but no door opened.

“Are you afraid?” Mara asked.

“No.”

“Do you want to go?”

Elias thought for a long moment.

“Someday,” he said. “But not this way.”

The silver train passed into the western tunnel and vanished.

Beside Mara, Elias let out a breath he seemed to have been holding for most of his life.

He died the following winter, at home, with the window open to the rain.

After the funeral, Mara returned alone to Bellweather Station. On the ticket counter she found an iron box she had never seen before.

Inside lay a silver ticket.

This one bore a destination.

HOME, it said.

Mara carried it to the platform at 11:47. She waited until the rails began to tremble and amber light filled the eastern tunnel.

Then, when the silver train appeared, she tore the ticket in half.

The train passed without stopping.

From one of its windows, Elias tipped his cap.

Mara lifted her hand.

“Goodbye,” she said.

And because goodbye was not the same as abandonment, she turned toward the waiting room, where lamps needed lighting, floors needed sweeping, and the first train of morning would soon arrive.

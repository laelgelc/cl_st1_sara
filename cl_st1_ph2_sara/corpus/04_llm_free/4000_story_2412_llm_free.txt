Mara first heard the mountain breathe three nights after her father’s funeral.

She was sleeping in the room where she had grown up, beneath a sloping roof and a faded map of the northern constellations. The old house creaked in the wind, as it had for more than a century, but this sound did not come from the walls.

It rose through the floorboards.

A slow inhalation trembled beneath the bed. The windowpanes hummed. Then came a long, deep exhalation, as if something enormous had sighed under the earth.

Mara sat up and listened.

Silence followed.

On the bedside table, her father’s brass watch ticked steadily. He had carried it every day for forty-two years, though its hour hand ran seven minutes slow. When Mara was a child, she had believed that those seven minutes belonged to him alone, a secret pocket of time in which he could hide whenever the world became difficult.

Now the watch belonged to her.

She took it in her hand and waited for the mountain to breathe again.

It did not.

By morning, Mara had almost convinced herself that the sound had been part of a dream. Grief played tricks on the senses. It put familiar voices in empty rooms and made shadows pause in doorways. She had spent the previous week sorting through her father’s life: boots with cracked soles, jars of bent nails, notebooks filled with weather records, shirts that still held the smell of woodsmoke. Perhaps her mind had invented the breathing because it could not bear the stillness he had left behind.

Outside, the village of Bellweather lay under the first snow of winter. Thirty stone houses clustered around a narrow road, their chimneys releasing pale ribbons of smoke. Above them rose Mount Aster, white and immense, its summit hidden by clouds.

Mara had returned to Bellweather only for the funeral. She had intended to leave the next morning, taking the noon train south to the city where she had an apartment, a demanding job restoring old books, and friends who knew better than to ask why she never visited home.

But at breakfast she found an envelope under her father’s chair.

Her name was written on it in his precise hand.

Inside was a single sheet of paper.

Mara,

If you hear the mountain breathe, go to the observatory.

Take the watch.

Trust Ilyan, even when he lies.

Forgive me if you can.

—Father

Mara read the note three times.

Then she looked toward the mountain.

High on its eastern shoulder, almost invisible against the snow, stood the abandoned observatory.

Her father had worked there when she was young. Officially, he had been the caretaker, responsible for keeping the road clear and the machinery oiled after the astronomers left. Unofficially, as he used to tell her, he was “the guardian of everything people had forgotten to look at.”

The observatory had closed twenty years ago, after an avalanche damaged the main telescope. Mara remembered the night it happened. She had been twelve. Her father had come home before dawn with blood on his coat and refused to explain where it came from.

Her mother disappeared two days later.

Not died. Not left.

Disappeared.

The villagers searched the forests and ravines for weeks. No body was found. Her father said little, and his silence grew between him and Mara until it became a wall neither of them knew how to climb.

At nineteen, she had gone to the city. At thirty-four, she returned to bury him.

Now he was asking her to climb the mountain.

By nine o’clock, Mara had packed food, water, rope, a lantern, and the brass watch. She found her father’s heavy coat hanging beside the back door. It was too large, but warm.

Before leaving, she crossed the road to Ilyan’s workshop.

The sign above the entrance read:

ILYÁN VOSS  
CLOCKS, LOCKS, AND OTHER STUBBORN THINGS

Ilyan was standing at his bench, examining the open body of a wooden cuckoo clock. He had been Mara’s closest friend when they were children and the only person from Bellweather who had written to her after she left. At first, she had answered his letters. Then less often. Eventually not at all.

He looked older now, of course. His black hair was threaded with silver, and a burn scar crossed his left palm. But his expression was unchanged: calm, amused, and slightly suspicious, as if the world were a puzzle badly assembled by someone else.

“You heard it,” he said.

Mara stopped inside the doorway.

“Heard what?”

“The mountain.”

“You could at least pretend not to know.”

“I could,” he said. “But your father told you to trust me even when I lie.”

She stared at him. “How did you know that?”

Ilyan fitted a tiny brass gear into the clock. “Because I wrote the note.”

The room seemed to contract around her.

“What?”

“Your father dictated it before he died. His hands were too weak to write. I copied it in his handwriting.”

Mara pulled the envelope from her pocket and threw it onto the bench.

“You forged a message from a dead man?”

“From a dying man. There’s an important moral distinction.”

“There is absolutely no moral distinction.”

“He said you wouldn’t come if the handwriting was mine.”

“He was right.”

“Yes.”

Mara turned toward the door.

Behind her, Ilyan said, “Your mother is in the observatory.”

She stopped.

Snow blew in through the open doorway and melted on the floor.

Mara did not turn around.

“That is a cruel thing to say.”

“I know.”

“She’s dead.”

“No.”

“She vanished twenty-two years ago.”

“Yes.”

“You expect me to believe she has been living in a ruined observatory for twenty-two years?”

“No.” Ilyan put down his tools. “I expect you to believe something much worse.”

The mountain breathed again.

This time, the sound rolled through the village. Roofs shuddered. Snow slid from the eaves. Somewhere, a dog began barking.

On Ilyan’s shelves, a hundred clocks stopped at once.

Every pendulum froze.

Every ticking mechanism fell silent.

Mara’s father’s watch continued to move.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Ilyan looked at it.

“We don’t have much time,” he said.

They began climbing before noon.

The old observatory road wound through pine forests above the village, but winter had swallowed it. In places, the snow reached Mara’s knees. Ilyan led, carrying a red canvas pack and a long iron staff. He walked with the confidence of someone who knew exactly where danger lay.

For the first hour, neither of them spoke.

Mara concentrated on breathing. The cold sharpened the air until every inhalation hurt. Beneath the trees, the world was blue and gray, stripped of birdsong. She kept remembering childhood winters when she and Ilyan had climbed these slopes with wooden sleds. Her father would wait at the bottom, waving a lantern.

Back then, the mountain had seemed smaller.

“Why didn’t you tell me?” she asked at last.

Ilyan did not look back. “Your father made me promise.”

“My father is dead.”

“The promise isn’t.”

“You wrote letters for fifteen years.”

“Sixteen.”

“You could have said something.”

“And what would I have written? Dear Mara, I hope the city is pleasant. Your mother exists outside ordinary time beneath an abandoned telescope. Please visit soon.”

“That would have been better than nothing.”

He stopped walking.

Snow clung to his eyebrows and shoulders.

“I asked you to come home,” he said. “Again and again. You stopped answering.”

The accusation landed because it was true.

Mara looked away. Through the trees, she could see Bellweather far below, no larger than a handful of stones.

“Tell me now,” she said.

Ilyan continued climbing.

“The observatory wasn’t built to study stars,” he said. “Not originally. The first structure on the mountain was a monastery. The monks believed certain places held echoes—not echoes of sound, but of moments. Joy, terror, death. Events strong enough to leave an impression on the world.”

“Ghosts.”

“No. A ghost is supposed to be a person. These were pieces of time.”

Mara nearly laughed, but the dead clocks in the workshop had unsettled her.

Ilyan went on. “The monks built a chamber inside the mountain. They called it the Hour Well. Centuries later, astronomers found the ruins and built the observatory over them. They thought the chamber was a natural cavern with unusual magnetic properties.”

“And my father?”

“He discovered the truth.”

A gust of wind swept loose snow across the path.

“When?” Mara asked.

“The night of the avalanche.”

They reached the tree line. Above them, Mount Aster rose into cloud. The observatory stood half a mile away, its silver dome blackened with rust. One side of the building had collapsed, exposing broken beams and stone.

Ilyan pointed to a dark seam running across the snowfield.

“The slope has cracked. Step where I step.”

They crossed carefully.

The mountain inhaled beneath them.

The crack widened with a sound like tearing cloth.

Mara fell forward. Ilyan seized her arm as the snow behind her collapsed into darkness. For one terrible second, her boots hung over the opening. She saw blue ice descending farther than the daylight reached.

Then Ilyan pulled her onto solid ground.

They lay in the snow, gasping.

“That,” Mara said, “would have been useful to mention.”

“I said step where I step.”

“You also said my dead mother is waiting in an observatory.”

“I said she was in the observatory. I never said she was waiting.”

Before Mara could demand an explanation, a low rumble passed through the slope.

They ran.

The observatory’s main doors had been torn away years ago. Snow covered the entrance hall, drifting around shattered furniture. On the walls hung photographs of astronomers standing beside instruments, their faces faded into pale blurs.

Mara remembered visiting her father here. She remembered the smell of machine oil and coffee, the spiral staircase, the way he lifted her so she could look through the telescope.

She also remembered her mother standing beneath the great dome on the last afternoon Mara ever saw her. Her red scarf had moved in the wind.

For years, Mara had forgotten that detail.

Now it returned with painful clarity.

Ilyan crossed the ruined hall and opened a narrow metal door.

Behind it, stairs descended into darkness.

“How often have you been here?” Mara asked.

“Every month for twenty-two years.”

She raised the lantern. “Why?”

“To wind the mechanism.”

“What mechanism?”

“You’ll see.”

The stairs spiraled downward through bare rock. At first, the walls showed marks left by drills and chisels. Deeper down, they became smooth, almost polished.

The air grew warmer.

After several minutes, they reached a circular chamber. Its ceiling vanished into darkness. In the center stood a machine of brass rings, glass tubes, iron weights, and hundreds of interlocking gears. It filled most of the cavern.

Every part of it was moving.

Chains rose and fell. Pendulums swung. Wheels turned within wheels. Blue light pulsed through the glass tubes like captured lightning.

At the machine’s heart stood a narrow door.

Mara approached it.

The brass watch in her pocket began ticking faster.

“What is this?”

“The monks built the first version,” Ilyan said. “The astronomers added to it. Your father completed it.”

“Completed what?”

“A lock.”

The mountain exhaled. Dust fell from the ceiling. The gears stuttered, then resumed.

Ilyan removed his pack and began taking out tools.

“The Hour Well is behind that door. Your mother is inside.”

Mara stared at him. “Open it.”

“I can’t.”

“Then why did you bring me here?”

“Because you can.”

He pointed to the center of the door. There was a small circular hollow, exactly the size of her father’s watch.

Mara drew it from her pocket.

“No,” Ilyan said quickly. “Not yet.”

Her fingers closed around the watch.

“You are going to explain everything. No riddles. No monks, no echoes. Tell me what happened to my mother.”

Ilyan looked at the moving machinery, as if searching for the beginning of a story.

“The night of the avalanche, your parents were both here. They had discovered that the Hour Well was becoming unstable. Moments trapped inside it were leaking out.”

“What does that mean?”

“People saw things. A shepherd met himself as an old man. A woman entered her kitchen and found it had burned down, though the fire did not happen until three weeks later. Your father spent an hour speaking with his own grandfather, who had been dead for sixty years.”

Mara said nothing.

“The avalanche damaged the lock,” Ilyan continued. “Your parents repaired it, but someone had to enter the Well and seal the door from inside. Your mother volunteered.”

“No.”

“She believed they could bring her back within days.”

“Why didn’t they?”

“Because time inside the Well doesn’t move in one direction. Opening the door might release everything trapped there—past events, possible futures, lives that happened and lives that almost happened. Your father spent the next twenty-two years trying to find a safe way to open it.”

“And did he?”

Ilyan glanced at the watch. “He believed so.”

Mara remembered her father’s notebooks, all those columns of temperatures, dates, moon phases, and numbers she had mistaken for weather records.

“Why now?”

“The lock is failing. The mountain isn’t breathing. The Well is. If the mechanism breaks, the door will open on its own.”

“And what happens then?”

“Bellweather will be the first place swallowed. After that, I don’t know.”

“Swallowed by what?”

“Every moment at once.”

A sharp metallic crack rang through the chamber.

One of the chains snapped. An iron weight crashed to the floor, missing Ilyan by inches. The machine lurched. Several gears began spinning wildly.

Ilyan grabbed his tools.

“Your father calculated that the watch would create a stable path into the Well. Seven minutes.”

Mara looked down at it.

“Why seven?”

“Because the watch isn’t slow. He designed it that way. It stores seven minutes each day.”

“That makes no sense.”

“I know. He tried to explain the mathematics to me for twelve years.”

“And if I put it in the door?”

“You’ll have seven minutes to enter, find your mother, and return.”

“Twenty-two years for seven minutes?”

“Inside, it may feel like seconds. Or a lifetime.”

“And you?”

“I stay here and keep the machine running.”

The blue light flickered.

For an instant, Mara saw a child standing beside the machinery.

The girl was twelve years old, with tangled brown hair and a green wool coat. Young Mara stared at her with furious, frightened eyes.

Then she vanished.

Mara stumbled backward.

“What was that?”

“A leak,” Ilyan said. “We have to do this now.”

He climbed onto the machine and forced a loose gear back into alignment. Sparks scattered around him.

Mara stood before the brass door.

All her life she had imagined meeting her mother again. In some versions, Mara wept and embraced her. In others, she turned away. Sometimes she demanded an explanation. Sometimes she said nothing at all.

She had never imagined she would have seven minutes.

Mara pressed the watch into the hollow.

The door opened.

There was no room beyond it.

There was a summer sky.

She stepped through.

Warm grass brushed her knees. Sunlight touched her face. The observatory stood whole on the hill above her, its dome shining. Bells rang in the distant village.

A woman in a red scarf stood with her back to Mara.

“Mother?”

The woman turned.

Elena Vale was thirty-six years old, the same age she had been when she disappeared. Her dark hair was tied at the neck. There was a cut across her forehead, and blood stained one sleeve.

For a moment, neither of them moved.

“Mara?” Elena whispered.

Mara had prepared a lifetime of words. None of them came.

Her mother crossed the grass and reached for her, then stopped.

“You’re older.”

“You’re not.”

Elena looked toward the observatory. “How long?”

“Twenty-two years.”

The color left her face.

“No,” she said. “The door closed a minute ago.”

“Not for us.”

Behind Mara, the brass doorway stood upright in the field. Through it, she could see Ilyan struggling with the machine.

The watch ticked.

Elena covered her mouth. Tears filled her eyes, but did not fall.

“Your father?”

“He died last week.”

Elena shut her eyes.

Mara felt the old anger rise in her, fierce and familiar.

“You left us.”

“I tried to save you.”

“You left me with a man who couldn’t speak your name.”

“He promised he would explain.”

“He didn’t.”

Elena looked toward the doorway. “That sounds like him.”

The simple bitterness in her voice broke something open between them.

Mara laughed once, painfully. “He spent his whole life trying to bring you back.”

“I knew he would.”

“You sound proud of him.”

“I’m furious with him.”

“So am I.”

The grass changed beneath their feet.

Suddenly they stood in Mara’s childhood kitchen at night. Her father sat at the table, younger and exhausted, holding Elena’s red scarf to his face. Twelve-year-old Mara watched from the hallway.

The scene vanished.

Now they were in a city apartment. Mara, aged twenty-five, opened a letter from Ilyan, read it, and placed it unanswered in a drawer.

Then came the observatory. Her parents arguing beneath the telescope. Her mother shouting that there was no time. Her father begging her not to go.

Moments flashed around them faster and faster.

Mara saw herself old and gray.

She saw Bellweather buried under ice.

She saw Ilyan kissing her on the workshop roof in a summer that had never happened.

She saw her father alive, waiting at the bottom of a snowy hill with a lantern.

The world shook.

Through the brass doorway, Ilyan shouted, “Four minutes!”

Elena grabbed Mara’s hand.

“I can’t leave.”

Mara stared at her. “What?”

“I sealed the lock from inside. I’m part of it now.”

“No. The watch makes a path.”

“For one person.”

The sky split from horizon to horizon. Behind the blue appeared a darkness filled with countless stars and countless versions of the same mountain.

Mara tightened her grip on her mother’s hand.

“I didn’t come here to leave you again.”

“You came here to close the door.”

“I came because Father told me to.”

“No.” Elena touched Mara’s cheek. “You stopped doing what your father told you years ago.”

The grass disappeared.

They stood on a black shore beside a silver sea. Every wave carried voices.

Mara heard her father calling her name.

Not as he had near the end, when illness weakened him, but as he had in her childhood—strong, impatient, alive.

He stood knee-deep in the silver water.

“Mara,” he said. “I’m sorry.”

She stepped toward him.

Elena pulled her back.

“That isn’t him.”

“It knows what he would say.”

“That doesn’t make it him.”

Her father held out his hand. Behind him appeared a hundred memories: birthdays, winter mornings, quiet dinners, slammed doors. All the moments she had spent with him and all the moments they had wasted.

Mara wanted those seven stolen minutes. She wanted to hide inside them with the dead.

The watch ticked louder.

Three minutes.

She turned to Elena. “There must be a way.”

“There is.”

Mara understood from her mother’s face.

“No.”

“You return. I close the path.”

“No.”

“It’s what I chose before.”

“You don’t get to choose it twice.”

Elena smiled sadly. “You’re exactly as stubborn as I hoped.”

The silver sea began to rise.

Through the doorway, alarms screamed. Blue fire poured from the machine. Ilyan had one hand inside a nest of gears, holding something in place.

“Mara!” he shouted. “Two minutes!”

She looked at the doorway, then at her mother.

Twenty-two years of grief stood between them. There was no time to cross it. No time to forgive, accuse, understand, or become strangers properly.

Only time to choose.

Mara took the watch from the door.

The brass doorway began to close.

“What are you doing?” Elena cried.

Mara pressed the watch into her mother’s hand.

“Seven minutes stored each day for forty-two years,” she said. “That’s more than seven minutes.”

Understanding dawned in Elena’s eyes.

The watch had never been meant merely to open a path.

It was a reservoir.

Elena snapped open the back. Inside, where ordinary gears should have been, golden light burned.

The silver sea stopped rising.

Every voice fell silent.

“How?” Elena whispered.

“Father.”

Together they turned the small winding key.

The watch released forty-two years.

Time poured out.

Mara saw her father building the mechanism in secret, year after year. Saw him at the kitchen table, recording calculations until sunrise. Saw Ilyan beside him, first as a teenager, then as a man. Saw her father write letters to Mara and burn them. Saw him practice apologies in empty rooms.

At the end, she saw him place the watch in Ilyan’s hand.

If there is enough time, he said, bring them both home.

The golden light expanded across the shore.

Elena seized Mara’s hand and ran.

They crossed the threshold together as the world behind them shattered into birdsong, starlight, summer rain, and snow.

Ilyan pulled them through.

“Close it!” he shouted.

Elena tore the watch from the door. The brass rings slammed shut.

The machine stopped.

For one breathless instant, the cavern was completely still.

Then the floor cracked.

“Run,” Ilyan said.

They climbed as the chamber collapsed behind them. The spiral stairs twisted under their feet. Stone fell from the ceiling. Mara reached the entrance hall first and turned back as Ilyan emerged, supporting Elena.

Outside, dawn shone over the mountain.

They had entered the observatory in the afternoon.

Below them, Bellweather rested beneath fresh snow. Chimneys smoked. Bells rang.

The mountain released one final sigh.

Then it was silent.

Elena stood in the snow, staring at the village.

“My house,” she said.

“Still there,” Mara replied.

“The pear tree?”

“Dead.”

“Mrs. Orrin?”

“Ninety-three and still cruel.”

“The bakery?”

“Burned down. Rebuilt. Burned down again.”

Elena laughed. The sound became a sob.

Mara took her hand.

Ilyan sat on a broken stone wall, wrapping cloth around his injured fingers. “I feel I should warn you,” he said, “that the village may find this situation complicated.”

Elena looked at him. “You’re little Ilyan Voss.”

“I was.”

“You broke our window with a turnip.”

“It was an experiment.”

“You said it was an accident.”

“I lied.”

Mara began to laugh.

She laughed until her ribs hurt, until tears froze on her cheeks. Elena laughed with her, and after a moment, even Ilyan joined them.

They descended slowly.

Halfway to Bellweather, Mara found the brass watch in her coat pocket. Its glass was cracked, its golden light gone.

The hands pointed to the correct time.

When they reached the first houses, doors opened. Villagers stepped into the road. Some crossed themselves. Some called Elena’s name. Old Mrs. Orrin, wrapped in three black shawls, walked forward and slapped her.

Then she embraced her.

News traveled faster than explanation. By noon, half the village had gathered outside Mara’s house. Everyone wanted to ask questions. No one knew which question should come first.

Mara left Elena among them and went inside.

The rooms no longer felt empty.

On the kitchen table lay her train ticket, still valid for that afternoon. She picked it up, carried it to the stove, and held it over the flame.

Then she changed her mind.

She folded the ticket and put it in her pocket.

Staying, she realized, meant little if one believed there was no longer anywhere else to go. She would return to the city eventually. She would finish the book waiting on her workbench. She would show her mother electric trains, glass towers, and grocery stores open at midnight. She would answer Ilyan’s old letters, perhaps one at a time. She would decide later whether to forgive her father.

There would be time for all of it.

Outside, Elena stood beneath the bare pear tree, listening as three villagers argued over what year it was.

Ilyan leaned against the gate.

“You’re going back to the city,” he said when Mara joined him.

“For a while.”

He nodded.

“You could come with me.”

“I dislike cities.”

“You’ve never visited one.”

“I dislike them in principle.”

Mara looked at his bandaged hand. “Was there really a summer when you kissed me on the workshop roof?”

Ilyan’s expression remained calm, but his ears turned red.

“You saw that?”

“I saw many things.”

“It was only a possible future.”

“Was it?”

“Yes.”

“Are you lying?”

He glanced at her.

“Yes.”

The village clocks began to strike noon.

Some were fast, some slow, and some rang thirteen times, but together they filled the valley with music.

Mara took her father’s broken watch from her pocket.

For a moment, she thought she felt it tick.

Just once.

A final second, given freely.

Then it was still.

The town of Bellweather had not heard its clock strike in nineteen years.

It stood in the center of the market square, a narrow tower of gray stone with four pale faces and a copper roof turned green by rain. Ivy climbed halfway up its walls. Pigeons nested behind the Roman numerals. The hands remained fixed at eleven minutes past four, though no one could remember whether that had been morning or afternoon.

Once, the clock had governed everything.

Bakers lit their ovens at the first chime. Children ran home at the sixth. The last evening bell warned travelers that the eastern gate would soon close. Farmers in the surrounding hills set their watches by it, and newly married couples kissed beneath its shadow at noon for luck.

Then, one autumn night, the clock stopped.

The clockmaker, Elias Vale, went up into the tower to repair it. He never came down.

Search parties found his coat, his tools, and a cold cup of tea beside the mechanism. There was no sign of the man himself. The tower had one door and no windows wide enough for a person to pass through. After a week, the mayor ordered the clock chamber locked. After a year, people stopped speaking of Elias Vale. After nineteen years, only the oldest townspeople still glanced up when crossing the square.

Mara Vale did not glance up.

She looked directly.

Every morning, on her way to the railway office, she stopped beneath the eastern face and studied the motionless hands. She had inherited her father’s dark eyes and narrow fingers, though she remembered little else about him. A low voice humming over a workbench. The smell of oil and oranges. A silver watch held to her ear.

“Listen,” he had told her. “Every clock has a heart, but not every heart keeps the same time.”

She had been six when he disappeared.

Now she was twenty-five, and Bellweather considered her habit of staring at the tower an eccentricity best ignored.

At the railway office, Mara sold tickets, stamped parcels, and wrote delays on a blackboard. The trains came twice each day, carrying letters from the capital and passengers who never stayed long. She liked the timetable because it did not pretend to be mysterious. Eight seventeen meant eight seventeen. A train was early, late, or on time. There were no vanished fathers inside it.

On a wet Monday in November, the morning train brought a package addressed to her.

The parcel was small, wrapped in brown paper, and tied with red thread. No sender’s name appeared on it. The postmark was blurred, but the date was clear.

It had been mailed the following Thursday.

Mara checked the station clock. She checked the date on the newspaper. She checked the postmark again.

Thursday, 14 November.

Today was Monday, 11 November.

The parcel gave a faint tick.

Mara carried it into the storeroom and locked the door. Inside the paper was a wooden box. Inside the box lay a brass pocket watch, badly scratched, with a crack across its glass.

She knew it immediately.

Her father had carried it in his left waistcoat pocket. On the back, beneath years of tarnish, were the engraved initials E.V.

The watch was running backward.

Mara sat very still.

Beneath the watch lay a folded strip of paper. The writing was hurried, but unmistakable.

Mara,

When the tower strikes, do not let them open the eastern gate.

Trust the boy who remembers tomorrow.

Father

She read the note until the words lost their shape.

Then someone knocked on the storeroom door.

“Mara?” called Mr. Pell, the stationmaster. “There’s a young gentleman asking for you.”

She put the watch in her pocket and came out.

The young gentleman was neither a gentleman nor, in Mara’s first opinion, particularly trustworthy. He was perhaps seventeen, thin as a matchstick, with muddy boots and a torn blue coat. Wet black hair hung over his forehead. He stood near the ticket window, examining the railway timetable as if it had personally offended him.

When he saw Mara, his face changed with relief.

“You got the watch,” he said.

Mr. Pell looked from the boy to Mara.

“My cousin,” Mara said quickly.

The boy nodded. “Her cousin.”

“I thought your cousins lived in the north,” said Mr. Pell.

“We are an adventurous family.”

Mara seized the boy’s sleeve and pulled him outside.

Rain silvered the tracks. The morning train gave a whistle and rolled away, leaving them alone beneath the station awning.

“Who are you?” Mara demanded.

“Jonas Rill.”

“How do you know about the watch?”

“You showed it to me tomorrow.”

Mara released his sleeve.

Jonas sighed. “That usually goes badly.”

“What does?”

“The first explanation.”

He reached into his coat and produced a sheet of railway-office paper. Mara recognized her own handwriting at once.

Give Jonas the tower key. Believe him sooner this time.

Below the message, she had signed her name.

Mara turned the page over. “Where did you get this?”

“From you. Tomorrow afternoon. Or yesterday afternoon, depending on which way you fall through the hour.”

She looked toward town. Beyond the roofs, the silent clock tower pierced the rain.

“I’m going back inside,” she said.

“At noon tomorrow, the tower starts again.”

She stopped.

“It strikes thirteen times,” Jonas continued. “On the thirteenth strike, everyone in Bellweather loses an hour.”

“Loses?”

“They vanish from it. The streets empty. Fires go out. Soup stays warm on abandoned tables. Then the hour repeats, and no one remembers except me.”

“Why you?”

“I don’t know. Maybe because I wasn’t born here. Maybe because I was beneath the tower when it happened. Maybe because time has poor taste.”

Mara folded her arms. “And my father?”

Jonas glanced at the pocket containing the watch.

“He’s inside the missing hour.”

For nineteen years Mara had imagined many answers. Her father had fled Bellweather. He had been murdered. He had climbed through some hidden opening in the tower and died in the river. Each possibility had hurt differently, but all of them belonged to the ordinary world.

This answer did not.

“That makes no sense,” she said.

“No,” Jonas replied. “But it’s true.”

He told her that he had first arrived in Bellweather three days ago—though he had lived those three days more than thirty times. Each cycle began when the clock struck thirteen and ended an hour later. At the end, the town returned to noon, forgetting everything. Jonas alone remembered.

In some cycles he had fled. The eastern gate would not open.

In others he had climbed the tower. He had found no one in the clock chamber, only a great brass mechanism turning without weight or pendulum.

Once, he had met Mara there. Together they had discovered a narrow staircase hidden behind the clock face. Before they could descend it, the hour reset.

In another cycle, Mara had given him the tower key. In another, she had written the note he now carried. In another, they had reached a circular room where shadows moved against the walls though nothing cast them.

“And tomorrow?” Mara asked.

“Tomorrow will be the thirty-seventh time for me.”

“Why send the watch today?”

“I didn’t. Your father did.”

The rain had weakened to a mist.

Mara took out the brass watch. Its hands revolved steadily backward.

“If this came from Thursday,” she said, “then whatever happens tomorrow doesn’t end tomorrow.”

“That,” said Jonas, “is what worries me.”

The key to the tower was kept in the mayor’s office inside a glass-fronted cabinet. It was long, black, and ceremonial, made in a time when people believed a large key proved a lock important.

Mara had no intention of asking for it.

At ten that night, she and Jonas entered the town hall through the coal cellar. Jonas knew which steps creaked. He knew the night watchman would leave his desk at fourteen minutes past ten to investigate a cat in the alley.

“You’ve done this before,” Mara whispered.

“Eleven times.”

“And the cat?”

“Very reliable.”

They took the key and crossed the deserted square.

Up close, the clock tower looked less abandoned than asleep. The ivy shivered in the wind. Rainwater ran from the mouths of stone lions over the doorway.

Mara fitted the key into the lock.

For one moment, she was six years old again, standing in the square while neighbors called her father’s name.

Then she turned it.

The door opened inward.

The tower smelled of dust, iron, and something unexpectedly sweet.

Oranges.

Mara gripped the lantern harder.

A spiral stair climbed along the inner wall. They passed the storage room where festival banners had once been kept, then the bell chamber, where twelve bronze bells hung beneath green beams. At the top they found the clock mechanism.

The gears were motionless.

Mara raised the lantern. The largest wheel stood twice her height, its teeth furred with rust. Chains hung loose. The pendulum rested against the wall.

“It was moving tomorrow,” Jonas said quietly.

On a table lay Elias Vale’s tools and a teacup filmed with dust. His dark coat still hung from the back of a chair.

Mara touched its sleeve.

Nineteen years collapsed into a single breath.

She searched the pockets. Empty. She examined the floor, the walls, the narrow gaps behind the clock faces. Jonas watched without speaking.

At last Mara found a shallow drawer beneath the worktable. Inside was a leather-bound notebook.

Most of the pages contained ordinary repair records. Replaced western minute hand. Adjusted bell lever. Cleaned escapement. Near the end, however, Elias’s writing grew cramped and feverish.

October 3: Clock gaining seven seconds after midnight. Cause unknown.

October 9: Heard thirteenth bell during maintenance. No corresponding movement in mechanism.

October 12: Found additional gear behind eastern assembly. Metal unfamiliar.

October 15: The clock is not gaining time. It is collecting it.

Mara turned the page.

Every day, seconds are lost in Bellweather. A pause before a name is remembered. A dream forgotten upon waking. A final word left unsaid. The mechanism gathers these fragments. It has done so for centuries, perhaps since the tower was built.

When enough time accumulates, the hidden hour opens.

I believe someone designed it to do this.

The final entry was dated the night he disappeared.

The hidden gear cannot be removed. I will attempt to stop the clock before the hour fully opens. If I fail, the town must not be sealed. The eastern gate predates the tower. Its iron contains fragments from the original bell. It may provide an escape for the gathered time.

Mara read the sentence again.

“The gate,” she said. “Father wanted it opened.”

“But his note said not to let them open it.”

“The note said, ‘When the tower strikes, do not let them open the eastern gate.’”

“That sounds fairly clear.”

“It sounds urgent. Not clear.”

A noise came from inside the wall.

Tick.

The brass watch in Mara’s pocket answered.

Tick.

Though the tower gears remained still, a thin golden light appeared behind the eastern clock face. It traced the outline of an extra gear, almost invisible between two larger wheels.

Jonas backed away. “That didn’t happen before.”

The hidden gear turned one tooth.

Somewhere below, a bell gave a soft, muffled groan.

Mara opened her father’s notebook to the final page. There, pressed so deeply into the paper it had nearly torn through, was one more line.

The hour does not wish to remain hidden.

The next morning dawned bright and windless.

Mara and Jonas spent the early hours examining the eastern gate. It stood at the end of a steep cobbled street between two round watchtowers. The gate itself was oak reinforced with black iron, wide enough for wagons and old enough to have survived three wars.

Above it hung a small bell.

“The original bell,” Mara said.

At eleven thirty, the mayor arrived with two constables.

Mr. Pell had discovered the missing office paper. The night watchman had remembered the cellar door. Bellweather was not a large town; secrets rarely lasted until lunch.

Mayor Thorne was a square woman with silver hair and no patience for explanations involving stolen keys. Mara tried anyway.

To her surprise, the mayor did not laugh.

When Mara showed her Elias’s notebook, the older woman’s face became pale.

“You knew,” Mara said.

“I knew your father feared the clock.”

“You locked the tower.”

“After he vanished, we all heard noises from it. Chimes at impossible hours. Voices in the walls.” Mayor Thorne looked toward the square. “We thought silence would starve whatever was inside.”

“You were wrong.”

The first bell struck at noon.

The sound rolled over Bellweather like thunder.

Birds erupted from the rooftops. Windows shook. People stepped from shops and houses, staring toward the tower.

The second bell struck.

Then the third.

With each note, color drained from the sky. Shadows lengthened in several directions at once. The clock hands, frozen for nineteen years, began to move.

Four.

Five.

“Open the gate,” Mara said.

Mayor Thorne gestured to the constables. They lifted the locking bar.

Six.

The gate would not move.

Seven.

Men and women rushed to help. They pulled at the iron handles, but the oak doors seemed fused to the stones.

Eight.

The air filled with whispers. Mara heard voices speaking unfinished sentences, forgotten apologies, names never said aloud.

Nine.

Jonas staggered. “This is different.”

Ten.

The streets flickered. For an instant they were crowded; then empty; then crowded again.

Eleven.

The brass watch burned against Mara’s chest. She pulled it out. Its backward-moving hands had stopped at eleven minutes past four.

Twelve.

Silence fell.

The thirteenth strike was not loud. It was almost gentle.

Bellweather vanished.

Mara stood beside Jonas in an empty town.

The gate remained closed. Sunlight hung motionless in the air. A fallen leaf paused above the ground. In the bakery window, steam had frozen over a loaf of bread.

Jonas exhaled. “Now we have an hour.”

“No,” said a voice behind them. “You have less.”

Elias Vale stood beneath the gate bell.

He looked exactly as Mara remembered him: tall and stooped, with rolled sleeves and a pencil behind one ear. Not nineteen years older. Not a day older.

Mara could not move.

His eyes filled with tears. “My little star.”

She struck him.

The slap echoed along the empty street.

Elias touched his cheek.

“Nineteen years,” Mara said. “You don’t get to call me that.”

“I know.”

“I thought you left.”

“I tried to come back.”

“You could have sent word.”

“I had no way to send anything until the hour widened. Even then, I could only reach backward a few days.”

She wanted to strike him again. Instead she embraced him so fiercely that the pocket watch pressed between them.

For a while, the impossible town waited.

Then Jonas said, “I’m sorry, but you mentioned we had less than an hour.”

Elias stepped back. “The hidden hour has grown unstable. When it ends today, it will not reset. It will replace the ordinary hour. Bellweather will remain outside time.”

“How do we stop it?” Mara asked.

“Open the gate.”

“Your note said not to let them.”

“The people. When the bells strike, no living hand can open it. The hour defends itself through anyone caught in the final chime.”

“Then who can?”

Elias looked at the stationary leaf, the silent houses.

“Someone the hour has already taken.”

He climbed the ladder to the small gate bell. Only then did Mara see the mechanism behind it: a narrow slot in the stone, shaped like a keyhole.

Elias took the backward-running watch from her.

“This is the key,” he said. “I made it from the hidden gear, piece by piece. When placed in the gate mechanism, it will release all the stolen time.”

“And you?”

He smiled, and Mara hated him for making it gentle.

“I am stolen time.”

“No.”

“Mara.”

“No. We find another way.”

“I looked for nineteen years.”

“You said it was an hour.”

“Inside it, an hour can be very long.”

The emptiness in his eyes told her how long.

Jonas stared down the silent street. “Something’s coming.”

Shadows poured from windows and doorways. They gathered into figures—hundreds of them, thin and trembling, their mouths open in whispers. Mara recognized faces among them: a woman who had died when Mara was ten, a child who had lived only a day, old Mr. Winter from the mill. Lost moments. Last breaths. Forgotten dreams.

They moved toward the gate.

“The hour won’t let me open it,” Elias said.

Mara seized the watch. “Then I’ll do it.”

“If you turn the key, it may take you instead.”

“May?”

“I never tested it.”

“Clockmakers,” Jonas muttered.

The shadows rushed forward.

Mara climbed the ladder. Elias and Jonas braced it as dark hands reached from the crowd. Cold passed through Mara’s boots. She felt memories loosening inside her: her mother’s face, her childhood room, the sound of her father humming.

She pushed the watch into the slot.

It fit.

Below, Elias shouted, but the words stretched and broke apart. Time thickened around Mara. She saw every possible turn of her hand.

In one, the gate opened and she vanished.

In another, the mechanism shattered.

In a third, Bellweather remained forever beneath a motionless sun.

Then she remembered her father’s first lesson.

Every clock has a heart, but not every heart keeps the same time.

Mara did not turn the watch forward.

She turned it backward.

The small bell rang.

The eastern gate burst open.

Wind tore through Bellweather. The shadows rose like smoke, carrying laughter, cries, lullabies, arguments, and final words. Clocks throughout the town spun wildly. The frozen leaf touched the ground.

Mara fell from the ladder.

Her father caught her.

For a moment they stood together in the gateway as years rushed past them. Elias’s hair turned silver. Lines spread across his face. His hands trembled. Nineteen missing years reclaimed him in seconds.

Then the tower clock began to strike again.

One.

Elias smiled.

Two.

His body became transparent in the sunlight.

Three.

Mara clung to him. “Stay.”

“I am trying.”

Four.

“I have questions.”

“I know.”

Five.

“I’m still angry.”

“You should be.”

Six.

“You owe me nineteen birthdays.”

“At least.”

Seven.

The tower’s shadow crossed the gate.

Eight.

Elias kissed her forehead.

Nine.

“Listen,” he whispered.

Ten.

His heartbeat sounded like a watch beneath her ear.

Eleven.

Then it stopped.

Twelve.

The last of him scattered into the morning air, carrying the scent of oil and oranges.

The streets filled again.

People stumbled where they stood, each holding a faint memory of somewhere they had been. Mayor Thorne found Mara kneeling beneath the open gate. Jonas lay nearby, laughing because, for the first time in thirty-seven tomorrows, the clock had passed noon.

The tower did not strike thirteen.

In the months that followed, Bellweather became famous for its restored clock. Visitors came from distant cities to hear its bells. They complained that it lost seven seconds every midnight, but Mara refused to correct it.

She left the railway office and reopened her father’s workshop near the square. Jonas became her apprentice, though he remained careless with springs and claimed to remember repairs before she taught him.

Above the workbench hung the brass pocket watch. It no longer ran backward. In fact, it did not run at all.

Years passed.

Bellweather changed. Children grew tall. Shops changed owners. The eastern gate was never closed again.

Every morning Mara crossed the square and looked directly at the clock.

Sometimes, at eleven minutes past four, the pocket watch in her workshop gave a single tick.

Whenever she heard it, Mara stopped what she was doing.

She listened until the silence returned.

Then she smiled and went on with her day.

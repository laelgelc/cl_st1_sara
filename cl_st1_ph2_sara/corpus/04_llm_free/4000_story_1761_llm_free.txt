# The Cartographer of Lost Roads

On the morning the road disappeared, Mara Venn was repairing a map of the northern marshes.

The map had arrived rolled inside a copper tube, its edges softened by damp and its ink blurred into violet clouds. Mara bent over it beneath the high windows of the Royal Archive, teasing apart river, footpath, and boundary line with a brush made from three white hairs. Around her, ladders leaned against shelves crowded with centuries. Dust drifted through shafts of sunlight. Somewhere in the building, a clock struck nine.

Then the doors burst open.

A young messenger stumbled inside, breathing hard. Mud covered his boots to the knees, and a torn red scarf flapped from one shoulder.

“Master Cartographer,” he gasped.

Mara did not look up. “Mistress.”

“Mistress Cartographer. The western road is gone.”

That made her lift the brush.

“Washed out?”

“Gone.”

“Collapsed?”

“No, ma’am. It isn’t there.”

Mara placed the brush across the inkstone. “Roads do not simply vanish.”

The messenger swallowed. “This one has.”

His name was Lio, and he had been sent from Bellweather, a village two days west of the capital. He told his story in the archive kitchen while Mara packed bread, measuring cord, charcoal, and a compass into her leather case.

At dawn, Lio had left Bellweather carrying letters for the capital. He knew every bend of the western road: the stone bridge over the Nale, the hill crowned by three pines, the abandoned tollhouse with its blue door. But after an hour’s walk, the road ended in a field of silver grass.

Not crumbled. Not overgrown. Ended.

Behind him, it still led east to Bellweather. Ahead lay only grass stretching to the horizon.

He had searched north and south, certain he had missed a turning. At last, he had found a shepherd’s trail and followed it until it joined the King’s Highway near noon. The detour had taken six hours.

“Did you see any mile markers?” Mara asked.

“None.”

“Wagons? Wheel tracks?”

“Nothing.”

“Ruins?”

“No.”

Mara tightened the buckle on her case. “Show me.”

They left the capital before midday.

For the first several hours, the western road behaved as roads were expected to behave. It passed between barley fields, descended through a beech wood, and crossed the Nale on an old stone bridge. Mara stopped often to compare the landscape with her official survey.

“The bridge is six feet farther south than the map says,” Lio observed.

“The river moved.”

“Rivers can do that?”

“Everything moves. Maps merely persuade us otherwise.”

By evening they reached an inn called the Sleeping Fox. Over stew, the innkeeper laughed when Lio spoke of the vanished road.

“Too much fog,” he said. “Boy wandered off.”

“There was no fog,” Lio protested.

“Then too much ale.”

“I don’t drink.”

“A more dangerous condition.”

But the laughter faded when Mara unrolled the western survey. A thin red line marked the road from the capital through Bellweather and onward to the coast. Between the Nale and Bellweather, someone had drawn a small black cross.

Mara frowned. “Was this here before?”

The innkeeper leaned close. “Looks fresh.”

She touched the mark. The ink came away on her fingertip.

That night, Mara slept badly. She dreamed of roads uncoiling like ribbons and slipping into the sea. When she woke, the black cross had vanished from the map, leaving behind a pinprick in the paper.

The next day they continued west.

Near noon, the road left the shelter of the hills. The land opened into a broad plain where silver grass bent under the wind. Lio stopped so abruptly that Mara nearly walked into him.

“There,” he said.

The road ended at their feet.

Its final stones formed a clean line, as though cut by an enormous knife. Beyond them, the grass stood waist-high and unbroken. No ditch, no milestone, no trace of traffic disturbed the plain.

Mara knelt.

The last paving stone was old granite, polished by centuries of wheels. She scraped the soil beyond it and found tangled roots beneath the surface. The grass had not recently invaded. It had been growing there for years.

“That’s impossible,” Lio whispered.

Mara took out her compass. The needle spun once, trembled, and pointed west.

She began to walk.

Lio followed, pushing through the grass. “Bellweather is east of here.”

“I know.”

“So where are we going?”

“To find where the road went.”

They continued west until the sun hung low. Mara measured the ground, studied the horizon, and checked her position against the hills. According to the map, they should have crossed the western road twice as it curved around a shallow valley. They found nothing.

Then Lio called out.

Half hidden beneath the silver grass stood a wooden signpost. Its arms were gray with age, but the lettering remained clear.

EAST: BELLWEATHER — 12 MILES  
WEST: ORISON — 8 MILES

“Orison?” Lio said. “I’ve never heard of it.”

Mara had.

Years before, as an apprentice, she had discovered the name in a coastal ledger. Orison had once been a town known for glassmaking and bells. It appeared in tax records for eighty years, then vanished from every account. No plague was recorded. No fire, flood, or war explained its disappearance. Later maps showed only empty farmland.

Her master had dismissed it as a clerk’s mistake.

Mara touched the signpost. The wood was cold.

“Orison was real,” she said.

“Was?”

A faint sound crossed the field.

A bell.

It rang once, distant and deep.

The grass ahead leaned away from them. Not with the wind, but in two long, parallel lines. Beneath the bending stalks, pale stones emerged one after another, revealing a road that had not existed a moment before.

Lio stepped back.

Mara stepped forward.

“Mistress Cartographer,” he said carefully, “I don’t think we should follow a road that appears out of nowhere.”

“I have spent my life following lines drawn by dead men,” Mara replied. “This is not so different.”

“It feels different.”

The bell sounded again.

They followed the road.

Twilight gathered as the silver grass gave way to hedges. The hedges rose high on either side, thick with white flowers. Small houses appeared beyond them, their windows glowing amber. Smoke curled from chimneys. A dog barked, and a woman called it home.

The road descended toward a town built around a square.

Orison looked neither ruined nor abandoned. People hurried beneath strings of lanterns. Shopkeepers carried baskets inside. Children chased one another around a dry fountain. Above the rooftops stood a bell tower of greenish glass.

No one seemed surprised by Mara and Lio until an old woman dropped a basket of apples.

She stared at Mara’s case.

“You’re a mapmaker,” she said.

Mara nodded.

The old woman crossed herself. “Go to the tower.”

Before Mara could ask why, the woman hurried away, leaving apples rolling across the stones.

At the tower door, they were met by a tall man in a dark coat. His white hair was tied at the neck, and one of his eyes was clouded.

“My name is Iven Rusk,” he said. “I keep the bell.”

“Mara Venn. Royal Cartographer.”

“I know.”

He looked past her toward the road, as if expecting someone else.

“You had better come in.”

The interior of the tower smelled of metal and rain. A spiral staircase wound upward around a hollow shaft. At the center hung a glass bell, enormous and colorless, suspended by black chains. Though the evening was still, the bell swayed slightly.

Iven led them to a room crowded with maps.

Some were drawn on parchment, some on silk, some on scraps of wallpaper. All depicted Orison and the country around it. In several, the town stood beside the western road. In others, it lay in a forest, on an island, or at the edge of a desert. One map showed Orison alone in a blank white space.

“What happened here?” Mara asked.

Iven poured three cups of bitter tea.

“We were erased.”

“By whom?”

“A cartographer.”

Mara almost laughed, but his expression stopped her.

Eighty-seven years earlier, Iven explained, King Ardin had ordered a new western highway. The shortest route passed through Orison’s orchards and required the demolition of half the town. The people protested. Surveyors were driven away. A royal cartographer named Edric Vale arrived to settle the dispute.

Vale carried a legendary instrument called the First Compass, said to point not north, but toward the truest version of the world.

“He stood in this square,” Iven said, “and offered us a choice. Accept the road, or be removed from it.”

“And you chose removal?”

“We thought he meant the road would go around us.”

Vale redrew the royal map. With one stroke, he moved the highway east and left Orison outside its line. But the First Compass gave maps power over more than travelers. As copies of the new survey spread, Orison faded from memory. Roads stopped reaching it. Rivers changed course. Visitors became rare, then ceased altogether.

“Why didn’t the people leave?” Lio asked.

“Some tried. They walked until nightfall and found themselves entering Orison from the other side. Others crossed the fields and vanished. Only the bell keeps us connected to the world. Glass remembers every shape reflected in it, and our bell remembers the land as it was.”

Mara looked up through the open doorway at the enormous bell.

“Then why has the western road disappeared now?”

Iven unfolded a map.

It was a copy of Mara’s official survey.

A dark stain spread across its center. The red line of the western road faded as it approached Bellweather, then resumed near the coast. In the empty space between, Orison was drawn in delicate gray ink.

“The bell is breaking,” Iven said. “When it breaks, Orison will disappear completely. But first, the land around us will be pulled loose. Roads, houses, perhaps entire villages. The world is trying to decide which map is true.”

Lio went pale. “Bellweather?”

“Likely the first to go.”

Mara studied the map. “Where is Vale’s original?”

“At the top of the tower.”

“And the First Compass?”

“With it.”

They climbed.

The staircase narrowed as it wound around the bell. Through the glass, Mara saw distorted fragments of the town: roofs curving like waves, windows floating upside down, lanterns stretched into golden threads.

Halfway up, she noticed a crack.

It began near the bell’s rim and climbed like a bolt of lightning. With each gust of wind, the crack widened by a hair.

At the top of the tower, they entered a circular chamber open to the night sky. A stone table stood in its center. Upon it lay a map beneath a sheet of glass. Beside the map rested a tarnished brass compass.

Mara approached slowly.

Vale’s map was beautiful. Every hill was shaded, every river illuminated with blue and gold. The western highway cut boldly across the page. Orison appeared as a cluster of miniature roofs, crossed through with a single black line.

She lifted the First Compass.

Its needle spun, pointing first north, then down toward the map, then directly at Mara.

“What does that mean?” Lio asked.

“Perhaps nothing.”

“Comforting.”

Mara placed her official survey beside Vale’s map. The two disagreed in dozens of small ways. Forests stood where the older map showed farms. Rivers bent differently. Towns had expanded, vanished, or changed names.

“Can you restore Orison?” Iven asked.

“If Vale erased it by changing the map, perhaps.”

“Then draw us back.”

Mara uncapped her ink.

Below them, the bell rang.

The tower shook. A sharp note filled the chamber, so powerful that the map table shuddered. In the distance, the lanterns of Orison flickered.

Mara set her pen to the official survey and drew a road from Bellweather toward Orison.

The ink vanished into the paper.

She tried again. The new line appeared for an instant, then curled away like smoke.

“The compass,” Iven said. “Use it.”

Mara placed the instrument on the map. The needle pointed toward Orison, then swung east toward Bellweather, then west toward the coast. Three directions. Three claims.

“What is the truest world?” Lio asked.

Mara suddenly understood.

“There isn’t one.”

She thought of the Nale moving its bridge six feet south. She thought of old maps, each certain and each wrong. She thought of borders redrawn after wars, villages renamed for kings, footpaths omitted because no noble traveled them. Maps did not preserve the world. They selected it.

Vale had believed truth could be fixed with ink. That was his mistake.

The bell rang again.

The crack split upward. A fragment of glass broke away and fell silently into the tower.

“We are out of time,” Iven said.

Mara turned Vale’s map over.

The back was blank.

“What are you doing?” he demanded.

“Making room.”

She placed the First Compass in the center of the empty page. The needle spun wildly. Instead of forcing it still, Mara dipped her pen and drew its movement: a circle.

From the circle, she drew four roads leading north, south, east, and west. She added Orison, then Bellweather, then every hamlet and farm she knew. She marked the paths shepherds used, the tracks made by charcoal burners, the seasonal routes across frozen marshes. Where she was uncertain, she left dotted lines. Where the land might change, she wrote: Here the road chooses with the traveler.

The compass needle slowed.

Iven stared at the map. “That is not a proper survey.”

“No,” Mara said. “It is an invitation.”

A sound like thunder split the air.

The glass bell shattered.

Thousands of fragments fell inside the tower, but none struck the floor. They hung suspended, catching the moonlight. In each shard, Mara saw a different landscape.

Orison in ruins.

Orison beside the sea.

Orison surrounded by orchards.

An empty field.

A highway.

A forest.

Then the fragments rushed upward like a flock of bright birds and vanished into the night.

Silence followed.

Mara looked down at the new map.

The compass needle pointed north.

From below came the rumble of wheels.

At dawn, they walked out of Orison along a broad road lined with white flowers. Farmers passed in carts, complaining about the price of onions. A merchant asked the distance to Bellweather. Two children carrying fishing poles argued about whether the river lay east or south.

After three miles, the road forked.

One branch ran east toward Bellweather. Another curved toward the capital. A third, absent from every map Mara had ever seen, wandered north between low green hills.

A wooden sign stood at the crossing.

ORISON — 3 MILES  
BELLWEATHER — 9 MILES  
THE CAPITAL — 41 MILES  
ELSEWHERE — AS NEEDED

Lio read the final line twice.

“Did you write that?”

“No.”

They reached Bellweather by noon. The village had not disappeared, though everyone remembered a strange night. Doors had opened onto unfamiliar streets. Wells had reflected stars no one recognized. One old man claimed a glass city had floated above his cabbage patch until sunrise.

The western road returned over the following days, but not exactly as before. In places it split into several routes. Some travelers reached the coast in half the usual time. Others found themselves passing through villages they had never heard of, where people spoke with forgotten accents and accepted coins bearing the faces of dead queens.

The Royal Council summoned Mara.

“You will correct the western survey,” commanded Lord Haver, Minister of Roads. He was a narrow man who disliked uncertainty. “One road, clearly marked. The kingdom cannot operate on riddles.”

“The kingdom has always operated on riddles,” Mara said. “We simply printed them in red ink.”

She unrolled her new map across the council table.

It showed all the roads she knew, along with blank spaces for those she did not. Orison stood west of Bellweather, rendered in gray and gold. Around it, pathways branched like the veins of a leaf.

Lord Haver’s face reddened. “This is useless. Which road is the official road?”

“All of them.”

“A road cannot be in several places.”

“Neither can a river, yet it branches.”

“People will get lost.”

“They already do.”

The council dismissed her from royal service before lunch.

Mara left the palace carrying her leather case, the new map, and the First Compass. She expected to feel afraid. Instead, she felt lighter than she had in years.

Lio waited outside the gates.

“I heard,” he said.

“News travels quickly on official roads.”

“What will you do?”

Mara opened the compass. The needle pointed north, then west, then toward a narrow alley between a bakery and a wall. She had passed that alley a hundred times. Today, sunlight shone at its far end, though the buildings should have blocked it.

“I think,” she said, “I will make a more honest map.”

They entered the alley.

It led them out of the capital and onto a hillside covered with blue flowers. Below lay a lake shaped like a crescent moon. Beyond it stood mountains neither of them had ever seen.

Mara sat on a warm stone and took out a fresh sheet of paper.

“What is this place called?” Lio asked.

“I don’t know.”

“How can you put it on the map?”

Mara handed him the pen.

“We ask.”

Near the lake, a figure in a yellow coat raised one arm in greeting. A path appeared through the flowers, narrow at first, then widening beneath their feet.

Mara drew it exactly as it was.

Not straight. Not permanent. Not alone.

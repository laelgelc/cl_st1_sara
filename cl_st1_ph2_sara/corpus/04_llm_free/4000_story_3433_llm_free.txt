## The Last Lightkeeper

On the morning the sea disappeared, Mara Vale was polishing the brass lens of the lighthouse.

She noticed the silence first.

For twenty-three years, the sea had surrounded Saint Orra’s Rock with a voice that entered everything: the walls, the windows, the iron stairs, even Mara’s sleep. Some days it whispered over the shingle. Some days it struck the cliffs like a great hand testing a locked door. It was never absent.

That morning, Mara heard only the dry scrape of cloth against brass.

She stopped polishing.

Far below, gulls circled in confusion. Beyond them, where the western sea should have stretched to the horizon, lay a plain of dark, glistening mud. Channels wound through it like veins. Fish twisted in shallow pools, and the wrecks of ships—some new, some ancient—leaned from the exposed seabed.

The water had retreated beyond sight.

Mara watched from the lantern room until her tea went cold.

Then the telephone rang.

The sound came from two floors below, sharp and mechanical. Since the mainland cable had failed eleven years earlier, the telephone had served no purpose. Mara kept it because her father had kept it, and because removing anything from the lighthouse felt like pulling out a tooth.

It rang again.

She descended the spiral staircase.

The black telephone waited on the wall beside the weather log. Dust trembled on its bell.

Mara lifted the receiver.

“Saint Orra Light,” she said.

For a moment, she heard only static. Then a man spoke.

“Is this Mara?”

She gripped the receiver.

“Yes.”

His breath crackled across the impossible distance.

“Mara, listen carefully. The sea will return at sunset.”

“Who is this?”

“You need to leave the rock before then.”

“Who is this?”

A pause.

Then the voice said, “It’s Daniel.”

Mara nearly dropped the receiver.

Her brother had been dead for thirty-one years.

Daniel had been seventeen when he took their father’s boat out during a summer storm. Only one oar had returned, washed into a cove three miles north. They had never found the boat or Daniel’s body.

Mara pressed the receiver hard against her ear.

“This isn’t funny.”

“No,” the voice said. “It never was.”

She closed her eyes.

Memory was a dangerous thing in the lighthouse. Sounds traveled strangely through its stone passages, and more than once Mara had believed she heard her father coughing in the kitchen or her mother humming near the coal room. But those were fragments, echoes made by wind and loneliness.

This voice was different.

It had Daniel’s slight hesitation before each sentence, as if he had to ask permission from the words.

“You died,” Mara whispered.

“I know.”

“Where are you?”

“Near enough to call. Too far to come home.”

She looked through the narrow window above the telephone. The exposed seabed shone under a pale sun. Something moved in the mud—a long shape dragging itself toward a distant pool.

“What happened to the sea?” she asked.

“It has gone to gather what it lost.”

“That doesn’t mean anything.”

“It will.”

The line crackled.

“Mara, there’s a ship in the mud southeast of the lighthouse. You’ll see three masts. Go to it.”

“You said I should leave.”

“You must leave, but first you have to open the captain’s safe.”

“Why?”

“Because there’s a letter inside.”

The impatience rose in her then, hot and welcome.

“I don’t know who you are, but my brother is dead, and I won’t have his voice used for some cruel—”

“When I was twelve,” the man interrupted, “I stole Mum’s blue hair ribbon and tied it around the neck of that injured fox. You helped me hide it in the net shed. It bit your thumb. You told Dad you cut yourself opening a tin.”

Mara stared at the old white scar below her knuckle.

“No one knew that,” she said.

“You knew.”

“You could have heard it from me.”

“But you’ve never told anyone.”

She had not.

Outside, the gulls screamed over the emptied world.

“Daniel,” she said, and the name broke in the middle.

“I’m sorry, Mare.”

She slid down the wall until she was sitting on the cold floor, the receiver cord stretched above her.

“I waited,” she said.

“I know.”

“For years.”

“I know.”

“Dad kept the lamp burning every night, even after they built the automatic beacon. He thought you might see it.”

“I did see it.”

She covered her mouth.

“Where were you?”

But static rushed over his answer. His voice faded, returned, then faded again.

“Daniel?”

“Go to the ship,” he said. “The safe is in the captain’s cabin. The combination is three, one, seven, nine. Take the letter and climb to the old abbey on the mainland.”

“The mainland is six miles away.”

“You can walk now.”

“I can’t cross six miles of seabed before sunset.”

“You can if you start.”

The line snapped dead.

Mara sat beneath the useless telephone, listening to the hollow dial tone.

Then she stood.

She packed water, bread, rope, a hammer, and her father’s brass compass into a canvas bag. She put on her mud boots and red storm coat. Before leaving, she climbed to the lantern room and placed one hand on the great glass lens.

For the first time since she was nineteen, she considered abandoning the light.

“I’ll come back,” she told it.

The lighthouse said nothing.

Outside, the air smelled of salt, iron, and opened graves.

A ladder descended from the eastern platform to rocks that had not seen daylight in thousands of years. They were furred with weed and crowded with pale crabs. Mara climbed carefully, then stepped onto the seabed.

Her boots sank to the ankles.

She turned southeast.

The ship stood about two miles away, exactly as Daniel had said. Three broken masts leaned above the mud. As she approached, the vessel grew larger until its black hull seemed like the carcass of some legendary animal.

The name on the stern was still visible beneath barnacles.

MERCY BELL  
LONDON

Mara knew the name.

The Mercy Bell had vanished in 1847 while carrying sixty-two passengers from London to Halifax. It had last been seen near Saint Orra’s Rock, but no wreckage had ever been found.

A split in the hull allowed Mara to climb inside.

The ship groaned around her. Water dripped from the beams. In the dim passageways, small silver fish flapped across the floorboards.

She found the captain’s cabin behind a swollen oak door. The room had collapsed on one side, but a desk remained bolted to the wall. Beneath it stood a green iron safe.

The dial turned stiffly.

Three.

One.

Seven.

Nine.

The lock clicked.

Inside lay a pistol, a leather ledger, a velvet purse full of blackened coins, and a sealed letter.

The envelope was dry.

On its face, in faded ink, someone had written:

TO THE LIGHTKEEPER OF SAINT ORRA, WHEN THE SEA GIVES BACK THE ROAD

Mara broke the seal.

The letter contained a single sheet.

If you are reading this, the Mercy Bell has been found, and the sea has withdrawn from Saint Orra.

Do not celebrate.

At sunset the water will return, bearing every vessel, every body, and every sorrow it has taken in these waters. The dead will seek the light, but they must not reach it. Carry this letter to the bell tower of Saint Orra’s Abbey. Ring the abbey bell continuously until the sea is full again.

The lighthouse calls sailors home.

The abbey bell sends the dead to rest.

If the light shines while the waters return, the drowned will follow it onto land.

Mara read the letter twice.

At the bottom was a signature.

Captain Elias Vale.

Vale.

Her own family name.

Behind her, a floorboard creaked.

Mara turned.

A young woman stood in the cabin doorway.

Her grey dress hung wet against her body. Seaweed threaded her dark hair. Her skin was pale and swollen, and a purple wound circled her throat.

“You opened it,” the woman said.

Mara backed against the desk.

“Who are you?”

“Clara Vale. Elias was my husband.”

“You’re from this ship.”

Clara looked around the ruined cabin.

“Yes.”

“You’re dead.”

“So are many.”

Mara’s hand found the hammer in her bag.

Clara smiled sadly. “That won’t help.”

“Why did your husband know my lighthouse?”

“He was born there. A keeper’s son. He knew the old stories.”

Mara folded the letter and placed it in her coat.

“I have to reach the abbey.”

“Yes.”

“Will you stop me?”

“No.”

Something struck the hull outside.

Then came another blow, and another, as though many fists had begun knocking against the ship.

Clara looked toward the sound.

“But they will.”

Mara ran.

She climbed through the broken hull and dropped into the mud. All across the seabed, figures were rising.

They pulled themselves from shipwrecks and pools, from beneath coils of chain and forests of weed. Men in naval uniforms. Women in long dresses. Children with shells tangled in their hair. Some were little more than bone. Others looked newly drowned.

All turned toward the lighthouse.

Its white tower stood against the eastern sky, bright in the afternoon sun.

“The light,” they murmured.

Thousands of voices joined in one soft exhalation.

“The light.”

Mara began walking toward the mainland.

At first, the dead ignored her. They moved west toward Saint Orra’s Rock, dragging ruined limbs through the mud. But as she passed between two wrecks, a drowned sailor lifted his head.

“She has the letter,” he said.

The others stopped.

Mara ran.

The mud fought every step. It sucked at her boots and splashed her coat. Behind her, the whispers grew louder.

“She has the letter.”

“Stop her.”

“The light must shine.”

A child appeared ahead of her, standing knee-deep in a shallow pool. He could not have been more than eight. One side of his face was hidden under wet hair.

“Please,” he said. “Have you seen my mother?”

Mara slowed.

Then the child reached for her, and she saw his fingers were jointed like the legs of a crab.

She stumbled backward. The drowned rushed from both sides.

Mara swung the hammer. It passed through the first sailor with the resistance of cold smoke, but he recoiled long enough for her to escape. She ran between the ribs of a shattered ship and over a bank of mussels that cracked under her boots.

The mainland cliffs seemed no closer.

Clouds covered the sun.

For more than an hour, Mara struggled east. Her lungs burned. Once, she lost a boot and had to dig it from the mud while the dead gathered behind her. Another time, she crossed a channel on the half-buried mast of a wreck, balancing above black water while pale hands reached up from below.

At last, she climbed onto a ridge of stones.

The abbey stood on the headland ahead.

Its roof had fallen in generations ago, but the square bell tower remained. Mara could see the bell hanging in its arch.

Between her and the headland lay one final stretch of mud.

And on it stood Daniel.

He looked seventeen.

He wore the red wool sweater he had taken from the house on the morning he died. His hair was wet and swept across his forehead. There was a cut on his cheek.

The sight of him emptied Mara of strength.

“Mare,” he said.

She wanted to run to him.

Every sensible part of her knew he was dead. Every lonely part answered that death was a poor reason not to embrace her brother after thirty-one years.

“Is it really you?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“You called me.”

“Yes.”

“Then let me pass.”

Daniel looked toward the lighthouse. Behind him, the drowned continued their slow march across the seabed.

“You don’t need the bell,” he said.

Mara felt something inside her go still.

“You told me to ring it.”

“I didn’t understand then.”

“Understand what?”

“What the light can do.”

He stepped closer.

“We followed it, Mara. All of us. Every storm, every winter, we saw it turning above the water. We knew someone was waiting. Now the road is open.”

“The letter says you’ll go onto land.”

“We’ll go home.”

“You’re dead.”

“We’re lost.”

His face was so young. Mara suddenly became aware of her own age: the ache in her knees, the grey in her hair, the decades he had not lived.

“Where have you been?” she asked.

“In the dark. Sometimes sleeping. Sometimes dreaming of the surface.”

“And the phone?”

“The sea carries voices. Today it carried mine to you.”

“Why did you tell me about the letter?”

“I thought the bell would release us. But Clara lied. Her husband lied. The bell will send us back.”

“To rest.”

“To nothing.”

The sun dipped beneath the clouds, staining the exposed world red.

Daniel held out his hand.

“Come with me to the lighthouse.”

Mara imagined it: relighting the lamp as the sea returned, watching Daniel climb the familiar stairs. Their father’s room was still as he had left it. She could make tea. She could ask every question that had lived inside her for thirty-one years.

And behind Daniel would come the others.

Thousands upon thousands of drowned men, women, and children, walking into villages, knocking on doors, calling to people who had mourned them.

Perhaps the dead only wanted what everyone wanted—to go home.

“Will you hurt anyone?” she asked.

Daniel hesitated.

It was a small hesitation.

It was enough.

“We’ll need places,” he said. “Bodies. Warmth. The living have so much warmth.”

Mara’s eyes filled with tears.

Daniel extended his hand farther.

“You promised you’d always look after me.”

“No,” she said. “I promised I’d try.”

His face changed.

For an instant, the boy vanished. Something pale and swollen stared through his features. Then Daniel returned.

“The sea is coming,” he warned.

Far to the west, a line appeared across the horizon.

At first it looked like mist. Then Mara heard a deep roar.

The water was returning.

She ran for the abbey.

Daniel seized her coat. His fingers were unbearably cold. Mara twisted free, leaving a strip of red cloth in his hand.

The other dead moved to block her path, but the first surge of water swept around their legs. Some cried out. Others laughed and opened their arms.

Mara reached the rocks below the headland and climbed.

The ocean raced behind her, filling channels, lifting ships, rolling bones through forests of weed. The Mercy Bell shuddered as water took its weight. One by one, the drowned rose on the waves.

Mara climbed until her hands bled.

At the top, she ran through the abbey graveyard. The earth trembled under her feet. Below the cliffs, water thundered against stone.

The bell tower had no stairs. They had rotted away years ago. A narrow maintenance ladder ran up the inner wall, several rungs missing.

Mara climbed.

Wind tore through the broken arches. From halfway up, she could see Saint Orra’s Rock. The lighthouse stood above the returning sea.

The lantern room flashed.

Mara froze.

She had not lit the lamp.

Yet a beam swept across the darkening water.

The drowned had reached it.

Ships rose around the rock: trawlers, warships, merchant brigs, fishing boats, vessels built in ages when maps still bore drawings of monsters. Their torn sails filled with wind. Their bells rang. Their crews crowded the decks and turned their faces toward the moving light.

“Mara!”

Daniel’s voice came from below.

He stood in the graveyard, dripping seawater.

“You’re too late,” he called. “The light is ours.”

Mara resumed climbing.

At the top of the tower, she hauled herself onto the bell platform.

The great bronze bell hung from a blackened beam. Its rope had long ago fallen away, but the clapper remained. Mara took the hammer from her bag and struck the bell.

The sound shook the tower.

It rolled across the headland and over the sea, vast and low.

The dead screamed.

Mara struck again.

A crack appeared along the handle of the hammer.

Below, Daniel began climbing the ladder.

“Stop!” he shouted.

She struck a third time.

Across the water, the nearest ghost ship dissolved into spray. Its crew lifted like smoke and vanished into the darkening sky.

Mara kept ringing.

Each blow traveled through her arms and into her chest. Ships disappeared. The dead on the rocks faded. The beam of the lighthouse weakened.

Daniel reached the platform.

He no longer looked seventeen.

His skin was grey, his eyes milk-white. A deep wound split his forehead. Behind his lips, seawater moved.

“Please,” he said.

Mara raised the hammer.

He became a boy again.

Not seventeen now, but six, standing barefoot in their mother’s kitchen. He held a broken cup in his hands.

I didn’t mean to, his face said.

Mara’s grip shook.

“I was afraid,” Daniel told her.

The sea below churned with vanishing ships.

“That day in the boat, I was afraid. I called for Dad. I called for you.”

Mara lowered the hammer.

Daniel stepped closer.

“It was cold,” he whispered. “You can’t know how cold.”

She remembered the years after he vanished: her father pacing the cliffs with a lantern, her mother setting an extra place at Christmas, Mara waking at every midnight knock.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

“Then let me stay.”

The lighthouse beam swept over them.

For one impossible moment, Daniel appeared warm and alive. His cheeks had color. His eyes were clear. Mara could smell rain and wool and the soap their mother used to buy.

Then the light moved on, and he was drowned again.

Mara understood.

The lighthouse did not guide the dead home.

It showed them the home they wanted.

The bell showed them what they were.

She touched her brother’s cold cheek.

“I did wait for you,” she said.

“I know.”

“I never stopped.”

“I know.”

“But you aren’t coming home.”

Daniel closed his eyes.

“No.”

Mara kissed his forehead. Then she turned and struck the bell.

The hammer broke.

The bell gave one final, tremendous note.

Daniel vanished beneath her hand.

Across the sea, the last ghost ships collapsed into rain. The false light in the lighthouse went dark.

Night covered Saint Orra.

Mara remained in the bell tower until the water settled.

At dawn, a rescue boat found her asleep beneath the bell, her hands cut and swollen. The crew had come from the mainland after witnessing what they later described as the largest tidal event in recorded history. They had seen no dead sailors, no phantom fleet, and no ancient wrecks. Only Mara believed otherwise.

The official report blamed an undersea landslide.

The telephone in the lighthouse was found hanging from its cord. The captain’s letter had become a blank sheet of water-stained paper.

Mara retired the following spring.

For the first time since childhood, she moved inland, taking a small cottage where the sea was only a thin blue line beyond the hills. She planted beans, learned to sleep without the sound of waves, and kept her father’s brass compass on the windowsill.

Saint Orra’s lighthouse was fully automated. Its lamp turned every night, though fewer ships passed that way as the years went on.

Mara never returned to the rock.

But once each winter, on the anniversary of Daniel’s death, her telephone rang shortly before dawn.

When she answered, no one spoke.

She heard only distant waves and, beneath them, the faint ringing of a bell.

Mara always stayed on the line until the sound faded.

Then she would place the receiver back in its cradle, open the curtains, and watch the morning light travel slowly over the hills—not searching, not calling, asking nothing of the lost.

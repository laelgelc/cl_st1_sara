The first time Mara heard the mountain breathe, she was eight years old and hiding beneath her grandmother’s kitchen table.

Outside, rain hammered the roof of the little stone house. Thunder rolled over the valley, shaking cups in their cupboard. Beneath the table, Mara pressed her palms to the floor and felt a faint vibration passing through the tiles.

“That isn’t thunder,” she whispered.

Her grandmother continued kneading bread as if storms were ordinary guests. Her hands folded the dough, turned it, folded it again.

“No,” Grandmother Elian said. “That is Mount Varro remembering its dreams.”

Mara crawled out and went to the window. The mountain filled half the world beyond the glass. Its black slopes rose above the village, and its peak disappeared inside the storm.

“Mountains don’t dream.”

“Everything dreams.”

“What does it dream about?”

Her grandmother dusted flour from her hands. “Fire, mostly.”

Eight years later, Mount Varro breathed again.

By then Grandmother Elian was gone, the kitchen table had one leg shorter than the others, and Mara lived alone in the stone house at the edge of Bellweather. She was splitting kindling when the ground trembled. Birds erupted from the pine trees. A bucket toppled beside the well.

From deep beneath the earth came a long, hollow sound.

Mara stood very still.

Across the valley, villagers emerged from homes and shops. They stared at the mountain. Varro’s summit was clear beneath the autumn sky, but a thread of gray smoke curled from a crack near its peak.

The oldest people in Bellweather crossed themselves.

The youngest cheered.

Before sunset, the council bell rang.

The meeting hall smelled of damp wool, lamp oil, and fear. Nearly every villager had come. Shepherds stood beside merchants; farmers squeezed onto benches with miners. At the front of the room, Mayor Vale repeatedly wiped his forehead with a blue handkerchief.

“We have survived tremors before,” he announced. “The mountain has smoked before. There is no reason for panic.”

A violent jolt rattled the windows.

Someone shouted. A baby began crying.

Mayor Vale gripped the lectern. “As I was saying, there is no reason for panic.”

Tomas Reed, the village blacksmith, rose from the back. He was broad enough to block the doorway and loud enough to make the mayor seem to shrink.

“My father told me about the last awakening,” Tomas said. “Ash buried the northern fields. Half the livestock died.”

“That was eighty years ago,” said the mayor.

“Mountains keep longer memories than men.”

Mara glanced sharply at Tomas, but his attention was on the mayor.

Old Sella, the village midwife, lifted a trembling hand. “There was a warning system once.”

Whispers spread through the hall.

“What system?” the mayor asked.

“The bell at the observatory.”

Everyone knew of the abandoned observatory high on Varro’s eastern ridge. Children dared one another to climb there, though few ever did. The path had been broken by landslides, and stories said lightning haunted the iron tower.

Sella continued. “Before Bellweather had a council, watchmen lived there. They studied smoke, water, and stone. If the mountain became dangerous, they rang the Great Bell, and the villages evacuated west through the river pass.”

Mayor Vale looked relieved. “Then we simply wait for the bell.”

“There are no watchmen anymore,” Mara said.

The room turned toward her.

She disliked speaking before crowds. Words were safer when written in the margins of books or whispered to someone who would not live long enough to repeat them.

“My grandmother kept records,” she continued. “The observatory was abandoned forty-two years ago. The bell mechanism failed, and no one repaired it.”

The mayor’s relief vanished.

Tomas folded his arms. “Can it be repaired?”

“Perhaps.”

“By whom?”

Mara knew the answer before anyone looked at her. Grandmother Elian had been the last keeper of Bellweather’s clocks. She had taught Mara how to replace gears, mend springs, and listen for faults hidden inside a mechanism.

“A clock is only a promise made by metal,” her grandmother used to say. “Treat it kindly, and it will keep its word.”

A bell mechanism would be larger than a clock, but its language would be the same.

Mara felt every gaze in the hall settle upon her.

“I can try,” she said.

They left at dawn: Mara, Tomas, and Lucen Vale, the mayor’s son.

Lucen had volunteered before his father could forbid him. He was seventeen, a year older than Mara, and widely considered useless in all practical matters. He painted scenery for village festivals, played three musical instruments badly, and had once fallen into the fountain while attempting to impress a baker’s daughter.

“You need someone educated,” he had argued.

“In what?” Tomas asked.

“Ancient observatories.”

“You know about ancient observatories?”

“No, but I read quickly.”

So Lucen joined them, carrying the old observatory journal Mara had found in her grandmother’s chest.

The ascent began gently through birch woods silvered by frost. Soon the trail narrowed. Roots twisted beneath fallen leaves, and the eastern face of Varro loomed above them.

By midday, the ground shook every hour.

They found dead foxes near a stream, their bodies untouched by wounds. The water smelled of rotten eggs.

“Poisoned gas,” Lucen said after consulting the journal. “It says animals die before eruptions because fumes gather in low places.”

Tomas stared at the foxes. “How long before?”

“The journal does not say.”

“Useful book.”

“It was written by people expecting their readers to have brains.”

Tomas stepped toward him.

Mara moved between them. “Save your strength.”

Tomas muttered something about educated fools, but continued uphill.

Beyond the stream, the old path vanished beneath a landslide. To the left stood a cliff; to the right, a steep slope descended into a ravine. Broken stones shifted beneath their boots.

Halfway across, the mountain gave a sudden roar.

The slope moved.

“Mara!” Tomas shouted.

The earth slipped beneath her feet. She dropped her pack and threw herself forward, catching a pine root. Stones poured into the ravine. Her body swung over empty air.

Tomas flattened himself against the slope and reached down, but his hand stopped a foot above hers.

“Let go with one hand,” he said. “Reach for me.”

“I’ll fall.”

“You’ll fall if the root breaks.”

As if agreeing, the root tore slightly from the soil.

Mara’s breath locked in her chest.

Then a rope dropped beside her.

Lucen lay above Tomas, bracing his feet against a boulder. He had tied the rope around his waist.

“Take it!” he yelled.

Mara released the root with one hand and seized the rope. Tomas caught her wrist. Together they dragged her back onto the trail.

For a while, none of them spoke.

Mara lay against the cold earth, listening to her heart strike her ribs. Tomas sat nearby, breathing heavily.

Lucen retrieved Mara’s pack from where it had caught on a bush.

“You saved my tools,” she said.

“I was aiming for you,” he replied. “The pack got in the way.”

By dusk, they reached the observatory.

It stood on a shelf of rock above the clouds: a circular building of black stone with a copper dome green from age. Beside it rose an iron bell tower. The Great Bell hung beneath a pointed roof, vast and dark, its surface covered in lines of ancient writing.

One side of the tower had collapsed. Gears lay among the weeds like enormous rusted bones.

Mara approached the bell and placed her hand against it.

The metal was cold.

“There’s no clapper,” Tomas said.

They searched until darkness fell. Inside the observatory they found ruined furniture, cracked lenses, rotting charts, and bird nests. The clapper was nowhere.

Lucen read the journal by lantern light while Tomas built a fire in an old stove. Mara examined the bell mechanism.

The design was clever. A turning drum pulled a chain, swinging the clapper with greater force than any person could manage. But the chain had rusted through. Several gears were broken, and the central axle had warped.

Even if they found the clapper, they would never repair the mechanism in time.

“How bad?” Tomas asked.

“Bad.”

“You can fix it?”

“No.”

The word seemed to darken the room.

Lucen looked up from the journal. “There may be another way.”

He carried the book to them and pointed at a faded diagram. It showed the bell tower and, beneath it, a tunnel descending into the mountain.

“The original bell was not rung by gears,” he said. “The first keepers used a counterweight. See? The clapper was lifted up the central shaft. When released, it swung against the bell.”

“Where is the clapper now?” Mara asked.

Lucen turned several pages. Near the end was a hurried entry, written in a different hand.

“‘Mechanism disabled after the lightning fire. Clapper lowered to the furnace chamber until repairs can be made.’”

Tomas leaned over the book. “Where is the furnace chamber?”

“Below us.”

They found the tunnel entrance behind a fallen cabinet.

Stone stairs spiraled downward. The air grew warmer with each step. Mara carried the lantern, Tomas an iron bar, and Lucen the journal.

The stairs ended at a narrow passage lined with pipes. Some were copper, others clay. They groaned and hissed as the mountain trembled around them.

“This place was more than an observatory,” Lucen said. “The keepers measured pressure underground. These pipes carried steam to instruments above.”

Mara touched a wall. It was warm enough to heat a house.

The passage divided.

One corridor descended. Another curved north. The markings in the journal did not match those on the walls.

“The builders used the old calendar,” Lucen murmured. “East was marked with the rising sun in spring, not—”

“Which way?” Tomas demanded.

Lucen closed the book. “Down.”

“Are you certain?”

“No.”

“Excellent.”

They descended.

The heat became oppressive. Moisture dripped from the ceiling, though the drops were hot. Then they heard the sound: a deep, rhythmic thudding.

Mara stopped.

It resembled a giant heart.

Boom.

Silence.

Boom.

Dust drifted from the ceiling.

“The mountain breathing,” she whispered.

The corridor opened into the furnace chamber.

A chasm split the room from end to end. Red light pulsed far below, revealing slow rivers of molten rock. An old wooden bridge crossed to a platform on the other side.

There lay the clapper.

It was a solid iron shaft longer than Tomas was tall, suspended in a cradle beneath a crane. A heavy chain ran from it into a vertical tunnel in the ceiling.

“The chain leads to the bell,” Mara said. “We don’t need to carry the clapper. We only need to raise it.”

They crossed one at a time. The bridge creaked but held.

Mara studied the crane. Unlike the machinery above, it had been protected from weather. Rust covered its surface, yet its gears remained whole. A long wooden lever controlled the brake.

“If we release this, the counterweight should fall,” she said. “That will pull the clapper up.”

“And then?” Lucen asked.

“The chain catches at the top. We release it from the tower, and the clapper swings.”

“Should?”

“Should.”

Tomas took hold of the brake lever and pulled. Nothing moved.

He planted both feet and tried again. The wood groaned.

“It’s seized.”

Mara opened her tool roll. She cleaned rust from the hinge and poured oil into the joint. Tomas pulled once more.

The lever shifted.

At that moment, the floor jumped.

The sound that followed was not a breath but a scream.

Rock burst from the chasm. A wave of heat threw them backward. The bridge split near its center, one side dropping into the red darkness.

Lucen’s lantern fell and vanished below.

Emergency lamps—old bowls of oil set into the walls—had ignited from the heat. In their wavering glow, Mara saw Tomas lying beneath part of the crane.

They ran to him.

A metal support had fallen across his leg. Blood darkened his trousers.

“I think it’s broken,” he gasped.

Lucen and Mara lifted the support enough for Tomas to drag himself free. He tried to stand and nearly collapsed.

“We have to go back,” Lucen said.

Tomas shook his head. “Ring the bell.”

“The bridge is broken.”

“Not completely,” Mara said.

Half its planks remained, slanting sharply above the chasm. Crossing would mean crawling along a strip of wood scarcely wider than her shoulders.

Another tremor passed through the chamber.

The crane’s counterweight hung above them: a massive block of stone. If the ceiling shifted, it would crush them.

“We release the brake,” Mara said. “Then we cross.”

Tomas looked at his injured leg. “You release it. I’ll cross first.”

“You can barely stand.”

“That is why I go first. If I fall, you won’t be trapped behind me.”

Lucen knelt beside him. “No one is falling.”

Tomas gave a strained laugh. “The educated fool has spoken.”

Mara and Lucen supported him to the bridge. Tomas lowered himself onto the remaining planks and began to crawl.

Each movement drew a groan from him. Below, the molten river surged. Orange light shone through cracks in the wood.

At last he reached the far side and rolled onto solid ground.

Lucen followed.

Mara returned to the brake.

She wrapped both hands around the lever.

“Ready?” she called.

“No,” Lucen answered. “Do it.”

Mara pulled.

The brake released with a deafening crack.

The counterweight plunged.

Gears spun. Chains snapped tight. The iron clapper rose from its cradle and vanished up the shaft. Wind rushed after it, tugging Mara’s hair toward the ceiling.

The lever kicked back and struck her shoulder. She fell.

The crane shuddered apart.

Mara scrambled toward the bridge as metal beams crashed behind her. She dropped onto the sloping planks and crawled. The bridge shook wildly.

A plank broke beneath her knee.

Her leg plunged through. She caught herself on her elbows, staring down into the chasm.

Lucen crawled back toward her.

“Stop!” she shouted. “Your weight—”

He ignored her. Lying flat, he reached across the broken section.

“Take my hand.”

The mountain roared again.

Mara seized his wrist. Lucen pulled as she kicked free. Together they slid toward the far side just as the bridge tore loose and fell into the light below.

Tomas was waiting by the stairs, pale and shaking.

They climbed slowly.

Mara and Lucen made a seat with their arms and carried Tomas between them. Every few steps they stopped to breathe. Behind them, steam shrieked through broken pipes.

When they reached the observatory, night was fading.

A red dawn spread across the eastern horizon, though the sun had not yet risen. Smoke poured from Varro’s summit. Ash drifted like black snow.

Mara ran to the tower.

The clapper now hung beside the bell, held by an iron catch. Beneath it was a release wheel.

She turned the wheel.

It did not move.

“No,” she whispered.

She tried again. Rust flaked beneath her hands.

Tomas sat against the observatory wall, unable to help. Lucen joined Mara, and together they strained against the wheel.

It shifted an inch.

From the valley came a distant sound.

Bellweather’s council bell was ringing—small, frantic strokes that vanished beneath the rumbling mountain. Mara pictured the villagers gathering in the square, looking upward, waiting for a warning from an empty tower.

“They haven’t left,” she said.

“Then we make them hear us,” Lucen replied.

They pulled.

The wheel turned another inch. The catch began to rise.

Then it stopped.

Mara examined the release assembly. A toothed gear beneath the wheel was jammed by a bent iron pin.

She reached for her tools.

Her pack was gone.

It must have fallen in the furnace chamber.

“Tomas,” she called. “Your knife.”

He tossed it. She caught it by the handle and wedged the blade beside the pin. The knife bent.

She pushed harder.

The pin shifted slightly but remained trapped.

Lucen looked toward the sky. “Mara.”

Above the summit, smoke folded into itself. A column of ash rose with terrible speed.

They had minutes. Perhaps less.

Mara remembered sitting at her grandmother’s workbench, crying over the first clock she had tried to repair. She had forced the mechanism, snapped a spring, and insisted the clock was ruined.

Grandmother Elian had taken Mara’s small hands in hers.

“Metal is stubborn,” she said. “That is different from being strong. Do not fight where it resists. Find where it is weak.”

Mara looked at the release assembly.

Not the pin, she thought.

The housing around it.

She placed Tomas’s knife against a rusted seam and struck the handle with a loose stone. Once. Twice. On the third blow, the corroded plate cracked.

Lucen understood. He picked up another stone.

Together they hammered the housing until a section broke away. The bent pin fell free.

The release wheel spun.

For one silent instant, nothing happened.

Then the clapper dropped.

It swung in a vast arc and struck the Great Bell.

The sound seemed to split the morning.

It rolled across the ridge, deeper than thunder, powerful enough to tremble in Mara’s bones. The bell swung back.

A second note rang out.

Far below, tiny figures filled Bellweather’s streets. Even at that distance, Mara could see them beginning to move west.

“It worked,” Lucen breathed.

The mountain answered.

Varro’s summit exploded.

A wall of ash and fire climbed into the sky. Glowing stones arced over the slopes. Clouds rolled downward toward the northern fields.

“Inside!” Tomas shouted.

They dragged him into the observatory as debris struck the roof. Windows shattered. The building shook so violently that Mara could not remain standing.

The bell continued to ring.

Each movement of the earth set it swinging, sending wave after wave of sound across the valley.

Mara crouched beneath an old stone desk. Lucen pressed beside her; Tomas lay against the wall, arms over his head.

In the darkness, Mara thought of her grandmother kneading bread while thunder rolled around the kitchen.

Everything dreams.

“What does it dream about?”

Fire, mostly.

The roaring grew until it swallowed all other sound. Then, little by little, the tremors faded.

The mountain exhaled one final time.

Silence followed.

They waited until noon before leaving the observatory.

The sky had turned gray. Ash covered the ridge and softened every edge. The copper dome had collapsed, but the bell tower still stood.

Below, lava burned through the northern fields, carving a bright scar across the valley. Bellweather’s roofs were buried in ash, yet the village itself remained untouched. Along the western road, a line of people wound toward the river pass.

They had escaped.

The descent took two days.

Lucen and Mara carried Tomas on a stretcher made from branches and torn coats. Twice they hid from falling stones. They had no food, and warm ash turned to mud beneath their feet.

At sunset on the second day, they saw lanterns moving through the trees.

Villagers surrounded them.

Mayor Vale reached Lucen first, pulling his son into a fierce embrace. Tomas’s wife and daughters knelt beside the stretcher. Old Sella touched Mara’s soot-blackened face as if confirming she was real.

“You rang it,” the midwife said.

Mara looked back toward the mountain. The Great Bell was hidden by cloud, but she imagined she could still feel its voice moving through the earth.

“We all did,” she said.

Bellweather rebuilt farther west, beyond the river pass.

The old village was abandoned beneath gray fields, though every spring red flowers grew through the ash. Tomas recovered, but his leg never healed straight. He walked with a cane he claimed made him look distinguished. Lucen wrote an account of the eruption, which grew less accurate and more entertaining each time he told it.

As for Mara, she returned to the observatory the following summer.

She did not go alone. A dozen villagers climbed with her, carrying timber, tools, glass, and food. They repaired the eastern path and rebuilt the fallen tower. New instruments were installed in the observatory. Watchmen once again studied smoke, water, and stone.

Above the entrance, Mara carved a line from her grandmother’s old notebook:

LISTEN FIRST. THE WORLD IS ALWAYS SPEAKING.

Years later, children from the new village would climb to the ridge and ask Mara whether the mountain was alive.

She would show them the needles trembling on their instruments. She would lead them into the bell tower and let them place their hands against the ancient bronze.

“Is it dreaming now?” they would ask.

“Yes,” Mara would say.

“What does it dream about?”

By then the slopes of Varro had become green again. Pines rooted in old ash. Foxes returned to the streams. In summer, clouds gathered around the summit, and rain silvered the black cliffs.

Mara would look across the valley at the red roofs beside the river. She would think of a crooked kitchen table, flour on her grandmother’s hands, and a promise kept by metal.

“Not only fire,” she would answer.

## The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, Mara Venn climbed the narrow stairs of the clock tower and collected the hours that people had lost.

They appeared inside the great brass mechanism as small glass beads, each no larger than a raindrop. Some were clear, some clouded with gray, and a few shone with a pale gold light. They gathered beneath the gears during the night, trembling whenever the clock struck.

Mara swept them into a velvet-lined box.

No one else in Bellweather knew they existed.

To the town, Mara was simply the keeper of the clock. She wound the mechanism, polished its four faces, and rang the bells on holidays. Children sometimes waved to her from the square, and merchants complained whenever the clock ran a minute slow. They did not know that beneath the wooden floor of the tower lay hundreds of boxes filled with all the time they had misplaced.

A lost hour was not always an hour spent badly. That was what Mara’s grandfather had taught her.

“Time enjoyed is never lost,” he used to say. “Nor is time given freely. Lost time is different. It is an hour that should have mattered, but slipped away unseen.”

An afternoon forgotten in grief might leave a bead. So might a sleepless night spent fearing something that never happened. A conversation avoided for years could shed dozens. A childhood ignored until it was over could fill an entire box.

Mara’s grandfather had been keeper before her, and his mother before him. Their family had guarded Bellweather’s lost hours for nearly two centuries.

“What happens if we don’t collect them?” Mara had asked when she was young.

Her grandfather had looked toward the gears turning above them.

“The clock begins to remember.”

At the time, she had imagined hands spinning backward and dead people walking into the square. But her grandfather explained that the danger was quieter.

Uncollected hours returned to those who had lost them, but not as gifts. They returned as moments repeated endlessly: a regret at breakfast, a sorrow before sleep, the same unspoken words rising day after day until the present was buried beneath the past.

“So we keep the hours safe,” he said, fastening the lid of a box. “We do not judge them. We do not use them. And above all, we never break the glass.”

Mara had obeyed these rules for twenty-three years.

Then, one winter morning, she found a golden bead with her name inside it.

The letters floated at its center, bright and unmistakable:

MARA VENN.

She stared until the tower bell began to ring above her.

The bead jumped in her palm with every strike.

Seven.

Eight.

Nine.

Mara closed her fingers around it.

The metal stairs spiraled beneath her. Far below, Bellweather was waking under a thin layer of snow. Chimneys breathed into the cold. Bakers opened their doors. Carts rattled over the frozen street.

Nothing seemed changed.

Mara carried the bead to her worktable and set it beneath a magnifying lens. The words remained.

Lost hours were never marked. The keeper was supposed to know nothing about them—not their owner, not their origin. They were fragments without labels, stripped of everything except the feeling that colored them.

Clear for distraction.

Gray for sorrow.

Blue for fear.

Gold for love.

Mara had found only six golden hours in all her years as keeper. She had placed each one in the oldest vault beneath the tower, where they shone like captured stars.

She had never found one belonging to herself.

All day she tried to ignore it.

She repaired a worn tooth on the second wheel. She oiled the western dial. She crossed the square to buy bread and onions from Mr. Vale, who had been in love with the same woman for forty years and had never told her. Mara knew this only because every spring the clock produced three or four golden beads after Mrs. Vale’s sister visited from the coast.

“Cold morning,” Mr. Vale said.

“It is.”

“Clock’s loud today.”

“The air carries the sound.”

He nodded as though this explained everything.

At the fountain, a group of children were building a crooked snowman. One of them, a red-haired girl named Lio, saw Mara and ran over.

“Miss Venn, is it true the clock has a room no one can enter?”

“It has several.”

“Are there ghosts?”

“Only dust.”

“My brother says there’s treasure.”

“Your brother is mistaken.”

Lio narrowed her eyes. “That’s what someone with treasure would say.”

Mara almost smiled.

Then she noticed a man standing beneath the awning of the old apothecary.

He wore a dark green coat and held no hat, though snow had settled in his black hair. He was looking directly at her.

Mara forgot the bread beneath her arm. She forgot the cold. She forgot the golden bead hidden in her pocket.

The man crossed the square.

He was older than the last time she had seen him. There was silver near his temples and a pale scar at the edge of his jaw. Yet his walk was unchanged: slightly hurried, as if he had just remembered somewhere else he ought to be.

“Hello, Mara,” he said.

“Elias.”

Lio looked between them.

“Is he a ghost?” she asked.

“No,” Mara said.

Elias gave the girl a solemn look. “Not as far as I know.”

This answer satisfied her. She ran back to the snowman.

Mara and Elias stood without speaking.

Twelve years earlier, he had left Bellweather on a rain-soaked morning. He had promised to write. For a while, he did. Letters came from southern ports, mountain villages, and cities where the streets were lit all night. Then the letters stopped.

Mara had continued answering them for months.

Eventually she understood that she was writing to silence.

“What are you doing here?” she asked.

“I came to see you.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

“You’ve had twelve years to find a better one.”

His face tightened, though his voice remained gentle. “May we talk somewhere warmer?”

Mara wanted to refuse. Instead, she led him to the tower.

The keeper’s room was halfway up, behind a blue-painted door. It contained a stove, two chairs, a table, and shelves crowded with tools. Elias looked around as though he had entered a chapel from his childhood.

“You still keep tea in that red tin?” he asked.

“No.”

She opened the red tin and measured tea into the kettle.

He pretended not to notice.

While the water heated, Elias stood at the window overlooking the square. Snow drifted over the roofs. Beyond the town, the hills faded into white.

“I used to imagine this view,” he said. “Everywhere I went.”

“You could have returned.”

“Yes.”

“But you didn’t.”

“No.”

The kettle began to whistle.

Mara poured two cups. Her hands were steady until the golden bead rolled from her pocket and struck the table.

Its sound was almost nothing.

Elias turned.

The bead shone between them.

Mara snatched it up, but he had already seen the floating letters.

“What is that?”

“A piece of the clock.”

“It has your name in it.”

“An accidental mark.”

“Mara.”

“You lost the right to ask me questions.”

He flinched.

The bead grew warm in her fist.

From far above came a grinding noise.

Mara looked at the ceiling.

The clock had stopped.

Silence spread through the tower.

It was an unnatural silence, deeper than the absence of bells. The building seemed to hold its breath. Outside, the people in the square began pointing upward.

Mara ran to the stairs.

The great mechanism was frozen. The pendulum stood motionless to one side. Between the gears, hundreds of tiny lights flickered like eyes.

“Stay back,” she told Elias.

He followed her anyway.

Mara climbed onto the maintenance platform and examined the central wheel. Nothing blocked it. No tooth was broken. The weights hung properly in the shaft.

Then she saw an empty space beneath the wheel.

The morning’s golden bead had not been the only hour waiting there.

A crack ran through the collection tray.

“Has this happened before?” Elias asked.

“No.”

The crack widened with a faint, crystalline sound.

Mara jumped down and reached for the tray, but it split before her hands touched it.

Lost hours spilled across the floor.

Hundreds of beads scattered between their boots, gray and blue and clear and gold. Some slipped through the gaps between the boards and fell into the tower below. Others struck the walls and shattered.

The first broken hour released a woman’s laughter.

It burst into the room so vividly that Mara turned, expecting to see someone behind her. Then came the smell of summer grass, the warmth of sunlight, and a man’s voice saying, “Stay a little longer.”

A second bead broke.

Rain filled the air without wetting anything. A child sobbed. A door slammed.

Then ten broke at once.

Memories flooded the tower.

Mara saw fragments without context: a candlelit supper, an empty bed, a hand withdrawing from another hand, a road not taken, an apology rehearsed but never spoken. The visions passed through her like cold wind.

Below, cries rose from the square.

The lost hours that had fallen through the floor were breaking throughout the tower and spilling into the town.

People staggered in the streets. Mr. Vale stood outside his shop, speaking to someone who was not there. The children around the snowman had begun calling for parents long dead or long grown old.

“The vault,” Mara whispered.

“What?”

“If the loose hours reach the vaults, thousands will break.”

“What do we do?”

Mara seized a broom and began sweeping the beads away from the gaps, but they rolled against the bristles as if driven by a current. All moved toward the staircase.

Toward the lower floors.

Toward the vaults.

Elias pulled off his coat and spread it across the steps, forming a barrier. The beads piled against it, chiming softly.

“It won’t hold,” he said.

Mara knew he was right.

She took the golden hour from her pocket.

It pulsed in time with her heartbeat.

A keeper never used a lost hour. To break one deliberately was to call the moment back with such force that it could alter the present. Her grandfather had warned her that the hour would offer a choice, but choices made inside lost time carried a price.

“What are you doing?” Elias asked.

“Stopping the clock from remembering.”

She placed the bead on the worktable.

“No,” he said. “You don’t know what’s inside.”

“I know enough.”

Gold for love.

Her love. Her lost hour.

She lifted a small iron hammer.

Elias caught her wrist.

“Mara, wait.”

The sound of breaking glass echoed below them. The tower trembled. Outside, the sky darkened as memories rose like smoke over Bellweather.

“There is no time,” she said.

“There must be another way.”

“There isn’t.”

“How do you know?”

“Because I am the keeper.”

“And has being the keeper ever allowed you to live?”

The question struck harder than his hand around her wrist.

Mara pulled free.

“You do not get to return after twelve years and speak to me about living.”

“You’re right.”

“You left without explanation.”

“I know.”

“I waited for you.”

“I know.”

“No, you don’t.” Her voice broke at last. “You don’t know what waiting became.”

Elias looked at the golden bead.

“Yes,” he said. “I do.”

He reached beneath his shirt and drew out a thin chain. A small glass bead hung from it, pale gold and glowing.

Mara stared.

“That’s impossible.”

“I found it the morning I left.”

The bead contained his name.

ELIAS VALE.

“You stole an hour from the clock?”

“Your grandfather gave it to me.”

Mara felt the tower tilt beneath her, though it did not move.

“He would never—”

“He said the clock had begun producing hours that belonged to us. One for me, and someday, perhaps, one for you. He said Bellweather was keeping us in place. That as long as I remained, I would always choose you because I had never learned who I was without you.”

Mara’s anger turned sharp and clean.

“So you left to discover whether you loved me?”

“Yes.”

“And did you?”

“Every day.”

Below them, another vault door burst open.

A golden light blazed up through the stairwell.

The clock’s hands began racing backward.

People in the square moved in broken patterns. Footprints vanished from the snow. A horse backed rapidly along the street, dragging its cart. The children’s snowman rose from scattered pieces, then collapsed into untouched snow.

Time was folding.

Mara gripped the table.

“Why didn’t you come back?” she asked.

Elias closed his eyes.

“Because I was ashamed. At first I thought I needed one more month, one more city, one more certainty. Then too much time had passed. Every year made returning harder. I wrote a hundred letters and sent none of them.”

“You let me believe I meant nothing.”

“I know.”

“You don’t know.”

“No,” he said. “But I am here now, and I know that may not matter.”

The beads piled higher against his coat.

Mara looked at his golden hour and then at her own.

Two lost pieces of the same life.

Her grandfather had known.

Perhaps he had hoped separation would grant them freedom. Perhaps he had only created a different kind of prison.

The tower groaned.

“What happens if we break them?” Elias asked.

“We return to the moments inside. We may choose again.”

“Then break mine.”

“Why yours?”

“Because mine began this.”

Mara shook her head. “There is no yours or mine anymore. The clock made both.”

She placed the two beads side by side.

At once, threads of golden light reached between them.

The tower disappeared.

Mara stood beneath the old ash tree beyond the northern gate. It was spring, twelve years ago. Rain trembled on the new leaves.

Her younger self stood several paces away, wearing a yellow dress and mud-stained boots. Elias, young and unscarred, faced her with a travel bag over his shoulder.

This was the morning he had left.

Mara remembered every word.

“I’ll write when I reach the coast,” young Elias said.

“You had better.”

“I’ll be back before winter.”

“You had better.”

Neither of them said, Stay.

Neither said, I love you.

They had believed such truths were obvious. They had trusted time to speak for them.

The present Elias stood beside Mara, watching their younger selves.

“This is the hour,” he said.

“No,” Mara replied. “This is only the moment.”

The golden light widened.

They saw what had surrounded it.

Her grandfather stood in the tower, holding Elias’s bead and telling him to go. Mara sat at her window that night, writing the first of many letters. Elias read them beside foreign rivers and in crowded inns, always planning to answer when he found the correct words.

Years unfolded.

Mara buried her grandfather on a windless autumn day.

Elias stood outside Bellweather once, hidden among the trees, unable to take the final steps home.

Mara laughed at supper with friends and felt guilty for being happy.

Elias loved other people kindly but never completely.

Their lost hour was not a single hour. It was a door left open for twelve years.

“How do we change it?” Elias asked.

Mara watched her younger self smile bravely beneath the ash tree.

“We tell the truth.”

She stepped forward.

The memory resisted. Wind tore at her clothes. Rain flew upward into the clouds. Yet she reached the young woman she had been.

“You will survive him leaving,” Mara said.

Her younger self could not hear her.

“You will become strong. But strength is not the same as silence.”

The girl flickered.

Mara turned to Elias. “Now you.”

He approached his younger self.

“You will never be certain,” he said. “Go if you must, but certainty is not waiting for you out there.”

The younger Elias looked toward him, almost seeing.

The golden world began to crack.

Mara understood then that they had not been brought back to erase the past. Lost time could not be reclaimed by pretending it had never been lost.

It could only be released.

She faced Elias across the rain.

“I loved you,” she said.

He nodded, tears bright on his face. “I loved you.”

“You hurt me.”

“I did.”

“I may never forgive you.”

“I know.”

“And I will not build my future around the person you were.”

“You shouldn’t.”

The memory shattered.

Mara awoke on the tower floor.

The great clock was striking noon.

Elias lay several feet away. Around them, the lost hours had gone dark. The beads were now ordinary glass, empty and harmless.

Outside, Bellweather stood still beneath the winter sun.

Then sound returned.

A dog barked. Someone shouted. A baby began to cry. The people in the square looked around in confusion, waking from dreams they would soon forget.

Mara rose slowly.

The vault doors below were open, but every box inside was empty. Two centuries of lost hours had vanished.

The clock no longer needed them.

On the floor beside Mara rested two broken golden beads.

Elias sat up.

“Are we back?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Did we change anything?”

Mara looked through the clock face at the town. Mr. Vale was crossing the square toward the woman he had loved for forty years. He moved with terrified determination.

Perhaps some things had changed.

“Not what happened,” she said. “Only what happens next.”

Elias stood and retrieved his coat from the stairs.

“I’ll leave if you want me to.”

Mara considered him.

Once, she had imagined his return so often that it had become another form of waiting. In some versions, she struck him. In others, she forgave him before he spoke. In her loneliest dreams, nothing had changed, and they simply continued where they had stopped.

But time did not continue where it stopped.

It moved, carrying scars, questions, and possibilities.

“Stay until the snow melts,” she said. “After that, we’ll see.”

Elias gave a small, uncertain smile. “All right.”

Mara climbed to the mechanism. The gears turned smoothly now, cleaner than she had ever seen them. Beneath the central wheel, the cracked collection tray had disappeared. In its place was a narrow opening through which sunlight fell.

The next morning, Mara climbed the tower at six.

She brought the velvet-lined box out of habit, though she no longer knew whether she was still a keeper.

The clock struck.

The gears turned.

Nothing gathered beneath them.

Mara waited until the final echo faded over Bellweather. Then she carried the empty box downstairs, opened the tower door, and stepped into the morning.

People crossed the square beneath the falling snow. Some hurried. Some lingered. Some were already wasting time, though perhaps not losing it.

Elias waited beside the fountain with two cups of tea.

Mara took one.

Together, without promise and without certainty, they walked into the hour that had been given to them.

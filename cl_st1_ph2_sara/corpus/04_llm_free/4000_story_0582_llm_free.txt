### The Cartographer of Lost Roads

Every morning, before the bells of Saint Orra announced the hour, Elias Vale opened his shop and drew a road that no longer existed.

He worked by the blue light before dawn, when the city was quiet enough for memory to speak. With a goose-feather pen, he marked the road in brown ink: a narrow lane leaving the northern gate, passing an abandoned mill, crossing a river at a stone bridge, and disappearing into the hills.

By sunrise, the ink would fade.

By breakfast, the parchment would be blank again.

Elias had drawn the road every day for seventeen years.

His shop stood on a crooked street between a locksmith and a baker. Above the door hung a wooden sign painted with a compass rose and the words:

**ELIAS VALE, CARTOGRAPHER**  
**Maps Made, Mended, and Remembered**

Most customers came for practical reasons. Merchants wanted routes around flooded valleys. Captains required clean copies of coastal charts. Wealthy travelers ordered maps decorated with sea monsters and golden forests they had no intention of visiting.

But occasionally someone came with a different kind of request.

“Can you map a place I saw in a dream?” a child once asked.

“Yes,” Elias said.

“Can you map the way to the day before yesterday?” asked an old drunk.

“Only if you can describe the weather.”

“Can you show me where my husband died?” whispered a widow.

“If you are certain you wish to know.”

Elias believed every place left a trace. A path worn by footsteps remained in the earth long after grass covered it. A house remembered the shape of its vanished walls. Even a forgotten village might survive as a bend in a stream or a row of apple trees growing in an empty field.

And some roads, he knew, disappeared not because they were abandoned, but because they were taken.

The northern road had been taken seventeen years earlier, along with everyone traveling on it.

Including Elias’s younger brother.

On the morning everything changed, rain fell over the city in silver threads. Elias was repairing a harbor chart when a girl entered his shop carrying a red umbrella.

She looked about sixteen, though her expression belonged to someone older. Her dark hair was cut unevenly at her jaw, and mud covered her boots. She closed the umbrella, leaned it carefully against the door, and approached the counter.

“I need a map,” she said.

“What kind?”

“A true one.”

“All my maps are true.”

The girl glanced at the decorative dragon curling around the edge of the harbor chart.

“Mostly true,” Elias amended.

She reached inside her coat and placed a small brass compass on the counter.

Its glass was cracked. Its needle did not point north. Instead, it trembled toward Elias’s workroom, where the blank parchment lay on his desk.

He stared at it.

“Where did you get this?”

“It was my mother’s.”

“And where did she get it?”

“She said a cartographer made it for her.”

Elias picked up the compass. On the back, beneath years of scratches, were two engraved initials: **T.V.**

For a moment, he heard nothing but rain.

“My brother made this,” he said.

The girl nodded. “His name was Tomas.”

Elias gripped the counter.

“Who are you?”

“Lina Arven. My mother traveled with your brother before the road vanished.”

“Your mother was on the northern road?”

“She still is.”

Elias looked at her sharply.

Lina unfolded a damp piece of paper. It was not a map, exactly, but a child’s drawing of a valley surrounded by black hills. In the center stood a village with yellow windows. Above it hung a crescent moon.

“My mother drew this when I was little,” she said. “She told me there is a place between places, and sometimes roads lead there by mistake. She escaped before I was born, but the others couldn’t follow.”

Elias wanted to dismiss the story. He wanted to say that grief invented doors in locked walls. Yet the compass needle continued pointing toward his workroom.

“Why come now?” he asked.

“Because three days ago the compass began to move.”

Elias carried it to his desk.

As he approached the blank parchment, the needle swung wildly. Then, before his eyes, a thin brown line appeared on the paper.

The northern road.

It traced itself past the mill, over the bridge, and into the hills. But this time, it did not stop there. The line continued, curling eastward into a region Elias had never been able to draw. Tiny marks emerged: trees, cliffs, a lake shaped like an eye.

Then came the village from Lina’s picture.

Elias sank into his chair.

For seventeen years, the road had vanished with the dawn.

Now, under the clear light of morning, it remained.

“When can we leave?” Lina asked.

“We?”

“You don’t know the way without the compass. I don’t know the way without the map.”

Elias looked at the lines. Somewhere at their end, his brother might be alive.

Or something might be waiting that knew how to write roads in a dead man’s hand.

“We leave at noon,” he said.

They passed through the northern gate beneath a sky the color of old iron. Elias carried the map in a leather case, along with ink, pens, measuring cord, and enough food for a week. Lina carried a knife, the brass compass, and an impatience that kept her several steps ahead.

Beyond the city, the old road began as a muddy track between wheat fields. After five miles, it narrowed. By evening, they reached the abandoned mill.

Its wheel turned slowly, though the stream beneath it had dried to stones.

“Was it like this before?” Lina asked.

“No.”

They slept in the mill. During the night, Elias woke to the sound of footsteps crossing the floor below. They moved from one end of the building to the other, steady and soft, as if invisible travelers were following a road through the walls.

At dawn, Elias checked the map.

The mill had disappeared from it.

“We should go back,” he said.

Lina was tightening her boots. “You don’t mean that.”

“No,” he admitted.

They reached the stone bridge in the afternoon.

The river below ran backward.

Leaves and broken branches drifted upstream, climbing toward the mountains. Lina crouched at the bank and dipped her fingers into the water.

“It’s warm,” she said.

Elias examined the bridge. Hundreds of names were carved into its stones. Some were fresh; others had been softened by centuries of weather.

Near the center, he found his own name.

**ELIAS VALE**

Beneath it, in smaller letters, someone had carved:

**TURN BACK**

Lina saw it over his shoulder.

“Your brother?”

“Perhaps.”

“Will you listen?”

Elias touched the carved letters. Tomas had always formed his E with three slanted strokes. This inscription had the same habit.

“No,” he said. “He would know I wouldn’t.”

They crossed.

On the other side, the world changed.

Birdsong stopped. The sky brightened, though the sun was setting behind them. Wildflowers opened as they passed and closed when they looked back. The road became pale and smooth, untouched by wheel ruts or rain.

According to the map, the path should have gone east around a hill. Instead, it divided into three.

The compass pointed toward the middle road.

The map showed only the left.

“Which do we trust?” Lina asked.

Elias studied the ground. The left path bore faint footprints. The middle was empty. On the right lay a child’s red ribbon, clean and bright.

“None of them,” he said.

He took out his pen and drew a fourth road along the map’s lower edge.

The grass before them bent. A new path appeared, narrow but clear, descending between the others.

Lina stared. “Can all cartographers do that?”

“Only when the land is confused.”

“And is it?”

“Very.”

They followed the new path into the hills.

That night, they camped beneath a leafless tree. Elias could not sleep. The stars were in the wrong places, arranged in spirals rather than constellations.

Lina sat beside the fire, turning the compass in her hands.

“Your mother,” Elias said. “What did she tell you about Tomas?”

“That he was kind. That he laughed too loudly. That he cheated at cards.”

“He did.”

“She said he saved her life.”

Elias waited.

“There were twelve travelers,” Lina continued. “They took the northern road during a storm. Near the bridge, the world went dark. When the light returned, they were somewhere else.”

“The village?”

“Not at first. They wandered for weeks. The roads kept changing. If they tried to go south, they arrived farther north. If they crossed a hill, they found themselves at the bottom of it again.”

“How did your mother escape?”

“She was injured. Tomas carried her to a gate they found in the forest. It opened only once. He pushed her through before it closed.”

“Why didn’t he follow?”

Lina looked into the flames. “There wasn’t time.”

Elias imagined his brother as he had been: twenty-three, careless and smiling, always arriving late and leaving early. Elias had spent years thinking of Tomas as lost.

He had never thought of him as choosing to remain.

At dawn, voices woke them.

A procession moved along the road: men and women in old-fashioned clothing, some on foot, others leading thin horses. None looked at Elias or Lina. Their faces were gray with exhaustion.

“Travelers,” Lina whispered.

A boy at the end of the procession dropped a wooden toy.

Lina moved to retrieve it, but Elias caught her arm.

“Wait.”

The boy turned.

His face had no features. No eyes, no mouth—only smooth skin.

The other travelers stopped.

One by one, they turned their blank faces toward the camp.

Elias snatched the map and drew a circle around their tree.

At once, the procession vanished. The road was empty. The wooden toy remained, half buried in dust.

They did not speak again until noon.

On the fourth day, they reached the lake shaped like an eye. Its black water reflected not the sky above but a bright summer afternoon. In the reflection, Elias saw himself standing on the shore beside Tomas.

His brother had not aged.

“Do you see him?” Lina asked.

“Yes.”

She stared into the water. Tears filled her eyes.

“My mother,” she whispered.

Elias saw only Tomas. His brother smiled from beneath the surface and raised one hand.

Then the reflection changed.

Elias saw his shop. Dust covered the counter. The windows were boarded shut. On the wall hung a notice declaring him missing.

The image shifted again. Lina was back in the city, older now, sitting alone beside a hospital bed. A gray-haired woman slept beneath white blankets.

The lake offered futures as easily as memories.

“Don’t look,” Elias said.

But Lina stepped into the water.

He seized her coat and pulled her back. Beneath the surface, pale hands reached upward.

Lina struggled. “She’s there!”

“No. Only her reflection.”

“She was calling me!”

“She wasn’t moving her lips.”

Lina froze.

The hands sank. The summer sky in the lake shattered into ripples.

They walked around the shore without looking at the water again.

By evening, the village appeared below them.

It rested in a green valley, surrounded by black hills. Small houses clustered around a square. Smoke rose from chimneys. Yellow lamps glowed behind windows, though the sky was still bright.

The moment Elias saw it, the map began to burn.

A flame raced along the parchment’s edge. He dropped it and stamped it out, but the roads had already blackened. Only the village remained, drawn in brown ink at the center.

The compass needle pointed downhill.

They entered through an arch of white stone.

People emerged from the houses.

There were fewer than Elias expected: nine men and women, dressed in patched clothes. Some appeared no older than twenty; others had gray hair and bent backs. They stared at the newcomers in silence.

Then a man stepped from the crowd.

Elias knew him at once.

Tomas’s hair was longer, and a white scar crossed his left cheek, but otherwise he looked almost unchanged. He still had the same crooked smile, the same restless hands.

“Eli,” he said.

Elias had imagined this moment for seventeen years. In some versions, he struck his brother for disappearing. In others, he embraced him. Sometimes he said something wise enough to repay all the silent years.

Instead, he said, “You look terrible.”

Tomas laughed too loudly.

They held each other in the village square.

When they parted, Tomas looked at Lina. His smile faded.

“You have Mara’s eyes.”

“Where is she?” Lina asked.

Tomas glanced at Elias.

“Your mother isn’t here.”

“She escaped.”

“Yes.”

“Then why did her compass lead me to this place?”

“Because it isn’t her compass.”

Tomas turned it over and pressed his thumb against the engraved initials. The brass back sprang open, revealing a tiny wheel of black metal.

“I made this compass to find the road,” he said. “Not the way home. When the village feels the gate weakening, the needle wakes.”

“The village feels?” Elias asked.

Tomas looked toward the houses.

“This place is alive.”

He took them inside the largest building. Maps covered its walls—hundreds of them, drawn on parchment, cloth, wood, even pieces of broken furniture. Every map showed different roads around the valley.

None showed a way out.

“The village feeds on direction,” Tomas explained. “It draws in roads, paths, travelers. Once they arrive, it folds the land around them. Most wander until they forget where they came from. When their memories are gone, the village takes those too.”

“The faceless travelers,” Lina said.

Tomas nodded. “What remains of earlier victims.”

Elias examined the maps. “But Mara escaped.”

“Seventeen years ago, we found the heart of the village beneath the square. I opened a gate using the compass. It lasted only a few seconds.”

“You could do it again,” Lina said.

“I’ve tried. The village learned from me.”

A bell rang outside.

Every lamp went dark.

Tomas went pale. “It knows why you’re here.”

The building shuddered. Maps tore free from the walls and twisted in the air like birds. Outside, doors slammed. The streets began to move, cobblestones flowing past the windows like river water.

“Get to the square!” Tomas shouted.

They ran outside.

The village folded around them. Houses stretched taller. Alleys narrowed and vanished. From every doorway emerged faceless figures.

Elias opened his map case. The original map was ruined, but he carried blank sheets. He spread one on the ground and began to draw.

“What are you doing?” Lina shouted.

“Reminding the village what it is.”

He drew the square, the houses, the arch, and the streets as they had been when they arrived. With every line, the moving buildings slowed.

But the ink faded almost immediately.

“The village is stronger than the map!” Tomas said.

“No,” Elias replied. “The map isn’t true enough.”

He tore it in half.

For years, he had believed maps recorded land: rivers, mountains, borders, roads. But places were more than their shapes. Saint Orra was not a pattern of streets; it was the smell of morning bread, bells before sunrise, rain on red roofs. The northern road was not dirt and stone; it was Tomas leaving on a gray morning, waving without turning around.

A true map did not show where things were.

It showed where they belonged.

Elias dipped his pen into the ink.

On the blank parchment, he drew no village. He drew the northern gate of Saint Orra. The abandoned mill. The stone bridge with a river flowing in the proper direction. The wheat fields beyond the city.

Then he drew twelve small figures walking home.

The ground cracked.

A sound rose beneath them, deep and furious. The village understood what he had done. Houses bent inward. Windows opened like mouths. The faceless travelers rushed across the square.

“Now!” Elias shouted.

Tomas placed the brass compass at the center of the map.

Its needle spun.

Lina knelt beside them. “It needs a direction!”

“There is no north here,” Tomas said.

“Not north.” Lina pulled a small object from beneath her coat: a house key tied to a blue cord. “Home.”

She laid it across the compass.

The needle stopped.

It pointed south.

A line of golden light spread from the map across the square, cutting through the cobblestones. The white arch at the village entrance filled with rain and darkness.

Beyond it lay the northern road.

The villagers ran toward the gate. The faceless figures followed.

Elias held the pen against the parchment, keeping the road open as the map smoked beneath his hand.

One traveler crossed. Then another.

The village shook.

Three more escaped.

A house collapsed, blocking half the square.

Lina helped an old man over the stones. Tomas dragged a woman toward the gate. Elias continued drawing the same line as fast as the ink vanished.

“Eli!” Tomas shouted. “Come on!”

The final villager passed through.

Lina followed.

Tomas waited beside the arch.

The map caught fire.

Elias stood, but the ground opened under him. He fell hard. His pen rolled away.

The golden road flickered.

Tomas ran back.

“No!” Elias shouted.

His brother seized his arm and pulled him up as the square broke apart. Beneath the stones was not earth, but darkness filled with countless pale roads, winding endlessly through nothing.

They raced toward the arch.

The gate began to close.

For one terrible instant, Elias knew the pattern. Tomas would save him, as he had saved Mara. He would push Elias through and remain behind.

Elias caught his brother’s coat.

“Together,” he said.

They jumped.

The arch vanished behind them.

Elias landed in mud beneath cold rain. Around him, the rescued travelers lay scattered across the northern road. Lina knelt nearby, laughing and crying at once.

Tomas rolled onto his back.

Above them stretched an ordinary gray sky.

“How long?” one of the travelers asked.

No one answered.

They reached Saint Orra the next evening.

For the city, seventeen years had passed. For some of the travelers, only months had gone by. For others, decades. Families were reunited, though not always as they had hoped. Parents found children older than themselves. Lovers discovered graves. One woman learned that her house had become a tailor’s shop and asked for a room above it.

Lina returned to her mother.

Mara had grown ill while her daughter was away, but she recovered in spring. When Tomas visited, she slapped him, embraced him, and then slapped him again.

As for Elias, he reopened his shop.

Tomas moved into the rooms above it and began making compasses, though he refused to repair clocks. Lina became Elias’s apprentice. She had little patience for coastlines and drew mountains too large, but she understood that a map could be accurate without being true, and true without being accurate.

Years later, travelers still came with unusual requests.

“Can you map the place where I was happiest?” they asked.

“Yes,” Elias would say.

“Can you show me the road I should have taken?”

“Perhaps.”

“Can you find something that wishes to remain lost?”

At this, he was more careful.

Each morning, before the bells of Saint Orra announced the hour, Elias opened his shop and examined a particular map.

It showed the northern road leaving the city, passing the old mill, crossing the stone bridge, and winding through peaceful hills. The road no longer vanished at sunrise.

But sometimes, in the blue light before dawn, a tiny village appeared at the far edge of the parchment.

Its windows were yellow.

Its streets moved when Elias looked away.

Whenever it appeared, he took up his pen and drew a wall around it. Lina added a river. Tomas placed a compass on the map, its needle pointing home.

Then the bells rang, the city woke, and the village faded.

Yet the three of them never forgot that somewhere beyond the borders of ordinary places, it was still waiting for another road.

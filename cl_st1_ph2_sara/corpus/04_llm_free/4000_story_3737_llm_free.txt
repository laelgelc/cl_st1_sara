# The Museum of Unfinished Things

On the morning Mara Vale turned thirty-seven, a parcel arrived containing half a wooden bird.

It lay inside a narrow box cushioned with yellow tissue paper. The bird had one wing, one painted eye, and a beak pointed toward some destination the rest of its body had never reached. Beneath it was a card.

**For the museum, if you still have room.**

There was no name and no return address.

Mara carried the bird downstairs to the Museum of Unfinished Things, which occupied the ground floor of her narrow brick house. She had opened the museum eight years earlier, after her father’s death, using the money he left her and the objects he did not.

At first, the collection had consisted entirely of his abandoned projects: a chair with three legs, a violin without strings, a map of a country that did not exist, and twelve clocks that had faces but no hands. Her father, Elias Vale, had been a carpenter by profession and an inventor by temperament. He loved beginnings. He approached them with sawdust in his hair and thunder in his voice.

“Look, Mara,” he would say. “This will change everything.”

Nothing ever did.

After he died, she could not bring herself to throw away the things he had failed to complete. So she arranged them in the empty workshop, attached small labels, and opened the door.

People came.

Some laughed at the three-legged chair. Some stood for an hour before the handless clocks. Soon visitors began bringing their own unfinished things: a sweater missing one sleeve, the first chapter of a novel, a wedding dress without buttons, an unsent letter folded so often its edges had turned soft as cloth.

Mara accepted almost everything. Her only rule was that an object had to carry evidence of honest intention. A blank canvas was not unfinished, she told visitors. A canvas with one blue stroke across it might be.

The half-bird clearly qualified.

She placed it in the central gallery beneath a glass dome. On a card, she wrote:

**Wooden bird. Artist unknown. Date unknown. Flight incomplete.**

At ten o’clock, she unlocked the front door.

The museum’s first visitor that day was a boy named Kit, who came every Wednesday while his mother worked at the bakery across the street. He was eleven, solemn, and small for his age. His dark hair looked as if it had been trimmed with kitchen scissors, which it had.

“What’s new?” he asked.

Mara pointed to the glass dome.

Kit examined the bird from every side.

“It looks cut in half.”

“It may never have had another half.”

“That’s worse.”

“Why?”

“Because if it was cut in half, at least the other part might be somewhere.”

Mara considered this. Kit often said things that rearranged the furniture in her mind.

“Do you know who sent it?” he asked.

“No.”

“Maybe it’s a threat.”

“A wooden bird?”

“Threats can be symbolic.”

“What would it mean?”

Kit narrowed his eyes. “Finish something, or else.”

Mara smiled, but uneasily.

The museum closed at six. At half past six, as Mara turned the sign in the window, she noticed a man standing across the street beneath the bakery awning.

He was tall and gray-haired, with a green scarf wound twice around his neck. For a moment she thought he was watching her. Then a bus passed between them, and when it was gone, so was he.

That evening, Mara ate soup in the upstairs kitchen. Rain ticked against the windows. The half-bird occupied her thoughts with unreasonable persistence.

She tried to imagine who might have carved it. The surface bore fine parallel marks, probably made by a narrow chisel. The paint around its eye had faded into a delicate ring of blue. A brass pin protruded from the flat side of its body, suggesting the missing half had once been attached—or was meant to be.

At midnight, unable to sleep, Mara returned to the gallery.

The bird was no longer facing east.

She was certain she had arranged its beak toward the front door. Now it pointed toward the rear of the museum, where a curtain concealed the stairs to the cellar.

Mara stared at it.

Old floorboards shifted. Vibrations traveled. Buildings breathed. There were a dozen reasonable explanations, and she believed none of them.

She lifted the glass dome and turned the bird toward the door.

“Stay,” she told it.

In the morning, it was pointing toward the cellar again.

Kit arrived at nine fifty-eight and found Mara standing over the display with her arms crossed.

“It moved,” she said.

“Things don’t move by themselves.”

“I know.”

“Did you ask what it wants?”

“It’s half a bird.”

“That isn’t a no.”

Mara glanced toward the cellar curtain.

The cellar had once been her father’s storage room. She had not entered it since the week after his funeral. The door at the bottom of the stairs was locked, and the key remained in an envelope in her bedroom drawer.

As a child, Mara had believed the cellar was where her father kept completed things. This theory arose because she never saw him carry anything down there. Adults, she reasoned, were secretive about success and noisy about failure.

Years later she learned that the room contained lumber, old tools, jars of nails, and boxes of household papers. After Elias died, she went through some of it, became overwhelmed, locked the door, and did not return.

Now the bird pointed there.

“I’ll look tonight,” she said.

“Why not now?”

“The museum is open.”

“I’ll guard it.”

“Against whom?”

Kit looked around at the scattered visitors: an elderly couple whispering beside an unglazed clay pot, a student sketching the three-legged chair, and a woman silently reading the unsent letter.

“Finishers,” he said.

At six fifteen, armed with the key and a torch, Mara descended the stairs. Kit had gone home, after extracting a promise that she would telephone the bakery if she discovered bones.

The cellar door resisted at first. Damp had swollen the wood. Mara put her shoulder against it and pushed until it opened with a groan.

The room smelled of stone, dust, and cedar shavings. Shelves crowded the walls. Long boards rested on brackets near the ceiling. Her father’s tools hung above the workbench, each outlined in pencil, as if he had expected the objects to disappear but wanted their ghosts to remain.

Mara swept the torch beam across boxes labeled **TAX**, **WINTER**, **MARA—SCHOOL**, and **IDEAS, GOOD**.

There was no box labeled **IDEAS, BAD**.

On the bench lay a rectangle of clean wood amid the dust.

Something had recently been removed from it.

Mara stepped closer. The clear patch was the size of a narrow parcel.

Behind the bench, pinned to the wall, she found a photograph. It showed her father as a young man, standing beside someone whose face had been cut away. Elias was laughing. In his hands he held a wooden bird.

A whole one.

Mara took the photograph upstairs.

The next morning, she showed it to Kit.

He studied the jagged white space beside Elias. “Someone didn’t want you to know who that is.”

“Or the person cut themselves out.”

“Why?”

“Maybe they disliked the photograph.”

“Then why not throw away the whole thing?”

Mara had no answer.

She spent the afternoon searching the museum’s records. When she founded the collection, she had cataloged her father’s abandoned work from memory, but he had kept notebooks of his own. Most were chaotic lists of measurements, overheard sentences, and inventions such as **umbrella for two people who are quarreling**.

In a notebook dated thirty-nine years earlier, she found sketches of birds.

There were dozens: wings open, wings closed, wings made of overlapping slats. On one page, two halves of a bird were drawn separately. Beneath them Elias had written:

**Made in partnership. Neither half complete alone. Mechanism works only when joined. S. says this is sentimental nonsense. S. is right but likes it anyway.**

On the following page, a single line appeared:

**She took her half. Fair enough.**

Mara read it twice.

Her mother’s name had been Helen. She had died when Mara was six, and Elias rarely spoke about her. There had been no other family, as far as Mara knew. No aunt or uncle, no grandparents beyond a few faded names.

Who was S.?

That evening, the man in the green scarf returned.

He stood across the street in the blue dusk, looking directly at the museum. Mara hurried outside, but he began walking away.

“Excuse me!”

He stopped at the corner.

“Did you send the bird?” she called.

The man turned. His face was long and tired, his eyebrows white.

“No,” he said.

“Do you know who did?”

“Yes.”

A cyclist rang a bell and swerved around Mara. By the time she reached the corner, the man had taken a folded piece of paper from his pocket.

“She asked me to give you this if you followed me,” he said.

“If I followed you?”

“She wasn’t sure you would.”

Mara accepted the note.

“Who are you?”

“Simon.”

“S?”

He looked surprised. “No. S was my wife.”

“Was?”

“She died three weeks ago.”

The street seemed to tilt slightly beneath Mara’s feet.

“What was her name?”

“Silvie Arden.”

Mara waited for recognition, but the name meant nothing.

Simon tucked his hands into his coat. “You should read the note.”

It contained an address and one sentence.

**Bring the bird before Thursday, if you want to see it fly.**

Thursday was the next day.

The address led to a railway cottage thirty miles north of town. Mara closed the museum for the first time in four years. She left a sign apologizing for the interruption and found Kit waiting beside it with a backpack.

“No,” she said.

“My mother says I can go.”

“No.”

“She packed sandwiches.”

Mara looked through the bakery window. Kit’s mother waved and held up a thermos.

“This may be a private family matter,” Mara said.

“You don’t know if she was family.”

“Exactly.”

“Then you need a witness.”

In the end, Kit came.

They took the nine twenty train north. Rain streaked the windows, blurring fields and villages into a gray-green wash. Mara kept the half-bird wrapped in tissue inside her bag.

“What if the other half is evil?” Kit asked through a mouthful of cheese sandwich.

“Why would it be evil?”

“Things separated for years usually have a reason.”

“That is not universally true.”

“It is in stories.”

“This isn’t a story.”

Kit looked out at the rain. “That’s what people always say in stories.”

The cottage stood alone beside an abandoned railway platform. Simon opened the door before they knocked.

Inside, every room was filled with birds.

They perched on shelves, crowded windowsills, hung from the ceiling, and stood in flocks across the floor. Birds made of tin, clay, wire, glass, paper, and bone. Some had wheels. Some had locks. One enormous heron appeared to be constructed from silver spoons.

“My wife was a sculptor,” Simon said.

“I gathered that,” Mara replied.

“She preferred the word maker.”

He led them into a bright back room. An unfinished mobile turned slowly overhead, disturbed by the warmth of the stove.

On a table sat the other half of the wooden bird.

Its single eye was painted blue. Its wing swept backward. A small socket in its flat side matched the brass pin on Mara’s half.

She did not touch it.

“Why did Silvie have this?” she asked.

“Because she made it.”

“With my father?”

Simon nodded. “They met at art school. They were engaged for a while.”

Mara felt an unexpected stab of anger, as if her father had concealed something not merely important but rightfully hers.

“What happened?”

“Life, I suppose. Pride. Timing. The usual machinery.” Simon poured tea into three mismatched cups. “They built the bird for an exhibition. Silvie designed the wings. Elias carved the body and made the mechanism. The night before the exhibition, they quarreled.”

“About what?”

“Whether it was finished.”

Kit nodded gravely, as if this were the most sensible reason for two people to quarrel.

“Elias said it needed paint,” Simon continued. “Silvie said paint would ruin the wood. He painted one eye to prove his point. She painted the other out of spite. Then the argument spread into every corner of their lives. By morning they had ended their engagement and divided the bird.”

“That’s absurd.”

“Yes,” Simon said. “Most decisive moments are.”

Mara looked at the two halves across the table. “Did they ever speak again?”

“Once. At a funeral, years later. By then Elias had married your mother, and Silvie had married me.”

“You knew about him?”

“Silvie told me before we married. A previous love is not a crime.”

Mara traced a stain on the tabletop. “Why send me the bird now?”

“In her last days, Silvie spoke of unfinished business. I assumed she meant practical things—papers, debts, the leaking roof. Then she asked me to find Elias. I learned he had died, but I found the museum.” Simon smiled faintly. “She was delighted by the idea of it.”

“So she mailed her half?”

“No. She asked me to deliver it. When I came here to pack it, the table was empty.”

Mara looked up.

“The bird disappeared?”

“The morning after she died.”

Kit gave Mara a triumphant look.

Simon continued. “Two weeks later, I received a note in Silvie’s handwriting. It said the bird had gone to find its other half and that I should follow.”

“That’s impossible,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

“You don’t seem troubled by it.”

“I spent thirty-four years married to Silvie. My idea of impossible has become flexible.”

Mara unwrapped the half-bird and placed it on the table. The two pieces lay six inches apart.

Nothing happened.

Kit leaned forward. “Maybe they need privacy.”

Simon stepped back. Mara did the same.

Still nothing.

She felt foolish, disappointed, and obscurely betrayed. Of course the bird could not move. The shifting display at the museum, the missing object, the note from a dead woman—each mystery would have an explanation, even if she never found it.

She reached for Silvie’s half.

At that moment, both pieces trembled.

They slid across the table and joined with a sharp wooden click.

The bird shuddered. Hidden slats unfolded from its sides, spreading into layered wings. Its tail opened like a fan. Inside the body, a mechanism began to tick.

Once.

Twice.

Then the wings moved.

Kit laughed.

The bird beat its wooden wings and rose unsteadily from the table. It flew in a crooked circle around the room, knocking against the mobile and scattering paper swallows. Simon covered his mouth. Mara stood frozen as it passed her face, its blue eyes bright in the window light.

For one impossible minute, it flew.

Then the mechanism slowed. The bird descended and landed on the table with a small, final tap.

No one spoke.

Kit was the first to move. He examined the bird without touching it.

“Now it’s finished,” he said.

Mara looked at the uneven paint, the visible seam, and the wings extended in permanent effort.

“No,” she said. “Now it’s whole.”

Simon began to cry quietly.

They stayed at the cottage until evening. Simon showed Mara photographs of Silvie and Elias at art school: young, paint-stained, theatrical, and certain that life would wait for them to decide what it meant. In one picture, Silvie held a chisel like a sword. In another, Elias wore the wooden bird on his head.

Mara recognized her father’s grin. She also recognized something of herself in Silvie’s steady gaze, though there was no blood between them.

Before Mara left, Simon offered her the bird.

“It belongs in the museum,” he said.

She shook her head. “The museum is for unfinished things.”

“Nothing is ever as finished as people claim.”

“That sounds like something Silvie would say.”

“It was.”

Mara considered the bird. “Then it should belong to both of us.”

They agreed it would spend half the year at the cottage and half at the museum. Kit proposed transporting it every six months by train, with ceremonial sandwiches.

Back in town, Mara unlocked the museum after dark. The familiar objects waited in the shadows: the handless clocks, the sleeveless sweater, the unglazed pot, the map of nowhere.

For years she had believed she understood them. Unfinished things were evidence of failure, yes, but also of hope. To begin was to imagine a future in which completion remained possible.

Now she saw something else.

Some things were unfinished because a person had stopped. Others because two people had walked away carrying separate halves. Time did not complete such things. Neither did regret. They waited not for more work, but for reunion.

Mara placed the whole bird beneath the glass dome. She removed the old label and wrote a new one.

**Wooden bird, made by Elias Vale and Silvie Arden. Separated for thirty-nine years. Rejoined on Thursday. Capable of brief flight.**

She hesitated, then added:

**On loan.**

Kit, peering over her shoulder, approved.

The next morning, a line formed before the museum opened. News had traveled from the bakery, where Kit’s mother had apparently promised secrecy to everyone individually.

Visitors gathered around the glass dome. They asked Mara to make the bird fly, but it did not move. She turned it over, searched for a key or switch, and found nothing. The hidden mechanism had become silent.

Some people doubted the story. Others believed it immediately. A little girl pressed both palms to the glass and whispered encouragement.

Near noon, Simon arrived without his green scarf. He brought a box of Silvie’s unfinished work: a copper nest without eggs, a clay owl lacking a face, and plans for a mechanical flock she had designed but never built.

Mara made space.

Over the following months, she began going through the boxes in the cellar. She found no further miracles, only papers, tools, receipts, and memories. These were more difficult to sort.

She repaired the swollen door. She cleaned her father’s workbench. With Kit’s assistance, she completed the fourth leg of the chair, though they made it deliberately shorter than the others.

“Does it still belong in the museum?” Kit asked.

Mara sat on it. The chair rocked dangerously.

“Absolutely.”

In spring, she and Simon carried the bird to the cottage by train. They packed sandwiches for the journey. At the cottage, Simon placed it beneath the unfinished mobile, and for a moment the paper birds turned above it as if stirred by invisible wings.

Before leaving, Mara noticed something on the worktable.

Two pieces of pale wood lay side by side. One had been roughly carved into a feather. The other still showed the pencil outline of a wing.

“Are those Silvie’s?” she asked.

Simon shook his head. “Mine.”

“I didn’t know you were a maker.”

“Neither did I.”

On the return journey, Kit fell asleep against the window. Mara watched the countryside pass: fields divided by hedges, rivers divided by bridges, towns gathered around stations where people met and parted.

She thought of her father, who had spent his life beginning things. For years she had judged him for it. Then she had forgiven him. Now both judgment and forgiveness seemed too simple. A life could not be measured only by what it finished.

When they reached town, evening light filled the museum windows. Mara unlocked the door, though it was past closing time.

Inside, the empty glass dome waited in the central gallery.

She did not put anything beneath it.

Instead, she wrote a small card and placed it where the bird had been.

**Currently elsewhere. Expected to return.**

Then she opened the museum doors and let the spring air in.

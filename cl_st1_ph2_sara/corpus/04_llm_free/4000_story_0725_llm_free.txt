# The Cartographer of Lost Hours

Every morning at seven, Mara Vale unlocked the narrow blue door between the bakery and the watchmaker’s shop, climbed thirty-nine steps, and mapped the hours people had lost.

The office above the street was small enough that she could touch both walls if she stretched. It contained a desk, two chairs, a brass kettle, and a cabinet with hundreds of shallow drawers. On the window, painted in gold letters, were the words:

**MARA VALE — CARTOGRAPHER**  
**LOST HOURS RECOVERED WHEN POSSIBLE**

Most people assumed she made ordinary maps.

They entered carrying directions scribbled on envelopes and asked for the road to Bellweather, or the shortest route through the northern hills. Mara would politely send them to the surveyor across the square.

Her true customers arrived quietly.

They brought gaps.

A woman might say she had gone into the garden at noon and returned at dusk with dirt beneath her nails, unable to remember where the afternoon had gone. A man might confess that every Tuesday between three and four, he found himself standing by the river with tears on his face. Children came with missing minutes. The elderly came with missing decades.

Mara listened. Then she asked questions.

What could they smell before the hour vanished? Which way were the shadows pointing? Did they hear bells, music, rain? Had they recently broken a promise?

From their answers she drew maps in black ink on thin white paper. Lost hours had terrain like any other country. Regret formed marshes. Fear raised steep mountains. Repeated thoughts became roads. At the center of each map, if Mara worked carefully, she could mark the place where the lost hour waited.

Recovery was not guaranteed.

Some hours did not wish to be found.

On a rainy morning in late October, Mara arrived to find a boy sleeping against her blue door.

He looked about twelve. His coat was too large, its cuffs dark with rain. In his arms he held a square wooden box.

When Mara touched his shoulder, he woke at once.

“Are you the cartographer?”

“I am.”

“My father has lost a year.”

Mara glanced toward the bakery. The ovens had just been opened, and warm air smelling of cinnamon drifted into the wet street.

“People misplace years more often than you’d think,” she said.

“This one is different.”

“All lost time is different to the person who lost it.”

The boy frowned. “He didn’t forget it. It disappeared.”

Mara studied him more closely.

“What is your name?”

“Elias Wren.”

“And your father?”

“Jonas Wren. He keeps the clock tower.”

At that, Mara’s hand tightened around her key.

Everyone in the city knew Jonas Wren. He maintained the great clock in the eastern square, the one whose bell governed the markets, courts, schools, and trains. It was said the clock had never lost so much as a second in two hundred years.

Mara opened the door.

“Come upstairs.”

Elias sat in the customer’s chair while Mara lit the stove. He placed the wooden box on the desk but kept both hands around it.

“When did the year disappear?” she asked.

“Last night.”

“A whole year in one night?”

“Yes.”

“Which year?”

“Next year.”

The kettle began to whistle.

Mara did not move.

Elias opened the box.

Inside lay a clockwork bird no larger than a sparrow. Its silver wings were folded against its body. Where its eyes should have been were two empty sockets, and around one delicate leg was tied a paper label.

Mara leaned closer.

On the label, in Jonas Wren’s cramped handwriting, were six words:

**THIS BIRD HAS SEEN THE MISSING YEAR.**

“Where is your father?” Mara asked.

“In the tower. He won’t come down.”

“Can he speak?”

“Only about things that have already happened. If I ask what he wants for breakfast, he tells me what he ate yesterday. If I ask whether he is ill, he says he had a fever when he was eight. It’s as if his future has been cut away.”

Mara looked toward the cabinet of maps.

“Has the tower clock stopped?”

“No. But it struck thirteen at midnight.”

Outside, wheels hissed over rain-dark stones. Somewhere below, a baker laughed. The ordinary world continued with offensive ease.

Mara took a blank sheet of paper from her desk.

“Tell me everything.”

Elias began with the clockwork bird.

His father had built it in secret over seven years. He called it a chronolark. Ordinary clocks measured time after it arrived, but the bird, he claimed, could hear time approaching. On clear evenings he carried it to the tower roof and wound the key beneath its left wing. It would tilt its head toward tomorrow and sing notes no human throat could make.

Three nights ago, the bird had stopped singing.

The following night, it tore out its own jeweled eyes.

And last night, just before midnight, it flew directly into the face of the great clock.

“Into the glass?” Mara asked.

“Through it.”

“That is impossible.”

“I know.”

“Go on.”

Elias had followed his father up the tower stairs. When they reached the mechanism room, the bird was perched among the gears. Its silver beak pointed at a darkness between two turning wheels.

Jonas whispered, “There should be a year there.”

Then the bell struck thirteen.

Every candle went out. For one breath, Elias heard thousands of voices speaking at once. When the lights returned, the bird lay broken on the floor, and his father could no longer imagine anything beyond the final day of December.

Mara asked question after question. What had the voices said? Elias did not know. What did the darkness smell like? Snow and burnt sugar. Were the gears turning clockwise? All except one. Had Jonas broken a promise?

At this, the boy hesitated.

“Everyone has,” Mara said.

“My father promised my mother he would stop trying to predict the future.”

“Where is your mother?”

“She died six years ago.”

“Before or after the promise?”

“After.”

Mara dipped her pen in ink.

The first line she drew curved like the edge of a clock face. Then came a stairway, a broken bridge, and a forest of bare black trees. Near the northern margin, she drew a small bird without eyes.

The map resisted her.

Ink beaded on the paper and rolled away. Roads shifted when she looked aside. The river she drew flowed upward and erased its own source.

After an hour, only one feature remained stable: a circular emptiness at the center of the page.

Mara touched it with one finger.

The paper was cold.

“This isn’t a map of your father’s lost year,” she said.

Elias looked alarmed. “Then whose is it?”

“Everyone’s.”

They crossed the city beneath a low ceiling of clouds.

The clock tower rose above the eastern square, a black stone needle threaded through mist. Merchants glanced nervously at its four faces. Though the hands appeared correct, the minute hand shuddered each time it moved, as if forcing its way through thick ice.

Elias unlocked a side door and led Mara inside.

They climbed.

The public stairs ended at the bell chamber, but Elias took a hidden passage behind a wooden panel. The steps there were narrow and furred with dust. Higher still, the stone walls gave way to the iron skeleton of the clock itself.

Jonas Wren sat on the floor among the gears.

He was a broad man with white in his beard and oil on his hands. Around him lay hundreds of pages filled with calculations. On each page, the equations ended at the same date: December thirty-first.

“Father,” Elias said.

Jonas looked up. “You were born during a thunderstorm.”

“I brought the cartographer.”

“Your mother wanted to name you Samuel.”

“This is Mara Vale.”

Jonas turned to her. His eyes were red from sleeplessness.

“Mara Vale found her first lost hour when she was nine,” he said. “It was beneath her sister’s bed.”

Mara went still.

“That is not common knowledge.”

“Your sister had hidden there during the fire.”

“Stop.”

“You searched for her until dawn.”

Mara stepped toward him. “How do you know that?”

Jonas’s face twisted with effort. “I knew it tomorrow.”

Silence filled the mechanism room, broken only by the heavy clicking of gears.

Mara had indeed lost an hour at nine years old. Her family’s house had burned. She remembered smoke, shouting, and then standing in the street at dawn, watching flames consume the roof. Her younger sister, Lysa, was never found.

Years later, when Mara learned to chart missing time, she had mapped that night. The map always led beneath Lysa’s bed, but whenever Mara tried to enter the lost hour, she woke elsewhere with ash in her mouth.

Jonas picked up one of his pages.

“The chronolark looked into the coming year,” he said. “It saw no hours.”

“You can speak of the future now,” Elias whispered.

“No. Only of the knowledge I acquired before it vanished.”

Mara knelt beside the largest gear. Between its teeth was a sliver of absolute darkness.

She held her map near it. The blank circle at the center widened.

“Time hasn’t been destroyed,” she said. “It has been removed.”

“By whom?” Elias asked.

“Not whom. What.”

The clock struck nine.

On the ninth blow, the darkness between the gears opened like an eye.

The mechanism room disappeared.

Mara stood beneath a colorless sky.

A vast plain stretched around her, covered with abandoned clocks. Sundials lay face down in the dust. Pocket watches hung from thorn trees. Tall clocks marched in rows toward the horizon, their pendulums still. No hands moved. No bells rang.

Elias stood beside her, clutching the wooden box. Jonas was there too.

Behind them rose a door with no wall around it.

Mara tried the handle. Locked.

“Where are we?” Elias asked.

“The missing year.”

“But it’s empty.”

“No,” Mara said. “It’s waiting to be filled.”

She unfolded her map. Roads now spread from the central circle, crossing one another in an intricate web. One path was marked by tiny silver feathers.

They followed it.

As they walked, shadows appeared around them—faint figures moving through days that had not yet occurred. Mara saw a woman opening a letter, a sailor falling from a mast, two nations signing a treaty, a child taking a first step. The visions flickered and vanished.

“These are possibilities,” Jonas said. “A year is not one road. It is millions.”

“Then why steal it?” Elias asked.

A voice answered from behind them.

“To spare you.”

A girl stood on the path.

She was nine years old, with soot on her face and a burn along one arm. Her dark hair had been cut unevenly at the chin.

Mara forgot how to breathe.

“Lysa.”

The girl smiled.

Elias looked from Mara to the child. “Your sister?”

“No,” Mara said, though she recognized the green ribbon around Lysa’s wrist. “My sister would be thirty-one.”

“Only if she had lived those years,” the girl replied.

Mara’s map trembled in her hands.

Lysa walked closer. “I waited beneath the bed. I called for you.”

“I went back.”

“You were afraid.”

“The ceiling collapsed.”

“You chose the door.”

“I was a child.”

“So was I.”

Each word struck with the clean precision of a bell.

Mara had imagined this accusation all her life. She had built her profession around it. Every hour she returned to others was, in some hidden chamber of her heart, an apology to the one she could not recover.

“You’re not Lysa,” she said.

The girl’s expression emptied.

Her shape unfolded into something tall and thin, dressed in scraps of unfinished days. Its face changed constantly: infant, widow, soldier, king. Behind its eyes burned a pale, patient light.

Jonas whispered, “The Unmaker.”

The creature inclined its head.

“You know me.”

“I suspected,” Jonas said. “Every clockmaker does. Time creates. Therefore something must uncreate.”

The Unmaker moved among the frozen clocks, trailing silence.

“The coming year contains famine,” it said. “War. Flood. Betrayal. I removed it before it could wound you.”

“It also contains births,” Mara replied. “Treaties. First steps.”

“Brief lamps. All extinguished eventually.”

“That does not make them worthless.”

“It makes them cruel.”

The Unmaker touched a thorn tree. The pocket watches hanging from it dissolved into dust.

“I have watched you creatures for longer than your oldest mountains have names. You beg for more time, then use it to suffer. You grieve what is gone and fear what approaches. So I have given you peace. No tomorrow. No loss.”

“No choice,” Jonas said.

“Choice is the instrument by which you injure yourselves.”

Mara looked down at her map. A new route had appeared in red ink. It led past the Unmaker toward a distant hill, where something bright beat beneath the ground.

The heart of the year.

The Unmaker saw her glance.

Its shifting face became Lysa’s again.

“If you restore the year,” it said, “millions will suffer.”

“And if I don’t?”

“No one will suffer after December.”

“Because no one will exist.”

“Existence is merely loss in progress.”

Mara folded the map.

“You sound very certain for something that has never lived through a single hour.”

The creature’s face flickered.

Mara ran.

Jonas seized Elias’s hand and followed. Behind them, the plain split with a roar. Rows of clocks toppled. The silver-feather path twisted toward the hill while shadows gathered on both sides.

Possible disasters flashed around Mara: cities burning, hospital rooms, soldiers in snow. She saw Jonas lying beneath the clock gears. She saw Elias alone at a grave. She saw herself old and blind, still drawing maps no one wanted.

Then she saw gentler fragments: Elias repairing the chronolark; Jonas laughing at a kitchen table; a garden bright with summer rain.

The Unmaker pursued without footsteps.

At the base of the hill stood twelve doors.

Each bore the name of a month.

Only one could be opened, Mara understood, and the year would unfold from that point. Begin incorrectly, and the months would emerge disordered.

Jonas stumbled to a halt. His face had turned gray.

“Which door?” Elias asked.

Mara examined her map.

The red road ended before all twelve.

“No indication,” she said.

The Unmaker approached across the plain, wearing the faces of the dead.

Jonas sank to his knees. “I cannot see forward.”

“You don’t need to,” Mara said.

She turned to Elias. “When did the clock strike thirteen?”

“Midnight.”

“What did you smell?”

“Snow and burnt sugar.”

“What direction did the darkness open?”

“Between the winter gear and the escapement.”

Mara looked at the doors.

Snow suggested January. Burnt sugar suggested the city’s New Year festival, when vendors caramelized almonds in copper pans.

But lost time rarely concealed itself in the obvious place.

“What did the voices say?” she asked.

“I told you, I don’t know.”

“You heard them. Don’t remember the words. Remember the shape.”

Elias closed his eyes.

The ground shook. Cracks raced up the hill.

“Were they asking or answering?” Mara pressed.

“Answering.”

“Were they angry?”

“No.”

“Afraid?”

“No. They were…” His eyes opened. “Counting.”

“To what number?”

“Twelve.”

Mara faced the doors.

Not months, then. Hours.

At midnight, twelve was both ending and beginning.

She ran to the door marked December.

The Unmaker cried out in Lysa’s voice.

“Mara, don’t leave me again!”

Her hand froze above the latch.

For twenty-two years, those words had waited inside her.

She turned.

Lysa stood alone at the foot of the hill. Flames rose behind her. She was coughing, reaching out.

Mara could almost feel the hot floorboards beneath her knees. She remembered crawling through smoke. She remembered Lysa screaming from the bedroom. She remembered a beam crashing between them.

And she remembered something she had buried deeper than fear.

Lysa had not begged Mara to stay.

She had shouted, **Run.**

The Unmaker had built its lie from Mara’s guilt, but guilt was not memory. It was a map drawn by pain, and pain was a poor cartographer.

Mara opened the December door.

Inside was the last second of the stolen year.

It floated in darkness, small and luminous, pulsing like a star.

“Why the end?” Elias asked.

“Because every lost thing is found from its farthest edge,” Mara said. “You begin where it stopped.”

She reached for the second.

The Unmaker struck her.

Cold passed through Mara’s body. She fell into the oldest of her missing hours.

Smoke filled her mouth.

She was nine again, crawling beneath her sister’s bed while fire consumed the walls. But this time Lysa was there, curled against the floor.

Mara took her hand.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

Lysa’s face was calm.

“You were always trying to find me.”

“I should have saved you.”

“You tried.”

“It wasn’t enough.”

“No,” Lysa said. “But it was all you had.”

The room collapsed in sparks.

Mara woke on the hill with the final second clenched in her fist.

The Unmaker towered above her, its many faces cracked.

“You choose suffering,” it said.

“I choose the chance of everything else.”

She opened her hand.

The second began to tick.

Once.

Light raced from the hill across the dead plain. It entered every clock, first as a spark, then as a flood. Pendulums swung. Bells rang. Hands spun backward through the stolen year, from its final second to its first, laying out days, nights, storms, meals, mistakes, kisses, funerals, and mornings.

The sound was enormous.

The Unmaker came apart within it, scattering into scraps of shadow.

Yet its voice remained.

“I will return. Every hour ends.”

Mara stood as the world blazed around her.

“Yes,” she said. “That is why it matters.”

The plain vanished.

She found herself in the mechanism room beneath the great clock. Elias lay beside the broken chronolark. Jonas stood with both hands braced against a gear.

Outside, the bell began to strike.

One.

Two.

The sound rolled over the city. In houses and shops, people paused to listen.

The bell struck twelve.

Then stopped.

For a moment, no one spoke.

Jonas looked at his son. His face changed as some locked door within him opened.

“What would you like for breakfast tomorrow?” Elias asked.

Jonas began to laugh.

“Anything,” he said. “Anything at all.”

Winter passed.

The missing year arrived one hour at a time.

It brought trouble, as years do. The river flooded in March. A factory closed in June. In September, a fever moved through the southern districts.

It also brought cherry blossoms, three public weddings, and the invention of a safer railway brake. Elias turned thirteen and rebuilt the chronolark with blue glass eyes. Jonas kept his promise to his late wife and stopped trying to predict the future, though he continued repairing clocks.

As for Mara, she returned to the narrow office above the bakery.

One spring morning, she took her oldest map from the bottom drawer. Its lines still led toward the hour of the fire, but the path no longer ended beneath Lysa’s bed.

It continued to the front door.

Mara placed the map in the stove.

The paper curled. The ink turned gold, then vanished.

At seven, someone knocked.

A young woman entered, twisting a handkerchief between her fingers.

“I think I’ve lost an afternoon,” she said. “Last Thursday. I can remember lunch and sunset, but nothing in between.”

Mara motioned to the empty chair.

“What could you smell before it vanished?”

“Rain,” the woman said. “And roses, perhaps.”

“Which way were the shadows pointing?”

“I don’t know.”

“That’s all right.”

Mara drew out a clean sheet of paper.

Beyond the window, the clock tower marked the hour. Carts rattled through the square. Bakers lifted warm loaves from their ovens. Children hurried late to school, spending minutes carelessly, as if time were endless.

Mara dipped her pen in ink.

“Start at the beginning,” she said.

And the woman did.

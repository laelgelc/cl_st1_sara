# The Keeper of Lost Hours

At precisely seven minutes past midnight, the clock in Bellweather began to run backward.

Only Mara Venn noticed.

She was awake in the workshop above her father’s abandoned shop, sorting screws by size because sleep had become an unreliable visitor. Outside, rain pressed silver fingers against the windows. Inside, fifty-three clocks ticked at slightly different speeds, filling the room with the sound of dry leaves skittering over stone.

Then, all at once, they stopped.

Mara held her breath.

The clocks resumed, but their hands moved counterclockwise. The little carriage clock on the shelf. The brass marine chronometer. The blue porcelain clock decorated with birds. Even the tall walnut grandfather clock her father had forbidden her to touch.

Tick.

Backward.

Tick.

Backward.

Mara went to the window and looked down at the town square.

Bellweather’s great clock tower stood above the rooftops, black against a purple sky. Its four illuminated faces showed the same impossible time: 12:07.

Then 12:06.

Then 12:05.

A bell rang.

The sound rolled across the sleeping town, deep enough to tremble in Mara’s bones.

From the alley below came a cry.

Old Mrs. Vale, who lived beside the bakery, stumbled into the rain wearing only her nightdress and slippers. She looked up at the tower, raised both hands, and shouted a name Mara had not heard in fifteen years.

“Edmund!”

A man appeared beneath the clock tower.

He was young, although Mrs. Vale was old. He wore a soldier’s uniform from the Northern War, muddy at the knees and torn at one shoulder. He smiled as Mrs. Vale ran to him.

They embraced in the rain.

Mara knew who he was. Everyone in Bellweather knew. Edmund Vale had died before Mara was born, struck by a shell on a battlefield across the sea. His portrait hung above Mrs. Vale’s fireplace: the same narrow face, the same uneven smile.

The clock showed 11:58.

Mara grabbed her coat and hurried downstairs.

By the time she reached the square, other doors had opened. Lamps shone in windows. People emerged into the streets, calling to one another in frightened whispers.

And the dead kept returning.

A little girl in a red coat walked out from behind the fountain and was swept into the arms of a gray-haired man who dropped to his knees, sobbing. A fisherman appeared beside the well, smelling of salt and seaweed, though Bellweather lay fifty miles inland. Two brothers who had died in a mine collapse stood bewildered outside the tavern while their mother, now bent with age, touched their faces.

No one asked how.

Grief does not question a miracle while it is happening.

Mara moved through the crowd until she reached the tower door. It was usually locked, but tonight it stood open.

Inside, the air smelled of iron, dust, and something faintly sweet, like apples stored through winter. A spiral staircase wound upward into darkness.

“Mara.”

She turned.

Her father stood at the edge of the square.

Elias Venn looked exactly as he had the week before his death: tall, thin, his sleeves rolled to the elbow. A pencil rested behind his right ear. There was oil on his fingers.

Mara could not move.

Three years earlier, he had climbed the clock tower to repair a fault in the striking mechanism. A gear had cracked. A platform had given way. He had fallen sixty feet.

Mara had been downstairs when it happened.

For three years she had remembered the terrible sequence: the splintering wood, the crash, the sudden silence.

Now he smiled.

“You haven’t wound the Lemaire,” he said.

It was such an ordinary complaint that Mara laughed. The laugh broke into a sob.

She crossed the space between them and threw her arms around him.

He felt real. His coat was damp from the rain. His chest rose beneath her cheek. She could hear his heart.

“You came back,” she whispered.

“For a little while.”

She pulled away. “What does that mean?”

Elias glanced at the tower. The hands had reached 11:43.

“The clock is returning what it took.”

Mara wiped rain from her face. “The clock didn’t take Mrs. Vale’s husband. Or the miners. Or the drowned fisherman.”

“Not that clock.” He pointed toward the tower. “Time itself.”

Around them, the square filled with reunions. Some were joyful. Others were silent and stunned. The baker stared at a woman Mara recognized as his mother, but neither approached. Near the church steps, Mayor Orra was arguing with a younger version of himself.

“How long do we have?” Mara asked.

“Until the clock reaches midnight again.”

“It’s moving away from midnight.”

“For now.”

A second bell rang.

The clock showed 11:00.

The world shuddered.

Rain leaped from the cobblestones into the sky. Water streamed upward from gutters to clouds. A broken bottle reassembled itself beside the tavern. Footprints vanished as boots stepped backward out of them.

Mara watched a man in the crowd grow younger. White faded from his beard; wrinkles smoothed around his eyes.

Only the returned dead remained unchanged.

Elias took Mara’s hand. “We need to go up.”

“Why?”

“Because this isn’t supposed to happen.”

She tightened her grip. “Then let it.”

His expression softened. “You don’t mean that.”

“All these people have someone back. Mrs. Vale has Edmund. Mr. Salt has his daughter. I have you.”

“And what will they lose in exchange?”

“Why must there be an exchange?”

“Because a clock does not make time. It measures it.”

The great gears groaned overhead.

Mara looked through the tower doorway. Bellweather was changing. Ivy retreated down brick walls. Fresh paint spread across old signs. Shingles lifted from roofs and flew neatly into stacks on the street. The statue in the square lost its green patina and shone bronze beneath the rising rain.

People began to understand.

A woman screamed as the baby in her arms became smaller. An elderly dog bounded across the square, young again, while its owner stared at her own unwrinkled hands. A row of cottages at the town’s edge vanished, each dismantling itself board by board.

The clock was not merely summoning the dead.

It was undoing Bellweather.

Mara followed her father into the tower.

They climbed past the first landing, where ropes hung like vines, and the second, where the bell’s enormous shadow swung across the wall. At the third landing they entered the mechanism chamber.

Mara had not been there since the accident.

The room was wider than the shop below her apartment. Interlocking gears filled it from floor to ceiling: bronze wheels, iron teeth, spinning pinions, chains pouring upward. Moonlight flashed through narrow windows.

At the center stood the escapement, the device that controlled the clock’s motion.

Its pendulum swung backward.

Beneath it, the floor remained broken where Elias had fallen.

Mara stopped at the edge.

For a moment she was twenty-four again, kneeling beside her father’s body. She remembered reaching toward him. She remembered someone pulling her away.

Elias stood on the other side of the gap.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

“You told me the platform was safe.”

“I believed it was.”

“I offered to come with you. You said you didn’t need help.”

“I was proud.”

“You died because you were proud.”

“Yes.”

The simplicity of his answer struck harder than any excuse.

The clock continued its reverse thunder.

“How do we stop it?” Mara asked.

Elias pointed above the escapement. A small silver gear spun faster than the others. Unlike the old, blackened mechanism surrounding it, the gear was clean and bright.

“I put that in before I fell,” he said. “An auxiliary regulator. My own design.”

“You told the council the old regulator could last another century.”

“I lied.”

“Why?”

He looked away. “I wanted them to need me.”

Mara almost smiled. It was such a pathetic, human confession. “And now?”

“The regulator has failed. It’s releasing the hours stored in the mechanism.”

“Stored hours?”

“Moments no one finished. Farewells never spoken. Love never confessed. Regrets held so tightly they became heavier than memory. Bellweather has fed them to this clock for two hundred years.”

“That makes no sense.”

“Most true things don’t, when explained badly.”

The hands outside passed ten in the evening.

A wave of change moved through the tower. Dust flew from the floor and settled onto untouched beams. Rust vanished from chains. The gap in the platform began to close as splintered pieces rose and joined together.

Mara watched the place of her father’s death repair itself.

Elias stepped over the shrinking crack.

“To stop the clock,” he said, “you must remove the silver regulator.”

“Then remove it.”

“I can’t touch it.”

“Why not?”

“I belong to the hours it released.”

Mara looked at the mechanism. The regulator turned near the center, beyond a maze of gears. Reaching it would require climbing along the maintenance frame above the escapement.

The same frame that had failed beneath her father.

“No,” she said.

The clock showed 9:17.

Below, shouts echoed through the square. Some people pleaded for the clock to be stopped. Others guarded their returned loved ones and threatened anyone who approached the tower.

Mara saw Mrs. Vale enter the doorway at the bottom of the stairs, Edmund behind her.

“Don’t you dare!” the old woman cried. “I waited fifty-two years!”

More voices joined hers.

“Leave it alone!”

“Give us one night!”

“My son just came home!”

Elias listened, his face filled with sorrow.

Mara gripped the railing. “What happens if we do nothing?”

“Bellweather will continue backward. By dawn, none of us will have been born. By tomorrow night, there will be no town. Eventually there will be no clock to hold the hours, and they will spread.”

“How far?”

“I don’t know.”

“The whole world?”

“I don’t know.”

Below them, the crowd climbed.

Elias touched Mara’s shoulder. “You must decide quickly.”

She looked at him. At his familiar hands, the crease between his eyebrows, the pencil behind his ear. She thought of three years of waking above the workshop and listening for footsteps that would never come. Three years of finding notes in his handwriting tucked into manuals. Three years of anger disguised as loneliness.

“If I stop it, you’ll go.”

“Yes.”

“What if I’m not ready?”

“No one is.”

Mrs. Vale reached the mechanism chamber. She looked small beneath the gears, but grief had made her fierce.

“You have no right,” she said.

Others gathered behind her: Mr. Salt with his red-coated daughter, the miners’ mother with both sons, the baker’s dead mother, a dozen more.

Mara faced them.

“If the clock continues, the town disappears.”

“Then let it,” said Mr. Salt. He held his daughter against his chest. “What has this town given me except her grave?”

The girl looked up at him. “Papa?”

His face collapsed.

Mrs. Vale’s voice trembled. “Just one night. Give us until morning.”

“We may not have until morning.”

“You have your father,” she said. “You’re no different from us.”

“No,” Mara replied. “I’m not.”

The clock reached nine.

Another bell rang.

Age rolled backward through the chamber. Mrs. Vale’s white hair turned brown. Her spine straightened. Edmund stared as the decades fell from her face. The miners’ mother became younger than her returned sons.

Elias flickered.

For one heartbeat, Mara could see the gears through him.

“Hurry,” he whispered.

Mrs. Vale saw it too. She turned to Edmund. He touched her cheek.

“You must let me go, Vi.”

“No.”

“You already did, once.”

“I survived. That isn’t the same.”

He smiled with unbearable tenderness. “It is the only way the living ever let go.”

Mara climbed onto the maintenance frame.

The metal was slick, the beams trembling with the motion of the clock. Beneath her, gears ground together with enough force to crush bone. She placed one boot on a narrow brace and reached for the next.

Elias climbed beside her, though the frame did not bend under his weight.

“When you reach the regulator,” he said, “pull the locking pin first.”

“The black pin?”

“The copper one.”

“There are two copper pins.”

“The one on the left.”

“You always label everything. Why didn’t you label this?”

“I was in a hurry.”

“You were always in a hurry.”

She advanced another step.

Below, arguments erupted. Some townspeople tried to follow her. Others held them back. Mr. Salt wept into his daughter’s hair. The baker’s mother slapped him sharply, then kissed both his cheeks.

At the center of the frame, Mara paused.

The silver regulator spun an arm’s length away. To reach it, she would have to cross the section that had once broken.

The repaired metal looked solid.

Her body did not believe it.

“I can’t,” she said.

Elias stood beyond the beam. “Yes, you can.”

“You said that platform was safe.”

“I was wrong.”

“You might be wrong again.”

“Yes.”

“That isn’t helpful.”

“I know.”

The tower shook. The clock showed 8:31.

Beyond the window, houses vanished. The railway tracks unlaid themselves, iron rails sliding toward the eastern horizon. The church steeple sank into its own roof. Trees shortened, drawing branches into trunks.

Mara stared at the beam.

“I spent three years blaming you,” she said.

“You had every right.”

“I thought if I stayed angry, I wouldn’t miss you so much.”

“Did it work?”

“No.”

“It never does.”

She stepped forward.

The beam held.

Then it cracked.

Mara fell.

Her hands struck the frame, and she caught a crossbar. Her legs swung above the churning teeth. Elias dropped beside her, reaching instinctively, but his hands passed through her wrists.

“Mara!”

She had never heard fear in his voice before.

The crossbar groaned.

Below, people screamed. Mrs. Vale, young again, pushed through the crowd and seized a hanging maintenance rope. Edmund and the miners grasped it with her.

“Reach!” she shouted.

The rope swung toward Mara. She caught it with one hand. The townspeople pulled, dragging her away from the gears and against the side of the frame.

Mrs. Vale wrapped the rope around an iron post.

Mara hauled herself upward.

“Stop it,” Mrs. Vale said.

Mara nodded.

She climbed the final braces until she stood beside the regulator. Up close, she saw letters engraved on the silver rim in her father’s precise hand:

WHAT IS LOST STILL MOVES US.

“The left pin,” Elias reminded her.

She wrapped her fingers around it.

“Wait,” he said.

She looked at him.

He was fading quickly now. Only his face remained clear.

“I should have told you I was proud of you,” he said.

“You told everyone.”

“I told customers. I told neighbors. I told strangers on trains. I’m not sure I told you.”

“You did. Once.”

“When?”

“I was ten. I repaired the music box with the silver dancer.”

“That was terrible work. You put the spring in backward.”

“You said you were proud anyway.”

“I must have been going soft.”

She laughed, and tears blurred the mechanism.

The clock showed 8:07.

“Will I remember this?” she asked.

“I don’t know.”

“What if stopping it undoes tonight?”

“Then say what you need to say now.”

Mara thought she had a thousand things to say, but the thousand became three.

“I love you.”

Elias smiled.

“I know.”

She pulled the pin.

The regulator screamed.

Silver teeth sheared from the gear and flew like sparks. The pendulum slowed. Every wheel in the tower shuddered to a halt.

For one vast second, Bellweather existed outside time.

Rain hung above the streets. Bells froze in mid-swing. The living and the dead stood together without age or distance between them.

Then the clock began to move forward.

8:08.

8:09.

The silver regulator cracked.

The returned dead faded.

Edmund kissed Mrs. Vale’s forehead and vanished in her arms. The red-coated girl disappeared while her father whispered her name. The miners dissolved like breath from glass.

Elias remained a moment longer.

“Wind the Lemaire,” he said.

Then he was gone.

Time rushed to reclaim itself.

The tower darkened. Gears spun. The repaired beam broke again, but Mara had already reached the ladder. Outside, trees stretched upward, houses rebuilt themselves, and railway lines raced back toward Bellweather. People aged in seconds. Mrs. Vale’s hair whitened, though she stood straight.

The clock hands whirled forward, crossing hours in heartbeats.

At midnight, they stopped.

Silence settled over the town.

Then the clock began its proper work.

Tick.

Forward.

Tick.

Forward.

By dawn, the rain had ended.

Bellweather looked almost as it had before, though not entirely. The church weather vane pointed west instead of north. A blue house stood where a green one had been. No one could remember the mayor’s first name, including the mayor.

Some remembered the dead returning. Others believed they had dreamed it. Mrs. Vale said nothing, but every morning afterward she placed two cups of tea on her table.

Mara returned to the workshop.

The fifty-three clocks had stopped at 12:07. One by one, she reset them. When she reached the Lemaire, she found its spring unwound.

“You were right,” she murmured.

She wound it carefully.

Months passed. The tower clock kept perfect time. Mara repaired watches, music boxes, kitchen clocks, and once a mechanical bird that sang every hour. She stopped sorting screws after midnight. She began opening the curtains in the morning.

The town council asked her to become Bellweather’s official clock keeper. At first, she refused. Then she imagined what her father would tell strangers on trains.

She accepted.

Years later, children visiting the tower would ask why one silver gear hung framed on the workshop wall. Its rim was cracked, and most of its teeth were missing, but the engraving remained:

WHAT IS LOST STILL MOVES US.

Mara never explained it fully.

She would only tell them that clocks were strange machines. People thought they measured what they had, but clocks truly measured what they were losing.

Then she would lead them up the spiral stairs and show them the great mechanism, every wheel moving in harmony.

Before leaving, she always placed a hand on the maintenance frame.

Sometimes, beneath the ringing bells and turning gears, she imagined she heard her father’s voice complaining about the oil, the dust, or the disgraceful state of her tools.

She never answered.

She did not need to.

At seven minutes past midnight each year, Mara stood alone in the square and watched the illuminated hands move forward.

And forward.

And forward.

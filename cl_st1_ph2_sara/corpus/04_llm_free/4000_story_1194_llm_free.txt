On the morning the sea returned the road, Mara Venn was the only person in Bellwether awake to see it.

She stood at the window above her bakery, tying her apron, and watched the tide withdraw beyond the harbor wall. It slid backward without a wave or whisper, exposing the black stones of the seabed, then the pale ribs of shipwrecks, then a narrow road of white sand stretching toward the horizon.

Mara forgot the bread in the oven.

The road had appeared twice before in the town’s history. Everyone in Bellwether knew the stories. In 1742, a fisherman named Edrin Bale had walked out upon it and returned carrying a silver bell that rang before storms. In 1889, three sisters had followed the road and come back as old women, though they had been gone less than an hour.

“Never take the sea’s road,” Mara’s mother used to say. “It leads where grief wants to go.”

By seven o’clock, the whole town had gathered along the harbor wall.

Children pointed at stranded fish flashing in the dawn. Dogs barked down at the wet sand. Old Mrs. Reed made the sign against evil, while Mayor Tallow climbed onto an overturned barrel and urged everyone to remain calm.

“No one is to descend into the harbor,” he announced. “No one is to touch anything. No one is to walk upon the road.”

Naturally, that was when Mara saw the boy.

He stood far out on the white sand, perhaps half a mile from shore. He wore a red coat, and he was facing the sea.

Mara’s hands tightened around the iron railing.

The coat was too large for him. Its sleeves hung over his wrists, and one of its brass buttons was missing. She knew that coat. She had sewn the tear beneath its left shoulder. She had watched it disappear beneath a winter wave eleven years ago.

“Jonas,” she whispered.

Beside her, Mayor Tallow continued talking, but the words faded into a dull murmur.

Jonas turned.

Even at that distance, she recognized the way he stood, with his right foot angled outward. He raised one hand.

Mara climbed over the wall.

Someone shouted after her. Boots struck cobblestones. She dropped onto a pile of seaweed and half slid down the harbor slope. The smell of salt was overwhelming, mixed with tar, rot, and things usually hidden beneath deep water.

“Mara!” called a familiar voice.

She looked back.

Theo March was clambering over the wall after her. He had not even put on his boots. His graying hair whipped around his face, and his shirt was buttoned wrong.

“Go back,” Mara said.

“So should you.”

She turned toward the white road. Jonas was walking away now, his red coat bright against the dark seabed.

Mara began to run.

The sand felt dry beneath her shoes. That frightened her more than anything. On either side of the road, crabs stirred among broken anchors and drowned beams. Pools of trapped seawater reflected a sky growing pink with sunrise.

Behind her, Theo cursed as a shell cut his bare foot, but he kept following.

“Jonas!” Mara called.

The boy did not turn.

The road extended farther than it had seemed from the harbor. Bellwether shrank behind them until its church spire looked like a black thorn. The farther they walked, the colder the air became.

Mara remembered the last day she had seen her brother. He had been nine, angry because she would not take him fishing. She had been nineteen and impatient, full of plans to leave Bellwether. To silence him, she had said the cruelest thing she could think of.

You ruin everything.

An hour later, he had stolen their father’s boat.

The storm arrived before sunset. Searchers found the boat split open on the eastern rocks, but they never found Jonas.

Afterward, Mara did not leave Bellwether. She took over her mother’s bakery, slept in the room where she had grown up, and kept her brother’s coat hanging behind the kitchen door until the damp claimed it.

Yet there it was ahead of her, whole again.

“Mara, stop,” Theo said.

She turned impatiently, but the fear in his face silenced her.

The road behind them was gone.

Not covered by water—gone. A gray mist had closed across it, and beyond that mist there was no town, no harbor, no rising sun.

Only the narrow path remained, floating between two fields of darkness.

Theo reached her and bent with his hands on his knees. Blood marked each of his footsteps.

“We have to go back,” he said.

“Jonas is here.”

“No. Something that looks like Jonas is here.”

“You didn’t see him drown.”

“Neither did you.”

The words struck with the force of a slap.

Theo straightened. Once, long ago, they had planned to marry. That had been before the storm. Before Mara became a person who could not speak without tasting the last words she had said to her brother.

Theo had waited two years. Then five. Then he had married someone else.

His wife, Elian, died of fever the previous spring.

Mara looked at him and understood.

“Who did you see?” she asked.

Theo glanced toward the darkness beside the road.

“No one.”

“The road showed you someone too.”

His jaw tightened.

A woman laughed nearby.

It was a gentle laugh, low and warm. Theo went still.

From the darkness stepped Elian March.

She wore the yellow dress in which she had been buried. Her black hair lay in a braid over one shoulder. She looked healthy, younger than Mara remembered, and entirely alive.

“Theo,” she said.

He made a sound like a breath breaking.

Elian smiled and held out her hand.

“Don’t,” Mara said.

Theo took one step toward her.

At once, the darkness beneath Elian’s feet rippled like disturbed water. Her smile remained, but her shadow stretched in the wrong direction, long fingers reaching over the road.

Mara seized Theo’s arm.

“Elian,” he whispered.

The dead woman’s expression changed. For one instant, Mara saw not anger but hunger. Then Elian’s face became kind again.

“You took so long,” she said.

Theo pulled against Mara’s grip.

“She’s gone,” Mara said. “You buried her.”

“I know.”

“You were there.”

“I know.”

The shadow’s fingers reached the edge of his bleeding footprints.

Theo looked down. He jerked backward as the first black fingertip touched the blood. The road trembled.

Elian opened her mouth, and seawater poured from it.

Then she vanished.

Theo stumbled into Mara. Together they fell hard upon the sand.

Ahead of them, Jonas stopped.

He looked back with a puzzled expression, as if he had only just noticed they were following.

“You said you’d come,” he called.

Mara rose.

“Come where?”

“Home.”

He pointed down the road.

A house stood in the distance where nothing had been before.

It was Mara’s childhood home, white-walled and leaning slightly toward the west. Golden light shone from the windows. Smoke rose from the chimney.

Mara smelled onions frying in butter. Her mother’s winter stew.

Theo caught her sleeve.

“You know it isn’t real.”

“I know.”

But her feet moved anyway.

They approached the house as evening gathered around them, though only minutes had passed since dawn. Through the front window, Mara saw her father at the table, mending a net. Her mother stood over the stove. Neither looked old or ill.

Jonas opened the blue front door.

Everything inside was exactly as Mara remembered. Her father’s raincoat hung on its hook. A cracked china horse stood on the mantel. The kitchen clock ticked three minutes slow.

“Mara?” her mother called. “Wash your hands. Supper’s ready.”

Mara entered.

Warmth wrapped around her. It reached through her skin and settled into the empty places between her ribs.

Theo remained outside.

“Mara,” he said.

Her father looked up from his net.

“There’s my girl.”

He had died fifteen years ago with Mara’s hand in his. In the end he had forgotten her name, but now he spoke it easily.

Her mother turned from the stove. “You’re thin.”

She always said that, no matter how much Mara weighed.

Jonas squeezed past her and took his usual seat. “She ruins everything,” he said cheerfully.

Mara stopped.

Her mother placed bowls on the table. “That isn’t a kind thing to say.”

“She said it first.”

The room dimmed.

Jonas looked at Mara. His face was still nine years old, but his eyes were not a child’s eyes. They were pale and endless, filled with the distant movement of tides.

“Sit down,” he said.

Outside, Theo struck the wall with his palm. “Mara, the water is coming.”

Through the window, she saw waves rolling toward them across the darkness. They moved on both sides of the white road, towering and silent.

Her mother set a bowl before Mara’s empty chair.

“If you stay,” Jonas said, “you can say you’re sorry.”

Mara’s heart twisted.

“Can you forgive me?”

Jonas tilted his head. “Stay and see.”

It was what she had wanted for eleven years. Not happiness. Not even absolution. Only the chance to replace her last words with better ones.

She sat.

The chair recognized her weight with a familiar creak. Her father smiled. Her mother tore bread and passed it across the table.

Mara took it.

It had no smell.

She looked at the stew. The surface was smooth and dark. Something moved beneath it.

Outside, Theo shouted again, but his voice seemed very far away.

Jonas watched her.

“What happened that day?” Mara asked.

His smile vanished.

“You know.”

“No. I know what I said. I know you took the boat. I know the storm came. But I don’t know what happened.”

“You didn’t come for me.”

“I searched for three days.”

“You were glad.”

Mara flinched.

Around them, the walls breathed in and out. Water dripped from the ceiling. Her father continued mending the same knot in his net. Her mother tore the same loaf again and again.

Mara stared at the thing wearing her brother’s face.

“No,” she said. “I wasn’t glad.”

“You wanted to leave.”

“Yes.”

“You hated me.”

“Sometimes.”

Her mother’s hands stopped moving.

The answer seemed to surprise the house.

Mara stood.

“I loved him too,” she said. “Those things can live together. That’s what makes it hurt.”

The false Jonas rose as well. “Then stay.”

“I can’t.”

“Why not?”

“Because you aren’t asking me to stay for him. You’re asking me to stay for me.”

His face tightened.

The house lurched. Seawater burst through cracks in the walls. Her father’s net unraveled into strands of black weed. Her mother’s skin became gray and swollen.

Jonas remained unchanged.

“You don’t deserve to go home,” he said.

Mara’s throat closed.

There it was—the sentence she had carried inside herself, spoken aloud in her brother’s voice.

“No,” she said. “Maybe I don’t.”

The water reached her ankles.

“But I’m going.”

She ran.

The front door stretched away as the room lengthened around her. The table splintered. Her dead parents called her name, first tenderly, then in anger, then with voices like gulls.

Theo reached through the doorway.

Mara leaped and caught his hand.

He pulled her outside as the house folded inward. Its roof collapsed like wet paper. For a moment, Jonas stood in the ruins, his red coat lifting in a wind Mara could not feel.

Then the darkness took him.

The sea rushed closer.

The white road remained behind them, a narrow line leading into mist.

They ran.

Theo limped badly. Each step left more blood, and shadows stirred along the road’s edge, eager for it. Mara put his arm over her shoulders.

“I’m slowing you down,” he gasped.

“You came barefoot.”

“I was in a hurry.”

“That was stupid.”

“I agree.”

The waves rose on either side of them, walls of green water filled with suspended fish, broken ships, and pale human faces. Mara saw Elian among them. She swam beside Theo, one hand pressed against the water’s glasslike surface.

He saw her too.

“I left the window open,” he said.

“What?”

“The day she died. She was burning with fever, and she asked me to close it. I said fresh air would help.” His breath came raggedly. “That night turned cold.”

“It wasn’t your fault.”

“You don’t know that.”

“No,” Mara said. “I don’t.”

He glanced at her.

“The road doesn’t want our dead,” she continued. “It wants what we owe them.”

A crack appeared across the sand.

Water burst through it.

They jumped over, but the road was splitting ahead. The mist remained impossibly distant. Behind them came the roar of the collapsing house and the cries of all the people they had failed.

Theo stumbled.

Mara caught him before he fell into the dark.

“Leave me,” he said.

“No.”

“Elian is here.”

“No, she isn’t.”

“Maybe it’s enough.”

Mara slapped him.

He blinked.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “But we don’t have time for a speech.”

He laughed once, breathlessly. Then he stood.

They went on.

The road narrowed until they had to move single file. Mara led, gripping Theo’s hand behind her. The sea curled overhead, forming a tunnel. Within the water, objects drifted from Bellwether’s past: the silver storm bell, a child’s wooden horse, wedding rings, knives, letters sealed with red wax.

A small boat floated above Mara’s head.

Its hull was split open.

Inside sat Jonas.

This time he looked different. His hair was plastered to his forehead. Blood darkened one side of his face. He was shivering.

“Mara,” he said.

She stopped.

The sea groaned around them.

Jonas reached down through the water. His hand passed through the surface as if through a curtain.

“I was scared,” he said.

Mara took his hand.

It was cold, small, and real.

“I know.”

“I waited.”

“I know.”

“You didn’t come.”

“I tried.”

His fingers closed around hers.

The force of his grip almost pulled her from the road. Theo seized her waist.

Jonas began to rise in the broken boat, and Mara saw that below his chest there was nothing human. His red coat opened into a long body of weed and white bone coiling through the water.

“Mara,” he pleaded.

She could not let go.

Eleven years vanished. She was nineteen again, standing on the shore while rain erased the world, screaming her brother’s name until her mouth filled with blood.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

Jonas smiled.

It was the smile of something that had heard those words a thousand times and never once been satisfied.

The sea cracked overhead.

Mara pressed her lips to her brother’s cold knuckles.

“I’m sorry,” she said again. “But you’re dead.”

She released him.

The creature shrieked.

Theo dragged her forward as the tunnel shattered. Water crashed onto the road behind them, swallowing the broken boat. Mara ran blindly through spray, expecting every step to be her last.

Then bells rang.

Not the church bells of Bellwether. These were deeper, vast enough to shake the sky. The silver storm bell from the old story spun within the wave, ringing and ringing.

The mist tore open.

Morning appeared beyond it.

Mara and Theo fell onto the harbor slope as the sea struck the wall.

The impact sent a plume of water above the rooftops. People screamed and scattered. Boats slammed against their moorings. The church bell began clanging in answer.

For several minutes, Mara could do nothing but lie among the wet stones and breathe.

Hands lifted her. A blanket settled over her shoulders. Mayor Tallow shouted questions. Mrs. Reed declared that she had warned everyone. Somewhere, a child was crying.

Theo lay nearby while the town physician wrapped his feet.

Mara looked toward the harbor. The sea had returned to its ordinary place. It rolled beneath the gray morning, innocent and unreadable.

“How long?” she asked.

Mayor Tallow frowned. “How long what?”

“Were we gone?”

He checked his pocket watch.

“Seven minutes.”

Theo began to laugh.

Mara laughed too. She laughed until it became sobbing, and then she let it.

That afternoon, despite the broken windows and flooded cellars, Mara opened the bakery.

People needed bread after disasters. It was one of the few truths she trusted.

Theo sat near the oven with both feet bandaged, slicing apples badly. Neither of them spoke about the road. Not yet. Some stories had to cool before they could be handled.

At sunset, Mara went upstairs and opened the old chest beneath her bed.

Inside lay the only thing of Jonas’s she had kept: a brass button from his red coat.

She carried it to the cliff above the eastern rocks. The storm had cleared, leaving the horizon sharp and gold. Far below, waves moved over the place where her brother’s boat had been found.

Mara closed her fist around the button.

For years she had imagined his last moments. In some, he called for her. In others, he hated her. In the worst, he believed she had not come because she did not care.

She had treated those imaginings as memories, punishing herself with each one.

Now she understood that grief was a poor historian. It changed the past to keep itself alive.

“I did love you,” she said to the sea. “I still do.”

She threw the button.

It flashed once in the evening light and vanished.

Behind her, a cane tapped against the path. Theo approached, leaning heavily upon it.

“You’re supposed to be resting,” Mara said.

“I rested all afternoon.”

“You sliced apples.”

“Very restful.”

He stood beside her. For a while they watched the dark gather over the water.

“Did you forgive yourself?” he asked.

Mara considered the question.

“No.”

Theo nodded.

“But I think,” she continued, “I’m done asking the dead for permission.”

The wind smelled of salt and distant rain. Down in Bellwether, windows glowed one by one. Someone rang the church bell for evening, and all across the harbor people paused to listen.

Theo offered Mara his arm.

She took it carefully.

Together they walked back toward the town, where the ovens were warm, the streets were wet, and the living were waiting.

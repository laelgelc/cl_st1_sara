### The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every town loses time.

A minute slips beneath a shop door while the owner argues over change. An hour vanishes beside a hospital bed. Whole afternoons disappear into attics, train stations, school corridors, and kitchens where someone is waiting for a letter that never comes.

In Bellwether, those lost hours collected in the clock tower.

No one knew this except Mara Venn.

She lived in a narrow room behind the clock face, where moonlight shone through the glass numerals and gears larger than cartwheels turned with the patience of planets. Her bed stood beneath the number four. Her stove was tucked between two iron supports, and her kettle rattled whenever the great bell struck.

Mara was the Keeper of Lost Hours, as her mother had been before her, and her grandfather before that.

Each midnight, she climbed the winding stairs to the highest chamber. There, beneath a copper dome, stood hundreds of glass jars. Some contained only a few pale seconds, flickering like fireflies. Others held minutes that rolled in silver beads around the bottom. The largest jars were full of hours, blue and restless as captive storms.

Every jar had a label.

**Seven minutes lost searching for a wedding ring.**

**One hour wasted in pride.**

**Twenty-three minutes between the knock and the opening of the door.**

**A summer afternoon forgotten by two sisters who no longer speak.**

Mara’s task was to keep the lost time safe until it could be returned.

Most of it never was.

Time could only be restored under strict conditions. The person who had lost it had to recognize the moment, regret its loss, and ask sincerely for another chance. Even then, Mara could not change what had happened. She could only give the person the missing time in which to act differently.

Her mother had explained the distinction when Mara was a child.

“We do not undo,” she had said. “We extend.”

At nine, Mara had not understood.

“If someone says something cruel, can’t you give them back the minute before they said it?”

“No. But I may give them one more minute afterward.”

“So they can apologize?”

“If they choose.”

“What if they don’t?”

“Then the minute is lost again.”

Mara had considered this a very foolish system. At twenty-eight, she still did.

Most nights, the tower was quiet. She polished the jars, adjusted the regulators, and recorded new arrivals in a leather ledger. Lost seconds drifted up through vents in the floor. Minutes came through brass pipes with soft metallic sighs. Hours arrived rarely, shaking the whole tower as they settled into empty glass.

Mara had grown used to the sound.

Then, one rain-soaked evening in November, someone knocked at the tower door.

The public entrance had been sealed for forty years. Ivy covered the lower steps, and a notice from the town council declared the building unsafe. Mara received food through a hidden hatch and spoke to people only when her work required it.

The knock came again.

Three quick taps. A pause. Two more.

Mara descended the spiral stairs carrying a lantern. At the bottom, she opened the inspection panel set into the door.

A boy stared back at her.

He was perhaps twelve, with wet black hair plastered across his forehead. His coat was too large, and rainwater dripped from his nose.

“Are you the clock woman?” he asked.

“There is no clock woman.”

“You answered the door.”

“I answered a panel.”

The boy glanced at the panel. “Are you the clock panel woman?”

Mara nearly shut it.

“Wait,” he said. “I need an hour.”

“Go home.”

“I know you keep them.”

Mara froze.

Above them, the tower machinery ticked on.

“Who told you that?”

“My sister.”

“Who is your sister?”

“Elsie Vale.”

Mara knew the name.

Everyone in Bellwether did.

Elsie Vale had disappeared six months earlier.

She was seventeen, an apprentice at the town print shop, known for drawing small birds in the margins of official notices. One spring morning, she had left home carrying a parcel of freshly printed invitations and never arrived at her destination.

Search parties had combed the woods. Boats had dragged the river. Her face had remained in windows until rain bleached the posters white.

Mara opened the door.

The boy stepped inside, bringing cold air and the smell of wet stone. He looked around the circular entrance hall.

“My name’s Tomas,” he said.

“I know. Your name was in the newspapers.”

“They spelled it Thomas. There’s no h.”

Mara locked the door. “What did Elsie tell you?”

“That if I ever needed more time, I should come here after dark and ask the woman inside the clock.”

“People say strange things to children.”

“She said you had jars.”

Mara’s grip tightened on the lantern.

Tomas saw her expression and nodded as if she had confirmed everything.

“I need the hour before she vanished,” he said.

“That is not how this works.”

“I followed her that morning.”

Mara said nothing.

Tomas pushed wet hair from his face. “No one knows. We had a fight at breakfast. She said she was leaving, and I thought she meant leaving town. So I followed her.”

“Why didn’t you tell the constables?”

“I was afraid.”

“Of what?”

His eyes dropped.

Mara recognized the silence. The tower was full of silences like it.

She led him upstairs.

They climbed past the pendulum chamber, where the great brass disk swung through shadows. They passed the clockworks and Mara’s small room. At last they entered the copper-domed chamber.

Tomas stopped breathing for a moment.

The jars glowed across tiered shelves, hundreds upon hundreds of them. Their combined light painted the walls in shades of pearl, blue, and gold.

Mara set down her lantern.

“When someone loses time in Bellwether,” she said, “it comes here. But I cannot simply hand it back.”

“I regret losing that hour.”

“Regret is not enough.”

“You said—”

“I said nothing. Listen carefully. Lost time can only be returned to the person who lost it. And it must be used to do what was left undone. Whose hour are you asking for?”

“Mine.”

“Are you certain?”

Tomas hesitated.

A faint tremor traveled through the shelves. Somewhere above them, rain struck copper.

“I saw where she went,” he whispered.

Mara waited.

“She crossed the old bridge and walked toward the quarry road. I followed until the chapel. Then Mr. Rusk’s carriage came past.”

Avery Rusk owned the paper mill, three warehouses, and half the houses along the eastern riverbank. He sat at the head of the town council. When Elsie disappeared, he had offered a reward so large that strangers arrived from two counties away to search for her.

“What happened?” Mara asked.

“The carriage stopped. Mr. Rusk got out. Elsie gave him the parcel, but he got angry. He grabbed her arm. She shouted at him.”

“What did she shout?”

“That she had made copies.”

“Copies of what?”

“I don’t know. Papers, maybe. She said if anything happened to her, the whole town would find out.”

Mara felt the tower’s ticking like a pulse in the floor.

“And then?”

“I ran away.”

The words seemed to tear themselves from him.

“I thought he’d see me. I thought he’d grab me too. I went home and climbed into bed. Later, when Elsie didn’t come back, I wanted to tell someone. But Mr. Rusk came to our house. He told my mother that Elsie had probably run away because she was troubled. Then he looked straight at me.”

Tomas wiped his eyes angrily.

“I lost the hour because I was scared. Give it back, and I’ll stay. I’ll watch where he takes her. I’ll tell the constables before it happens.”

Mara looked toward the shelves.

“The hour before she vanished would belong to Elsie,” she said. “Or Rusk. Or perhaps to no one. Time is not lost merely because something terrible happens.”

“I ran away when I should have stayed.”

“That may have created lost time.”

“Then find it.”

There were seventeen thousand jars in the chamber.

Mara spent the next four hours searching.

The records were arranged by date, then location, then duration. Tomas followed with a lamp as she moved between shelves, reading labels.

**April 17. Four minutes lost beneath the pear tree.**

**April 17. Eleven seconds before a confession.**

**April 17. Thirty-eight minutes waiting for courage in an empty church.**

At the last label, Tomas stopped.

“That’s it.”

Mara lifted the jar. Inside, thirty-eight blue minutes circled one another like fish.

“Only thirty-eight,” he said.

“Time measures itself honestly.”

“Is it mine?”

She held the glass against the ledger lens. Letters appeared on the label, darkening from invisible ink.

**Tomas Vale.**

He gave a broken laugh. “I knew it.”

“This time was lost in the chapel, not on the road.”

“I hid there after I ran.”

“You said you went home.”

“I lied.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know. I’m used to it.”

Mara placed the jar on the table.

“You had thirty-eight minutes in the chapel,” she said. “During that time, you could have gone back.”

“Yes.”

“You understand that these minutes cannot take you into the past?”

Tomas stared at her. “But Elsie said—”

“Your sister knew a story, not the truth. Returning time does not reverse a clock. It adds what was lost to the present.”

He sank onto a stool.

“So it’s useless.”

“No time is useless.”

“She’s gone.”

“Yes.”

“You don’t know that.”

“No,” Mara said gently. “I don’t.”

Tomas glared at the jar. “What am I supposed to do with thirty-eight minutes now?”

“What did you leave undone?”

“I didn’t follow the carriage.”

“Then follow it.”

“Six months later?”

“You remember the direction.”

“To the quarry road.”

“And what lies there?”

“Woods. The abandoned quarry. Rusk’s old mill.”

Mara nodded.

Understanding came slowly into the boy’s face.

“You think she made it to the mill?”

“I think the returned minutes must be used for their original purpose. You wanted to discover where Rusk had taken your sister. The time may help you do that.”

“How?”

“I don’t know.”

“That’s not much of a system.”

“No,” Mara said. “It isn’t.”

She uncorked the jar.

The blue minutes rose in a twisting ribbon. Tomas flinched, but the light entered his chest without sound. For an instant his bones shone through his skin, bright as the framework of a lantern.

Then the light vanished.

The tower bell began to strike two in the morning.

After the second chime, everything stopped.

Rain hung motionless beyond the windows. The gears ceased turning. The pendulum paused at the edge of its swing. Even the flame of Mara’s lantern became a still golden leaf.

Only Tomas could move.

He stared at Mara. “Are these my minutes?”

She could not answer. Returned time belonged only to the recipient. For the next thirty-eight minutes, he would move through a sleeping world alone.

He ran.

Mara remained frozen beside the table, conscious but trapped between ticks. Keepers were permitted to witness returned time, never enter it.

Tomas disappeared down the stairs.

The silence that followed was absolute.

Mara counted seconds in her head.

At first she imagined his progress: out the tower door, across the market square, past the darkened bakery and the statue of Bellwether’s founder. He would follow the eastern road, running through suspended rain.

After ten minutes, he might reach the chapel.

After fifteen, the quarry turn.

After twenty, the old mill.

Mara hoped he had brought a lantern. Then she remembered he had not.

Twenty-eight minutes passed.

Thirty.

At thirty-four, Tomas stumbled back into the chamber.

Mud covered his boots. Blood ran from one palm. He carried a metal box against his chest.

He tried to speak, but no sound existed inside the returned minutes. Instead, he set the box on the table and opened it.

Letters filled the interior.

Dozens of them.

On top lay a stack of mill invoices and wage records tied with red thread. Beneath those were photographs of cracked machinery, lists of injured workers, and statements signed by families. At the bottom rested a folded page decorated with a small ink drawing of a bird.

Tomas handed it to Mara, forgetting that she could not move.

Realizing his mistake, he unfolded the paper himself and held it before her eyes.

The message was written in Elsie’s hand.

**Tomas,**

**You were always terrible at doing what you were told, so I hope, for once, you disobey me. If I do not come home, do not come looking. Take this box to Mara Venn in the clock tower. She will know when it is safe to open.**

**Mr. Rusk has been stealing relief payments from the mill families and bribing inspectors to ignore dangerous machinery. I printed copies. He found out. I am going to meet him because he says he will pay back what he took if I surrender the evidence. I do not believe him, which is why this box is hidden beneath the chapel floor.**

**If you find this, I am sorry.**

**You will think you should have saved me. You could not. That was my hour to lose, not yours.**

**Elsie**

Mara read the last lines twice.

Tomas lowered the letter.

The tower shuddered.

Thirty-eight minutes had ended.

Rain struck the windows. The pendulum completed its swing. The lantern flame trembled, and sound returned in a rush: gears, thunder, Tomas’s sob.

Mara caught him as he folded forward.

For a long while, neither of them spoke.

At dawn, they carried the box to the constable’s office.

By noon, Avery Rusk had been arrested.

The evidence in the box was enough to expose theft, fraud, bribery, and years of deliberate neglect. It was not enough to prove murder. Rusk admitted meeting Elsie on the quarry road but claimed she had fled into the woods.

Searchers returned to the old mill. They found Elsie’s empty satchel in a drainage tunnel, one shoe near the river, and a trail that vanished at the water’s edge.

Winter came early that year.

Rusk went to prison. The council was dissolved. Money recovered from his accounts was returned to the mill families. People called Elsie brave. They painted her birds on walls and printed her name in letters taller than she had ever been.

But they did not find her.

Tomas visited the tower every week.

At first, Mara refused to let him in. Then he began leaving food outside the door: apples, bread, once an entire roasted chicken stolen from his mother’s kitchen. When the snow grew deep, Mara relented.

He learned to polish jars and oil the smallest gears. He was impatient, careless with labels, and prone to asking questions for which no answers existed.

“Where does time come from?”

“No one knows.”

“Who made the first jar?”

“No one knows.”

“Can animals lose time?”

“Cats never do.”

“Why?”

“They believe every moment belongs to them.”

In February, Tomas found a jar hidden behind a row of empty decades.

It contained one hour.

Unlike the others, its light was not blue or silver, but warm amber. Its label had faded almost completely.

He brought it to Mara.

“What’s this one?”

She took it too quickly.

Tomas noticed.

“Whose is it?”

“No one’s.”

“All time is someone’s.”

“Not this.”

He reached for it. Mara moved the jar out of reach.

On the label, one word remained visible.

**Mara.**

Tomas sat down.

“You lost an hour.”

“Everyone does.”

“Why haven’t you used it?”

“Because Keepers cannot return time to themselves.”

“Who says?”

“The rules.”

“Who made them?”

“No one knows,” he said, echoing her.

She almost smiled.

The hour had entered the tower fifteen years earlier, on the night Mara’s mother died.

Her mother had been ill for months. On her final evening, she asked Mara to sit beside her. But Mara, nineteen and terrified, had insisted on repairing a fault in the western clock face.

“There will be time when I finish,” she had said.

There had not been.

The lost hour arrived before dawn.

Mara had kept it hidden ever since.

She told Tomas none of this, but children often recognize the shape of grief even when adults cover it with rules.

“What did you leave undone?” he asked.

“That is not your concern.”

“You said lost time has to be used for its original purpose.”

“Yes.”

“And Keepers can’t return their own time?”

“No.”

“But could someone else return it?”

Mara looked at him.

The tower seemed to lean closer.

“There has never been an apprentice,” she said slowly, “young enough to ask that question and rude enough to expect an answer.”

“So that means maybe.”

“It means go home.”

He did, eventually.

He returned the next night carrying two cups of hot chocolate. Without asking permission, he climbed to the chamber, found the amber jar, and placed it on the table.

“If you give time to me,” he said, “you open the jar.”

“Yes.”

“If I give time to you, I should open it.”

“You are not a Keeper.”

“Make me one.”

“It takes years.”

“I’ve got time.”

Mara laughed then.

It surprised her. The sound rose into the copper dome and disturbed dust that had rested there for decades.

She placed one hand over the jar.

“There may be consequences.”

“There are always consequences.”

“I may lose the hour again.”

“Then at least you’ll lose it doing something.”

She studied him, this stubborn boy who had once fled and then returned.

At last she nodded.

Tomas pulled the cork.

Amber light spilled into the room.

It entered Mara’s chest, warm as sunlight through a childhood window. The clock stopped. Snow froze beyond the glass. Tomas became motionless, one hand still wrapped around the mouth of the jar.

Mara stood alone in the quiet.

She expected to see her mother. Some part of her had imagined that returned time might open a door to the dead.

No door appeared.

Instead, she noticed the chair beside the western window.

It had belonged to her mother. Mara had never sat in it.

She crossed the room and lowered herself onto the worn cushion. From there, she could see all of Bellwether: the snowy roofs, the river, the chapel, the mill, and the eastern road fading into white hills.

Her mother had spent years in that chair, watching lost time enter the tower.

Mara understood at last what she had left undone.

She had not merely failed to sit beside a dying woman. She had refused, for fifteen years, to sit with the grief that followed.

And so she sat.

She remembered her mother’s rough hands, her crooked singing, and the way she burned every loaf of bread. She remembered their arguments. She remembered the final request and her own retreat toward the gears.

Mara wept until regret loosened its grip and became sorrow, clean and ordinary.

When the hour ended, the clock resumed.

Tomas blinked.

“Did it work?” he asked.

Mara wiped her face.

“Yes.”

“Did you change anything?”

“No.”

He looked disappointed.

Then Mara turned toward the shelves. All around them, thousands of jars glimmered in the morning dark.

“We do not undo,” she said. “We extend.”

She took down the jar labeled with his thirty-eight minutes. It was empty now, but a faint blue shine remained inside.

“What will happen to it?” Tomas asked.

“We keep it.”

“Why?”

“To remember that lost time is not always lost forever.”

Years later, when Mara’s hair had turned white and the tower stairs had become too steep for her knees, Tomas became the next Keeper.

By then, the townspeople knew they could knock at the sealed door when regret grew too heavy to carry alone. Most who came were not given minutes or hours. They were given tea, a chair, and the difficult mercy of being heard.

Sometimes that was enough.

The clock tower continued to gather what Bellwether lost. Seconds flickered through its pipes. Hours rolled like storms inside glass. The shelves grew crowded with hesitation, anger, fear, and love delayed too long.

And on the highest shelf stood two empty jars.

One held the memory of thirty-eight minutes used to uncover the truth.

The other held an hour spent sitting quietly with the dead.

Between them was a new label, written in Tomas’s untidy hand:

**Time does not return.**

**But sometimes, if we are brave, it waits.**

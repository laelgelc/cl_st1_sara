Mara Venn first heard the lighthouse speak on a windless night in October.

She was alone in the lantern room, polishing the great glass lens while the sea lay black and smooth below. The lamp had been dark for nearly thirty years. No ships passed this coast anymore, not since the harbor silted up and the railway carried trade inland. Yet every evening Mara climbed the spiral stairs, checked the rusting gears, and cleaned the lens because her father had done so, and his father before him.

The island council paid her to maintain the building as a monument. Tourists came in summer, leaving fingerprints on the brass and asking whether anyone had ever died there. In winter, however, the lighthouse belonged only to Mara, the gulls, and the weather.

That night there was no weather at all.

She had just folded her cloth when a low hum shivered through the glass.

Mara stopped.

The hum deepened until the floor vibrated beneath her boots. Then, from somewhere inside the dark lamp, a voice said, very clearly:

“Please light me.”

Mara stared at it.

The lamp was an old electric model fitted in 1948, its copper wires disconnected and coiled against the wall. She circled it slowly, expecting to find a radio hidden by some summer visitor. There was nothing.

“Please,” the voice repeated.

It was neither male nor female. It sounded distant, as if speaking through deep water.

Mara leaned close to the glass. “Who are you?”

The humming ceased.

She waited for an answer, but none came. At last she took her coat from its peg, locked the lantern room, and descended the stairs faster than dignity required.

Her cottage stood fifty paces from the lighthouse. She bolted the door, made tea, and told herself that old towers produced old noises. Metal contracted. Wind entered cracks too small to feel. Solitude turned noises into words.

In the morning she found a dead starling on the lantern room floor.

All the windows were sealed.

She carried the little bird outside and buried it beneath the wild thyme. Then she examined the lamp again. A web of fine cracks had appeared in the glass housing, radiating from the place where she had heard the voice.

“Please light me.”

Mara spun around.

The room was empty.

This time the words had come from the lens itself.

She stepped closer. “Why?”

For a long moment, silence.

Then: “A ship is coming.”

“No ships come here.”

“One is coming.”

Mara looked east through the curved windows. Sunlight spread across an empty sea. Beyond the horizon lay the mainland, though on clear nights its coastal towns stained the clouds orange. The island had no proper harbor now, only a stone pier where the weekly ferry tied up. Any captain carrying a modern chart would know to keep well south of the reefs.

“What ship?”

But the lighthouse would say no more.

At noon Mara walked to the village and found Edrin Hale repairing lobster pots outside his shed. Edrin had been the last keeper before automation. He was eighty-six and had hands like bundles of rope.

“Did the lighthouse ever make noises?” Mara asked.

“Always,” he said. “Groans, whistles, knocks. Once screamed for three days, but that was a gull in the ventilation shaft.”

“Did it ever speak?”

Edrin stopped weaving the pot.

His eyes rose to hers. “What did it say?”

The seriousness of his voice unsettled her more than laughter would have.

“It asked me to light it. It said a ship was coming.”

Edrin set down the rope. “Come inside.”

His shed smelled of tar, salt, and damp wool. From behind a stack of buoys he pulled a tin box. It contained old photographs, maintenance records, and a narrow book bound in cracked blue leather.

“This was Keeper Arlen’s journal,” he said. “He served from 1891 to 1919. Most of it is lamp oil, cloud cover, and complaints about his knees. But read the last pages.”

Mara opened the book. The handwriting was angular and faded.

January 11. Heard a voice in the lens. Requested flame though lamp already burning.

January 12. Voice says vessel approaching from east. No vessel expected.

January 13. Increased watch. Nothing sighted.

The next page bore dark stains along its edge.

January 14. At 2:10 morning, sighted three-masted ship beyond Widow’s Reef, running without sails against the wind. Lamp extinguished itself. Voice says: DO NOT LET THEM SEE THE SHORE.

January 15. No ship at dawn. Found six dead birds in lantern room.

The final entry had been written in a shaking hand.

January 17. They are still out there.

“That’s all?” Mara asked.

“He left the island the following morning,” Edrin said. “Never returned.”

“What ship did he see?”

“No one knows. There was no wreck, no missing vessel. People decided he’d gone mad.”

“And what do you think?”

Edrin gazed toward the lighthouse, its white tower visible through the shed window.

“I think you should leave the lamp dark.”

Mara returned home before sunset. Clouds gathered in the east, thin at first, then thickening into a wall. The radio forecast calm conditions. Nevertheless, she brought extra oil for the cottage lamps and checked the shutters.

At midnight, bells woke her.

Their iron notes rolled across the island—slow, uneven, impossibly deep. Mara knew every bell in the village. None sounded like these.

She pulled on her boots and stepped outside.

Fog had swallowed the lighthouse. Its tower rose only halfway before disappearing into gray. The bells came from the sea, accompanied by the creak of timber and the distant boom of canvas filling with wind.

But the grass around Mara’s feet did not move.

Then the lighthouse spoke, loud enough to shake dust from the cottage roof.

“LIGHT ME.”

Mara ran to the tower.

Inside, the air was bitterly cold. She climbed through coils of fog that drifted down the stairwell like pale smoke. When she entered the lantern room, every pane of glass was white with frost.

The old lamp glowed.

Not brightly, but with a tiny blue flame no larger than a match head. It burned at the center of the disconnected housing, pulsing in time with the bells.

“A ship is coming,” the voice said.

Mara wiped frost from the eastern window. At first she saw nothing. Then the fog shifted.

Far beyond the reef, black masts emerged.

There were three, each carrying ragged sails. Beneath them glided a long wooden hull, its bow rising and falling on a sea that remained smooth everywhere else. No lights shone on deck. No figures moved along the rails.

The ship came directly toward the island.

“Should I light you?” Mara whispered.

The blue flame trembled.

“Yes,” said the lighthouse.

She reached for the auxiliary power switch installed for demonstrations. It operated a small modern bulb, not the ancient lamp. Her fingers closed around it.

Then she remembered Arlen’s journal.

DO NOT LET THEM SEE THE SHORE.

Mara pulled her hand away. “What happens if I do?”

The glass lens began to turn by itself. Rusted gears shrieked beneath the floor.

“They will be saved.”

“Saved from what?”

“The sea.”

“And if I don’t?”

“They will sail forever.”

The ship drew nearer. Through a break in the fog, Mara glimpsed pale shapes crowded on its deck. Passengers. Dozens of them, perhaps hundreds, standing shoulder to shoulder.

Something struck the lighthouse door far below.

Three heavy blows.

Mara froze.

Again: boom, boom, boom.

A voice rose through the stairwell.

“Keeper!”

It sounded like a child.

“Keeper, please open!”

Mara backed against the window. “They’re already here.”

“Some,” said the lighthouse.

The pounding resumed. More voices joined the first.

“Please!”

“We’re cold!”

“Let us in!”

Mara thought of the dead starling, the sealed windows, and Keeper Arlen fleeing without explanation. She looked through the eastern glass. The ship was close enough now that she could see its broken rigging and the silver crust of salt covering its hull.

The people on deck were not facing the island.

Every one of them faced backward, looking out to sea.

Something moved behind the ship.

It was vast enough to change the shape of the horizon. A darkness gathered beneath the fog, wider than the island, taller than the cliffs. For a moment Mara thought a wave was rising, but the surface of it opened.

An eye stared over the ship’s stern.

The people on deck began to scream.

“Light me,” urged the tower.

Mara understood then that the lighthouse had not summoned the vessel. It was trying to guide it home.

She threw the switch.

The modern bulb flickered weakly. A narrow beam crawled across the fog and vanished before reaching the reef.

“Not enough,” said the lighthouse.

“That’s all I have.”

“Give me flame.”

“The old lamp has no fuel.”

“Give me yours.”

The words passed through Mara like winter.

“What does that mean?”

Below, the lighthouse door splintered. Footsteps began ascending the stairs—not running, but climbing steadily, hundreds of wet feet striking stone.

The blue flame inside the lamp stretched toward Mara.

“Every light requires a burning.”

She understood why Arlen had fled. Perhaps he had refused. Perhaps the ship came once in every keeper’s life, offering the same choice.

Mara looked again at the crowded deck. Among the passengers stood men in naval coats, women in old-fashioned dresses, soldiers, fishermen, children. They came from different centuries, all stolen from storms, wrecks, and forgotten crossings. They had sailed for years—or for hundreds of years—with that immense shadow following.

A small figure at the rail turned toward her.

The child wore a yellow coat.

Mara’s breath caught.

Her younger brother had owned a coat like that. Tomas had been eight when the sea took him. She had been twelve. They had climbed down to the northern beach despite their father’s warning, and a wave had torn Tomas from her hand.

His body was never found.

The figure lifted its face.

Even at that distance, she recognized him.

“Mara!” he cried.

She stumbled toward the window.

The footsteps reached the final spiral below the lantern room.

“Mara!” Tomas called again. He had not aged a day. His dark hair lay wet across his forehead, and one sleeve of the yellow coat was torn where she had tried to hold him.

“Is he alive?” she asked.

“No,” said the lighthouse.

“Can you bring him back?”

“No.”

The answer was cruel in its simplicity.

“Then what does saving mean?”

“An end.”

The lantern-room door shook as something struck it from below. The lock bent.

On the sea, the great eye rose higher. Water streamed from a lid like a collapsing waterfall. Beneath it, a mouth opened across the horizon.

Mara placed one hand on the lamp. The glass was warm.

“Will it hurt?”

“Yes.”

“Will I die?”

“Yes.”

She almost laughed. “You could lie.”

“I am a light. I reveal.”

The door burst inward.

Fog flooded the room, carrying figures in dripping clothes. Their faces were gray, their eyes clouded with salt. Some wore seaweed like necklaces; others bore the wounds of their deaths. They did not attack Mara. They crowded around the walls and watched her with desperate silence.

Keeper Arlen stood among them.

She knew him from an old photograph in the tower museum. He looked younger than he had in the picture, but terror had aged his eyes.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

“You left them,” Mara replied.

“I was afraid.”

“So am I.”

Outside, the shadow descended upon the ship.

Tomas reached toward the lighthouse.

Mara laid both hands on the glass housing.

“Take it,” she said.

The blue flame leaped into her palms.

Cold struck first, so intense that her bones seemed to ring. Then came heat. It passed up her arms, through her chest, and into the private chambers of her heart. Memories opened like doors: her father teaching her the names of stars; her mother singing while she baked bread; Tomas putting a crab in Mara’s shoe and laughing until he fell over; summer visitors climbing the tower; winter rain against the cottage windows.

The lighthouse took everything but did not destroy it. Each memory became light.

The dead stepped back.

Mara could no longer feel the floor. Her body had become transparent, her veins burning gold beneath her skin. The ancient lens began to turn, smoothly now, gathering her brightness and shaping it.

“Look homeward,” she whispered.

The beam erupted across the sea.

Fog vanished before it. The island flashed white as noon. Far below, villagers woke and ran to their windows. Edrin Hale came from his cottage and fell to his knees.

The beam crossed Widow’s Reef and struck the ship.

For one terrible second, nothing happened.

Then the passengers turned toward the island.

Their faces changed. Fear left them. The years of wandering loosened their grip. The ship’s broken sails filled with golden wind, and its salt-black hull became pale timber once more.

The darkness behind it recoiled.

Its eye closed, but the beam found it. Light poured across scales larger than houses, across drowned towers and tangled forests carried upon its back. The thing had fed on all that was lost, growing vast in the uncharted places of the world.

It opened its mouth.

The lighthouse beam entered.

A sound like the cracking of the moon shook the sea. The creature folded inward, collapsing into shadow, then mist, then a single black point that trembled above the water and went out.

The ship sailed on.

It passed through the reef as though through an open gate. When it reached the cove below the lighthouse, it began to fade. One by one, the passengers became sparks, rising from the deck in a luminous cloud.

Tomas was the last.

He stood at the bow, smiling up at Mara.

“You let go,” he said.

The words reached her through glass, wind, and light.

“I tried to hold on,” she answered.

“I know.”

“I’m sorry.”

“I know that too.”

He lifted a hand. Mara lifted hers.

Then her brother became a small golden flame and joined the others climbing toward the stars.

The ship disappeared beneath him.

Inside the lantern room, the drowned figures faded as well. Keeper Arlen removed his cap and bowed to Mara before the light took him. Soon only one figure remained: a woman in an old keeper’s uniform, standing beside the lamp.

Mara had never seen her before.

“Who are you?” she asked.

“The one who burned before you.”

“Did you speak through the lighthouse?”

“Sometimes.”

Mara looked down at her hands. They were no longer hands, not exactly. They had become patterns of brightness reflected in the lens.

“What happens now?”

“You keep the light.”

“For how long?”

“Until it is needed again.”

The woman touched Mara’s cheek. Her fingers felt like warm sunlight.

“Do not be afraid. A lighthouse is not a prison. It is a promise.”

Then she, too, vanished.

At dawn, Edrin climbed the tower.

He found the lantern-room door broken and the old lens turning silently. The disconnected lamp shone with a clear golden flame. There was no sign of Mara except her boots, placed neatly beside the eastern window.

He searched the island. Everyone did. Boats circled the cliffs, and divers explored the cove, though Edrin told them they would find nothing.

The council called it a tragic accident. They fenced the tower and discussed removing the lamp to a mainland museum.

But when workmen arrived, their machines failed. Ropes snapped. Batteries died. One man swore that a woman’s voice had spoken from the lens, politely instructing him to put down his hammer.

The lighthouse remained.

Years passed. The island harbor was dredged, and ships returned to the coast. Captains soon learned that the old lighthouse, absent from modern charts, shone on dangerous nights. Its beam reached farther than any ordinary light, revealing reefs hidden by storms and guiding vessels safely through fog.

Sometimes passengers saw figures inside the beam: sailors, children, people dressed in the fashions of vanished centuries. They always appeared peaceful.

Edrin visited every evening until he became too old to climb. On his final visit, he sat in the lantern room while sunset reddened the sea.

“You did well,” he said.

The lamp hummed.

“Were you lonely?” Mara asked.

Edrin smiled. He had been waiting many years to hear her voice again.

“Sometimes.”

“Are you afraid?”

“Yes.”

The lens turned, scattering gold across his weathered face.

“Every light requires a burning,” Mara said. “But not all burning is an end.”

Edrin closed his eyes.

When the village keeper climbed the tower the next morning, he found the old man’s body sitting peacefully beside the lamp. On the glass rested a single warm handprint, shining like sunlight.

That night, two voices spoke within the lighthouse.

And far beyond the horizon, where maps gave way to darkness, a lost ship turned toward home.

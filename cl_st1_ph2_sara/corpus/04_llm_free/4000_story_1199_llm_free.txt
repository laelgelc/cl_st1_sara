### The Cartographer of Lost Roads

By the time Mara Venn reached Bellweather, the road behind her had disappeared.

She noticed this only when she stopped to rest beneath an alder tree and looked back for the milestone she had passed an hour earlier. There was no milestone. There was no road. A field of silver grass stretched to the horizon, bending under a wind she could not feel.

Mara removed the folded map from inside her coat.

The road was still there in ink: a narrow line running east from Harrow’s Bridge to Bellweather. She traced it with one finger. The paper felt unusually warm.

Ahead, the village sat between two green hills. Its slate roofs shone after the morning rain, and smoke rose from chimneys in straight, dark columns. At the highest point stood a clock tower without hands.

Mara returned the map to her coat and continued walking.

She was accustomed to unreliable roads. For twelve years she had worked as a surveyor for the Royal Office of Boundaries, mapping mountain passes, forest tracks, disputed fields, and coastlines that storms rearranged each winter. Roads flooded. Bridges collapsed. Farmers moved fences under cover of darkness. Once, in the northern marshes, an entire river had changed course because a family of beavers objected to the Crown’s latest chart.

But roads did not usually vanish.

At the village entrance, a wooden sign read:

BELLWEATHER  
POPULATION: 312

Someone had crossed out the number and written 311 beneath it. That number had also been crossed out.

Below, in smaller letters, was a warning:

TRAVELERS MUST LEAVE BEFORE SUNSET.

Mara took out her notebook and copied the words.

The main street was deserted. Curtains moved in windows as she passed, but no one came outside. A loaf of bread cooled on a bakery sill. A broom lay in front of the inn. In the square, a stone fountain trickled into a basin full of copper coins.

Mara approached the clock tower. A woman waited beneath it, wearing a red scarf over her gray hair.

“You’re late,” the woman said.

“I wasn’t told to arrive at a particular time.”

“Not late for an appointment. Late in general.”

The woman peered at Mara’s face as if searching for something familiar.

“You’re the cartographer?”

“Surveyor.”

“Is there a difference?”

“Cartographers draw maps. Surveyors discover what belongs on them.”

“Then you’re the one we need.”

The woman introduced herself as Nella Pike, Bellweather’s schoolteacher and, since the disappearance of the mayor, its acting magistrate.

“When did your mayor disappear?” Mara asked.

“Eight days ago.”

“And the others?”

Nella glanced toward the silent houses.

“You saw the sign.”

“Yes.”

“It began with three hundred and twelve. We’re at two hundred and eighty-six now.”

“Twenty-six people in eight days?”

“Twenty-seven, if you count the mayor.”

“Why wouldn’t I?”

“Because some people believe he left on purpose.”

“Did he?”

“No.”

Nella led Mara into the clock tower.

Inside, hundreds of maps covered the walls. Some were official county surveys, neatly printed and stamped. Others had been drawn on scraps of parchment, flour sacks, pages torn from books, and pieces of bark. Every map showed Bellweather and the surrounding hills.

None of them agreed.

On one, three roads left the village. On another, there were seven. A child’s drawing showed a blue path winding into the sky. A map dated sixty years earlier placed Bellweather beside the sea, though the nearest coast lay four hundred miles west.

Mara moved from wall to wall.

“Who made these?”

“Everyone,” Nella said. “We started after Tom Arlen vanished. His wife went to fetch him from the orchard, but there was no orchard behind their house. Only a road.”

“What happened to the road?”

“It vanished the next morning. So did Tom.”

Mara examined a charcoal sketch. It showed the village square and a narrow alley between the inn and the bakery.

“There’s no alley there,” she said.

“There was yesterday.”

“And today?”

“Today there’s a well.”

Mara looked through the tower window. Between the inn and bakery stood an old stone well, its rim furred with moss. She was certain it had not been there when she entered the square.

She took out her instruments: compass, chain, sighting rod, brass dividers. The compass needle spun once, settled north, then slowly turned to point at the well.

“Tell me everything,” she said.

Nella did.

The disappearances had begun three weeks earlier. At first, objects went missing: a garden gate, a toolshed, six apple trees, a footbridge. Then places began to change. A lane led to a pond one morning and a cemetery by afternoon. The schoolhouse acquired a second floor that no one remembered building. A shepherd opened his front door and found himself on a hillside miles away.

Then people vanished.

Some disappeared while traveling alone. Others stepped briefly out of sight and never returned. A girl named Elsie Row went into a pantry for sugar. When her mother followed, the pantry contained shelves of dust and a small window overlooking an unfamiliar city.

“Was the girl in the city?” Mara asked.

“No. But someone was waving from one of the rooftops.”

“Someone she recognized?”

“Her mother won’t say.”

Mara spent the afternoon measuring the village.

Bellweather resisted measurement.

The main street was four hundred and twelve yards long when she walked north and three hundred and ninety when she returned. The clock tower’s shadow pointed east, though the sun hung in the west. The schoolhouse had eleven windows from outside and fourteen from within.

At three o’clock, Mara drove an iron stake into the square. She tied one end of her survey chain to it and walked toward the northern hill.

After sixty paces, she reached the same stake from the opposite direction.

The watching villagers murmured.

Mara crouched beside the stake. “Has anyone tried leaving?”

“Of course,” said a blacksmith named Orro.

“What happened?”

“I took the west road with my sons. We walked until dark and came to my own forge. We tried again the next morning. Came back through the church.”

“The church door?”

“The crypt.”

“Has anyone successfully reached another village?”

“You did,” Nella said.

“I came from outside.”

“That’s not the same as getting back.”

Sunset was two hours away.

Mara studied the maps inside the tower again. There were patterns beneath the contradictions. Certain landmarks appeared repeatedly: the tower, the northern hill, the well, a ring of standing stones in the forest. Some maps included a place called Wren’s Crossing, though Nella insisted no such place existed.

One map, drawn in faded green ink, showed only a single road. It began in Bellweather’s square and ended at a black circle under the northern hill.

“Who made this?” Mara asked.

Nella went pale.

“My brother.”

“When?”

“Thirty-one years ago.”

“What happened then?”

“Nothing.”

Mara waited.

Nella touched the edge of the map. “Nothing we were allowed to discuss.”

Outside, the handless clock rang four times.

Nella lowered herself into a chair.

“When I was nine, my brother Cal found a road in the woods. He said it wasn’t always there. It appeared when he was lonely.”

“Roads don’t appear because someone is lonely.”

“In Bellweather, they might.”

“What was at the end?”

“He never told me. One winter night, he packed food and blankets. I saw him leave, but I didn’t wake our parents. I thought he wanted to frighten us. He was sixteen. He never came home.”

“And the map?”

“I found it under his bed.”

Mara held the paper near the window. The green ink shone faintly.

“Was your brother left-handed?”

“Yes.”

“This was drawn by a right-handed person.”

Nella stared at her.

Mara pointed to the hatching along the hills. “A left-handed draftsman usually shades in the opposite direction. Whoever made this wanted you to think Cal drew it.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know.”

A cry rose from the square.

They hurried outside.

The well had vanished. In its place stood a narrow alley between the inn and bakery. At the far end, beneath a yellow sky, was a boy in a blue coat.

Nella stopped breathing.

“Cal,” she whispered.

The boy raised one hand.

Nella ran.

Mara caught her scarf and pulled her back just before she entered the alley.

“That isn’t your brother.”

“You don’t know that.”

“Your brother would be forty-seven.”

“He’s trapped.”

“Or something wants you to follow.”

The boy smiled. His teeth were green with moss.

The alley narrowed. Its walls stretched upward, becoming gray cliffs. A freezing wind rushed through, carrying the smell of deep earth.

Mara drew a piece of chalk from her pocket and marked an X on the cobbles. The alley shuddered. For an instant, she glimpsed a forest where the walls had been, then a long corridor lined with doors, then darkness.

The boy lowered his hand.

Behind him, something enormous moved under the yellow sky.

“Close your eyes,” Mara told Nella.

Mara unfolded the official county map. She placed it on the ground over the chalk mark and drove one of her brass dividers through the paper into the cobblestone.

The alley snapped shut like a book.

The well returned.

Nella fell to her knees.

“What did you do?”

“Gave the place a choice,” Mara said.

“A choice?”

“Between the map and itself.”

“And it chose?”

“Not to be pinned down.”

The clock rang five times.

Villagers gathered around them. Their fear had changed into desperation.

“Can you stop it?” Orro asked.

Mara looked toward the northern hill.

“I can try.”

She spent the remaining daylight collecting every map in Bellweather. The villagers brought charts from attics, family Bibles, desk drawers, and locked chests. Mara laid them across the floor of the clock tower, arranging them by age.

The oldest was not the green map. It was a survey made one hundred and eighty-three years earlier, when the village consisted of eleven houses and a chapel.

Beneath the northern hill, the surveyor had drawn a symbol Mara had seen only twice before: a circle crossed by an open line.

A road unfinished.

In the margins, almost erased, were five words:

DO NOT MAP THE WAY THROUGH.

Mara read them aloud.

Nella stood beside her, twisting her red scarf. “What does it mean?”

“Maps don’t merely describe places,” Mara said. “Not always. A good map shapes how people understand the land. It tells them where they can go. Enough belief, repeated over enough years, can turn a path into a road and a border into a wall.”

“You’re saying maps change the world?”

“People change the world. Maps give them instructions.”

She tapped the symbol.

“Someone found a passage under the hill. The first surveyor refused to chart it. Later maps copied his work, but they left out the warning. The passage was forgotten.”

“Until Cal?”

“Perhaps.”

“What’s inside?”

Mara looked at the maps covering the walls.

“Every road Bellweather might have had. Every version of the village imagined, forgotten, or abandoned. The passage doesn’t lead to one place. It leads to possibilities.”

Nella sank into a chair.

“And it’s opening?”

“I think someone opened it years ago. Only a little. Now it’s widening.”

The light outside faded. The clock tower began to groan.

Mara selected three maps: the original survey, the green map, and her official county chart. She placed them in her leather case, along with chalk, compass, cord, and a small flask of black ink.

“You’re going to the hill,” Nella said.

“Yes.”

“I’m coming.”

“No.”

“My brother is there.”

“Something that remembers your brother is there.”

“Then I’m still coming.”

Mara considered refusing again, but the windows had begun to show different skies. In one, stars glittered. In another, red clouds boiled over a desert. The third revealed an ocean beating against the tower walls.

There was no time to argue.

They left through the eastern door and emerged from the western side.

Bellweather no longer resembled a village. Roads uncoiled through the square like ribbons. Doors stood unsupported in the grass. Houses flickered between shapes: cottages, ruins, mansions, mounds of freshly turned soil.

The villagers tied themselves together with rope so no one could vanish alone.

Mara fastened one end of her measuring cord around her waist and handed the spool to Orro.

“Keep it tight.”

“What if the road moves?”

“It will.”

“What do we do then?”

“Don’t follow me.”

Mara and Nella started toward the northern hill.

The journey should have taken fifteen minutes. Instead they crossed a wheat field under summer sunlight, a frozen river, and a street crowded with silent people wearing silver masks. Each time the landscape changed, Mara drew a chalk line at their feet and consulted the oldest map.

“Don’t look into windows,” she warned Nella.

“Why?”

“Because you may see somewhere you’d rather be.”

They entered the forest at dusk.

The trees grew upside down, their roots tangled against the darkening sky. Birds flew beneath the soil, visible as ripples through fallen leaves. Between the trunks, the boy in the blue coat appeared and vanished.

Nella kept walking.

“What was Cal like?” Mara asked.

“Restless. Kind when no one was watching. Cruel when he thought people expected it.”

“Did he want to leave Bellweather?”

“Everyone does at sixteen.”

“Did you?”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you?”

Nella was quiet for several steps.

“Our parents were broken after he disappeared. I stayed for them. Then I stayed for the school. Then one day I realized I had become someone who stayed.”

Ahead, the trees parted.

The northern hill rose from a clearing. Its side had split open, revealing a road descending into golden mist. Stone markers lined the way, each engraved with the name of a missing villager.

Tom Arlen. Elsie Row. Mayor Vale.

At the entrance stood Cal, still sixteen, still smiling with mossy teeth.

“You let me go,” he said to Nella.

His voice was not a boy’s voice. It was a crowd speaking from the bottom of a cavern.

Nella trembled.

“I was nine.”

“You were glad.”

“I was angry.”

“You wished I would disappear.”

“I wished you would come back sorry.”

Cal stepped closer.

“Come with me now.”

Beyond him, figures moved in the golden mist. The missing villagers called to their families. Their voices drifted through the forest, sweet and clear.

Mara took out the green map.

“Who drew this?” she asked.

Cal’s smile widened.

“You did.”

“Not yet.”

“Time is only another road.”

Mara understood then.

The map had been waiting in Bellweather for thirty-one years because she would draw it tonight. The passage did not merely contain possible places. It contained possible histories, each seeking a path into the world.

“Why bring me here?” she asked.

“You make roads true.”

“No. I measure them.”

“You decide where they begin.”

The golden mist rolled forward. In it, Mara saw a harbor city beneath white cliffs.

Her breath caught.

A woman stood on the quay, waving.

Mara’s mother had been dead for twelve years, drowned during a storm while Mara was surveying the western coast. Mara had watched the sea take her boat. She had spent months mapping every current, reef, and inlet, as though accuracy could become a form of apology.

In the golden mist, her mother smiled.

“There’s a road to the morning before she died,” Cal said. “A road to every hour you regret.”

Mara took one step forward.

Her mother’s face sharpened. Wind lifted the woman’s hair exactly as Mara remembered.

“You could warn her,” Nella whispered.

Mara looked at her.

Nella’s eyes were fixed on Cal. Tears ran down her cheeks.

“You could save her,” Nella said, but the words sounded like something she was telling herself.

Behind them, Bellweather’s clock rang though the tower was far away.

Six chimes.

At the final note, the hill opened wider.

Roads burst outward.

One tore through the forest toward the village. Another climbed into the sky. A third vanished beneath Mara’s boots, and suddenly she was standing in her childhood kitchen.

Rain tapped the window. Her mother hummed at the table, mending a sail.

Mara was ten years old again.

She knew this morning. There would be warm bread. Her father would return before noon. Her mother would live another fifteen years.

“Mara?” her mother said.

For one terrible moment, she almost answered.

Then she noticed the map on the table.

It showed Bellweather.

Her mother had never heard of Bellweather.

Mara picked up the map and tore it in half.

The kitchen vanished.

She fell to her knees in the forest. Nella was beside her, struggling toward the golden road while Cal held out his hand.

Mara seized Nella’s scarf.

“He isn’t waiting for you,” Mara said.

“I have to know.”

“You already know. Your brother left.”

“He was a child.”

“So were you.”

The thing wearing Cal’s face tilted its head.

Mara pulled out her ink flask and poured a circle on the oldest map, enclosing the northern hill. Then she drew a line across the road symbol.

Nothing happened.

The road laughed.

“You can’t erase what people remember,” it said.

Mara stared at the maps. The old survey. The official chart. The green map she had not yet drawn.

It was right. She could not erase the road. Bellweather had fed it for generations—with stories, grief, imagined escapes, and all the lives its people believed they might have lived elsewhere.

But a road was not defined only by where it went.

It was defined by where it began.

Mara turned the green map over. On the blank side, she drew the clock tower, the square, and the northern hill. Her hand moved quickly, copying every false mark exactly. At the bottom, she drew a small figure standing at the mouth of the passage.

Then she gave the figure a blue coat.

Cal’s smile faded.

“This is your map,” Mara said. “Not mine.”

She drew a line from the figure into the hill, but no line leading out.

“You came here thirty-one years ago,” she continued. “You opened the way because you wanted somewhere else to be true. The road learned your loneliness. It wore your face. But you were not its prisoner.”

Nella stared at the boy.

“You were its door,” she said.

Cal began to change.

He grew taller, older. His hair whitened. His face became a hundred faces: lost children, vanished travelers, dead parents, unborn sons and daughters. At last it became a hollow shape filled with branching roads.

Mara closed the ink circle.

“You are not every way the world could be,” she said. “You are only the way Cal chose.”

The forest shook.

The golden mist rushed backward. The roads snapped one after another, recoiling into the hill. The false figures screamed in borrowed voices.

Nella reached toward her brother.

For an instant, the real Cal looked back from inside the hollow shape. He was no longer sixteen. He was tired, middle-aged, and afraid.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

Nella removed her red scarf and laid it on the road.

“So am I.”

Mara drew the final line.

The hill closed.

Dawn found them in the square.

The clock tower had hands again. They pointed to seven minutes past six.

Around the fountain stood twenty-seven missing villagers, dazed but alive. Tom Arlen clutched a basket of apples. Elsie Row held a cup of sugar. Mayor Vale wore one slipper and appeared irritated that anyone had doubted him.

Cal was not among them.

The northern road was gone.

For the next three days, Mara surveyed Bellweather from end to end. The main street remained four hundred and twelve yards in both directions. The schoolhouse had eleven windows outside and eleven within. No unexpected oceans appeared beyond pantry doors.

When she finished, she produced a new map.

It showed two roads leaving Bellweather: one west to Harrow’s Bridge and one south toward the county town. Beneath the northern hill, Mara left an empty space.

Nella studied it.

“Shouldn’t there be a warning?”

“Warnings attract the curious.”

“What if the passage opens again?”

“It might.”

“That doesn’t worry you?”

“Everything opens again eventually. Roads. Wounds. Questions.”

Nella rolled up the map.

At the village entrance, the sign had been repainted:

BELLWEATHER  
POPULATION: 312

The warning beneath it was gone.

Mara adjusted her pack and took the western road. Nella walked with her as far as the alder tree.

“Will this road stay where it is?” Nella asked.

“For today.”

“And tomorrow?”

“You’ll have to walk it to find out.”

Nella smiled. “That sounds like something a cartographer would say.”

“Surveyor.”

“Is there a difference?”

Mara looked west.

The road curved through fields bright with morning. Far away, beyond the hills, it divided into paths she could not yet see. Some would end at rivers. Some at locked gates. Some might lead toward lives she had once wanted.

But behind her stood Bellweather, real and imperfect, its chimneys smoking beneath the handless blue of the sky.

“Yes,” she said. “A cartographer tells you where the road goes.”

She stepped forward.

“A surveyor goes there first.”

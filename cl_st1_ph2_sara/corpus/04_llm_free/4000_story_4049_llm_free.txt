## The Cartographer of Lost Hours

Every morning at seven, Mara Vale unlocked the narrow green door between the bakery and the watchmaker’s shop and entered a room that did not officially exist.

The brass plate beside the door read:

**DEPARTMENT OF TEMPORAL IRREGULARITIES**  
**MAPS, RECORDS, AND RECOVERIES**

Most people assumed it was decorative. Children knocked on the glass and ran away. Tourists photographed the plate, believing it part of the old city’s charm. Only those who had lost an hour—truly lost it—ever tried the handle.

Inside, the department smelled of dust, rain, and clock oil. Shelves rose from floor to ceiling, filled with rolled maps tied in colored thread. Blue thread marked hours lost in sleep. Red marked accidents. Yellow marked hours surrendered willingly. Black marked those that had been stolen.

Mara’s worktable stood beneath a skylight. On it lay her instruments: a silver compass, a ruler made from smoked glass, a bottle of midnight ink, and a small clock with no hands.

She was the city’s last cartographer of lost hours.

At seven fifteen, her first visitor arrived.

He was an elderly man in a brown coat, holding his hat against his chest.

“Miss Vale?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“I’ve misplaced Thursday afternoon.”

Mara motioned for him to sit.

“All of it?”

“From two until nearly five. I remember feeding pigeons in Bell Square. Then I was standing on the river bridge at sunset. My shoes were wet, and I had this in my pocket.”

He placed a white chess piece on the table: a queen, carved from bone.

Mara opened the registry.

“Name?”

“Edwin Pike.”

“Age?”

“Seventy-three.”

“Any history of temporal wandering?”

“Not that I know of.”

“Did the missing hours feel frightening?”

Mr. Pike considered this.

“No,” he said. “That’s the strange part. They felt happy.”

Mara dipped her pen in midnight ink. The liquid reflected a sky full of stars, though morning light filled the room.

“Happy hours are difficult,” she said. “They often resist recovery.”

“Why?”

“Because they may have left on purpose.”

She asked him to hold the handless clock. Its face remained blank for a moment, then a thin golden line appeared across the glass.

Mara set the silver compass beside it. The needle spun, slowed, and pointed east.

“Your Thursday is somewhere near the botanical gardens,” she said. “Possibly in the old maze.”

Mr. Pike leaned forward. “Can you bring it back?”

“I can try.”

He smiled with such relief that Mara had to look away.

Once, she had believed every lost hour should be returned. Years in the department had taught her otherwise. Some hours escaped because they were unbearable. Others departed because they contained truths their owners were not ready to remember. And a few—rare, bright, dangerous—went missing because time itself had taken pity.

Mara rolled a blank sheet across the table and began drawing Mr. Pike’s route. Bell Square. The river. The gardens. She marked each location in black ink, then used gold where memory failed.

At the edge of the paper, near the old maze, a door appeared.

Mara stopped breathing.

It was no larger than her thumbnail: a green door with a brass knob.

She knew that door.

For twenty years, she had drawn maps of other people’s missing time, and for twenty years the same door had appeared at the edge of her own dreams. Behind it waited the hour she had lost when she was twelve—the hour in which her younger brother, Eli, had vanished.

She had searched for it in every district of the city. She had followed echoes into abandoned stations, empty theaters, flooded cellars, and rooms where clocks ran backward. She had never found even a trace.

Until now.

“Is something wrong?” Mr. Pike asked.

Mara covered the door with her palm.

“No,” she said. “I’ve found your Thursday.”

The botanical gardens closed at dusk, but Mara had keys to every public gate in the city and several that opened into less public places.

That evening she entered through the eastern arch.

Winter had stripped the gardens bare. Frost silvered the paths. The glass conservatory glowed beneath the darkening sky, and statues watched from beds of sleeping roses.

The old maze lay beyond a grove of ash trees. Its hedges had grown wild, knitting together overhead. A sign warned visitors that the maze was unsafe after sunset.

Mara lit a lantern and went in.

Ordinary mazes are designed to confuse space. This one confused time.

At the first turn, dawn flashed across the sky. At the second, Mara heard summer insects. Around the third corner, she passed herself walking in the opposite direction, older by perhaps ten years. Neither version spoke.

She kept one hand on Mr. Pike’s map. The golden path shifted as she moved.

Left. Right. Left again.

At the center of the maze stood a dry fountain. A stone queen rose from its basin, her hands extended. One hand was empty.

Mara placed Mr. Pike’s chess piece in the queen’s palm.

Water burst from the fountain.

It flowed upward, silver streams rising into the air. Images flickered in the water: Mr. Pike laughing beneath a green umbrella; a woman with white hair moving a chess piece across a café table; two hands clasped on the river bridge.

Then Mara saw the green door.

It stood behind the fountain, set into a wall of ivy.

Her lantern trembled.

According to departmental procedure, she should have collected Mr. Pike’s lost hours, sealed them in a glass vial, and returned at once. Unmapped doors were never to be opened alone. Personal temporal artifacts were never to be pursued during an official recovery.

Mara had written those rules herself.

She crossed the basin and touched the brass knob.

The door opened.

Warm air brushed her face. Beyond the threshold stretched Bell Square in summer, crowded with market stalls. Colored banners hung from the rooftops. Musicians played beside the fountain, and pigeons erupted into the blue sky.

Mr. Pike sat at an outdoor café across from the white-haired woman.

They were playing chess.

Mara stepped through.

No one noticed her. Lost hours rarely acknowledged visitors.

She approached the café. Mr. Pike looked younger, though only by a little. The woman studied the board with a smile.

“You’ll lose your queen again,” he told her.

“I always do.”

“You could be more careful.”

“I could,” she said. “But then how would you find me?”

She moved the white queen to the edge of the board.

Mr. Pike’s hand hovered over a knight. His expression softened.

“I waited every Thursday,” he said.

“I know.”

“For fifty-two years.”

“I know that too.”

The woman reached across the table and touched his face.

Mara understood then. The woman was not merely absent. She was dead.

This was no forgotten appointment or spell of confusion. Mr. Pike’s lost Thursday was a gift: three hours with someone time had already taken.

The music slowed. Shadows lengthened across the square.

Mr. Pike glanced toward the river.

“What happens when the bells ring?” he asked.

“I go,” the woman said.

“Where?”

“Where I was before.”

“Will I remember this?”

“That depends.”

“On what?”

“Whether you choose memory or mercy.”

The first bell rang.

The square blurred at the edges.

Mara looked down at her handless clock. A thin crack had appeared across its face. The hour was collapsing. If she did nothing, Mr. Pike would keep only the happiness without its cause. If she recovered the memory, he would remember the woman—and lose her again.

The second bell rang.

The white-haired woman turned her head and looked directly at Mara.

“You shouldn’t be here,” she said.

Mr. Pike did not seem to hear.

Mara stepped back. “You can see me?”

“You’re carrying a door that doesn’t belong to you.”

“What door?”

The woman nodded toward Mara’s shadow.

There, stretched across the sunlit stones, was the outline of the green door.

“Where does it lead?” Mara asked.

“To the hour you buried.”

“I didn’t bury it. It was taken from me.”

“Those are sometimes the same thing.”

The third bell rang.

The market stalls vanished. The musicians faded. Mr. Pike and the woman remained at their table in a square rapidly emptying of light.

Mara took out a glass vial.

“I came for his hours.”

“Then ask him if he wants them.”

Mr. Pike looked up at last. His gaze passed through Mara, then settled.

“Someone’s there,” he whispered.

The woman nodded. “She has come to take you back.”

“Must I go?”

“Yes.”

“Will I remember?”

“That is your choice.”

Mara held out the vial. “Edwin Pike, do you reclaim these hours?”

He looked at the woman.

Tears filled his eyes, but his voice was steady.

“No,” he said. “Let me keep only the warmth.”

The vial shattered in Mara’s hand.

The fourth bell rang, and the lost Thursday folded inward like a sheet of burning paper.

Before darkness swallowed the square, the woman spoke.

“Open your hand, Mara.”

Mara looked down.

The white chess queen lay in her palm.

Then she was back in the maze.

The fountain was dry. Snow had begun to fall. The green door remained behind the basin, but now it stood open.

Beyond it was a corridor lined with blue wallpaper.

Mara knew that wallpaper. Her family’s old house had been demolished years ago, but the pattern—tiny silver birds among curling vines—had never left her memory.

At the end of the corridor, a boy laughed.

“Eli?”

The name came out as a breath.

She stepped through and found herself twelve years old.

Not in body—her hands were still the hands of a forty-one-year-old woman—but in terror. It seized her with the completeness of childhood, when fear had no edges and no proportion.

Rain struck the windows. The hallway smelled of damp wool and candle smoke. Somewhere downstairs, her father was shouting.

Mara remembered this night.

Eli had been seven. Their mother had died the previous winter, and their father had begun drinking. The house had become a place of slammed doors and unfinished meals. That evening, Mara and Eli had hidden upstairs while a storm flooded the streets.

But there had always been a gap in her memory.

At eight, she and Eli were in the attic.

At nine, she was alone on the front steps, screaming his name.

Between them: nothing.

Now the missing hour waited ahead.

She climbed the attic stairs.

Eli sat beneath the round window, arranging wooden animals along the sill. He was small and serious, with dark hair falling into his eyes.

“You’re late,” he said.

Mara gripped the railing.

He looked exactly as he had in her dreams, yet more alive than any dream had allowed: a scab on one knee, ink on his fingers, one sock falling down.

“Eli.”

“You said we’d leave at eight.”

“Leave?”

He frowned. “You promised.”

Memory stirred like something waking underwater.

Their father shouting. A glass breaking. Eli crying. Mara whispering, *We’ll run away. We’ll go to Aunt Lysa’s. I promise.*

She had packed bread, two apples, and a blanket. She had taken coins from her father’s desk. At eight, she had led Eli out through the attic window and onto the roof.

Rain had made the tiles slick.

“No,” Mara whispered.

The scene moved around her.

Her younger self opened the window. Wind rushed in. Eli climbed out first.

Adult Mara followed, though her legs had gone numb.

The roof sloped toward the alley. Across a narrow gap stood the abandoned house next door. From there they could reach the fire escape and the street beyond.

Twelve-year-old Mara jumped first.

She landed badly but safely. She turned and held out her hands.

“Come on, Eli!”

The boy crouched on the opposite ledge. Rain streamed down his face.

“I can’t.”

“Yes, you can. It’s easy.”

“It’s too far.”

Behind him, the attic door shook beneath a heavy blow.

Their father’s voice thundered from inside the house.

Eli flinched.

“Jump!” young Mara shouted.

He did.

For one instant, he hung between the buildings, arms reaching.

Then his foot struck the gutter.

Mara saw herself catch his wrist.

She had caught him.

This was not what she remembered. She had spent twenty years believing she had failed to reach him.

But there they were: Eli hanging above the alley, young Mara flat on the tiles, both hands locked around his arm.

“I’ve got you,” she gasped.

The attic window opened behind them.

Their father climbed onto the roof.

His face was gray with fear. He crawled toward Mara and grabbed her around the waist.

“Pull!” she screamed.

Together, they lifted Eli.

For a moment it seemed they would save him.

Then the old gutter tore free from the roof. Metal shrieked. Bricks cracked. Their father lost his grip on Mara, and Mara slid forward.

Eli looked up at her.

Strangely, he was no longer afraid.

“Let go,” he said.

“No!”

“You’ll fall too.”

“I won’t let go!”

“You have to.”

His hand slipped through hers.

Mara lunged, caught two fingers, one finger, then nothing.

The memory stopped.

Rain hung motionless in the air. Eli remained suspended below the roof, his face turned upward. Young Mara reached after him, frozen in a scream.

Adult Mara stood beside her, unable to breathe.

This was the moment she had buried. Not his fall, but her choice to survive it.

“You didn’t let go,” said a voice behind her.

The white-haired woman from Mr. Pike’s lost Thursday stood on the roof, untouched by rain.

“Not willingly,” Mara said.

“No.”

“I promised to protect him.”

“You were twelve.”

“I brought him up here.”

“You were trying to save you both.”

“I killed him.”

The woman’s eyes were kind, which made Mara furious.

“Don’t,” Mara said. “Don’t forgive me. You don’t know me.”

“No,” the woman replied. “But I know lost hours. They are not empty places, Mara. They are rooms we lock and continue living inside.”

She pointed toward the frozen boy.

“You can reclaim this hour. You can remember every second. Or you can close the door again.”

Mara looked at Eli.

For twenty years she had imagined a hundred endings. A stranger taking him. A hidden passage. A tear in time. She had built her life around the possibility that he might still be found.

The truth offered no possibility.

Only a rooftop. A storm. A hand slipping from hers.

“What happens if I close it?” she asked.

“You return to your search.”

“And if I remember?”

“You return to your life.”

Mara stepped closer to the edge.

Below, the alley waited in darkness, but Eli did not fall. Not yet. The hour would not continue without her consent.

She knelt beside her younger self.

The girl’s face was twisted with effort and horror.

Mara placed a hand on her shoulder.

“You held on,” she whispered.

The girl could not hear her.

“You held on as long as you could.”

Mara took the white chess queen from her pocket. It had become warm.

She placed it in Eli’s frozen hand.

“I reclaim this hour,” she said.

Time resumed.

Eli fell.

Young Mara screamed.

Their father dragged her away from the edge and held her while the rain came down. He was weeping, saying both children’s names over and over.

The scene dissolved, but not all at once.

For a moment, Eli stood before adult Mara in the rain. He looked seven and ageless.

“I knew you’d find me,” he said.

Then the rooftop vanished.

At seven the next morning, Mara unlocked the department’s green door.

Mr. Pike was waiting outside.

“Well?” he asked. “Did you find my Thursday?”

Mara invited him in and made tea.

“You spent it with a woman,” she said. “Her name was Clara.”

His eyes filled with sudden light.

“Clara,” he repeated.

“You played chess in Bell Square. You walked to the river. At the end, you chose not to remember.”

“Why?”

“You wanted to keep the happiness without suffering the loss again.”

He looked toward the shelves of maps.

“Did it work?”

“I don’t know.”

Mr. Pike smiled sadly. “No. I suppose it didn’t.”

Mara placed the white queen on the table.

He touched it with one finger.

“She always sacrificed her queen,” he murmured. “Said the game was more interesting when you stopped protecting the most important piece.”

“Do you want the full memory restored?”

“Can you restore it?”

“No. The hours are gone. But I can tell you what I saw.”

He considered this for a long time.

Then he shook his head.

“Just tell me one thing. Was she happy?”

“Yes.”

“And was I?”

“Yes.”

He closed his hand around the chess piece.

“That will be enough.”

After he left, Mara climbed the ladder to the highest shelf. At the back lay a map tied with black thread, untouched for twenty years.

She carried it to her desk.

The paper showed the old house, the flooded street, the rooftops, and the alley. For the first time, there was no green door at the edge.

Mara untied the thread.

She did not burn the map. Maps were not judgments, and memory was not a sentence. She rolled it carefully and retied it with gold.

Then she opened the registry and turned to a clean page.

At noon, a young woman entered the department. She wore a hospital uniform and held a broken wristwatch.

“I think I’ve lost a night,” she said.

Mara gestured to the chair.

“All of it?”

“Almost. I remember leaving work. Then I woke at the train station with no shoes and a bouquet of paper flowers.”

“Did the missing hours feel frightening?”

The woman hesitated.

“No,” she said. “They felt like being brave.”

Mara opened her bottle of midnight ink.

Outside, bells rang across the city. Bakers carried warm loaves into the street. The watchmaker next door raised his shutters. People hurried beneath umbrellas, losing minutes, wasting seconds, giving whole afternoons to love or grief without understanding their value.

Mara set the handless clock upon the table.

For years, she had thought her work was to recover what time had taken.

Now she understood.

Some hours needed to be found. Some needed to remain lost. And some waited patiently behind locked doors, not to punish those who had survived them, but to be carried at last into the light.

“Tell me everything you remember,” Mara said.

The young woman began.

And Mara drew the map.

The Bell Under the Lake

On the morning the lake began to ring, Mara Venn was repairing a clock that had not worked for thirty-two years.

It was a walnut mantel clock, blackened by smoke, with a silver moon painted on its face. The hands had stopped at seventeen minutes past two—the exact hour, according to its owner, when the old village disappeared beneath the reservoir.

“Don’t make it accurate,” Mrs. Vale had warned when she left it at Mara’s shop. “Make it faithful.”

Mara had not asked what that meant. People brought clocks to her because she asked few questions and returned every memory polished.

Her shop stood at the lowest end of Bellweather, where the street sloped toward the lake. Through its front window she could see the water lying smooth and gray under the November sky. Forty years earlier, engineers had built a dam across the valley and flooded the village of Lower Bellweather. The residents had been moved uphill, along with their furniture, their gravestones, and whatever grief they could carry. A new village grew above the water, though no one called it New Bellweather. People preferred to behave as if nothing had been replaced.

At first Mara thought the ringing came from the clock.

She removed her eyeglass, bent closer, and listened.

The mechanism lay open on the green felt mat. Its mainspring was broken. Its hammer had rusted solid. No sound could possibly come from it.

Yet the note continued: low, distant, and solemn.

Mara stepped outside.

Along the street, doors opened. A baker came out with flour on his forearms. Two schoolchildren stopped beside the postbox. Old Len Mercer stood in the road holding an empty birdcage.

The sound rolled through the stones beneath their feet.

Bong.

Everyone looked toward the lake.

Bong.

“It’s the church bell,” Len said.

No one laughed at him.

The tower of St. Orison’s had stood at the center of the drowned village. Its bell had been too large to remove before the flooding. Divers had later reported that the tower remained upright, though its roof had collapsed and weeds grew through the belfry.

A bell could not ring underwater. Everyone knew that.

Still, it rang eleven times.

After the last note, the lake became smooth again. The villagers remained in the street, waiting for an explanation to rise from the water.

None did.

By noon, the story had traveled beyond Bellweather. Cars appeared on the narrow road from the city. Reporters set up cameras near the shore. A scientist from the university arrived with metal cases, microphones, and a young assistant who looked disappointed by the absence of lightning.

Mara returned to her work.

She cleaned the mantel clock’s gears and fitted a new mainspring. On the inside of the case, hidden beneath years of soot, she found a line scratched into the wood:

WHEN THE BELL CALLS, BRING BACK WHAT WAS BORROWED.

The letters were uneven, perhaps cut with a nail.

Mara read the sentence twice.

Then the shop door opened, and Mrs. Vale entered without knocking.

She was ninety-four, small and straight-backed, wrapped in a blue coat. Water dripped from the point of her umbrella, though it had not rained.

“You heard it,” she said.

“So did everyone.”

Mrs. Vale looked at the clock on the workbench. “Did you find the message?”

Mara set down her screwdriver. “You knew it was there?”

“My father carved it.”

“What was borrowed?”

Mrs. Vale’s gaze moved to the window. “That depends on who is asking.”

“The bell, apparently.”

Outside, reporters were questioning Len Mercer, who seemed delighted by the attention and was inventing details with each answer.

Mrs. Vale took the chair beside the counter. “Before they flooded the valley, there was a meeting in St. Orison’s. The engineers had finished the dam. The government men said the river would be released at dawn. Everyone was told to leave.”

“I know the history.”

“You know what they printed.”

Mara waited.

“The villagers wanted one final service,” Mrs. Vale continued. “Not because they were all religious. Some came to pray. Some came to curse. Most came because they wanted to sit together in the one building no family owned.”

She unbuttoned her coat but did not remove it.

“The vicar rang the bell at midnight. After the service, he found something on the altar—a little wooden box. Nobody knew who had put it there. Inside was a key.”

“What did it open?”

“That was the question. The vicar tried the church doors, the crypt, the vestry cupboards. Nothing. Then Elian Venn, your grandfather, recognized it.”

Mara felt the old tightening in her chest.

She had been eleven when Elian disappeared. His coat had been found folded beside the lake, his shoes placed neatly on top of it. Since there had been no body, every version of his death had remained unfinished.

“What did he recognize?” she asked.

“The key to the bell.”

Mara frowned. “Bells don’t have keys.”

“This one did. There was a lock inside the tower, behind the staircase. The vicar had never opened it. Elian used the key, and behind the door they found a room.”

Mrs. Vale leaned closer.

“It was filled with things people had lost.”

Mara almost smiled, but the old woman’s expression stopped her.

“Wedding rings,” Mrs. Vale said. “Letters. Toys. Teeth. Tools. A violin that had burned in a house fire. A soldier’s medal buried with him in France. Shelves and shelves of things that could not be there.”

“How?”

“No one asked how. Not at first. They rushed in and took what belonged to them. Or what they thought belonged to them.”

Mrs. Vale’s voice thinned.

“My mother found her first child’s blanket. The baby had lived three days. The blanket had been buried with her. My mother held it to her face and wept.”

The low clouds outside pressed against the rooftops. Mara became aware of every clock in her shop ticking at a different pace.

“What happened to the room?” she asked.

“People emptied it. By sunrise, nothing remained but dust. Then the water came.”

“And now the bell wants the objects returned?”

“That was what your grandfather believed. He spent his life collecting them.”

Mara remembered Elian’s locked shed, his lists, the parcels he carried at night. Her mother had called him obsessed. Others called him a thief.

“He never told me,” Mara said.

“He tried to return the objects to the church. He rowed out to the tower for years, but he could never reach the room. The door had vanished. Then one night, so did he.”

Mrs. Vale’s umbrella had left a dark pool on the floor.

Mara nodded toward it. “Where have you been?”

The old woman looked down, as if surprised.

“At the shore.”

“You couldn’t have walked there and back so quickly.”

“No,” Mrs. Vale said. “I suppose not.”

From the lake came a single deep note.

The shop windows trembled.

Mrs. Vale stood. “It has begun counting.”

“Counting what?”

“Us.”

That afternoon, the lake withdrew from its shore by six feet.

Mud appeared where water had been, shining black and littered with pale roots. Dead branches surfaced. Then fence posts. By evening, the curved top of a stone arch rose above the water far out in the reservoir.

The scientists insisted that pressure changes in the dam were responsible. The dam keeper insisted all gates were closed. Reporters spoke rapidly into cameras while behind them the lake continued to sink.

At sunset, the bell rang ten times.

An hour later, Mr. Dorr from the butcher’s shop walked down to the shore carrying a red wooden horse. He placed it in the mud.

“My brother’s,” he told the gathered crowd. “He lost it when he was five.”

Mr. Dorr had no brother anyone remembered.

The horse stood upright for a moment. Then the mud opened beneath it and swallowed it without a ripple.

The following morning, the lake rang nine times.

Mara did not open her shop. She climbed the hill to her grandfather’s house, where her mother had lived alone since his disappearance.

Nessa Venn answered the door in her nightdress. She had once been a music teacher, but arthritis had curled her fingers inward. Her white hair hung loose around her shoulders.

“You heard about Grandfather,” Mara said.

“I heard many things about him. Most were unkind.”

“Mrs. Vale says he collected objects taken from a hidden room in the church.”

Nessa’s face closed.

“So it’s true.”

“Truth is a door people use when they want to enter someone else’s house.”

“Mama.”

Nessa turned away. “The shed.”

They crossed the overgrown garden. The shed leaned beneath an ash tree, its windows boarded, its padlock furred with orange rust. Nessa took a key from a chain around her neck.

Inside were hundreds of empty shelves.

Mara stared at them. Dustless rectangles marked places where objects had once stood. Labels remained: MERCER, VALE, DORR, HASK, VENN.

“They’re gone,” Mara said.

“No. They were returned.”

“When?”

“Over the years. Your grandfather gave them back to the families who had taken them. He believed each person had to surrender their own object freely. He could not do it for them.”

“Why didn’t anyone talk about this?”

“Shame is quieter than grief.”

Nessa walked to the shelf labeled VENN. From behind a loose board she removed a narrow tin wrapped in oilcloth.

“One remained,” she said.

Inside lay a brass clock key.

It was dark with age. The bow was shaped like a pair of wings.

Mara lifted it. “What does it wind?”

“Your father’s watch.”

“My father never had a watch.”

“He did before he died.”

Mara’s father, Tomas, had drowned when she was an infant. His boat overturned in calm water, less than a hundred yards from shore. Like Elian, his body had never been found.

Nessa sat on an old crate.

“The night before the valley flooded, your grandfather entered the hidden room. He found Tomas’s watch there, though Tomas had not yet been born. The watch was engraved with his name and the date of his death.”

Mara’s fingers tightened around the key.

“Elian could have left it. Instead, he took it. He thought that if an object connected to Tomas’s death was removed from the room, the death might never happen.”

“But it did.”

“Yes.”

“Where is the watch?”

“In the clock shop.”

Mara looked toward the village below. Beyond the roofs, the drained edge of the reservoir gleamed like an open wound.

“Where?”

“Inside the moon clock.”

Mrs. Vale’s mantel clock.

Nessa’s mouth trembled. “Your grandfather hid it there after Tomas died. He believed the room had allowed the death to happen because the watch remained unwound. Every year he opened the clock, took out the watch, and turned the key. He was trying to reverse something that had already occurred.”

“Then why give the clock to Mrs. Vale?”

“He didn’t. It belonged to her family. He used the case as a hiding place and returned it before he vanished.”

At noon, the bell rang eight times.

They hurried down the hill.

The streets were crowded with people carrying objects: a silver spoon, a child’s shoe, a cracked mirror, a soldier’s helmet. One by one they walked toward the shore.

Mara reached her shop and found the door open.

Mrs. Vale stood beside the workbench. The moon clock had been dismantled. In her hands was a silver pocket watch.

Its glass was shattered. On its lid were the words:

TOMAS VENN  
BELOVED BELOW ALL WATERS  
1959–1987

“You knew,” Mara said.

Mrs. Vale nodded. “My father took the clock. Your grandfather took the watch. They hid one theft inside another.”

“Give it to me.”

The old woman obeyed.

The watch was cold and unexpectedly heavy. Its hands showed seventeen minutes past two.

“Why that time?” Mara asked.

“The hour the flooding began.”

“No. The flooding started at dawn.”

“So the records say.”

The bell rang again, though it had already completed its count.

A crack split the shop window from top to bottom.

Mrs. Vale flinched. “They opened the gates early. There were still people in the church.”

Mara stared at her.

“How many?”

“Eleven.”

The first count.

Mrs. Vale began to cry without sound. “The officials said everyone had been evacuated. The survivors agreed to keep silent. They were afraid compensation would be withheld, afraid they would be blamed for entering the church after the deadline.”

“Who died?”

“No one knows. The bodies were never recovered.”

“One was my grandfather?”

“No. Elian escaped. But he believed Tomas’s watch appeared in the room because one day Tomas would die for what happened there.”

Outside, shouts rose from the lake.

The water had drawn back far enough to reveal a road descending into the valley.

Along it, hundreds of villagers walked toward the ruins.

Mara closed her hand around the watch and key.

At the mud’s edge, the road became slick, but the old stones remained firm beneath the slime. Roofs emerged on either side: broken cottages, chimneys, the rusted skeleton of a bus. Fish twisted in shallow pools. The air smelled of weeds and iron.

The crowd moved silently.

People placed their objects at doorways they remembered. An elderly man left a teacup on a windowsill. Two sisters lowered a pearl necklace into a well. Len Mercer opened his birdcage and set it before the remains of a green door.

Mara, Nessa, and Mrs. Vale continued toward the church.

St. Orison’s stood at the end of the road. Its roof had collapsed, but the square tower remained. Water poured from its windows.

Seven notes sounded as they entered.

Inside, the pews had rotted into dark ribs. A stone saint lay face down near the altar. Carved names covered the walls where the trapped villagers had waited for the water.

Mara found Elian’s name among them.

ELIAN VENN.

Beneath it, in smaller letters:

TOMAS, FORGIVE ME.

“He was here,” Mara whispered.

Mrs. Vale touched the carving. “Yes.”

“You said he escaped.”

“He did.”

“Then why was his coat found here years later?”

Mrs. Vale did not answer.

A staircase curled up the tower. Mara began to climb.

Nessa called after her, but Mara kept going. The steps were narrow and carpeted with weed. Through arrow-slit windows she saw the ruins spreading below, the exposed valley crowded with people returning what they had borrowed.

At the top hung the bell.

It was green with age and draped in strands of weed. No rope descended from it. No hammer moved inside it.

Yet six notes boomed through the tower.

Behind the staircase, Mara found a small wooden door.

There was no lock.

She opened it.

The room beyond was larger than the church, larger perhaps than the hill, the valley, or the world above. Shelves vanished into darkness. Objects rested upon them in patient rows: photographs, rings, books, keys, bones, spectacles, dolls, sealed letters, clocks.

Some were ancient. Some appeared new.

Mara stepped inside.

Each object seemed to hum with the faintest echo of a human voice. She heard laughter cut short, arguments never resolved, promises made beside hospital beds. She heard all the things people said when they believed there would be time to say them better later.

In the center of the room stood Elian Venn.

He looked as Mara remembered him: broad-shouldered, bearded, wearing the brown sweater Nessa had knitted. Water streamed from his clothes, but his eyes were warm.

“You took long enough,” he said.

Mara could not breathe.

“Are you dead?”

“Probably.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is the only one I have.”

She wanted to embrace him. She also wanted to strike him.

Instead, she held up the pocket watch.

“You knew my father would die.”

“I knew there would be a watch with his name on it. That is not the same as knowing.”

“You tried to change the future.”

“I tried to own it.”

The bell rang five times, shaking dust from the endless shelves.

“What happens when it reaches zero?” Mara asked.

“The room closes.”

“And the village?”

“The water returns.”

“There are people below.”

“They will have time if they leave now.”

Mara looked at the watch. “Why must this come back?”

“Because it never belonged to us.”

“It has my father’s name.”

“All lost things carry the names of those who love them.”

Elian walked to an empty place on a shelf.

“I thought the room contained what was lost,” he said. “But I was wrong. It contains what people refuse to lose. That is why the objects are dangerous. We take them and think we have defeated grief. Instead, we preserve the moment of losing forever.”

Mara saw her father as a collection of secondhand stories: his terrible singing, his habit of feeding stray cats, the scar on his thumb. She had spent her life repairing clocks because broken time seemed more honest than time that moved on.

“Did the watch cause his death?” she asked.

“No.”

“Did winding it keep him alive longer?”

“No.”

“Then all of this was for nothing.”

Elian shook his head. “Nothing is for nothing. But some things are not for what we hoped.”

Four notes.

Mara inserted the brass key into the watch.

It fitted perfectly.

“What happens if I wind it now?”

“I don’t know.”

“You must know.”

“My dear,” Elian said, smiling sadly, “death is where knowing ends.”

She turned the key.

Once.

Twice.

On the third turn, the watch began to tick.

The sound was small, but every object in the room fell silent to hear it.

The cracked glass cleared. The hands moved backward, away from seventeen minutes past two. The tower brightened.

For an instant, Mara stood in sunlight.

The church roof was whole. Voices filled the nave below. Eleven people argued near the altar while water slid beneath the doors. A younger Elian was among them, carrying the moon clock. Beside him stood a dark-haired woman with an infant wrapped in a blanket.

The woman saw Mara.

Not through her, but directly.

“Take him,” she said.

She held out the child.

Mara knew, with the irrational certainty of dreams, that the infant was Tomas.

She stepped forward.

If she took him, perhaps she could carry him out. Perhaps she could change everything: her father’s drowning, her mother’s loneliness, Elian’s obsession. Time had opened like a door, and all it asked was that she enter.

Three notes rang.

The church windows burst inward. Water rushed through the nave.

The woman still held out the child.

“Take him!”

Mara reached—

Then stopped.

The infant wore the silver watch around his neck.

This was not the past. It was longing dressed as the past.

Mara closed the watch.

Sunlight vanished. The endless room returned.

Elian stood by the empty shelf.

“Well done,” he said.

“I almost believed it.”

“That is what the room offers. Not lies. Something worse.”

“Possibilities.”

“Yes.”

Two notes rang.

Mara placed the watch on the shelf. Elian’s figure faded at the edges.

“Will I see you again?” she asked.

“I hope not soon.”

“Did my father forgive you?”

Elian looked toward the darkness behind the shelves.

“He understood me. Sometimes that is the closest we come.”

One note.

Mara stepped out of the room.

“Grandfather?”

He raised a hand in farewell.

The door closed.

A sound like a thousand clocks stopping at once passed through the tower.

Mara ran down the stairs.

Nessa and Mrs. Vale waited below. Outside, people were already fleeing uphill. Water surged through the distant streets, filling wells and cellars, lifting dead branches.

“The objects?” Mara asked.

“Gone,” Nessa said.

They joined the others on the road.

The lake returned faster than a person could walk. It poured around the houses, over gardens, across the square. Mara supported Mrs. Vale while Nessa hurried beside them. Behind, the church tower seemed to sink, though Mara knew it was the water rising.

They reached the shore as waves swallowed the last stones of the road.

The crowd climbed onto higher ground. Reporters had abandoned their cameras. Scientists dragged equipment through the mud. Len Mercer stood holding his empty cage, laughing like a boy.

Far out on the lake, only the tip of St. Orison’s tower remained.

Then it disappeared.

The water settled.

No bell rang.

In the following weeks, people tried to explain what had happened. The university blamed a rare seismic event. The reservoir authority blamed faulty instruments. The government denied the existence of the exposed village despite hours of recorded footage.

Bellweather spoke less about explanations.

Mr. Dorr remembered that he had once had a brother named Samuel. The sisters who returned the necklace began speaking after twenty years of silence. Len Mercer bought a yellow bird but never shut the cage door.

Mrs. Vale did not return for her mantel clock. She died quietly before winter, seated beside her bedroom window with her blue coat buttoned and her damp umbrella leaning against the wall.

Mara repaired the clock anyway.

She replaced its spring, cleaned its gears, and restored the silver moon. She left the hands at seventeen minutes past two, not because the mechanism demanded it, but because some times deserved a marker.

On the first day of spring, she carried the clock to the lake.

Nessa came with her. They sat on the grass above the water, where new flowers grew along the bank.

“Faithful, not accurate,” Mara said.

“What?”

“Something Mrs. Vale told me.”

Nessa nodded as if she understood.

Mara wound the clock and placed it between them.

It began to tick.

The sound was ordinary, steady, and alive.

Across the lake, sunlight moved over the water. For a moment Mara imagined towers, rooftops, and roads below the surface. She imagined the dead walking them—not trapped, not waiting, merely continuing somewhere beyond the reach of memory.

Then the clock chimed.

Once.

Only once.

And from beneath the lake, nothing answered.

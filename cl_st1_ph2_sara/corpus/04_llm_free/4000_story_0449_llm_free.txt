The first time Mara heard the lighthouse speak, it said her name.

Not through the radio, which had been dead for three winters, nor through the cracked brass speaking tube that ran from the lantern room to the keeper’s kitchen. The voice came from the walls themselves, low and hollow, as if the tower had drawn a breath from the sea.

“Mara.”

She froze halfway up the spiral stairs.

Outside, the storm pressed against the lighthouse with both hands. Rain rattled the windows, and the wind screamed around the black cliffs of Saint Orra. Far below, waves struck the rocks and burst into white ghosts.

Mara lifted her oil lamp.

“Who’s there?”

Only the wind answered.

She waited, one hand gripping the cold iron rail. The tower smelled of salt, lamp oil, and wet stone. Nothing moved above her except the slow sweep of the great lens, turning faithfully around its flame.

Then the walls whispered again.

“Mara Vale.”

Her lamp nearly slipped from her fingers.

No one on Saint Orra knew her full name.

To the fishermen who passed once a month with flour, coal, and mail, she was simply Miss Mara, the new keeper. To the officials on the mainland, she was M. Vale, Assistant Lighthouse Keeper, promoted by necessity after old Keeper Thorne had vanished. Even Thorne himself had never asked what the M stood for.

Mara climbed.

Each step complained beneath her boots. The voice did not return. By the time she reached the lantern room, she had almost convinced herself that the storm had shaped the wind into words.

The lamp burned steadily behind its crystal panels. The lens revolved on its brass mechanism, throwing a blade of light across the darkness.

Mara checked the oil, trimmed the wick, and polished a speck from the glass. These were practical things. Reliable things. They required hands, not courage.

Beyond the panes, the beam swept over the sea.

For a moment, at the farthest edge of the light, Mara saw a ship.

It lay north of the island, where no ship should have been. Three masts leaned against the storm, their sails hanging in strips. The hull was long and pale, like the ribs of some drowned animal.

The beam moved on.

Darkness covered the vessel.

When the light returned, there was nothing there.

Mara stood at the glass until dawn.

In the morning, the storm was gone.

The sea shone hard and blue beneath a cloudless sky. Gulls gathered on the roof, shouting over one another. From the kitchen window, Mara could see the narrow path winding down to the abandoned keeper’s cottage and, beyond it, the little cove where her rowing boat tugged at its chain.

Everything appeared ordinary.

That made the night seem worse.

Mara poured tea and opened Keeper Thorne’s logbook.

He had kept precise records for thirty-two years. Wind direction. Visibility. Oil consumption. Passing vessels. Repairs to the lens. The final pages, however, were different.

September 14. Heard bell beneath water. No vessel sighted.

September 19. Light turned north at 2:13 a.m. Mechanism inspected. No fault found.

September 27. Dreamed of the white ship again.

October 3. It knows my name.

Mara stopped reading.

The next page had been torn out.

After that, there was only Thorne’s final entry.

October 8. The light does not guide them away. It guides them home.

He had disappeared that same night.

The mainland authorities decided he had fallen from the cliffs. They searched the shore and found one of his boots wedged between two rocks. Mara had found that explanation thin even then. Thorne had been cautious and steady, a man who tested every knot twice.

She carried the logbook upstairs and examined the mechanism.

The great lens floated in a circular trough of mercury, turning with almost no friction. A system of weights and gears kept it moving. Mara knew every tooth and chain. Nothing in the machine could make it change direction. Nothing in the tower could call a person by name.

Yet, near the base of the lens, she found something she had never noticed before.

Letters had been scratched into the brass.

NOT NORTH.

The marks were old and deep.

That evening, clouds gathered on the horizon.

Mara locked the lighthouse door before sunset. She lit every lamp in the living quarters and placed Thorne’s logbook on the kitchen table. Beside it, she set a hammer, a knife, and the flare pistol from the emergency cabinet.

At nine, rain began.

At eleven, the tower spoke.

“Mara.”

She remained in the kitchen.

“Mara Vale.”

She opened the logbook to Thorne’s final entry and read it again.

The light does not guide them away. It guides them home.

At midnight, the gears overhead groaned.

The lighthouse beam stopped.

Mara seized her lamp and ran upstairs.

The mechanism still moved. The weights descended, the gears turned, and the lens rotated smoothly, but the light no longer swept across the southern shipping channel.

It faced north.

Worse, it remained there.

The lens was turning, yet each time its bright face should have moved away, the flame seemed to bend inside it, holding the beam fixed upon the northern sea.

In that beam stood the white ship.

It was closer now.

Water streamed from its broken masts. Figures crowded the deck, still as fence posts. Their clothes belonged to another century: long coats, narrow hats, dresses dark with seawater.

One figure stood at the bow.

Keeper Thorne.

Mara recognized his broad shoulders and square gray beard. His skin was bloodless. One bare foot rested on the deck; the other wore the boot that had never been found.

He raised his hand.

The tower shook.

From deep below came the ringing of a bell.

Not the lighthouse fog bell. This sound rose through rock and water, vast and mournful.

The people on the ship lifted their faces.

All at once, Mara understood.

She took the hammer and struck the lens.

The blow rang through the lantern room. Pain shot up her arms. The glass remained unmarked.

“Mara,” the walls said, almost gently.

She struck again.

The ship came nearer without sails, gliding over the waves against the wind.

“Mara.”

A crack appeared in the outer pane—not in the lens, but in the glass wall of the lantern room. Seawater began to leak through it, although they stood two hundred feet above the ocean.

Mara backed away.

The trickle thickened. Water ran across the floor, curling around her boots. Small silver fish flickered in it and vanished.

The stairs below filled with whispers.

Hundreds of voices spoke hundreds of names.

Mara turned toward the central mechanism. Breaking the lens was impossible, but the light itself had a source. She opened the brass door protecting the flame.

Heat struck her face.

The wick burned white, far brighter than oil should burn. Within the flame, she saw shapes moving: hands reaching, mouths opening, bodies sinking through green water.

She reached for the fuel lever.

A hand closed around her wrist.

Keeper Thorne stood beside her.

He dripped seawater onto the floor. Up close, Mara could see grains of sand in his beard and tiny shells clinging to his coat.

“Don’t,” he said.

His voice was the voice of the tower.

Mara tried to pull away, but his grip was cold and immense.

“You’re dead,” she whispered.

“Not yet.”

Behind him, the lantern room was changing. Barnacles spread over the walls. Seaweed wound around the railings. Through the windows, the white ship drew alongside the cliffs, though no water was deep enough there to hold it.

Thorne looked older than Mara remembered. Not merely tired, but worn thin by terror.

“I tried to extinguish it,” he said. “The light won’t die while the tower stands.”

“What is it?”

“A promise.”

“To whom?”

His gaze shifted toward the sea.

The bell rang again.

Long ago, before Saint Orra had a lighthouse, the islanders had made their living by wrecking ships. On stormy nights, they tied lanterns to horses and led them along the cliffs. Sailors mistook the moving lights for ships safely passing through a channel. They followed and shattered their vessels on the northern rocks.

The islanders robbed the dead.

Then, one winter, they lured a ship carrying plague.

Every family on Saint Orra died.

“Before the last islander passed,” Thorne said, “she climbed this hill and lit a lamp. She promised the drowned that Saint Orra would always show them the way home.”

The lighthouse had been built on the same spot generations later.

Mara stared at the flame.

“How do you know this?”

“The tower tells its keepers. Eventually.”

“Why did you leave?”

“I thought if I boarded the ship, it would be satisfied.” Thorne’s fingers tightened around her wrist. “But the dead are never satisfied. They only become lonely.”

On the deck below, the drowned figures began to climb the rigging. One by one, they stepped from the masts onto the air.

They walked toward the lighthouse.

“Turn it away,” Thorne said. “Before they reach the beam.”

“The brass says not north.”

“I carved that.”

“How do I turn it?”

“You can’t. Not from inside.”

A drowned sailor reached the lantern room window. His palms flattened against the glass. Water poured from his sleeves. Behind him came a woman carrying a baby wrapped in sailcloth, then a boy with a rope burn around his throat, then dozens more.

Cracks spread across the windows.

“You said the light can’t die while the tower stands,” Mara said.

Thorne’s expression changed.

“No.”

Mara tore free of his hand and ran down the stairs.

The tower called her name.

It spoke in her mother’s voice now.

“Mara, wait.”

She nearly stopped.

Her mother had been dead for twelve years. Fever had taken her in a single week, leaving Mara with a small silver comb and the memory of a song she could no longer finish.

“Mara,” the voice pleaded. “Don’t leave me again.”

She kept running.

On the ground floor, seawater burst from the walls. It rose to her knees as she waded into the storage room.

Powder barrels lined the back wall.

Saint Orra’s fog signal had once used small explosive charges. Most had been removed when the new bell was installed, but Thorne, who disliked wasting anything, had kept two barrels of blasting powder for emergencies.

Mara had always wondered what kind of emergency required them.

She rolled the first barrel into the stairwell.

Above, glass shattered.

The voices of the drowned poured down the tower.

Some wept. Some laughed. Some called for mothers, husbands, children, gods. Beneath them all, the lighthouse repeated Mara’s name.

She dragged the second barrel beside the first, pried open both lids, and poured a trail of black powder toward the outer door.

Keeper Thorne appeared on the stairs.

Behind him came the sea.

“You’ll bring the whole tower down,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You’ll die.”

“Maybe.”

“Not maybe.”

Mara looked past him. Pale hands gripped the railing above. The drowned were descending.

Thorne stepped into the rising water.

“There may be another way.”

“You had thirty-two years to find it.”

His face folded with shame.

The water reached Mara’s waist. It was bitterly cold. Things brushed against her legs.

“The tower chose you,” he said.

“No. A clerk chose me because no one else wanted the post.”

“It knows your name.”

“That doesn’t mean it knows me.”

A deep crack split the ceiling. Dust rained down.

Mara opened the lighthouse door.

Wind tore it from her grasp. Outside, the storm had transformed the island. Waves climbed the cliffs in shining walls. The white ship hovered beyond the edge, its empty sails spread like wings.

Mara took the flare pistol from her belt.

The powder trail was already wet near the barrels.

She had one chance.

“Mara,” Thorne said.

For the first time, the voice was only his own.

“I am sorry.”

She aimed.

A wave struck the lighthouse.

The floor tilted. The flare shot wide, hit the stone wall, and fell hissing into the water.

Darkness rushed over its red glow.

The powder did not catch.

The drowned reached the foot of the stairs.

Mara backed through the doorway into the storm. Rain blinded her. Her boots slipped in the mud. Behind her, pale figures crowded the entrance but did not cross the threshold.

The lighthouse wanted a keeper.

It could call the dead, but it needed the living to tend its flame.

Mara looked up.

The tower rose black against the clouds. Its foundation stood at the cliff’s edge, where decades of wind and rain had eaten away the earth. Thorne had complained about it in his maintenance reports. The western side needed reinforcement. The mainland had delayed the work until spring.

Near the keeper’s cottage lay a coil of heavy chain used to secure supply boats during storms. One end was fixed to an iron post. The other ended in a hook.

Mara seized it.

The chain was almost too heavy to lift. She dragged it across the ground while the tower called to her in a hundred beloved voices.

Her mother.

Keeper Thorne.

Friends she had not seen since childhood.

Even her own voice.

She hooked the chain around the lower support of the exterior stair, then ran the remaining length toward the cliff. At the edge, half buried in mud, stood the rusted capstan once used to haul cargo from the cove.

Mara wrapped the chain around it and pushed the bar.

Nothing happened.

She pushed again.

The capstan groaned. The chain tightened.

From the lighthouse doorway, the drowned watched.

Mara leaned her whole weight against the bar. Metal shrieked. A crack opened in the ground between the tower and the cliff.

The beam swept over her.

Suddenly the storm vanished.

She stood in her childhood kitchen. Sunlight lay across the table. Her mother sat by the window, silver comb in her hair.

“You’ve worked enough,” her mother said. “Come sit with me.”

Mara’s hands loosened on the bar.

A kettle sang on the stove. Bread cooled on a wooden board. Everything smelled of rosemary and summer.

Her mother smiled.

Mara took one step toward her.

Then she remembered the song.

Her mother had always sung while baking, but the woman by the window was silent.

“You’re not her,” Mara said.

The kitchen walls trembled.

Her mother’s smile widened.

Mara closed her eyes and pushed.

The vision broke.

The capstan turned.

The chain snapped tight, tearing the iron stair from its bolts. The weakened ground collapsed beneath the tower. For one suspended instant, the lighthouse leaned over the sea, its beam pointing into the clouds.

The drowned began to scream.

Then Saint Orra’s lighthouse fell.

Stone struck rock with a sound like thunder. The lantern room burst apart, and the great lens shattered at last. Its fragments flashed in the air, each holding a tiny, inverted storm.

The flame dropped into the open powder barrels.

The explosion split the night.

Mara felt herself lifted and thrown. She struck the wet grass, rolled, and knew nothing more.

At dawn, she woke near the ruined cottage.

The storm had passed.

Half the lighthouse was gone. Its broken foundation opened onto the sky, and black stones covered the cliffside down to the sea. There was no sign of the white ship.

Among the wreckage, something moved.

Mara stumbled closer.

Keeper Thorne lay beneath a fallen beam. He no longer looked drowned. His coat was dry, his face peaceful. When Mara knelt, he opened his eyes.

“Is it gone?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He looked toward the sunrise.

“Good.”

His body loosened into seawater, soaking silently into the earth. One empty boot remained.

Months later, the mainland built a new lighthouse on the southern hill.

It was an iron tower, painted red, with an electric lamp and a modern radio. Mara returned as its keeper despite every official insisting she had earned a quieter post.

She kept Thorne’s boot beside the door.

Ships passed safely through the southern channel. No bells rang beneath the water. No voices spoke from the walls.

Yet, on certain winter nights, Mara would climb to the lantern room and look north.

Far beyond the reach of the new light, she sometimes saw a pale ship on the horizon, sailing away beneath a crowd of stars.

It never turned back.

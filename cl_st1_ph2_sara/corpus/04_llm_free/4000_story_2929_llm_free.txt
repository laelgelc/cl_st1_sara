The Clockmaker’s Orchard

Every morning at seven, Elias Vale wound the clocks in his shop.

There were thirty-two of them: brass carriage clocks, walnut mantel clocks, painted cuckoo clocks, and one tall grandfather clock that had once stood in the entrance hall of a hotel. Elias followed the same path each day, beginning at the front window and moving clockwise around the room. He carried a ring of keys at his belt, and the keys chimed softly as he walked.

When all the clocks were wound, the shop seemed to breathe.

Tick, tick, tick.

Thirty-two separate hearts, never quite agreeing.

The people of Bellwether often brought Elias clocks that had stopped. They brought watches dropped into wells, kitchen clocks darkened by grease, and music boxes whose dancers no longer turned. Elias repaired almost everything. He cleaned rust from gears, replaced cracked springs, and cut tiny teeth into wheels no larger than coins.

He refused only one kind of work.

“I don’t change the time,” he would say.

Most customers laughed, thinking this a clockmaker’s joke. If someone asked him to set a clock five minutes ahead, however, Elias became solemn.

“You can move the hands,” he explained, “but time remains where it is.”

Bellwether accepted this as one of his harmless peculiarities. Every town had a man who spoke to pigeons, predicted rain from the ache in his thumb, or kept a jar of buttons from people he had never met. Elias happened to distrust clocks that lied.

Behind his shop stood an orchard surrounded by a high brick wall. The orchard had belonged to his family for generations, but Elias allowed no one inside. The gate was iron, and its lock was larger than a man’s fist. In autumn, children climbed onto one another’s shoulders to peer over the wall, hoping to glimpse the hidden trees.

They saw only black branches and pale grass.

No one had tasted fruit from the orchard in many years.

Some claimed the trees grew golden apples. Others said the orchard was haunted by Elias’s wife, Mara, who had disappeared seventeen years before. The oldest residents remembered her as a laughing woman with a red scarf and mud on her boots. They remembered how she had planted herbs in cracked teapots outside the clock shop. Then, one winter morning, she was gone.

Elias said she had died.

There was no funeral.

Because Bellwether was a small town, everyone knew this and no one asked about it directly.

One November afternoon, a girl named Nell Rowan entered the shop carrying a broken pocket watch.

She was thirteen, narrow-shouldered, and dressed in a coat that had once belonged to someone larger. Rain glittered in her dark hair. She placed the watch on Elias’s counter.

“Can you fix it?”

Elias picked it up. The silver case was dented, and the glass had shattered. On the lid, almost erased by years of handling, was the image of an apple tree.

“Where did you get this?” he asked.

“It was my father’s.”

“Jon Rowan?”

Nell nodded.

Elias had known Jon. Everyone had known Jon, who had delivered letters throughout Bellwether for twenty-one years and could remember every birthday in town. Three months earlier, his bicycle had been found beside the north road. Jon himself had vanished.

Some believed he had fallen into the river. Others whispered that he had run away from his debts. Nell and her mother rejected both explanations. They waited each evening for the familiar scrape of his bicycle against the garden wall.

“It stopped the day he disappeared,” Nell said. “At four minutes past six.”

Elias opened the case. The hands showed 6:04. He turned the crown, but it resisted.

“I’ll have to take it apart.”

“When will it be ready?”

“Tuesday.”

“It is Tuesday.”

“Next Tuesday, then.”

Nell studied him. “You know something about it.”

“I know it is broken.”

“You looked frightened when you saw the tree.”

“I was surprised.”

“Adults call fear surprise when they don’t want children asking questions.”

Elias set the watch down. “Come back next Tuesday.”

Nell did not move. Her gaze drifted beyond him to the curtained door at the rear of the shop. Behind that door lay the passage to the orchard.

“My father came here before he disappeared,” she said.

Elias’s hand tightened around the watch.

“How do you know?”

“He wrote every delivery in his route book. On the last page, he wrote your address. Six in the evening.”

“I don’t remember seeing him.”

“You’re lying.”

Thirty-two clocks ticked around them.

Elias slid the watch into a drawer and locked it.

“Next Tuesday,” he repeated.

Nell left, but she did not wait until Tuesday.

That night, Elias woke to the sound of breaking glass. He descended the stairs above the shop with a candle in one hand and an iron poker in the other. The clocks shone faintly in the moonlight.

A cold draft moved through the room.

The small window beside the workbench was broken. Beneath it stood Nell, holding a brick.

“You are a very poor burglar,” Elias said.

She jumped, then raised the brick defensively.

“I came for the watch.”

“The drawer is locked.”

“I noticed.”

“Put down the brick.”

“Give me the key.”

“No.”

Nell glanced toward the curtained door. “Then I’ll go into the orchard.”

Elias set down the poker.

For the first time, Nell saw that he was not angry. He looked exhausted, almost ill.

“Listen to me,” he said. “Your father entered that orchard. If you follow him, you may not return.”

“So he is there.”

“I don’t know.”

“You just said—”

“I said he entered. That is not the same thing.”

Nell lowered the brick.

Outside, rain tapped against the broken pane.

Elias lit two lamps and pulled the curtains over the shop window. Then he unlocked the drawer, removed the pocket watch, and laid it on the workbench.

“There are ordinary clocks,” he said, “and there are clocks that measure more than hours.”

Using a narrow blade, he opened the back of the watch. Inside, where gears should have been, lay a disk of dark wood marked with silver lines. The pattern resembled the rings of a tree trunk.

Nell leaned closer.

“What is it?”

“Wood from the oldest tree in the orchard.”

“Why is it in my father’s watch?”

“Because he made it.”

“My father was a postman.”

“Before that, he was my apprentice.”

Elias turned the disk with the tip of his blade. The watch began to tick.

The sound was unlike the ticking of the clocks around them. It was deeper and slower, as though heard through the earth.

Elias carried the watch to the rear door.

“Bring the lamp,” he said.

The passage beyond was narrow and smelled of oil. At its end, Elias drew back three bolts and unlocked the iron gate.

It opened with a groan.

Nell had expected a dead orchard. Instead, she saw trees heavy with leaves, though winter had stripped every other tree in Bellwether. Apples gleamed among the branches—some green, some red, some silver-white. The air was warm and full of the scent of summer rain.

At the center of the orchard stood an enormous tree with a trunk twisted like braided rope.

Its leaves were blue.

“What is this place?” Nell whispered.

“My family called it the Orchard of Hours.”

Elias locked the gate behind them.

They followed a path between the trees. On either side, fruit moved slightly though there was no wind.

“Every apple contains a moment,” Elias said. “Not a memory exactly. The moment itself.”

“That’s impossible.”

“Yes.”

Nell frowned at him.

“The impossible,” he added, “does not stop being impossible merely because it happens.”

They passed a low tree bearing small yellow apples. In one of them, Nell glimpsed a woman kneading bread. In another, a boy splashed through a pond. The images vanished when she changed her angle.

“Are those people trapped?”

“No. The moments are trapped. The people continued with their lives.”

“Why keep them?”

“Long ago, my ancestors believed people wasted too much time. They wanted to preserve the best hours as fruit is preserved in jars. A wedding, a birth, a final conversation. For a price, the clockmakers of Bellwether would save an hour so it could be visited again.”

“Visited how?”

Elias stopped beside a tree with crimson bark.

“You eat the apple.”

Nell reached toward a low branch.

He seized her wrist.

“Never touch the fruit with bare skin.”

She pulled away. “Why?”

“Because the orchard decides what you long for before you do.”

They continued toward the blue-leaved tree. The ticking of Jon’s pocket watch grew louder with each step.

“What went wrong?” Nell asked.

“People did not visit only once. A widow returned to her wedding morning until she forgot her husband was dead. A soldier spent thirty years reliving the hour before a battle. Children ate moments belonging to strangers and woke believing they had other mothers.”

Elias looked toward the branches overhead.

“The orchard does not preserve the past. It feeds on the present.”

At the center tree, the watch began to shake in his palm.

Dozens of silver apples hung among the blue leaves. Nell walked around the trunk, searching.

“How do we find my father’s?”

“We don’t. The watch does.”

Elias opened the case. The hands spun backward, faster and faster, until they blurred. Then they stopped at 6:04.

A branch cracked above them.

One silver apple fell.

Elias caught it in a square of black cloth.

Inside the fruit, Jon Rowan stood at the orchard gate, one hand raised as if knocking. His face looked pale and desperate.

“He came to see me that night,” Elias said. “He wanted an hour from the orchard.”

“What hour?”

“The last hour he spent with your brother.”

Nell became very still.

Her younger brother, Samuel, had died five years earlier after falling through river ice. Nell had been eight. Jon had carried the boy home in his arms and afterward refused to speak of the river.

“He wanted to see Sam?” she asked.

“He blamed himself. He had promised to repair Samuel’s skates, but he forgot. One blade came loose.”

Nell stared into the apple.

“Did you give him the hour?”

“No. I told him the orchard was too dangerous. He accused me of keeping the past for myself.”

“Mara,” Nell said.

Elias looked at her sharply.

“People say your wife is here.”

For a moment he said nothing.

“Yes.”

A path opened between the blue leaves, revealing a single golden apple high in the tree.

Elias turned away from it.

“Jon knew about Mara,” he continued. “He knew I had failed to destroy the orchard because I could not destroy the hour it held for me. He called me a coward. He was right.”

“What happened next?”

“He stole the gate key.”

“And you let him?”

“I followed, but the orchard had already shown him Samuel. He ran among the trees. When I found the pocket watch, Jon was gone.”

Nell looked again at the silver apple. Her father’s image had changed. He was walking down a road at dusk, pushing his bicycle. In the distance, a small boy waited beside the river.

“Is he inside?”

“Perhaps.”

“Then I’m eating it.”

Elias closed the cloth over the apple. “You may experience the hour he sought. Or you may enter his last moment and become caught in it. You might find him. You might forget why you went.”

“I have to try.”

“No.”

“You kept your wife.”

“And for seventeen years I have lived beside a locked gate, afraid of both losing her and seeing her again. Do not mistake endurance for wisdom.”

Nell snatched at the cloth. Elias moved back, but the pocket watch slipped from his fingers and struck a root.

Its wooden disk cracked.

Every clock in the shop began to strike.

The sound passed through the walls in a tremendous wave—thirty-two clocks ringing midnight, noon, dawn, and hours that had no names. The orchard shuddered. Apples fell from branches and burst on the grass.

Around Nell, moments unfolded like sudden rooms.

A summer market appeared between the trees. A woman danced beneath lanterns. Snow filled the warm air. A newborn cried. Somewhere a gun fired. Someone whispered, “I forgive you.” Someone else said, “Please don’t go.”

Elias shouted, but his voice was lost.

The earth tilted. Nell saw her father walking toward the river, and she ran after him.

She was no longer in the orchard.

Ice covered the river beneath a colorless sky. Jon stood on the bank with Samuel’s red scarf in his hands. Samuel waited on the ice, five years old again, smiling as he tightened his skates.

“Dad!” Nell called.

Jon turned.

He looked exactly as he had three months earlier: tired eyes, gray at his temples, mud on his boots. When he saw her, his face broke with grief.

“Nell?”

She ran to him. He caught her in his arms.

For several seconds she was a child again, safe against his coat, breathing in the familiar smell of wool and cold air.

“You came,” he said.

“We have to leave.”

“I can’t.”

“Yes, you can. The orchard is breaking. Elias brought me.”

Across the ice, Samuel waved.

Jon looked at him.

“I only wanted one hour,” he whispered. “I wanted to warn him. I thought I could change it.”

Samuel pushed away from the bank, skating toward the center of the river.

“Sam!” Jon shouted.

A sharp crack crossed the ice.

Jon started forward.

Nell held his sleeve. “That isn’t him.”

“He needs me.”

“It already happened.”

The ice split beneath Samuel’s feet. He vanished into black water.

Jon tore free and ran onto the river.

The scene began again.

Samuel stood on the bank, smiling as he tightened his skates.

Nell understood then. The hour was not preserving her brother. It was preserving her father’s guilt. Each time Jon tried to save Samuel, the moment returned to its beginning.

“How many times?” she asked.

Jon did not answer.

“How many times have you watched?”

“I almost reach him now.”

“You’ll never reach him.”

“I have to.”

“No. You want to.”

His face twisted as if she had struck him.

The river cracked again.

This time Nell stepped in front of him.

“Look at me.”

“Nell, move.”

“Sam is dead.”

“Move!”

“I’m alive.”

The words echoed across the ice.

Jon stopped.

Behind Nell, water closed over Samuel’s red scarf. Jon’s whole body leaned toward the river, but his eyes remained on his daughter.

“I’m alive,” she repeated. “Mum is alive. We have been waiting for you while you stay here with someone who isn’t real.”

“He was real.”

“Yes. And he died.”

The world trembled. Snow rose from the ground into the sky.

Jon covered his face.

“I forgot the skates,” he said.

“I know.”

“He asked me three times.”

“I know.”

“It was my fault.”

Nell thought of her mother sitting beside the window each evening, refusing to light the lamp until darkness made waiting impossible.

“Maybe it was,” she said.

Jon lowered his hands.

She had wanted to forgive him. That was what people said in stories: the right words broke every chain. But forgiveness felt too large to offer on behalf of the dead, and too clean for the pain they carried.

“Maybe it was your fault,” she said again. “But you don’t get to leave us because you’re sorry.”

Samuel appeared once more on the bank.

This time he had no skates. He wore the red scarf and held a small yellow apple.

“Dad,” he called.

Jon began to weep.

Samuel smiled. Then he bit into the apple.

The river, the ice, and the sky folded inward like paper burning at the edges.

Nell felt herself falling.

She landed on wet grass beneath the blue-leaved tree.

The orchard was collapsing.

Branches thrashed overhead. Apples split open, releasing fragments of vanished days. Faces appeared in the rain and dissolved. Elias stood near the trunk, holding the golden apple against his chest.

Jon lay a few feet away.

Nell crawled to him.

“Dad?”

His eyes opened.

Before she could speak, the golden apple in Elias’s hands began to glow.

A woman appeared between the trees.

She wore a red scarf. Mud stained her boots, and her dark hair was pinned carelessly behind her head. She looked first at Elias, then at the apple.

“You took your time,” Mara said.

Elias stared at her.

“I thought you died,” he whispered.

“I did.”

“I could bring you back.”

“No.”

The word was gentle.

The orchard tore another memory open. For an instant, the trees vanished, and Nell saw a younger Elias kneeling beside a broken cart on a mountain road. Mara lay in his arms, blood bright against the snow. He held an apple to her lips while she shook her head.

Then the orchard returned.

“I saved our last hour,” Elias said.

“You saved the worst one.”

“It was all I had.”

Mara stepped closer. She seemed both solid and transparent, a woman made from rain and memory.

“No,” she said. “You had every hour before it.”

The golden apple cracked.

Light poured through Elias’s fingers.

“What do I do?” he asked.

“What you should have done seventeen years ago.”

Elias looked toward the ancient tree. Its trunk pulsed with silver light, and beneath its bark thousands of clocks ticked.

He raised the golden apple.

Then he threw it to the ground.

The sound it made was very small.

The orchard went silent.

All around them, the apples darkened.

Elias grabbed Nell with one hand and Jon with the other.

“Run.”

They raced toward the gate as branches fell behind them. Jon stumbled, weak from his long imprisonment, but Nell pulled him onward. The paths shifted. Trees leaned together, blocking the way. Whispering figures stepped from shattered fruit, reaching for Elias.

He did not turn.

At the iron gate, they found the lock covered in twisting roots.

Elias thrust his ring of keys into Nell’s hand.

“The largest one!”

She found it, forced it into the lock, and turned. The gate opened just as the blue-leaved tree split from crown to root.

A wind rushed through the orchard, carrying laughter, screams, music, and the murmur of countless ordinary conversations. The three of them fell into the passage. Elias kicked the gate shut.

Beyond it, the orchard blazed without fire.

Then darkness swallowed everything.

Morning found them in the clock shop.

The clocks had stopped.

Jon sat on the floor with his back against the counter. Nell slept beside him, one hand gripping his coat. Elias stood at the broken window and watched dawn spread across Bellwether.

Behind the shop, the brick wall had collapsed.

The orchard was gone. In its place lay a field of frost-covered grass and thirty-three black stumps.

At seven, the townspeople began to arrive.

Mrs. Rowan came first. She ran down the street without a coat and struck Jon twice before embracing him. Others followed, drawn by rumor and by the strange silence from the clock shop.

Elias told them that Jon had been lost.

This was true.

He told them he had been found.

This was also true.

No one asked where.

Over the following weeks, Elias repaired the thirty-two clocks. One by one, their voices returned to the shop. He replaced springs, straightened hands, and cleaned each movement until it shone. But he did not repair Jon’s pocket watch. The wooden disk had split beyond saving.

Jon kept it anyway.

He returned to delivering letters, though he no longer rode along the north road. He spoke about Samuel sometimes—not often, but enough. On the anniversary of the boy’s death, the Rowan family walked to the river and left a red scarf tied to a willow.

Winter passed.

In early spring, Nell came to the shop carrying a clay pot. A green shoot rose from the soil.

“I found it where the blue tree stood,” she said.

Elias nearly dropped the clock he was holding.

“You planted something from the orchard?”

“It’s only a seed.”

“There is no such thing as only a seed.”

He examined the shoot. Its two leaves were an ordinary green.

“We should burn it,” he said.

“All right.”

Neither of them moved.

Outside, bells rang from the church tower. For once, every clock in Elias’s shop agreed with them.

Nell touched one of the leaves.

“What if it grows ordinary apples?”

“It may.”

“What if it doesn’t?”

Elias looked through the rear window at the empty field. He thought of all the hours he had preserved and all the years he had lost preserving them.

Then he took the pot from Nell and placed it on the sunniest part of the workbench.

“We’ll let it grow,” he said. “But we will not ask it to remember.”

Years later, an apple tree stood behind the shop.

It was not magical, as far as anyone could tell. Its leaves were green, its bark was brown, and worms frequently spoiled its fruit. Children climbed its branches without vanishing. Birds nested in it. In autumn, Elias gathered the apples in baskets and left them by the roadside for anyone to take.

He never kept one.

On warm afternoons, when the shop door stood open, the scent of apples mingled with oil and brass. Nell, now his apprentice, worked at the bench while Elias wound the clocks.

There were thirty-three of them by then.

The newest was a silver pocket watch with an apple tree engraved on its lid. It did not measure forgotten hours or open doors into the past. It merely ticked, faithfully and imperfectly, losing about a minute each month.

Elias could have corrected it.

He never did.

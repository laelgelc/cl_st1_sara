Mara first noticed the extra window on a Tuesday morning.

It stood between the bakery and the abandoned tailor’s shop, where no window had been the night before. She was certain of that. For eleven years, she had walked the same route to work, past the same cracked pavement, the same leaning drainpipe, and the same faded sign promising alterations while you waited. She knew every doorway on Bell Street.

The window had no wall around it.

It was simply there, framed in dark wood, standing upright at the edge of the pavement. People passed without glancing at it. A cyclist swerved around Mara when she stopped.

Through the glass, she saw a small room lit by afternoon sun. Dust floated above a blue carpet. Against the far wall stood a narrow bed, and beside the bed was a desk piled with books. A red scarf hung from the chair.

Mara knew the room.

It had belonged to her younger brother, Leo.

She stepped closer. Her reflection did not appear in the glass. Instead, she saw Leo sitting at the desk, his head bent over a page. He was seventeen again, thin and restless, with black hair falling into his eyes. He turned a pencil between his fingers.

Mara forgot how to breathe.

“Leo?”

He did not look up.

A delivery van sounded its horn. Mara stumbled backward, and when the vehicle passed between her and the window, the room vanished. In its place stood the brick wall of the bakery, patched with pale mortar.

She remained there for several minutes.

At the library, Mara shelved returned books in the wrong sections. She put astronomy among cookery and travel guides among crime novels. At noon, her colleague Priya found her holding a book upside down.

“You look as if you’ve seen a ghost,” Priya said.

Mara set the book on the trolley.

“No.”

“Something worse?”

“I’m not sure.”

Priya waited, but Mara could not explain. Leo had been dead for twenty-three years. To speak his name still felt like touching an exposed wire.

That evening, she returned to Bell Street. The bakery closed at six, and the tailor’s shop had been empty since before Mara moved to the town. Between them was only a stained brick wall.

She touched the bricks. They were solid and cold.

For the next week, she walked that way earlier than usual. The window did not return. By Friday, she had almost convinced herself that exhaustion had made a trick of memory and light.

Then, on Saturday, she found a door in the park.

It stood beneath an old chestnut tree, painted green, complete with a brass handle and a keyhole. There was no fence, house, or wall attached to it. Children played nearby. Dogs chased leaves. No one seemed to notice.

Mara approached slowly.

The brass handle was warm.

When she opened the door, she expected darkness or perhaps the grass on the other side. Instead, a kitchen lay beyond it. Rain tapped the windows. A kettle trembled on the stove.

Her mother stood at the sink, washing a plate.

Mara made a sound that was half gasp, half cry.

Her mother turned. She looked as she had near the end: smaller than Mara remembered, silver-haired and tired, but alive. Her brown cardigan was buttoned incorrectly.

“Mara?” she said.

The plate slipped from her hands and shattered in the sink.

Mara gripped the doorframe.

“Mum?”

Her mother stared at her. “You’re older.”

The words struck with such force that Mara nearly closed the door.

Her mother dried her hands on a dish towel. “How much older?”

“Seventeen years.”

“Oh.”

Neither of them moved.

Behind Mara, a boy shouted after a ball. The ordinary noise of the park seemed impossibly far away.

Her mother took one step forward. “Did I die?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

Mara answered, and her mother closed her eyes.

“I thought so,” she whispered. “There are days missing here. I wake up and know I have forgotten something important.”

Mara wanted to cross the threshold. Every part of her ached to enter the kitchen, feel the steam from the kettle, and sit at the scarred wooden table. But something in the doorway resisted her, not physically, but with the quiet warning of deep water.

“Where are you?” she asked.

Her mother looked around. “Home.”

“That house was sold.”

“It’s still here.”

“For you?”

“Perhaps.”

Mara swallowed. “Is Leo there?”

Her mother’s face changed.

“Sometimes,” she said.

“Can I see him?”

“I don’t know.”

“Mum, please.”

Her mother came closer until only the threshold separated them. Mara could smell lavender soap.

“You shouldn’t look for the dead,” her mother said.

“I wasn’t looking.”

“Something is looking for you.”

A gust of wind shook the chestnut branches. The door slammed shut.

Mara seized the handle and opened it again.

There was only grass on the other side.

She sat beneath the tree until the sun went down.

Over the following days, impossible openings appeared throughout the town.

A round porthole surfaced in the wall of a bus shelter. Through it, Mara saw her father repairing a radio at the dining-room table, though he had died when she was nine.

A silver lift door appeared at the back of an alley. When it opened, she glimpsed the corridor of her old school and heard Leo laughing around a corner. The doors closed before she reached him.

A trapdoor waited in the floor of the library’s history section. Beneath it lay a summer beach where Mara’s family had spent one week when she was twelve. She could hear her mother calling everyone to lunch.

Each opening vanished within moments.

Mara began carrying a notebook. She recorded the place, time, weather, and what she had seen. There seemed to be no pattern except that the doors came more often when she was alone.

She stopped sleeping well. She wandered the town before dawn and after midnight. Priya covered her shifts twice, then confronted her in the staff room.

“You’re frightening me,” she said.

Mara rubbed her eyes. “I keep seeing places.”

“What places?”

“Places that don’t exist anymore.”

Priya leaned against the table. “Dreams?”

“No.”

“Hallucinations?”

“No.”

“How do you know?”

“Because my mother spoke to me.”

Priya did not laugh. That made it worse.

She poured two cups of tea and sat down.

“Tell me everything,” she said.

So Mara did.

When she finished, Priya was quiet for a while.

“My grandmother used to tell a story,” she said at last. “About a house that appeared to people who had lost someone. It had a thousand doors, and behind every door was a moment they wanted back.”

“What happened if they went inside?”

“In the story?”

“Yes.”

“They forgot which side they belonged to.”

Mara pushed the tea away. “That’s only a story.”

“So is every warning, once enough time has passed.”

The next door appeared inside Mara’s flat.

She woke at 3:17 in the morning and found a narrow staircase descending from the wall beside her wardrobe. Pale light rose from below. Somewhere in the distance, a piano played the first bars of a melody and stopped, then began again.

Leo had practiced that melody for months.

Mara put on her slippers and stepped onto the stairs.

The wall closed behind her without a sound.

She descended farther than the height of the building allowed. The staircase curved gently, its white plaster marked with pencil lines and dates.

LEO — 8 YEARS — 131 CM.

MARA — 13 YEARS — 156 CM.

She ran her fingertips over the writing. Her father’s handwriting.

At the bottom stood a yellow door.

On the other side was the hallway of her childhood home.

The wallpaper was patterned with green vines. A telephone rested on a small table near the stairs. Raincoats hung from brass hooks. The house smelled of furniture polish, burnt toast, and the roses her mother used to bring in from the garden.

The piano stopped.

Leo leaned out of the sitting room.

“You came,” he said.

Mara could not answer.

He was seventeen, just as he had been through the window on Bell Street. He wore a grey sweater with a hole at the wrist. There was a tiny scar above his left eyebrow from when they had tried to build a raft.

He smiled uncertainly. “You look ancient.”

Mara laughed, and the laugh became a sob.

Leo crossed the hallway. This time there was no threshold between them. She pulled him into her arms.

He felt real. Solid shoulders, warm skin, the sharp edge of his collarbone. He smelled of rain and the peppermint gum he always chewed to hide cigarette smoke.

“I’m sorry,” Mara said into his hair.

“For what?”

“You know.”

He stepped back.

“No,” he said. “I don’t.”

She searched his face. “What do you remember?”

“Most things. School. Mum and Dad. You shouting at me because I scratched your records.” He smiled. “The river.”

Mara flinched.

Leo’s smile faded. “I remember going there.”

“You don’t remember what happened?”

“No. I thought maybe you would tell me.”

The hallway clock ticked above them.

“You drowned,” Mara said.

He looked down at his hands.

“We went swimming after the storm. The river was too high. You jumped in first.” Her voice began to shake. “I told you not to, but you laughed. You always laughed when you were scared.”

“And you came after me?”

“Yes.”

“Then what?”

“The current pulled you under. I tried to reach you. I caught your hand once, but I lost it.”

Leo stood very still.

“They found you two days later,” she continued. “Near the old railway bridge.”

“Oh.”

“I should have stopped you.”

“How?”

“I should have called Dad. I should have gone for help. I should have—”

“I was seventeen, Mara. You couldn’t make me do anything.”

“I was older.”

“By four years.”

“I left you there.”

“You went for help.”

“I left you.”

Leo studied her with the solemn, irritating patience he had never possessed while alive.

“Is that what you’ve been doing all this time?” he asked. “Standing by the river?”

Mara turned away.

The house around them creaked. Through the sitting-room doorway, she saw the piano, the worn sofa, and a chessboard on the rug. Upstairs, pipes groaned as if someone had turned on a tap.

“Are Mum and Dad here?” she asked.

“Sometimes. Not always at the same age. Dad was thirty-four yesterday. He didn’t know me.”

“What is this place?”

Leo shrugged. “A collection, maybe.”

“Of memories?”

“Not exactly. Memories don’t argue with you.”

“Are you real?”

“Would my answer help?”

Mara looked at him.

“No,” she admitted.

He grinned. “Then I’m definitely real.”

She laughed again. For one bright moment, time collapsed. She was twenty-one and annoyed with him. He was seventeen and impossible. Their parents were alive upstairs. The river had not yet risen.

Then Leo glanced toward the yellow door.

“You should go.”

“I just got here.”

“You’ve been here for hours.”

“No, I haven’t.”

The hallway window had been dark when she entered. Now morning sunlight poured through it.

Mara reached for the yellow door, but there was only wallpaper where it had stood.

“Leo?”

He ran to the wall and struck it with both hands.

A distant bell rang.

Mara recognized the sound: the library’s old service bell, the one kept on the front desk.

It rang again.

The wallpaper split down the center, revealing a thin line of darkness. Mara tore at it with her fingernails. Plaster crumbled. On the other side, she glimpsed tall bookshelves and Priya’s frightened face.

“Mara!”

Priya reached through the gap.

Mara took her hand.

The hallway pulled back as if the house had inhaled. Leo seized Mara’s other arm.

For one terrible second, she hung between them.

“Stay,” Leo said.

His voice was no longer seventeen. It echoed with every age he had never reached.

Mara looked at him. Behind his face flickered other faces: a young man, a middle-aged man, an old man with silver hair. Lives he had not lived. Birthdays, mistakes, loves, and disappointments stolen by the river.

“I can’t lose you again,” she said.

“You already did.”

The words were not cruel. That made them unbearable.

Priya tightened her grip.

Leo let go.

Mara fell backward onto the library floor.

The opening snapped shut. Books tumbled from the shelves, striking the carpet around her. Priya knelt beside her, breathless and pale.

For several minutes, neither spoke.

Then Priya slapped Mara across the face.

Mara stared at her.

“Sorry,” Priya said. “I’ve always wanted to do that to someone who came back from another world.”

Mara began to laugh. Priya laughed too, and soon they were both sitting among fallen books, crying until the library’s automatic lights flickered on.

Mara had been missing for three days.

Her front door had been locked from the inside. The police had searched her flat, the streets, and the river. Priya had found the opening only because a book returned itself.

“It flew off the shelf,” Priya explained later. “The Complete Poems of Christina Rossetti. Nearly broke my nose. Behind it was a hole, and I heard you shouting.”

“I wasn’t shouting.”

“Well, someone was.”

Mara thought of Leo striking the wall.

For weeks afterward, she avoided Bell Street, the park, and every alley where a door had appeared. She filled the staircase wall in her bedroom with shelves. She slept with the lights on.

No openings came.

Winter settled over the town. Frost silvered the rooftops. Christmas decorations appeared in shop windows, and children pressed mittened hands to the library glass.

Mara returned to work. She repaired damaged books, answered questions, and helped an old man learn to use email. Ordinary life seemed both painfully small and infinitely precious.

Yet she carried Leo everywhere.

Before, he had existed in her mind only at the river’s edge: laughing, vanishing, gone. Now other memories returned. Leo stealing the burnt edges of lasagna. Leo singing badly in the bath. Leo leaving apologetic notes after borrowing money. Leo terrified of moths but willing to swim in a flooded river.

Grief had frozen him at seventeen. Love began, slowly, to let him move again.

In January, Mara went back to the river.

The water ran dark beneath the railway bridge. Rain had swollen it, though not to the height of that long-ago storm. Bare trees leaned over the banks.

Mara stood at the place where she had last seen Leo alive.

“I forgive you,” she said.

A crow flapped from a nearby branch.

She closed her eyes.

“And I forgive myself.”

The words felt too easy for twenty-three years of pain. She said them again. Then again, until they no longer sounded like a declaration but a direction.

When she opened her eyes, a door stood on the opposite bank.

It was yellow.

Leo waited beside it.

The river separated them, but she saw him clearly. He raised one hand.

Mara raised hers.

He opened the door. Beyond it lay a summer afternoon. Their mother spread a blanket beneath an apple tree while their father slept with a newspaper over his face. A younger Mara chased a younger Leo through tall grass.

Leo looked back once.

“Go on,” Mara whispered.

He stepped through.

The door closed and faded into the winter air.

For a long time, Mara listened to the river.

Years passed.

She eventually became head librarian, though Priya claimed this happened only because Mara had developed “unnatural authority over shelving.” They grew old enough to complain about their knees and young enough to ignore their own advice.

The town changed. The bakery became a café. The tailor’s shop became a florist, then an estate agency, then stood empty again. The old railway bridge was repaired, and a safe footpath was built along the river.

Mara never saw the house of doors again.

But sometimes visitors came to the library carrying the particular silence of people who had lost someone.

They wandered without purpose. They pulled books from shelves and forgot to read them. They stared too long through windows.

Mara never offered explanations. Instead, she made tea.

She listened.

And if they spoke of strange doors, impossible rooms, or familiar voices calling from places that no longer existed, Mara did not tell them they were dreaming.

She said, “Be careful what you ask the past to give back.”

Most looked confused. A few understood.

One evening, many years after the river had taken Leo, Mara remained late to close the library. Snow fell outside, softening the streetlights. Priya had gone home, and the building was silent except for the old pipes.

As Mara turned off the lamps, she heard the service bell ring.

Once.

She looked toward the front desk.

No one was there.

The bell rang again.

Behind it stood a narrow window framed in dark wood.

Through the glass, Mara saw no lost room and no dead relative. She saw the library on a summer morning many years in the future. Sunlight lay across the carpet. The shelves had been rearranged. A young woman sat at Mara’s desk, reading a story to a group of children.

Near the history section stood an elderly woman with silver hair.

Mara recognized herself.

The older Mara looked directly through the glass and smiled.

Then she lifted a hand, not in farewell, but in greeting.

Mara touched the window.

It was warm.

For the first time, she understood that the house did not belong only to the dead. It belonged to every moment beyond reach: the rooms abandoned behind us, the rooms waiting ahead, and the countless doors we close simply by walking through one life instead of another.

The older Mara pressed her palm to the other side of the glass.

Neither spoke.

There was no need.

Outside, snow covered Bell Street. Somewhere in the town, a child laughed. Somewhere, a river moved steadily under the dark, carrying rainwater toward the sea.

The window began to fade.

Mara kept her hand raised until only the library wall remained.

Then she put on her coat, turned out the final light, and stepped through the ordinary front door into the night.

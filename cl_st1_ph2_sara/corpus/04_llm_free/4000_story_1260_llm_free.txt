The first time the lighthouse spoke, Mara Vale was asleep with her boots on.

She woke to three deep knocks beneath the floorboards.

Not the frantic scratching of rats, nor the soft complaint of old timber shifting in the cold, but three measured blows that seemed to rise through the iron bones of the tower.

Knock.

Knock.

Knock.

Mara sat up in bed. The room was dark except for the pale rotation of the lantern above her. Every twelve seconds, its beam swept past the narrow window and washed the walls in silver.

She listened.

The wind prowled around Blackglass Island. Rain hissed against the panes. Far below, waves struck the cliffs with the sound of doors being slammed in another house.

Then a voice came through the speaking tube beside her bed.

“Keeper.”

Mara stared at the brass mouthpiece.

The tube connected the keeper’s quarters to the lantern room. No one was upstairs.

“Keeper,” the voice repeated.

It was faint, warped by distance and metal, yet unmistakably human.

Mara took the lamp from her bedside table and climbed the spiral stairs. Her knees protested; the damp always found the old injury in her left leg. At the top, she opened the hatch and entered the lantern room.

The great lens revolved in solemn silence. Its prisms caught the flame and broke it into fragments. Beyond the glass, the storm had erased the sea and sky.

No one was there.

Mara checked the gallery, the fuel valves, and the emergency shutter. Everything was as she had left it. She bent over the mouth of the speaking tube.

“Who is this?”

For several seconds, there was only the hollow breath of the tower.

Then the voice said, “The Bellweather will strike the north reef at dawn.”

Mara gripped the brass rim.

The Bellweather was a coastal mail ship. It passed Blackglass Island every Thursday before sunrise, running north toward Fallowport.

“What did you say?”

“The Bellweather will strike the north reef at dawn. Twenty-three aboard. Four will drown.”

The voice sounded neither male nor female. It had a flattened quality, as if it had traveled a great distance or crossed through deep water.

Mara snatched up the signal lever and sent an urgent sequence to the harbor station on the mainland: danger at north reef, warn approaching vessel.

The cable trembled in her hand.

No reply came.

Blackglass stood nine miles offshore, connected to the mainland only by a telegraph wire laid across the seabed. Storms sometimes damaged it, but Mara had tested the line at sunset.

She sent the warning again.

Nothing.

The voice in the tube whispered, “Too late.”

At dawn, the Bellweather struck the north reef.

Mara saw it happen through her telescope. The ship appeared as a darker shape inside the gray morning, its bow rising and falling. It should have passed east of the island, but the storm had pushed it toward the hidden rocks.

Mara fired the red flare.

A moment later, the Bellweather turned. For one hopeful instant, she thought it might clear the reef. Then its hull shuddered. The forward mast pitched down, and black water swallowed the deck.

Mara launched the lighthouse boat alone.

By the time the rescue vessels arrived from Fallowport, she had pulled six people from the sea. The Bellweather’s crew had saved thirteen more.

Four drowned.

For the next three nights, the speaking tube was silent.

Mara considered writing a full account in the lighthouse log, but each time she lifted her pen, she hesitated. She could record wind speed, visibility, sea conditions, and ship movements. Those were facts.

A voice from an empty room belonged to another category.

She wrote only:

October 17. Severe storm before dawn. Bellweather grounded on northern reef. Signal cable failed. Four lives lost.

On the fourth night, the lighthouse spoke again.

“Keeper.”

Mara was awake this time. She sat at the small kitchen table, cleaning salt from the flare pistol.

“What ship?” she asked.

“Not a ship.”

The flame in the oil lamp bent, though no window was open.

“What, then?”

“At noon tomorrow, a boy will fall from the western cliff.”

Mara stood so quickly that her chair toppled.

There were no children on Blackglass Island. There was no one at all except Mara.

“Who is he?”

“Elias Trent. Nine years old.”

She knew the name. Elias was the son of Tomas Trent, a fisherman from Fallowport. The boy had once brought her a jar of blackberry jam when he visited the lighthouse with his father.

“Why will he be here?”

No answer came.

Mara sent a message to the harbor station. This time the reply arrived immediately.

TRENT BOAT SCHEDULED SUPPLY DELIVERY TOMORROW. WILL WARN FATHER.

Mara did not sleep.

At ten the next morning, Tomas Trent’s blue fishing boat approached the eastern landing. Elias stood at the bow in a red coat, waving both arms.

Mara met them on the dock.

“He stays with you,” she told Tomas.

The fisherman frowned. “Morning to you too.”

“Keep him away from the western cliff.”

Tomas glanced toward the lighthouse. “Why?”

“The ground is unstable after the storm.”

It was a reasonable lie, and Tomas accepted it. While he unloaded lamp oil, flour, and mail, Elias remained beside him. Mara watched the boy so closely that he became uneasy.

At eleven forty, a rope snapped.

A barrel rolled down the dock. Tomas lunged after it, slipped, and struck his head against a mooring post. As Mara knelt to examine him, Elias cried out.

“My father’s bleeding!”

“It’s only a cut,” Mara said. “Stay here.”

But the boy panicked. Before she could catch him, he ran up the path toward the lighthouse.

Mara followed.

Her injured leg slowed her. Elias disappeared around the eastern side of the tower. She heard him calling for help, his voice carried and distorted by the wind.

Then she understood. He was trying to reach the signal shed on the western cliff.

Mara forced herself faster.

She found him near the edge. The storm had split the turf, leaving a dark crack across the path. Elias stepped over it just as the ground beneath him collapsed.

Mara caught the back of his red coat.

For a terrible second, the boy hung above the rocks, kicking in empty air. Pain tore through Mara’s leg. She dropped to one knee, dragged him up, and held him until they were both shaking.

At noon, the cliff beneath them fell into the sea.

That night Mara sat beside the speaking tube.

“You were wrong,” she said.

The tower answered at once.

“Yes.”

“Elias didn’t fall.”

“No.”

“Then the things you tell me can be changed.”

A pause.

“Sometimes.”

Mara touched the cool brass.

“What are you?”

The flame above turned. Light swept across her face.

“I am what remains.”

“Of whom?”

But the tower spoke no more.

Winter closed around Blackglass.

The supply boats came less often. Ice formed in the crevices of the cliffs, and the sea became the color of hammered lead. Mara kept the lantern burning through long nights.

The voice warned her of a fire in a warehouse, a fishing boat trapped in fog, and a crack in the lighthouse’s eastern window. Each warning came true, though Mara prevented the worst of them. She began keeping a second log beneath a loose stone in her bedroom.

November 3. Voice predicted fire at Dallin warehouse. Harbor master alerted. One wall damaged, no deaths.

November 19. Voice predicted loss of fishing vessel Mercy Bell. Flare signal redirected her. All safe.

December 2. Voice warned of lantern glass failure. Found fracture before storm.

She developed rules.

The voice never answered a question about anything beyond the event it described. It never spoke more than a day in advance. It never warned her of harmless things. It gave names, numbers, and times with perfect precision.

And with every disaster Mara prevented, the voice became clearer.

By January, it no longer seemed to come from the speaking tube alone. It vibrated through the stairs, whispered in the water pipes, and hummed behind the lantern glass.

Mara wrote to the Coastal Authority requesting an inspection. Two engineers arrived, examined the foundations, tested the tubes, and declared the structure sound.

“Lighthouses make noises,” the older engineer said. “Especially ones this old.”

“Do they predict shipwrecks?” Mara asked.

He laughed until he saw that she was serious.

His report recommended that Keeper Vale be granted leave.

Mara burned it in the stove.

She had served on Blackglass for twelve years, ever since the winter her husband and daughter drowned within sight of the island. Mara had not been keeper then. The former keeper had failed to light the lamp because he was drunk.

The wreck had changed everything.

She took his post the following spring.

People in Fallowport said she had chosen the lighthouse out of grief. Some said revenge. Mara thought both explanations too simple. She had chosen it because ships needed warning, and warnings had to come from somewhere.

On the last night of January, the voice called her to the lantern room.

“Keeper.”

Mara climbed the stairs with her hidden log beneath one arm.

“What happens tomorrow?”

“The lighthouse falls.”

She stopped halfway through the hatch.

The lens turned before her. Beyond it, the stars shone with unusual brightness. The sea was calm.

“When?”

“At six seventeen in the evening.”

“Why?”

“The foundation fails.”

“Is anyone coming to the island?”

“No.”

“Then no one dies.”

The voice seemed to pass around the room in a slow circle.

“You die.”

Mara looked down through the glass floor at the long spiral of stairs below.

“Can it be prevented?”

No answer.

She struck the brass tube with her palm.

“Can it be prevented?”

“Sometimes.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only answer.”

Mara spent the rest of the night inspecting the tower.

At the lowest level, behind shelves of fuel and spare parts, she found a thin line running through the stone wall. Dust trickled from it when the wind rose. She pressed her ear against the crack and heard water.

By sunrise, the line had widened.

Mara telegraphed the mainland.

FOUNDATION FAILURE POSSIBLE. REQUEST IMMEDIATE EVACUATION AND ENGINEERING CREW.

The harbor station answered:

SEA ICE HEAVY. BOAT CAN DEPART AT NOON. ARRIVAL APPROX 1600.

Plenty of time.

Mara packed a small case: clothing, the official log, her private record, and a silver-framed photograph of her husband, Daniel, holding their daughter on his shoulders. In the picture, Rose was seven years old and laughing at something outside the frame.

At eleven, the temperature dropped.

By noon, fog covered the island.

At twelve thirty, the harbor station reported that the rescue boat had struck ice and damaged its propeller. A second vessel was being prepared.

ESTIMATED ARRIVAL 1830.

Twelve minutes too late.

Mara could still use the lighthouse boat, though it was a poor vessel for winter water. She carried her case to the eastern landing.

The boat was gone.

Only a severed rope remained tied to the post.

Mara stared at it, remembering the previous night’s wind. She had heard something strike the cliffs but had not gone outside. The boat might already be miles away.

At two, she climbed to the lantern room.

“If I stay outside the tower, will I live?”

The voice answered, “No.”

“If I go to the signal shed?”

“No.”

“The eastern beach?”

“No.”

“Could I build a raft?”

“No.”

Anger rose in her, hot and sudden.

“Then tell me what to do.”

“I warn. You choose.”

“A warning without escape is cruelty.”

The light revolved.

“I know.”

Something in the voice made Mara’s anger falter.

For the first time, she heard sorrow.

She moved closer to the speaking tube. “Who are you?”

The lighthouse shuddered. A prism in the great lens rang like a glass bell.

Then the voice said, “Mara.”

She stepped back.

It had never spoken her name.

“What are you?”

“I am what remains.”

“Of me?”

“Yes.”

Mara looked at her own reflection in the dark window: gray-streaked hair, lined face, one hand braced against the lens assembly.

“How?”

“When the foundation fails, the lantern falls through the tower. The lens breaks. The light enters the glass, the copper, the stone.”

“That makes no sense.”

“No.”

“And somehow I become you?”

“Not all of you. A pattern. A final thought repeated.”

Mara’s mouth had gone dry. “What final thought?”

The tower was silent so long she feared it would not answer.

Then it whispered, “Warn them.”

Below, a stone cracked with a sound like a pistol shot.

Mara sank onto the iron stool.

Every warning had come from this moment—from a fragment of her own dying mind caught inside the lighthouse, reaching backward through the machinery and the turning light.

“Why warn me about my death if it creates you?” she asked.

“Because you asked.”

“Can I prevent it?”

“Sometimes.”

Mara almost laughed. “You still don’t know.”

“I remember what happened. I do not remember what did not.”

At three fifteen, the telegraph clicked.

Mara hurried down to the communications room.

A message came through in broken bursts.

PASSENGER FERRY NORTH STAR OFF COURSE IN FOG. TWENTY-EIGHT ABOARD. RADIO FAILED. LAST POSITION NEAR BLACKGLASS. ACTIVATE HORN AND MAINTAIN LIGHT.

Mara looked at the clock.

The North Star carried workers and families between Fallowport and the northern villages. If it was near Blackglass, the currents would pull it toward the reef.

She ran outside.

Fog pressed against the tower, thick enough to hide the ground beyond twenty paces. The foghorn stood near the western cliff, connected to the lighthouse by a steam line.

Mara opened the valve.

Nothing happened.

The pressure gauge read zero.

She returned inside and found the pipe ruptured where the foundation had shifted. Repairing it might take hours she did not have.

The telegraph clicked again.

CAN YOU ACTIVATE HORN?

Mara replied:

HORN DISABLED. LIGHT OPERATIONAL.

The answer came:

NORTH STAR REPORTS LIGHT NOT VISIBLE.

Of course it was not visible. The lantern had been designed for clear distances, its beam thrown low across the water. In dense fog, much of the light scattered uselessly overhead.

Mara climbed to the lantern room and opened the ventilation panels. Cold vapor curled inside.

“Will the North Star strike the reef?” she asked.

“Yes,” said the tower.

“How many die?”

“Twenty-eight.”

“All of them?”

“Yes.”

The clock read four ten.

“Unless I change it.”

“Unless you change it.”

Mara examined the lens. It weighed nearly three tons and floated on a circular bed of mercury. The rotating assembly could not be aimed downward without dismantling its supports.

But beneath the main mechanism was an emergency tilt gear, installed decades earlier and never used. It allowed engineers to angle the lamp during maintenance.

She found the iron crank in a cabinet. The wheel resisted her. Rust flaked beneath her gloves.

Mara pulled harder.

The mechanism groaned. The beam dipped by half a degree.

Below her, the tower trembled.

“Stop,” said the voice.

Mara froze.

“You told me to warn them.”

“The lens supports the central column. Tilting it worsens the fracture.”

“If I lower the beam, will the ferry see it?”

“Yes.”

“Will they survive?”

“Yes.”

“And the tower?”

“Falls sooner.”

“How much sooner?”

“Unknown.”

Mara looked at the clock.

Four twenty-two.

The rescue vessel would not arrive until half past six. The predicted collapse was at six seventeen. Earlier could mean five minutes or two hours.

She turned the crank.

Metal screamed beneath the floor. The beam lowered through the fog, changing from a pale halo to a bright horizontal wound.

Mara descended to the telegraph.

LIGHT LOWERED. ASK NORTH STAR TO CONFIRM.

Minutes passed.

The crack in the wall widened. Seawater seeped through and spread across the floor.

At four thirty-eight, the reply came.

NORTH STAR HAS VISUAL. ALTERING COURSE EAST.

Mara sat down, weak with relief.

Then another message followed.

REQUEST MAINTAIN LIGHT UNTIL CLEAR OF REEF. ESTIMATED FORTY MINUTES.

The tower shook again.

Mara climbed back to the lantern room. Several bolts had risen from the floor. The lens tilted toward the sea, rotating unevenly. Each turn sent a pulse through the central column.

“Will it last forty minutes?” she asked.

“I do not know.”

“Did it last before?”

“There was no before.”

For the first time, Mara understood that the voice was changing too. Every choice she made carried her beyond its memory. The future speaking to her was becoming a future that might never exist.

At five, part of the western gallery broke away and vanished into the fog.

At five twelve, the rotation mechanism jammed. The beam stopped with its light pointed north, toward the reef.

It was exactly where Mara needed it.

At five nineteen, the telegraph cable failed.

Mara returned to the lantern room and watched the white beam narrow into the fog. Somewhere beyond it, twenty-eight people were following the light to safety.

The floor leaned beneath her feet.

“You should go down,” said the voice.

“Where?”

“The eastern doorway. When the tower falls, run toward the cliff path.”

“You said I die outside.”

“That was before.”

Mara smiled despite herself. “You’re learning uncertainty.”

“I am losing memory.”

The voice had weakened. It no longer filled the room. It came softly through the tube, as it had on the first night.

“Are you afraid?” Mara asked.

“Yes.”

“So am I.”

At five thirty-one, she saw a shape in the fog.

The North Star emerged broadside to the island, so close that Mara could distinguish people crowded along its upper deck. The ferry’s horn bellowed. Passengers waved coats, scarves, and bare hands.

The vessel had cleared the reef.

Mara raised one arm, though she knew they could not see her behind the glare.

The ferry turned east and disappeared.

“You warned them,” said the tower.

“We did.”

The lens gave a violent lurch.

Mara hurried down the stairs. She had nearly reached the bottom when the upper column snapped.

The sound was enormous.

Glass burst overhead. The iron staircase twisted. Mara threw herself through the eastern doorway as the lantern plunged through the tower’s center.

The ground rolled beneath her. She ran.

Her injured leg buckled halfway to the path, and she fell. Behind her, Blackglass Lighthouse folded inward. Walls that had endured ninety winters collapsed in a cloud of stone and copper dust.

Something struck the ground beside Mara—a curved shard from the great lens. It flashed once, holding a fragment of light, then went dark.

The cliff cracked.

Mara crawled forward.

The earth behind her dropped into the sea, carrying the ruins of the tower with it. A wave rose against the rocks and washed across the path. Icy water closed over Mara’s legs, pulled her backward, and then withdrew.

She clung to the roots of a thornbush.

When the ground became still, she rolled onto her back.

The fog had begun to lift. Above her, the evening sky was streaked with red. The island seemed impossibly empty without the lighthouse.

Mara checked her watch.

Six seventeen.

From the eastern water came the chug of an engine.

The rescue vessel appeared through the thinning mist, its deck crowded with sailors. They lowered a boat and rowed toward shore.

As they carried Mara aboard, she kept one hand closed around the glass shard.

Months later, a smaller iron lighthouse was built on Blackglass Island. It had an electric lamp, a radio, and foundations sunk deep into the rock. The Coastal Authority offered Mara a pension.

She refused it.

On her first night back, she climbed the new tower and placed the shard from the old lens beside the window.

At midnight, the lamp rotated north.

The glass caught the beam, and for an instant Mara heard a faint sound like a voice at the far end of a long brass tube.

“Keeper.”

Mara waited.

The light turned again.

No warning came.

Outside, the sea was calm, and every ship reached home.

# The Lighthouse Keeper’s Last Guest

On the morning the sea disappeared, Elias Vale was polishing the brass lens of the lighthouse.

He noticed the silence first.

For thirty-two years, the ocean had breathed against the black cliffs of Bellweather Island. Even on calm days, water whispered in the caves below the tower, and gulls argued over mussels on the rocks. That morning there was nothing—no surf, no wind, no birds.

Elias stepped onto the balcony.

The sea was gone.

It had withdrawn beyond the horizon, leaving miles of dark seabed exposed beneath a colorless sky. Ridges of sand stretched where waves had rolled the night before. Shipwrecks lay tilted in the mud: fishing boats, a navy cutter, the ribbed skeleton of an ancient galleon. Fish flapped helplessly in shallow pools. Far away, through the mist, something enormous moved along the ocean floor.

Elias lowered his telescope.

“Well,” he said, “that’s new.”

He went inside and rang the emergency bell.

The mechanism worked perfectly. Gears turned, the hammer swung, and the bronze bell boomed over the island. No answering signal came from the village.

Bellweather’s village stood on the southern slope, twenty red-roofed houses gathered around a chapel and a harbor. Through his telescope Elias saw doors hanging open. Laundry stirred on lines, though he felt no wind. A cart stood in the square with a basket of apples spilled behind it.

There was not a person in sight.

Elias took his coat from its peg, checked the lighthouse lamp, and descended the spiral stairs. By long habit, he locked the tower door behind him.

The path to the village passed through fields of silver grass. Halfway down, Elias found a shoe. It belonged to Tomas Reed, the baker’s son; Elias recognized the bright green laces. A few steps later he found the other shoe.

There were no footprints.

At the first cottage, a kettle screamed on the stove. Elias removed it from the flame. Two cups waited on the table. One contained tea. The other was empty except for a small white seashell.

The next cottage was the same.

And the next.

Every house was deserted. In each, Elias found a shell placed somewhere it did not belong—on a pillow, in a cradle, balanced atop a clock. The shells were identical, smooth and pale as teeth.

In the chapel, thirty-six shells had been arranged in neat rows upon the pews.

Elias stood beneath the wooden figure of Saint Orra, patron of sailors and lost children. The saint’s painted eyes gazed over him with a look of exhausted forgiveness.

“Forty-three people,” Elias murmured. “Thirty-six here.”

He counted the shells he had seen in the cottages. Seven.

One for every villager.

A floorboard creaked behind him.

Elias turned.

A girl stood in the chapel doorway.

She was perhaps ten years old, barefoot, wearing a yellow raincoat. Her black hair hung wet around her face, though no rain had fallen. She held a red bucket in one hand.

“Are you Mr. Vale?” she asked.

Elias’s heart gave one hard beat. “I am.”

“The lighthouse keeper?”

“Yes.”

“Good.” She entered the chapel. “I’ve come to see the light.”

“Who are you?”

“Mara.”

“Mara what?”

She considered the question. “Just Mara.”

“Where are your parents?”

She looked toward the harbor. “Out there.”

“On the seabed?”

“In the sea.”

“There is no sea.”

“There will be.”

Elias had spent enough winters alone to know that fear often disguised itself as irritation.

“How did you get here?”

“I walked.”

“From where?”

Mara pointed north, toward the empty horizon.

Elias looked at her bare feet. They were clean. “Did you see the villagers?”

“Yes.”

“Where did they go?”

“They heard the singing.”

“What singing?”

“The kind people follow.”

Her answers were too calm to be nonsense and too strange to be useful. Elias crouched so their eyes were level.

“Listen to me, Mara. Something dangerous has happened. We need to reach the wireless station and call the mainland.”

“The wireless won’t work.”

“You don’t know that.”

“The wires are full of water.”

“The wires are on poles.”

“All wires lead somewhere.”

Elias straightened. “You can come with me, or you can stay here alone.”

“I’ll come.”

The wireless station occupied a gray building above the harbor. Its door was locked, but Elias had a key. Inside, the equipment appeared untouched. He switched on the transmitter. Valves glowed, dials trembled, and the speaker issued a low hiss.

“This is Bellweather Light calling Northport,” he said into the microphone. “Emergency. Do you receive?”

The hiss deepened.

“Northport, this is Bellweather. The sea has withdrawn. The island’s inhabitants are missing. Do you receive?”

A voice answered.

It was faint and warped, like speech heard through a wall.

“Elias.”

He froze.

Mara sat on the table, swinging her feet.

The speaker crackled.

“Elias, come down.”

He knew that voice. It belonged to his wife, Anna, who had been dead for eleven years.

He reached for the power switch.

“Please,” said the radio. “It’s cold here.”

Elias switched it off.

For a moment, neither he nor the girl spoke.

At last Mara said, “That’s the singing.”

“Some kind of interference.”

“It knows what you miss.”

“My wife is dead.”

“Yes.”

“And you will not speak of her again.”

“All right.”

Elias turned the radio back on. Only static emerged. He adjusted the frequency, tried again, and received no response from Northport or any other station. On every channel, beneath the crackle, he heard the echo of waves.

When he stepped outside, the exposed seabed had changed.

Figures stood among the wrecks.

Hundreds of them faced the island. Some were close enough to appear human, though they remained perfectly still. Others were distant shadows in the mist. All seemed to be waiting.

Mara came beside him.

“What are they?” Elias asked.

“People who followed the singing.”

“Are the villagers among them?”

“Not yet.”

“Then where are they?”

“Underneath.”

He looked at the mud, the stranded boats, the deep channels glistening in the weak daylight. “Underneath what?”

“The bottom.”

Before Elias could question her further, the lighthouse bell began to ring.

One strike.

A pause.

Then two.

Elias stared up the hill. The bell was operated by a rope inside the tower. He had locked the door.

Three strikes followed.

“They’re calling you,” Mara said.

“Who?”

“The ones underneath.”

Elias started toward the lighthouse.

Mara caught his hand. Her fingers were as cold as snow.

“Don’t answer.”

“I am the keeper. The bell is my responsibility.”

“The light is your responsibility.”

“That too.”

“The light is the only thing that matters.”

The bell rang four times.

Elias looked at the figures on the seabed. They had moved closer.

“What happens when the sea returns?” he asked.

“Everything in its path will drown.”

“This village was built above the storm line.”

“This won’t be a storm.”

“How long do we have?”

Mara raised her face to the sky, as if reading a clock hidden in the clouds.

“Until sunset.”

It was not yet noon.

Elias led her back to the tower. The door remained locked, but the bell continued to sound. Five strikes. Then six.

Inside, the rope swung by itself.

Elias seized it. The rope jerked violently, burning through his gloves. He wrapped it around an iron cleat and tied it off. Overhead, the bell shuddered into silence.

“Stay here,” he told Mara.

He climbed the spiral stairs, inspecting each floor. The oil room was undisturbed. The clockwork mechanism ticked steadily. In the lantern chamber, the great Fresnel lens stood at the tower’s heart, a crystal cage taller than a man.

Beyond the glass, clouds pressed low over the vanished sea.

Elias checked the fuel tank. Full. He trimmed the wick, cleaned the burner, and tested the rotation mechanism. The lens began to turn, casting pale daylight across the room in slow, distorted arcs.

Mara had followed him.

“I told you to stay below.”

“I didn’t.”

“So I see.”

She placed her red bucket on the floor.

It contained seawater.

Elias frowned. “Where did you get that?”

“The sea.”

“The sea is five miles away.”

“Not this part.”

At the bottom of the bucket lay a brass key.

Elias reached into the cold water and took it. The key was green with age, its bow shaped like an eye.

“What does it open?”

“The door beneath the lighthouse.”

“There is no door beneath the lighthouse.”

“Yes, there is.”

“I’ve worked here for thirty-two years.”

“You never needed it before.”

She lifted the bucket and poured the water onto the stone floor.

Instead of spreading, the water gathered into a narrow stream and flowed against the slope of the floor, uphill toward the stairs. They followed it down, round and round, past the keeper’s quarters and the oil room, to the ground level.

The stream slipped beneath a cabinet.

Elias dragged the cabinet aside. Behind it, concealed beneath layers of paint and plaster, was the outline of a narrow iron door.

The green key fit the lock.

When Elias turned it, he heard a mechanism far below them come awake. Bolts withdrew one after another, descending into the earth with heavy metallic echoes.

The door opened onto a staircase carved into the rock.

A damp wind rose from the darkness.

“No,” Elias said.

Mara peered down. “The villagers are there.”

“How do you know?”

“They left their shells.”

“You keep saying things as if that explains them.”

“It does to me.”

Elias took a lantern from the wall and lit it. “You’re staying here.”

“You’ll get lost.”

“It’s a staircase.”

“It doesn’t stay one.”

He studied her. She appeared small and solemn in her yellow coat, but nothing about her seemed helpless. He suspected that if he locked her in the lantern room, she would simply appear ahead of him underground.

“Stay close,” he said.

They descended.

At first the stairs followed the foundation of the lighthouse. Soon the walls narrowed and became wet, natural stone. The air smelled of salt and rust. Far below, voices murmured.

The staircase ended at a cavern.

Elias raised his lantern.

A second lighthouse stood upside down beneath the island.

Its black tower plunged from the cavern ceiling into a lake without ripples. Its inverted balcony touched the water, and its lantern chamber glowed faintly in the depths. The structure was an exact reflection of the lighthouse above, though ancient, corroded, and covered with shells.

Around the lake stood the missing villagers.

They faced the submerged light.

Tomas Reed was there, barefoot. His father stood beside him in a flour-stained apron. The chapel priest, the harbor master, the twins who delivered Elias’s coal—all forty-three waited in silence at the water’s edge.

“Don’t listen,” Mara whispered.

At first Elias heard nothing. Then a woman began to sing from the drowned lantern chamber.

Anna’s voice filled the cavern.

She sang the lullaby she had once sung while repairing nets by the fire. Elias had forgotten the words, but his heart remembered them. The years peeled away. He saw her as she had been before illness hollowed her cheeks: laughing at his solemnity, dancing barefoot on the lighthouse balcony, accusing the moon of spying on them.

The villagers stepped into the lake.

“Stop!” Elias shouted.

No one turned.

Water covered their shoes, then their ankles.

Elias ran to the nearest person, Tomas, and pulled him back. The boy resisted with unnatural strength.

“Mam is down there,” Tomas said dreamily.

“Your mother is standing three feet away.”

“Other Mam.”

Elias slapped him.

Tomas blinked. His eyes cleared. He looked down at the water and screamed.

The scream woke the others. Confusion rippled through the crowd. Some stumbled backward, but the singing grew louder, changing now into many voices. Each villager heard someone different. The cavern filled with pleas from the dead.

Children cried out for grandparents. Widows called to husbands. The priest fell to his knees, reaching toward the lake.

Shapes moved beneath the surface.

Hands rose from the water.

They were white, swollen things with too many joints. They caught wrists and clothing, dragging villagers forward.

“Back!” Elias shouted. “Everyone back to the stairs!”

He grabbed the priest under the arms. Mara struck at the pale hands with her red bucket. Wherever the bucket touched them, they collapsed into ordinary seawater.

The villagers fought free one by one.

Then the submerged lighthouse opened its eye.

A beam of black light revolved beneath the lake.

Where it passed, the cavern vanished. For a heartbeat Elias saw an abyss deeper than the world, crowded with drowned cities. Towers leaned in the darkness. Vast creatures wound between rooftops. Millions of faces stared upward, mouths open in one endless song.

The beam caught Elias.

He stood again in his old kitchen.

Anna sat at the table, alive and warm, pouring tea.

“You’ve kept me waiting,” she said.

He knew it was not real.

Knowing did not help.

Her hair shone in the firelight. Her blue cup rested beside her hand. Elias had broken that cup the week after her funeral because he could not bear to see it without her.

“I’m sorry,” he whispered.

“You always say that when you’re late.”

“I couldn’t leave the lamp.”

“You can now.”

Outside the kitchen window, the sea had returned. Sunlight danced on gentle waves. There was no vanished ocean, no cavern, no waiting dead.

Anna held out her hand.

“Come home, Elias.”

He stepped toward her.

A red bucket struck him in the knee.

The kitchen fractured like glass. Elias found himself waist-deep in the cavern lake. Mara clung to his coat, trying to drag him back. Pale hands crawled over his boots.

“Wake up!” she shouted.

Elias kicked free and staggered onto the shore.

Most of the villagers had reached the staircase. The upside-down light continued to turn. Each sweep drew them toward the water again.

“How do we stop it?” Elias asked.

“We don’t.”

“Every light can be extinguished.”

“Not that one.”

“Then why bring me here?”

“To show you.”

“Show me what?”

“What happens if your light goes out.”

A crack split the cavern ceiling.

Water burst through in a thin, high-pressure stream.

The sea was returning.

Elias understood.

The lower lighthouse was not a reflection. It was a doorway, and the thing beneath the world had been calling through it. The lighthouse above kept the door shut—not merely by shining over reefs and waves, but by casting its beam against this other, darker light.

“Everyone upstairs!” he shouted.

The villagers climbed. Elias followed last with Mara. Behind them, cracks spread across the cavern roof. Water poured through in widening columns. The song rose to a furious chorus.

They reached the iron door as the staircase twisted.

Stone steps softened beneath Elias’s feet, becoming the ribs of a shipwreck. The walls fell away. For an instant they climbed through open black water, while drowned sailors drifted on either side. Elias felt the pressure of an entire ocean above him.

“Keep going!” Mara cried.

He fixed his gaze on her yellow coat and climbed.

The illusion broke. They stumbled through the door into the lighthouse.

Outside, the western horizon had become a wall.

The sea returned not as a wave but as a moving continent of water, towering into the clouds. Wrecked ships tumbled in its face. Lightning flickered inside it. Before it rolled a wind that tore roofs from the village and bent the silver grass flat against the earth.

The villagers panicked.

“Up the hill!” Elias ordered. “Get into the tower!”

“The tower won’t hold us all,” shouted the harbor master.

“It has stood for two hundred years.”

“Not against that!”

Elias looked at the approaching wall. “Then you’re welcome to find something taller.”

They crowded into the lighthouse. Elias sent the children and older villagers to the upper floors. Everyone else packed shoulder to shoulder along the stairs.

In the lantern room, the afternoon had darkened to twilight. Elias lit the main burner. Flame bloomed behind the lens, bright and steady.

The rotation mechanism groaned.

The lens turned once, then stopped.

Elias opened the clockwork housing. Seawater streamed from the gears.

“The wires are full of water,” Mara said.

“This machine has no wires.”

“All things lead somewhere.”

Below them, the villagers began to sing.

The melody rose through the staircase in Anna’s voice.

They had heard the call again.

Elias tried to move the lens by hand. It resisted. The lamp’s beam remained fixed toward the village, while the wall of water advanced from the north.

“It has to face the sea,” Mara said.

“I know.”

He strained against the brass frame. Nothing moved.

The song grew louder.

On the balcony, the first wind struck. Glass panes rattled. Elias saw figures walking ahead of the wave—thousands of drowned people crossing the exposed seabed. Among them came a woman in a blue dress.

Anna.

This time Elias did not look away.

He had spent eleven years fearing that memory would fade. He had repeated her face to himself each night: gray eyes, crooked smile, the small scar near her chin. Yet the woman approaching the lighthouse was too perfect. She was Anna preserved, Anna without illness, Anna as nostalgia demanded.

The real Anna had hated blue dresses.

Elias took the emergency axe from the wall and smashed the clockwork housing.

Mara jumped back.

“What are you doing?”

“Improvising.”

He hacked through the jammed gears. Brass teeth scattered across the floor. When the mechanism had been reduced to glittering wreckage, the lens moved half an inch.

Elias shoved.

Slowly, painfully, the enormous glass assembly rotated.

The beam swept across the island.

Where its light touched the drowned figures, they became columns of spray. The false Anna looked up. For a moment, her face changed into something ancient and furious.

Then the beam erased her.

Below, the singing stopped.

The returning sea reached Bellweather Island.

Water struck the cliffs with a sound that seemed to split the sky. The tower lurched. Glass shattered. Villagers screamed. The lower floors flooded instantly, and black water spiraled up the staircase.

Elias held the lens toward the north.

The beam pierced the wave.

A tunnel of light opened through the water, dividing the impact around the lighthouse. The tower shook as the sea roared past on both sides. Rocks, ships, and uprooted trees whirled inches beyond the broken windows.

The force pushed against the lens. Elias’s boots slid across the floor.

Mara joined him, pressing both hands to the frame.

“You have to keep it moving!” she shouted.

“Help me, then!”

Together they turned the lens.

The beam circled the tower. Wherever it passed, the flood recoiled. But the moment the light moved on, water surged closer. Elias and Mara walked the lens around and around while the island disappeared beneath the sea.

Minutes became hours.

Elias’s shoulders burned. His palms bled. The lantern flame sputtered as water leaked through the roof.

Each rotation became slower.

“Mr. Vale,” Mara said, breathless, “when the sun rises, you can stop.”

“Sunrise is twelve hours away.”

“Yes.”

“You might have mentioned that.”

“I said until sunset.”

“That was not the useful half of the information.”

They kept walking.

In the dark beyond the glass, enormous shapes circled the tower. Sometimes Elias saw an eye wider than the village square. Sometimes a hand reached through the waves and withdrew before the beam.

The villagers formed a chain on the stairs, passing up bottles of oil. When Elias weakened, the harbor master and the baker came to help. Then the priest and Tomas. Four people at a time turned the lens while the others rested.

All night, the lighthouse shone.

Shortly before dawn, the water receded from the lantern windows. The top of the island emerged. Then the chapel roof, the fields, and the ruined village.

At sunrise, the sea lay once more beneath the cliffs.

It was calm.

Of the dark figures and stranded wrecks, there was no sign.

The lighthouse door opened, and the villagers walked into the golden morning. Their houses were damaged but standing. Fish glittered in the streets. The chapel bell had washed into an apple tree.

Elias remained in the lantern room.

Mara stood on the balcony, looking north.

“Will it happen again?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“When?”

“Not for a long time.”

“How long?”

She shrugged. “Long enough for people to forget.”

“They’ll remember.”

“People always think they will.”

Elias looked at the broken clockwork. “Who are you?”

Mara lifted her red bucket. It was empty now.

“My father kept the light before you.”

Elias knew the names of every keeper back to 1812. None had mentioned a daughter called Mara. Then he remembered the oldest entry in the lighthouse log, written by the tower’s first keeper:

_The sea withdrew today. My daughter returned with the key, though she has been gone these twenty years._

“You’re dead,” Elias said.

“So are many people.”

“Why did you help us?”

“Someone helped my father.”

She climbed onto the balcony rail.

Elias moved toward her. “Wait.”

Mara glanced back.

“What happens to you now?” he asked.

“I go where the sea goes when it disappears.”

“Will I see you again?”

“Not if the light stays lit.”

She smiled, and for the first time she looked like an ordinary child.

Then she stepped from the railing.

Elias rushed forward.

A yellow raincoat drifted on the wind, empty. It fluttered out above the cliffs, folded once like the wings of a bright bird, and fell into the sea.

The red bucket remained on the balcony.

Inside it lay a small white shell.

Years later, when Elias had grown too old to climb the lighthouse stairs, Tomas Reed became keeper. On his first night, Elias showed him how to trim the wick, polish the lens, and record the weather in the log.

He also showed him the iron door.

“What’s down there?” Tomas asked.

“A reminder.”

“Of what?”

“That every light has two purposes,” Elias said. “One for those trying to find their way home, and one for the things that must never be allowed to follow them.”

Tomas laughed uncertainly.

Elias did not.

Before leaving Bellweather, he placed the green key in the red bucket and set it beneath the lantern room window.

Then he descended the spiral staircase for the last time.

The sea breathed against the cliffs. Gulls argued over mussels. From the village came the noise of hammers, voices, and ordinary life.

At the bottom of the hill, Elias paused and looked back.

The lighthouse beam swept over him, warm and brilliant in the dusk. Beyond it, the ocean darkened toward the horizon.

For an instant, between one flash and the next, Elias thought he saw a girl in a yellow coat walking across the water.

Then the light returned, and she was gone.

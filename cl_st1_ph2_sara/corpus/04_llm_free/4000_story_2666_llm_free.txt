The Last Train to Bellwether

At eleven minutes past midnight, the last train arrived at Bellwether Station, though no train had been scheduled to stop there for twenty-three years.

Mara Venn was the only person who saw it.

She had been standing beneath the broken station clock, shaking rain from her umbrella, when a pale light appeared beyond the eastern bend. At first she thought it was a truck on the old service road. Then the rails began to hum under her boots.

The sound traveled through the abandoned platform, stirring scraps of paper and dust. A whistle called from the darkness—low, mournful, and impossibly near.

The train emerged without steam or smoke. Its black engine shone as if freshly painted, though silver moss clung to its wheels. Behind it came six passenger carriages, each glowing with amber light. Rain slid around the train but never seemed to touch it.

Mara stepped back.

For years, the railway authorities had promised to tear up the Bellwether line. For years, they had postponed it. The tracks remained, rusting among weeds, leading east into a tunnel blocked by a landslide and west toward a bridge that had collapsed before Mara was born.

Yet the train stopped with effortless precision.

A door opened.

A conductor in a midnight-blue uniform leaned out. He was an elderly man with a silver beard, a round cap, and eyes the gray-green color of the sea before a storm.

“Bellwether,” he announced. “One minute only.”

Mara stared at him.

The conductor checked a pocket watch. “Are you boarding, Miss Venn?”

She gripped the umbrella handle. “How do you know my name?”

“Your ticket.”

“I don’t have a ticket.”

He pointed to her coat pocket.

Mara almost laughed. Then she reached inside.

Her fingers closed around a rectangle of stiff paper.

It was cream-colored, edged in gold, and dry despite the rain. Printed across the center in faded green letters were the words:

MARA VENN  
BELLWETHER TO MORNING  
SINGLE JOURNEY  
NO RETURN

She looked up. “What does this mean?”

“Exactly what it says.”

“I’m not going anywhere.”

“Then why did you come to the station?”

Mara opened her mouth but found no answer.

She had lived in Bellwether all her life. She knew every narrow lane, every leaning chimney, every face in the bakery queue on winter mornings. She also knew the station was locked at night. The gate had been chained after local children began daring one another to sleep in the ticket office.

Still, at half past eleven, unable to remain in her silent house, she had found herself walking through the rain with her father’s old umbrella. The chain had been lying open on the ground. The station door had yielded at her touch.

She had told herself she needed air.

But the truth was that morning would bring the demolition crews to Venn’s Clockmakers, the little shop where her family had worked for four generations. The council had purchased the entire row to widen the road, and Mara had spent the day packing brass gears, cracked watch faces, invoices, tools, and clocks that no one wanted repaired.

At seven that evening, she had stopped the shop’s great regulator clock.

At eight, she had gone home.

At nine, she had realized she no longer knew who she was.

The conductor glanced at his watch again. “Thirty seconds.”

“Where does the train go?”

“To Morning.”

“That isn’t a place.”

“It is tonight.”

The rain thickened. Mara looked past him into the first carriage. It seemed warm inside. Dark wooden panels gleamed under brass lamps. A woman in a red scarf sat by one window, sleeping with her head against the glass.

“What happens if I stay?”

The conductor’s expression softened. “The usual things.”

Somewhere in town, a clock struck the quarter hour, though it was neither the hour nor the quarter.

Mara put one foot onto the train.

The conductor took her umbrella and folded it neatly. “Mind the gap.”

She boarded.

The door closed behind her, and Bellwether Station began to slide away.

Mara rushed to the window. The platform passed slowly at first—the broken clock, the peeling sign, the empty benches—then faster. Darkness swallowed everything.

“But the western bridge is gone,” she said.

The conductor handed back her umbrella. “Only during the day.”

The carriage held nine passengers.

The sleeping woman in the red scarf looked no older than twenty. Across from her sat a broad man wearing a baker’s apron powdered with flour. A boy in a green school blazer held a glass jar containing three fireflies. Near the rear door, twin sisters with white hair played cards on a suitcase.

No one seemed surprised to see Mara.

She chose an empty seat beside the window. The cushion sighed beneath her.

Outside, the night unrolled in impossible landscapes. The train crossed a bridge over a forest of silver trees. It ran along a black beach where enormous waves stood motionless beneath the moon. It passed a city composed entirely of lit windows, with no walls between them.

The conductor moved down the aisle, punching tickets. Each click of his silver punch sounded like a tiny lock closing.

When he reached Mara, he held out his hand.

She hesitated. “You said there’s no return.”

“There seldom is.”

“From Morning?”

“From any journey worth taking.”

She surrendered the ticket.

He inspected it, then frowned.

“Is something wrong?”

“It hasn’t printed your arrival time.”

“I didn’t print it.”

“No. You wouldn’t have.”

He punched a star-shaped hole in one corner and returned the ticket. “You’ll need to decide before the final stop.”

“Decide what?”

But he had already moved on.

The baker across the aisle leaned toward her.

“First journey?” he asked.

“Is it obvious?”

“You keep expecting explanations.”

His face was round and kind, though his eyes were swollen from recent tears. He carried a cardboard cake box on his knees.

“Where are you going?” Mara asked.

“To see my wife.”

“Does she live in Morning?”

“She lives nowhere now.”

Mara looked down.

The baker patted the cake box. “Forty-two years ago, I made her a birthday cake. Terrible thing. Burned at the edges, raw in the middle. I wrote her name in icing, but I was so nervous I misspelled it.”

“What did she say?”

“She married me.” He smiled. “Last week, I made the cake again. Perfectly this time. She died before she could taste it.”

“I’m sorry.”

“So am I.” He looked out at the moonlit sea. “I heard the train whistle last night. I knew she was calling. Or I wanted to know.”

“Will you be able to stay with her?”

The baker’s smile faded. “That’s what I intend to find out.”

The train entered a tunnel. The carriage lights dimmed.

For a moment, Mara saw her own reflection in the window: dark hair escaping its knot, tired eyes, rainwater on the collar of her coat. Then the glass brightened, and another reflection appeared beside hers.

Her father sat in the empty seat.

He looked as he had on the last morning of his life—thin, gray, and annoyed by illness. A magnifying lens was clipped over one eye.

“You’re holding it wrong,” he said.

Mara froze.

He reached toward her hands. Only then did she realize she was holding a tiny brass watch between her fingers.

It was the first watch he had taught her to repair.

“Thumb under the movement,” he said. “Not over it. You’ll bend the balance staff.”

She was twelve again, sitting at his workbench while rain tapped the shop windows.

“I can’t do it,” she heard herself say.

“You can.”

“The parts are too small.”

“Small things still matter.”

“That doesn’t make them easier.”

“No.” His smile appeared slowly. “But that is why we learn patience.”

The train burst from the tunnel.

The watch vanished. The seat beside Mara was empty.

She stood so quickly that her knee struck the table.

The conductor was at the far end of the carriage. Mara pushed past the card-playing twins and caught his sleeve.

“What did you do?”

“Nothing.”

“I saw my father.”

“Yes.”

“Was it him?”

The conductor considered. “Does it matter?”

“Of course it matters.”

“To the living, perhaps.”

Before Mara could answer, the train began to slow.

Beyond the windows lay a station lit by hundreds of candles. The sign on the platform read YESTERDAY.

“Five minutes,” the conductor called. “Passengers may disembark, but must return before the bell.”

The doors opened.

The baker rose, clutching his cake box. The woman in the red scarf woke with a gasp. The boy put the jar of fireflies in his pocket. All the passengers stepped onto the platform.

Mara remained seated.

The conductor paused by the door. “You may see him again.”

“My father?”

“If that is who you are looking for.”

“I’m not looking for him.”

“No?”

“He died six years ago. I know that.”

“Knowing a door is locked does not stop us from trying the handle.”

He stepped outside.

Mara followed.

Yesterday Station had no walls. Beyond the platform stood a field in summer sunlight, though the sky above the tracks remained midnight-black. Warm air moved through tall grass. Somewhere, unseen, bees hummed.

Paths led away from the platform, each marked by a wooden sign.

FIRST LOVE, read one.

LAST WORD.

THE DAY BEFORE.

IF ONLY.

Mara saw the baker hurrying down a path marked ONE MORE MEAL. The woman in the red scarf had chosen BEFORE I SAID YES. The schoolboy stood beneath a sign that read HOME AT FOUR O’CLOCK, unable to move.

Mara walked along the platform until she found a narrow path almost hidden by weeds.

THE SHOP, it said.

She followed.

The field vanished behind her. She smelled oil, dust, and metal. Then she was standing outside Venn’s Clockmakers on a winter evening six years earlier.

Every clock in the window showed a different time.

Through the glass, she saw her father at the workbench. He was bent over a carriage clock, his hands trembling as he fitted a gear into place.

Mara remembered this night.

She had been offered a position in Edinburgh by a company that restored museum clocks. It was everything she had once dreamed of: travel, difficult work, the chance to learn from masters of the trade. Her train left the following morning.

That evening, her father had collapsed in the shop.

The doctors found the illness. Mara canceled her journey. She told herself she would apply again next year, but next year brought hospital visits, then debts, then grief. After her father’s death, the shop became hers.

She had never left Bellwether.

Inside the memory, her younger self came down the stairs carrying a suitcase.

“I’ll miss the train,” Young Mara said.

Her father did not look up. “Then go.”

“You can barely hold the tweezers.”

“I’m tired.”

“You need a doctor.”

“I need to finish this clock.”

“You’re being stubborn.”

“So are you.”

Young Mara put down the suitcase. “I can catch the afternoon train.”

Her father’s hands stopped moving.

This was the moment Mara had replayed more than any other. If he had told her to go, she might have gone. If he had asked her to stay, she might have stayed without resentment.

Instead, he had said, “Do what you think is right.”

Mara had hated him for that answer.

She watched her younger self take off her coat.

Then she entered the shop.

Neither figure noticed her. She walked to the workbench and stood opposite her father.

“You knew,” she said.

His head remained bowed.

“You knew I wouldn’t leave if you were ill.”

Still he said nothing.

“You could have told me to go.”

The clocks ticked around them, hundreds of mechanical hearts refusing to beat together.

At last, her father looked up.

This time he saw her.

“I could have,” he said.

Mara forgot to breathe.

“Is this really you?”

He removed the magnifying lens. “You always ask questions when you are frightened.”

“You let me give up everything.”

“No.”

“I stayed for you.”

“You stayed because you loved me.”

“And what’s the difference?”

“Love is not a prison, Mara. But memory can be.”

The anger she had carried for six years rose into her throat.

“You left me with the shop. With the debts. With all those clocks no one cares about.”

“I left you my tools. You chose what to build with them.”

“I preserved everything.”

“Yes,” he said. “That was the trouble.”

A bell rang in the distance.

Mara turned toward the door.

“One minute,” called the conductor’s voice.

Her father picked up the tiny gear with his tweezers.

“What happens if I stay here?” she asked.

“We have this conversation again.”

“For how long?”

“For as long as you need to lose it entirely.”

She looked at the suitcase by the stairs. “I don’t know how to leave.”

“You already did.”

The station bell rang a second time.

Mara ran.

She raced back along the path, through the summer field, and onto the candlelit platform. The conductor stood beside the train.

“Wait!”

He held the door as she climbed aboard.

The baker had not returned.

Through the window, Mara saw him far out in the field, sitting at a table beneath an apple tree. A woman sat opposite him. Between them was a cake covered in white icing. He was laughing and crying at once.

The conductor rang his bell.

The train moved.

“Shouldn’t we wait for him?” Mara asked.

“He has made his choice.”

“He’ll be trapped there.”

“Perhaps. But not every trap feels cruel.”

The woman in the red scarf had returned. She sat upright, clutching her ticket in both hands. The schoolboy had returned too, but his jar was empty. The fireflies drifted over Yesterday Station as it disappeared behind them.

The baker’s abandoned seat still held a dusting of flour.

Mara sat beside the window.

She understood now why her ticket had no arrival time.

Morning was not merely a destination. It was a decision, and the train could not make it for her.

The journey continued.

They passed through stations called NEVER WAS, FORGOTTEN NAME, and JUST THIS ONCE. At each stop, one or two passengers left. Some returned before the bell. Some did not.

The white-haired twins stepped off at DIFFERENT ROADS, each walking in the opposite direction. The woman in the red scarf left at UNSPOKEN and came back without her scarf.

At last only Mara, the woman, and the schoolboy remained.

The sky outside began to lighten.

The conductor entered the carriage carrying a tray with three cups of tea. He gave one to each passenger and sat across from Mara.

“Final stop soon,” he said.

Mara wrapped her hands around the warm cup. “What is at Morning?”

“Whatever comes next.”

“That’s not very helpful.”

“The future rarely is, until it becomes the past.”

She took out her ticket. The blank space beneath the destination had begun to shimmer.

“What if I don’t know what I want?”

“You need only know what you refuse to keep carrying.”

Mara thought of the shop: the stopped regulator clock, the packed boxes, the dustless outline where her father’s coat had hung. She had believed preserving his life would preserve her love for him. Instead, every object had become evidence in a trial without a verdict.

“What do the others refuse to carry?” she asked.

The conductor glanced at the schoolboy, who was watching the empty jar.

“Guilt,” he said. Then he nodded toward the woman without the scarf. “A promise made to the wrong person.”

“And me?”

“You tell me.”

Outside, the horizon turned gold. The strange landscapes faded. Ordinary hills appeared, green with rain. Telephone poles ran beside the tracks. Sheep lifted their heads in morning fields.

Mara looked down at her ticket.

“I refuse,” she said slowly, “to spend the rest of my life waiting for the life I missed.”

The ticket grew warm.

Green letters appeared in the blank space:

ARRIVAL: 6:12 A.M.

The conductor smiled.

The whistle sounded.

Ahead stood Bellwether Station.

But it was not the station Mara had boarded from. Sunlight shone on new glass shelters. The old ticket office had become a café. Flower boxes hung beneath the restored clock, which displayed the correct time.

On the opposite platform, commuters waited with phones and briefcases. A bright red train stood beneath a sign reading:

NORTHBOUND—EDINBURGH—6:20

Mara stared.

“This isn’t my Bellwether.”

“It can be.”

“What year is it?”

“Morning.”

The train stopped.

The schoolboy was first to leave. On the platform, a middle-aged woman cried out and dropped her handbag. She ran to him, and he ran into her arms.

The woman who had worn the red scarf stepped down next. A younger woman waited beside the café, holding two coffees and smiling nervously.

Mara remained in her seat.

“What if I get off and everything disappears?” she asked.

The conductor opened the door. “Everything disappears, Miss Venn. That is no reason to remain seated.”

She stepped onto the platform.

Cold morning air filled her lungs.

When she turned, the black train was already moving. The conductor leaned from the doorway and touched two fingers to the brim of his cap.

“Mind the gap,” he called.

Then the train passed into sunlight and was gone.

Mara stood alone.

The station slowly changed around her.

The glass shelters faded. The café became the locked ticket office. The flower boxes vanished, and the clock cracked across its face. The opposite platform emptied. The red northbound train dissolved into mist.

For one terrible moment, Mara thought the journey had changed nothing.

Then she heard a diesel engine.

A demolition van stopped outside the station. Two workers climbed out, arguing about where to find coffee. Beyond them, Bellwether woke beneath a pale sky.

Mara checked her phone.

6:12 a.m.

She walked home.

At nine, the demolition crew arrived at Venn’s Clockmakers. Their supervisor found Mara waiting outside beside twelve sealed boxes and the great regulator clock.

“You’re cutting it close,” he said. “We need the building empty.”

“It is.”

“You taking that clock?”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

Mara looked down the road.

She had made three calls since returning home. The first was to a railway company. The second was to an old friend in Edinburgh. The third was to the council officer overseeing the abandoned station.

“For now,” she said, “across town.”

Six months later, Bellwether Station reopened.

Not for trains—the line remained broken in both directions—but as a workshop, café, and small museum. The restored sign above the entrance read:

Venn’s Clockmakers  
Repairs, Restorations, and Departures

Mara placed her workbench in the old ticket office. She taught classes on Saturdays and repaired clocks during the week. Once a month, she traveled to Edinburgh to assist with the museum collection. She became skilled at leaving and, more importantly, at returning without mistaking return for surrender.

The great regulator clock stood on the platform beneath the broken station clock, which Mara never repaired.

Visitors sometimes asked why.

“One correct clock is useful,” she would say. “Two only start an argument.”

Years passed.

On a rainy evening in late October, Mara was closing the workshop when she found a boy waiting beneath the platform lamps. He wore a green school blazer of an old-fashioned cut.

At first she did not recognize him.

Then he held up a glass jar. Three fireflies glowed inside.

“You were on the train,” Mara said.

“So were you.”

He had not aged.

Mara set down her keys.

“Are you here to take me back?”

“No.” He placed the jar on the bench. “The conductor asked me to deliver this.”

Inside the jar, beneath the fireflies, lay a cream-colored ticket edged in gold.

Mara did not touch it.

“What does it say?”

The boy smiled. “Not yet.”

A whistle called in the distance.

The rails, red with rust and buried beneath weeds, began to hum.

Mara looked toward the eastern bend. A pale light moved behind the trees, though the tunnel beyond them was still sealed with stone.

“Who is the ticket for?” she asked.

The boy pointed toward the station entrance.

A woman stood there, soaked by the rain. She was perhaps twenty years old and wore a red scarf. She looked exhausted and frightened.

“Is this Bellwether?” the woman asked.

Mara glanced at the clock.

It was eleven minutes past midnight.

She took her father’s umbrella from the stand, walked to the platform edge, and opened it over the stranger.

“Yes,” Mara said as the last train approached. “But only for one minute.”

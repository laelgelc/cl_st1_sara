## The Cartographer of Lost Roads

On the morning the sea returned a road, Mara Venn was the first to see it.

She had gone down to the harbor before sunrise, as she did every day, carrying a brass compass that no longer pointed north and a notebook swollen by salt. The tide had retreated unusually far, exposing black rocks, anchors, broken bottles, and the curved ribs of ships that had sunk before Mara was born.

Beyond them, stretching into the gray distance, was a road.

It was narrow and paved with white stones. Seaweed hung from iron posts along its edges, and small silver fish flickered in the pools between the stones. The road began beneath the harbor wall and ran straight toward the horizon, where the fog swallowed it.

Mara stood perfectly still.

She knew every road in Bellweather. It was her profession to know them.

Her maps hung in the council chamber, the schoolhouse, the customs office, and nearly every fishing vessel in the harbor. She had charted paths through the northern marshes, where the ground shifted after every storm. She had marked the alleyways that twisted behind the old market. She had even drawn the narrow tunnel beneath the church, though Father Elian insisted it did not exist.

But this road appeared on none of her maps.

Behind her, the town bells rang six times. A window opened above the bakery.

“Mara?” called a sleepy voice. “What are you staring at?”

She did not answer.

At the edge of the road stood a sign, its letters obscured by barnacles. Mara climbed down the harbor steps and crossed the wet sand. Cold mud seeped into her boots. When she reached the sign, she scraped it clean with her sleeve.

TO ORISON  
SEVEN MILES

Her breath caught.

Orison was the village where her younger brother had vanished twenty-two years earlier.

Everyone in Bellweather knew the story, though they told it differently. Some said Orison had been destroyed by a landslide. Others claimed it had been abandoned during the fever years. A few old sailors swore it had never existed at all.

Mara knew better.

She had been nine when she and Tomas walked there with their mother one summer afternoon. The road had wound through green hills covered in yellow flowers. Orison had been a small village beside the sea, with blue roofs and a clock tower that chimed every quarter hour. They had eaten honey cakes in the square. Tomas had chased pigeons around a fountain until their mother scolded him.

Three weeks later, Tomas disappeared.

He was seven years old. Mara had been watching him while their mother gathered mussels at the eastern cove. One moment he had been drawing circles in the sand. The next, he was gone.

His footprints led to the water.

For months, boats searched the coast. Mara’s father walked the cliffs each night with a lantern. Her mother wrote letters to towns all along the shore. No body was found.

Then winter came, and Orison vanished too.

When Mara tried to return there with her father, the road ended at a cliff. No ruins lay below. No blue roofs, no clock tower, no fountain. The oldest maps in the council archives showed only open water where Mara remembered the village.

Her parents decided grief had confused her memory.

Mara became a cartographer because maps, unlike people, were supposed to tell the truth.

Now the impossible road lay before her.

The tide would turn in less than an hour.

Mara opened her notebook and drew the harbor wall, the first iron post, the angle of the road. Her hand shook. She closed the book, removed her boots, tied their laces together, and hung them around her neck.

Then she stepped onto the white stones.

The road was slick but solid. Water moved on both sides, rippling with the dawn wind. Farther out, the seabed dropped away, and the road crossed dark depths. Waves rolled toward it but broke soundlessly against an invisible boundary.

Mara glanced back.

Bellweather looked small from the water. Chimneys rose from crowded roofs. The lighthouse stood on the western cliff, its beam pale in the growing day.

At the end of the harbor, a figure hurried down the steps.

“Mara!” shouted Old Ren, the harbormaster. “Come back!”

She kept walking.

“The tide’s coming!”

“I know.”

“There’s nothing out there!”

Mara touched the barnacle-crusted signpost beside her.

“Yes,” she said, though he could no longer hear her. “There is.”

She walked until the town disappeared into fog.

At first she tried to count her steps. After two thousand, the sound of dripping water distracted her, though there was nothing above her from which water could fall. The air smelled of rain and apples. Somewhere in the mist, a child laughed.

Mara stopped.

“Tomas?”

The laughter came again, farther ahead.

She began to run.

The road tilted upward. The fog thinned, revealing a low hill covered in yellow flowers. Mara stumbled onto dry grass beneath a bright summer sky.

Behind her there was no sea.

The road wound down toward Orison.

Nothing had changed.

Blue roofs clustered around the square. White sheets stirred on clotheslines. Smoke curled from chimneys. A baker placed a tray of honey cakes in his window. Above it all rose the clock tower, its golden hands fixed at a quarter past three.

People filled the streets, but their movements were oddly repetitive. A woman swept the same patch of doorstep. A man lifted a barrel, carried it three paces, and set it down before lifting it again. Two girls skipped rope without speaking, their faces solemn.

Mara walked toward the square.

No one looked at her.

At the fountain, pigeons scattered before a boy in a red shirt.

He was small and thin, with dark curls that bounced as he ran.

Mara could not breathe.

“Tomas.”

The boy turned.

His face was seven years old, exactly as she remembered it. A white scar crossed his chin from the day he had fallen out of an apple tree. His eyes widened.

“Mara?”

She crossed the square in three strides and dropped to her knees, pulling him against her.

He smelled of sunlight and sand.

“You came back,” he said into her shoulder.

Mara held him so tightly that he complained, just as he used to.

She released him enough to look at his face. “You know me?”

“Of course I know you.” He frowned at the silver in her hair. “But you look old.”

A laugh escaped her, broken by a sob. “I am old.”

“That was fast.”

“Tomas, where have you been?”

“Here.”

“For twenty-two years?”

His expression became puzzled. “It hasn’t been that long.”

He pointed to the clock tower. The hands remained fixed.

“I got lost at the cove,” he said. “There was a road under the water. I followed it because I thought you were ahead of me. Then I came here. I waited in the square like Mama said to do if we were ever lost.”

“Why didn’t you leave?”

“I tried. The roads all come back.”

Mara looked toward the street by which she had entered. It was no longer there. A row of narrow houses stood in its place.

A bell rang from the clock tower.

The people in the square stopped moving.

Every face turned upward.

The clock’s minute hand advanced with a metallic click.

One minute past a quarter.

The people screamed.

The sound lasted only an instant. Then they resumed their tasks. The woman swept. The man lifted his barrel. The girls skipped rope.

Tomas gripped Mara’s hand.

“That happens when someone comes,” he whispered.

“What happens when someone leaves?”

“No one leaves.”

A shadow moved in the clock tower.

Mara saw a figure standing behind the glass face, impossibly tall and thin. Then the sun flashed on the golden hands, and it was gone.

She led Tomas away from the square.

They tried the northern street first. After ten minutes, they emerged beside the fountain. The western lane took them past a school, a stable, and a field of yellow flowers before returning them to the bakery. They entered a house and climbed through a rear window, only to step out of the door of the same house.

Orison folded in on itself like a badly drawn map.

Mara took out her compass. Its needle spun wildly, then settled, not toward north but toward the clock tower.

She opened her notebook.

The sketch she had made of the sea road was gone. In its place was a single sentence written in a hand she did not recognize:

EVERY ROAD REQUIRES A DESTINATION.

Beneath it, slowly, another line appeared.

EVERY DESTINATION REQUIRES A PRICE.

Mara showed Tomas.

“Did you write that?” he asked.

“No.”

“Maybe the town did.”

“Towns don’t write.”

“This one does things.”

The bell sounded again.

The clock advanced another minute.

All around them, doors slammed. The townspeople cried out as if struck. A crack appeared in the blue sky, thin and white, running from one horizon to the other.

“How many times can it move?” Mara asked.

“I don’t know. It never moved before today.”

She studied the tower. “Then we don’t have long.”

They entered the square. Mara approached the baker, who was placing the same tray in his window.

“Excuse me,” she said.

He did not react.

She grabbed his arm.

His face turned toward her, but his eyes remained fixed on the tray.

“You shouldn’t touch the remembered,” he whispered.

Mara let go.

The baker continued arranging cakes.

“What are you?” she asked.

His lips barely moved. “What remains.”

“Who built the road?”

“The one above.”

“How do we leave?”

This time, his eyes shifted toward the clock tower.

“Correct the map.”

His hand jerked backward, knocking the tray from the counter. The cakes fell, but before they struck the floor, they vanished and reappeared in his hands.

“Correct what?” Mara demanded.

The baker smiled sadly.

“You drew us wrong.”

Then he went still.

Mara stepped back.

A memory surfaced: herself at nine years old, sitting beneath the kitchen table after Tomas disappeared. Her mother wept in the next room while her father spoke to the constable.

Mara had taken a sheet of paper and drawn the road to Orison. She had drawn the hills, the village, the sea, and a tiny figure in a red shirt standing beside the fountain.

At the bottom of the page she had written: TOMAS IS HERE.

She had placed the map beneath her pillow and whispered that if the world had lost her brother, the map should keep him safe.

The following morning, the paper was blank.

“Mara?” Tomas tugged her sleeve. “What is it?”

“I made this place.”

He laughed. “You can’t make a whole village.”

“Not the village. A copy. A memory of it.”

Orison must already have been gone when she drew that childish map. Perhaps the landslide story had been true. Perhaps the sea had taken it. In her grief, she had drawn the only Orison she knew: the village on a summer afternoon, fixed forever at a quarter past three.

And she had placed Tomas inside it.

The bell rang.

The minute hand moved.

The crack in the sky widened. Darkness showed beyond it, filled with distant stars.

The townspeople began to flicker.

“We have to reach the tower,” Mara said.

Its door was locked, but Tomas knew a way through the adjoining chapel. They climbed a drainpipe to the chapel roof, crossed a narrow gutter, and entered the tower through a broken window.

Inside, thousands of sheets of paper hung from threads.

Maps.

Some were ancient, drawn on cracked leather. Others were sketched in charcoal, ink, or colored pencil. Mara saw maps of vanished islands, burned forests, demolished houses, and cities whose names she did not know. Each contained small moving figures.

“This place is bigger than Orison,” she whispered.

A staircase spiraled upward through the swaying pages.

At the first landing, they passed a map of a battlefield. Toy-sized soldiers fought beneath a red sky. At the second was a child’s drawing of a farm, with three green cows and a purple sun. At the third, an old woman wandered through the rooms of a house while snow fell outside.

“Are they trapped too?” Tomas asked.

“Yes.”

“Can we help them?”

Mara looked up the endless staircase.

“I don’t know.”

The tower shook. Somewhere below, the bell rang again.

They climbed until Mara’s legs trembled. Tomas seemed tireless. He was still seven; he had been seven for twenty-two years.

At last they reached the clock chamber.

Golden gears filled the room, turning with slow, painful groans. Behind them stood the tall figure Mara had seen from the square. Its coat was made of folded maps. Its face was blank parchment.

“You have come to correct your work,” it said.

Its voice sounded like pages turning.

“What are you?” Mara asked.

“A keeper of destinations.”

“You took my brother.”

“I take no one. I preserve what is given.”

“I didn’t give him to you.”

The blank face tilted.

“You drew the place. You named the traveler. You made your desire clear.”

“I was a child.”

“Maps do not distinguish between the hands that make them.”

Mara held Tomas close. “Send us home.”

“One may return.”

The words fell with the weight of stones.

“No,” Mara said.

“One road. One traveler. This is the rule.”

“You said every destination requires a price.”

“The road opened for you. Your years are its price.”

Mara touched her face. “What does that mean?”

The figure pointed toward a tarnished mirror.

Mara looked.

An old woman stared back at her. Her hair was white, her skin deeply lined, her shoulders bent. While Orison had preserved Tomas, the journey had taken from Mara all the years she might have had.

Tomas stared at her reflection.

“You look like Grandmother,” he said softly.

Mara felt no shock, only a strange calm. Time had always been a kind of distance. She had crossed it quickly, that was all.

“Then I’ve paid,” she said. “Let us both go.”

“You paid to arrive.”

The clock gears shifted.

Below, the bell began to swing.

“How long until the town is destroyed?”

“When the clock reaches half past three.”

“Why?”

“Memory cannot survive correction unchanged. Your arrival introduced time. Time will complete its work.”

Tomas stepped forward. “Send Mara home.”

“No,” she said.

“You came to find me.”

“I came to bring you back.”

“But you’re old now.”

“And you’re still my little brother.”

“I won’t be if I leave.”

Mara looked at the keeper. “What does he mean?”

The parchment face remained expressionless. “What was withheld will be restored.”

“If he returns, he’ll become twenty-nine?”

“In body, yes. In memory, no. His memory contains only seven years.”

Tomas squeezed her hand. “I’ll still know you.”

The bell rang.

The hands moved to twenty-five minutes past three.

Five minutes remained.

The tower walls split. Through the cracks, Mara saw the square below. Roof tiles lifted into the sky and dissolved into scraps of blue paint. The townspeople continued their loops, but now some had lost their faces.

“Where is the original map?” Mara asked.

The keeper raised one long arm.

At the center of the largest gear was a small, yellowed sheet of paper.

Mara recognized the crooked hills, the square houses, and the sea drawn in blue pencil. Beside the fountain stood Tomas in his red shirt.

She reached for it.

The keeper blocked her path.

“To alter the map is to alter the place.”

“That’s what correction means.”

“Correction is not rescue.”

“What happens if I draw a road home?”

“All roads require a destination.”

“Bellweather.”

“And all destinations require a price.”

“You already have my years.”

The keeper’s coat rustled. “There are other prices.”

Mara understood.

Her hand tightened around the notebook. Bellweather had been her entire life: its steep alleys, black cliffs, rain-polished roofs, and crowded harbor. She knew every cellar and garden wall. She knew where violets bloomed first in spring and which chimneys leaned east after winter storms.

A map was more than roads. It was a promise that a place could be found again.

To make a road from Orison to Bellweather, she would have to surrender her destination—not the town itself, but her place within it.

“If I draw the road,” she said, “I can never go home.”

The keeper bowed its blank head.

“What does that mean?” Tomas asked.

“It means you can.”

“No.”

The clock clicked forward.

Twenty-seven minutes past.

Mara pulled the childhood map from the gear.

The tower screamed.

Not the keeper, not the bell—the tower itself. Gears shuddered. Maps tore loose from their threads and circled the chamber in a storm. Mara saw a thousand lost worlds spin around her.

She flattened the paper on the floor.

From her coat she took a mapping pen and a vial of black ink. Her hands were old now, knuckles swollen, fingers unsteady. Tomas knelt beside her.

“Don’t,” he said.

“Do you remember the eastern cove?”

“Yes.”

“Do you remember Mama singing while she gathered mussels?”

“Yes.”

“Do you remember the way home?”

He swallowed. “Past the white cliff, through the grass, then over the bridge.”

“Good.”

Mara placed the pen on the map.

She drew a line from Orison’s square, through the yellow hills, and beyond the edge of the page. As the ink moved, the chamber wall opened. A road appeared outside, suspended over the sea. At its far end, beneath storm clouds, lay Bellweather.

“You have to go now,” she said.

Tomas shook his head.

The years had begun to find him. His arms lengthened. His face thinned. The red shirt stretched against his shoulders.

“Mara, come with me.”

“I can’t.”

“We’ll pay something else.”

“There’s nothing else.”

“Your maps. Give it all your maps.”

“They’re only paper.”

“Your memories, then.”

She smiled. “Those are what I’m using to draw the road.”

The clock moved.

Twenty-nine minutes past.

Orison broke apart.

The bakery collapsed into pencil lines. The fountain flattened into a wash of blue. The yellow flowers rose like sparks and vanished. Through the gaps in the floor, Mara saw not earth but white paper.

Tomas was nearly a man now, though fear made his face seem young.

Mara pushed the brass compass into his hand.

“It doesn’t point north,” he said.

“No. It points toward what you’ve lost.”

“What will you do?”

She looked at the maps spiraling around the chamber.

“I’ll find another road.”

The keeper extended its hand to her. For the first time, lines appeared on its blank face: not features, but roads, rivers, borders, coastlines.

“The tower requires a cartographer,” it said.

Mara understood then that the keeper had once been human. Perhaps every fold of its coat was a map it had made, every line on its face a road surrendered.

She turned to Tomas.

“Go.”

He embraced her. Though he had the body of a grown man, he still held her as a child would, fiercely and without restraint.

“I waited for you,” he whispered.

“I know.”

“I knew you’d come.”

The final gear shifted.

Mara shoved him onto the road.

The clock struck half past three.

White light consumed Orison.

Tomas woke on the wet sand below Bellweather harbor.

The tide was coming in. Waves washed over his legs, and rain fell from a dark morning sky. For several minutes he lay still, staring at his adult hands.

People shouted from the harbor wall. A rope was thrown. Old Ren—older than Tomas remembered, but still unmistakable—climbed down and helped him from the water.

“Who are you?” the harbormaster demanded.

Tomas tried to answer.

He knew his name. He knew his sister. He knew that he had been seven years old that morning and was not seven anymore.

In his hand, the brass compass needle spun, then pointed toward the horizon.

Years passed.

Tomas learned how to inhabit the life time had returned to him. He mourned his parents, who had died believing him lost. He worked first at the bakery, drawn by the smell of honey cakes, and later became a teacher at the schoolhouse.

People said he adjusted remarkably well, but this was not true. For a long time, doors frightened him because he expected them to open into the same room. Bells made him tremble. He never entered the sea.

Mara’s maps remained throughout Bellweather, though no one remembered the woman who had drawn them.

When Tomas asked about her, people frowned.

There had never been a cartographer named Mara Venn, they said.

The house where they had grown up contained no trace of her. Family portraits showed Tomas alone between his parents. Letters had lost her name. Even his memories blurred when he tried to recall her face.

Only the compass remained.

It pointed east every day for forty-three years.

Then, on an autumn morning, the needle moved.

Tomas was old by then. He followed it through town, past the harbor, and up the western cliff. The compass led him to a path absent from every map.

At the end stood a small cottage overlooking the sea.

Inside, the walls were covered with maps of impossible places: forests growing upside down, cities beneath deserts, houses hidden in dreams. At a table by the window sat a woman in a coat made of folded paper.

Her hair was white. Roads and rivers crossed her skin like fine ink.

Tomas knew her at once.

“Mara,” he said.

She looked up.

For a moment there was no recognition in her eyes. She had spent decades guiding travelers out of lost places and had paid for every road with a memory of her own.

Then she saw the compass in his hand.

The lines on her face shifted, forming a path beside a fountain, two children beneath a summer sky, and a road leading home.

“Tomas,” she whispered.

He sat beside her.

Outside, the sea withdrew from the shore. Far below, white roads surfaced one by one, stretching toward destinations that had waited years, centuries, or lifetimes to be found.

Mara opened her notebook to a blank page.

Together, they began to draw.

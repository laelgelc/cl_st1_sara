# The Museum of Forgotten Weather

On the morning the rain forgot how to fall, Mara Vale received a letter from her dead father.

The rain had been suspended above Bellweather since dawn: thousands of silver droplets hanging motionless in the air, trembling but never descending. People walked through them slowly, emerging jeweled and damp. Children leaped from walls to catch the drops in jars. Shopkeepers stood beneath striped awnings and argued about whether suspended rain counted as rain at all.

Mara watched from the doorway of her clock shop.

She had inherited the shop from her father, along with three hundred unfinished clocks, a mountain of debt, and his habit of leaving tea untouched until it grew cold. The letter lay on the counter behind her. It was addressed in his narrow handwriting:

**Mara Vale  
Vale & Daughter, Clockmakers  
Bellweather**

No one had called the shop “Vale & Daughter” since Mara was twelve.

She turned the envelope over. The seal was a small black sun.

Her father had been dead seven years.

Mara locked the shop door, lowered the blind, and opened the letter with a screwdriver.

Inside was a brass key and a single sheet of paper.

*Dear Sparrow,*

*If you are reading this, Bellweather has begun to lose its weather.*

*Go to the old railway station. Platform Six. The key will show you the door.*

*I am sorry I did not tell you the truth.*

*—Father*

Mara read it three times.

Then she carried it to the stove and held one corner over the flame.

The paper did not burn.

“Of course,” she muttered. “You couldn’t simply stay dead.”

She put on her coat.

Outside, the town shone beneath the motionless rain.

Bellweather had always been famous for its climate. Tourists came to see the violet thunderstorms that rolled across the rooftops every midsummer, or the warm winter snow that tasted faintly of cinnamon. There were windy Thursdays, foggy festivals, and one famous afternoon in 1882 when it had rained tiny frogs for exactly eleven minutes.

No one knew why Bellweather’s weather behaved as it did. Most people preferred not to ask.

The old railway station stood at the edge of town, abandoned since the tracks had buckled during the Great Heat of ’91. Ivy wrapped the iron pillars. Pigeons nested in the ticket office. The station clock, made by Mara’s grandfather, still displayed seventeen minutes past nine, the time of the last departure.

There were only five platforms.

Mara walked to the end of Platform Five and found a brick wall covered in faded advertisements. When she removed the brass key from her pocket, it pulled gently toward the wall.

A keyhole appeared between two bricks.

“Subtle,” she said.

She inserted the key.

The wall opened like a curtain.

Beyond it stretched a narrow platform lit by blue lamps. A black train waited beside it, breathing steam. On the nearest carriage, gold letters read:

**THE MUSEUM OF FORGOTTEN WEATHER**

A thin man in a blue conductor’s uniform leaned from the carriage door.

“Miss Vale,” he said. “You’re late.”

“My father has been dead for seven years.”

“Yes.”

“The rain is stuck.”

“So I noticed.”

“And there is a train hidden inside a wall.”

“Also true.”

Mara folded her arms. “I’m not late. I’m having a difficult morning.”

The conductor considered this.

“Fair enough,” he said. “Climb aboard.”

His name was Orin. He had a long, pale face and the exhausted courtesy of a man who had been apologizing for the same disaster for decades.

The train was empty except for them. Its seats were upholstered in dark green velvet, and brass luggage racks held glass jars filled with mist. Through the windows, Mara could no longer see the station. There was only a gray landscape where distant clouds wandered like grazing animals.

“Where are we going?” she asked.

“We’re already there.”

The train entered a tunnel, and darkness swallowed the windows.

A moment later, they emerged into an impossible hall.

The Museum of Forgotten Weather was larger than any building Mara had ever seen. Its ceiling was a midnight sky crowded with slow-turning stars. Staircases crossed the air without visible support. Thousands of glass cabinets stretched into the distance, each containing some lost piece of the world’s weather.

A golden wind stirred in one cabinet, carrying the scent of oranges and dust.

In another, black snow rose upward.

Mara saw bottled monsoons, folded rainbows, a hurricane no wider than a teacup, and a pale red lightning bolt that tapped impatiently against its glass.

The train stopped.

Orin led her into the museum.

“We preserve weather that has vanished from the world,” he explained. “The first frost of a forgotten kingdom. The final storm over a dried sea. Breezes that once blew through forests now buried under cities.”

“And Bellweather?”

“Bellweather is different. Its weather was never natural.”

Mara looked at him.

Orin sighed. “Your father built it.”

She laughed once, sharply.

“My father repaired clocks.”

“Time and weather are closer than people think. Both are systems of change. Both move forward. Both become dangerous when forced to stop.”

They passed beneath a suspended thundercloud. Lightning flickered inside it, illuminating a label:

**LAST TEMPEST OF THE SOUTHERN ISLES  
DO NOT SHAKE**

“Elias Vale was the museum’s chief mechanic,” Orin continued. “Brilliant. Difficult. Frequently right in ways that made everyone miserable.”

“That sounds like him.”

“Forty years ago, Bellweather was dying. The mines had closed. The river had dried to mud. People were leaving. Your father believed unusual weather could save the town.”

“It did,” Mara said. “Tourists came. Orchards grew again.”

“Yes. Elias built a machine beneath the museum to borrow small amounts of weather from the collection and release them over Bellweather. At first, the machine worked perfectly.”

“At first?”

Orin glanced at the droplets of rain still clinging to Mara’s coat.

“The machine is failing.”

They descended a spiral staircase into warmer air. The walls hummed. Pipes ran overhead, some carrying water, others packed with fog or flashing with lightning.

Mara remembered her father’s hands: scarred fingers, blackened nails, a burn shaped like a crescent moon across his palm. She had assumed the injuries came from clockwork.

“Why didn’t he tell me?”

“He tried to keep you away from the museum.”

“He succeeded.”

“That was the problem.”

At the foot of the stairs stood a brass door taller than a house. In its center was a circular clock face with thirteen numbers and no hands.

Orin placed his palm against it.

“Elias believed the machine would outlast him. It didn’t. For seven years, I’ve managed repairs. But I am a curator, not a clockmaker.”

“You waited seven years to send the letter?”

“Elias arranged for it to be delivered only when the machine reached a critical failure.”

“The rain has stopped falling.”

“That is not the critical failure.”

A deep clang sounded beyond the door.

The floor trembled.

Somewhere in the museum, glass shattered.

Orin’s expression tightened.

“That is.”

The door opened.

The machine filled an underground chamber so vast that clouds formed beneath its roof. It was a universe of turning gears, swinging pendulums, pumping bellows, and rotating copper rings. Pipes vanished into a central sphere of blue glass. Inside the sphere, a miniature version of Bellweather spun beneath changing skies.

But the machine was shuddering. Several gears had stopped. One enormous pendulum hung frozen at the end of its arc. Cracks spread across the blue sphere.

Mara forgot her anger for a moment.

Her father had built beautiful things. Even his simplest clocks contained hidden flourishes—silver birds behind plain faces, tiny constellations engraved where no one would see them. This machine was his masterpiece.

And it was dying.

Mara approached the central controls. They resembled a clock’s interior, though the gears were labeled not with minutes and hours but with *pressure*, *memory*, *season*, and *chance*.

One small dial spun wildly between *rain* and *regret*.

“He always did overcomplicate things,” she said.

“Can you fix it?”

“No.”

Orin went pale.

“I haven’t even examined it,” Mara added.

“You might have said that first.”

“My father would have known where to begin.”

“Your father expected you to know.”

“My father knew nothing about me.”

The chamber groaned. A pipe burst overhead, releasing a flock of tiny clouds. They flew around the rafters like frightened birds.

Mara opened the control panel.

Inside, hundreds of gears moved in conflicting directions. Several were stripped. A chain had snapped. At the center lay an empty space shaped like a human heart.

She stared at it.

“What was here?”

Orin said nothing.

“What was here?”

“The regulator.”

“Where is it now?”

“Destroyed.”

“How?”

“Your father removed it.”

Mara turned on him. “Why?”

“To save you.”

The chamber seemed to become very quiet, though the machinery still thundered around them.

Orin took off his cap.

“When you were nine, you came here.”

“I didn’t.”

“You did. Elias brought you because you had a fever no doctor could break. It was winter, the coldest Bellweather had ever known. He believed the museum contained a summer warm enough to cure you.”

Mara remembered fragments: blue fire in a grate, her father carrying her through a dark place, the smell of hot grass. For years she had thought it was a dream.

“He opened the Cabinet of Endless June,” Orin said. “The summer cured your fever, but it escaped into the machine. Seasons began colliding. Elias stopped the failure by tearing out the regulator. The machine needed something living to replace it.”

Mara looked again at the heart-shaped space.

“No.”

“Not a literal heart,” Orin said quickly. “A memory. A powerful one. The machine has been running on one of Elias’s memories for thirty-one years.”

“Which memory?”

“The day you were born.”

Mara gripped the edge of the panel.

Her father had never spoken of her birth. He had barely spoken of her mother, who had died before Mara could remember her. Whenever Mara asked, his face became distant and guarded.

“What happens to a memory when it powers this machine?”

“It wears away.”

“Did he forget?”

“Piece by piece.”

The answer entered her like cold.

She remembered how, in his final years, her father had sometimes looked at her as though trying to place her face. Once, on her twenty-second birthday, he had called her “Miss” and asked whether she had come to collect a repaired watch.

She had believed illness was taking him.

Perhaps it had been sacrifice instead.

That realization did not feel like forgiveness. It felt too heavy and complicated to have a name.

“Why didn’t he replace the regulator?” she asked.

“The original was made from a rare substance called stormglass. There is only one piece left in the museum.”

“Then let’s use it.”

“It is currently containing something.”

“Of course it is.”

Orin led her through a side passage while the machine shook behind them.

They climbed into the museum’s western wing. The air grew colder with every step. Frost feathered the walls. At the end of a long gallery stood a single glass cabinet.

Inside was a storm.

It had no rain, no thunder, no lightning. It was a darkness that twisted like smoke. Now and then shapes appeared within it—houses collapsing, trees bending, people running beneath a colorless sky.

The stormglass case was barely larger than a coffin.

Its label read:

**THE UNHAPPENED STORM  
COLLECTED TOMORROW**

Mara read the words twice.

“What does that mean?”

“This storm does not yet exist,” Orin said. “It is the future disaster the machine was built to prevent. Every time your father borrowed weather, every time he forced the skies to behave unnaturally, the rejected possibilities gathered here.”

“You’re storing a storm made from everything that didn’t happen?”

“Yes.”

“And you want me to break its case?”

“No. The storm would destroy Bellweather within minutes.”

“Then we don’t have a regulator.”

“No.”

Behind them came a distant boom. The stars in the museum ceiling blinked out, then returned.

Mara examined the case. The storm pressed against the glass as she approached. For one instant she saw the clock shop in ruins. Then the image vanished.

“How long until the machine fails?”

“An hour, perhaps less.”

“And then?”

“Bellweather’s missing weather returns all at once. Forty years of postponed droughts, storms, freezes, and floods.”

Mara thought of the town beneath its beautiful suspended rain. Children collecting droplets. Bakers lighting ovens. Neighbors complaining about wet shoes and late deliveries. Ordinary people living ordinary lives because her father had decided his home should not die.

There was arrogance in that choice.

There was love, too.

Mara touched the brass key in her pocket.

“Why did the letter contain this?”

“It opens Platform Six.”

“It opened the museum. What else?”

Orin frowned. “Nothing.”

“My father never made anything with only one purpose.”

She held the key to the stormglass.

It pulled toward the cabinet’s base.

A small keyhole appeared.

Orin stepped back. “I have worked here for sixty-three years and am beginning to find your family extremely irritating.”

“That means he liked you.”

Mara inserted the key.

A hidden drawer slid open beneath the case.

Inside lay a silver pocket watch.

It was the same watch her father had carried every day of his life. Mara remembered its chain across his waistcoat, its ticking in the silence between them. After his death, she had searched everywhere for it.

She lifted it from the drawer.

The watch was warm.

Its face had no numbers, only an engraving: a sparrow in flight.

Mara pressed the crown.

The museum vanished.

She stood in the clock shop as it had looked when she was a child. Sunlight poured through the windows. Every clock displayed noon.

Her father sat at the workbench, younger than she remembered him, his dark hair only beginning to gray.

He looked directly at her.

“Hello, Sparrow.”

Mara could not speak.

“This is a memory,” he said. “Not a ghost. I know you dislike ghosts.”

“I dislike you at the moment.”

“That seems fair.”

His familiar half smile appeared, then faded.

“I made mistakes,” he said. “Many began as good intentions, which made them harder to recognize. Bellweather was dying, and I thought I could repair it as I repaired a clock. Replace winter here. Tighten rainfall there. But towns are not clocks, and weather is not obedient.”

“You should have told me.”

“Yes.”

“You let me think you forgot me.”

His eyes lowered.

“I did forget parts of you. Your first word. The songs you sang badly. The exact color of your hair in summer. Each loss terrified me. So I pushed you away before I could lose more.”

“That was cowardly.”

“Yes.”

She wanted him to argue. She wanted him to offer an excuse she could tear apart. His agreement left her anger with nowhere to go.

“Why leave me the watch?”

“Because the machine requires a regulator, and the stormglass cannot be safely removed. But a clockmaker knows the purpose of a regulator is not to provide power. It is to create balance.”

The clocks around them began ticking.

“The machine takes weather from the museum and gives it to Bellweather,” he continued. “That was always the flaw. It is a one-way mechanism.”

“So I reverse it?”

“Not exactly. You complete the exchange.”

The memory trembled. The shop windows darkened at the edges.

“What does Bellweather give back?” Mara asked.

“Time.”

The clocks grew louder.

“Every place receives days,” her father said. “Most spend them carelessly. Bellweather has been given forty borrowed years. The town must surrender what remains of those years—the weather it would have had, the futures it might have lived—and accept uncertainty again.”

“Will that save everyone?”

“I don’t know.”

“You don’t know?”

“No machine can guarantee a future. That is what I failed to understand.”

The shop began dissolving into light.

“Mara,” he said, rising. “There is one more thing.”

She waited.

“I remember the day you were born.”

Her throat tightened.

“I thought the machine consumed it,” she said.

“I hid the last moment here.”

He looked toward the sunny window, and his expression softened.

“It was raining,” he said. “Ordinary rain. Your mother was exhausted. I held you near the glass, and you opened your eyes. At that exact instant, the sun came through the clouds. Nothing magical happened. No rainbow. No thunder. Just rain and sunlight together.”

The memory broke apart.

His final words followed her into darkness.

“It was the finest weather I ever saw.”

Mara stood before the cabinet again, clutching the watch. Less than a minute had passed.

Orin peered at her. “Did it contain instructions?”

“Yes.”

“Did they make sense?”

“No.”

“Definitely Elias, then.”

They ran back to the machine.

The chamber was coming apart. Gears jumped from their axles. Wind roared from broken pipes. Inside the blue sphere, miniature Bellweather flickered between drought and flood.

Mara climbed onto the control platform and opened the panel.

The empty heart-shaped space waited.

She placed the pocket watch inside it.

Nothing happened.

“Balance,” she whispered.

The mechanism around the watch had four connecting arms. Three were labeled *taking*. The fourth had no label and had never been attached.

Mara pulled a screwdriver from her coat. She loosened the main transfer gear, ignoring Orin’s alarmed shout, and reversed it. Then she connected the unused arm to the gear marked *chance*.

The machine screamed.

Every clock in the chamber began running backward.

“I sincerely hope that is intentional!” Orin called.

“So do I!”

The pocket watch sprang open. Golden light flooded the panel.

A new word appeared on the fourth arm:

**RETURNING**

Mara grabbed the control lever with both hands.

It would not move.

Orin joined her. Together they pulled.

The lever shifted one notch.

The blue sphere cracked from top to bottom.

Wind tore through the chamber. Mara’s feet left the floor, but Orin caught her coat and dragged her behind the panel.

Above them, cabinets burst open.

Forgotten weather escaped into the museum. Red lightning crawled across the ceiling. Black snow rose through stairwells. The golden wind roared past, carrying oranges, dust, and distant laughter.

The Unhappened Storm expanded inside its case.

Then the machine’s enormous pendulum began to swing.

Once.

Twice.

On the third swing, the pocket watch chimed.

A clear tone passed through the museum.

The escaped weather stopped.

Slowly, gently, it reversed. Lightning folded itself into glass. Snow descended upward into its cabinet. Clouds hurried through pipes. Winds curled into jars.

Inside the stormglass case, the darkness thinned. Visions of ruined streets dissolved. The storm shrank until only a small gray cloud remained.

The blue sphere repaired itself.

Within it, Bellweather turned beneath a blank sky.

“What now?” Orin asked.

Mara watched the little town.

“Now we find out.”

They returned by train.

When Mara stepped through the wall onto Platform Five, the rain was still suspended over Bellweather.

For a breath, nothing changed.

Then every silver droplet fell.

Rain struck roofs, streets, leaves, and upturned faces. Children shrieked with delight. Gutters overflowed. Shopkeepers scrambled beneath awnings.

Mara stood in the downpour and laughed.

It rained for three ordinary hours.

The next day was cloudy. The day after that was warm. A week later, an unexpected frost killed half the town’s strawberries, and everyone complained bitterly. Summer brought no violet thunderstorms, only several dull gray ones that ruined two picnics and a wedding.

Tourists stopped coming.

For a while, Bellweather faltered. Hotels closed. Souvenir shops emptied their windows. People spoke angrily of the days when snow tasted of cinnamon and Thursdays were reliably windy.

But the town did not die.

The old railway station reopened as a market. Orchards changed crops. A factory began making stormglass instruments, though none of the workers knew why their designs seemed familiar to Mara.

She reopened the clock shop under its old name: **Vale & Daughter**.

Orin visited on the first Monday of every month, always claiming the museum clocks required inspection and always staying for tea. He continued to complain about the Vale family, which Mara understood as affection.

Years later, a little girl came into the shop carrying a broken barometer.

“My mother says you fix weather,” she said.

“Your mother is mistaken,” Mara replied.

The girl looked disappointed.

Mara opened the barometer. Its spring had slipped. Outside, clouds gathered above the town, dark and unscheduled.

“Can you tell me if it will rain?” the girl asked.

Mara glanced through the window.

Once, Bellweather had known exactly what each sky would bring. Its people had mistaken certainty for safety and wonder for control.

Now the first raindrop struck the glass.

Then another.

Mara smiled.

“No,” she said. “Isn’t it marvelous?”

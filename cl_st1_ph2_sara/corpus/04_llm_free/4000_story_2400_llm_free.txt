The Bell at the Bottom of the Lake

Every morning at six, the bell beneath Lake Arlen rang once.

It was not loud. People standing on the shore heard only a low bronze note passing through the water, but in the village houses the cups trembled in their cupboards, windowpanes hummed, and sleeping dogs lifted their heads.

No one knew why the bell rang.

The oldest villagers said it belonged to a church drowned when the valley was flooded. The oldest maps, however, showed no church in the valley—only farms, orchards, and a narrow road that followed the vanished river north.

Some claimed the bell was rung by currents. Others blamed the shifting of the lakebed. Children whispered that a drowned priest pulled the rope, tolling once each morning for the soul he had failed to save.

Mara Vale did not believe any of these stories.

She maintained the village clocks.

There were seventy-three of them: wall clocks, grandfather clocks, carriage clocks, a brass marine chronometer in the mayor’s office, and the large white clock in the railway station, though no train had stopped there for twenty years. Mara wound, cleaned, and repaired them all. She knew that every mystery had a mechanism. What people called magic was often just a spring hidden behind a face.

The underwater bell, she believed, had a mechanism too.

Her father had believed otherwise.

“The lake keeps time,” he used to say. “It doesn’t measure it. There’s a difference.”

He disappeared on Lake Arlen when Mara was twelve. His boat was found drifting near the northern cliffs, with one oar missing and the other laid neatly across the seat. His coat, lunch tin, and pocket watch were still inside. The watch had stopped at six in the morning.

For years, Mara refused to go near the shore.

Then, on the first day of autumn in her thirty-fourth year, the bell rang twice.

The first note came at six, as always.

The second followed three seconds later.

Across the village, people woke and waited in frightened silence. The sound faded through the foundations of their homes.

That afternoon, a shepherd found a dead swan on the eastern bank. The next day, milk soured before noon. By evening, rumors had multiplied like flies.

Mara spent the day repairing Mrs. Pell’s mantel clock, which had not chimed correctly since her husband’s funeral.

“Two bells,” Mrs. Pell said, watching Mara fit a pin into the striking mechanism. “It means the lake wants another sacrifice.”

“The lake doesn’t want anything.”

“That’s what your father said before it took him.”

Mara’s hand slipped. The pin scratched the brass plate.

“My father said the lake kept time.”

Mrs. Pell’s lined face softened. “Did he?”

“Yes.”

“I remember him saying many things.”

“So do I.”

Mara closed the clock case and wound it. At four, the little clock chimed four clear notes.

That night, someone knocked on Mara’s door.

Outside stood a boy of about ten, barefoot despite the cold. His dark hair dripped water onto the step.

“Are you the clock mender?” he asked.

“Who are you?”

“Eli.”

“Where are your shoes?”

The boy looked down, as if surprised by his bare feet. “I lost them.”

“In the rain?”

“It isn’t raining.”

Mara glanced past him. The lane was dry beneath the moon.

“Where do you live?”

Eli pointed toward the lake.

A chill moved through her, but she did not believe in ghosts. Children ran away. Children became confused. Children told strange stories when they were afraid.

She let him inside, wrapped him in a blanket, and put a kettle on the stove.

“Your family will be worried.”

“They’re waiting.”

“Where?”

“Below.”

The kettle began to whisper.

Mara sat opposite him. “Did someone tell you to come here?”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“Your father.”

The kettle screamed.

Mara stood so abruptly that her chair fell backward.

The boy watched her without blinking.

“My father is dead.”

“No,” Eli said. “He’s late.”

Mara ordered him to stay where he was and hurried next door to fetch Constable Rusk. She was gone less than five minutes. When she returned, the blanket lay folded on the chair.

The back door was bolted from within. The front door had been in her sight as she crossed the lane.

On the table, in a small puddle of lake water, rested a brass key.

Rusk examined it beneath the lamp.

“Old,” he said.

“I can see that.”

“Could belong to a cabinet. Maybe a music box.”

“The boy left it.”

“What boy?”

Mara stared at him.

Rusk rubbed his mustache. “You said no one was here when you returned.”

“He was here before.”

“Of course.”

“You don’t believe me.”

“I believe you saw someone.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No,” he admitted. “It isn’t.”

At six the next morning, the bell rang three times.

By sunrise, half the village had gathered at the shore. The lake lay smooth and gray, untouched by wind. Each ring had sent a perfect circle across its surface. The last ripples were only just disappearing.

Mayor Cale called an emergency meeting in the town hall. He spoke of safety, investigation, and the importance of avoiding panic. Everyone panicked immediately.

The fishermen refused to launch their boats. The school closed. Mrs. Pell began hanging iron nails over windows. Old Amos Reed walked through the streets shouting that the drowned valley was rising.

Mara returned to her workshop and placed the brass key under a magnifying lens.

On its shaft she found three tiny letters: A.V.C.

Arlen Valley Clockworks.

She knew the name. Long before the valley was flooded, the company had made tower clocks. Only a few survived, including one in the provincial museum. Mara had seen it as an apprentice: a massive iron mechanism whose central wheel was taller than she was.

According to the museum plaque, the company factory had stood somewhere near the old river.

Somewhere beneath Lake Arlen.

Mara spent the afternoon in the municipal archive. At the bottom of a dust-coated drawer, she found construction plans for the dam. Most buildings in the valley had been demolished before flooding, but one structure had been left intact: the Arlen Valley Clockworks factory.

A red handwritten note in the margin explained why.

FOUNDATIONS TOO DEEP. DEMOLITION UNSAFE. MACHINERY REMOVED.

A second note had been added below.

LOWER CHAMBER SEALED.

Mara copied the coordinates.

At dusk, she went to the boathouse once owned by her father. The door resisted, swollen by years of damp, before opening with a groan.

His small blue boat remained inside.

Mara ran her hand along its peeling side. Dust covered the benches, but the old anchor was there, along with rope, two oars, and a hand-cranked depth line. On a shelf she found her father’s diving lantern, sealed in thick glass.

Beneath it was a notebook.

Most pages contained sketches of fishing hooks and weather observations. Near the end, his handwriting grew hurried.

Bell rang at 5:57. Village clocks showed 6:00.

Next day: bell at 5:54. Clocks still 6:00.

It isn’t following the clocks. They are following it.

On the final page, he had drawn a circle divided into gear teeth. Beneath it were the words:

If it stops, time returns to the valley.

Mara read the sentence until darkness filled the boathouse.

At six the next morning, the bell rang four times.

Mara was already on the lake.

Constable Rusk sat in the bow, pale and unhappy. Mara had told him only that she had discovered the possible location of the bell. He brought grappling hooks, a coil of rope, and an old diving suit from the fire station.

“Your plan,” he said, “is terrible.”

“You volunteered.”

“I volunteered to prevent you from going alone.”

“That was kind.”

“It was administrative.”

Mist covered the water. Mara rowed north, counting strokes. When the depth line showed thirty fathoms, she adjusted their course toward the cliffs.

The coordinates led them to a spot where the lake darkened from silver to black.

Rusk peered over the side. “How far down?”

“Forty feet to the factory roof. Maybe sixty to the lower chamber.”

“I dislike every word in that sentence.”

Mara fastened the diving suit. It was stiff, patched, and smelled of rubber. A hose connected the helmet to a hand pump in the boat.

“If I pull twice, give me more air. Three times means pull me up.”

“And four?”

“Four means I’ve forgotten the instructions.”

“Mara.”

She looked at him.

“Come back up.”

She nodded, put on the helmet, and descended.

The cold struck first.

Then came the silence.

The lake closed above her in layers of green and gray. Rusk and the boat vanished. Only the rope remained, trembling beside her.

The factory roof emerged from the darkness like the deck of a sunken ship. Chimneys leaned at impossible angles. Pale fish drifted through broken windows.

Mara landed in the courtyard. Silt rose around her boots.

An iron sign lay half buried near the entrance:

ARLEN VALLEY CLOCKWORKS  
WE MAKE THE HOURS HONEST

She entered.

Worktables stood in long rows. Tools still hung from hooks, though the construction note had claimed the machinery was removed. On one table rested dozens of unfinished clock faces, each without hands.

Mara’s lantern swept across the walls.

Someone had drawn arrows in white chalk.

They pointed toward a stairwell.

The chalk should have dissolved decades ago.

She followed.

At the bottom of the stairs, she found a steel door. Its keyhole was bright, untouched by rust.

Mara removed the brass key from the pouch at her belt.

It turned easily.

The lower chamber was dry.

Water rolled from her suit as she stepped across the threshold. Behind her, the open doorway remained filled with lake water, held back by nothing she could see. It trembled like glass.

Mara removed her helmet.

A ticking filled the chamber.

It came from thousands of clocks.

They covered the walls from floor to ceiling. Clocks made of oak, gold, tin, bone, porcelain, and stone. Watches hung from the ceiling on chains. Pendulums swung in perfect unison.

All showed six o’clock.

At the center stood the bell.

It was smaller than Mara expected, no larger than a washbasin. Green corrosion coated its bronze sides. No rope hung from it. Instead, it formed part of an enormous clock mechanism sunk into the floor.

Iron gears turned beneath glass panels. Each rotation pulled back a hammer by one notch.

The hammer was now five notches from the bell.

“Mara.”

She froze.

Her father stepped out from behind the mechanism.

He looked exactly as he had on the morning he vanished. Brown coat. Muddy boots. Gray beginning at his temples. His face was tired but alive.

Mara could not speak.

He smiled uncertainly. “You grew up.”

She crossed the room and struck him in the chest.

He staggered.

“You left me,” she said.

“I know.”

“You were gone twenty-two years.”

“Not for me.”

“How long?”

He looked at the clocks. “A day. Perhaps two.”

She hit him again, but this time her fist remained pressed to his coat. He covered her hand with his.

“I tried to come back.”

“You left your watch.”

“It stopped when I entered.”

“Why are you here?”

“To keep the mechanism running.”

Mara looked at the gears. “What does it do?”

Her father led her to the glass floor.

Deep below, beneath the gears, the old valley lay in sunlight.

She saw orchards in bloom. Cattle moved through green fields. Smoke curled from chimneys. People walked along the vanished road, unaware of the lake above them.

“That isn’t possible,” Mara whispered.

“No.”

“Are they ghosts?”

“I don’t know.”

A horse-drawn cart passed beneath their feet. Its driver raised a hand to someone beyond view.

Her father pointed to the largest gear. Five teeth were broken.

“The clockworks owner built this before the flood. He thought time was like water—that it could be dammed, diverted, preserved. When the valley was drowned, the mechanism trapped its final morning.”

“And the bell?”

“Keeps the moment sealed. One ring each day resets everything below. Sunrise begins again. The same conversations, the same journeys, the same birds crossing the same sky.”

Mara thought of the boy dripping water onto her floor.

“Eli.”

Her father’s face changed.

“You saw him?”

“He brought me the key.”

“He’s the owner’s son. He knows more than the others. Children sometimes notice repetition. He has been trying to escape for decades.”

“Why did the bell ring twice?”

“The mechanism is failing. Each broken tooth makes the hammer strike again. Tomorrow it will ring five times. Then six. When the final gear breaks, all the years held outside the valley will fall in at once.”

“What will happen?”

“To the people below? They will age eighty years in a second.”

“And the village?”

“The same force may pull your time backward. Your homes, your families—everything built since the flood could vanish.”

Mara stared at the mechanism.

“How do we repair it?”

“We don’t.”

“You repaired clocks.”

“I maintained a prison.”

The words echoed among the ticking.

Her father touched the bell. “I thought keeping it running would protect everyone. Each day I told myself I needed one more day to understand it. But the machine takes years from those outside and gives them here. It took twenty-two from you.”

“Then stop it.”

“I can’t. Someone from outside must choose. The clock doesn’t recognize me anymore.”

Mara moved toward the main lever. It was red, polished by her father’s hand.

A small brass plate bore two words:

RELEASE / RETAIN

“What happens if I release it?”

“The valley returns to time. The lake remains. The people below die as they should have died long ago. The village above continues.”

“And you?”

“I continue too.”

“For how long?”

He did not answer.

She understood.

“No.”

“Mara.”

“No. Come with me.”

“I can’t cross the water. I tried.”

“Then I’ll find another way.”

“The hammer has already moved.”

The gears clanked.

Four notches remained.

All around them, the clocks ticked faster.

Through the glass floor, sunlight flickered. The trapped villagers repeated tiny movements: a woman opened a gate, a man lifted a sack, a child ran after a hoop.

Eli stood in the road.

Unlike the others, he looked up.

He could see her.

He raised one hand and pressed it to the invisible ceiling of his world.

Mara knelt above him.

Her father said, “He is asking you to let the morning end.”

Mara remembered waking every day to the bell. She remembered her father’s empty chair, his untouched tools, and the villagers lowering their voices whenever she entered a room. For years she had wished that one morning could be undone.

But a morning without an evening was not life.

It was only a clock with painted hands.

She stood and gripped the lever.

“Will it hurt?”

“For them? No.”

“For you?”

Her father smiled. “I’ve missed you longer than I knew.”

The hammer moved another notch.

Mara pulled the lever to RELEASE.

The ticking stopped.

For one vast second, every clock in the chamber showed a different time.

Then the hands began to turn.

Some raced forward. Others backward. Pendulums swung wildly. Watch springs snapped with sharp metallic cries.

Below the glass, the valley’s sun crossed the sky.

Day became night, then day again. Seasons rippled over the fields. Leaves burned gold and vanished. Snow covered the road. Houses sagged. Orchards grew wild.

The people were gone.

Eli remained for one moment longer than the rest. He stood beneath falling snow, smiling up at Mara.

Then he vanished too.

The great mechanism split down the center.

Water burst through the doorway.

Her father pushed the diving helmet into her arms.

“Go.”

She tried to hold him, but water struck them apart. The chamber filled with thunder. Clocks tore from the walls and spun through the flood.

Mara forced on the helmet. The air hose pulled tight.

Through the churning water, she saw her father beside the bell. He raised his hand.

Not goodbye, she decided.

Just a wave from one shore to another.

The rope jerked upward. Rusk was pulling.

The factory collapsed beneath her.

For a few terrible seconds she was trapped in black silt. Then she rose through broken beams and clouds of silver bubbles. Her lungs burned. The hose bucked. Somewhere below, the bell began to fall.

It rang once.

The note moved through Mara’s bones.

Then the lake released her.

Rusk hauled her into the boat. The diving helmet rolled against the oars. Mara lay gasping while waves struck the hull.

The mist had disappeared.

On the northern shore, something extraordinary was happening. The lake had withdrawn several yards, exposing a strip of old road. Cracked stones gleamed in the morning sun.

Along the road stood a single milestone.

Rusk helped Mara sit up.

“What happened?”

“I stopped a clock.”

He waited for more.

She had nothing else she could say.

They returned to the village shortly before noon. Everyone crowded around them, asking about the bell, the waves, and the road.

Mayor Cale demanded an official explanation.

Mara told him the truth.

He wrote down “submerged mechanical failure.”

That evening, the villagers stayed awake, waiting for the next morning. Some gathered by the shore. Others sat in kitchens with lamps burning. Mrs. Pell held her husband’s repaired mantel clock on her knees.

Mara waited alone in her workshop.

At five fifty-nine, every clock in the village began to tick.

At six, there was no bell.

No cups trembled. No windows hummed. The lake remained silent.

People looked at one another, uncertain what to do with a morning that had not been announced.

Then Mrs. Pell’s mantel clock chimed.

Another clock answered from across the lane. Then another, and another, until the village filled with mismatched ringing—fast clocks, slow clocks, bright clocks, cracked clocks, each keeping its own imperfect hour.

Mara opened her father’s pocket watch.

It had rested motionless in her drawer for twenty-two years.

The second hand moved.

Once.

Twice.

Then steadily onward.

In the weeks that followed, the exposed road became a place where villagers left flowers. No one agreed on what had happened beneath the lake, but the stories changed. They no longer spoke of a drowned priest or a hungry water spirit. They spoke of a hidden valley that had finally reached evening.

Mara returned to repairing clocks.

She no longer made them all agree.

When Mayor Cale complained that the town hall clock ran thirty seconds behind the station clock, Mara told him, “They have different distances to travel.”

He did not understand, but he stopped complaining.

Years later, when Mara’s hair had begun to gray, a girl brought a broken music box to the workshop. Inside, beneath the cylinder, Mara found the maker’s mark of Arlen Valley Clockworks.

A.V.C.

The girl watched as Mara cleaned the gears.

“Can you fix anything?” she asked.

“No.”

“Most things?”

“Some.”

“What do you do when you can’t fix something?”

Mara considered the question.

Outside, Lake Arlen shone beneath the afternoon sun. Wind moved over its surface, breaking the light into a thousand pieces. There had been no bell for many years.

“You let it change,” she said.

She wound the music box and set it on the table.

For a moment, nothing happened.

Then the cylinder turned, the teeth caught, and a simple melody filled the room. It was not quite in tune. One note came late, and another trembled longer than it should.

Mara listened until the last note faded.

The clock on the wall ticked on, honest and imperfect, carrying them all toward evening.

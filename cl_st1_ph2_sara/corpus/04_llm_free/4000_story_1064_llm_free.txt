### The Map of Unfinished Roads

On the morning the sea returned the bell, Mara Vale was repairing a map no one had asked for.

The map lay across her worktable in four yellowed sections, held flat by river stones. It showed the western coast as it had looked seventy years earlier, before storms ate the headlands, before the railway closed, before the village of Saint Orra lost its church, its harbor, and nearly all its children to larger towns inland.

Mara bent over a tear running through the blue ink of the bay. With a fine brush, she spread paste along the split, pressed the paper together, and covered the wound with a strip of nearly transparent tissue.

Outside, rain tapped the windows of the old post office. The building had not handled a letter in twelve years, but its red sign still hung above the door, faded to the color of dried blood. Mara lived upstairs and worked below among cabinets of charts, deeds, shipping records, and maps abandoned by families who had left Saint Orra.

She liked maps because they admitted, quietly, that the world could be lost.

At nine o’clock someone struck the brass knocker.

Mara waited. People in the village usually entered without knocking. Then came another blow, hard enough to shake dust from the frame.

She opened the door and found Tomas Reed standing in the rain.

He was seventeen, too thin for his coat, with wet black hair pasted across his forehead. In both hands he held a bundle wrapped in fishing net.

“You should come,” he said.

“Where?”

“To the beach.”

“If this is about another dead porpoise—”

“It isn’t.”

“What is it?”

He glanced down at the bundle.

“The church bell.”

Mara looked at him for a moment, then stepped aside.

“Bring it in.”

Together they set the bundle on the sorting table. Tomas pulled away the net, revealing a lump of green-black metal crusted with shells. It was no larger than a bucket, and one side had been cracked open. Mara touched the rim.

Saint Orra’s church had stood on Bell Hill until the winter storm of 1952. During that storm, half the hill collapsed into the sea. The church followed before dawn. No one died, but the building vanished so completely that for years villagers claimed its bell still rang below the waves when a storm approached.

Mara had never believed them.

“This is too small,” she said.

“It has writing.”

Tomas turned the bell carefully. Beneath the salt and corrosion, Mara saw a line of raised letters.

LET THE LOST—

The rest was hidden by a thick white shell.

“Where did you find it?”

“Low tide. Beyond the black rocks.”

“You went beyond the black rocks in this weather?”

“The sea was farther out than I’ve ever seen.”

Mara looked toward the window. Rain slid down the glass in crooked lines. The tide charts on her wall predicted an ordinary low tide at seven thirty. Nothing unusual.

“Show me.”

They crossed the village under one umbrella that the wind repeatedly turned inside out. Saint Orra consisted of thirty-six houses, a closed bakery, a public house open four evenings a week, and a stone pier pointing into an empty harbor. Most roofs shone with rain. Smoke leaned eastward from a few chimneys.

At the bottom of the cliff path, the beach spread before them.

Mara stopped.

The sea had withdrawn almost half a mile.

A vast plain of wet sand and rock lay exposed beneath the gray sky. Channels glimmered between reefs. Tangled weeds covered shapes that had not seen daylight for decades: iron posts, broken walls, the curved ribs of boats. Villagers moved cautiously across the newly uncovered ground, calling to one another.

Beyond them, near the distant waterline, stood the church.

Not the whole church. Its roof and tower were gone, and the seaward wall had fallen. But the foundation remained, along with three pointed windows and an archway filled with darkness.

“My grandfather said the ruins were smashed to pieces,” Tomas said.

“People say many things when there’s nothing left to prove them wrong.”

They descended the path.

Near the ruins, Mara recognized Agnes Bell, the village’s oldest resident, kneeling in the mud. At eighty-nine, Agnes had the narrow, wind-cut face of someone carved from the same stone as the cliffs. She was scraping seaweed from a rectangular slab.

When she saw Mara, she raised one muddy hand.

“Come look.”

It was a memorial stone. Names appeared beneath Agnes’s fingers.

ELIAS WREN  
JOAN WREN  
MATTHEW WREN  
LUCY—

The last name vanished under barnacles.

“The Wren family,” Mara said.

Agnes nodded. “From Gull Cottage.”

“Gull Cottage is inland.”

“Now it is.”

Agnes pointed toward the exposed plain. A line of foundation stones extended from the church toward the sea, suggesting streets, gardens, and houses where no houses were supposed to have been.

Mara had studied every map of Saint Orra in the county archive. According to all of them, the church had stood alone on Bell Hill. The oldest part of the village had always been north of the harbor.

Yet here were the bones of another village.

More people gathered. Someone found a well. Someone else uncovered a rusted cooking pot. Children raced between walls until their parents shouted at them to stop.

Tomas stood inside the broken church, staring at the sand beneath his boots.

“The bell was here,” he called.

Mara joined him. A round hollow marked the place where he had found it. Around the hollow, patterns had been scratched into a flat paving stone.

At first Mara thought they were random grooves made by chains or anchors. Then she knelt.

They were roads.

A coastline curved along one edge. Tiny squares indicated buildings. A compass rose occupied the corner, and at the center, beneath the hollow where the bell had rested, someone had carved the church.

“It’s a map,” Tomas said.

“Yes.”

“Of this place?”

“Some of it.”

She brushed sand from the lower edge. A road ran inland from the church, climbed the cliff, crossed the present village, and continued east into the hills. Beside it was a symbol Mara did not recognize: an open circle crossed by three lines.

Agnes lowered herself beside them.

“I know that mark,” she said.

“From where?”

“Your father’s notebook.”

Mara’s hand became still.

“My father drew coastlines.”

“He drew whatever he was looking for.”

Mara rose too quickly and felt the ruined walls tilt around her.

Daniel Vale had disappeared when she was twelve. He had left home one autumn afternoon carrying a compass, a canvas bag, and a notebook. Three days later, his bag was found near the eastern marsh. The compass lay inside, but the notebook was gone.

People said he had drowned. Mara’s mother said he had become confused in the fog. Agnes, who had joined the search, said nothing at all.

“What notebook?” Tomas asked.

Mara ignored him.

“Why didn’t you tell me?” she asked Agnes.

“I promised him.”

“He’s been dead for twenty-four years.”

“I promised while he was alive. Those are the promises that matter.”

The wind dropped.

Across the exposed seabed, villagers fell silent one by one. The rain continued, but now it fell straight down. From far beyond the ruins came a low rushing sound.

The tide was turning.

They hurried toward shore. By the time Mara reached the cliff path, seawater was already filling the channels. The drowned roads vanished quickly, then the walls, then the memorial stones. The broken church remained above water for a few final minutes, black against the silver tide.

As the waves closed over the archway, Mara thought she heard a bell ring once.

That evening Agnes came to the post office carrying a biscuit tin.

Mara let her in without speaking.

They sat at the sorting table. Between them lay the cleaned bell. More of its inscription was visible now:

LET THE LOST BE LED HOME.

Agnes placed the tin beside it.

“I should have given you this years ago.”

Inside was a notebook wrapped in oilcloth.

Mara knew it before she touched it. Her father’s name was written on the cover in his blocky hand. Below it he had drawn the symbol from the church floor: an open circle crossed by three lines.

She opened to the first page.

The notebook contained sketches of the coast, records of tides, and copied fragments from parish documents. Her father’s handwriting crowded the margins. Certain words appeared repeatedly: second village, king tide, pilgrim road, the hollow mile.

Near the middle she found a map. It showed a road beginning at the drowned church, passing through Saint Orra, and ending in the hills at the open-circle symbol.

“What was he searching for?” Mara asked.

“A road.”

“Roads don’t disappear.”

Agnes gave her a tired look. “You repair old maps for a living. You know better than that.”

Mara turned another page.

According to her father’s notes, the settlement uncovered by the tide had been abandoned long before the church fell. A landslide in 1811 buried part of the village and diverted the river. Survivors rebuilt on higher ground. Within a generation, the old streets had been erased from official maps.

But local records mentioned something stranger: a ceremonial road used to carry the names of the dead from the church to a place called the House of Returning. There, the names were preserved so no one from Saint Orra would be forgotten.

In 1811, the House of Returning vanished too.

“Your father believed it wasn’t a house,” Agnes said. “He thought it was a room cut into the hill. An archive.”

“Why?”

“Because names kept disappearing.”

Mara looked up.

Agnes folded her hands.

“After the landslide, some families were left out of the new parish register. Poor families. Travelers. People no one thought important. Daniel found references to them in letters, but not in any official history. He said forgetting them was a second death.”

“That sounds like him.”

“He believed the old archive might still exist. The exposed church gave him the beginning of the route, but the last part was missing.”

“And he went anyway.”

“Yes.”

“Alone?”

Agnes did not answer.

Mara closed the notebook.

“You went with him.”

“To the marsh. The fog came down. He told me to wait by the standing stone while he searched ahead.”

“And you let him?”

“I followed. I lost sight of him for perhaps ten minutes. Then I found his bag beside a pool. There were footprints leading in, but none leading out.”

“Why did you keep this?”

“He had handed it to me before he went ahead. He said the fog would ruin the pages.”

For years Mara had imagined her father’s last hours: cold water, darkness, panic. Now another possibility opened before her, no kinder than the first. Perhaps he had found the road. Perhaps he had fallen into some hidden shaft. Perhaps his body still waited inside a hill that everyone in Saint Orra passed without noticing.

“Tomorrow,” Mara said, “I’m going to the marsh.”

Agnes shook her head. “The route begins at the church.”

“The church is underwater.”

“The tide will go out again.”

But the next morning the sea behaved normally. It covered the black rocks and filled the harbor. Fishermen checked distant buoys and reported nothing strange. Officials from the county came to inspect the bell and asked where it had been found. Tomas, after looking at Mara, pointed to a less dangerous section of beach.

For five days, Mara studied her father’s notebook.

She compared his sketches to modern surveys and repaired the old coastal map section by section. The map’s tears passed through roads, hills, and fields. As she joined the fragments, she discovered faint marks hidden beneath old stains—marks matching the pilgrim road.

The road had not run directly east. It curved north first, following the buried river.

On the sixth night, a storm arrived.

Wind hammered the post office. Rain poured beneath the door. Mara woke shortly before dawn to the sound of the bell ringing downstairs.

She found Tomas in the workroom, soaked and breathless, holding the bell’s broken clapper in one hand.

“I didn’t touch it,” he said.

The bell rang again.

Its cracked voice was soft but unmistakable.

On Mara’s table, the repaired map trembled. Water dripped from the bell and ran across the paper, following the faded pilgrim road.

“Look outside,” Tomas whispered.

The sea had withdrawn.

They left at once.

No villagers wandered the exposed seabed this time. Windows glowed along the cliff, but doors remained shut against the storm. Mara and Tomas crossed the ruins alone, carrying lanterns.

Inside the church, water swirled around their boots. Mara set the bell into the hollow at the center of the carved map. It fit perfectly.

The bell rang.

A line on the paving stone filled with pale water. It flowed inland, tracing the road.

They followed.

The route climbed from the seabed to a narrow crack in the cliff. Behind a curtain of ivy, worn steps led upward. They emerged near the abandoned quarry, crossed fields, and entered the eastern hills as daylight seeped into the clouds.

At every doubtful turning, they found the open-circle symbol carved into stone.

By noon they reached the marsh.

Fog lay over the reeds.

“You can stay here,” Mara told Tomas.

“So can you.”

“I’m not going back.”

“Neither am I.”

The path became a chain of half-submerged stones. Mara followed her father’s instructions until they reached the standing stone Agnes had described. Beyond it, the notebook showed only a blank page.

Mara took out the bell.

It rang once, though no hand moved it.

From somewhere in the fog came an answer.

Not a bell. A voice.

“Mara?”

She stopped breathing.

Tomas raised the lantern.

A figure stood twenty paces ahead, barely visible. A man in a brown coat held one arm across his face as if shielding himself from rain.

“Mara,” he called again. “Where are you?”

Her father’s voice had not aged.

She stepped off the stones.

Tomas seized her sleeve. “Wait.”

The figure turned and walked deeper into the fog.

Mara wanted to follow. Every part of her that was still twelve years old strained toward him. But the mapmaker in her noticed what grief did not.

The reeds moved through his body.

“It isn’t him,” she said.

The figure stopped.

Then, in her father’s voice, it said, “You took so long.”

The marsh shuddered. Mud split between the stepping-stones, revealing a narrow stone stair descending underground.

The figure vanished.

Mara and Tomas entered.

The stairs ended at a wooden door swollen with damp. The symbol was carved above it. Mara pushed, and the door opened inward with a sigh.

Beyond lay a long room cut into the hill.

Shelves lined the walls. On them stood ledgers, boxes, rolled maps, bundles of letters, and hundreds of wooden tablets marked with names. Roots hung from the ceiling. Water covered the floor to Mara’s ankles.

At the center of the room sat a table.

Her father’s compass rested on it.

Beside the compass lay a human skeleton in the remains of a brown coat.

Mara did not cry at first. She knelt in the water and touched the rusted buckle at his wrist. She remembered it shining when he lifted her onto his shoulders. She remembered his hands smelling of pencil shavings and rain.

Tomas moved quietly around the room, examining the shelves.

“He found it,” he said.

“Yes.”

On the table, beneath the compass, was one final page.

Mara unfolded it.

The writing was faint.

If anyone finds this, take the names. The lower passage has collapsed, and the water is rising. There are records here older than the village. They were hidden after the landslide by those who survived. No treasure except memory, which may be the same thing.

Below that, squeezed into the remaining space, he had written:

Mara, unfinished roads are not dead ends. They wait for another hand.

A rumble passed through the chamber.

Water burst from a crack in the wall.

Tomas grabbed the nearest ledger. “We can’t carry all of this.”

Mara looked at the endless shelves. Her father had died trying to preserve them. For one wild moment she imagined doing the same—remaining until the room filled, guarding forgotten names beneath the hill.

Then the bell rang inside her bag.

Let the lost be led home.

“Take the oldest ledger,” she said. “And the tablets from the top shelf.”

“What about the rest?”

“We come back with help.”

They wrapped the records in oilcloth, lifted her father’s remains in the brown coat, and climbed the stairs as water roared behind them. The fog outside had begun to thin.

When they reached the standing stone, Agnes was waiting.

She saw the bundle in Mara’s arms and bowed her head.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

Mara was too tired for anger.

“You’ll help me bring the archive out.”

“If the hill allows it.”

“The hill doesn’t decide.”

Agnes almost smiled. “You are Daniel’s daughter.”

“No,” Mara said. “I’m the village cartographer.”

Behind them, the fog lifted from a landscape none of them recognized.

Lines of stone crossed the marsh, revealing the old pilgrim road. Foundations rose among the reeds. The buried river shone like a silver thread. For a few minutes the past and present occupied the same ground, neither willing to surrender.

Then sunlight touched the hills, and the vision faded.

They carried Daniel Vale home.

In the weeks that followed, Saint Orra changed.

The county sent archaeologists, engineers, archivists, and reporters. Pumps emptied the House of Returning. The records were removed, dried, and cataloged in the old post office, which became crowded for the first time in decades.

Villagers came to search the ledgers.

Agnes found the names of two sisters her grandmother used to mention but whose existence no official had believed. The owner of the public house discovered that his building stood over the original school. Tomas found the name Reed on a wooden tablet dated 1742.

Mara found hundreds of people omitted from every map and history she had known.

She added them.

All winter, she worked on a new map of Saint Orra. It showed the modern village, the drowned settlement, the buried river, the pilgrim road, and the House of Returning. In the margin, she printed the names recovered from the archive in letters small but clear.

She also marked the place where her father had been found.

Not as a grave.

As the end of a road.

The bell was placed above the post office door. No one could make it ring with a rope, and experts said the crack had made it useless. Yet on certain mornings, usually when fog covered the harbor, its quiet voice traveled through the village.

Years later, children would ask Mara whether it was true that the sea had once gone out and revealed an entire forgotten town.

She always told them yes.

They would ask if a ghost had shown her the hidden archive.

To that, she gave different answers depending on the child.

But when they asked why old maps mattered, she led them into the post office. She showed them the repaired charts under glass, the ledgers saved from the hill, and the wall where the names of the forgotten had been written.

“A map is not a picture of a place,” she would say. “It is a promise that the place has been seen.”

Then she would point to the road that crossed the village, disappeared beneath the marsh, and continued in a finer line beyond the edge of the paper.

“And what’s there?” the children often asked.

Mara would look toward the window, where the sea breathed against the cliffs.

“I don’t know yet,” she said.

Outside, above the red door of the old post office, the broken bell waited in the wind.

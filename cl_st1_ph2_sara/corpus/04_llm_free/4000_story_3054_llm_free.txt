# The Keeper of Lost Hours

On the oldest street in Bellweather, between a bakery that opened before dawn and a tailor who never finished anything on time, stood a narrow shop with a blue door.

Above the door hung a wooden sign:

**ELIAS VALE  
CLOCKS MENDED  
LOST HOURS RESTORED**

Most people assumed the final line was a joke.

Elias Vale did not correct them.

Every morning, precisely at eight, he unlocked the shop and turned the sign from CLOSED to OPEN. Then he wound the clocks. There were hundreds: brass carriage clocks, painted cuckoo clocks, silver watches, ship chronometers, kitchen clocks shaped like fruit, and tall grandfather clocks whose pendulums swept through the dimness like patient scythes.

At first, their ticking sounded chaotic. After a while, however, a careful listener could hear that every clock kept the same rhythm. Some moved faster, some slower, but all of them seemed to breathe together.

Elias was small, gray-haired, and so quiet that customers often forgot he was in the room. He repaired cracked springs, worn gears, bent hands, and broken chimes. Yet beneath his workbench, behind a locked panel, he kept the true business of the shop.

There, in a drawer lined with black velvet, lay twenty-three glass vials.

Each held an hour.

The hours shimmered like captured weather. One glowed with the gold of late afternoon. Another contained the dark blue before sunrise. A third flashed with summer lightning. They had been misplaced, wasted, stolen, abandoned, or forgotten, and each still carried the possibility of what might have happened within it.

Elias had learned long ago that people lost time carelessly.

They lost hours waiting for apologies that never came. They lost them in hospital corridors and railway stations, in arguments, in fear, in sleep. Most lost hours could not be recovered. Once lived, time hardened into memory.

But occasionally an hour went unlived.

Those hours wandered.

They slipped behind mirrors, collected beneath floorboards, or hid in clocks that had stopped at important moments. Elias could draw them out, bottle them, and keep them until someone arrived who truly needed one.

He had strict rules.

A lost hour could not undo death.

It could not create love.

It could not be used for wealth.

And it could never return a person to a past they were unwilling to leave.

For forty-two years, Elias had obeyed these rules.

Then, on a rainy Tuesday in November, a girl named Mara Quinn entered the shop carrying a clock that had no hands.

She was perhaps seventeen, with wet black hair and an oversized green coat. The clock she placed on the counter was small and round, made of pale wood. Its face bore twelve faded numbers, but the center was empty.

“Can you fix it?” she asked.

Elias adjusted his spectacles. “That depends.”

“On what?”

“On why it has no hands.”

Mara glanced toward the crowded walls. “Clocks don’t have reasons.”

“Everything broken has a reason.”

“It belonged to my mother.”

Elias looked again at the clock. Along its rim, almost hidden beneath scratches, were carved tiny birds in flight.

“Did she remove the hands?”

“No. They were gone when I found it.”

“And where did you find it?”

“In the attic, after she disappeared.”

At that word, every clock in the shop missed a beat.

The silence lasted less than a second, but Elias heard it.

“When?” he asked.

“Eleven years ago.”

Mara’s fingers tightened around the edge of the counter. “The police said she left us. My father said there had been an accident. Then he stopped talking about it. He died last month, and I found this clock in a box with her name on it.”

Elias turned the clock over. On the back, written in faded ink, was a date.

November 17.

Eleven years earlier.

The current date was November 16.

Elias felt suddenly cold.

“Come back tomorrow,” he said.

“Will it be fixed?”

“Come back tomorrow at four.”

She frowned. “Why four?”

But Elias had already taken the clock into his hands. Something stirred inside it, faint as breath against glass.

“Because,” he said, “that is when it stopped.”

After Mara left, Elias locked the door and drew the blinds, although it was not yet noon.

He carried the clock into the back room. There, surrounded by shelves of tools and ledgers, he placed it beneath a lamp.

The clock had no mechanism.

No gears. No spring. No balance wheel.

Only a hollow space and a thin circle of ash.

Elias touched the ash with one finger. Instantly, he saw a woman running through rain. She held the clock under her coat. Behind her rose the dark outline of Bellweather’s railway station, abandoned for more than a decade. A bell rang. The woman turned, frightened, and vanished behind a white burst of light.

The vision ended.

Elias sat motionless.

He knew the woman.

Her name had been Celia Quinn, and eleven years ago she had come to his shop asking to purchase a lost hour.

“My daughter is sick,” she had told him. “The doctors say she won’t survive the night.”

Elias had refused. A lost hour could not undo death.

But Celia had insisted the girl was not dead yet. An hour, she argued, might be enough for the fever to break. Enough for medicine to work. Enough for morning to come.

Elias remembered her face: exhausted, desperate, full of a hope so fierce it had almost seemed like anger.

He had given her an hour.

Not sold it. Given it.

A summer hour, warm and bright, found years earlier inside a stopped sundial. Celia had taken the vial and asked how to release it.

“Break the glass beside her,” Elias had said. “But understand this: the hour must come from somewhere. Time always balances its accounts.”

“Take it from me.”

“It does not work that way.”

“Then how?”

“The hour will choose.”

Celia had run from the shop before he could warn her further.

The next morning, Elias heard that six-year-old Mara Quinn had survived.

He also heard that Celia had disappeared.

For eleven years, he had told himself the hour had taken her as payment. He had told himself there had been no way to know, no way to help.

Now her clock lay open before him, filled with ash from a moment that had never properly ended.

Elias unlocked the panel beneath his bench and examined the twenty-three vials. Near the back was one he had never been able to identify. Its light was white and sharp, and whenever he touched it, he heard distant rain.

A label circled its neck.

**FOUND AT BELLWEATHER STATION  
NOVEMBER 17, ELEVEN YEARS AGO  
4:00 P.M.**

Elias closed his eyes.

The hour had not taken Celia as payment.

It had taken her by mistake.

And he had kept her imprisoned ever since.

At half past three the following afternoon, rain began to fall.

Mara arrived early. Water shone on her green coat, and she carried a red umbrella with one broken rib.

Elias had placed the handless clock on the counter. Beside it stood the vial of white light.

Mara stared at it. “What is that?”

“The truth,” he said.

“That’s not an answer.”

“No. But it may be the only one I can give you.”

He told her about lost hours.

At first, she laughed. Then she became angry. Finally, she became very still.

“You expect me to believe my mother has been trapped inside a bottle for eleven years?”

“Not inside the bottle. Inside the hour.”

“That makes no sense.”

“Time rarely does when examined closely.”

She snatched the vial from the counter. The light inside flared, illuminating her face from below. Within it, rain moved sideways. A tiny station bell swung soundlessly.

Mara nearly dropped it.

“How do I get her out?”

“We must return the hour to the place where it was lost.”

“The station?”

“Yes.”

“Then let’s go.”

Elias did not move.

Mara looked at him. “What aren’t you telling me?”

“The hour has remained outside time for eleven years. Restoring it will require a clock capable of holding the moment open.”

She glanced at her mother’s clock.

“It has no hands.”

“It needs hands freely given.”

“Use one from another clock.”

“Not those kinds of hands.”

Understanding came slowly. “You mean human hands?”

“The clock needs someone to hold the hour in place while it unfolds.”

“And what happens to that person?”

“They will remain in the lost hour until it closes.”

“For how long?”

“I don’t know.”

Mara placed the vial on the counter with great care.

“You did this,” she said.

Elias lowered his eyes. “Yes.”

“Then you’ll hold it.”

“Yes.”

The answer surprised her. Some of the anger left her face, though not much.

At ten minutes to four, they reached the abandoned station.

Bellweather Station had once connected the town to the coast. Now weeds grew between the rails, windows stared blindly from soot-blackened walls, and the great clock above the entrance had been frozen at four for eleven years.

Elias carried the wooden clock.

Mara carried the vial.

Rain drummed against the platform roof. Their footsteps stirred dust in the waiting room, where old timetables still hung behind cracked glass.

“This is the place,” Elias said.

“How can you tell?”

Every dead clock in the station began to tick.

There were seven of them: the great clock outside, two platform clocks, one in the ticket office, a brass clock above the fireplace, and two small clocks left among the rubbish. Their hands remained fixed, but the sound grew louder.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Elias set the handless clock on the floor beneath the station bell. He opened his tool case and removed a piece of white chalk.

Around the clock, he drew a circle marked with twelve numbers.

“What happens when you break the vial?” Mara asked.

“The missing hour will begin again.”

“And my mother will appear?”

“If I am right.”

“If you’re wrong?”

“The hour may collapse.”

“What does that mean?”

Elias looked at the old station walls. “It will try to recover the eleven years it lost.”

Mara understood. “It’ll take them from us.”

“Perhaps from the whole town.”

Outside, the great clock shuddered.

Its minute hand moved backward.

3:59.

“We should hurry,” Elias said.

He knelt and placed both hands over the empty center of Celia’s clock.

At once, pain shot through his wrists. Thin lines of golden light curled around his fingers, binding them to the wood.

Mara crouched opposite him, holding the vial.

“On my word, break it inside the circle.”

Her face was pale. “Will I see her?”

“I hope so.”

“Can I bring her back?”

“That depends on whether she chooses now or then.”

“What does that mean?”

The station bell rang.

The vial cracked in Mara’s hand.

White light spilled through her fingers.

“Now!” Elias shouted.

She smashed the glass against the floor.

The lost hour exploded.

Rain froze beyond the broken windows, each drop suspended in the air. Dust rose and became stars. The station walls straightened, paint spreading over them in ripples. Weeds sank into the ground. Benches repaired themselves. Lamps flared to life.

People appeared everywhere.

Travelers stepped backward through doors. Porters wheeled vanished luggage. A newspaper unfolded itself on a bench, its date November 17, eleven years earlier.

Mara stared.

Across the waiting room stood Celia Quinn.

She was exactly as Elias remembered her: thirty-five, dark-haired, breathless, with rain on her face. She clutched the wooden clock, though another version of it lay beneath Elias’s hands.

Celia looked around wildly.

“Mum?” Mara whispered.

Celia turned.

For a moment, neither moved.

Then Mara crossed the room and threw her arms around her.

Celia staggered. Her face filled first with confusion, then wonder. She touched Mara’s hair, cheeks, and shoulders.

“Mara?”

“I’m here.”

“You were six.”

“I know.”

“You were ill.”

“I got better.”

Celia began to cry. “It worked.”

Mara held her more tightly. “You left.”

“No. I was coming back. The hour opened here and—”

The station darkened.

Above them, the clocks began racing. Their hands spun so quickly they blurred.

The lost hour was collapsing.

Elias felt the wooden clock pull at him. The golden lines tightened around his arms, turning his skin translucent. Beneath it, he could see his bones and, within them, countless tiny gears.

“Mara!” he called. “She must choose!”

Mara drew back from her mother.

“Come with me,” she said. “Please.”

Celia looked toward Elias. She seemed to understand at once.

“What will happen to him?”

“He stays,” Mara said.

“For how long?”

“No one knows.”

Elias forced himself to speak. “There is no time.”

The ceiling flickered between ruin and restoration. Travelers vanished and reappeared. Outside, rain fell upward.

Celia took Mara’s face between her hands.

“I need you to listen.”

“No.”

“Mara.”

“I didn’t get to say goodbye. Dad never told me anything. I thought you didn’t want me.”

“I wanted you more than every hour I had.”

“Then come back.”

Celia looked at her daughter with unbearable tenderness. “I can’t.”

“You can choose.”

“Yes,” Celia said. “But so can you.”

The great station clock cracked.

A black line spread across its face, and through the crack came a wind that smelled of old rooms, wilted flowers, and winters already passed.

Elias groaned. The clock beneath his hands was growing hot.

Celia turned toward him. “The hour took me because I offered my life.”

“You did not know the cost,” he said.

“I knew enough.”

“I should have stopped you.”

“You gave my child a chance.”

“I gave her stolen time.”

“All time is stolen from something.”

The crack widened.

Beyond it was not darkness but years: birthdays, funerals, meals, quiet mornings, and nights spent awake. Mara saw herself learning to ride a bicycle without her mother watching. She saw her father sitting alone at the kitchen table. She saw school concerts, slammed doors, unfinished letters, and the hospital room where she had held her father’s hand as he died.

The missing years pulled at Celia.

Mara seized her wrist.

“No!”

Celia kissed her forehead.

“You survived,” she whispered. “That was all I asked of the world.”

Then she pulled free.

But instead of stepping into the past, she moved toward Elias.

Before he could stop her, Celia placed her hands over his.

The golden light leaped from his fingers to hers.

“What are you doing?” he cried.

“Balancing the account.”

The wooden clock gained two slender hands.

They were not made of metal. They were made of light.

Both pointed to four.

Celia looked at Elias. “You kept the hour safe for eleven years. Let me close it.”

“You’ll be trapped.”

“No,” she said. “I’ll be finished.”

Mara shook her head, sobbing. “Please.”

Celia smiled at her.

It was not the smile of someone unafraid. It was the smile of someone who had found one thing greater than fear.

“You have already lived eleven years I never expected to see,” she said. “That is not a loss. That is a gift.”

The clock chimed.

Once.

The station folded inward.

Travelers became rain. Lamps became sparks. Walls crumbled into dust and rose again as ruins. The spinning clocks stopped one by one.

Celia was the last to fade.

For one heartbeat she stood in the ruined waiting room, surrounded by white light, her hand raised in farewell.

Then the hour closed.

Silence followed.

Mara knelt on the dusty floor, holding the little wooden clock. It now had two black hands fixed at four.

Elias sat beside her. His own hands were scarred with thin golden lines, but they were free.

Outside, rain fell naturally again.

After a long while, Mara asked, “Is she gone?”

“Yes.”

“Can you find her hour again?”

“No.”

“Would you, if you could?”

Elias considered lying.

“No,” he said at last. “Some doors become cruel if opened twice.”

Mara wiped her face with her sleeve. “I hate that answer.”

“So do I.”

They left the station at dusk.

The next morning, Elias opened his shop at eight, as always. He wound the clocks, swept the floor, and placed his tools in order.

At nine, Mara entered carrying two cups of coffee.

“I don’t drink coffee,” Elias said.

“You do now.”

She set one beside him and removed her coat. Under her arm was a thick book about clock repair.

“What is that?”

“You need an apprentice.”

“I have worked alone for forty-two years.”

“Exactly. You’ve had enough practice.”

Elias looked at her. “Repairing clocks requires patience.”

“I waited eleven years for an answer.”

“It requires precision.”

“I found the right shop, didn’t I?”

“It requires respect for rules.”

Mara raised an eyebrow.

Elias sighed. “That may be a difficulty.”

She smiled for the first time.

Years passed.

The people of Bellweather grew accustomed to seeing Mara in the blue-doored shop. At first she swept floors and polished cases. Then she learned to replace springs, cut gears, and listen for faults too small for ordinary ears. Eventually Elias showed her the hidden drawer and the vials inside.

Together they returned a blue winter hour to a sailor who had missed his father’s final story. They refused a rich man who wanted another hour to change a business deal. They gave a quiet hour before dawn to a woman who needed time to forgive her sister.

Mara never asked to recover her mother again.

But each year, on November 17, she closed the shop at four and went to the abandoned station. She brought the wooden clock and sat beneath the silent bell until sunset.

Sometimes Elias joined her.

Sometimes he did not.

On the seventh anniversary of the restored hour, Mara returned to the shop after dark and found Elias asleep at his bench. In front of him lay an unfinished letter and his old silver pocket watch.

The watch had stopped.

Mara touched his shoulder and knew.

On the desk beside him stood a new glass vial, glowing with the gentle amber light of evening. Around its neck was a label in Elias’s careful handwriting:

**ONE HOUR, FREELY GIVEN  
FOR WHEN THE KEEPER NEEDS TO REST**

Mara wept.

Then she opened the hidden drawer and placed the vial inside.

She did not use it.

Some hours, she had learned, were gifts because they could be returned. Others were gifts because they could be released.

The following morning, the blue door opened at eight.

Mara wound the clocks. Their hundreds of voices gathered around her, uneven at first, then slowly joining in one great rhythm.

On the counter stood the wooden clock with birds carved around its rim. For the first time in eighteen years, its hands moved.

At noon, a boy entered carrying a broken watch.

“Can you fix it?” he asked.

Mara examined the watch.

“That depends.”

“On what?”

“On why it broke.”

The boy frowned. “Watches don’t have reasons.”

Mara looked toward the hidden drawer, where lost hours shone in the dark like distant windows.

Then she smiled.

“Everything broken has a reason,” she said.

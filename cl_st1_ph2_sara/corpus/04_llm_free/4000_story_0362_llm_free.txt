## The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, before the city shook itself awake, Mara Venn unlocked the narrow green door beneath the western clock tower and climbed one hundred and thirteen steps to the room where lost hours were kept.

The room was circular, lined with shelves, and warmed by the slow heartbeat of machinery. Brass gears turned behind the walls. Chains descended through holes in the floor. Above, the four great clock faces admitted a pale, fractured light.

On the shelves stood hundreds of glass jars.

Each contained an hour someone had lost.

Some hours glowed gold: afternoons wasted beside rivers, childhood mornings spent building castles from blankets, evenings when two people almost confessed their love. Others were gray and restless: delays in hospitals, sleepless vigils, arguments repeated long after everyone had forgotten how they began.

Mara’s task was simple. She dusted the jars, checked their labels, and ensured that no hour escaped.

Her predecessor, Mr. Pell, had explained the work on her first day.

“People say they lose time,” he had told her, tapping a jar in which a summer storm flashed again and again. “They think it’s only a manner of speaking. It isn’t.”

“What happens if an hour gets out?”

“It goes looking for the person who lost it.”

“That doesn’t sound so terrible.”

Mr. Pell had looked at her for a long moment.

“No,” he said. “Not until it finds them.”

He retired three weeks later and disappeared to a village on the coast. Mara had worked alone ever since.

For eleven years, nothing went wrong.

Then, on a cold morning in November, she found a jar cracked open on the floor.

The glass lay in a glittering circle beneath shelf seven. The label, yellow with age, had been written in Mr. Pell’s narrow hand:

**ELIAS VENN  
14 OCTOBER, 4:00–5:00 P.M.  
UNFINISHED**

Mara stopped breathing.

Elias Venn had been her father.

He had vanished when she was eight years old.

The hour inside the jar was gone.

At first she thought she had made a mistake. There were thousands of labels, and Venn was not an uncommon name. Yet she knew her father’s full name. She knew the date too. The fourteenth of October had been the day he disappeared.

Mara knelt beside the broken glass.

“Where did you go?” she whispered.

Behind her, the clock struck seven.

The blow thundered through the tower. Gears engaged. The walls trembled.

Then a voice answered from somewhere below.

“Mara?”

She sprang to her feet.

The voice was faint but unmistakable. She had not heard it for twenty-eight years, yet recognition moved through her faster than thought.

Her father had always spoken her name as though it were a question he was glad to ask.

“Mara?”

She ran.

Down one hundred and thirteen steps, around the winding iron staircase, past the pendulum chamber and the maintenance floor. At the bottom, she flung open the green door.

The street outside was empty.

Dawn crouched between the buildings. Rain had darkened the cobbles, and the lamps still burned beneath their glass hoods. A tram groaned two streets away.

“Papa?”

Only the rain answered.

Then she noticed the footprints.

They began in front of the tower door: wet impressions of a man’s boots. They led across the pavement toward the old quarter.

Mara locked the door and followed.

The footprints turned down Lantern Street, where bakers were raising their shutters. They passed the fountain of Saint Orra, crossed the square, and stopped before a blue house squeezed between a bookshop and a tailor’s.

Mara knew the house.

She had lived there as a child.

It seemed smaller now. The iron fence was rusted, and ivy concealed half the windows. A brass knocker shaped like a fox still hung on the door.

The final pair of footprints pointed toward it.

Mara lifted the knocker.

Before she could strike, the door opened.

A boy stood inside.

He was perhaps nine years old, with black hair falling over one eye. He wore an oversized red sweater and held a wooden sword.

“You’re early,” he said.

“For what?”

The boy shrugged. “Whatever you’re here for.”

A woman called from deeper in the house. “Tomas, who is it?”

“I don’t know.”

The woman appeared, wiping her hands on an apron. She was young, tired, and suspicious.

Mara introduced herself and apologized. “I used to live here. A long time ago.”

The woman’s expression softened. “We’re renting. The owner died last spring.”

“I won’t trouble you. I only wondered whether you saw a man come inside.”

“No.”

“He would be around sixty. Tall, with gray eyes.”

The woman shook her head.

But Tomas said, “I saw him.”

His mother frowned. “When?”

“Just now. He went upstairs.”

A silence entered the hallway.

The woman turned toward the staircase. “There’s no one upstairs.”

“I saw him,” Tomas insisted. “He was wet, but the floor stayed dry.”

Mara felt the air grow cold.

“May I look?” she asked.

The woman hesitated, then stepped aside.

The staircase creaked exactly as Mara remembered. The wallpaper had changed, but not the smell of the house: dust, apples, and the faint mineral odor of old pipes.

At the top, she turned toward what had once been her bedroom.

The door stood open.

Inside, a man sat on the floor beside the window.

He was not sixty.

He was thirty-four, the age her father had been when he vanished. His dark hair was rain-soaked. His brown coat was buttoned crookedly, and there was a small scar beside his left eyebrow.

Mara had inherited that scar’s shape in the form of a pale birthmark.

He looked up.

For a moment neither spoke.

Then Elias Venn smiled.

“There you are,” he said. “I’ve been looking everywhere.”

Mara gripped the doorframe.

He looked exactly as he had on the final afternoon: worried, hurried, alive.

“You’re not real,” she said.

His smile faded. “That’s a cruel thing to say.”

“What year is it?”

He glanced toward the window. “What?”

“What year?”

“Mara, we don’t have time for games.”

“What year?”

He answered.

It was the year of his disappearance.

Below, the woman called, “Is someone there?”

Mara shut the bedroom door.

Elias stood. “You’ve grown.”

She almost laughed, but the sound broke before it emerged.

“You’ve been gone twenty-eight years.”

“No.” He shook his head. “No, I left you downstairs ten minutes ago.”

“I’m thirty-six.”

“That isn’t possible.”

“You vanished on the fourteenth of October. They searched the river. They searched the station. Grandmother said you had abandoned me.”

“I would never abandon you.”

“I waited at the window every night for three years.”

He reached for her, but she stepped back.

Pain crossed his face.

“What happened?” Mara asked.

Elias looked around the room as if the answer might be hidden in its corners.

“I came upstairs because I heard the clock.”

“What clock?”

“The western tower. It struck four, but it kept striking. Thirteen, fourteen, fifteen times. Then a door appeared behind your wardrobe.”

Mara remembered the wardrobe: enormous, walnut, impossible to move. She had hidden inside it during thunderstorms.

“I opened the door,” he continued. “There was a staircase. I thought someone was playing a trick. At the bottom, I found a room full of jars.”

Her skin prickled.

“And a man?” she asked. “Thin, with white hair?”

“Yes. He called himself Pell.”

Mara turned toward the window. The clock tower rose above the roofs, its copper dome green against the clouds.

“What did he do?”

“Nothing. He seemed frightened. He told me I’d entered an hour that didn’t belong to me. Then the room began to shake. A jar fell from a shelf, and—”

Elias stopped.

“And?”

“And now I’m here.”

A knock sounded at the bedroom door.

“Everything all right?” asked the woman.

“Yes,” Mara replied. “I’m sorry. I’ll be down in a moment.”

When she turned back, Elias was fading.

The wallpaper showed through his coat.

He stared at his hands. “Mara?”

She reached for him, but her fingers met cold air. His outline flickered.

“Go to the clock tower,” she said. “Find the green door.”

“I don’t understand.”

“Go!”

He vanished.

The room remained empty except for a drop of rain on the floor.

Mara ran back to the tower.

The city had awakened. Carts and bicycles filled the streets. Office workers hurried beneath umbrellas, and vendors shouted the prices of oranges. Mara pushed through them, terrified that the escaped hour would dissolve among all those ordinary minutes.

At the tower, the green door was unlocked.

She was certain she had locked it.

Inside, wet footprints spiraled up the stairs.

Mara climbed after them.

She found her father in the jar room, walking slowly along the shelves.

Sunlight passed through him.

“So many,” he whispered.

“Every jar is an hour someone lost.”

He examined a vessel filled with falling snow. “Am I dead?”

“I don’t know.”

“Are you the keeper?”

“Yes.”

“Then you must know.”

“I dust shelves. I repair seals. No one explained how any of this began.”

“Pell knew.”

“Pell told me only what he thought I needed.”

Elias smiled sadly. “That sounds like him.”

“You knew him for less than an hour.”

“An hour can be enough.”

A warning bell rang inside the wall.

Mara looked up. Above shelf seven, a small brass lever had begun to move. Black symbols turned on a wheel: the tower’s oldest alarm, one she had never seen activated.

“What does that mean?” Elias asked.

Mara crossed the room and opened the keeper’s manual on its iron lectern. The book was thick, handwritten by generations of keepers. She turned through brittle pages until she found the symbol now displayed on the wall.

The entry consisted of three sentences:

**An unfinished hour seeks completion.  
At the final stroke, it will take back all the years that followed it.  
Return it sealed, or finish what was begun.**

Mara read the lines twice.

“What does ‘take back all the years’ mean?” Elias asked.

“I think the hour is trying to correct itself. If it does, everything after your disappearance may be erased.”

“Including you?”

“Perhaps this version of me.”

The tower clock clicked toward eight.

They had less than an hour.

Elias sat at the worktable. His translucence had worsened; now the shelves were visible through his chest.

“What was I doing before I opened the door?” he murmured.

“You said you heard the clock.”

“No. Before that.”

Mara tried to remember the final day.

She had spent years rebuilding it from fragments. Rain against the windows. Her father in his brown coat. A cup broken in the kitchen. A train ticket on the table. Her grandmother arriving after dark.

“You were packing,” she said.

He looked at her sharply.

“There was a suitcase in the hall.”

Memory moved across his face.

“Yes.”

“You were leaving.”

Elias lowered his eyes.

“So Grandmother was right.”

“No. Not about you.”

“Then what?”

He stood and paced, gathering the remnants of an afternoon nearly three decades old.

“I had been offered work in Bellweather,” he said. “A position repairing observatory clocks. I planned to take you with me.”

“You never told me.”

“I was going to. That afternoon.”

“Why were we leaving in secret?”

“Because your grandmother intended to stop us.”

Mara remembered her grandmother’s strict black dresses, her hard hands, the way every kindness became a debt.

“After your mother died, she believed you belonged to her,” Elias said. “She told the court I was unstable. Poor. Unsuitable. She had arranged for you to be taken to her house permanently the next morning.”

Mara stared at him.

“She raised me after you vanished.”

“I’m sorry.”

“She told me you ran away alone.”

“I bought two tickets.”

The memory of the ticket returned: cream-colored paper on the kitchen table. At eight, Mara had been too young to read the destination. She remembered only the number in the corner.

Two.

Elias closed his eyes.

“I packed. I made tea. You were upstairs drawing. I meant to explain everything before the five o’clock train.”

“Then the clock rang.”

“Yes.”

The warning bell sounded again.

Mara searched the manual. “An unfinished hour seeks completion. What was unfinished? Telling me?”

“Perhaps.”

“Then you have to tell the child I was.”

“How?”

The tower shuddered.

All four clock faces darkened at once. The city beyond the glass vanished, replaced by a rain-soaked afternoon.

The jar room began to change.

Shelves stretched into wallpapered walls. Gears became picture frames. The worktable narrowed into a kitchen table. The iron stair twisted itself into the staircase of the blue house.

Mara stood in her childhood kitchen.

Rain whispered at the windows.

A suitcase waited in the hall.

At the table lay two train tickets.

Elias was solid again. His coat no longer shone with borrowed light.

From upstairs came the thump of a child jumping from a bed.

Mara’s heart tightened.

Her father picked up the tickets. “The hour has brought us back.”

“Not us,” Mara said. “It brought you back. I don’t belong here.”

Her hands were already turning transparent.

Footsteps sounded overhead.

She heard herself—her eight-year-old self—humming on the stairs.

Elias moved toward the hallway, but Mara caught his arm.

“If you leave with her, everything changes.”

“That is what we want, isn’t it?”

“Is it?”

“You said you waited for me.”

“I did.”

“Then let me come back.”

Mara wanted to say yes.

She imagined a childhood in Bellweather: her father at a workbench beneath observatory stars, breakfasts over burnt toast, stories before bed. She imagined never learning her grandmother’s cold silences, never waking from dreams of the river search, never taking the lonely position at the clock tower because its mystery resembled grief.

Then she thought of everything that had grown from those lost years.

At seventeen, she had sat beside her grandmother’s bed and held the old woman’s hand as she died. In that final hour, her grandmother had wept and confessed to lies without naming all of them. Mara had hated her, pitied her, and forgiven only part of what needed forgiving.

At twenty-three, Mara had become assistant keeper. At thirty, keeper.

She had rescued a hundred escaped minutes. She had learned that some grief could not be repaired, only carried carefully. She had lived alone, yes, but not without purpose.

If the past changed, who would guard the jars?

Glass began to crack on the edges of the false kitchen. Behind the walls, hundreds of lost hours stirred.

“Mara?” called the child on the stairs.

Elias looked toward the voice.

Mara held his sleeve.

“You must finish the hour,” she said, “but you cannot change it.”

“I don’t understand.”

“Tell her the truth. Tell her you meant to stay with her. Then go back to the tower before five.”

“That means I still disappear.”

“Yes.”

“And you still grow up without me.”

“Yes.”

His face twisted. “Why would I choose that?”

“Because if you take her away now, the keeper I became will vanish. The jars may break. Thousands of unfinished hours will escape.”

“Let them.”

“You don’t mean that.”

“I have a chance to save my daughter.”

“You are saving me.”

The child reached the bottom stair.

Mara turned.

There she was: small and solemn, with crooked braids and green socks. She carried a drawing of a silver bird.

Young Mara looked directly at her older self.

“Who are you?” she asked.

The question hurt more than any accusation.

“A friend of your father’s,” Mara said.

The child considered this. “Are you coming on the train?”

Elias knelt before her.

“Mara, I need to tell you something.”

The kitchen clock showed twenty minutes to five.

He explained about Bellweather, the observatory, and the two tickets. He explained that he loved her and had never meant to leave her behind. He told her there was a door upstairs that he had to close, and that strange things might happen.

“Will you come back?” the child asked.

Elias looked at the older Mara.

She could not tell him how to answer.

He took his daughter’s hands. “I will try harder than I have ever tried.”

“That isn’t yes.”

“No.”

The child’s lip trembled.

Mara remembered that moment now—not as it had been, but as it was becoming. A new memory settled beside the old one. She remembered her father kneeling. She remembered his wet gray eyes. She remembered a woman standing near the table, transparent as window glass.

Elias hugged the child.

The clock showed ten minutes to five.

A door appeared behind the hallway wardrobe.

Beyond it, the iron staircase rose toward the jar room.

“Come with me,” Elias said to the older Mara.

“I can’t. I’m only a visitor here.”

“Then how do I seal the hour?”

She removed the empty jar from her coat pocket. She did not remember putting it there, but lost hours obeyed their own logic.

“When the clock strikes, think of this hour as finished.”

“That’s all?”

“No. You have to mean it.”

They climbed the hidden staircase.

Below, the child began to cry.

Every step cost Elias something. His shoulders bent. Gray appeared in his hair. Lines formed around his eyes.

The years were finding him.

By the time they reached the jar room, he was sixty-two.

He looked like the father Mara had imagined in dreams, though thinner, and far more tired.

The clock prepared to strike.

Mara placed the jar on the table.

“I don’t want to lose you again,” she said.

“You haven’t.”

“I have.”

“No.” Elias touched her cheek. “You lost an hour. That is not the same as losing a life.”

The first stroke rang.

Light poured from the walls and circled him.

The second stroke rang.

The false kitchen below dissolved.

At the third, the child’s crying faded.

At the fourth, Elias became transparent once more.

He leaned toward the jar. Golden light flowed from his mouth and hands, winding into the glass.

The fifth stroke shook the room.

Mara seized the lid.

“Papa.”

His face hovered within the light.

“Yes?”

“Did you know what I would become?”

“A keeper?”

“Yes.”

He smiled.

“I knew you would become someone who stayed.”

Then he was gone.

Mara sealed the jar.

Silence fell.

Outside, the city returned. It was eight in the morning. Rain slid down the clock faces, and traffic moved through the streets as if nothing had happened.

The label on the jar had changed:

**ELIAS VENN AND MARA VENN  
14 OCTOBER, 4:00–5:00 P.M.  
FINISHED**

Mara held it against her chest.

New memories unfolded gently inside her.

She remembered her father’s promise. She remembered waiting at the window, but no longer believing he had chosen to abandon her. She remembered finding the two train tickets beneath the kitchen table after he vanished. She remembered hiding them from her grandmother.

And she remembered the transparent woman.

All her life, young Mara had thought that woman was a ghost.

Now she knew better.

For the first time in twenty-eight years, the western clock lost a minute.

Mara did not correct it.

She placed her father’s jar on the highest shelf, where the morning sun could reach it. Then she opened the great maintenance window, letting rain-scented air fill the room.

Far below, the city hurried after its appointments.

People cursed delayed trams. They watched kettles boil. They sat beside hospital beds, stood beneath station clocks, lingered at doors, and forgot to say what they had meant to say.

Hours slipped from their hands everywhere.

Most would never know where those hours went.

Mara knew.

She also knew that not every lost thing wished to be recovered. Some moments needed only to be witnessed. Some wounds could not be undone without undoing the person who had survived them.

At nine, she began her morning rounds.

She dusted the golden hours and calmed the gray ones. She repaired a loose seal on an August evening and turned a jar of childhood snow so that its light would not fade.

When she reached the highest shelf, she paused.

Inside her father’s jar, a tiny kitchen appeared. A man knelt before a child. Near the table stood a woman made of light.

The scene repeated once, then dissolved into gold.

Mara touched two fingers to the glass.

The tower ticked around her, steady and alive.

For years, she had believed time was a thief, always taking, never returning what it stole. But time had returned one thing—not the past, not her father, not the childhood she might have lived.

It had returned the truth.

At noon, Mara descended one hundred and thirteen steps and stepped into the brightening street.

She locked the green door behind her.

Above the roofs, the great clock began to strike.

And this time, when the final note faded, no hour was missing.

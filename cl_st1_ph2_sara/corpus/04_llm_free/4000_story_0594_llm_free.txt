## The Cartographer of Lost Roads

On the morning the road disappeared, Mara Venn was painting a blue line across a map of Bellwether Valley.

She worked in the attic of the town hall, where dust slept on the rafters and the windows were round as ship’s portholes. For thirty-two years, Mara had drawn every lane, footpath, bridge, and boundary within a hundred miles. She knew where fox trails crossed farmers’ fields, where the river divided around a black rock, and where the old railway vanished beneath brambles.

So when a messenger arrived from the eastern farms and announced that the road to Lark’s Hollow had gone missing, Mara did not look up.

“Roads do not go missing,” she said.

The messenger, a boy named Tovin, shifted from one muddy boot to the other. “This one has.”

“They flood. They collapse. They are blocked by trees.”

“It isn’t flooded.”

“Then collapsed.”

“There’s nothing there to collapse.”

At last Mara set down her brush.

Tovin could not have been more than fourteen. His hair looked as if it had been cut with a kitchen knife, and his gray coat was several years too large. He carried a leather mailbag, though Bellwether had not employed a proper postman since the war.

“What do you mean,” Mara asked, “there’s nothing there?”

“I mean the road ends.”

“All roads end.”

“This one ends in the middle.”

That was how, two hours later, Mara found herself sitting beside Tovin in a rattling town cart, holding a map across her knees while a stubborn white mare dragged them east.

The map clearly showed the road to Lark’s Hollow. It passed the millpond, wound between two hills, crossed Elder Bridge, and continued for nine miles through pine woods. Mara had drawn that line herself, in blue ink, after walking the route in spring rain.

Lark’s Hollow was a village of forty houses, a chapel, and an orchard famous for small red apples. Mara had not visited in seventeen years, but she remembered the place well. She remembered stone walls covered in moss and chimneys leaning toward one another like gossiping neighbors.

She also remembered a man named Elias.

That memory she folded away.

They passed the millpond before noon. Beyond it, the eastern road narrowed and climbed between banks of yellow grass. Everything appeared ordinary until they came around the shoulder of the first hill.

The road stopped.

There was no barricade, no fallen tree, no chasm. The packed earth simply ended in a clean, straight edge. Beyond it stretched a field of silver thistles.

Tovin jumped down from the cart.

“See?”

Mara climbed after him. She knelt at the road’s edge and touched the ground. On one side, wheel ruts and hoofprints marked the dry earth. On the other, thistles whispered in the wind, thick and undisturbed.

“This field wasn’t here,” Tovin said. “I came through three days ago.”

Mara took out her compass. The needle trembled, swung north, then spun once in a slow circle.

“Where were you going?” she asked.

“To Lark’s Hollow. I carry letters there every Wednesday.”

“And when you came back?”

“The road was still there.”

Mara stepped among the thistles. Their pale heads brushed her sleeves. She walked east for fifty paces, expecting to find some trace of the road—a ditch, a boundary stone, a change in the soil.

There was nothing.

On the hill to the south stood a shepherd’s cottage. Smoke rose from the chimney. Mara and Tovin left the mare grazing and climbed to the house.

An old woman opened the door before they knocked.

“We’re looking for the road to Lark’s Hollow,” Mara said.

The woman frowned. “The road to where?”

“Lark’s Hollow.”

“No place by that name around here.”

Tovin laughed nervously. “Mrs. Saye, I delivered a letter to you last month. It came from your sister in the Hollow.”

“I have no sister.”

“You gave me honey cakes.”

“I don’t keep bees.”

Behind her, on a shelf, sat a jar labeled Saye’s Clover Honey.

Mara saw Tovin notice it. His face went pale.

“Thank you,” Mara said, and pulled him away before he could argue.

Back at the vanished road, he paced in angry circles.

“She knows me.”

“Perhaps she did.”

“What does that mean?”

Mara opened her map. The blue road remained, but the words Lark’s Hollow had faded. She rubbed the paper with her thumb. Faint black flecks came away like ash.

A coldness entered her chest.

Years earlier, her predecessor had told her about roads that vanished.

Old Master Quill had been blind by then. He spent his last days beside the western window, dictating corrections to maps he could no longer see. Most of his stories were impossible: rivers that changed direction to avoid cruel kings, mountains that migrated in winter, staircases appearing in wheat fields at midnight.

But once he had gripped Mara’s wrist and said, “A place survives only while it remains connected. A road is not dirt and stone. It is an agreement between here and there.”

She had dismissed it as the poetry of an old man.

Now she remembered his final warning.

When a road disappears, do not wait for the maps to forget.

“We need to continue east,” she said.

Tovin stared at the thistles. “Through that?”

“If Lark’s Hollow is still there, we’ll find it.”

“And if it isn’t?”

Mara looked at the fading letters.

“Then we must find out why.”

They left the cart and mare with Mrs. Saye, who accepted them without remembering whose animals they were. Mara packed her instruments, food, rope, and map into a canvas satchel. Tovin carried water and his mailbag.

The thistle field extended farther than it should have. They walked until the sun hung low, yet the hills never seemed to shift behind them. No birds crossed the sky. No insects hummed underfoot.

At twilight, Mara found the first milestone.

It lay on its side, almost buried. She tore away the weeds and uncovered an inscription:

LARK’S HOLLOW — 7 MILES

Tovin touched the carved letters.

“I knew it.”

“Knowing may not be enough.”

They camped beside the stone. Mara did not sleep. She watched the stars and tried to measure their positions, but they drifted whenever she looked away.

Near midnight, she heard music.

It came faintly from the east: a violin playing a tune she had not heard in seventeen years.

Elias used to play it badly in the orchard after supper. He had called it “The Lantern Dance,” though Mara suspected he had invented both the song and its name. He would saw at the strings while she lay beneath the apple trees, criticizing every wrong note.

She stood before realizing it.

“Mara?” Tovin whispered.

The violin continued.

She followed it through the thistles. The moon turned their pale leaves into hundreds of small blades. Behind her, Tovin scrambled to gather their bags.

The music led them to a sunken path.

It was barely visible, no more than two parallel depressions in the earth, but Mara recognized the curve of it. They had found the road.

She hurried forward.

“Mara, wait.”

She did not.

The path descended between pines. The trees leaned overhead, sealing away the sky. Branches scraped Mara’s shoulders. Roots caught at her boots. The violin grew louder, then abruptly stopped.

In the silence, a voice said, “You took your time.”

A man stood ahead, holding a lantern.

He was tall and broad-shouldered, with a weathered face and dark hair streaked by gray. Mara knew him instantly. She also knew he should have been fifty-three years old.

The man before her was no older than thirty-six.

“Elias,” she said.

His expression shifted from amusement to disbelief.

“Mara?”

The lantern slipped from his hand. It struck the ground but did not go out.

For a long moment, neither moved. Then Elias crossed the distance and took her face in both hands, as if confirming she was solid.

“You’re old,” he said.

She slapped him.

He stumbled back, shocked. Tovin made a small choking sound that might have been laughter.

“Seventeen years,” Mara said. “Not one letter.”

“Seventeen?”

“You told me you needed a month. You said you were settling your father’s estate. I waited a year before deciding you were dead, and another before deciding you were a coward.”

“Mara, I’ve been here nine days.”

She wanted to strike him again, but something in his voice stopped her.

“Nine days?”

“I arrived last Tuesday.”

Tovin opened his mailbag and pulled out a bundle. “That’s when you sent these.”

The letters were addressed to Mara. There were nine of them, one for each day.

Her hands shook as she took the first.

Dear Mara,

The orchard is exactly as I remember it, except the trees are dying. Father refuses to speak about the spring. I’ll return as soon as I can.

E.

The next letter described a crack in the village well. The third mentioned that strangers had appeared at the edges of the woods. The fourth said no one could leave. The ninth contained only one sentence:

The road is forgetting us.

Mara folded the letters.

“Take us to the village.”

Lark’s Hollow stood beneath a violet sky.

Every house was lit, though the hour was late. Villagers filled the main street, speaking in low voices. Some looked exactly as Mara remembered them. Others had aged decades. A girl of eight held the hand of a bent old man who called her his younger brother.

Time had not stopped in the Hollow. It had shattered.

At the chapel, Elias explained.

A spring rose beneath the orchard, older than the village. It supplied every well and trough. Nine days earlier—nine days for Elias, though years had passed beyond the woods—the spring had begun to dry. Soon afterward, the road started fading.

“We sent people for help,” he said. “Some returned within minutes, saying they’d been gone for months. Others never came back. Then strangers appeared.”

“What strangers?”

Elias pointed toward the chapel windows.

Outside, at the edge of the lamplight, tall figures stood between the houses. They wore dark cloaks, and their faces seemed blank, as if seen through rain.

“They come closer every night,” he said. “People forget things after speaking to them. Names first. Then faces. Then whole lives.”

Tovin gripped his mailbag. “What do they want?”

“To finish the forgetting.”

Mara examined her map on the chapel altar. The road’s blue line had faded almost completely. So had the village itself. Houses dissolved into blank parchment one by one.

“What happened to the spring?” she asked.

Elias looked toward the orchard.

“My father buried it.”

They found the old man sitting beneath the largest apple tree. Abram Vale had once been a giant of a farmer. Now he was a thin figure wrapped in blankets, his eyes bright with fever.

“You came back,” he said when he saw Mara.

“Why did you bury the spring?”

Abram smiled. “To keep the village safe.”

“From what?”

“From being taken.”

His story came slowly.

Long before Bellwether Valley was settled, roads had behaved differently. They followed memory rather than geography, joining places whose people longed for one another. Travelers could walk a mile and cross a kingdom if someone waited for them strongly enough.

But such roads also attracted the Unremembered—beings born from abandoned homes, erased names, and promises no one kept. They could enter a place only when its connections weakened.

The spring beneath Lark’s Hollow anchored the village to the valley. Its water fed the land; its underground streams touched rivers, wells, and rain barrels for miles. As long as it flowed, the Hollow remained part of the world.

Abram had covered it because he believed outsiders planned to seize the orchard.

“Surveyors came,” he said. “They carried documents from the crown. Said the water belonged to Bellwether now. Pipes, taxes, gates—thieves dressed as clerks.”

“So you sealed the spring,” Mara said.

“With stones and iron. Let them steal dry earth.”

“You cut the village loose.”

“I protected what was ours.”

Beyond the orchard, something moved among the trees.

The Unremembered had come closer.

One stood beneath a lantern. Its cloak hung without stirring. Where its face should have been, Mara saw a dim reflection of the attic in town hall. Her desk was covered in dust. Her maps had blank spaces. No one waited for her.

A voice entered her thoughts.

You left him.

She turned away.

Elias had seen something too. His face tightened.

“What did it show you?” Mara asked.

“A life where you never came.”

For seventeen years, she thought, I didn’t.

Aloud she said, “Where is the spring?”

Abram began to laugh.

The entrance lay beneath the oldest tree. Villagers removed baskets of soil, uncovering a stairway sealed by an iron plate. By then the Unremembered had reached the orchard fence.

Mara organized the work. Chains were fetched from the smithy. Elias tied them through the plate’s handles. Twenty people pulled.

The iron groaned but did not move.

“Again,” Mara ordered.

They pulled until hands bled.

Nothing.

Tovin walked around the plate, studying it. “There are words here.”

Mara knelt. Letters had been hammered into the iron, crude but legible.

WHAT IS CLOSED IN FEAR OPENS ONLY IN TRUST.

“A riddle?” Elias asked.

“A rule,” Mara said.

Abram had sealed the spring not with iron alone but with his certainty that everyone beyond the Hollow was an enemy. No force from within the village could undo it.

Mara looked toward the road.

“We need someone from outside.”

“You’re from outside,” Tovin said.

“Not enough. I belonged here once.”

“You left.”

“And spent every year remembering.”

The Unremembered crossed the fence.

Where they passed, apple trees vanished. Not broke, not burned—vanished, leaving smooth ground behind.

Tovin stepped toward the iron plate.

“I’m from outside.”

“You’re a child,” Elias said.

“I’m the postman.”

He took the unread letters from his bag and spread them across the plate. There were dozens: messages from Bellwether, invitations, bills, birthday wishes, apologies. Thin threads of ink glimmered between envelopes.

“These belong to people out there,” Tovin said. “People writing to people in here. That means the road still connects them.”

The iron shuddered.

Mara understood. “Read the names.”

Tovin picked up the first letter.

“From Ina Saye to her sister, Beatrice Saye.”

Far beyond the woods, perhaps, an old woman sat beside a jar of honey and suddenly remembered keeping bees.

The plate lifted a fraction.

“From Cal Morrow to his son, Petren.”

A villager cried out. “My father.”

“From the Bellwether schoolmaster to Lysa Thorn, offering her a place at the academy.”

A young woman covered her mouth.

With every name, the plate rose higher. The villagers took up the letters and read aloud.

Promises traveled both directions. Debts. Jokes. Condolences. Recipes. Confessions of love written badly and too late.

The Unremembered entered the circle of lanterns.

Mara felt their voices press around her.

No one wrote to you.

That was true.

No one waited.

Perhaps that was true as well.

Elias seized her hand.

“I did.”

She looked at him.

“For nine days,” he said. “For seventeen years. Whatever time means here, I waited.”

The last chain snapped taut. The iron plate tore free.

A roar rose from underground.

Water burst through the stairway, black at first, then silver in the moonlight. It flooded the orchard, curling around roots and boots. The villagers shouted and laughed. Tovin fell backward and came up soaked, still clutching his empty mailbag.

The Unremembered recoiled.

But they did not leave.

They gathered at the water’s edge, taller now, their cloaks joining into a wall of darkness. Faces appeared across their blank surfaces—hundreds of forgotten people, lost villages, demolished houses, unmarked graves.

Mara realized they were not invaders. Not exactly.

They were what remained when connections failed.

The restored spring could anchor Lark’s Hollow, but the road had already been erased from the land. Without a path, the village would remain trapped, repeating and unraveling until the spring failed again.

Mara opened her satchel and removed her map.

The road was gone from it.

“Give me your violin,” she said to Elias.

“What?”

“The case.”

He brought it from beneath the apple tree. Inside were spare strings, resin, and a small bottle of black varnish.

Mara poured the varnish into her ink pot. She added spring water and stirred it with her brush.

Then she laid the map upon the ground.

“I need to draw the road again.”

“Can you?” Tovin asked.

“A map is an agreement between here and there.”

“So?”

“So I cannot make it alone.”

Mara knelt at one edge of the parchment. Elias knelt opposite her. Tovin placed his hand between theirs. Around them, the villagers gathered.

“Tell me where the road goes,” Mara said.

“Past Elder Bridge,” someone called.

“By the standing stones.”

“Along Mrs. Saye’s hill.”

“Through the pine woods.”

“To the millpond.”

“To Bellwether.”

“To home,” Elias said.

Mara touched brush to paper.

The new ink shone like starlight.

She drew the first line through Lark’s Hollow, past the chapel and orchard. As the brush moved, the earth beneath them trembled. She continued through the woods, following remembered turns.

The Unremembered reached for the map.

Hands emerged from their cloaks—pale hands, transparent hands, hands stained by soil and age. Mara felt them tugging at every memory she possessed.

She forgot the name of the town hall.

She forgot Master Quill’s face.

She forgot why she had spent her life drawing roads.

The brush hesitated.

Then Tovin began to recite the route.

“From the orchard to the chapel. From the chapel to Elder Bridge. From Elder Bridge through the pines.”

Others joined him.

Mara drew.

The line reached the silver thistle field. It passed the fallen milestone and joined the eastern road.

When the final stroke connected with the map of Bellwether, the world inhaled.

Wind rushed through the orchard. The apple trees bent, scattering blossoms though they had been bare moments before. Bells rang in the village. Somewhere beyond the forest, a mare began to neigh.

The Unremembered broke apart like smoke.

Not all vanished. Some remained as faint figures beside the new road. They watched without hostility.

Mara understood their request.

“Names,” she whispered.

She turned the map over and began writing the names she had seen upon their shifting faces. The villagers helped, recording those they recognized and inventing respectful names for those they did not.

Each name became a small mark on the map: a ruined cottage, an old grave, a path to a place no longer standing.

By dawn, the Unremembered were gone.

The road home lay beyond the orchard gate.

It was not the road Mara remembered. It curved where it had once run straight and crossed a stream she had never seen. New milestones stood along its edge, their letters sharp and black.

LARK’S HOLLOW — 9 MILES  
BELLWETHER — 9 MILES  
NEITHER PLACE FORGOTTEN

The villagers walked the road together.

When they emerged from the pine woods, they found carts, riders, and farmers waiting. Mrs. Saye stood among them with a jar of honey in her hands. At the sight of her sister, she began to weep.

Time settled unevenly.

Some villagers regained the years they had lost. Others kept the age the Hollow had given them. Elias remained thirty-six, seventeen years younger than Mara.

He seemed troubled by this.

Mara was not.

“You’re still a terrible violinist,” she told him.

“And you’re still old.”

She kissed him before deciding whether to slap him again.

In the months that followed, Bellwether rebuilt Elder Bridge. The crown abandoned its claim to the spring after Mara added several inconvenient cliffs to the official survey. Lark’s Hollow appointed Tovin as its first postmaster, though he continued carrying letters himself because he disliked sitting indoors.

As for Mara, she returned to the attic of the town hall.

But her maps changed.

She no longer drew only roads of earth and stone. She marked the route of Ina Saye’s weekly visits to her sister. She recorded the path children took to swim in the millpond, the distant city where Lysa Thorn attended school, and the orchard where Elias played his violin in the evenings.

In the margins, she wrote names.

Some belonged to abandoned villages. Some belonged to people no history book mentioned. When she did not know a name, she left a space and asked travelers to help fill it.

Years later, when Mara’s hands became too stiff to hold a brush, Tovin climbed the attic stairs and found her at the western window.

“I’ve brought you a letter,” he said.

“From whom?”

“Lark’s Hollow.”

He gave her an envelope addressed in Elias’s untidy hand.

Mara smiled. “He could have walked here.”

“He said letters matter.”

“He’s finally learned something.”

Tovin looked at the enormous map covering the attic wall. It was crossed by thousands of colored lines. Roads met rivers, trails, shipping routes, family trees, and threads of correspondence. Nothing stood alone.

“Do you think a place can still disappear?” he asked.

“Of course.”

“Even now?”

“Especially now. People are always building walls and calling them safety.”

He considered this.

“What keeps a place from being forgotten?”

Mara opened Elias’s letter. A single apple blossom fell from between the pages, still fresh.

“Not maps,” she said. “Not really.”

“Then what?”

Outside, bells sounded from the road. Travelers approached the town from every direction: merchants, pilgrims, shepherds, runaways, musicians, and children returning home.

Mara watched them cross the bridge.

“The willingness,” she said, “to keep a road open.”

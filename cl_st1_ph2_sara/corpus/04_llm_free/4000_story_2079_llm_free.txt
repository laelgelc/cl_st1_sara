# The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at seven, Mr. Vale unlocked the clock shop on Bellweather Street and listened to the town begin.

First came the baker’s cart, rattling over the cobbles. Then the bells of Saint Orison’s struck the hour, always three seconds late. Windows opened above the shops. Kettles whistled. Dogs barked at the milkman as if surprised by his daily betrayal.

And then, beneath all those ordinary sounds, came the clocks.

They ticked from every wall of Mr. Vale’s narrow shop. Tall clocks, brass clocks, painted clocks, clocks made for ships and kitchens and mantelpieces. Some hurried. Some hesitated. Some had stopped altogether, their hands fixed upon private disasters.

Above the door hung a sign:

**ELIAS VALE  
REPAIRS, RESTORATIONS, AND LOST TIME**

Most people assumed the final words were a joke.

Mr. Vale never corrected them.

He was a thin man with silver hair and a face that seemed designed for listening. He had repaired clocks for forty-two years, though no one could remember him ever looking young. His suits were always brown. His spectacles were always sliding down his nose. He drank tea from a blue cup with a crack along the handle, and every evening at six he locked the shop, walked home alone, and ate soup beside an empty chair.

On the first Monday in November, a girl named Nora Wren entered carrying a clock wrapped in a green scarf.

The bell above the door gave a tired jingle.

Mr. Vale looked up from a pocket watch.

“Good morning.”

“Are you Elias Vale?”

“So I’m told.”

Nora frowned. She was twelve and had little patience for adults who gave peculiar answers to simple questions.

“My mother says you repair clocks.”

“Your mother is correct.”

She placed the bundle on the counter and unwound the scarf. Inside was a small wooden clock carved in the shape of a house. Two tiny doors stood above its face. At each hour, a wooden bird should have appeared and called. But the clock was silent.

Mr. Vale picked it up carefully.

“Cuckoo clock,” he said. “Black Forest. Late nineteenth century, perhaps. Has it fallen?”

“No.”

“Been dropped?”

“No.”

“Thrown?”

“No.”

“Submerged?”

“Of course not.”

“You would be astonished.”

He turned the clock over. On the back someone had carved two initials: **M.W.**

“It belonged to my grandmother,” Nora said. “She gave it to my mother, and my mother gave it to me.”

“A sensible line of succession.”

“It stopped last Thursday at thirteen minutes past four.”

Mr. Vale’s hands became still.

Outside, the baker’s empty cart rattled back along the street.

“Thirteen minutes past four?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“What happened then?”

Nora looked at him. “Nothing.”

“Clocks rarely stop for nothing.”

“It’s just broken.”

“Perhaps.”

He opened the back panel. The gears inside were clean, the weights balanced, the chain unbroken. Yet among the brass wheels he saw a thread of pale blue light, thin as a strand of hair.

Mr. Vale closed the panel at once.

“How long will it take?” Nora asked.

“A day. Possibly two.”

“I need it tomorrow.”

“Why?”

She wrapped the green scarf around her fingers. “My mother is ill. She likes hearing it.”

“I see.”

“It reminds her of home.”

Mr. Vale glanced at the clock again. “Come back after school tomorrow.”

Nora left without saying goodbye.

Mr. Vale turned the sign on the door to **CLOSED**, though it was not yet ten.

He carried the clock into the back room, where the ordinary repairs ended.

The room had no windows. Shelves rose from floor to ceiling, and upon them stood hundreds of small glass bottles. Each bottle contained a flicker of light. Some glowed gold, others white, red, or blue. Handwritten labels hung from their necks.

**Seven minutes lost waiting for a train.**

**An afternoon forgotten after fever.**

**The final hour before forgiveness.**

**Three seconds between the fall and the pain.**

In the center of the room stood a black worktable. Mr. Vale placed Nora’s clock upon it and lit a lamp.

Lost time gathered in strange places. Most escaped unnoticed: a minute misplaced in daydreaming, an hour swallowed by grief, a summer that vanished between childhood and growing old. Usually lost time dissolved into the world, harmless as mist.

But sometimes it lodged inside a clock.

When that happened, the mechanism could no longer move forward. It had to carry not only the hours to come but also the hour someone could not release.

Mr. Vale removed the back panel and drew the blue thread free with silver tweezers. It trembled in the air, lengthening until it filled the room with cold light.

A picture appeared within it.

A kitchen. Rain on the windows. A woman—Nora’s mother, thinner than she should have been—stood beside a table. Nora faced her, wearing the same yellow coat she had worn in the shop.

“You promised,” Nora said.

“I know.”

“You said you were getting better.”

“I hoped I was.”

“You lied.”

The woman reached toward her. Nora stepped back.

The cuckoo clock on the wall clicked. Its hands pointed to four thirteen.

“I hate you,” Nora said.

The words hung in the kitchen.

Then the memory shattered, snapping back into the clock so sharply that Mr. Vale dropped the tweezers.

For several moments, only the shop clocks could be heard.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Mr. Vale sat down.

He knew the weight of a final sentence. Everyone carried one somewhere: the last thing said to a departing friend, the question left unasked before a door closed, the careless cruelty that became permanent because death arrived before an apology.

On the highest shelf stood a bottle covered in dust.

Its label read:

**One hour belonging to Clara Vale.**

He did not look at it.

Instead, he returned to the front room, turned the sign to **OPEN**, and resumed repairing the pocket watch.

At half past three the next afternoon, Nora came back.

“Is it fixed?”

“No.”

Her face hardened. “You said it would be.”

“I said a day. Possibly two.”

“It’s been a day.”

“And now it will be two.”

She snatched the green scarf from the counter. “You’re useless.”

“Occasionally.”

“My mother asked for it.”

“Then bring her here.”

Nora stared at him. “She can’t come here.”

“Why not?”

“She’s in the hospital.”

“Ah.”

Mr. Vale polished his spectacles.

Nora’s anger faltered. “Can you fix it or not?”

“Yes,” he said. “But I may need your help.”

“With what?”

“The clock has not stopped because of a broken spring. It has stopped because it is holding a moment you wish had never happened.”

The color left her face.

“That’s ridiculous.”

“It often is.”

“You don’t know anything about me.”

“Last Thursday, at thirteen minutes past four, you said something to your mother. You did not mean it, but the clock believes you did.”

Nora backed away from the counter.

“How do you know?”

“I repair clocks.”

“That doesn’t explain anything.”

“No.”

“You were spying on us.”

“I was here last Thursday, replacing the escapement in Mayor Thorne’s carriage clock.”

“You’re lying.”

“Also possible.”

Nora wrapped the scarf tightly around the cuckoo clock and lifted it.

“Where are you going?”

“To find someone normal.”

“If you take the clock now, it will never run again.”

“I don’t care.”

“Yes, you do.”

She glared at him with tears in her eyes.

Mr. Vale softened his voice.

“When time stops at a painful moment, one must pass through that moment to set it moving again.”

“You mean remember it?”

“More than remember.”

“I’m not doing that.”

“You may choose not to.”

“And then?”

“The clock remains silent.”

“That’s all?”

Mr. Vale looked toward the back room.

“No,” he said. “Not always.”

Nora waited.

“The moment may spread. You will begin to find thirteen minutes past four in other places. You will wake then. Dream of it. Hear the words again whenever a room grows quiet. After many years, you may forget whole days but remember those few seconds perfectly.”

Nora held the bundle against her chest.

“That already happens.”

Mr. Vale nodded.

She lowered her eyes. “If I do what you say, will it change what happened?”

“No.”

“Then what’s the point?”

“It may change what happens next.”

“My mother might not have a next.”

The room seemed to shrink around them.

Mr. Vale thought of the dusty bottle on the highest shelf.

“My wife’s name was Clara,” he said.

Nora said nothing.

“She played the violin badly, cultivated roses well, and made coffee strong enough to frighten a horse. She was ill for a long time. Near the end, she asked me to sit with her, but I had work to finish.”

He looked at the clocks on the wall.

“There is always work for those who prefer it to sorrow.”

“What happened?”

“I told her I would come upstairs in an hour.”

“Did you?”

“Yes.”

His voice nearly failed.

“Fifty-nine minutes too late.”

Nora’s anger disappeared.

“I’m sorry.”

“So am I.”

He led her into the back room.

At first, she seemed frightened by the bottles. Then wonder overtook fear. She walked along the shelves, reading labels.

“Are these all lost hours?”

“Hours. Minutes. Moments.”

“Who lost them?”

“Everyone loses time.”

“Can you give it back?”

“Sometimes.”

She pointed to a small green bottle. “What’s that?”

“Five minutes a soldier lost before a battle. He spent them watching snow fall.”

“Did he survive?”

“Yes.”

“This one?”

“A woman’s last dance with her father.”

“And that red one?”

Mr. Vale took her hand away from it. “Best not.”

On the worktable he unwrapped the cuckoo clock.

“What do I have to do?” Nora asked.

“Touch the hands.”

She hesitated, then placed one finger on the minute hand and another on the hour hand.

“Close your eyes.”

“Will it hurt?”

“Yes.”

Her hands jerked back.

“You could lie,” she said.

“I could.”

“Would it be dangerous?”

“Not if you remember where you are.”

“And where am I?”

“In a clock shop on Bellweather Street. It is Tuesday, November seventh. It is raining. Your name is Nora Wren, and you have not yet said everything you need to say.”

She breathed slowly.

Then she closed her eyes and touched the hands.

The blue light burst outward.

The back room vanished.

They stood in the kitchen from the memory. Rain streaked the windows. A saucepan steamed on the stove. The clock hung upon the wall, its hands approaching four thirteen.

Nora’s mother stood near the table.

She was younger than she had looked in the vision, or perhaps merely alive in a way memories could not fully preserve.

“You promised,” the remembered Nora said.

“I know.”

“You said you were getting better.”

“I hoped I was.”

“You lied.”

Her mother reached out.

Nora stepped back.

Mr. Vale watched from the corner. He could not interfere. A trapped hour had its own gravity, and those who entered it were only witnesses until the moment broke.

The clock clicked.

Four thirteen.

“I hate you,” the remembered Nora said.

Her mother’s hand dropped.

In the original memory, the moment had ended there. But now it continued.

Nora, standing beside Mr. Vale, cried out, “No!”

The figures in the kitchen froze.

The rain stopped against the windows.

Mr. Vale had never seen that happen.

The remembered Nora turned.

She looked directly at the real Nora.

“You don’t hate her,” she said.

“I know.”

“Then why did you say it?”

“Because she scared me.”

“She was scared too.”

“I know.”

“Why didn’t you tell her?”

“Because—”

Nora’s voice broke.

The kitchen darkened at the edges.

The remembered girl came closer. “Because what?”

“Because if she knew how scared I was, it would make it real.”

The remembered Nora looked toward her mother.

“She already knows it’s real.”

Nora began to sob. “I want to take it back.”

“You can’t.”

“I know.”

“Then say something else.”

The kitchen trembled. Cups rattled in the cupboards. The walls stretched like shadows at sunset.

Nora ran to her mother.

The woman remained frozen, one hand half raised, sadness resting in her face.

Nora threw her arms around her.

“I don’t hate you,” she said. “I’m angry, and I’m frightened, and I don’t know what I’m supposed to do if you die. I’m sorry. I’m sorry. I’m sorry.”

The clock’s doors flew open.

The wooden bird sprang out and called once.

The kitchen shattered into blue light.

Nora woke on the floor of the back room.

Mr. Vale was kneeling beside her. The cuckoo clock ticked on the worktable.

Fourteen minutes past four.

Then fifteen.

Then sixteen.

Nora wiped her face.

“Did I change it?”

“No.”

“Did she hear me?”

“Not there.”

Nora stood shakily. “Then I need to go.”

“Where?”

“To the hospital.”

She grabbed the clock and ran toward the door. Before leaving, she turned back.

“Mr. Vale?”

“Yes?”

“You should open the bottle.”

He glanced at the highest shelf.

“I cannot change it.”

“That isn’t what you told me.”

Then she was gone.

For the rest of the afternoon, Mr. Vale repaired nothing.

At six, he locked the front door. The rain had stopped, and the wet street reflected the lamps. Across the road, the baker swept flour from his doorway. Far above the roofs, Saint Orison’s clock began striking the hour.

Three seconds late.

Mr. Vale entered the back room and stood beneath the bottle labeled with Clara’s name.

For forty years, he had kept that hour.

He had found it inside the blue cup on the morning after she died. The cup had cracked when he poured tea into it, and from the crack came a golden light. He had captured it before it vanished, telling himself he would release it when he was ready.

He had never been ready.

He climbed the ladder, took down the bottle, and returned to the worktable.

The cork resisted him.

“Don’t be childish,” he muttered, though whether he spoke to the bottle or himself, he did not know.

He pulled harder.

The cork came free.

Golden light flooded the room.

He stood in the old bedroom.

Clara lay propped against white pillows. Winter sunlight rested on the blanket. Her violin case stood in the corner, unopened for months.

Elias—forty years younger, dark-haired and impatient—stood at the door.

“Sit with me,” Clara said.

“I have work.”

“You always have work.”

“Only one more clock.”

“There is always one more clock.”

Young Elias looked at the watch in his hand.

“I’ll come upstairs in an hour.”

Clara studied him.

Mr. Vale remembered that look. For decades, he had believed it contained disappointment. Now, standing within the hour, he saw something else.

Recognition.

She knew.

Perhaps she had known better than he did.

Young Elias left the room.

The door closed.

For a while, Clara listened to his footsteps descend the stairs. Then she turned toward the empty corner where old Mr. Vale stood.

“You took your time,” she said.

He stopped breathing.

Clara smiled faintly. “That is like you.”

“You can see me?”

“I could always see you.”

He approached the bed.

“This is not real,” he said.

“No. But it is true.”

He sat beside her.

“I am sorry.”

“I know.”

“I should have stayed.”

“Yes.”

“I was afraid.”

“I know that too.”

He bowed his head.

For forty years, he had imagined what he would say if time could be persuaded to open. He had prepared speeches, confessions, pleas. But in the remembered room, before the remembered woman he had loved, only one question mattered.

“Did you think I didn’t love you?”

Clara reached for his hand.

Her fingers were warm.

“No,” she said. “I thought you loved me so much that you could not bear to watch me leave.”

The winter light grew brighter.

Mr. Vale wept.

“I wasted the last hour.”

“No,” Clara said. “You lost it. There is a difference.”

“What difference?”

“Wasted things are thrown away. Lost things can be found.”

The room began to fade.

He gripped her hand. “Wait.”

“You have kept me long enough, Elias.”

“Clara—”

“Go downstairs. There are clocks waiting.”

The light folded around him.

He awoke with his head on the black worktable.

The bottle was empty.

From the front room came a great confusion of bells, chimes, clicks, and wooden birds. Every clock in the shop had begun to strike, though it was seventeen minutes past six.

Mr. Vale laughed.

It was an unfamiliar sound, rusty but genuine.

The next morning, Nora returned.

She entered just after seven, as the baker’s cart rattled down Bellweather Street.

The cuckoo clock was not with her.

“How is your mother?” Mr. Vale asked.

“Asleep. But I talked to her.”

“Did she hear?”

“Yes.”

“And the clock?”

“It’s beside her bed. It woke everyone at five this morning.”

“Temperamental mechanism.”

Nora looked toward the back room.

“Did you open the bottle?”

“I did.”

“And?”

Mr. Vale picked up the blue cup from beside his tools.

A thin crack still ran along its handle. He had repaired porcelain before and could have mended it easily, but some breaks were not failures. Some were doors.

“I found what I had lost,” he said.

Nora nodded as if this were the most ordinary answer in the world.

She turned to leave, then paused beneath the sign.

“You should change that.”

“Change what?”

“‘Repairs, Restorations, and Lost Time.’ People think it’s a joke.”

“Most people do.”

“You should make it clearer.”

Mr. Vale considered this.

That afternoon, he painted a new sign.

For many years afterward, it hung above the shop on Bellweather Street, where everyone in town could read it:

**ELIAS VALE  
CLOCKS REPAIRED  
LOST HOURS FOUND  
NO MOMENT CHANGED  
NO MOMENT BEYOND MENDING**

Some people laughed.

Some entered.

And every morning at seven, Mr. Vale unlocked the door and listened to the town begin.

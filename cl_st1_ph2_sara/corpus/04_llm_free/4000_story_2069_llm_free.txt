By the time Mara reached the lighthouse, the sea had swallowed the road behind her.

She stood on the final black rock, clutching her suitcase in one hand and the iron railing in the other. Rain struck the hood of her yellow coat like thrown gravel. Thirty feet below, waves crashed through the gap where the causeway had been only minutes earlier.

Ahead, the lighthouse rose from the island like a white bone. Its lantern was dark.

Mara checked her watch. Six twelve.

The light should have been on before sunset.

She climbed the slippery steps and hammered on the door.

“Mr. Vale?”

No answer.

She hammered again. The brass knocker was shaped like a fish with an open mouth.

“Mr. Vale, it’s Mara Venn. From the mainland office.”

The door creaked inward beneath her hand.

Inside, the lighthouse smelled of salt, kerosene, and something sweetly rotten. A narrow staircase spiraled up through the stone tower. To her left was the keeper’s room: a bed, a table, a cold stove, and shelves crowded with jars.

A cup of tea sat on the table. Steam no longer rose from it, but when Mara touched the porcelain, it was warm.

“Mr. Vale?”

A scrape came from above.

Mara set down her suitcase and took out the envelope she had been told to deliver. It bore no stamp and no address, only the keeper’s name in the director’s careful handwriting.

ELIAS VALE.

“Hello?” she called.

The scrape sounded again, followed by three slow knocks.

Mara looked up the spiral staircase.

She had not wanted this assignment. For seven years she had worked in the mainland office, copying weather reports, scheduling supply boats, and answering letters from fishermen who complained that the lighthouse horn was too loud or not loud enough. She had never been sent to the island before. No one had, except the monthly supply crew.

But that morning the director had summoned her.

“Vale has missed three reports,” he said.

“Then send the crew.”

“The sea will be too rough by noon.”

“Send the radio operator.”

“He refuses.”

Mara had laughed, because she thought the director was joking. He had not laughed with her.

Now she understood the radio operator.

A flash of lightning illuminated the tower. For an instant, Mara saw a figure leaning over the railing far above.

Then darkness returned.

“Mr. Vale, the lamp is out,” she said, forcing firmness into her voice. “There’s a passenger ship due past the eastern reef at eight.”

A voice drifted down.

“I know.”

It was hardly louder than the rain.

Mara gripped the envelope. “Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Then why haven’t you lit it?”

A long silence followed.

“Because,” the keeper said, “the light is what lets it find us.”

Mara stared upward.

The mainland office had an entire cupboard devoted to strange letters from lighthouse keepers. Isolation did things to the mind. She had read reports of voices in fog, ships sailing beneath the sea, dead wives calling through radio static. The official procedure was simple: remain calm, avoid confrontation, ensure the keeper could not harm himself or others, and restore essential operations.

Mara began climbing.

The iron steps were wet. Not rain-wet, she realized, but covered in a thin layer of seawater. It trickled downward in silver threads.

Halfway up, she passed a window. The storm had covered the island, turning the world beyond the glass into a gray wall.

Three knocks came from above.

Mara stopped.

This time the sound had not come from the lantern room. It came from the window.

Knock.

Knock.

Knock.

She turned slowly.

Nothing was outside except rain and empty air. The window stood nearly eighty feet above the rocks.

A pale shape swept past the glass.

Mara stumbled backward, nearly losing her footing.

“It knows you’re here,” the keeper called.

She hurried upward.

Elias Vale waited at the top of the stairs.

Mara had seen his photograph in the office files. It showed a heavy man in his fifties with a square beard and amused eyes. The man before her was thin enough that his sweater hung from his shoulders like cloth on a frame. His beard was white. Dark crescents hollowed the skin beneath his eyes.

He pointed a flare pistol at her chest.

Mara froze.

“You shouldn’t have come,” he said.

“The door was open.”

“I left it open for someone else.”

Thunder trembled through the tower.

Behind him, the great lens stood motionless. Its glass panels surrounded the unlit lamp in concentric rings. Even in darkness, they caught stray flashes of lightning and broke them into faint, shivering rainbows.

Mara raised the envelope. “The director sent me.”

Vale looked at it but did not lower the pistol.

“What does it say?”

“I don’t know.”

“Read it.”

“It’s addressed to you.”

“I said read it.”

Keeping one eye on the weapon, Mara tore open the envelope. Inside was a single sheet.

She read aloud.

“Elias. The western station saw it last night. You were right. Do not light the lamp. Sink the lens if you can. May God forgive us.”

At the bottom was the director’s signature.

Mara read it twice.

“But he told me to make sure the light was operating.”

“He needed someone to bring the warning.”

“He could have told me.”

“He thought you wouldn’t come if you knew.”

“He was right.”

For the first time, Vale almost smiled. Then something slammed into the outer glass.

The entire lantern room rang.

Mara spun around.

A hand was pressed against the window.

It was larger than a human hand, with long translucent fingers joined by delicate webs. Black nails scratched the glass. Behind it, barely visible in the rain, a face hovered.

Its skin was the color of a drowned man’s lips. Its eyes were round and lidless. Where its nose should have been were two narrow slits, opening and closing like gills.

The face disappeared.

Mara could not breathe.

Vale lowered the flare pistol.

“It began six nights ago,” he said. “I saw a boat beyond the reef. No sails, no smoke. I signaled, but it didn’t answer. At midnight it came closer. At dawn it vanished.”

Another blow struck the glass.

“On the second night, there were three boats,” he continued. “On the third, they came without boats.”

Mara backed away from the windows. “What are they?”

“Old.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

The storm brightened with lightning, and the sea appeared below them. Figures clung to the rocks all around the island. Dozens of pale bodies crawled from the surf, moving on narrow arms and bent legs.

Mara covered her mouth.

“They can climb,” Vale said. “But they won’t enter darkness. Not complete darkness. They gather near the lighthouse every night and wait for the beam.”

“Why?”

Vale looked at the silent lens.

“I think they once followed another light, long before ours was built. A light under the water. Maybe they mistake this one for it. Maybe they don’t. All I know is that each time the beam passes over them, they come closer.”

The lamp mechanism gave a sudden metallic click.

The lens began to turn.

Vale swore and rushed to the control cabinet. He tore open the panel, seized a lever, and pulled it down. The lens continued rotating.

“The automatic system,” Mara said.

“I disabled it yesterday.”

“Apparently not.”

The lamp coughed.

A blue spark flickered at its center.

Outside, the creatures became still.

Every pale head turned toward the tower.

Vale smashed the control panel with the butt of the flare pistol. The spark vanished. The lens slowed but did not stop.

“Help me,” he said.

“With what?”

“We have to break it.”

Mara stared at the great Fresnel lens. It was taller than either of them, a masterpiece of cut glass and brass, worth more than the lighthouse itself. Hundreds of crystal prisms curved around the central lamp.

Vale took an iron maintenance bar from the wall.

“You heard the letter,” he said. “The director said to sink it.”

“He also lied to me.”

“That doesn’t make him wrong.”

The outer glass shuddered under another impact. A thin crack appeared in one pane.

Mara took the bar from him.

Together they struck the lens.

The first blow glanced away, sending pain through Mara’s wrists. The second chipped a prism. Vale struck the damaged place, and a bright shard fell to the floor.

Outside, the creatures began to shriek.

The sound was soft at first, almost lost beneath the storm. Then more voices joined it. The tower vibrated with a chorus like whales singing through broken teeth.

Mara swung again.

Cracks raced across the nearest panel.

Vale lifted the bar, but before he could strike, the lantern room door slammed shut behind him.

The lamp ignited.

White radiance exploded through the lens.

For one perfect second, the room became brighter than noon. The damaged prisms scattered knives of light in every direction. The beam swept across the sea.

Below, the creatures rose.

Hundreds stood in the water beyond the island, though Mara could not understand how. Their bodies extended downward into the waves, pale columns reaching into unseen depths. Farther out, enormous shapes moved beneath the surface.

The beam completed half a turn.

Something answered from the horizon.

A green light opened under the sea.

It was not a reflection. It grew slowly, illuminating the water from below. Mara saw the eastern reef, the drowned skeletons of ships, and vast forests of weeds bending in the current.

Then a shadow moved over the green glow.

It was larger than the island.

The tower shook.

“Break the lamp!” Vale shouted.

Mara swung the bar at the burning center. The heat scorched her face. Glass burst outward, slicing her cheek, and the lamp went dark.

But the green light remained.

It advanced beneath the water.

Vale opened the lantern room door and pointed down the stairs. “Go.”

“What about you?”

“I’ll destroy the mechanism.”

“We’ve destroyed the lamp.”

“It relights itself.”

“That’s impossible.”

He looked at her as the machinery clicked behind them.

“No,” he said. “It isn’t.”

From below came the sound of the lighthouse door opening.

Mara and Vale looked at one another.

Wet footsteps entered the keeper’s room.

One step.

Then another.

The lower stairs groaned.

Vale raised his flare pistol.

“How many shots do you have?” Mara whispered.

“One.”

“That’s useful.”

“I was saving it for myself.”

The footsteps continued upward.

Mara searched the lantern room. There was nowhere to hide. The shattered lens turned slowly, grinding fragments of glass beneath its brass frame. Pale beams from the approaching underwater glow passed through the windows.

The envelope lay on the floor near Mara’s feet.

She saw the director’s message again.

Sink the lens if you can.

Not break it. Sink it.

“Why does it want the lighthouse?” she asked.

Vale watched the stairs. “What?”

“You said it follows the beam. But the beam isn’t moving now, and it’s still coming. Why?”

“I don’t know.”

“The lens,” Mara said. “The light under the water—maybe it isn’t answering the lamp. Maybe it’s answering the lens.”

“The lens is just glass.”

“So is a telescope. So is a mirror. Glass can show things. Glass can call things closer.”

Vale glanced at the rotating prisms.

The wet footsteps reached the final turn of the staircase.

Mara grabbed the maintenance bar and wedged it beneath the brass base of the lens.

“Help me tip it.”

“It weighs two tons.”

“Then help me make it weigh less.”

Vale understood. He fired the flare pistol into the mechanism.

The flare struck the central shaft. Red fire sprayed through the lantern room. Grease ignited. Gears screamed and spun. The lens lurched sideways.

A creature crawled into view at the top of the stairs.

Up close, it wore the remains of a human face like a mask stretched over something narrower. Seaweed hung from its mouth. A brass chain circled its neck, and from the chain dangled a corroded disk marked with the name of a ship.

SAINT ORISON.

Mara knew the name.

“The Saint Orison sank in 1846,” she whispered.

Vale seized a burning rag and threw it.

The creature recoiled from the flame. Its back struck the railing, and it made a sound like a child coughing.

“Now!” Vale shouted.

They pushed the maintenance bar together.

The lens base rose half an inch.

Mara’s boots slid through oil and broken glass. She screamed with effort, feeling something tear in her shoulder. Vale pressed his whole weight against the bar.

A bolt snapped.

The lens tilted.

The outer windows cracked as if struck by invisible hammers.

Below, more creatures filled the staircase. They climbed over one another, clawing at the stone. The first sprang at Vale.

He turned, catching it by the wrists.

Its jaws opened wider than any human mouth.

Mara let go of the bar, grabbed a shard of prism, and drove it into the creature’s neck. Instead of blood, black water poured from the wound. The creature collapsed, twisting like an emptied sack.

Without their combined weight, the lens dropped back into place.

“No,” Mara gasped.

The green glow beneath the sea reached the island. Light flooded upward through cracks in the rock. The lighthouse seemed to float above a luminous abyss.

Vale kicked the dead creature down the stairs. It struck the others, knocking them backward.

“We need more leverage,” he said.

Mara looked at the emergency release wheel on the wall. It controlled the iron shutters fitted around the lantern room, relics from wartime blackout procedures.

“What if we don’t tip the lens?” she said. “What if we tip the room?”

Vale followed her gaze.

“That releases the shutters.”

“And the shutters are attached above the windows.”

Understanding appeared in his exhausted eyes.

They worked quickly. Vale looped the maintenance chain through the lens frame. Mara ran it across the floor and hooked it to the lowest shutter. When she spun the emergency wheel, counterweights groaned inside the walls.

Iron shutters descended around the lantern room.

The chain tightened.

For a moment, nothing happened.

Then the shutter tore free.

Its full weight dragged against the chain. The great lens leaned toward the eastern window. Bolts burst from the floor one after another.

Creatures spilled into the room.

Vale fought them with the iron bar. Mara kept turning the wheel. Claws caught her coat and dragged her backward. She slipped one arm out, leaving the yellow fabric in a creature’s hands.

The lens struck the window.

Glass exploded into the storm.

Wind roared through the opening. Rain swept sideways across the floor. The lens hung halfway out, balanced on the broken frame.

“It’s stuck!” Mara shouted.

Vale swung the bar into the last brass support.

The support bent but held.

A creature seized him from behind.

Vale’s eyes met Mara’s. He did not call for help. He only nodded toward the bar.

Finish it.

Mara took the iron tool and struck the support.

Once.

Twice.

On the third blow, the brass split.

The lens fell.

For an instant, it remained visible beyond the window: a palace of glass turning through the rain, scattering green light into a thousand stars.

Then it vanished into the sea.

The underwater glow went out.

Every creature stopped moving.

The one holding Vale released him. All around the lantern room, pale heads tilted, listening to a sound Mara could not hear.

Far below, something vast cried from the depths.

The note was so deep that Mara felt it in her bones rather than her ears. The island trembled. Cracks shot up the lighthouse walls.

The creatures fled.

They hurled themselves down the stairs, through the keeper’s room, and out into the storm. Those outside crawled across the rocks and disappeared beneath the waves.

On the horizon, the sea rose into a black hill.

Mara stared at it, certain that some impossible body was about to surface.

Instead, the hill sank.

The storm followed it.

Clouds unraveled above the island. Rain softened and stopped. The waves withdrew from the rocks, leaving foam behind. Through the broken windows, the first stars appeared.

Vale collapsed against the railing.

Mara sat beside him among the shattered glass.

For a while neither spoke.

At last Vale touched the cut on her cheek.

“You’ll have a scar.”

“So will you.”

He looked down at the deep scratches along his arms. “Mine will be easier to explain.”

“What will you say?”

“Accident during maintenance.”

“And the lens?”

“Accident during maintenance.”

“And the drowned people who climbed the tower?”

He looked toward the dark sea.

“Very persistent inspectors.”

Mara laughed.

The sound surprised her. It rose through the ruined lantern room, unsteady at first, then clear. Vale began laughing too. They laughed until it hurt, until the terror drained from them and left only exhaustion.

Then Mara remembered the passenger ship.

She checked her watch.

Seven forty-three.

“The Eastern Star is due near the reef in seventeen minutes,” she said. “Without the lighthouse, it won’t see the channel.”

Vale struggled to stand.

“The radio.”

They hurried down. Black water covered the stairs, but the creatures were gone. In the keeper’s room, chairs had been overturned and every jar on the shelves had shattered.

Vale switched on the radio.

Only static answered.

He adjusted the frequency. “Eastern Star, this is Saint Orison Lighthouse. Do you copy?”

The speaker hissed.

He tried again.

A voice emerged, faint but understandable.

“Saint Orison, this is Eastern Star. We copy. Your light is out.”

“Alter course ten degrees north immediately,” Vale said. “Keep north for three miles, then return to your heading.”

“Reason?”

Vale looked at Mara.

“Maintenance,” she said.

He pressed the transmitter. “Emergency maintenance.”

There was a pause.

“Understood, Saint Orison. Altering course.”

Mara sank into a chair.

An hour later, the ship passed safely beyond the reef. Its deck lights moved along the horizon like a small golden town. The sight of it made Mara ache with relief.

At dawn, the causeway emerged from the falling tide.

The director arrived before the supply crew. He crossed alone in a gray coat, stepping carefully around pools left in the road. He stopped when he saw the broken lantern room.

“You received my letter,” he said.

Mara stood in the lighthouse doorway, wearing one of Vale’s sweaters. “Eventually.”

The director removed his hat. “I’m sorry.”

“You sent me without warning.”

“I needed the message delivered.”

“You have a telephone.”

“The line was dead.”

“A radio.”

“Vale wasn’t answering.”

“You could have told me before I left.”

The director looked ashamed. “Would you have gone?”

“No.”

“That is why.”

Mara slapped him.

The sound echoed across the rocks.

Vale, standing behind her, nodded with approval.

The director touched his reddening cheek. “I suppose I deserved that.”

“You suppose correctly.”

He looked past them into the tower. “Is it gone?”

“The lens is,” Vale said.

“And the other thing?”

Mara watched the calm water around the island. Sunlight glittered on the surface. Nothing moved beneath it.

“I don’t know,” she said.

The director sighed. “We’ll build an electric beacon on the mainland. No lens. No keeper.”

Vale showed no reaction, but Mara saw his hand tighten around the doorframe.

“What happens to him?” she asked.

“Retirement, if he wants it.”

“And if I don’t?” Vale said.

The director glanced at the damaged lighthouse. “There’s nothing left to keep.”

Vale looked upward, toward the empty lantern room.

“There’s always something left to keep.”

By noon, the supply crew had arrived. They boarded the windows, repaired the radio line, and searched the rocks for wreckage. They found brass bolts, pieces of iron, and Mara’s yellow coat tangled near the water.

They did not find a single fragment of the lens.

Mara returned to the mainland that evening. For several weeks she woke each night hearing three knocks at her window. She would sit in darkness until dawn, telling herself it was a branch or rain or the ordinary language of an old building.

In November, she resigned from the office.

The director asked where she would go.

“Back to the island,” she said.

“There’s no position there.”

“I know.”

Winter came early that year. Mara moved into the keeper’s room, and Vale took the smaller chamber beside the storehouse. They repaired the tower but left the lantern room empty. During the day they recorded the weather, watched passing ships, and searched the sea with ordinary binoculars.

At night they lit no lamps above the ground floor.

Sailors began reporting a strange darkness near Saint Orison Island, a patch of night where stars seemed to vanish. Some claimed they heard singing beneath their ships. Others saw a green light moving far below the waves.

Mara and Vale wrote down every report.

Years passed.

Nothing climbed the tower again.

Then, on a windless night in the first week of spring, Mara woke to three quiet knocks.

She sat up.

The room was dark. Moonlight touched the floor.

Three more knocks came, not from the window, but from beneath the bed.

Mara reached for the iron bar she kept beside her.

Before she could move, a child’s voice spoke from under the floorboards.

“Please,” it whispered. “We found your light.”

Mara held her breath.

Far out at sea, something green opened its eye.

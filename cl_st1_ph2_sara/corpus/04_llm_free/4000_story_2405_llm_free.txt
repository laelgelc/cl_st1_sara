# The Museum of Lost Weather

On the morning the rain disappeared, Mara Vale found a cloud in the bread drawer.

It was no larger than a cat, gray at the edges and white in the middle, trembling faintly between a loaf of rye and a jar of cinnamon. When she opened the drawer, it rolled forward and released three drops of water onto her slippers.

Mara stared at it.

The cloud gave a small, embarrassed rumble.

“Well,” she said, “you cannot stay there.”

She fetched the blue glass jar from the top shelf, the one her grandmother had once used for preserving plums. With both hands, she coaxed the cloud inside. It resisted by growing colder, but eventually it folded itself through the mouth of the jar like wool being pushed into a bottle.

Mara sealed the lid. Thunder murmured against the glass.

Outside, the town of Bellwether was waking beneath a sky as clean and empty as a sheet of polished steel.

For three hundred and eleven years, it had rained every Tuesday in Bellwether. The rain began at seven in the morning and ended at seven in the evening. It fell steadily but politely, rinsing the red roofs, filling the public cisterns, and giving everyone an excellent reason to drink tea indoors.

That day was Tuesday.

The hour was seven fifteen.

There was no rain.

Mara carried the jar to the window. Across the street, neighbors stood on their steps in robes and dressing gowns, looking upward. Mr. Pimm, the clockmaker, held out a palm as if waiting for change. Two children ran in circles, laughing at the dry stones. Mrs. Orrel from the bakery was already shouting that the wells would fail and the wheat would die.

At seven thirty, the town bell rang.

At seven thirty-two, it rang again.

At seven thirty-five, Mara put on her green coat, slipped the jar into her satchel, and walked toward the Museum of Lost Weather.

The museum stood at the northern edge of Bellwether, where the cobbled streets gave way to pale grass and chalk hills. It was an old train station that had not seen a train in nearly a century. Its clock always showed four minutes past midnight, and ivy covered the walls so thickly that swallows nested above the ticket windows.

Mara had worked there since she was fifteen. At twenty-six, she was its curator, caretaker, guide, cleaner, and only employee.

Most people believed the museum was a harmless collection of antique instruments. They came to see brass barometers, cracked thermometers, and paintings of famous storms. Schoolchildren pressed their noses to display cases while teachers explained how their ancestors had once feared lightning.

But behind the public galleries were locked rooms, and inside those rooms the lost weather of the world waited in silence.

There were bottles of fog collected from vanished harbors. There was a snowflake that had fallen in midsummer and refused to melt. A wooden box contained the last warm wind from an island swallowed by the sea. A silver cage held seven sunbeams stolen during an eclipse. On the lowest shelf of the western vault sat a thimbleful of hail that whispered the names of everyone it had ever struck.

Mara had inherited the keys from her grandmother, Elianor Vale, who had inherited them from hers.

“Weather remembers,” Grandmother Elianor used to say. “It remembers where it has been, and sometimes it remembers where it belongs.”

Mara reached the museum to find the mayor waiting on the steps.

Mayor Tallow was a wide man with a narrow mustache. He wore his ceremonial chain over his nightshirt and had put his boots on the wrong feet.

“You are late,” he said.

“The museum opens at nine.”

“This is not a museum matter.”

“Then you are early.”

He glared at her. Behind him stood three members of the town council, a farmer, the chief constable, and Mrs. Orrel, who appeared to have come straight from her bakery with flour in her hair.

“The rain is gone,” the mayor announced.

“So I noticed.”

“You are responsible.”

Everyone looked at Mara.

She considered denying it. Instead, she unlocked the door and walked inside.

They followed her into the entrance hall, where a painted blue ceiling showed every kind of cloud. Sunlight poured through the tall windows and illuminated dust that should have been invisible on a rainy Tuesday.

Mayor Tallow jabbed a finger at her satchel.

“What is in the jar?”

“A cloud.”

Mrs. Orrel gasped. The farmer removed his hat.

“A cloud?” said the mayor.

“A small one.”

“You have stolen the rain!”

“I found it in my bread drawer.”

“That is exactly where a weather thief would hide it.”

“If I had stolen twelve hours of rain,” Mara said, “I would require a larger drawer.”

The chief constable tried not to smile.

Mara led them down the east corridor to the Forecasting Room. It had once been the stationmaster’s office. Maps covered the walls, and dozens of instruments clicked and spun on long oak tables. Needles trembled over paper. Brass cups turned in the still air. A row of glass spheres glowed with dim inner colors.

Every instrument pointed toward the same conclusion.

Nothing was coming.

The mayor studied the devices with suspicion. “Can you repair it?”

“The rain?”

“The schedule.”

“It may not be broken.”

“Then where is it?”

Mara set the blue jar on the table. The little cloud bumped anxiously against the glass.

“Lost,” she said. “Or taken.”

A silence fell.

Mrs. Orrel crossed herself.

Mayor Tallow leaned close. “By whom?”

Before Mara could answer, something struck the roof.

Once.

Twice.

Then came a sound like a thousand fingers tapping on slate.

Everyone looked upward.

“Hail?” whispered the farmer.

Mara shook her head. The air had grown warm.

She hurried outside.

Black seeds were falling from the sky.

They rattled on the museum roof, bounced across the steps, and collected in the grass. Each was glossy, hard, and shaped like a teardrop. When Mara picked one up, it cracked open in her palm.

A tiny green shoot emerged.

Within seconds it curled around her finger.

The council members shouted as the seeds began sprouting everywhere. Vines crawled over the road. Flowers opened between stones. A young pear tree burst from the mayor’s left boot and lifted his foot six inches off the ground.

Mara tore the vine from her hand and held the broken seed close to her eye.

Along its inner shell, someone had carved a symbol: a circle crossed by a crooked line.

She knew that mark.

Her grandmother had drawn it in the margins of old catalogues, always beside entries that were missing or destroyed.

The Sign of the Weathermaker.

“Inside,” Mara said.

They retreated as green vines swallowed the museum steps.

In the locked archive beneath the building, Mara found Grandmother Elianor’s final ledger. It was bound in sealskin and smelled of dust, salt, and the faintest trace of smoke.

Mayor Tallow hovered while she turned the pages.

“What is a Weathermaker?” he demanded.

“Someone from a story.”

“I dislike stories during emergencies.”

“Then you must dislike most explanations.”

Mara found the marked page.

The entry was dated eighty-seven years earlier.

The Weathermaker does not command weather. He bargains with it. He carries no storms of his own but persuades storms to abandon their paths. He offers the wind new countries, the snow warmer dreams, the lightning towers higher than mountains. Where he passes, seasons lose their way.

Beneath the words was a pencil sketch of a tall, thin man holding an umbrella made of feathers.

The rest of the page had been torn out.

The farmer shifted uneasily. “Why would he take our rain?”

“Perhaps because it was tired of falling here,” Mara said.

“Tired?”

“It has followed the same route for three centuries.”

“It is rain,” said Mayor Tallow. “Rain does not grow tired.”

From inside the blue jar came a low growl.

Everyone turned toward it.

Mara lifted the jar. The cloud had darkened. Tiny sparks moved through its center, illuminating an image within the mist: a road, a black carriage, and wheels that did not touch the ground.

The image vanished.

Mara understood.

“This cloud knows where the rain went.”

Mayor Tallow nodded sharply. “Excellent. Constable, gather six men. We will pursue the thief.”

“No,” Mara said. “Storms dislike crowds.”

“Storms do not have opinions.”

The jar cracked.

A single bolt of lightning leaped out, struck the mayor’s ceremonial chain, and knocked him into a cabinet of bottled moonlight.

After that, Mara was permitted to go alone.

She left Bellwether at noon on the museum’s oldest bicycle. The blue jar was strapped into the front basket, cushioned with her green scarf. Whenever Mara reached a fork in the road, the little cloud pressed itself toward the correct direction.

Behind her, the black-seed rain had stopped, but Bellwether was now covered in vines. Chimneys sprouted roses. Cabbages grew from gutters. A single oak tree had risen in the town square, taking the statue of Bellwether’s founder into its branches.

Ahead, the chalk hills rolled toward the horizon.

Mara pedaled for hours beneath the empty sky.

She found the first sign of the Weathermaker near an abandoned mill. A circle of frost glittered in the grass, though the afternoon was hot. In its center lay a dead butterfly, perfectly preserved in ice.

The second sign appeared beyond the hills, where a warm wind blew from the north carrying the smell of oranges and distant seas.

At sunset, Mara reached the Old King’s Road.

The black carriage waited there.

It was long and narrow, painted with constellations. Its wheels hovered a hand’s breadth above the ground. Six white birds stood in place of horses, harnessed with silver thread. On the roof sat an enormous closed umbrella made of black and crimson feathers.

A man was making tea beside the road.

He was tall and thin, just as in her grandmother’s sketch, but younger than Mara expected. His hair was the pale gold of winter sunlight. His coat changed color whenever he moved: gray, blue, green, then gray again.

He poured a second cup.

“Mara Vale,” he said.

“You know me?”

“I knew your grandmother.”

“That is not what I asked.”

He smiled. “No. It rarely is.”

Mara dismounted. The cloud in the jar had begun to churn.

“Did you take Bellwether’s rain?”

“I invited it to leave.”

“Where is it?”

“In the carriage.”

Mara looked at the narrow door. Water dripped steadily from its lower edge, though the ground beneath remained dry.

“You cannot keep it.”

“I do not intend to.”

“Then return it.”

The Weathermaker lifted his tea. “Ask it yourself.”

He opened the carriage door.

Mara expected a flood.

Instead, she saw a vast ocean hanging in darkness.

Rain fell upward from black waves into endless thunderclouds. Lightning moved like silver fish beneath the surface. In the distance stood a door of yellow light.

The impossible space within the carriage smelled of wet stone, pine forests, and every storm Mara had known as a child.

She stepped inside.

The sea did not drown her. She walked across its surface as if it were glass. Above her—or perhaps below her—the stolen rain of Bellwether gathered into the shape of a woman.

Her hair streamed in rivers. Her eyes were gray pools. When she spoke, water trembled all the way to the horizon.

“Vale,” said the rain.

Mara’s mouth had gone dry. “You know my family?”

“Your grandmothers kept what the world abandoned.”

“They protected it.”

“They locked it away.”

Mara thought of the museum vaults: the caged sunbeams, the bottled fog, the wind sealed inside its wooden box.

“Some weather is dangerous,” she said.

“All weather is dangerous.”

“Then why did you leave Bellwether?”

The rain descended until its face was level with hers.

“Do you know how many times I have fallen on the same roofs?”

“One rain each Tuesday for three hundred and eleven years.”

“Sixteen thousand two hundred and twenty-seven Tuesdays,” said the rain. “I have polished every chimney. I have traced every street. I know which windows leak and which children leave buckets beneath the eaves. I have filled your wells and turned your mills. I have listened to generations complain that I came too often, too cold, too loudly.”

Its voice deepened, and the ocean beneath Mara’s feet began to ripple.

“No one asked whether I wished to fall.”

Mara wanted to say that rain did not wish. Yet she remembered the cloud sulking in her bread drawer and lightning striking the mayor.

“What do you wish?” she asked.

The whole ocean became still.

“To wander.”

The word echoed like distant thunder.

“Where?”

“Over deserts. Into burning forests. Across cities that have forgotten the smell of wet earth. I wish to become snow on mountains. I wish to sleep in glaciers and wake in rivers. I wish to fall where no one expects me.”

“And Bellwether?”

“Let Bellwether learn the sky is not a clock.”

Mara looked toward the yellow door in the distance. Through it she could see her town: the red roofs, the museum, the square where a new oak tree swayed. She saw dry reservoirs. Withered gardens. Empty wells.

“They depend on you,” she said.

“They depend on habit.”

“They will suffer.”

“All change brings weather of its own.”

Mara closed her eyes.

The easy choice was to demand the rain’s return. The museum held instruments older than Bellwether itself. Grandmother Elianor’s notes described knots that could bind a north wind and bells that could call storms from the sea. Mara could restore Tuesday. She could send everyone home beneath umbrellas, relieved and unchanged.

But she had spent eleven years caring for lost weather without ever wondering whether it wanted to be found.

She opened her eyes.

“Give us one last rain.”

The rain watched her.

“One day,” Mara continued. “Enough to fill the cisterns and soften the fields. Enough time for Bellwether to prepare. After that, you may go where you choose.”

“And what will you give me?”

Mara thought of the locked rooms beneath the museum.

“A map,” she said.

The rain tilted its head.

“A map of every lost thing in my care. The fogs, the winds, the snow, the sunbeams. Some may wish to remain. Some may wish to leave. I will ask them.”

A smile moved across the rain’s face like sunlight over water.

“Your grandmother would disapprove.”

“I know.”

“That is why she chose you.”

Before Mara could ask what that meant, the ocean rose around her.

She fell through thunder.

She landed on the road beside her bicycle. Night had come. The black carriage was closed, and the Weathermaker was packing away his teacups.

“Is it settled?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Then you should hurry.”

The six white birds lifted their heads. Far to the south, clouds were gathering.

Mara mounted her bicycle.

“Why did my grandmother mark your sign?” she asked.

The Weathermaker fastened his shifting coat.

“Because she spent her life trying to catch me.”

“Why?”

“I once stole a rainbow from her wedding.”

“Did you return it?”

“Eventually.”

He climbed into the carriage.

“Where?”

The carriage began to move.

He leaned out the window, smiling.

“In you.”

Then the birds ran forward, wings spread, and the carriage rose into the night.

Mara rode back to Bellwether beneath a sky full of stars.

At dawn, she reached the first hill above the town. People were already waiting in the streets. They held buckets, bowls, kettles, barrels, flowerpots, helmets, and one polished silver trumpet. Every face turned upward.

The clouds arrived from the south.

They covered the sky in minutes, vast and dark and alive.

At seven in the morning, though it was Wednesday, the rain began.

It fell harder than anyone in Bellwether had ever known. It drummed on roofs and roared through gutters. It filled the cisterns before noon. Children danced barefoot in the square while adults dragged barrels beneath overflowing eaves. Farmers opened channels to guide water into ponds. Mrs. Orrel set out every mixing bowl in her bakery.

Mayor Tallow stood under an umbrella, shouting instructions no one could hear.

Mara did not seek shelter.

She stood on the museum steps as water ran through her hair and down her face. For a moment, the rain gathered around her in the shape of two gray eyes.

Then it fell apart.

By evening, the clouds had moved east.

The next Tuesday was dry.

The Tuesday after that brought a brief shower at midnight.

Bellwether complained, adapted, argued, and adapted again. The town built new reservoirs. Farmers planted deeper-rooted crops. Roof gardens appeared where vines from the seed storm had taken hold. The oak in the square became a meeting place, though the founder’s statue remained stuck in its branches.

As for Mara, she changed the museum.

She began with the warm wind in the wooden box.

When she opened the lid, the wind circled the room, smelling of salt and hibiscus. It hesitated at the window.

“You may stay,” Mara told it. “Or you may go.”

The wind rushed out so joyfully that it tore every label from the eastern gallery.

The seven sunbeams chose to remain until winter.

The bottled fog left at once and settled over the mayor’s house for nine days.

The midsummer snowflake asked to see the mountains.

Some weather stayed. Some departed. Some returned years later with stories written in frost, dust, and rainbows across the museum windows.

Mara kept the blue glass jar on the shelf above her bread drawer. The little cloud had escaped during the final storm, leaving behind only three drops of water and a thin crack in the glass.

Many years later, on a bright Tuesday morning, a child visiting the museum asked Mara whether weather truly remembered.

Mara looked through the window.

Over Bellwether, the sky was clear except for one small cloud. It hovered above the red roofs, gray at the edges and white in the middle.

As Mara watched, it released exactly three drops of rain.

Then it rolled eastward, free and unhurried, following no schedule but its own.

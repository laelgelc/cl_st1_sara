## The Cartographer of Lost Roads

Every morning, Mara Vale drew a road that no longer existed.

She worked in the eastern tower of the Royal Survey Office, in a room with tall windows, slanted light, and enough dust to soften the edges of everything. Before her lay maps of the kingdom: official maps, military maps, farmers’ maps marked with rainfall and wells, and children’s maps where sea monsters filled the empty corners.

Mara’s task was to correct them.

A bridge washed away? She erased it.

A village abandoned after a winter fever? She removed its name.

A new road cut through the king’s forest? She measured its turns and added it in precise red ink.

But each morning, before she began the day’s official work, Mara took out a square of forbidden paper and drew something that had vanished.

On Monday, she drew the old footpath from Bellweather to the coast, closed after the cliffs collapsed.

On Tuesday, she drew a lane that once led to an orchard outside Greyhaven, though the orchard had become a quarry and the lane was buried under broken stone.

On Wednesday, she drew the narrow road to her childhood home.

She remembered every bend.

It ran north from the village of Dalmere, crossed a bridge no wider than a cart, climbed through a wood of pale birches, and ended beside a blue gate. Beyond that gate had stood a house with a leaking roof, a wild garden, and a kitchen that always smelled of rosemary.

The house was gone. The village was gone. Even the river had changed its course.

Yet Mara drew the road carefully, and when she finished, a small black door appeared in the wall behind her desk.

She stared at it.

The door was no taller than her knee. It had a brass handle and a keyhole shaped like a star.

Mara crouched beside it. The tower wall was solid stone, nearly three feet thick. There could be no room behind the door, only open air and a long fall into the courtyard.

She touched the handle.

The door opened onto darkness.

Cold air breathed through the gap, carrying the scent of rosemary.

Mara slammed it shut.

For the rest of the morning she made mistakes. She placed a river two miles east of its proper course. She misspelled the name of a mountain she had known since childhood. When the chief surveyor asked whether she was unwell, she said the light was troubling her eyes.

At midday, the king’s bells rang over the capital.

No one in the eastern tower knew why.

By evening, soldiers had closed the bridges, and notices were posted in every square:

**By order of His Majesty, all maps are to be surrendered for inspection. Unauthorized charts, diagrams, and geographic records are forbidden.**

Mara read the notice twice.

Then she returned to her office and locked the door.

The little black door waited behind her desk.

She opened it again.

This time she put her face close to the gap.

On the other side was not darkness but night: a deep country night, silvered by stars. A road stretched away between low stone walls. Birches shivered in the wind.

It was the road to Dalmere.

Mara sat back on her heels.

She had always believed maps were records. They showed where things were or, in the case of her secret drawings, where things had been. They could lie, of course, but even a false map was only ink and intention.

A map could not open a door.

Someone knocked.

Mara covered the little door with a stack of atlases just as the chief surveyor entered.

Edrin Holt was a thin man with silver spectacles and a habit of rubbing ink from his fingers, though the stains had become permanent years before.

“You are still here,” he said.

“So are you.”

“The inspectors are coming at dawn.”

“Why?”

Edrin closed the office door behind him. “The king’s western army has disappeared.”

Mara almost laughed. “Armies do not disappear.”

“This one did. Ten thousand men, three thousand horses, wagons, physicians, engineers. They marched toward the border six days ago. Yesterday, a messenger returned alone. He claims the road turned beneath their feet.”

“Turned?”

“He said the milestones changed. The mountains moved. By sunset, the army was marching through a valley that appears on no chart.”

Mara glanced at the atlases hiding the door.

Edrin noticed.

“What is behind your desk?”

“Dust.”

“Mara.”

She trusted Edrin as much as she trusted anyone, which was not the same as trusting him completely. He had found her half-starved at seventeen, sleeping beneath the Survey Office stairs, and had offered her work copying labels. He had taught her to mix waterproof ink and calculate distance from shadows. He had also taught her that kings valued maps because maps made the world appear obedient.

She moved the atlases.

Edrin knelt before the little door. His spectacles slipped down his nose.

“When did this appear?”

“This morning.”

“What did you draw?”

Mara showed him the road to Dalmere.

For a long time, he said nothing.

Then he whispered, “Burn it.”

She pulled the map away. “Why?”

“Because this is old craft.”

“You know what it is?”

“I know stories.”

“I have no use for stories.”

“Then develop one quickly, because soldiers will search this room tomorrow.”

He stood, walked to the window, and looked down at the torchlit courtyard.

“Before the first kings,” he said, “roads were not built. They were invited. A skilled cartographer could draw a path to a place that had been forgotten, hidden, or not yet born. But every new road displaced another. Nothing can connect without separating something else.”

Mara touched the map’s edge.

“That is superstition.”

“So is a door through a tower wall.”

“Why would the army disappear now?”

Edrin turned. “Because three months ago, the king hired a cartographer from the south.”

Mara knew the man by reputation: Orsino Vey, a traveler dressed in white who never carried a compass. He had dined twice at the palace and then vanished from public view.

“What did the king ask him to draw?”

“A road to victory.”

The words seemed to chill the room.

Edrin continued. “His Majesty wishes to conquer the western republics before winter. Their fortresses block every pass. Their rivers are mined. So he ordered Vey to create a road around all obstacles.”

“And Vey did?”

“Perhaps. But a road to victory may not lead where a king expects.”

Below, the courtyard gates opened. Soldiers entered in two lines.

“They are early,” Edrin said.

Mara lifted the forbidden map. “Can this door take us to Dalmere?”

“If that is what you truly drew.”

“Dalmere was destroyed twenty years ago.”

“Lost is not the same as destroyed.”

The soldiers reached the tower stairs.

Edrin gathered several official charts and shoved them into the stove.

“What are you doing?”

“Giving them something to find.”

Smoke filled the office. Footsteps thundered below.

The little door was too small to crawl through. Mara stared at it, then at her drawing. In the corner, she had written the scale: one inch to one mile.

She dipped her pen into black ink and changed it.

One inch to one hundred yards.

The door grew.

Stone groaned as the frame expanded upward. In seconds it was large enough for a person to pass through.

Edrin stared at her. “You learn quickly.”

The soldiers began striking the office door.

Mara rolled the drawing into a tube and tucked it inside her coat.

“After you,” she said.

They stepped through the black door.

The road was damp beneath Mara’s boots. Wind moved through the birches, stirring thousands of white leaves. Behind them stood the doorway alone, upright in the night, with the Survey Office visible beyond it.

The office door burst open.

Mara shut the black door and turned the star-shaped key. The doorway vanished, leaving a patch of grass between the stones.

For the first time in twenty years, she stood on the Dalmere road.

She remembered it as broader. That was the trick of childhood: every lane had been a highway, every hill a mountain, every summer nearly endless.

Edrin looked up at the stars. “We are far north of the capital.”

“We are far from anything.”

“Where does the road lead?”

“Home.”

They walked.

Mara expected the way to feel familiar, but memory came in fragments. Here was the ditch where her brother had found a fox cub. There was the tree split by lightning. Beyond the next hill should be the river bridge.

It appeared through the mist, narrow and silver with frost.

Someone stood in the middle of it.

Mara stopped.

The figure was an old woman wearing a red shawl. She leaned on a crooked staff and held a lantern whose flame burned blue.

“You took your time,” she called.

Edrin whispered, “Do you know her?”

“No.”

The old woman raised the lantern, and Mara saw a face lined like folded paper.

“But I know you,” the woman said. “Mara Vale, daughter of Elian, granddaughter of Sella, last mapmaker of Dalmere.”

“My father made chairs.”

“Poorly.”

That was true. Mara’s father had possessed great enthusiasm and little skill.

“Who are you?” Mara asked.

“Keeper of this road.”

“This road has no keeper.”

“Every lost road has one.”

The woman turned and walked across the bridge. “Come. They are already searching for you.”

“Who?”

“Everyone who fears where you might go.”

Beyond the bridge, the old birch wood opened around them. The woman led them down a trail Mara did not remember and into a clearing where a cottage leaned beneath a roof of moss.

Inside, maps covered every wall.

They were painted on cloth, scratched on bark, sewn into blankets, and carved into thin sheets of bone. None resembled the clean, measured charts of the Royal Survey Office. Rivers curled like sleeping animals. Mountains were drawn with faces. Roads changed direction when Mara looked away.

Edrin removed his spectacles. “Impossible.”

“People say that when they mean inconvenient,” said the old woman.

She introduced herself as Ina and poured tea into three mismatched cups.

Mara remained standing. “Why did my map bring us here?”

“Because you remembered the road accurately.”

“I have drawn lost roads before.”

“You remembered those with your mind. This one you remembered with grief.”

Mara disliked the answer because it felt true.

Ina pointed to a map stitched in blue thread. “Long ago, mapmakers understood that places exist in more than one manner. There are places made from stone and soil, and places made from memory, promise, fear, and hope. Usually they overlap. Sometimes they separate.”

“Dalmere still exists somewhere?” Mara asked.

“Many Dalmeres exist. The village before the fire. The village after it. The village as you remember it. The village as your father dreamed it might become. A skilled mapmaker can reach any of them.”

Mara set her untouched tea on the table. “Then I can go back.”

“To what?”

“Before the soldiers came. Before they burned the houses.”

“To warn your family?”

“To save them.”

Ina’s expression softened, which angered Mara more than cruelty would have.

“You may reach a memory,” the old woman said. “You cannot force it to become your present.”

“What good is this craft, then?”

“The same good as any road. It allows passage. It does not promise that you will like what waits at the end.”

Edrin walked among the maps. “Can this help us find the king’s army?”

Ina looked toward a dark corner.

There hung a huge white chart with a single gold line crossing its center. The line began at the capital, curved west, then twisted into a knot.

“Vey’s road,” she said. “He drew a path to victory, but the word has too many meanings. Victory for the king. Victory for his enemies. Victory for the soldiers who wish to return home. Victory for the dead, who are patient and eventually take every battlefield.”

“So the road divided,” Mara said.

“It is dividing still. With each mile, it creates new destinations and erases old routes. Soon the kingdom will be cut into pieces no ordinary traveler can cross.”

“Can we stop it?”

“Vey could.”

“Where is he?”

Ina tapped the knot in the golden road. “At the end.”

Mara studied the map. “Draw us a way there.”

“I cannot.”

“You are keeper of the road.”

“One road. One memory. That is the limit of my gift.”

Ina opened a wooden box and took out a compass. Its needle spun wildly.

“Vey was the greatest living cartographer of hidden paths. You may be greater, if you survive long enough to make your mistakes.”

“I am not going to war.”

“The war is already walking toward every door.”

Outside, a horn sounded in the wood.

Ina extinguished the lantern.

Through the window, Mara saw torchlight between the birches.

“The king’s soldiers?” Edrin asked.

“Worse,” Ina said. “His inspectors.”

Men in grey coats entered the clearing. Unlike ordinary soldiers, they carried no swords. Each held a long iron ruler.

Their leader unrolled a document.

“By authority of the Crown,” he announced, “all unregistered roads, paths, gates, bridges, and passages are declared unlawful. Surrender all maps.”

Ina muttered, “Kings have been making that declaration for a thousand years.”

The inspectors advanced.

Where their iron rulers touched the earth, the ground became blank and white. Grass vanished. Stones lost their shape. One ruler struck the cottage fence, and half the fence ceased to exist.

Mara understood why the Royal Survey Office had been searched. The king did not merely want maps. He wanted ownership of every possible way to move.

Ina thrust the compass into Mara’s hand. “Draw.”

“With what?”

“Anything.”

Mara seized a charred stick from the fireplace and knelt on the floor.

“What destination?” Edrin asked.

The front door shook beneath a blow.

Mara thought of the capital, but they would be arrested there. She thought of Dalmere, but grief was a circle, not an escape. She thought of the western army and Vey’s road knotted around itself.

Then she remembered an unfinished chart in the Survey Office archives. It showed an ancient route from Dalmere to a monastery in the western mountains. No one had found the route in two hundred years.

She began to draw.

The cottage door splintered.

Mara marked the bridge, the ridge, and the dry riverbed. She drew the mountain as she remembered it from the archive: three peaks like broken teeth. The charcoal line flowed beneath her hand.

A doorway opened in the cottage floor.

Not a hole—a doorway, upright despite lying flat, filled with a pale mountain sky.

Edrin jumped through.

Ina shoved the box of maps into Mara’s arms.

“Aren’t you coming?” Mara asked.

“I keep this road.”

The inspectors entered.

Ina swung her crooked staff and struck the first man across the face.

Mara jumped.

She fell sideways and landed on a slope of red stones beneath a cold dawn. Edrin pulled her away as the doorway snapped shut.

They stood high in the western mountains.

Below them, an army filled the valley.

Thousands of tents crowded the banks of a black river. Riderless horses wandered among cold fires. Men moved without purpose, exhausted and afraid.

Across the valley floor ran dozens of golden roads. Some climbed sheer cliffs. Some crossed the river and returned to the same bank. Some ended in the sky.

At their center stood a white pavilion.

“Vey,” Mara said.

They descended into the camp.

No one tried to stop them. The soldiers had lost interest in strangers. Mara passed men arguing over maps that changed in their hands. One captain insisted the capital lay north; another pointed south. A young soldier sat beside a milestone that read:

**HOME — 1 MILE**

He had walked around it until his boots bled.

Inside the white pavilion, Orsino Vey was dying.

He lay on a couch surrounded by maps. Golden ink stained his lips and hands. He was younger than Mara expected, perhaps thirty, with black hair and a narrow, fevered face.

When he saw her, he smiled.

“Elian’s daughter.”

“How does everyone know me?”

“Your father spoke of you often.”

Mara forgot the army, the roads, and the king.

“You knew my father?”

“He taught me.”

“My father made chairs.”

“Poorly.”

Edrin gave a startled laugh.

Vey pushed himself upright. “Elian hid his craft after Dalmere was destroyed. He believed mapmakers had caused enough suffering. But I found him years later. I begged him to teach me.”

“And then you made a weapon for the king.”

“I intended to betray him.” Vey coughed, and gold flecked his sleeve. “I drew a road not to his victory, but to the end of his wars.”

“You failed.”

“Clearly.”

Outside, the valley shifted. A golden road rose from the earth like a ribbon caught in wind. Tents overturned as men ran.

Vey looked at the box in Mara’s hands. “Ina is dead, then.”

“I don’t know.”

“You carry her maps. She would never surrender them while she lived.”

Mara set the box down.

“How do we undo this?”

“We cannot erase the road. The king’s desire is too strong. So is the fear of his army. So is the hatred of his enemies. The map feeds on all possible victories.”

“Then we choose one.”

Vey nodded.

Edrin said, “A victory without conquest.”

“A road home,” Mara answered.

Vey shook his head. “Home means ten thousand different places.”

“Good.”

She spread a blank sheet over the largest table.

Vey watched her. “What are you doing?”

“Dividing the road.”

“It is already divided.”

“Not enough.”

Mara drew the valley first. Every map needs an honest beginning. She marked the river, the camps, the white pavilion, and each visible thread of gold.

Then she began drawing roads outward.

One went to a fishing village in the north. Another to a farm beside a green lake. Another to a crowded street in the capital. She could not know every soldier’s home, but perhaps she did not need to. The soldiers knew.

Mara carried the map outside.

“Mark where you wish to go!” she shouted.

At first, the men only stared.

Then the young soldier with bleeding feet approached. Mara gave him a pen.

He drew a crooked line east and wrote a name at its end: **Lysa**.

A road appeared beneath his feet.

It was a simple dirt lane bordered by summer grass. At the far end stood a woman outside a cottage, shading her eyes.

The soldier dropped the pen and ran.

Others crowded around.

They marked towns, islands, houses without names, and graves they wished to visit before going elsewhere. Some drew roads to wives. Some drew roads away from them. One old cook drew a tavern that had burned down thirty years before, laughed when its door appeared, and stepped through.

With every new path, the golden road weakened.

Officers tried to restore order, but their commands meant little beside open roads. By noon, half the army was gone.

By sunset, only the king’s most loyal regiment remained.

Their general entered the pavilion and tore the map in two.

The valley groaned.

Every road flickered.

“You are deserters,” the general said. “All of you.”

Edrin stepped between him and Mara. The general struck him with the hilt of his sword.

Mara caught the torn pieces before they touched the ground.

The general raised his weapon again.

A hand seized his wrist.

Vey stood behind him, trembling with effort, golden light leaking through his skin.

“There is one final road,” Vey said.

He pressed his stained palm against the general’s chest.

A golden doorway opened behind the man.

Through it, Mara saw the royal throne room. The king stood beside a table, surrounded by ministers and inspectors. He looked up in surprise.

Vey shoved the general through, then followed.

The doorway began to close.

“What are you doing?” Mara cried.

“Taking the road to its destination.”

The king’s face twisted in rage. He called for guards.

Vey smiled at Mara across the narrowing threshold.

“Every road to victory,” he said, “must end.”

The door vanished.

For one breath, nothing happened.

Then all the golden roads collapsed.

Light swept across the valley. The ground rolled like water, throwing everyone to their knees. Distant mountains slid along the horizon and settled into their proper places. The black river straightened.

Mara looked at the torn map in her hands.

The ink was ordinary.

Months later, the kingdom’s official charts showed the western war as a narrow red line ending at an unnamed valley. The king had disappeared. So had his general, his ministers, and every inspector in the palace.

Some claimed they had been swallowed by a road beneath the throne. Others said they still wandered through a corridor with no doors.

Mara returned to the capital only once.

She found the Royal Survey Office abandoned, its rooms stripped and its maps piled in the courtyard. No one had dared burn them. People feared maps now, though not enough to stop needing them.

In the eastern tower, Mara recovered her pens.

Then she went north with Edrin.

They rebuilt Ina’s cottage beside the Dalmere road. Travelers began to arrive: former soldiers, lost children, widows seeking vanished graves, explorers searching for cities that existed only in songs.

Mara helped when she could.

She learned to draw paths to forgotten wells, hidden gardens, and dreams abandoned too soon. She learned that some doors should remain closed. She learned never to promise that a road would be safe merely because it was beautiful.

Years passed.

One winter morning, Mara took out a square of paper and drew the road to her childhood home.

She marked the bridge, the birch wood, and the blue gate. Beyond the gate she drew the house with its leaking roof. Smoke rose from the chimney. Rosemary grew beneath the kitchen window.

A black door appeared beside the fire.

Mara opened it.

Her father sat at the kitchen table, struggling to repair a chair. Her mother kneaded bread. Her brother leaned against the window, forever twelve years old.

They did not see her.

Mara stood on the threshold for a long time.

Then her father looked up.

Not directly at her, but almost.

He smiled as if hearing a familiar footstep on the road.

Mara could have crossed. She could have sat beside them in a morning preserved by love and grief. Perhaps the door would have closed behind her. Perhaps she would not have cared.

Instead, she whispered goodbye.

She shut the door and erased the road.

Outside, someone called her name. A traveler had arrived carrying a map that ended at the edge of the sea.

Mara put on her coat and went to meet him.

# The Cartographer of Lost Roads

On the morning the road disappeared, Mara Venn was sharpening a pencil.

She sat by the eastern window of the map room, turning the cedar shaft against a small silver blade while rain wandered down the glass. Beyond it, the village of Bellweather crouched beneath the hills, all slate roofs, smoking chimneys, and gardens going brown with autumn.

The knock came just as the pencil point became perfect.

Three quick taps. A pause. Then two more.

Mara sighed. Everyone in Bellweather knew the map room opened at nine, and the brass clock above the door insisted it was only seven minutes past eight. Still, she crossed the room, stepping around cabinets labeled in her father’s narrow handwriting: RIVERS, BOUNDARIES, DISPUTED FIELDS, ROADS—CURRENT, and, in the lowest drawer, ROADS—LOST.

A boy stood outside in the rain.

He was perhaps twelve, with wet black hair pasted across his forehead and a wool coat too large for him. Mud covered his boots to the ankles.

“Are you the cartographer?” he asked.

“I am.”

“My name is Oren Vale. The road to my house is gone.”

Mara looked past him toward the village square. Three streets led away from it as they always had: Mill Lane, Church Street, and the old northern road that passed the cemetery before climbing into the hills.

“Which road?”

“Larkspur Way.”

“There is no Larkspur Way.”

“There was yesterday.”

Mara almost closed the door. She had heard many odd claims in the map room. Farmers blamed maps when fences moved. Travelers blamed them when they got lost. Once, a woman had demanded that Mara erase her husband’s favorite tavern, believing that if it vanished from the map, it would vanish from his habits as well.

But the boy’s face stopped her. He did not look confused. He looked frightened.

“Come in,” she said.

Oren stepped inside, dripping onto the floorboards. Mara handed him a towel, then unlocked the cabinet marked ROADS—CURRENT. From it she removed the map of Bellweather Parish, revised by her father sixteen years earlier and updated each spring by Mara.

“Show me.”

The boy leaned over the table. His finger hovered above the northern hills.

“Here. Larkspur Way turns off after the stone bridge, passes between two ash trees, and goes east. Our house is at the end.”

Mara examined the place he indicated. According to the map, there was only a ridge of uncultivated land beyond the bridge. No road. No house.

“What is your father’s name?”

“Elias Vale.”

“And your mother?”

“Seren.”

Neither name was familiar.

“Are you certain you’re from Bellweather?”

Oren frowned. “Where else would I be from?”

There were questions she could have asked, reasonable questions about relatives, school, the magistrate, and whether the boy had struck his head. Instead, Mara looked at his boots.

The mud on them was pale yellow.

There was no pale yellow soil in Bellweather Valley. The ground near the river was black, the hills red with iron. But her father had kept samples from every road he surveyed, and Mara remembered a small glass vial stored in the back cabinet. On its faded label he had written: LARKSPUR WAY—CHALK CLAY, EAST RIDGE.

Her heart began to beat faster.

“Wait here,” she told Oren.

She crossed to the lowest drawer.

ROADS—LOST had not been opened since her father’s death. The key resisted, and the wood groaned when she pulled the drawer out. Inside lay a dozen rolled maps, each tied with gray ribbon.

Mara found Larkspur Way near the bottom.

The parchment crackled as she spread it across the table. There it was: a thin road branching from the north road after the bridge, winding east between two ash trees. At its end, her father had drawn a house and written, in tiny letters, Vale residence.

Oren touched the inked roof.

“That’s my home.”

Mara stared at the date. The map had been made twenty-three years ago, when she was six.

Across one corner, in red ink, her father had added:

LOST, 14 OCTOBER. DO NOT ATTEMPT RECOVERY.

It was the fourteenth of October now.

The brass clock ticked above the door.

“My mother is still there,” Oren said. “She couldn’t cross.”

Mara looked up. “Cross what?”

“The place where the road ends.”

Outside, the rain thickened.

Mara rolled the old map and placed it in a leather tube. From hooks beside the door she took her waterproof cloak, compass, measuring chain, notebook, and surveyor’s staff.

“You’re coming?” Oren asked.

“A road cannot simply disappear.”

“It did.”

“Then we’ll find out why.”

They left the map room at half past eight.

Bellweather was waking slowly. The baker raised his shutters. A milk cart rattled over the square. No one paid much attention to Mara and the boy until they passed the last houses and took the northern road toward the hills.

The rain softened to mist.

At the cemetery, Oren glanced through the iron fence.

“My father is buried there,” he said.

Mara slowed. “When did he die?”

“Three years from now.”

She stopped.

Oren kept walking for several paces before realizing she was no longer beside him.

“What did you say?”

He turned, and for the first time Mara saw something older than childhood in his expression.

“It’s difficult to explain.”

“Try.”

“My father said the road doesn’t only go through the hills. It goes through years. Not always. Most days it’s an ordinary road. But every so often it becomes… loose.”

“Loose?”

“That was his word. Things wander in. Things wander out.”

Mara’s hand tightened around the surveyor’s staff.

“My father mapped your road twenty-three years ago.”

“I know.”

“How?”

“Because we have his map hanging in our kitchen.”

The mist collected on Oren’s lashes. He looked tired and very young.

“He came to our house when my mother was a girl,” he continued. “He warned her that Larkspur Way would disappear. She didn’t believe him then.”

“Why didn’t he bring her away?”

“He tried. She wouldn’t leave her parents.”

Mara remembered little of her sixth year. Her mother had still been alive. Her father had often vanished for days to measure boundaries and settle claims. Once he had returned with a broken arm and refused to say how it happened. Another time, Mara had woken in the night and found him burning a map in the garden.

At the stone bridge, they left the northern road.

Or tried to.

Beyond the bridge stood a grassy bank where Oren insisted the turning should be. Two ancient ash trees grew there, their wet leaves flashing silver in the wind, but no lane passed between them.

Oren climbed the bank and vanished.

Mara shouted his name.

His hand emerged from empty air.

She stumbled back.

“It’s here,” he called, his voice strangely distant.

The hand waited.

Mara thought of her father’s warning in red ink. She thought of Oren’s pale yellow mud. Then she gripped his fingers and stepped between the ash trees.

The world tilted.

For an instant she smelled hot dust, snow, roses, and wood smoke all at once. Voices rushed past her—laughing, crying, whispering words she could not understand. She saw the bridge collapse beneath a summer flood and stand whole again. She saw the ash trees as saplings, then as white stumps, then as shadows beneath unfamiliar stars.

Her boots struck yellow earth.

Larkspur Way stretched ahead.

The rain was gone. A cold, bright afternoon lay over fields of purple heather. Behind Mara, the ash trees framed not Bellweather but a wall of gray fog.

Oren released her hand.

“We’re close,” he said.

Mara opened her compass. The needle spun.

She took out the old map instead.

The road matched her father’s lines exactly. It curved along a low stone wall, crossed a stream by means of three flat rocks, and climbed toward a wood. Yet the map also contained details she had not noticed before. Small numbers crowded the margins: years, perhaps, connected by arrows. Some were crossed out. Others circled.

At the center of the page, her father had drawn a black mark.

“What is that?” Oren asked.

“I don’t know.”

They soon reached the same place on the road.

A milestone stood crooked beneath a hawthorn bush. It bore no village name, only a sentence carved deep into the stone:

EVERY ROAD REMEMBERS BOTH DIRECTIONS.

Mara touched the letters.

The landscape shuddered.

Heather became snow. The wood ahead burst into spring green, burned gold with autumn, and went bare. Day turned briefly to night. Stars wheeled overhead, then vanished into a copper dawn.

Oren seized her sleeve.

“It’s getting worse.”

“When did you leave home?”

“This morning.”

“Your morning or mine?”

He did not answer.

A sound came from beyond the milestone: the steady tap of wood against earth.

A man walked toward them.

He was tall and thin, wearing a dark coat of an old-fashioned cut. His left arm was bound in a sling, and he carried a surveyor’s staff in his right hand.

Mara knew his face before he came near enough for certainty.

Her father had been dead for eleven years.

Here, he was thirty-five.

He stopped when he saw her.

The surveyor’s staff slipped from his hand.

“Mara?”

She could not breathe.

He looked exactly as he did in her earliest memories: dark hair swept back, ink on his fingers, one eyebrow cut by a pale scar. She had forgotten the sound of his voice. Hearing it now opened something inside her she had kept locked since the winter he died.

“Papa,” she whispered.

He crossed the distance between them but halted an arm’s length away. His gaze moved over her face, her cloak, the leather map case at her shoulder.

“How old are you?”

“Twenty-nine.”

His eyes closed.

“So I fail.”

Oren tugged at Mara’s sleeve. “We have to hurry.”

Her father opened his eyes again. “The boy is right.”

“You knew this would happen,” Mara said. “You marked the map.”

“I suspected.”

“What is this road?”

“A mistake.” He bent to retrieve his staff. “Or a miracle behaving badly.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

The road trembled again. For a heartbeat her father became an old man, white-haired and stooped. Then he was young once more.

He glanced toward the fog behind them.

“You came from Bellweather?”

“Yes.”

“What year?”

She told him.

His face tightened. “Then the break spans at least twenty-three years.”

“My mother is trapped,” Oren said.

“Seren?” Mara’s father asked.

Oren nodded.

“Then she stayed after all.”

“Stayed after what?” Mara demanded.

But her father had begun walking quickly toward the wood.

They followed.

As they walked, he explained. Roads, he said, were agreements: between one place and another, between travelers and destinations, between memory and the ground beneath it. Most agreements held. Some weakened when villages emptied or names were forgotten. Larkspur Way was different. It had been built over an older path whose beginning and end had existed in different centuries. Each generation repaired the same road, never realizing they were pulling it through time.

“Today the agreement breaks,” he said. “If the road separates entirely, every traveler and house along it may be scattered across different years.”

“Can it be stopped?” Mara asked.

“Perhaps. At the far end is a gate. The road was first measured from there. A cartographer can redraw its course and fasten it to a single time.”

“You’re a cartographer.”

“I tried.”

He nodded toward his broken arm.

“You wrote not to attempt recovery.”

“I wrote that after I failed.”

“Then I’ve seen your future.”

“One of them.”

The wood rose around them. The trees flickered between acorns and ash. Birds sang backward. Once, Mara heard a woman call her name from among the trunks in her mother’s voice. She nearly left the road before her father caught her shoulder.

“Not every turning belongs to you,” he said.

She looked at his hand. Strong, warm, alive.

“Do you know how you die?” she asked.

“No.”

“Do you want to?”

“No.”

She had expected that answer. It hurt anyway.

At last they saw a house beyond the wood, white-walled and roofed in green slate. A woman stood before it holding a lantern. Though Oren had called her his mother, she appeared scarcely older than Mara.

“Oren!”

The boy ran to her. She dropped the lantern into the grass and embraced him fiercely.

“You crossed,” she said. “You foolish, impossible child.”

“I brought help.”

Seren looked at Mara, then at Mara’s father.

“You came back,” she said to him.

“I said I would.”

“You said that last winter.”

“For me, it has been four hours.”

Seren laughed once, without humor. “For us, thirteen years.”

The road groaned. A crack split the earth between the house and wood. Inside it, Mara glimpsed other places: a market crowded with people in bright clothing, a field beneath falling bombs, a silent city overgrown with vines.

“The gate,” her father said.

They hurried around the house.

At the end of Larkspur Way stood two stone pillars and an iron gate. Beyond it there was nothing—no field, no sky, only a whiteness so complete that Mara’s eyes watered.

Names had been carved into the pillars. Hundreds of them. Road builders, landowners, surveyors.

Near the bottom, she found her father’s name.

Beneath it, cut into the stone in letters weathered by centuries, was her own.

MARA VENN, CARTOGRAPHER.

“I’ve never been here,” she said.

“Not yet,” her father replied.

The map shook in her hands. Ink crawled across the parchment like ants, roads redrawing themselves, dates changing faster than she could read.

“What must I do?”

Her father pointed to a shallow circular hollow before the gate. “Place the map there. Draw the road as it truly is, from the gate to Bellweather.”

“It crosses twenty-three years.”

“You must choose one.”

Oren and Seren stood together near the house.

Mara understood.

“If I fasten the road to my time, what happens to them?”

“They enter your year,” her father said.

“And you?”

“I return to mine.”

“Will you remember this?”

“If the road allows it.”

The ground cracked again. The house blurred, its windows alternately broken, shuttered, and glowing with candlelight.

Mara knelt beside the hollow and spread the map over the stone.

She took out the pencil she had sharpened that morning.

The point touched parchment.

Nothing happened.

“You must erase the old route first,” her father said.

Mara turned the pencil and rubbed at the line of Larkspur Way. The ink resisted. She pressed harder. Slowly, the road faded, and with it the world.

The wood vanished tree by tree. The stream dried into bare stones. The house became transparent.

Oren cried out.

“Keep going,” Seren said.

Mara erased until only the gate remained.

Around them stretched white emptiness.

Four people stood in it: Mara, her father, Seren, and Oren.

“Now draw,” her father said.

Mara placed the pencil at the gate.

Her hand trembled.

She drew a line westward.

Yellow earth appeared beneath them. With the next stroke came the house, its green roof dark with rain. Another line summoned the wood, fixed now in autumn. She drew the stream, the low wall, the fields of heather, and the milestone.

As the pencil traveled, she felt the road resisting. It wanted to branch toward summer afternoons long dead, toward winters not yet born. It wanted every footstep that had ever crossed it. Voices whispered from the empty whiteness.

Turn here, said her mother.

Turn here, said the child Mara might someday have.

Turn here, said her father’s dying voice.

Mara kept the line straight.

She drew the ash trees and the stone bridge. Finally, with tears blurring her sight, she drew Bellweather as it existed that morning: the cemetery, the smoking chimneys, the square, and the eastern window of the map room.

The road settled.

Rain fell.

The gate opened onto a gray autumn field.

For a moment no one moved.

Then Mara’s father began to fade.

She caught his hand.

“No.”

“It’s all right.”

“There must be a way to bring you with us.”

“There may be. But you must not look for it.”

“That sounds like something written in red ink.”

He smiled, and suddenly she saw the old man he would become: the patient father who taught her to measure a river’s width by shadows, who packed too much bread for every journey, who never passed a milestone without touching it for luck.

“I have so many questions,” she said.

“I know.”

“You die in winter.”

He flinched, though he had said he did not want to know.

“I’m sorry,” Mara whispered.

“Was I alone?”

“No.”

“Then that is enough.”

Rain passed through his shoulders. Behind him, another landscape emerged: Larkspur Way in high summer, twenty-three years earlier.

He stepped back into sunlight.

“Mara,” he said, “when you find my map in the drawer, obey the warning.”

She almost laughed.

“I didn’t.”

His smile widened. “No. I suppose you didn’t.”

The years closed between them.

Mara stood before the gate with her hand outstretched.

Only rain touched it.

By noon, Larkspur Way had returned to Bellweather.

The villagers were astonished to discover a road beyond the stone bridge, though several claimed it had always been there and accused the others of inattention. Seren’s house stood at the end, weathered but whole. Records appeared in the parish office documenting generations of the Vale family. A gravestone bearing Elias Vale’s name waited in the cemetery, its date of death three years in the future.

Seren refused to look at it.

Oren began attending the village school. He was placed in a class with children his age, though he knew historical events that had not yet happened and had great difficulty remembering which king was presently on the throne.

Mara returned to the map room.

For several days, she avoided the drawer marked ROADS—LOST. When at last she opened it, the old map of Larkspur Way was gone.

In its place lay a small black notebook.

It had belonged to her father. She recognized the cracked leather and the thread he had tied around the cover after its clasp broke. Most of the pages contained ordinary survey notes. Near the end, she found an entry dated the fourteenth of October, twenty-three years earlier.

Met Mara today.

She is twenty-nine and has become exactly as stubborn as I feared and exactly as brave as I hoped.

The road is safe, though not because of me. I must leave instructions that she will ignore. This appears to be necessary.

She warned me of winter. I wish she had not.

I am glad I will not be alone.

Below the entry he had drawn a small map—not of Larkspur Way, but of Bellweather cemetery. A path led between the graves to the old yew tree where he would one day be buried.

Beside the tree he had written:

SOME ROADS SHOULD ONLY BE TRAVELED ONCE.

Mara closed the notebook.

Years passed.

She mapped new lanes and erased abandoned ones. She settled disputes over wells, bridges, walls, and orchards. She taught Oren the cartographer’s trade, though she warned him never to trust a compass that spun clockwise twice and stopped pointing east.

Larkspur Way behaved like an ordinary road.

Mostly.

Now and then travelers arrived in Bellweather wearing unfamiliar clothes. A soldier once appeared beneath the ash trees carrying a rusted sword and asking whether the northern war was over. An old woman came searching for a house that would not be built for another sixty years. Each time, Mara opened the black notebook, walked to the milestone, and guided the traveler back to the proper turning.

She never followed them.

Then, on a snowy evening eleven years after the road returned, Mara sat beside her father’s bed.

His illness had lasted nine days. The fire was low, and the room smelled of lavender, candle wax, and the bitter medicine he no longer had strength to drink.

Outside, the bells announced midnight.

Her father opened his eyes.

For most of the winter he had not known her. Now his gaze was clear.

“Mara,” he said.

“I’m here.”

He studied her face as though comparing it with a memory.

“Twenty-nine,” he murmured.

She leaned closer. “What did you say?”

But he only smiled.

His hand moved weakly across the blanket. Mara took it.

“Did I do well?” he asked.

She swallowed.

“You did.”

“Was I alone?”

“No.”

He let out a long, peaceful breath.

At that same moment, far beyond the village, a road shifted beneath the snow. Two ash trees shivered though there was no wind. For an instant, sunlight gleamed between them, warm and golden, and the figure of a young surveyor passed on his way toward a house at the end of a lost road.

Then the snow fell more heavily, covering every footprint.

In the morning, Mara walked to the cemetery.

She stood beneath the old yew tree while the gravediggers worked. The black notebook rested in her coat pocket. Bells sounded over Bellweather, and beyond the stone wall the roads stretched toward all the places they had promised to reach.

Mara thought of lines on parchment, of gates and agreements, of all the distance hidden inside a single step.

Before leaving, she placed one hand on her father’s cold gravestone.

Then she turned toward the village.

She did not look back, because she knew the road remembered both directions, and because some goodbyes, like maps, must have an edge if they are to mean anything at all.

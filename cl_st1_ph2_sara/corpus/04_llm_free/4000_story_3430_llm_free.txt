# The Cartographer of Lost Roads

On the morning the road vanished, Elian Voss was sharpening a pencil.

He sat at the back table of his shop, turning the cedar shaft against a small brass blade, when the baker burst through the door without knocking.

“It’s gone,” Mara said.

Elian brushed a curl of wood from his map. “What is?”

“The north road.”

He looked past her through the window. The village square lay under a pale autumn sun. Carts creaked over the stones, chimney smoke drifted above slate roofs, and the fountain continued spilling water from the mouth of its weathered lion.

“Roads do not vanish,” he said.

“This one has.”

Mara seized his sleeve and pulled him outside.

At the northern edge of the village, beyond the last cottages, the cobbled road had indeed disappeared. It ended as cleanly as a ribbon cut by scissors. On one side stood Mara’s bakery, its windows clouded with flour. On the other stood the abandoned tollhouse. Between them, where the road had always climbed toward the hills, there was now an unbroken field of silver grass.

Several villagers had gathered at the boundary. Old Fen held a walking stick over the grass as if testing deep water. The mayor stood with his hands clasped behind his back, trying to look as though he had ordered the change himself.

“My brother used that road yesterday,” Mara said. “He took two sacks of flour to Bellweather.”

“He may return by the western track,” the mayor suggested.

“The western track adds three days.”

“Then he will have a pleasant journey.”

Mara glared at him.

Elian knelt where the cobbles ended. He touched the last stone. It was cold and smooth, worn by generations of wheels. Beyond it, the silver grass bent in the wind, though no wind touched the trees.

He took a folded map from his coat. It was his own work, surveyed only the previous spring. The north road ran from the village of Vale to Bellweather, then crossed the hills and continued toward the capital. Every bridge, milestone, and roadside shrine was marked in brown ink.

Now the ink was fading.

As Elian watched, the line north of Vale thinned from brown to yellow. Then it disappeared altogether.

A murmur rose behind him.

The mayor cleared his throat. “Master Voss, as our cartographer, perhaps you can explain.”

Elian refolded the map. “Not yet.”

“Can you restore it?”

“I draw roads. I do not build them.”

“But surely your maps—”

“Show what exists.”

Mara folded her arms. “And if the maps decide something no longer exists?”

Elian looked again at the restless field.

“Then,” he said, “I find out why.”

His shop stood on the square between a clockmaker and a seller of funeral flowers. Above the door hung a painted sign: ELIAN VOSS, MAPS OF EVERY KIND. Inside, rolled charts filled shelves from floor to ceiling. There were maps of forests, coastlines, rivers, mines, and royal estates. Small maps for travelers hung beside great wall maps intended for council chambers. One drawer contained only imaginary islands, drawn for children.

Elian’s most valuable possession lay beneath the floorboards.

He locked the shop, waited for Mara to leave, and lifted a loose plank under the back table. From the hollow below, he withdrew a black leather case.

Inside was a map drawn on deerskin, older than the village and perhaps older than the kingdom. No roads appeared on it. No towns, borders, or mountains. Instead, faint golden lines moved across its surface like sunlight at the bottom of a stream.

His mother had called it the First Map.

“It does not show where things are,” she had told him when he was a boy. “It shows where they wish to go.”

She had also warned him never to use it.

Elian placed the deerskin on the table. Most of the golden lines wandered slowly, joining and separating. But around Vale, they had knotted into a dark tangle. One thread stretched north, trembled, and broke.

The bell above the shop door rang.

Elian covered the map with both hands.

Mara stood inside, carrying a travel pack and a loaf of bread.

“You locked the door,” he said.

“You left the window open.”

“It is on the second floor.”

“You also left a ladder in the alley.”

She set down the pack. Her gaze fell to the strip of deerskin visible beneath his fingers.

“What is that?”

“A family heirloom.”

“A useful one?”

“Potentially dangerous.”

“Then it is almost certainly useful.”

Before he could stop her, she pulled the map free. The golden lines brightened beneath her touch.

Mara stared. “What does it show?”

“Desire. Intention. Possibility. My mother’s explanations varied.”

“And this?” She pointed to the broken northern thread.

“I believe it was the road.”

“Roads have desires?”

“Everything that carries people somewhere has a purpose.”

Mara traced the dark knot around the village. “What is this?”

“I don’t know.”

“My brother is somewhere beyond that field.”

“I know.”

“Then we go north.”

Elian glanced at the rows of orderly maps around him. He had spent twenty years drawing the world into certainty. A hill was measured, a river named, a boundary agreed upon in ink. The First Map belonged to another kind of world, one in which rivers changed their minds and roads refused to be found.

He rolled the deerskin carefully.

“Pack water,” he said. “And bring another loaf.”

They left before noon.

The silver field rose waist-high beyond the final cobblestone. Elian stepped into it first. The grass whispered against his coat in a language made of dry leaves.

After twenty paces, Vale vanished behind them.

There was no mist, no hill, no turn in the path. One moment the village was there; the next there was only grass stretching to the horizon.

Mara spun around. “Where did it go?”

Elian consulted the First Map. Two golden lights marked their position. Behind them, the knot around Vale pulsed like a closed fist.

“We are still near it,” he said.

“That is not comforting.”

They continued north by compass. The needle shook constantly but held its direction. For hours they crossed the field. They found no footprints, no wheel ruts, and no sign of Mara’s brother.

Near dusk, they discovered a milestone.

It leaned among the grass, half-buried and covered in moss. Elian cleared its face.

VALE — 3 MILES  
BELLWEATHER — 14 MILES

“We should have passed this in the first hour,” Mara said.

“We may not be walking through distance in the usual way.”

“Is there an unusual way?”

“Apparently.”

A voice behind them said, “You won’t reach Bellweather before dark.”

An old woman sat on the milestone.

Neither of them had seen her arrive. She wore a red shawl and muddy boots. A bundle of wooden signs rested across her knees.

Mara stepped back. Elian held the First Map tightly.

“Who are you?” he asked.

“Someone waiting for directions.”

“To where?”

“If I knew, I would not need directions.”

The old woman smiled. Her teeth were very white.

She lifted one of the signs from her bundle. It read THIS WAY in faded paint.

“The roads are upset,” she said.

“Why?”

“People have forgotten what roads are for.”

“To travel,” Mara answered.

“To arrive,” said Elian.

The old woman shook her head at both.

“Roads are promises. They say: Leave, and you may return. Go forward, and somewhere will receive you. But promises require belief.”

She pointed north with the sign.

“Something in Bellweather has broken the promise.”

“Mara’s brother traveled there yesterday,” Elian said. “Did he pass you?”

“Many pass me. Few notice.”

“His name is Tomas. Tall, dark hair, driving a flour cart.”

“Oh, him. He reached the inn at sunset. The road still remembered itself then.”

Mara released a breath.

“Is he safe?”

“He was.”

The old woman climbed down from the milestone. “You may sleep here. Do not walk after moonrise. At night, lost roads lead where they please.”

She handed Elian the wooden sign.

“What am I to do with this?”

“Point it somewhere.”

Then she walked into the grass and disappeared.

They camped beside the milestone. Mara divided the bread while Elian studied the First Map by firelight. The broken thread still reached toward Bellweather, but other faint lines now emerged around it—abandoned tracks, forgotten footpaths, routes never taken.

“What happened to your mother?” Mara asked.

Elian kept his eyes on the map. “She got lost.”

“On a road?”

“While using this.”

He had been twelve. His mother had left one winter morning with the First Map and returned three years later without having aged a day. She spoke of a city built beneath a bridge and a highway that circled the moon. After that, she sometimes forgot Elian’s name. Sometimes she forgot her own.

One spring evening she walked out of the house and did not return.

“I became a cartographer because of her,” he said. “Proper maps. Measured maps. Maps that stay still.”

“And yet you kept this one.”

“I could not bring myself to destroy it.”

Mara handed him half the loaf. “Perhaps she knew you would need it.”

“Or perhaps dangerous objects are good at making themselves seem necessary.”

After moonrise, the grass began to move.

Not with the wind. It bent in long, deliberate lines. Pale tracks opened across the field, crossed one another, and vanished. Elian saw shadowy travelers walking them: a soldier carrying a broken sword, a bride in a rain-soaked dress, a child rolling a red hoop. None looked toward the fire.

Then a road of white stones appeared a few yards away.

At its far end stood Elian’s childhood home.

Warm light shone in the windows. His mother waited in the doorway, young and smiling.

“Elian,” she called. “You took so long.”

He rose before he realized he was moving.

Mara caught his wrist.

“That is not a road,” she whispered.

His mother extended her hand.

He could smell pine smoke and the lavender soap she had used. He saw the blue cup from which she drank tea. Every detail was perfect except the stars above the house. They were unfamiliar.

Elian drove the wooden sign into the earth, pointing away from the vision.

“This way,” he said aloud.

The white road shuddered. The house folded like painted paper, and the darkness swallowed it.

By morning, the silver field was gone.

They stood on the old north road, with hedges on both sides and the hills ahead. The milestone remained beside them, but its writing had changed.

VALE — 300 MILES  
BELLWEATHER — 4 MILES

Mara read it twice. “Three hundred?”

“Do not worry.”

“You say that whenever I should worry.”

“We will reach Bellweather first.”

They found the town before midday.

Bellweather was famous for its bells. Hundreds hung from towers, eaves, arches, and garden posts. Great bronze bells marked the hours. Tiny silver bells warned of opening doors. Farmers tied bells to sheep, and children wore them on their shoes during festivals.

Now the town was silent.

People crowded the main square, speaking in low voices. A line of wagons stood outside the inn, unable to leave. Tomas was among them.

Mara ran to him. He lifted her from the ground in a floury embrace.

“I tried to return,” he said. “Every road brought me back to the square.”

Elian walked to the center of the crowd.

There, beneath the bell tower, a broad circle had been painted on the stones. Twelve roads led outward from it. Each should have continued through the town gates, but instead each curved impossibly and returned to the square.

The mayor of Bellweather, a narrow man with a silver chain, hurried forward.

“Are you the cartographer from Vale?”

“Elian Voss.”

“Thank heaven. We require new maps.”

“You require new roads.”

“Same thing, surely.”

“No.”

Elian looked up at the tower. The largest bell hung motionless overhead.

“When did the bells stop?”

The mayor frowned. “Three nights ago.”

“And what happened three nights ago?”

“Nothing of relevance.”

From the crowd, someone laughed bitterly.

The mayor’s face tightened.

A young girl stepped forward. She carried a small handbell with no clapper.

“They took the heart from the Promise Bell,” she said.

“Be quiet, Lysa,” the mayor snapped.

Elian glanced up again. “What is the Promise Bell?”

The girl pointed to the largest bell. “It was cast when the first road reached Bellweather. Travelers rang it when they arrived, and again when they left. But the council said it was too old. They removed the clapper to melt it into coins.”

“It was valuable metal,” the mayor said. “The town has expenses.”

Mara stared at him. “You broke the bell for money?”

“We improved an obsolete custom.”

The girl held up her empty handbell. “Afterward, all the bells went silent. Then the roads started turning back.”

Elian unrolled the First Map on the stones.

The golden lines converging on Bellweather were twisted into a circle. The town had become a closed loop, a place where every departure was also an arrival.

“Where are the coins?” he asked.

The mayor touched the silver chain at his neck. “Distributed.”

“How many?”

“One hundred.”

“Bring them here.”

“That is impossible.”

“Then no one leaves.”

The crowd shifted. The mayor looked around and discovered that authority was less persuasive than several hundred stranded travelers.

By sunset, ninety-eight coins lay in a bowl beneath the tower.

One had been sent with a merchant before the roads closed. Another had been dropped down a well by a boy making a wish.

“We have almost all of them,” the mayor said.

“Almost is not whole,” Elian replied.

The town’s blacksmith melted the coins in a furnace. The metal glowed orange, but when she poured it into the clay mold, it filled only nine-tenths of the clapper.

“We need more bell metal,” she said.

“Use another bell,” suggested Mara.

The young girl, Lysa, shook her head. “The Promise Bell knows its own heart.”

Elian studied the incomplete clapper. Then he looked at the First Map.

One golden line still entered the town without turning back. It was so faint he had not noticed it before. It descended from the hills and ended at the dry fountain in the square.

“Where does the fountain water come from?” he asked.

“An old spring,” said Lysa. “It dried up years ago.”

“What was there before the fountain?”

The mayor sighed. “Must we investigate every stone in Bellweather?”

“Yes.”

An elderly innkeeper answered. “A shrine to Saint Orra, keeper of wanderers. Travelers left broken horseshoes there for luck.”

Elian knelt beside the fountain. Beneath layers of white paint, he found a rusted iron plate. Mara and Tomas helped him lift it.

A narrow stair descended into darkness.

At the bottom lay a tunnel. Water whispered somewhere below the stones. The walls were lined with offerings: bent nails, snapped keys, cracked buckles, and hundreds of broken horseshoes.

At the far end stood the old woman in the red shawl.

“You again,” Mara said.

“Me still,” the woman replied.

She sat beside a black spring, washing mud from her boots. Above the water hung a bronze coin on a red thread.

“The missing coin,” Elian said.

“The merchant who carried it took a road through the hills,” she told him. “That road has not existed for eighty years. He will be late.”

“And the coin in the well?”

She pointed into the spring. Far below the surface, something shone.

Mara knelt, but the old woman raised a hand.

“That wish was honestly made. Take the coin, and you steal the wish.”

“What did the boy wish for?” Elian asked.

“For his mother to come home.”

The words struck him with unexpected force.

“Will she?”

“No.”

“Then the wish is wasted.”

“No honest wish is wasted. Some simply change the person who carries it.”

Elian thought of the road to his childhood home. He thought of his mother walking out one evening, following a golden line only she could see.

“How do we mend the bell?” he asked.

The old woman looked at the sign he still carried.

“You are the cartographer.”

“I map what exists.”

“That is why your maps are accurate and useless.”

He bristled. “People rely on my work.”

“To tell them where they already are. The first cartographers did more. They gave shape to journeys. They drew not only roads, but reasons to travel them.”

She touched the First Map.

“What is the purpose of the north road?”

“To connect Vale and Bellweather.”

“That is its location.”

“To carry bread, wool, letters, and travelers.”

“That is its labor.”

Elian was silent.

Mara answered. “To let people leave home without losing it.”

The spring trembled.

On the First Map, the broken golden thread began to glow.

The old woman smiled. “There is your bell metal.”

Elian understood.

He returned to the square and asked for ink.

Not ordinary ink, but soot from the bell-maker’s furnace, water from the old spring, flour from Tomas’s cart, and a drop of blood from one traveler heading north and another heading south.

On the largest sheet of parchment in his case, Elian began to draw.

He drew Vale’s square and Bellweather’s towers. Between them he marked the hills, bridges, hedges, and milestones. He drew Mara’s bakery with smoke rising from its chimney. He drew the inn where Tomas had slept. Along the road he placed carts, pilgrims, shepherds, and children chasing one another through summer dust.

At the top of the map he wrote:

THE NORTH ROAD, BY WHICH THOSE WHO DEPART MAY RETURN.

As the final letter dried, the map grew warm.

A thin line of bronze lifted from the parchment like molten thread. It coiled in the air, bright as sunset, then dropped into the unfinished clapper.

The metal cooled.

The blacksmith fixed the clapper inside the Promise Bell. Ropes were attached. Every person in the square took hold.

The first pull produced only a dull scrape.

The second made the tower shake.

On the third, the bell rang.

Its voice rolled over Bellweather, deep enough to be felt in bone. At once, every smaller bell answered. Bronze bells, silver bells, iron bells, sheep bells, door bells, and the bells on children’s shoes filled the town with wild music.

Beyond the gates, the roads straightened.

The western road ran toward sunset. The eastern road crossed the river. The southern road entered the orchards.

And the north road led home.

The villagers cheered. The mayor quietly removed his silver chain.

Mara found Elian near the fountain, holding the First Map. The knot around Vale had loosened. Golden lines flowed freely once more.

“Tomas will travel with us,” she said. “We can leave at dawn.”

Elian nodded.

“Or,” she added, “you could stay. Bellweather wants a new town map.”

“I thought the mayor wanted one.”

“The mayor is currently hiding in a wine cellar.”

Elian looked down at his ink-stained fingers. “I have spent my life believing maps should tell the truth.”

“Shouldn’t they?”

“Yes. But perhaps truth includes what we hope a place can become.”

Mara smiled. “That sounds dangerous.”

“Potentially.”

They left at sunrise.

The north road curved through the hills, solid beneath their feet. Tomas drove his cart while Mara sat beside him. Elian walked behind, stopping often to make notes.

At the old milestone, they found the woman’s red shawl folded on the ground. There were no footprints nearby.

Elian picked it up. Beneath it lay the wooden sign.

Its faded words had changed.

NOT THIS WAY, it read.

On the reverse, in fresh white paint, were two other words:

KEEP GOING.

When Vale appeared in the distance, Mara rang a small Bellweather bell she had tied to the cart. Its bright note traveled ahead of them.

The village came running.

For the rest of the autumn, Elian redrew every map in his shop. The new maps remained precise, but in their corners he added small notes.

Here strangers may become friends.

This bridge is strongest when crossed together.

Beyond this hill, the homesick should turn back.

At the edge of Vale, he drew the north road in brown ink mixed with a trace of bronze. Under its name, he wrote its purpose.

Travelers began seeking his work from across the kingdom. Some wanted routes to distant cities. Others asked for paths through grief, indecision, or fear. Elian warned them that such maps were less reliable.

They wanted them anyway.

Years later, when his hair had gone gray, a young girl entered his shop carrying a red shawl. She placed an old deerskin map on the table.

Golden lines moved across it like sunlight beneath water.

“I’m lost,” she said.

Elian looked at the map, then at the open door behind her.

“Where are you trying to go?”

“I don’t know.”

He smiled and took a newly sharpened pencil from the drawer.

“Then,” he said, “we should begin with why you left.”

The story adopts the manner of a Renaissance fabliau or picaresque conte: bawdy, episodic, irreverent, and driven by contests of cunning. Its world is materially vivid and morally elastic, populated by greedy innkeepers, fraudulent students, drunken peasants, soldiers, servants, priests, and quarrelling spouses. Rather than separating virtue neatly from vice, the narrative treats nearly everyone as self-interested and makes ingenuity, appetite, and comic timing more consequential than morality. Religious observance, legal authority, marriage, hospitality, and social rank are repeatedly reduced to occasions for jokes, fraud, or bodily embarrassment.

The narrator is conspicuous, sociable, and judgmental. He speaks with the confidence of an oral storyteller, freely summarising characters, exaggerating their qualities, addressing the audience—“You can imagine”—and supplying amused moral commentary. Character portraits are built from accumulating comic details: the landlord is not merely miserly but a man who “knew how to skin a flint,” would charge for the air if he could, and advances or retreats according to whether he fears losing his money or receiving a kick. Such descriptions favour caricature over psychological subtlety. People are introduced as fixed comic types—the old fox, the limb of Satan, the passionate Picardian, the artful Angevin—and their actions elaborate those types.

The prose is expansive and cumulative. Long sentences proceed through strings of clauses, participles, inventories, comparisons, and digressions, often postponing the main point while the narrator adds another joke. Lists are especially prominent: lists of towns, foods, tricks, domestic faults, table arrangements, oaths, and compliments. This abundance creates a bustling, overfed rhythm suited to a story preoccupied with consumption and excess. Parallel constructions and repeated formulas intensify the comedy, while shorter lines of dialogue deliver reversals and punchlines. The syntax and occasional awkwardness—such as unusual prepositions, inconsistent punctuation, and phrases that sound literally transferred from another language—suggest an older translation imitating archaic French prose.

The diction blends mock-formal elevation with coarse colloquialism. Honorifics, legal phrases, Latin tags, theological language, and references to confession, penance, paradise, and the saints sit beside insults, thieves’ slang, sexual euphemisms, and jokes about excretion. Elevated expressions such as “plenary indulgences,” “the axiom of Justinian,” and “the road to salvation” are deliberately applied to drinking, fraud, adultery, and prostitution. This incongruity is central to the voice. Archaisms and regional names—“sols,” “crowns,” “Pardieu,” “ventre Dieu,” “fauborg,” “halberd”—lend historical colour, while proverbial metaphors give the prose a folk texture. Many similes are comic and concrete: men are as uneasy as fish on straw, a woman is as mild as onion peel, and a miser becomes as round as a barrel.

Dialogue is theatrical, competitive, and strongly performative. Speakers swear, boast, flatter, interrupt, and use grand titles in obvious bad faith. The three students maintain their imposture through exaggerated courtesy, calling one another “Viscount” and pretending to dispute over who should pay. Their politeness is therefore another form of theft. Dialogue also carries the embedded tales, each of which imitates oral performance through direct address, local claims, rhetorical preliminaries, and a final joke. Repetition around the punchlines—especially the chorus praising the landlord’s story—creates the feel of a rowdy audience responding to a tavern entertainer.

Structurally, the story uses a frame narrative containing a sequence of comic anecdotes. The opening establishes the innkeeper’s avarice, the students’ lawlessness, and the unpaid account. The central tension is delayed while three stories are told in turn, each more indecent than the last. These inset tales are compact fabliaux based on misunderstanding, sexual exploitation, domestic conflict, or bodily functions. Each builds toward a sharp verbal reversal: the penance misremembered as a feast, the servant girl’s brutal discovery of what “service” means, the wife who literally produces the “filth” her husband demands, and the bride’s failed attempt to conceal her flatulence. The final tale belongs to the landlord and unexpectedly implicates his own wife, allowing the students’ laughter and praise to become the mechanism of escape. The frame then closes with a second punchline: instead of payment, the host receives another of his wife’s “offerings.”

Humour arises from escalation, literalisation, incongruity, and delayed revelation. Figurative or ambiguous language is repeatedly interpreted in a bodily sense, while solemn institutions are mocked by being forced into contact with appetite and excretion. The narration relies heavily on hyperbole: the students eat more destructively than soldiers, make noise worth a hundred crowns, and commit enough mischief to make the devil seem gentlemanly. Sexual material is generally conveyed through elaborate euphemism—“ploughing in the wrong land,” “love’s conflict,” “philosophical soldiers”—whereas scatological jokes are sometimes delivered with blunt directness. This alternation between circumlocution and sudden plainness sharpens the punchlines.

The tone remains exuberant and amoral even when the events involve cruelty, assault, theft, or domestic violence. Suffering is subordinated to comic pattern, and the narrative rarely pauses for sympathy. The students are openly condemned as blackguards, yet their audacity and verbal skill make them the story’s effective heroes. Likewise, the innkeeper’s greed makes his deception feel like comic justice. The governing ethic is not that goodness triumphs, but that the sharper rogue defeats the more complacent one.

Overall, the style is characterised by an intrusive raconteur, archaic and proverb-rich diction, sprawling cumulative sentences, carnivalesque social inversion, embedded anecdotal storytelling, mock-erudition, regional colour, and a sustained mixture of gastronomic, sexual, and scatological comedy. Its ideal effect is that of a noisy tavern performance: crowded, digressive, indecorous, and carefully engineered so that every apparent delay ultimately prepares another trick or punchline.

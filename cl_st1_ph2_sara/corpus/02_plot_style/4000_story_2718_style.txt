The story adopts a late nineteenth-century Gothic and psychological-realist style, combining clinical observation with the atmosphere of a supernatural confession. Its frame narrative begins in an asylum, where a first-person observer encounters a wasted, violently disturbed inmate and is invited by a doctor to read the man’s diary. Most of the story is therefore a first-person narrative nested inside another first-person account. This structure creates layers of mediation: the inmate interprets his own obsession, the visitor reacts to it, and the doctor supplies a detached medical judgment. The return to the asylum at the end restores the clinical frame but leaves the central mystery unresolved, particularly when the physical tress of hair is produced.

The prose is formal, literary and strongly descriptive. Its diction repeatedly blends medical or abstract terms—“necrophile,” “seizures,” “immaterial idea,” “annihilation”—with sensual language concerning touch, warmth, perfume, softness and bodily intimacy. This collision between diagnosis and desire is central to the effect. The narrator’s madness is presented not as incoherence but as an idea pursued with unnerving lucidity. His journal traces the stages of obsession almost analytically, even while its language becomes increasingly emotional and feverish. Occasional archaic or period expressions, along with minor awkwardness suggestive of translation, reinforce the story’s historical character.

Syntax varies according to psychological intensity. The external narrator tends to use balanced, observational sentences, although these are charged with accumulating adjectives and parallel phrases. The diary begins in a relatively calm, explanatory manner, then develops longer, more rhythmical sentences filled with repetitions, questions and exclamations. Dashes, abrupt fragments and reiterated words—especially “I loved it,” “I waited,” and “Do the dead come back?”—register the speaker’s loss of control. Rhetorical questions dominate passages about the unknown dead woman, allowing conjecture to replace evidence and showing imagination transforming an object into a complete erotic fantasy.

Repetition and accumulation are characteristic devices. Adjectival sequences such as “invisible, impalpable, intangible, immaterial” magnify the destructive power of an idea, while repeated verbs of touching, caressing, kissing and possessing make the obsession physically palpable. The same concepts recur with incremental force: the hair is first a curiosity, then a relic, then a living presence, then a lover. This gradual semantic transformation provides the principal narrative movement. Abstract reflection also broadens the private mania into meditations on time, mortality and loss, particularly through recurring images of clocks, passing centuries and vanished women.

The imagery is sensuous, morbid and often paradoxical. The tress is at once dead matter and the “only living part” of a vanished body. It falls like a “golden shower,” shines like a comet’s tail and finally flies toward the visitor “like a golden bird.” Such luminous imagery makes the relic beautiful and animate, even as its association with decay, coffins and possible crime makes it repellent. Hair functions as the story’s governing symbol: resistant to decomposition, it appears to defeat time and becomes a material bridge between erotic desire and death. The antique cabinet similarly acts as a threshold between the present and an imagined past, while the hidden drawer externalizes the concealed chamber of the protagonist’s mind.

The tone moves from pitying unease through melancholy fascination to erotic horror. Early descriptions prepare the reader to regard the diarist as diseased, but the intimacy and eloquence of his journal temporarily draw the reader into his emotional logic. His nostalgia for dead women and vanished beauty initially appears wistful and poetic; only gradually does it become fetishistic and macabre. The inserted quotation about “last year’s snows” deepens the elegiac mood and places his obsession within a broader literary tradition of lamenting beauty destroyed by time. The eventual claim that the dead woman physically returned marks the point at which poetic longing becomes delusion—or, less certainly, supernatural experience.

Pacing is controlled through escalation. The initial asylum scene is brief and visually concentrated. The diary then proceeds step by step from emotional detachment, to antiquarian interest, to discovery, ritualized handling, dependency, hallucinated consummation and confinement. Short transitional statements such as “I went on my way” or “I succeeded on the following day” interrupt the ornate descriptions and lend the account a deceptive factual simplicity. The final scene accelerates sharply: a scream, a terse medical explanation, the sudden production of the hair and the visitor’s involuntary response create a compact shock ending.

Dialogue is sparse and functional. The doctor’s speech is blunt, skeptical and diagnostic, contrasting with the inmate’s lyrical expansiveness. Yet the doctor’s theatrical act of tossing the hair at the visitor destabilizes his apparent rational authority. The visitor’s response—simultaneous “disgust and desire”—is especially important because it shows the obsession’s contagious imaginative power. The ending aphorism, “The mind of man is capable of anything,” provides thematic closure without fully explaining events. It directs attention away from a simple supernatural solution and toward the terrifying creative capacity of desire, fantasy and fixation.

Overall, the style depends on oppositions: reason and madness, clinical distance and sensual immersion, beauty and corruption, life and death, evidence and hallucination. Its horror is generated less by violence than by the progressive eroticization of a dead remnant. The result is an elegant, psychologically probing tale whose ornate imagery and confessional intensity make the reader feel both the attraction and the repulsiveness of the protagonist’s obsession.

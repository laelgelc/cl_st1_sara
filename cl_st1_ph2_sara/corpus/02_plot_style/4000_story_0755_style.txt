The story adopts the form of a comic autobiographical essay rather than a conventionally plotted short story. Its first-person narrator speaks directly and confidentially to the reader, presenting himself as both cautionary example and principal target of the joke. The voice is candid, garrulous, self-dramatizing and knowingly unreliable: the opening promise to tell the truth immediately turns into an admission that exaggeration and self-praise are inevitable. This creates a genial tension between genuine regret and theatrical self-mockery. The narrator considers himself exceptionally talented, exceptionally distractible and therefore exceptionally foolish, allowing vanity and humility to coexist for comic effect.

The central stylistic device is the extended metaphor of “goat-feathers,” an absurd invented object representing distractions, unnecessary duties and fruitless side pursuits. The phrase is repeated insistently and developed through definitions, measurements, lists and visual transformations until it becomes the essay’s organizing motif. Abstract advice about concentration is thus rendered concrete, memorable and ridiculous: wasted effort is weighed by the pound or bale, gathered in groves, worn on the body and imagined as making the narrator resemble a goat, llama or stuffed owl. A contrasting image, “eagle-feathers,” briefly distinguishes worthwhile patriotic service from pointless activity. Other analogies—the wandering express train, the acrobatic cow, the unfocused sunbeam and the plumber making violins—reinforce the moral through homely comic pictures.

The humour depends heavily on hyperbole, incongruity and cumulative escalation. Telephone calls occur thousands or millions of times; visitors ask for impossibly large sums to support extravagantly improbable causes; a pause between two paragraphs expands into the construction of an electrically lit doll’s house. Exact figures are often attached to absurd claims, producing mock precision: tasks consume specified months, days and hours, while useless feathers acquire carefully calculated weights and monetary values. The narrator repeatedly begins with a plausible circumstance and enlarges it step by step until it becomes surreal. His fantasy of literary fame, for example, progresses from professional recognition to strangers reverently stealing blades of grass from his lawn.

Sentence structure alternates between blunt aphorisms and long, accretive sentences that enact the very distractibility being criticized. Short declarations such as “Then he begins to wabble” or “He may be making a goat of himself” provide comic punctuation. In contrast, extended catalogues of offices, committees, interruptions and stray thoughts pile clause upon clause, often joined by repeated conjunctions. These catalogues generate breathless momentum and make administrative busyness feel physically overwhelming. Parallel constructions and anaphora are common: repeated patterns such as “The most important…” and “Every…is a goat-feather” turn the argument into a comic sermon. Dashes, exclamation marks, quoted dialogue and sudden interjections preserve the rhythms of animated speech.

The diction mixes colloquial American phrasing with mock-grandiose language. Expressions such as “cracking myself up,” “I don’t care a hang,” “mighty poor business” and “kick myself” keep the voice informal and accessible, while capitalized titles, public offices and literary distinctions are treated with exaggerated solemnity. Formal moralizing is repeatedly undercut by slang or ludicrous imagery. The historical setting is visible in references to Liberty Loans, the Red Cross, wartime committees, telegrams, typewriters and contemporary literary celebrities, but these details are incorporated casually as part of the narrator’s working life rather than explained.

Structurally, the essay moves associatively while remaining unified by its refrain. It begins with a comic confession, defines the governing metaphor, supplies increasingly elaborate evidence from public obligations and private procrastination, and then broadens into advice about work, specialization and success. Personal anecdotes become general propositions, and the ending converts self-reproach into a direct warning to the reader. This progression gives the piece a didactic core beneath its comic surface. The narrator openly states the lesson many times—concentration is essential, while distraction produces failure—but repetition is made entertaining through fresh examples and escalating variations.

The overall tone is rueful, exuberant and satirical rather than bitter. Genuine anxiety about age, debt, wasted talent and unrealized literary ambition periodically darkens the comedy, especially when the narrator imagines dying with little accomplished. Yet pathos is rarely allowed to remain unqualified; it is quickly redirected into a boast, grotesque image or comic calculation. The resulting style resembles an after-dinner monologue or humorous magazine essay: conversational, episodic, full of topical allusions, and driven more by personality and rhetorical invention than by suspense. Its characteristic effect comes from turning a sincere piece of practical wisdom into an expansive performance of precisely the wandering, excessive temperament it condemns.

After forty years as the village church’s leading soprano, Candace Whitcomb is dismissed because her voice has declined and is replaced by the younger Alma Way. Humiliated by the choir’s attempt to soften the dismissal with a surprise party and a photograph album, Candace defiantly plays her parlor organ and sings competing hymns through her open windows during church services, disrupting Alma’s solos.

Neither the minister’s appeals nor her nephew Wilson Ford’s angry confrontation changes her mind. When Wilson threatens to destroy her organ, Candace declares that she will disinherit him, knowing that her house and money would allow him to marry Alma, whom he has courted for years but cannot afford to support separately from his difficult mother.

Exhausted and feverish after her rebellion, Candace falls gravely ill. As she weakens, she repents of her harshness, reconciles with the minister, and summons Wilson and Alma. She reveals that she never changed her will, leaving the couple her home so they can finally marry and live together. She then asks Alma to sing a hymn. Listening peacefully, Candace accepts both death and her successor—though, with her last flash of pride and musical judgment, she remarks that Alma sang one note slightly flat.

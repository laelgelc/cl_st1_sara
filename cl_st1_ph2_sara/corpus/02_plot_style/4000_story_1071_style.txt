The story adopts a conversational first-person mode that combines eyewitness testimony, social observation and retrospective judgment. The narrator presents himself as an amused but morally alert outsider, free to move around the gathering and scrutinise conduct that insiders either overlook or accept. His opening self-correction—beginning with a wedding, then turning instead to a Christmas tree—creates the impression of spontaneous speech while concealing a carefully designed circular structure. Direct addresses and confessional phrases such as “I noticed,” “I like to observe,” and “I don’t know why” strengthen the sense of an informal anecdote told by a distinctive personality.

The narrative architecture is retrospective and ironic. Most of the story recounts a children’s New Year celebration exactly five years before the wedding described at the end. The opening hints at the later event, the extended flashback reveals Julian Mastakovich’s calculations and courtship strategy, and the closing scene confirms their success. This delayed confirmation gives the story the shape of a social revelation: what initially appears to be a festive domestic scene becomes the origin of a calculated marriage. The five-year interval is crucial, turning the Christmas party into both a miniature of the adult social order and a grim forecast of the future.

The prose mixes economical narration with sharply detailed comic description. Characters are often captured through a few conspicuous physical traits, gestures or habits: the neglected provincial endlessly strokes his whiskers, the host’s children are “remarkably well-fed,” and Julian Mastakovich is repeatedly rendered as round, flushed, perspiring and pompous. Such descriptions verge on caricature, but they remain psychologically revealing. Bodily appearance externalises moral condition: corpulence suggests privilege and appetite, while the poor boy’s thinness, freckles and shabby jacket mark his vulnerability within the household hierarchy.

The dominant tone is satirical, shifting between playful amusement, discomfort, indignation and horror. Much of the comedy arises from the discrepancy between respectable appearances and selfish motives. Julian Mastakovich behaves with ceremonious dignity in public, yet privately calculates compound interest on an eleven-year-old girl’s dowry, courts her furtively and attacks a powerless child out of jealousy. The narrator’s mock-respectful labels—“personage,” “great man,” “worthy man”—are therefore heavily ironic. Polite social vocabulary repeatedly masks exploitation, and the more ceremonious the language becomes, the more morally grotesque the scene appears.

Diction is generally clear and accessible, but it is enriched by formal social terminology, financial language and occasional foreign or elevated expressions. Words associated with rank, recommendation, connections, courtesy and patronage establish a world governed by status. Against this, the blunt vocabulary of money—“three hundred thousand,” “four per cent,” “five hundred thousand”—exposes the material calculations beneath social refinement. Julian’s muttered arithmetic is one of the story’s central stylistic devices: fragmented numbers, pauses and self-interruptions dramatise greed directly, without requiring explicit authorial explanation.

Sentence structure varies with mood and action. Longer, balanced sentences are used for observation, social analysis and descriptive irony, often accumulating qualifications that gradually sharpen a judgment. Short sentences and exclamations accelerate moments of exposure or alarm: “We’re playing”; “Go away!”; “Impossible, impossible!” Ellipses, dashes, repetitions and broken calculations reproduce spontaneous thought and agitation. Parenthetical qualifications—particularly ironic alternatives such as “dislike (or was it jealousy?)”—allow the narrator to guide interpretation while maintaining the pose of a curious observer.

Dialogue is theatrical and socially diagnostic. Characters reveal themselves less through introspection than through what they say, how they say it and whom they address. Julian’s speech changes according to power: he whispers sugary endearments to the wealthy girl, issues harsh commands to the poor boy, offers excuses to the host and produces fulsome compliments for the girl’s mother. The children’s speech is simpler and morally clearer. Their instinctive loyalty to one another contrasts with the adults’ euphemism, flattery and calculation. Repetition in the girl’s protest—“I don’t want him to… Let him alone!”—gives emotional force to her resistance.

Characterisation depends strongly on contrast and social mirroring. The wealthy girl and the governess’s son are linked by genuine companionship, while the adults classify them according to dowry and usefulness. The distribution of Christmas presents explicitly reproduces the parents’ hierarchy: gifts diminish in value with social rank. The party therefore functions as a compressed model of society, teaching children “their place” even before they fully understand it. The poor boy’s attempts to win acceptance by surrendering his apple and carrying another child are particularly poignant because they show hierarchy already shaping his behaviour.

The imagery repeatedly opposes childhood innocence to adult appetite. Dolls, sweets, toys and the Christmas tree initially suggest generosity and festivity, but each becomes implicated in status and possession. The doll identifies the wealthy girl as a prized object, while Julian’s explanation that dolls are made “out of rags” carries an unintended dehumanising undertone. Predatory suggestion is conveyed through his furtive movements, quivering excitement, pinching and attempted kisses. By the wedding scene, the girl herself has effectively become the valuable object whose future ownership was calculated five years earlier.

Humour operates through exaggeration, physical farce and exposure. The whiskered provincial is described as though his whiskers existed before the man; Julian chases the boy with a handkerchief while puffing and panting; his flushed face and embarrassed glances reduce a supposedly imposing figure to absurdity. Yet the comedy is never merely light. Laughter becomes the narrator’s weapon against social authority, and Julian experiences it as a threat because it strips away his dignity. The narrator’s open question about whether Julian is married similarly uses deliberate indiscretion to puncture the collective reverence surrounding him.

The setting is socially enclosed and almost stage-like. Drawing-room, sitting-room, conservatory and dining-room provide successive spaces for public performance, concealment and exposure. Doorways, plants, corners and mirrors are important because they regulate who can see whom. The narrator’s concealed position allows him to witness what respectable society is not meant to see, while Julian’s repeated glances around reveal his awareness of guilt. The final movement into the church broadens the audience but preserves the same theatrical quality: the wedding is another public spectacle in which wealth and respectability disguise coercion.

The ending is compressed, bleak and retrospective. The rainy weather and crowded church replace the bright domestic festivity with a dreary ceremonial atmosphere. Recognition proceeds visually: the narrator sees the corpulent bridegroom, then the pale sixteen-year-old bride, and finally understands that Julian’s earlier arithmetic has become reality. The exclamation “Good God!” marks the collapse of amused satire into moral shock. The final line returns to the language of calculation—“his calculations were correct”—and delivers the story’s central irony with restraint. Financially, Julian has succeeded; morally, that very success constitutes the indictment.

Overall, the style is that of a socially observant satirical anecdote whose apparent lightness conceals a severe critique of wealth, patronage, marriage and adult corruption. It relies on a personable but intrusive narrator, grotesque physical detail, dramatic dialogue, tonal contrasts, symbolic objects and a circular time structure. Its most distinctive effect comes from placing innocent childhood behaviour beside adult calculation, allowing comedy to expose cruelty before the final scene converts that cruelty into an accomplished social fact.

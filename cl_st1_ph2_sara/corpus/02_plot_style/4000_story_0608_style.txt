The story is written as a comic realist character study with a strongly satirical, ironic tone. Its central method is to expose the distance between Alderman Keats’s cultivated public image and his timid, socially insecure nature. His gout, clothing, port-drinking, mannerisms, and revolver are treated as theatrical props in an elaborate impersonation of “the fine old English gentleman.” The humour is affectionate but sharp: Keats is ridiculous and vain rather than malicious, and his eventual honesty under pressure gives the comedy a faintly humane moral edge.

The narrator is omniscient, confident, and conspicuously interventionist. He moves freely between public appearances and private motives, first showing how other characters interpret Keats and then correcting those interpretations with blunt disclosures such as “But he was wrong.” Parenthetical comments, explanatory asides, and mock qualifications allow the narrator to converse indirectly with the reader. This voice often adopts the sober language of historical record or social analysis to describe absurdly minor events, creating a mock-epic effect. Phrases such as “epoch-making in the history of music-hall enterprise” and the careful definition of what constitutes “the whole town” comically inflate provincial life while also mapping its social hierarchy.

The diction mixes formal, sometimes elevated narration with colloquial speech and period-specific vocabulary. Words such as “indubitably,” “traducers,” “demur,” and “exacerbated” lend the prose an urbane, ceremonious surface, while expressions such as “barker,” “twinge,” “a fearful start,” and “a nice chap” anchor it in everyday provincial conversation. Repeated labels—including “old English,” “fine old English gentleman,” “dog of the old sort,” “gout,” and “revolver”—become comic refrains. Quotation marks frequently isolate Keats’s adopted phrases, emphasizing that his identity is assembled from clichés and borrowed language.

The syntax is generally clear and controlled, but it varies between compact comic statements and long cumulative sentences. Short paragraphs often deliver reversals or punchlines: “It was this habit of mind that led to the revolver.” Longer sentences pile up causes, consequences, qualifications, and parallel constructions, mirroring the escalating complications produced by Keats’s vanity. Repetition and anaphora are used for comic insistence, as when the narration stresses that he failed to hit the keyhole, the doors, and even the ground floor. Semicolons, dashes, parentheses, and delayed clauses regulate timing, frequently withholding the decisive absurdity until the end of a sentence.

Dialogue is economical, socially revealing, and built around status contests. Keats speaks in bluff declarations, clipped assertions, and rehearsed masculine formulas: “Every man ought to have a revolver.” Joe Keats answers with dry, deflationary wit, functioning as the story’s principal truth-teller. The exchanges rely on repetition, understatement, and abrupt contradiction, while dialect is suggested lightly through idiom and syntax rather than represented phonetically. Keats’s preference for impressive words such as “barker” shows his delight in performing a role even at the level of vocabulary.

Structurally, the narrative follows a chain of comic escalation. It begins with gout as a prestigious affectation, expands into a broader imitation of traditional gentility, and then introduces the revolver as the logical but dangerous completion of that persona. The accidental “keyhole” shot creates a false reputation; the discovery of the bullet hole forces Keats into deception; the deception requires blank cartridges and repeated staged practice; and the tiger emergency finally turns the harmless performance into a moral test. Each attempted defence of his reputation produces a more elaborate predicament. The ending is concise and circular: after his revolver myth collapses, Keats also stops mentioning the gout, revealing that both belonged to the same failed self-invention.

Much of the comedy depends on incongruity. Provincial civic respectability is juxtaposed with Wild West marksmanship, an alderman’s vanity with lethal weaponry, and supposedly terrifying Bengal tigers with a solution involving burning newspaper. Bathos repeatedly reduces heroic or dangerous situations to domestic and social embarrassment. Dramatic irony is equally important: the community believes Keats to be formidable while the reader knows that he is frightened of firing his own weapon. The final scene reverses that irony in public, making his confession of the blank cartridges both humiliating and morally creditable.

The setting is rendered through selective local detail rather than broad description. Place names such as Hanbridge, Bursley, Crown Square, Slippery Lane, the Big Pits, and Birches Pond create a dense provincial geography, while references to aldermen, manufacturers, tradesmen, music halls, pawnbrokers, stables, and venison dinners establish a socially stratified industrial town. The locality behaves almost like a collective character: news travels quickly, reputations are communal property, and masculine prestige is manufactured through gossip. Overall, the style combines social observation, narrator-led irony, brisk dialogue, escalating farce, and precise comic timing to satirize class aspiration, performative masculinity, civic self-importance, and the fragility of reputation.

The story adopts a polished, urbane comic style built on social irony, aphorism, and elegant narrative economy. Its opening establishes the governing voice through confident paradoxes—romance belongs to the rich, while the poor ought to be practical—and this amused, worldly narrator continues to judge characters and society with affectionate detachment. Broad pronouncements are followed by briskly selective details, so Hughie’s character is conveyed through memorable contrasts: he is beautiful but intellectually undistinguished, accomplished in everything except earning money, and both socially charming and professionally ineffectual. The humour frequently depends on balanced antitheses, reversals, and epigrammatic formulations, giving the prose the rhythm of sophisticated conversation.

The narration is third-person omniscient but lightly intrusive. It offers direct commentary, exclamations, rhetorical questions, and compressed verdicts rather than sustained psychological analysis. Characters are stylised through a few vivid physical traits and comic biographical details: the Colonel has lost both his temper and digestion in India; Trevor is rough in appearance but refined in artistic ability; the beggar resembles wrinkled parchment. Such descriptions mix caricature with precision, making social types immediately legible while preserving an atmosphere of fairy-tale elegance. Repetition and parallel construction sharpen the comedy, particularly in lists of Hughie’s failed occupations and in paired terms such as “bulls and bears,” “pekoe and souchong,” and “painters, poets, and physicians.”

The diction combines cultivated English with fashionable social vocabulary, art-world references, and occasional French phrases. Terms such as “entree,” “trouvaille,” and “Que voulez-vous?” lend the dialogue a cosmopolitan, upper-class texture, while references to Velázquez and Rembrandt reinforce the aesthetic setting. The prose remains accessible despite this ornamentation because sentences are generally clear, rhythmic, and tightly controlled. Longer descriptive passages alternate with short comic statements, and punctuation—especially dashes, exclamation marks, and rhetorical questions—helps reproduce the animation of drawing-room speech.

Dialogue carries much of the story’s pace and characterization. Each exchange is quick, theatrical, and shaped around a joke, misunderstanding, or epigram. Trevor speaks in flamboyant aesthetic paradoxes, Hughie in sincere and impulsive terms, and Baron Hausberg’s representative in ceremonious formality. The speakers often debate the moral and aesthetic meanings of poverty: Hughie sees suffering, whereas Trevor sees picturesqueness. This opposition is expressed wittily rather than solemnly, allowing the story to criticize aesthetic detachment without abandoning its own delight in artifice.

Structurally, the narrative resembles a compact social comedy or modern fairy tale. It begins with a lack—Hughie needs ten thousand pounds to marry—introduces a test of character, conceals the beggar’s true identity, and resolves the obstacle through a generous reward. Foreshadowing is subtle but clear: the model’s interest in Hughie’s circumstances and his private amusement prepare the revelation. The central irony is double-layered. Hughie mistakes a millionaire for a beggar, while the millionaire discovers that Hughie, despite being poor, possesses genuine generosity. The final pun on “millionaire models” and “model millionaires” condenses the entire moral into a polished epigram.

The overall tone is light, amused, and sentimental without becoming earnest. Poverty, wealth, marriage, art, and philanthropy are treated through comedy of manners rather than realism. Material distinctions are sharply observed, but virtue is ultimately rewarded in a deliberately improbable fashion. The resulting style is decorative yet economical: visually vivid, socially observant, conversationally brilliant, and organized around the transformation of moral paradox into comic resolution.

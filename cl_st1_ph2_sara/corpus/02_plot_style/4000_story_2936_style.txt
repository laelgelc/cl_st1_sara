The story adopts a late-nineteenth-century adventure and horror style, combining maritime romance, castaway narrative, ethnographic travel writing, and the “manuscript found in a bottle” device. Its structure is framed: an unnamed narrator first describes receiving the document from a personified, storm-tormented sea, after which Amasa D. Keating’s first-person testimony becomes the main narrative. This layered presentation lends the tale an air of documentary authenticity while preserving uncertainty, since the recovered writing is said to be aged, fragmentary, and imperfectly deciphered.

The prose is formal, elevated, and mildly archaic. Expressions such as “haply,” “whithersoever,” “thenceforward,” “befell us,” and “I deem it wholly unnecessary” evoke historical testimony rather than casual storytelling. The narrators favor abstract nouns and ceremonious formulations—“violent mental disturbance,” “complete isolation,” “a peculiar sense of desolation”—which make even extreme events sound like entries in a sober report. Nautical vocabulary, dates, coordinates, place names, occupations, and logistical details reinforce the fiction’s pseudo-historical credibility.

Sentence structure is predominantly long and syntactically elaborate. The prose frequently uses semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, participial phrases, and balanced clauses to accumulate information while delaying the main point. This produces a measured, retrospective cadence, as though the narrator is struggling to impose rational order on traumatic experience. At moments of danger, however, the syntax contracts into rapid sequences of actions: “snatched,” “leaped,” “split,” “turned,” “cut,” “caught,” and “fled.” Such abrupt acceleration makes the climactic escape feel explosive after the story’s prolonged descriptive and explanatory passages.

The opening is highly figurative and atmospheric. The storm is represented as a battle between elemental beings: the wind “renews war” with the peninsula, lashes the sea, rends the cypresses, and arms a wave with a “bludgeon.” The ocean is repeatedly personified as a dangerous old friend capable of speech, violence, travail, and revelation. This grand, almost Gothic treatment of landscape establishes a world in which nature is both antagonist and messenger. Once the embedded account begins, the imagery becomes more practical and observational, though moments of horror retain strong visual contrasts, especially Arthur’s pale naked body amid the surrounding brown-skinned crowd, the waiting hot stones, and the ritual fire.

The narrative voice is earnest, controlled, and morally serious. Keating repeatedly insists on the urgency and intelligibility of his report, even while declaring himself nearly incapable of coherent expression. That tension between composure and distress is central to the style: terror is conveyed less through emotional outburst than through a witness’s effort to remain precise. Understatement intensifies the effect. Phrases such as “a matter of some moment” or “an extraordinary preparation” describe scenes whose implications are appalling, allowing the reader’s apprehension to outrun the narrator’s explicit conclusions.

Suspense develops through gradual reversal and delayed interpretation. The island first appears fertile, beautiful, hospitable, and unusually civilized. The king seems gentle, generous, and morally superior; restrictive measures are introduced as mild precautions; and the removal of sick colonists can initially be interpreted as benevolent evacuation. Each reassuring detail later acquires a sinister meaning. The story repeatedly raises the possibility of cannibalism, partially dismisses it, and then restores it through overheard fragments. This method depends on dramatic irony and euphemism: the islanders speak of people being “delicious,” becoming “fat,” being “given” to selected groups, and being made “tender,” while avoiding direct explanation. The horror emerges cumulatively from practical, almost culinary details.

Dialogue is sparing but strategically important. Much of it appears as overheard conversation, a device that withholds context and obliges both Keating and the reader to infer meaning. The islanders’ calm, matter-of-fact exchanges contrast with the grotesque acts they describe, creating black irony. Captain Campbell’s speech, by comparison, is formal and communal, externalizing a fear that everyone already shares. Characters are generally drawn by role, conduct, and social designation rather than psychological depth: captain, king, carpenter, farmer, milliner, priests, warriors, and guards populate a tale concerned more with collective peril than individualized interior life.

Pacing is deliberately uneven. The voyage, settlement, geography, and customs are recounted patiently, with many identifying particulars appropriate to a rescue document. As the colonists’ confinement becomes clear, intervals shorten and disappearances occur “with greater frequency.” The ritual scene slows almost to real time, dwelling on the fire, stones, crowd, implements, and distribution plans before erupting into sudden violence. The ending then becomes stark and compressed, returning to the immediate present of thirst, exposure, and likely death.

The overall tone moves from sublime exhilaration through curiosity and gratitude to oppression, dread, grotesque revelation, and desperate urgency. Stylistically, the story’s chief effect comes from placing sensational horror inside the language of sober testimony. Its ornate framing imagery, reportorial specificity, formal diction, incremental disclosure, and controlled understatement create a plausible yet uncanny narrative in which apparent civilization conceals systematic brutality.

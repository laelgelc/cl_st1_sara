The story is written as a comic Australian bush sketch: part informal essay, part anecdotal memoir, and part documentary account of pastoral life. Rather than following a conventional plot, it moves associatively through cattle-country economics, animal behaviour, droving lore, comparisons with sheep, and scenes of stockmanship. Its structure progresses from broad generalisation to increasingly vivid examples, culminating in an extended, almost cinematic description of a steer being cut from a mob.

The narrator is authoritative, experienced, and conversational. He speaks as an insider familiar with stations, drovers, stock-riders, cattle camps, droughts, disease, and speculative ownership, while occasionally stepping forward in the first person, as in “As I write.” The prevailing voice combines practical observation with the confidence of bush folklore. Sweeping claims are presented as self-evident truths, creating the impression of communal wisdom rather than detached analysis. Direct address and second-person constructions—“You get your country,” “you can’t shift cattle”—draw the reader into this shared perspective.

The diction mixes plain standard English with Australian pastoral vocabulary such as “damper,” “run,” “mob,” “plant,” “bail,” “cut out,” and “camp-horse.” Colloquial phrases including “playing the dickens,” “done for,” and “making a hash of it” keep the register informal and oral. The period’s colonial attitudes are also embedded in the language, particularly in its dated and derogatory descriptions of Indigenous workers and its broad ethnic, occupational, and gender stereotypes. Capitalisation and quotation marks are used emphatically—“ON PAPER,” “IN THE SAME DIRECTION,” “YOU”—to reproduce vocal stress and sharpen jokes.

Humour is the dominant stylistic force. It depends on deadpan exaggeration, mock logic, anthropomorphism, ironic reversals, and aphoristic punch lines. Business optimism is punctured by “There is a curse on cattle”; rain “always comes—after the cattle are dead”; and the stockman is said to occupy the better position because he receives wages while his employer never earns a profit. Animals are assigned human motives and social roles: sheep prefer death, cattle are governed by Fate, a bullock becomes an “agitator,” and a cow displays “fiendish ingenuity.” This personification is comic but also conveys close practical knowledge of animal behaviour.

The syntax alternates between long accumulative sentences and short emphatic verdicts. Extended lists build momentum and suggest the abundance of hazards, temperaments, and mishaps in station life, while clipped statements provide comic closure: “Alas! for such hopes”; “The hand of Fate is against them”; “Cattle see ghosts, sure enough.” Parallel constructions and repeated alternatives—“Or Providence sends the pleuro… Or else the tick attacks them”—give the prose a rhythmic, storytelling cadence. Parenthetical qualifications and abrupt dashes mimic a speaker enlarging upon a tale in company.

The descriptive method is concrete, kinetic, and visually alert. Heat, dust, flies, mud, long grass, horns, nervous horses, and moving masses of cattle establish the physical reality of the bush. In the final cutting-out scene, verbs such as “scuttles,” “dashes,” “doubles,” “dodges,” “wheels,” and “trots” create speed and precision. The mob becomes a “restless sea,” with animals “eddying” through it, an extended metaphor that captures both collective motion and individual struggle. Exclamations and fragments—“Way!—make way!”—bring the scene into an immediate dramatic present.

Characterisation is primarily occupational and behavioural rather than psychological. Competence is shown through restrained action: the Queensland drovers work “without a shout, or crack of a whip,” in contrast with the noisy, theatrical incompetence of the sheep-men. The brief line of dialogue, “Many’s the time YOU never cut-out cattle,” is laconic, idiomatic, and cutting; it embodies the story’s admiration for understated expertise. Human beings are judged by their handling of animals, and horses themselves become sensitive indicators of good or bad horsemanship.

The tone shifts fluidly between sardonic pessimism, affectionate mockery, danger, and lyrical admiration. Drought, disease, drowning, and stampede are treated with fatalistic humour, yet the closing passages become respectful and almost elegiac when describing the coordination of horse and rider. The inserted poetic quotation about dead men and frightened cattle briefly introduces a supernatural, ballad-like atmosphere, linking practical bush experience with legend. Overall, the style is robust, anecdotal, hyperbolic, and distinctly oral, using comic generalisation to celebrate skill, resilience, and hard-earned knowledge while exposing the folly of outsiders who imagine pastoral life to be simple.

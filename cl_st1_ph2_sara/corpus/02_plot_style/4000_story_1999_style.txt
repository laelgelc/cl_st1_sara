The story uses a brisk, economical comic-realist style centred on domestic conflict. Its prose is lucid and largely unornamented, with concrete details—the extinguished fire, worn clothes, bills, pipes, tea things, stained glass and linoleum—establishing a lower-middle-class household in late-Victorian or Edwardian England. Period vocabulary and monetary expressions such as “thirty-five bob,” “two hundred pounds a year,” and “sixteen pounds thirteen shillings and fourpence” reinforce the social setting. The narrator adopts a dry, detached manner, presenting absurd or cruel behaviour with formal composure rather than explicit moral commentary.

The narration is third-person omniscient but stays chiefly close to Mr. Gribble’s perspective. His thoughts are rendered through free indirect discourse, allowing his selfish assumptions to condemn him without direct authorial intervention. Statements such as the conclusion that his wife must have been run over because only that would excuse the absence of tea reveal his character while maintaining the narrator’s deadpan tone. This focalisation also creates dramatic irony: readers understand Mrs. Gribble’s growing strategy before her husband fully does, while his self-serving interpretation of events remains transparently ridiculous.

Humour arises from irony, understatement, inversion and incongruity. Mr. Gribble treats his wife’s inheritance as naturally his own, calls himself a “gentleman of independent means,” and portrays personal indulgence as prudent management while condemning her basic needs as extravagance. Elevated or mock-heroic phrasing is applied to trivial domestic matters: his authority has “echoed down the corridors of thirty years,” language proves inadequate in a marital quarrel, and his wife’s cough becomes an instrument of emancipation. Euphemistic phrases such as “go off the hooks” and the doctor’s “heart-to-heart talk” soften threats while sharpening the comedy. The narrator’s occasional figurative flourishes—especially Mr. Gribble’s visions of walking “along the primrose path arm-in-arm with two hundred a year”—satirise his fantasies of leisure and masculine entitlement.

Dialogue carries much of the story. Exchanges are short, adversarial and precisely timed, with repetition used for comic emphasis: “Leave the house!”, “Place!”, and “Own way in everything?” Mr. Gribble’s speech is loud, imperative and colloquial, marked by contractions, nonstandard negatives and dropped sounds: “ain’t,” “d’ye,” “o’,” and “sha’n’t.” Mrs. Gribble initially speaks in hesitations, stammers and timid qualifications, but these gradually become tactical. Her apparent frailty is encoded through broken syntax, coughs, pauses and quavering tones, while her increasing control is shown less through declarations than through carefully managed actions. The doctor’s concise, authoritative speech provides the mechanism by which domestic power is reversed.

Sentences are generally medium-length and syntactically straightforward, alternating narrative summary with compact scenes of dialogue. Parallel constructions, lists and escalating clauses create comic rhythm. Paragraphs are short, often ending on a pointed remark or reversal. Physical gestures substitute for psychological exposition: fists strike tables, doors bang, handkerchiefs rise to eyes, hands clutch hearts, and Mr. Gribble retreats to an upturned bucket. These recurring actions turn the marriage into a visual contest of intimidation and counter-performance.

The plot has a tightly controlled arc of escalation. The inheritance first exposes Mr. Gribble’s greed, then encourages him to leave work, and finally makes him dependent on the wife he has dominated. Her cough introduces the central reversal: his fear of losing the income forces him to protect and indulge its legal owner. Each later episode—the servant, new clothes, the move, household purchases and possession of the monthly payment—advances Mrs. Gribble’s authority by a small degree. The story ends with a classic comic snap: Mr. Gribble hears what sounds like dancing upstairs, confirming that her illness has been at least partly strategic without stating this outright.

Characterisation is caricatural but socially observant. Mr. Gribble is bulky, blustering, hypocritical and obtuse; Mrs. Gribble is physically small and outwardly docile but increasingly resourceful. Their contrasting bodies mirror their initial power imbalance, which the plot overturns. The doctor serves as a knowing accomplice, his “wicked” grin briefly making explicit the narrator’s otherwise restrained hostility toward Mr. Gribble. Overall, the style combines farce with social satire, using deadpan narration, period colloquial dialogue and a neatly engineered reversal to criticise marital tyranny, economic control and male claims to ownership.

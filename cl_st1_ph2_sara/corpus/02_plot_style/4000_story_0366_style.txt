The story adopts a late-Victorian or Edwardian Gothic style, combining an upper-class country-house setting with psychological unease, supernatural suggestion, and a final moment of bodily horror. Its opening manner is urbane, detached, and faintly satirical: grouse shooting, inherited estates, dinner conversation, and fashionable society are viewed through Weigall’s cultivated but jaded sensibility. This social comedy gradually gives way to a darker register as the narrative moves from the castle into the nocturnal woods and finally to the Strid. The progression from civilized enclosure to wild, ancient landscape mirrors Weigall’s movement from rational confidence into terror.

The narration is third-person limited, closely aligned with Weigall’s perceptions and memories. Although the narrator can summarize background information confidently, the crucial sequence is restricted to what Weigall sees, assumes, and gradually understands. This limited viewpoint makes misinterpretation central to the suspense: he first treats Gifford’s disappearance as a practical joke, then mistakes the moving hand for that of a man only just swept into the river, and finally believes that he has rescued his still-living friend. The reader is held inside these hopeful deductions until the last sentence overturns them. Free indirect phrasing and direct access to Weigall’s recollections deepen the intimacy of the viewpoint without shifting into first person.

The prose is formal, literate, and often elaborate. Its vocabulary mixes patrician social language—“rent-roll,” “debutante,” “proffered,” “lineation”—with philosophical and macabre terms such as “vital principle,” “dissevered,” “immutable law,” and “man’s mortality.” Sentences are frequently long and syntactically layered, using subordinate clauses, balanced constructions, semicolons, and cumulative lists. This creates a leisurely, reflective rhythm in the early and middle sections. During the attempted rescue, the syntax becomes shorter and more forceful: actions arrive in rapid succession through active verbs such as “dashed,” “dragged,” “twisted,” “tugged,” “scrambled,” and “sprang.” The final statement—“There was no face”—is exceptionally brief and plain by comparison, deriving its force from the sudden collapse of the story’s ornate descriptive manner.

Description is strongly atmospheric and often personifies the landscape. The river alternately “scold[s],” recovers its “equanimity,” boils with “furious untiring energy,” and holds its victim in a feminized “roaring hold.” Water is presented as both animate and governed by pitiless natural law. Light and darkness are used with similar precision: cold stars, black woods, white foam, and the pale hand create stark visual contrasts that allow the horror to emerge in fragments. The setting is made uncanny through spatial uncertainty—rayless caverns, unseen underwater shelves, and a supposed natural vault filled with the dead. The river therefore functions not merely as scenery but as an ancient, predatory presence and a threshold between the living and the dead.

Dialogue is sparse but important. Gifford’s remembered conversation about the soul supplies the story’s supernatural framework while also characterizing him as witty, speculative, irreverent, and attracted to metaphysical experimentation. The exchange has an epigrammatic quality, with abstract questions answered in polished, paradoxical formulations. Its ideas initially resemble sophisticated after-dinner speculation, but later become ominously literal. The suggestion that the soul may linger in or return to damaged “machinery” prepares the possibility that the hand has acted despite the body’s advanced destruction. This foreshadowing is embedded naturally in the history of the friendship rather than announced as exposition.

The pacing follows a controlled pattern of delay, escalation, and reversal. A broad social introduction establishes Weigall’s temperament; exposition then explains the disappearance; the walk permits atmospheric description and retrospective dialogue; and the Strid sequence narrows attention to a series of partial revelations—the foam, the hand, the fingers, the cuff links, the arm, and finally the body. Repetition of the hand’s gestures sustains the illusion of conscious struggle. At the same time, references to skeletons, devoured flesh, and underwater shelves quietly imply that the body may have been submerged far longer than Weigall believes. The apparent rescue creates a brief release of tension before the smell, Weigall’s physical recoil, and the last visual disclosure transform rescue narrative into supernatural horror.

Emotionally, the story contrasts masculine restraint with intense male devotion. Weigall is initially “continental and detached,” yet his attachment to Gifford proves deeper than his love for any woman. At the climax, memory becomes kaleidoscopic and his cultivated composure gives way to “passionate will and muscle.” This emotional sincerity raises the stakes and prevents the episode from operating as a merely mechanical shock tale. The dominant stylistic effect is one of elegant dread: civilized wit, philosophical speculation, and sensuous landscape writing are methodically concentrated into a grotesque final image whose explanation is withheld. The ending remains deliberately unresolved, leaving the reader to infer whether Gifford’s soul animated the faceless corpse, whether some residual force produced the gestures, or whether the Strid itself has become the agent of a supernatural deception.

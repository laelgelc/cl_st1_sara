The story uses a polished Edwardian comic style built around an urbane, intrusive third-person narrator. The viewpoint stays largely aligned with Owen Bentley, giving access to his hopes, embarrassments and misinterpretations, but the narration remains free to step back and make broad, mock-philosophical pronouncements such as “Routine is death to heroism” or “Man’s power of endurance is limited.” These statements imitate the gravity of moral reflection before being undercut by mundane details involving bank work, telephone calls, postage stamps or badly sung ballads. The narrative voice is confident, amused and gently ironic rather than cruel.

The prose combines long, carefully balanced sentences with short comic conclusions. Extended sentences accumulate biographical facts, qualifications and increasingly absurd details before arriving at a neat punchline. Parenthetical remarks and explanatory digressions are frequent, but they generally contribute to characterization or prepare a joke. Paragraph endings are often used as comic beats, while abrupt sentences such as “He gaped,” “Owen was annoyed,” or “Audrey reflected” provide pauses that sharpen the timing. Formal constructions coexist with colloquial phrases, creating a productive contrast between elevated narration and trivial circumstances.

Humour arises chiefly from incongruity, exaggeration, understatement and reversal. Ordinary people and events are described in mock-heroic language: a bank clerk distributes documents like “a preoccupied monarch scattering largess,” an angry author resembles “Ajax defying the lightning,” and a thrown loaf is treated with the forensic specificity of a lethal projectile. Conversely, major emotional disappointments are met with restrained phrases such as “This is most unfortunate” or “It can’t be helped.” Hyperbolic comparisons, whimsical metaphors and literalized clichés recur throughout. Comic precision is especially important: exact figures, institutional names, postal charges and the loaf’s semolina content make absurd moments sound authoritatively factual.

Characterization is economical and caricatural. Each figure is organized around a dominant comic trait: Owen is amiable, dreamy and impractical; Audrey is affectionate, jealous and energetically certain; Mr Sheppherd embodies pompous paternal respectability; the bank manager is bureaucratically disapproving; and Prosser is explosive, domineering and unexpectedly fair-minded. Physical description is selective and metaphorical rather than realistic. The manager’s resemblance to a stuffed trout, Prosser’s bristling beard and Owen’s tendency to gape establish personalities instantly. Even minor characters receive memorable verbal signatures, such as the head of department’s lack of soul or Mrs Dorman’s repeated promise of “pots o’ money.”

Dialogue is brisk, theatrical and strongly individualized. Exchanges often consist of short lines that alternate rapidly, allowing misunderstandings and shifts of power to unfold with stage-like efficiency. Characters reveal themselves through rhythm as much as content: Audrey speaks in quick questions and decisive assertions, Mr Sheppherd uses hesitant formalities and broken objections, and Prosser delivers booming insults mixed with abrupt generosity. Repetition supplies comic momentum, as in the fortune-teller’s insistence on “pots,” while interruptions and dashes reproduce embarrassment or social discomfort. Dialect is rendered phonetically for rural characters, especially in George’s compressed Shropshire speech, but it is used selectively as a comic obstacle rather than as sustained social realism.

The structure resembles a light stage comedy. It begins with an apparently conventional romantic barrier—the heroine’s father rejecting an impoverished suitor—then develops through a sequence of planted details: Owen’s abandoned dramatization, Audrey’s suspicions, the fortune-telling, Prosser’s violent introduction, the unstamped letters and the theatrical production. These strands converge in the final encounter. The plot repeatedly creates disappointment and then reverses it: Owen discovers that his work was stolen, learns that the culprit believes him, hears that the play has failed, and finally discovers that its American success has made him wealthy. The last sentence delivers both the fulfillment of the prophecy and the decisive comic payoff.

Pacing is leisurely at the level of narration but efficient at the level of plot. Digressions about cricket, banking, theatrical audiences or rural life delay immediate action while planting information and enriching the comic world. Scenes are linked by summary passages that compress routine and emotional decline, while pivotal encounters are dramatized in full. Asterism breaks mark major changes of setting and phase, giving the story an episodic but controlled progression from parental interview to country interlude, bank comedy, theatre revelation and final resolution.

The setting and diction are distinctly British and period-specific. References to Middlesex cricket, musical comedy, bank departments, postal orders, dog-carts, the Savoy, Piccadilly and social distinctions create an early-twentieth-century metropolitan world connected to an older rural England. Money is expressed in pounds, sixpences and twopences, and social identity is closely tied to occupation, income, family influence and respectability. The vocabulary moves easily between formal terms such as “demeanour,” “deplorable” and “sociologist” and slang such as “shot his bolt,” “bad rot,” “by Jove” and “son of a gun.”

Imagery is vivid, playful and usually comic rather than atmospheric. Similes turn moods into visible objects: routine falls like a grey cloud, melancholy envelops Owen, and hope briefly makes him walk on air before his heart turns to lead. The countryside receives more conventionally lyrical description—green fields, miniature rivers, heat-mist and birdsong—but this serenity is soon disrupted by repetitive singing and airborne bread. Sentiment is therefore permitted, especially around Owen’s love for Audrey, but it is rarely left unpunctured for long.

Thematically, the story treats romance, money, authorship, chance and social convention through comic optimism. It mocks parental authority, office bureaucracy, literary vanity and sentimental popular fiction, yet its outlook is fundamentally benevolent. Owen’s incompetence does not lead to punishment; accidents, coincidences and misunderstood actions ultimately reward him. Apparent supernatural prophecy is fulfilled through an intricate chain of ordinary causes. The governing tone is buoyant and reassuring: the world may be officious, absurd and unfair, but comic providence eventually arranges matters in the hero’s favour.

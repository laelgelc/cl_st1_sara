The narrator argues that pantomime mirrors real life. The comic spectacle of a prosperous man falling and being robbed resembles society’s delight in the ruin of the wealthy. Stage pantaloons correspond to dissolute, scheming old men, while clowns resemble brazen real-world swindlers. As an example, the supposed Captain Fitz-Whisker Fiercy and his servant Do’em obtain a house and expensive goods on credit, sell everything, and are eventually arrested when the deceived tradesmen discover the fraud.

The comparison expands to ordinary fools, cast as life’s supernumeraries, and to politicians. Parliament becomes a grand pantomime in which ministers and legislators tumble, quarrel, reverse their positions, and obey the “magic wand” of office. Whoever holds political power can silence supporters, make them speak nonsense, or cause them to change principles and sides. When power passes to a new leader, the performers exchange roles and the spectacle begins again.

The story uses an impressionistic, naturalistic mode of war writing, presenting battle less as an orderly military action than as a bewildering collision of noise, colour, motion and chance. The third-person narrator moves between panoramic descriptions of the battlefield and close access to Fred Collins’s thoughts. This shifting perspective makes individual lives appear small within a vast, almost autonomous machinery of violence, while still giving Collins’s private confusion and fear psychological weight.

The prose is intensely visual and sensory. Colours recur sharply—dark uniforms, blue sky, white trousers, crimson explosions, green grass, brown earth—and physical details often register as momentary pictures caught amid chaos. Sound is equally prominent: guns “argue,” shells howl, volleys crash, officers shriek and canteens tinkle. Smell, heat and bodily sensation complete the effect. The accumulation of sensory impressions reproduces the soldier’s overwhelmed consciousness rather than offering a clear strategic account of the battle.

Figurative language is abundant, forceful and often grotesque. Armies become wrestlers, guns possess “stolidity and courage,” explosions have “demon fingers,” and shells are “practical angels of death.” The battle is repeatedly transformed into elemental or mythic struggle: island-sized animals fight behind the woods, the sea goes mad, and the sky fills with fiends. Personification gives weapons, landscapes and military formations a life of their own, while human beings and regiments are frequently rendered as animals or machines. This reversal suggests that war strips people of mastery and turns them into components of a larger, indifferent organism.

A central stylistic device is contrast. The “fair little meadow,” with its long green grass and gentle breeze, is violated by shells and converted into a scene of “red hate.” Mundane thirst, joking and military routine coexist with mangled horses, wounded men and sudden death. The well’s slow, indifferent water is set against Collins’s frantic urgency. Such juxtapositions produce both horror and dark comedy, making the battlefield simultaneously sublime, absurd and banal.

The syntax alternates between long, cumulative sentences and short, emphatic statements. Extended sentences pile up clauses, parenthetical observations and lists to convey crowded movement or prolonged carnage. Brief declarations—“He was, then, a hero,” “Heroes were not much,” “But Collins turned”—interrupt this flow with ironic or dramatic force. Repetition also shapes the rhythm, particularly in dialogue and in Collins’s self-questioning, while delayed details and sudden sentence breaks imitate shocks of perception.

Dialogue is colloquial, dialectal and socially specific. Elisions and nonstandard spellings such as “yeh,” “git,” “th’” and “wisht” reproduce enlisted men’s speech and sharply distinguish it from the narrator’s elevated descriptive language. The soldiers’ banter is teasing, competitive and performative. Collins’s dangerous mission grows not from formal heroism but from thirst, wounded pride and the pressure of his comrades’ jeers. Officers likewise speak in plain, sometimes comic language, undercutting any romantic grandeur associated with rank or battle.

The narrator’s tone combines compassion with detachment and irony. Heroism is scrutinised rather than celebrated. Collins does not experience himself as noble; he feels dazed, remembers his debts and his unkindness to his mother, and regards himself as an intruder in “the land of fine deeds.” His eventual return to aid the wounded officer is presented as impulsive and terrified rather than serenely courageous. The style therefore treats bravery as morally real but psychologically accidental: a frightened, compromised person may perform a heroic act without becoming an idealised hero.

Structurally, the story narrows from a broad battlefield tableau to Collins’s individual ordeal, then opens again when he rejoins the regiment. The water bucket serves as the organising object and symbolic centre. It begins as the answer to an ordinary bodily need, becomes the goal of a seemingly heroic quest, briefly offers mercy to a dying man, and is finally spilled through playful carelessness. The abrupt ending crystallises the story’s governing irony: enormous risk and genuine compassion culminate in waste. Overall, the style is vivid, cinematic, rhythmically varied and anti-romantic, using grotesque imagery, colloquial humour and psychological irony to expose the unstable boundary between cowardice, courage, absurdity and grace.

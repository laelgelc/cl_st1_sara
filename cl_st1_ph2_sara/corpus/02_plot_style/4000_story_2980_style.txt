The story uses a polished, urbane comic style built on social satire, controlled exaggeration, and a final explanatory twist. Its narrative voice is third-person omniscient, outwardly composed and authoritative but quietly mischievous. The narrator frequently adopts the assumptions and vocabulary of the suburban characters while maintaining enough ironic distance to expose their vanity, credulity, and hunger for excitement. Judgements are usually delivered through dry qualifications rather than direct ridicule: shopping remains “shopping” even if it is not sensational, going from a shop to a tea party becomes “living in a whirl,” and customers linger as if their domestic circumstances were dramatically intolerable. This deadpan treatment lets trivial behaviour acquire mock-heroic importance.

The diction mixes precise, cultivated English with exotic and historical references. Ordinary groceries—anchovy paste, coffee, oranges, dates, figs, and quail seed—are placed beside Assyria, Persian politics, the Old Testament, satraps, sanhedrims, and the Arabian Nights. The contrast between mundane suburban commerce and grandiose foreign intrigue is the story’s main stylistic engine. Elevated similes and allusions repeatedly dignify gossip and misunderstanding far beyond their true significance. At the same time, concrete visual details such as the brass bowl, dark olive skin, astrachan collar, black beard, biscuit-tin barrier, and metal-work purse create a vivid theatrical spectacle. The language is period-specific and socially observant, reflecting early twentieth-century British concerns with department-store competition, railway timetables, fashionable “days,” imperial geography, and popular entertainments.

Sentences are often long, balanced, and syntactically elaborate, with clauses arranged to delay or sharpen the comic point. Parenthetical qualifications, carefully placed adverbs, and formal constructions contribute to the narrator’s air of amused composure. Short sentences and abrupt dialogue are then used for contrast, particularly when a lie, suspicion, or revelation needs emphasis. Lists also play an important role: commercial attractions, improbable theories, grocery orders, and conflicting descriptions accumulate until the characters’ excitement becomes self-parody. Hyperbole remains elegant rather than noisy, as in comparisons involving ferrets, rabid dogs, rattlesnakes, or oppressed wives.

Dialogue drives most of the action and serves as the principal means of characterization. Mr. Scarrick begins in the practical, plaintive register of a struggling tradesman; the artist speaks with confident wit and psychological shrewdness; the shoppers reveal themselves through speculative gossip; and the supposed foreigners use clipped, ceremonious speech that sustains the illusion of danger. The women’s theories escalate rapidly from opium smuggling to Portuguese and Persian political intrigue and finally romance. Their speech is presented neither as wholly foolish nor malicious, but as the imaginative recreation of people bored by routine. Repeated questions about the boy, pomegranates, quail seed, and oranges create a comic pattern, while Mr. Scarrick’s conspicuous denials intensify both suspicion and audience interest.

Characterization is economical and caricatural. Individuals are defined by a few memorable traits, habits, or verbal tendencies rather than psychological depth. Mrs. Greyes and Miss Fritten function partly as representatives of an entire gossip network; Jimmy is the alert accomplice; the artist is the architect of the deception; and the disguised performers are theatrical types whose exotic appearance invites fantasy. Even minor figures are given sharply comic touches, such as the aunt who loses faith in the purity of the jam or the customer asking insincere questions about “the home life of the Bombay duck.” Names such as Scarrick, Fritten, Lipping, and Yorling have a faintly whimsical, socially comic flavour.

The structure resembles a miniature stage comedy or confidence trick. An opening conversation establishes the commercial problem and hints at a proposed solution without revealing it. A scene break then moves directly to the scheme in operation. Each successive shop scene adds more witnesses, more purchases, and more elaborate performances, so that curiosity is converted into increased trade. Repetition with variation produces rising momentum: the boy appears, the stranger follows, the grocer lies, the customers speculate, and the shop grows busier. The climactic encounter involving the veiled woman and the oranges supplies the most dramatic display, after which the mysterious figures disappear. A second scene break introduces the brief coda, where the artist’s model and hired beard reveal the artifice. The ending is concise and retrospective, causing every exotic detail to be reinterpreted as theatrical stage property.

Pacing is leisurely at first, allowing the social setting and premise to develop, then increasingly brisk as crowds and rumours multiply. Suspense is genuine at the level of scene construction even though the overall tone signals comedy. Information is carefully withheld: the artist’s proposed appeal to a universal instinct is interrupted, and the exact plan remains unstated until the final exchange. This omission makes curiosity both the subject of the story and the mechanism by which the reader is engaged.

The dominant humour is ironic, mock-heroic, and epigrammatic. It arises from incongruity, overinterpretation, social pretension, and the discrepancy between appearance and reality. The suburban shoppers imagine themselves witnesses to international conspiracy while actually participating in an advertising stunt. Their curiosity makes them easy to manipulate, but the satire is genial rather than savage because the artist, grocer, customers, and reader all share the appetite for mystery. The story ultimately treats curiosity, gossip, and the desire for spectacle as universal commercial forces. Its style is therefore best characterized as elegant satirical realism enlivened by theatrical plotting, exotic parody, precise social observation, and a restrained but persistent narrative wit.

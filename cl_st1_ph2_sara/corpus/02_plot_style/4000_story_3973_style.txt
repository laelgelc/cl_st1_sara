The story uses a polished comic style built around an omniscient third-person narrator who remains close to Mr Birdsey’s perceptions while freely supplying background, judgment, and ironic commentary. The narrator treats Birdsey with affectionate amusement: his timidity, baseball obsession, and subordinate place within his family are exaggerated, but never cruelly mocked. This sympathetic focalisation is essential to the story’s moral logic, because the reader is encouraged to understand why loyalty between “exiled fans” temporarily matters more to him than law, property, or conventional respectability.

The tone blends urbane irony, farce, and light suspense. Serious matters—kidnapping, exile, bank robbery, arrest, and armed confrontation—are repeatedly translated into comic terms. Birdsey is “scooped” from his chair “as if he had been a clam,” transported like a liquid to his shipboard cabin, and described as having to “jump through hoops and sham dead” for his wife and daughter. Such incongruous imagery reduces domestic tyranny and criminal danger to the scale of comic performance. The narration often adopts mock-heroic language, describing champagne as a relieving army, a dinner invitation as a grand enterprise, and Birdsey’s football tackle as the resurrection of an ancient warrior instinct. Elevated phrasing is placed beside colloquial concerns, producing a characteristic contrast between grand form and ridiculous circumstance.

The diction combines formal British narrative prose with energetic American speech. The narration uses phrases such as “crowning benevolence,” “peculiarly immoral remark,” and “dignified reproof,” while Birdsey’s dialogue is full of Americanisms: “You bet,” “for the love of Mike,” “side-step it,” “raw work,” and “you’ve got to hand it to him.” Baseball terminology—“home-runs,” “ninth innings,” “bleachers,” “root,” and “fan”—acts both as social shorthand and as the story’s emotional vocabulary. Differences in register also define character: Birdsey is expansive and impulsive, Waterall clipped and self-righteous, and Benyon hesitant, fragmented, and increasingly desperate.

Sentences are generally fluent, balanced, and syntactically elaborate, particularly in exposition. Long periodic sentences accumulate qualifications before arriving at a comic payoff, as in the account of Birdsey’s forced removal from New York or his belated return to footballing form. These are offset by short declarative paragraphs and rapid dialogue at moments of revelation or action. Repetition is used for emphasis and rhythm—“Wine, wine!” and “Run! Run, you fool! Run!”—while parenthetical qualifications and narrator interventions sharpen the irony. Figurative language is abundant but concrete, drawing on food, warfare, animals, sport, machinery, and theatrical performance.

Characterisation depends on caricature moderated by precise human feeling. Birdsey is physically and socially comic—a “small, stout, red-faced little man”—but his childlike enthusiasm, generosity, and need for companionship make him emotionally credible. Waterall’s “vulturine” face anticipates his predatory role, though the narrator briefly acknowledges his concealed nervousness and thereby prevents him from becoming entirely mechanical. Benyon is introduced through bodily signs—pallor, gleaming eyes, clenched teeth, trembling hands—so that the mystery surrounding him is registered before it is explained. Secondary information is delivered economically through retrospective summary, especially the account of Birdsey’s marriage, exile, and domestic submission.

The structure is tightly controlled and escalatory. It begins with apparent fulfilment after the baseball game, moves into a chance social gathering, introduces unease through Benyon’s reactions and Waterall’s scrutiny, and then converts convivial comedy into a recognition scene. The dinner functions as a confined stage on which suspicion intensifies through pauses, evasions, repeated questions, and failed small talk. After the criminal revelation, the story shifts into melodrama—the pistol and telephone call—before resolving through physical farce. The final line supplies a second reversal: Birdsey’s heroic defiance collapses into fear of his wife. This circular ending restores the domestic predicament introduced at the beginning and ensures that the criminal escape remains subordinate to the comic character study.

Dialogue carries much of the later narrative and is paced through interruptions, hesitations, and strategically brief exchanges. Dashes and broken phrases expose concealment or anxiety, while understated stage directions—someone crumbling bread, taking out a cigarette case, or turning toward the telephone—build tension without abandoning the light tone. The narrator frequently interprets gestures for comic effect, making external action and internal response work together.

The central comic device is the elevation of fandom into an ethical code. To Birdsey, devotion to baseball forms a fraternity stronger than citizenship or law: Benyon’s crime becomes almost irrelevant beside his willingness to risk capture for a game. This absurd hierarchy is presented with enough emotional sincerity to become temporarily persuasive. The story’s style therefore rests on a sustained tension between conventional morality and personal sympathy, between respectability and rebellion, and between melodramatic danger and comic inconsequence. Its overall effect is brisk, theatrical, warmly ironic, and carefully engineered toward a concise closing punchline.

The story’s style is comic-realist and satirical, built on the collision between provincial dullness, social pretension, and sudden farce. Its world is rendered through an accumulation of concrete, incongruous details: decaying plaster, muddy streets, pigs with “grave faces,” unfinished buildings, shared carriages, military moustaches, crowded dinner tables, and absurd assortments of market goods. Description rarely remains neutral. Physical objects and social customs are personified, exaggerated, or arranged into comic catalogues, so that the setting itself becomes a caricature of stagnant provincial life.

The narrator is omniscient but conspicuously present, speaking in a conversational, digressive manner and occasionally claiming imperfect knowledge or memory. Remarks such as an inability to recall why the general gave his dinner, uncertainty over the scandal that ended Tchertokoutski’s military career, and ignorance of how a glass of punch reached his elbow create mock innocence. The narrator plainly understands more than is admitted, and this feigned uncertainty intensifies the irony. Direct addresses to the reader are limited, but the narration retains the air of an amused raconteur who pauses to gossip, qualify, and comment.

The tone combines genial amusement with sharp social ridicule. The satire is rarely openly moralising; instead, characters condemn themselves through their habits, possessions, speech, and vanity. Provincial officials are reduced to comic routines—the chief of police sleeps almost continuously, while the judge’s domestic arrangement is mentioned with casual impropriety. Officers are animated chiefly by tobacco, gambling, horses, drink, rank, and flirtation. Tchertokoutski is defined by his desire to appear aristocratic and military: his coat, spurs, moustache, lavish purchases, mortgaged peasants, exaggerated hospitality, and implausible claims about his calash all expose a life organised around display rather than substance.

Characterisation proceeds through grotesque external details and socially revealing anecdotes rather than psychological analysis. Bodies, clothing, gestures, and possessions function as shorthand for personality. A stout man’s arms resemble potatoes; moustaches are as harsh as clothes brushes; drunken guests are carried away like bundles; Tchertokoutski returns with thistles caught in his moustache. Even the mare is given the ceremonious human name “Agrafena Ivanovna,” making the officers’ solemn admiration of her comically social. Similes are vivid, domestic, and deliberately undignified, repeatedly lowering supposedly grand figures into the realm of animals, household objects, food, and physical awkwardness.

The prose favours long, syntactically expansive sentences packed with qualifications, lists, subordinate observations, and comic afterthoughts. These sentences often begin with an ordinary descriptive point and drift into increasingly absurd particulars. Enumeration is a central device: foods, bottles, market wares, guests, vehicles, and the alleged contents of the calash are catalogued until abundance itself becomes humorous. Against these elaborate passages, short declarative sentences and abrupt dialogue produce comic emphasis. Repetition also shapes the rhythm, especially the general’s “puff—puff” interruptions and the escalating cries of “Get up!” when the forgotten invitation returns as a crisis.

Dialogue is strongly marked by rank and social performance. Tchertokoutski’s repeated “your excellency” and “condescended” reveal obsequiousness disguised as refinement. The general’s habit of making junior officers repeat themselves turns hierarchy into a recurring verbal joke. Much of the conversation consists of empty agreement, boasting, polite formulas, and inflated claims. Speech therefore functions less as genuine communication than as an instrument of status, self-advertisement, and mutual flattery. Drunken dialogue becomes increasingly disconnected, mirroring the collapse of decorum and reason.

The narrative structure moves from broad social panorama to tightly focused comic exposure. It opens slowly with an extensive portrait of the town, then introduces the regiment as a source of superficial animation. The general’s dinner gathers the social types together, while the discussion of the calash plants the central boast and the invitation that will undo its maker. The card-playing and drinking sequence delays Tchertokoutski’s departure through a mock-fatal chain of minor temptations. The following morning initially slows into an elegant description of his wife’s toilette and the summer garden, creating calm before the approaching vehicles transform the story into rapid farce. From that point, the pace accelerates through panic, concealment, denial, inspection, and discovery.

A dominant technique is bathos: elevated language and aristocratic ceremony repeatedly collapse into trivial or humiliating reality. Tchertokoutski imagines a magnificent dinner but sleeps through the preparations; he claims ownership of an extraordinary Viennese calash, yet the officers find an ordinary vehicle; he attempts to preserve his dignity by hiding inside the very object of his boast. The final revelation is especially economical. The general’s understated “Hullo, there you are,” followed by his calmly covering Tchertokoutski again, is more devastating than an explicit rebuke. The ending converts the calash into a visual trap and makes the pretender himself its ridiculous hidden contents.

The overall effect is one of controlled comic escalation. Realistic observation supplies the social credibility, grotesque exaggeration enlarges every vanity, and narrative irony ensures that self-importance produces its own punishment. The style carries a slightly formal, nineteenth-century translated texture in expressions such as “equipage,” “turn-out,” “nobiliary elections,” and “had taken up its quarters,” but this formality strengthens the humour by contrasting ceremonial diction with mud, drunkenness, debt, animal imagery, and physical embarrassment.

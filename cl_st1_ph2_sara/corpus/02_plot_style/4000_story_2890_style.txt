The story uses a retrospective first-person voice: an elderly, cultivated narrator looks back across several decades with a mixture of tenderness, irony, moral concern, and self-conscious sentiment. His personality is strongly present. He interrupts the narrative to qualify his judgments, address Jimmy directly, question his own motives, and comment upon age, memory, fashion, charity, and human weakness. Apostrophes such as “Ah, poor, poor Jimmy” and “God guard us all” create the impression of an oral elegy, while confessions about tears and dotage make the account intimate and deliberately vulnerable. The narrator is broadly sympathetic but not uncritical; his compassion is accompanied by wry observations about vanity, social hypocrisy, and the instability of prosperity.

The diction is ornate, antiquarian, and distinctly nineteenth-century. Latinate abstractions such as “transmutation,” “resplendence,” “prodigality,” and “contumelious” coexist with colloquial expressions including “clean smash,” “burst,” and “snug lump of coin.” This contrast distinguishes the narrator’s elevated, reflective discourse from the brisk, often callous speech of the commercial world. The language also delights in elaborate labels and playful formulations: Jimmy becomes a “promenading pauper,” his compliments are “alms” to the rich, and Time is called the “true plagiarist of the seasons.” Social titles, old-fashioned forms of address, and references to tea, toast, claret, militia generals, drawing rooms, and counting houses establish a historically specific urban milieu.

The syntax is expansive and highly periodic. Sentences frequently accumulate subordinate clauses, parenthetical remarks, appositions, lists, and delayed conclusions. The narrator may begin with an architectural detail, digress into an analogy or personal aside, and only later return to the grammatical thread. Semicolons and dashes regulate these long movements, while short declarative sentences—“Jimmy was a ruined man” and “Jimmy Rose is dead!”—provide emphatic shocks. Repetition is a central rhetorical device. Words such as “poor,” “roses,” “old,” and “still” recur with incantatory force, and parallel constructions intensify both comedy and pathos. Exclamations, rhetorical questions, direct addresses, and deliberate self-corrections give the prose the cadence of impassioned speech.

Description is dense, visual, and symbolically charged. The old house is rendered through architectural particulars—arched brick bins, oak timbers, carved mantels, shutters, wainscots, and faded wallpaper—but these objects do more than furnish the setting. The house embodies the survival of an aristocratic social past inside a modern commercial city. Its damaged peacocks symbolize Jimmy’s ruined magnificence, while the wallpaper’s enduring roses correspond to the inexplicable bloom in his cheeks and, more broadly, to the persistence of grace beneath poverty. The imagery repeatedly joins splendor to decay: jeweled birds are bedraggled by damp, grand parlors become storage spaces, and a once princely host survives in a threadbare coat. These visual antitheses supply the story’s governing emotional pattern.

The narrative structure moves associatively from place to memory and from memory to moral meditation. An account of the narrator’s inherited house leads to the parlor wallpaper, which leads to Jimmy Rose, whose rise, collapse, disappearance, impoverished old age, and death form the central embedded biography. The plot is therefore less a steadily unfolding action than a commemorative portrait assembled from scenes and recollections. Major transitions are marked by compressed statements, refrains, or abrupt temporal leaps. Dialogue is used sparingly but vividly, especially to expose character: Jimmy’s elegant compliment displays his courtliness, his former guest’s casual invitation after reporting the bankruptcy reveals selfishness, and Jimmy’s rejection of charitable books preserves both his pride and his comic irritability.

The tone continually balances humor and lament. Comic exaggeration appears in the narrator’s treatment of fashionable wallpaper, gluttonous guests, militia titles, stale compliments, and genteel charity. Yet the humor rarely cancels sympathy; instead, it protects the story from becoming wholly mournful and makes Jimmy’s humiliation more human. Sentiment is overt and sometimes melodramatic, but it is moderated by irony and by the narrator’s awareness that moralizing can itself be ungenerous. The result is a compassionate social satire in which the respectable rich are often less graceful than the impoverished dependent they merely tolerate.

Pacing varies according to emotional emphasis. Architectural description and social commentary proceed slowly, with lavish enumeration, whereas Jimmy’s financial catastrophe is delivered in sudden, forceful strokes. Dramatic scenes—the unanswered door, the voice through the keyhole, the furtive tea visits, and the deathbed episode—are isolated within broad stretches of summary. This alternation gives the story the quality of remembered life: long periods are condensed, while certain painful or emblematic moments remain sharply preserved.

Overall, the style is elegiac, digressive, rhetorical, and symbolist in its use of physical surroundings. It combines the manners of a personal reminiscence, a moral essay, an urban social sketch, and a funeral tribute. Its characteristic effect comes from oppositions between past and present, fashion and commerce, abundance and want, pride and dependence, outward decay and inward generosity. The closing return to the parlor unifies these strands, transforming the faded peacocks and perpetual roses into a final emblem of Jimmy’s fallen worldly state and surviving spiritual beauty.

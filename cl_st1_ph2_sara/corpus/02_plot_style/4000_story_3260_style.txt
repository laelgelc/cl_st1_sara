The story uses a retrospective first-person narrator who combines the roles of traveller, social observer and sympathetic witness. He begins with a direct, rhetorically energetic address to the reader, challenging common prejudices through repeated questions, exclamations and appeals such as “Let us… be just.” This creates an urbane, conversational voice with a gently satirical edge. The narrator initially treats bureaucracy and rank with comic irony, but gradually shifts into a more restrained, elegiac mode. His authority derives from personal experience—years of travel and acquaintance with postmasters—while his occasional admissions of youthful arrogance and curiosity make him fallible, humane and self-aware.

The narrative has a framed, layered structure. A broad essay-like defence of postmasters narrows into the narrator’s memories of one station, after which the postmaster recounts Dounia’s disappearance in an extended embedded narrative. A final return to the station supplies an epilogue through the testimony of a village boy. Information therefore reaches the reader indirectly, through memories, reports and belated discoveries. This mediation preserves uncertainty about Dounia’s motives and experience: she is central to the plot but rarely granted an independent voice. The structure depends on repeated visits separated by years, allowing physical surroundings and altered appearances to register the passage of time.

The prose balances economical narration with carefully selected concrete detail. Interiors are rendered through memorable domestic objects—the balsam pots, speckled curtains, tea-urn, faded ribbons and wall pictures—rather than through elaborate description. These objects establish class, atmosphere and emotional continuity. On later visits, their disappearance or deterioration communicates loss with little explicit commentary. Clothing, vehicles, ranks, money and forms of address likewise locate characters precisely within the social hierarchy. The use of abbreviated place-names such as “N——” and “A——” gives the account the air of an authentic but discreet memoir.

Syntax varies according to function. The opening relies on parallel questions, accumulations and exclamatory punctuation, producing a brisk polemical rhythm. Narrative passages generally use lucid, chronological sentences linked by clear temporal transitions: “The next day,” “Another day passed,” and “Several years passed.” Emotional scenes employ repetitions, pauses, ellipses and short questions, while the postmaster’s speech becomes cumulative and plaintive, particularly in his repeated invocations of Dounia. Parenthetical remarks and direct appeals keep the narrator visibly present even when he is reporting another person’s story.

Dialogue is plain, socially marked and dramatically functional. Titles such as “Your Excellency,” diminutives such as Dounia and Vanka, and deferential formulas expose inequalities of rank and power. The postmaster’s language is emotional, proverbial and religious—misfortune is “decreed,” and Dounia becomes an “erring ewe-lamb”—whereas Minsky speaks with polished assurance and treats irreversible harm as a practical fact. The child’s simple repetition of “such a kind lady” gives the conclusion an artless pathos. Dialogue often carries moral meaning through contrast rather than authorial explanation.

The tonal range moves from social comedy to domestic tragedy without abandoning irony. Rank-conscious travellers, self-important officials and the doctor who retrospectively boasts of his suspicions are treated humorously, yet the postmaster himself is never reduced to a caricature. Even his drinking and sentimental language are presented with affectionate qualification. The narrator acknowledges that the punch contributed to his tears, then insists that those tears remain genuine. This mixture of irony and compassion prevents the story from becoming purely melodramatic.

Symbolism is understated but structurally important. The pictures of the Prodigal Son foreshadow Dounia’s departure and possible return, yet the biblical pattern is revised: she does return, but too late to receive her father’s forgiveness or restore the household. Roads, stations and vehicles symbolize transience and unequal mobility. Wealthy travellers can arrive, command resources and disappear, while the postmaster remains fixed in place. Seasonal imagery reinforces the emotional progression: the first meeting occurs amid spring rain and youthful vitality, whereas the final visit takes place under autumn clouds among reaped fields and falling leaves.

Characterization is concise and often visual. The narrator first sees the postmaster as fresh, strong and decorated with medals, then as grey, unshaven, bent and prematurely aged. Dounia is presented through beauty, composure and social adaptability; Minsky through charm, calculated deception and coercive privilege. Characters are morally complex rather than wholly schematic. Dounia appears to leave voluntarily after initially weeping, Minsky seems genuinely attached to her while remaining brutal toward her father, and the postmaster’s love contains both tenderness and possessiveness. The story allows these contradictions to stand.

Pacing is controlled through summary and scene. Years pass in a sentence, while decisive moments—the feigned illness, the journey to church, the attempted recovery of Dounia and the visit to the grave—are slowed into detailed action and dialogue. Suspense often arises from delay: waiting for horses, waiting for Dounia to return, waiting outside locked doors, and receiving news only after it can no longer alter events. The ending avoids a direct reunion and instead offers second-hand evidence of Dounia’s remorse, producing emotional closure without complete resolution.

Overall, the style blends social satire, sentimental pathos and early psychological realism. It is accessible and seemingly simple, but carefully patterned through repetition, foreshadowing, altered settings and narrative distance. Its dominant effects are compassionate irony, belated recognition and quiet tragedy. The translation retains a somewhat formal, period quality through constructions such as “Scarcely had…,” elevated exclamations, capitalized titles and culturally specific terms supplemented by footnotes.

The story adopts the manner of an orally transmitted Irish folktale, combining plain communal storytelling with visionary symbolism and supernatural menace. Its third-person narrator remains close to Hanrahan while retaining the authority of a traditional storyteller who knows the customs, geography, and beliefs of the community. Phrases such as “there are many go wandering and forgetting like that” present extraordinary events as part of accepted folk knowledge rather than as phenomena requiring rational explanation. The narrative voice is calm, grave, and largely non-psychological: Hanrahan’s inner life is conveyed through simple impulses, remembered obligations, fear, weariness, and enchantment rather than through extended analysis.

The prose is deliberately archaic and Hiberno-English in construction. Idioms such as “died from her,” “has happened her,” “the length of a year,” “with the dint of,” and “he eat it” reproduce regional speech patterns and give the narrative a translated or spoken quality. Diction is generally concrete and accessible, drawing on rural objects and materials—flannel, frieze, barrels, dip candles, loose stones, heather, frost, thatch—while proper names such as Ballylee, Slieve Echtge, Lough Greine, Daire-caol, and Druim-da-rod root the supernatural action in a precisely imagined Irish landscape. Against this plain vocabulary, occasional lyrical images acquire unusual force: the old man’s hand is “thin and withered like a bird’s claw,” the cards become “rings of fire,” and the queen’s sigh contains “the sound…of hidden streams.”

Sentence structure is cumulative and paratactic, with clauses repeatedly linked by “and,” “but,” “then,” and “for.” This produces the forward flow of spoken narration, in which one event follows another with little formal interruption. Many sentences are long, but their grammar remains simple, relying on coordination rather than elaborate subordination. Repetition supplies rhythm and emphasis: “I will follow, I will follow on,” “That’s not fair, that’s not fair,” “He is weak, he is weak,” and “It is a pity, it is a great pity.” Formulaic descriptions—“Hanrahan the learned man and the great songmaker,” “the woman that was like a queen”—further evoke legend and oral recitation.

Dialogue is frequent, direct, and minimally framed. Speakers reveal themselves through idiom and practical concerns rather than through individualized verbal styles. The villagers’ teasing, hospitality, warnings, and proverbial explanations establish a collective social voice, while the supernatural figures speak in compressed, ritualistic declarations. The repeated card formula, “Spades and Diamonds, Courage and Power; Clubs and Hearts, Knowledge and Pleasure,” converts an ordinary game into an occult system. Later, the four women’s single-word utterances—“Pleasure,” “Power,” “Courage,” and “Knowledge”—give the visionary scene the quality of a test, initiation, or allegorical ceremony.

The narrative moves from realistic domestic detail into enchantment by gradual escalation. It begins in a cluttered barn with drink, song, gossip, and cards; fixation on the game then creates a dreamlike suspension of ordinary purpose. The transformation of cards into hare and hounds marks the full crossing into supernatural experience. From there, space and time become unstable: the hunt travels over recognizable terrain but rises into the sky, a hidden doorway opens into daylight, and one night becomes an entire missing year. The transition back to reality is abrupt and physical, marked by frost, soreness, hunger, damaged clothing, and the decay of Hanrahan’s book. This contrast between sensuous vision and bodily ruin gives the wonder a tragic rather than escapist character.

Pacing is leisurely and accretive at the outset, lingering over setting, conversation, and the mechanics of the card game. It quickens during the magical hunt through repeated movement verbs and geographical progression, then slows almost to stillness in the shining house, where symbolic objects are presented one by one. The final section accelerates through recognition and belated action, ending not with reunion or explanation but with irreversible loss. The structure is circular: the second Samhain gathering echoes the first through the fire, whiskey bottle, hospitality, and cards. This repetition restores Hanrahan’s memory, but too late to undo the consequences of his enchantment.

The dominant atmosphere is liminal. Samhain, the full moon, thresholds, half-doors, underground rivers, mountain paths, and hidden entrances all mark boundaries between ordinary and otherworldly existence. Light and darkness are used symbolically: candles and fire belong to communal human space; moonlight casts deceptive shadows; the card trick reduces the barn to darkness and fiery hands; the otherworld is impossibly bright; and Hanrahan finally departs in the “white light of the morning.” Seasonal dislocation reinforces the same effect, since he enters a place filled with summer flowers and scents but wakes amid winter frost.

Characterization is archetypal but not wholly impersonal. Hanrahan is introduced through memorable physical and social traits—tall, strong, red-haired, learned, musical, sociable—and through emblematic possessions: his inkpot, primer, and Virgil. His defining weakness is susceptibility to diversion and enchantment. He intends to choose love and domestic responsibility, yet abandons that purpose for play, curiosity, and supernatural glamour. His failure in the shining house is presented as a failure of speech and will: although ordinarily eloquent and fearless, he cannot ask the necessary questions. The later deterioration of his Virgil and his inability to read it externalize the loss of identity, learning, and vocation.

Symbolism is overt but remains partly unexplained. The suits of cards correspond to four abstract powers; the cauldron, stone, spear, and sword embody Pleasure, Power, Courage, and Knowledge; and the sleeping Echtge appears to await a liberating question or choice that Hanrahan cannot make. The hare and hounds suggest an irresistible supernatural hunt, while the cards represent chance, temptation, and the surrender of agency. The “hungry grass” and the idea of being “given the touch” invoke folklore as a communal vocabulary for disorientation, compulsion, and disappearance. The story never fully decodes these elements, preserving mystery and allowing symbolic and folkloric meanings to coexist.

The tone blends hospitality, humour, awe, dread, and melancholy. Early teasing and conviviality make Hanrahan’s departure from ordinary life more poignant. The supernatural is beautiful as well as dangerous, but its beauty produces paralysis rather than fulfilment. The ending is understated: Mary Lavelle has not died spectacularly but has been dispossessed, married another man, and disappeared into migrant labour. This social realism gives material consequence to the magical episode. The central themes are missed opportunity, the fragility of intention, enchantment as self-loss, the conflict between wandering art and settled love, and time’s indifference to human delay. Overall, the style is folkloric, incantatory, geographically rooted, symbolically charged, and tragic in its treatment of a man who passes out of ordinary time and returns only after the life he meant to choose has vanished.

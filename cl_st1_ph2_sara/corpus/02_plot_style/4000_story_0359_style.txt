The story adopts the manner of a nineteenth-century Christmas reminiscence, combining domestic realism, urban antiquarianism, oral folklore, gentle comedy, and restrained supernatural horror. Its frame is intimate and autobiographical: a first-person narrator looks back on his youth as a newly commissioned lieutenant, recovering from illness and stranded in Christiania on Christmas Eve. The retrospective voice is genial, observant, and lightly self-mocking. Although the narrator begins in loneliness and “black” spirits, his melancholy never becomes psychologically severe; it mainly prepares the emotional contrast between isolation, fireside companionship, nocturnal unease, and the final joyful return home.

The narrative has a pronounced frame-tale structure. The opening establishes the narrator’s circumstances and the old-fashioned household, after which the action becomes a communal storytelling session. A sequence of embedded folktales—about brownies, trolls, Peter Gynt, and a congregation of the dead—occupies most of the story. These tales are introduced through conversation and attributed to distinct speakers, preserving the sense that folklore passes from servant to mistress, parent to child, and one generation to another. The frame resumes near the end, when the stories mingle in the narrator’s feverish dream before ordinary reality breaks in with the arrival of his father’s servant. Structurally, the movement is circular and restorative: the narrator begins separated from home and ends travelling back to it.

The prose is descriptive, leisurely, and strongly atmospheric. Exterior scenes rely on wintry visual and auditory details: whistling wind, sweeping snow, black sky, dark façades, sledge-bells, and lamplit windows. Interiors are rendered through firelight, old furniture, portraits, deep rooms, passages, garrets, and heavy architectural features. The physical setting does more than provide background; it creates a credible habitat for inherited superstition. The old house, with its gloomy staircases and ghostly associations, harmonises with the age and habits of the landladies, while the Christmas setting makes the transition from ordinary domestic life to folklore feel natural.

Diction is formal and period-specific, with spellings and expressions such as “cosey,” “countenances,” “by my troth,” “gude-wife,” and “Lor’ bless and preserve us.” Social titles—“Lieutenant,” “Madame,” “Miss,” and “Mother”—are used frequently and help establish a ceremonious, hierarchical world. The vocabulary often has an antiquarian flavour, especially in descriptions of buildings, clothes, furniture, and customs. At the same time, the embedded tales use plainer, more repetitive language, conventional formulas such as “once upon a time,” and colloquial exclamations. This contrast distinguishes the educated narrator’s polished recollection from the oral style of nursery and household storytelling.

Sentence structure tends toward long, coordinated or subordinated sentences containing qualifications, digressions, and accumulations of detail. Semicolons, dashes, and commas sustain a conversational flow, while periodic sentences allow descriptions or anecdotes to unfold gradually. The narrators within the story frequently interrupt themselves to correct uncertain details—whether an event happened on a Thursday or Christmas Eve, where someone lived, or what a forgotten name was. These hesitations imitate spontaneous speech and lend the tales an air of oral authenticity. Repetition is also important: phrases such as “tell us,” “Christmas Eve,” and “the brownie” create rhythm, reinforce the communal setting, and reproduce the eager insistence of children.

Dialogue is abundant and theatrical. Characters are differentiated less through deep interior analysis than through speech habits, gestures, and recurring physical details. The children speak in excited choruses, using emphatic repetitions and direct emotional declarations. Miss Mette and Miss Cicely are courteous and old-fashioned; Mother Skau is blunt, sceptical, garrulous, and bodily comic, punctuating her stories with coughing, choking, and pinches of snuff. Thor’s rural speech is direct and repetitive, especially when he reports the father’s instructions. These voices give the piece the quality of a performed fireside gathering rather than a private literary meditation.

The handling of the supernatural is deliberately ambiguous. Within the inset tales, brownies, trolls, and ghosts are treated as factual presences, but the frame narrative does not confirm their objective reality. Stories are repeatedly supported by chains of hearsay—someone knew a servant who saw a brownie, or a truthful mother had witnessed evidence—yet the speakers also admit uncertainty and contradiction. Humour tempers fear: trolls mistake a bear for a cat, brownies are tricked into noisy houses, and frightened children discover that their own raised legs have caused the sensation of being touched. Even the narrator’s climactic encounter with the peasant from his dream receives a natural explanation. The style therefore preserves the imaginative force of folklore without requiring firm belief in it.

Tone shifts fluidly between nostalgia, amusement, suspense, and warmth. The brownie stories are mischievous and earthy, often ending with violence presented as comic justice. The church story is quieter and more genuinely uncanny, using pallor, silence, recognition, and the torn cloak to intensify dread. The narrator’s subsequent dream turns that fear into grotesque comedy when his dead grandfather somersaults from the pulpit and calls for a dance. This tonal modulation prevents the story from becoming either solemnly supernatural or merely farcical. Its dominant mood is affectionate nostalgia for old customs, old houses, local legends, and communal Christmas life.

Pacing is slow and expansive in the opening description, episodic through the central sequence of tales, briefly accelerated during moments of supernatural danger, and swift in the concluding journey home. The repeated pattern of invitation, story, audience reaction, and request for another tale creates a cumulative fireside rhythm. The final section resolves the frame rapidly, with the father’s servant arriving, the mare carrying the narrator through good roads, and the family home appearing almost as the fulfilment of a Christmas wish.

The story’s imagery depends heavily on contrasts: darkness and firelight, cold streets and warm rooms, sickness and restored health, solitude and company, fear and laughter, the dead congregation and the living family. Food functions as a recurring emblem of domestic abundance and supernatural exchange. Christmas porridge may appease a brownie, stolen delicacies provoke punishment, troll feasts become grotesque parodies of human celebration, and the landladies’ cakes, jellies, wine, porridge, and pork restore ordinary cheer. The closing homecoming completes this movement from deprivation to nourishment and belonging.

Overall, the style is that of a folkloric Christmas tale presented through a polished memoiristic frame. Its defining features are an avuncular first-person narrator, detailed antiquarian settings, long conversational sentences, vivid character dialogue, nested oral narratives, comic treatment of superstition, carefully moderated ghostly suspense, and a sentimental but controlled conclusion. The prose values sociability and inherited tradition above plot economy: its central pleasure lies in hearing different voices tell old stories while winter and darkness gather outside.

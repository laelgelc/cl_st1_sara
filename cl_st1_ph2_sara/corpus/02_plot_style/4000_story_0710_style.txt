The story adopts the style of an early twentieth-century British supernatural tale, combining pastoral description, social comedy and gradually intensifying folk horror. Its central effect depends on moving almost imperceptibly from an ordinary, recognisable world into one governed by animistic and fairy-tale logic. The opening is practical and geographical—roads, directions, stiles, fields and a distant red house—but the landscape soon acquires imaginative force. The exposed countryside is exuberant and liberating, while the enclosed wood is watchful, deceptive and actively hostile.

The narration is third-person and closely focalised through the clerk, often slipping into free indirect discourse. Descriptions, judgments and rationalisations are coloured by his consciousness without always being formally marked as thought. This proximity lets the narrative preserve ambiguity: impossible events are presented vividly, then attributed by the protagonist to wind, light, nettles, confusion or optical illusion. Repeated qualifications such as “of course,” “no doubt,” “it seemed” and “apparently” record his increasingly strained attempts to maintain a rational interpretation. The narrator neither fully confirms nor denies the supernatural, though the accumulation of incidents strongly favours it.

The protagonist is characterised through ironic contrasts. He begins as a respectable, practical, middle-aged surveyor’s clerk whose profession depends on accurate measurement and spatial judgment, yet he is repeatedly disoriented. His mundane status—his salary, Croydon office, umbrella, hat and official dignity—is set against his suppressed romantic imagination. In the open air he dreams of swords, spurs, castles and princesses; in the wood that appetite for adventure is answered in a darker, archaic form. His occupational confidence makes the assault on his perceptions especially comic and unsettling. Colloquial speech forms such as “I’ll ’ave a look” and “the trees has mixed me up” add class and regional colouring while keeping him sympathetic and unheroic.

The diction alternates between concrete everyday language and lyrical, personifying description. Outside the wood, the wind is “tearing” and “shouting,” clouds “cannon” across the sky, the Downs resemble a wave and Chanctonbury Ring becomes a ship. Inside, light flickers “like silver wings,” vegetation crowds and grasps, and the wood appears to shift, watch, laugh and make decisions. Nature is not passive scenery but an animate presence. Verbs carry much of this energy: trees “dart,” branches “close,” glades “carry” the clerk inward, and the oak “deliberately” moves to intercept him. The recurring attribution of agency to the landscape prepares the reader to accept the wood itself as the antagonist.

Sentence rhythm is used to control pace. The opening contains broad, balanced descriptive sentences suitable to the expansive landscape. As unease grows, syntax becomes more crowded and kinetic, with accumulating clauses, participial phrases, lists and abrupt reversals. Semicolons and dashes reproduce the clerk’s changing perceptions and self-corrections. During the climax, repeated verbs and compressed sequences—running, turning, searching, charging—create breathlessness. Short emphatic statements such as “It watched him” interrupt the longer movement and give the supernatural threat sudden certainty.

Repetition supplies both structure and psychological pressure. The clerk repeatedly loses and regains the road, the gate, the fields and the red house; each apparent escape turns into deeper entrapment. Light and shadow alternate, the wind sounds like voices and footsteps, and the brown-green figures appear first as an illusion, then as moving beings, and finally as agents who expel him. These recurrences form a tightening spiral rather than a straightforward journey. The wood manipulates direction itself, converting a tiny geographical space into a labyrinth.

The tone develops from genial amusement through whimsical fantasy to claustrophobic terror. Early mishaps involving the hat, umbrella and pheasant are lightly comic, and even the altered warning—“Trespassers will be persecuted”—resembles a rustic joke. The comedy remains present during the frightening episodes, particularly in the image of the clerk hanging upside down in the gate, but it becomes increasingly cruel. His dignity is stripped away along with his directional confidence and professional authority. The story avoids graphic violence; fear arises instead from sensory uncertainty, spatial distortion and the suspicion that an apparently familiar environment possesses intention.

Dialogue is sparse and functional. Directions, self-address and the client’s final explanation anchor the story in ordinary social reality. The clerk talks aloud to reassure himself, so his colloquial remarks become a barometer of fear: confident assertions gradually give way to silence and thoughts he “dared not utter.” The imagined statement “It’s ours” is especially important because it voices the wood’s implied claim to ownership. Human legal language—business, property, trespass, prosecution and plans to cut trees—is repeatedly opposed by an older territorial law embodied in the fairy wood.

The narrative is carefully framed by written instructions. The illegible sentence on the postcard creates an initial mystery, and its later recovery provides a punning warning: “care” resembles “dare.” The weathered notice similarly shifts from “prosecuted” to “persecuted” and back again. These unstable texts mirror the instability of visual perception within the wood. The ending returns to maps, measurements and proposed alterations, but this restoration of practical order is ironic. The client’s casual invitation to re-enter the wood leaves the threat unresolved and confirms the larger conflict between modern improvement and an ancient place resistant to possession.

Overall, the style is atmospheric, ironic and psychologically intimate. It blends sensuous landscape writing, comic realism, fairy folklore and escalating uncanny suspense. Its signature technique is to make supernatural agency emerge through ordinary sensations that remain individually explicable but become collectively impossible.

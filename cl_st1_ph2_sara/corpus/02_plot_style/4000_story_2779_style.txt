The story uses a framed first-person narrative: an unnamed observer introduces an eccentric boating enthusiast, who then becomes the principal narrator and recounts a disturbing experience from his past. This shift lends the tale the quality of an oral reminiscence or confession. The outer narrator is restrained and economical, while the boatman’s voice is animated, reflective, and intermittently poetic. His practical familiarity with the river gives credibility to the account, even as his heightened sensitivity makes the supernatural-seeming impressions psychologically plausible.

The prose combines realist precision with Gothic atmosphere. Concrete details—distances, the size of the boat, the anchor chain, reeds, weather, rum, and rowing procedures—establish a solid physical world. Against this factual background, the river is presented as mysterious, treacherous, and almost animate. Personification is central: the sea “shrieks” and “roars” but is “honest,” whereas the river is “silent and perfidious.” This contrast turns the landscape into a moral and psychological force. Images of black depths, slime, cemeteries, whispering reeds, fog, and unseen creatures create an atmosphere of concealed death.

The diction moves between plain narration and elevated, lyrical description. The boatman’s introductory meditation employs rhetorical contrasts, repetition, exclamation, and poetic imagery, giving the river a mythic dimension. During the central episode, the language becomes strongly sensory. Sound is particularly important: croaking frogs, lapping water, knocks against the boat, distant howling, rustling reeds, and the metallic notes of bullfrogs punctuate long stretches of silence. Visual imagery alternates between obscurity and uncanny brilliance, especially in the transformation of the fog into dazzling white walls beneath the moon. These contrasts—silence and noise, darkness and radiance, beauty and corruption—sustain the story’s unease.

Sentence structure is generally expansive and cumulative. Long sentences trace the narrator’s perceptions, speculative thoughts, and escalating fears through linked clauses, while shorter statements mark shocks or decisive changes: the anchor catches, a knock sounds, a shadow passes, the corpse appears. Repetition is used to dramatize fixation and delay, as in the repeated attempts to raise the anchor and the narrator’s inability to make himself move. Intensifiers and superlatives—“most marvellous,” “most astonishing,” “horribly uncomfortable”—reflect the extremity of his emotional state and contribute to the storytelling voice’s urgency.

The pacing is deliberately gradual. A calm evening and an apparently trivial inconvenience develop into prolonged isolation, sensory uncertainty, and terror. The immobilized boat creates a confined setting in which very little happens externally, so suspense arises from minute changes in sound, light, weather, and bodily sensation. Temporary relief repeatedly interrupts the fear: smoking, drinking, reasoning, the clearing fog, and the arrival of fishermen all seem to restore normality. Each respite is incomplete, preserving tension until the final discovery.

Psychological analysis is unusually explicit. The narrator distinguishes between his rational will and an involuntary, fearful self, describing them as “two rival personalities.” Fear is therefore treated not simply as a response to an external threat but as an autonomous force within the mind. The story maintains ambiguity for most of its length: every apparent disturbance may be natural, imagined, or supernatural. The concluding revelation retrospectively grounds the unease in physical reality—the anchor has caught a drowned body—without fully dispelling the uncanny mood.

The ending is abrupt, macabre, and understated. After extensive atmospheric preparation, the corpse is disclosed in a plain final sentence, with no moral commentary or emotional aftermath. This tonal restraint makes the revelation more forceful. Structurally, it also converts earlier impressions of hidden presences and death beneath the water into grim foreshadowing. Overall, the style is that of a realist psychological horror tale: observational and materially specific, yet lyrical in its treatment of landscape, patient in suspense, and preoccupied with the unstable boundary between imagination and objective danger.

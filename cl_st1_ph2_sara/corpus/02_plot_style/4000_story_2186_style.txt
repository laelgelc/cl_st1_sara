The story uses a controlled, realist narrative style that gradually admits the supernatural without changing its outward composure. It begins with an exact date and an almost clinical account of illness, domestic arrangements, medicines, and furnishings. This documentary surface anchors the action in wartime Berlin and makes the later apparitions feel materially present rather than dreamlike. The narration remains largely external and matter-of-fact even as Frau Ebermann’s fever distorts space, scale, and perception, so that hallucination, haunting, and moral revelation are never definitively separated.

The point of view is third-person and closely aligned with Frau Ebermann’s consciousness. The narrator reproduces her sensations, assumptions, prejudices, and habits of thought while retaining enough distance to expose their absurdity. Free indirect discourse allows her self-satisfaction to enter the narration: her obsession with order, her confidence in official war reports, and her conviction that she has behaved wisely all colour the prose. This creates sustained dramatic irony. The reader understands the significance of the children before she does, while her attempts to explain them away reveal the mechanisms of denial and propaganda.

Diction moves between clinical precision, domestic specificity, and grotesque sensory detail. Lists of remedies—“formamint,” “ammoniated quinine,” “eucalyptus”—establish an officious, practical atmosphere, while exact descriptions of imitation lace, imitation marble, plush, enamel, and bronze-painted pipes suggest comfort that is fussy, artificial, and faintly vulgar. Colours and materials are repeatedly named, giving the room a crowded, over-decorated texture. Against this setting, the children’s dirt, fatigue, injuries, and blood become increasingly disturbing. The language of fever is especially physical and surreal: hands expand into “huge pillows of nothingness,” the radiator recedes toward a slanting horizon, and the body alternately swells and contracts. These distortions foreshadow the breakdown of Frau Ebermann’s ordered moral world.

Sentence structure is generally formal and carefully balanced, with long descriptive or reflective sentences punctuated by abrupt exclamations and fragments in dialogue. Parenthetical qualifications and accumulative clauses mimic Frau Ebermann’s pedantry and compulsive need to classify. Repetition is a major device. Phrases such as “perfectly straight,” “everything in order,” “wiped out, stamped flat,” and “waiting till our people come for us” acquire an incantatory force. At first repetition expresses routine and reassurance; later it becomes accusatory and inescapable. Italics, dashes, broken syntax, and repeated questions register fever, fear, and weakening self-command.

Dialogue is sharply individualized and carries much of the story’s moral pressure. Frau Ebermann speaks in commands, corrections, rationalizations, and appeals to propriety. Anna’s speech is practical and deferential on the surface, though her muttered insult introduces a brief note of domestic comedy. The children speak simply, politely, and collectively. Their schoolroom phrasing and literal repetitions make them uncanny, but their small details—the lost ribbon, new breeches, ducks, weariness, and desire to see horses—preserve their individuality. Their innocence is never sentimentalized through elaborate emotional language; instead, horror emerges from their calm statements and from the gap between what they understand and what the reader infers.

The tonal progression is from dry social comedy through feverish unease to supernatural horror and moral indictment. Early humour comes from Frau Ebermann’s rigid housekeeping rituals and disproportionate distress over a crooked lace cover. That comedy is progressively poisoned as the same obsession with neatness becomes a metaphor for suppressing inconvenient realities. Her patriotic satisfaction in “another victory” sits beside the arrival of children from villages that have been “wiped out,” creating savage irony. Euphemism is repeatedly exposed: military destruction becomes an “accident,” mass death is reduced to carelessness around horses and guns, and official certainty is used to deny the testimony of victims.

The story’s imagery is organized around order and contamination. Straight edges, equal margins, brushes laid side by side, and objects placed at right angles represent Frau Ebermann’s desire for a universe that confirms her beliefs. The children disrupt this geometry by climbing on furniture, touching surfaces, and finally leaving imagined blood on the floor. “Swept and garnished,” initially a complacent description of domestic virtue, becomes a dark biblical refrain. By the end, cleaning is no longer evidence of control but an act of madness and attempted moral erasure.

Structurally, the story escalates through repeated visitations. One child appears first and can be dismissed as a fever vision; five then arrive and speak; finally they invoke “hundreds and hundreds and thousands and thousands.” The widening numerical scale mirrors the collapse of Frau Ebermann’s denial. The ending circles back to the lace cover and the impulse to clean, but transforms both into emblems of guilt. Overall, the style combines precision, restraint, irony, repetition, and grotesque supernatural imagery to turn a bourgeois sickroom into a tribunal where private complacency is confronted by the human consequences of war.

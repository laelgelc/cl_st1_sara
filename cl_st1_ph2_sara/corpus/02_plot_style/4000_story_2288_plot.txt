An aging narrator recalls Yasha, his family’s proud, eccentric servant, who is devoted to the narrator’s village-doctor father. Yasha carefully maintains the household, ceremoniously drives the decrepit horse Matsko, and treats the shaggy dog Bouton sternly despite secretly caring for and feeding him. Usually dignified and industrious, Yasha occasionally goes on destructive drinking bouts, selling his possessions for vodka and then suffering intense remorse. Although the doctor once tries to dismiss him, Yasha refuses to leave and is eventually forgiven.

Yasha also makes a baffling attempt to marry an elderly, drunken woman, but immediately abandons the idea when his master disapproves. Later, after viewing the body of a stranger who hanged himself at a railway station, Yasha becomes strangely fascinated by the death. He imitates the dead man’s appearance before a mirror, says goodbye to the narrator’s little sister, and hangs himself in the attic. He is ruled to have acted while mentally unsound and is buried outside the village. Bouton runs to Yasha’s grave, mourns there, and then disappears. Years later, the narrator remembers Yasha as a faithful, pure, absurd, contradictory, and ultimately mysterious soul.

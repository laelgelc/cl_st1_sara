The story uses an omniscient third-person narrator whose chief mode is sustained comic irony. The narration moves freely between public opinion, broad social commentary and close access to Thomas Chadwick’s self-image, repeatedly exposing the distance between how he interprets his conduct and how it appears to everyone else. Although the narrator is amused by Chadwick’s vanity, weakness and pretension, the treatment is not wholly cruel. His absurd dignity is also a kind of resilience, and the closing description of him as “heroically loyal” and “a martyr” blends mockery with reluctant admiration.

The tone is urbane, dry and satirical, with comedy generated through understatement, inflated comparison and incongruity. Chadwick’s ordinary actions are described in grandiose terms: a tram becomes an Atlantic liner, fare collection becomes a rite, and a conductor resembles a court chamberlain. This mock-heroic elevation mirrors Chadwick’s own conception of himself while quietly puncturing it. Antithesis and paradox are frequent, as in the portrait of a man who looks authoritative but cannot exert authority, grows fatter through hardship, and objects to night duty despite possessing a formidable constitution. Repetition is another major comic instrument, especially the recurring language of “dignity,” “lady,” official status and incorruptibility. Each recurrence acquires greater irony as events undermine the claim attached to it.

The diction combines formal, often ceremonious narrative prose with regional and colloquial speech. The narrator favours words such as “mien,” “incorruptibility,” “defection,” “ministrations” and “pecuniary,” giving the prose a polished late-Victorian or Edwardian flavour. Against this, dialogue uses contractions, dialectal grammar and local idiom: “she’s a lady, she is,” “ye want to know,” “right oh,” and “put that in your pipe and smoke it.” This contrast differentiates social voices and reveals character. Chadwick’s speech shifts between official pomposity, political belligerence and obsequious deference, while Paul Ford’s language is brief, casual and teasing. Mrs Clayton Vernon speaks clearly and graciously, but her condescension is embedded in her choice of phrases—“this man,” “the chicks,” and her tiny gifts to the grandchildren.

Sentences are generally expansive, balanced and rhythmically controlled. Long cumulative constructions allow the narrator to build comic pressure through qualification, parenthesis and escalating lists. The catalogue of Chadwick’s many occupations is especially characteristic: its length and variety turn biography into farce while efficiently establishing his instability. Parenthetical asides frequently sharpen the irony, as when apparently incidental qualifications expose social assumptions or contradict a character’s grandeur. Short sentences are then used as comic verdicts or structural pivots—“He was,” “Things had come to that,” and “There was not a soul in the car.” Exclamation marks, dashes and repeated clauses reproduce Chadwick’s theatricality without making the overall narration lose control.

Characterization proceeds through repeated discrepancy rather than psychological complexity. Chadwick is built around contradictions: physically imposing but personally weak, obsessed with order but incapable of submitting to institutional discipline, politically devoted to hierarchy yet economically precarious, and scrupulously honest while hungry for recognition and reward. His characteristic gestures, clothing, official manner and formulas of speech provide a strong external silhouette. The narrator often translates those gestures into imaginary declarations—“His gestures seemed to say”—thereby dramatizing the grandiose internal script that accompanies his behaviour. Secondary figures function partly as social foils. Paul Ford is the amused observer who engineers Chadwick’s humiliation, while Mrs Clayton Vernon embodies the class ideal Chadwick worships and the casual condescension that ideal entails.

The setting is rendered as a compact, socially observant provincial world. Place names, tram routes, factories, furnaces, clubs, chapels and civic debates create a dense sense of the industrial Five Towns. Local politics are treated both seriously and comically: the Federation controversy gives Chadwick’s self-importance a public arena, while rosettes and partisan hostility turn municipal reorganization into quasi-heroic conflict. Modern corporate systems also shape the story. The electric tram company represents standardization, efficiency and impersonal discipline, all of which conflict with Chadwick’s desire to remain a singular “character.” The setting is therefore not merely decorative; it supplies the social hierarchy, civic pride and industrial modernity against which his pretensions operate.

The narrative is episodic and carefully prepared. The opening gossip supplies a hook and frames Chadwick as a local spectacle. A long retrospective portrait establishes his defining traits before the action narrows to the lost purse. The purse episode tests his honesty, vanity and class reverence simultaneously. Its apparent resolution at Mrs Vernon’s house is deliberately unsatisfying: instead of the gratitude and substantial reward he imagines, he receives beer and two threepenny pieces. The final tram encounter then converts private disappointment into public exposure. Chadwick’s lie about the sovereigns creates the decisive comic reversal when Mrs Vernon innocently reveals the truth in Paul Ford’s hearing. The ending completes the circular pattern by returning him to unemployment, suggesting that his “dignity” guarantees the continuation of his transformations.

Dialogue is used economically and often works as a contest over status. Paul Ford’s repeated “How do, Tommy?” attacks Chadwick’s preferred formality, while Chadwick’s insistence on Mrs Vernon’s being “a lady” becomes both a political weapon and a trap. Much of the humour depends on what cannot be openly admitted: Chadwick cannot confess that he expected a reward, cannot retract his praise of Mrs Vernon, and cannot acknowledge that her generosity was negligible. His final courteous response therefore carries the force of a comic defeat. The narrator’s imagined alternatives—throwing away the coin, abusing Mrs Vernon, attacking Paul Ford—magnify the violence of his humiliation before contrasting it with his outwardly unctuous composure.

The central concerns are class aspiration, self-deception, civic identity, institutional modernity and the social uses of dignity. The story satirizes deference to wealth and inherited status, but it also examines how a fragile man turns ceremony into psychological armour. Honesty is presented ambiguously: Chadwick returns the purse, yet his virtue is entangled with vanity and the desire to perform incorruptibility before a “great lady.” The resulting style is socially precise, theatrically comic and gently ruthless—a realist provincial narrative animated by mock-heroic language, authorial irony and an acute ear for the ways people preserve self-esteem in the face of public embarrassment.

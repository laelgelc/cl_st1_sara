After the Continental Army’s defeat on Long Island in 1776, George Washington faces the danger of being trapped on Manhattan by a stronger British force. Needing intelligence about the enemy’s plans, he asks Colonel Knowlton to find an officer willing to enter the British camp in disguise. Though others refuse the dishonorable and deadly work of a spy, the young, patriotic Captain Nathan Hale volunteers out of a sense of duty.

Disguised as a schoolmaster disillusioned with the revolutionary cause, Hale crosses Long Island Sound and spends about two weeks observing British positions, drawing fortifications, and recording troop movements. On his return, he stops at a Tory tavern while waiting for a boat. Someone apparently recognizes or suspects him and alerts the British. Mistaking an approaching British boat for his own, Hale is captured, and concealed notes and drawings prove that he is a spy.

Hale admits that he is an American officer and is condemned without trial. The next morning, after the provost marshal destroys the farewell letters he has written, Hale is led to an apple tree to be hanged. He meets death calmly, declaring that he regrets having only one life to lose for his country. His unmarked burial and sacrifice establish him as an early martyr of the American struggle for independence.

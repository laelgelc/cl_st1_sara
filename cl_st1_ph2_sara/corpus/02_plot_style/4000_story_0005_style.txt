The story is written in a flamboyant early twentieth-century pulp-Gothic mode, combining supernatural horror, imperial adventure, psychological guilt, and a final piece of macabre poetic justice. Its atmosphere is feverish and sensational rather than restrained. Africa is rendered as an exoticized nightmare landscape, while New York becomes a displaced version of the tropics in the climactic scene. The narrative repeatedly intensifies fear through lurid description, ominous recurrence, and the suggestion that guilt can assume an almost supernatural agency.

The third-person narrator remains closely aligned with Stuart McGregor’s consciousness. Much of the story is filtered through his obsessive recollection, so the past arrives not as an objective account but as a series of emotionally saturated images. Retrospective constructions such as “he remembered,” “he recalled,” and “as he reconstructed it” emphasize the compulsive nature of memory. The narrator nevertheless retains an authorial presence, occasionally stepping back into formal commentary with phrases such as “be it mentioned,” thereby giving the tale the manner of an oral legend or cautionary anecdote.

Structurally, the opening announces the central paradox: McGregor fears a man who has supposedly been dead for ten years. The narrative then moves through an extended flashback, gradually disclosing the desecration of the fetish, the Bakotos’ judgment, the poker game, and McGregor’s cheating. This delayed revelation turns the story into both a confession and a suspense mechanism. Repeated returns to the tattooed eagle bind the different time levels together. The final movement shifts to New York, where the arrival of the skin-covered drum appears to resolve McGregor’s fear before immediately fulfilling it. The ending depends on reversal: the proof of Hutchison’s death is also the instrument through which he metaphorically kills McGregor.

The prose is highly adjectival, ornate, and accumulative. Descriptions are built from long catalogues of colors, textures, noises, animals, weapons, and forms of decay. Favored adjectives include “ghastly,” “sinister,” “malign,” “obscene,” “slimy,” “crimson,” and “leering.” Colors are especially prominent—blackish green, ochre, ruby, purple, phosphorous green, red and blue—and tend to make the setting feel diseased, theatrical, or hallucinatory. The diction often mixes elevated, Latinate terms such as “phantasmal,” “maledictions,” “apprehension,” and “inevitable” with slangy dialogue and blunt pulp-horror language. This contrast gives the narration grandeur while keeping the characters rough, worldly, and melodramatic.

Syntax contributes strongly to the story’s emotional pressure. Long, semicolon-linked sentences accumulate sensory details until the environment seems to overwhelm the reader. These are countered by fragments, exclamations, and anaphoric repetitions: “Always,” “Isolated!”, “White faces. White slang,” and “He’ll get me.” Such repetitions mimic traumatic fixation and panic. Parallel constructions, rhetorical questions, italics of emphasis in effect if not typography, and abrupt dashes create a declamatory rhythm. At moments of crisis, syntax breaks down into stammering, interrupted speech, and short clauses, reproducing fear directly.

The imagery persistently animates the inanimate world. The jungle “creeps,” fear “stalks,” the moon “squints,” stars “leer,” and the wilderness seems capable of spewing out warriors. Similes are extravagant and often deliberately incongruous: trees retain heat “like boiler pipes in a factory,” the jungle closes “like a curtain,” and the Bakoto leader resembles both Nero and a Manchu emperor. The natural world is presented not merely as dangerous but as sentient and morally hostile. The tattooed eagle is the central visual emblem, its apparent wink externalizing McGregor’s guilty expectation of revenge. Drums, ants, knives, honey, cards, and the tattoo all function as recurring motifs of ritual, fate, bodily violation, and betrayal.

Sound is nearly as important as sight. Animal calls, insects, drums, laughter, curses, clicking nerves, rasping leaves, and the scorpion’s “tchk-tchk-tchk” form an elaborate acoustic background. Onomatopoeia and musical language—“basso,” “treble,” “staccato,” “discordant note,” and “rhythm”—turn fear into a kind of grotesque orchestration. Silence is also threatening, particularly when the warriors emerge noiselessly or when the signal drums seem to carry the voices of the dead.

Dialogue is theatrical and character-revealing. McGregor and Hutchison speak in slangy, clipped, masculine idiom, whereas the Bakoto chief uses grave, ceremonious politeness. His repeated insistence on justice and choice makes his courtesy more frightening than overt rage would be. Father O’Donnell’s gentle uncertainty provides a quieter moral counterpoint, but McGregor rejects the conventional language of repentance. His insistence that he feels fear rather than remorse defines the story’s psychological core: he is not ethically transformed by his betrayal, only haunted by its possible consequences.

The pacing alternates between expansive atmospheric passages and sharply staged confrontations. The jungle journey is prolonged through descriptive accumulation, while crucial moments—the judgment, the rigged poker deal, the confession, and the snakebite—are dramatized in close detail. Foreshadowing is conspicuous rather than subtle: the opening fear, the human-skin drums, the eagle’s wink, and McGregor’s prophecy all prepare the ending. The pleasure lies less in surprise than in watching the foretold punishment arrive through an ingeniously literal mechanism.

The tone combines horror with grotesque comedy. Animal noises are “ridiculously inadequate,” Hutchison’s final slang sounds absurd beside his impending torture, and McGregor’s terror is described as both pathetic and ridiculous. This black humor does not reduce the horror; it makes human bravado seem fragile and foolish. The narrator also favors theatrical metaphors—curtains, dramatic thrills, blood-curdling trimmings—which make the characters appear trapped in a consciously staged tragedy. McGregor himself calls the outcome “Greek,” accurately identifying its fatalistic pattern of transgression, guilt, and inevitable retribution.

The story’s worldview is shaped by the racial and colonial stereotypes of period adventure fiction. Its language exoticizes African people and landscapes, repeatedly associates Blackness and the tropics with savagery, sensuality, disease, and supernatural menace, and treats “white companionship” as synonymous with civilization even when the white characters are violent thieves. At the same time, the white adventurers are plainly the story’s moral offenders: they desecrate a sacred object, steal valuables, and betray each other, while the Bakoto chief is depicted as controlled, logical, and ceremonially just. This produces an uneasy tension between racist descriptive conventions and a plot that condemns colonial arrogance.

Overall, the style is baroque, melodramatic, rhythmically repetitive, and intensely sensory. Its signature features are an obsessional focal character, luxuriant horror imagery, personified landscape, conspicuous foreshadowing, slang set against elevated narration, ritualized dialogue, and a compact twist ending in which psychological haunting and physical causation become indistinguishable.

The story’s style is that of early twentieth-century pulp horror and colonial adventure fiction, combining an exoticised jungle setting, psychological guilt, supernatural suggestion, and a sharply engineered twist ending. Its tone is feverish, lurid, and melodramatic rather than restrained. Horror is created through accumulation: darkness, heat, insects, drums, predatory animals, ritual objects, bodily decay, and anticipated torture are layered until the environment seems actively hostile. Even ostensibly ordinary details are made sinister, grotesque, or diseased.

The narration uses a third-person perspective closely aligned with Stuart McGregor’s memory and fear. Much of the action is presented retrospectively through repeated constructions such as “he remembered” and “he recalled,” so the African episode feels less like a stable historical account than an obsessive mental replay. The narrator occasionally pulls back into a more declamatory, authorial voice—“Be it mentioned”—which gives the tale the manner of a sensational story being formally recounted. Although Farragut Hutchison is physically absent for most of the narrative, the focalisation makes him continually present as an image in McGregor’s mind, especially through the recurring tattooed eagle.

The structure is circular and heavily foreshadowed. It opens with McGregor’s terror of a man believed dead, then reconstructs the betrayal responsible for that terror, returns to his later life in New York, and finally literalises his fear that Hutchison will kill him “from beyond the grave.” The eagle tattoo provides the principal visual motif: first it winks on Hutchison’s living back, then haunts McGregor’s recollections, and finally appears on the human skin stretched across the fatal drum. Repetition—“Always,” “He’ll get me,” “Even as he died”—turns memory and prophecy into incantation. The ending resolves the plot through a material chain of causes, since a snake hidden in the drum delivers the venom, but it is framed as supernatural revenge. This deliberate ambiguity allows coincidence, guilty interpretation, and occult justice to coexist.

The prose is highly adjectival and sensuous. Colours are especially prominent—blackish-green, crimson, ruby, purple, ochre, phosphorous-green—and are associated with corruption, blood, heat, and danger. Visual description is supplemented by dense sound effects: growls, barks, whistles, drums, rasping leaves, clicking animals, and staccato laughter. Onomatopoeia and musical terminology such as “basso,” “treble,” “staccato,” and “discordant note” make the landscape audible and rhythmic. Smell and touch also contribute to the oppressive atmosphere through “sour-miasmic” air, sodden darkness, slimy creatures, furry trees, waxen flowers, and melting asphalt.

Sentence structure alternates between long, overloaded catalogues and short emphatic fragments. Semicolons and dashes allow images to pile up in breathless succession, while isolated declarations such as “Isolated!” and “White faces. White slang. White curses” mimic panic and obsessive thought. Parallel syntax and anaphora intensify key moments. The final paragraph’s repeated “Even as he died” slows the instant of death into a sequence of recognitions, giving the conclusion ritualistic force. Parenthetical qualifications and self-corrections create the impression of a narrator pressing for ever more exact—or ever more sensational—effects.

Figurative language relies heavily on personification and grotesque simile. The jungle “creeps,” “stalks,” and closes “like a curtain”; the moon squints; the stars leer; the wilderness seems to spew out the warriors. Natural objects are compared to industrial, theatrical, religious, or diseased things: trees resemble boiler pipes, the scene resembles a stage set, and the tropical sun appears decayed and apocalyptic. These comparisons turn the setting into a sentient antagonist and project McGregor’s guilt onto the external world. The tattoo’s apparently mobile eye is the clearest example of this psychological animation.

Characterisation is broad and emblematic. McGregor is presented as a hardened adventurer whose physical courage contrasts with his superstitious terror. His refusal to repent complicates the moral design: he is not tormented by remorse for cheating Hutchison but by fear of retaliation. His card manipulation is narrated through bodily automatism—his brain seems to telegraph instructions to his fingers—allowing him to treat the betrayal as both deliberate and involuntary. Father O’Donnell functions as a restrained moral counterweight, yet his uncertainty and guilty crossing of himself prevent rational religion from fully dispelling the supernatural atmosphere. The Bakoto chief is characterised through elaborate courtesy and emotional composure, a contrast designed to make the threatened punishment more terrifying.

Dialogue is theatrical, punctuated by interruptions, stammers, repetitions, and dashes. McGregor’s speech moves between bravado and whining panic; Hutchison’s colloquial “Geewhittaker” creates grotesque bathos immediately before his death; and the chief’s gentle politeness clashes with his detailed description of knives, ants, blood, and honey. This juxtaposition of courteous phrasing with cruelty is one of the story’s central tonal devices. Moments of dark comedy and absurdity—the tattoo’s wink, the lioness’s inadequate treble, Hutchison’s final slang—do not relieve the horror so much as make it more unstable and perverse.

The narrative’s pace is deliberately uneven. The opening and jungle descriptions linger through ornate catalogues, while the card game compresses moral catastrophe into minute hand movements and sharply exchanged lines. The New York section initially appears calmer and more expository, but the tropical heat recreates Africa in an urban setting and prepares the snake’s revival. The final revelation then arrives rapidly through a sequence of scream, recognition, bite, collapse, and death.

The story’s central themes are betrayal, self-preservation, punishment, obsessive fear, and the return of a repressed crime. Objects preserve and transmit the past: cards embody the betrayal, the tattoo preserves Hutchison’s identity, and the drum converts his mutilated body into the instrument of revenge. The recurring opposition between civilisation and savagery is intentionally destabilised at points—the chief’s cruelty is called worthy of “a more civilized race,” while the white adventurers are thieves, vandals, and murderers—but the tale remains deeply shaped by colonial stereotypes. Its exoticising descriptions, racial hierarchy, repeated emphasis on “white companionship,” and offensive period terminology should be understood as features of its dated imperial-pulp idiom rather than neutral description.

Overall, the style is maximalist, atmospheric, and sensation-driven. It favours ornate imagery, insistent repetition, grotesque physical detail, and moral irony over realism or psychological subtlety. Its most characteristic effect comes from transforming guilt into an environment: everything McGregor sees appears to squint, leer, whisper, or threaten, until the final coincidence makes his private superstition seem like an objective law of vengeance.

The story is written in a psychologically intense realist mode, using a controlled third-person narrator who moves closely between the consciousness of the husband and the wife. The perspective is mobile rather than fully omniscient: it begins by filtering the scene through the husband’s impatience, vanity, and self-consciousness, then follows the wife into the garden and gives sustained access to her memories and emotional disintegration, before returning to the husband’s injured pride. Free indirect discourse frequently blurs narration and thought, as in judgments about his social inferiority, her emotional absence, or the uncanny sense that her former lover is both physically present and no longer himself. This shifting focalisation allows both spouses to be understood without making either wholly sympathetic.

The prose combines precise physical observation with symbolic, emotionally charged description. Bodies, clothes, gestures, and small objects continually disclose character: the husband pulls or sucks his moustache, checks his watch, eats heavily, plants himself on the hearthrug, and later spreads his workman’s hands against the door. His smart jacket is humorously personified as “self-confident,” externalising the confidence he wants to possess. The wife’s white dress, parasol, folded skirt hem, rigid posture, and evasive gaze register vulnerability, secrecy, and resistance. Emotional states are rarely left abstract; they become muscular or bodily events—hearts tighten or pound, strength drains away, feeling seems torn like a membrane, and rage fills the veins. This gives the psychological drama a strongly somatic quality.

Nature imagery is sensuous, vivid, and symbolically active. The opening roses are “little bowls of fire,” while the secluded garden becomes a dreamlike realm of colour, scent, memory, and suspended identity. Roses appear as intimate, animate presences: they laugh, converse, crowd around the woman, or invite her to dissolve into them. Their luxuriance embodies both her recovered passion and the dangerous unreality of nostalgia. Colour is especially important—crimson, pink, white, flame-coloured, scarlet, greenish ice—and is often used to sharpen emotional contrasts. The brilliant garden stands against the house’s “blank, dark windows” and sterile appearance, just as the remembered vitality of love stands against the former lover’s vacant consciousness. Beauty is therefore never merely decorative; it intensifies the story’s dread and irony.

The syntax alternates between measured descriptive sentences and short, repetitive clauses at moments of crisis. Early passages accumulate small actions in sequence, producing an observant, faintly comic rhythm. During the garden encounter, repetitions such as “Yet it was not he” enact the wife’s inability to reconcile body and identity. Later, blunt formulations—“She had no being,” “She was not herself,” “He seemed to have gone small”—compress complex psychic states into stark declarations. Repeated words and structures convey fixation, shock, and emotional paralysis. Dashes, pauses, incomplete questions, and hesitant reformulations become more frequent in dialogue, making suppressed feeling audible.

The diction moves between lyrical elevation, plain domestic realism, and socially marked colloquial speech. Descriptive language can be almost visionary, with phrases such as “a gate of colour” or “transfigured with pain and joy,” but it is grounded by teacups, marmalade, tobacco, pipe ash, locked doors, and dinner being served. Regional and class inflections appear in contractions and idioms such as “ha’,” “pit,” “chucked you,” and “the whole hogger.” These speech patterns establish social position while also sharpening the conflict between the husband’s working-class directness and the wife’s sense of refinement and superiority. The narration itself occasionally adopts unusual or archaic formulations—“on sufference,” “sat unavailing,” “suppressedly angry”—which contribute to the story’s period texture and compressed emotional strangeness.

Dialogue is elliptical, adversarial, and rich in subtext. Characters rarely state their central feelings until forced to do so. The wife’s casual requests that her husband conceal her past create suspense, while his teasing response reveals both complacency and insecurity. Their final confrontation proceeds through interrogation, evasion, repetition, and increasingly brutal phrasing. Questions are used less to discover truth than to wound, control, or compel intimacy. The husband’s demand to know and the wife’s insistence that he has no right to know turn marital conversation into a struggle over possession and autonomy. By contrast, the former lover’s polite, disjointed speech is chilling because its gentlemanly surface remains intact while its logic has collapsed.

Characterisation depends on contradiction rather than fixed moral labels. The husband is vain, possessive, insensitive, and socially insecure, yet his final anguish is rendered with considerable force. The wife is secretive, contemptuous, and deliberately cruel, but her shock in the garden makes her emotional withdrawal comprehensible. Her former lover is simultaneously recognisable and absent: his familiar hands, ring, eyes, and military bearing survive, while coherent identity has vanished. This tension between bodily continuity and psychological loss is central to the story’s treatment of madness. The narration repeatedly separates appearance from inner being, asking implicitly what remains of a person when recognition, memory, and mutual consciousness are gone.

Structurally, the story moves from domestic comedy through lyrical reverie into uncanny encounter and marital tragedy. The leisurely opening establishes small irritations and class tensions; the secret excursion creates expectancy; the garden scene forms the emotional and symbolic centre; and the bedroom confrontation translates private shock into relational catastrophe. The return from the radiant garden to the confined bedroom is especially significant. Open paths, sea views, and proliferating flowers give way to a locked door, enclosed bodies, and blocked communication. The ending avoids reconciliation or melodramatic action. Instead, both figures become “impersonal,” stunned beyond immediate hatred, and the husband simply leaves, allowing the newly revealed breach to remain unresolved.

The overall tone is observant, sensuous, ironic, and increasingly tragic. Gentle mockery of masculine vanity gradually yields to horror and pity. The story’s dominant stylistic effect comes from the collision of external beauty with inner desolation, and from its insistence that emotional truth is revealed through involuntary gestures, bodily sensations, spatial arrangements, and failures of speech. Its psychological realism is therefore highly symbolic without becoming allegorical: roses, windows, doors, hands, clothing, and domestic rituals remain concrete objects while carrying the pressure of desire, class resentment, memory, madness, and marital estrangement.

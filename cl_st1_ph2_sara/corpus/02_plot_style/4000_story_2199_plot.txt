In 1857, during the Indian Mutiny, the overcrowded and poorly crewed steamer Sarah Sands carries the 54th Regiment from Portsmouth to India. After a slow, troubled voyage and the loss of her foremast in a squall, a fire breaks out near the ship’s ammunition magazines in shark-filled seas near Mauritius. While much of the insubordinate crew keeps away in the boats, the soldiers remain disciplined. They rescue the regimental Colours, remove and throw overboard nearly all the ammunition, cut away burning wreckage, build rafts, pump water, and repeatedly enter smoke-filled, blazing compartments despite fainting and injury.

Some remaining powder explodes, gutting the stern and nearly sinking the vessel, but the troops refuse to abandon ship. Following engineer Mr. Frazer’s plan, they clear burning coal from the bunkers, cut through a watertight bulkhead, and pour water onto the fire throughout the night. By morning they have subdued it without losing a single life.

The survivors then pump out the flooded hull, plug a large hole, secure loose water tanks, improvise steering gear, and sail the barely intact ship toward Mauritius using one remaining mast. After twelve days, they reach Port Louis, where they are publicly honored. They later continue toward India aboard another inadequate vessel, enduring further hardship. Near Calcutta, an American captain generously supplies the tobacco-starved troops with tobacco and cigars and refuses payment. Their conduct is ultimately praised in a general order as proof that discipline, subordination, and obedience can preserve lives under extreme danger.

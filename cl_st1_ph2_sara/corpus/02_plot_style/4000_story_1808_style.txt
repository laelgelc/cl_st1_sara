The piece uses the style of a popular historical chronicle rather than that of a tightly plotted short story. It presents the early settlement of Ohio in broadly chronological order, moving from Marietta and Gallipolis to Cincinnati, Manchester, Chillicothe, and Conneaut. Dates, names, routes, institutions, and settlement sequences provide a documentary framework, while anecdotes of celebrations, privation, combat, captivity, and cultural misunderstanding give the history narrative color. The structure is cumulative and episodic: each settlement introduces another group of pioneers, another set of hardships, and another contribution to the developing territory.

The narrator is omniscient, authoritative, and openly interpretive. Historical facts are frequently accompanied by judgments about character, competence, and historical fitness. The New England settlers are praised as disciplined, practical, civic-minded founders, whereas the French colonists at Gallipolis are depicted as naïve, ill-prepared, and excessively dependent on promises and theory. This contrast supplies much of the piece’s implicit argument: successful settlement requires labor, adaptability, communal duty, and endurance rather than refinement, optimism, or abstract philosophy. The narration occasionally turns directly to the reader—“Perhaps the reader has heard”—creating the genial, instructive manner of an older popular historian.

The tone combines admiration, irony, humor, and sober recognition of suffering. Promotional claims about Ohio’s climate and abundance are quoted at length and then undercut by frost, famine, disease, frozen rivers, and failed harvests. The irony is often dry and retrospective, as when the supposedly frost-free Ohio River freezes solid or the pioneers move from “a first summer of rejoicing” to “clearing farms, having ague, and saving their scalps.” Comic details, including the French colonists’ awkward method of felling trees and the Indigenous warrior bewildered by a captured wig, lighten an otherwise violent history. At the same time, descriptions of torture, scalping, starvation, and armed conflict can be blunt and sensational.

The diction is formal, literate, and distinctly period-inflected. It mixes administrative and geographical terminology—“Military District,” “Symmes Purchase,” “Northwest Territory”—with vivid frontier vocabulary such as “stockade,” “blockhouse,” “latchstring,” “woodcraft,” and “log rollings.” Elevated phrases including “goodly heritage,” “hapless colony,” and “the stuff that the founders of great states are made of” lend the account a moral and quasi-epic quality. The language also reflects the racial assumptions of its historical era, repeatedly using terms such as “savages,” “red friends,” and generalized tribal characterizations. Indigenous people are represented chiefly as threats, curiosities, or figures in frontier anecdotes rather than as communities with fully developed perspectives.

Sentences are often long, syntactically layered, and connected by semicolons, coordinating conjunctions, and subordinate clauses. These expansive constructions allow the narrator to accumulate facts, qualifications, contrasts, and ironic commentary in a single movement. Lists are a conspicuous stylistic feature: crops, animals, occupations, civic duties, routes, and provisions are catalogued to create impressions of abundance, variety, or absurd incongruity. Shorter declarative sentences appear at moments of conclusion or judgment, giving emphasis to claims such as the superiority of one settler group over another.

Quotation is used as both evidence and characterization. Promotional pamphlets, journals, civic rules, wagon inscriptions, and remembered remarks are embedded in the narrative, lending documentary credibility while preserving historical voices. Many quotations are allowed to expose their own extravagance, especially advertisements promising enormous fish, effortless abundance, mild winters, and freedom from taxes or military service. Italics and quotation marks draw attention to significant words and comic reversals, while exact dates, measurements, numbers, and place names reinforce the impression of researched history.

Characterization is collective more often than individual. Groups are defined through regional and national types: industrious New Englanders, impractical French émigrés, substantial New Jersey settlers, and embattled frontier communities. Individual figures usually appear briefly as exemplars or anecdotal subjects rather than as psychologically complex protagonists. Action is compressed into memorable episodes—the founding of a school, a Fourth of July feast, an Indian attack, a knife fight, a captivity and return—which function as illustrative historical miniatures.

The pacing alternates between broad summary and sharply dramatized anecdote. Years of settlement history may pass in a sentence, while a feast, ambush, or comic mishap receives detailed treatment. Transitions are geographical and chronological, with the narrator repeatedly situating one settlement in relation to another. The final paragraph converts the preceding narrative into a concise ordered list, giving the piece a textbook-like closure and restating its central historical scheme.

Overall, the style is informative, anecdotal, patriotic, and morally evaluative. It seeks to make regional history entertaining without surrendering factual density. Its characteristic effects come from the interplay of documentary specificity, expansive nineteenth-century prose, dry irony, frontier violence, civic admiration, and a strong belief that historical success reveals the practical and moral qualities of the people involved.

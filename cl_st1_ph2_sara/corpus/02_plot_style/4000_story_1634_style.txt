The story uses a first-person retrospective narrator whose voice combines journalistic practicality, literary sophistication, and dry self-mockery. As a small-town newspaper editor, he filters events through the habits of his profession: he instinctively reaches for a pad, imagines local-column copy, and repeatedly assesses whether the visitor’s tale is printable. His urbane, skeptical commentary provides comic distance, yet his confidence gradually weakens as the encounter acquires emotional and supernatural force. The narration is therefore both observant and unreliable in a productive sense: it offers rational explanations without decisively closing off the fantastic.

The prose alternates between ornate descriptive passages and brisk comic narration. Twilight in the newspaper office is rendered through elevated, richly coloured imagery—“saffron rays,” “amber glory,” and “firmamental darkness”—while mundane objects such as the paste-pot and revolving chair puncture the grandeur. This deliberate collision of high rhetoric with shabby provincial reality is central to the style. Long, balanced sentences support atmosphere and reflection, whereas short declarations and abrupt qualifications create punch lines, reversals, and tonal checks. Parenthetical comments, lists, mock-precise formulations, and exaggerated comparisons give the narrator a cultivated but playful manner.

The diction draws on several contrasting registers. The narrator uses formal literary language, classical and biblical references, newspaper clichés, mock-heroic phrasing, and occasional technical or antiquarian terms. Michob Ader’s speech, by contrast, is rendered in a strongly marked comic dialect through nonstandard spelling, Irish-inflected grammar, contractions, and phonetic forms such as “ye,” “wid,” “whin,” and “niver.” His ancient claims are incongruously expressed through modern slang, legal language, advertising idiom, and topical social references. Nero discusses divorce suits, housekeepers, anti-tobacco societies, and cigars as though imperial Rome were a contemporary American town. This anachronistic mixture initially makes history into burlesque, but the dialect later carries genuine grief, allowing the character to shift from comic impostor to tragic figure.

Humour is produced chiefly through incongruity, understatement, bathos, and parody. Sacred legend and world history are reduced to local gossip; famous rulers speak like ordinary acquaintances; historical catastrophes receive absurdly domestic explanations. The narrator’s imagined newspaper notices parody provincial journalism, while his description of Ader as “Father Time’s younger brother” exemplifies the story’s taste for fanciful comic comparison. Recurring phrases such as “‘Twas so commanded” begin as part of the old man’s theatrical routine but gather ominous and mournful resonance through repetition.

The tonal movement is unusually important. The opening is genial, whimsical, and satirical. Ader’s historical anecdotes deepen the low-comic mode until the narrator himself grows impatient with their “song-book levity.” The story then shifts sharply into grotesque horror through the vision of Pilate’s decomposing body, before arriving at sincere pathos when Ader sobs that he denied Christ rest. That emotional revelation prepares the final transformation of the legend: the old man is not literally identified with the Wandering Jew so much as morally and psychologically bound to the archetype by his rejection of his own daughter. Comedy, supernatural unease, horror, and tragedy coexist without any one mode entirely cancelling the others.

The structure follows a progressive reinterpretation of the central figure. A mysterious stranger first appears to be the legendary immortal, then a drunk, a lunatic, a local shoemaker, and finally a man whose private guilt has recreated the legend in his own life. Apparent explanations are repeatedly offered—whiskey, madness, literary reading—but each proves incomplete. The late disclosure about the daughter functions as the decisive reversal: Mike struck her and refused her refuge just as the legendary shoemaker struck Christ and denied him rest. The narrator’s final statement, “He is,” converts a literal question into a symbolic judgment. This is characteristic of a twist-ending story, but the twist illuminates character and theme rather than merely supplying surprise.

Dialogue occupies much of the narrative and is used for characterization, pacing, and embedded storytelling. Ader’s long monologues resemble oral tall tales, full of digressions, hyperbole, historical name-dropping, and repeated claims of eyewitness authority. Short exchanges between the editor and townspeople provide competing explanations and delay the crucial information. The narrator’s restrained responses act as comic counterpoints to Ader’s extravagance, while Uncle Abner’s seemingly casual reminiscence delivers the darkest material in a flat, colloquial manner. That emotional understatement intensifies the daughter’s fate.

The setting is sketched through a few vivid provincial details: the one-window newspaper office, cowbells, garden cornstalks, dusty books, the shoemaker’s dingy shop, and the town’s appetite for personal notices. Montopolis functions as a recognizable small community governed by gossip, conventional morality, and collective judgment. Its historical narrowness parallels the old legend’s cruelty. The townspeople attribute Mike’s condition to drink because that explanation is simple and socially convenient, while the narrator insists on looking for a deeper wound.

Imagery repeatedly links age, dust, decay, travel, and burden. Ader’s stained clothes, worn shoes, museum-like smell, lined face, and ceaseless walking make him appear physically inscribed by history. Birds serve as an ambiguous supernatural motif: they can be ordinary wild geese or the souls of those involved in the Crucifixion. Shoes and walking also form a coherent symbolic pattern. Mike is a shoemaker, the legendary Ader is condemned to wander, and the daughter is driven out on foot after being denied shelter. The mundane trade thus becomes inseparable from punishment, exile, and guilt.

Overall, the style is comic-grotesque, allusive, and twist-driven, with an affectionate satire of journalism and small-town life. Its defining technique is the juxtaposition of elevated legend with colloquial American reality. Beneath the verbal comedy lies a serious concern with guilt, exclusion, historical distortion, and the persistence of moral patterns. The ending preserves a controlled ambiguity: rationally, Mike’s delusion may arise from whiskey, books, and remorse; symbolically, his suffering makes him the Wandering Jew more truly than literal immortality would.

The story adopts a mock-archaic, Rabelaisian style that imitates the expansive manner of a Renaissance fabliau. Its diction mixes ceremonious formulations—“the said Carandas,” “thereupon,” “by reason of,” “failed not to come”—with earthy slang, sexual innuendo, domestic detail, and comic abuse. Place names, trades, foods, clothing, and local landmarks give the narrative a densely historical and regional texture, especially around Tours and the Loire. The language often feels deliberately antiquated rather than strictly period-authentic, and occasional awkward constructions suggest an older translation or pastiche.

The narrator is highly intrusive, garrulous, and performative. Rather than relating events economically, he interrupts them with moral reflections, mock philosophy, local history, direct addresses to the reader, and exuberant digressions. The long hymn to a street in Tours is the clearest example: the plot is suspended while the narrator indulges his civic affection and verbal ingenuity, then openly asks, “Where am I?” before resuming. Such self-conscious wandering creates the impression of oral storytelling by a raconteur more interested in wit, personality, and comic momentum than in strict narrative discipline.

Sentence structure is generally long, cumulative, and loosely coordinated. Clauses are piled together through conjunctions, participial phrases, parenthetical qualifications, and chains of elaboration. Short sentences and fragments then punctuate moments of action or dialogue, producing comic contrast. Repetition is a major rhythmic device: hatred becomes “a cardinal hate,” then “all known hates moulded into one single hate,” while the beloved street is praised through a succession of nearly identical phrases. These accumulations build toward deliberately excessive rhetorical climaxes.

Figurative language is abundant, concrete, grotesque, and hyperbolic. Admirers gather like bees; winter winds are “sharp as a hundred needle-points”; the hunchback is “as fidgety as thirty-two devils caught in a stole”; and his hatred is imagined as an infernal elixir boiling in hell. Similes frequently draw on food, animals, bodily discomfort, religion, and household objects. Beauty is rendered in appetising terms, while humiliation becomes physical and slapstick. This sensual imagery makes the prose vivid and supports its preoccupation with appetite in all forms—sexual desire, eating, drinking, revenge, wealth, and social advancement.

The dominant mode is bawdy comedy. Sexual activity is conveyed through euphemism, metaphor, double meaning, and knowingly coy circumlocution rather than explicit anatomical description. Phrases involving pies, holy water, birds, warmth, and “curbing phantasies” invite the reader to participate in the joke. Clergy, marriage, fidelity, and masculine pride are treated irreverently. The priest is both lover and confessor, the husband is a complacent cuckold, and the supposed moral authorities of society are absorbed into the same economy of appetite and deception as everyone else.

Characterisation is broad, satirical, and action-based. Figures are introduced through emblematic traits: the laundress is beautiful, cunning, and sexually resourceful; the dyer is prosperous, affectionate, credulous, and violent; Carandas is deformed, lustful, vindictive, and mechanically ingenious; the priest is handsome and opportunistic. Psychological subtlety is secondary to comic function. Characters become embodiments of cunning, jealousy, vanity, lust, or gullibility, though the laundress receives the greatest agency. She controls situations through improvisation, charm, theatrical performance, and her precise understanding of male weakness.

Dialogue is brisk and dramatically placed. It often consists of short exchanges whose apparent innocence conceals manipulation or innuendo. The laundress’s speech is especially strategic: she redirects suspicion, soothes her husband, entices Carandas, and finally saves her lover with a single audacious declaration. Dialogue therefore drives both the farce and the reversals of power. Repeated exclamations, direct questions, cries, and affectionate diminutives lend scenes an oral, theatrical energy.

The plot follows a folktale-like pattern of humiliation, revenge, and ironic reversal. Early episodes establish the laundress’s mastery through escalating practical jokes: Carandas is drenched, thrown into a sewer, and nearly suffocated in a chest. His long-nursed vengeance creates a darker middle section, but the final confrontation restores the comic logic. The poisoned weapon intended for the adulterous pair falls on the informer himself. The ending compresses this elaborate chain of lust, malice, and accident into the mock-simple moral, “This teaches us not to be spiteful.”

The tone remains jovial, conspiratorial, and morally unserious even when it touches on adultery, murder, bodily danger, or religious hypocrisy. The narrator pretends to offer universal wisdom, but his “principles of truth” are comic pseudo-logic built from appetite, prejudice, and paradox. The closing moral is technically valid yet comically inadequate to the outrageous events that precede it. Overall, the style is characterised by archaic diction, digressive narration, rhythmic excess, grotesque simile, bawdy euphemism, local colour, anti-clerical satire, farcical plotting, and a relish for verbal abundance.

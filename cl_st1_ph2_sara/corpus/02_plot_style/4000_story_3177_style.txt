The story adopts a highly self-conscious, first-person comic voice whose apparent sobriety continually clashes with the absurdity of what it reports. The narrator presents himself as rational, morally conscientious, and learned, yet his reasoning is pedantic, literal-minded, and spectacularly unreliable. He treats cruelty, vice, supernatural intervention, and decapitation with the same decorous composure he gives to literary criticism. This sustained discrepancy between manner and matter is the chief source of the story’s irony.

The tone is satirical, mock-serious, and grotesquely playful. It parodies moral tales by announcing its concern with moral instruction, then supplying a lesson through sophistry, cruelty, and a literalized idiom. It also ridicules critics who discover elaborate intentions in every work, intellectual fashions such as transcendentalism, and professional or institutional pretension. The humor is frequently dark: infant beating is discussed as educational theory, poverty is classified as a vice, and a headless man is treated medically before being sold for dog meat. Emotional formulas such as grief, friendship, and moral concern are repeatedly undercut by callous actions or commercial calculations.

The diction is ornate, antiquarian, and ostentatiously learned. Latin maxims, Spanish and Persian expressions, classical references, literary names, philosophical labels, and invented scholarly terminology give the narration a cosmopolitan façade. Elevated words such as “vituperate,” “expostulation,” “pertinacity,” and “pecuniary” sit beside colloquialisms, insults, puns, and slang. This deliberate mixture of registers turns erudition into comic performance. Foreign quotations and allusions are less important for their informational content than for the narrator’s display of learning and his habit of inflating trivial matters.

Sentences are often long, elaborately subordinated, and interrupted by dashes, parentheses, qualifications, and self-corrections. The prose repeatedly delays simple facts through digression and overexplanation. Parallel clauses and accumulative lists create a rhetorical crescendo, as in the escalating account of Toby’s infant vices or the sequence of failed attempts to reform him. Short clauses are then used for comic percussion: “I remonstrated—but to no purpose. I demonstrated—in vain.” Repetition, especially of “ahem” and the wager about the Devil and Toby’s head, establishes motifs while controlling rhythm and anticipation.

The narrative relies heavily on verbal wit. Idioms and figures of speech are interpreted literally, names become jokes, and phrases rebound upon their speakers. Toby Dammit’s surname permits dialogue that sounds profane even when the narrator insists otherwise, while the recurrent boast “I’ll bet the Devil my head” becomes the mechanism of the plot. Puns on “turn,” “stile/style,” “little,” “head,” and related terms crowd the prose. Comparisons are intentionally incongruous: babies are likened to steaks and olive trees, rhetorical embarrassment to bombardment, and facial expressions to a sheep’s reverie. Many jokes follow a pattern in which a polished abstraction ends in a vulgar or physical punch line.

The story is framed as a moral exemplum but structured as an extended setup for a macabre practical joke. Its opening digression establishes the parody of literary moralizing; the retrospective biography exaggerates Toby’s innate wickedness; the repeated wager plants the fatal formula; and the bridge episode converts that formula into literal action. Pacing is leisurely and digressive at first, with the narrator repeatedly pausing to comment on criticism, philosophy, manners, and his own reasoning. It tightens near the turnstile through repeated gestures, ritual dialogue, counting, and the staged leap, before returning to deadpan anticlimax in the medical and funeral aftermath.

Characterization is caricatural rather than psychologically realistic. Toby is defined by a single compulsive phrase, a repertoire of rude gestures, and exaggerated “vice.” The narrator is characterized more richly through his style: his vanity, hypocrisy, credulity, aggression, and lack of self-awareness emerge despite his claims to benevolence. The old gentleman is an understated comic Devil, identified through conventionally ominous details—black clothing, lameness, a strange apron, ritual formality—rather than explicit declaration. His exaggerated courtesy contrasts with his predatory purpose.

Dialogue is theatrical and stylized. Toby’s reported tirade appears as a compressed sequence of indirect questions, emphasizing volume and insolence rather than natural conversation. The old gentleman speaks with ceremonious politeness, making the supernatural bargain resemble a legal or sporting formality. The repeated “ahem” functions simultaneously as hesitation, signal, response, and ritual acknowledgment. Gestures are narrated in meticulous sequence, giving scenes the visual timing of farce or pantomime.

The dominant aesthetic is burlesque: lofty discourse is applied to low, foolish, or gruesome material, while supposedly serious institutions are reduced to absurdity. The story blends literary satire, slapstick, supernatural folklore, and body horror without abandoning its urbane narrative surface. Its violence is made more disturbing and more comic by euphemism. Decapitation becomes a “serious injury” or “terrible loss,” and death is discussed as though it were merely an inconvenient medical decline.

To reproduce the style, use a pompous but personable first-person narrator who insists on his reasonableness while revealing moral blindness. Build the prose from elaborate digressions, mock scholarship, parenthetical qualifications, rhythmic lists, and abrupt deadpan conclusions. Introduce a repeated colloquial phrase with latent figurative meaning, then make it literally and grotesquely true. Maintain an elevated, ceremonious register around increasingly ridiculous events, and allow the narrator’s confident explanations—not overt authorial commentary—to expose the satire.

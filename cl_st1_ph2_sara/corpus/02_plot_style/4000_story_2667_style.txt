The story uses a close third-person perspective centred mainly on Reginald Peacock, blending narration with his thoughts through free indirect discourse. His grievances, fantasies and self-justifications enter the prose without firm boundaries, so the reader experiences his inflated self-image from within while also recognising its absurdity. The narrator rarely condemns him directly; instead, a persistent ironic distance exposes the discrepancy between the “sensitive” artist he imagines himself to be and the vain, petulant, financially grudging man revealed by his behaviour. Occasional movement into another character’s perspective—most notably Adrian’s embarrassment at his father’s ceremonious manner—sharpens this satirical effect.

The tone combines social comedy, psychological observation and understated cruelty. Reginald’s domestic complaints are rendered in mock-heroic terms: being woken for breakfast becomes an assault on inspiration, bathing becomes an operatic performance, and ordinary marital tension becomes “open war.” Grand artistic language is repeatedly applied to trivial circumstances, puncturing his pretensions. The humour is often cumulative rather than dependent on isolated jokes, developing through repeated patterns of vanity, misinterpretation and theatrical self-presentation. Beneath the comedy lies a more melancholy sense of emotional estrangement, especially in the final bedroom scene, where his desire for intimacy collapses into the same polished phrase he uses on admiring female pupils.

The diction shifts between decorative lyricism and plain domestic detail. Reginald’s world is filled with perfume, silk, violets, flowers, feathers, mirrors and musical phrases, while his wife’s world consists of bacon, milk, servants, school and dairy bills. This contrast creates both texture and thematic tension: aesthetic “escape” depends upon labour and practical realities that Reginald refuses to acknowledge. Images are vivid, sensuous and frequently whimsical—the sunlight resembles “cream blotting-paper,” a pupil sings “like a pansy,” and words fall “like flowers down the telephone.” Such comparisons are graceful but also satirical, reflecting the ornamental way Reginald perceives himself and his admirers.

Sentence structure is highly flexible. Long, flowing passages reproduce Reginald’s spiralling self-pity and rationalisation, often linked by dashes, ellipses, repetitions and rhetorical questions. Short exclamations and fragments convey his nervous theatricality: “Very well—very well,” “Triumph upon triumph!” and “Glorious girl!” Italics, emphatic punctuation and strategically repeated words imitate the cadences of performance and internal melodrama. Parenthetical qualifications and self-corrections reveal a mind constantly revising reality to preserve its own innocence. The prose can also turn abruptly concise when practical facts intrude, making the contrast between fantasy and actuality more pointed.

Dialogue is central to characterization. Reginald speaks in affected, ceremonious formulations—“Dear lady, I shall be only too charmed”—and habitually converts conversation into performance. His language is full of artistic abstractions, pseudo-psychological distinctions and romantic imperatives, whereas his wife speaks briefly and concretely. Much of the conflict operates through implication: he interprets neutral remarks as accusations, while her restraint suggests long familiarity with his egotism. The pupils’ breathless admiration, blushes and emotional confessions create a chorus that sustains his vanity. Repetition turns his favourite phrase into a verbal motif; at the end, its involuntary reappearance shows that his social persona has displaced authentic speech.

The narrative is structured as a compressed day-in-the-life sequence, moving from morning irritation through lessons and evening triumph to nocturnal anticlimax. Individual scenes are arranged episodically, but the repetition of similar encounters gives them strong cohesion. Each female pupil offers a slightly different form of admiration, allowing Reginald’s confidence to swell toward the party scene. The return home reverses this expansion, and the circular movement back to the marital bedroom exposes the emptiness beneath his public success. The ending is concise and epiphanic without providing reform or reconciliation: Reginald briefly recognises his need for companionship but remains imprisoned in his own mannerisms.

Overall, the style is elegant, ironic and psychologically intimate. It relies on free indirect narration, comic disproportion, symbolic motifs and contrasts between artistic rhetoric and domestic fact. The peacock associations—display, ornament, vanity and the craving to be admired—permeate the characterization without requiring explicit explanation. The result is a subtle satire in which the protagonist condemns himself largely through his own language, while the polished surface leaves room for a quieter study of loneliness, failed communication and the difference between performing emotion and genuinely expressing it.

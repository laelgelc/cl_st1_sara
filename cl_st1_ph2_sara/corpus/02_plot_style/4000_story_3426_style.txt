The story uses a retrospective first-person narrator whose voice is practical, observant and mildly self-deprecating. He presents himself as an ordinary, respectable working man rather than an exceptional hero, grounding the narrative in employment, rent, marriage, household repairs and legal procedure. This matter-of-fact viewpoint makes the extraordinary coincidence at the centre of the plot seem plausible. The narrator generally reports events in chronological order and restricts the reader to what he knows at each stage, allowing suspicion and discovery to develop naturally.

The prose has a late-Victorian popular-fiction character. Its diction is formal but accessible, combining period expressions such as “with the majority,” “keep our own counsel,” “urbanity,” “mendacity” and “suppressio veri” with colloquial phrases such as “old brute,” “lucky old brute” and “put up the shutters.” Long, carefully coordinated sentences dominate the exposition, often qualifying statements through asides and subordinate clauses. These are balanced by short sentences at moments of urgency: “It was only new to us,” “His answer was vague,” and “He started as though struck.” Parenthetical comments and narrator-to-reader confidences create an intimate, conversational quality.

Humour is dry and frequently ironic. The decrepit “New House,” the landlord’s supposed generosity, the narrator’s admission of snobbery and the description of litigation as “a luxury which we could not afford” all turn discomfort into comedy. Character sketches are economical and caricatural: Gradder is repeatedly defined through hardness, greed, suppressed fury and violent exits, while the narrator and Mary are presented as decent, energetic and quick-witted. The moral contrast is clear, but the story avoids solemn moralising by making Gradder’s punishment comic as well as just.

Dialogue is abundant and plot-driven. Speakers reveal themselves through tone and verbal habits: Gradder is blunt, coercive and evasive; Mary is direct and excited; Wigley is slangy and satirical; the magistrate is crusty but benevolent. Exchanges are often brief and adversarial, accelerating scenes that might otherwise be slowed by legal or financial explanation. Exclamation marks, interruptions, repeated questions and physical accompaniments—hat-slamming, door-banging, nudging and sudden movement—give the dialogue a theatrical energy.

The narrative is structured as a compact mystery of accumulating clues. Early details that appear incidental—the former tenant’s secrecy, his career at sea, Mary’s absent father, the landlord’s desire to renovate the rooms and the two-house arrangement—later converge in the revelation of identity. Exposition is delivered through testimony, office gossip, a copied will, registry research and documentary evidence. The will functions as the principal embedded document and transforms the domestic mystery into a legal race against time. Exact addresses, sums of money, dates and bureaucratic procedures strengthen the air of authenticity while also supplying the mechanism of suspense.

Pacing moves from leisurely domestic setup to increasingly rapid discovery. The opening establishes the marriage, house and landlord at some length; repeated visits from Gradder then create unease; Wigley’s information reframes that unease as suspicion; the reading of the will produces the major reversal; and the deadline prompts immediate action. Time markers such as “the next evening,” “later in the day,” “next morning” and “within one hour” keep the sequence clear and intensify the final stages. The ending arrives at the moment of Gradder’s recognition, withholding any extended legal aftermath in favour of a sharp dramatic coup.

Suspense depends less on supernatural fear than on secrecy, greed, coincidence and incomplete knowledge. The neglected house and hidden treasure initially suggest a sensation or Gothic tale, but the resolution is legal and genealogical rather than uncanny. Dramatic irony becomes especially strong near the end: the reader and the couple understand that Gradder is destroying the house in a futile search, while he remains unaware that Mary’s claim threatens his entire inheritance. His final appearance, covered in soot and lime dust, externalises both his greed and humiliation.

The overall tone blends domestic realism, comic mystery and moral fable. Marriage is depicted as an affectionate partnership, with Mary actively interpreting clues and prompting action rather than serving merely as a passive beneficiary. Chance initiates the discovery, but promptness, practical intelligence and mutual trust secure the couple’s advantage. The dominant stylistic effect is one of brisk, lucid storytelling in which ordinary social detail, legal exactness, ironic characterisation and a neatly prepared reversal combine to deliver poetic justice.

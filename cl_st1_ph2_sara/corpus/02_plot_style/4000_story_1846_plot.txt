An idle gentleman living in an old-fashioned London neighborhood observes a close-knit community devoted to traditional English customs, local superstitions, civic pride, convivial clubs, and harmless rivalries. Its harmony is disrupted when the prosperous Lamb family, led by a retired butcher’s socially ambitious wife and daughters, adopts fashionable manners after one daughter attends the Lady Mayoress’s ball. They abandon neighborhood games, embrace French fashions, quadrilles, novels, piano music, and elite acquaintances, and offend their old neighbors by excluding them from a grand ball.

Although the community initially condemns the Lambs’ pretensions, many residents begin imitating them. Fashionable competition intensifies when the wealthy Trotter widow and her daughters challenge the Lambs with grander clothes, larger dances, and their own social following. The neighborhood divides into two hostile factions, traditional customs disappear, and gossip and rivalry replace its former fellowship.

Because both camps regard the narrator as the neighborhood’s resident gentleman, they confide in him and induce him to criticize their opponents. Fearing ruin if the families reconcile and compare what he has said, he decides to leave and seek another part of London where old English manners remain intact.

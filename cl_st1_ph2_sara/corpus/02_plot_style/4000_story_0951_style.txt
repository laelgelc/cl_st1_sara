The story uses a brisk, polished comic style built around the contrast between an unruly child’s logic and the decorous assumptions of adults. The narration is third-person omniscient, but it remains closely aligned with William’s interests, resentments and schemes. It moves freely into brief accounts of other characters’ motives while treating William’s outlook as the story’s imaginative centre. The narrator’s voice is urbane, dry and knowingly ironic, often interpreting childish behaviour through the language of politics, military strategy or public affairs: William keeps his promise “with true political address,” Evangeline canvasses for votes, and his plans require “capital.” This mock-serious framing gives small schoolroom conflicts an exaggerated institutional importance.

The tone is affectionate but unsentimental. William is troublesome, vindictive and opportunistic, yet his audacity, inventiveness and loyalty to Bettine make him engaging rather than merely disobedient. The narrator rarely condemns him directly; instead, humour arises from understated observations, ironic qualifications and deadpan consequences. Hyperbole is frequent but controlled, as in Robert’s romantic despair, Jumble’s digestive suffering and Evangeline’s almost supernatural capacity for cakes. Parenthetical remarks and aphoristic statements—such as the warning that when something jars on William’s nerves, “something always happened”—create complicity between narrator and reader.

The prose alternates between compact narrative summary and extended dialogue scenes. Narrative sentences are generally clear and rhythmic, often using repetition for comic emphasis: William dislikes facts, detail and questions; Evangeline continues “eatin’ and eatin’ and eatin’”; Robert speaks of ruined life and happiness in accumulating clauses. Lists and parallel constructions heighten absurdity, particularly when cataloguing clothes, cakes, grievances or ceremonial instructions. Longer passages accelerate through coordination and repeated conjunctions, while short sentences or fragments provide punch lines. Deliberate anticlimax is common, with elevated phrasing collapsing into mundane objects such as bull’s-eyes, cold tongue, burnt cork or a currant cake.

Dialogue is central to characterization and is sharply differentiated from the narrator’s standard English. The children’s speech is represented through colloquial contractions and phonetic spellings—“goin’,” “jus’,” “narsy,” “wot,” “’Ullo”—along with simple syntax, repetition and literal-minded misunderstandings. William’s dialogue is blunt, argumentative and evasively ingenious. He exploits exact wording, as when he promises to write Evangeline’s name but invalidates the vote, and he habitually turns reluctance into ostensible virtue. Bettine speaks softly and earnestly, making her admiration emotionally direct. Evangeline’s simpering self-consciousness emerges through boastful remarks and affected sweetness. Adult speech, by contrast, is ceremonious, patronizing or melodramatic, and its failure to understand the children supplies much of the comedy.

Characterization depends on vivid recurring details rather than psychological exposition. William is defined by his scowl, untidiness, pockets full of miscellaneous objects, instinctive resistance to authority and theatrical taste for moustaches. Evangeline is reduced comically to blue clothes, ringlets, a squeaky voice and greed, while Bettine is small, dark, shy and generous. These motifs make the cast immediately legible and support the story’s fairy-tale opposition between the officially chosen “angel” and the overlooked child whom William crowns. Names such as Evangeline Fish and Bettine Franklin have a slightly caricatural crispness suited to the comic mode.

The plot is linear and carefully escalated. It begins with boredom and the May Queen election, develops William’s hostility and his friendship with Bettine, introduces the obstacle of his ceremonial role, then plants the crucial observation of Evangeline’s appetite. A comic family digression explains both William’s character and his temporary lack of money before the uncle’s five shillings provide the means for the scheme. Earlier details return as payoffs: the cakes become bait, Ethel’s costume becomes royal dress, and William’s insistence on a May King becomes reality. The climax depends on dramatic irony, since the reader understands William’s substitution while the audience tries to interpret it as an intentional pageant. The final sentence cuts back to Evangeline finishing the last cake, delivering a concise visual punch line rather than a moral or formal resolution.

The humour combines satire, incongruity, repetition and misunderstanding. School ceremonies, romantic ideals, feminine “mellowing,” beauty contests and adult respectability are all gently mocked. Adults repeatedly impose sentimental meanings on events they do not comprehend, while William pursues concrete desires with ruthless practicality. The story also reverses conventional values: the angelic blonde is vain and greedy, the supposedly disruptive boy performs an act of rough kindness, and the shabby substitute queen experiences genuine happiness. Sentiment is present in William and Bettine’s bond, but it is protected from mawkishness by comic gifts such as a half-eaten apple, a centipede and a crooked costume.

The setting is an idealized early twentieth-century English middle-class world of mixed schools, village festivals, servants, coal cellars, smocks and Maypole dances. Social customs are recognizable but lightly caricatured, and there is little descriptive atmosphere beyond what the action requires. Objects carry more stylistic weight than landscape: sweets, marbles, cakes, clothes and improvised props drive both characterization and plot. Overall, the style is economical, episodic and highly performative, combining an elegant adult narrator with vigorous child dialogue to turn minor rebellion into mock-heroic comedy.

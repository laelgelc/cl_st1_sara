The story uses a vivid, muscular adventure style, combining panoramic landscape description with tightly sustained physical action. Its opening establishes an exoticised Central Asian setting through named mountains, bazaars, local plants, clothing, weapons, religious customs and transliterated expressions. This geographical specificity gives the narrative a strong sense of place, although the repeated emphasis on cultural difference, “hillmen,” folk medicine and picturesque speech reflects the conventions—and stereotypes—of older imperial adventure fiction.

The narration is third-person limited, closely aligned with Mortazu Khan’s perceptions and practical reasoning. The narrator moves easily between external observation, interior thought and imagined dialogue, allowing the protagonist’s urgency, humour, fear and remorse to shape the scene without using a fully stream-of-consciousness method. Mortazu Khan is characterised primarily through movement, bodily endurance, earthy invective, devotion to his wife and instinctive religious speech. His thoughts are direct and concrete: distances, time, speed, weapons and immediate risks matter more than abstraction. Yet the perspective broadens emotionally near the end, when he recognises the bear’s maternal motive and sees her as a fellow creature defending her young.

The diction is sensuous and highly visual. Colours recur with painterly precision—rust-brown bracken, azure gentians, blue-gray fur, ochre moss, gold and lavender peaks—while smells, textures and sounds make the landscape physically immediate. Rough bark, torn moss, bleeding toes, steam, saliva and sticky blood give the struggle a tactile, sometimes visceral quality. Similes are plentiful and forceful, often drawn from machinery, animals or natural motion: arms move like propeller blades, the bear slides with the precision of roller-skates, and Mortazu Khan eventually seems to “stream” like a liquid wave. Personification intensifies the environment, with stones clattering mockingly, twigs whining, the wind soaring and sunlight dipping down to observe the fight.

Sentence structure varies according to pace. Long, layered sentences dominate the landscape passages and accumulate topographical or sensory detail through participial phrases and coordinated clauses. During the confrontation, the prose shifts toward fragments, exclamations, abrupt repetitions and onomatopoeia: “Plump!”, “Waughrrr!”, “Wheet-wheet!” These compressed units imitate charges, evasions and impacts. Repeated words and constructions—“circling, circling,” “again and again and again,” “hurrying, hurrying”—create rhythmic insistence and convey exhaustion, dwindling time and obsessive purpose. Dashes and ellipses frequently interrupt the syntax, registering sudden thoughts or the pressure of Azeena’s unseen peril.

The action is prolonged through iterative narration. The bear repeatedly charges, misses, brakes and turns while Mortazu Khan circles the tree. Each recurrence adds a new physical cost: shredded moss, broken sandals, bleeding feet, raw hands and failing breath. This repetition risks stasis but is used deliberately to make the ordeal feel exhausting and to sustain suspense around the passage of time. References to the rising and dipping sun and to Azeena “dying” away beyond the hills keep the fight connected to the larger deadline.

Dialogue is theatrical, comic and culturally marked. Mortazu Khan’s insults, prayers and apostrophes to “Bibi Bear” turn the combat into a grotesque verbal duel. The bear’s roars are rendered almost as replies, giving the scene a mock-conversational structure. Humour arises from the contrast between mortal danger and elaborate abuse, as well as from the bear’s ungainly movements and “hurt, childish intonation.” This comedy does not erase the threat; instead, it makes the later shift toward pity more effective.

Tonally, the story moves from picturesque travel narrative to urgent quest, then to comic-grotesque combat, bodily horror and finally sentimental reconciliation. The central irony is that Mortazu Khan and the bear are fighting for parallel reasons: each is driven by concern for offspring. Once the cub is revealed, the language of hostility gives way to respect, kinship and tenderness. The killing remains violent, but the narration stresses that the blows contain “no hatred.” The closing adoption of the cub resolves the conflict through symbolic substitution, linking the hoped-for human child with the orphaned animal and ending on a mixture of injury, compassion and renewed haste.

Overall, the style is energetic, ornate and cinematic. It relies on strong colour, emphatic rhythm, anthropomorphism, physical detail and melodramatic emotional reversals. Beneath its adventure-story surface, it uses mirrored parenthood to transform a simple man-versus-animal struggle into a compact fable about courage, necessity and compassion.

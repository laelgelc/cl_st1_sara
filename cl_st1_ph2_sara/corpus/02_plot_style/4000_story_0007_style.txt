The story uses a vigorous, early twentieth-century adventure style, combining frontier exoticism, physical peril, broad humour and sentimental humanism. Its setting is established through panoramic geographical description: named mountains, bazaars, plants, peoples and local objects create a densely particularised Afghan landscape. Foreign terms such as “hakim,” “bazar” and “Bibi,” together with references to Allah, the Koran and local customs, lend the narration ethnographic colour. At the same time, the treatment reflects a period colonial perspective, presenting Afghan life through picturesque, sometimes generalized ideas about “hillmen,” wise-women and supposedly characteristic forms of speech.

The narration is in the third person, closely aligned with Mortazu Khan’s perceptions and thoughts. The narrator moves freely between external description, indirect thought and imagined dialogue, allowing the reader to share both Mortazu’s urgent calculations and his emotional shifts. His goal is established quickly and concretely: he must bring the English doctor to his labouring wife before nightfall. This deadline supplies the story with a strong narrative motor and ensures that the bear encounter is not merely a struggle for survival but an obstacle charged with domestic and moral urgency.

The prose is highly visual, tactile and kinetic. Landscapes are rendered through saturated colours and elaborate comparisons: mountains close “like a ragged tide,” gentians open “azure stars,” and blood appears as “black-red whips.” Description repeatedly animates the environment, with stones clattering “mockingly,” twigs whining and sunlight dipping down “to see what was happening.” Personification makes the natural world seem actively involved in the conflict. Similes are plentiful and vivid, often drawn from machinery, animals or familiar objects: Mortazu’s arms move like “propeller-blades,” the bear slides with the precision of “roller-skates,” and the cub’s descending influence on his mind resembles “the shadow of a leaf through summer dusk.” This figurative abundance gives the story a colourful, heightened, almost illustrated quality.

Sentence structure changes with pace. The opening relies on long, layered sentences packed with topographical and sensory detail. During the confrontation, clauses shorten, fragments proliferate and verbs become more forceful: “Plump! Down on her nose”; “Twist and squat and twist.” Repetition—“circling, circling,” “again and again and again,” and “hurrying, hurrying”—conveys exhaustion, duration and obsessive urgency. Sound effects such as “Waughrrrr-yi-yi,” “Wheet-wheet” and “Airrrh—whoof” give the action a theatrical immediacy. Dashes, exclamations and abrupt paragraph breaks further accelerate the rhythm.

The combat is staged with unusually detailed spatial choreography. The repeated circling of the fir tree becomes the central visual pattern, and the narration carefully explains the respective advantages of man and animal: Mortazu has agility, while the bear has weight and strength. The scene alternates between comic repetition and genuine terror. Mortazu’s extravagant insults—“Parent of Piglings,” “Illegitimate cow,” “Parent of naughty daughters”—make the struggle grotesquely funny, while descriptions of torn skin, bleeding toes, exhaustion and approaching death preserve its brutality. The narrator often juxtaposes the ludicrous and the sinister, especially in the bear’s clumsy momentum and stubborn determination.

Dialogue is stylized rather than naturalistic. Mortazu speaks in ceremonious, proverbial and religiously inflected phrases, often addressing Allah or the bear aloud. His imagined exchange with the English doctor efficiently characterizes both men: Mortazu is proud, practical, superstitious and ruefully self-aware, while the doctor is impatient, profane and decisive. The doctor functions as a familiar colonial-adventure figure—the irascible but effective European professional—whereas Mortazu’s speech gives him comic individuality and emotional warmth.

The central tonal movement is from suspenseful comedy to pathos. Initially the bear appears as an unreasonable obstacle, and Mortazu interprets her attack as wickedness. The revelation of the cub retrospectively explains both the mysterious cry in the branches and the mother bear’s aggression. This recognition creates the story’s moral pivot: Mortazu understands that both combatants are fighting for their young. His hatred gives way to respect and fellow feeling, even though the fight must continue. The final killing is therefore presented as tragic necessity rather than triumph, emphasized by his lament, “Poor Bibi Bear! Brave Bibi Bear!”

The ending completes this movement through an act of symbolic adoption. Mortazu takes the orphaned cub home in the hope that it will grow beside his unborn child. This closes the story on an image that mingles injury, tenderness and renewed urgency. The thin trail of blood prevents the conclusion from becoming purely comforting, while the repeated final “hurrying” restores the original race against time. Overall, the style is muscular, picturesque and melodramatic, with strong action choreography, luxuriant sensory description, comic invective and an ultimately compassionate emphasis on shared parental devotion.

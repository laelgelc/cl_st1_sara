The story uses a restrained, psychologically observant realist style with strong regionalist coloring. Its omniscient third-person narrator remains chiefly aligned with Tonie, moving freely between external description, communal judgment, and intimate access to his desires. The narration initially presents him with gentle irony as an awkward, inarticulate fisherman, then gradually exposes the violent and possessive intensity beneath his “rude, calm exterior.” This contrast between social appearance and hidden passion drives the story’s psychological tension.

The prose is formal and literary, with occasional archaic constructions, long periodic sentences, and explanatory authorial commentary. Balanced clauses and repetitions often emphasize emotional fixation: Tonie sees Claire everywhere, thinks of her continually, and understands the world only in relation to her. At moments of crisis, the syntax becomes shorter and more direct, as in the revelation of Claire’s death and Tonie’s declaration that he is glad. Parenthetical qualifications and narrator-led generalizations—especially about love, passion, and women’s perception of desire—give the voice the assured, sometimes aphoristic quality of late nineteenth-century fiction.

Regional detail is central to the style. French and Creole names, Catholic language, local foods, boats, occupations, and Louisiana place-names establish the island and Gulf communities with documentary specificity. Expressions such as “Ma’me Antoine,” “Mamzelle,” “curé,” “banquette,” “court-bouillon,” and “lugger” are incorporated without extensive explanation, making the culture feel inhabited rather than merely decorative. Dialogue is used sparingly and is generally simple, revealing differences of class, education, and temperament. Tonie’s hesitant, plain speech contrasts with Claire’s social ease and with the voluble gossip of the older women.

The natural environment is rendered sensuously and symbolically. Heat, salt wind, birds, flowers, waves, dusk, bells, incense, and music create a lush coastal atmosphere while reflecting Tonie’s emotional states. Claire first appears through organ music that seems celestial, so religious imagery becomes fused with erotic idealization. The Angelus interrupts Tonie’s murderous fantasy at sea, and after Claire’s death he converts his obsession into a belief in spiritual equality and eventual union. Recurrent images of birds, sailing, horizons, light, and water suggest freedom and transcendence, but they are shadowed by possession, death, and drowning.

Characterization proceeds largely through selective perception. Once Tonie falls in love, ordinary village events become inaccessible to him: he passes deaths, drunkenness, meals, and greetings without truly registering them. This narrowed attention formally reproduces obsession. Claire, by contrast, is observed through clothing, movement, social performance, and Tonie’s idealizing gaze. The narrator complicates her charm by identifying her gift of the silver chain as coquetry and sentimental imitation. She is neither demonized nor deeply individualized; her relative superficiality underscores the enormous disparity between her casual gesture and Tonie’s consuming interpretation of it.

The tonal movement is from lightly comic social observation to lyric infatuation, menace, grief, and finally unsettling serenity. Irony remains crucial throughout. Tonie’s recovery after Claire’s death appears at first paradoxical, but his explanation reveals that death has freed him from jealousy and from the prospect of her marrying another man. The ending is outwardly domestic and peaceful—he returns to his mother, lights the bread oven, and kisses her cheek—yet the peace rests on a possessive fantasy of claiming Claire’s soul in an egalitarian afterlife. This produces an emotionally ambiguous close: tender, grotesque, religious, and psychologically credible at once.

The story follows a compact, sectional structure organized around decisive encounters: Claire’s musical appearance, Tonie’s pursuit of her at Grand Isle, their solitary boat trip, the news of her death, and his final explanation to his mother. Scene and summary alternate efficiently, allowing an entire season and winter to pass while concentrating dramatic attention on a few emblematic moments. Overall, the style combines local-color realism, romantic natural imagery, Catholic symbolism, understated irony, and incisive psychological analysis, culminating in a conclusion that offers restoration without uncomplicated moral resolution.

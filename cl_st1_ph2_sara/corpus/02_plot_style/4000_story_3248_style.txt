The story uses an intrusive, conversational third-person narrator who speaks directly to the reader, offers candid judgments, supplies explanatory asides, and occasionally anticipates or corrects expectations. The opening establishes this storyteller’s presence immediately by contrasting the tale with earlier stories about “well-behaved people.” The narrator remains knowing but not solemn, describing characters as “disagreeable,” “odious,” foolish, or lacking discretion while treating danger with amused composure. Parenthetical comments, confidential phrases such as “To tell the truth,” and understated observations create the feeling of an adult telling a darkly comic tale aloud to children.

The tone combines nursery-story warmth with menace, farce, and dry moral irony. Kidnapping, predation, bones, knives, and the prospect of rabbit pie are presented plainly, but graphic consequences are avoided. Suspense is repeatedly softened by comic detail: Tommy Brock sleeps in his boots, Mr. Tod fusses about crockery and disinfectant, and a potentially lethal trap becomes an elaborate domestic contraption. The humour often depends on understatement, mock seriousness, repetition, and incongruity. Formal phrases such as “displayed a lamentable want of discretion” or “preparations for one person’s supper” are applied to absurd animal predicaments, giving the prose a gently satirical, mock-heroic quality.

The diction mixes simple, child-accessible vocabulary with precise rural, domestic, and slightly old-fashioned terms. Words such as “coppice,” “pollard willow,” “crag,” “faggots,” “tester bed,” “spud,” and “warming-pan” give the setting material specificity, while household objects—pie dishes, tablecloths, kettles, salt cellars, wash-basins, tobacco, and cleaning powders—make the animal world resemble a human country household. Animals retain species-specific traits through scent, tracks, burrowing, whiskers, teeth, and diet, yet wear clothes, smoke, serve wine, set tables, own houses, and observe family etiquette. This blend of natural history and domestic anthropomorphism is central to the style.

Characterization is economical, visual, and behavioural. Each major figure is defined by a compact cluster of repeated traits: Tommy Brock is fat, bristly, dirty, lazy, heavy-footed, and perpetually grinning; Mr. Tod is thin-legged, sandy-whiskered, fastidious, vindictive, and irritable; Benjamin is anxious and “all of a twitter”; Peter is practical, composed, and verbally reflective; old Mr. Bouncer is sociable but irresponsible. Recurrent gestures and sensory markers function almost like motifs. Tommy Brock’s grin and snores signal both indolence and cunning, while Mr. Tod’s bristling whiskers, stalking movements, and concern for his possessions expose his mixture of predatory menace and comic vanity.

The syntax alternates between short declarative statements and longer cumulative sentences linked by semicolons, conjunctions, and dashes. Short paragraphs often isolate a revelation or comic judgment—“He had half a dozen houses, but he was seldom at home”—giving the narration an oral, emphatic rhythm. Longer sequences catalogue objects or actions in escalating detail, especially during Mr. Tod’s trap-making and the kitchen battle. Repetition is heavily used for rhythm and comic intensification: characters go “in and out,” climb “up and up,” dig “and dug,” or fetch “all sorts of soap.” Parallel phrasing and recurring names keep the action clear for younger readers while heightening the farce.

Punctuation is expressive and conspicuous. Semicolons frequently divide closely related actions; dashes introduce interruptions, sudden discoveries, or changes in thought; parentheses contain humorous qualifications; ellipses prolong sleep, hesitation, or suspense; and exclamation marks animate dialogue without dominating the narration. Occasional capitals—“OUT,” “IN,” “SOMETHING,” and “NOT SNORING ANY LONGER”—create visual emphasis resembling a storyteller’s vocal stress. Some compound words are hyphenated in an old-fashioned or irregular way, and dialogue sometimes runs directly into narration, reinforcing the lively, spoken quality.

Dialogue distinguishes temperament more than social class. Benjamin speaks in urgent repetitions and questions, Peter in measured deductions and formal judgments, Tommy Brock in coarse comic complaints, and Mr. Tod in self-addressed plans. Polite family forms such as “Cousin Peter” and “Cousin Benjamin” contrast humorously with panic and violence. Much of the dialogue is practical—asking directions, reconstructing movements, devising rescues—but it also slows moments of urgency just enough to generate comic frustration. Peter’s composure beside Benjamin’s alarm is a recurring source of tension and humour.

The plot is linear and strongly cause-and-effect driven, but its pacing varies. The abduction and pursuit move briskly through tracks, smells, and eyewitness reports. The discovery at the house slows into visual inspection and nocturnal suspense. The central trap sequence becomes deliberately elaborate, stretching anticipation through repeated entrances, adjustments, hesitations, and domestic preparations. This delay culminates in a quick reversal when Tommy Brock substitutes the dressing-gown bundle. The subsequent fight is rendered through rapid catalogues of crashes, broken objects, spills, and bodily tumbling, after which the rescue and homecoming proceed swiftly.

Description is concrete and sensory rather than ornamental. Smell is especially important: fox, badger, flowers, dampness, dirty floors, and carrion all guide both characters and reader. Sound structures the suspense through snores, owl calls, jay cries, barking, rattling furniture, drips, and distant fighting. Light marks changes in mood and time: warm slanting afternoon sun gives way to red windowpanes, cold moonlight, darkness, sunrise mist, and renewed daylight. Pastoral beauty—a sea of blue hyacinths, golden treetops above mist—is repeatedly placed beside threat or disgust, producing a characteristic mixture of idyll and danger.

The story’s comedy is built through reversals of power. The rabbits appear most vulnerable but survive through observation, cooperation, and the quarrel between stronger predators. Mr. Tod devises an ingenious punishment but is deceived by the supposedly sleeping Tommy Brock. Tommy Brock controls the situation through stillness, feigned sleep, and opportunism rather than speed. Domestic order becomes a battlefield: beds, tea, crockery, cleaning equipment, and cooking utensils are converted into traps or weapons. The destruction of Mr. Tod’s kitchen is both physical climax and comic punishment for his possessiveness and predatory intentions.

The moral stance is present but deliberately light. Carelessness has consequences, as old Mr. Bouncer’s vanity and poor judgment endanger the babies, yet he is forgiven after their return. Predatory characters are not reformed or explicitly punished by a moral authority; instead, their selfishness turns them against each other. Family loyalty, practical courage, and cooperation restore safety, while greed, trespass, bad temper, and overconfidence generate disorder. The ending returns to food, forgiveness, tobacco, and storytelling, restoring domestic stability without resolving the predators’ battle. This open comic residue preserves the sense that the natural world remains unruly beyond the secure rabbit home.

Overall, the style is that of a rural animal adventure told with poised authorial intimacy, precise domestic observation, restrained peril, and elaborate farce. Its signature effects come from the contrast between polite language and savage instincts, pastoral scenery and macabre evidence, childish repetition and sophisticated irony, and small household concerns set against kidnapping, pursuit, and combat.

While resting beside the Swiftwater brook after fishing, the narrator admires the June forest but laments that it offers no fruit equal to its flowers and birds. Nature answers when he notices three wild strawberries hanging above him. Their intense sweetness convinces him that the small wild berry surpasses larger cultivated varieties.

This discovery prompts reflections on Doctor William Butler’s praise of strawberries, the history of the fruit, and the difference between natural quality and human-made abundance. The narrator then recalls chance encounters with wildflowers, trout, and other woodland treasures, arguing that uncertainty and discovery make nature more pleasurable than carefully managed gardens, stocked ponds, or regulated forests. He concludes by celebrating humanity’s enduring wild instinct and wishing always to escape convention occasionally—to fish a free stream, explore untended woods, and unexpectedly find a wild strawberry.

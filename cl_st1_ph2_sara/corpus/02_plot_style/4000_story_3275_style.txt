The story adopts an elevated late-Victorian or Edwardian literary style, combining an intimate epistolary confession with a theological supernatural vision. It begins as a letter addressed to “My Dear Violet,” and the first-person narrator speaks with the candour of private correspondence while sustaining the polish and intellectual density of a formal essay. Direct address, parenthetical qualifications and conversational turns such as “Well,” “Yes,” and “you shall have the whole tale” create personal immediacy, but the voice remains educated, reflective and rhetorically controlled.

The narrator’s tone moves through grief, metaphysical rebellion, Gothic terror and eventual spiritual serenity. His bereavement is analysed rather than merely reported: emotional states are translated into philosophical propositions, physical analogies and arguments about divine purpose. The voice is self-aware and scrupulous, frequently distinguishing what was perceived from what can be proved. Even during the apparition, the narrator insists that he never mistakes Harry for a living man. This careful testimonial manner lends plausibility to the supernatural material and balances visionary extravagance with psychological realism.

The diction is formal, learned and richly allusive. Classical mythology and literature, astronomy, Christian theology, French Romantic poetry and Latin historiography all enter naturally into the narrator’s thought. Untranslated French, Greek and Latin presume a highly educated readership and establish a cultured social milieu. Archaic or ceremonious words such as “Artificer,” “lustral,” “comeliness,” and “raiment” give the visionary passages a scriptural or mythic resonance, while precise practical vocabulary—riding equipment, architecture, machinery and drainage—grounds the fantastic scenes in material detail.

Sentences are generally long, syntactically layered and rhythmically varied. They often proceed through balanced clauses, accumulations and carefully staged qualifications, reproducing the movement of a mind testing an idea from several directions. Colons and semicolons introduce expansions or deductions; dashes register interruption, emotional pressure or sudden revision. Lists intensify both beauty and horror, as in the catalogues of stars, predatory animals and methods of torture. At moments of revelation, however, the prose contracts into short questions, answers and declarations. This shift gives the dialogue with Harry a grave, almost catechetical force.

Imagery is sensuous, symbolic and strongly patterned. Cosmic splendour is rendered through jewels, light and ordered movement, while earthly cruelty appears through predation, blood, mutilation and machinery. The central vision fuses amphitheatre, torture chamber, temple and cosmic landscape. Water is the governing symbol: first heard as the encircling Oceanus, then seen washing blood from the arena, and finally revealed as God’s tears flowing around the world. The imagery repeatedly transforms abstract theological problems into spatial and physical forms. Divine cruelty becomes an imperial spectator behind curtains; shared suffering becomes sobbing, tears and a flooded arena.

The descriptive method depends on gradual disclosure. Ordinary domestic realism—the library, lamp, frost, gravel drive and stable clock—opens seamlessly into the uncanny. The ride first passes through familiar countryside, then into an indeterminate mythic geography, and finally reaches the monumental amphitheatre. Details accumulate before their meaning is explained, sustaining dread and curiosity. The return is abrupt and circular: the supernatural gate becomes the lodge-gate of Sevenhays, restoring the everyday world while leaving the revelation intact.

Dialogue is sparse, restrained and purposeful. Harry speaks with affectionate understatement, calm authority and characteristic familiarity, which preserves his identity more effectively than elaborate ghostly effects would. His plain phrasing contrasts with the narrator’s anguished rhetoric. The climactic exchange consists of questions and interpretive answers, guiding both narrator and reader from the mistaken image of a sadistic God to the paradox of a God who experiences every creature’s pain.

Structurally, the numbered sections trace a clear spiritual progression: grief and accusation, supernatural visitation, descent into the vision of suffering, and return with transformed understanding. The narrative is therefore both ghost story and conversion narrative. Suspense serves philosophical inquiry rather than existing solely for shock. The governing theme is the problem of evil, but the resolution does not deny suffering or explain it as instrumentally useful. Instead, it offers a theology of divine participation: suffering remains terrible, yet no suffering is endured by the creature alone.

The overall effect is solemn, ornate and visionary, with elements of the epistolary tale, Gothic fiction, dream vision, classical allegory and Christian parable. Its distinctive stylistic tension lies between intimacy and grandeur, sceptical argument and mystical revelation, concrete realism and cosmic symbolism. To reproduce the style, one would use a cultivated first-person witness, leisurely argumentative exposition, learned allusion, exact physical description, escalating symbolic horror, restrained supernatural dialogue and a final image that resolves the narrative emotionally without reducing its mystery.

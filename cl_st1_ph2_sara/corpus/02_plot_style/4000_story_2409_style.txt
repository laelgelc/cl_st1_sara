The story adopts the style of a nineteenth-century Gothic moral tale, combining folkloric supernaturalism, domestic realism and an overtly cautionary purpose. Its atmosphere is bleak, oppressive and fatalistic. Moorland, churchyard, ancient trees, open graves, darkness and isolated cottages form a landscape that is not merely scenic but psychologically and morally charged. Repeated descriptions of black skies, unnaturally bright stars, deathlike silence and featureless expanses make the natural world seem complicit in Tom Chuff’s doom.

The narration is largely omniscient but intermittently personal and conversational. Phrases such as “On the night I speak of,” “I must describe now,” and “you may be certain” create the impression of a traditional storyteller addressing listeners around a hearth. The narrator moves freely between external action, characters’ thoughts and retrospective commentary, sometimes qualifying the account with expressions such as “I think” or “perhaps.” This produces a hybrid authority: the tale is presented as local history, remembered legend and moral exemplum at once.

The prose alternates between long, elaborately subordinated descriptive sentences and short, emphatic declarations. The opening sentence accumulates geographical and architectural detail through multiple clauses, establishing the house as dwarfed, primitive and ominous. By contrast, the introduction of Tom relies on blunt units—“He was a poacher. He was a drunkard. He beat his wife”—whose repetition delivers a prosecutorial moral verdict. During scenes of terror, syntax becomes cumulative and propulsive, with repeated conjunctions, participial phrases and escalating catalogues reproducing panic, falling and sensory overload. Repetition such as “Down, down, down” and “myriads and myriads” gives the supernatural passages an incantatory intensity.

The diction is elevated and archaic in the narrative voice, featuring words and phrases such as “ostensible pretence,” “appellative,” “torpor,” “sepulture,” “obsequies” and “bootless bene.” This formal register is mixed with concrete rustic detail—peat fires, gin bottles, cudgels, snares, reeds and churchyard walls—so that the supernatural emerges from a materially convincing rural world. Religious and allegorical language becomes especially prominent in the infernal vision, where Tom encounters judgment, punishment, a stern gatekeeper and the possibility of “one trial more.” The allusion to Bunyan explicitly places the episode within a tradition of Protestant spiritual allegory.

Dialogue sharply distinguishes social voices. Educated characters speak in standard English, while Tom and other rural figures use heavily marked northern dialect, with forms such as “forgie,” “sud dee,” “barn,” “gie,” “a’d” and “t’ sir.” The dialect lends local colour and oral authenticity, but it also reinforces social hierarchy by separating the narrator’s polished prose from the speech of the “rude and simple” community. Tom’s dialogue is generally terse and forceful; in the vision, his repeated questions and pleas expose a helplessness that contrasts with his habitual brutality.

Characterisation is economical and morally polarised. Tom is introduced through physical shorthand—“shock-headed,” broad-shouldered, bloodshot-eyed—and through a rapid catalogue of vices. His wife and children are primarily rendered through fear, cowering postures, silence and defensive gestures. Secondary figures function as moral or social types: the good-natured doctor, the kindly vicar, the avenging brother and the demonic father. Yet Tom is not wholly static. His temporary reform, shame and terror create a limited psychological complexity, while the narrator’s observation that fear cannot permanently alter essential character supplies the tale’s central moral psychology.

The narrative structure is strongly patterned. An initial domestic crisis leads to apparent death, a visionary journey into damnation, revival and repentance. This is followed by gradual relapse, the wife’s death and a final return to the exact setting and circumstances of the vision. Motifs recur with deliberate precision: the black sky, blinking stars, beech tree, open grave, whistle, approaching figure and backward fall. In the vision these elements are supernatural and symbolic; in the climax they receive plausible earthly counterparts. This doubling makes the ending simultaneously uncanny and rationally explicable. Fate fulfils the warning through coincidence, panic and Tom’s own guilty imagination rather than through an unequivocally visible supernatural agency.

Suspense is created through delay, silence and foreknowledge. Tom’s wordless arrival postpones expected violence; his apparent death is confirmed only gradually; the visionary threat gathers through successive sensory disturbances; and the final scene becomes tense because the reader recognises each returning detail before Tom’s death occurs. The story often pauses for description or moral reflection, but its major scenes are carefully escalated from unease to recognition, panic and catastrophe.

Imagery is predominantly visual and auditory. Darkness is punctured by glaring stars, red haloes, live-coal eyes and tiny points of unnatural light. Silence is broken by whistles, growls, distant cries, laughter, thunderous doors and the imagined chorus of the damned. Similes are monumental or grotesque: the beech resembles a pillar, the cottage chair has “cyclopean” weight, the black dog has a bear-like coat, and the underground passages resemble a gigantic mine. Such comparisons enlarge ordinary rural objects into emblems of judgment and terror.

The dominant tone is grave, melodramatic and morally assured, though touches of grim irony run throughout. The doctor privately considers Tom’s death a blessing; Tom’s promised grave away from the churchyard trees becomes the very grave into which he falls; and the vicar’s conscientious fulfilment of his request only sharpens fate’s mockery. The conclusion explicitly frames the narrative as a “homily,” confirming that terror is intended to instruct as well as entertain. Overall, the style depends on the fusion of oral legend, Gothic sensation, providential irony and Victorian moral commentary.

The story adopts an intimate, retrospective mode of narration in which an adult uncle addresses his nephew directly as “you.” This sustained second-person address gives the narrative the quality of a private confession or remembered conversation, while the first-person narrator’s hindsight allows him to interpret both the child’s feelings and his own conduct. His voice is self-aware but not wholly self-exonerating: he repeatedly exposes the vanity, laziness, anger, and hypocrisy concealed beneath his claim to be a “wise,” “stern and just” educator. Such descriptions are heavily ironic, since the reader can see that adult dignity has become an excuse for cruelty.

The structure is divided into seven Roman-numbered sections. It begins near the emotional resolution, with the child apologizing and again asking to be shown the figures, before moving backward to reconstruct the day’s quarrel. The narrative then proceeds through anticipation, conflict, punishment, estrangement, surrender, and reconciliation. This delayed explanation creates moral and psychological suspense even though the central event is domestic and small in scale. Each section marks a distinct emotional stage, and brief transitional statements—“But this time it was too great a quarrel” or “But it is here that the whole thing began”—give the recollection the rhythm of an oral account.

The prose combines affectionate lyricism with close psychological observation. The narrator dwells on gestures, tones, glances, and bodily details: lowered eyes, swinging feet, shining eyes, hoarse crying, deep sighs, and a pencil repeatedly moistened with the lips. These details render the child’s emotions physically visible. At the same time, the narration frequently enlarges a minor family dispute into an account of absolute longing, humiliation, pride, and submission. A wish to learn numbers becomes a “dream” possessing the child’s “whole soul,” while reconciliation offers “at least a drop” of happiness. This elevation of the ordinary gives the story a tender yet tragicomic intensity.

The emotional register is deliberately unstable. Warmth and nostalgia coexist with shame, irritation, violence, and sardonic humor. Comic moments arise from the child’s ceremonious bow, dramatic threats, improvised accusations, and exaggerated claim that he is dying; however, the comedy is repeatedly darkened by the adults’ calculated refusal to comfort him. The narrator’s abrupt aside after the slap—“There’s figures for you!”—is especially bitter, compressing his awareness of the grotesque disproportion between the child’s innocent desire and the punishment inflicted upon him. Sentiment is strong throughout, but it is complicated by irony and moral discomfort rather than presented as uncomplicated sweetness.

Dialogue is frequent, brief, and theatrically paced. The child speaks through repetitions, imperatives, protests, and unfinished phrases, which convey impatience and emotional transparency: “Now, please!” and “I do love him! I do love him!” The adults use measured, proverbial, or manipulative language, often pretending calm while pursuing a contest of wills. Their remarks expose family power indirectly: the mother invokes discipline, the grandmother alternates between sympathy and emotional coercion, and the uncle hides behind abstract principles. Dialogue is regularly followed by commentary explaining the motives beneath what was said, producing a layered contrast between outward speech and inward feeling.

The syntax shifts with emotional pressure. Reflective passages use long, accumulating sentences filled with subordinate clauses and parallel constructions, while moments of conflict break into short declarations, exclamations, and isolated paragraphs. Rhetorical questions repeatedly draw the addressee—and implicitly the reader—into judgment: “Could anyone be slow in responding to such an appeal?” Repetition intensifies feeling, especially in phrases such as “Happiness, happiness!”, “Please! Please!”, and “Give in, give in!” Ellipses represent hesitation, duration, suppressed emotion, and the uneven rhythms of crying or childhood speech. Exclamation marks, dashes, and sudden interjections similarly heighten the oral and dramatic character of the prose.

A central stylistic device is the narrator’s movement from concrete recollection to allegory. In the later sections, “Life” becomes a personified authority that delays gratification, issues commands, inflicts humiliation, and rewards submission. The uncle’s behavior is thus recast as an early lesson in the larger human struggle between desire and constraint. This philosophical expansion is expressed through direct, almost fable-like exchanges between the child and Life. The repeated vocabulary of dreams, happiness, longing, pride, conquest, and surrender gives the domestic episode symbolic weight.

The imagery remains mostly simple and sensory, appropriate to the child-centered subject, but it occasionally becomes lyrical or hyperbolic. The child’s joyful shout is described as something that would make God smile; his terror crosses his face like lightning; pain becomes a “dull knife”; and his hair has “the odor of little birds.” Musical imagery shapes the account of his crying through references to pitch, register, high and low notes, and rapid succession. Light and color also help establish mood: the unlit nursery, dark-blue windows, approaching winter evening, and finally the intimate lamplight around the lesson trace the movement from alienation toward restored closeness.

Overall, the style is confessional, psychologically acute, ironic, and emotionally expansive. It treats childhood consciousness with seriousness, portraying a child’s desire and grief as complete rather than trivial, while exposing the absurdity and cruelty of adult authority. Its signature effect comes from the tension between the narrator’s tenderness toward the remembered child and his unsparing recognition that pride transformed a minor inconvenience into a painful contest of domination.

The story uses a third-person omniscient narrator whose perspective moves freely among Stimson, the young man, Lizzie, and minor figures such as Jennie and the hackman. The narration repeatedly enters characters’ thoughts while maintaining an amused critical distance from them. It treats private feelings as sincere and powerful but also exposes their vanity, exaggeration, and self-deception. Stimson imagines himself possessed of an “infallible penetration” and a “granite will,” while the narrator quietly shows that he has merely noticed an obvious courtship and cannot control its outcome. The young lovers receive similar treatment: their emotions are authentic, yet their jealousies and gestures are inflated into mock-tragic crises.

The prevailing tone blends tenderness, comedy, irony, and lyrical seriousness. Much of the humor comes from mock-heroic elevation. Everyday scenes at a merry-go-round are described through the language of warfare, chivalry, tragedy, and imperial authority: children resemble “charging cavalrymen,” the ring attendant becomes a “general squire,” Stimson strides like a military commander, and his warning lands as though he had “blazed away with a shotgun.” Grand language is applied to small people and ordinary conflicts, creating comic disproportion. Yet the irony is rarely cruel. The narrator recognizes the dignity within youthful love and eventually allows Stimson’s comic fury to open into a genuine awareness of age, impotence, and mortality.

The diction alternates sharply between ornate literary description and colloquial speech. Narrative passages favor elevated, sometimes ceremonious words such as “indomitable,” “redoubtable,” “supplicated,” “immaterial,” and “denunciation.” Dialogue, by contrast, uses clipped vernacular forms and dialect spellings: “m့် makin’ eyes,” “By Jiminy,” “you bet,” “where in thunder,” and “I s’pose.” This contrast distinguishes social voices while intensifying the comedy: characters speak plainly, but the narrator frames their behavior as epic or metaphysical. Repetition in dialogue also establishes character. Stimson’s recurring “This is too much of a good thing” and “First thing you know, he’ll get fired” reveal a mind that converts paternal anxiety into managerial discipline.

The prose varies between compact declarative sentences and long, accumulating descriptive periods. Short statements—“He was very busy,” “They were very happy,” “He was a resolute man”—often carry ironic force because their apparent certainty is undercut by surrounding detail. Longer sentences build movement through coordinated clauses, participial phrases, lists, and rhythmic repetition. This is particularly evident in descriptions of the carousel, the beach crowd, and the final chase. The chase passage accelerates through active verbs, reiterated commands, mechanical sounds, and successive visual details: the horse “clacked,” the wheels “hummed,” and the vehicle “wheezed and groaned.” Dashes, stammered fragments, and interrupted speech convey agitation, while occasional aphoristic formulations broaden the immediate scene into a statement about love, youth, or human delusion.

Sensory imagery is dense and highly visual. Color is especially prominent: the girl’s red dress, garnet canopies, silvered netting, purple sea, indigo sky, yellow stars, and lanterns of red, violet, green, and gold. Light repeatedly transforms the setting, appearing as summer gold, electric bands, dim sails, reflected metal, moonlight, and flashing foam. Sound is equally important: surf booms, children shriek, the orchestrion bellows, the popcorn seller roars, carriage wheels hum, and Stimson repeatedly explodes into shouts. These overlapping sensations create a crowded, kinetic amusement-resort atmosphere against which the lovers conduct their almost silent courtship.

The imagery frequently joins the mechanical and the natural. The merry-go-round is an artificial world of varnished animals trapped in a “never-ending race,” while beyond it lie sea, sky, birds, stars, and long perspectives. The lovers’ emotional fluctuations echo both systems: they circle through jealousy and reconciliation like the carousel, but their hopes also reach toward the open sea and future. Nature is often personified or treated as emotionally suggestive—the sea has an “unknown emotion,” lanterns sing, and the future is treacherous—although the story also notes nature’s indifference. When the lovers are happy, they wonder how the beautiful scene can remain “phlegmatic and stolid”; had they been unhappy, they would have accused it of “criminal heartlessness.” This observation satirizes the human tendency to project private feeling onto the world.

Figurative language is abundant and deliberately conspicuous. Similes compare a distant woman to a spider on “the fabric of nature,” peace to a trusting babe, romantic uncertainty to a “dread angel,” and nighttime doubt to a hill-sized spectre. Metaphors of communication turn glances into messages and telegraphy: the lovers’ eyes speak, send explanations, and carry “caresses confided to the air.” Military metaphors characterize Stimson’s authority, while theatrical and religious metaphors elevate the lovers’ affair into a “personal drama,” a tragedy, and a form of worship. The little “Cashier” sign repeatedly interrupts their visual communion, serving as a comic concrete obstacle to idealized romance.

Characterization depends on emblematic physical details, habitual gestures, and ironic epithets. Stimson’s small body and “indomitable whiskers” establish the central contradiction between his self-image and his actual power. He glowers, strides, marches, roars, and commands, behaving as though every domestic difficulty were a military emergency. The young man is defined by his precarious perch and emotionally expressive glances; Lizzie by her position behind the netting, her evasions, and her shifting looks. Jennie is drawn more schematically as the self-sacrificing confidante “born to attend at tragedies,” but her homeliness and romantic reverence give the episode both humor and pathos. Even minor figures are captured through vivid comic responses, such as the perpetually dazed popcorn seller and the hackman who becomes exhilarated by the pursuit.

Structurally, the story moves from panoramic exposition to romantic complication and then to comic action. The first part establishes the resort, the carousel, the lovers’ wordless relationship, and Stimson’s attempted suppression of it. The second part allows the lovers direct contact, briefly settles their mutual feelings, and culminates in their elopement and Stimson’s failed chase. Recurrent objects and phrases provide cohesion: the silvered netting, the “Cashier” sign, the elevated platform, Stimson’s belief in his will, and the contrast between circular motion and forward escape. The carousel’s repetitive circuit reflects the lovers’ earlier cycles of doubt, whereas the fleeing buggy introduces linear movement toward the future.

The ending shifts the emotional register without abandoning irony. During the chase, Stimson realizes that the vehicle ahead represents youth itself—its speed, hope, renewal, and power to outlive him. His defeat is therefore larger than the loss of authority over his daughter; it is a confrontation with time and the limits of possession. Nevertheless, the final gesture, by which he decides that “at any rate he was not responsible,” restores the comic note and confirms his instinct for self-exoneration. Overall, the style combines realist social observation, impressionistic sensory description, mock-epic comedy, and compassionate psychological irony, presenting ordinary romantic rebellion as at once ridiculous, beautiful, and inevitable.

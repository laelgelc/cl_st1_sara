The story adopts the manner of an antiquarian historical romance, presenting a legendary episode as though it had been recovered from old Spanish and Moorish chronicles. Its narrator is learned, urbane, and gently ironic: he cites historians, supplies a scholarly footnote, comments on the doubtful boundary between fact and fiction, and repeatedly attributes picturesque or sentimental details to an “ancient chronicler.” This pseudo-historical framing lends authority to the tale while simultaneously undermining literal belief in it. The narrative voice remains omniscient and intrusive, freely explaining motives, anticipating objections, summarising background, and adding amused observations about Gascon pride, Bordeaux gastronomy, romantic conventions, and the chronicler’s supposed gravity.

The diction is elevated, archaic, and strongly coloured by medieval chivalric vocabulary. Terms such as “cavalier,” “burgher,” “damsel,” “palfrey,” “buckler,” “cuirass,” “benediction,” and “donzella” establish the period atmosphere, while Spanish and French expressions—including “hijo-dalgo,” “Bandoleros,” “Señor,” “Amiga,” and “chanson”—add local colour. Formulaic constructions such as “Now it happened one day,” “Behold,” “by my faith,” “had he not,” and “would fain” imitate chronicles and traditional romances. Moral qualities are expressed in similarly conventional terms: characters are noble-minded, gentle, fair, valiant, faithful, holy, or villainous. The prose is ornate without being uniformly solemn, because ceremonious description is frequently lightened by understated comedy.

Sentences tend to be long, balanced, and cumulative, often linking narrative information through semicolons, subordinate clauses, and parallel phrases. Periodic constructions delay the main point and create a stately, retrospective rhythm. Descriptive lists are common, especially when cataloguing weapons, clothing, landscape, food, or predicted calamities. Shorter sentences and direct speech appear at moments of confrontation, giving the combat scenes greater speed and clarity. Exclamation marks, rhetorical contrasts, and emphatic repetitions heighten prophecy and chivalric action, while parenthetical remarks allow the narrator to retain a conversational relationship with the reader.

The plot is largely linear, but it begins with an expository discussion of unreliable history before narrowing into a self-contained adventure from Pelayo’s youth. Its movement follows the structure of romance: pastoral or hunting activity is interrupted by danger; a helpless travelling party is attacked; the young hero performs feats of arms; captives are rescued; hospitality and courtly attraction follow; and a hermit reveals the hero’s providential destiny. Pacing alternates between expansive background narration and briskly staged action. Combat is vivid but economical, conveyed through concrete movements—lowering a lance, bracing a buckler, taking a narrow pass, cleaving a skull-cap—rather than prolonged psychological suspense. After the battle, the pace slows into scenes of feasting, nocturnal prophecy, morning travel, and emotional farewell.

Characterisation is archetypal and external. Pelayo embodies the ideal Christian knight before formally entering chivalry: he is physically hardy, sexually innocent, fearless, merciful, indifferent to wealth, respectful toward religion, and destined to redeem his country. His virtue is demonstrated through contrasted episodes—he defeats armed robbers but prevents their execution, ignores the merchant’s gold, protects women without exploiting their gratitude, and values prayers above payment. The merchant is a comic embodiment of prosperous bourgeois caution and appetite; his fears repeatedly misinterpret Pelayo’s hunters as another band of marauders. The daughter is fashioned according to the conventions of courtly love: beautiful, modest, emotionally transparent, and permanently faithful to a hero who remains unaware of the depth of her devotion. The robbers are colourful stock villains, while the hermit functions as a sacred messenger rather than a psychologically developed individual.

Landscape is central to the atmosphere and symbolism. The Pyrenees are rendered as a rugged border world of rocks, defiles, forests, glens, wild beasts, hidden marauders, echoing horns, and isolated sacred retreats. This setting tests and hardens Pelayo, separating him from the corrupt court and preparing him for national leadership. Repeated contrasts organise the imagery: mountain austerity opposes courtly luxury; wilderness opposes prosperous civic life; predatory violence opposes peaceful commerce; gold opposes spiritual worth; and present natural abundance opposes the ruined Spain imagined in the hermit’s prophecy. The hermitage, spring, oak, moonlight, and dawn give the prophetic section a biblical and pastoral solemnity.

Dialogue is sparse, formal, and functional. In the fight it consists of challenges and defiant retorts; in the hermit’s speech it becomes prophetic and scriptural; in the farewell it expresses humility, gratitude, and restrained love. Characters often speak in polished declarations rather than naturalistic conversation. The daughter’s gift of the ring is the emotional centre of the tale, but the scene avoids reciprocal romance by making Pelayo’s response innocent and fraternal. This creates gentle dramatic irony: the reader and narrator understand her passion, while the hero does not.

The tonal range combines heroic admiration, religious solemnity, pathos, and genial mockery. The narrator reveres Pelayo’s courage and moral purity, yet treats the surrounding conventions with playful scepticism. Humour arises from inflated chronicular claims, the Gascons’ pretensions to gentility, the merchant’s alarm over his money, his vigorous return to eating, and the insistence that Pelayo reached adulthood without once sighing for a woman. The ending deepens into tender melancholy, though even here the narrator preserves the air of a legendary source by explaining the maiden’s lifelong fidelity and the fanciful origin of her book.

The principal themes are the formation of a national hero, providential destiny, Christian redemption, chivalric virtue, chastity, mercy, disinterested service, and unrequited ideal love. History is treated as a mixture of evidence, legend, patriotic memory, and literary invention. Stylistically, the piece depends on that mixture: it sounds at once like a medieval chronicle, a nineteenth-century historical romance, and an urbane modern retelling whose narrator affectionately recognises the improbabilities of the tradition he preserves.

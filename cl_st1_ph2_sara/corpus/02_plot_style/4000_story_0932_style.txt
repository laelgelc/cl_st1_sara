The story uses a realist, observational style sharpened by comic irony. Its setting is rendered through concrete rural detail—the tavern bench, dusty road, cornfield, meadow insects, chickens, fences and woods—while ordinary village behaviour is described in the elevated language of war, heroism and tragedy. This mismatch is the central stylistic device: minor disturbances become “tempests,” frightened chickens clamour “in an insanity of fear,” and a hunt for a supposed rebel assumes the dignity of a military campaign. The narration repeatedly inflates the characters’ actions only to expose their vanity, credulity and fear.

The narrator is third-person omniscient, moving freely between individuals and the collective consciousness of the village. The point of view often settles close to Major Boldin or Peter Witheby, revealing their heroic fantasies and private anxieties, but it also treats the women and children as a single excitable social body. The narrator remains outwardly composed and rarely comments directly. Instead, irony emerges through discrepancies between what the characters imagine and what the reader can see: the major envisions public gratitude while hesitating at every boundary, Peter adopts an authoritative manner once others begin watching him, and the villagers transform familiar scenery into a theatre of hidden menace.

The prose alternates between polished descriptive narration and strongly marked vernacular dialogue. Narrative diction is formal, vivid and occasionally grandiose, using words such as “tempestuous,” “inscrutable,” “solemn,” “prophetic” and “tragedy.” The dialogue, by contrast, is colloquial and phonetically represented through contractions, dropped consonants and regional forms: “yeh,” “th’,” “hain’t,” “agoin’,” “hol’ on a minnet.” This contrast gives the characters distinct social and regional texture while heightening the comedy of their inflated self-importance. Repetition is crucial to the spoken rhythm. Peter’s recurring “Hol’ on a minnet,” the major’s impatient questions, and the repeated warnings of “S-s-sh!” become comic refrains that reveal nervousness beneath assertions of courage.

Sentence structure is generally clear and narrative-driven, with medium-length declarative sentences punctuated by longer descriptive passages. Short sentences and fragments intensify hesitation or suspense, especially around entrances into the corn and woods. Parallel constructions and accumulations portray crowd behaviour: questions multiply, people stream after one another, and fear passes through the group almost physically. Exclamation marks, dashes and interrupted phrases reproduce agitation in dialogue, while pauses, stammers and unfinished suppositions dramatise reluctance. The prose often gives a character’s bodily movement before or instead of explicit psychological explanation, so fear appears in wavering eyes, open mouths, hurried feet, clutched weapons and sudden retreats.

Sensory imagery is dense but economical. Heat is conveyed through dust, soft sky, swaying corn and the ceaseless chirping of insects. Sound is particularly prominent: sparrows wrangle, harnesses jangle, chickens squawk, leaves crackle and crickets erupt. At first these noises establish sleepy village life; later, once rumour has altered perception, the same landscape seems filled with warnings and omens. The story therefore shows atmosphere being psychologically produced. Cornfields and woods are not inherently sinister, but collective fear turns them into “mystic” and “inscrutable” spaces populated by imagined threats.

The pacing proceeds through comic escalation. A boy’s breathless report wakes the major; the major arms himself; rumour draws a crowd; the crowd follows; the ordinary landscape becomes threatening; and the major and Peter advance by stages through road, fence, cornfield and woods. Suspense is repeatedly delayed by arguments, pauses, false alarms and demands for reassurance. The section break enlarges the episode into a mock campaign, but the climax abruptly collapses when the “rebel” proves to be the drunken Milton Jacoby. The final sentence completes the deflation: the villagers erase their own credulity by blaming the major and claiming that he “might have known.”

The tone combines affection, satire and mild cruelty. The villagers are foolish, but their foolishness is recognisably human: they crave importance, imitate courage, fear social embarrassment and revise their positions after the fact. The humour depends on incongruity, repetition, collective panic and dramatic deflation rather than on jokes delivered by the narrator. Beneath the comedy lies a study of rumour and communal psychology. War has left the village primed to interpret the unknown as an enemy, while the absence of its able-bodied men allows older men, women and children to rehearse simplified versions of military courage. Overall, the style is picturesque rural realism transformed by mock-heroic narration, psychological irony and a finely controlled progression from idleness to panic and anticlimax.

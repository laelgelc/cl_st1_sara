The story uses a bleak colonial Australian realist style, intensified by naturalistic fatalism and elements of rural Gothic. Its bush setting is rendered through concrete local detail—stringy-bark, box saplings, rocky waterholes, slab huts, skillions, slip-rails, pannikins, ’possum skins and droving—so that the landscape feels socially and materially specific. Yet the environment is also strongly expressive: the gully grows “deeper and darker,” pools appear “ghastly” and “evil-looking,” and names such as Deadman’s Gap and Deadman’s Hollow foreshadow the conclusion. Darkness, scrub, fire, moonlight and enclosed gullies turn the bush into both a physical prison and an outward image of Mary’s dread.

The narration is predominantly third-person omniscient, moving between detached observation, authorial commentary and close access to Mary’s consciousness. Much of the opening is externally descriptive, but the point of view gradually narrows around her fear, shame and frustrated ambition. Free indirect discourse and interior monologue convey her obsessive thoughts without fully abandoning the narrator’s voice: disgrace becomes a “cruel hand,” trouble a “dark…cloud,” and escape is imagined as sailing beyond the horizon. The narrator occasionally intervenes with terse generalisations—“She had plenty to moan about”—or bitterly ironic judgments. These intrusions create a voice that is compassionate toward suffering but unsentimental about weakness, cruelty and self-deception.

The prose alternates between economical, reportorial narration and emotionally heightened passages. Domestic actions and material conditions are catalogued plainly: the clay floor, bark bed, improvised furniture, poor food and endless work establish deprivation through objects rather than abstract explanation. At moments of crisis, however, the syntax becomes repetitive and exclamatory. Phrases such as “always in trouble,” “God help me,” and “away from the bush” recur like refrains, reproducing Mary’s increasingly compulsive state of mind. Dashes, ellipses, italics or capitals, broken sentences and direct exclamations give the emotional scenes a jagged, breathless rhythm.

Dialogue is central to characterization and social texture. The speech is heavily marked by vernacular spelling and rural pronunciation—“carnt yer,” “git,” “agoin’,” “blarsted”—and by Australian bush vocabulary. Wylie’s clipped commands, threats and evasions communicate brutality more efficiently than exposition; Mrs. Wylie’s repetitions and timid questions reveal chronic fear; Mary’s sharper, more grammatically standard speech suggests education, sensitivity and resistance. Exchanges are often brief, confrontational and interrupted, making family life feel unstable. The gap between Mary’s inward eloquence and the coarse speech surrounding her emphasizes her isolation.

The tone combines pity, indignation, grim humour and savage irony. Comic touches appear in the horse’s seeming sympathy, the alleged “clear skin” steer, the dogs’ behaviour and Bob Bentley’s good-natured speech, but they never dispel the prevailing misery. Instead, humour provides momentary contrast and often sharpens the tragedy. The description of silence after the children are beaten as a “peaceful calm” descending on “that happy home” is especially caustic. Similar irony exposes euphemism and hypocrisy: theft becomes doubtful “buying,” punishment is imagined as a useful “lesson,” and social disgrace is treated as though it belongs to the innocent women rather than the guilty men.

Structurally, the narrative is divided into four numbered movements and proceeds episodically, with substantial jumps in time. Each section tightens the pattern of inherited or repeated disaster: the stolen steer leads to Wylie’s arrest; Tom’s return brings temporary hope before renewed trouble; Mary’s sweetheart is exposed as a criminal; her mother dies; Wylie returns with another woman; and Mary is finally driven from home. Transitions are often abrupt and laconic, allowing devastating events to land without sentimental preparation. The sentence announcing that Wylie “brought home a—another woman” uses hesitation and understatement to imply an entire new phase of degradation.

Foreshadowing is persistent and overt. Mary’s nervous glances at the waterholes, her longing to leave, the spectral kangaroos, the rising moon and the ominous place names all anticipate her death. Circular patterning strengthens the ending: the story begins with Mary passing the threatening pools on horseback and ends with her returning to the deepest pool on foot; kangaroos startled near the beginning reappear as “three grey ghosts” after the final splash. This closure gives the tragedy an air of inevitability.

Characterization is socially grounded but sometimes shaped by period assumptions. Mary is presented as unusually sensitive, intelligent and potentially gifted, yet trapped by poverty, gender, kinship and inherited stigma. Mrs. Wylie embodies exhausted endurance and a destructive ideal of marital duty. The men range from violent and criminal to weakly sympathetic, with Bob offering a brief image of ordinary decency too late to prevent catastrophe. The reference to hereditary “taint” introduces a naturalistic, quasi-determinist explanation of the family’s conduct, suggesting that environment and inheritance combine to defeat individual aspiration.

The dominant themes are domestic violence, rural isolation, poverty, inherited criminality, social shame and the unequal burden placed on women. The story repeatedly distinguishes guilt from disgrace: Mary commits no crime, but she experiences the family’s reputation as an inescapable punishment. Her desire to leave the bush is therefore more than geographical; it is a wish to escape kinship, class, memory and identity itself. The overall style is direct, colloquial and documentary at the surface, but symbolically patterned underneath, producing a tragedy in which realistic bush life gradually takes on the shape of nightmare.

The story is a moral satire built around sustained, escalating irony. It adopts the outward form of a didactic tale—two boys follow opposite paths, one virtuous and one dissolute—but systematically reverses the expected rewards. Edward’s goodness makes him easy to neglect and exploit, while George’s misconduct continually attracts attention, sympathy, money, and institutional support. The style therefore parodies conventional moral instruction while exposing a society more interested in dramatic repentance than in unobtrusive integrity.

The narrator speaks in a composed, apparently matter-of-fact voice, rarely announcing indignation directly. This restraint creates deadpan humour: grotesquely unjust events are reported with the calm cadence of ordinary biography. Loaded phrases such as “the good Brants,” “poor George,” “noble sisterhood,” and “sweetest and best” imitate the sentimental language of charitable and religious culture, but repetition and context make them increasingly sarcastic. The narrator often allows institutions and benevolent citizens to condemn themselves through their own formulas, ceremonies, and priorities.

The plot has a strongly parallel, accumulative design. Edward and George are repeatedly placed in contrasting situations: obedience against tantrums, work against flight, fidelity against vice, obscurity against publicity, and genuine sacrifice against theatrical reform. Each cycle enlarges the disparity between merit and reward. George falls, repents, receives public acclaim, and falls again; Edward quietly behaves well and consequently becomes less visible. Recurrent phrases—especially “An account of it was published”—function as satirical refrains, emphasizing that publicity rather than moral worth determines social value.

Characterization is deliberately schematic. Edward is less a psychologically complex individual than the embodiment of consistent virtue, while George embodies habitual selfishness and opportunism. Their flatness serves the fable-like argument. Secondary figures are similarly presented as social types: indulgent guardians, reforming women, temperance campaigners, charitable societies, newspaper readers, bankers, petitioners, and religious enthusiasts. The collective public effectively becomes the central satirical character, governed by sentimentality, spectacle, and a desire to rescue conspicuous sinners.

The prose mixes plain narrative with periodic bursts of mock-solemn rhetoric. Much of the syntax is additive and chronological, driven by phrases such as “by and by,” “finally,” “then,” and “while all these things were going on.” This gives the story the swift, simplified movement of a cautionary tale. Longer sentences often catalogue George’s offenses or society’s responses, producing comic excess through accumulation. Dashes, semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, and abrupt explanatory turns allow the narrator to sharpen contrasts without abandoning the conversational flow.

Dialogue is sparse and largely aphoristic. The Brants’ maxim—“Be pure, honest, sober, industrious, and considerate of others”—establishes the conventional moral law that the narrative proceeds to test and discredit as a promise of worldly success. Other quoted language consists mainly of clichés about duty, reform, providence, conversion, and sympathy. These quotations reveal how ready-made moral phrases can disguise selfishness or replace practical help.

The humour depends on understatement, hyperbole, bathos, and verbal inversion. Edward’s lifelong goodness “excited no remark” because it was his “natural bent,” whereas George’s temporary sobriety becomes a public spectacle. The national banking community’s gratitude amounts to “nearly three-eighths of a cent for each bank,” a mock-statistical detail that converts professed admiration into measurable stinginess. Similarly, charitable organizations refuse Edward because he lacks the qualifying disgrace that would make him an appealing beneficiary. Euphemisms such as “sent up” and ceremonious descriptions of George’s execution heighten the contrast between brutal reality and sentimental presentation.

The tone grows progressively darker without losing its comic composure. Early domestic unfairness is amusingly recognizable, but the same social mechanism eventually leads to bankruptcy, abandonment, murder, and the glorification of the murderer. This escalation turns comedy into savage social criticism. The targets include indulgent parenting, organized philanthropy, temperance culture, prison reform, religious sensationalism, newspapers, banks, memorial fundraising, and the public appetite for visible redemption.

Imagery is limited and functional rather than lush. Symbolic objects and settings carry the satire: candy, toys, new clothes, pledges, newspaper accounts, the gutter, the prison door, the bank safe, the gallows, gravestones, flowers, and the memorial church. Public rituals repeatedly replace substantive justice. Flowers, hymns, subscriptions, testimonials, and monuments provide emotionally satisfying displays while doing little for the deserving living.

The ending uses paired memorials as a final structural and rhetorical blow. George receives a complete, triumphant religious epitaph, while Edward’s lifelong maxim is cut off at “you will never—,” visually exposing the falsity of its promised conclusion. The unfinished inscription also invites the reader to supply the bitter truth: virtue does not guarantee friends, recognition, security, or reward. The final sentence intensifies the irony once more: society raises a large sum in Edward’s honour, but spends it on a church rather than on his impoverished family. The characteristic effect is concise, epigrammatic, and devastating—a sentimental gesture converted into proof of moral failure.

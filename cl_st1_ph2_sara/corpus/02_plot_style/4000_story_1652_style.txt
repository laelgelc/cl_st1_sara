The story uses a warm, conversational third-person narrator who is omniscient but highly intrusive. Rather than remaining invisible, the narrator addresses the reader directly, pauses the action to offer moral reflections, and comments knowingly on the act of storytelling itself. Phrases such as “take a look at the home,” “dear friends,” and “as you know” create the intimacy of an oral tale, while self-conscious asides such as “Forget the hashed metaphor” give the narration a playful, improvisatory quality.

The diction mixes plain domestic language with ornate, mock-formal phrasing. Everyday objects and financial details—pennies, rent, wages, a couch, a frying pan, an overcoat—anchor the story in working-class material reality. Against this simplicity, the narrator introduces elevated or deliberately grandiose words such as “parsimony,” “meretricious ornamentation,” and “appertaining thereunto.” This contrast produces comic irony: a shabby flat and its modest inhabitants are described in the language of ceremony, philosophy, and epic importance. Biblical and legendary references to the Magi, Solomon, and the Queen of Sheba similarly magnify the couple’s private sacrifices.

Sentence structure is varied and strongly rhythmic. Short fragments and repetitions heighten emotion and fix important facts in the reader’s mind: “One dollar and eighty-seven cents. That was all.” Longer sentences carry humorous qualifications, comparisons, and narrative commentary. Parallel constructions, lists, and antitheses are frequent, as in “sobs, sniffles, and smiles” or the contrast between “eight dollars a week” and “a million a year.” Exclamations, dashes, abrupt transitions, and isolated sentences give the prose an energetic, performative cadence.

Figurative language is vivid but knowingly extravagant. Della’s hair becomes “a cascade of brown waters,” time trips by “on rosy wings,” and Jim freezes “as immovable as a setter at the scent of quail.” Hyperbole is central to the style: the couple’s hair and watch are imagined as capable of provoking envy in biblical royalty. The narrator sometimes undercuts these flourishes immediately, preventing the sentiment from becoming entirely solemn. Personification also supplies humour, as when the diminished name “Dillingham” appears to consider contracting itself to a modest initial.

The tone continually balances tenderness, comedy, pathos, and irony. Poverty is presented sympathetically, but not naturalistically or bleakly; comic descriptions soften hardship without dismissing it. The narrator affectionately calls Jim and Della “two foolish children,” treating their impracticality as evidence of emotional wisdom. Sentiment is therefore filtered through wit, and wit ultimately serves rather than undermines the story’s moral seriousness.

Dialogue is colloquial, concise, and character-revealing. Della’s speech is rapid, repetitive, affectionate, and emotionally exposed, while Jim’s is slower and restrained. Informal forms such as “ain’t,” “yer,” “keep ’em,” and “dandy” establish social register and immediacy. The dialogue also controls suspense: Jim’s delayed, almost literal questions prolong the uncertainty surrounding his reaction before the paired revelations.

The narrative is tightly constructed around objects, symmetry, and reversal. The opening sum of money establishes the immediate problem, after which the plot follows a simple line of preparation, sacrifice, anticipation, and disclosure. Della’s hair and Jim’s watch function both as prized possessions and as structural counterparts. Each character secretly sells the very object required to make the other’s gift useful. This double irony produces the characteristic surprise ending, but the twist is prepared through careful emphasis on the two treasures and is subsequently reinterpreted as a moral paradox.

The ending shifts from domestic comedy into parable. Repetition of “wise” and “wisest” transforms apparent foolishness into spiritual insight, while the Magi motif gives the couple’s actions symbolic and religious resonance. The governing stylistic principle is paradox: materially useless gifts become proof of perfect generosity; foolish sacrifice becomes wisdom; an “uneventful chronicle” acquires the stature of a timeless moral tale. Overall, the style is sentimental yet ironic, economical yet ornamented, and intimate yet self-consciously literary.

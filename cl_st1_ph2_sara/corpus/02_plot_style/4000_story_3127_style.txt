The story adopts the manner of a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century children’s tale, combining sentimental fantasy, social comedy, and a gently satirical portrait of urban apartment life. Its central conceit—the fear that Santa Claus cannot enter a modern flat—sets traditional Christmas folklore against restrictive leases, guarded entrances, gas fireplaces, elevators, and other signs of metropolitan modernity. The tone remains warm and playful, but the narrative also mocks pretension, class anxiety, fashionable taste, bureaucratic rules, and adults’ inability to understand children.

The narrator is conspicuously present, omniscient, and conversational. Direct addresses such as “what do you suppose,” “perhaps you think,” and “you are mistaken” create the atmosphere of an oral storyteller speaking to children. Frequent exclamations and asides—“Dear, dear,” “Oh, me, yes!” and “Well, well”—make the narration animated and intimate. The narrator freely digresses, comments on the action, anticipates objections, and draws attention to the act of storytelling. This voice is affectionate toward the children while treating adult society with amused irony.

The prose alternates between elaborate, mock-formal description and simple, emphatic declarations. Long sentences accumulate clauses, qualifications, lists, and parenthetical observations, while short lines deliver comic or emotional emphasis: “Hope lay strangled!” Repetition is prominent, especially in the rules of the Lease, the blocked routes available to Santa, and the eventual abundance of Christmas gifts. Parallel constructions such as “not to run—not to jump—not to yell” give the prose a rhythmic, performative quality. Dashes, exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, and sudden interjections reproduce the momentum of excited speech.

The diction mixes elevated and colloquial registers for comic effect. Grand terms such as “ukase,” “Herodian pronunciamento,” “universal brotherhood,” and “aristocratic” inflate minor domestic matters into political or biblical crises. Against this stand children’s expressions such as “Goosey!” and “Gimini!”, along with references to suckers, gum, go-carts, and toy dishes. Mock-heroic phrasing is a defining device: the apartment lease becomes an instrument of tyranny, the investigation of fireplaces becomes a collective mission, and a storm becomes the miraculous solution to an architectural problem.

Humour arises chiefly through irony, exaggeration, incongruity, and comic digression. The adults praise the children’s new quietness without recognising their despair, interpret melancholy as possible sewer gas, and even administer medicine to a healthy child. Fashionable furnishings are treated with sly suspicion: the Bokara rug carries germs eager to investigate American throats, while unreadable calendars and oddly coloured vases signify “esthetic taste.” The janitor’s accidental fall down the elevator shaft is narrated with absurd understatement, and the final explanation of Santa’s entrance turns destructive weather into providential Christmas machinery.

Characterisation is broad, economical, and caricatural. The twenty-six children function largely as a collective protagonist united by a common fear, although Ernest, Roderick, Dorothy, and the Telephone Boy receive individual traits. Ernest is serious and alarmed, Roderick comparatively cheerful, and the Telephone Boy serves as an unofficial child-world authority. Adults are defined mostly by habits, social poses, or failures of perception. Small concrete details—the ladies rustling their skirts, fathers looking up from newspapers, the Telephone Boy tightening his belt against hunger—quickly establish class, behaviour, and circumstance. Beneath the comedy, the story shows real sympathy for working people, especially the overworked janitor and the underfed Telephone Boy.

Dialogue is lively and socially differentiated. Middle-class residents speak in polite complaints about hot-water pipes, carpets, and servants, whereas working-class characters are represented through heavily phonetic dialect. This dialect writing helps distinguish social groups and gives the dialogue immediacy, but it is historically dated and relies on ethnic and class-based conventions that may appear stereotyped to modern readers. The children’s dialogue is direct, repetitive, and practical, keeping the supernatural problem grounded in concrete questions of doors, chimneys, grates, and shafts.

Imagery is vivid but accessible. The restrictive apartment is rendered through polished banisters, uniforms, vestibules, speaking tubes, elevators, and artificial gas grates. Emotional states are personified: apprehension creeps around Ernest’s heart, darkness becomes a “soft black,” and children place their hands in the “shadowy clasp” of the Sandman. The storm sequence intensifies the imagery through animal similes and violent sound—wind with a hundred tongues, a hungry wolf’s howl, breaking glass, and crashing chimneys—before the bright abundance of stockings, trees, and boxes releases the tension.

Structurally, the story follows a clear comic-folktale arc. It first establishes the flat children and the oppressive Lease, then poses the apparently insoluble problem of Santa’s entry. Repeated investigations close off every possible route, and the children’s anxiety deepens as Christmas approaches. The violent storm supplies both climax and solution by removing the skylights. A celebratory revelation follows, after which the narrator adds several comic codas: the children’s poem, the Telephone Boy’s gifts, and the janitor’s eventual smile. These endings neatly resolve minor threads introduced during the digressions.

The emotional register blends pathos with reassurance. The children’s tears, the Telephone Boy’s poverty, and the bleak Christmas Eve are treated sincerely, yet sadness is rarely allowed to remain unrelieved by humour. The governing outlook is benevolent: adult systems may be foolish and modern architecture inhospitable, but imagination, fellowship, accident, and Christmas generosity ultimately defeat exclusion. The resulting style is exuberant, digressive, theatrical, and knowingly sentimental, with a strong storytelling voice that converts urban inconvenience into a comic seasonal miracle.

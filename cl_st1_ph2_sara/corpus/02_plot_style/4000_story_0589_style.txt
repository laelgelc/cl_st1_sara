The story is written as a rugged northern adventure tale, combining frontier realism, melodrama, physical ordeal, and a moral drama of revenge. Its atmosphere is severe and masculine, dominated by exposure, hunger, bodily injury, violence, and the constant agency of the Arctic environment. The plot moves toward an ironic reversal: George’s long-awaited opportunity to kill his enemy instead forces him to rescue that enemy, and the final joke converts moral triumph into the rough emotional language of frontier rivalry.

The narration uses a third-person, largely omniscient viewpoint, though it stays closest to Big George and occasionally to Charlie Captain. The narrator presents the setting panoramically, then narrows into immediate bodily sensations and actions. Exposition is often supplied through what characters observe or understand, as when George interprets the guide’s message and token. The story’s central history is delivered as an extended first-person oral narrative by George, creating a story-within-the-story that explains the feud while deepening his character. This embedded account occupies much of the text and temporarily shifts the mode from active siege narrative to remembered frontier legend.

The prose is vivid, muscular, and highly visual. It repeatedly animates the landscape through personification and violent verbs: huts “grovel,” the wind “tore,” the ocean is driven by a “lash,” and ice “beat and roared.” Nature is not passive scenery but an antagonistic force capable of frustrating or replacing human vengeance. Whiteness, glare, darkness, blood, and fire-like light form the principal colour imagery. The Arctic landscape is frequently rendered through stark silhouettes—black men and dogs against snow, spruces resembling headstones, and figures isolated upon glittering ice. Such imagery gives the setting an ominous, almost mythic scale.

Description is strongly kinetic. Verbs such as “plunging,” “whirled,” “trampling,” “crashed,” “lunged,” and “smashed” make action feel physical and continuous. Bodies are shown under pressure: eyes split or burn from glare, clothes freeze into a “carapace,” feet clatter like wood, and frost turns flesh white and solid. Similes and metaphors are concrete, often drawn from animals, machinery, combat, or elemental violence. George cuffs men “like a bear,” ice becomes armour or paper, and the storm creates a “threshing maelstrom.” This figurative language reinforces the story’s emphasis on strength, endurance, and hostile material conditions.

The diction alternates between elevated descriptive prose and heavily marked frontier speech. Narrative passages use ornate or literary words such as “forlornly,” “abasement,” “malevolence,” and “lamentations,” sometimes producing a deliberately grandiose tone. Dialogue, by contrast, is colloquial, compressed, and dialectal: “ye,” “’tis,” dropped final consonants, nonstandard verb forms, mining and Arctic vocabulary, and blunt idioms establish George as an old northern hand. Terms such as “Chechako,” “mukluks,” “malamoots,” “parka,” “tundra,” and “niggerheads” give the prose regional specificity. Some ethnic labels and racialised descriptions are dated and offensive by modern standards; they belong to the story’s period frontier idiom and its stereotyped representation of Indigenous people rather than to a neutral contemporary register.

Sentence structure varies with narrative function. Descriptive passages often use long, cumulative sentences containing participial phrases and coordinated clauses, allowing setting, motion, and suffering to accumulate in a single sweep. Action scenes shorten the syntax and rely on rapid sequencing, abrupt exclamations, and forceful verbs. George’s long monologue has an oral rhythm built from repetition, chronological markers, direct quotation, and digressive detail. Punctuation is emphatic and period-specific: frequent exclamation marks, dashes, semicolons, and dialogue tags heighten urgency and theatricality. Repeated cries—“Too late! Too late!” and “He is here! He is here!”—give moments of fear or revelation a melodramatic intensity.

Dialogue is the main instrument of characterization. George’s speech is boastful, profane, humorous, sentimental, and violent, often shifting rapidly between menace and tenderness. His dialect conveys authority and experience, while his extravagant phrasing turns him into a frontier raconteur. Charlie speaks in a more educated and morally explicit register, reflecting his background as a university man and engineer. He functions as George’s ethical counterweight, interrupting revenge with the language of shared humanity: “He’s a man” and “Fight like a man.” Orloff’s speech is comparatively restrained at the end, allowing his admission of guilt and expectation of death to trigger George’s final reversal.

Characterization is broad, forceful, and archetypal rather than psychologically delicate. George is the giant frontiersman: physically overwhelming, emotionally loyal, slow to relinquish hatred, yet governed at the decisive moment by a rough code of courage and mercy. His internal conflict is dramatized externally—his raised gun, Captain’s grip on his arm, and the immediate transition from murderous fury to rescue work—rather than explored through sustained introspection. Captain serves as companion, witness, and conscience. Orloff is initially constructed through George’s account as a corrupt priest and almost absolute villain, but his vulnerability on the ice and his acknowledgment of George’s goodness partly humanize him. Indigenous characters are generally treated collectively and stereotypically, functioning as an intimidated crowd, hostile guards, guides, or instruments of missionary power.

The story is carefully shaped around suspense and reversal. It opens in medias res with an exhausted arrival, establishes that George is a marked man, and creates a confined sanctuary under siege. The long retrospective narrative delays the anticipated confrontation while supplying the emotional stakes. A blizzard then acts as both reprieve and clock, after which Orloff’s sudden arrival appears to bring the feud to its climax. The treacherous ice unexpectedly reverses the balance of power: the hunter becomes helpless, while the intended victim controls whether he lives. A second reversal follows when George abandons vengeance and undertakes an extraordinary rescue journey. The closing exchange supplies an ironic comic release, recasting the saved enemy as something rarer and more valuable than a friend.

Thematically, the prose contrasts revenge with an unwritten frontier code. George believes violence can redress betrayal and bereavement, but when his enemy is helpless, killing him would cease to be a fair contest. Mercy therefore emerges not through abstract piety but through the values George understands: courage, action, endurance, and “fighting like a man.” The institutional Church is treated ambivalently. Its buildings provide sanctuary, and individual missions offer aid, but religious authority is also shown as vulnerable to coercion, hypocrisy, and personal corruption. This tension contributes to the story’s irony: George commits sacrilege by tearing down a cross, yet performs the most recognizably Christian act of mercy.

The overall tone moves among grim naturalism, lurid melodrama, heroic romance, and dry frontier comedy. Suffering is described with graphic immediacy, emotional conflict is heightened almost to legend, and moral resolution arrives through strenuous physical action rather than contemplation. The final line is characteristic of the whole style: blunt, comic, profane, and emotionally indirect. George cannot openly celebrate compassion, so he disguises attachment and moral relief as disappointment at losing an exceptional enemy.

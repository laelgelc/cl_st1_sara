The story uses a confident, intrusive third-person narrator who combines historical chronicle, frontier legend, moral commentary, and intimate character portraiture. The point of view is broadly omniscient, moving freely across the settlement and into the motives of Father de Smet and Ninon. The narrator frequently steps outside the immediate action to address the reader—“mind you,” “Fancy!” and “if you are a man like Father de Smet”—or to offer general judgments about vice, civilization, disease, courage, and conquest. This creates the impression of an experienced storyteller interpreting events rather than merely recording them.

The prose alternates between expansive, cumulative sentences and short, emphatic declarations. Long sentences gather landscape details, historical background, character traits, and parallel actions into sweeping rhythms, while abrupt statements such as “Ninon was universally popular. And so were her soups” supply comic timing and emphasis. Parenthetical remarks, semicolons, rhetorical questions, repetitions, and frequent dashes give the narration a conversational, digressive movement. Balanced constructions and catalogues are prominent, especially in passages listing frontier virtues and vices or the successive scenes of sickness attended by the priest.

The diction blends elevated literary language with practical frontier vocabulary. Formal and religious terms—“unavailing,” “anointed,” “temporal life,” “departing soul”—sit beside references to buffalo chips, bear’s grease, tepees, rifles, whiskey, mud, skins, and rafts. French words and forms such as “Mademoiselle,” “Mam’selle,” “voyageur,” “boudoir,” and “passée” reinforce Ninon’s cultural identity and lend the setting a cosmopolitan trace amid wilderness hardship. The story also retains period-specific racial and cultural terminology, including reductive descriptions of Indigenous people and women, which marks the historical register and assumptions of its era.

Imagery is strongly sensory and often organized through contrasts. The wilderness is rendered through cold, disease, mud, foul interiors, leaden skies, animal skins, greasy food, and decaying bodies, while Ninon’s cabin introduces perfume, silk, spices, cosmetics, rich furs, and decorative color. The narrative repeatedly opposes Paris and the frontier, refinement and brutality, sacred and profane, physical filth and moral purity, death and seasonal renewal. Color details—white lilies, gold hearts, red calico, blue bluffs, black buffalo, rouge-stained cheeks—give symbolic sharpness to an otherwise bleak environment.

The dominant tone is grave and elegiac, but it is continually relieved by dry irony, affectionate humor, and worldly skepticism. Father de Smet’s physical toughness and gastronomic weakness are treated comically without diminishing his heroism. Ninon’s ambiguous reputation is likewise handled through playful euphemism and narrative tact: her visitors are imagined to come for hot cakes, and the origins of her gifts are politely left unexplored. The humor often depends on incongruity, as when the supposedly holy chapel’s Virgin is cosmetically transformed into a saucy image of outdated Paris fashion. Beneath this levity lies a fatalistic awareness of disease, violence, and the fragility of life.

Characterization proceeds through anecdote, contrast, and action rather than sustained psychological analysis. Father de Smet is established by stories of travel, hunting, nursing, fighting, praying, and eating; Ninon by her movements across the continent, her lovers, her domestic artistry, her courage, and her evasive exchanges with the priest. Both figures are built around productive contradictions. He is holy yet practical, humorous, physically forceful, and indulgent; she is socially condemned yet generous, courageous, competent, and capable of reverence. Dialogue heightens these contrasts: his speech is gentle, biblical, and morally earnest, whereas hers is animated, theatrical, ironic, and resistant to easy conversion.

Structurally, the narrative begins with a panoramic account of the smallpox winter, pauses for extensive character histories, and then narrows into a sequence of shared acts during the epidemic. The approach of Easter supplies the final dramatic frame. Recurrent motifs—cleaning, nursing, food, flowers, prayer, and the preservation of life—bind the digressive middle sections to the ending. Foreshadowing appears through Ninon’s chills, rigidity, pallor, and repeated insistence on the lilies. The climax is compressed and restrained: her death is reported with little overt lament, while the final image transfers emotion into the lilies flowering on the wilderness altar.

The story’s moral mode is compassionate but not uncomplicated. It questions conventional definitions of virtue by showing that practical mercy may reside in a socially “fallen” woman, while formal holiness is most valuable when expressed through labor. The narrator repeatedly privileges courage, usefulness, endurance, and care over respectability. Christian symbolism remains central, especially through Easter, resurrection, the Virgin, absolution, and the white lilies, but it is filtered through irony and material necessity. The ending is therefore both sentimental and symbolically controlled: Ninon does not receive a conventional reform narrative, yet her final gift becomes an emblem of purity, sacrifice, and grace. Overall, the style is best described as a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century frontier romance shaped by literary realism, melodramatic pathos, authorial irony, and Christian-humanist symbolism.

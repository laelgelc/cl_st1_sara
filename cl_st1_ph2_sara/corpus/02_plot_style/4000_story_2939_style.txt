The story adopts the manner of a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century supernatural adventure tale, combining maritime crime, urban pursuit, grotesque horror, and occult fatalism. Its world is presented as ostensibly historical and factual: real San Francisco landmarks, nautical terminology, named witnesses, reported testimony, and precise movements across the city give the narrative the air of a documented local legend. Against this realistic framework, the supernatural remains technically unproven but strongly implied. The amulet may possess malign power, yet its influence is filtered through Rabaya’s claims, Freeman’s account, eyewitness reports, and the narrator’s reconstruction, preserving a calculated ambiguity.

The narrator is an intrusive, retrospective first-person compiler who is mostly absent from the action. Phrases such as “it is now known,” “my information is somewhat hazy,” and “came to my knowledge through Rabaya” imitate the language of an investigator assembling evidence after the event. This voice repeatedly distinguishes fact, rumor, inference, and hearsay, but its sources are unreliable, particularly the mystic whom the narrator says he could “hardly be persuaded to believe under oath.” The result is mock-documentary authority: the narrator sounds scrupulous and rational even while relating increasingly preposterous events. Dry skepticism and formal qualification make the final grotesque revelation more persuasive and more darkly comic.

The diction is elevated, antiquated, and often ceremonious. Words and constructions such as “contrariwise,” “albeit,” “thence,” “there went forward,” “almost beyond a peradventure,” and “worthy of his sobriquet” create a period voice. Specialized nautical, military, and geographical vocabulary adds material specificity, while exoticizing descriptions of Rabaya and the Malay reflect the racial attitudes and sensational conventions of the era. Characters are frequently defined through physiognomy: age, size, wrinkles, eyes, teeth, dress, and bodily agility are treated as outward signs of temperament or menace. The Flying Devil is therefore rendered less as a psychologically complex individual than as a compact embodiment of predatory energy, cupidity, violence, and supernatural corruption.

Sentence structure is predominantly long, balanced, and clause-heavy. The prose favors subordinate qualifications, parenthetical explanations, semicolons, and cumulative lists. These structures allow the narrator to supply background information, correct himself, rank probabilities, and postpone important conclusions. At moments of violence, however, the syntax contracts and the verbs become abrupt and forceful: the Malay “sprang,” “slipped” a blade between ribs, “bounded away,” and “sank” a knife into another body. This contrast between elaborate exposition and sudden physical action gives the murders a shocking, almost mechanical speed.

The story’s structure moves from broad exposition to an increasingly linear chase. It first establishes Rabaya, Freeman, the “Blue Crane,” the Flying Devil, and the amulet through rumor and description. Once the theft occurs, the narrative follows the fugitive through a chain of escalating incidents across San Francisco: murder on Telegraph Hill, robbery on the waterfront, confrontation with police, penetration of the military grounds, escape by boat, and apparent destruction under cannon fire. Geographic markers function like coordinates in a pursuit map, creating momentum and lending credibility to improbable feats. Short transitional statements—most notably “It was about this time that Captain Freeman cast off and started out to sea”—synchronize separate plot lines and prepare their eventual convergence.

Pacing depends on accumulation and escalation. Each apparent escape generates a new and more extreme danger, while the Flying Devil’s violence becomes progressively reckless. The amulet does not simply protect him; it seems to magnify his confidence, perversity, and self-destruction. The sequence has the logic of a fatalistic chain reaction, with coincidence repeatedly masquerading as supernatural design. Even incidental events acquire grotesque consequences, as when the first victim rolls through an opening in the parapet and crashes through a cottage window. Such episodes blend horror with macabre slapstick, maintaining a tone that is sensational yet controlled.

Dialogue is sparse and functional. Most characters are described or reported rather than dramatized through extended conversation. The brief exchange at the military gate uses imperfect English and misunderstanding to delay recognition for a few seconds before another abrupt murder. Elsewhere, speech is paraphrased by the narrator, reinforcing the sense that the story is an archival reconstruction rather than an immediate scene-by-scene account.

The tone combines urbane irony, sensational excitement, and deadpan morbidity. The narrator makes sardonic generalizations—such as the observation about the “natural affinity existing between idleness and lying”—and treats extraordinary claims with a pose of measured reasonableness. Euphemistic or formal phrases such as “disposing of the dog” and “an open jugular” coolly distance the prose from brutality. This restraint intensifies the grotesque imagery rather than softening it. The climactic “shower” of shredded flesh, bone, and cloth is described with quasi-forensic precision, while the narrator avoids overt emotional commentary.

The ending is built as a three-part evidentiary proof. The unexplained recoil of the sunset gun, the fall of organic fragments upon the ship, and the recovery of the powder-scorched amulet collectively invite the reader to infer that the wounded fugitive crawled into the cannon and was fired across the channel. The narrator never states this directly. Instead, he presents numbered “circumstances” and leaves the horrific mechanism to be assembled by the reader. This delayed inference is the story’s central stylistic coup: bureaucratic, testimonial language frames an absurdly violent supernatural coincidence, producing horror through understatement. The final sentence, noting simply that the Flying Devil was never seen again, closes the tale with the concise finality of a case file.

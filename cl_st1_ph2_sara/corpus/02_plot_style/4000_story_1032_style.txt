The story adopts the form of a Victorian autobiographical sketch, blending memoir, comic essay, and social observation. It is narrated retrospectively in the first-person plural, with “we” functioning both as the adult recollecting a private childhood and as the representative voice of the former pupils. This collective perspective gives the account a conspiratorial, communal quality while allowing the narrator to move between childish belief and adult judgment. Memory is openly presented as partial and unreliable: people survive as names, gestures, clothes, rumours, or physical peculiarities rather than as complete personalities. Repeated qualifications such as “we believe,” “it was whispered,” and “we have no idea now” preserve that uncertainty and turn imperfect recollection into a source of humour.

The tone combines nostalgia, satire, affection, and melancholy. School life is recalled warmly because it belongs to childhood, but the institution itself is exposed as shabby, mercenary, arbitrary, and often brutal. The narrator reproduces the pupils’ extravagant assumptions with mock seriousness, treating gossip about pirates, viscounts, concealed daggers, and secret fortunes as though it were historical evidence. Adult irony continually surrounds the child’s viewpoint without entirely extinguishing it. Comic passages often contain an undertone of cruelty or sadness, especially in the portraits of overworked teachers, neglected boys, and corporal punishment. The closing movement becomes elegiac: the railway’s destruction of the school makes physical change a figure for the erasure of memory, though the sentiment is checked by the sober admission that the school was hardly worth preserving.

The prose is expansive, rhythmic, and syntactically elaborate. Long periodic or cumulative sentences gather clauses, parenthetical comments, lists, corrections, and afterthoughts, mimicking the associative movement of recollection. Semicolons, dashes, colons, and parentheses regulate these digressions and allow the narrator to shift rapidly between description, anecdote, speculation, and commentary. Short statements are then used for comic impact or emotional emphasis, as in the repeated “Poor fellow!” Parallel constructions and anaphora are prominent, particularly in catalogues of habitual behaviour. Repetition—especially “always,” “we remember,” and “there was”—creates both oral momentum and the sense that memory has fixed each figure in a single permanent pose.

The diction mixes formal, Latinate narration with colloquial school language. Elevated phrases such as “principal solace of his existence” or “susceptible of much improvement” comically inflate trivial incidents and petty misconduct. Capitalisation gives ordinary people and institutions mock-ceremonial importance: “Our School,” “Chief,” “Establishment,” and “Railway” become almost allegorical powers. Children’s slang, rumours, quoted nicknames, and financial expressions coexist with literary allusions and bureaucratic phrasing. This contrast between grand language and mean realities is central to the satire.

Characterisation is caricatural but highly concrete. Individuals are built from a few exaggerated visual or behavioural details: a curled tail, a brown bonnet, a bloated ruler, onions in the ears, a broken trombone, a handleless umbrella, or a knife fitted out like a toolbox. Many figures are introduced through strings of participles and habitual actions, so they seem perpetually engaged in their defining activity. Similes and metaphors are vivid, eccentric, and often grotesque: the mutilated building resembles a flat-iron standing on end; the school becomes a mill grinding the life out of a teacher; Phil resembles a shipwrecked carpenter marooned on an island. Objects frequently carry as much personality as people, and physical detail becomes a means of moral revelation.

Humour arises through incongruity, hyperbole, euphemism, bathos, and deadpan inference. Violence is described in mock-administrative language; childish fantasies are pursued to absurdly logical conclusions; and solemn claims are punctured by anticlimactic qualifications. The narrator often states an obviously fanciful deduction as though it were perfectly reasonable, such as deriving a dog’s French origin from an association with a fiddle. Parenthetical interruptions sharpen this effect by supplying delayed corrections or dry adult judgments. The story also uses comic accumulation, allowing a portrait or rumour to become increasingly elaborate until it develops into a miniature narrative of its own.

The structure is episodic rather than plot-driven. It begins with the present-day discovery that the railway has destroyed the school, then moves backward through fragmentary memories of an earlier preparatory school before settling into an extended gallery of pupils, masters, servants, customs, pets, currencies, punishments, and legends. Transitions follow association rather than chronology. Recurring formulas introduce fresh subjects, making the piece resemble a spoken reminiscence or procession of comic portraits. The opening image of destruction returns at the end, providing a circular frame and converting the loose collection of anecdotes into a meditation on time and disappearance.

Dialogue is sparse but sharply selected. Brief quotations preserve characteristic moments of authority, fear, boasting, or school gossip. Exchanges such as the Chief’s rebuke to the ill Latin master expose institutional callousness more efficiently than direct explanation. Elsewhere, reported speech is more important than conversation: what “was whispered,” “was rumoured,” or “was understood” reveals the imaginative social world created by the boys.

The dominant style is therefore comic-reminiscent: richly descriptive, digressive, theatrical, and socially critical. Its emotional power depends on a double vision in which childhood absurdities are reconstructed from within while being interpreted from the distance of adulthood. Beneath the caricature lies sympathy for the vulnerable and contempt for pretension, greed, ignorance, and misused authority. The final effect is affectionate without being sentimental, nostalgic without idealising the past, and mournful without denying the possibility of progress.

The story uses a lucid, restrained realist style enriched by lyrical descriptions of landscape and weather. Its prose is formal and gently old-fashioned, with terms such as “mendicants,” “incumbent,” and “wherein,” yet it remains accessible. Rural details—the thatched shed, slimy pond, bamboo thickets, monsoon rain, earthen vessels, cooking fires, and river travel—create a vivid sense of place. Nature is frequently personified or used symbolically: the tired earth seems to breathe, a bird voices loneliness, the rain-swollen river becomes a stream of tears, and the final journey turns the surrounding landscape into an image of universal grief.

The narration is third-person omniscient, moving freely between the postmaster’s thoughts, Ratan’s feelings, and broader reflections on human nature. The narrator maintains a degree of emotional distance while occasionally intervening with commentary, irony, or exclamation. Phrases such as “God knows,” “inscrutable are the ways of a woman’s heart,” and “Alas for our foolish human nature!” give the narrative the quality of an intimate oral telling and widen the particular events into general observations about attachment, self-deception, and loss. The narrator is compassionate toward both characters but subtly exposes the postmaster’s self-absorption: his loneliness appears poetic to him, whereas Ratan’s devotion has practical and lasting consequences.

Sentence structure varies between plain declarative narration and long, rhythmic, carefully balanced sentences. Extended periodic constructions often accumulate sensory details before arriving at the central action, especially in descriptions of evening, rain, or departure. Repetition reinforces emotional patterns: Ratan repeatedly waits for the postmaster’s call, asks whether he has called her, and returns to her lessons, so ordinary habits gradually acquire emotional significance. Parallel clauses and enumerations create a measured cadence, while occasional dashes and exclamations heighten moments of reflection or sorrow.

Dialogue is simple, sparse, and socially revealing. Ratan’s recurring “Dada” conveys affection and a familial intimacy that exceeds the formal relationship between servant and master. The postmaster’s speech is casual and often unreflective, making his decisive announcements—“I am going home” and “I am not coming back”—especially stark. His laughter at Ratan’s request to accompany him is not presented as deliberate cruelty; rather, its emotional violence arises from his failure to understand what their relationship means to her. This contrast between benign intention and painful effect is central to the story’s understated pathos.

Characterization develops through domestic routines rather than extensive physical description. Shared meals, evening conversations, literacy lessons, nursing, and preparations for departure reveal the growing bond between the two characters. Ratan’s attachment is shown through action: she waits, studies, tends the sick man, draws his bath before sunrise, rejects his money, and remains near the abandoned post office. The postmaster is portrayed through his homesickness, idle poetic impulses, illness, and desire to escape. He accepts Ratan’s care as a substitute for family but does not recognize that she has come to regard him as family. This asymmetry gives the narrative its emotional force.

The pacing is unhurried and episodic. Seasonal transitions and recurring routines compress stretches of time, while decisive scenes—illness, the rejected transfer, the farewell conversation, and the boat journey—are slowed through dialogue, silence, and concrete detail. Small objects and sounds carry emotional weight: the dim lamp, dripping roof, dog-eared book, bathwater, carpet bag, umbrella, and tin trunk make the separation tangible without overt melodrama.

The tone blends tenderness, melancholy, quiet irony, and philosophical reflection. Sentiment is present but usually controlled by plain action and observational detail. The ending deliberately contrasts the postmaster’s consoling abstractions about universal meetings and partings with Ratan’s immediate, inconsolable grief. His philosophy allows him to continue his journey; she has no such protection and remains trapped in hope. The style therefore moves from intimate realism toward a broader meditation on abandonment, while preserving the sharper truth that suffering is unequal: one person turns loss into reflection, while another must live inside it.

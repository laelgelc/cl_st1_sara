Blenkinthrope, frustrated by the dullness of his life and others’ indifference to his mundane anecdotes, is advised by his friend Gorworth to invent sensational stories. He initially resists but soon tells his fellow commuters that a snake mesmerized and killed six of his pullets, while a half-blind seventh bird escaped the snake’s gaze and pecked it to death. The fabricated tale earns him attention, publicity, and social esteem.

When another commuter’s family incident eclipses his fame, Blenkinthrope invents increasingly extravagant stories. His companions enjoy them but come to regard him as a habitual liar and encourage him chiefly to test their credulity.

Eventually, Blenkinthrope’s wife completes a supposedly fatal card game associated with the deaths of her mother and great-aunt, then immediately dies herself. Though grief-stricken, he realizes that a genuinely sensational event has finally happened to him and tries to publicize it. His reputation as a liar causes friends and editors to dismiss the story as another fabrication, and her death receives only a bland notice attributing it to heart failure. Discredited and isolated, Blenkinthrope abandons his former audience and returns to telling strangers about trivial matters such as his canary and beetroot.

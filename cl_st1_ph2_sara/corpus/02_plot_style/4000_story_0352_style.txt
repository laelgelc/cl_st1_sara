The story adopts a mid-nineteenth-century sentimental and moral-didactic style, combining domestic melodrama with an optimistic tale of emigration. Its emotional register is earnest and elevated: poverty, separation, illness, loyalty and reunion are presented without irony, and the narrative repeatedly invites sympathy for the suffering couple. The tone moves from despondency and foreboding through anxiety and pathos to reassurance and moral affirmation. America functions conventionally as a “land of promise,” both a practical source of opportunity and a symbolic setting for renewal.

The narration is third-person omniscient, with ready access to the feelings, thoughts and motives of both Edward and Agnes. The narrator explains emotional states directly—grief, suspense, humiliation, fear and hope—rather than leaving them to implication. Characterisation is correspondingly idealised and morally legible. Edward is proud, independent and loving but misguided in his wish to suffer alone; Agnes is gentle, steadfast, resourceful and self-sacrificing. Secondary figures are lightly drawn and principally serve the plot, offering obstruction, information, care or assistance.

Dialogue occupies much of the story and carries both exposition and moral argument. The characters speak in polished, formal, emotionally explicit sentences, even during moments of crisis. Exclamations, repeated names, rhetorical questions and emphatic repetitions heighten feeling: “Agnes! Agnes!”, “Alone!”, and “It cannot—it must not be!” Archaic or biblical forms such as “thee,” “thou,” “thy,” and “Where thou goest I will go” elevate marital devotion into a sacred vow. Speech tags often specify vocal or emotional quality—“painfully desponding,” “with enthusiasm,” “in surprise”—ensuring that the intended mood is unmistakable.

The diction is formal, decorous and strongly evaluative. Typical phrasing includes “heart-crushing,” “fearful presentiment,” “tender young creature,” “salutary rather than injurious,” and “eager suspense.” Abstract nouns such as independence, humiliation, employment, privation, extremity and resolution give the prose a moral and social seriousness. Affectionate epithets—“gentle girl,” “young wife,” “dear Agnes,” “good angel”—reinforce the idealisation of Agnes. Euphemistic expressions such as “sleeping the sleep of death” soften harsh realities while preserving emotional intensity.

Sentence structure alternates between relatively elaborate narrative periods and short, dramatic utterances. Longer sentences frequently use balanced clauses, semicolons and cumulative qualifications to explain circumstances or trace changes in feeling. Short sentences and fragments punctuate decisive moments: “America!”, “Yes,” and “None.” Dashes mark interruption, hesitation, revelation and emotional reversal, while exclamation marks are used liberally. Inverted or old-fashioned constructions—“Long, tender, and hopeful was the letter” and “Sadly was her health beginning to suffer”—contribute to the period flavour.

The plot is linear and tightly causal, though it withholds selected information to create suspense. It begins with economic distress, introduces emigration as a possible solution, separates the couple, places Edward in mortal danger, and engineers a near-missed recognition at the hospital before delivering the reunion. Retrospective exposition then explains how Agnes reached America first. Coincidence is not treated as implausible but as an acceptable instrument of providential or moral design. The final scene resolves both the physical danger and the employment problem, allowing the story to close on a clearly articulated lesson.

Pacing varies according to emotional importance. Weeks of travel and waiting are compressed into summary, whereas intimate confrontations and the hospital recognition unfold slowly through dialogue, pauses, glances and bodily gestures. Recurrent physical signs—pale cheeks, sunken eyes, quivering frames, closed eyes, flushes and embraces—externalise inward emotion. Maritime space also carries symbolic weight: the ocean represents danger and separation, while crossing it demonstrates Agnes’s fidelity and courage.

The dominant motifs are companionship, marital duty, pride, dependence and the limits of solitary self-reliance. Edward’s “native independence” initially appears virtuous but becomes a flaw when it leads him to exclude his wife from hardship. Agnes corrects this error through action as well as speech. Her concluding insistence that a husband cannot “move safely on” unless his wife moves beside him states the governing moral directly. The closing contrast between “angel” and “wife” encapsulates the story’s domestic ideal: Agnes rejects exalted praise in favour of the ordinary marital role, which the narrative nevertheless presents as heroic.

Overall, the style is emotionally demonstrative, rhetorically emphatic, morally transparent and conventionally redemptive. It relies on formal dialogue, omniscient explanation, idealised characters, providential coincidence and escalating scenes of distress followed by consoling resolution. Its central aesthetic is not psychological ambiguity but the vindication of steadfast love, practical devotion and shared endurance.

The story adopts an antiquarian, mock-historical style that blends local legend, comic satire, maritime adventure, and Gothic supernaturalism. It begins with a pseudo-editorial letter that presents the tale as an imperfect piece of folklore submitted to a periodical. This frame creates an air of documentary authenticity while simultaneously parodying scholarly research, civic history, and the self-importance of local chroniclers. References to supposed archives, public officials, and historical collections give the narrative a playfully learned surface rather than strict realism.

The narrator is omniscient, sociable, and conspicuously present. He speaks as a genial storyteller addressing an educated audience, freely offering judgments, digressions, ironic qualifications, and proverbial observations. Formulae such as “it may be wondered,” “be that as it may,” and “I come now to the extraordinary fact” imitate oral storytelling and guide the reader through the legend. The narrator repeatedly balances assertion with rumor: events are attributed to neighbors, fishermen, charitable opinions, village gossip, or collective conjecture. This allows the supernatural to remain half-believed even when it becomes dramatically visible.

The diction is ornate, archaizing, and deliberately picturesque. Dutch names, regional place-names, nautical terminology, Latin-derived abstractions, colloquial idioms, and old-fashioned words such as “yclept,” “varlet,” “burghers,” “potations,” and “will-ye, nil-ye” produce a stylized colonial atmosphere. Characters are often introduced through humorous strings of epithets: Vanderscamp is a “rough, burly, bully ruffian,” while his companions are “roistering, swaggering varlets.” Such verbal accumulation is central to the prose’s exuberance. Periodic use of Dutch expressions and songs adds local color, while names such as Pluto and the Wild Goose carry symbolic or comic implications.

Sentences tend to be long, cumulative, and rhythmically varied. Semicolons coordinate successive actions or descriptive details, and lists expand scenes into comic catalogues. Parallel clauses and repeated conjunctions create a rolling, anecdotal cadence, particularly in accounts of pirate revelry, village alarm, and stormy weather. Short sentences are reserved for transitions, revelations, and emphatic turns, giving the climactic supernatural episodes greater force. Exclamation, rhetorical questioning, and direct address preserve the energy of a tale performed aloud.

The dominant tone is humorous and ironic, though it gradually darkens. Communipaw’s inhabitants are portrayed with affectionate condescension as placid, slow-witted, pipe-smoking Dutch burghers. Their domestic orderliness is repeatedly contrasted with Vanderscamp’s violent disorder and the pirates’ grotesque sociability. Much of the comedy comes from inflated language applied to provincial affairs, from euphemistic descriptions of crime, and from incongruous juxtapositions: pirates are called “merchants,” smuggling becomes a response to the “oppressions of government,” and corpses hanging in chains become objects of civic complacency. Even the macabre retains a streak of black comedy, most notably when the dead pirates accept Vanderscamp’s drunken invitation and resume their drinking song.

Description is strongly visual and theatrical. The ruined tavern, gallows-like signpost, exotic pirate furnishings, swinging bodies, blue brimstone light, and storm-lashed bay are presented as striking stage pictures. Weather functions as Gothic foreshadowing: lurid clouds, distant thunder, shrill wind, lightning, and equinoctial storms signal the approach of supernatural retribution. Sound is equally important, especially creaking chains, drinking songs, pistol shots, curses, shrieks, and revelry mingling with the tempest. The setting is geographically specific but imaginatively transformed into a legendary borderland where tavern, harbor, pirate anchorage, and gibbet island form a compact moral landscape.

Characterization is broad, caricatural, and behavior-driven. Teunis Van Gieson embodies indulgent good nature; the burghers represent cautious communal respectability; Vanderscamp develops from mischievous boy to swaggering pirate and furtive smuggler; and his wife is reduced to the comic type of the domineering shrew. Pluto is constructed as an enigmatic Gothic familiar associated with storms, water, secrecy, and the infernal. His origins and powers are never definitively explained, allowing him to function at once as servant, accomplice, omen, suspected demon, and eventual victim. His portrayal also relies heavily on dehumanizing racial stereotypes and period-specific racist language, repeatedly comparing him to an animal, goblin, sea creature, or devil. This prejudice is embedded both in the villagers’ perspective and in the narrative’s comic-Gothic characterization.

Dialogue is sparse but vivid. It chiefly appears at moments of confrontation or supernatural crisis and is shaped by colloquial exclamations, threats, nautical bravado, curses, and drunken repetition. Vanderscamp’s exchange with Pluto beside the gibbet establishes his fatal arrogance, while the conversation with his wife delays and sharpens the revelation upstairs. The dead pirates’ rhyming song turns the climax into a grotesque parody of convivial tavern fellowship.

The plot is chronological and episodic, covering many years before narrowing sharply around the central ghost story. Early sections establish the tavern, the village, Vanderscamp’s childhood, Pluto’s mystery, and the rise of the pirate household. Summary dominates this expansive background. As the tale approaches Vanderscamp’s return past Gibbet Island, narrative time slows, dialogue increases, and sensory detail intensifies. The aftermath then broadens again into rumor, communal fear, murder, disappearance, and a final folkloric explanation. Repetition gives the structure cohesion: periods of tranquillity alternate with disruptive returns, the tavern repeatedly opens and closes, and invitations to dangerous guests culminate in fatal visitation.

The principal stylistic contrasts are order versus riot, domestic respectability versus maritime lawlessness, historical fact versus folklore, and comic sociability versus death. Hospitality becomes a dark organizing motif: the tavern first hosts burghers, then pirates, then smugglers, and finally corpses. Drinking and fellowship are progressively transformed into sacrilege and supernatural punishment. The ending preserves the logic of oral legend by converting uncertain evidence into a memorable cautionary formula: Pluto has ventured once too often to invite “Guests from Gibbet-Island.”

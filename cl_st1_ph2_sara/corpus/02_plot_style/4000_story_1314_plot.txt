During the famine year of 1892, a wandering former student travels along the Caucasian coast behind a group of impoverished migrants seeking road work. Among them is a pregnant widow from Orlov whose husband recently died. Too ashamed to give birth in front of her companions, she leaves them and goes into labor alone beside the road.

The narrator hears her moans and finds her suffering in the bushes. Despite her fear, insults, and attempts to repel him, he draws on his limited experience to deliver her baby. He severs and binds the umbilical cord, washes the vigorous newborn in the sea, returns him to his mother, and helps her nurse him. He then prepares tea, buries the afterbirth according to her wishes, and allows her to believe that he is a medical student.

Although weak and uncertain about her child’s prospects, the woman insists on continuing toward Otchenchiri, trusting in the protection of the Virgin Mary. The narrator carries the sleeping infant while supporting the exhausted mother. As they walk through the beautiful landscape, her suffering gives way to awe, gratitude, and an overwhelming love for her son, whom she hopes to nurture into strength as they journey onward together.

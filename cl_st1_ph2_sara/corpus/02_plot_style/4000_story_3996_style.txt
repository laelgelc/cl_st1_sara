The story uses a polished comic-realistic style shaped by an urbane, conspicuously present narrator. The narration is third-person and generally follows Mary’s perceptions, but it frequently widens into omniscient commentary, aphorism, and direct address. The narrator knows more than Mary, gently exposes her misreadings, and invites the reader to enjoy the discrepancy between appearance and reality. Phrases such as “But who cares what carpers think?” create a conversational complicity with the audience, while broad observations about work, ambition, and human conduct give the comedy an essayistic edge.

The tone is light, ironic, and affectionate rather than cruel. Mary’s pride, romantic naïveté, and shifting judgments are treated with amused sympathy; Joe’s confidence and theatrical humour are admired; Eddy’s charm is gradually revealed as calculation. Much of the humour comes from ironic reversal. Mary expects the formerly awkward Joe to remain her worshipful “serf,” only to find him socially and professionally transformed, while the apparently polished Eddy proves morally crooked. The final romantic reversal—“New York has changed me too”—completes this pattern. Even moments of emotional seriousness are quickly relieved by a comic tag, as in the waiter’s interruption and the discovery that Eddy has left Joe to pay the bill.

The diction combines clear, conventional narrative prose with vivid colloquial phrasing, mock-formality, and extravagant comic comparison. New York is personified as a magician that changes people, raises its eyebrows, and says “Huh!” Crowds become a “giant fowl-run,” while a hectic office day condenses “a music-hall knock-about sketch, an earthquake, a football scrummage, and the rush-hour on the Tube.” Such images are energetic, concrete, and deliberately excessive. Elevated expressions—“ignominious retreat,” “the revolt of the serf,” “brother-in-arms”—are applied to ordinary social or office situations, producing mock-heroic humour. Character description often relies on compressed caricature: young Joe is “all hands, feet, and shyness,” and the office-boy’s dignity is captured through the fantasy that he is perpetually about to lunch with a duke.

Sentence structure is varied and rhythmically controlled. Long cumulative sentences build bustle, confusion, or psychological pressure, especially in descriptions of New York and the brokerage office. These are balanced by short emphatic statements such as “It changes,” “She was sorry for him,” and “He had force.” Parallelism and repetition are prominent: “What had seemed romantic became merely frightening. What had been exciting…”; “Where others trotted, he sprang. Where others raised their voices, he yelled.” This patterned syntax gives the narration snap and momentum while sharpening contrasts. Parenthetical qualifications and delayed punch lines help sustain the narrator’s amused, improvisatory manner.

Dialogue is brisk, economical, and central to characterization. Joe speaks in quips, evasive parables, comic understatements, and confident reversals; his verbal ease demonstrates how thoroughly city life has changed his outward manner. Eddy’s early speech is smooth but vague, with repeated “Yes, yes” responses that reveal inattentiveness beneath his charm. When his scheme emerges, his dialogue becomes fragmented, repetitive, and defensive, exposing the strain behind the performance. Mary speaks more directly and emotionally, using blunt labels such as “pig” and “eavesdropper”; her impulsiveness makes her both sympathetic and vulnerable. Dialogue often functions as a contest in which moral and emotional realities emerge through verbal rhythm rather than explicit explanation.

The pacing alternates between summary and sharply dramatized scenes. The opening rapidly establishes the city, Mary’s background, and her altered circumstances; subsequent office, restaurant, and street encounters unfold through dialogue and comic action. Asterism-separated sections mark shifts in time and stages in Mary’s reassessment of Joe and Eddy. The plot is tightly constructed around planted information and delayed revelation: Joe’s warning about “Pearl Street,” the former secretary’s abrupt departure, Eddy’s conspicuous interest in Mary, and the switched letters all prepare the exposure of his dishonesty. Suspense remains light, serving romantic comedy rather than danger.

Characterization depends heavily on contrast, misinterpretation, and changing perspective. Mary initially confuses surface polish with goodness and confident humour with callousness. Joe’s competence in moments of professional crisis gradually revises her judgment, while Eddy’s elegant appearance and musical voice become increasingly hollow. Physical description is selective and functional: clothes, posture, eyes, hands, and vocal quality indicate social confidence or moral unease. New York itself operates almost as a character and as the story’s governing metaphor. It transforms awkwardness into authority, homesickness into resilience, charm into opportunism, and old indifference into love, while also revealing traits that were latent rather than wholly creating them.

The emotional mode is sentimental but disciplined by wit. The story permits sincere nostalgia, loneliness, professional admiration, betrayal, and romantic recognition, yet avoids prolonged pathos by puncturing solemnity with jokes. Its moral scheme is clear without becoming heavily didactic: competence, loyalty, and genuine feeling prevail over glamour, manipulation, and self-interest. Overall, the style is that of an early twentieth-century urban romantic comedy—fast-moving, narrator-driven, rich in comic simile and personification, structurally neat, and founded on the pleasurable exposure of the gap between what characters seem to be and what they are.

The story uses a first-person, stream-of-consciousness mode in which a trivial visual stimulus—the unexplained mark on the wall—becomes the anchor for an extended sequence of memories, fantasies, arguments and self-questioning. External action is deliberately sparse: the narrator remains seated after tea, looks at the mark, and resists getting up to inspect it. Most of the narrative therefore occurs within the mind. Thought is represented as mobile, associative and only partly controllable, passing from the room and its former occupants to lost possessions, death, Shakespeare, vanity, social conventions, antiquarianism, knowledge, nature and war. The mark periodically returns as a refrain, giving the apparent digressions a loose circular structure and preventing the monologue from dissolving altogether.

The narrative voice is intimate, speculative and self-aware. It frequently qualifies its own assertions with phrases such as “perhaps,” “I think,” “I suppose,” “I daresay” and “I cannot be sure,” making uncertainty a defining feature of both voice and worldview. The narrator watches her own mind at work, noticing its vanity, evasions and desire for flattering fantasies. This produces a double perspective: she is simultaneously absorbed in thought and amused by the mechanisms of thinking. Direct addresses and inclusive pronouns—“one,” “we,” and occasional rhetorical questions—expand private reflection into a meditation on shared human habits without turning it into a formal argument.

The syntax imitates the movement of consciousness. Long, loosely accumulating sentences use semicolons, dashes, parentheses, repetitions and ellipses to connect ideas by association rather than strict logic. Short exclamations and abrupt corrections interrupt these flowing passages: “No, no, nothing is proved, nothing is known.” Questions often generate further questions instead of answers. Sentences may begin analytically and then drift into image, anecdote or fantasy, while incomplete constructions and sudden pivots preserve the sense of thought unfolding in real time. Repeated phrases such as “I like to think,” “worshipping,” “tumbling,” and “if it were not” create rhythmic momentum. The text also moves between expansive lyrical sentences and clipped dialogue, with the final exchange sharply puncturing the meditative flow.

The diction combines ordinary domestic vocabulary with philosophical, historical and poetic language. Cigarettes, tea, dust, tablecloths, furniture and newspapers coexist with asphodel meadows, mortality, civilization, epistemology and social hierarchy. Proper names and institutional references—Shakespeare, Troy, Nelson, Whitaker’s Almanack and the Table of Precedency—lend cultural specificity, but they are often treated ironically. Abstract ideas are repeatedly made concrete through objects, while familiar objects become strange under sustained attention. The result is a style that moves easily between the colloquial and the elevated, often deflating solemn speculation with homely detail.

Imagery is dense, sensuous and metamorphic. Colours recur vividly: yellow firelight, crimson flags, black rock, pale blue canisters, purple and red light, and indistinct pinks and blues. Thought itself is rendered through physical comparisons: ideas swarm like ants carrying straw, life resembles being blown through the Tube, people tumble like parcels down a postal chute, and thinking moves through the world as a fish moves through water. Natural imagery—trees, flowers, rivers, insects, fish and grass—offers an organic alternative to rigid human classifications. Images often enlarge unexpectedly from the miniature to the cosmic, so that a tiny mark can lead to burial mounds, the afterlife or the tumbling earth.

The tone is fluid, blending reverie, curiosity, melancholy, satire and quiet rebellion. Much of the humour comes from disproportion: elaborate hypotheses gather around an object that finally proves to be a snail, while learned institutions are reduced to absurd lists and museum curiosities. Social satire targets hierarchy, masculine authority, professional expertise and inherited standards of respectability. The narrator is especially suspicious of “generalizations,” rules and official systems that claim to stabilize reality. Yet the critique remains playful and exploratory rather than programmatic, since every position is liable to be revised by the next movement of thought.

A central stylistic tension exists between fact and imagination. The narrator desires the solidity of the external world but also resists the act of verification because uncertainty permits thought to remain free. The mark is alternately imagined as a nail hole, leaf, protruding object, tomb-like mound and crack, and each possibility opens another mental path. Reality is both comforting and restrictive: definite objects provide refuge from disturbing abstractions, but factual knowledge can terminate imaginative possibility. This tension gives the story its distinctive rhythm of expansion and return—consciousness travels outward from the mark, becomes entangled in unsettling ideas, and retreats to the mark again.

The structure is recursive rather than conventionally plotted. Successive associative episodes are linked by motifs rather than causal events: disappearance, instability, reflection, social order, nature and solidity. The repeated postponement of physical action creates suspense from an almost actionless premise. The eventual revelation is intentionally mundane and comic, but it does more than supply a punch line. The snail embodies the story’s preference for the overlooked, material and nonhuman, while demonstrating how little the imaginative journey depended upon the object’s actual identity.

Overall, the style is modernist, inward and essayistic, with lyrical description embedded in a dramatic representation of consciousness. It replaces conventional incident with the textures of perception and association, treats thought as fragmented yet patterned, and uses self-interruption to expose the instability of identity and knowledge. Its characteristic effect comes from holding opposites together: domestic intimacy and philosophical scope, sensuous precision and radical uncertainty, meditative beauty and comic deflation, private reverie and social critique.

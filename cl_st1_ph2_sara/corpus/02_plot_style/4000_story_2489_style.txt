The story adopts a layered pseudo-historical form, framing an orally transmitted workers’ tale between two scholarly editor’s notes from a remote future. The notes use formal, documentary language, precise dates, institutional references, and claims based on “internal evidences,” statute books, and court records. This apparatus gives the fiction the appearance of an authenticated historical fragment while creating dramatic irony: one future age coolly studies the brutal social order that an earlier future regarded as permanent. The contrast between archival detachment and the tale’s emotional immediacy is central to the style.

Within the frame, the dominant voice is that of a working-class storyteller addressing a live audience. His repeated vocative—“my brothers” or simply “brothers”—produces the cadence of a sermon, political speech, and clandestine lesson. He frequently speaks directly to his listeners, asks rhetorical questions, anticipates their ignorance, and relates unfamiliar events to their own experience: “You know yourselves what form such punishment takes.” The narration therefore feels communal rather than private. Its purpose is not merely to entertain but to educate, persuade, preserve suppressed history, and recruit the audience into literacy and resistance.

The diction combines plain, accessible storytelling with openly polemical language. Masters and institutions receive morally loaded descriptions such as “hell-hound,” “accursed,” “slave pens,” and “terrible industrial oligarchy,” while rebels are called “heroes” and “comrades.” Social relations are reduced to the stark vocabulary of “masters” and “slaves,” giving the story the clarity of a political parable. Even where the imagined system has its own laws and administrative arrangements, the language strips away their legitimacy and identifies industrial labour as slavery. Occasional archaic or elevated constructions—“wherein,” “thereafter,” “save to,” “had it not been,” and “whereon”—lend the oral tale a legendary, inherited quality.

The syntax is generally straightforward, cumulative, and highly repetitive. Important facts are reiterated in slightly altered forms, as in the changing names “Bill Vange,” “Sly Vange,” and “Bloody Vange,” or the insistence that the storyteller has read the truth “with my own eyes.” Repetition serves an oral mnemonic function while also intensifying the political argument. Short declarative sentences—“This is fact. I have read it with my own eyes in the records”—alternate with longer historical summaries and accumulative lists. Parenthetical interruptions, dashes, ellipses, and direct asides imitate the pauses and emphases of live speech.

The rhetoric is didactic and propagandistic in a deliberate rather than concealed way. The narrator repeatedly links literacy with knowledge, knowledge with strength, and strength with liberation. Historical exposition is shaped into moral instruction, and episodes are interpreted for the audience as they are told. Irony is often blunt and accusatory: workers possess rights “under the law,” but have no access to the law; Vanderwater’s minor headache is contrasted with Tom Dixon’s arm being torn from its socket; and a luxurious bedroom is called Paradise only to remind the audience that it was created by thousands of exploited lives. The storyteller’s questions—especially why one slave or the son of a slave should become master over others—invite a collective answer while making the desired conclusion unmistakable.

Structurally, the narrative delays its central incident through a long genealogy of the Vanderwater family. This digression establishes the ruling dynasty’s origins in betrayal and exposes class identity as historically manufactured rather than natural. The narrator then explicitly circles back—“I come back to the beginning of my tale”—and narrows the focus from centuries of oppression to one severed arm. The arm becomes the story’s governing symbol: an injured worker’s body is transformed into evidence, petition, accusation, and political spectacle. Its placement among blood-red strawberries creates the most vivid visual contrast in the story, opposing aristocratic delicacy and luxury to mutilated labour.

Violence is described plainly and concretely rather than through elaborate sensationalism. Beatings, executions, mutilations, and threatened punishments are presented as routine mechanisms of the social system. This matter-of-fact delivery makes the brutality feel institutional rather than exceptional. At the same time, the climactic tableau is strongly theatrical: the white arm against the red fruit, the petition clutched in dead fingers, the bleeding messenger surrounded by guards, and the master reading in silence. Colour contrast and physical stillness give the scene an emblematic, almost allegorical force.

Characterisation is economical and class-oriented. Individuals are defined chiefly by their place within the system and by the moral choices they make. The literate slave is never named, which allows him to represent an entire suppressed class, yet his courage, friendship, fatalism, and restrained speech make him personally affecting. Roger Vanderwater is neither rendered wholly benevolent nor allowed to escape responsibility. His eventual punishment of the overseers supplies temporary justice, but the narrator immediately qualifies it: the fund is managed properly only “for a time.” This prevents the episode from becoming a tale of salvation by a good master and redirects attention toward systemic change.

Dialogue is sparse, compressed, and aphoristic. The dying slave’s statement, “I have been sorry all my life that I was born,” condenses a lifetime of oppression into a single devastating reply. Such exchanges function less as naturalistic conversation than as memorable oral set pieces. They are designed to be repeated, retained, and passed from teller to listener.

The tone moves among scholarly detachment, indignation, grim irony, pathos, and revolutionary hope. The editor’s notes sound authoritative and retrospective; the storyteller sounds urgent, intimate, and morally certain. The ending reveals his personal connection to the central episode and turns family memory into political testimony. It closes not with narrative resolution but with an appeal to action, making the story itself an example of the “power in the printed word.” Overall, the style blends dystopian historiography, oral folklore, socialist sermon, martyr narrative, and political fable, using repetition, direct address, documentary framing, and stark symbolic imagery to make storytelling an instrument of resistance.

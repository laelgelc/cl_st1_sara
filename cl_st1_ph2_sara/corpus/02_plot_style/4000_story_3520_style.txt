The story uses a lyrical, memory-driven prose style in which childhood experience is reconstructed through adult nostalgia. Its first-person narrator speaks from a double perspective: the adult voice is reflective, elegiac and conscious that many Christmases have blurred together, while the remembered child’s consciousness is exuberant, literal-minded and endlessly imaginative. Memory is treated not as an exact chronology but as a store of vivid fragments that the narrator can reach into and retrieve. This gives the narrative a dreamlike, associative structure rather than a tightly plotted one.

The syntax is highly rhythmic and often expansive. Long, cumulative sentences gather images, actions and comic details through repeated conjunctions, parallel clauses and catalogues. These rolling constructions imitate both the abundance of Christmas and the unstoppable flow of recollection. They are balanced by short declarative sentences and crisp dialogue, which supply comic timing and prevent the lyricism from becoming static. Repetition is central to the cadence: words, grammatical patterns and refrains such as snow, bells, uncles and presents return in slightly altered forms, creating the effect of an oral performance or prose poem.

The diction combines ordinary domestic language with extravagant poetic invention. Familiar objects are repeatedly transformed through compound adjectives, unusual verbs and surprising comparisons: snow is “wool-white” and “bell-tongued,” gaslight “bubbled like a diver,” and a gong “bombilated.” Nouns frequently become verbs, while objects, animals, weather and buildings are personified. The language is sensuous and densely textured, drawing on colour, temperature, smell, taste, sound and touch. Food, smoke, snow, wool, firelight and cold air are rendered with particular physical richness.

Figurative language is profuse and deliberately excessive. Similes and metaphors enlarge the small sea-town into an Arctic wilderness, a legendary Wales, a battlefield, a fairy-tale landscape and a private cosmology. Childhood imagination constantly overlays reality: boys become trappers and explorers, cats become jaguars or polar prey, dogs become rescue animals, and imagined hippopotamuses charge through the snow. The exaggeration is affectionate rather than deceptive; it conveys how ordinary events felt to a child. Hyperbole also supports the story’s comic mode, especially when relatives, presents and minor accidents are described with mock-heroic grandeur.

Sound is one of the prose’s strongest organising features. Alliteration, assonance, internal rhyme, onomatopoeia and consonantal clusters give the narration a musical, incantatory quality. Bells ring, gongs boom, ships hoot, fires spit, relatives rumble and children shout. Lists are often arranged for their sonic pleasure as much as for meaning. The sea-town is therefore remembered not only visually but as a chorus of domestic, religious, maritime and wintry noises. Periodic moments of silence—the muffled snow, the dark house, the still streets—heighten this acoustic richness.

The tone moves fluidly between wonder, comedy, tenderness and faint melancholy. Much of the humour comes from incongruity, antic exaggeration and deadpan understatement: snowballs are thrown into a burning room, a fireman is offered something to read, and useful presents are described as grotesque instruments of discomfort. Relatives are caricatured through sharply chosen habits and bodily details, yet the satire remains warm. Beneath the comedy lies an awareness of distance, loss and mortality. The Christmas world is “close and holy,” but it survives only through recollection; vanished aunts, dead birds, fading voices and the darkness beyond the warm house introduce an elegiac undertone.

Dialogue is concise, interruptive and naturalistic. The child listener repeatedly challenges the narrator, asks literal questions and demands that he “get back” to the interesting parts. These exchanges puncture poetic excess and dramatise the difference between adult nostalgia and contemporary childhood impatience. Children’s conversations elsewhere are similarly direct, competitive and fanciful, moving instantly from observation to invented adventure. Adult speech tends to be defined by comic practicality, complaint or eccentricity.

Structurally, the story proceeds through linked tableaux rather than conventional dramatic development. The opening establishes the instability of memory; the fire episode provides an anecdotal comic set piece; the narrative then expands into seasonal catalogues of snow, postmen, presents, food, relatives, games, streets, ghost stories, carolling and music. Transitions are associative, often triggered by a word, image or remembered ritual. The movement is broadly from public outdoor adventure toward domestic warmth and finally inward to the bedroom, prayer and sleep. This closing narrows the earlier abundance into stillness and gives the story a gentle ceremonial resolution.

Overall, the style is poetic, oral, theatrical and richly baroque. It turns communal memory into verbal music, balancing elaborate description with colloquial dialogue and balancing enchantment with comic deflation. Its defining effect is to preserve the emotional truth of childhood Christmas—not through documentary realism, but through rhythm, sensory abundance, exaggeration and imaginative transformation.

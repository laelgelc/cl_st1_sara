The story adopts the manner of a late-Victorian or Edwardian comic tale, blending mock-detective reasoning, sentimental melodrama, and a final ironic reversal. Its central stylistic principle is incongruity: the elevated language and emotional gravity normally associated with romance and tragedy are applied to laundry lists, collars, cuffs, shirt-fronts, starch, and handkerchiefs. The narrative treats these mundane objects as a coded record of an unseen life, creating humour through the disproportion between the apparent evidence and Ah-Yen’s elaborate conclusions.

The narration is framed and retrospective. An unnamed first-person narrator introduces Ah-Yen, describes the dim room behind the laundry, and then yields most of the story to his friend’s extended monologue. This embedded narration allows the reader to experience Ah-Yen’s reconstruction almost as a self-contained tragic biography before the outer narrator returns to reinterpret it. The frame also creates controlled distance: Ah-Yen speaks with complete conviction, while the narrator initially serves as a receptive audience and only at the end supplies the facts that undermine the entire account. The closing disclosure that the narrator himself was customer Fifty-Six produces a twist ending based on dramatic irony and retrospective revaluation.

The prose is formal, polished, and deliberately old-fashioned. It favours words and constructions such as “fain,” “ensued,” “hitherto,” “suffice it to say,” “I bestowed upon it,” and “I cannot look back … without a sigh.” Sentences are often long and syntactically layered, using subordinate clauses, balanced phrases, parenthetical qualifications, and semicolons. The leisurely cadence supports the atmosphere of fireside reminiscence while also sustaining the parody: trivial sartorial details are expressed with the ceremonious weight of moral history. Occasional inversions and rhetorical formulations—“Well do I remember,” “Of what my poor friend suffered … I can give you no idea”—heighten the mock-heroic quality.

The tone moves between subdued intimacy, analytical curiosity, extravagant pathos, and dry comedy. The opening establishes melancholy through the winter evening, tallow candle, dingy room, silent pipe-smoking, and portrait of “infinite sadness.” Ah-Yen’s account then steadily intensifies, treating academic examinations, courtship, rejection, madness, and suicide with mounting emotional solemnity. Yet the clues supporting this tragedy remain comically material: collar height indicates modesty, annotations on cuffs indicate examinations, wine stains indicate graduation, and declining shirt numbers indicate romantic estrangement. The story rarely signals its joke directly; instead, it maintains a grave, sincere surface, allowing deadpan overstatement to generate the humour.

The method of characterization is indirect and object-based. Fifty-Six never appears and is known entirely through his washing, so clothing functions as a system of metonymy: shirts, collars, stains, and buttons stand in for education, social ambition, moral development, love, and despair. Ah-Yen’s character emerges through the same process. His painstaking deductions reveal imagination, emotional hunger, professional pride, and a tendency to transform scraps of evidence into a consoling human drama. His attachment to the customer is described with the vocabulary of intimate friendship even though the two have never met, making him both a parody of the brilliant literary detective and a sympathetic portrait of loneliness.

The story specifically imitates analytical detective fiction. The narrator’s reference to “the recent publications of a favourite novelist” gestures toward the Sherlock Holmes tradition, while Ah-Yen’s deductions mimic the movement from minute physical traces to confident biographical conclusions. The parody lies in the weakness of the inferential chain. Some observations are plausible, but Ah-Yen repeatedly converts possibility into certainty, then builds fresh deductions upon earlier assumptions. His emotionally satisfying narrative becomes an “airy castle” whose coherence is mistaken for truth. The final explanation—red ink rather than blood, a cigar burn rather than a bullet hole, and accumulated washing rather than a dead man’s effects—exposes the danger of interpretive overreach.

Structurally, the account follows a carefully graded rise and fall. It begins with Fifty-Six’s student life, moves through graduation and legal study, rises into courtship and apparent acceptance, then descends through quarrels, abandonment, deterioration, and supposed suicide. Repeated numerical and seasonal patterns reinforce this arc. Saturdays regulate Ah-Yen’s emotional life; shirt counts rise from one or two to seven and then fall again; collars grow taller during confidence and shrink during despair; winter softens into spring during hope, while summer fades into autumn as the romance collapses. These recurring details give the invented biography an almost diagrammatic neatness, which contributes both to its persuasiveness and its comic artificiality.

Imagery is restrained in the framing passages but vivid at moments of emotional climax. Candlelight, shadow, smoke, cold pipes, and the bare back room create an atmosphere of contemplative sadness. Within Ah-Yen’s narrative, visual emphasis falls on textures and colours: spotless white shirts, gaudy neckties, blue and grey flannel, a red handkerchief, and the supposedly bloodstained breast of the final shirt. White clothing becomes associated with courtship, refinement, and hope, while coarser fabrics and darker colours mark decline. The crimson stain acts as the melodramatic culmination, only to be deflated into spilled ink.

Repetition is an important rhythmic and comic device. Phrases involving “one Saturday,” weekly bundles, shirts, cuffs, collars, and waistcoats accumulate into a catalogue that turns laundry into narrative evidence. Exclamations such as “Alas!” and declarations of anguish recur as the imagined tragedy deepens. Antithesis is also frequent: hope alternates with despair, false shirt-fronts disappear and return, seven shirts fall to two, and apparent fraud in clothing is treated as evidence of moral corruption. The result is a sustained mock-sentimental register rather than rapid joke-telling.

The ending is understated rather than explosively comic. The narrator does not confront Ah-Yen or categorically declare the truth; he offers hesitant recollections—“if I mistake not,” “I cannot feel absolutely certain”—before revealing the decisive fact of his laundry number. This qualified delivery preserves the melancholy mood even as it overturns the tragedy. The final irony is emotionally complex: Ah-Yen’s story is false, but it expresses the affection and imaginative sympathy missing from both men’s secluded lives. The narrator therefore protects the fiction, and the tale closes on a mixture of amusement, tenderness, and sadness rather than simple ridicule.

The social description reflects the period’s racial attitudes. Terms such as “celestial,” “little Eastern friend,” and broad claims about “the Eastern character” exoticize Ah-Yen and treat national identity as a set of inherited psychological traits. Although the narrator admires his intelligence and imagination, the portrayal remains paternalistic and stereotyped. At the same time, Ah-Yen’s dignified, highly articulate speech complicates the stereotype, and the text locates the central failure not in a lack of intelligence but in a universally recognizable desire to find meaning, intimacy, and narrative order in fragmentary evidence.

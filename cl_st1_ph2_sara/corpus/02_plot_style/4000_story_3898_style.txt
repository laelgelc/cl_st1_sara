The story uses an ornate, cultivated late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century literary style, combining psychological realism, aestheticism, supernatural fantasy, and social comedy. Its prose is elevated and sensuous, especially in descriptions of dying, the afterlife, art, landscape, and moments of spiritual exaltation. Long, carefully balanced sentences accumulate subordinate clauses, appositions, and parallel phrases, creating a slow, drifting rhythm suited to altered consciousness and reverie. This expansiveness is periodically broken by short statements—“Help!”, “He is here,” “No matter”—which sharpen moments of crisis or comic reversal.

The narration is in the third person but remains closely aligned with the woman’s perceptions. Physical sensations shade into visual hallucinations and metaphysical experience without a hard boundary: darkness becomes a “tepid tide,” pain resembles sheet lightning, and the afterlife gradually resolves from opaline uncertainty into mountains, river, and trees. Repetition, particularly “sinking, sinking” and “higher and higher,” gives these passages an incantatory quality. The narrative voice is sympathetic to the protagonist’s hunger for beauty and intimacy, yet maintains enough distance to expose her self-dramatisation and inconsistencies.

Imagery is painterly, tactile, and richly chromatic. Blue-black darkness, rosy marble, fiery windows, jewel-like incrustations, sapphirine space, and hyaline distances turn scenes into composed works of visual art. Similes and metaphors are elaborate and often extended: consciousness has “vanishing edges,” a woman’s nature is a house full of rooms, history becomes a mighty river, and sympathetic voices resemble answering fountains. Natural imagery—tides, stars, flowers, birds, rivers, clouds, and sunset—frequently expresses emotional or spiritual states. The language favours refined and sometimes archaic or rare diction, including words such as “torpor,” “lassitude,” “presentments,” “inexorableness,” “opaline,” “cryptal,” and “sapphirine.”

The story is densely allusive. References to Darwin, Wallace, Dante, Shakespeare, Shelley, Leonardo, Botticelli, Mantegna, Orcagna, Bellini, the Parthenon, the Decameron, and medieval Christianity establish the protagonist as aesthetically sophisticated and culturally ambitious. These allusions are not merely decorative; they dramatise her belief that true life consists in a shared responsiveness to art, history, beauty, and ineffable feeling. At the same time, their abundance contributes to the comedy. Her ideal communion with the kindred soul becomes a rapid exchange of mutually confirming references, so perfect that it begins to feel formulaic and faintly self-satisfied.

Dialogue dominates the latter part and gives the supernatural premise the form of a philosophical examination. The Spirit of Life speaks in calm, concise, almost judicial questions, while the woman answers expansively, emotionally, and often defensively. This contrast exposes the gap between her stated ideals and her deeper attachments. Her conversation with the kindred soul is lyrical, elliptical, and antiphonal, built from unfinished questions and delighted recognitions. By contrast, her memories of her husband revolve around boots, doors, dinner times, stamps, umbrellas, visiting cards, and railway novels. The juxtaposition of sublime culture with mundane domestic detail is the story’s principal comic mechanism.

The tone moves through several registers: elegiac and dreamlike during the death scene, rapturous in the vision of the afterlife, ecstatic in the account of Florentine art, lightly satirical in the discussion of marriage, and finally tenderly ironic. The irony is structural rather than cruel. The woman believes that fulfilment requires a perfectly responsive kindred soul, yet when one is provided she rejects him for the imperfect husband whose habits had irritated her. Domestic familiarity proves stronger than aesthetic compatibility. Repeated motifs—especially the husband’s creaking boots—change meaning over the course of the story: initially they represent vulgarity and confinement, but finally become the cherished signal of belonging.

The two-part structure supports this reversal. The first section presents death from within and ends with the husband walking away in the despised boots. The second expands into metaphysical possibility, then gradually contracts from eternity, art, and ideal love back to the practical intimacies of marriage. The ending mirrors the opening while transforming its central image: what she once hoped never to hear again becomes what she waits forever to hear. The final sentence is restrained and poignant, allowing the recurring comic detail to carry emotional weight without explicit commentary.

Overall, the style depends on the tension between lyrical idealism and worldly irony. It takes aesthetic yearning seriously while questioning the fantasy that life can be perfected through absolute mutual understanding. The resulting voice is urbane, psychologically perceptive, allusive, and gently satirical, with emotional force arising from the discovery that habit, responsibility, and imperfect affection may bind people more deeply than consciously chosen ideals.

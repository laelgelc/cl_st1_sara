The story adopts a sustained mode of political satire, using extravagant praise to expose hypocrisy, opportunism, and empty parliamentary rhetoric. Its dominant tone is mock-solemn and exuberantly ironic: the narrator repeatedly celebrates “our honourable friend” while supplying evidence that he is evasive, inconsistent, self-serving, and intellectually hollow. Polite formulas such as “honourable,” “noble,” “free and independent,” and “glorious triumph” acquire an increasingly derisive force through repetition. The satire is broad and comic rather than understated, but it is sharpened by indignation at electoral corruption, sectarian manipulation, crowd violence, and the substitution of patriotic slogans for coherent policy.

The narrative voice speaks in the first-person plural, presenting itself as the representative of a supposedly admiring public. This communal “we” creates a conspiratorial relationship with the reader, who is expected to recognize that the praise means its opposite. The narrator frequently mimics the partisan habit of redefining “mankind,” “all sides,” or public opinion as merely the politician’s own supporters. Parenthetical corrections—especially formulations such as “by which we mean, of course”—puncture inflated claims and expose the narrow interests beneath them. The repeated epithet “our honourable friend” functions as an ironic refrain, giving the piece unity while steadily enlarging the gap between official reputation and observable conduct.

The diction alternates between grandiose political abstraction and bluntly comic specificity. The politician’s language is crowded with patriotic clichés, ceremonial formulas, moral absolutes, and undefined threats: “sacred bulwarks,” “hireling tools,” “No surrender,” “nationality,” “hearths and altars,” and the “illimitable perspective.” These phrases sound momentous while avoiding concrete commitments. Their emptiness is highlighted by absurd combinations, most notably the climactic sequence “Hearths and Altars, Labour and Capital, Crown and Sceptre, Elephant and Castle,” in which elevated national symbols collapse into a mundane place-name. Similar bathos appears when lofty electoral enthusiasm is embodied by drunken fighters, burglars with life-preservers, free public houses, and an inexplicable lunatic distributing gold.

The syntax is expansive, cumulative, and deliberately overburdened. Long periodic sentences pile up qualifications, parallel clauses, parenthetical interruptions, and escalating examples. This style imitates the very verbosity it ridicules. Repetition and syntactic recursion are especially important in passages about what the member “meant then” and “means now,” where logical language twists into comic incoherence. Antithesis—white against black, yes against no, alive against dead—is used to demonstrate how contradictory positions can be rhetorically reconciled without actually being resolved. Lists and anaphora generate momentum, while semicolons and dashes allow claims to proliferate until they become grotesque.

The story’s humour relies heavily on hyperbole, reductio ad absurdum, bathos, and literalized contradiction. Political ambiguity is exaggerated into a condition in which twelve allies hold mutually incompatible views while declaring themselves an “impregnable phalanx of unanimity.” Empty patriotism is pushed to the point where an ordinary demand for clarification becomes treason, religious apostasy, and sympathy with a foreign faith. The mock reportorial details—stage directions such as “Vehement cheering,” “Cries of ‘Shame,’” and “man expelled”—reproduce the atmosphere of a public meeting while revealing how noise, intimidation, and mob loyalty replace debate. Capitalization and typographical emphasis, including “YOU,” “NOT,” and “THAT,” recreate the speaker’s theatrical delivery and show emphasis being used as a substitute for meaning.

Characterization is caricatural and functional. The honourable member is not psychologically developed; he is a representative type, defined by rhetorical evasiveness, shameless inconsistency, and mastery of partisan spectacle. Even his constituency, “Verbosity,” is allegorically named, turning the political setting into an embodiment of excessive and meaningless speech. Tipkisson serves as the plain-speaking antagonist whose simple question—what are they “driving at”?—threatens the entire rhetorical performance. His sobriety and immobility contrast with the speaker’s energetic display and the crowd’s volatile reactions. Yet the narrative does not idealize him in elaborate terms; his primary role is to reveal how direct inquiry is defeated by abuse, distraction, and manufactured suspicion.

Structurally, the piece begins and ends with declarations of delight at the politician’s return, producing a circular frame of ironic congratulation. Between these points, it moves from general observations about his rhetoric and voting record to an extended exemplary episode at an election meeting. The progression is cumulative: contradictions become more elaborate, the speech becomes more vacuous, and the crowd’s response becomes more irrational. The Tipkisson exchange provides the central comic climax, after which the satire widens from one politician to the broader political culture that rewards him. The conclusion returns to ceremonious praise, attributing public apathy and degraded citizenship to precisely the kind of contests it pretends to celebrate. Its final assurance that the member will be “found” in every discussion is double-edged, suggesting not useful participation but the inescapable presence of noisy, contentless political speech.

Overall, the style is energetic, theatrical, densely rhetorical, and morally pointed. It parodies parliamentary oratory by reproducing its rhythms, formulas, and patriotic imagery in exaggerated form. The chief stylistic principle is ironic inversion: honour signifies dishonour, clarity becomes confusion, unanimity masks contradiction, religious devotion conceals opportunism, and democratic enthusiasm appears as bribery, drunkenness, violence, and credulity. The result is a comic prose performance whose own controlled abundance contrasts with the politician’s meaningless excess.

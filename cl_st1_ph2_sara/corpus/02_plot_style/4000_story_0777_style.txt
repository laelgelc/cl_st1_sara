The story uses a retrospective first-person frame narrated by a sensitive but largely unobtrusive participant. The opening “we” establishes a close-knit community of expatriate American artists in Paris, while the narrator’s individual “I” supplies precise recollection and emotional interpretation. This framing voice is observant, cultivated, and elegiac rather than dramatically assertive. Its chief function is to create atmosphere, characterize Hartwell from the outside, and authenticate the long embedded confession that forms the story’s center. Hartwell’s own first-person account gradually displaces the frame narrator, creating a story-within-a-story whose intimate oral quality is emphasized by pauses, gestures, pipe smoking, falling darkness, and the listeners’ silence.

The structure is leisurely and retrospective. A subdued farewell gathering provides the immediate occasion, Bentley’s impending return to America prompting Hartwell to recount his own reluctant homecoming. The narrative moves from present unease into an extended memory, then returns briefly to the Paris studio and Bentley’s departure. This circular design gives “going home” both literal and symbolic force. The external action is slight: the real movement is inward, from detachment to belonging, and from inherited history as abstraction to history as an intensely personal revelation. The ending is restrained and anti-climactic in action but resonant in implication; Hartwell’s understated observation that going home is “always an experience” compresses the emotional weight of the entire recollection.

The prose is formal, polished, and distinctly literary, with long, balanced sentences, periodic constructions, layered subordinate clauses, and carefully controlled rhythms. Syntax often accumulates descriptive particulars before arriving at an interpretive or emotional conclusion. Semicolons, dashes, parenthetical qualifications, and triadic or cataloguing structures are frequent. Short sentences and fragments appear sparingly, usually to sharpen emphasis or mark a decisive recognition. The diction is elevated but not uniformly ornate, combining abstract psychological terms such as “kinship,” “destiny,” “vitality,” and “realization” with exact physical details drawn from art, landscape, industry, clothing, weather, and domestic objects. Occasional conversational idioms in dialogue—“Hang me,” “cut away like this”—keep the cultivated narration from becoming wholly ceremonious.

Description is highly pictorial and often composed as though for painting or sculpture. Figures are defined by silhouette, posture, mass, light, and material: Hartwell has “bronze-like shoulders,” while his profile is “cut clean against the gray light.” Landscapes are arranged through color and texture—burnished brown chestnuts, purple asters, smoky blue-green vegetation, pale roses, blackened locust bark, and a leaden river. The story repeatedly uses the vocabulary of the visual arts, not merely as decoration but as a way of perceiving character and memory. The uncle’s painted portrait, the clay soldier, the crude drawing of the flag, and Hartwell’s mature monument form a chain of representations through which private feeling becomes art.

The imagery depends strongly on contrasts. Paris is crisp, sociable, mobile, and bright with autumn fashion, greetings, returning students, and renewed work; western Pennsylvania is damp, smoky, overgrown, wounded, and oppressed by industrial force. Youth is set against age, vitality against decay, return against departure, artistic aspiration against duty, and organic inheritance against modern dislocation. Yet these oppositions are not absolute. The industrial landscape that initially repels Hartwell also embodies tremendous American energy, and the neglected ancestral garden becomes the place where he is finally rooted. The story’s central transformation therefore reconciles alienation with belonging rather than simply preferring Europe to America or nature to industry.

Personification and metaphor are prominent. Luggage waits in “discouraged resignation”; dullness “yawned” through conversation; industrial exhalations “brooded”; gulches resemble wounds; energy follows like a searchlight; the river carries away days and weeks; trees appear doomed before an advancing force. Such figures make settings responsive to the characters’ inner lives. Natural and mechanical sound also carries emotional meaning: crashing factories, creaking derricks, splashing pipes, sighing boughs, boatmen’s calls, and the softly flapping flag create an acoustic field that moves from oppression toward revelation. The bugle becomes “the very golden throat” of sacrificed boyhood, a characteristic fusion of sound, color, and symbolic abstraction.

The tone is predominantly wistful, grave, and elegiac, but it avoids sentimentality through social observation, irony, and understatement. The early account of the long luncheon, the perspiring waiter, the thirsty Poles, and the entertaining Gascon introduces light comic realism. Hartwell’s father’s pursuit of “Art,” and the reference to ingenuous marble works, carry a faintly satirical edge toward expatriate aestheticism. As the embedded narrative deepens, however, humor recedes and the tone becomes meditative, haunted, and finally numinous. Emotional intensity is delayed until the attic discovery and the night beneath the flag, where the prose rises into visionary language without abandoning concrete sensory anchors.

Characterization is indirect and economical. Hartwell is established through physical presence, reticence, professional achievement, and the reactions of younger artists before he explains himself. His guardedness makes his confession feel exceptional and gives the listening scene ceremonial gravity. The dead uncle is reconstructed from fragments—a portrait, a veteran’s story, a planted tree, schoolbooks, sketches, clothing, and a flag—so that characterization becomes an act of imaginative recovery. The aunt is rendered through frailty, repetition, and failing memory rather than extensive dialogue. Even minor figures are caught through vivid gestures and selected details, revealing a style that favors emblematic observation over exhaustive biography.

Dialogue is limited, naturalistic, and strategically placed. It initiates disclosure, distinguishes the informal studio circle from the more formal narration, and then largely yields to Hartwell’s uninterrupted monologue. Speech is accompanied by bodily business—shifting, fumbling, knocking ashes from a pipe, settling among cushions—which sustains the sense of an actual oral performance. The listeners’ growing stillness is as important as anything they say. Silence functions as a measure of Hartwell’s emotional authority and of the story’s movement from casual fellowship into shared contemplation.

Pacing is deliberately slow. The opening postpones Hartwell’s tale through social background, seasonal description, the studio setting, and discussion of the sculpture. Within his recollection, extensive landscape passages suspend action and reproduce the stagnation of his two-year vigil. Summary covers education, travel, and family history efficiently, while scenes involving the grave, attic trunk, marked Æneid, and nocturnal flag are expanded in close sensory detail. This modulation gives key objects and moments the force of revelation. The Civil War episode itself is concise but graphic, its violence conveyed through a few stark physical actions rather than prolonged battlefield spectacle.

The style’s deepest concern is the conversion of memory into identity and art. National belonging is presented not as a political argument but as an involuntary experience of “race and blood and kindred,” awakened through objects, place, sacrifice, and imaginative sympathy. The flag operates simultaneously as historical relic, childhood doodle, battlefield standard, sensory presence, and artistic symbol. Roots, trees, soil, blood, and inherited vitality form a recurring symbolic network, culminating in Hartwell’s sensation that the earth has grasped and rooted him. His sculpture is thus shown to arise from a fusion of personal inheritance and collective history.

Overall, the style is atmospheric, contemplative, image-rich, and psychologically retrospective. It combines social realism with symbolic romance, restrained framing with an emotionally heightened inner narrative, and visual precision with philosophical reflection. Its characteristic effect comes from allowing physical environments and cherished objects to accumulate meaning slowly, until an apparently modest recollection opens into a meditation on exile, homecoming, artistic truth, historical memory, and the discovery of belonging.

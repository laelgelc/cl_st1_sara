The story is a satirical character sketch written in the manner of an mock-anthropological essay. Rather than developing a conventional plot, it identifies a social “type”—the “Good Soul”—and catalogues that type’s habits, tastes, rituals, and relations with others. Its structure moves loosely through a Good Soul’s life cycle and spheres of activity: childhood, maturity, illness and hospitality, social etiquette, holidays, conversation, culture, humor, advice-giving, and eventual exploitation. The repeated classification of Good Souls as a “race,” “species,” or congenital condition gives the piece a comic semblance of scientific observation.

The narrative voice is urbane, confident, conversational, and knowingly superior. It speaks in the first-person plural and frequently addresses the reader directly, as in “gentle reader” and “as usual, you are way ahead of me.” This creates complicity between narrator and audience: both are invited to recognize the type and share the joke at its expense. The voice pretends to pity Good Souls while continually expressing irritation with them. Its ostensible compassion—“piteous,” “heartrending,” “terrible plight,” “doomed child”—is so extravagantly disproportionate that it becomes mock pathos. The narrator’s attitude is therefore double-edged, blending amused affection, social impatience, and faint cruelty.

Irony is the governing technique. Virtues such as kindness, generosity, optimism, tact, self-denial, and conscientiousness are reframed as social defects because they are intrusive, inconvenient, sentimental, or embarrassingly earnest. The Good Soul’s helpful acts reliably worsen the recipient’s situation: pillows are smoothed out of comfort, tea is altered beyond recognition, invalids are subjected to unwanted food and depressing conversation, and theater companions are harassed into exchanging seats. Much of the humor comes from this gap between benevolent intention and irritating effect. The final wish that Good Souls might already be enjoying their heavenly reward is a polished euphemism for wishing them dead, giving the conclusion a sharp epigrammatic sting.

The prose relies heavily on accumulation. Long lists of behaviors, objects, holidays, authors, gifts, and petty indignities build comic momentum through repetition and increasingly specific detail. Parallel constructions such as “It is the Good Souls who…” and “It is they who…” create anaphoric rhythm and make the catalogue sound like an indictment. Sentences often begin with a broad generalization and then expand into a sequence of concrete examples. These periodic, elaborately balanced sentences are offset by short declarative judgments—“There is no accounting for Good Souls” and “That settles it”—which function as comic punch lines and transitions.

The diction combines formal social commentary with colloquial phrasing. Elevated expressions such as “impassable barrier,” “fulfillment of his destiny,” “indelible mark of his pariahdom,” and “observation of correct little ceremonies” mock the triviality of the subject by treating minor social awkwardness as tragedy or ritual law. Against this, phrases such as “right there,” “way ahead of me,” “rough deal,” and “he won’t mind” keep the voice intimate and brisk. Capitalization is also satirical: “Good Souls” and “Occasions” turn ordinary traits and events into official categories. Repeated diminutives—“little lives,” “little ceremonies,” “little gifts,” “little reminders,” “little endeavors”—suggest fussiness, sentimentality, and constrained emotional scale.

Characterization is external and typological rather than psychological. The Good Soul is not an individual with a private inner life but a composite figure defined through recognizable gestures, possessions, quotations, preferences, and social routines. Material details do much of the characterization: postcards of public buildings, souvenir thimbles, burned-leather mottoes, carnations, greeting cards, blanc-mange, and wholesome popular literature all evoke conventional respectability and middle-class sentiment. Proper names and periodical references root the satire in the popular culture of its era, while also establishing the narrator’s taste hierarchy. The Good Soul’s preference for optimistic, morally improving entertainment is contrasted implicitly with the narrator’s more sophisticated and skeptical sensibility.

The humor ranges from understatement to hyperbole. Petty annoyances are called “hideous outrages” and “fresh atrocities,” while lifelong exclusion is discussed in the language of cheerful social observation. Bathos repeatedly collapses grand language into mundane detail, as when martyrdom consists of sitting in a draught or behind a pillar. Comic misquotation and malapropism—“cast any asparagus” and “three times in concussion”—provide a more direct form of verbal caricature. Antithesis is also prominent: Good Souls live among humanity but remain apart from it; they seek to help but cause discomfort; they are morally admirable yet socially unwelcome; they suffer exploitation but return eagerly for more.

The piece has a strong oral and performative quality. Rhetorical questions guide the reader through supposedly familiar scenes, imperatives such as “Go now” and “Mark that child well” dramatize the argument, and direct quotations reproduce the formulas by which society identifies or dismisses the Good Soul. Recurring phrases act as refrains, especially “he means well” and “he won’t mind.” The narration resembles an after-dinner monologue or magazine humor essay, with each paragraph developing a recognizable situation toward a neat comic turn.

Its pacing is episodic and cumulative rather than dramatic. Early paragraphs establish the mock theory, the central body supplies an expansive social catalogue, and the final section changes emphasis from the nuisance Good Souls cause to the abuse they endure. This shift complicates the satire slightly: the targets are not only the Good Souls’ intrusive virtues but also the selfish people who exploit their patience. Even so, the closing line restores the narrator’s mischievous hostility and prevents the piece from becoming openly sentimental.

Overall, the style is polished, metropolitan, and period-specific, combining social observation with affectionate caricature. It depends on exaggerated seriousness, ironic praise, rhythmic enumeration, precise domestic detail, and a sustained discrepancy between moral worth and social desirability. The result is a comic essay in which benevolence is treated as an incurable condition and ordinary etiquette becomes the basis for mock tragedy.

In 1892 at Mentone, the narrator meets a wealthy man called Smith, who reveals a long-held secret about the supposedly deceased painter François Millet.

Years earlier, Smith, Millet, Claude Frère, and Carl Boulanger were impoverished young artists in France, unable to sell their work or obtain further credit. Carl observed that neglected artists often become famous only after death and proposed that one of them should pretend to die. They drew lots, and Millet was chosen. While Millet rapidly produced paintings, sketches, and fragments, the other three traveled through France selling his work, presenting him as a celebrated but gravely ill master. They also planted newspaper reports about his declining health, creating international interest and driving up demand.

Once Millet’s reputation had been sufficiently inflated, he staged his death. A wax figure was placed in the coffin, and Millet, disguised as a distant relative, helped his friends carry it at the widely attended funeral. Afterward, his paintings soared in value, making all four men enormously rich.

Smith finally discloses that the sad, reclusive man the narrator noticed at the hotel is Millet himself, alive under the name Théophile Magnan and enjoying the fortune that artists usually receive only posthumously.

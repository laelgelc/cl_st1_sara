The story adopts the manner of a late nineteenth-century newspaper yarn, combining maritime adventure, eyewitness reportage and comic satire. Its central stylistic device is the contrast between an editorial’s confident moral condemnation and the subsequent reconstruction of what actually happened. By opening with the New York Daily Mentor’s pompous warning about “ocean racing,” the narrative establishes an apparently authoritative judgment, then gradually exposes how little that judgment rests on direct knowledge. The result is sustained dramatic irony: the supposedly reckless captain proves calm, attentive and technically accomplished, while the newspaper that lectures him is shown to be speculative and self-important.

The narration is first-person and retrospective, delivered by a journalist travelling aboard the Arrowic. The narrator presents himself as an observer rather than a protagonist and maintains a dry, amused tone. His profession allows the story to satirise journalism from within. He responds to the purser’s attacks with mock reasonableness, defending newspaper omniscience so extravagantly that the defence becomes an admission of its absurdity. Phrases such as “this palpably absurd contention” reverse the obvious truth for comic effect: the purser’s claim that an experienced captain understands his own responsibilities is plainly sensible, but the narrator treats it as ridiculous. This deadpan irony is one of the story’s defining features.

The prose moves between three registers. The quoted editorial uses formal, accusatory and moralising language, with rhetorical questions, repeated references to responsibility, and an escalating catalogue of ship, cargo and human life. The narrator’s surrounding prose is conversational, urbane and lightly facetious, often using qualifications and parenthetical asides to create an intimate relationship with the reader. The maritime action, by contrast, is rendered in clear reportorial detail through observable movements, sounds, timings and spatial relations. This mixture gives the story both comic sophistication and documentary immediacy.

Dialogue is prominent and serves chiefly to reveal attitude. The purser speaks in energetic, repetitive bursts, building his complaint through parallel examples involving statesmen, banks, railways and strikes. His indignation is vivid but also structurally useful, since it articulates the story’s critique of editorial presumption. The narrator’s replies are brief, mild and teasing, creating a comic mismatch between the purser’s anger and his own studied composure. The “nautical passenger” later performs a different function: his explanatory dialogue translates the captain’s silent manoeuvres for a general audience and turns technical navigation into intelligible suspense.

Sentence structure is generally expansive and coordinated, with clauses linked by commas, semicolons and conjunctions. Long sentences accumulate qualifications, examples or simultaneous activity, particularly in the description of the crowded wharf. Shorter statements appear at moments of danger or decision—“Then we came to a standstill,” “Then full steam ahead”—and sharpen the pace. Repetition is used rhythmically throughout: the fog lifts and closes several times, the ship advances in stages, and the nautical passenger repeatedly measures the diminishing interval before the tide is lost. This patterned alternation supplies the climax with a countdown structure.

The diction is accessible but period-specific. Expressions such as “penny-a-liner,” “the cheek of it,” “under the sun,” and “as thick as pea-soup” give the prose a contemporary journalistic and conversational flavour. Maritime terms—“the bar,” “twin screws,” “anchor chain,” “bridge,” “pilot,” and “chronometer”—create technical credibility without overwhelming the reader. The occasional literary allusion, especially “even the ranks of Tuscany could scarce forbear a cheer,” adds playful elevation and reflects the narrator’s educated, slightly performative wit.

Description relies heavily on motion and sound. The departure scene is crowded with rushing passengers, banging luggage, escaping steam, cranes, bells and cheers. In the fog sequence, visibility contracts while sound becomes dominant: bells clang, a gong is struck, and answering signals emerge from unseen points. The fog is rendered as a physical presence—cold, piercing, dripping, repeatedly blotting out the world—and is mildly personified when it closes down “as if it had heard” the passenger’s hopeful remark. The contrast between the “thick white blank” and the sudden revelation of the “whole blue ocean” gives the ending its strongest visual release.

The pacing follows a deliberate shift from argument to spectacle. The opening editorial and the purser’s extended complaint establish the satirical theme at leisure. The wharf scene then broadens the setting and introduces the rivalry between the Arrowic and the Dartonia. Once the fog descends, the narrative becomes progressively tighter, organised around brief clearances, renewed obscurity and repeated time checks. The captain remains almost entirely silent, so his competence is conveyed through controlled gestures: pacing the bridge, watching the chronometer and ordering bursts of speed. His reserve contrasts with the verbosity of both journalists and passengers, suggesting that professional authority is demonstrated through action rather than proclamation.

The story’s dominant themes are expertise, responsibility, rivalry and the unreliability of public judgment. It does not deny the danger of ocean racing; instead, it questions whether distant commentators are qualified to interpret risk. The captain’s manoeuvre remains daring, but the detailed account reframes it as calculated rather than reckless. The ending is intentionally understated: once the Arrowic crosses the bar, the narrator simply confirms that this is how it was done. That plain conclusion quietly overturns the editorial’s sensational account and leaves the practical achievement to serve as its own rebuttal.

Overall, the style is reportorial, ironic and suspenseful. It combines the credibility of an eyewitness account with the compression of a newspaper anecdote and the structure of a maritime adventure. Its humour depends on verbal overconfidence being defeated by silent competence, while its suspense arises from precise physical obstacles, repeated delays and a steadily narrowing window of time.

A calculating narrator surveys Ireland’s widespread poverty and the burden of children whom destitute families cannot support. Claiming that conventional remedies have failed, he proposes that poor parents raise their infants until age one and then sell them as food to the wealthy. He details how many children should be reserved for breeding, estimates their weight and price, suggests methods of preparation and uses for their skin, and argues that the scheme would enrich parents, reduce the poor and Catholic populations, stimulate trade, benefit landlords and taverns, and encourage marriage.

The narrator dismisses a related proposal to eat adolescents and treats the suffering and deaths of impoverished adults with similar indifference. He then rejects humane reforms—such as taxing absentee landlords, supporting domestic goods, curbing luxury, promoting honesty, and showing mercy to tenants—as unrealistic because society lacks the will to enact them. Insisting that his plan is practical and selfless, he concludes by claiming no personal financial interest, since his own children are too old to be sold. The grotesque proposal satirically exposes the ruling classes’ exploitation of Ireland’s poor and the cruelty of reducing human suffering to economic calculation.

The story adopts a warm, didactic, socially conscious style characteristic of nineteenth-century domestic moral fiction. Its central purpose is openly instructive: it contrasts fashionable, obligatory gift-giving with charity motivated by affection and genuine need. Rather than concealing this lesson beneath ambiguity, the narrative develops it through a sequence of concrete examples and culminates in an explicitly Christian argument about remembering the poor at Christmas. The tone is earnest and sentimental, but it is softened by domestic warmth, gentle irony, and comic family banter.

The narrative voice is an unobtrusive but personable third-person narrator who occasionally steps forward with informal commentary, as in the closing observation that “our story spins on too long.” This creates the impression of an amiable storyteller addressing a familiar audience. The narrator generally allows dialogue to carry both characterization and argument, intervening briefly to describe gestures, expressions, and changes of mood. There is a mild omniscience in the presentation of Eleanor’s embarrassment, dawning sympathy, and moral transformation, though the story does not pursue deep psychological analysis. Character is revealed chiefly through speech, behavior, and emblematic details.

Dialogue dominates the story and gives it the form of an extended domestic conversation or moral debate. Eleanor begins with languid, repetitive complaints about the difficulty of buying for people who already possess everything. Her aunt answers through patient questioning, illustrative anecdotes, and imagined reversals of circumstance. This Socratic method allows the lesson to appear discovered rather than simply imposed: Eleanor is asked to picture herself as an abandoned old woman and to compare an unwanted ring with food, fuel, clothing, and comfort. The dialogue is rhetorically shaped, with accumulating examples, parallel contrasts, exclamations, and pointed questions. In the later comic scene, rapid exchanges, teasing remarks, and interruptions enliven the pace and prevent the moral argument from becoming uniformly solemn.

The diction combines accessible domestic language with period-specific vocabulary and orthography. Expressions such as “O, dear,” “every body,” “any thing,” “pedler,” “day wages,” “centre table,” and “good fairyism” give the prose a distinctly nineteenth-century texture. Material objects are named with unusual frequency—gold thimbles, card cases, shawls, stockings, tea, oranges, grapes, coal, a stove, and a purple velvet cap—because the ethical contrast is embodied in things. Luxury objects are described as decorative, repetitive, and emotionally empty, whereas humble necessities become vivid carriers of comfort and love. Religious vocabulary, including “Savior,” “manger,” “lowly,” and “outcast,” raises the domestic argument into a Christian moral framework.

The syntax varies according to emotional function. Conversational passages use short questions, exclamations, interruptions, dashes, and colloquial fragments, producing immediacy and a spoken rhythm. The aunt’s more serious speeches expand into long, cumulative sentences filled with coordinated clauses and lists. These sentences often build by accretion, moving from one hardship or charitable possibility to another until Eleanor is emotionally overwhelmed. Repetition is central: the opening recurrence of “every” and “got” evokes satiation, while later catalogues of hunger, cold, illness, loneliness, and deprivation intensify compassion. Rhetorical questions are especially prominent and are used to guide both Eleanor and the reader toward the intended conclusion.

The characterization is economical and typological. Eleanor is initially privileged, bored, and “sick, and sated,” but she is not cruel; her spontaneous “Poor fellow!” signals a dormant capacity for sympathy. Her aunt functions as a benevolent moral guide, combining practical knowledge of poverty with religious seriousness and affectionate tact. The poor families are presented in brief sentimental tableaux: the consumptive shoemaker craving oranges, the thrifty young mother admiring a baby’s cap, the two elderly women in a freezing garret, and the lame child awaiting a single orange. These figures are individualized through memorable details, yet they also represent broader classes of deserving poor. Tom and Will serve as comic foils whose mockery eventually turns into approval and material assistance.

Structurally, the story follows a clear conversion pattern. It opens with abundance experienced as tedium, moves through the aunt’s catalogue of deprivation, reaches an ethical and theological climax, and then demonstrates Eleanor’s changed values through action. The asterisked section break marks a shift from reflective discussion to comic enactment. The bulky parcels provide visual evidence of her transformation before their purpose is openly confirmed. The ending does not narrate the charitable deliveries themselves; instead, it gestures toward the recipients’ future happiness and invites the reader imaginatively to “call at the doors.” This preserves a light, conversational finish while extending the story’s appeal beyond its fictional world.

Humor operates through affectionate irony rather than satire. Eleanor’s jeweled boredom is quietly exposed as absurd, and the cousins’ deliberate misreading of shawls, flannel, a stove, and a baby’s cap creates a playful scene of incongruity. Their joking also dramatizes the social embarrassment attached to “unfashionable” charity. Eleanor’s answer—that she bought the objects to make people laugh who are not accustomed to laughing—compresses the story’s moral into a memorable, emotionally appealing line. The narrative thus balances pathos with levity and rebuke with encouragement.

The imagery relies heavily on contrasts between glitter and warmth, fullness and want, fashionable interiors and crumbling dwellings. Expensive gifts are “hard, cold, glittering,” while charity produces “warm and heartfelt gladness.” Prosperous rooms contain marble-topped tables and redundant ornaments; poor rooms contain cracked stoves, failing embers, snow-blown gaps, and sickbeds. Fairy-tale language—“good fairy,” “magic dream,” and “good fairyism”—translates philanthropy into an imaginative role attractive to the young heroine, while the later Christian imagery corrects and deepens that fantasy by grounding charity in duty, fellowship, and Christ’s identification with the poor.

Overall, the style is conversational, sentimental, reformist, and explicitly moral. It seeks to change conduct by joining domestic realism to emotional appeal: concrete scenes of suffering awaken sympathy, practical lists show how money can be redirected, humor makes reform socially inviting, and Christian rhetoric supplies the final authority. Its ideal stylistic effect is not subtle uncertainty but benevolent persuasion—the reader should finish entertained, touched, and prompted to reconsider the meaning of generosity.

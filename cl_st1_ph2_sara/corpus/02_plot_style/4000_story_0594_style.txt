The story is written in a late-Victorian realist mode, combining social observation, psychological portraiture, restrained melodrama, and bitter irony. Its setting is rendered through concrete urban details—park gates, iron seats, hospital beds, public houses, sandwich boards—and through sharply observed signs of poverty, exhaustion, and bodily decline. The opening contrast between the fresh spring morning and the “bedraggled” homeless crowd establishes the governing tension between an indifferent natural world and human misery. Description is visually precise but morally charged: clothes, posture, eyes, and facial movements serve as evidence of class, experience, and character.

The narrator is third-person and broadly omniscient, moving between panoramic social description and close access to the dying young man’s consciousness. At times the voice adopts an essayistic authority, pausing to make aphoristic observations such as the satisfaction of recognising oneself as “the architect of your own misfortune.” These generalisations give the prose a knowing, slightly detached quality, yet the focalisation becomes intimate in the hospital scenes, where sensations, memories, shame, longing, and self-justification are presented in a fluid sequence. Free indirect discourse allows the young man’s sardonic phrasing and defensive humour to colour the narration without requiring explicit thought tags.

The sentence style alternates between long, carefully balanced descriptive periods and short, emphatic statements. Dashes, semicolons, parenthetical remarks, exclamations, rhetorical questions, and sentence fragments reproduce shifts in perception and emotion. Repetition and parallel phrasing intensify crucial moments, particularly the patient’s longing for “his mother, or his brothers, or his little sister” and his gradual confrontation with death. Sensory motifs are important: rain, pallor, warmth, lamplight, hospital murmurs, and especially the smell of acetic acid create transitions between the immediate environment and involuntary memory.

Dialogue is frequent and strongly differentiated by class and education. Darkey’s speech uses eye dialect, dropped consonants, colloquial grammar, and pub slang, while the younger man speaks with greater fluency, wit, and self-conscious irony. His educated vocabulary and literary phrasing contrast with his degraded circumstances, suggesting a fall from a more secure social position. Speech often works indirectly: jokes, interruptions, evasions, and casual remarks reveal hunger, embarrassment, fear, and moral limitation more effectively than explicit confession would. Darkey’s repeated concern with food and drink undercuts the sailor’s stories and keeps the narrative grounded in immediate physical need.

The tone mixes compassion with unsentimental detachment. The protagonist is neither idealised as an innocent victim nor condemned as wholly corrupt; he regards himself as responsible for his ruin, but the narrative also exposes his youth, vulnerability, and need for attachment. Pathos is generated through understatement rather than overt authorial pleading. The hospital’s routine calm, the doctor’s clinical prediction, and the unfinished address on the letter make the death more affecting because institutional life continues without ceremony.

Structurally, the story has three clearly marked movements: collapse in the park, recognition and remorse in the hospital, and the letter’s destruction in the public house. The first two sections build the expectation of a final emotional connection between the dying son and his mother. The third overturns that expectation through cruel coincidence and human carelessness. Darkey briefly assumes the role of messenger, but drink, vanity, and forgetfulness defeat the possibility of reconciliation. His appropriation of the dead man’s Nagasaki story further shows how suffering is converted into disposable entertainment.

The ending is deliberately abrupt and anti-sentimental. The letter is not merely lost but torn apart and burned to light a pipe, turning the protagonist’s last attempt at repentance into a trivial consumable object. The nearby smoker’s ordinary request, “After you, mate,” closes the story on bathos and dramatic irony: the surrounding men remain unaware of the emotional significance of what has been destroyed. Overall, the style depends on social realism, psychologically inflected omniscience, class-marked dialogue, controlled pathos, and a final twist of bleak, almost naturalistic irony.

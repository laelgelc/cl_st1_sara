A self-centered Water-rat boasts that devoted friendship is the noblest virtue, though he believes a friend’s duty is simply to be devoted to him. To challenge this view, a Linnet tells him about Hans, a poor but kind gardener, and Hugh, a wealthy Miller who claims to be Hans’s best friend.

The Miller repeatedly takes Hans’s flowers and produce while giving nothing in return. During winter, when Hans is hungry and alone, the Miller deliberately avoids him. In spring, he promises Hans a broken wheelbarrow, then uses that promise to pressure him into surrendering flowers and a plank, carrying flour to market, repairing a roof, herding sheep, and performing other exhausting errands. Hans neglects his own garden but continues helping because he accepts the Miller’s grand speeches about friendship.

One stormy night, the Miller sends Hans to fetch a doctor for his injured son but refuses to lend him a lantern. Hans completes the journey to the doctor, then loses his way on the return trip and drowns. At the funeral, the Miller takes pride of place as Hans’s supposed best friend and complains that Hans’s death has left him burdened with the worthless wheelbarrow he never actually gave away.

The Water-rat fails to recognize the Miller’s hypocrisy or the story’s application to himself. Angered when told that the tale has a moral, he dismisses it and retreats into his hole.

The story adopts a nineteenth-century didactic and sentimental style, shaped like a moral parable. Its central opposition—contentment versus wealth—is established in the opening dialogue, tested through William’s suffering, and explicitly restated at the conclusion. Rather than leaving the lesson implicit, the narrator interprets events for the reader and closes with direct counsel. The resulting tone is earnest, admonitory, emotionally heightened, and strongly assured of its moral values.

The narration is omniscient and conspicuously intrusive. It moves freely across years, locations, and characters’ inner lives, summarising long periods before pausing over exemplary scenes. Direct addresses such as “There—his face is toward you,” “And here we leave them, reader,” and “which may you be spared!” create the manner of a storyteller speaking personally to an audience. Rhetorical questions—“Has he won the glittering prize?” and “And Ellen?”—manufacture suspense while guiding interpretation. Exclamations and evaluative interjections such as “Ah!”, “Oh,” “Poor stricken heart!” and “Happy maiden!” openly prescribe the desired emotional response.

The structure is theatrical and episodic. The headings “SCENE SECOND” and “SCENE THIRD” divide the narrative into moral tableaux: departure and contrast, punishment and disillusionment, then recognition and restoration. Large chronological leaps compress ten or more years into broad retrospective narration, whereas decisive moments—the brothers’ farewell, the wife’s death, the father’s rejection, and William’s return—are dramatised through dialogue. Parallelism governs the design: William goes outward into the “restless world,” while Robert remains at the homestead; Ellen weeps while another maiden anticipates marriage; the gilded mansion becomes a prison, while the quiet cottage becomes a paradise. These contrasts make the story’s ethical argument visibly symmetrical.

Dialogue is formal, declamatory, and aphoristic rather than conversationally naturalistic. Characters often speak in complete, polished statements that embody opposing philosophies: “Contentment is better than wealth” is answered by “A proverb for drones.” Repetition gives key lines the force of refrains, especially “Too late! too late!” and the promise that the old home’s doors will remain open. Emotional revelations are frequently staged through names spoken alone—“William!” and “Ellen!”—followed by bodily reactions such as starting, trembling, clasping, or embracing.

The diction is elevated and distinctly period-marked. Words and constructions such as “in vain,” “patrimony,” “meed,” “bourne,” “therefor,” “ere,” “thence,” “verity,” and “bestow” create an archaic, literary register. Religious and moral vocabulary—“mammon-worship,” “soul,” “paradise,” “perfidy,” and “truth”—casts ambition as spiritual error rather than merely imprudent conduct. Wealth is repeatedly described through hostile or deceptive imagery: the “apples of Sodom,” “golden attractions,” “gilded bars,” alluring fortune, and a fiery draught. Home, by contrast, is associated with hearth-fire, childhood, flowers, children, peace, and enduring affection.

The prose alternates between compact moral pronouncements and long, highly wrought sentences. Semicolons, dashes, colons, and exclamation marks produce an oratorical rhythm, while inversions such as “Pale was the other’s face” and “Eagerly sought he the prize” intensify the old-fashioned cadence. Repetition and balanced clauses are prominent: “ever-attractive and ever-fading,” “ever sharp and ever piercing,” and “poor, humiliated, broken in spirit.” Personification is frequent—Time writes “Death” on a forehead, hope mocks, fortune cheats, and years look through the cottage window—giving abstract forces an active role in William’s downfall.

Imagery is symbolic and melodramatic. William’s marriage is both a thorn and a prison; ambition offers a poisoned cup; regret opens a “gloomy gulf”; death is a plunge into a “dark profound.” The domestic scenes use idealised pastoral imagery, presenting Robert’s family life as virtually Edenic. Characterisation is correspondingly schematic: Robert represents wisdom and contentment, Ellen fidelity and forgiveness, William worldly ambition and repentance, and the wealthy wife and her father the sterility of mercenary relationships. Psychological complexity is secondary to exemplary function.

Overall, the story’s signature is a combination of sentimental melodrama, Christian-inflected moral instruction, omniscient authorial commentary, theatrical scene construction, elevated diction, and emphatic rhetorical patterning. Its emotional and narrative machinery is designed to prove a proverb through suffering and then convert that suffering into a direct warning to the reader.

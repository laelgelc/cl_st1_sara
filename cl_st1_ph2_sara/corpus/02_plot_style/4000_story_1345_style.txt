The story uses a retrospective first-person narrator whose educated, self-conscious voice is the chief source of comedy. He addresses the reader directly, anticipates familiar plot expectations, and then pointedly refuses to fulfil them. His narration is therefore strongly metafictional: the lonely road, empty stagecoach, possible highwayman, romantic aristocrat, and providential heroine are introduced as conventions of popular fiction rather than as genuine developments. Suspense repeatedly arises, only to be punctured by mundane explanations. The narrator appears imaginative and perceptive but is also comically overcivilised, allowing discomfort, darkness, and fatigue to inflate minor uncertainties into elaborate melodrama.

The dominant tone is dry, urbane, and mock-serious. Trivial actions are described with the gravity appropriate to ritual, catastrophe, or supernatural mystery. The bystanders’ spitting becomes a “weird” baptism; a hairpin receives quasi-forensic scrutiny; the driver’s expectoration serves as reassuring proof that he has not been gagged by highwaymen. This disparity between elevated presentation and coarse or ordinary subject matter creates sustained incongruity. The humour is rarely delivered as a blunt punchline. Instead, it accumulates through solemn phrasing, delayed qualification, understatement, and anticlimax. The final exchange follows the same pattern, revealing that the narrator slept through the very event whose absence might otherwise have justified a dramatic mystery.

The diction combines formal literary English with regional and colloquial touches. Words such as “deportment,” “volition,” “obscurity,” “contemplation,” and “predisposed” give the narration an ornate, cultivated surface, while names like Wingdam and Slumgullion, references to “bully” conditions, and phonetic speech such as “I sez to Mariar” locate the action in a rough frontier setting. The contrast satirises both genteel pretension and frontier crudity. Foreign exclamations, classical mythology, opera, Faust, Joshua, French romance, and popular-fiction formulas create a conspicuously learned texture, though these allusions are usually comic rather than reverential.

The syntax is expansive and carefully balanced. Long, multi-clausal sentences permit qualifications, parenthetical comments, escalating lists, and comic reversals to unfold gradually. Semicolons and dashes regulate digressions, while parallel constructions intensify absurdity. Repetition is especially prominent: “It was of no use” reproduces obsessive thought, and “Praise-to-the-face-is-open-disgrace” turns the stagecoach’s jolting into a maddening mechanical refrain. Short sentences and questions interrupt these elaborate periods at moments of imagined danger—“How quiet the driver was!” and “Was there any driver?”—temporarily accelerating the pace before the narrator launches into another fantasy.

Description alternates between atmospheric vividness and satirical personification. The stagecoach is a “gloomy abyss” or “chasm”; its straps beckon like ghostly hands; its springs, bolts, and axle sigh, complain, and expostulate. Darkness is rendered through sound, smell, pressure, and uncertain outlines rather than sight alone, making the journey sensorially immediate. At other moments the landscape becomes genuinely lyrical: isolated stars possess “patient sorrow,” and distance gives each one an “immeasurable loneliness.” Yet lyrical elevation is seldom allowed to remain intact. The moon is promptly converted into theatrical scenery from an opera, and its grandeur becomes a “cheap enchantment” framed by the coach like a private box.

The narrative structure is episodic and digressive rather than plot-driven. It begins with an explicit rejection of sensational possibilities, surveys the hotel and its inhabitants, dwells on the discomforts of travel, wanders into an improvised French adventure, and ends with arrival. External action is minimal; the real movement occurs in the narrator’s perceptions and associations. Physical rhythms—the stage’s swaying, jolting, thumping, and sudden acceleration—shape both the prose and the progress of his thoughts. Pacing is deliberately leisurely, mimicking the slow coach journey and allowing observations to expand into comic set pieces.

Characterisation is economical and caricatural. Minor figures are defined by a single repeated attitude or gesture: the hotel guests are oppressively solemn, the clerk is obstinate, the driver is weary and taciturn, and the loiterers perform variations on ceremonious expectoration. The narrator is characterised far more fully through his language than through explicit description. His vanity, literary saturation, nervousness, and susceptibility to theatrical fantasy emerge from the elaborate interpretations he imposes on ordinary experience.

Dialogue is sparse and strategically placed. Most quoted speech is either remembered, stylised, or imagined, while the brief realistic exchange at the end is plain and colloquial. That contrast gives the ending its force: the driver’s practical explanation collapses the narrator’s entire night of literary speculation. Overall, the style blends frontier local colour, travel sketch, parody, social satire, Gothic atmosphere, and mock-romantic fantasy. Its signature method is to view a coarse, uncomfortable, uneventful journey through a narrator so rhetorically sophisticated and fiction-haunted that banality continually threatens to become drama, only to return, with comic inevitability, to banality.

A weary psychoanalyst tells the narrator that years of entering deeply into troubled lives have left him feeling choked by inherited beliefs and desperate to escape, become clean, and begin again. The narrator doubts that anyone can truly understand or cure the universal suffering caused by the human need for love.

The narrator later recounts painter LeRoy’s experience with a lonely, sexually repressed music teacher from Iowa. Raised among sisters with almost no contact with men, she comes to Chicago longing for love but is terrified whenever men approach her. Her contradictory behavior—provoking attention and then recoiling from touch—causes the men in her boardinghouse to resent her, and the landlady orders her to leave.

In panic, the woman enters LeRoy’s room and begs him to take her. When the landlady follows, LeRoy impulsively claims they are engaged and promises to care for her. He finds her another room and speaks with her repeatedly, but they do not become lovers, and she eventually returns west.

LeRoy initially believes that becoming her lover might solve her problem, but he comes to understand that she needs sustained, patient love rather than merely sex. Her predicament changes him, convincing him that everyone is similarly distorted by loneliness, inherited ideas, and the unmet need to be loved. Like the psychoanalyst, LeRoy becomes exhausted and frightened by what he has glimpsed beneath other people’s lives, longing to shed the forces that choke him and to be purified and free.

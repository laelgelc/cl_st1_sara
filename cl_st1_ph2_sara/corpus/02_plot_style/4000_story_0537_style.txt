The story adopts the style of an early twentieth-century children’s fairy tale, expanding a familiar nursery rhyme into a comic, morally instructive origin story. Its plot is linear and neatly circular: it begins by quoting the rhyme, supplies the circumstances that lead to Miss Muffet sitting on a tuffet with curds and whey, and ends by returning to the rhyme’s iconic details. This etiological structure gives the narrative an air of playful inevitability, as each episode moves the heroine closer to the scene already known by the reader.

The narrator is omniscient, genial, and mildly intrusive. The voice occasionally addresses the reader directly—“Perhaps you think”—or admits limited knowledge for whimsical effect, as when it claims not to know Miss Muffet’s christened name. Although sympathetic to the child, the narration maintains enough distance to expose the absurdities of the adults around her. Its moral judgments are clear but lightly delivered: wealth and luxury are shown to be poor substitutes for affection, freedom, useful activity, and healthy play.

The diction is accessible and concrete, with a slightly formal, period quality. Expressions such as “scarce knew them,” “enquired,” “personage of great importance,” and “earn her board” coexist with childlike superlatives such as “the awfulest, biggest, blackest spider.” Descriptions rely on straightforward contrasts: granite, marble, servants, and rich food are set against green fields, straw, fresh air, farm work, and simple curds and whey. Repetition is used for emphasis and comic rhythm, especially in lists of servants, prohibitions, and the heroine’s complaints. Historical spellings and typography—including “cheque,” “did n’t,” and “She ’s”—reinforce the antique storybook register.

Sentence structure alternates between flowing explanatory sentences and brisk dialogue. Longer sentences often accumulate details through coordination, particularly when describing Miss Muffet’s privileged confinement or her escape into the country. Shorter exchanges accelerate the action and reveal character efficiently. Dialogue is plainly tagged and easy to follow, with each speaker given a socially distinctive manner: Nurse Holloweg is officious and self-interested, Mrs. Muffet is detached and perfunctory, the farmer and his wife are practical, and Miss Muffet speaks with impulsive candour. The frequent use of “so,” “and,” and “then” gives the prose an oral, storytelling cadence suitable for reading aloud.

Characterization is broad, economical, and satirical rather than psychologically intricate. Miss Muffet begins as sullen, irritable, and physically frail, but the narration makes clear that these traits arise from neglect and excessive restriction. Her escape allows her curiosity, appetite, energy, and desire for independence to emerge. The adults are rendered through defining habits: her father is absorbed in business and clubs, her mother in society and novels, the nurse in rules and convenience, and the doctor in solemn advice and profitable fees. Even the farmer’s wife, though hospitable, is unsentimentally committed to labour. These simplified figures suit the fable-like design.

Humour is produced chiefly through irony, understatement, and social satire. The parents’ emotional neglect is exposed in lines suggesting that Miss Muffet’s mother would be merely “quite disappointed” if the child were lost. The doctor’s final decision to charge an extra-large fee punctures his dignified authority, while the father’s unthinking payment completes the joke. There is also situational comedy in Miss Muffet’s rapid change of heart: she initially delights in farm work but becomes eager to return to her servants once the work proves tiring and a spider appears.

The story’s central pattern is one of corrective experience rather than punishment. Miss Muffet learns that escape and rustic life are not wholly idyllic, while Nurse Holloweg learns that excessive control is dangerous and counterproductive. The resolution depends on negotiation: the child promises not to run away if she is allowed to run, romp, and dig like other children. The closing transformation—rosy cheeks, sparkling eyes, beauty, and robustness—expresses the period’s faith in fresh air, exercise, simplicity, and country life. Overall, the style is warm, lucid, gently didactic, and mischievously critical of affluent adult society, combining nursery-tale familiarity with comic realism and a child-centred argument for freedom and healthy play.

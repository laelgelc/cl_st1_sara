The story uses a highly literate, nineteenth-century realist style infused with frontier romance, social satire, and moral irony. Its omniscient narrator speaks with confident authority, moving freely between external action, psychological interpretation, historical commentary, and broad generalization about human nature. The voice is urbane and sophisticated even when describing a rough mining camp or wilderness setting. This contrast between polished narration and coarse frontier society is central to the story’s effect: the narrator observes violence, prejudice, cowardice, and political hypocrisy with a composure that makes the satire sharper.

The narrative stance is knowingly ironic. Elijah Martin is introduced through the community’s contemptuous judgment as “shiftless” and “worthless,” but the narration steadily exposes the relativity of that judgment. Qualities condemned in Redwood Camp—passivity, gentleness, sobriety, caution, and lack of aggression—become signs of sacred authority among the Minyos and eventually contribute to effective government. The story repeatedly reverses appearances and expectations: cowardice resembles dignity, paralysis becomes endurance, accidental disguise becomes divine legitimacy, and an ineffectual outcast becomes a “born king.” The narrator often states these paradoxes in aphoristic form, as in the observation that “Courage is too often only the memory of past success.” Such maxims raise the tale above a simple adventure plot and give it the character of a social and psychological fable.

The prose favors long, syntactically elaborate sentences built from balanced clauses, interruptions, qualifications, and antitheses. Semicolons, dashes, parenthetical remarks, and carefully staged lists allow the narrator to accumulate detail while continually revising or complicating an initial claim. Parallel constructions are common, especially in counterfactual sequences such as “Had he been…” or in contrasts between what different kinds of men would have done. Sentences often move from concrete description to ironic explanation, ending on a pointed reversal. Short sentences are reserved for emphasis and transition—“He was saved. More than that, he was re-created”—or for moments of sudden realization. This variation gives the prose an alternation between discursive breadth and theatrical impact.

The diction combines formal, sometimes Latinate vocabulary with colloquial frontier speech. Words such as “incompetency,” “contiguity,” “preternaturally,” “perfunctorily,” and “implacable” belong to the narrator’s elevated register, while nicknames such as “Poker Dicks” and “Profane Pete,” expressions such as “no slouch,” “you bet,” and “only a bluff,” and clipped dialogue establish the vernacular world of miners and packers. The resulting register is deliberately mixed: the narrator can describe Elijah as part of the “dramatis personae” of the camp and then report that he was known simply as “that coot.” This mixture produces both historical texture and comedy.

Characterization is primarily analytical rather than impressionistic. Elijah’s psychology is repeatedly explained through combinations of weakness, instinct, vanity, inertia, and adaptability. The narrator does not idealize him after his rise to power; instead, his virtues remain inseparable from his defects. His survival depends on fear, his dignity on immobility, his political success on imitativeness, and his transformation on overheard praise. The story therefore treats identity as socially produced. Redwood Camp’s contempt helps keep Elijah abject, while the Minyos’ faith and the captives’ admiration create the confidence they assume he already possesses. His development is plausible within the tale’s comic framework because it is shown as a feedback loop between reputation and conduct rather than as an abrupt revelation of hidden nobility.

Description is vivid, selective, and often metaphorical. Natural settings are rendered through movement, sound, texture, and light: floodwater scatters the camp among willows, pools, meadows, and tributaries; sunlight enters the sweat-house “as if searching it with fiery spears”; the coast is evoked through rollers, curlews, reeds, warm sands, and the drone of bees. The landscape is not merely decorative. It controls the plot, isolates Elijah, supports the atmosphere of accidental destiny, and reflects his mental state. The flood strips him of his old social identity, while the sea later becomes an image of vague escape and unfulfilled longing. Similes frequently draw on animals, theatre, ritual, and religion, presenting Elijah as an awkward quadrumane, a passive supernumerary, a sacrificial victim, an idol, and finally a ruler.

The story’s humor is dry, cumulative, and often macabre. Redwood Camp’s violence is described through mock-civic language: even cemetery headboards are consulted during an election, stray bullets confer “fleeting honor,” and gunfights are “liberal and broadly comprehensive encounters.” Bureaucracy, frontier masculinity, missionary culture, and government policy are all satirized. The Indian treaty is described as unusually successful because the Indigenous negotiators understand the whites while the whites remain ignorant of them, and the government’s approach is summarized as “half-paternal, half-aggressive, and wholly inconsistent.” Capitalized words such as “FEAR,” “NOT,” and “FROM THE SEA” imitate argumentative or melodramatic emphasis and underscore the narrator’s corrective, polemical tone.

The pacing alternates between summarized history and extended dramatic scenes. Years, reputations, and political developments may be compressed into a paragraph, while decisive episodes—the awakening in the sweat-house, the presentation of captives, or the encounter with Mrs. Dall—are slowed into gestures, glances, fragments of speech, and sensory detail. Major turns are marked by typographical breaks and emphatic declarations. Coincidence drives much of the plot, but it is treated not simply as providence; rather, accidents reveal the instability of social interpretation. Elijah’s declaration that he came “from the river,” his borrowed ceremonial clothing, and his frightened concealment of his face all acquire meanings he never intended.

Dialogue is comparatively sparse and therefore highly functional. Elijah’s limited speech protects his disguise and makes gesture more important than verbal eloquence. The miners’ and packers’ colloquial exchanges provide comic dramatic irony because readers understand the conduct they misinterpret as majesty. Mrs. Dall’s dialogue is brisk, teasing, and self-possessed, introducing a more intimate and destabilizing energy. Her recognition—“That’s no Injun!”—uses blunt vernacular as a sharp scene-ending revelation. Speech is thus used less for sustained conversation than for social labeling, mistaken inference, and sudden shifts in power.

The thematic structure rests on reversals of civilization and savagery, strength and weakness, reality and reputation. The supposedly civilized frontier community is reckless, brutal, and exploitative, while the supposedly “savage” tribe is peaceful, commercially rational, and capable of prosperity. At the same time, the account retains period-specific terminology, stereotypes, and racialized descriptions, including generalized physical and cultural characterizations of Indigenous people. The narrator challenges white supremacist assumptions about violence and civilization, but does so from within a distinctly nineteenth-century colonial vocabulary and occasionally paternalistic perspective.

Overall, the style is expansive, ironic, psychologically observant, and rhetorically balanced. It blends adventure narrative with comic social criticism, uses coincidence as a mechanism of transformation, and repeatedly converts defects into situational virtues. Its signature effect comes from the tension between melodramatic events and the narrator’s controlled, skeptical intelligence: heroism is exposed as partly theatrical, authority as partly misrecognition, and character as something created as much by the beliefs of others as by the individual himself.

The story adopts the manner of an early twentieth-century detective yarn: formal, urbane, and initially leisurely, but increasingly driven by practical observation and suspense. It opens with a first-person frame narrator, Sir Henry, whose cultivated voice establishes the Washington setting and introduces Captain Walker as an efficient but unimaginative head of the Secret Service. Walker then becomes the principal narrator, recounting the Atlantic City case in an extended first-person monologue. This nested structure gives the tale the quality of an anecdote told in a library between experienced professionals, while allowing Sir Henry to voice the reader’s final questions.

The prose alternates between polished literary description and plain procedural exposition. Atmospheric details—winter twilight, shaded library lamps, the vague outlines of Sheridan Circle—are concise and faintly ironic, whereas Walker’s account is factual, sequential, and rich in operational detail. Short emphatic statements such as “The memoir was a report” and “It was a hell of a thing!” punctuate longer explanatory passages. Parenthetical qualifications, rhetorical questions, direct addresses, and pauses repeatedly imitate spoken storytelling. Walker’s precise, even delivery contrasts with the sensational nature of the events and helps preserve an air of plausibility.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization. Sir Henry speaks with educated playfulness and invokes romantic mystery conventions, while Walker uses the blunt vocabulary of law enforcement. The apparent hobo speaks in heavily marked colloquial dialect, with contractions, slang, dropped consonants, comic circumlocutions, and repeated forms of address such as “Governor.” His physical gestures—flourishing a cigarette stub, shuffling, whining, working his mobile nose—are described almost theatrically. The contrast between his degraded appearance and exceptional intelligence produces both comedy and misdirection. Period-specific terms and references, including rolling chairs, Liberty Bonds, “sawbuck,” “sleuthin’,” and contemporary theatrical figures, firmly locate the narrative in its historical milieu.

The narrative is built as a chain of deductions. Each clue is presented visually and then explained in practical terms: the goosestep suggests military service, flattened thumbs suggest tanning, the spacing of footprints indicates a heavy burden, displaced sand distinguishes forward from backward movement, and air bubbles reveal disturbed earth. These explanations give the story an instructional, almost forensic quality. Walker repeatedly doubts the hobo and supplies plausible alternative interpretations, so suspense arises less from physical danger than from the contest between competing readings of evidence. Repetition—especially of the “banker man” description—marks the detail that Walker cannot explain and quietly prepares the revelation.

Pacing is cumulative. The opening frame is reflective, the central account proceeds through increasingly persuasive discoveries, and the recovery of the plates appears to resolve the mystery. The final letter then delivers a compact reversal: the hobo was Mulehaus, and the entire investigation was manipulated so that the criminal could abandon unusable plates yet recover his expenses through the official reward. This ending retroactively transforms apparent drunkenness, servility, and improvisation into calculated performance. The dominant tone is therefore one of dry irony. Institutional expertise is outwitted by theatrical intelligence, social prejudice blinds the detective, and the supposedly worthless derelict proves to be the most accomplished actor and investigator in the story.

The style depends on contrast: romance versus bureaucracy, refinement versus slang, appearance versus identity, professional authority versus intuitive cunning, and dry memoir versus vivid human anecdote. Character is revealed through speech, gesture, and deduction rather than psychological analysis. The central thematic interest lies in disguise as complete embodiment rather than superficial costume, and in the danger of judging intelligence by class, cleanliness, or manner. The final effect is witty, tightly contrived, and lightly satirical, with the elaborate middle narrative serving a crisp, epistolary punch line.

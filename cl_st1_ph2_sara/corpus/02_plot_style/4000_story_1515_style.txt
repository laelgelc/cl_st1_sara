The story uses a highly ornamented comic-Western style, blending frontier adventure, romantic melodrama, local colour, and the carefully engineered irony of a twist tale. Its narrator is omniscient, urbane, and conspicuously present: rather than merely reporting events, the voice interprets characters, addresses the reader, offers witty generalisations, and occasionally suspends realism to frame an incident as fable or legend. Phrases such as “Consider that at that time” and “Let no one ask or presume to tell” create a confidential, storytelling relationship with the audience. The narrator’s cultivated eloquence stands in deliberate contrast to the rough border setting and vernacular speech.

The diction ranges widely. Narrative passages favour formal, Latinate, and mock-heroic language—“painful indemnity,” “propitiatory reporters,” “mandatory pride,” “counterpoise and compensation”—while dialogue reproduces cowboy slang, regional grammar, comic malapropisms, and phonetic accents. Expressions such as “plum got him,” “first rattle,” and “pulled his freight” establish frontier character, while mistakes such as “Triggernometry” turn ignorance into verbal comedy. Mexican speech is rendered through simplified English and Spanish insertions, and the story repeatedly juxtaposes elevated terminology with crude violence or ordinary discomfort. This stylistic collision makes gunfights, saloons, heat, and bravado sound at once heroic and faintly ridiculous.

Sentence structure is generally expansive and rhythmically controlled. Long cumulative sentences carry descriptive catalogues, balanced clauses, appositions, and rhetorical flourishes, often culminating in a joke or ironic qualification. Short sentences and fragments are reserved for revelations, commands, gunfight action, and comic emphasis. Semicolons, dashes, and parenthetical interruptions allow the narrator to digress without losing forward movement. Repetition is used theatrically—“down, down, down,” “Slowly, slowly,” and “Kuku, the great python; Kuku, the magnificent”—to intensify emotion while preserving the manner of an oral raconteur.

Description is vivid, sensuous, and exaggerated. Heat, dust, vegetation, clothing, weapons, and architecture are rendered through dense visual detail, but the descriptions rarely remain neutral. Metaphor and personification transform the landscape into a participant: the town has a “dishonourable fringe” upon its “jowl,” a stream “perished” at the canyon, and a shirt-waist becomes an “oasis.” Character portraits similarly combine physical specificity with theatrical abstraction. Alvarita is described through darkness, fire, castanets, mantillas, and adventure; Buckley’s fear is embodied through constricted breath, dry mouth, cold sweat, and leaden feet. The prose repeatedly enlarges mundane objects and sensations into emblems of personality.

The story’s structure is built around parallel character arcs and a convergence. The opening establishes Buckley as outwardly fearless but inwardly terrified. A substantial shift then introduces Alvarita as outwardly and inwardly fearless, along with the escaped python. Their separate plots move toward the same secluded ravine, where Garcia provides the immediate threat. After their exchanged glance, their defining qualities are symbolically redistributed: Buckley acquires effortless courage, while Alvarita acquires feminine vulnerability. The final caterpillar episode completes the reversal in miniature. Kuku’s unresolved presence functions less as a conventional plot problem than as an ironic witness and agent of poetic revenge.

Characterisation depends strongly on paradox. Buckley is reputed to be the bravest man on the border precisely because he is terrified and continually forces himself into danger. His courage is therefore moral and volitional rather than temperamental. Alvarita’s public identity as a commanding “Queen” is undercut by her ordinary American name, “Alviry,” her homespun mother, and her sudden adoption of romantic shyness. Garcia is both dangerous and comic: a renowned bravo whose disguise succeeds but whose final knife attack is defeated almost instantly. Secondary characters are sketched through a few distinctive traits, especially their speech, and often operate as a comic chorus commenting on Buckley’s reputation.

Dialogue is brisk, idiomatic, and socially differentiating. The rangers’ banter mixes admiration, fatalism, literary misunderstanding, and competitive wit. Buckley speaks tersely and practically, reinforcing his controlled exterior. Alvarita’s early commands are crisp and contemptuous, but after meeting Buckley her speech shifts into coyness and conventional femininity. This change is intentionally conspicuous rather than psychologically subtle; the story treats dialogue as part of a stylised romantic transformation.

Humour arises chiefly from irony, incongruity, hyperbole, understatement, and mock solemnity. A bullfight, cattlemen’s convention, and barbecue are described as “gently social relaxations”; a gunfighter is called a “disturber of the peace”; and Buckley handles his revolvers “as a belle gives the finishing touches to her toilette.” Learned references to Tybalt and Horatius are filtered through cowboys who misremember them. The narrator’s polished phrasing repeatedly deflates masculine posturing, while also affectionately celebrating it. Even the climactic fistfight combines genuine peril with ethnic caricature and a jocular claim about the superiority of the “good Saxon knock-out blow.”

The tone shifts fluidly among suspense, satire, romance, and tall-tale exuberance. Violence is real enough to generate danger, but it is rarely treated with grim naturalism. Instead, the action obeys a “mad code of bravery” associated with legendary frontier masculinity. The romantic episode is likewise presented with both lyrical intensity and knowing artifice. The narrator invites the reader to enjoy the miraculous exchange of courage while recognising its implausibility and conventionality.

The ending is epigrammatic and circular. Alvarita, who confidently handles an eleven-foot python, screams at a two-inch caterpillar; the grand title “Queen of the Serpent Tribe” collapses before an absurdly minor creature. The closing Spanish acclamation gives this comic abdication mock-ceremonial grandeur. Overall, the style is characterised by ornate narration, vernacular dialogue, theatrical contrasts, affectionate caricature, physical and verbal exaggeration, and a final reversal that converts the adventure plot into a witty parable about courage, gender, reputation, and romantic transformation.

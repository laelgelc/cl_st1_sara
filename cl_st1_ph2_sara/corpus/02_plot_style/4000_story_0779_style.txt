The story is written in a late-nineteenth-century regional-realist mode, combining social comedy, romantic irony, and a strong sense of the American West. Its prose is clear, brisk, and character-centred, with practical details of banking, cycling, local society, and landscape grounding the emotional plot. The western setting is not merely decorative: its heat, wind, distances, business culture, and relative informality shape Tommy’s identity and provide a pointed contrast with the more refined but less capable eastern type represented by Jessica and Harper.

The narration uses a confident third-person omniscient voice that moves freely between exposition, social observation, and access to individual thoughts. The narrator is conspicuously present, often offering amused generalisations about gender, class, regional customs, and human attachment. Phrases such as “Needless to say” and comments on what people “in the West” expect give the voice a conversational, knowing authority. Although the narration sympathises most deeply with Tommy, it maintains a lightly ironic distance from everyone, exposing self-deception without becoming cruel.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization. Tommy’s speech is direct, colloquial, teasing, and rich in practical judgment; she uses contractions, western idiom, comic understatement, and deliberately unsentimental phrasing. Her verbal toughness frequently conceals emotional vulnerability. Harper’s manner is more formal, hesitant, and decorous, while Jessica’s speech is exclamatory and romantic. The Old Boys speak in humorous, proverbial formulations that function almost like a communal chorus. These sharply differentiated voices allow social type, temperament, and emotional position to emerge without lengthy psychological explanation.

The diction mixes standard literary narration with colloquialisms such as “weak brother,” “gone at sixes and sevens,” “uppish grade,” and “fallen by the wayside.” Concrete period details—white carnations, eyeglasses, gas jets, telegrams, bank ledgers, billiards, cocktails, and bicycles—create historical texture. The prose also uses humorous labels and diminutives, including “little muff,” “little white rabbit,” and “the seven Old Boys,” which reinforce its tone of affectionate satire. Occasional ethnic dialect is rendered phonetically, reflecting period conventions and social attitudes.

Sentence structure varies according to function. Expository passages often use long, loosely coordinated sentences that accumulate biographical and social information, while dialogue and action scenes rely on shorter, sharper units. Parenthetical qualifications and balanced contrasts are frequent, especially when the narrator describes Tommy’s contradictory qualities: masculine yet emotionally exposed, logical yet foolish in love, socially unconventional yet deeply loyal. Repetition and parallelism sharpen key ideas, particularly the contrast between “her kind” and “his sort,” or between practical capability and romantic desirability.

Characterization is built through antithesis. Tommy is capable, blunt, athletic, economically competent, and coded as masculine; Jessica is pale, languid, perfumed, sheltered, and conventionally feminine. Harper is aesthetically polished but professionally helpless. The story repeatedly overturns expected relations between appearance and value: the supposedly unfeminine woman performs the decisive rescue, while the ornamental man and delicate woman remain the socially compatible pair. Tommy’s competence is admired, but it does not protect her from emotional loss. This produces the story’s characteristic blend of comedy and pathos.

The plot is structured around escalating contrasts and a final act of renunciation. A broad expository middle establishes the triangular relationship and the town’s social network, after which the bank run compresses the pace and converts emotional conflict into physical action. The bicycle ride is the principal set piece, narrated through tactile and visual imagery: scorched fields, dancing bluffs, hidden cattle, locust noise, brass-like sun, and furnace-hot wind. The harsh landscape tests character directly, stripping Jessica of romantic posturing and displaying Tommy’s endurance. The rescue itself is handled with comic coolness, which heightens rather than diminishes Tommy’s sacrifice.

Imagery tends to be concrete, kinetic, and ironic. The wind comes “like the sweep of cavalry,” the bluffs perform “serpentines and skirt dances,” and Jessica collapses “like a little white rabbit.” Such comparisons are vivid but accessible, often drawing on movement, animals, performance, or battle. The white carnation serves as the central symbolic object: initially it signifies Harper’s ornamental fastidiousness, and at the end it becomes a token of Tommy’s unrequited attachment. Her act of dropping it into the grate provides a restrained visual climax.

The prevailing tone is amused, worldly, and affectionate, with an undercurrent of melancholy. Sentiment is repeatedly checked by jokes, understatement, and self-mockery. Tommy refuses elevated romantic language even at the moment of greatest pain, and the narration follows her example. The ending therefore avoids overt lament: the shrug, the discarded flower, and the comic complaint about men communicate grief indirectly. This emotional indirection is essential to the style. Vulnerability appears through gestures, pauses, dropped objects, and ironic remarks rather than through explicit confession.

Overall, the style depends on regional detail, socially observant omniscience, lively colloquial dialogue, gender-based contrast, and an ironic balance between romantic feeling and practical action. Its most distinctive effect comes from treating competence as both Tommy’s strength and her tragic disadvantage: she can save the bank, protect Harper, and arrange his future, but she cannot make herself the kind of woman he instinctively desires.

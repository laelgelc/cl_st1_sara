A shy, impressionable young man with a quiet life and a happy engagement to Delia is unwillingly appointed dramatic critic for a newspaper, despite never having attended the theatre. His first performance shocks him with its exaggerated gestures, artificial speech, and flamboyant emotions. Because he is unusually susceptible to vivid impressions, repeated theatre visits cause him unconsciously to imitate the actors’ mannerisms.

His natural personality is gradually buried beneath theatrical poses, gestures, and speech. Though he recognizes the absurd transformation and repeatedly tries to resign, his forceful editor continually distracts or overrules him. Delia becomes embarrassed and repelled by his behavior. When she confronts him, his attempt to explain his condition emerges as yet another melodramatic performance, and she ends their engagement.

The narrator continues to grow increasingly theatrical in his appearance, language, relationships, and conduct. Unable to recover his former identity or escape his job, he concludes that ordinary society will soon regard him as insane or drunk. He fears that his only remaining option is to abandon his old self, adopt a stage name, and become an actor—the very profession and behavior he detests.

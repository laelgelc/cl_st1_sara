The story adopts the elevated, gently didactic style of a nineteenth-century literary fairy tale. Its omniscient narrator speaks with calm authority but remains accessible and intimate, occasionally using inclusive formulations such as “we wake by day” and conversational asides such as “You see.” This creates the atmosphere of an oral storyteller explaining a moral wonder to both children and adults. The tone blends tenderness, melancholy, reverence, and quiet consolation: death is repeatedly presented as natural and peaceful, while Christian ideas of redemption and eternal life ultimately transform loss into spiritual elevation.

Personification is the dominant technique. The oak thinks, speaks, remembers, dreams, yearns, and possesses an almost human heart, while flies, storms, flowers, birds, and other parts of nature are given voices and intentions. This animation of the natural world makes abstract questions about mortality and eternity concrete. The oak and the Ephemera function as contrasting symbolic figures: one measures life in centuries, the other in moments, yet the ending reveals that both lives are equally brief when viewed from a larger temporal or divine perspective. The oak’s final uprooting literally fulfils the liberation imagined in its dream, allowing physical destruction and spiritual release to coincide.

The prose is descriptive, lyrical, and sensuously abundant. Long catalogues of flowers, fragrances, birds, historical figures, sounds, and colours create a richly inhabited woodland: “clover-fields and wild roses,” “elder-blossoms and honeysuckle,” knights, lovers, butterflies, bells, and birds all accumulate into a panoramic vision. Visual imagery is especially strong, with foliage like “beaten copper plates,” clouds like “large white swans,” and grass like a “long, green ribbon.” Smell and sound are also prominent, producing an almost synaesthetic fullness. Nature is not merely scenery but a spiritual language through which transience, memory, fellowship, and transcendence are expressed.

Sentence structure alternates between expansive periodic narration and short, emphatic dialogue. The long sentences often build through coordination, repetition, and cumulative clauses, mirroring growth, abundance, and visionary ascent. By contrast, brief exchanges—“We are here, we are here” or “All—all are here”—supply rhythmic intensity and emotional clarity. Repetition has an incantatory quality: “Sleep sweetly,” “higher and higher,” “both great and small,” and “good-night, good-night” resemble lullabies, prayers, or ritual responses. Exclamation marks, direct address, rhetorical questions, and archaic constructions such as “anon,” “drew nigh,” and “hadst”-era formal rhythms contribute to the antique storybook register.

The narrative is carefully shaped around analogies and cyclical patterns. A year in the oak’s existence is compared with a human day, while the Ephemera’s single day is treated as a complete lifetime. Spring becomes morning, summer noon, autumn evening, and winter night. The opening philosophical conversation establishes this system of relative time; the middle sections broaden it through seasonal history and memory; and the climactic dream gathers all earlier images into a vision of universal ascent. The conclusion then closes the structural circle: the oak’s 365 years pass “as the single day of the Ephemera.” This symmetrical design gives the story the force of a parable.

The dream sequence shifts the style from pastoral realism into visionary, almost apocalyptic fantasy. Spatial movement repeatedly turns upward: the oak rises above the clouds, sees the stars, draws the forest after it, and finally feels its roots loosen. The language becomes increasingly ecstatic, using intensifiers, parallel constructions, and images of light, glory, song, and collective reunion. Crucially, the oak cannot be satisfied with solitary transcendence; it wishes every tree, flower, and humble blade of grass to share its happiness. The spiritual ideal is therefore communal rather than individual, and the repeated inclusion of “both great and small” gives the vision an egalitarian moral emphasis.

Dialogue serves less to create psychologically complex characters than to stage philosophical positions. The Ephemera represents complete absorption in present joy, while the oak initially regards duration as the measure of value. Their exchange is simple, balanced, and Socratic, allowing the fly’s innocent reasoning to undermine the oak’s assumptions. Irony emerges when the supposedly enduring oak eventually dies as suddenly, from a divine or cosmic perspective, as the fly. The story does not treat either character cynically; instead, it validates the fly’s delight in the present and the oak’s eventual longing for eternity.

The ending combines pathetic natural imagery, public memorial, and explicit Christian symbolism. Christmas bells, the surviving ship, festive flags, prayer, and the carol about redemption interpret the oak’s fall as more than a natural death. The sailors’ short “funeral oration” supplies a restrained human perspective, while the narrator draws the theological parallel between the carol’s promise and the oak’s final dream. The result is sentimental but controlled: sorrow over impermanence is balanced by faith in continuity beyond earthly measurement.

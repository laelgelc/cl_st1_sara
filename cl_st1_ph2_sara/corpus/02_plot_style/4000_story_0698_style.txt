The story uses a formal, late nineteenth-century Gothic style tempered by urbane irony and sceptical wit. Its language is elevated but generally lucid, combining atmospheric description with the detached precision of a report. Archaic or period-specific expressions such as “stout-hearted lad,” “bodiless malevolences,” and “venturesome boys” establish historical distance, while concrete sensory details—the unglazed window, the dead-yellow face, the screech-owl’s cry, the pool of saliva beneath the corpse—give the supernatural framework a physical and often unsettling immediacy.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, moving between external observation, characters’ thoughts, and authorial commentary. The narrator repeatedly interrupts suspense with dry generalisations and sardonic asides, as in the suggestion that a deserted house in a rural region is virtually equivalent to a haunted one, or that rumours of vice are the revenge dull minds take on genius. This creates a characteristic doubleness: the narrative invites fear while simultaneously examining, mocking, and manipulating the mechanisms by which fear is produced. The voice remains controlled even when describing terror or death, and its composure makes the grotesque details more disturbing.

Sentence structure varies according to function. Descriptive passages often use long, balanced, carefully subordinated sentences that delay key information and accumulate tension. Shorter declarative sentences punctuate moments of revelation or action: “He believed the man to be dead,” “The boy took to his heels,” and “Here was the dead body of a man.” Dashes, semicolons, colons, and parenthetical qualifications are frequent. Dashes in particular reproduce agitation, emphasize reversals, or isolate ominous details. Parallel constructions and rhetorical triads lend the prose an oratorical quality, especially in Colston’s argument about the proper conditions for reading a ghost story.

The opening relies on classic Gothic scenery: midnight, a dense forest, a lost traveller, an abandoned house, a single candle, and a figure whose status as living or dead is uncertain. Suspense develops through restricted perception. Darkness reduces sight to touch, the distant light is first glimpsed through foliage, and the boy sees the seated man through an empty window frame. Information is disclosed incrementally, with each spatial advance bringing a more alarming image. Sound is used sparingly but forcefully: the audible heartbeat, the owl’s shriek, the whip-poor-will, and the beetle’s “roaring wings” break otherwise oppressive silence.

The narrative architecture is nonlinear and highly controlled. It begins with the apparent climax, moves backward under the heading “THE DAY BEFORE” to explain the circumstances, then advances to “THE DAY AFTER” for the discovery of the body. The embedded “MANUSCRIPT” introduces another textual layer, while the closing attribution to “THE TIMES” imitates documentary evidence. These frames create an illusion of factual authentication while withholding the central ghost story itself. The reader never sees the supposedly lethal passage, only its consequences and its threatening postscript. This strategic omission preserves mystery and forces the imagination to supply what the text refuses to reveal.

Dialogue occupies the central section and is more expansive, argumentative, and theatrical than the surrounding narration. Colston speaks in rhetorical questions, analogies, emphatic repetitions, and escalating claims. His insistence on solitude, night, and candlelight functions both as literary criticism and as preparation for the plot. Marsh’s replies are comparatively plain, sceptical, and teasing, giving the exchange the shape of a contest between artistic vanity and practical disbelief. The challenge-and-dare structure converts an abstract debate about readerly susceptibility into a fatal experiment.

The tone moves among ominous, philosophical, comic, and macabre registers without fully settling into any one of them. Humour frequently appears at the edge of horror: the boy’s belated penitence is described as proportionate to its uselessness; his solemn pride beside the corpse is treated with mock-heroic dignity; and “Scepticism apologizing to Truth” elevates a rustic compliment into an abstract moral event. Such wit does not dispel the supernatural atmosphere. Instead, it makes the story’s worldview more unstable by refusing to tell the reader whether events should be understood as ghostly intervention, psychological suggestion, coincidence, or some combination of them.

Characterisation is economical and functional. The boy is defined by inherited frontier courage, physical fear, and stubborn persistence. Marsh embodies rational self-confidence and becomes vulnerable chiefly because his courage is challenged. Colston appears intense, pale, brilliant, and possibly unstable; rumours of opium use introduce a plausible natural explanation without resolving anything. Secondary figures are rendered collectively as sceptical investigators, except for Breede’s son-in-law, whose destruction of the manuscript adds a final act of concealment and prevents definitive interpretation.

The story’s dominant themes are fear, suggestion, artistic power, scepticism, and the obligations of the reader. Reading is presented not as passive reception but as a psychologically dangerous act requiring the proper mood and environment. Colston’s theory that an author may induce terror if the reader makes himself “accessible” to it becomes the story’s governing principle. The manuscript may possess supernatural power, but Marsh’s expectation of a midnight visitor may itself be enough to kill him. The fiction therefore turns reading into both subject and weapon, while the missing central text makes the audience experience the same imaginative vulnerability that Colston describes.

Overall, the style is distinguished by atmospheric economy, elaborate narrative framing, precise macabre imagery, ironic authorial intrusion, and a tightly engineered final ambiguity. It constructs terror less through explicit supernatural spectacle than through anticipation, withheld information, temporal rearrangement, and the collision between rational explanation and suggestive circumstance.

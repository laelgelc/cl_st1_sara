The story adopts a highly conversational first-person style, presented as an anecdote told by an amiable, leisured clubman. The narrator, Reggie Pepper, addresses the reader directly with phrases such as “don’t you know,” “mind you,” and “believe me,” repeatedly digresses, corrects himself, and comments on his own storytelling. This creates the impression of spontaneous speech rather than polished literary narration, despite the careful comic construction beneath it. His claim that he cannot supply “literary style” is itself ironic: the narrative possesses a strongly controlled and distinctive comic voice.

The diction mixes upper-class British slang, sporting language, and colloquial exaggeration. Expressions such as “old top,” “silly ass,” “rotter,” “chump,” “topping,” “rummy,” “in the soup,” and “bucked him up” establish an early twentieth-century social milieu of clubs, inherited money, polo, theatres, hotels, and idle young men. Sporting metaphors are especially frequent: intelligence is discussed through golf handicaps, unpleasant experiences are “beaten in a canter,” clues are “treed,” and ideas are flushed from the “undergrowth.” Formal or elevated language is often placed beside slang for comic incongruity, as in “Marriage, the dynamite of the soul” or the description of Bobbie receiving a thunderbolt “in the thorax.”

The syntax reinforces the narrator’s oral manner. Sentences often proceed through interruptions, dashes, parenthetical qualifications, fragments, and afterthoughts. Short emphatic units—“He married,” “Gone!” “Think.”—punctuate longer passages and sharpen the timing of jokes. Repetition is used rhythmically, particularly in dialogue, while lists accumulate increasingly absurd details. The narrator frequently builds toward an apparently serious conclusion and then undercuts it with a clipped aside or bathos. Phrases such as “Quite the romance,” “most interesting,” and “not” operate as deadpan reversals, allowing the narrator’s understated commentary to carry much of the humour.

The dominant mode is comic hyperbole. Bobbie is not merely forgetful but requires letters, telegrams, telephone calls, and a taxi-borne messenger to reach a dinner engagement. Mary’s hair weighs “a ton and a half,” Reggie wants to be “eight hundred miles away,” and her eyes pass through him like bullets through butter. Similes are deliberately elaborate and unexpected: Bobbie looks “as merry and bright as a lonely gum-drop at an Eskimo tea-party,” while his experience of marriage is compared to a bull-pup chasing a bee. These images begin from a recognisable emotion but render it absurdly concrete. Personification works similarly: Nature is imagined as proudly manufacturing chumps and equipping them with protective armour, while money “parts as if it enjoyed doing it.”

The humour also depends on the contrast between the narrator’s confidence and his limited understanding. Reggie considers himself an astute observer and dispenser of moral wisdom, but his interpretations are often naïve, self-serving, or disastrously incomplete. He can diagnose Bobbie’s marriage yet fails to understand Mary’s emotional change of heart. His repeated insistence on his good motives makes the final reversal more effective. The narrative therefore uses an unreliable but not deceptive narrator: Reggie reports events candidly, while the reader is invited to perceive more than he does.

Dialogue is brisk, idiomatic, and structured around misunderstanding. Exchanges frequently consist of short questions and literal answers, with comedy arising from incompatible assumptions. Bobbie’s inability to narrow his wife’s birthday beyond the calendar year is typical of this technique. Reggie’s mock-detective interrogation turns domestic forgetfulness into a parody of a Sherlock Holmes investigation, complete with clues, archives, deductions, restoratives, and false solutions. The serious form of detective reasoning is applied to a trivial but emotionally consequential mystery, generating both farce and narrative momentum.

Characterization is economical and caricatural but internally consistent. Bobbie is defined by good nature, privilege, and monumental absent-mindedness; Mary by competence, determination, pride, and genuine affection; Reggie by idleness, loyalty, vanity, and social obtuseness. Repeated motifs substitute for extensive psychological description: Bobbie’s memory, Mary’s hair and chin, and Reggie’s clubland observations become comic shorthand. Nevertheless, the characters are not wholly mechanical. Bobbie’s anxiety demonstrates sincere love, Mary’s anger reveals that she cannot bear the suffering produced by her own scheme, and Reggie’s sympathy makes him more than a detached spectator.

The plot is episodic but tightly escalated. It begins with a broad character sketch, moves through the forgotten anniversary and lesser lapses, then culminates in Mary’s disappearance and the search for her birthday. Each episode intensifies the same central flaw. Apparent digressions—astrological booklets, theatrical cats, cheque stubs, frozen feet—ultimately contribute either clues or comic complications. Section breaks mark stages in Bobbie’s education and help the anecdote retain the pace of a serial comic narrative.

Suspense is managed through strategic withholding. The narrator appears to omit the exact text of Mary’s letter after announcing it, and the later telephone conversation reveals that Reggie and Mary had conspired to frighten Bobbie into reform. This delayed disclosure retrospectively alters the meaning of the investigation. The reader first experiences the situation largely from Bobbie’s perspective, then discovers that the crisis was engineered, only for Mary to turn on Reggie and make him the final victim. The story’s climax is therefore not Bobbie’s successful deduction but Reggie’s failure to predict the emotional consequences of the scheme.

The thematic core concerns the mismatch between male and female expectations within marriage, though it is treated as social comedy rather than realistic domestic drama. Bobbie believes love should excuse failures of attention; Mary believes love must be demonstrated through remembrance and considerate action. Reggie reduces this conflict to a supposed universal rule—women “want to be shown”—but the ending exposes the inadequacy of his tidy formulations. Friendship, loyalty, and good intentions are similarly treated with irony: Reggie helps Bobbie, but also enjoys the spectacle and lectures him at every opportunity.

The “Moral Lesson” is both genuine and parodic. Bobbie learns attentiveness only through emotional shock, supporting the narrator’s theory that he needs “dynamite.” Yet the stronger final lesson belongs to Reggie: acting from the best motives offers no protection against blame, especially when one meddles in a marriage. The epitaph—“He was a man who acted from the best motives. There is one born every minute”—closes the story with self-mockery, adapting the language of gullibility to Reggie’s benevolent foolishness. Overall, the style combines clubland raconteur narration, verbal exuberance, farcical plotting, comic similes, ironic moralizing, and precise reversals, producing a tone that is buoyant, affectionate, and persistently self-undermining.

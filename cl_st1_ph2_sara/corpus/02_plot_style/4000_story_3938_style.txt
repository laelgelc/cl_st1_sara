The story adopts the mode of a literary fairy tale or moral fable, combining an apparently simple quest narrative with symbolic characterization, lyrical description, and a sharply ironic conclusion. Its setting is deliberately timeless and idealized: gardens, rose-trees, moonlight, princes, balls, jewels, and personified creatures create a dreamlike world rather than a socially realistic one. The atmosphere moves from romantic longing through nocturnal beauty and sacrificial tragedy to comic disillusionment.

The narration is third-person and omniscient, but it frequently yields the foreground to direct speech. Characters reveal themselves less through psychological analysis than through stylized declarations: the Student speaks in romantic formulas and later in abstract, utilitarian judgments; the Nightingale speaks with devotional eloquence; and the Professor’s daughter expresses material vanity in brisk, dismissive language. The narrator generally maintains an elegant, detached manner, allowing the contrast between words and actions to generate irony. Readers understand the Nightingale’s sacrifice and the rose’s value while the Student remains incapable of doing so.

The prose is highly lyrical and ornamental. It relies on sensuous colour imagery—red, white, yellow, crimson, gold, pearl, silver, blue, and purple—and on comparisons drawn from flowers, precious stones, the sea, dawn, and celestial bodies. Similes are abundant and often elaborately balanced: hair is compared to hyacinth blossom, a face to pale ivory, the Moon to crystal, and the developing rose to mist, morning, silver wings, and reflected shadows. Capitalization of abstractions and natural entities such as Love, Life, Philosophy, Power, Moon, and Tree gives them allegorical weight. Nature is extensively personified: trees speak, the Moon listens, Echo carries music, and reeds transmit its message to the sea.

Sentence structure alternates between compact dialogue and long, rhythmically coordinated passages. Polysyndeton—the repeated use of “and”—creates a biblical or incantatory cadence, especially during the Nightingale’s sacrifice. Parallel constructions and inversions lend the prose a formal, archaic elegance, as in formulations such as “what is joy to me, to him is pain” or “crimson as a ruby was the heart.” The diction is elevated but accessible, mixing fairy-tale simplicity with poetic compounds and ceremonial phrasing: “heart’s-blood,” “ocean-cavern,” “top-most spray,” and “chariot of pearl.” Recurrent exclamations and rhetorical questions heighten emotional intensity.

Repetition is the principal organizing device. “Give me a red rose,” “the Tree shook its head,” “press closer, little Nightingale,” and “all night long she sang” function as refrains. The Nightingale’s visits to the three rose-trees form a traditional three-part fairy-tale sequence, while each tree’s response follows nearly the same syntactic pattern with a different colour and set of images. During the climactic song, repetition charts escalation: the thorn moves deeper, the song grows louder and wilder, the rose changes from white to pink to crimson, and the bird’s voice becomes “fainter and fainter.” This patterned recurrence gives the story the quality of an oral tale, song, or ritual.

The characterization is symbolic and deliberately polarized. The Nightingale represents idealized art, love, faith, and self-sacrifice; the Student represents book learning without emotional understanding; the girl and her aristocratic suitor represent materialism and social status. Even minor creatures embody attitudes: the Lizard is a cynic, while the Butterfly and Daisy echo superficial incomprehension. Characters are therefore types as much as individuals, and their capitalized labels—“Student,” “Nightingale,” “Tree”—reinforce the allegorical design.

The tonal structure depends on the collision between sincerity and irony. The Nightingale’s language treats Love as sacred and more valuable than life, while the Student repeatedly mistakes abstraction for understanding. His criticism that the bird is “all style, without any sincerity” is dramatic irony of the strongest kind, since she is about to die for another. Further irony arises when he calls the miraculous rose “a wonderful piece of luck,” imagines that its distinction lies in having a Latin name, and discards it once it fails to secure the dance. The ending reverses the opening: the Student begins by believing love makes life meaningful and ends by dismissing it as impractical and retreating into Metaphysics.

The pacing is carefully staged. The opening establishes the problem quickly through lament and repetition; the middle expands into a quest; the moonlit sacrifice slows into richly descriptive, ceremonial prose; and the final social encounter proceeds rapidly toward rejection and anticlimax. The death of the Nightingale is treated with tragic grandeur, but the destruction of the rose is abrupt and mundane: it falls into a gutter and is crushed by a cart-wheel. This contrast converts pathos into satire without erasing the beauty of the sacrifice.

Overall, the style is ornate, musical, symbolic, and epigrammatically ironic. It draws on fairy-tale repetition, Romantic nature imagery, Decadent aestheticism, Christian-like sacrificial symbolism, and social satire. Its central stylistic tension lies between lyrical beauty and emotional blindness: art and love create something miraculous, but practical, status-conscious human beings cannot recognize its meaning.

The story adopts an elevated, archaic, heavily allusive Gothic style, combining historical romance, moral fable, and speculative fiction. Its omniscient narrator speaks with the authority of a chronicler while repeatedly qualifying the record through phrases such as “it was thought,” “some averred,” and “uncertainty falls on what now followed.” This tension between documentary confidence and hearsay preserves the central mystery: events receive a rational mechanical explanation, yet the narrative never wholly dispels their supernatural aura.

The diction is ornate and deliberately antiquated. Words and constructions such as “nigh,” “ere long,” “aught,” “naught,” “cometh,” “twain,” “durst,” and “it were but natural” create a pseudo-biblical and early modern register. Technical vocabulary relating to casting, architecture, clocks, and machinery sits beside religious, mythological, and artistic language. Bannadonna is understood through references to Babel, Shinar, Noah, Anak, the Titans, Vulcan, Prometheus, Talus, Haman, Sisera, Jael, and Deborah. These allusions enlarge a local engineering project into a universal drama of creation, rebellion, pride, and punishment.

The prose is syntactically elaborate. Long periodic sentences accumulate subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, semicolon-linked expansions, and balanced antitheses before arriving at their principal point. In moments of emphasis, however, the narrative turns to short fragments and repetitions: “Higher, higher”; “No response”; “Again the mortar”; “So the creator was killed by the creature.” Dashes frequently interrupt or redirect thought, especially in dialogue, where they suggest evasion, unease, correction, or concealed meaning. Inversions and unusual word order further heighten the ceremonial tone. The style often favors abstraction and nominal phrasing over direct statement, making the narration feel meditative as well as dramatic.

Imagery is monumental, metallic, and organic at once. The tower is introduced as the stump of an immeasurable fallen pine, its ruined extension a fixed shadow across the plain; bells become silver-throated birds housed in a “metallic aviary”; molten metal bays like unleashed hounds; the automaton glitters like a dragon-beetle. Such sustained metaphors blur distinctions between architecture, nature, animals, and machines. This is crucial to the story’s imaginative logic: Bannadonna attempts to make mechanism imitate life, while the prose itself repeatedly animates inert matter and reduces human beings to mechanical gestures.

The atmosphere develops through foreshadowing and symbolic recurrence. Images of falling, prostration, flaws, severed clasps, concealed surfaces, and unstable heights appear well before the catastrophe. The blood-tainted casting anticipates the bell’s fatal weakness; Una’s ominous face anticipates Bannadonna’s death beneath her hand; the unexplained footfalls and moving cloak prepare the revelation of the automaton. Even casual phrases carry double meanings. Bannadonna’s assurance that no “soul” remains above technically excludes, without denying, the presence of his soulless creation, while his statement that the stroke of one will “sever that loved clasp” predicts both the mechanism’s action and his own severance from life.

Characterization is indirect and emblematic. Bannadonna is defined less by psychological intimacy than by posture, speech, reputation, and symbolic association. He stands alone above the crowd, hides his work, treats murder as an incident of artistic passion, and speaks with ostentatious humility that barely conceals contempt. His “Vulcanic face” and forge-like inward brightness present him as both inspired craftsman and impious creator. The magistrates function partly as moral and interpretive witnesses: their uneasiness registers dangers that civic enthusiasm and Bannadonna’s pride refuse to acknowledge. Dialogue is formal and ceremonious, but its politeness is edged with irony, social tension, and suppressed fear.

The narrative structure moves from panoramic description to public history, then into Gothic suspense, catastrophe, retrospective explanation, and final moral reckoning. Early sections proceed slowly, dwelling on construction, ceremony, and reputation. The veiled object and the magistrates’ visit narrow the focus and intensify suspense. Bannadonna’s death is disclosed as a tableau before being reconstructed causally, allowing horror to precede explanation. A second catastrophe—the bell’s collapse—reveals the concealed casting defect, and the earthquake finally destroys the tower itself. Each ending enlarges the previous one: first the inventor falls, then his masterpiece, then the entire monument.

The narrator’s tone mixes grandeur, irony, and moral severity. Bannadonna’s achievement is genuinely admired, but admiration is steadily undercut by hints of monstrosity. Civic and religious institutions are also treated ironically: homicide is excused as aesthetic passion, absolution quiets conscience, and public celebration rewards dangerous excess. The final sequence abandons ambiguity for aphoristic parallelism: slave and lord, creature and creator, bell and tower, blood and flaw, pride and fall. This cadence gives the conclusion the force of a biblical proverb or tragic chorus.

Overall, the style is baroque, symbolic, and rhetorically dense. It uses archaic diction, biblical cadence, mythic allusion, extended metaphor, ominous dialogue, and layered foreshadowing to transform a tale of mechanical failure into a parable about artistic egotism, technological domination, moral corruption, and creation turning against its creator.

The story is a period crime mystery written in a brisk, plot-driven mode, combining the atmosphere of an English clubland detective tale with the directness of American popular fiction. Its governing technique is misdirection: two apparently unrelated transactions—the coded notice about “nine hundred horses” and Hargrave’s purchase of sapphires with gold coins—proceed in parallel until a piece of counterfeit money reveals that “horses” is criminal slang. The narrative is therefore constructed backward from a final verbal and material coincidence, with earlier details planted so that they acquire new significance at the close.

The narration is third-person and largely follows Douglas Hargrave’s perspective, although the narrator frequently steps back to supply background, social commentary, or corrective explanation. The voice is confident, conversational, and mildly intrusive, using phrases such as “as has been said,” “the fact is,” and “of course” to address the reader almost as a storyteller speaking aloud. This produces an oral, anecdotal quality. The narrator sometimes anticipates objections, explains professional customs, or contrasts romantic convention with practical reality, especially in the discussion of jewel dealers. The result is less psychologically intimate than expository: characters are understood through occupation, behavior, social type, and role in the mechanism of the plot.

The prose favors plain declarative sentences, frequent repetition, and concrete physical actions. Short statements—“Then he went out,” “A steward entered,” “It is impossible”—often mark transitions or sharpen dramatic beats. These are mixed with longer explanatory paragraphs that establish logistics, professional knowledge, travel constraints, prices, and commercial procedure. Repeated words and constructions create emphasis: the message is “mystery” in every direction; Mrs. Farmingham is repeatedly called “dominant,” “aggressive,” “energetic,” and “impatient”; the French bankers are repeatedly described as “stupid” precisely because they have obeyed instructions. This repetition can feel blunt, but it also plants the terms needed for the final reversal.

The diction is accessible and concrete, with a strong period flavor. British institutions and expressions—“Baronet,” “tweed coat,” “my word,” “lift,” “sovereigns,” “personal column,” and “C. I. D.”—sit beside American commercial speech such as “O. K.,” “junk,” “mighty young,” “on my own hook,” and “how-de-do.” The contrast helps distinguish English officialdom from American business energy. There is also specialized vocabulary from jewel dealing, banking, shipping, ciphers, and counterfeiting. Prices, commissions, routes, hotels, newspapers, and deadlines are specified in detail, giving the sensational plot a documentary surface. Some ethnic and national labels, assumptions, and stereotypes are conspicuously dated, reflecting the conventions and prejudices of early twentieth-century popular fiction.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization and exposition. Sir Henry speaks in emphatic exclamations and clubman slang, while Hargrave is restrained, courteous, and professionally precise. Mrs. Farmingham’s speech is clipped, imperative, and transactional. She interrupts, dismisses explanations, demands certainty, and reduces people to their usefulness: “Get in,” “Come along,” and “I don’t buy junk.” Her verbal force establishes the persona later exposed as a criminal disguise. Major Mikos, by contrast, speaks with ceremonious melancholy and syntactic stiffness, presenting himself as an aristocratic foreigner compelled to sell a family treasure. The criminologist’s multilingual exclamations provide a lightly comic version of continental expertise. Dialogue is seldom subtle; it identifies social position and advances the puzzle efficiently.

Characterization is typological rather than deeply interior. Hargrave is the practical young professional whose competence makes him useful but whose assumptions make him vulnerable. Sir Henry is the energetic official investigator temporarily baffled by an unfamiliar cipher. Mrs. Farmingham is drawn through recurring gestures, especially the motion “as of one who cracks a dog whip,” which conveys command, impatience, and aggression. Major Mikos is assembled from costume, accent, pallor, decorations, and an appeal to family honor. These figures are designed to be recognized quickly, and their apparent identities matter more than complex emotional development because the story depends on impersonation and deception.

The setting is atmospheric but economical. London is rendered through Piccadilly Circus, Trafalgar Square, the Strand, Bond Street, hotels, clubs, carriages, and a persistent yellow fog. The fog functions both as period-local color and as a metaphor for obscurity: it muffles sounds, enlarges moving shapes, enters the room at the moment of revelation, and visually links London’s physical uncertainty with the hidden criminal scheme. Interiors are similarly functional. The polished colony room suggests official respectability; the cheap hotel sitting room supports the invented urgency of the sapphire sale; Hargrave’s private room becomes the place where the separate plots converge.

The pacing follows a sequence of clearly marked episodes. The opening presents the cipher and establishes a question. Sir Henry then leaves, allowing Hargrave to enter a second mystery whose relevance is initially concealed. The extended jewel transaction slows the narrative with apparently practical detail, but almost every element—the need for gold, the exact sum, the hurried departure, the banking arrangements, and the impersonated social identity—serves the fraud. The return of Sir Henry accelerates the story toward a double solution. First the cipher yields “London”; then the counterfeit coin transforms “nine hundred horses” from a literal shipment into criminal merchandise. The ending arrives abruptly at recognition rather than continuing into pursuit or arrest, making the intellectual reversal the true climax.

Suspense depends heavily on dramatic irony created after the fact. On a first reading, the repeated insistence on gold appears to be merely part of an eccentric transaction. Once the “horse” is identified as a counterfeit twenty-dollar piece, the reader retrospectively understands why the criminals needed genuine gold for Major Mikos while unloading a case of plated counterfeits onto Hargrave. The sapphires serve as convincing bait because Hargrave’s expert judgment is genuine: he is not deceived about the stones, only about the people, the money, and the larger structure of the exchange. This is characteristic of the story’s logic, in which professional competence coexists with social gullibility.

The tone blends seriousness, irony, and light comedy. The international counterfeiting operation creates genuine stakes, but the story repeatedly amuses itself with national manners, professional vanity, and emphatic personalities. The central joke is that Mrs. Farmingham ridicules the French bank for doing exactly what she requested, while that exact obedience enables the fraud. Another irony lies in Hargrave’s early reflection that jewel buyers do not experience romantic adventures; he is already inside one. The closing recognition—“One of the nine hundred horses!”—turns an ordinary coin into the solution of the entire puzzle with theatrical economy.

Mechanically, the text shows features of older magazine prose and possibly imperfect transcription. There are occasional awkward commas, inconsistent spellings or names, typographical slips, and malformed phrases, including “news papers,” “Picadilly,” “genera,” and shifts between “Don Carlow” and “Don Carlos.” Such irregularities are not central stylistic choices, though they contribute to the archival feel. The essential style remains lucid, repetitive, externally focused, and engineered for a final reveal: atmospheric crime fiction in which commercial detail, coded language, social impersonation, and a single tangible clue lock together at the last moment.

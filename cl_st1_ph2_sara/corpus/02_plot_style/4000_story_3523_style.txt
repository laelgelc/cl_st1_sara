The story uses a framed, retrospective narrative in which an informal philosophical discussion prompts Ivan Vasilievich to recount the decisive experience of his youth. The outer narrator is restrained and observant, briefly characterising Ivan and recording the listeners’ interruptions, while the embedded first-person account supplies most of the emotional and descriptive force. This arrangement gives the tale the quality of an anecdote told aloud: Ivan digresses, corrects himself, answers objections and repeatedly dismisses details with phrases such as “that does not matter,” even when those details prove essential to the story’s effect. His voice combines sincerity, modesty, nostalgia and occasional comic irritability.

The structure is strongly binary. The first half presents the ball as a scene of beauty, harmony and expansive love; the second presents the punishment on the parade ground as a scene of violence, coercion and disgust. The transition occurs over a single night, with the glittering interior of the ballroom giving way to a foggy, wet dawn. Parallel figures and motifs bind the two scenes together: the colonel’s stately movement in the mazurka becomes his firm march beside the prisoner; his suede glove, sword, military bearing and insistence that everything be done “according to rule” acquire a sinister meaning; rhythmic dance music is replaced by shrilling fifes, beating drums and repeated blows. The method depends on juxtaposition rather than explicit authorial judgment, allowing the contrast itself to expose the coexistence of domestic tenderness and institutional cruelty.

The prose is realist, concrete and attentive to socially revealing particulars. Clothing, footwear, decorations, uniforms, gestures and bodily posture are described precisely. Objects often carry emotional or symbolic weight: Varinka’s white dress, pink sash, fan feather and glove embody romantic idealisation, while the colonel’s square-toed boots initially seem touching evidence of paternal sacrifice. Later, sticks, guns, cords and the prisoner’s mutilated back overturn that sentimental interpretation. Colour is especially important. The ballroom is white, pink, puce, silver and sparkling; the field is dominated by black uniforms, dirty mist, wet snow and the unnatural red of injured flesh.

The sentence style alternates between expansive recollection and abrupt dramatic impact. Descriptions of the ball frequently use long, cumulative sentences, coordinated clauses and repeated intensifiers that reproduce Ivan’s intoxicated happiness: he is “pleased and gay,” “happy, delighted,” and filled with love for everyone around him. The punishment scene retains some long sentences, but their clauses enumerate repeated physical actions—falling, dragging, striking and pulling—so that accumulation becomes oppressive rather than exuberant. Short exclamations and questions, including “My God!” and “What can that be?”, interrupt the flow at moments of recognition. Repetition is a central device: “waltzing and waltzing,” the endlessly repeated mazurka tunes, the prisoner’s plea for mercy, the alternating blows and the colonel’s furious questions all create rhythm while stressing obsession, mechanical routine and inescapability.

Dialogue is frequent, naturalistic and socially varied. The listeners’ teasing comments keep the frame conversational and prevent Ivan’s account from becoming a polished monologue. At the ball, dialogue is light, flirtatious and ceremonious, with French phrases helping establish the cultivated upper-class setting. On the parade ground, speech contracts into desperate pleading and brutal commands. This contrast mirrors the larger movement from social grace to dehumanising authority. Ivan’s habit of quoting exact words also suggests that the experience remains vividly present in his memory.

The emotional perspective is deliberately subjective. While in love, Ivan interprets every person and object benevolently, projecting tenderness even onto servants, horses and a romantic rival. After witnessing the punishment, the same associative faculty links Varinka irrevocably with her father’s violence. The narration therefore demonstrates how perception is shaped by emotional state. Yet the physical details of the flogging resist sentimental reinterpretation: the prisoner becomes first a “thing” and then a “writhing, falling creature,” language that reflects both the observer’s horrified incomprehension and the punishment’s destruction of human identity.

Irony is pervasive but understated. The colonel appears exemplary in both scenes—disciplined, paternal, dignified and devoted to rules—yet those same qualities enable cruelty when transferred from the ballroom to military authority. “Brothers, have mercy on me” is bitterly ironic because the soldiers participate collectively in the victim’s torment. Ivan’s refusal to declare the punishment simply wicked is also significant. He assumes that an act performed publicly and confidently must rest on knowledge unavailable to him, exposing the moral paralysis produced by institutional legitimacy. The story avoids abstract argument at its climax and instead registers shame, nausea, confusion and recurring sensory memories.

The tone moves from genial and nostalgic through lyrical rapture to horror, moral bewilderment and subdued regret. Humour appears in Ivan’s vanity, his indignation at modern attitudes and the listeners’ affectionate mockery, but it disappears as the central contrast sharpens. The ending returns to the opening debate without resolving it theoretically. Ivan claims that chance redirected his life, while the narrative itself suggests a more complex interaction among conscience, perception, social custom and circumstance. The final unfinished phrase—“And you say . . .”—gives the story a circular, conversational close and leaves its moral challenge open rather than reducing it to a formal conclusion.

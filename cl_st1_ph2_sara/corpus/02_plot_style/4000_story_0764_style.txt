The story adopts the style of an early twentieth-century comic detective tale, combining small-town realism, farce, verbal misunderstanding, and an accidentally successful mystery plot. Its central method is sustained incongruity: Philo Gubb appears foolish, speaks in malapropisms, and relies on correspondence-school formulas, yet his eccentric reasoning repeatedly brings him into contact with genuine crime. The narrative treats him with amused irony but not contempt. His vanity and limited education are comic, while his persistence, suspicion, and practical resourcefulness make him more capable than the supposedly sensible people around him.

The narrator is third-person omniscient, moving freely between Gubb, Marshal Wittaker, Billy Getz, and other townspeople. The voice is clear, brisk, and outwardly matter-of-fact, often reporting absurd events as if they were entirely logical. This deadpan presentation intensifies the humor, particularly in explanations of “un-burglary” and in Gubb’s distinction between “retail” and “wholesale” detecting. The narrator occasionally enters Gubb’s thought process in free indirect style, allowing his bizarre deductions to unfold with apparent seriousness: wedding presents, guilty conscience, and senile restitution become a plausible chain because the prose temporarily accepts his premises.

The diction mixes standard narrative English with strongly individualized colloquial speech. Gubb’s dialogue is marked by rural or nonstandard grammar—“them shares,” “I ain’t no expert,” “he done it”—and by comic professional jargon such as “deteckative,” “deteckating,” “induc-i-tively,” and “docket.” His speech repeatedly inflates simple ideas into clumsy abstractions, as in “the indebtedness of the liability I now owe you.” This contrast between grand terminology and imperfect understanding is one of the story’s main comic signatures. Other characters have similarly recognizable verbal patterns: Millbrook’s agitated remarks repeatedly “gurgle”; Wittaker speaks in relaxed small-town idiom; Billy Getz uses quick, teasing banter; and Frank the Tailor is represented through exaggerated phonetic dialect. The latter reflects period conventions of ethnic caricature and would read as conspicuously stereotyped to a modern audience.

Dialogue dominates the story and performs several functions at once. It supplies exposition, differentiates characters, generates jokes, and advances the mystery. Exchanges often operate through repetition, correction, or literal-minded extension. Medderbrook solemnly corrects “Perfectly Worthless” to “Utterly Hopeless”; Wittaker and Griscom debate whether a window was broken “into” or “out of”; and Gubb builds an elaborate theory from the grammatical possibility that if one can “burgle,” one can also “un-burgle.” Comic timing depends on short replies, interruptions, delayed corrections, and speakers accidentally revealing more than they intend. Billy’s line, “Oh, you won’t catch—I mean, we will leave that to you,” is characteristic: the near-slip alerts both Gubb and the reader to the prank while preserving the scene’s playful surface.

The syntax is generally straightforward and chronological, with longer sentences reserved for explanations, deductions, or enumerated physical action. Repetition supplies rhythm, especially in dialogue and parallel constructions: a man can “tie” and “un-tie,” “hitch” and “un-hitch,” therefore “burgle” and “un-burgle.” Such sequences mimic formal reasoning while exposing its absurdity. Parenthetical clarifications and appositions establish the provincial scale of Riverbank, as when the marshal is explained as the equivalent of a chief of police “in a larger city.” Exclamation points, rhetorical questions, and repeated words convey theatrical agitation without requiring psychological depth.

The humor is primarily verbal, situational, and structural. Verbal comedy arises from malapropisms, dialect, invented compounds, pedantic corrections, and over-literal reasoning. Situational comedy appears in the reversed burglary, the flour-shooting “lung-tester,” the detective carrying false beards to an ordinary meeting, and several men independently staining their trousers by falling into flower beds. Structural comedy comes from dramatic irony: Billy and Wittaker think they are manipulating Gubb, Gubb believes Billy is the culprit, and all of them overlook the separate, genuine criminal plot that Gubb discovers while pursuing the wrong theory. The story’s deepest joke is that erroneous reasoning produces a correct and valuable result.

The plot is episodic but tightly linked by recurring clues and motifs. It begins with financial and romantic continuity from an earlier adventure, shifts into the self-contained un-burglary mystery, and ends by reconnecting Gubb with the dynamiters mentioned in the opening sentence. This creates a circular construction: the case that previously failed is solved incidentally through a different investigation. Apparent clues are frequently misread or reassigned. The chicken bone seems worthless, the pansy stains appear incriminating, and Westcote’s bundle suggests wedding restitution; in reality, these details belong to overlapping stories involving a harmless un-burglar, a blackmail payment, and the dynamiters. The convergence is deliberately improbable, fitting the farcical rather than strictly puzzle-based mode.

Pacing alternates between leisurely comic conversations and rapid nocturnal action. The first half spends substantial time establishing the joke and allowing characters to debate its logic. Once Gubb shadows Westcote, the prose becomes more visual and kinetic: movement through hazel brush, streets, lumber piles, railway yards, and boxcars is described in quick succession. The climax is notably economical. Gubb overhears enough to identify the criminals, locks them in the car, and is carried away on the train; the mechanics of his later success are compressed into a telegram. This abrupt reporting preserves the comic emphasis on the townspeople’s embarrassment rather than turning the ending into a conventional action sequence.

Characterization is caricatural but efficient. Each figure is built around a dominant trait: Medderbrook is miserly, Billy mischievous, Wittaker complacent, Millbrook excitable, Westcote notoriously mean, and Gubb solemnly self-important. Physical tags reinforce these traits, such as Gubb’s lankness, Millbrook’s short stout body and husky gurgling voice, and Westcote’s tottering gait and poor eyesight. Emotional complexity is limited, but Gubb receives a measure of sympathy through his love for Syrilla, his financial obligation, and his genuine concern that the un-burglar may be ruining himself. These touches prevent him from becoming merely a fool.

The setting is a compact, socially interconnected American town in which everyone knows the local joker, household finances, dinner habits, and personal reputations. Specific street names, businesses, municipal rooms, railway sidings, newspaper-wrapped bundles, telegrams, and shop signs create a concrete period atmosphere. The town functions almost like a stage set: the same citizens, jokes, rumors, and clues circulate rapidly across a small number of recognizable locations. Modern technologies of the period—the telephone, telegram, railway, and correspondence course—are treated as ordinary but also supply comic possibilities.

The story repeatedly parodies detective-fiction conventions. Gubb invokes Sherlock Holmes, “inductive” reasoning, shadowing rules, disguises, notebooks, reports, and professional badges, but applies them mechanically or incorrectly. The Rising Sun correspondence school represents packaged expertise: Gubb has absorbed the gestures and vocabulary of detection without mastering its principles. Yet the parody remains double-edged, because formal authorities fare no better. Wittaker dismisses the mystery as a joke, Purcell catches only the harmless culprit, and Billy’s superior amusement collapses when Gubb secures the dynamiters and their reward. The narrative therefore mocks both amateur pretension and official complacency.

The ending uses reversal and understatement rather than celebration. Gubb’s triumph arrives indirectly in the sheriff’s terse telegram, while the final lines remain with Wittaker and Billy, who confess disgust and “bilious” embarrassment. This preserves the story’s comic tone and makes social humiliation, rather than criminal capture, the true punch line. Overall, the style is genial, fast-moving, dialogue-heavy, and elaborately ironic: a burlesque of logical detection in which linguistic confusion, coincidence, and stubborn literalism repeatedly outperform conventional intelligence.

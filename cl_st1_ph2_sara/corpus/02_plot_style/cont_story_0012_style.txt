The story uses a close third-person perspective that remains tightly aligned with the unnamed woman’s consciousness. Although the narration is grammatically external, it moves freely through her perceptions, memories, judgments, and dissociative states, often slipping into free indirect discourse without explicit transitions. This restricted viewpoint preserves her emotional privacy: other people are understood through visible details and conjecture, while her own trauma emerges gradually and involuntarily. Her lack of a personal name further emphasizes identities imposed by circumstance—wife, asylum seeker, and, above all, “Ugonna’s mother.”

The prose is lucid, restrained, and concrete, with emotional force produced through accumulation rather than overt commentary. Plain declarative sentences are interspersed with long, layered constructions that imitate memory’s associative movement. Repetition and parallel phrasing are prominent, especially in sequences of “because,” “as though,” “perhaps,” and “not that,” which convey circular thought, disbelief, and self-correction. Sentence fragments such as “A breathing sidewalk,” “Three men,” and “Killed” isolate crucial perceptions and give them the weight of traumatic recognition. Understatement is central: extreme violence is narrated without melodramatic language, allowing ordinary physical details to carry the horror.

The narrative has a braided temporal structure. Its present action—the embassy queue and subsequent interview—provides a narrow chronological frame, but sensory triggers repeatedly open into memories of the preceding days. Blood recalls palm oil; a soldier’s swagger recalls the intruders; conversation about journalism recalls the husband’s political work; visa advice anticipates the demand that grief be converted into evidence. These transitions are fluid and largely unannounced, reproducing the way traumatic memory interrupts rather than politely follows chronology. The full circumstances of Ugonna’s death are disclosed in stages, creating tension through delayed revelation while keeping the emphasis on the mother’s inability to control what she remembers.

Sensory imagery is vivid, localized, and symbolically recurrent. Heat, sweat, dust, food smells, voices, whistles, cracking packets, enamel bowls, plastic files, and newspaper titles create a densely inhabited Lagos. The most important motif is the bright red of fresh palm oil, which becomes the mind’s evasive substitute for blood. Its domestic familiarity makes the image especially painful: a substance associated with cooking and motherhood is transformed into the visual language of her child’s death. Other recurring objects—Ugonna’s LEGO blocks, the grave, the ixora flowers, the visa documents—contrast intimate memory with bureaucratic proof. Color is used precisely, from blue plastic and black clothing to auburn hair, faded pink lips, and reddish-gold hair, sharpening the social and psychological distance between observer and observed.

Dialogue is naturalistic and socially differentiated. Nigerian English, Pidgin-inflected expressions, local idioms, and discourse markers such as “abeg,” “abi,” “oh,” “small-small,” and “Oga” establish place and class without explanatory glosses. The man behind her speaks in an earnest, aspirational register; the security agents use threatening, broken interrogation; embassy officials rely on polite bureaucratic formulas. Public speech is frequently ironic. The visa queue generates friendliness and shared complaint, but it also reduces complex lives to strategies for passing an interview. The interviewer’s apparently sympathetic language becomes coercive because it requires the protagonist to narrate her son’s death in institutionally acceptable terms.

Characterization is indirect and economical. People are rendered through gestures, clothing, speech habits, and the protagonist’s speculative judgments: a crisp shirt and careful English suggest ambition, while a persistent adjustment of hair suggests insulation or impatience. The protagonist herself is characterized through refusal, omission, bodily stillness, and minute acts of attention. Her grief appears as dissociation, physical pain, aversion to food, fractured concentration, and resistance to being interpreted. Her conflicted view of her husband complicates any simple heroic narrative. Public discourse celebrates his courage, but she sees the selfishness and domestic cost embedded in political martyrdom.

The setting operates as more than background. The embassy queue is a compressed social world containing vendors, beggars, soldiers, professionals, students, religious opportunists, and visa hopefuls. It becomes a threshold between state violence and imagined refuge, yet the embassy reproduces another form of power through gates, quotas, glass screens, evidentiary demands, and the arbitrary authority to grant a “new life.” Spatial contrasts reinforce this dynamic: applicants wait in exposed heat outside guarded compounds with lawns, then speak through glass to officials whose faces determine their futures.

Irony structures the story at several levels. The protagonist reaches the embassy because her husband’s journalism has exposed political violence, yet proving that violence requires her to commodify its most intimate consequence. The state’s agents kill her child while searching for a man praised for giving “a voice to the voiceless,” but her own voice is valuable to the asylum process only if it supplies sufficient detail. The promised “new life” is finally redefined not as America but as a return to Ugonna’s grave and the private ritual of arranging flowers like his blocks. The ending therefore gains force through reversal and refusal rather than resolution.

The dominant tone is controlled, mournful, and quietly accusatory. Satirical touches appear in the visa market, the “American Visa Miracle Ministry,” the photograph promising “correct” specifications, and the crowd’s theories about embassy policy, but the satire never overwhelms the grief. Political critique remains embedded in scenes and institutional encounters rather than delivered as abstract argument. The final departure is deliberately understated. By walking away, the woman rejects safety purchased through the public display of her son’s memory. The style makes that choice both an assertion of dignity and a devastating sign that grief has altered what survival means.

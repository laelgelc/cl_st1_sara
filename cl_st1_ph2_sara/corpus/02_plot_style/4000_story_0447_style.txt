The story adopts the manner of a late-medieval chronicle retold through a consciously antiquarian, nineteenth-century voice. It presents scandalous court history as though recovering a suppressed anecdote, surrounding the central episode with genealogical details, historical names, dates, locations, epitaphs, and references to other collections of tales. This documentary scaffolding gives the narrative mock authority, while the narrator’s partiality, exaggeration, and delight in gossip continually undermine any appearance of sober historiography. The result is a pseudo-historical fabliau: a tragic sexual intrigue narrated with the expansive energy of a bawdy fireside tale.

The narrator is highly intrusive, conversational, and performative. He addresses the reader indirectly, pauses to comment on national character and human folly, interprets motives, passes moral judgments, and openly controls the transition into the tale with phrases such as “But now for mine.” His stance is unstable by design. At first he appears amused by aristocratic libertinism and celebrates youth, wine, sensuality, and merriment; later, as the duke’s conduct becomes coercive and destructive, the narration condemns him as wicked, disloyal, diabolical, and villainous. This shifting perspective creates a tension between relish in the telling and horror at what is told. The voice is worldly and cynical, yet ultimately affirms fidelity, innocence, and moral consequence.

The prose is ornate, exuberant, and accumulative. Sentences are often long and syntactically layered, using subordinate clauses, parenthetical historical explanations, parallel constructions, and extended catalogues. Lists are a signature device: forms of hunting, varieties of sexual behaviour, classes of disreputable women, courtly luxuries, and methods of temptation are piled up until they become comic through excess. Repetition and amplification repeatedly turn a simple assertion into a rhetorical performance. Short exclamations and abrupt statements—“I am undone!”, “Never, while I live,” or “Ha! Ha! Ha!”—punctuate these elaborate periods, sharpening moments of danger or theatrical confrontation.

The diction mixes archaism, courtly refinement, earthy insult, and euphemistic obscenity. Titles, saints’ oaths, formal modes of address, old-fashioned inversions, and expressions such as “said lady,” “my lord and master,” and “thereupon” sustain the historical illusion. Alongside them appear vigorous colloquialisms such as “rapscallion,” “muckworms,” “hussies,” and “tarred with the same brush.” Sexual acts are rarely described in direct anatomical language; instead, they are elaborated through metaphors of feasting, riding, hunting, warfare, religion, jewellery, confectionery, and paradise. This figurative indirection allows the prose to be simultaneously decorous and lewd. Occasional grammatical awkwardness and unusual phrasing contribute to the impression of an older work mediated through antiquated translation.

Figurative language is abundant and deliberately extravagant. Beauty is expressed through flowers, radiance, whiteness, and heavenly gifts; lust becomes fever, fire, appetite, sport, or horsemanship; corruption appears as mud, poison, worms, and suffocation. The duke’s seduction speech develops sensual images in arabesque profusion, while the lady’s decline is compressed into the melancholy image of “a flower deprived of air and eaten by a worm.” Comic comparisons are often grotesquely incongruous, as when abstract social claims are illustrated through oysters’ plumage or birds’ shells. Such images cultivate a Rabelaisian atmosphere in which bodily appetite, verbal invention, and moral satire are inseparable.

Dialogue is lengthy, rhetorical, and strongly differentiated by moral position. The Lady d’Hocquetonville speaks in balanced, principled arguments centred on children, reputation, marriage, age, and enduring affection. Her language is formal and controlled even under threat, making speech her first weapon before she turns to the stiletto. The duke speaks through wit, sophistry, teasing obscenity, threats, and seductive verbal abundance. Raoul’s initial dialogue establishes him as loyal and domestically virtuous, which heightens the cruelty of the trap. Conversations therefore do more than convey information: they stage contests between chastity and appetite, integrity and manipulation, self-command and intoxication.

The narrative structure is linear but heavily framed. A discursive opening introduces the duke’s reputation and the supposed history of the anecdote; the central action then proceeds through temptation, entrapment, banquet, deception, and violation; a compressed aftermath records murder, death, burial, and retrospective judgment. Suspense depends on dramatic irony. The imprisoned wife hears her husband being coaxed toward the adjoining room, while he remains unaware of her identity; the reader understands the duke’s scheme before Raoul does. Repeated thresholds—the marked floorboard, the locked chamber, the secret door, and the garden exit—give the episode a claustrophobic theatricality. The banquet scene slows time through speeches and drinking, prolonging the wife’s dread before the catastrophe.

Tonally, the story combines farce, erotic comedy, melodrama, moral exemplum, and tragedy. The opening boasts, sexual metaphors, drinking scene, and masculine banter belong to comic fabliau, but the coerced situation and fatal aftermath expose the violence beneath courtly gallantry. Laughter is especially double-edged: it signals sociability and verbal pleasure, yet also the duke’s cruelty and the courtiers’ loss of shame. The closing movement replaces exuberance with elegiac gravity, using the tomb inscription and Burgundy’s melancholy reflection to recast the anecdote as the destruction of two honourable lives.

The central thematic contrast is between appetite without restraint and virtue placed under intolerable pressure. Other recurring concerns include the abuse of aristocratic power, male complicity, the fragility of reputation, the corrupting effects of wine and group behaviour, marital fidelity, and the delayed but inevitable cost of private wrongdoing. The final maxim universalises the episode into a lesson about folly and retribution, though its claim that “there is a woman at the bottom of everything” retains the narrator’s characteristic mixture of proverb, irony, misogynistic convention, and historical fatalism. Overall, the style is sensuous, digressive, mock-scholarly, theatrically rhetorical, and morally ambivalent: it revels in bawdy invention while finally condemning the cruelty that such libertinism can conceal.

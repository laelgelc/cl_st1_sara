The story adopts a comic Gothic style, combining the apparatus of a medieval German romance—an ancient castle, ancestral violence, hunting parties, melancholy, supernatural visitations and contemplated suicide—with a persistently irreverent, satirical treatment. Its Gothic scenery is deliberately conventional and exaggerated: moonlit galleries, howling forests, gloomy chambers and a corpse-like apparition are introduced only to be undercut by practical explanations, bathos or absurd detail. The ancestor’s murder, for example, is treated as a financial and administrative matter, while his chapel becomes an “apology” and a “receipt from Heaven.” Horror is therefore less an end in itself than a framework for farce, social criticism and moral comedy.

The narrator is conspicuous, conversational and knowingly intrusive. He addresses the reader indirectly, admits uncertainty, digresses into personal opinions and repeatedly interrupts the tale with mock explanations. Remarks such as “I needn’t say,” “I contend that this is not fair” and “I have no means of knowing” create the manner of an anecdote told aloud by an amused, opinionated raconteur. This voice claims limited knowledge while exercising complete comic control, using feigned innocence to expose the absurdity of aristocratic customs, pedigree, marriage, politics and sentimental convention. General reflections often grow out of verbal associations rather than strict narrative necessity, giving the prose an improvisatory, club-story quality.

The dominant tone is genial but sharp-edged irony. Nobility is mocked through inflated titles, ridiculous names and mechanical hierarchies: the baron’s followers are reduced to “four-and-twenty Lincoln greens,” distinguished from their master chiefly by coarser cloth and thicker soles. Violence, drunkenness and domestic tyranny are narrated with jaunty understatement. The baron inserts a knife into a traveller, threatens to cut off a rival’s nose and kicks his retainers as though these were minor breaches of etiquette. Conversely, supposedly elevated emotions are translated into physical or comic actions: a father “shed a wink of joy,” filial sacrifice prevents a castle from being thrown out of a window, and solemn pledges are measured in quantities of wine. This recurring mismatch between subject and treatment produces the story’s characteristic burlesque.

The diction alternates between mock-heroic formality and lively colloquial speech. Elevated phrases such as “venerable building,” “dangerous distinction,” “appalling significance” and “ere the sun goes down” coexist with “queer customer,” “look sharp,” “a good one” and “put a good face on the matter.” Archaic or romance-inflected vocabulary establishes the period atmosphere, while colloquialisms puncture it. Comic specificity is important: moustachios, russet boots, imperial pints, coffin handles, a sharpened hunting knife and a nursery window barred against the moat all make the fantasy tangible while preserving its caricatural quality. Invented Germanic names such as Koldwethout, Grogzwig and Swillenhausen contribute sound-based humour and signal that the setting is an English comic fantasy of Germany rather than realistic cultural representation.

Sentences are often long, cumulative and parenthetical, driven by semicolons, qualifications and escalating lists. The narrator likes to begin with a simple proposition and then extend it through comic logic until it reaches an absurd conclusion, as in the meditation on why later-born people must possess longer pedigrees. Repetition supplies both rhythm and exaggeration: “little by little, and bit by bit, and day by day, and year by year” charts the baron’s domestic defeat, while repeated numerical formulas turn people and actions into comic inventory. Parallel constructions, reversals and antitheses are frequent, especially formulations that correct or intensify themselves, such as the “pleasures of the table, or the pleasures of under the table.” The prose also favours accumulations of near-synonyms—“jolly, roystering, rollicking, merry-making”—whose abundance enacts the excess being described.

Dialogue is brisk, theatrical and built around repetition, literal-mindedness and reversal. Exchanges frequently resemble comic duels, with one speaker’s wording immediately seized and returned by the other. The supernatural conversation is especially laconic: the baron’s blunt questions and the apparition’s dry answers strip the scene of conventional terror. The Genius of Despair and Suicide is rendered simultaneously grotesque and bureaucratic, complaining of a busy schedule and treating self-destruction as a professional transaction. His increasing impatience contrasts with the baron’s growing curiosity, so that the intended victim gradually takes control of the interview. Dialogue also individualises through tempo rather than subtle psychology: the baron is forceful and practical, the baroness manipulative through soft imperatives and swooning, and the genius petulant, evasive and allergic to laughter.

The narrative structure is episodic and strongly shaped by escalation. It begins with a broad comic portrait of the baron’s bachelor life, introduces marriage as an attempted cure for boredom, then converts that marriage into a sequence of accumulating restrictions, children, interfering relations and financial trouble. These pressures lead to the apparent crisis of suicide. The supernatural visitation then reverses the downward movement: every objection offered by the genius prompts the baron to imagine a remedy, and every attempt to deepen despair produces hope. The climax is not an act of violence but laughter, which physically repels the allegorical embodiment of despair. A compact retrospective ending confirms the baron’s reform and supplies an explicit moral.

Characterisation is deliberately broad and caricatural. The baron is defined by appetite, physicality, impulsiveness and resilience rather than introspection. His retainers operate as a comic chorus, pale faces and permanently coloured noses responding in unison. The women are presented through a period domestic satire in which marriage becomes a contest for authority; the narrator’s remarks about wives and Members of Parliament extend the private joke into social commentary. The characterisation relies on stereotypes and comic types, but the baron’s recovery gives him a simple emotional arc from heedless pleasure, through subjection and despair, to purposeful self-command.

The supernatural figure functions as an embodied abstraction, linking the tale to allegory and moral fable. His costume literalises death through coffin furniture, pall cloth and the stake through his body, yet these macabre details are handled with visual extravagance and deadpan comedy. The apparition’s weakness before moderation, hope and laughter makes the moral mechanism transparent: despair survives by encouraging one-sided thought, while comic perspective restores proportion. The final advice states this philosophy openly, recommending that sufferers examine “both sides of the question” and magnify the better one. Although drinking and smoking are playfully offered as remedies, the deeper prescription is action, perspective and refusal to brood.

Overall, the style is exuberant, digressive, performative and anti-solemn. It repeatedly erects the expectations of romance, tragedy or terror and collapses them into common sense, wordplay and comic materialism. Its principal techniques are mock-heroic inflation, bathos, hyperbole, numerical repetition, intrusive narration, grotesque visual detail, rhythmic listing and rapid repartee. Even its moralising remains playful: the tale advocates optimism not through earnest sermonising, but by making despair look pompous, uncomfortable and ridiculous.

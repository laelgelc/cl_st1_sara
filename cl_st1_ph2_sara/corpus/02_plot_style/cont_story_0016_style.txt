The story uses a restrained, realist literary style in the present tense, narrated through a close but unobtrusive third-person perspective. The viewpoint moves subtly between husband and wife, though it remains most closely aligned with the woman’s perceptions, memories, and emotional disorientation. The narrator rarely interprets their feelings directly; instead, grief and marital history emerge through gestures, domestic behavior, evasions, and small discrepancies in conversation. This creates an atmosphere of intimacy without full psychological disclosure.

The opening is immediate and sensorial, beginning with the emphatic capitals of “THE JET LAG DOESN’T” and moving into sharply observed impressions of arrival: light like a blade, familiar fields and cupolas, plane-tree bark resembling military camouflage. Throughout, the diction is lucid, concrete, and largely unornamented, with occasional vivid similes. Rome is rendered not through monumental landmarks but through ordinary urban textures—shutters, coffee bars, market stalls, balconies, worn bricks, overheated piazzas, breezes in stairwells, and neighborhood rituals. Italian words such as “piazza,” “cornetto,” “visciola,” and “prego” are incorporated naturally, giving the setting local specificity without explanatory excess.

Description is unusually attentive to interiors and objects. Furniture, crockery, tablecloths, keys, piano books, damaged fixtures, cords, medicines, and food accumulate in precise catalogues. These details initially appear incidental but gradually acquire emotional and symbolic force. The rented apartment feels both inhabited and inaccessible: it offers the traces of another family life while withholding the locked room the woman becomes desperate to enter. Domestic space is therefore treated as an external form of memory, fantasy, and grief. The chipped piano, drooping chandelier, locked door, scattered keys, slamming French doors, and shattered glass all suggest damage, failed access, and the danger of what has been left unsecured.

Dialogue dominates much of the story. The exchanges are colloquial, understated, and often built from short questions, corrections, and practical observations. The couple talk about food, heat, reservations, sightseeing, furniture, and errands while circling subjects they cannot initially name. Their speech frequently works at cross-purposes: one person asks a literal question while the other answers an emotional one, or changes the subject to avoid an unbearable implication. Repetition—“What?”, “Who?”, “Where?”, “Tomorrow’s Sunday”—creates a natural conversational rhythm while also emphasizing estrangement, forgetfulness, and the couple’s difficulty reaching the same version of the past. Their conflicting memories of their son’s first steps foreshadow the fuller revelation of his death.

The syntax alternates between compact dialogue and longer descriptive sentences layered with lists, spatial coordinates, and parenthetical remarks. Parentheses are used sparingly to create intimacy and retrospective shading, as in the comparison between the couple’s light luggage and that of young travelers. Paragraphs are generally brief, producing a measured, scene-based pace. Time unfolds almost continuously over a single day, but remembered scenes and implied histories deepen the temporal field. The effect is quietly dramatic: nothing appears hurried, yet ordinary details steadily gather pressure.

The story’s structure depends on delayed revelation. Early references to children, family life, memory, and the locked room carry an unexplained charge. The son is introduced obliquely through a disagreement about his first steps, and only much later does the narrative disclose that he died at twelve, eleven years earlier. This withholding avoids melodrama while retroactively transforming earlier details. The woman’s fascination with Roman mothers and daughters, her question about raising a child in the apartment, her fixation on the room, and her sudden wish to flee all become manifestations of bereavement. The husband’s blunt statement that there is “nothing waiting” in the room except the grief already inside her serves as the story’s clearest articulation of its emotional logic.

The public procession and the private grief are arranged as parallels. The procession preserves communal memory through repetition, ceremony, music, and a sacred maternal figure; the couple’s mourning is solitary, unstable, and marked by conflicting recollections. The woman expects the ritual to remain unchanged after thirty years, but she cannot bear to watch it once the piazza fills with women, children, and multigenerational families. The Virgin never actually appears in the narrated scene. Her absence echoes the inaccessible room and the absent son, making omission itself a central technique.

Motifs of thresholds, enclosure, and failed entry recur throughout. Windows frame distant observers; streets branch from the piazza like hallways; the apartment contains a forbidden room; doors slam or break; keys fail to open what the woman seeks. The piazza is explicitly compared to a living room, linking public and domestic space. Breezes initially signify pleasure and freedom, then become the force that shatters the glass. Light undergoes a similar transformation: the bright arrival feels energizing, but the chandelier’s final glare turns the dining room into an operating room, abruptly connecting the present apartment to the son’s death.

The emotional tone is controlled, melancholic, and compassionate, with moments of dry marital humor and ordinary irritation preventing the story from becoming uniformly solemn. The husband and wife are characterized through care as much as conflict: he complains and questions, but watches her closely, holds her when she cries, gathers the keys, offers water, and helps return the crisis to the realm of practical action. The closing exchange restores their habitual conversational rhythm. Their plan to visit Porta Portese, where they may “get lost,” converts an earlier image of being lost into a tentative possibility of shared experience. The ending remains unresolved but gently stabilizing, suggesting endurance rather than cure.

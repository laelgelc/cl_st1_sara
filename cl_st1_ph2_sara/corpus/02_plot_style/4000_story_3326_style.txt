The story is written as a nineteenth-century sentimental and didactic moral tale, combining domestic realism with explicit Christian instruction. Its central purpose is not psychological complexity or suspense, but moral persuasion: the death of an impoverished seamstress becomes an exemplary lesson in faith, charity, humility, and spiritual preparedness. Social contrast supports this design. Fashionable, comfortable Mrs. Ellis initially treats Anna May with indifference and recoils from death, while the obscure and materially poor young woman possesses the spiritual wisdom her social superior lacks. The narrative therefore reverses worldly hierarchies, presenting the “poor sewing-girl” as a moral teacher and a “rich jewel” hidden in a “plain casket.”

The narration uses an omniscient third-person voice that freely reports outward conduct, inward reactions, and moral judgments. The narrator is conspicuously authoritative rather than neutral, describing characters in evaluative terms and occasionally addressing the reader through rhetorical questions and exclamations. Passages such as “Was it the rustle of angels garments” elevate the deathbed scene beyond literal observation, while the closing declaration directly states the lesson. This intrusive commentary gives the tale the character of a sermon or devotional tract. Characterization is correspondingly schematic: Mrs. Ellis represents worldly fear and complacency, Mrs. Bell acts as guide and mediator, and Anna embodies pure, selfless Christian faith.

Dialogue dominates the opening and much of the story, allowing the moral argument to unfold as a sequence of questions and answers. The conversation initially sounds socially casual, moving abruptly from Anna’s illness to a fashionable bonnet, which exposes Mrs. Ellis’s superficial priorities. It then becomes increasingly theological and catechetical. Mrs. Ellis poses doubts about death, certainty, salvation, and spiritual preparation; Anna or Mrs. Bell answers each doubt with a concise religious principle. Repetition—especially “God is love” and the injunction that human beings must become like God by loving others—gives the dialogue a homiletic rhythm and establishes the story’s doctrinal refrain.

The diction is elevated, devotional, and emotionally charged. Archaic or biblical expressions such as “hath ascended,” “spake,” “jot or tittle,” “clothed upon with immortality,” and “the waters of Jordan” situate the prose within Protestant religious discourse. Death is rarely treated in bluntly physical terms. Instead, it is transformed through euphemism and metaphor into a “change,” a “departure,” sleep, homecoming, dawn, or a journey across a river. Heaven is imagined as the “Father’s house,” a realm of “eternal light and unfading beauty,” and death as an angelic messenger. These recurring images soften mortality and replace terror with reassurance.

Sentence structure alternates between brief, pointed exchanges and long, balanced narrative or expository sentences. The dialogue often relies on fragments, interruptions, dashes, and exclamations to convey spontaneous feeling: “What!—that poor seamstress” and “The preparation, Anna!” In the climactic passages, syntax becomes more ceremonial and rhythmic, with parallel clauses, accumulations of adjectives, and repeated abstractions such as “loving, and true, and faithful.” Rhetorical questions are frequent and generally imply their own answers, reinforcing the persuasive rather than exploratory nature of the prose.

The imagery is built around binaries: light and darkness, morning and evening, poverty and spiritual riches, bodily weakness and inward strength, earthly shadows and heavenly permanence. Anna’s paradoxical statement that her closing “day” is really an approaching “morning” encapsulates the story’s governing technique: conventional images of death are systematically inverted. Her body is pale, shrunken, and “shadowy,” but her face appears illuminated by a heavenly radiance. Physical decline thus becomes visual evidence of spiritual ascent.

The emotional register is reverent, tender, and openly sentimental. Details such as Anna’s snowy cheek, drooping lashes, faint smile, and childlike repose are designed to evoke pity and awe. The death itself is idealized, serene, and almost decorporealized; bodily suffering recedes so that spiritual peace can occupy the foreground. Exclamations and heightened scenic pauses—“How hushed the room”—slow the climax and invite devotional contemplation.

Structurally, the story follows a conversion pattern. It begins with indifference and fear, moves through reluctant exposure to a deathbed example, reaches a revelation at Anna’s peaceful passing, and ends with Mrs. Ellis’s moral awakening. The final exchange restates the lesson in explicit terms, leaving little ambiguity. Overall, the style is earnest, accessible, allegorical, and sermon-like: a socially observant domestic anecdote shaped into a Christian exemplum about the “true Euthanasy,” or peaceful death attained through selfless love and confidence in divine goodness.

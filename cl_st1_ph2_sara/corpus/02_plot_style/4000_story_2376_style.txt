The story is a compact comic satire built around competitive self-mythologising. Its tone is dry, amused, and gently mocking, with the narrator exposing the vanity and hypocrisy of prosperous men who romanticise poverty only when safely removed from it. The humour depends on incongruity: the men’s “well-fed faces,” sausage-like fingers, heavy rings, and enormous waistcoats immediately contradict their professed enthusiasm for tar barrels, hog’s food, and deprivation. Their nostalgia is presented not as sincere reflection but as another form of status competition.

The narration is third-person and largely external, although it occasionally adopts an intrusive, conversational stance, most notably in “what we commonly call successful business men.” This creates a sense of complicity between narrator and reader. Characterisation is economical and caricatural. Physical exaggeration establishes the men as complacent and overindulged, while their personalities emerge almost entirely through dialogue, gesture, and mutual interruption. Jones and Robinson are deliberately similar rather than psychologically distinct; they function as comic doubles, each mirroring and intensifying the other’s boastfulness.

Dialogue dominates the story and provides its rhythm. The speech is energetic, colloquial, and period-specific, combining American expressions such as “I guess,” “darned,” and “mighty” with exclamations such as “By George,” “By gad,” and “By Jove.” Repeated forms of address—“sir,” “my dear fellow”—give the contest a veneer of gentlemanly civility that contrasts with its increasing aggression. Frequent interruptions, exclamation marks, dashes, repetitions, and rhetorical challenges reproduce the momentum of an argument. Speech tags and physical actions—sneering, shouting, striking the table, scowling—heighten the mock-heroic seriousness with which the men treat their absurd rivalry.

The narrative structure is linear and tightly controlled. It begins with a brief visual setup, moves quickly into a pattern of escalating one-upmanship, and culminates when the waiter forces the men to translate their claims into an actual dinner order. Each boast raises the stakes: fifteen cents becomes no money, a tar barrel becomes a damaged piano box, a draught becomes a missing plank, two or three companions become half a dozen, and scraps become animal feed. This cumulative escalation is the principal comic mechanism. The waiter’s arrival acts as a reality test, producing a pause that sharply changes the tempo. The men initially maintain the performance, but silence and mutual scrutiny expose their reluctance before Robinson retreats into luxury and Jones immediately follows.

The prose mixes plain storytelling with vivid comic hyperbole. Similes such as “fingers like sausages” and a waistcoat “a yard and a half round the equator” create grotesque visual comedy, while the final image—burying the memory “as far out of sight as a home for the blind under a landslide”—delivers an intentionally excessive and darkly absurd punch line. Food imagery is especially important: cold porridge, potato peelings, bran mash, partridge, oysters, Stilton, and champagne turn appetite into a measure of the gap between public pose and private desire. The contrast between humble and luxurious foods also gives the ending a sensuous comic payoff.

Sentence style varies according to function. Narratorial passages tend to be balanced, descriptive, and moderately elaborate, whereas the dialogue uses long, loosely connected sentences filled with repetitions, parenthetical qualifications, and abrupt changes of direction. Lists are used for comic accumulation, particularly in the final order, where Robinson disguises extravagance through comic understatement: “a little” partridge, “an oyster or two,” “a mouthful” of soup, and “a dab” of fish. This euphemistic minimising makes the abundance more conspicuous.

The central themes are social pretension, masculine rivalry, selective memory, and the romanticisation of hardship. The men have converted former deprivation into a badge of superiority, but their stories become increasingly implausible as pride overtakes truth. The story avoids direct moral commentary; instead, dramatic irony and the dinner-order reversal expose the contradiction. Overall, the style is dialogue-led, economical, caricatural, and structurally geared toward a single satirical climax, with exaggeration, repetition, contrast, and deadpan narration supplying most of the humour. The supplied text repeats the story verbatim, which appears to be a duplication artifact rather than an intentional stylistic feature.

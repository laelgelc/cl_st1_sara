Rev. Andrew Adkin, a Mayberry storekeeper and occasional lay preacher, struggles to reconcile his religious duties with his consuming interest in business. During one week, he underpays a corn seller by concealing a rise in the market price, sues a debtor who insults him, and quarrels with a congregant over disputed charges. These conflicts trouble his conscience but also provoke his pride and anger.

Late Saturday night, Adkin is unexpectedly asked to preach the next morning. Unprepared and unable to free his mind from business concerns, he hurriedly chooses a text. In church, he sees the corn seller, the debtor, and the disgruntled customer, whose accusing expressions remind him of his conduct and destroy his composure. His sermon is confused and spiritually empty. Afterward, someone hands him a note rebuking him for failing to nourish the congregation. The experience forces him to recognize that worldly preoccupations, inadequate preparation, and inconsistency between his preaching and daily behavior have undermined his usefulness as a minister, suggesting that he may abandon or reconsider lay preaching.

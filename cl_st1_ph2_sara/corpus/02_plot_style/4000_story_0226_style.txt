The story has the style of a traditional comic folktale rendered in a plain, accessible nineteenth-century literary voice. Its narration is omniscient, linear, and strongly event-driven, opening with the formulaic “In a village there once lived” and briefly addressing the audience with “Now we shall hear what happened to them.” This creates the atmosphere of an oral storyteller recounting a supposedly “true” tale, although the events are deliberately improbable. Exposition is economical: characters are defined by a few conspicuous traits, such as Great Claus’s wealth, violence, greed, and gullibility or Little Claus’s poverty, quick wit, and opportunism. Psychological depth is limited; motives are transparent and exist chiefly to propel the next trick.

The diction is simple, concrete, and domestic, drawing on village life, farming, churchgoing, inns, trades, food, livestock, money, and household objects. Descriptions are brief but vivid, especially when they establish comfort, temptation, or danger: a “dark, gloomy forest,” a table laden with wine and roast meat, a deep and rapidly flowing river, or an imagined underwater landscape covered in flowers and grass. Evaluative adjectives such as “good,” “wicked,” “splendid,” “beautiful,” and “dreadful” give the language the direct moral colouring of a children’s tale. Archaic or period expressions—“betimes,” “wholl buy skins,” “a nights lodging,” “Heaven preserve us,” and payment by a “bushel of money”—reinforce the old-fashioned setting. The text also displays the punctuation and apostrophe irregularities of an older or imperfectly transcribed edition, with forms such as “farmers wife,” “doesnt,” “Ill,” and occasional missing spaces or malformed compounds.

Sentences are generally straightforward but often linked into long narrative chains by “and,” “but,” “so,” “then,” and “for.” This paratactic construction imitates oral storytelling and maintains rapid forward motion: one action causes another with little pause for reflection. Repeated temporal markers—“presently,” “at this moment,” “during the night,” “by sunrise,” and “as soon as”—keep the sequence easy to follow. Paragraphs frequently alternate between compact narration and direct speech. Dialogue is plainly attributed and usually serves an immediate practical purpose: issuing threats, bargaining, inventing lies, or revealing credulity. Exclamations, rhetorical questions, repetitions, and comic imperatives give it a performative quality, as in “Skins, skins!” or “Oh, no; oh, no.” Distinctive speech is less important than the recurring roles of deceiver and dupe.

Structurally, the story is cumulative and episodic. Each episode follows a similar pattern: Great Claus harms or threatens Little Claus; Little Claus converts the apparent disaster into profit through observation and deception; Great Claus imitates only the visible action, misunderstands its cause, and suffers a worse result. The dead horse leads to the “conjuror,” the dead grandmother leads to another payment, and the attempted drowning leads to the cattle trick and Great Claus’s death. Repetition with escalation supplies both coherence and comic momentum. Stock quantities and objects—the bushel of money, sack, chest, cart, hatchet, and river—function as memorable folktale devices rather than realistic details.

The prevailing tone is mischievous, matter-of-fact, and darkly comic. Violence and death are narrated with striking emotional restraint: horses and grandmothers are killed, a drover is drowned, and Great Claus sinks to the riverbed, yet the narration rarely dwells on suffering. Instead, brutality becomes part of a grotesque cause-and-effect comedy. Little Claus’s lies are treated admiringly because they represent cleverness overcoming strength and wealth, while Great Claus’s punishment seems justified by his cruelty, greed, and repeated refusal to learn. The morality is therefore folkloric rather than conventionally virtuous: cunning, adaptability, and verbal ingenuity triumph over literal-minded imitation and physical force.

Humour arises chiefly from dramatic irony and reversals of knowledge. The audience knows that the “conjuror” is only a squeaking horsehide, that the sexton is hidden in the chest, and that the grandmother is already dead, while the deceived characters interpret appearances incorrectly. Little Claus repeatedly improvises stories from information unavailable to others, turning accidents into convincing supernatural explanations. Great Claus’s extreme literalism creates the central comic pattern: he assumes that killing horses, killing a grandmother, or being thrown into a river will mechanically reproduce Little Claus’s rewards. The closing “Plump!” is an emphatic piece of sound symbolism, ending the tale with abrupt, sardonic finality.

Overall, the style combines oral-tale formulas, transparent syntax, brisk dialogue, repetition, escalating trickery, grotesque violence, and uncomplicated characterization. Its most distinctive feature is the calm narrative treatment of morally dubious ingenuity: the prose presents every deception with cheerful clarity, allowing rhythm, irony, and cumulative reversal to carry both the humour and the tale’s rough justice.

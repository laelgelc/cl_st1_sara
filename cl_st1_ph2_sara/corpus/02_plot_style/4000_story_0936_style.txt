The story adopts a first-person, retrospective travelogue voice in which an American visitor records his initial impressions of London. The narrator is observant, self-mocking, digressive and deliberately unreliable in scale: porters, cabmen, horses, lifts, hats and advertisements receive more attention than famous landmarks. His professed ignorance becomes the chief instrument of perception, allowing ordinary encounters to appear strange, theatrical and newly significant. Cultural comparison, especially between London and New York, structures much of the narration, but the tone is more comic and exploratory than conventionally judgmental.

The dominant mode is ironic observational comedy. Minor inconveniences are inflated into crises, while mundane competence is described as heroism, sainthood or supernatural achievement. Hyperbole repeatedly turns social discomfort into warfare, catastrophe or epic drama: baggage threatens “hideous confessions of imbecility,” a hansom becomes a cannon, indifference destroys cities, and advertising attacks with “horse, foot, guns.” The narrator often establishes an absurd proposition, pursues it with mock-logical seriousness, and then overturns or qualifies it. This creates a dry, deadpan humour founded on disproportion, false deduction and ceremonious overstatement.

The prose combines long, winding, carefully balanced sentences with short emphatic declarations. Extended sentences accumulate qualifications, parenthetical remarks and argumentative pivots, reproducing a mind revising its impressions as it speaks. Abrupt statements such as “It was drill, plain, simple drill” or “It was a tragedy” provide rhythmic contrast and lend comic authority to questionable conclusions. Repetition is prominent, particularly when a phrase becomes a thematic refrain: “the porter and the cabman,” “my cylinder,” “the silence of London,” and above all “drill.” Paragraphs frequently move from concrete observation to analogy, from analogy to anecdote, and from anecdote to an exaggerated general theory of society.

The diction mixes formal, Latinate and quasi-philosophical language with colloquial American phrasing. Elevated terms such as “respectable rumination,” “mental amalgamation,” “palpable warm-heartedness” and “informative sign” are applied to trivial circumstances, creating mock-solemnity. Americanisms, frontier slang and blunt expressions such as “go to Hades,” “low-down cusses” and “scuffling for coin” interrupt this ceremonious register. British pronunciation is rendered phonetically in dialogue—“nyme,” “gowing,” “byby,” “roight”—so that speech itself becomes part of the traveller’s estrangement. Italics and emphatic stress reproduce the rhythms of spoken admonition.

Imagery is vivid, kinetic and often surreal. London is rendered through darkness, rain, gaslight and restricted visibility: streets become tunnels or monstrous caves, lamps resemble miners’ lights, and each person inhabits a private “cylinder of vision.” The city’s lights are “little gas fishes” swimming in a sea of night. Vehicles become torrents or battalions; buses resemble elephants; fallen horses are dismantled and “reconstructed.” These metaphors do more than decorate the scene: they translate unfamiliar urban experience into military, mechanical, theatrical, aquatic and frontier terms familiar to the narrator.

Sound and silence form a central sensory contrast. New York is personified as a roaring, aspiring creature whose citizens seem legally required to carry cymbals and drums, whereas London possesses a disciplined, paradoxical silence—a low collective drone perceived as quiet. This contrast supports the story’s broader interest in social order. London’s civility is not sentimentalized as innate goodness but interpreted as “artificial virtue,” the product of habit, convention and drill. Porters, cabmen, rescuers, police and crowds follow recognized forms, even when those forms require ritual reproach before practical assistance.

The chaptered structure is episodic rather than plot-driven. Each section isolates an encounter or phenomenon—the railway station, the cab ride, skating horses, a fallen horse, the top-hat, the hotel lift, traffic control and advertising—and develops it into a comic meditation. Digressions can become elaborate stories in their own right, most notably the Western anecdote about the top-hat, but they eventually return to the immediate London scene. Pacing therefore alternates between sharply observed moments and expansive associative detours.

Characterization is achieved through gesture, manner, occupation and social ritual rather than psychological depth. The porter and cabman are defined by disciplined courtesy; the rescuers by formulaic rebuke followed by effective cooperation; the policemen by unruffled authority; the hotel staff by weary or funereal slowness. Animals and objects are frequently anthropomorphized, while people are compared to mechanisms, troops, corpses or theatrical figures. This reversal contributes to the story’s comic estrangement.

Overall, the style is impressionistic, satirical and essayistic: a blend of travel narrative, urban sketch, tall tale and social commentary. Its signature qualities are the collision of precise observation with extravagant analogy, the mock-philosophical elevation of trivial details, the use of naïveté as a critical lens, and a rhythm of assertion, qualification and comic reversal. Beneath the humour lies a sustained inquiry into how cities train their inhabitants—how manners, traffic, class symbols, speech and advertising shape behaviour more powerfully than individual intention.

The story adopts a late-Victorian adventure style that gradually develops into fantastic horror and moral irony. Its narrative begins like a pursuit romance, with mounted men tracking fugitives through an exotic wilderness, but the realistic chase is interrupted by an uncanny natural phenomenon: airborne masses of web carrying enormous spiders. The fantastic element is presented through careful observation rather than immediate explanation, giving the tale the air of a traveller’s account or speculative natural history. The final movement returns to the pursuit plot, but with the leader’s heroism exposed as brutality, cowardice, and self-serving persistence.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, though its focalisation shifts. Early passages survey the landscape from a detached, panoramic viewpoint; much of the middle is filtered through the little man’s wandering thoughts; the crisis then narrows around the perceptions of the man with the silver bridle. This mobility allows the narrator to contrast outward hierarchy with inward judgement. Characters are usually identified by recurring physical or social tags—“the little man,” “the gaunt man with the scarred lip,” and “the man with the silver bridle”—rather than personal names. The effect is archetypal and faintly allegorical, reducing them to rank, temperament, and visible emblem. The leader’s changing designation is especially significant: once his authority and possessions have been stripped away, he becomes “the man who once had a silver-studded bridle,” a quietly mocking verbal demotion.

The prose alternates between expansive description and terse action. Landscape sentences are long, cumulative, and syntactically layered, using coordinated clauses, parenthetical qualifications, and repeated spatial markers to create breadth: valleys spread into “purple distances,” hills recede into haze, and mountains seem suspended in the sky. During danger, the syntax contracts into rapid verbs, fragments, cries, and rhythmic sound effects. Repetitions such as “Clatter, clatter, thud, thud” imitate galloping, while sequences of active participles—rising, falling, sinking, rebounding, soaring—convey the smooth but relentless movement of the webs. Ellipses and spaced punctuation repeatedly suspend action or leave violence partly unstated, most notably in the little man’s death.

The diction is formal, precise, and occasionally archaic. Expressions such as “for a space,” “athwart,” “sere,” “behold,” “suffice it,” “minded to,” and “unavailing” give the narration a period quality. This elevated vocabulary is mixed with concrete physical detail: parched lips, bent grass, blood smears, wriggling legs, torn cobweb, and smashed spider bodies. Some phrasing carries the assumptions of imperial adventure fiction, particularly references to “white men” and the “half-caste girl,” while the supposedly civilised pursuers are steadily shown to be morally compromised. The story therefore uses the idiom of colonial romance while undermining its heroic pretensions.

Visual imagery dominates. The setting is painted in muted, desiccated colours—yellow grass, olive-dun trees, purple-black sticks, bluish slopes, grey webs—producing a scorched and nearly lifeless atmosphere. Against this still background, movement becomes ominously conspicuous. A snake, a fleeing dog, galloping hogs, bending reeds, and drifting white globes form a chain of warnings before the spiders are revealed. Similes initially domesticate the strange objects by comparing them to thistle-down, sheets, jellyfish, balloons, fog, and sails, but each comparison also makes them more grotesque. The webs seem simultaneously organic and mechanical: they are “airships,” “air bundles,” and an advancing “army.” This blend of scientific scrutiny and imaginative analogy is central to the uncanny effect.

Atmosphere is built through delayed recognition. The valley’s silence is noticed before its cause is understood; animals flee before the riders appreciate the danger; the white globes first appear beautiful or merely curious; only gradually do threads, eyes, bodies, and jointed limbs become visible. The narrative repeatedly moves from perception to uncertainty to reinterpretation. Questions such as what has vanished from the soundscape, why the dog is running, and whether the drifting material is thistle-down make discovery experiential. The horror comes less from surprise than from the accumulation of ambiguous signs.

Dialogue is sparse, clipped, and strongly hierarchical. The leader speaks in commands, curses, and rhetorical dismissals—“Come on!” and “What do these things matter?”—while his followers use shorter, cautious observations. Their speech reveals character more directly than exposition: the gaunt man is loyal and practical, the little man reflective and resentful, and the master violently impatient. After the disaster, dialogue becomes a moral confrontation. The little man’s plain accusation, “You are a coward like myself,” punctures the leader’s cultivated authority. The master responds not by disproving it but by redefining cowardice, invoking reputation, and calculating the usefulness of the remaining horse. His euphemistic logic turns murder into a practical necessity.

Characterisation depends on contrast between action and self-image. The master imagines himself as a commanding, almost providential figure, but he abandons the follower who has just saved him. He then murders the surviving witness and interprets his own survival as divine preservation. His reliquary and expression of “serene resignation” create sharp dramatic irony, since the reader has just witnessed his treachery. The little man, though fearful and subordinate, possesses the clearest moral intelligence. His private reflections expose the arbitrary basis of authority: the master’s power lies largely in the force with which he expects obedience. The gaunt man’s loyalty makes his abandonment particularly damning.

The pacing follows a controlled escalation. A slow opening establishes geography, pursuit, thirst, fatigue, and social tension. A prolonged middle section introduces environmental anomalies one by one. The spider attack then accelerates into chaotic, almost cinematic fragments. Afterward, the pace slows again in the shelter of the ravine, creating room for ethical reckoning. The abrupt offstage killing is followed by a deceptively tranquil sunset, and the distant smoke restores the original chase. This circular return produces the final irony: the supernatural ordeal has not reformed the pursuer but has merely taught him to become more cunning.

The central themes are the fragility of human dominance, the moral emptiness of authoritarian power, and the ease with which fear strips away heroic identity. Nature is not deliberately evil, but it is vast, alien, and indifferent to rank. Horses bolt, swords break, and titles become meaningless before a migrating swarm. At the same time, the leader is more morally dangerous than the spiders. The creatures act according to instinct and circumstance; he rationalises betrayal, murder, and renewed persecution. Web imagery ultimately acquires a figurative meaning. In the final line, “The next time I must spin a web,” he converts his encounter into a lesson in entrapment, suggesting that calculated deception—not courage—is the form his power will now take.

The overall tone combines wonder, menace, grim comedy, and sardonic judgement. Humorous touches—the curse against white horses, the little man’s thoughts about the authority contained in “Come on!”, and the master’s offended vanity—coexist with visceral horror. The narrator rarely delivers an explicit moral verdict, instead using labels, contrasts, understatement, and irony to expose the leader. The result is a vividly descriptive speculative adventure whose monstrous spectacle serves as a test of character, revealing that the most poisonous creature in the valley is the man who survives it.

The story uses a vigorous early adventure-fiction style, combining frontier realism, melodrama, physical comedy, and heroic romance. Its setting is established through broad, atmospheric description: the Arctic is a hostile, almost sentient force, figured through “ravening” winds, “starving waters,” grinding ice, and cold that cuts like a knife. Personification and violent similes make the landscape an active antagonist rather than a passive backdrop. Descriptions favour stark contrasts—warmth and frost, courage and fear, intoxication and sobriety, communal shelter and desolate wilderness—and repeatedly draw on images of weapons, machinery, animals, and armour.

The narrative voice is confident, omniscient, and occasionally sardonic. It treats Big George’s drunken tyranny with comic exaggeration before shifting into earnest admiration once the rescue begins. Hyperbole is central to the humour: George’s voice resembles “the cough of a bull-walrus,” his limbs are “rock pillars,” and his hand closes like an armoured claw. The narrator often compresses character judgments into witty aphoristic statements, as in the claim that camp activity stops whenever George drinks. This creates a larger-than-life tone in which personalities and dangers are deliberately magnified.

The prose is action-oriented and highly physical. Bodies strain, crash, freeze, bleed, grapple, and stagger; courage is expressed through bodily endurance rather than introspection. Verbs are forceful and kinetic—men are whirled, hurled, dragged, crushed, and driven—while participial phrases sustain movement across long sentences. During fights and rescue scenes, the syntax accumulates actions rapidly, giving the impression of continuous struggle. Shorter declarations and commands interrupt these passages at moments of decision, sharpening urgency: “We’ve got to get them off,” “I’ll stay here,” and “Come, come.” The pacing therefore moves from a leisurely comic opening, through a compact brawl, into an extended survival sequence whose repeated obstacles create mounting suspense.

Dialogue is the chief means of characterization. Frontier and maritime vernacular is represented through dropped consonants, nonstandard grammar, phonetic spellings, contractions, and colourful slang: “ain’t,” “fellers,” “yourn,” “strickly,” “grub-stake,” and “jolt of this disturbance promoter.” George’s speech is boastful, aggressive, and extravagantly idiomatic, while Captain speaks in a more controlled, educated register. The contrast initially marks social antagonism, but their shared plainness under danger gradually erases it. Much of the dialogue has a performative, masculine quality: insults, threats, laconic jokes, and refusals to admit fear substitute for direct emotional confession. Even reconciliation is expressed through competitive banter about finishing the interrupted fight.

Characterization relies on decisive acts and visible traits. Big George begins as a comic bully whose immense body, scars, drunken vanity, and violent reputation dominate the camp. The rescue reveals the honour beneath his brutality: he volunteers without hesitation, demonstrates practical competence, and offers to sacrifice himself. Captain follows the complementary arc of the apparent “educated dude” proving his physical courage, loyalty, and endurance. Their conflict resolves through mutual recognition, producing a familiar frontier ideal in which antagonistic men become “partners” after testing one another. Secondary figures are drawn economically through occupational labels, reputations, gestures, and speech rather than psychological depth.

The structure is strongly symmetrical. The opening paralysis caused by George’s drinking gives way to purposeful communal action; the private, unnecessary fight is replaced by a collective struggle against nature. George first blocks Captain’s exit, then enables his mission. Captain first strikes and humiliates George, then publicly honours him. George’s threatened violence—his promise to “make over” the newcomer—is finally transformed into respect for an equal. The closing joke about resuming the fight recalls the opening conflict while confirming that hostility has become affection.

The dominant themes are courage, redemption through action, loyalty, masculine honour, and the moral testing imposed by extreme environments. The Arctic strips social distinctions down to practical worth: education, reputation, and bravado matter less than willingness to act. The camp initially appears cowardly when its men refuse the rescue, but George and Captain recover the frontier code through self-sacrifice. The story also values competence—dog handling, navigation, paddling, knowledge of ice—as strongly as raw bravery. George’s Indigenous wife is presented as loyal, perceptive, and exceptionally capable, ultimately helping save both men, though her characterization and the repeated use of period terms such as “squaw” reflect a dated colonial perspective. Indigenous and Eskimo characters are often rendered collectively or through simplified speech, whereas the white male protagonists receive fuller individuality.

Emotion is generally externalized through action, bodily sensation, and restrained gestures. Fear appears as numbness, panic, exhaustion, or refusal to move; gratitude appears through handshakes, tears, and “broken words.” Sentiment is present but controlled by rough humour and understatement. The result is a robust, theatrical style that seeks immediacy rather than subtle psychological analysis: expansive scenic description, slang-heavy dialogue, sensational physical danger, and a redemptive climax combine to celebrate fellowship forged through ordeal.

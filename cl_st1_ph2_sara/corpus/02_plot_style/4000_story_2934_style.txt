The story adopts the form of a retrospective first-person testimony, presenting supernatural events with the measured confidence of a factual report. Its narrator is articulate, observant, and apparently rational, yet also morally detached and conspicuously fascinated by the spectacle of fear and death. This tension makes him unreliable without rendering him obviously deceptive: his claim that he merely wishes to “observe” the case conflicts with his covert surveillance, manipulation of the medium, pursuit of the officer, and excitement at being present for an extraordinary murder. The narrative voice therefore combines the authority of a witness with the evasiveness of someone who may be concealing his own implication.

The diction is formal, literary, and distinctly period-inflected. Latinate abstractions such as “solicitude,” “acerbity,” “transformations,” and “purport” sit beside vivid physical descriptions and occasional colloquialisms. Characters are frequently defined through compressed, figurative comparisons: Gratmar is “proud as a tiger-lily and sensitive as a blue-bell,” while the officer has fought “like a bulldog.” The prose also uses dryly comic circumlocution, as in the description of the commanding general as “constructed on dignified and impressive lines.” Such phrasing gives the narration an urbane, ironic polish even when the subject is suicide, haunting, or murder.

Sentence structure is generally elaborate and balanced, with long periodic sentences that accumulate qualifications, alternatives, and subordinate clauses before delivering their main point. This creates the impression of careful reasoning while also allowing the narrator to delay, obscure, or delicately reframe disturbing facts. Semicolons and parenthetical asides are prominent, and lists often proceed in measured triads, as with the prediction that Gratmar will become “a deserter, or a suicide, or a murderer.” Against this syntactic formality, dialogue is brief, urgent, and emotionally heightened. Broken utterances, exclamations, repetitions, and dashes convey panic, particularly in the medium’s speech, while the narrator’s replies remain controlled and faintly sardonic.

The tone blends Gothic dread, psychological curiosity, black comedy, and deadpan irony. Horrific details are delivered with almost clinical composure: Gratmar is blown into “a million fragments,” and the officer’s corpse is described as vulgar and misshapen rather than dignified or tragic. The narrator’s remark that “it is not often that one may be in at such a murder as that” exposes a macabre enthusiasm beneath his pose of disinterested investigation. Humor repeatedly intrudes upon terror through understatement, bureaucratic phrasing, and incongruous practical details, preventing the story from becoming straightforwardly solemn.

Structurally, the narrative is a mystery built through progressive reinterpretation. It begins by establishing an apparently supernatural premise: a sensitive soldier commits suicide in order to gain spiritual power over an abusive officer. A sequence of observed symptoms then seems to confirm the haunting, with the officer’s altered behavior, nightmares, invisible interlocutor, and visit to a medium serving as cumulative evidence. The pursuit and discovery of the body provide a Gothic climax, but the later Swedish letter destabilizes the entire explanation. It proposes that the explosion may have been staged, that Gratmar remained alive, and that the “spirit” was actually a living murderer. The final signature, “Ramtarg,” reverses “Gratmar” and supplies a concealed verbal clue without explicitly resolving the case.

Suspense arises less from fast action than from controlled disclosure. The narrator repeatedly announces that something is extraordinary, terrible, or puzzling before providing its particulars, encouraging the reader to share his interpretive framework. Apparent evidence is filtered through hearsay, observation at a distance, and testimony from compromised witnesses. Even the final explanation arrives indirectly in a letter whose contents are partly summarized rather than reproduced. This layered mediation preserves ambiguity: the supernatural account may be false, the rational account may be a calculated confession, and the narrator may understand more than he admits.

Characterization is economical and caricatural, built from sharply selected physical and temperamental traits. Gratmar is romantic, sensitive, and uncanny; the officer is short, apoplectic, overbearing, and irascible; the medium is defined chiefly through terror. These figures function partly as psychological types within an intellectual puzzle. Physical symptoms—blushing, gasping, sweating, bulging eyes, staggering, and involuntary starts—externalize mental disturbance in a melodramatic but precise manner. The narrator alone remains difficult to classify because his polished language and professed ignorance conceal his motives.

A central stylistic device is dramatic irony. The narrator treats obvious clues as inexplicable, especially the reversed name, and closes by claiming that it merely “haunts” him like a forgotten dream. This exaggerated failure of recognition invites the reader to solve what the speaker will not acknowledge. The ending thus converts the narrative from a ghost story into a possible crime story while leaving a residue of uncanniness. Its characteristic method is to offer a natural explanation that is itself so elaborate, theatrical, and improbable that it does not wholly dispel the supernatural atmosphere.

Overall, the style is that of a fin-de-siècle psychological Gothic tale presented as a sober case history. It favors ornate but controlled prose, ironic detachment, grotesque physical detail, unreliable first-person narration, and a final cryptographic twist. Its most distinctive effect comes from the collision between grave subject matter and levity of treatment: terror is narrated with wit, murder with curiosity, and revelation through deliberate obtuseness.

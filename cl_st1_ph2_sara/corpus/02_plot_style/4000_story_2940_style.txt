The story uses a late-nineteenth-century sentimental-realist style, combining social observation, melodrama, local colour, humour, and moral pathos. Its prose is formal and literary in the narration but sharply contrasted with the simple, dialect-heavy speech of its central character. The narrative voice is omniscient, confident, and openly interpretive: it does not merely describe Baker’s appearance and actions but explains their emotional significance, directs the reader’s sympathy, and frames events in moral terms. This narrator moves readily between close observation, broad social commentary, and dramatic pronouncement.

Description is highly visual and often caricatural. Baker is introduced through his elongated shadow and then assembled detail by detail—stooped shoulders, dusty hair, rawhide suspenders, swollen pockets, and canoe-like boots. Physical features function as outward signs of inner history. His prematurely grey hair, lined face, defensive movements, and imagined shackles gradually disclose trauma before the narrative explicitly explains it. The style frequently relies on emblematic objects: the invisible ball and chain represents psychological captivity, the scarred back becomes “a map” and “a fine piece of mosaic,” and the advancing epidemic is repeatedly figured as a shadow or destructive force. Such images are clear, forceful, and morally charged rather than subtle or ambiguous.

A distinctive feature is the personification of Baker’s boots. They appear rakish, submissive, wistful, cheerful, grateful, and finally lifeless. This running conceit provides comic relief while also acting as an indirect emotional register for Baker, whose damaged mind and limited speech prevent him from articulating his feelings fully. The humour is affectionate but sometimes broad, arising from disproportionate physical description, mock-serious commentary, and the narrator’s elaborate treatment of trivial subjects. The extended account of the boots’ “moral” improvement parodies social elevation and genteel respectability. This whimsical humour heightens rather than cancels the tragedy, because the same familiar objects become solemn witnesses to Baker’s suffering and death.

The diction alternates between elevated abstraction and concrete bodily detail. Phrases such as “calm consciousness of responsibility,” “empty exaltation,” and “an acquiescence to an inevitability” give the narration a ceremonious, rhetorical quality. In moments of crisis, the language becomes more compressed and sensational: “Cholery!” is isolated for maximum impact, while death is personified as sitting upon Baker’s face. Exclamations, dashes, semicolons, and periodic sentences contribute to an emphatic, sometimes theatrical rhythm. Long descriptive passages are balanced by brisk dialogue and short declarative statements, especially at moments of revelation.

Dialogue is the principal means of individualising Baker. His speech uses phonetic dialect and nonstandard grammar—“jess,” “reckin,” “Georgy,” “pertickler,” “curryder,” and “Hunder’d’n One”—to establish region, class, limited education, and mental injury. More important than the dialect itself is his repetitive conversational pattern. He echoes questions with “Me?” or repeats the subject—“Pie?” “Die?”—before answering through formulas such as “Well, I’ll tell you.” These repetitions create comedy early on, but they also reveal confusion, caution, and a habit of obedience formed under coercion. As the story progresses, the same verbal manner acquires dignity: Baker may struggle with ordinary social questions, yet in the epidemic he speaks with practical certainty and acts decisively. His fragmented deathbed speech finally releases the suppressed facts of his wrongful imprisonment.

Characterisation proceeds through delayed revelation and reversal. Baker first appears to be a comic tramp or “lunatic,” then a brutalised prisoner, then a gentle servant, and finally the most competent and courageous person in the hotel. The respectable characters initially possess authority but not understanding; during the cholera outbreak, they become hesitant while Baker assumes command. This reversal is central to the story’s moral design. Social status, polished surroundings, and conventional intelligence prove less valuable than endurance, compassion, and experiential knowledge. The hotel functions as a compressed social world in which wealth and refinement coexist with selfishness, prejudice, and dependence upon an exploited outsider.

The tonal movement is carefully staged. The opening blends grotesque comedy with unease; the hitching-post scene turns abruptly toward horror; the middle section offers warmth and domestic humour; and the final section accelerates into epidemic crisis, heroism, and sacrificial death. Foreshadowing links these phases. Baker’s fear of confinement, familiarity with mass death, and instinctive knowledge of illness all anticipate later disclosures. Repeated motifs—the shadow, the mocking-bird, the imagined chain, Cell 101, and the boots—give the two-part structure cohesion. The return of the mocking-bird and boots in the final sentence produces a circular ending, but their earlier animation has vanished, leaving a deliberately still and elegiac image.

The story’s emotional method is openly sentimental. It seeks pity and indignation through innocence persecuted, kindness restoring a damaged person, hidden nobility revealed in crisis, and death arriving just as pardon and freedom become imaginable. Contrasts are strong and morally legible: polished brass against dusty boots, luxurious guests against the scarred labourer, bodily bondage against nominal freedom, and social insignificance against heroic usefulness. The narrative occasionally intensifies these oppositions through hyperbole—Baker “did the work of fifty men—a hundred men”—and through direct appeals to emotion. The result is a style that values moral clarity, dramatic revelation, and compassionate identification over psychological ambiguity.

Overall, the prose is ornate, rhetorical, and episodically comic, with a strong local-colour voice and a pronounced humanitarian purpose. Its signature effect comes from placing a damaged, repetitive, almost childlike mode of speech inside an elaborately narrated tale of trauma and heroism. Humour makes Baker memorable, recurring symbols make his suffering visible, and the final melodramatic reversal transforms an apparent fool and outcast into the story’s moral and practical authority.

The story adopts the manner of a nineteenth-century moral history for younger readers, combining chronicle, legend, religious instruction, and exemplary biography. Its narrator is omniscient, authoritative, and openly judgmental rather than detached. Historical events are continually interpreted through firm moral categories: peoples and actions are described as “wicked,” “cruel,” “noble,” “unfaithful,” or “infamous,” while the narrator occasionally qualifies doubtful traditions with phrases such as “as some say,” “it was said,” “seems to have,” and “it may thus be hoped.” This creates a double stance in which ancient reports are vividly retold but their reliability is sometimes questioned.

The prose is formal and archaic by modern standards, with British spellings such as “colours” and “splendor” alongside historical and biblical vocabulary: “propitiate,” “disaffection,” “conscript fathers,” “heathen,” and “foul and cruel rites.” The diction repeatedly contrasts civic virtue with savagery, luxury with hardiness, and pagan error with Christian truth. Carthage is framed through biblical associations with Tyre, Zidon, Baal, Ashtoreth, and Moloch, while Roman conduct is treated more ambivalently: Roman soldiers are “plain, hardy, fierce, and pitiless,” capable of cruelty, yet Regulus embodies an elevated Roman ideal of duty, constancy, self-command, and fidelity to one’s word. The cultural perspective is explicitly Christian and providential. Regulus is presented as unknowingly approaching divine truth through natural virtue, “ignorantly” worshipping the true God through the limited religion available to him.

Sentences are generally long, cumulative, and heavily coordinated, often carrying a sequence of historical facts, explanations, and moral comments through semicolons and repeated conjunctions. Parenthetical qualifications and relative clauses give the narration a discursive, instructive rhythm. Paragraphs tend to proceed chronologically, but the narrator pauses to explain geography, religious customs, military tactics, political institutions, or the moral significance of an incident. Transitions such as “however,” “in the meantime,” “then,” and “thus” maintain the movement of a historical lesson. Numbers, names, dates, and concrete details—the seven-acre farm, the 120-foot serpent, the three hundred villages—lend the account an air of documentary specificity even when the underlying material is legendary.

The narrative structure moves from broad historical background to an increasingly focused character study. It begins with Rome’s conflict with Carthage, follows Regulus through invasion, victory, pride, defeat, and captivity, and reaches its emotional centre in his return to Rome under oath. The concluding paragraphs then separate the essential moral truth of his conduct from sensational stories about his death. This produces a martyr-like arc without making Regulus a Christian martyr: his greatness lies not in physical suffering but in voluntarily accepting likely death rather than betray Rome or break his pledged word.

Characterization is economical and exemplary. Regulus is defined through decisive actions, restrained emotions, and brief declarations rather than psychological introspection. His poverty, concern for his farm, severity toward enemies, refusal to claim senatorial rank, and rejection of release all reinforce a single ideal of austere Roman integrity. His flaw—proud harshness after victory—is acknowledged, but even this is folded into the claim that he is as merciless toward himself as toward others. Marcia functions as a moral counterpoint: grief becomes vindictiveness, illustrating how private vengeance can corrupt loyalty and love.

Dialogue is sparse, elevated, and aphoristic. Regulus’s speeches sound ceremonial and compressed, with balanced oppositions such as life versus honour, torture versus guilt, and slavery versus Roman spirit. Quotations serve less to reproduce natural conversation than to crystallize ethical principles. Emotional effects are produced through restraint: Regulus pauses outside the city, refuses his family’s caresses, stands rather than sits, and returns to captivity “as calmly as if he had been returning to his home.” These visual, emblematic gestures give the episode the quality of a historical tableau.

The tone mixes didactic gravity, patriotic admiration, religious earnestness, and occasional horror. Descriptions of sacrifice, war, and alleged torture are vivid but not lingered over for their own sake; they heighten the moral stakes. Direct authorial intrusions—“it must be feared,” “it was wonderful,” and “it may thus be hoped”—guide the reader’s sympathies and conclusions. Overall, the style is that of an edifying historical legend: expansive in background, linear in narration, morally explicit, biblically inflected, and designed to present constancy, public duty, and truthfulness as virtues greater than safety or life.

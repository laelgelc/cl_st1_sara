A visitor to London becomes fascinated by the city’s omnipresent advertising posters and reflects humorously on how vague, repeated slogans could torment anyone with a guilty conscience. While examining a derelict warehouse buried beneath layers of decaying bills, he notices a procession of advertising vans carrying sensational newspaper headlines.

Seeing a figure reclining inside one van, he follows it and discovers a genial, middle-aged man who calls himself the King of the Bill-Stickers. Invited aboard, the narrator rides through London with him while they smoke and share rum-and-water. The King recounts the history and practices of his trade: its origins in small proclamations, its expansion during the State Lottery era, the rise of larger posters and specialized tools, and the fierce competition among rival bill-sticking companies, which sometimes led to arrests and violent fights over valuable hoardings.

He also describes the profession’s divisions, wages, territories, advertising sites, informal rules, technical innovations, and decline following the abolition of lotteries and the loss of good posting locations. He laments the inferior standards of newer competitors and explains that effective posters should contain only a few striking lines.

Eventually the narrator becomes dizzy and ill, though he jokingly blames poisonous paste or printer’s ink rather than the tobacco and alcohol. He is helped from the van and recovers on a doorstep, but the procession and its self-proclaimed monarch have vanished. Despite later searching other advertising vans, he never encounters the King again.

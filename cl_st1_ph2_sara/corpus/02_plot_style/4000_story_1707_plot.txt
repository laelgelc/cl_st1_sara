Colonel Aquila Telfair, a proud guardian of Southern tradition, becomes editor of a Georgia magazine devoted exclusively to Southern writers. He fills its pages with antiquated literature and contributions chosen more for their authors’ Confederate pedigrees and family connections than for merit, leaving the publication obscure and unprofitable.

T. T. Thacker, a practical New York magazine promoter, offers to invest in the magazine and expand its circulation by publishing popular work from prominent writers across the country. Though Telfair resists Northern influence, he reveals that he is considering an exceptional eight-thousand-word submission by a famous Northerner. He admires the article but refuses to print it until he can establish that its author has acceptable Southern connections.

Two weeks later, Thacker discovers that Telfair has published President Theodore Roosevelt’s message to Congress, presenting Roosevelt as a member of Georgia’s well-known Bulloch family through his maternal ancestry. Thus, Telfair admits Northern material only after finding a way to claim its author for the South.

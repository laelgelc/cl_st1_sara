The story is written as a comic Australian bush yarn, combining social realism, vernacular speech and dry, understated satire. Its first-person plural narration—predominantly “we,” with occasional shifts to “I”—creates the sense of a communal anecdote recalled by one participant on behalf of the whole travelling party. The narrator is observant, worldly and wry rather than emotionally confessional. He participates in events but also stands slightly apart from them, generalising about human behaviour and exposing the passengers’ vanity, irritability and gullibility.

The prose alternates between vividly descriptive narration and brisk, dialogue-led scenes. Descriptive passages use concrete physical detail to communicate the misery of coach travel: cramped bodies, numb feet, freezing rain, darkness, defective furniture and greasy candlelight. Exaggeration and incongruous comparisons turn discomfort into comedy, as when the travellers’ feet become “as big as camel’s,” the South Pole seems close, or the passengers climb down “like so many cripples.” The hostile bush is rendered through bleak, compressed imagery—“tree-walls,” “the bed of the bush,” and a shanty as “cold and dark as a bushman’s grave”—but atmospheric seriousness is repeatedly undercut by jokes.

The diction mixes standard literary narration with strongly marked Australian colloquial language. Terms such as “shearers,” “bagmen,” “squatter,” “cockatoo,” “spieler,” “battlers,” “stoush,” “shout” and “black gin” establish the social and historical milieu. Dialogue is phonetically rendered through contractions, dropped consonants and nonstandard grammar: “‘Tain’t fur,” “wanter,” “ter,” “somewheres,” “ain’t,” and “them horses.” This gives each exchange an oral, performative quality and makes the piece sound like a tale told among travellers. The vocabulary also reflects its period and includes racial and class labels that would now be regarded as dated or offensive.

Sentence structure is flexible. Longer sentences accumulate observations through clauses, dashes and semicolons, often building toward an ironic qualification or punch line. Short statements and repeated interjections—especially “Hush!”—control the comic timing. Parenthetical remarks provide mock explanations and sly reversals, while recurring phrases such as “an abstract question” become comic motifs. The narrator often begins with a plausible psychological observation, elaborates it in mock-serious language, and ends by revealing something petty, stubborn or absurd.

Humour arises chiefly from deadpan narration, repetition, character contrast and delayed revelation. The driver’s surliness is treated as if it were a complex philosophical temperament, although his thoughts concern harness, horses and whether anything is “his affair.” The publican’s solemn concern for his supposedly dying wife forces the passengers into exaggerated sympathy and silence; the eventual disclosure that she left years earlier retrospectively transforms every whispered exchange into part of a calculated fraud. The passengers’ moral indignation is also satirised because they first suppress the only men who protest and later suppress the know-it-all who claims to have understood the trick from the beginning.

Characterisation is economical and typological. Most figures are identified by roles or nicknames—the Joker, the Pilgrim, the driver, the publican, the growler—rather than by personal names. They are defined through speech habits, physical gestures and predictable social behaviour. The driver scratches his head, grunts and refuses responsibility; the Joker converts hardship into dry wit; the Pilgrim protests ineffectually; and the landlord weaponises solemnity and outrage. These types support the story’s collective emphasis and reproduce the social variety of itinerant bush life.

The narrative follows a compact anecdotal arc: prolonged discomfort and anticipation, arrival at a seemingly tragic household, enforced delay, uneasy accommodation, departure, investigation and comic exposure. Suspense depends less on danger than on the discrepancy between appearance and reality. The travellers imagine hospitality, encounter deprivation, accept a moral explanation for it, and finally discover that their decency has been exploited. The Joker’s calculation of the publican’s profits supplies a practical reckoning and crystallises the fraud.

The overall tone is laconic, sardonic and unsentimental. Hardship is acknowledged without pathos, and fellowship is shown as fragile, governed by discomfort, pride and suspicion. The ending avoids reconciliation or heroic retaliation: the passengers disperse “in an atmosphere of mutual suspicion and distrust.” This anti-climactic close reinforces the story’s central comic insight that ordinary people are readily manipulated by convention, embarrassment and the desire to appear considerate, then become most resentful when forced to recognise their own complicity.

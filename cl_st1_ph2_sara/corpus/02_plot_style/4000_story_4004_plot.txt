A group of young women who idealize men are shaken when Poll, compelled by her father’s will to read every book in the London Library, reveals that most books written by men are dreadful. Questioning the assumption that women should bear children while men create civilization, they form a society to investigate whether men actually produce “good people and good books.” They vow not to have children until they can judge whether humanity deserves to continue.

Over five years, the women infiltrate male institutions, examine scholarship, art, law, the military, business, science, politics, and literature, and question men about honor, morality, work, wealth, and power. Their findings are absurd and contradictory: male civilization has achieved remarkable scientific and technological feats, yet it is also vain, unjust, exploitative, intellectually dishonest, and artistically mediocre. Castalia breaks the society’s vow after falling in love and becoming pregnant, leading the group to challenge conventional ideas of chastity.

Before the women can reach a conclusion, the First World War begins—a catastrophe whose purpose none of them can explain. After the war, Cassandra and Castalia revisit the society’s records and recognize the inadequacy of their old ideals. Castalia argues that women themselves have enabled men’s destructive intellectual activity by bearing, nurturing, and freeing them to pursue power and prestige. She jokingly proposes that men should bear children so they might have a harmless occupation, but Cassandra points out that society cannot care properly even for existing children.

Realizing that literacy inevitably leads women to question inherited beliefs, they give the society’s papers to Castalia’s young daughter Ann and appoint her president of a future society. Ann, still happily playing with a doll, bursts into tears at the burden placed upon her.

The story adopts the form of a colonial Australian bush yarn: anecdotal, episodic, and grounded in the practical routines of station life. It is narrated retrospectively in the first-person plural, with “we” representing the community of unpaid jackaroos rather than a strongly individualized speaker. This collective perspective creates the intimacy of a tale exchanged among people who share the same working culture, while occasional retrospective comments—such as the narrator’s later recognition of M’Gregor’s compassion—add maturity and emotional insight.

The narrative voice is dry, observant, and gently satirical. Hardship and incompetence are described through comic understatement, as when the jackaroos “got nothing a year, and earned it,” or when the pursuit of barely valuable brumbies exhausts several good horses. Humour often depends on incongruous comparisons, mock-solemn phrasing, and laconic exaggeration: a camp-horse is said to be as useful as “side-pockets to a pig,” while the cunning brumbies become “as cunning as policemen.” The prose is amused by bush pretensions and rivalries, but it does not treat its characters contemptuously. Beneath the comedy lies sympathy for poverty, illness, loyalty, and the emotional reserve expected of working men.

The diction combines relatively formal narration with dense Australian pastoral vocabulary. Terms such as “jackaroos,” “boundary riders,” “mobs,” “drovers,” “swagman,” “moke,” “camp-horse,” “brumbies,” and “back-block” establish the social and occupational world without extensive explanation. This gives the story an insider’s authority and makes bush life feel like a complete culture with its own hierarchies, prejudices, skills, and idioms. Period expressions and financial references—“fifteen notes,” “thirty shillings,” and “a fiver”—further locate the story in a late-colonial environment.

Dialogue is central to characterization. The drover’s speech is sparse, plain, and emotionally guarded, supporting the narrator’s claim that genuine bushmen are “monosyllabic.” His repeated judgment that the horse is “white when he’s wanted” becomes both the animal’s name and the story’s governing idea: true worth reveals itself under pressure rather than in appearance or everyday behaviour. M’Gregor’s dialogue is rendered in emphatic Scots eye dialect—“horrse,” “whaur,” “verra,” “muckle,” and “ye ken”—which supplies comic energy and makes his voice instantly recognizable. His long, irritable speeches contrast with the drover’s brevity, yet his practical generosity exposes a tenderness that his abrasive language ordinarily conceals.

Sentences vary according to narrative function. Expository passages use long, balanced constructions and catalogues to explain station practices or evoke the cumulative hardships of fever. Action scenes accelerate through repeated clauses, geographical lists, and parallel phrasing: the horse runs “from hill to hill, from range to range,” over flats, gullies, scrub, and ridges. This accumulation conveys distance, speed, and endurance. Short sentences are reserved for turns of judgment or plot—“How he did open our eyes!” and “And then one day he disappeared”—giving key developments the force of oral-storytelling beats.

The structure is linear but carefully staged around reversals of expectation. It begins with a broad account of Buckalong, narrows to M’Gregor’s hostility toward drovers and horse sellers, introduces an apparently worthless horse, then gradually uncovers both M’Gregor’s compassion and the animal’s exceptional ability. The horse’s rise from “pariah” to local legend forms the central comic-heroic arc. The final revelation—that the original drover has apparently stolen or reclaimed him—echoes the opening sale and completes the story with an ironic circularity. The ending remains deliberately unresolved: legal authority cannot reach the remote interior, and the men accept that neither drover nor horse will return.

Characterization is largely indirect. M’Gregor is first presented as miserly, prejudiced, and combative, but his shared experience of Queensland fever alters his voice and conduct. His generosity is never sentimentalized; it appears through the price he pays and his terse defence of the drover. Likewise, the drover’s attachment to the horse is communicated by a hand laid on its neck, an awkward farewell, and a repeated phrase rather than an explicit declaration. The horse itself is anthropomorphized only lightly. Its laziness, appetite, excitement, and extraordinary professional instinct make it a recognizable bush character, but its abilities remain rooted in concrete horsemanship.

The dominant tone blends comedy, admiration, and restrained pathos. Social antagonisms, dangerous work, illness, and theft are filtered through the stoic humour of people accustomed to uncertainty. Emotional moments are brief and oblique, preventing the story from becoming sentimental. Even the ending treats the likely theft with rueful acceptance rather than indignation. This tonal restraint reinforces the story’s values: competence matters more than display, loyalty is expressed through action, and both men and animals may possess hidden qualities invisible to casual judgment.

Description is selective and functional. Landscapes are named through working terrain—paddocks, roads, ranges, gullies, hop-scrub, and stringybark ridges—rather than through lyrical contemplation. Physical portraits similarly emphasize evidence: the drover’s scar, feverish colouring, and poverty; the horse’s ribs, hips, legs, and length of rein. The imagery often draws on animals or rural objects, preserving the perspective of station workers. During the brumby chase, however, the prose becomes more expansive and kinetic, briefly elevating the horse into a near-legendary figure that moves “like a kangaroo” and “smothered” the quarry for pace.

Overall, the style is that of a polished oral anecdote: colloquial but controlled, humorous without frivolity, and emotionally powerful because feeling is seldom stated directly. Its defining features are the communal narrator, occupational authenticity, vivid dialect, aphoristic wit, escalating anecdotal structure, masculine reticence, and an ironic ending that leaves moral judgment open.

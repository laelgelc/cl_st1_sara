The story is written as a literary fairy tale or philosophical fable, combining an apparently simple supernatural premise with increasingly dark social satire. Its narrative begins in the expansive, conversational manner of an oral storyteller, then develops through repetition, reversals of status, and an inexorable moral decline. The world is only loosely specified as the “hot lands,” the “cold lands,” and a princess’s kingdom, giving it the universality of folklore while allowing recognisable features of nineteenth-century European society—scholars, newspapers, professors, spas, courts, class etiquette, and royal marriage—to become objects of criticism.

The narrator is omniscient, conspicuous, and gently intrusive. Rather than maintaining a neutral distance, the voice comments, explains, exaggerates, and occasionally addresses what an observer might have noticed: “if anyone had paid particular attention.” Phrases such as “sure enough,” “yes,” “in truth,” “as you know,” and “that was wisely thought” create the impression of a storyteller speaking directly to an audience. At first this voice seems genial and amused, but its apparent innocence becomes ironic as the events grow crueler. Horrific developments are often related in the same matter-of-fact cadence as comic ones, making the conclusion especially chilling.

The diction is archaic and visibly shaped by an older English translation. Forms such as “thou,” “thee,” “thy,” “wert,” “didst,” “hast,” and “canst” coexist with formal constructions including “Whom have I the honor of speaking?” and “You will oblige me.” Frequent words such as “nay,” “marvellous,” “circumspect,” “saloon,” and “watering-place” reinforce the nineteenth-century register. The translation also preserves Danish social and linguistic distinctions, most notably the contrast between familiar “thou” and formal “you.” Footnotes explain cultural references and wordplay rather than naturalising them fully into English. Some racial terminology and ideas about complexion are period-specific, dated, and offensive by modern standards; they belong to the historical voice and translation context rather than to contemporary usage.

The prose is syntactically energetic and accumulative. Long sentences are built from clauses linked by semicolons, dashes, and repeated conjunctions, especially “and.” This produces a bustling, breathless effect in descriptive passages: tradespeople, bells, animals, funerals, carriages, songs, and street cries are piled into catalogues. Elsewhere, short exclamations and abrupt questions puncture the flow—“Poesy!” “Everything!” “What is that?”—giving scenes a theatrical rhythm. Parenthetical qualifications and self-corrections mimic spontaneous speech, while dashes frequently create pauses, afterthoughts, or comic turns.

Repetition is one of the dominant stylistic devices. Words and formulas recur with slight variation: “I saw everything, and I know everything”; “the true, and the good, and the beautiful”; “he made himself little; he made himself great”; “years and days passed away.” Such repetition gives the narrative a folktale cadence, but it also exposes character. The shadow’s repeated claim to universal knowledge becomes a boast that conceals emptiness, while the learned man’s repeated devotion to truth, goodness, and beauty marks both his integrity and his helplessness. Parallel phrasing supports the story’s central reversal: the shadow becomes master and the master becomes shadow.

Dialogue occupies much of the narrative and functions as a contest for social authority. The learned man speaks earnestly, abstractly, and courteously; the shadow speaks fluently, opportunistically, and with calculated ambiguity. Their shifting use of pronouns dramatises their changing hierarchy. The shadow’s language is especially sophistic: it turns lies into plausible social narratives, presents domination as generosity, and disguises threats as practical advice. The princess’s dialogue is similarly governed by status, appearances, and inference. Characters often reveal less through direct confession than through the assumptions embedded in their speech.

The imagery is organised around light, darkness, reflection, and visibility. The fierce sun shrinks the original shadow; lamplight projects it across the street; moonlight allows it to move unseen; the house of Poesy glows with supernatural radiance; and public illumination finally celebrates falsehood’s triumph. Light does not simply represent truth. It can expose, distort, burn, or create shadows, while darkness enables secret knowledge and social power. This instability gives the central metaphor unusual complexity: the shadow is at once an alter ego, a servant, a spy, a social mask, and the embodiment of the learned man’s disowned worldly capacities.

Personification is sustained with deliberate literalness. The shadow acquires flesh, clothes, money, manners, and political influence, yet continues to behave according to shadow-like principles: it peers into private spaces, thrives through proximity to others, changes apparent size, and depends upon appearances. Clothing imagery is particularly important. Boots, black cloth, gold chains, seals, and diamond rings constitute the “human varnish” by which society recognises personhood. External polish repeatedly outweighs inward substance, and the shadow becomes socially real because it looks and performs the part.

The tone moves from comic wonder through urbane irony to grotesque menace. Early absurdities—the heat shrinking a shadow, the shadow hiding behind a cake seller, the need to grow a beard—retain the playfulness of a children’s tale. Yet the humour steadily darkens as voyeurism, blackmail, vanity, class deference, and political credulity reward the shadow. The final execution is understated rather than graphically described: the learned man is simply “deprived…of life.” That euphemism imitates the moral evasions of the society being satirised.

The story’s satire targets a world that values spectacle, confidence, wealth, fashionable polish, and compromising knowledge more than truth or wisdom. Intellectual and artistic ideals are powerless when they cannot adapt to social appetite; “the true, and the good, and the beautiful” are compared to “roses for a cow.” Institutions confer legitimacy upon the shadow because it is useful, intimidating, and impressive. Even genuine learning becomes evidence of the impostor’s greatness once the scholar has been publicly relabelled as a subordinate.

Overall, the style blends folkloric simplicity, Romantic symbolism, philosophical allegory, social comedy, and macabre irony. Its signature effects come from the tension between a warm, almost childlike storytelling voice and a pessimistic vision of adult society; between supernatural fantasy and precise observations of status; and between verbal brightness and moral darkness. The ending offers no restorative fairy-tale justice. Instead, the story completes its central inversion with brutal economy: appearance becomes authority, the copy destroys the original, and society applauds.

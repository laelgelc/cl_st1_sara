“Lark Street” uses a contemporary literary-realist style disrupted by grotesque magical realism. Its impossible premise—the appearance of two aborted fetuses as speaking, mobile children—is introduced immediately and treated with deadpan matter-of-factness. The surreal is not explained or framed as a dream; instead, it functions as a physical manifestation of guilt, avoidance, and moral uncertainty. This flat acceptance of the impossible allows the story to remain emotionally and socially realistic even when its imagery becomes macabre.

The story is narrated in the first person by a young, emotionally evasive man. His voice is colloquial, self-conscious, and only partially reliable. He can describe his own cowardice with considerable accuracy, yet repeatedly minimizes it through phrases such as “I guess,” “kind of,” and “basically,” or retreats into passive constructions and qualifications. His narration continually exposes the distance between what he tells himself and what he has actually done. Brief self-corrections—“we couldn’t handle it, I mean you—I mean a child, children”—make this conflict audible. The voice is confessional without being fully absolving: the narrator recognizes his failures, but recognition does not reliably lead to responsibility.

The diction mixes casual speech, profanity, adolescent slang, and sharp lyrical imagery. Expressions such as “whatever,” “Pops,” “cojones,” and “gonna” give the dialogue a contemporary, youthful texture, while more figurative lines elevate moments of emotional pressure. Disappointment “rains,” a dying fetus resembles “a plucked leaf,” and the narrator carries the twins as if guarding “a flame from the wind.” This contrast between ordinary language and poetic compression keeps the story from becoming either clinically detached or sentimentally ornate. Medical and anatomical details are rendered plainly but vividly: translucent skin, webbed extremities, visible brains, blood, slime, ash. The body imagery is deliberately uncomfortable, forcing the narrator—and the reader—to confront what he has avoided seeing.

Dialogue drives much of the story. It is quick, adversarial, and darkly comic, with Jackie Gunner acting as an aggressive embodiment of accusation and Jamie Lou initially appearing gentler and more conciliatory. Their childlike behavior is combined with adult verbal intelligence, producing tonal instability: they bicker, kick, hug, mock, moralize, and ask existential questions. Humor arises from their size, their precocious language, and the narrator’s awkward attempts at parenting them, but each joke carries an undertow of horror. Jackie’s line about being “a trimester from genitalia,” for example, is comic in rhythm while intensifying the story’s central unease. Repetition gives the dialogue additional force, particularly recurring phrases such as “look at me,” “we’re not gonna be people,” “it wasn’t my fault,” and “it’s okay.” These phrases change meaning as the story progresses, moving from accusation or reassurance toward irony.

The syntax is generally direct and accessible, favoring short declarative sentences and compact paragraphs. Fragments are frequent and often used for emphasis: “Smaller than a field mouse.” “Twins, I thought.” “Mommy.” At moments of anxiety, the prose becomes more recursive and hesitant, reproducing the narrator’s inability to speak cleanly about what happened. At other points, longer accumulative sentences catalogue suppressed memories, as in the passage describing the pregnancy-test purchase. That extended sequence of repeated “How” clauses slows the narrative and reveals how intensely the narrator observed details while remaining emotionally passive. Rhythm therefore mirrors his psychology: clipped when he is defending himself, expansive when involuntary memory overtakes him.

The structure alternates between the present-night journey and retrospective scenes involving Jaclyn, the abortion, the clinic, and the couple’s earlier relationship. These flashbacks are triggered associatively rather than announced formally. A request to describe the mother leads into the faulty Volvo and an early date; discussion of the pregnancy leads into the drugstore; the psychic visit recalls the narrator’s secret manipulation. This braided structure gradually revises the reader’s understanding of him. Information is withheld not chiefly to create a conventional twist, but to reproduce his pattern of delayed admission. The most consequential revelation—that he paid the psychic to influence Jaclyn—is disclosed during confrontation, when concealment is no longer sustainable.

Concrete objects carry symbolic weight throughout. The unreliable Volvo reflects a relationship and a narrator who keep moving by avoiding full stops. The psychic’s reading literalizes the narrator’s desire to control uncertainty and outsource responsibility. Hands recur as instruments of care, concealment, interpretation, and harm: he fails to hold Jaclyn’s hand, carries the twins in his palm, hides them in his pocket, offers his bloodstained hand for a reading, and watches Jaclyn finally gather the children into hers. Looking is equally important. Jackie repeatedly demands to be seen, while the narrator’s defining action is refusal: he cannot look into the bathroom, cannot fully look at the fetuses, and repeatedly looks away from Jaclyn. Vision becomes an ethical act, and avoidance becomes the story’s central form of failure.

The tone balances horror, bleak comedy, tenderness, and moral ambiguity. The abortion itself is not presented through a simple ideological judgment. Instead, the story concentrates on unequal experience, emotional responsibility, coercive influence, and the narrator’s fear that caring openly might alter Jaclyn’s choice. Jaclyn is given less narrative space because the story is confined to his perspective, but her autonomy ultimately corrects his self-centered interpretation: she makes clear that neither the psychic nor the narrator made the decision for her. Her restrained exhaustion contrasts with his dramatized guilt, and her practical care for the dying twins exposes his tendency to treat remorse as a substitute for action.

The ending is deliberately anti-cathartic. There is no reconciliation, supernatural explanation, or definitive moral resolution. The twins wither, Jaclyn assumes responsibility once more, and the narrator returns to the bloodstained pillow. That circular image brings the story back to its opening setting while showing that nothing has been cleansed or escaped. The final style is therefore haunting rather than conclusive: surreal events externalize guilt, but they do not redeem the person who feels it.

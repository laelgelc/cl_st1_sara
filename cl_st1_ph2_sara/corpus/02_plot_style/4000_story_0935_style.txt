The story uses an immersive, first-person plural mode of reportage, combining eyewitness observation with literary naturalism. The narrating “we” moves through the colliery as an initially unfamiliar visitor, allowing the industrial landscape to be introduced through shock, curiosity, and mounting dread. The perspective occasionally shifts into the more impersonal “one,” broadening private reactions into universal psychological claims, while brief first-person singular references lend the piece the authority of documentary testimony. Although the account records machinery, wages, working roles, depths, and occupational dangers with journalistic specificity, it is not neutral: description repeatedly becomes social criticism.

Its dominant stylistic device is extended personification. Breakers are “preying monsters” with nostrils, teeth, jaws, appetites, and voracious mouths; the mine swallows workers; nature possesses the strength of giants and kills with “method and precision.” This transforms industry into a predatory organism and mining into warfare. Coal extraction is figured as an “endless battle between man and nature,” but the breaker’s final identification as an emblem of greed complicates that opposition: the workers are threatened not only by the earth but by the industrial system that feeds upon both the land and human labor. Recurring infernal imagery—imps, spectres, ghouls, satanic smiles, dungeons, dead darkness—gives the mine the character of an underworld.

The diction is vivid, elevated, and emotionally charged. Words such as “inscrutable,” “implacable,” “ponderous,” “sombre,” “ghastly,” and “voracious” create an atmosphere of sublimity and terror, while concrete technical vocabulary—“culm,” “gangway,” “slate-pickers,” “shaft,” “chutes,” and “fan-wheel”—anchors the account in actual industrial practice. This mixture of ornate language and factual detail is central to the style. Precise figures, including the mine’s depth, daily wages, and the angle of the slate-pickers’ floor, intensify rather than soften the moral force by making exploitation measurable.

Imagery is strongly sensory. Darkness is rendered as tangible matter: curtains of ink, sable skies, black streets, and swirling chaos. Light appears in fragile, isolated forms—tin lamps, orange points, luminous smiles, glittering eyes, and coal shining like diamonds. Sound is equally important, conveyed through cumulative sequences such as “crash and thunder,” “shrieks and blares and bellows,” and “booming and banging and crashing.” These piled verbs and nouns imitate the machinery’s relentless din. Smell, touch, and bodily sensation also contribute: powder smoke, oil, wet earth, dripping walls, rebellious hands, alarmed lungs, and the loss of balance during the elevator’s descent. The mine is thus experienced physically rather than merely surveyed.

The syntax alternates between long, accumulative descriptive sentences and short, emphatic statements. Extended sentences catalogue buildings, dangers, noises, or impressions, often using parallel clauses and repeated conjunctions to produce momentum and overload. Against them stand abrupt declarations such as “It was a plunge, a fall,” “It is war,” and “She possesses eternity.” Sentence fragments and isolated paragraphs slow key moments, especially during the descent, making the experience feel suspended and psychologically disorienting. Repetition—“Sometimes,” “At another time,” “Perhaps,” and “It was”—creates rhythmic continuity and reinforces obsession, uncertainty, or menace.

Figurative comparisons are frequent, immediate, and often grotesque. Workers resemble apparitions, skulls, wolves, imps, and resurrected ghouls; mules resemble enormous rats; lamps struggle like tethered birds; trains crawl painfully; distant hills stand like monarchs. The similes make unfamiliar industrial scenes legible while also revealing the visitors’ fearful, sometimes sensationalizing gaze. Human faces are repeatedly reduced to shining teeth and eyes within darkness, producing striking chiaroscuro effects. Yet the miners’ ordinary greetings, jokes, smiles, and generosity puncture these supernatural impressions and restore their humanity.

Dialogue is sparse, colloquial, and phonetically rendered. Forms such as “yeh,” “goin’,” “wanta,” and “ain’t” reproduce working-class speech and sharply contrast with the narrator’s formal prose. The miners joke about being killed, treating mortal danger with laconic familiarity. This gallows humor conveys toughness and communal resilience without requiring explicit explanation. The slate-pickers’ profanity, swagger, games, and ambition are described with sardonic affection; the statement “They are very ambitious” is bitterly ironic because their imagined advancement leads toward injury, disease, or premature death.

The tone moves between awe, horror, pity, irony, and indignation. At times the workers are viewed through exoticizing language as strange or infernal beings, but the narrative steadily exposes the material conditions responsible for their appearance. The children’s independence is set against damaged lungs and poverty; the mules’ composure conceals years of imprisonment; the miners’ humor exists beside constant death. Sentimental reflection is most pronounced in the passages about the underground mules, where the lost surface becomes a fragrant paradise of seasons, grass, trees, and breezes.

Structurally, the story follows a descent-and-return pattern. It begins with a panoramic view of a landscape poisoned by industry, narrows into the breaker and its child laborers, plunges through the shaft into the labyrinthine mine, and finally rises into sunlight. The contrast between underground blackness and the radiant surface organizes the entire piece. Blue sky, green growth, fresh air, and golden light acquire almost religious significance after the darkness below. The return does not provide genuine release, however: the final image reconnects the beautiful surface to the hidden labor through the endless procession of coal cars and the insatiable breaker. This circular ending converts the tour into an indictment of a system that conceals suffering beneath ordinary production.

Overall, the style is dramatic, image-saturated, rhythmically forceful, and morally engaged. It blends industrial reportage, Gothic atmosphere, naturalist fatalism, and impressionistic perception. Its characteristic effect comes from making factual labor conditions feel mythic without losing their concrete social reality: machinery becomes monstrous, nature becomes an eternal adversary, light becomes salvation, and the mine becomes both a literal workplace and a symbolic underworld.

The story adopts a warm, gently comic mode of domestic realism, rooted in a close-knit rural Canadian community. Its world is built from winter fields, neighbouring farms, wood stoves, pantries, preserves, household work, illness, and local gossip. Concrete seasonal and domestic details create an atmosphere that is both picturesque and practical: blue shadows on snow, frost forming “silver palms” on windows, the ticking grandfather clock, and the crowded tea table all give the setting tactile presence. Descriptions frequently use colour—plum, blue, amber, ruby, gold, silver—to lend visual richness to otherwise ordinary surroundings.

The narration is third-person omniscient, moving freely between David’s and Josephine’s thoughts and occasionally revealing the assumptions of secondary characters such as Mrs. Tom Sentner. This shifting access to consciousness is essential to the humour. David interprets Josephine’s hospitality, wedding china, and attentiveness as romantic encouragement, while Josephine regards the same actions as courtesy, thrift, duty, or pity. The narrator repeatedly places these incompatible interpretations side by side, producing affectionate dramatic irony rather than sharp satire. Direct authorial remarks—such as observations about what “everybody in Meadowby admitted” or rhetorical questions like “what wonder if David lost his head”—give the narration the sociable, knowing quality of an accomplished storyteller addressing readers personally.

The tone is genial, amused, and sympathetic. Characters’ weaknesses are exposed without cruelty: David is shy, sentimental, impractical, and comically persistent, while Josephine is proud, commanding, self-deceiving, and excessively emphatic. Their foibles make them endearing rather than ridiculous. Much of the comedy arises from understatement, reversal, and discrepancy between feeling and expression. Josephine speaks most “crossly” when she is most deeply moved, frames love as pity and duty, and proposes marriage in the form of a practical housekeeping arrangement. David’s repeated use of poetry before proposing becomes a running joke, culminating in Josephine’s resolve that she, at least, will not begin by quoting verse.

Characterisation depends heavily on domestic action and habitual gesture. Josephine’s aggressive knitting signals conversational discomfort; her cookery expresses pride, generosity, and suppressed affection; her treatment of David’s clothing and food reveals care before she acknowledges any emotional attachment. Her competence is conveyed through active verbs such as “whisked,” “bustled,” “poked,” “packed,” and “pounded.” David’s meek speech, guilty blushes, gloomy departures, and almost reverential response to comfort establish his gentleness and longing. Small physical details—the wedding ring lost in the dimples of Josephine’s fingers, David’s smooth-shaven chin, the candlelight around her homespun dress—combine mild caricature with tenderness.

The dialogue uses informal, period-appropriate rural speech, including contractions and colloquialisms such as “s’pose,” “ain’t,” “’twas,” “real well,” “for a spell,” and “like as not.” These features establish social setting without making the speech difficult to follow. Josephine’s dialogue is brisk, declarative, practical, and often imperious; David’s is hesitant, repetitive, and full of qualifying phrases. Their contrasting rhythms dramatise the balance of power between them. Secondary dialogue broadens the social world and introduces communal attitudes toward marriage, property, gender, and respectability.

Sentence structure alternates between expansive descriptive passages and short comic or decisive statements. Longer sentences accumulate food, furnishings, landscape, and household detail, often through parallel clauses and catalogues. Short sentences punctuate emotional turns: “David got up”; “Josephine knew what was coming”; “I’ll have to do it.” Parenthetical comments and explanatory asides reinforce the narrator’s intimate, conversational voice. Repetition is used for comic emphasis, especially in David’s proposal—“I wish you would, Josephine”—and in Josephine’s forceful prohibitions.

The plot follows a clear romantic-comedy pattern. An initial proposal is firmly rejected; a crisis forces the protagonists into closer domestic contact; practical experience transforms Josephine’s view; and the courtship concludes through reversal, with Josephine compelled to propose because she has forbidden David ever to do so again. The pacing is leisurely at first, dwelling on tea, conversation, and character habits, then accelerates with Zillah’s illness before slowing again around Josephine’s gradual recognition. The illness functions less as tragedy than as a socially credible mechanism for bringing Josephine into David’s home and allowing affection to emerge through work and care.

Food and housekeeping operate as the story’s principal emotional language. The magnificent tea table represents Josephine’s artistry and David’s vision of married companionship. The disorder of David’s house awakens not merely her disapproval but her protective attachment, while cooking his favourite pudding signals that her resistance has already collapsed. Love is therefore rendered through service, order, nourishment, and shared routine rather than through overtly lyrical passion. Even Josephine’s final decision is rationalised as “duty” and “the pointing of Providence,” preserving her practical self-image while allowing the reader to recognise the love she cannot comfortably name.

Overall, the style blends regional realism, domestic comedy, sentimental romance, and benevolent social observation. Its emotional effect comes from the contrast between plainspoken surfaces and unspoken tenderness. The ending completes that pattern neatly: David briefly becomes “masterful,” Josephine’s authority yields without disappearing, and the reciprocal kiss confirms what the narrator and reader have understood before she does.

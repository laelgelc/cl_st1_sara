The story is an antiquarian supernatural tale presented with the sober confidence of a historical case record. Its narrative voice is educated, urbane and ostensibly rational, combining scholarly precision with conversational asides. The principal narrator recounts events learned from Dennistoun and occasionally steps forward through phrases such as “Dennistoun tells me” or “which I possess,” creating a layered testimony that lends documentary credibility to the impossible events. Dates, place names, inscriptions, provenance, footnotes and references to real-seeming institutions reinforce the impression that the story is an authenticated episode rather than an invented ghost story.

The diction is formal and distinctly late-Victorian or Edwardian, with words such as “sacristan,” “appellation,” “termagant,” “purlieu,” “vouchsafed” and “effigy.” French and Latin expressions appear without disrupting the narrative, reflecting both the protagonist’s antiquarian expertise and the story’s cosmopolitan scholarly milieu. Translations and explanatory notes ensure intelligibility while preserving an aura of arcane learning. The prose also contains a dry, understated humour, especially in the narrator’s parenthetical qualifications, Dennistoun’s collectorly calculations and the final joke about the price of Masses. This mild comedy makes the eventual horror more effective by establishing an atmosphere of civilized normality.

Sentences are often long, balanced and syntactically elaborate, accumulating qualifications and concrete observations before reaching their main point. Parentheses, dashes, semicolons and self-corrections reproduce the rhythms of an intelligent speaker recalling and refining an account. At moments of terror, however, the syntax becomes abrupt and fragmented: “A penwiper? No… A rat? No…” The switch from measured description to short questions, exclamations and isolated sensory details conveys panic directly. Repetition is used for emphasis, particularly in warnings, refusals and formulas such as “It is he,” while lists catalogue ecclesiastical objects, manuscript treasures and the demon’s physical features.

The setting is rendered through selective topographical and architectural detail. The decayed Pyrenean town, nearly empty cathedral, boarded mansion, shadowed chamber and old manuscript all embody historical decline and buried guilt. Rather than describing the landscape lavishly, the narrative selects resonant features—mountain streams, fading daylight, empty aisles, moving fire-shadows and the tolling Angelus—to create a solemn religious atmosphere. Sound is especially important: metallic laughter, muffled footsteps, distant voices, the bell and the landlady’s disturbing laugh anticipate the apparition before it becomes visible.

Characterization is economical and largely external. Dennistoun is defined by his scholarly enthusiasm, scepticism, conscientiousness and occasional brusqueness. His habit of talking to himself gives access to his rationalizing mind while exposing its growing unease. The sacristan is established through repeated gestures—glancing behind him, crouching, turning white, hesitating and then acting with sudden resolve. His daughter’s fear and insistence on the crucifix communicate danger without explanation. Minor figures remain functional witnesses whose presence corroborates the supernatural event.

The story’s suspense depends on delayed disclosure. Early anomalies—the sacristan’s hunted manner, the laughter in the tower, his anguish before the painting and his relief that Dennistoun will have companions—are presented as puzzles that admit mundane explanations. The manuscript then becomes both scholarly treasure and cursed object. The ominous drawing supplies a detailed visual preview of the monster, but the protagonist’s acquisitive excitement temporarily outweighs the warning. Protective measures are offered without explicit explanation, and the full threat emerges only after Dennistoun removes the crucifix. This structure converts curiosity into apprehension and apprehension into sudden physical horror.

The supernatural is depicted with unusually concrete bodily detail. The demon is not vague or spectral but hairy, skeletal, taloned and horribly strong, with features compared to a bird-catching spider. Its most disturbing quality is its intermediate intelligence—“beyond that of a beast, below that of a man”—which makes it both animalistic and morally malevolent. The description emphasizes tactile and anatomical particulars such as tendons, nails, teeth, pupils and the shape of the jaw. Yet the narrator also claims that language cannot adequately reproduce the image’s effect. This combination of meticulous description and professed inadequacy encourages the reader to imagine something worse than the words can fully contain.

Religious and antiquarian motifs are central to the style. The sacred architecture, crucifix, saintly intercession, biblical texts, Latin prayers and demonic punishment are treated with grave suggestiveness rather than doctrinal explanation. Scholarship uncovers the evil but cannot master it; traditional religious objects and rites offer the only effective protection or consolation. The protagonist remains cautious about interpretation, and the narrator closes with quotations, historical records and institutional provenance rather than a definitive theory. This restrained ambiguity preserves the supernatural mystery while implying that old beliefs may register realities neglected by modern rationalism.

The conclusion returns deliberately to documentary understatement. Canon Alberic’s inscription, the date of his death, Dennistoun’s guarded reflections, the visit to the tomb and the book’s final location all extend the episode beyond the immediate climax. The dry final statement that the drawing was photographed and burned gives the tale archival closure while leaving behind supposed material evidence. Overall, the style blends antiquarian erudition, restrained humour, precise observation, gradual atmospheric unease and a brief eruption of grotesque horror, making the impossible seem credible through the language and apparatus of sober scholarship.

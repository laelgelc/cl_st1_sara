Fanny Lovering is preparing to marry Edward Allen, a prosperous shopkeeper whom she believes to be loving and honorable. Her perceptive aunt, Mrs. Waring, is troubled by his worldly, superficial character and urges Fanny to consider whether she truly knows and esteems his moral qualities.

To test him, Mrs. Waring visits his store without being recognized. Allen exploits her apparent ignorance by raising the price of a silk dress and lying about its value. That evening, when Fanny mentions how badly her aunt was cheated, Allen’s guilty reaction exposes him as the dishonest seller. Though Fanny’s father initially dismisses the deception as ordinary business shrewdness, Mrs. Waring argues that a man willing to wrong a stranger for profit cannot be trusted to act unselfishly toward a wife.

Allen stays away and eventually offers to end the engagement. Fanny accepts, though deeply hurt. Soon afterward, he marries a wealthy but unsuitable woman, confirming that money rather than genuine love guided him. Fanny comes to recognize her narrow escape and learns that true love must rest on proven virtue and principle, not charm, appearance, or social advantage.

Two years later, Fanny is happily married to a genuinely honorable man who values her character. Allen, meanwhile, has enriched himself through sharp business practices and marriage, but he and his wife live without affection, tolerating each other only for appearances. Whenever Fanny sees them, she is grateful that she did not become his wife.

The story uses close third-person narration, tightly focalized through Chika. Although the narrator remains grammatically outside her, the prose frequently slips into her assumptions, fears, prejudices, and self-corrections, creating a strong free-indirect quality. Chika’s consciousness is therefore both the reader’s main point of access and an object of scrutiny. Her reflexive judgments about the woman’s cheap scarf, presumed ignorance, limited possessions, and supposed intellectual capacity reveal her class privilege, while later observations quietly undermine those judgments. Character development occurs through this gradual revision of perception rather than through explicit authorial commentary.

The dominant tense is the present, which gives the hiding-place scenes immediacy and physical pressure. This present is repeatedly interrupted by proleptic passages beginning with “Later,” which disclose the consequences of the riot before Chika herself understands them. These anticipations establish early that Nnedi will never be found, that bodies will fill the streets, and that public accounts will reduce the violence to sanitized formulas. The resulting structure alternates between an endangered present and a grief-stricken future. Repetition of “But now” returns the narrative to the store, emphasizing the gap between lived uncertainty and retrospective knowledge. This temporal pattern creates dramatic irony: the reader knows the scale of Chika’s loss while she continues to hope.

The prose is lucid, controlled, and predominantly realist. Sentences often begin simply and then accumulate sensory details, memories, qualifications, or parallel clauses. Lists and repetitions reproduce panic, as in Chika’s uncertainty about where Nnedi has gone, whether strangers are friends or enemies, and “who was who or who was killing whom.” At quieter moments, the syntax becomes measured and observational. This alternation between cumulative urgency and restrained stillness regulates the pacing without resorting to melodrama.

The narrative depends heavily on concrete sensory imagery. Dust billows in the sealed store; smoke resembles that produced by burning hair from goat carcasses; metallic water can be smelled; blood resembles tomato paste; burned flesh retains heat. Such details make political violence bodily and immediate. Smell is especially prominent, joining the external catastrophe to the confined interior through smoke, sweat, soap, dust, urine, water, and charred flesh. The imagery is often domestic or ordinary rather than conventionally poetic, which makes the horror more intimate and recognizable.

The store functions as a compressed dramatic space. Its darkness, dust, abandoned shelves, rusted tap, and narrow window create claustrophobia, but it also becomes a temporary refuge in which two women from opposed social and religious categories encounter one another as individuals. The spatial opposition between the store and the street is central: outside, people are classified, hunted, and killed; inside, the women share water, medical knowledge, fear, bodily vulnerability, and concern for missing daughters and sisters. The setting thus operates both realistically and symbolically, as a fragile interval outside the riot’s imposed divisions.

Dialogue is spare, practical, and socially differentiated. The Hausa woman speaks in nonstandard English marked by omitted articles, altered pronouns, reduplication, and Nigerian idioms such as “small-small,” “well-well,” and “Sorry, oh.” Her speech is rendered consistently but not treated as comic. Its apparent simplicity contrasts with her competence, emotional restraint, and practical authority. Chika’s standard English reflects education and privilege, yet she is often less capable in the immediate crisis. This reversal unsettles any easy equation between linguistic prestige and intelligence.

Characterization is indirect and object-based. Chika’s Burberry handbag, imported clothes, university education, gated family home, and doctor-on-call background establish her class position. The woman’s wrapper, scarf, hidden naira notes, onion stall, and dependence on buses establish a sharply different material life. These details initially reinforce social distance, but shared objects gradually acquire relational meaning. The wrapper becomes a place to sit, the tap enables washing and prayer, and the scarf becomes a bandage and finally a keepsake. The transformation of the scarf from something Chika dismisses as garishly cheap into something she asks to keep condenses her altered understanding.

The emotional register is restrained and unsentimental. Grief appears through physical gestures, pauses, private crying, distracted speech, and practical acts rather than through extended declarations. The woman’s tears are described as a “private” ritual, and her prayer is likewise treated as an intimate experience that Chika deliberately does not intrude upon. The title’s idea of privacy therefore applies to pain, faith, bodily exposure, and the temporary bond between the women. Even the most devastating information—especially Nnedi’s disappearance—is delivered with calm certainty, which intensifies rather than diminishes its effect.

Irony shapes both the characterization and the political treatment. Chika studies medicine but initially lacks confidence; in the store, however, her examination of the woman’s cracked nipples allows her to act with competence. She invents a story about her mother’s experience in order to bridge their social distance, making a lie serve an empathetic purpose. Religious identity is similarly complicated: Chika wears a Christian rosary without conviction, while the Muslim woman’s prayer later influences Chika’s attitude toward her family’s Masses. The story avoids presenting mutual understanding as complete or idealized, but it shows private contact disrupting inherited abstractions.

Public language is treated with suspicion. Newspaper and radio phrases such as “religious with undertones of ethnic tension” package mass death into concise, impersonal categories. Against this abstraction, the story insists on specific bodies, possessions, relationships, and names: Nnedi, Halima, a baby, cracked nipples, a bloodied leg, a burned corpse. The charred body whose ethnicity and religion cannot be identified becomes the clearest rebuke to sectarian classification. Political analysis is present, particularly through Chika’s memories of Nnedi, but it remains embedded in character and experience rather than becoming an authorial lecture.

The tone moves among fear, discomfort, irony, tenderness, and foreknowledge of grief. Moments of connection are deliberately modest: repeating a sister’s name, tending a wound, sharing a wrapper, offering a blessing, asking after family. The narrative does not claim that this encounter resolves class, ethnic, or religious conflict. Instead, it presents intimacy as temporary, incomplete, and morally significant precisely because it occurs amid violence.

Overall, the style combines close psychological realism, restrained political critique, sensory precision, and a carefully patterned temporal structure. Its most distinctive device is the oscillation between “now” and “later,” through which immediate experience is continually shadowed by future knowledge. The story’s power comes from opposing generalized accounts of communal violence with the irreducible specificity of a private encounter.

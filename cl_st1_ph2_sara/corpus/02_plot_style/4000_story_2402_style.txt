The story adopts the manner of an early nineteenth-century Gothic moral tale, presented as a sober clerical memoir. Its first-person narrator is educated, reflective and conscientious, speaking with the retrospective authority of someone recording an extraordinary event whose factual accuracy he repeatedly affirms. His voice combines religious conviction with qualified rationalism: he resists crude superstition, seeks medical or psychological explanations, and yet remains receptive to providential warnings and supernatural intervention. This unresolved tension between reason and belief governs the narrative. Apparent certainties—death, recovery, reform, natural causation—are repeatedly destabilised, leaving the final mystery deliberately open.

The prose is formal, expansive and heavily periodic. Long sentences accumulate subordinate clauses, qualifications, parallel constructions and parenthetical reflections before arriving at their main point. Expository passages often proceed through rhetorical questions, antitheses and balanced abstractions, as in the opposition between dream and prophecy, reason and superstition, repentance and damnation. Repetition intensifies emotional pressure, especially in moments of terror: words such as “dead,” “hell,” and “damned” recur in escalating sequences. Dashes, semicolons, exclamation marks and interrupted phrases reproduce agitation, while slower, syntactically elaborate passages convey the narrator’s meditative or moralising habits. The style retains period features such as “any thing,” “old fashioned,” partially anonymised names and dates, and occasional irregularities that contribute to the effect of an archival document.

The diction alternates sharply between elevated narration and vernacular speech. The narrator uses Latinate, theological and medical vocabulary—“reprobate,” “reclamation,” “preternatural,” “apoplexy,” “catalepsy”—as well as solemn moral categories such as sin, salvation, vice and repentance. Pat Connell and his family speak in an Irish-inflected dialect marked by phonetic spellings and colloquial forms such as “afeard,” “asy,” “heerd,” “crathurs” and “thry.” This contrast establishes class and education while also lending Connell’s testimony the immediacy of oral folklore. His account of hell is therefore simultaneously framed as a humble man’s dream, an eyewitness deposition and a visionary narrative. Dialogue is generally embedded within substantial narrative framing, and the narrator frequently interprets speakers’ expressions, tones and motives for the reader.

Description is strongly atmospheric and pictorial. Darkness, November cold, dampness, narrow streets, decaying stairs, offensive smells and dim candlelight establish a claustrophobic urban Gothic setting. Interiors are cramped, elevated and unstable: garrets, steep staircases, rotten planks and dangerous landings become physical equivalents of moral and spiritual insecurity. Light is used selectively to isolate horrifying details. The candle throws the supposedly dead man’s blue, swollen face into “startling relief,” while the infernal dream is illuminated by rolling globes of blood-coloured fire. Bodily imagery is graphic but controlled, concentrating on blackened lips, fixed teeth, white eyeballs, clenched hands, blood and the broken neck. The grotesque arrangement of cloths around Connell’s revived face produces a moment of visual horror that briefly encourages a demonic interpretation before receiving a medical explanation.

The narrative structure is carefully escalatory. It opens with an essay-like meditation on dreams, then narrows into a specific case history. The sick call leads to the first apparent death, the corpse’s shocking revival, Connell’s infernal vision, his moral reformation, and finally his relapse and fatal fall. Suspense depends on reversals and delayed disclosure: the dead man lives, the apparently transformative dream seems beneficial, and the reformed sinner nevertheless dies in circumstances that echo the vision. Foreshadowing is explicit but effective. The narrator announces that “stranger things are coming,” repeatedly stresses the dangerous landing, and records Connell’s attempt to strengthen the floor. The ending closes the pattern by returning his body to the very place through which he dreamed he had descended into hell.

Pacing varies between reflective delay and sudden dramatic shock. The narrator lingers over settings, ethical implications and states of mind, thereby preparing scenes that erupt in screams, revivals, shouted confessions or fatal crashes. Transitional phrases such as “I have little more to add” create an illusion of documentary restraint immediately before the final catastrophe. The embedded dream forms the story’s imaginative centre and changes the spatial scale from a poor garret to an immeasurable cavern. Its imagery is monumental and uncanny rather than conventionally folkloric: an endless table, numberless seated figures, a rock roof, trembling fires and a gigantic presiding being. Sound is as important as sight, with sighing crowds, weak voices, rushing flames, cavernous echoes and furnace-like reverberations amplifying the oppressive atmosphere.

The narrator frequently pauses to draw general lessons from individual events. The child’s endurance prompts a reflection on premature suffering; Connell’s recovery becomes evidence of the possible spiritual use of dreams; his relapse occasions an extended warning about the tenacity of addiction. These interventions give the tale a homiletic quality and position it as an exemplum about drunkenness, repentance and human frailty. Yet the moral framework is more complicated than a simple temperance lesson. Connell genuinely reforms, works, pays his debts and regains respectability, but one lapse appears to fulfil the infernal promise. The story therefore combines didacticism with fatalism, suggesting both personal responsibility and the frightening possibility that unseen powers exploit moments of weakness.

Its dominant emotional register is solemn, compassionate and horrified. Poverty is described with social sympathy, especially through the dignified child and the suffering family, while Connell is condemned as a drunkard but not denied pastoral concern. The narrator’s own reactions—pity, revulsion, doubt, awe and nightmare-like disorientation—guide the reader’s response. Formulae asserting truthfulness and eyewitness authority imitate the conventions of supernatural case histories, while literary allusions and the opening epigraph place the account within a cultivated tradition of dream literature. Overall, the style blends clerical testimony, Gothic sensationalism, moral sermon, Irish oral narration and rational inquiry, sustaining its effect by refusing to decide whether the fatal coincidence is providence, supernatural fulfilment or chance.

The story adopts a compact, realist maritime style, combining brisk action, strongly differentiated dialogue, and restrained moral sentiment. Its narrative voice is third-person omniscient but unobtrusive, remaining chiefly aligned with the mate’s fear and guilty calculations while occasionally reporting the skipper’s attitudes. The narrator rarely interprets events at length; character is instead disclosed through physical behaviour, terse exchanges, and practical decisions. This produces an outward-looking, dramatic mode in which moral change is inferred rather than explained.

The prose is plain, economical, and concrete, with a pronounced nautical vocabulary: “helm,” “handspike,” “painter,” “rigging,” “life-belt,” and “take in sail” establish the working environment without elaborate exposition. Descriptions of weather and water are atmospheric but controlled. The chill evening, deepening troughs, fading lights, wet deck, and indistinct riverbanks create a bleak setting that echoes the hostility and apprehension aboard the barge. Visual details are often selected for dramatic effect, particularly the skipper’s wound, the trailing boat, the apparently dead sailor, and the lights of Littleport emerging at dusk.

Sentence structure varies according to narrative pressure. Longer, coordinated sentences establish setting or describe sequences of physical action, while short clauses and abrupt statements intensify moments of danger: “He waited,” “Then the dawn came,” and “The mate relinquished it.” Semicolons and accumulative phrasing allow action to proceed rapidly without extensive reflection. Repetition is used for emphasis, especially in passages of silence, waiting, and mutual observation. Transitions such as “Suddenly,” “A moment later,” and “Half an hour passed” give the narrative a direct, chronological momentum.

Dialogue is central to the characterization. It is colloquial, adversarial, and marked by nonstandard grammar and phonetic contraction: “I don’t want none,” “Gimme,” “I’ve wrote,” and “I’ve seen it afore.” Maritime commands such as “Ay, ay” reinforce occupational authenticity, while insults including “dog,” “lump,” and “murdering hound” express the hierarchy and brutality of the relationship. The skipper speaks with domineering relish and physical confidence; the mate’s speech becomes increasingly sparse and submissive as guilt overtakes anger. Their verbal conflict therefore charts the transfer of power more clearly than explicit psychological commentary would.

The pacing is tightly controlled around a sequence of reversals. Initial hostility erupts rapidly into attempted murder; suspense then stretches through the skipper’s night in the trailing boat. His return appears to settle the story into a revenge-and-punishment pattern, but the rescue of the drowning sailor interrupts that trajectory. The practical urgency of resuscitation forces the enemies into cooperation, temporarily suspending their quarrel. A second period of suspense develops as the threatened arrival at Littleport approaches. The final reversal is understated: after repeatedly promising prosecution, the skipper supplies the mate with an innocent explanation for his injuries and walks away.

Tone shifts from grim and threatening to suspenseful, humane, and quietly comic. The early violence is presented starkly, but not sensationally; even the attempted killing is narrated through swift physical detail rather than melodrama. Dark humour arises from the skipper’s stubborn survival, his insistence on legal revenge, and the grotesque practicality with which he preserves evidence. The ending introduces warmth without becoming openly sentimental. The skipper does not announce forgiveness, and the mate does not plead for it. Mercy is expressed indirectly through a lie about a “row down Wapping,” preserving both men’s rough masculine reserve.

The central technique is dramatic irony. The skipper’s repeated declarations that the mate will receive “no mercy” encourage the expectation of arrest, while his conduct during the rescue gradually reveals a capacity for fellow feeling. The saved sailor functions as a moral catalyst: by working together to restore another man to life, the captain and mate symbolically counterbalance the earlier attempt to take life. Their shared labour revives not only the sailor but also their damaged human relationship. This parallel remains implicit, in keeping with the story’s preference for action over explanation.

Characterization is economical and physically grounded. The skipper is repeatedly associated with size, force, rough speech, and energetic competence; the mate is younger, fierce-eyed, impulsive, and later sickened by fear. Neither character is idealized. The mate accepts responsibility rather than seeking excuses, while the skipper’s vindictiveness is genuine before compassion modifies it. The resulting moral scheme is generous but unsentimental: wrongdoing carries psychological consequences, yet courage, remorse, and cooperative kindness make forgiveness possible.

The ending is concise, indirect, and emotionally resonant. Its power comes from withholding an explicit reconciliation. The mate’s “thickness in his throat,” delayed response, bewilderment, and deep breath convey relief and gratitude through bodily signs. The final movement below deck returns him to the rescued sailor and completes the story’s ethical redirection—from violence and self-preservation toward care. Overall, the style is robust, accessible, dialogue-driven, and neatly plotted, with realistic maritime detail supporting a compact tale of guilt, shared labour, and tacit forgiveness.

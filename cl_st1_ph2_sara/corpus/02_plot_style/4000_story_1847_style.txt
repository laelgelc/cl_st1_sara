The story is written as a public epistle: a formal letter addressed repeatedly to “Mr. Editor” and signed by the persona Geoffrey Crayon. In practice, it functions as a discursive periodical essay rather than a narrative short story. Its argument develops associatively but coherently, moving from personal names to place names, from local examples to state nomenclature, and finally to the need for a distinctive national name. The opening salutation, deferential close, and direct appeals to the editor create a conversational framework for what is otherwise a sustained cultural and political argument.

The narrative voice is educated, urbane, opinionated, and knowingly whimsical. It presents strong preferences as the reflections of a cultivated gentleman, tempering prescription with humor, self-deprecation, and courteous qualifications. Phrases such as “it appears to me,” “if I am rightly informed,” and “These are crude ideas” soften the speaker’s authority, while exclamations such as “Odds my life!” and comic warnings about “Tubbsvilles and Pottsylvanias” give the voice theatrical liveliness. The persona repeatedly invokes taste, poetry, elegance, and historical consciousness as standards of judgment.

The diction is elevated, Latinate, and distinctly nineteenth-century. Words such as “appellations,” “nomenclature,” “euphonious,” “circumlocution,” “coparceny,” and “acceptation” lend the prose intellectual formality. Archaic or period-specific usages—“reliques,” “wo unto us,” “forsooth,” and “matter of small concernment”—reinforce the antiquarian tone. This learned vocabulary is deliberately set against blunt, comic, or contemptuous expressions such as “hum-drum,” “riff-raff,” “kicked out,” “Cow Bays,” and “Mosquito Coves.” The contrast embodies the essay’s central opposition between poetic dignity and vulgar naming.

Sentences are generally long, balanced, and syntactically elaborate. They often accumulate clauses through semicolons, colons, parentheses, and coordinated lists, allowing the speaker to qualify a proposition, supply examples, and add a humorous afterthought within a single movement. Parallel constructions are common, as in the repeated divisions between names that inspire and names that degrade, or between what the nation wants “poetically” and “politically.” Shorter sentences and fragments are reserved for emphasis: “We want a NATIONAL NAME.” Capitalization, italics, quotation marks, and exclamation points give the prose an oratorical and typographical energy.

The dominant rhetorical method is persuasive amplification. The speaker begins with a seemingly minor topic, enlarges its implications, and argues that names shape imagination, literature, civic identity, and national unity. Repetition supplies momentum, especially through anaphoric patterns such as “Then we have,” “I want,” and “We want.” Rhetorical questions simulate debate—“As an American?” and “what can you make of it, either in poetry or prose?”—while hypothetical examples make abstract claims vivid. Lists of objectionable and desirable names create both comic rhythm and evidentiary weight.

Humor is central to the style. It arises through caricature, hyperbole, bathos, incongruous comparison, and mock solemnity. The possibility that political patronage might cover the country with “Tubbsvilles and Pottsylvanias” is treated as a national calamity, while the adjective “New” is attacked through comparisons with the Pont-Neuf and the Vicar of Wakefield’s elderly “colt.” The prose frequently adopts the gravity of legislative or philosophical discourse to discuss matters of taste, producing a gently satirical effect. Its targets include bureaucratic vanity, derivative classicism, colonial imitation, political flattery, and philistine literalism.

Allusion helps establish the speaker’s cultural authority. References to Mr. Shandy, Lemprière, Halleck, Bryant, Cooper, Homer, Troy, the Vicar of Wakefield, and classical antiquity assume a literate audience. These allusions are not merely ornamental: they support the argument that names acquire power through literature and historical association. The essay also makes strategic use of picturesque imagery, describing streams that wind through pastoral valleys or the “wildest recesses of Sleepy Hollow,” and imagining literature as the force that can weave new names into popular currency.

The structure is cumulative and widening. It starts with an analogy between personal and geographical names, diagnoses several causes of bad American place-naming, proposes the restoration of Indigenous names, illustrates the proposal with local examples, imagines an organized reform movement, and then expands toward names for New York and the nation itself. Transitions often mimic spontaneous conversation—“Speaking of New-York, reminds me”—so that the argument feels informal even when carefully staged. The close returns to epistolary modesty, presenting the proposals as provisional ideas intended to provoke public discussion.

The tone blends earnest nationalism, aesthetic idealism, nostalgia, satire, and reforming zeal. The speaker treats language as capable of dignifying or vulgarizing the physical world, and therefore regards naming as a serious cultural act. Indigenous names are valued for euphony, originality, local rootedness, and their status as historical remnants; this admiration is filtered through a romantic, settler-colonial perspective that aestheticizes Indigenous presence even while acknowledging its disappearance. The later argument for an explicitly Anglo-Saxon national identity introduces an exclusionary ethnonationalism characteristic of the speaker’s historical context.

Overall, the style is best characterized as a polished nineteenth-century belletristic essay in epistolary form: expansive, allusive, syntactically ornate, gently comic, and rhetorically persuasive. Its signature effect comes from treating nomenclature with mock-heroic grandeur while maintaining a genuine conviction that beautiful, historically rooted language can help create a mature national culture.

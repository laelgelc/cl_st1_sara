The story is written as a comic frontier Western that blends caricature, action, sentiment, and irony. Its setting is established through broad, atmospheric images of an arid landscape: dusty trails, grey prairie, cottonwoods, distant mountains, and an isolated roadhouse overlooking a ford. The descriptive vocabulary is colourful and often grotesque, making people and buildings seem animal-like or ominous: the hacienda “squats” like a vulture, while Shorty is variously compared to a whisky barrel, a bug, a cannon ball, a pigeon, and a stocky catapult. This reliance on exaggerated physical imagery gives the narrative the expansive, larger-than-life quality of a tall tale.

The narration is third-person and initially surveys Bailey and the roadhouse from an external, knowing perspective. It later moves much closer to Shorty, entering his thoughts during his sleepless infatuation, drunken concealment behind the bar, and hesitant decision to intervene. The narrator is amused but not wholly detached. Shorty’s appearance and behaviour are presented comically, yet the narrative increasingly reveals his loneliness, wounded dignity, longing for acceptance, and acute awareness of his body. This movement from caricature to sympathy is central to the story’s emotional design: the supposedly ridiculous figure becomes its most capable, honourable, and generous man.

The prose alternates between relatively formal narration and heavily marked frontier dialogue. The narrative voice uses elaborate or literary words such as “invidious,” “horrent,” “modicum,” “beatified,” and “contemporaneously,” often for comic contrast with crude action or drunken thought. Dialogue, by comparison, is compressed and phonetic, with dropped endings, nonstandard grammar, colloquial contractions, and cowboy idioms: “git,” “reckon,” “ain’t,” “lemme,” and “‘tain’t.” Figurative threats and jokes are especially vivid—men may be taken apart until they resemble “cut feed,” while alcohol makes “a new man” only to get both versions dismissed from work. The collision between elevated narration and vernacular speech creates much of the humour.

Speech is also the primary means of characterization. Bailey’s guarded questions and “unnecessary heat” make him suspicious before his treachery is exposed. Turney’s brisk, confident joking marks him as youthful and courageous. Shorty’s booming bass voice, elaborate threats, self-mocking remarks, and abrupt loss of fluency around the bride establish his contradictory nature: he is violent yet peace-loving by profession, socially aggressive yet painfully shy, physically formidable yet emotionally vulnerable. His dialogue often depends on ironic self-description, particularly when he calls himself “violently addicted to peace and quiet” or worries that returning late will suggest he has been drinking. These contradictions keep him comic without reducing him to a single joke.

Sentence structure changes with the action. Scenic and psychological passages use long, layered sentences with multiple clauses, participial phrases, and accumulating details. Comic violence is conveyed through rapid sequences of active verbs—Shorty rushes, bounces, lands, seizes, kicks, and belabours. During the nighttime ambush, sentences become shorter and more sharply segmented, while sensory details such as footsteps, matchlight, gunshots, murmurs, and a woman’s scream create suspense. The climax is staged visually, almost theatrically: figures enter from the stairs, prisoners face the wall, gun muzzles wander, and the bride’s appearance repeatedly disrupts Shorty’s concentration.

The tonal range is unusually broad. The opening carries menace and moral unease, the early encounter with Shorty is slapstick, his private reflections are melancholy, and the ambush becomes a tense gunfight. Sentiment enters through his idealization of the bride and his response to Turney’s uncomplicated camaraderie, but it is repeatedly checked by humour. Even the heroic resolution ends with a joke: Shorty values the captured outlaws because, apart from the bride, they are the only people present who did not laugh at him. This prevents the conclusion from becoming purely melodramatic and restores the tall-tale tone.

The plot follows a compact reversal structure. Shorty first appears to be a volatile comic grotesque whose violence is disproportionate to every slight. His drunkenness then seems likely to make him useless at the decisive moment. Instead, his sensitivity to insult becomes part of a deeper moral sensitivity, and his erratic drunken gunplay proves sufficient to defeat the outlaws. The story repeatedly builds suspense around his divided attention: alcohol weakens his balance, wounded pride discourages him from helping Turney, and bashfulness before the bride causes him to lower his eyes at dangerous moments. Heroism therefore emerges not from polished self-command but through an unstable combination of skill, rage, tenderness, and chance.

The main themes are dignity, prejudice, masculine recognition, and the discrepancy between appearance and worth. Shorty has built an aggressive persona to defend himself against ridicule, while whisky offers an illusory escape from shame. The bride’s kindness and Turney’s treatment of him as an equal provide the recognition he has lacked. His insistence on sharing the reward confirms that his deepest desire is not money but fellowship and respect. The story also plays with frontier masculinity: courage is measured through physical confrontation, gun skill, stoicism, loyalty, and generosity, but Shorty’s emotional vulnerability ultimately motivates his bravery rather than undermining it.

The piece reflects the conventions and prejudices of an older popular Western. Ethnic identity is rendered through stereotypes, slurs, caricatured dialect, and comic mistreatment, particularly in the portrayal of the Chinese cook. Women are idealized through beauty, purity, gentleness, and emotional influence rather than developed as fully independent characters. Violence is stylized and treated as both entertainment and a test of character. These period features sit alongside a more sympathetic challenge to ridicule based on physical difference, producing a story that is humane toward its central outsider while retaining other forms of conventionalized prejudice.

Overall, the style is exuberant, theatrical, and strongly oral in flavour. It combines panoramic Western description, ornate comic narration, dialect-driven dialogue, grotesque simile, rapid action, and restrained pathos. Its signature effect comes from contrast: formal diction against rough speech, monstrous appearance against tenderness, proclaimed peacefulness against explosive violence, drunken incompetence against expert gunplay, and public comedy against private pain.

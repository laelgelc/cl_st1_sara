The story uses a polished comic-realist style built around affectionate satire, escalating irony, and a decisive punch-line ending. Its third-person omniscient narrator speaks with urbane confidence, moving freely between external action, characters’ motives, and broad mock-sociological observations. Authorial intrusions such as the claim that “statistics show” rejected men fall in love with their beloved’s best friend establish a playful, knowingly unreliable voice. The narrator treats absurd propositions as sober truths, creating humor through deadpan understatement and mock seriousness.

The tone is light, sophisticated, and gently mocking rather than cruel. Herrick’s idealism is presented as sincere and even admirable, but his wealth, humorlessness, and sentimental conception of nature make him an inviting comic target. Jackson embodies frivolous wit, while Kelly supplies combative skepticism; together they act as a chorus that punctures Herrick’s romantic assumptions. The narrative does not wholly endorse their cynicism, however. Herrick’s compassion gives him emotional credibility, so the final reversal feels like a comic correction of naïveté rather than punishment for kindness.

Characterization is economical and typological. Principal figures are introduced through compact formulas—“gentle disposition,” “frivolous disposition,” “cynical and combative disposition”—and then made vivid through speech and behavior. Herrick is defined by earnest literalism, Jackson by teasing invention, and Kelly by blunt contradiction. Secondary characters are sketched through one memorable trait or action, as with the chef who climbs a tree for safety and then remembers his oven when told bears can climb. The animals are also anthropomorphized, especially Ikey, whose “natural-born comedian” persona makes him function almost like a human vaudeville performer.

Dialogue is central to the story’s energy. Exchanges are rapid, antiphonal, and shaped like comic debate, with one speaker advancing a lofty claim and another deflating it through practical objections, slang, or an absurd analogy. Repetition and parallelism sharpen these confrontations: Herrick insists on “nature’s children,” while his friends repeatedly call him a “nature faker.” The dialogue mixes cultivated references with colloquial American phrasing such as “Rot!”, “darned sight,” and “show off,” giving the prose a socially observant early-twentieth-century flavor. Capitalization—“USE,” “ENJOY,” and “YOU”—occasionally reproduces emphatic speech in a visibly theatrical way.

The diction alternates between elevated romantic language and comic demystification. Herrick’s preserve is described as a “holy of holies,” filled with “mysterious caves,” “tiny gurgling streams,” and “wild laurel,” language that reflects his reverence for an idealized wilderness. Against this, the urban scenes emphasize patchouli, cigarette smoke, steam heat, theatrical costumes, liquor, and gaudy decoration. The contrast initially establishes nature as pure and the city as artificial, but the ending collapses the opposition: the supposedly liberated bears voluntarily reproduce their stage routine in the moonlit country setting. This reversal exposes the inadequacy of Herrick’s rigid categories rather than simply declaring either nature or civilization superior.

The prose generally favors clear, medium-to-long declarative sentences, often expanded by balanced clauses, lists, and ironic qualifications. Descriptive passages accumulate concrete details for visual and comic effect, especially in the first appearance of the bears. Bruno’s resemblance to “a Hebrew gentleman in a brown bathing suit who had lost his waist-line” exemplifies the story’s period caricature, while the costumes, gestures, and routines make the scene strongly theatrical. The narrative frequently uses triads and catalogues—different kinds of gardens, the animals’ tricks, the guests’ objections—to create abundance and rhythmic escalation. Short sentences and abrupt exclamations then punctuate the longer narration at moments of dispute or revelation.

Structurally, the story is a comic demonstration organized around setup, experiment, apparent confirmation, and reversal. The opening establishes Herrick’s failed romance and compensatory worship of nature. The vaudeville episode supplies the provocation; the restaurant debate turns a passing indignation into a fixed scheme; the release of the bears provides suspense and seems to vindicate him. This false resolution is prolonged through Herrick’s triumphant report and moonlit conversation with Miss Waring. The final scene then delivers the inevitable contradiction: Bruno and Clara waltz, while Ikey drinks and performs drunkenness. The ending is carefully foreshadowed by the friends’ predictions that the bears enjoy applause and will perform for woodland creatures, as well as by Madame Zichy’s account of Ikey’s self-invented tricks and taste for beer.

Pacing varies according to comic purpose. Exposition is brisk and aphoristic, while set pieces—the stage act, the release, and the terrace revelation—are narrated in slower visual detail. Suspense before the release is repeatedly delayed by cowardly spectators, makeshift barriers, and objections, turning fear into farce. At the climax, the scream momentarily shifts the register toward melodrama and apparent horror. Phrases such as “the blood of all that heard it turned to water” deliberately inflate the danger before the revelation of the dancing bears converts terror into laughter.

The dominant devices are dramatic irony, bathos, incongruity, anthropomorphism, foreshadowing, and reversal. Elevated moral language is repeatedly attached to ridiculous circumstances: Herrick imagines himself rescuing “unwilling slaves from bondage,” only to discover that the bears cherish the supposedly degrading habits of captivity. The story also uses literalization as a comic engine. Kelly sarcastically tells Herrick to buy and release the bears, and Herrick, lacking humor, takes the suggestion as a serious plan. Likewise, the bears literalize the friends’ jokes by returning from the forest to perform.

The style is strongly shaped by vaudeville and stage comedy. Scenes are arranged around entrances, exits, props, routines, callbacks, and audience reactions. Even outside the theatre, characters behave as performers: Jackson provokes debate for entertainment, the guests form an audience at the preserve, and Herrick stages the bears’ liberation as a public spectacle. The final image operates as a visual curtain line, needing little commentary because the accumulated irony is embodied in Ikey’s pose.

At a thematic level, the story satirizes romantic primitivism, sentimental philanthropy, and the confidence with which privileged people impose their theories of happiness on others. Herrick assumes that authenticity must mean freedom from culture and training, but the bears have incorporated performance into their identities and pleasures. The conclusion suggests that habit, education, appetite, and temperament complicate any simple “return to nature.” Its governing style is therefore one of comic correction: generous ideals are tested against unruly facts, and abstract convictions are defeated by concrete behavior.

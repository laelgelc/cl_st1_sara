The story adopts a mock-medieval, picaresque style that combines historical pageantry with bawdy comedy and anticlerical satire. Its world is crowded with archbishops, cardinals, bishops, courtesans, servants, soldiers and petty clerics, but ecclesiastical dignity is continually reduced to appetite, vanity and competition. Sacred language and institutions are treated through comic inversion: prayers conceal erotic wishes, benefices become bargaining chips, church offices are compared with courses in a feast, and religious authority is exercised chiefly to secure money, food or sexual access. The dominant tone is irreverent, exuberant and knowingly scandalous rather than solemnly condemnatory.

The narrator is conspicuous, omniscient and conversational. Rather than effacing himself, he comments on events, supplies mischievous explanations, digresses into historical jokes and guides the reader with transitions such as “To return, however.” His voice resembles that of an amused storyteller addressing an audience already receptive to the joke. Characters are introduced with apparently respectable descriptions that are immediately undercut: a prelate may possess “good morals and great science,” only for his chastity to be attributed to incapacity. This recurrent movement from elevated claim to coarse qualification creates much of the humour. The narrator also uses mock logic, presenting corruption or absurdity as if it were a theological proof or perfectly reasonable canon law.

The diction mixes ceremonial and archaic vocabulary with earthy colloquialism. Titles, place names, ecclesiastical terms and historical references—“Monseigneur,” “benefices,” “Rota,” “breviary,” “Matins,” “Vespers,” and “in articulo mortis”—give the narrative an antiquarian surface. These coexist with vigorous idioms, insults and bodily expressions such as “bellyful,” “fat old bullock,” “brainless priest” and “coughing like a cow.” Mild archaisms, including “thou,” “thee,” “wilt” and “’tis,” reinforce the period effect, while French words and names preserve a continental flavour. The prose occasionally contains irregular grammar or awkward constructions suggestive of an older translation, but this roughness contributes to its energetic, old-fashioned texture.

Sentences are often long, cumulative and loosely coordinated. They gather descriptions, qualifications, jokes and parenthetical asides in a single flowing structure, producing the impression of oral narration delivered at speed. Dashes and parentheses frequently interrupt a statement to add a satirical correction. Lists are prominent, especially lists of ranks, foods, luxuries and clerical privileges; their abundance conveys both sensuous excess and institutional congestion. Short sentences and fragments are then used for comic emphasis, as in abrupt questions, verdicts or reversals. The rhythm therefore alternates between expansive narrative overflow and sharp punch lines.

Figurative language is profuse, physical and deliberately extravagant. Characters are compared to dogs catching flies, greyhounds in love, hooded rooks, wet dogs, dolphins, wagtails, cows and goats. Such animal similes strip social elites of grandeur and make desire appear instinctive and ridiculous. Religious and erotic imagery continually overlap: Philippe is “beatified” by Imperia’s beauty, her chamber becomes a paradise, and romantic devotion imitates worship. Food imagery performs a similar function. Love, theology, money and eating merge into one vocabulary of hunger, consumption and indulgence. Descriptions of Imperia use conventional idealising features—ivory skin, black eyes, thick hair and tiny feet—but exaggeration and comic context prevent them from becoming purely lyrical.

Characterisation is broad, theatrical and appetite-driven. Philippe is an innocent yet opportunistic young priest whose timidity competes with desire and native cunning. His progression from tongue-tied voyeur to clever intriguer gives the episode its picaresque movement. Imperia is presented as dazzling, imperious and capricious, with more practical power than the assembled churchmen; her intelligence is expressed through rapid changes of mood, strategic performance and verbal command. The Bishop of Coire is reduced to gluttony and bodily bulk, while the Cardinal of Ragusa embodies sophisticated corruption and political calculation. Characters are less psychologically subtle than vividly typified, though Imperia’s final tears briefly introduce emotional vulnerability beneath the farce.

Dialogue is frequent, fast and combative. Exchanges are built from flirtation, threats, bargains, insults and double meanings. Speakers continually try to dominate one another through verbal ingenuity, and status is repeatedly reversed: the courtesan commands bishops and cardinals, while the apparently powerless priest manipulates his superiors. Dialogue also accelerates the comic timing. Entrances occur at moments of anticipated consummation, each new visitor creating another obstacle, and each obstacle is removed through a fresh lie or stratagem. Repetition—especially demands to leave, promises of reward and appeals to religious authority—heightens the farcical structure.

The pacing begins with a relatively broad account of Philippe’s temptations and then narrows into a sustained scene of escalating interruption. Much of the opening functions as comic exposition, establishing the Council as a marketplace of sanctity, influence and pleasure. Once Philippe enters Imperia’s house, the action becomes almost stage-like: characters arrive, confront one another and are expelled in sequence. Suspense rests less on danger than on whether the lovers will ever be left alone. The final revelation that Philippe has hidden behind the mirror supplies the culminating reversal and rewards his newly acquired cunning.

Overall, the style is ornate, carnivalesque and satirical. It delights in excess—of language, food, desire, rank and hypocrisy—and treats the official hierarchies of church and state as material for comic degradation. Its signature effects come from the collision of sacred and profane registers, the narrator’s gleeful intrusions, hyperbolic sensual description, animal comparisons, elaborate lists, and rapid dialogue shaped around deception and reversal. Beneath the erotic farce lies a consistent vision of institutions governed not by doctrine but by appetite, money, fear and theatrical displays of authority.

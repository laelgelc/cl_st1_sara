The story is written as a retrospective first-person sea yarn, narrated by a seasoned merchant sailor whose practical outlook, prejudices, limited education, and dry self-awareness shape the entire account. The narrator speaks as though addressing companions informally, using conversational pivots such as “But as I said,” direct appeals to the reader, incidental recollections, and personal judgments. This creates the impression of an anecdote told aloud rather than a polished literary memoir. His reliability is partial: he reports events clearly but casually reveals his own ignorance, vanity, racial prejudice, and susceptibility to shipboard folklore. Occasional moments of self-deprecation—particularly his admission that his history is “a bit uncertain”—make him both comic observer and participant.

The diction combines nautical vocabulary, nineteenth-century American colloquialism, and deliberately nonstandard grammar. Terms such as “mate,” “bo’s’un,” “watch and watch,” “all hands,” “beam ends,” “aft,” and “lee bulwarks” establish an occupationally specific maritime setting without extensive explanation. The narrator favors contractions, dropped endings, vernacular forms, and idioms such as “come to time,” “half heeled over,” and “parted our braces.” Exaggerated similes—swallowing lies “like an albacore does a flying-fish,” being knocked over “with a fish-hook,” or looking like “an oar with clothes on”—give the prose a salty, performative humor. Profanity is partly censored with dashes, suggesting periodical publication conventions while preserving the roughness of sailors’ speech.

Sentence structure is generally loose, cumulative, and speech-like. Long sentences gather qualifications, comic asides, and afterthoughts through coordinating conjunctions, while short exclamations and fragments sharpen moments of surprise: “Holy Moses!” or “Kerosene!” Parenthetical remarks mimic a storyteller leaning aside to explain a character or invite the audience into the joke. The narrative repeatedly delays its main action with character sketches and contextual digressions, but these apparent detours establish the social hierarchy aboard ship and prepare the later comedy. Punctuation is expressive and abundant, particularly dashes, exclamation marks, semicolons, and quotation marks, reinforcing the sense of animated oral delivery.

Dialogue dominates the central portion and supplies much of the characterization. Captain Pratt’s blunt profanity, Warriner’s ceremonious and pompous standard English, the narrator’s practical vernacular, and Prince’s and Cornwallis’s heavily represented dialect create sharply differentiated voices. Comic friction arises from collisions between registers: elevated rhetoric meets galley mishap, Biblical solemnity conceals improvised sailing directions, and Warriner’s mock funeral oration transforms a ruined pudding into a tragic hero. Repetition, malapropism, literal misunderstanding, and false erudition are recurring devices. Cornwallis’s claim to descend from Lord Cornwallis, his explanation that the devil forced him to drink the brandy, and Prince’s invented scriptural punishments all depend upon linguistic performance rather than subtle psychological narration.

The plot follows the compact architecture of a comic tall tale. An ordinary proposal—to make a Thanksgiving plum pudding—introduces an unfamiliar ritual and a volatile ingredient. Suspense builds through the cook’s boastfulness, the premature delivery of the brandy, and the substituted feathers. The pudding’s spectacular ignition provides the physical climax, after which the story shifts into a mock investigation. Prince’s counterfeit reading produces the confession, and Cornwallis’s elaborate excuse supplies a second verbal climax. Warriner’s theatrical burial of the pudding then functions as an extended comic coda, ending the episode with ritualized absurdity rather than moral punishment. Escalation is central: each attempt to repair or explain the original mistake makes the situation more extravagant.

The prevailing tone is boisterous, satirical, and affectionate toward shipboard disorder. Authority figures are repeatedly deflated: the captain is habitually drunk, the “Honorable” passenger is pompous, the narrator is poorly educated, and the cook survives through brazen invention. Even so, the story admires practical cleverness and theatrical wit, especially Prince’s manipulation of Cornwallis’s superstition and Warriner’s ability to turn disappointment into communal entertainment. Thanksgiving is treated less as a sacred observance than as a pretext for cultural collision, culinary farce, and temporary fellowship among mismatched people.

The piece also bears strong marks of the racialized popular humor of its period. Black characters’ speech is rendered through exaggerated eye dialect, and the narration repeatedly uses slurs, stereotypes, dehumanizing comparisons, and assumptions about ignorance, dishonesty, superstition, and racial sameness. These features are not incidental to the style; they are built into both characterization and comic mechanism. At the same time, Prince is portrayed as capable, literate, physically imposing, and strategically intelligent, while the white characters are also exposed as drunken, pompous, gullible, or ignorant. The result is a socially hierarchical ensemble comedy in which competence is distributed more broadly than the narrator’s prejudiced language initially suggests, though the representation remains firmly constrained by racist conventions.

Overall, the style blends maritime realism with tall-tale exaggeration, broad farce, dialect comedy, and mock-heroic rhetoric. Its signature movement is from plainspoken anecdote into increasingly theatrical absurdity, culminating in the grandiose elegy for the “dark and sodden mass.” The prose is most distinctive when low subject matter is expressed in elevated language, or when apparent solemnity masks deception. The intended effect is less refined irony than the communal pleasure of a vividly told, rhythmically paced, and extravagantly embellished shipboard story.

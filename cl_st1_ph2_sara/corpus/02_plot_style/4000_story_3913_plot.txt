Three years after fashionable painter Jack Gisburn abruptly abandons his successful career and marries a wealthy widow, an acquaintance visits him on the Riviera to discover why. Gisburn appears to enjoy a luxurious, art-filled retirement, but his discomfort around his own paintings and his fixation on a small sketch by the neglected master Stroud reveal a deeper dissatisfaction.

Gisburn explains that after Stroud’s death, Stroud’s widow asked him to paint the dead artist’s portrait. Confronted by Stroud’s lifelike face and a simple but masterful sketch of a donkey, Gisburn suddenly recognized that his own celebrated technique consisted largely of flashy tricks concealing weak foundations. Unable to continue the portrait, he destroyed it, abandoned painting, and recommended the shallow but fashionable Victor Grindle in his place, inadvertently launching Grindle’s career.

Gisburn now keeps Stroud’s sketch as a reminder of this revelation. Though he cannot attain Stroud’s artistic integrity, he believes that recognizing his limitations and quitting is the closest he can come. He concludes bitterly that superficial art persists regardless, since Grindle now produces the same kind of work Gisburn once did.

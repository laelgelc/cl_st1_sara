The story uses a warm, conversational realist style shaped by small-town observation, comic exaggeration, and underlying pathos. Its first-person narrator is both participant and self-conscious author: she begins within the social world of Bascom’s department store, then openly turns Millie Whitcomb’s request for an ugly heroine into the story of Pearlie Schultz. This metanarrative frame allows the narrator to comment on literary conventions while simultaneously testing them. The voice is intimate and confiding, frequently addressing the reader directly and treating storytelling as a shared joke.

The tone moves fluidly between satire, affection, and sentiment without becoming wholly cynical. Conventional romantic fiction is mocked through references to “adorable” throats, changeable eyes, hidden beauty, and miraculous transformations produced by flattering gowns. Pearlie’s plainness is insisted upon with deliberately blunt comic force, especially in the emphatic declaration that she is “FAT,” yet the narration does not reduce her to a caricature. Humor protects and intensifies the story’s sadness: Pearlie’s unsuccessful exercises, her family’s tactful reassurances, and the claim that her smile is “awful” are funny, but they also expose her loneliness and social exclusion. The final request for a kiss is handled with restraint and dignity, and Sam’s removal of his hat gives the moment a near-ceremonial tenderness.

The diction combines polished narrative prose with colloquial American speech. Descriptive passages can be lyrical and visually rich, using details such as “soft mists of gray,” “dim old rooms,” “mahogany and old brass,” and “moonless, velvety” evenings. Dialogue, by contrast, is lively, idiomatic, and socially specific, filled with contractions, slang, commercial jargon, comic malapropisms, and period expressions such as “My land,” “quit kiddin’ me,” “you bet,” and “pippin.” Distinct speech patterns establish character quickly: Millie is practical and critical, Kate O’Malley persuasively familiar, Pearlie brisk and self-deprecating, and Sam boyish, homesick, and voluble.

The syntax is varied and performance-oriented. Long, cumulative sentences build social detail or comic momentum, while short sentences and fragments deliver punch lines: “Oh, Pearlie could cook!” Parenthetical asides, dashes, lists, direct commentary, and emphatic repetition reproduce the rhythms of an animated oral storyteller. Parallel constructions are especially prominent, as in the catalogue of New York’s supposedly superior sky, grass, girls, steaks, buildings, streets, and air. Repetition also creates thematic patterns: the “kindly shadows,” the habits of traveling men, and Pearlie’s cooking recur until they acquire emotional significance.

Characterization relies on concrete habits, clothing, work, food, and speech rather than extended psychological analysis. Bascom’s clerks are defined through their knowledge of customers’ lives; Pearlie through her body, spectacles, typewriter, domestic generosity, and culinary skill; Sam through his sales trip, private letter, complaints about hotel food, and eager talk of his fiancée. Material details anchor the narrative in an early twentieth-century world of department-store counters, public stenographers, traveling salesmen, vaudeville theaters, gas stoves, arc lights, and boarding-hotel meals. Food imagery is unusually sensuous and functions as both comedy and emotional language: Pearlie expresses care through cooking, while Sam’s hunger for home cooking represents homesickness and the desire for domestic security.

Structurally, the story is framed by Millie’s challenge and her later verdict. The central narrative proceeds largely chronologically, moving from Pearlie’s habitual loneliness to her meeting with Sam, the shared meal, and the requested kiss. Foreshadowing comes from the earlier description of courting couples stopping beneath the trees; when Pearlie later guides Sam toward the same “deepest, blackest shadow,” the setting becomes the means by which she briefly enters an experience from which she has long been excluded. The closing return to Millie deliberately undercuts a conventional romantic resolution. Pearlie does not become beautiful, win Sam, or marry, so the story preserves its critique of fiction’s demand that heroines be attractive and rewarded with husbands.

Overall, the style is humane, ironic, and socially observant. It exposes narrow standards of femininity while retaining some period-bound assumptions about beauty, weight, marriage, class, ethnicity, and gender. Its signature effect comes from balancing broad comedy with emotional precision: the narrator laughs at literary formulas and human pretensions, but treats unfulfilled longing, generosity, and fleeting kindness with seriousness.

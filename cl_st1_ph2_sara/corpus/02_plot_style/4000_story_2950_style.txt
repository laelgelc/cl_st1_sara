The story adopts a polished Edwardian comic style built around social embarrassment, cultivated absurdity, and a sharply delayed punch line. Its third-person narration stays close to Jerton’s anxious perspective while retaining an amused, detached authority. The narrator exposes the disproportion between his ordinary appearance and his conviction that he is under intense public scrutiny, establishing him as a passive, self-conscious observer who is easily recruited into another person’s disorder. Free indirect touches—his visions of “harassed railway officials and distraught Kestrel-Smiths,” for example—allow his misgivings to surface without interrupting the brisk narrative flow.

The diction is formal, urbane, and lightly ornate. Phrases such as “spuriously interested,” “polite inquiry,” “the Fair Unknown,” and “the elusive identity” elevate trivial social situations into mock-serious dilemmas. This decorous vocabulary contrasts with colloquial speech such as “Thanks awfully,” “I’m blest if I can,” and “those sort of people.” The contrast is central to the comedy: theft, lock-breaking, gambling, and financial exploitation are discussed in the language of good manners and practical inconvenience. Period-specific references to peerages, railway porters, illustrated weeklies, clubs, hotel pages, and titled society root the story in an affluent British social world. Some labels and social terminology are conspicuously dated and reflect the prejudices and conventions of that setting.

Sentences are generally long, balanced, and syntactically controlled, often accumulating qualifications until they arrive at a dry comic turn. The prose favours precise clauses, parenthetical observations, and lists of escalating detail. These constructions reproduce the forms of rational explanation while describing increasingly irrational conduct. Shorter sentences are reserved for dialogue, revelations, or abrupt reversals, helping the final stages move quickly. The narration rarely uses overt exclamation or tells the reader that something is funny; humour emerges from exact wording, incongruity, understatement, and the calm presentation of outrageous behaviour.

Dialogue dominates the middle of the story and performs most of the characterization and exposition. The unidentified woman speaks fluently, confidently, and at length, turning her supposed amnesia into an elaborate social game. Her reasoning is superficially logical but comically perverse: she identifies herself by consuming shellfish, flirting or refusing to flirt, tipping waiters, and gambling. Each “test” creates a new inconvenience while being treated as a legitimate act of deduction. Jerton’s replies are brief, cautious, and literal, making him an effective straight man. Repeated assurances that he has not noticed, judged, or intended anything emphasize his timidity and allow her to control both the conversation and the plot.

Characterization is economical and behavioural rather than psychological. Jerton is defined by nervousness, conventional politeness, and an inability to resist social pressure. The woman is defined by charm, improvisational intelligence, self-possession, and a blithe disregard for consequences. Secondary characters appear as functional social types—the discreet waiter, the efficient page boy, the informed clubman, and the indignant luggage owner—each introduced only long enough to sharpen the joke. Names such as Starping, Braddleshrub, Mousehilton, Befnal, and Ulwight are deliberately fanciful, parodying aristocratic nomenclature and making high society sound both exclusive and ridiculous.

The pacing follows a controlled comic escalation. A minor attempt to discuss roses leads to a claim of lost identity; that claim leads to appropriated luggage, forced locks, expensive food, gambling, a loan, and the abandonment of the stolen property. The narrative repeatedly offers the possibility of a rational solution, only for the woman to redirect it into a more elaborate scheme. Suspense is playful rather than dramatic, centring on whether her identity will be discovered and whether her story is genuine. The near-revelation—“She is the Lady—”—briefly prolongs the mystery before the actual disclosure overturns its premise: she is not an aristocrat but a married golf champion with a recurring tendency to lose her memory.

The ending uses a characteristic double reversal. The stranger’s explanation confirms that the woman’s amnesia is genuine, preventing her from being reduced to a simple confidence trickster, yet it also reveals that her conviction of possessing a title was mistaken. Immediately afterward, the appearance of the real Kestrel-Smith converts Jerton’s vague moral unease into direct social danger. His flight to the Turkish bath is an anticlimactic escape rather than a resolution, leaving him to absorb the practical consequences of his passivity. The final humour therefore depends on irony: the one person most terrified of public attention becomes entangled in a conspicuous scandal through his efforts to remain polite and unobtrusive.

Overall, the style is concise, dialogue-driven, socially observant, and epigrammatically ironic. It treats etiquette as both a comic constraint and an engine of disaster. The governing tone is dry rather than farcical, even when events become improbable, and the narrative maintains an elegant surface over a plot driven by deception, embarrassment, and irresponsibility. Its most distinctive technique is the conversion of social conventions—introductions, titles, tipping, luggage, club membership, and polite assistance—into mechanisms of escalating absurdity.

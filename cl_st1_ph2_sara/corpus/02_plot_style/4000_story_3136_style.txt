The story uses an urbane, late-nineteenth-century Gothic style in which decadence, supernatural dread, social satire, and moral allegory are closely intertwined. Its narrative voice is omniscient, worldly, and confidently judgmental. The narrator treats Tim O’Connor with genuine sympathy while maintaining an ironic distance from him, repeatedly balancing admiration with mockery: Tim is at once a ruined drunkard, a gifted stylist, a “perfect gentleman,” and a figure whose innate purity stubbornly survives his chosen degradation. This tonal doubleness governs the whole story. Tragic or spiritual implications are often capped by a sardonic phrase, while comic observations carry an undertone of damnation, regret, or wasted possibility.

The diction is elevated, literary, and period-specific, with Latinate abstractions such as “profanation,” “assiduities,” “idiosyncrasy,” and “nothingness” set beside colloquial insults, journalistic slang, and convivial banter. Classical, biblical, and literary references contribute to the cultivated atmosphere: Mæcenas, the Beloved Apostle, the Eternal Feminine, the Devil, “Faust,” and quotations from Shakespeare all place the characters within a self-conscious culture of art and letters. These references are not merely decorative. They heighten the disparity between the men’s intellectual refinement and their dissipated lives, while giving Tim’s downfall the stature of a spiritual tragedy. Irish identity is rendered through names, wit, sentiment, Catholic associations, and genial extravagance rather than through heavy dialect.

Sentences tend to be long, cumulative, and elaborately balanced, often gathering qualifications and ironic reversals through commas, semicolons, dashes, and parenthetical asides. The narrator frequently begins with a direct assertion and then complicates it through humorous commentary. Repetition and parallelism sharpen the central paradoxes: Tim debases himself yet remains pure, professes agnosticism yet fears ghosts, and dreads not evil but the visible embodiment of unrealized goodness. Aphoristic formulations—most notably “What I fear is Fear”—give the prose a theatrical and philosophical edge. Exclamation, apostrophe, and mock-grand language create the feeling of an animated oral performance, as though the narrator were telling the tale to a sophisticated audience.

Characterization is compressed and caricatural but psychologically suggestive. Tim is established through contradictions rather than extensive interior analysis: saintly appearance and profane conduct, noble prose and shabby finances, gentleness and addiction, skepticism and supernatural terror. His wife is described through sensuous, symbolic imagery—pale gold skin, yellow eyes, straw-colored hair, and a “fatal” smile—until she becomes less an individualized person than an emblem of predatory appetite and sterile femininity. The narrator’s repeated designation of her as a “little beast” creates a deliberately cruel, misogynistic strain characteristic of decadent-era femme-fatale writing. Dodson embodies witty cynicism and compromised affection, while Jim O’Malley is magnified into a comic-cultural institution, an Irish patron saint of drink, talk, and literary fellowship.

Dialogue is quick, epigrammatic, and performative. The men speak as cultivated bohemians who turn confession into repartee and use wit to keep moral seriousness at a distance. Dodson’s teasing, mock oaths, literary quotations, and paradoxes create camaraderie while also revealing evasiveness. Tim’s dialogue becomes more fragmented and solemn as the supernatural element approaches, with hesitations and repetitions suggesting that his polished social manner can no longer contain his fear. The contrast between the narrator’s ornate exposition and the characters’ agile exchanges keeps the story lively despite its philosophical subject.

The structure moves from broad biographical summary to increasingly focused dramatic scenes. The opening rapidly recounts Tim’s decline from youthful religious aspiration to journalism, aestheticism, marriage, drinking, and ennui. Subsequent sections isolate his fear of darkness, dramatize it through office banter and nocturnal confession, and finally realize it in the apparition. This narrowing movement gives the story the shape of an anecdote becoming a Gothic revelation. Foreshadowing is conspicuous but effective: Tim’s saintly appearance, persistent “purity,” fear of darkness, guilty allusions, and claim that fear has a shape all prepare for the apparition. The supernatural climax is brief and visually precise, after which the narrative returns to summary for Tim’s madness and death.

Imagery is organized around light, darkness, purity, and corruption. Electric lights provide mundane protection from supernatural terror, while dawn appears smoky, muggy, and “offensive” rather than redemptive. The apparition reverses conventional Gothic expectations: it is not monstrous or infernal but white, beautiful, fragrant, and radiant. Its goodness produces a glacial chill, making moral possibility more terrifying than evil. This inversion is the story’s central stylistic and thematic device. Tim fears the self he failed to become, and the Shape externalizes remorse as an exquisitely beautiful ghost.

The ending preserves the story’s characteristic mixture of pathos and black comedy. Tim dies “patiently and sweetly,” Catholic ritual returns in the form of a high mass, and his friends respond to death through macabre jokes about darkness and electric illumination. The final image refuses straightforward consolation, but neither is it wholly cynical. Beneath the polished cruelty, the narrative mourns squandered talent, suppressed goodness, and a social world whose wit allows its members to recognize destruction without preventing it. Overall, the style is ornate yet brisk, satirical yet elegiac, morally suggestive without becoming conventionally didactic, and Gothic chiefly through psychological and spiritual inversion rather than through overt horror.

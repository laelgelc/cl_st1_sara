The story adopts the manner of a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century medical sensation tale, blending Gothic suspense, detective reasoning, surgical adventure, and an ironic psychological twist. Its tone is initially grave and urgent, with the wounded man, the embedded weapon, and the delayed physician establishing mortal danger at once. The narration then becomes clinically fascinated and at times darkly humorous, before ending in macabre irony. Horror is generated less through graphic excess than through the calm, detailed discussion of an apparently impossible bodily predicament.

The story is told retrospectively in the first person by a participant-observer. The narrator is emotionally close to Arnold but is neither the chief intellectual force nor the central patient, allowing him to mediate between the reader and the extraordinary surgeon. His knowledge is limited enough to preserve suspense, while his presence lends the account the air of personal testimony. Frequent evaluative remarks—such as admiration for Arnold’s courage, wonder at Entrefort’s skill, and sympathy for the later collapse of Arnold’s character—guide the reader’s emotional response. The voice is formal, lucid, and period-inflected, using constructions such as “said I,” “as yet,” “were the blade withdrawn,” and “that gentleman,” along with exclamatory moral commentary.

The prose alternates between rapid dramatic action and long passages of technical exposition. Short sentences and abrupt dialogue dominate moments of crisis: “Stop!”, “I do,” “Well,” and “Proceed with your work.” These clipped exchanges emphasize Arnold’s stoicism and Entrefort’s authority. By contrast, the surgeon’s explanations are elaborate, syntactically layered, and rich in anatomical terminology, including “ascending aorta,” “thoracic cavity,” “traumatic tetanus,” “carotid,” and “secondary circulation.” This pseudo-clinical precision gives the fantastic premise an appearance of plausibility. The medical language also functions as suspense: each explanation introduces another possible catastrophe, so exposition becomes a steadily accumulating catalogue of dangers.

Characterization is economical and theatrical. Arnold is defined first as daring, imperious, and almost superhumanly composed, then transformed into a fearful hypochondriac. Entrefort is presented through quickness, confidence, observational brilliance, and unnerving emotional detachment. His deductions about the weapon and assailant resemble those of a detective, while his sardonic replies—especially “You do me too great honor” when called the devil—give him the air of an eccentric scientific mastermind. Dr. Rowell serves as a cautious, conventional foil, and Hippolyte provides nervous comedy while also enabling the mechanical solution. The woman is scarcely developed as an interior character; she operates primarily as a concealed motive, a source of danger, and the instrument of the final irony.

Dialogue carries much of the narrative. Speakers interrogate, diagnose, command, confess, and challenge one another, often in a stage-like sequence of short turns. Attribution is generally explicit and accompanied by gestures, facial expressions, or changes in pallor, reinforcing the melodramatic quality. Dashes, exclamation marks, interrupted phrases, and repeated words reproduce shock and hesitation. Entrefort’s extended speeches slow the immediate action but intensify anticipation by making both Arnold and the reader imagine every way the operation might fail.

Description is selective and functional. Visual emphasis falls on the jewelled hilt, white lips, pale faces, blood, exposed ribs, surgical tools, and the surgeon’s “keen” or “glittering” eyes. The ornate stiletto combines beauty with violence and becomes the story’s governing image: an antiquated, decorative object lodged within the modern body and subjected to modern surgical ingenuity. Physical reactions—gasping, trembling, grinding teeth, fainting, growing pale—externalize emotion in a conventionally melodramatic fashion. Repeated references to eyes and facial expression make hidden knowledge, fear, and recognition visibly legible.

Structurally, the tale proceeds through successive revelations. The apparent knife is identified as a stiletto; the fatal wound becomes potentially survivable; the assailant is deduced to be a woman; the blade is left inside the body; four years later Arnold is revealed to have survived but psychologically deteriorated; the woman reappears as Entrefort’s wife; and the autopsy finally reveals that the blade had already dissolved. Each reversal revises the meaning of what came before. The time jump, marked by a typographical break, shifts the story from immediate surgical suspense to delayed psychological consequence and prepares the coincidence-driven climax.

Foreshadowing is conspicuous but effective. Entrefort’s insistence that excitement or shock could loosen the blade establishes the precise fear that later kills Arnold. The mysterious muriatic-acid prescription anticipates the blade’s dissolution, while Entrefort’s refusal to explain it preserves the final revelation. His deduction that a woman delivered the blow prepares the reappearance of the former assailant. The conclusion depends on dramatic irony: Arnold believes himself physically vulnerable when he has long been healed, and the woman completes her revenge without touching him.

The central contrast is between bodily healing and psychological ruin. Scientific ingenuity saves Arnold’s life, but the knowledge of possible death destroys his courage and ultimately kills him. Fear becomes more lethal than the original wound, while imagination turns a cured man into a permanent invalid. The story also explores the ambiguous power of expertise: Entrefort is brilliant and lifesaving, yet secretive, manipulative, and almost diabolically amused by danger. Its final effect is that of a cautionary paradox—Arnold survives a pierced aorta and an unprecedented operation, only to die from a false belief about his own body.

Overall, the style is formal, sensational, dialogue-heavy, technically explanatory, and strongly plotted. It combines melodrama with scientific vocabulary, grotesque bodily suspense with dry wit, and an improbable medical conceit with the authoritative manner of a case history. The ending supplies the characteristic sting: a concise autopsy explanation converts the apparent supernatural or physiological catastrophe into a psychological death and closes the narrative with grim, symmetrical irony.

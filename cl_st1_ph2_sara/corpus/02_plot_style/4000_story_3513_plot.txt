An elderly narrator recalls a childhood incident in which a schoolmaster tried to identify a thief by making every boy reach into a soot-filled sack, assuming the guilty child would refuse. Since every boy’s hand emerged black, the thief remained undiscovered.

This memory prompts a humorous reflection on how fortunate people are that most of their sins, deceptions, and weaknesses are never exposed. The narrator imagines the chaos that would follow if everyone—from pupils and teachers to clergy, politicians, socialites, writers, and critics—were discovered and punished for every fault. Through anecdotes about guilty people accidentally betraying themselves, braggarts exposed as cowards, hypocrites fearing discovery, and frauds enjoying undeserved reputations, he portrays society as sustained by mutual concealment and partial blindness. He concludes that people should be grateful their loved ones see them through the forgiving “halo” of affection rather than judging them at their exact worth.

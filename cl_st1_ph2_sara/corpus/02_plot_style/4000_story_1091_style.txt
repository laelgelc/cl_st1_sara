The story uses a polished late-Victorian realist style, combining social comedy, professional detail, romantic irony, and a lightly moralising account of personal reform. Its language is formal and urbane but generally accessible, with period-specific diction such as “lucky dog,” “country mammas,” “gall and wormwood,” and “vouchsafe.” Medical terminology—“iridectomies,” “locomotor ataxia,” “tenotomy knife,” “compound” fracture—supplies technical authenticity while also defining the characters through their professional competence. Concrete markers of status and setting, including the brass plate, chestnut mare, consulting room, gig, groom, and country practice, firmly establish the provincial professional world.

The narration is third-person omniscient but closely aligned with Dr. Ripley’s consciousness. His assumptions, resentments, and rationalisations are frequently presented through free indirect discourse, allowing the narrative to expose his prejudice without constant explicit condemnation. Statements such as his belief that a woman’s medical education is “nothing short of shameless” reproduce the logic of his mind while the surrounding events steadily discredit it. The narrator’s voice remains amused, controlled, and mildly satirical, using dry observations and pointed contrasts rather than overt polemic. Ripley’s thinning hair being “worth quite a hundred a year to him,” for instance, neatly combines physical description, social insight, and comic irony.

Characterisation is economical and strongly behavioural. Ripley is introduced through a compact catalogue—“reserved, learned, unmarried”—and then developed through his habits, professional vanity, rigid etiquette, and responses to challenge. Dr. Verrinder Smith is initially filtered through his startled, hostile gaze, but her composure, humour, precision, and practical skill gradually overturn that perspective. Objects and gestures often carry characterising force: her pince-nez, scientific instruments, controlled smile, handling of the surgical knife, and tapping of the stethoscope all reinforce her intelligence and self-possession. Minor figures are sketched briskly through memorable social details, often with comic exaggeration.

The syntax alternates between balanced, explanatory sentences and shorter emphatic statements. Longer sentences accumulate qualifications and parallel clauses, giving the prose a measured, confident narrative rhythm. Antithesis is especially prominent: “Professionally, he was always at their service. Socially, he was a drop of quicksilver”; she is “courteous while he had been rude, self-possessed when he had been angry.” Such pairings clarify conflicts and sharpen irony. Lists are also used to convey professional learning, social history, or mounting evidence, while occasional fragments and exclamations intensify moments of shock.

Dialogue is crisp, adversarial, and socially coded. Early exchanges between the doctors resemble fencing: questions, corrections, evasions, and concise counterstrokes establish their intellectual equality and Ripley’s discomfort. Titles are significant, especially her correction of “Miss Smith” to “Dr. Smith,” because forms of address embody the central struggle for professional recognition. Later dialogue becomes gentler but remains restrained; emotional change is expressed through understatement rather than confession-heavy sentiment. Even Ripley’s proposal and rejection retain the vocabulary and habits of medical life, culminating in the wry question, “What, and unite the practices?”

The plot is linear and tightly causal. It begins with an expository portrait of Ripley’s secure position, introduces the rival who threatens both his livelihood and worldview, escalates through demonstrations of her superior skill, and turns on the accident that places him literally in her hands. This reversal is the story’s central structural irony: while racing to prevent her from gaining a patient, he becomes her patient himself. His physical helplessness enables intellectual and emotional change. The recovery sequence slows the pace and converts professional rivalry into companionship, after which the proposal appears to promise conventional romantic closure. The refusal defeats that expectation and gives the ending a bittersweet rather than comic resolution.

The tone moves from genial satire through rivalry and crisis into subdued pathos. Humour is often generated by incongruity, especially between Ripley’s self-image and his behaviour, or between solemn social beliefs and practical reality. The surgical episode is vivid without becoming gruesome, and the anaesthetic passage briefly shifts into lyrical, sensory prose through images of green water, descent, bells, and golden lights. This dreamlike interlude contrasts with the otherwise concrete, rational surface of the narrative.

Thematically, the story examines prejudice, professional identity, wounded pride, and the capacity to revise entrenched beliefs. It supports women’s intellectual and vocational autonomy by making Verrinder Smith not merely equal to Ripley but more current, courageous, and decisive in practice. At the same time, it avoids turning her into a simple ideological emblem: her wit, kindness, ambition, and refusal of marriage preserve her individuality. The ending resists the conventional absorption of the professionally successful woman into domestic life. Ripley achieves moral growth, but too late to receive the romantic reward he expects, and his lasting sadness supplies the tale’s final note of chastened irony.

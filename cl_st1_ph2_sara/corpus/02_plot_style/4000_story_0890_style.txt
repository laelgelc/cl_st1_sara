The story adopts a light, urbane comic style built around social satire, verbal escalation, and affectionate caricature. Its tone is playful rather than cruel: the characters’ vanity, insecurity, pretension, and romantic naïveté are exaggerated for amusement, yet Mr. Pottle remains sympathetic. The central joke targets packaged “culture” and the belief that education consists of accumulating impressive facts. Intellectual aspiration is repeatedly reduced to consumer goods, memorized encyclopedia entries, and fashionable self-improvement manuals.

The narration is third-person omniscient but stays chiefly aligned with Mr. Pottle’s perceptions and emotions. Mrs. Gallup is therefore described in idealized terms—as “a dream of loveliness, a poem, an idyl”—while Deeley appears through the jealous suitor’s comic hostility. The narrator moves freely between external description, concise psychological summary, and mock-authoritative commentary. Aphoristic intrusions such as the comparison between first love at twenty and at thirty-six give the narration the manner of a witty raconteur addressing an audience. Free indirect touches, including Pottle’s melodramatic thoughts of a “watery grave,” preserve closeness to the protagonist while gently mocking his excesses.

The diction mixes ornate, mock-romantic language with commercial names, colloquialisms, and technical jargon. Elevated phrases such as “spring-scented eventide,” “hesitant heart,” and “delicious tinglings radiating from his spinal cord” comically inflate an ordinary courtship call. This lyricism is undercut by prosaic details: union suits, barbers’ supplies, hay and feed, chicken gravel, trolley schedules, and boxes of “Choc-O-late Nutties.” Invented brand names and self-improvement titles lend the setting a period flavor while satirizing aggressively marketed refinement. Alliteration and rhythmic pairing—“rubicund and rotund,” “diffident steps and hesitant heart,” “cheery and chatty”—make the prose buoyant and conspicuously crafted.

Characterization relies on grotesque but memorable descriptive shorthand. Mr. Pottle is defined by painstaking grooming, bashfulness, and good-hearted inadequacy; Mrs. Gallup by sentimental susceptibility and secondhand notions of culture; Deeley by bulk, confidence, a “pleased mustache,” and intellectual fraudulence. Objects and gestures frequently reveal character more efficiently than explanation. Pottle’s elaborate dressing ritual exposes his romantic earnestness, Deeley’s crushing handshake and appropriation of the chocolates display his dominance, and Mrs. Gallup’s shiny unread library embodies aspirational culture. Names such as Pottle, Blossom Gallup, and Deeley have a deliberately comic, provincial sound, while Deeley’s repeated distortion of “Pottle” into “Bottle,” “Poodle,” “Puddle,” and “Pobble” becomes both a running gag and a social weapon.

The syntax is varied for comic timing. Long cumulative sentences catalogue clothes, books, facts, species, products, and chemical terms, often building toward absurdity through sheer abundance. These lists imitate advertisements, encyclopedias, and lectures, turning informational excess into rhythmical comedy. They are balanced by short declarative sentences—“He was in love,” “He couldn’t,” “Pottles are made of stern stuff”—which deliver punch lines or mock-heroic emphasis. Parenthetical qualifications and delayed conclusions also sharpen jokes, as when Mrs. Gallup says she has thought of remarrying “and I haven’t,” abruptly reversing Pottle’s hope.

Dialogue dominates the action and is highly theatrical. Each speaker has a distinct verbal mode: Pottle is hesitant, plainspoken, and emotionally sincere; Mrs. Gallup responds with breathless formulas such as “How fascinating!”; Deeley speaks in fluent lectures, lists, puns, and patronizing questions. Repetition supplies much of the humor, especially Deeley’s claim that every subject is “rather a specialty of mine.” The conversation operates as a contest for status and romantic possession. Interruptions, corrections, strategic questions, rising volume, and changing forms of address dramatize the transfer of power from Deeley to Pottle. Pottle’s eventual appropriation of Deeley’s pedantic style gives the climax the feel of a comic duel.

The plot has a clean, symmetrical farcical structure. An initial romantic approach is blocked when Mrs. Gallup declares her preference for a literary man. Deeley arrives as an apparently unbeatable rival, defeats Pottle through encyclopedic display, and sends him into exaggerated despair. A clue hidden in Deeley’s alphabetically ordered topics produces the reversal: Pottle studies both “A” and “B,” returns, and overwhelms a man whose knowledge stops after the first letter. The final revelation—that Deeley sells “Hog Culture”—retrospectively tightens the satire and restores Pottle’s moral advantage. The closing promise to go through the encyclopedia together converts the instrument of deception into a domestic-romantic joke.

Humor arises from incongruity, hyperbole, bathos, malapropism, misinformation, and recurring motifs. Deeley’s false explanation that “carnivorous and piscivorous” means eating “corn and pigs” exposes the emptiness behind his authority, while Mrs. Gallup’s “Polly Gammy” turns scholarly vocabulary into innocent misunderstanding. Mock-heroic language makes minor social embarrassment sound epic: Pottle’s jealousy becomes a “concrete lump,” his soul “curdles,” and his educational campaign resembles preparation for battle. The story also uses dramatic irony, since readers are encouraged to suspect Deeley’s fraudulent learning before Mrs. Gallup does.

The setting and idiom suggest an early twentieth-century American small-town milieu. References to union suits, trolley travel, fraternal orders, bachelor homes, “new-art” bungalows, and mail-order-style self-culture establish a world of rising consumerism and middle-class aspiration. The prose reflects period comic fiction in its omniscient narrator, fanciful names, broad physical caricature, phonetic brand styling, and genial treatment of courtship. Some descriptions and assumptions are historically dated, but the fundamental satire remains legible: performance is mistaken for knowledge, information for wisdom, and conversational dominance for personal worth.

At its emotional center, the story favors sincerity, persistence, and self-awareness over pretension. Pottle succeeds by imitating the fraud, but secures Mrs. Gallup only when he admits that he was bluffing. The ending therefore partly corrects the story’s culture-versus-business opposition: neither encyclopedic memorization nor fashionable literary talk is presented as genuine cultivation. The ideal relationship is instead founded on confession, mutual affection, and the comic possibility of learning together. Overall, the style is brisk, polished, list-heavy, dialogue-driven, mock-heroic, and warmly satirical, with escalating verbal comedy organized around a precise romantic reversal.

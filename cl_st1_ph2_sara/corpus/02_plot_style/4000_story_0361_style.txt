The story adopts the form of a retrospective first-person confession addressed to a specific, largely silent listener. This direct address creates the intimacy of an oral testimony while the references to “notes,” publication, and the narrator’s approaching death give it the shape of a written deathbed document. The opening establishes a conversational, faintly theatrical voice: courteous, self-conscious, digressive, and already touched by morbidity. The narrator repeatedly anticipates the reader’s reactions, asks for pardon, comments on his own method, and insists upon his sincerity. These gestures create apparent candour while also drawing attention to his efforts to manage how the story is received.

The narrative voice is the principal stylistic feature. Procopio presents himself as rational and honest, but his account gradually reveals an unreliable moral consciousness. His language changes almost imperceptibly from confession to self-defence. At first he calls the colonel’s death a crime and himself a murderer; later it becomes a “struggle,” then an act of self-defence, then something close to a medical coincidence. Qualification, euphemism, rhetorical questioning, and incremental revision reproduce the process by which guilt is softened into exculpation. Statements such as “who knows” and “it was even most probable” mimic careful reasoning while exposing motivated reasoning. The reader is therefore encouraged to interpret the psychological movement beneath the narrator’s declared beliefs.

The dominant tone is darkly comic and ironic. Physical cruelty, murder, remorse, inheritance, religion, and death are treated with an urbane lightness that never entirely removes their seriousness. Polite formulas sit beside grotesque images and blunt moral facts. The narrator’s request that his listener overlook any “disagreeable odor” in the human document, his offer of his future dead man’s shoes, and the final revision of the Beatitudes all turn solemn subjects into mordant jokes. The irony is often structural rather than explicitly announced: the abused attendant kills his master, is praised for his devotion, inherits the victim’s fortune, and gradually converts guilt into comfort. The ending condenses this moral irony into an aphoristic epigram: possession, rather than virtue or mourning, provides consolation.

The diction combines formal, nineteenth-century narrative language with colloquial speech. The narrative passages use elevated or Latinate terms such as “forbearance,” “stupefaction,” “lugubrious,” and “exculpatory”-type moral abstractions, while the dialogue is brisk, insulting, and concrete. The colonel’s speech is especially vivid, full of accusations, commands, threats, and comic abuse. Lists such as “an ignoramus, a camel, a bumpkin, an idiot, a loggerhead” create cumulative comic force and characterize his habitual violence economically. Religious and biblical vocabulary—confession, redemption, eternal rest, Cain, the grave, souls, Mass, and the Sermon on the Mount—provides a solemn moral register that is repeatedly compromised by worldly calculation.

The syntax is generally expansive and balanced, suited to recollection and moral analysis. Long sentences accumulate clauses, qualifications, explanations, and afterthoughts, often tracing the narrator’s shifting mental states in real time. Colons and semicolons support digression, while dashes and ellipses suggest hesitation, evasion, or conversational interruption. These longer reflective passages are contrasted with short declarative sentences at decisive moments: “I strangled him.” The abruptness strips the killing of ornament and gives it exceptional force. Repetition also plays an important role. Phrases such as “I no longer,” “I was,” and “I reread” intensify experience, while repeated denials and reformulations expose the narrator’s obsession.

Dialogue is used sparingly but efficiently. Exchanges are brief, sharply individualized, and often comic in rhythm. The colonel’s questions and insults dramatize dominance; Procopio’s submissive answers establish his initial strategy of endurance; and the villagers’ interruptions later provide a chorus of public judgment against the dead man. Dialogue also creates dramatic irony. Praise for Procopio’s loyalty sounds accusatory to him because the other characters unknowingly describe the murderer as a model servant. Their innocent interpretations of his trembling, melancholy, and piety repeatedly protect him, turning signs of guilt into evidence of devotion.

The pacing is carefully graduated. The opening summarizes the circumstances that brought Procopio into the colonel’s household, then compresses months of abuse through iterative description. As resentment grows, the narrative slows. The night of the killing is rendered scene by scene, with close attention to the cold porridge, the broken plate, the book, the medicine, the thrown bottle, and the strangulation. After the murder, clock sounds, silence, hallucinated figures, bodily sensations, and small practical decisions produce claustrophobic suspense. Once legal danger passes, time begins to accelerate again: remorse fades over weeks, charitable intentions diminish over months, and the remaining years are summarized in a few paragraphs. This temporal pattern formally enacts the erosion of conscience.

Sensory imagery is concentrated around guilt and fear. The “tick-tick” of the clock makes silence more oppressive; the still night and indifferent stars contrast with the narrator’s panic; the corpse’s open mouth and eyes become an emblem of eternal accusation. Hallucinated faces, voices, and watchers externalize conscience without requiring supernatural confirmation. Elsewhere, imagery tends to be metaphorical and satirical: remaining life is a candle-flame, hatred a seething cauldron, and secret moral pleasure a “tape-worm” that continually reforms. Such images are vivid but controlled, serving psychological analysis rather than decorative description.

Characterization is achieved through anecdote, speech, and contradiction rather than extensive physical portraiture. The colonel is introduced through reputation, feline eyes, malicious smiles, and escalating examples of abuse. Procopio is characterized less by what he claims than by the sequence of his rationalizations and compromises. Minor figures—the vicar, doctor, barber, notary, servants, and villagers—are lightly sketched as social types. Together they create a compact provincial world whose judgments both constrain and assist the narrator. Public opinion becomes an active moral force: first it pressures him to remain with the colonel, then praises his patience, and finally supplies arguments that make his crime easier to bear.

The story’s structure is tightly ironic and circular. It begins with death, confession, and the promise of a “human document,” then moves chronologically through employment, abuse, murder, concealment, inheritance, and retrospective self-absolution. The final deathbed request returns to the opening concern with memorialization, but the desired tomb is itself funded symbolically by the profitable story of the crime. The conclusion does not offer repentance or punishment. Instead, it reveals that wealth has outlasted remorse, giving the narrative the quality of an anti-moral tale.

Overall, the style is psychologically acute, economical, urbane, and satirical. It combines confessional realism with black comedy and moral ambiguity. Its most distinctive technique is the close alignment between narration and self-deception: the prose allows readers to watch a guilty man manufacture innocence through increasingly plausible language. The result is neither melodramatic crime fiction nor straightforward moral instruction, but a poised study of conscience, social hypocrisy, and the consoling power of material interest.

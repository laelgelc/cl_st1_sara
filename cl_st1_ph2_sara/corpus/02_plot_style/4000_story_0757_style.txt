The story adopts the style of a brisk comic farce combined with bureaucratic satire. Its central joke grows from the literal interpretation of a minor shipping rule, then escalates through procedural delay, departmental compartmentalisation and the guinea-pigs’ unchecked reproduction. The humour depends on disproportion: a ten-cent disagreement becomes an administrative crisis involving hundreds and eventually thousands of animals. Repetition with variation—letters are forwarded, rulings reconsidered, numbers revised and instructions overtaken by events—creates a cumulative, increasingly absurd effect.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, moving freely among Flannery, Morehouse and the company officials. The narrator’s tone is dry, genial and lightly ironic. Rather than directly condemning the characters, it exposes their follies through understated observations, such as the claim that corporate correspondence is handled “with the care that characterizes corporations when they are systematically conducted.” Brief generalisations about human behaviour, including the guilty reactions of Morehouse’s son and wife, interrupt the action to add mock-philosophical humour. The narrator remains emotionally detached enough to make the mounting chaos seem funnier.

Characterisation is economical and caricatural. Flannery is stubborn, dutiful, literal-minded and ultimately practical; Morehouse is irascible, sarcastic and self-important; the officials are complacent representatives of an impersonal system. None is psychologically complex, because each primarily embodies a comic attitude. Flannery’s repeated maxim, “pigs is pigs,” crystallises both his inflexibility and the rule-bound logic of the company. His eventual reversal—everything from pigs to tigers must be treated as a pet—provides an ironic form of education rather than genuine reform.

Dialogue carries much of the story. Morehouse speaks in emphatic, grammatically standard sentences filled with insults, rhetorical questions and exaggerated explanation. Flannery’s speech is rendered in a heavily phonetic Irish dialect through forms such as “thim,” “ut,” “loike,” “wan” and “twinty-foive.” His idiom is colourful, hyperbolic and metaphorical, especially when he describes the animals’ appetites or imagines interviewing pigs in numerous languages. This dialect sharply individualises him and supplies verbal comedy, though it also reflects the period’s reliance on ethnic caricature. The contrast between Flannery’s vivid speech and the officials’ clipped corporate language is one of the story’s main stylistic engines.

Official correspondence is presented as a parody of administrative prose. File numbers, departmental titles, abbreviations, passive constructions and stock phrases such as “We are in receipt of your letter” create an air of precision while ensuring that no one takes effective responsibility. Flannery’s letters, by contrast, are breathless, loosely punctuated and urgently practical. The juxtaposition of these registers—angry private speech, phonetic dialect, scholarly zoological terminology and sterile business jargon—reveals how language obscures a simple problem rather than solving it.

The plot is linear and episodic, organised around exchanges of letters, telegrams and instructions. Transitions such as “A week later,” “A few weeks later” and “Some months later” compress time, while the steadily increasing counts of guinea-pigs serve as comic milestones. The pacing accelerates near the end: bureaucratic delays give way to frantic packing, cages, hired boys, overflowing warehouses and guinea-pigs being moved with baskets and a coal scoop. Long stretches of summary are punctuated by short dramatic scenes, allowing the narrative to cover months without losing momentum.

Sentence structure is generally clear and direct. Action scenes rely on short clauses and energetic verbs—characters shout, slam, seize, run, shovel and telegraph—whereas administrative passages use longer, more formal sentences that imitate institutional procedure. Lists and numerical sequences heighten the sense of uncontrolled accumulation. Exclamation marks, dashes, repeated questions and comic misspellings reproduce agitation in dialogue and correspondence. The diction remains accessible, with occasional technical or learned terms introduced chiefly for comic contrast, as in the zoological classification of the guinea-pig.

The governing themes are literalism, institutional inertia, divided responsibility and the conflict between rules and common sense. Every participant behaves according to a narrow logic: Flannery obeys the higher-rate rule, Morehouse insists on semantic accuracy, and each department addresses only the part of the dispute assigned to it. The system is not malicious, but its slowness makes it destructive and ridiculous. Nature supplies the perfect counterforce to bureaucracy: while paperwork circulates at an official pace, the guinea-pigs multiply at a biological one.

The ending uses reversal, callback and understatement. After suffering because he insisted that “pigs is pigs,” Flannery resolves to classify all livestock as low-rate pets. His final speculation about elephants pushes the premise into one last image of impossible escalation. The closing line is concise, cheerful and absurd, restoring his optimism while leaving the reader with the sense that he has learned the wrong lesson in exactly the right comic way.

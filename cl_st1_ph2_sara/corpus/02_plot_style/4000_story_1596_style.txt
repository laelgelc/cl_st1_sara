The story uses a brisk, comic-realist style shaped by an urbane, intrusive narrator who views the characters with affectionate irony. The narration is confident and aphoristic—“they who dine on the lotus rarely consume it plain”—and often converts setting, emotion, or misfortune into a witty conceit. Elevated phrases such as “the fruit of the reply appeared upon the tree of events” sit beside slang, colloquial exaggeration, and mock-heroic descriptions. This contrast gives the prose a lightly satirical tone without making the central characters unsympathetic.

The diction combines formal bureaucratic English, American vernacular, tropical local color, and occasional Spanish or French expressions. Terms such as “mozo,” “zapato,” “baile,” “cuartel,” and “triste” establish the Central American setting, while phrases like “Great bunions,” “hill of beans,” “pull out the think stop,” and “old codger” give the dialogue a distinctly informal American flavor. Official language is frequently parodied: the consul’s letter adopts ceremonious formulas—“I have the honor to inform you” and “Your Obt. Servant”—to disguise a practical joke. The resulting collision between administrative dignity and personal irresponsibility is a major source of humor.

Sentence structure varies between compact comic statements and longer, elaborately balanced descriptive sentences. Short declarations such as “The tropics gobbled him up” deliver forceful summaries, while extended passages accumulate details and comparisons for humorous effect. Lists are especially important: the inventory of Coralio’s shoe-wearing inhabitants, the prospective buyers, and the absurd businesses supposedly needed by the town all build comedy through escalation. Parenthetical qualifications and narrator’s asides—such as Johnny’s “promptly and incorrectly” claiming not to own shoes—quietly undercut what characters say.

Dialogue drives much of the story. It is rapid, idiomatic, and structured around comic partnership: Johnny is volatile, self-deceiving, proud, and sentimental, while Keogh is patient, practical, and amused. Their exchanges use repetition as characterization. Johnny repeatedly insists that he has forgotten Rosine, and Keogh repeatedly agrees despite knowing otherwise; the ritual becomes both a running joke and evidence of Johnny’s emotional fixation. Misnaming Pink Dawson as “Dink Pawson” similarly turns jealousy into a recurring verbal gag. The dialogue also conveys exposition economically, allowing backstory and motive to emerge through banter rather than sustained explanation.

Descriptions of Coralio are vivid but caricatural. Heat, idleness, drink, dim streets, guitars, insects, barefoot citizens, and tropical vegetation create an atmosphere of languor and displacement. Similes are conspicuous and comic: Keogh grins “like a pleased shark,” tree frogs sound like minstrel-show bones, and glowing cigarettes make the inhabitants resemble “brunette ghosts” mixed with “insane fireflies.” Such imagery is visually sharp but filtered through the period’s exoticizing perspective, including dated ethnic labels and stereotypes. The setting functions less as a fully naturalistic society than as a comic pressure chamber in which American ambitions and assumptions become absurd.

The characterization is economical and type-based but enlivened by precise habits. Johnny’s hammock, brandy, neglected duties, family pride, and nightly denials define him quickly. Keogh’s perpetual good humor and willingness to perform Johnny’s work establish him as both comic foil and loyal confidant. Mr. Hemstetter is introduced as an impractical, change-seeking businessman, while Rosine is presented largely through Johnny’s romantic anxiety and the admiring male gaze. Characters are often summarized through epithets—“the monument of patience,” “the conqueror of Cupid,” “that inert official”—which reinforce the mock-epic tone.

Structurally, the story depends on reversal and delayed consequence. A casual joke is forgotten, then returns materially in the form of a prospective father-in-law and a four-thousand-dollar stock of unsellable shoes. The plot tightens as private embarrassment, commercial disaster, and romantic hope become the same problem. Foreshadowing is playful rather than ominous: the narrator announces that the “fruit” of the letter will appear, preparing the reader for an ironic harvest. The closing telegram introduces another improbable scheme, preserving suspense while promising an ingenious comic solution.

Overall, the style is plot-forward, theatrical, and highly controlled. It combines local-color description, sentimental romance, verbal comedy, and an escalating practical dilemma. Its signature effect comes from treating foolish behavior in ceremonious language, then puncturing that ceremony with slang, physical comedy, or a sudden reversal. Beneath the farce lies a gentler theme of self-deception: Johnny’s claim to have escaped love is as false as the official claim that Coralio urgently needs a shoe store.

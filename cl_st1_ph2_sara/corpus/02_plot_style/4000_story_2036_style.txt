The story adopts a polished late-Victorian comic style built around conversational storytelling, anecdotal escalation, and solemnly presented absurdity. Its framing narration is first-person and retrospective, delivered by a sociable, observant speaker who participates in the opening discussion but largely withdraws once the embedded tales begin. The narrator’s manner is urbane and understated: bizarre claims are reported with the composure of ordinary facts, allowing incongruity rather than overt commentary to generate the humour.

The tone is genial, dry, and satirical. The story treats human beings as creatures capable of adapting to almost anything—bad cigars, atrocious wine, marital abuse, or an inverted daily schedule—provided the experience is repeated often enough. Its comedy depends on extending a plausible proposition until it becomes grotesquely literal. A cheap cigar eventually makes good tobacco intolerable; a widower requires a second wife to nag him to sleep; and a rigorously punctual American remains psychologically governed by his former longitude after moving to London. Each absurdity is argued with mock-logical precision, giving the piece the air of a philosophical discussion whose reasoning is internally consistent but whose conclusions are ridiculous.

The diction is educated, idiomatic, and distinctly period-inflected. Formal phrases such as “admitted his premise,” “sole legatee and executor,” and “examined the problem in all its bearings” coexist with colloquialisms including “dead beat,” “pumped out,” “old fool,” and “bet my bottom dollar.” British social vocabulary—“half a sovereign,” “claret,” “whist,” “chapel,” “the City,” and “the Row”—anchors the narrative in its historical milieu. Specific place names, prices, occupations, meal times, and domestic rituals lend documentary solidity to otherwise improbable events. Hyperbolic sensory descriptions are used sparingly but memorably, as in the cigar smoke’s “odour suggestive of bilge water and cemeteries.”

The prose alternates between compact dialogue and longer narrative passages. Dialogue is crisp, responsive, and often constructed as comic counterpoint: one speaker makes a confident claim, another quietly exposes its implications, and the first responds without recognising—or while calmly absorbing—the joke. Parenthetical remarks provide understated qualifications and miniature punchlines, while balanced clauses and parallel constructions reinforce the central idea of habit. Lists of times and routines are especially important. Their methodical rhythm mimics the mechanical lives being described, and repeated clock references give the final anecdote both its structure and its comic inevitability.

Structurally, the story is a framed sequence of escalating anecdotes. It begins with a casual smoke-room debate, moves through several illustrative stories told by the narrator’s friend, and then passes to the unobtrusive stranger’s extended “true story.” The stranger’s tale functions as the main set piece and follows a clear progression: an exaggeratedly regular life is established; relocation produces inexplicable disorder; a geographical explanation is discovered; and an absurd practical solution restores equilibrium. The closing frame supplies a restrained punchline. Rather than arguing about the tale’s truth, the sceptical friend silently pays his wager, and the lack of explicit explanation makes the conclusion sharper.

Characterisation is economical and caricatural. People are defined chiefly by one governing trait: the friend is an enthusiastic theorist of habit, the widower is dependent on abuse, and the American businessman is punctuality embodied. Social labels and routines substitute for psychological depth. The businessman is described through comic pairings—“a drysalter from nine to four, and a Presbyterian in his leisure moments”—and through mock-heroic exaggeration, such as church clocks being set by him and astronomers using him to check the sun. This compression suits the oral-anecdotal form, in which characters exist primarily to demonstrate a comic principle.

The humour combines understatement, hyperbole, reversal, bathos, and deadpan pseudo-science. Respectability is repeatedly placed beside disreputable or ridiculous behaviour: burglars are incapacitated by claret, a devout businessman joins a Soho gambling den to preserve his whist hours, and family prayers are conducted in the middle of the night. The prose avoids signalling jokes too aggressively. Instead, it maintains a grave narrative surface, presenting each development as the reasonable consequence of prior facts. Even the erroneous calculation involving longitude is less important as science than as a parody of explanatory certainty.

Overall, the style is that of an urbane comic monologue expanded into a nested short story. It favours clear chronology, precise circumstantial detail, rhetorical repetition, vivid but controlled exaggeration, and punchlines delivered through understatement. Its governing aesthetic is to begin with a familiar observation about human nature, pursue it with impeccable seriousness beyond the limits of common sense, and end at the point where absurdity has acquired the appearance of logic.

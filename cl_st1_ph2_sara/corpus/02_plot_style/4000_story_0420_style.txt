The story adopts the style of a traditional children’s folktale retold in clear, didactic prose. Its tone is warm, earnest, and reassuring, even when depicting hunger, cruelty, or loneliness. Hardship is presented plainly rather than psychologically, and the narrative assumes a morally ordered world in which patience, industry, kindness, and honesty are ultimately rewarded. The opening formula, “There was once,” immediately establishes a fairy-tale register, while the ending completes the familiar rise-from-poverty-to-prosperity pattern.

The narration is third-person, omniscient, and strongly sympathetic to the protagonist. Dick is repeatedly described with affectionate or pitying expressions such as “little lad,” “poor Dick,” and “poor boy.” The narrator explains motives and feelings directly rather than implying them through complex characterization. Characters are drawn as simple moral types: Dick is industrious, humble, and generous; Mr. Fitzwarren is just and charitable; Alice is compassionate; and the cook is cross and abusive. Emotional states are readily legible, with people crying, jumping for joy, feeling sorry, or speaking their wishes aloud.

The prose is simple and accessible, designed for young or developing readers. Sentences are generally short or moderately long, with frequent coordination through “and,” “but,” and “so.” Cause and effect are made explicit, producing an easy narrative flow: Dick is hungry, therefore he seeks work; rats disturb him, therefore he buys a cat; the foreign court is plagued by rats, therefore the cat becomes immensely valuable. Paragraphs usually contain one action, exchange, or narrative development, which keeps the plot easy to follow.

The diction is concrete, familiar, and mildly old-fashioned. Words and phrases such as “lad,” “babe,” “puss,” “mouser,” “garret,” “venture,” “make haste,” “good fortune,” and “cross cook” evoke an older storybook idiom. Social titles and civic terms—“lord mayor,” “sheriff,” “merchant,” and “knight”—lend historical colour without requiring detailed historical explanation. Repetition and intensification are common, as in “great, great many things,” “purred and purred,” and “pussy, pussy, pussy,” giving the language an oral, performative quality.

Dialogue is frequent, direct, and functional. It reveals character quickly, advances the plot, and often states moral positions explicitly. The cook speaks in commands and insults, Mr. Fitzwarren in fair and paternal language, and Dick in humble, sincere phrases. Speech is theatrical and easily distinguishable, sometimes incorporating rhyme or legendary formula, most notably “Turn again, Whittington, / Thrice Lord Mayor of London.” Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, and repeated calls add liveliness and make the text suitable for reading aloud.

The story is arranged episodically under Roman-numbered headings—“The City,” “The Kitchen,” “The Venture,” “The Cat,” and “The Fortune.” Each section centres on a distinct stage in Dick’s advancement. The plot moves rapidly, often using transitional formulas such as “One day,” “By and by,” “Some time after that,” “At last,” and “After that.” These transitions compress time and minimize digression. Scenes of particular importance, especially the cat’s triumph and the revelation of Dick’s fortune, receive fuller dramatic treatment.

The narrative relies on repetition, contrast, and reversal. London is first imagined as a city whose streets are paved with gold, then discovered to be dirty and indifferent, and finally becomes the place where Dick genuinely earns wealth and status. His single penny becomes the means of acquiring a fortune; the mocked cat becomes the most valuable cargo; and the despised kitchen boy becomes “Mr. Whittington,” a merchant, mayor, and knight. These reversals give the tale its providential and fairy-tale logic.

Humour is broad and child-friendly. The king jumps so high that his turban falls off, the queen mispronounces “pussy” as “putty,” and the cat’s attack on the rats is described with delighted exaggeration. Violence and suffering remain stylized rather than graphic. Even the cook’s cruelty functions mainly as an obstacle to be endured, while the destruction of the rats is treated as comic spectacle.

The typography reflects an older educational edition. Multisyllabic words are often broken with hyphens—“break-fast,” “gen-tle-men,” “tor-ment-ed,” and “un-pleas-ant”—and some names include stress marks, as in “Whit´ting-ton” and “Fitz-war´ren.” These features suggest a text prepared to assist pronunciation or elementary reading. The use of double hyphens before dialogue also gives the page an antiquated printed appearance.

Overall, the style is that of a moralized, accessible legend for children: linear, episodic, emotionally explicit, lightly humorous, and governed by poetic justice. Its defining features are simple syntax, affectionate narration, formulaic transitions, vivid dialogue, archetypal characterization, and a strong emphasis on perseverance, generosity, rightful ownership, and social advancement through virtue and good fortune.

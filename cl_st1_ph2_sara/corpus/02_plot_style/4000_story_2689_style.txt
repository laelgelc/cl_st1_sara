The story adopts a restrained nineteenth-century realist style, combining social observation, psychological pathos, and bitter dramatic irony. Its prose is clear, formal, and largely unornamented, with emotion generated through accumulated circumstance rather than overt authorial judgment. The narrative treats adultery, illegitimacy, class difference, maternal guilt, and fear of public opinion with a sober, almost clinical directness. Respectable bourgeois life is set against rural poverty, and the contrast exposes the personal cost of preserving social appearances.

The narration is third-person and mostly follows Madame de Cadour’s emotional perspective. It moves easily from external action into retrospective summary and free indirect reflection, allowing the reader to experience her memories without sustained first-person interior monologue. Her past is presented through fragments and exclamatory recollections—“And then,” “And the next day!”—which reproduce the pressure of traumatic memory. The narrator remains outwardly detached, but the selection and rhythm of detail create strong sympathy for her. Monsieur d’Apreval is presented more externally and pragmatically, so his anxiety about exposure, blackmail, and money contrasts with her maternal longing.

Dialogue occupies a large part of the story and is used both for exposition and concealment. The opening marital exchange establishes character quickly: Monsieur de Cadour is complacent and mocking, his wife is secretive and driven, and d’Apreval retains the gestures of an old-fashioned lover. Later conversations become tense exercises in evasion. At the farm, ordinary questions about milk, poultry, prices, and a supposedly lost watch cover the visitors’ real purpose. The plainness of the rural dialogue intensifies the emotional disparity between what is said and what is felt. Repetition, interruptions, dashes, exclamations, and short questions convey agitation: “At last! at last!”, “Is it possible?”, and “My child!” recur as verbal signs of obsession.

The syntax alternates between long, cumulative sentences and abrupt emotional units. Extended sentences catalogue memories, farm buildings, physical sensations, or possible social consequences, creating a measured narrative flow and a sense of burdens accumulated over decades. Short sentences and sentence fragments puncture that composure at moments of distress. Repetition is especially important: words such as “never,” “always,” and “forty years” emphasize duration, deprivation, and irreversible loss. Some phrasing has the slightly formal or literal quality of an older translation, including constructions such as “to imagine to herself,” “other people’s opinion,” and frequent explanatory transitions.

Description is concrete and selective. The landscape is rendered through heat, dust, dazzling light, yellow grass, grasshopper cries, tree-lined lanes, farm implements, livestock, and humble domestic buildings. These details establish Normandy as a tangible social world rather than an idealized pastoral setting. The oppressive weather functions symbolically as well as physically: the “blaze” of sunshine, “burning heat,” interminable ascent, thirst, breathlessness, and threatened collapse externalize Madame de Cadour’s ordeal. Sound is used with similar precision—the blacksmith’s hammer, the dog’s barking, hoofs muffled by straw, and the absent child’s remembered cry all heighten expectation.

Pacing is built around delayed revelation and frustrated recognition. The opening hints at an unusual purpose; the lovers’ private conversation reveals the existence of a son; the long walk and extended recollection deepen anticipation; directions, roadside encounters, and negotiations at the farm postpone the meeting further. Even when the son appears, there is no mutual recognition or emotional reunion. He is first seen bent double, dragging a cow and cursing it, then disappears into a dark cow house. The anticlimax is deliberate: forty years of idealized maternal longing culminate in a brief view of an indifferent, exhausted peasant.

Characterization often depends on physical gesture and social behavior. Madame de Cadour squeezes d’Apreval’s hand, walks with a haunted gaze, sobs, falters, and nearly faints. D’Apreval bows gallantly at first but later becomes gruff, pale, evasive, and defensive. The peasant wife’s suspicious looks, weathered face, faded clothing, and reluctance to offer milk suggest hardship and habitual mistrust. Pierre Benedict receives almost no interior characterization; his coarse laboring presence is enough to shatter his mother’s imagined image of the infant she lost. This refusal to sentimentalize him is central to the story’s realism.

The tone develops from lightly comic marital irritation into secrecy, nostalgia, anxiety, and grief, before ending in cruel irony. Monsieur de Cadour’s jokes about madness and sunstroke are harmless only because he does not know the truth. His final question about whether they enjoyed their walk forces d’Apreval to answer, “A delightful walk,” turning polite social language into an instrument of repression. The ending is concise and unsparing: the public fiction remains intact, while the private emotional catastrophe goes unspoken.

Overall, the style is economical, observational, and emotionally controlled. It relies on realistic detail, sustained dramatic irony, symbolic landscape, contrast between speech and feeling, and an anticlimactic encounter to produce its effect. Its governing tension is between socially acceptable appearances and buried intimate truth; its characteristic emotional note is not melodramatic release but belated, irreversible sorrow.

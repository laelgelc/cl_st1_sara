The story uses a plain, economical realist style with the moral clarity of a folk tale or religious parable. Its language is deliberately accessible: vocabulary is concrete and domestic, centred on clothes, food, tools, animals, errands, wages and household labour. Specific details such as worn boots, hot porridge, samovars, kopeks and a red knitted jacket establish a materially sparse peasant world. The narration avoids ornament and treats deprivation without melodramatic embellishment, allowing Alyosha’s exploitation and suffering to emerge through the steady accumulation of ordinary facts.

The narrator writes in the third person, remaining close to Alyosha while retaining an outward, observant perspective. Character is disclosed less through psychological analysis than through repeated actions, habits and brief reactions: Alyosha runs, works, listens, smiles and obeys. His inner life is rendered in simple formulations suited to his limited education and unquestioning temperament. Free indirect touches—such as his anger with his aching feet or his fear that affection might interfere with work—give access to his consciousness without disrupting the story’s restrained narrative voice.

Sentence structure is generally straightforward and sequential, often linked by “and,” “but” and “so.” This creates an oral, cumulative rhythm and makes Alyosha’s life seem like an uninterrupted chain of duties. Lists of chores accelerate the prose while conveying the exhausting range of demands placed upon him. Repetition is central: his name is called again and again, commands recur, and similar patterns of obedience appear at home, in service, in love and finally in death. The repetition gives the narrative both social realism and fable-like inevitability.

Dialogue is brief, colloquial and sharply functional. Most characters speak to Alyosha through orders, rebukes or decisions made on his behalf, reinforcing the hierarchy of the household and his lack of agency. Alyosha’s own speech is abrupt, literal and accepting. His habitual smile acquires complex force: at first it signifies cheerfulness and endurance, but later it accompanies pain, renunciation and tears. The restrained dialogue leaves much emotion unstated, especially in the failed courtship with Ustinia.

Characterisation depends on contrast. Alyosha’s slight body and social insignificance are opposed to his extraordinary usefulness, toughness and generosity. The merchant’s family and Alyosha’s father are not elaborately villainised; instead, their selfishness appears through practical calculations about wages, boots, marriage and service. Ustinia is distinguished by reciprocal care. Her small acts—saving food, mending clothes and watching him eat—introduce Alyosha to the idea that a person might be valued for himself rather than for his labour. This conceptual contrast between usefulness and human affection forms the emotional centre of the story.

The pacing is strongly compressed. Childhood, adolescence and long periods of service are summarised rapidly, while the scenes involving Ustinia, the father’s prohibition and Alyosha’s death receive greater dramatic attention. Even these climactic episodes remain understated. The fatal fall arrives abruptly out of another routine task, making death seem like the final consequence of a life spent obeying commands. The ending slows into stillness and wonder, shifting from social observation toward spiritual contemplation.

Imagery is sparse but memorable, usually physical and humble rather than lyrical. Alyosha’s “ears like wings,” enormous nose, protruding toes, red jacket and injured body make him visually vulnerable. Food and clothing function as emotional symbols: porridge represents care, mended trousers intimacy, boots economic dependence, and the red jacket his rare experience of personal ownership and delight. Religious imagery is similarly modest. Alyosha cannot articulate formal prayer, yet he prays “with his hands” and later “with his heart,” presenting spirituality as instinctive conduct rather than doctrine.

The tone combines tenderness, irony and quiet severity. Irony arises from the discrepancy between Alyosha’s goodness and the way others value him chiefly as a servant, as well as from his belief that the prevention of his marriage was fortunate because it spared Ustinia future hardship. The narrator does not explicitly condemn the social order; the injustice is made more powerful by being reported calmly. Emotional restraint culminates in the final death scene, where Alyosha’s acceptance is neither sentimentalised nor explained at length.

Overall, the style is minimalist, compassionate and ethically charged. It uses repetition, concrete detail, compressed narration and unadorned dialogue to turn the life of an overlooked servant into a parable about obedience, exploitation, love and spiritual innocence. Its dominant effect is one of simplicity on the surface and profound moral unease beneath it.

The story is written as a one-act theatrical farce rather than as conventional prose fiction. It opens with an elaborate scenic description, proceeds through stage directions and character-labelled dialogue, and ends with the formal descent of the curtain. The implied narrator functions partly as dramatist and partly as sardonic master of ceremonies, occasionally intruding with arch comments such as “I regret to say.” This creates a deliberately artificial, self-aware atmosphere in which the characters appear less like psychologically rounded individuals than personified fragments of desire, inhibition, vanity, and social convention.

The tone combines sophisticated satire, erotic grotesquerie, decadent fantasy, and broad comedy. Polite upper-class courtship is treated as a brittle performance concealing appetite, calculation, embarrassment, and fear. The humour often depends on the collision between refined language and bodily impulse: literary conversation and “purely æsthetic” admiration coexist with lust, sweat, perfume, faintness, teeth, blood, and predatory flowers. The text maintains an amused, cynical distance from its characters, allowing moments of apparent tenderness or remorse while undermining them through heckling alter egos, mechanical dummies, or bathos. Its comedy can become deliberately cruel, and its representation of Cain relies on racist caricature, plantation dialect, and dehumanising imagery characteristic of offensive period stereotypes.

The diction is highly stylised and wide-ranging. Scenic passages employ lush, sensuous, almost fin-de-siècle vocabulary—“palpitating,” “serpentine,” “suppurating,” “languorous,” “chryselephantine”—while the spoken exchanges mimic fashionable social chatter, cultivated literary discourse, childish exclamations, and pseudo-scientific exposition. Learned references to Freud, Mendel, Darwin, Henry James, Humboldt, Aristophanes, and botanical taxonomy lend the comedy an intellectual veneer. These references are rarely neutral: they parody cultivated self-consciousness and the tendency to disguise instinct in the language of art, science, and moral responsibility. Invented botanical names such as the “twangum tree,” “kadapoo tree,” and “Alocusia Cantatrix” mix plausible nomenclature with nonsense, strengthening the mock-scholarly tone.

The imagery is dense, visual, tactile, olfactory, and frequently anatomical. The conservatory is imagined as a feverish erotic ecosystem filled with flowers that possess eyes, tongues, breasts, teeth, skins, tentacles, and throats. Similes turn vegetation into wounds, spiders, traps, predatory animals, and machines. Scents, heat, music, colour, and bodily movement work together to produce an atmosphere of intoxication. The central visual contrast is between the frozen black-and-white landscape outside and the humid, coloured interior: frost and death oppose tropical fertility, yet the apparent vitality within is also threatening, diseased, and carnivorous. This makes the setting both literal stage scenery and an externalisation of repressed sexual life.

Sentence style varies according to function. The opening description uses long, cumulative sentences packed with semicolons, appositions, catalogues, and escalating detail. Stage directions are often similarly expansive, choreographing facial expressions, gestures, dances, lighting effects, and shifts in bodily control with novelistic precision. Dialogue is generally short and rhythmically repetitive, especially when reproducing strained small talk: “a very good band,” “a perfect floor,” and “at the Necropole” recur as empty social formulas. Ellipses, pauses, exclamations, parenthetical instructions, and repeated phrases convey hesitation, trance, excitement, or embarrassment. Cain’s “nyum nyum,” Jasper’s mocking commentary, Belle’s eager prompting, and Henrika’s fearful protests operate as recurring verbal motifs.

Characterisation is allegorical and divided. Aston and Topsy each carry a dummy representing their socially acceptable public persona, while the surrounding “family” members embody impulses and internal voices. Cain represents crude appetite, Jasper detached intelligence and self-mockery, Belle sensual curiosity and manipulation, and Henrika innocence, fear, and vulnerability. The device literalises psychological conflict: decorous conversation is conducted through puppets while desire and commentary hover visibly around the speakers. The characters repeatedly speak across or over one another, producing a layered theatrical effect in which outward behaviour, inward motive, and critical interpretation are all presented at once.

The dialogue is structured around irony. Aston’s elevated compliments, professional generosity, and concern for “the cause of Art” are exposed by Jasper as rehearsed seduction tactics. Topsy’s apparent independence and intellectual seriousness coexist with susceptibility to flattery, while Belle actively stages her sister’s attractiveness. Euphemism and aesthetic language continually conceal sexual motives, only for the alter-ego figures to state or enact those motives openly. After the crisis, the principals return to their dummies and resume banal ballroom conversation, suggesting that social etiquette does not resolve experience but mechanically covers it over.

The plot develops through controlled escalation. It begins with awkward small talk, moves into intellectual flirtation, then into increasingly sensual music, perfume, touch, dizziness, and physical intimacy. The tour of fantastic flowers supplies a sequence of comic set pieces while also functioning as seduction: each botanical curiosity intensifies the atmosphere and narrows the distance between Aston and Topsy. The chloroform plant makes the metaphor of intoxicated desire literal. The kiss brings the suppressed conflict into open violence and terror, after which remorse, denial, and etiquette restore the social surface. The circular ending repeats the opening conversation almost verbatim, reinforcing the sense that the characters are trapped in ritualised behaviour.

The dominant techniques are grotesque personification, parody, repetition, bathos, symbolic doubling, mock-scientific invention, and metatheatricality. The flowers react like an audience, screaming when exposed to frost and chuckling after the humans leave; the final hiccough reduces the evening’s cultivated erotic drama to a gross bodily joke. The story’s overall style is therefore extravagant, theatrical, intellectually playful, and satirically merciless. Beneath its fantasy lies a sustained critique of cultivated manners as puppetry: civilisation supplies elegant phrases and graceful poses, but appetite, vanity, predation, shame, and fear remain visibly active behind them.

The story adopts the manner of an urban psychological thriller, combining social realism with crime melodrama and a final dream reversal. Its opening is deliberately material and documentary: cramped lodging, shared beds, improvised cooking, irregular meals, rent arrears and precarious employment are described in practical detail. This establishes poverty not merely as background but as the condition governing Blake’s thoughts, sensations and vulnerability. The New York setting is rendered as both a recognisable modern city and a threatening social system, populated by sensational newspapers, police courts, blackmailers, free-lunch bars and boarding-house transients. Period expressions such as “in very low water,” “third floor back,” “green-goods men” and “caught red-handed,” together with references to gaslight and dollar bills, give the prose a late-nineteenth- or early-twentieth-century character.

The narrator is third-person omniscient but stays closely aligned with Blake’s consciousness. External description repeatedly passes into free indirect thought, allowing the reader to experience his hunger, homesickness, professional frustration, hope and suspicion without a sharp boundary between narrator and character. The narrator nevertheless maintains a mildly ironic distance. Blake’s literary ambitions are treated with amused condescension: his “psychological masterpiece” is overcomplicated, his abstract sentence is probably meaningless, and his classification of readers into those who invent a meaning and those who pretend to understand is comic and self-protective. This irony humanises him while also foreshadowing the story’s own movement into an elaborate psychological construction.

The prose alternates between long, cumulative sentences and short, emphatic statements. Early sentences are expansive, parenthetical and explanatory, often accumulating qualifications through commas, semicolons and dashes. They reproduce both the crowded conditions of Blake’s life and the associative drift of his mind. As danger develops, the syntax contracts into sharper units—“It was the police!”—and paragraphs become shorter, accelerating the pace. Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions and repeated words convey mounting agitation, while abrupt temporal markers such as “suddenly,” “instantly,” “in another second” and “at the same moment” create the breathless mechanics of melodrama.

Description is selective and suspense-oriented. The visitor is introduced through oblique details: his sideways stance, crablike movement, lowered eyes, oily voice, shabby respectability and unexplained black bag. These features withhold a complete impression while making him increasingly sinister. The repeated emphasis on what Blake can and cannot see controls revelation, especially when the mirror exposes the visitor’s concealed, blood-smeared face. Ordinary objects—the bag, receipt, gloves, knife, door handle and mirror—become components in an apparently ingenious frame-up. Sensory detail is especially important: cooking smells torment Blake, new banknotes “crackle” like laughter, blood glistens in gaslight, the handle feels wet and slippery, and the bell rings with a “dreadful clamour.” Such details give physical immediacy to a plot that is ultimately revealed as dream experience.

The dialogue is economical, formal and strategically repetitive. Blake’s questions express disbelief and attempt to impose rational order, while the visitor’s controlled answers preserve secrecy and prolong uncertainty. Repetition of Blake’s full name and of the phrase “a well-wisher” creates an almost ritualistic unease. The visitor’s evasiveness contrasts with Blake’s increasingly hurried internal reasoning, so that dialogue supplies the surface calm beneath which panic develops. Speech tags often interpret tone—“oily,” “firmly,” “bluntly”—in keeping with the story’s direct, accessible method of characterisation.

Pacing follows a carefully graded arc. A substantial exposition establishes deprivation and solitude; the knock at the door creates the first decisive interruption; the offer of money converts anxiety into hope; suspicion then grows through delays, evasions and visual anomalies. The discovery of Barclay’s body shifts the story into outright horror, after which clues accumulate rapidly until Blake appears trapped by overwhelming circumstantial evidence. At the point of arrest, the policemen’s grip transforms seamlessly into the Dane’s hands waking him. The twist is prepared through Blake’s earlier slide from concentrated thought into reverie, his fatigue, hunger and proximity to sleep, but the narrative suppresses explicit signs that the crime sequence is a dream. The result is a conventional yet effective “false awakening” structure in which a mundane gesture is retrospectively reinterpreted.

Imagery frequently turns mental states into physical experiences. Hunger and sleep are personified as “slight acquaintances”; troubles melt “like bubbles in the sun”; imagination has gates flung open; terror resembles a nightmare in which knocking makes no sound; and the thought of police crashes through Blake’s brain “like thunder.” The story relies more on vivid similes and embodied sensations than on elaborate symbolism. Nonetheless, money functions as a central double image: first as miraculous rescue, then as contaminating evidence. Blood likewise moves from the murderer’s face to Blake’s own, visually transferring guilt. The mirror is the crucial instrument of reversal and entrapment, exposing both the watcher and Blake’s incriminating appearance.

Characterisation is economical and socially typed. Blake is a struggling English journalist and would-be author, proud, imaginative, hungry and somewhat naïve, but made cautious by police-court experience. The Frenchman, Dane, landlady, miser and mysterious visitor are drawn through national or occupational shorthand typical of popular period fiction. They serve the plot more than they develop independently, although the opening account of communal hardship gives Blake’s absent companions warmth and comic vitality. The visitor is intentionally less a rounded person than a figure of concentrated menace: controlled, unreadable and almost mechanically efficient.

Tonally, the narrative moves from rueful comedy through unease to sensational horror, then returns abruptly to domestic comedy. The final image of the companions carrying beer, food and good news releases the accumulated tension without extended explanation. It also reconnects the nightmare to Blake’s waking preoccupations: hunger generates the promised supper, financial desperation generates the money, police-court work generates the frame-up, and his attempt at psychological fiction generates an involuntary psychological tale more compelling than the one he was writing. The style’s chief effect therefore comes from joining detailed social observation to a tightly escalated nightmare plot, while using gentle authorial irony to keep both Blake’s literary pretensions and the story’s melodramatic machinery in view.

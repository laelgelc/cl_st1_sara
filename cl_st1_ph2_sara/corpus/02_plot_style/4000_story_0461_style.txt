The story is written in a polished early-twentieth-century collegiate realism, combining social comedy, Christmas sentiment, and a gentle moral arc. Its setting is rendered through recognisable period details—telegram forms, boarding houses, college clubs, proctors, smoking pipes, waistcoats, streetcars, and class-year designations such as “‘02”—which give the narrative a distinctly upper-class New England university atmosphere. The story initially contrasts Satherwaite’s affluent, mahogany-and-cut-glass surroundings with Doyle’s shabby but homelike room, using physical space to dramatise the difference between material comfort and genuine fellowship.

The narration is third-person and closely aligned with Satherwaite’s consciousness. Although the narrator sometimes supplies background information or makes general observations, most scenes are filtered through the protagonist’s moods, assumptions, and self-conscious calculations. This produces a mild form of free indirect discourse: phrases such as “Phil would keep; and so would Phil’s sister” or “By Jove, he would stay” carry Satherwaite’s own idiom into the narrative voice. The narrator treats him with affectionate irony, exposing his vanity, privilege, and theatrical self-pity without making him unsympathetic. His conviction that he is the most miserable person in New England, his careful selection of a waistcoat to impress Phil’s sister, and his notion that tidying a room might consume an evening all establish him as spoiled but fundamentally good-hearted.

The prose is expansive, fluent, and conventionally literary. Sentences are often long and cumulative, especially when describing movement, surroundings, or a sequence of thoughts. The opening sentence strings together a succession of physical actions before arriving at Satherwaite staring into the fire, immediately conveying restless irritation. Semicolons, em dashes, parenthetical qualifications, and balanced clauses are frequent. Short sentences and fragments—“Satherwaite was troubled,” “And now--!” or “Satherwaite was thinking”—punctuate these longer constructions, giving emphasis to changes of mood. The syntax is controlled rather than experimental, and its formal narrative poise contrasts pleasantly with the loose, colloquial speech of the students.

The diction mixes genteel literary vocabulary with period slang. Descriptive language includes words such as “untenanted,” “perfunctorily,” “assemblage,” “melancholia,” and “benignant,” while the dialogue is full of expressions like “awfully,” “jolly,” “old man,” “beggar,” “By Jove,” “Great Scott,” “baccy,” and “chop it off.” This contrast helps define the social milieu and gives the conversations a buoyant, youthful quality. Class difference is registered less through dialect than through possessions, habits, furnishings, and social confidence. Doyle and his friends speak standard English, but their worn clothes, cheap gifts, battered furniture, and financial limitations distinguish them from Satherwaite’s circle.

Dialogue is central to the story’s tonal and emotional development. At first it is stiff, hesitant, and crowded with polite evasions, reflecting the group’s suspicion of Satherwaite and his awareness that he is intruding. Repetitions, interruptions, false starts, and qualifying phrases reproduce embarrassment: Doyle’s “little—a sort of a Christmas-eve party” is characteristic. As mutual trust grows, the dialogue becomes quicker, louder, and more playful, with teasing, exclamations, mock ceremoniousness, and collective responses. The recurring phrase “Chop it off!” becomes a sign that Satherwaite has entered the group’s informal social language. Speech therefore does more than provide liveliness; it measures his transition from outsider to companion.

Characterisation is largely behavioural and relational. Satherwaite’s development appears through small acts: accepting Doak’s battered pipe without condescension, entertaining the gathering with a self-mocking account of his misfortune, valuing the cheap memorandum book, and discreetly placing his paper knife among the gifts. These gestures soften the class barrier without requiring an explicit declaration of reform. The other students are sketched rapidly through memorable physical or biographical details—the faded cricket blazer, eaglelike face, heavy whiskers, scholarships, debates, and homes far away. They remain a collective “gang” more than fully individualised figures, which suits their function as an embodiment of fellowship.

The tone moves from comic petulance through social discomfort to warmth and restrained sentiment. Early humour is directed at Satherwaite’s disproportionate misery and privileged expectations. In Doyle’s room, the comedy becomes more communal, arising from awkwardness, inexpensive joke gifts, student banter, and improvised ritual. The sentimental passages are carefully prepared by concrete sensory imagery: tobacco haze, firelight, tiny candles, cranberries, popcorn, snow, frosted streetcar windows, and mellow light. The story’s most lyrical moment pauses over the silent blue-white city outside the window, briefly suspending the joking atmosphere and allowing Christmas feeling to emerge without overt preaching.

The pacing is linear and scene-driven. A relatively brisk opening establishes the failed holiday plan, after which retrospective summary explains Satherwaite’s afternoon and his social background. The walk to Doyle’s room acts as a transition, while the visit itself unfolds in increasingly detailed real time. The initial awkwardness is deliberately prolonged through silences, glances, and the business of finding a chair and pipe. Once Satherwaite wins acceptance, the pace accelerates through anecdotes, gift-giving, singing, and midnight greetings. The ending extends the newfound intimacy into physical action: the snowball exchange transforms the winter street from a symbol of loneliness into a space of play.

Imagery is strongly organised around contrasts. Darkness is opposed to firelight and candlelight; cold streets to warm interiors; wealth to shabbiness; isolation to fellowship; expensive abundance to emotionally meaningful trifles. Satherwaite’s grand room is initially lifeless, whereas Doyle’s cramped study is animated by flames, smoke, conversation, and homemade decorations. Gifts carry particular symbolic force. Satherwaite’s costly paper knife seems “useless” beside the cheap comic presents, yet it becomes meaningful through spontaneous generosity; conversely, the inexpensive red memorandum book matters to him because it signifies acceptance. Material value is thus displaced by emotional and communal value.

The story’s structure follows a compact conversion pattern. Satherwaite begins absorbed in disappointment, romantic expectation, and social rank. His accidental visit exposes him to people he has casually regarded as belonging to another “coterie.” He initially stays partly from loneliness and partly from pride, wanting to disprove their assumption that he is a snob. Nevertheless, performance gradually becomes sincerity: his tact and charm open the group to him, while their modest celebration awakens in him a new appreciation of family, tradition, and uncomplicated companionship. The transformation is understated and credible because his essential personality does not change; his sociability and generosity simply find a less self-centred direction.

Overall, the style is genial, ironic, observant, and ultimately affirmative. It belongs to the tradition of periodical holiday fiction in which a temporary crisis produces moral recognition and social reconciliation. Its sentiment is tempered by humour, its moral is embodied in objects and interactions rather than stated directly, and its closing note is energetic rather than solemn. The final laughter and snowballs leave the reader with an image of spontaneous fellowship, completing the movement from a solitary man brooding before an elegant fireplace to a young man joyfully hailed by new friends in the snow.

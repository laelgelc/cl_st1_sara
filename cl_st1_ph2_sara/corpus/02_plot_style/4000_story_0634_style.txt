The story adopts an intimate epistolary style, presenting a sequence of dated letters from Mary Jane Dement to her confidante Irene. Its first-person narration creates the impression of spontaneous self-revelation: Mary addresses Irene directly, anticipates her reactions, asks rhetorical questions, and repeatedly invokes their shared knowledge. This private correspondence allows exposition, confession, romantic suspense, and social commentary to emerge naturally while giving the narrative a strongly performative voice.

The narrator’s voice is exuberant, self-mocking, and conversational. Mary oscillates between cultivated sophistication and the plainspoken idiom of her frontier childhood. Elevated phrases such as “the Valley of Humility” and “unworthy to unloose the latchet” coexist with colloquialisms including “little wretch,” “played it upon,” and “marry him alive.” Her habit of correcting, qualifying, or ridiculing her own statements makes her sound both impulsive and knowingly theatrical. Emotional declarations are frequently undercut by bodily embarrassment or comic practicality, most notably when a seemingly romantic collapse is revealed to be caused by a sleeping leg.

The prose is marked by long, flexible sentences filled with parentheses, dashes, interruptions, afterthoughts, and rhetorical asides. These reproduce the movement of excited speech and the narrator’s rapidly changing feelings. Short exclamations and fragments—“Fancy!”, “The strangest thing!”, “Irene, I BLUBBERED!”—supply emphatic comic beats. Capitalised words imitate vocal stress and heighten the sense that Mary is speaking confidentially rather than composing polished prose. Repetition, direct questions, and self-interruptions further dramatise her agitation.

Humour depends heavily on irony, incongruity, hyperbole, and bathos. Mary magnifies Dr. Barritz into a godlike, possibly supernatural figure, only for the story to reveal that his mystery was fabricated and that he is her impoverished childhood companion Dumps. Her romantic idealisation is continually punctured by unglamorous details involving blushing, hair, handkerchiefs, noses, pinafores, suspenders, and tingling limbs. Elaborate comparisons parody literary and social pretension: gossip is described through hunting laws, childhood poverty through theatrical allusion, and a man’s voice as an entertainment for which she “ought to have paid at the door.” Much of the irony arises from the gap between Mary’s confidence in her interpretations and the reader’s growing suspicion that she is being deceived.

Characterisation is achieved principally through voice rather than detached description. Mary’s vanity, insecurity, loyalty, romantic imagination, and comic intelligence reveal themselves in the way she tells the story. Her shame about being “Gunny” conflicts with her refusal to disown anyone who treated her kindly at Redhorse, giving emotional substance to the social comedy. Dr. Barritz is initially constructed through extravagant praise, rumour, and fantasy, while Raynor functions as gossip, trickster, and mediator. The eventual revelation reinterprets the supposedly exotic cosmopolitan as someone rooted in Mary’s own past.

The story contrasts fashionable hotel society and imported occult glamour with the rough mining world of Redhorse. Its diction draws on both spheres: references to art, Paris, Garrick, Blavatsky, and “vraisemblance” sit beside mining terms such as “pay ore,” “chloriding the dumps,” and “Syndicate Mill.” This mixture supports a satire of class aspiration and social reinvention. Wealth can transform Gunny into Miss Dement and Dumps into Dr. Barritz, but neither can entirely escape the comic persistence of personal history.

Structurally, the letters operate as stages in an escalating romantic mystery. Curiosity becomes fascination, fascination becomes love, and fear of supernatural influence gives way to fear of social exposure. The long beach scene slows the narrative into detailed physical comedy before delivering the recognition scene and final reversal. The closing postscript supplies a second, lighter anticlimax by disposing of the occult explanation altogether. Overall, the style is witty, ornate yet colloquial, sentimentally warm without becoming solemn, and driven by the comic collision of romance, social pretension, bodily reality, and concealed identity.

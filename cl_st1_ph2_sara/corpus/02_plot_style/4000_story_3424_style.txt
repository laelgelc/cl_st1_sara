The story is written in a late-Victorian Gothic mode, combining travel narrative, supernatural folklore and escalating horror. It begins in a relatively realistic, conversational register and gradually moves into an atmosphere of isolation, persecution and occult danger. The governing tonal movement is from amused scepticism to unease, terror and stunned recognition. Suspense depends on the collision between the English narrator’s rational self-confidence and a landscape whose inhabitants, traditions and natural phenomena all warn that rational explanations are inadequate.

The narration is first-person, retrospective and largely chronological. The narrator reports events with the authority of personal testimony, repeatedly grounding extraordinary claims in sensory experience and emphatic assurances such as “as I am a living man.” His perspective is tightly limited: the reader knows only what he sees, hears, remembers or imperfectly understands. Fainting, semiconsciousness and gaps in memory create strategic discontinuities at moments of greatest danger. This restricted viewpoint preserves ambiguity around the wolf, the mysterious stranger, the force that throws or drags him, and the nature of Dracula’s protection. The narrator initially presents himself as practical, curious and culturally superior, but his misreadings create dramatic irony: warnings that he dismisses as superstition are clearly ominous to the reader.

The prose favours formal, period-specific diction, with expressions such as “I was fain,” “aught,” “whilst,” “perforce,” “sepulchre” and “till.” Narratorial language is more polished and syntactically elaborate than the dialogue. Johann’s broken English, German phrases and repeated “Walpurgis nacht!” establish cultural distance while turning linguistic difficulty into a suspense device. His inability to articulate the danger makes the unsaid more threatening than a complete explanation would be. Repetition, exclamations, dashes and interrupted phrases reproduce panic, particularly in frightened speech: “It—it—indeed!” and “A wolf—and yet not a wolf!” Dialogue frequently alternates with physical gestures—crossing oneself, checking a watch, paling, trembling or looking around—so that bodily behaviour communicates what words cannot.

Sentence structure varies with dramatic pressure. Early passages use long, leisurely sentences filled with qualifications, observations and subordinate clauses, reflecting the narrator’s confidence and touristic curiosity. As danger intensifies, clauses accumulate through coordination, producing a breathless sequence of impressions. Short declarative sentences and isolated exclamations mark moments of revelation: “This was Walpurgis Night!” Longer periodic sentences often delay their central image until the end, while lists magnify both the violence of the weather and the narrator’s mounting fear. Recurrent constructions such as “then,” “presently,” “every now and then,” “nearer” and “darker and darker” give the narrative a steady forward pulse.

Description is strongly visual, auditory and tactile. The colour scheme is dominated by black, white and red: dark clouds and cypresses, snow and marble, blood and torchlight. White repeatedly shifts from purity to deathliness, becoming snow, shroud, pall, marble and the vague forms of sheeted dead. Red signifies both vitality and predation through rosy cheeks, red lips, blood, fire, sunrise and the wolf’s mouth. Sound is equally important: distant yelps, muffled roars, sighing wind, thunder, screams and wolves’ howls move from indistinct warnings to overwhelming supernatural noise. Cold is almost personified as an invasive force that grips the heart, numbs the body and threatens “snow sleep,” while the wolf’s warmth is both protective and terrifying.

The natural setting functions as an active Gothic agent rather than a neutral background. Sunshine vanishes, clouds race overhead, snow obscures the road, lightning exposes forbidden sights and the storm appears to reverse direction deliberately. Pathetic fallacy is explicit when nature’s stillness seems to stop the narrator’s heart. The landscape progressively removes ordinary bearings: town gives way to plateau, side road, valley, wilderness, ruined village, grove and graveyard. This spatial narrowing resembles an initiation into a hidden supernatural realm. Yew and cypress trees, the isolated tomb, the iron stake, blood-filled corpses and crossroads burial draw on traditional emblems of death, vampirism and spiritual pollution.

Suspense is built through foreshadowing and repeated motifs. Johann’s watch establishes urgency; the horses’ instinctive terror validates dangers invisible to human reason; the wolf cry recurs at decreasing distance; and the phrase “Walpurgis Night” gains force each time it is repeated. Information is disclosed incrementally: first there is an avoided road, then a suicide’s grave, then an abandoned village, then revenants found red-mouthed in opened graves. The central tomb scene gathers these earlier hints into a concentrated supernatural revelation. After the climax, the rescue offers only partial relief, because the soldiers’ evasions and Dracula’s telegram reinterpret the entire episode as part of a larger occult design.

The pacing follows a clear Gothic crescendo. The opening carriage exchange is deliberate and dialogue-heavy. The solitary walk initially slows into scenic contemplation, allowing the narrator’s complacency to reassert itself. The storm then accelerates the action through worsening weather, loss of direction and diminishing visibility. At the tomb, rapid flashes of lightning create almost cinematic glimpses: inscription, corpse, flame, blood and phantoms. The semiconscious aftermath slows time again, dwelling on bodily sensations before revealing the wolf. The soldiers’ arrival shifts briefly toward action and explanation, but the final telegram supplies a quieter second climax based on implication rather than spectacle.

Characterisation is economical and contrastive. The unnamed Englishman is adventurous, obstinate and proud of his national identity; his declaration that Walpurgis Night “doesn’t concern Englishmen” encapsulates his comic arrogance and fatal misjudgment. Johann is defined through superstition, urgency and physical fear, yet proves more perceptive than the supposedly rational traveller. The soldiers occupy an intermediate position: they have witnessed the uncanny but agree to suppress it under the socially acceptable label of “a dog.” Dracula never appears directly, yet the final message gives him commanding presence. His polished solicitude is unsettling because it suggests foreknowledge, wealth, control and a possessive interest in the narrator’s survival.

The story’s dominant themes are rational pride, forbidden curiosity, the persistence of folklore and the instability of boundaries between life and death. It repeatedly contrasts places “where the living lived and the dead were dead” with the unholy village where that distinction has collapsed. Other oppositions—warmth and cold, shelter and entrapment, protection and predation, scepticism and belief—remain deliberately unresolved. The wolf may be saving the narrator by warming him, threatening him, guarding him for Dracula or performing all three functions simultaneously. The ending therefore converts rescue into a more disturbing form of captivity: the narrator survives, but discovers that he may be “the sport of opposite forces.”

Stylistically, the piece relies on accumulative atmosphere rather than immediate shock, concrete sensory detail rather than abstract explanation, and ambiguity rather than full supernatural disclosure. Its signature effect comes from treating the impossible with documentary seriousness while allowing folklore, weather and instinct to expose the limits of the narrator’s confidence. Occasional typographical irregularities—missing spaces after punctuation, doubled words and inconsistent spacing around dashes—appear to be transcription artefacts rather than intentional features of the literary style.

The story uses a polished late-Victorian mode of popular supernatural fiction, combining domestic realism, social comedy and a compact tale of ominous prophecy. Its surface world is orderly and comfortable: a young married couple, an old college friend, dinner, cigars, music and a summer evening on the moor. Against this reassuring setting, the narrative introduces the uncanny through the gipsy camp and its commanding Queen. The supernatural is never conclusively authenticated; instead, the prophecy works psychologically, altering the characters’ behaviour until fear almost brings about the predicted event. This gives the story the structure of a self-fulfilling prophecy, though the final accident provides a relieving, narrowly literal fulfilment rather than a tragic one.

The narration is in the third person, with a broadly omniscient perspective that moves between Joshua, Mary and Gerald while retaining some authorial distance. The narrator supplies background efficiently, summarising Joshua and Mary’s courtship and marriage before returning to the immediate action. There are occasional generalising or parenthetical observations—such as the comment on “the universal kind” of man who tolerates being admired by a pretty woman—which create a knowing, lightly ironic narrative personality. Focalisation often settles on Gerald when an external observer is needed: he notices Mary’s “strange, settled look,” understands Joshua’s motives and registers the growing unease without being as emotionally overwhelmed as the married couple.

The prose is formal but accessible, with period British vocabulary and spelling, including “barrister,” “dog-cart,” “loth,” “parsimoni­ous” and “gipsy.” Sentences are frequently medium-long or extended, built through coordination, semicolons, dashes and subordinate clauses. Descriptive passages tend to be balanced and flowing, whereas moments of alarm are rendered in shorter clauses, abrupt exclamations and rapid physical actions. The narration makes liberal use of intensifiers such as “very,” “quite,” “great” and “extreme,” reflecting the heightened emotional idiom of nineteenth-century melodrama.

Dialogue occupies much of the story and is the chief means of characterisation. Joshua and Gerald speak in an educated, urbane register filled with irony, legalistic jokes and friendly banter. Joshua’s scepticism is expressed through pedantic corrections to the wording of the prophecy, especially his distinction between a present murderer and one who may become a murderer. Gerald functions as a comic chorus, contributing dry interjections such as “Amen!” and mocking the commercial side of fortune-telling. By contrast, the gipsy Queen speaks in imperatives, ritual formulas and compressed prophetic images: “Be warned,” “On your own heads be the doom,” and “the broken circle of a severed ring.” Mary’s speech is softer and more emotionally direct, becoming fragmented and repetitive when fear overtakes her. These contrasting registers make conversations theatrical and sharply differentiated.

The tonal movement is carefully controlled. The opening is playful and affectionate, with flirtation jokes, whistling and domestic happiness. At the camp, humour continues to undercut the Queen’s solemnity, but her imagery of blood, murder and the severed ring gradually darkens the atmosphere. Once Mary hears the prophecy, comedy becomes dangerous: Joshua’s mock-tragic performance causes her to faint, and his later attempt to reject superstition precipitates the accident. The dominant tonal pattern is therefore one of ironic reversal, in which rational mockery and nervous credulity both help create the feared circumstances. The conclusion returns to tenderness and reassurance, but its relief remains slightly uneasy because the prediction has proved symbolically exact.

Pacing alternates between summary and scene. The courtship and early marriage are compressed into exposition, while the fortune-telling encounters, Mary’s collapse and the knife accident are presented moment by moment. Suspense is intensified through repeated warnings, delayed disclosure and escalating physical motifs. The initial prophecy is vague, then becomes explicit; Mary secretly seeks confirmation; the household knives are discovered to be blunt; Joshua produces an exceptionally sharp weapon; and the anticipated violence finally occurs by accident. Each stage narrows the range of possible outcomes and makes an ordinary domestic object feel threatening.

The story relies heavily on foreshadowing and symbolic repetition. Hands are central throughout: fortunes are read from them, payment is placed upon them, Joshua’s hand is declared murderous, Mary’s hand is wounded, and the ending focuses on Joshua kissing it. Gold and silver connect commerce, marriage and fate. The wedding ring represents the marital bond and fulfils the image of the “broken circle” when the blade severs it. Blood, sharp edges and knives move from verbal prediction to domestic concealment and finally to physical reality. The peaceful opening landscape also serves as contrastive foreshadowing: its exaggerated serenity makes the later eruption of fear more pronounced.

Characterisation is economical and functional. Joshua is affectionate, sanguine, rationalistic and proud of the complete trust within his marriage, but his humour lacks emotional tact. Mary is loving yet highly suggestible, and her attempts to prevent fate paradoxically create the conditions for its apparent fulfilment. Gerald combines medical authority, worldly scepticism and sensitivity to nervous distress; he repeatedly warns Joshua that psychological reactions matter even when a prophecy is false. The Queen is not psychologically developed but presented as a hieratic figure of fate. Her “imperial” bearing, ceremonial speech and mysterious knowledge give her symbolic force. At the same time, the depiction of the gipsy community depends on period stereotypes of exoticism, secrecy, superstition and hereditary occult insight.

Overall, the style is concise, dramatic and plot-driven, with a strong oral-storytelling quality. It balances witty drawing-room dialogue against sensational imagery, uses domestic details to ground the supernatural premise, and builds toward a visual, mechanically precise twist. Its characteristic effect comes from the collision of modern scepticism with archaic fatalism: the characters laugh at prophecy, fear it, resist it and finally enact a distorted version of it themselves.

The story adopts the style of a comic Cornish folktale, combining oral storytelling, regional anecdote, domestic farce, and an increasingly extravagant dream journey. Its opening formula, “Once upon a time,” immediately places it within the fairy-tale tradition, while precise references to Wendron, Helston, Penryn, local fairs, farming, mining, and seafaring give the fantasy a strongly rooted regional identity. Supernatural events are treated less as sources of terror than as ordinary extensions of village life and popular superstition.

The narrative voice is genial, confident, and conversational, resembling a practiced storyteller addressing an audience aloud. The narrator moves easily between summary, explanation, and direct comment, using phrases such as “to tell truth,” “the worst of it was,” and “that I am going to tell you.” This creates intimacy and encourages the reader to accept even the most absurd developments. The third-person viewpoint stays close to Joby’s limited, literal-minded understanding, so that marvels are usually presented as he experiences them rather than explained from outside. The narration occasionally appeals directly to common experience—most notably in the assurance that Joby’s eyes become “as straight as yours or mine.”

Regional speech is central to the style. Dialogue uses Cornish and broader West Country dialect forms such as “’twas,” “you’m,” “naybour,” “ax,” “afore,” “conjurin’,” and “west o’ Grinnidge,” alongside nonstandard grammar and clipped endings. These features establish place, social class, and the oral character of the tale without making the speech wholly opaque. Joby’s language is practical, courteous, and naïvely rational even in impossible circumstances. His habitual phrases—“to be sure,” “thankin’ you kindly,” and similar civilities—make him an amiable comic innocent.

The prose alternates between brisk narrative summary and dialogue-led scenes. Sentences are generally clear and sequential, frequently linked by “and,” “but,” “so,” and “then,” reproducing the cumulative rhythm of spoken storytelling. Repetition is used as a structural and comic device: characters “walked and walked”; the bed, fairground ride, weathercock, and clock hands all turn “widdershins”; and Joby repeatedly applies ordinary reasoning to extraordinary situations. Parenthetical qualifications and dashes preserve the sense of a narrator adding details as they occur to him. Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, and abrupt conversational turns give the scenes lively performative energy.

The humour depends chiefly on deadpan incongruity. Absurd consequences are reported with complete seriousness: a baby develops a permanent squint from watching a dance; a farmer’s furrows veer north like a compass needle; a sailor walks down a shark’s throat “in a huff”; and the Equator is a physical line that sings when scraped and can be rubbed out with a wet thumb. The characters rarely question the underlying impossibility of these events. Instead, they worry about manners, local geography, payment, marriage, or practical navigation. This collision between the marvellous and the mundane produces the tale’s distinctive comic tone.

Characterisation is economical and caricatural. Joby is credulous, literal, good-natured, and attached to home. Tommy Warne is a rogue whose reputation for magic begins in accidental deception, while Joby’s wife is affectionate, superstitious, and practical. Secondary figures are defined by one or two vivid traits: the brusque sea-captain speaks in mock-nautical terms, and the church weathercock is both ceremonious and talkative. None is psychologically complex; each serves the folktale’s action and humour.

The plot follows a cumulative, episodic pattern. A comic origin story explains Joby’s squint; the episode of the hidden pack-saddles establishes Tommy’s supposed powers; the reversed bed ritual launches the nocturnal adventure; and a series of escalating encounters carries Joby from Penryn Fair to the Equator and back to Wendron Church. Motifs recur with altered meanings. Turning “widdershins” begins as magical instruction, becomes the motion of the fairground ride, redirects Joby’s squint, governs the weathercock’s cure, and finally appears in Joby’s attempt to reverse the church clock. This repetition binds together an otherwise freely associative sequence.

The fantasy has a dreamlike logic rather than a system of consistent magic. Transitions occur through visual or verbal association: a fairground boat becomes a real vessel, a painted-looking line becomes the Equator, and turning a clock hand becomes twisting a sleeping wife’s arm. The ending reveals the adventure as a dream or magical sleep experience, yet validates it by leaving Joby’s eyes cured. This preserves both interpretations and follows the folktale convention in which nonsense nevertheless produces a real transformation.

Imagery is concrete, colourful, and kinetic. The fair is evoked through swing-boats, shooting galleries, lamps, striped horses, and a steam organ; the ocean is reduced to a blue surface crossed by a white line; and the gilt weathercock sprawls like a trapped living fowl. Movement dominates the imagery: dancing, circling, spinning, sailing, flying, sliding, and winding backward. The circular motion reflects both the physical squint and the story’s return from distant fantasy to Joby’s own bed.

The overall tone is warm, whimsical, and gently satirical. The tale mocks credulity, fraudulent conjuring, medical helplessness, folk remedies, and the pretensions of technical knowledge, but it does so without cruelty. Its governing sensibility is that of the tall tale: local life is exaggerated until it becomes cosmically absurd, while the storyteller maintains a perfectly straight face. The resulting style is rhythmically oral, regionally specific, structurally repetitive, and driven by affectionate nonsense.

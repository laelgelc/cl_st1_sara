A curious traveler named Fuller explores Seneca Lake in hopes of explaining two local mysteries: an ancient floating tree known to settlers as the “Wandering Jew” and an unexplained booming noise called the “Lake Gun.” He hires Peter, an old boatman who treats the tree as a living, supernatural being, but after two weeks of searching they find nothing.

Fuller then meets an educated young Seneca who identifies the tree as the “Swimming Seneca.” According to tribal tradition, it is See-wise, a demagogic chief who flattered his followers, rejected sacred laws, and encouraged fishing out of season. For his offenses, the Great Spirit condemned him to float in the lake for a thousand winters, while the mysterious booms are the Spirit’s warnings whenever he approaches the fish.

Soon after the lake produces a cannon-like report, Fuller, the Seneca, and Peter locate the floating trunk. Its weathered end resembles a selfish, indecisive human face, reinforcing the legend in their minds. The young Seneca uses the sight as a warning against leaders who gain power by flattering the ignorant, inflaming passions, and discarding inherited wisdom. Fuller recognizes a parallel with contemporary political demagogues, and the Seneca concludes that although such figures may prosper temporarily, divine justice and truth will ultimately overcome them.

The story is a brisk, first-person comic adventure narrated by an amiable, wealthy English bachelor whose confidence far exceeds his intelligence. He addresses the reader as though recounting an anecdote over drinks, using conversational asides such as “don’t you know,” “old top,” and “by Jove.” This creates an intimate, clubman-like tone: worldly, breezy and cheerfully indiscreet. The narrator is essentially decent and occasionally ingenious, but also obtuse, privileged and emotionally awkward. Much of the comedy arises from the gap between his self-assessment and what the reader can plainly infer.

The diction mixes upper-class British slang with sporting, theatrical and American-inflected expressions. People are “old scouts,” money is “a chunk of o’ goblins,” a detective is a “sleuth,” and an assault becomes “slugging a Serene Highness.” Elevated or conventional phrases are repeatedly dragged into comic bathos: Fate “swats” George “all over the ring,” while a potential suicide is objectionable partly because the narrator cannot afford to lose a valet skilled at preserving a trouser crease. Foreign titles and ceremonious language are treated with amused irreverence, especially through the narrator’s running jokes about the word “Serene.” The language belongs to an early twentieth-century social world, but its energy comes from slang, exaggeration and unexpected verbal collisions rather than ornate description.

Sentences are generally clear, fast and speech-like. Short declarative statements—“I popped,” “He read,” “It did”—deliver punchlines with sharp timing, while longer sentences imitate the narrator thinking aloud, complete with qualifications, digressions and self-corrections. Parentheses, dashes and repeated phrases preserve the rhythm of spontaneous storytelling. Descriptive passages are deliberately compressed: “Blue sea, gleaming Casino, cloudless sky, and all the rest of the hippodrome.” The setting is sketched just enough to establish glamour before attention returns to social embarrassment and plot mechanics.

Dialogue dominates the story and carries both characterization and momentum. Exchanges are rapid, economical and built around reversals, misunderstandings and literal-minded replies. The narrator’s exuberant slang contrasts with Voules’s grave, formal “Yes, sir,” George’s nervous volatility, the Count’s ceremonious Germanic phrasing and Stella’s cooler restraint. Repetition is frequently used as a comic device, as in the discussion of the identifying mole or the clipped conversation about the moon. Characters often answer a figurative or dramatic statement from an unexpectedly practical angle: when told that the injured prince will recover, George replies that this does not matter, and the narrator observes, “It does to him.”

The plot has the architecture of a farce. A romantic misunderstanding leads to drunken confusion, an apparent royal assault, a missing hat, a detective’s arrival and an improvised twin-brother impersonation. Each attempted solution creates a fresh complication. The false identity protects George from arrest but prevents him from claiming his inheritance and later from receiving credit for his heroism. Information is carefully staggered so that assumptions repeatedly collapse: George is not the prince’s attacker but his rescuer; the twin brother does not exist; the inheritance has vanished; Voules’s betrayal arrives just when exposure becomes beneficial. Coincidence is extensive but purposeful, producing a tightly interlocked chain of comic reversals.

The humour depends chiefly on incongruity, understatement and bathos. Serious matters—assault, fraud, lost wealth, blackmail and threatened suicide—are filtered through the narrator’s shallow but genial priorities. He is troubled by emotional scenes because he has no suitable remarks to make, and he evaluates moral crises in terms of comfort, etiquette and domestic service. Hyperbole adds further comic force: nerves stick “a yard out” of George’s body, a breakfast guest appears to have “taken root,” and a valet’s raised voice becomes a battle. The story also uses recurring motifs as comic anchors, particularly George’s hat, the mole, Voules’s impassive professionalism and the distinction between George and the invented Alfred.

Characterization is broad, efficient and performance-based. George is impulsive, romantic and highly suggestible; Stella functions as the sensible romantic obstacle; Voules combines immaculate servantly composure with wounded pride, opportunism and melodrama; the narrator acts as meddler, confidant and accidental problem-solver. Minor figures are established through a few vivid traits rather than psychological depth. Social hierarchy is central to the comedy, but it is treated flexibly: servants manipulate employers, aristocratic dignity is punctured by slang, and Voules’s gambling success instantly allows him to imagine himself a gentleman of property.

The narrative pace is quick and cumulative. Scenes enter late, establish a problem, and end on a new complication or punchline. Exposition is embedded in conversation rather than presented at length, while transitions such as “The next item in the day’s programme” give the whole affair the feel of a comic itinerary. The climax gathers nearly every principal character on deck and resolves multiple strands at once through successive disclosures. A brief romantic coda then returns to the original misunderstanding and closes with the narrator being curtly dismissed, giving the story a neat final joke rather than a sentimental resolution.

Overall, the style is light, urbane and intricately plotted, with a strong oral quality. Its signature features are the unreliable yet lovable raconteur, period slang, compressed description, dialogue-driven scenes, class-based contrasts, escalating mistaken identities, and a persistent conversion of danger or emotion into social inconvenience. Even when the stakes are high, the prose remains buoyant, treating life as a sequence of baffling but ultimately entertaining scrapes.

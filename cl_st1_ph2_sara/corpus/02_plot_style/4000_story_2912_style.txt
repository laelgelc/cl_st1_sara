The story uses a warm, omniscient third-person narrator who moves freely between outward action, social commentary, and the private feelings of Tommy and Miss Octavia. The narrator is morally sympathetic and gently instructive, guiding the reader past Tommy’s bad reputation toward his hidden tenderness. Judgments are often stated directly—Tommy is neglected, Bessie is patient, Miss Octavia has been unfair—but they are tempered by affectionate humour and occasional irony. Brief asides such as “Most people’s did” and qualifications such as “brazenly, Miss Octavia thought” subtly distinguish public prejudice from the narrator’s more compassionate understanding.

The diction combines polished, old-fashioned literary prose with colloquial dialogue. Descriptive passages use graceful, sentimental language, especially when depicting flowers, affection, or deprivation: Bessie’s friendship is “one little white blossom of pure affection” in “the arid desert of Tommy’s existence,” while the unrealised garden contains the hidden promise of “crimson and purple and gold.” Biblical and elevated phrases, including “the substance of things hoped for,” lend moral and emotional resonance to ordinary village life. In contrast, Tommy’s speech is represented through dialect spellings, contractions, and informal constructions—“ain’t,” “jist,” “tech,” “’Course,” and “You bet”—which establish his class, age, and lack of schooling without depriving him of intelligence or dignity. Miss Octavia’s idioms, particularly “for the land’s sake,” give her a recognisable, mildly comic voice.

The syntax is generally clear, fluent, and accessible, with medium-length declarative sentences carrying the narrative forward. Longer sentences accumulate visual details through lists, as in the descriptions of geraniums, annuals, the doll’s clothing, and the Knox household. Repetition is used for emphasis and emotional patterning: rows of flowers, Bessie’s repeated exclamations over the doll, and the recurring contrast between what Tommy appears to be and what he truly is. Dialogue is frequent and interwoven with concise narrative interpretation, producing a steady alternation between scene and summary. Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, and emphatic fragments heighten moments of childish excitement, distress, surprise, and reward.

Characterisation depends strongly on contrast and reversal. Tommy is first presented through the town’s hostile view as ragged, impudent, and mischievous, then progressively revealed as loyal, self-denying, chivalrous, and passionately responsive to beauty. Miss Octavia begins as rigid, suspicious, and class-conscious, but her devotion to flowers creates an unsuspected point of kinship with him. Bessie functions as the catalyst who draws out Tommy’s goodness, while the flowers become the means by which Miss Octavia discovers it. Physical and domestic details carry social meaning: shabby houses, broken palings, muslin curtains, clean yards, rags, and cultivated gardens distinguish neglect from care and prejudice from genuine worth.

The plot has the compact, symmetrical construction of a moral tale. It opens with conflict over Miss Octavia’s flowers, shifts to Tommy’s love for Bessie and her longing for the doll, and then joins the two strands through the threatened frost. Tommy’s unprompted rescue of the flowers earns the doll for Bessie and ultimately secures him work with a florist. Coincidence is prominent but purposeful: Miss Octavia happens to have bought the exact doll, and her brother happens to need a flower-loving boy. These providential turns support the story’s ethical design, in which kindness circulates and is repaid, hidden virtue is recognised, and an act performed without hope of reward transforms several lives.

The overall tone is sentimental, tender, optimistic, and gently humorous. Pathos is created through childhood poverty, disability, neglect, and Bessie’s illness, but suffering remains softened rather than starkly realistic. Emotional states are made visible through conventional signs such as tears, pallor, lumps in the throat, radiant faces, and breathless joy. The ending provides complete restorative closure: Bessie recovers, Miss Octavia revises her judgment, Tommy gains affection and opportunity, and his deepest reward is learning that Bessie values him above the coveted doll. The governing style is therefore that of an early popular children’s or family story: lucid, picturesque, morally explicit, socially compassionate, and designed to produce both tears and reassurance.

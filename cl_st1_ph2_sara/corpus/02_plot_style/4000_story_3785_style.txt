The story uses a polished, late-nineteenth- or early-twentieth-century adventure style that combines outdoor realism, genial comedy, and an ultimately elegiac view of hunting. Its narrator is omniscient but unobtrusive, moving easily between detailed external observation, Hemenway’s private dissatisfaction, and—in the climax—the imagined instincts of the moose. The narrative voice is cultivated and mildly ironic, treating human vanity with affectionate amusement rather than harsh satire. Authorial generalisations such as the complaint about railway punctuality establish a conversational rapport with the reader and give the prose the air of a seasoned raconteur’s tale.

Description is concrete, atmospheric, and cumulative. The opening builds the railway station from an inventory of sensory details: chilly haze, wet rails, smoky lamps, somnolent travellers, telegraph clicks, battered luggage, and blurred village lights. These particulars create a damp, stagnant mood while also grounding the story in a recognisable regional and technological setting. Later, the prose shifts into a more lyrical register when describing moonlit wilderness or the moving locomotive. Natural and mechanical forces are repeatedly personified: the engine “hissed and sizzled patiently,” possesses a “great eye,” and becomes an “obedient and joyful monster” that conquers distance and devours darkness. This prepares the final confrontation between two figurative beasts—the locomotive and the moose—without making the symbolism feel detached from the physical action.

The syntax alternates between spacious descriptive sentences and short, emphatic narrative beats. Long sentences often accumulate objects, sounds, or successive stages of movement through semicolons and coordinated clauses. In scenes of anticipation, the prose slows through exact spatial explanation and repeated sensory cues. In moments of action, it contracts into rapid verbs, sound effects, and abrupt declarations: “slosh, slosh, slosh—thud, thud, thud,” followed by sudden silence or escape. This modulation gives the storytelling an oral, suspenseful rhythm. Parenthetical asides and comic qualifications frequently interrupt the action, preserving a light tone even during scenes of danger.

Dialogue dominates the central section and serves both as characterization and as the principal narrative frame. Hemenway’s hunting story is embedded within the station and locomotive episode, so that the text becomes a tale told aloud to a responsive listener. McLeod’s interruptions, jokes, sceptical comments, and moral objections continually shape the account. The result is less a solitary heroic narrative than a performance between companions. Repetition—especially McLeod’s mock accusation of “murder”—creates a running comic motif that gradually acquires moral weight. The conductor’s cry interrupts Hemenway at the precise point where his hunting tale reaches another failure, allowing the external journey to resume and setting up the ironic completion of the hunt.

Speech is strongly differentiated by dialect. McLeod’s Scots-inflected pronunciation, the station employee’s clipped regional vernacular, and the Pullman porter’s eye dialect contrast with Hemenway’s educated standard English. The dialect is extensive and phonetic, functioning as an important period marker as well as a source of humour, warmth, and social texture. McLeod’s language is especially expressive: his biblical, theological, and sentimental turns of phrase make him both comic foil and moral commentator. The story’s handling of some voices, particularly the porter’s, reflects dated racial conventions, while its broader use of dialect reflects an era in which regional speech was commonly represented through conspicuous phonetic spelling.

The humour is dry, companionable, and often built on anticlimax. Hemenway repeatedly approaches the glorious shot only to be defeated by darkness, poor positioning, a snapped twig, an insect flying down Billy’s throat, or simple bad luck. Elevated language is juxtaposed with absurdity: Billy’s moose call is “lonelier than a love-sick hippopotamus on the house top,” while a gathering of animals becomes a “meenadgerie.” Religious language is playfully applied to hunting accidents through references to commandments, Calvinism, providence, catechism, and predestination. These jokes gently undermine the hunter’s self-importance while allowing his enthusiasm and skill as a storyteller to remain attractive.

The story is carefully structured around delay, repetition, and reversal. The delayed train creates the leisure necessary for Hemenway’s account, while the repeated failed encounters with Silverhorns establish a pattern of near success. Each attempt is more promising than the last, yet each ends in comic frustration. The apparent conclusion—that the moose has fairly won and remains alive—brings Hemenway toward reluctant admiration. The final appearance of a moose on the railway line then reverses the hunting situation: the animal comes to the men, confronts not a rifle but industrial machinery, and dies through its own defiance. The coincidence is intentionally heightened and folkloric, but the careful mirroring of moose and locomotive gives it thematic coherence.

Tone evolves from irritation and comic reminiscence to exhilaration, suspense, and sober admiration. The death of the moose is not presented as a hunter’s clean triumph. It is “frightfully shattered,” and Hemenway’s response lacks jubilation. The final exclamation—“what a fighter!”—honours the animal’s courage and converts the long-sought trophy into a troubling victory. This produces the story’s central irony: Hemenway gets his first moose only when he is no longer truly hunting it, and the kill belongs as much to the railway as to either man. The wilderness monarch is defeated by modern machinery, suggesting an understated conflict between nature and industrial expansion.

Overall, the style blends regional realism, sporting anecdote, tall-tale comedy, and romantic nature writing. Its most distinctive qualities are dense sensory scene-setting, warmly ironic narration, vividly phonetic dialogue, suspense managed through repetition and interruption, and a final turn from comic failure to tragic irony. The prose admires masculine fellowship, storytelling, machinery, and wilderness adventure, but its deepest sympathy ultimately rests with the magnificent animal whose resistance makes the nominal human victory feel morally ambiguous.

The story uses a brisk, genial mode of social comedy, combining journalistic clarity with affectionate political satire. Its premise is mildly farcical—a newly formed carpet-tackers’ union halts urgent work just before a royal visit—but the narration treats the escalating absurdity with enough seriousness to make the comedy feel plausible. The dominant tone is amused, buoyant and civic-minded rather than caustic. Politicians, officials, labourers and dignitaries are teased for their pride and self-importance, yet the story remains broadly sympathetic to them. Even conflict becomes an opportunity to celebrate initiative, fellowship and an informal national character.

The narration is third-person and largely omniscient, moving freely among public scenes, private schemes and individual reactions. The narrator generally remains outside the characters but occasionally steps forward with knowing commentary, as in the playful correction that there were “good, bad and—no, there were no indifferent,” or the observation that “There should have been a Boswell there.” Such interventions give the voice the character of an entertaining raconteur: socially observant, lightly ironic and confident that the reader shares its amusement. The perspective is panoramic, well suited to a story about collective action. Rather than following one central protagonist throughout, it passes among the Governor, Harry Weston, Senator Bruner, the carpet-tackers and Prince Ludwig, allowing the State-house itself to function as the story’s communal centre.

The prose alternates between efficient exposition and dialogue-heavy dramatic scenes. Narrative sentences often gather detail through parallel clauses and cumulative lists: work stops “in the Senate Chamber and in the House, on the stairways and in the corridors,” while later the action embraces Republicans and Democrats, friends and enemies, senators, judges, bankers and union leaders. This repeated use of enumeration creates bustle, enlarges the scale of the episode and reinforces the theme of disparate people being drawn into common activity. Short declarative sentences punctuate these longer constructions—“There was magic in its note”; “That settled it”; “It went without a hitch”—giving the narrative a confident, anecdotal rhythm. Exclamation marks, dashes, parenthetical interruptions and repeated words reproduce excitement and spontaneous speech. The onomatopoeic “Stretch—stretch—stretch, and tack—tack—tack” briefly converts the work itself into a comic refrain.

Dialogue is the principal means of characterisation. It is colloquial, quick and strongly marked by early twentieth-century American idiom: “pretty bum,” “hummer,” “tickled,” “got next to the situation,” “jolly up,” “the boys,” and “sure-enough thing.” Grammatical informality—“We was anxious,” “What’s the matter with us?”—distinguishes working-class and conversational voices without making them opaque. Public figures speak in a mixture of political rhetoric and everyday slang, and much of the humour comes from applying grand oratory to carpet-laying. Harry Weston transforms manual work into a patriotic campaign; Senator Arnold speaks of driving tacks as another form of service to the State; and the Governor issues solemn pronouncements about carpets, collars and the potentially distracting “feminine element.” The gap between elevated language and mundane activity supplies the story’s characteristic comic incongruity.

Characterisation is economical and behavioural. Figures are established through a revealing phrase, gesture or manner of speaking rather than through sustained psychological analysis. The Governor is energetic, proud and resistant to coercion; Weston is charismatic and tactically shrewd; Bruner is mischievous but frames his mischief as patriotic duty; the Prince is distinguished chiefly by his relief at escaping ceremony. Minor characters are similarly vivid in miniature: the striker takes courage from the floor’s splinters, the elderly senator tests his muscle, and Bridget Haggerty dispenses coffee, sandwiches and loud moral judgment. Many characters are identified by office rather than by name, making them simultaneously individuals and representatives of political or civic types.

The plot is tightly organised around a deadline. The opening gives exact times, the Prince is expected at four, and later references to noon, two o’clock and the approaching finish sustain momentum. The structure proceeds through clean stages: stoppage, explanation, failed attempts to hire replacements, collective improvisation, the secret acceleration of the Prince’s arrival, covert observation and final revelation. Each development raises the comic stakes. The first reversal turns lawmakers into carpet-tackers; the second allows the royal visitor to see them before they can restore their formal appearance. Suspense depends not on danger but on timing, concealment and the possibility of dignified people being caught undignified.

The humour is warm, cumulative and situational. It draws on reversals of status, political rivalries temporarily suspended, exaggerated solemnity and the collision between ceremonial expectations and physical labour. The story repeatedly places prestigious men in undignified postures: dirty, collarless, nursing bruised fingers, arguing over hammers or collaborating with old enemies on wrinkles in a carpet. Running jokes about official rivalry, legislative competition, labour politics and public dignity deepen the comic texture. Hyperbole—“millennium-like unity,” “worked like a Trojan,” “Indian traditions to burn”—keeps the narrative lively while preserving its newspaper-anecdote accessibility.

The setting is rendered primarily as a social arena rather than through lyrical description. The State-house’s chambers, offices, corridors, stairways, rotunda, tunnel and storerooms create a theatrical architecture in which public roles can be rearranged. Before the work, the bare boards are splintered and “miserable”; afterward, the building is restored through communal effort. The contrast between the formal Capitol and its improvised backstage areas supports the central opposition between official display and unguarded reality. The hidden vantage point above the rotunda is especially important: it turns the Prince into a spectator watching authentic national life as though it were a play.

The thematic emphasis falls on democratic informality, self-reliance and authenticity. The story idealises an America revealed not by bands, speeches, uniforms or polished receptions, but by people abandoning rank and working together. It does not offer a complex treatment of labour conflict: the strike is largely framed as an opportunistic “hold-up,” and the officials’ amateur labour becomes the more celebrated form of solidarity. Nevertheless, the union figures are not portrayed as villains of great menace; they belong to the same comic civic world, and even the teamsters’ union president eventually joins the effort. Political antagonisms likewise prove superficial when practical action is required.

The ending condenses the story’s governing idea into an epigram: “the only way to see America is to see it when America is not seeing you.” This polished final line resolves the farce while converting it into a statement about national identity. It also crystallises the story’s stylistic method. Throughout, public ceremony is displaced by backstage observation, formal rhetoric by slang, and political dignity by physical comedy. The resulting style is energetic, conversational and theatrically constructed, with a strong period flavour and an optimistic belief that character is most truthfully displayed through spontaneous action rather than prepared spectacle.

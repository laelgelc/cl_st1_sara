The story uses a richly descriptive, late nineteenth-century romantic-realist style, combining regional local colour with the conventions of sentimental romance. Its atmosphere is initially sensuous and idyllic, but an undercurrent of social tension and dramatic irony gradually darkens the narrative. The prose treats the Gulf Coast not merely as a setting but as an active emotional presence: winds “meet and conflict,” waves leave a “laughing kiss,” and the sea glows with “phosphorescent flame.” Such personification and luminous imagery give the landscape an enchanted quality while foreshadowing the conflict between desire, social expectation, and reality.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, though it remains closest to Annette’s perceptions and emotional development. The narrator frequently addresses the reader directly through phrases such as “you must know,” “you can ever see,” and “If ever you go,” creating a conversational, intimate relationship with the audience. These interventions resemble the voice of an experienced local guide, explaining customs, geography, and regional pleasures while gently teasing outsiders. The narrator is warm but not wholly uncritical: romantic scenes are sometimes qualified by humorous observations, as when the harvest moon is said to sympathise with the lovers despite probably watching “hundreds of other equally romantic couples.”

The diction alternates between ornate lyrical description and light, socially observant comedy. Scenic passages favour accumulative adjectives, rhythmic parallelism, and sensory detail: breezes are “resinous, fragrant, cold,” while the Gulf wind is “warm and soft and languorous.” Visual imagery dominates, especially contrasts among darkness, fire, moonlight, white sand, and glowing water, but smell, sound, temperature, and texture are also prominent. Musical language runs throughout the story, from mandolins and guitars at the pier to Annette’s vocal studies and the climactic opera scenes. This recurring motif links natural sound, courtship, ambition, and professional performance.

Sentence structure is often expansive and flowing in descriptive passages, with coordinated clauses and participial phrases that reproduce the movement of wind, waves, boats, and crowds. Shorter sentences and fragments sharpen comic or emotional moments. Philip’s repeated “Hum,” for example, compresses jealousy, disapproval, and wounded pride into a single sound. The prose also employs repetition for emphasis, as in “study, study, sing, practise,” and uses catalogues to evoke abundance, whether listing fish, musical instruments, operatic roles, or the contents of the fisherman’s hut.

Dialogue is lively and strongly characterising. Annette speaks with impulsive enthusiasm, Philip with dry sarcasm and possessiveness, and LeConte with formal courtesy and controlled ambiguity. French words and phrases—“mademoiselle,” “monsieur,” “mais non,” “entrez”—reinforce the Francophone Creole setting and LeConte’s cultivated identity. Natalie’s dialect is represented phonetically and grammatically, marking regional and social difference while contributing to the local-colour effect. The contrast among standard English, formal French-inflected speech, and Creole patois establishes a culturally layered community, though the representation reflects the class-conscious conventions of its period.

The story’s pacing moves from leisurely scenic immersion to a sequence of episodic encounters. The pier rescue, fish-fry, hut visit, sailing excursion, farewell, operatic revelation, and final disclosure function as increasingly consequential stages in Annette’s attachment. Transitions such as “some days later,” “as the days passed on,” and “It was the night” allow the narrative to cover time quickly without losing its anecdotal quality. The central revelation—that the supposedly humble fisherman is a celebrated tenor—uses melodramatic coincidence and disguise, while the final revelation of his marriage reverses the romantic promise and produces a subdued rather than explosive ending.

Characterisation relies heavily on contrast and gesture. Annette is vivacious, imaginative, socially unconventional, and prone to romantic projection. Philip is jealous and class-conscious, his frustration displaced into growling, dry replies, and even savage treatment of the fish he catches. LeConte remains poised, courteous, and elusive; because his interior life is largely withheld, his charm always carries an element of uncertainty. Physical details often externalise emotion: Annette’s glowing cheeks, Philip’s ostentatious attention to another woman, and the heroine’s final whiteness and stillness make feeling visible without extended psychological analysis.

Social hierarchy is a central stylistic and thematic pressure. The apparent gulf between a “city demoiselle” and a fisherman structures both the humour and suspense, while LeConte’s true profession exposes the superficiality of class assumptions without entirely dismantling them. The opera world introduces a second hierarchy based on talent, celebrity, and access. Annette’s artistic ambition is interwoven with romantic longing, so that LeConte’s praise becomes an incentive to study and his marriage destroys not only her hopes of love but, momentarily, her desire for a career.

The tone blends affection, playfulness, romance, and irony. Early comic episodes and picturesque excursions encourage the reader to share Annette’s delight, while the narrator quietly signals that she misunderstands the nature of her relationship. The ending is emotionally restrained: instead of a dramatic outburst, Annette speaks in a “quiet little tired voice.” This tonal contraction—from glowing seas, bonfires, applause, and operatic grandeur to private exhaustion—gives the conclusion its poignancy. Overall, the style is lush, musical, regionally specific, and gently satirical, using sensory landscape, conversational narration, social comedy, and romantic irony to turn a seemingly idyllic summer attachment into a story of illusion and disappointment.

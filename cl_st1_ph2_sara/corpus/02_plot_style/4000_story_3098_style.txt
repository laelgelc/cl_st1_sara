The story is written as an intense first-person interior monologue, confining the reader almost entirely to the unnamed speaker’s consciousness. External action is minimal: a woman waits beside a telephone for a promised call. The narrative movement instead comes from her rapidly changing thoughts, which cycle through hope, prayer, rationalisation, anger, shame, pride, jealousy and despair. This tight psychological focus creates an immediate, claustrophobic atmosphere and makes the waiting itself feel dramatic.

The prose imitates anxious, compulsive thought. Short sentences, fragments and repeated phrases—especially “Please,” “I mustn’t,” “Maybe,” and “If”—produce a breathless, incantatory rhythm. Counting by fives serves both as a ritual of self-control and as a structural refrain, measuring time while revealing her inability to escape the same mental loop. Longer passages often accumulate through loosely connected clauses, showing one justification generating another. Abrupt reversals repeatedly interrupt these chains: she wants the man alive and then dead, vows never to telephone him and immediately invents reasons to do so, appeals to God and then attacks Him.

The diction is mostly plain, colloquial and emotionally direct. Simple words such as “little,” “sweet,” “bad,” “lovely” and “darling” acquire force through obsessive repetition. Endearments contrast sharply with bursts of profanity and violent imagery, as when the telephone becomes a “damned, ugly, shiny thing” with a “smug black face.” This contrast exposes the instability beneath the speaker’s effort to appear gentle, cheerful and undemanding. Dialogue appears mainly as remembered or imagined speech, and tiny variations in wording—“I’ll call you” versus “Call me”—become objects of desperate interpretation.

The tone combines pathos with dark comedy and irony. The speaker treats a telephone call as a matter of divine intervention, salvation and damnation, while intermittently recognising that her behaviour is disproportionate. Her self-awareness does not free her; it only adds another layer to the cycle. The gap between what she knows and what she feels creates much of the story’s bitter humour. God is addressed in shifting registers, from childlike pleading to formal Christian invocation, bargaining, accusation and defiance. Religious language is therefore both sincere and parodically domestic, reducing cosmic theology to the question of whether a man will call.

A prominent stylistic feature is self-division. The speaker continually commands, corrects and debates herself, so that the monologue resembles an argument among competing voices: the abandoned lover, the rational observer, the proud woman, the submissive romantic and the furious accuser. Generalisations about “they” broaden her private anguish into a critique of romantic power: men supposedly dislike women who telephone, cry, speak honestly or become too available. Her intended performance of being “sweet” and “gay” reveals a social world in which emotional candour is treated as dangerous and affection must be managed through games.

The telephone functions as the central symbol and dramatic object. Silent and impersonal, it becomes alternately an idol, an enemy, a possible instrument of humiliation and the imagined source of deliverance. Time is similarly psychological rather than merely chronological: the clock, the delayed call and the repeated counting rituals stretch a few hours into an emotional ordeal. The ending remains suspended within another count, withholding resolution and returning the narrative to its compulsive pattern. This circular structure suggests that the central subject is not whether the telephone finally rings, but the speaker’s entrapment in desire, uncertainty and self-abasement.

Overall, the style is spare in setting but dense in psychological detail, using repetition, fragmentation, contradiction, apostrophe, rhetorical questions and tonal volatility to dramatise emotional dependence from the inside. Its most distinctive effect comes from balancing sympathy with satirical precision: the speaker’s suffering is real, yet the prose exposes every irrational manoeuvre by which she tries to preserve hope. Occasional typographical irregularities appear to be textual or transcription errors rather than meaningful elements of the style.

The story uses a polished, nineteenth-century realist style that blends lyrical romanticism with satirical comedy. It opens in an expansive, sensuous register, presenting spring as a force that acts directly upon the body and emotions. Warm air, sunlight, greenery, birdsong and the movement of the river create an atmosphere of intoxication and erotic possibility. Nature is strongly personified: the earth “puts on” green, love lies in ambush, and spring seems to infect the whole city with longing. This heightened description deliberately encourages the reader to share the narrator’s susceptibility before the story begins to undermine it.

The narration is first-person and retrospective, but the narrating voice remains close to the impulses of the experiencing self. The narrator is observant, impressionable and faintly self-mocking, though less self-aware than the stranger who interrupts him. His gaze idealizes the young woman almost instantly, transforming small physical details—a curl of hair, pale down on the skin, a glance or smile—into signs of tenderness, poetry and imagined happiness. This disproportion between what is actually seen and what is emotionally inferred is central to the style. Romantic feeling is represented as an act of projection, in which ordinary details acquire extravagant significance.

The diction alternates between elevated sensual language and bluntly comic phrasing. Descriptions of spring and attraction contain words such as “awakening,” “fragrant,” “tenderness,” “poetry,” “intoxicating” and “ideal,” while the stranger’s account introduces practical, bureaucratic and bodily vocabulary: flannel, bronchitis, pleurisy, office portfolios, charcoal dealers and domestic gossip. The contrast punctures romantic illusion. Repeated comparisons between love and illness, alcohol, danger or entrapment turn emotion into a physical hazard. The mock public warning—“Return of spring. French citizens, beware of love!”—is typical of the story’s ironic method, applying the solemn language of civic safety to private desire.

Sentences are often long, cumulative and rhythmically flowing, especially in descriptive passages. Coordinated clauses and lists reproduce the abundance of spring and the narrator’s swelling excitement: people move, talk, sing, look and wander in a continuous stream. Exclamations, repetitions and rhetorical questions dominate the stranger’s speech, giving it the quality of an agitated comic monologue. His recurring “monsieur,” repeated warnings and emphatic interjections make him sound both absurd and urgently sincere. Shorter sentences and direct exchanges interrupt the lyrical flow at decisive moments, particularly when attraction is checked by embarrassment, resistance or ridicule.

Dialogue occupies much of the story and is used for characterization as well as narrative framing. The stranger speaks in a theatrical, digressive manner, continually moralizing, revising his own judgments and turning personal misfortune into a universal theory. His story mirrors the narrator’s present situation almost exactly: a spring day, a riverboat, an attractive working girl, exchanged glances and an excursion to Saint-Cloud. This embedded narrative creates a cautionary tale within the main tale and produces dramatic irony, since the reader recognizes the pattern even while the narrator still intends to repeat it.

The structure moves from atmosphere to temptation, from interruption to confession, and finally to comic prevention. Its opening is leisurely and immersive; the central monologue slows external action while supplying a compressed history of courtship, marriage and disillusionment; the ending accelerates into physical farce as the stranger grabs the narrator’s coat and publicly prevents him from disembarking. The circular parallel between the two men gives the story the neatness of an anecdote or moral fable. The final line delivers an ironic punch: the stranger considers himself a benefactor, while the narrator experiences rescue as persecution.

The tone is amused, skeptical and knowingly cynical rather than tragic. The story satirizes male sentimentality, especially the tendency to confuse physical attraction with spiritual revelation. It also treats marriage as a comic trap and relies on period-specific class and gender stereotypes, particularly in the stranger’s contemptuous portrait of the working-class wife. Yet the stranger is not presented as wholly authoritative. His bitterness, vanity and generalizations make him ridiculous, while his earlier romantic foolishness closely resembles the narrator’s. The humor therefore cuts in several directions: at impulsive men, at idealized love, at domestic disappointment and at the pretension that one unhappy experience constitutes universal wisdom.

Recurring stylistic devices include sensory imagery, personification, hyperbole, parallelism, comic enumeration and refrain. “Beware of love” and “the song of Musette” function as repeated motifs, gathering greater comic force with each return. Glances and smiles serve as condensed erotic signals, while spring operates both as setting and as the story’s governing metaphor for irrational desire. The overall effect is light, vivid and conversational, with romantic lyricism repeatedly inflated and then deflated by worldly detail. Occasional awkward constructions, punctuation irregularities and anomalous words suggest an older translation or imperfectly transmitted text, but the dominant voice remains lucid, energetic and sharply anecdotal.

The story uses a restrained, naturalistic realism focused on the routines, humiliations, and frustrations of urban working life. Its setting is rendered through concrete social detail—offices, pawnshops, public houses, tram routes, foggy streets, clerical hierarchies, drink prices, and local slang—giving the narrative a strongly Dublin-Irish character. The diction is generally plain and exact, but occasional vivid comparisons sharpen the grotesque or oppressive atmosphere: a bald head resembles an egg, a fist vibrates like the knob of an electric machine, and Farrington’s face is repeatedly described in terms of dark wine or meat. Such images reduce people to physical objects and reinforce a world governed by appetite, bodily force, and petty power.

The narration is in the third person and remains closely aligned with Farrington’s perceptions, impulses, and resentments. It frequently moves into free indirect discourse, allowing his thoughts to enter the prose without quotation marks: questions, curses, rationalisations, and anticipations appear in his own idiom while still being grammatically contained by the narrator. This creates intimacy without sympathy becoming endorsement. Farrington is understood from within, but the narrative’s cool detachment exposes his self-deception, vanity, alcoholism, and displacement of anger. He is often called simply “the man,” a distancing choice that makes him seem both representative and diminished.

Sentence structure alternates between measured descriptive passages and brisk sequences of physical action. Repetition is prominent: Farrington walks “heavily,” stares “fixedly,” feels thirsty, imagines violence, buys rounds, and retells his successful retort. These recurrences establish a compulsive, circular rhythm. Ellipses, abrupt exclamations, fragments, and rhetorical questions imitate his distracted or agitated consciousness, while lists of movements and transactions give the drinking episodes a mechanical momentum. The prose often accumulates detail rather than pausing for explicit interpretation, letting behaviour reveal character.

Dialogue is central and highly socially marked. It reproduces accent, idiom, workplace formulas, pub slang, and class distinctions through expressions such as “g.p.,” “half-one,” “stand a drink,” “name their poisons,” and “my nabs.” Mr. Alleyne’s interrogative repetitions and affected authority convey bureaucratic bullying; Farrington’s brief answers show enforced submission until his accidental witticism momentarily reverses the balance of power. In the public houses, storytelling becomes a form of masculine performance. Farrington repeatedly embellishes the office incident to recover status, and the dialogue shifts from convivial banter to rivalry and aggression as his money and confidence disappear.

Characterisation is predominantly indirect. Farrington’s bulk, inflamed complexion, dirty eyes, thirst, fantasies of violence, and heavy movements make his emotional condition bodily and visible. Other figures are captured through a few satirical traits: Mr. Alleyne is small, pink, hairless, and manikin-like; Miss Delacour is defined by perfume, feather, and social influence; Weathers by theatrical self-display and physical skill. Descriptions often contain an understated irony that punctures characters’ pretensions. Farrington imagines himself masterful and strong, yet he is economically powerless, professionally trapped, easily manipulated, and ultimately defeated by a younger man.

The structure traces a continuous chain of frustrated attempts at compensation. Humiliation at work produces thirst; drinking produces boastfulness; boastfulness leads to financial depletion and physical competition; defeat leads to renewed rage; and rage is finally directed at the only person weaker than Farrington. Repeated motifs—heat and cold, light and darkness, thirst, fists, heads, money, masculine reputation, and failed escape—bind the episodes together. Public houses promise warmth, fellowship, sexuality, and release, but repeatedly deliver disappointment. The story’s progression is therefore downward and ironic rather than redemptive.

The tone combines social observation, bleak comedy, and mounting menace. Much of the middle section has a superficially convivial energy, yet the narrator continually registers squalor, resentment, calculation, and failure beneath it. The ending becomes stark and unsentimental. It offers no explanatory moral statement; instead, the child’s terrified promise to pray for his father closes the story on a cruel collision of innocence, religion, and domestic violence. Overall, the style is economical, psychologically penetrating, socially specific, and governed by an irony that reveals how institutional humiliation and personal weakness reproduce themselves as cruelty toward the vulnerable.

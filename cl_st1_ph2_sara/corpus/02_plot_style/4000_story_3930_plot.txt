Diadema Bascom discovers that moths have ruined clothes and keepsakes belonging to her deceased twin sister, Lovice. Rather than lose the remnants, she decides to turn them into a rug that will silently chronicle their intertwined lives. As she works beside her boarder, Priscilla Hollis, she explains the meaning of each element: two roses growing from one stalk represent the twins; scraps from childhood garments, wedding dresses, and their husbands’ neckties recall their upbringing, courtships, and shared wedding; and rosebuds symbolize their sons.

Through these memories, Diadema recounts Lovice’s joyful marriage to the minister Reuben Granger and her own affectionate, if less practical, husband Jot. She then recalls how Lovice came to care tenderly for Diadema after little Jot’s birth. Three years later, Diadema returned the kindness by attending Lovice during childbirth. Lovice gave birth to a son but died three days later, and the baby soon followed. Mother and child were buried together, with the infant placed in Lovice’s arms.

Diadema completes the rug with white everlasting flowers symbolizing the deaths of Lovice and her baby and the hope of eternal life. She preserves the finished rug almost reverently, protecting it from footsteps as a private memorial to love, family, motherhood, and loss. Finally, she tells Priscilla that if the rug outlives her, Priscilla should add another border of white flowers made from Diadema’s shawl, completing the rug’s story with Diadema’s own death.

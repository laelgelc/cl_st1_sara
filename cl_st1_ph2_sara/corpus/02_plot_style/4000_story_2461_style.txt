The story adopts a stark naturalist-adventure style, combining physical extremity, fatalism, psychological suspense, and grim irony. Its world is governed by violence, endurance, chance, and power rather than moral justice. From the opening declaration, “It was the end,” the narrative establishes an atmosphere of inevitability: Subienkow’s death is certain, and the central question is not whether he will survive but whether he can control the manner of his dying. This produces a fatalistic tone in which human ingenuity can alter the form of fate, though not escape fate itself.

The narration is third-person and closely aligned with Subienkow’s consciousness. The narrator moves freely between external observation, remembered history, and near-free-indirect thought, allowing phrases such as “This would never do” or “Why didn’t Ivan die?” to carry the immediacy of the protagonist’s inner voice without shifting into first person. The perspective is psychologically intimate but emotionally hard-edged. Subienkow’s fear is presented less as fear of pain than as an aesthetic and spiritual horror of degradation: he wants to die “bravely and cleanly,” rather than be reduced to “the veriest beast.” This distinction gives his final deception the character of an existential victory.

The prose alternates between compressed, emphatic statements and long accumulative passages. Short sentences—“It was the end,” “There had been no chance to escape,” “It had been nothing but savagery”—serve as structural blows, repeatedly returning the narrative to its governing ideas. Longer sentences trace geographical journeys, catalogue ordeals, and pile hardship upon hardship through parallel clauses and repeated conjunctions. Lists of places, peoples, dangers, and deaths create epic scale while also conveying exhaustion. Repetition is especially prominent: “From the beginning,” “Again,” “thrice,” “south, south,” and above all “savagery” turn Subienkow’s life into a recurring pattern from which there appears to be no exit.

The diction is forceful, concrete, and often bodily. Snow, blood, nerves, flesh, knives, lashes, bones, and axes dominate the sensory field. Violence is described directly enough to be disturbing, but many of the worst acts remain partially withheld or are registered through reactions, cries, and aftermath. This selective explicitness intensifies horror by encouraging the reader to imagine what is not fully stated. Elevated or archaic constructions—“it were foolishness,” “there be plenty,” “was come the harvest,” “loth to go”—coexist with blunt physical language, giving the narrative both the cadence of a grim legend and the immediacy of frontier storytelling.

Figurative language is vivid but controlled. Fate is personified as a force that has driven Subienkow across continents, while his life is imagined as a “trail” ending in the snow. The recurring “wall of savagery” turns geography into psychological imprisonment. Agricultural imagery shapes the moral climax: the fort’s brutality was “a sowing of blood,” and the massacre is its “harvest.” Animal comparisons—dove, ox, ape, beast—measure different forms of vulnerability and dehumanization. The setting is correspondingly symbolic: the remote, frozen landscape becomes “a dark place beyond the last boundaries of the world,” reflecting Subienkow’s sense of exile from European culture and from civilized identity.

The story’s structure is retrospective and funnel-shaped. It begins at the execution ground, expands through a long account of Subienkow’s journey from Poland across Russia, Siberia, and Alaska, and then contracts back to the immediate scene. This extended analepsis delays the climax while explaining his cultivated sensibility, resourcefulness, guilt, and fatalism. The geographical sweep also makes his final position feel historically and psychologically earned. Once he invents the medicine, the pace changes: dialogue replaces summary, actions are broken into smaller stages, and suspense depends on bargaining, preparation, and repeated tests of credibility.

Dialogue is terse, strategic, and theatrical. Subienkow speaks with calculated arrogance, using ritualistic phrasing, delays, added demands, and displays of contempt to make his lie convincing. His bargaining resembles commercial exchange—he plays “as coolly as if he were bartering for a foxskin”—which creates dark comedy within the execution scene. Makamuk’s suspicion and credulity are manipulated through performance rather than argument. Each new demand raises the supposed value of the medicine, while Subienkow’s feigned anxiety about applying it between blows functions as reverse psychology. The dialogue therefore reveals character through tactics: Subienkow is imaginative and self-controlled; Makamuk is status-conscious; Yakaga is suspicious and practically cunning.

Suspense is built through dramatic irony. The reader gradually understands that the medicine is fraudulent, but the exact purpose of the fraud remains uncertain until the axe falls. Details that initially suggest an escape plan—the sleds, dogs, rifle, provisions, escort, and chief’s daughter—are ultimately props in Subienkow’s performance. The execution itself is narrated with sudden visual clarity and mechanical precision, ending the elaborate deception in one clean stroke. The result is a reversal: apparent failure becomes success because the true objective was never escape but release from torture.

The ending is governed by savage, paradoxical humour. Subienkow dies, yet he wins; Makamuk kills him, yet is defeated; the onlookers’ laughter converts death into social humiliation for the chief. Reputation matters as much as physical survival, and “lost face” becomes both Makamuk’s punishment and the story’s final organizing image. The conclusion extends beyond the immediate moment into anticipated oral legend, imagining the tale repeated at future gatherings. This gives the ending a folkloric quality and transforms Subienkow’s private stratagem into communal memory.

Characterization rests heavily on contrast. Subienkow is physically sensitive but mentally resilient, while Big Ivan is physically formidable but reduced by torture to helplessness. European refinement is set against frontier brutality, though the story complicates that opposition by showing that Subienkow and the fur-traders have themselves committed massacres, coercion, and murder. He is neither an innocent victim nor a conventional hero. His ingenuity and courage coexist with blood guilt, arrogance, and participation in colonial violence. The narrative’s repeated use of historically dated racialized terms and broad descriptions of Indigenous people reflects a colonial frontier viewpoint; at the same time, the plot explicitly frames the attack on the fort as retaliation for forced labour, hostage-taking, flogging, and tribute.

Overall, the style is muscular, rhythmic, fatalistic, and theatrically ironic. It combines panoramic historical summary with close psychological focus, uses repetition and accumulation to create a sense of inescapable destiny, and culminates in a tightly staged trick whose success depends on redefining victory. Its most distinctive effect comes from the tension between brutal material reality and Subienkow’s determination to preserve a final fragment of autonomy, dignity, and authorship over his own death.

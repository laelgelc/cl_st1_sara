The story uses a lively first-person retrospective voice, narrated by a professional ballplayer who speaks as both participant and insider. His viewpoint is collective as often as individual: “I” repeatedly gives way to “we,” making the Rochester team’s shared irritation, pride, fear, and delight the emotional center of the narrative. The narrator is knowledgeable about baseball but not solemn about it. He explains specialist terms and tactics naturally, while treating the players’ vanity, gamesmanship, and physical suffering with amused familiarity.

The tone is comic, boisterous, and increasingly farcical, with a strong undercurrent of poetic justice. The professionals initially regard the Canadian players as provincial “rubes,” but their complacency is unsettled by the locals’ extraordinary speed, strength, and fielding. The illicit “rabbit” ball then turns the game into slapstick chaos and ultimately punishes the men who introduced it. Exaggeration is central to the humor: balls behave like animals, fielders are battered or sent staggering, crowds resemble buffalo or cannibals, and Merritt figuratively shrinks as his scheme rebounds upon him. Physical danger is presented as both genuine and comic, giving the game an “element of tragedy” without disturbing the story’s exuberant mood.

The diction combines standard narrative prose with early twentieth-century American baseball vernacular. The narration includes such period expressions as “twirler,” “grasscutter,” “safe hitting,” “in-shoots,” “stiffs,” “cinch the game,” “up in the air,” and “take your medicine.” Dialogue is heavily colloquial, using contractions, dropped consonants, phonetic spellings, and slang—“fellars,” “goin’,” “hev,” “enny,” and “Gimmee”—to distinguish the rough, informal culture of players, managers, and umpires. Merritt’s speech is especially brusque and money-minded, while the players’ exchanges are full of taunts, complaints, and clubhouse mockery. The locals receive less individualized dialogue and are characterized primarily through their physical prowess and rustic appearance, reflecting the professional players’ initially condescending perspective.

Sentence structure varies with narrative function. Expository passages use long, balanced sentences that explain baseball conditions, the properties of the trick ball, or the players’ reasoning. Action scenes shift toward shorter clauses, emphatic fragments, abrupt exclamations, and sound effects such as “Crack!” and “Bing!” Lists are frequently used for comic accumulation, as when every player invents an ailment to avoid the exhibition game or when the “rabbit” successively humiliates the fielders. Parenthetical qualifications and ironic asides allow the narrator to comment wryly on events without halting the action. Repetition—particularly escalating formulations such as “from indifference we passed to determination, and from that to sheer passionate purpose”—heightens momentum.

The pacing follows a clear comic escalation. A leisurely opening establishes the team’s resentment at losing its day off; the journey and arrival build curiosity; the early innings create frustration as the supposedly inferior Canadians repeatedly make spectacular plays. The introduction of the “rabbit” provides the central reversal. At first it seems to guarantee an easy professional victory, but the inability to remove it from play transfers its destructive force to the Rochester team. From that point, the narration accelerates into a succession of hits, rebounds, injuries, errors, and jeers. The climax comes when Merritt, architect of the scheme, is struck by the ball, after which the brief ending delivers both defeat and moral satisfaction.

Characterization is broad, economical, and caricatural. Players are identified by nicknames, ailments, physical traits, positions, or signature abilities: Deerfoot is the swift base runner, Gillinger the huge captain, Hathaway the aging finesse pitcher, and Merritt the irritable, avaricious manager. Rather than deep psychological portraits, the story offers a vivid ensemble whose members reveal themselves through baseball behavior. Their ethics are flexible—they willingly cheat—but their internal code values toughness, skill, loyalty, and the comic punishment of pretension. Merritt is not condemned abstractly; he is made to “take his medicine” physically and publicly.

Imagery is muscular, kinetic, and often drawn from animals, weapons, machinery, and frontier life. The pitcher’s ball resembles a pea from an air gun; hard hits crack like pistols; the rubber ball behaves like a squirrel or “jumping jackrabbit”; players are compared to cats, tigers, railsplitters, and charging buffalo. Verbs such as “slammed,” “scooted,” “caromed,” “lambast,” “pounced,” and “writhed” keep bodies and objects in vigorous motion. Similes are plentiful, concrete, and hyperbolic, making the action easy to visualize while amplifying its comic violence.

The story’s structure depends on irony and reversal. The professionals resent the exhibition, underestimate their opponents, and resort to deception when honest play fails. Their cheating produces a huge lead, but the same device then becomes uncontrollable and exposes them to the punishment they intended for others. The resulting defeat feels like victory to the narrator because team solidarity and revenge on Merritt matter more than the score or gate receipts. The closing emphasis on the game as the “most memorable” achievement of the “rabbit” preserves the tall-tale quality: the episode is framed less as a moral confession than as a legendary piece of baseball folklore.

Overall, the style is that of a period sports yarn: insider narration, colorful slang, quick dialogue, broad characterization, escalating physical comedy, and an anecdotal plot governed by comeuppance. Its chief effects come from energetic action, affectionate mockery of professional ballplayers, and the transformation of a technical baseball trick into an almost animate agent of chaos and justice.

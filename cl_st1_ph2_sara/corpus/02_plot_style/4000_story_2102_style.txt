The story adopts the manner of a nineteenth-century social-realist tale, combining domestic comedy, generational conflict, and understated moral satire. Its tone is initially genial and lightly amused, but becomes increasingly ironic as the merchant’s lofty praise of youth is tested by his son’s actual independence. The governing irony lies in the gulf between professed ideals and instinctive behaviour: public eloquence celebrates progress, while private authority demands deference. The final sentence—“And the teacher was right”—delivers the conclusion with epigrammatic bluntness, turning the student’s idealism into an instance of a recurring human pattern.

The narration is third-person and omniscient, moving freely among the merchant, his wife, the student, and the teacher. The narrator is conspicuously present as a knowing social observer, offering generalisations about smokers, teachers, youth, age, and human nature. Parenthetical qualifications such as “a rarer thing with old teachers than is generally supposed” create a conversational, essayistic voice. This narrator maintains affectionate distance from the characters: their emotions are treated as genuine, but their vanity, self-deception, and conventional behaviour are continually exposed. Brief anticipatory and retrospective comments give the impression that the episode is one version of a drama repeatedly enacted across generations.

The diction is formal, period-inflected, and occasionally archaic or continental in flavour. Expressions such as “disquieted,” “peroration,” “bid adieu,” “Fatherland,” “recherché,” and “rôles” contribute to an older translated-European register, while references to Rhine wine, Norse dining customs, adjuncts, and patriarchal family life establish a bourgeois Scandinavian setting. Social position is encoded in titles and occupations—the merchant, the judge, the solicitor, the Adjunct, the student—so that characters often function both as individuals and as representatives of institutions, generations, or classes.

Sentences are generally long, balanced, and syntactically elaborate, with semicolons, dashes, parenthetical asides, and accumulative lists. The prose often builds an argument through qualification and contrast: “not only … but also,” “not that … but that,” and “and yet” are characteristic patterns. Short sentences are reserved for comic or decisive emphasis, especially at transitions and at the ending. The merchant’s speech uses rhetorical parallelism, repetition, rising clauses, and abstract nouns to imitate ceremonial eloquence. By contrast, the quarrel proceeds through clipped exchanges, corrections, and escalating questions, allowing the rhythm to tighten as social embarrassment grows.

Dialogue is central to the story’s method of characterisation. The merchant’s stammering opening exposes his insecurity, while his later fluency reveals sincere paternal pride as well as susceptibility to festive rhetoric. His rapid shift from joviality to wounded authority occurs through changes in tone rather than extensive description. The student’s directness, repeated insistence that he was “right,” and impassioned vow convey intellectual confidence mixed with youthful naïveté. The teacher speaks in placid maxims and paternal formulas; his language converts a specific injustice into a universal law of human conduct. Minor voices—the anxious hostess, the snickering guests, and the lawyer with his proverbial joke—form a social chorus that reinforces hierarchy and pressures the son into submission.

The plot is tightly organised around reversal and repetition. It begins with the merchant preparing a speech, reaches an apparent harmony in his toast to “The Young,” and then immediately subjects the speech’s principles to a practical test. The movement from dining room to dessert table, smoking room, and garden also traces a shift from public ceremony to masculine debate and finally to private reflection. The toast acts as the structural hinge: its promise that children should “outgrow” their fathers is contradicted almost word for word when the son demonstrates superior knowledge. The teacher’s closing prediction extends the pattern beyond the immediate family, turning the quarrel into a cyclical account of generations.

Humour arises through incongruity, social observation, and dramatic irony rather than broad farce. Comic details—the merchant pouring Rhine wine into port, guests poised to drain their glasses at the first hint of a conclusion, ladies becoming “smoke-dried,” and unreliable claims presented as “historical facts”—soften the critique while sharpening its accuracy. The narrator repeatedly punctures solemnity with an aside or qualification. Even the student’s picturesque moment in the sunset is undercut by the observation that his transporting eloquence would have impressed anyone “but the teacher,” whose age has made him immune to it.

Characterisation is economical and socially grounded. The merchant is neither a simple hypocrite nor a villain: he believes his speech while delivering it, but cannot tolerate its implications when they threaten his dignity. The son is sympathetic, yet his moral fervour is gently mocked as conventional youthful idealism intensified by wine and agitation. The hostess serves as an anxious mediator attuned to her husband’s temper, while the teacher embodies experienced resignation. This refusal to idealise either generation gives the satire its complexity, although the ending ultimately endorses the teacher’s pessimistic view.

The dominant themes are generational recurrence, the conflict between theory and practice, paternal authority, social conformity, and the erosion of youthful idealism. Public language is shown to be aspirational but performative: people applaud liberal principles more readily than they enact them. Age claims wisdom, youth claims truth, and the surrounding society privileges decorum over factual correctness. Stylistically, the story turns these themes into a compact social parable, using an intrusive omniscient narrator, formal rhetoric, brisk dialogue, symmetrical reversals, and a dryly conclusive final line to suggest that rebellion against inherited attitudes often ends in their repetition.

The story has the character of a traditional children’s tale told by a sympathetic, unobtrusive third-person narrator. Its perspective remains close to Nadya and her family, moving freely between outward observation and simple accounts of what characters feel, imagine, or dream. The narrator’s language often reflects a child’s way of seeing: adults are identified by conspicuous traits, unfamiliar medical procedures become mysterious actions, and the elephant is described through homely comparisons such as pillars, shovels, a broom, and a snake. This child-centred viewpoint makes extraordinary events feel concrete and emotionally credible without requiring psychological complexity.

The prose is plain, lucid, and gently old-fashioned. Vocabulary is largely accessible, though period details and formal expressions—“drawing-room,” “nursery,” “menagerie,” “eye-glasses,” “perambulator”—locate the story in an affluent domestic world of an earlier era. The translated quality of the English is visible in occasional stiff formulations, formal modes of address, diminutives, patronymics, and Russian measurements. Sentences are generally straightforward but often accumulate actions or descriptive details in coordinated sequences. Repetition is prominent: Nadya “doesn’t want anything,” her father “smoke[s] ... and smoke[s] ... and smoke[s],” and the doctors and parents repeatedly try to stimulate her interest. These repetitions imitate speech and emphasize emotional states, particularly listlessness, anxiety, and determination.

Dialogue carries much of the narrative. It is direct, lightly theatrical, and strongly differentiated by role. Nadya speaks with childlike literalness, courtesy, and quiet persistence; her parents use affectionate diminutives and anxious persuasion; the German manager speaks through cautious practical questions; and the policeman’s absurdly calm remark provides comic relief. Ellipses frequently mark hesitation, fatigue, embarrassment, or suppressed emotion. The dialogue often advances the plot through negotiation: first the adults try to discover Nadya’s desire, then her father tries to persuade the manager, and finally Nadya establishes a playful relationship with Tommy.

The story is divided into six numbered sections, each functioning as a distinct stage in a simple restorative arc. The opening establishes the illness and the doctors’ prescription; the second section intensifies the family’s helplessness; the third introduces the impossible wish; the fourth turns that wish into a practical problem; the fifth narrates the elephant’s comic arrival; and the sixth expands into the long, joyful encounter and rapid recovery. Pacing therefore moves from slow, repetitive stagnation to purposeful action and then to exuberant abundance. The early scenes linger over stillness, smoke, tears, ceilings, and grey dreams, whereas the later scenes fill with food, toys, movement, conversation, laughter, and visitors.

Tone shifts carefully between melancholy, tenderness, domestic comedy, and gentle fantasy. The first half is emotionally restrained: the parents’ distress is shown through physical gestures rather than explicit declarations, especially the father’s trembling shoulders, furtive tears, pacing, and smoking. Once the elephant enters the story, the mood becomes playful and expansive. Humour arises from treating the fantastic situation as a logistical matter—police permission, stair width, ceiling height, flooring, payment, and the removal of door latches—as well as from the contrast between Tommy’s enormous body and his civilized conduct. The fantasy is never presented as magical; instead, its realism is strengthened through practical obstacles and precise physical details.

Description is selective but vivid. The sickroom is defined by emotional atmosphere rather than elaborate visual detail, while Tommy receives an extended, almost inventory-like portrait that reproduces Nadya’s fascinated scrutiny. Food imagery is especially frequent and sensuous: pistachio tart, rolls, vegetables, rum, cocoa, and walnut cake all contribute to the return of appetite and pleasure. The elephant’s movements are rendered through concrete verbs and comic personification. He nods, laughs, jokes, listens, examines pictures, dines formally, and dreams, yet he remains convincingly animal through his trunk, skin, weight, appetite, and physical power.

Characterization depends on repeated actions and affectionate contrasts. Nadya is passive during her illness but becomes animated, sociable, imaginative, and politely authoritative in Tommy’s presence. Her father is impulsive, irritable, desperate, and deeply loving; his willingness to solve an apparently absurd problem gives the plot its momentum. The mother is gentler and more conventionally nurturing, while the manager’s initial suspicion gives way to empathy when he learns that Nadya is the same age as his own daughter. Tommy is characterized as both immense and delicate: his size creates spectacle, but his careful handling of Nadya’s hand reveals kindness. This balance between strength and gentleness is central to his appeal.

Thematically, the story contrasts medical helplessness with the healing force of attention, wonder, play, and fulfilled desire. Nadya’s illness is framed less as a bodily disease than as “indifference to life,” and her recovery begins when something awakens genuine curiosity. Parental love is expressed through action rather than sentimentality, especially through the father’s determination to make the impossible possible. The tale also suggests that imaginative companionship can restore appetite, language, laughter, and social connection. Its ending is concise and circular: Nadya’s loud demand for milk reverses her earlier refusal of everything, while her sly message that she is already well confirms both physical recovery and the return of her lively personality.

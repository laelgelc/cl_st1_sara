While butterfly hunting with the narrator, veteran Private Mulvaney argues that inexperienced young soldiers are especially effective because they have not yet learned to fear danger. To prove it, he recounts a campaign in Burma, where he and twenty-four young troops under Lieutenant Brazenose were hunting dacoits.

After forcing a captive to reveal the location of their stronghold, Mulvaney persuades Brazenose to launch an immediate nighttime raid on Lungtungpen rather than wait for reinforcements. The soldiers trek through the jungle and discover a broad river in their path. They strip naked, float their weapons and equipment across on logs, and reach the far bank near the town. Before they can dress, the defenders open fire over their heads.

Brazenose orders an assault. Still naked, the soldiers break through the town’s defenses and charge in with bayonets and rifle butts. Their bizarre appearance and ferocity terrify the dacoits, who flee or are defeated. By morning the British have captured Lungtungpen, killed seventy-five dacoits, and seized elephants, firearms, blades, and other supplies without losing a man.

The victors then endure the embarrassment of forming ranks and patrolling unclothed until they recover clothing. The astonished headman wonders what English soldiers must be capable of when dressed. Mulvaney later tells Brazenose that, despite his courage, he relies too much on theory, but also declares that he and the men would follow him anywhere. He concludes that young, fearless troops under a good officer can accomplish feats seasoned soldiers would consider too dangerous.

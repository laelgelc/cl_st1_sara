The story adopts a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century mode of urban Gothic realism, combining detailed physical hardship, criminal intrigue, psychological horror, and a final supernatural or hallucinatory twist. Its atmosphere is relentlessly ominous: rain, cold, dim streets, basement kitchens, private saloon booths, absinthe, dice, and deathly pallor create a claustrophobic world in which ordinary city life borders on nightmare. Material realities—hunger, money, food, alcohol, and bodily collapse—are rendered concretely, while ambiguity surrounds the precise boundary between intoxication, delirium, and death.

The narration is third-person and largely aligned with Arthur Kimberlin’s perceptions. It moves freely between external observation and his immediate thoughts, often through free indirect discourse. At first the narrator describes Kimberlin with sober authority, explaining his social background and desperate condition; as hunger and absinthe distort his consciousness, the prose increasingly reproduces his manic reasoning. His extravagant fantasies of buying restaurants and ordering impossible quantities of food are presented from within his delirium, allowing the reader to experience his euphoria while recognizing its fatal unreality. The narrator occasionally steps back into a generalized, aphoristic register, as in reflections on blinking, honor, desperation, and hunger, giving the tale the voice of a confident moral storyteller.

The diction is formal, literary, and period-inflected. Expressions such as “thereon,” “embrasure,” “aught”-like constructions, “won’t you,” “I mean no offence,” and elaborate formulations about courage, decency, and honor place the narrative in an older genteel tradition. This elevated vocabulary contrasts with the blunt colloquial speech of the bartender and with the story’s sordid setting. Physical states are described through strongly charged adjectives—“death-like,” “ghastly,” “unearthly,” “viciously,” “deadly”—and the stranger is repeatedly defined by whiteness, hardness, fixity, and cold. Repetition reinforces the central sensations: Kimberlin is always hungry, the stranger always pale and staring, and the money always accumulating.

Sentence structure is often long, cumulative, and heavily coordinated, with semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, and chains of clauses used to build pressure. The opening paragraph is especially expansive, presenting cause after cause in an almost forensic account of Kimberlin’s decline. In moments of acute distress, the syntax contracts into parallel declarations—“He could not reason; he could not understand”—or into urgent questions and exclamations. During the final delirium, sentences again lengthen dramatically, carrying inventories of imagined dishes and spiralling arguments about the sublimity of starvation. This alternation between controlled exposition and feverish accumulation mirrors the protagonist’s movement from exhaustion to intoxicated mania.

The imagery is intensely sensory. Food is described through size, fatness, warmth, smell, color, and juiciness, making abundance almost physically painful in relation to Kimberlin’s starvation. Heat and cold form a recurring opposition: restaurant odors and alcohol promise warmth, while rain, pallor, the stranger’s hand, and his gaze convey lethal cold. Money is also sensualized; the banknote feels “warm and comfortable” and sends a tingling through Kimberlin’s arm, temporarily substituting for nourishment. Later, money and food merge in his imagination as symbols of limitless access, though neither can save him. The fixed gaze supplies the principal horror image, converting a seemingly watchful antagonist into a corpse whose eyes have merely remained open.

Characterization is economical and functional. Kimberlin is established through the conflict between physical desperation and gentlemanly scruple: he cannot beg, steal, or initially keep money that is not honorably his. The stranger is constructed less as a psychologically rounded person than as an uncanny assemblage of signs—shaved face, purple lip, veined hands, rigid expression, and absent-minded gesture toward a vanished beard. Dialogue preserves this contrast. Kimberlin speaks politely and compliantly, whereas the stranger’s elaborate courtesy has an alarming precision and hardness. His insistence on honesty, even while he is a master criminal and gambler, adds grim irony. The detectives’ overheard conversation functions as compressed exposition, identifying the stranger and explaining the money without interrupting the immediate scene with a full backstory.

Pacing is carefully graduated. The beginning lingers over starvation and the restaurant display, then the encounter initiates a repetitive cycle of drinks, dice throws, rising stakes, and increasingly abnormal behavior. Each repetition intensifies the situation: more absinthe is consumed, more money appears, the stakes rise, and the stranger becomes less animate. Suspense depends less on overt action than on delay, especially the final throw and the prolonged mutual stare. The apparently liberating street sequence then accelerates into exuberant movement and verbal excess before breaking off abruptly with a dash and typographical divider. The concluding return to the detectives resets the viewpoint and retrospectively overturns the preceding episode.

Structurally, the tale is built around misdirection and delayed recognition. Readers are encouraged to suspect that the stranger is monstrous, predatory, or supernaturally powerful, but the immediate revelation is that he has died during the game. A second deception follows: Kimberlin seems to escape with the money, yet the final tableau shows that he too never left the booth. His triumphant walk through San Francisco was therefore a death vision or terminal hallucination. The mirrored description of the two men staring across the table transforms earlier terror into macabre symmetry. The ending is concise and visual, resembling a frozen stage picture in which the money, bottle, glasses, dice, and bodies serve as the evidence from which the truth must be reconstructed.

Irony governs the story’s moral and emotional effect. Kimberlin wins a fortune but cannot obtain a meal; the unbeatable gambler wins the decisive throw but dies before taking the money; the young man’s recovered strength is actually the onset of death; and his fantasy of feeding all hungry people arises only when he is beyond rescue. His scrupulous honesty delays the theft that might have saved him, while his later abandonment of those scruples comes too late. The hyperbolic banquet list turns hunger into grotesque comedy, but the comedy remains inseparable from pathos. The story’s final effect is thus both sensational and fatalistic: wealth, luck, and apparent freedom arrive at the exact moment when they have become useless.

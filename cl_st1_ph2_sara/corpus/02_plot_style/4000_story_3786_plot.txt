A narrator who loves French Canada rejects accusations that Quebec is cowardly or disloyal during the First World War and recalls several encounters that reveal the character of its people.

In 1894, guide Jean Morel tells him that if France and Britain went to war, his body would march under Britain’s flag while his heart marched under France’s. Years later, when the two nations fight together against Germany, Jean’s divided loyalty seems reconciled.

In a 1917 Oxford hospital, the narrator meets a badly wounded French-Canadian lumberjack who enlisted because Britain was defending France. Though his injured arm will prevent him from returning to woodcutting, the young man remains glad that he served and considers becoming a camp cook.

Later that year, on Quebec’s Sainte Marguerite River, the narrator questions his guide Iside about not joining the war. Iside says his priest taught that men should fight only when their own homes and churches are attacked. The narrator explains that defeating Germany abroad is necessary to preserve Canada’s liberty at home. Iside begins to understand and promises to share this broader view with his priest and community.

Finally, reports of an anti-conscription riot in Quebec City seem to confirm doubts about French-Canadian loyalty. The narrator argues, however, that the disturbance was stirred up by opportunistic politicians and hired troublemakers, not by ordinary French-Canadians. The riot quickly collapses, the draft proceeds, volunteers enlist, separatist proposals are rejected, and church leaders increasingly present the war as a Christian struggle against tyranny. The narrator concludes that Quebec’s rural people are fundamentally loyal and courageous; once properly informed about what is at stake, they will defend freedom alongside the other peoples of Canada.

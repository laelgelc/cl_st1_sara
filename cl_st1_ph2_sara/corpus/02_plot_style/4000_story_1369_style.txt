The story adopts a genial, mock-serious comic style, narrated retrospectively in the first person by an educated, self-conscious observer. The narrator presents ordinary neighborhood incidents with the gravity of history, philosophy, or law, creating a persistent disparity between subject and treatment. A mischievous child, a missing bunch of bananas, and an untidy courtyard are described in elevated terms such as “strange cognomen,” “animo furandi,” and “utter and complete extermination.” This inflated register is the principal source of humor. The narrator is observant but not wholly reliable: he embellishes, speculates freely, and repeatedly converts limited evidence into elaborate theories. His amused awareness of his own idleness and pretension makes him a target of the comedy as much as Melons is.

The diction mixes formal, Latinate, and literary language with colloquial speech. Biblical and literary echoes—“through a glass darkly,” “by merit raised to that bad eminence,” and the allusion to the Ancient Mariner—lend mock grandeur to the setting and events. Euphemistic circumlocutions are frequent: procrastination becomes a “methodical system,” hunger is “a more practical void,” and theft becomes an offense discussed under the statutes of California. Against this polished narrative voice, Melons speaks in compressed vernacular forms such as “They is a cirkis,” “wot,” “hosses,” and “to onct.” The contrast distinguishes social position and education while also dramatizing the absurd mismatch between the adult narrator’s forensic rhetoric and the child’s evasive inventions.

The syntax is often long, balanced, and elaborately subordinated. Sentences accumulate qualifications, parenthetical comments, hypothetical explanations, and ironic reversals before reaching their point. This delayed delivery enables comic anticlimax: the reader is led through solemn reasoning only to arrive at a trivial or ridiculous conclusion. Short sentences and fragments are then used selectively for emphasis, especially during the banana episode and the confrontation. Repetition also supplies rhythm and comic pressure, as in the succession of banana-eating boys, the repeated accusation “You took those bananas,” and the ceremonious recurrence of Melons’s name.

The humor is dry, urbane, and largely deadpan. It depends on understatement, hyperbole, incongruity, parody, and personification rather than on overt jokes. McGinnis’s Court “refused to conform to circumstances”; Melons’s white head represents “aged iniquity”; and his oversized trousers become both a visual motif and an impossible hiding place. Social satire runs beneath the farce. The “aristocratic” neighboring families attempt to prohibit contact with the poor child, only to make him more fascinating to their children. Respectability, parental authority, literary ambition, property ownership, and legalistic reasoning are all treated with gentle skepticism. The poor courtyard is depicted as unruly and shabby, yet also more energetic and imaginative than the fashionable streets surrounding it.

Characterization is caricatural but affectionate. Melons is built from a few memorable external traits: his pale, prematurely venerable head, enormous borrowed clothing, acrobatic mobility, solitude, and serene amorality. He is less a psychologically explained child than a comic force who resists adult control and social boundaries. His motives remain opaque, preserving his fascination. The narrator is characterized indirectly through digressions and admissions, especially the extended description of his supposed literary work, which reveals a habit of procrastination disguised as intellectual method. Their relationship rests on a tacit kinship between two idlers and outsiders, expressed in the narrator’s phrase “a vagabond instinct we felt in common.”

The structure is anecdotal and digressive. It begins with speculative discussion of the nickname, widens into a portrait of the court, catalogs Melons’s habits and exploits, and only gradually reaches the central banana theft. The narrator explicitly calls much of the opening “purely introductory,” drawing attention to the leisurely construction. Episodes involving the blind harper, Tommy’s ascent to the roof, literary procrastination, and the stolen fruit function as escalating demonstrations of Melons’s character. The disappearance after the narrator’s pompous legal speech supplies a mock-tragic climax. The ending then enlarges a minor loss into mystery, remorse, mortality, and metaphysical doubt, while finally suggesting that Melons has conveniently “filled the void” in the narrator’s literary project.

Description is highly visual and often grotesquely precise. Physical details are selected for comic suggestiveness: the transparent chicken-like down on Melons’s scalp, the single suspender supporting enormous trousers, the dusty fish-geranium, and bare feet quivering above a fence. Setting is not passive background but an active social organism. Windows, fences, roofs, clotheslines, backyards, and outhouses create a vertical stage on which Melons climbs above ordinary rules. The narrator’s low window serves as both viewing frame and point of contact, giving the story the quality of a neighborhood sketch observed from partial seclusion.

The emotional tone combines amusement, fondness, irony, and mock melancholy. Although the narrator calls Melons a hypocrite and thief, the severity is theatrical, and the closing regret reveals genuine attachment. Sentiment is continually checked by comic alternatives: Melons may have gone to sea, died of old age, buried himself in his trousers, or never existed. This refusal to settle into uncomplicated pathos preserves the sketch’s lightness. Overall, the style is that of a nineteenth-century humorous character sketch: expansive, allusive, theatrically formal, socially observant, and sustained by the comic elevation of small events into matters of epic, legal, and philosophical importance.

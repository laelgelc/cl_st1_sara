Two longtime friends reunite in Rome after a year apart. One has returned for her elderly father’s funeral while confronting her husband’s infidelity and the collapse of her marriage; the other, a professor of ancient Roman history, is back for a sabbatical and feels deeply attached to the city.

They have lunch at a secluded trattoria tied to the grieving woman’s childhood and memories of her father. As they discuss bereavement, family, and separation, the professor—who has darker skin and hair—grows uneasy when the owner calls her “la moretta” and treats her differently from her friend. Her discomfort intensifies when a little girl repeatedly blocks her path, declares that the grieving woman is “nice,” and calls the professor “bad.” The adults make no meaningful effort to correct the child or apologize.

Outside, the friends discuss the incident, but the grieving woman dismisses it as mere rudeness. They part with unusual heaviness. The professor sits alone, humiliated and overwhelmed by sadness, while her friend sits elsewhere sorting through condolence messages addressed to her dead father, each woman absorbed in a different form of grief.

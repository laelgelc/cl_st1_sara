The story uses a brisk, omniscient third-person narration that stays close to Mr. Coombes’s aggrieved consciousness while retaining enough distance to expose his vanity, timidity, and self-deception. The narrator is conspicuously present, addressing the reader directly, supplying generalisations about marriage and class, withholding “unedifying” details, and making mock-documentary qualifications such as “it would appear” and “it is beyond dispute.” This creates the air of a sober case history while continually undermining that sobriety through irony.

The dominant tone is comic, satirical, and faintly macabre. Domestic frustration, suicidal fantasy, poisoning, madness, and violence are treated with amused detachment rather than tragic solemnity. Much of the humour comes from disproportion: Coombes interprets late dinner, Sunday music, unsuitable clothes, and noisy guests as signs of social ruin, while the narrative describes his grotesque intoxication in ceremonious language. Elevated phrases such as “new convert to joie de vivre” and “decisive action” are juxtaposed with slapstick pursuits, broken crockery, stout, fungi, and a blacking brush. Euphemistic narrative omissions—especially the veil drawn over the evening’s final incidents—further strengthen the deadpan comedy.

The diction mixes formal literary narration with colloquial lower-middle-class speech. The prose includes precise, sometimes technical vocabulary—“pileus,” “agaric,” “respiration,” “physiology”—alongside commercial and domestic language involving stock, capital, housekeeping money, customers, and respectability. Dialogue is rendered phonetically through dropped initial consonants and compressed forms such as “’ave,” “’im,” “arst,” and “’njoyment.” These speech patterns establish class position, but they also individualise the characters: Coombes’s clipped moralising expresses anxious respectability, Mrs. Coombes and Jennie speak with combative fluency, and Clarence adopts the complacent formulas of a fashionable young man. When the fungus takes effect, Coombes’s progressively slurred language gives the altered state an immediate physical presence.

Sentences often run long, accumulating clauses, qualifications, lists, and parenthetical asides. This expansive syntax is particularly effective when reproducing Coombes’s spiralling grievances or cataloguing the humiliations of domestic life. Lists escalate comically, as with the imagined weapons and letters surrounding his suicidal thoughts, or the relatives who “eat up capital, insult him personally, upset business arrangements, annoy good customers, and generally blight his life.” Short sentences and fragments then punctuate moments of confrontation: “He had to speak,” “Deadly poisons!” and “Turn, turn, turn.” Repetition functions both rhythmically and psychologically, embodying obsession, provocation, and impaired consciousness.

Characterisation depends on selective physical detail, social behaviour, and ironic contrast. Coombes is introduced through shabby aspirations to gentility: the military-looking collar, astrachan-trimmed overcoat, bright gloves, silk hat, and Sunday frock-coat. Clothing repeatedly registers his status and transformation. Before eating the fungus he is constricted by his collar and social anxieties; afterwards the collar is torn away, the coat turned inside out, and the waistcoat decorated with furze. Five years later his improved coat, softer collar, and glossy hat outwardly confirm prosperity and restored self-esteem. The other characters are drawn more broadly, almost as comic social types, but the shift to their conversation after Coombes leaves gives their complaints enough substance to prevent his account from being wholly authoritative.

The setting combines mundane suburban realism with an uncanny natural agent. The shop-home, piano, arm-chair, tea-table, passage, kitchen, and scullery create a cramped domestic arena in which business, marriage, leisure, and status collide. Against this stands the damp October pine wood, richly crowded with colourful, evil-smelling fungi. The fungi are described sensuously through colour, texture, smell, taste, and chemical change, making them at once repellent and inviting. Nature does not carry an overt supernatural meaning, yet the purple fungus functions like a mischievous instrument of fate, temporarily overturning personality and social hierarchy.

Structurally, the story begins at a point of emotional crisis, then retreats into a detailed reconstruction of the quarrel. It moves from domestic realism into woodland temptation, intoxication, grotesque farce, and finally a five-year epilogue. The central transformation is swift: the repressed, ineffectual shopkeeper becomes expansively cheerful and then violently authoritative. The later time jump reveals the lasting social consequences while also rewriting the event through Coombes’s memory. He suppresses the fungus entirely and converts accidental madness into a deliberate display of masculine resolve. This retrospective distortion supplies the final irony: his prosperity rests on an episode he fundamentally misunderstands.

The story’s principal concerns are class insecurity, economic pressure, marital power, masculine humiliation, and the unstable boundary between respectability and violence. It satirises Coombes’s creed of self-help and his conviction that commercial survival justifies domestic authority, but it also recognises the material trap in which a small shopkeeper is caught. Marriage is represented less as romance than as an economic partnership distorted by incompatible expectations. The ending deliberately adopts the formula of marital success—“happy ever after”—only to expose the fear and coercion beneath it. The final exchange about fungi completes the pattern of dramatic irony: Coombes piously assumes they serve some “wise purpose,” unaware that the fungus has furnished him with the violent authority on which his improved life depends.

Overall, the style combines social realism, intrusive comic narration, class-marked dialogue, grotesque physical comedy, and speculative transformation. Its characteristic effect comes from narrating absurd and disturbing events in a polished, reasonable, almost judicial manner, allowing the discrepancy between what characters believe and what the reader sees to generate both humour and criticism.

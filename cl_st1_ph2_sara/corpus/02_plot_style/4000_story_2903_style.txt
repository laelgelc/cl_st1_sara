The piece adopts the style of a nineteenth-century literary-critical essay rather than that of a conventional short story. It is written in a cultivated, authoritative first-person voice that combines aesthetic judgment, comparative criticism, historical commentary, and occasional personal confession. The narrator repeatedly qualifies his authority with phrases such as “as far as I can judge,” “in my opinion,” and “I should be inclined to believe,” yet these gestures of modesty coexist with firm, sometimes sweeping verdicts. The resulting persona is erudite, urbane, opinionated, and conversational: a critic addressing educated general readers rather than specialists.

The tone is predominantly analytical and evaluative, with a persistent undercurrent of irony. Praise is usually balanced by reservation, and admiration quickly turns into diagnosis of a defect. Gogol is described as observant, lively, and exact, but also as careless in construction, excessively satirical, and too fond of detail. This antithetical movement gives the prose its characteristic rhythm: strengths and weaknesses are paired, then weighed against one another. The critic’s own wit frequently mirrors the ironic quality he attributes to Gogol, especially in remarks about bandits, madmen, dogs, and melodramatic effects. The humour is dry and cultivated, often taking the form of comic exaggeration or a deliberately provocative judgment.

The diction is formal, Latinate, and historically inflected. Words and phrases such as “dénouement,” “perusal,” “superfluous,” “predilection,” “melancholy incidents,” and “medico-legal study” establish an elevated register. Foreign terms, accented names, and cultural references are used without extensive explanation, presuming a well-read audience. The vocabulary of criticism is especially prominent: “realist,” “satire,” “grotesqueness,” “probability,” “construction,” “naturalness,” “general effect,” and “fantastic tale.” At the same time, the prose is enlivened by concrete, colloquial images—a missing front tooth, a broken poodle’s paw, a loaded gun, a storm shaking the casements—which prevent the discussion from becoming wholly abstract.

Sentences are generally long, syntactically elaborate, and carefully balanced. They make frequent use of semicolons, dashes, parenthetical qualifications, parallel clauses, and concessive structures beginning with “but,” “although,” “in spite of,” or “on the other hand.” Many sentences unfold as miniature arguments, moving from proposition to qualification and then to conclusion. Shorter declarative sentences are occasionally inserted for emphasis, as in the judgment that Gogol “does not see men or things in a bright light.” The syntax reflects a mind reasoning in public, refining claims as it proceeds.

The essay is structured associatively rather than according to a rigid thesis-and-proof design. It begins with a general assessment of Gogol, moves through his satire, realism, descriptive method, and use of language, then turns to individual works and genres. Digressions on Rabelais, Balzac, Aristophanes, Pushkin, Walter Scott, Homer, filibusters, and contemporary bandits broaden the discussion. These excursions are not merely ornamental: they locate Gogol within a wide European literary and historical tradition. Nevertheless, they create a leisurely, desultory progression that resembles the loose narrative practice the critic identifies in Gogol himself.

Comparison and analogy are central stylistic devices. Gogol is placed beside English humorists, Teniers, Callot, Rabelais, Balzac, Sterne, Scott, Chamisso, Hoffmann, and others. Abstract critical ideas are repeatedly clarified through visual or physical analogies. Reading Rabelais is compared to examining animalcules under a microscope; the Russian language is likened to a fine pencil in the hand of a draughtsman; satire becomes a weapon whose severity and sharpness may be ill suited to its target; the satirist pursues human types as a hunter pursues game. Such comparisons make the criticism vivid and give it the quality of imaginative prose.

The dominant rhetorical method is the qualified generalization. The critic advances broad claims about satirists, national character, provincial life, fantastic fiction, madness, and sentimental devices, then supports them with compact examples. Aphoristic formulations frequently emerge from this process, such as the contention that selecting among nature’s details is harder than observing and recording them. The essay also uses rhetorical paradox: Gogol’s descriptions are truthful while his plots lack probability; his irony is too harsh for folly but too weak for crime; elaborate execution is both a gift and a potential source of monotony. These paradoxes sharpen the evaluations and make them memorable.

The treatment of quoted works combines synopsis with judgment rather than close textual analysis. Plots and historical backgrounds are summarized briskly, after which the critic assesses their plausibility, emotional effect, generic suitability, or tonal coherence. “Taras Bulba” is discussed through the mismatch between grotesque characterization and melodramatic setting; “Viy” is praised because realism allows the marvellous to enter imperceptibly; “Memoirs of a Madman” is rejected partly on moral and emotional grounds; and “An Old-time Household” is celebrated for simplicity, truth, and the harmonious contribution of every detail. This reveals a critical preference for naturalness, proportion, tonal fitness, structural unity, and emotional effects that appear earned rather than mechanically induced.

Although the voice is intellectual, it is also openly subjective. Personal reactions—liking bandits, disliking stories about madness, imagining the ideal conditions for reading a ghost story—are treated as legitimate critical evidence. The narrator occasionally stages himself as a character, ashamed of his admiration for outlaw energy or humorously reluctant to cross dark corridors after reading a supernatural tale. These moments soften the didactic authority and create intimacy with the reader.

The pacing is moderate and discursive. Individual paragraphs often begin with a broad claim, expand through examples and comparisons, and close with a concise judgment. Transitions such as “I have said,” “for my own part,” “in short,” “moreover,” and “I hasten to go on” make the progress of the argument explicit. The final praise of “An Old-time Household” provides a warm emotional resolution after the essay’s many reservations, while the translator’s note abruptly marks the extract as incomplete and gives the ending a documentary rather than artistic character.

Overall, the style is learned, cosmopolitan, ironic, and judicial. It blends formal criticism with memoir-like asides, vivid analogy, historical digression, and conversational wit. Its most distinctive features are balanced praise and censure, long periodic sentences, abundant literary allusion, confident aesthetic generalization, and a critic-persona whose personal tastes are inseparable from his judgments.

The piece adopts the form of a humorous critical essay that gradually becomes a performance manual and anthology of examples. Its governing mode is mock-serious exposition: the narrator offers confident distinctions among the American “humorous,” English “comic,” and French “witty” story, presenting subjective judgments with the composure of settled aesthetic theory. This authoritative posture is repeatedly undercut by exaggeration, playful contempt, and self-aware irony. The opening disclaimer—claiming not to be able to tell a story properly, but to know how it should be told—immediately establishes a persona that is at once modest, expert, and mischievously paradoxical.

The narrative voice is conversational, direct, and strongly performative. It addresses the reader as a listener through phrases such as “understand,” “Let me set down,” “You can practice,” and “mind you,” reproducing the intimacy of oral instruction. The narrator moves freely between critic, raconteur, stage director, and entertainer. His judgments are delivered deadpan, even when extravagantly biased: comic narration is said to require no art, its emphatic delivery is called “pathetic,” and its typographical insistence supposedly makes one “want to renounce joking and lead a better life.” The comedy often comes from this mismatch between the measured tone and the absurd severity of the verdict.

Structurally, the piece proceeds by contrast, illustration, and demonstration. It first establishes a series of binary distinctions: manner versus matter, rambling length versus brevity, gentle bubbling versus bursting, concealed amusement versus advertised laughter, and the understated “nub” versus the shouted punch line. It then tests these claims through anecdotes and embedded performances. The wounded-soldier story is first presented in an overexplicit comic form, complete with capitalization and excessive exclamation marks, then described as transformed by a master performer into a long, confused recital. The Artemus Ward example isolates incongruity and the strategic pause, while “The Golden Arm” becomes a full performance script. The essay therefore enacts its argument rather than merely stating it, expanding from abstract taxonomy into increasingly theatrical examples.

Pacing is central to the style. The prose alternates between compact aphoristic formulations—“The humorous story bubbles gently along, the others burst”—and long accumulative sentences that imitate the rambling method being praised. Lists of participial phrases reproduce the old farmer’s digressive storytelling: he forgets, corrects, explains, backtracks, inserts irrelevant details, and pauses to recover information that proves unnecessary. Repetition such as “and so on, and so on, and so on” gives the reader an audible sense of duration. In the ghost story, pacing becomes suspenseful and incremental, with repeated approaches, pauses, and increasingly close sounds carrying the action from grave to house, upstairs, into the room, beside the bed, and finally to the listener.

The syntax is flexible and speech-oriented. Short declarative sentences provide emphasis, while semicolon-linked clauses and extended cumulative constructions create controlled discursiveness. Parentheses and dashes frequently interrupt the flow with qualifications, asides, corrections, and stage business. These interruptions are not incidental: they imitate spontaneous speech while remaining carefully engineered. Parallel phrasing sharpens conceptual contrasts, and periodic sentences often postpone the decisive phrase. The prose consequently demonstrates the principle it identifies—the importance of withholding, delay, and apparently casual placement.

Diction mixes elevated critical language with colloquial American speech. Terms such as “strictly a work of art,” “perfectly simulated,” and “incongruities and absurdities” coexist with “nub,” “snapper,” “booby,” “whopping exclamation-points,” and “horse-laughter.” This register-switching punctures pretension and keeps the essay accessible. Hyperbolic descriptions—audiences exhausted by laughter, bodies quaking “in a jelly-like way,” or listeners springing out of their shoes—give physical force to comic effects. Vivid verbs such as “spun out,” “bubbles,” “burst,” “slur,” “shouts,” and “explode” turn abstract theories of humor into sensory actions.

Typography is used as a representation of performance. Italics, capitals, quotation marks, repeated punctuation, stretched-out words, and phonetic spellings indicate stress, volume, rhythm, and vocal duration. The grotesquely capitalized punch line in the wounded-soldier anecdote parodies the comic teller’s anxiety that the audience might miss the joke. In “The Golden Arm,” spaced and hyphenated utterances slow the ghost’s voice, while capitals mark sudden intensification. Parenthetical instructions prescribe pauses, facial expressions, listening attitudes, shivering, changes in vocal quality, and the selection of an audience member. The printed page thus attempts to preserve features normally belonging to live delivery.

Dialogue is used less for psychological realism than for timing, characterization, and comic collision. Speakers are rapidly distinguished by idiom and attitude: the officer is impatient, the soldier literal-minded, the farmer confused, and the ghost accusingly repetitive. The longest embedded story employs a heavily stylized historical African American vernacular rendered through eye dialect, altered grammar, and phonetic spelling. This creates a strong auditory impression and supports the oral-performance frame, although it also reflects the racial caricature and minstrel-influenced conventions of its period. The dialect should therefore be understood as both a rhythmic device and a historically situated, stereotyped representation.

The defining comic devices are deadpan delivery, incongruity, digression, repetition, anticlimax, exaggeration, and strategic indirection. Humor is often placed in subordinate or apparently unnecessary details rather than announced directly. The Ward anecdote exemplifies this technique: the toothless man appears to promise a remarkable consequence, but the delayed revelation that he could play the drum is wholly unrelated. The joke depends upon violated expectation and the speaker’s apparent failure to notice the irrelevance. Similar effects arise from narrators who misunderstand their own stories, overexplain simple points, or treat trivial matters with solemn care.

A notable meta-humorous quality runs throughout. The essay explains comic mechanisms while using them, mocks bad performance through an intentionally bad performance, and praises wandering narration by wandering productively. Even the claim that the pause must be “exactly the right length” is reiterated until the discussion itself becomes a prolonged exercise in timing. The reader is made conscious not only of what is funny but of how laughter is manufactured through control of attention, anticipation, and release.

Overall, the style is best characterized as colloquial literary satire shaped by oral storytelling. It combines a genial but opinionated first-person persona, crisp comparative claims, deliberately rambling demonstrations, theatrical notation, and concealed punch lines. Beneath its national stereotypes and comic exaggerations lies a serious aesthetic principle: effective humor depends less on the bare content of an anecdote than on persona, rhythm, apparent innocence, and the precise management of an audience’s expectations.

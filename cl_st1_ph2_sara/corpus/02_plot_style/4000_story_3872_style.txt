The story adopts the form of a club-room anecdote framed by a first-person witness. Its narrator is educated, sociable and initially sceptical, presenting events retrospectively while restricting his claims to what he personally saw and heard. This limited viewpoint gives the supernatural material an air of testimony without confirming its cause. The opening establishes a comfortable masculine setting—firelight, pipes, cigars, golf and dinner—whose material solidity contrasts with the ghostly tale. Because Clayton is introduced as an accomplished liar and performer, the narrative makes credibility its central problem from the outset.

The prose combines polished literary narration with colloquial Edwardian speech. Descriptive passages use precise, sometimes ornate phrasing and carefully chosen physical details, while the dialogue is full of conversational markers such as “you know,” “I say,” “Well,” and “Good Lord!” Period vocabulary and idiom—“rum,” “funked,” “lark,” “poor devil,” “jolly awkward”—create an informal upper-middle-class English register. Social distinctions are conveyed economically through accents, manners, club membership, dress and occupation. Clayton’s authoritative ease contrasts with the ghost’s deferential “sir,” weak qualifications and nervous repetitions.

Syntax varies according to dramatic function. The framing narration tends toward balanced, expansive sentences containing qualifications, parenthetical remarks and ironic asides. Clayton’s embedded account is looser and more oral, with dashes, interruptions, repetitions and self-corrections that imitate spontaneous storytelling. At moments of tension, the prose contracts into fragments and emphatic repetitions: “He didn’t! He wasn’t!” and “It changed. It changed.” Ellipses, broken sentences and abrupt paragraphing create hesitation, suspense and the impression that characters are struggling to articulate an uncanny experience. Capitalised or italic-like verbal emphasis and frequent exclamation marks reproduce the storyteller’s performance.

Dialogue dominates the story and performs several functions at once. The listeners’ questions break the long account into manageable stages, test its logic, supply comic objections and gradually chart the group’s movement from amusement to unease. Individual voices are lightly differentiated: Sanderson is technical and analytical, Wish intuitive and apprehensive, Evans brashly rationalistic, and the narrator sceptical but increasingly susceptible. Their interruptions create the atmosphere of an actual conversation rather than a formally composed report. Repeated challenges—whether the ghost was genuine, whether the gestures could work, whether Clayton should perform them—also dramatise the reader’s own likely objections.

The tonal method depends on a sustained blend of comedy and dread. The ghost is introduced not as a majestic or horrifying apparition but as a shabby, incompetent young man who cannot even vanish properly. His feeble “Boo,” baggy trousers, bad ears and bureaucratic exclusion from a private club parody conventional Gothic haunting. Clayton treats the supernatural as a breach of club etiquette, and the comic incongruity demystifies the ghost while making him socially and psychologically recognisable. Yet the ridicule is tempered by pity: the apparition’s failures in death merely continue his failures in life. This mixture of satire, cruelty and sympathy gives the tale more emotional weight than a simple comic ghost story.

Characterisation is largely indirect and theatrical. Clayton is revealed through his timing, pauses, cigar handling, mock authority and command of an audience. The ghost is constructed through physiognomic detail, clothing, posture and speech, all of which signify weakness before his history is explained. Recurrent physical gestures—lighting cigars, drawing on pipes, looking into the fire, shifting in chairs—anchor the conversation in observable reality. They also regulate tempo, allowing pauses before revelations. The final hand movements transform gesture from social performance into possible ritual, so that storytelling, acting and supernatural action become dangerously indistinguishable.

The narrative structure is carefully recursive. A convivial frame contains Clayton’s account; within that account, the ghost demonstrates the passes to Clayton; Clayton then reproduces them before his friends, and Sanderson supplies the missing detail. Each repetition moves the gestures closer to completeness and raises the stakes. Suspense develops incrementally rather than through sudden shocks: sceptical laughter gives way to curiosity, curiosity to technical corroboration, and corroboration to collective tension. Wish’s fear is especially important because it changes the emotional temperature before anything outwardly supernatural occurs.

Imagery is concrete and atmospheric but not excessively lush. Oak panelling, shadowed passages, candles, mirrors, brass fittings and the waning fire draw on Gothic conventions, while domestic details such as chintz, slippers and a landing clock keep the setting ordinary. Light imagery is particularly significant. The ghost is translucent; candles flare into “astonished” flames; Clayton’s face finally changes like a lit house whose lights have gone out. Transparency initially becomes a comic metaphor for truthfulness, but extinguished light becomes an image of death. The mirror adds another layer of mediation, allowing Clayton to observe the ghost indirectly and reinforcing the story’s concern with uncertain perception.

The humour is dry, understated and often aphoristic. Statements such as a transparent ghost being unable to avoid telling the truth are delivered with “inimitable assurance,” and implausible events are described in matter-of-fact terms. Euphemism and decorum heighten the irony: haunting is discussed as though it were an issue of membership, manners or professional competence. The story also satirises masculine confidence, rationalism and amateur expertise. The men believe themselves secure in a world of clubs, medicine, Masonry and common sense, yet their certainty is progressively undermined by the very performance they encourage.

The ending preserves ambiguity rather than resolving it. Clayton does not literally disappear; he dies in a medically plausible way at the exact instant when the ritual is completed. The narrator explicitly presents both supernatural and rational explanations, then refuses to judge between them. This restraint is crucial to the style: the uncanny emerges not from proof but from coincidence, timing and unresolved causation. The coroner’s verdict supplies an official explanation, while the narrator’s memory retains the event as “black and amazing.” The final exclamation converts an apparently objective account into a lingering emotional shock.

Overall, the style is that of a sophisticated comic-Gothic tale: conversational, ironic and socially observant, yet capable of tightening into genuine horror. Its characteristic technique is to place an absurd supernatural premise inside a highly credible social performance, use sceptical dialogue to authenticate rather than dismiss it, and end at the boundary between theatrical illusion, psychological suggestion and inexplicable death.

The story adopts the manner of an early children’s fairy tale: whimsical, briskly adventurous, morally legible, and touched by matter-of-fact darkness. Its central conceit is sustained anthropomorphism. Eggs speak, walk, hold hands, form ambitions, and fear injury, while still remaining physically vulnerable eggs that may be crushed, cooked, or become chicks. Much of the humour arises from this double status, especially in literal-minded jokes about being “bad eggs,” remaining “fresh,” and possibly becoming “omelets or angel’s-food.”

The narration is third-person omniscient and confidently authorial. It recounts events in a clear chronological sequence, occasionally explaining motives or passing judgement with phrases such as “as was but natural.” The narrator maintains emotional distance even during violent incidents. Deaths are described vividly—Coutchie-Coulou’s “golden heart” spreads over the gravel, while Humpty lies “crushed and mangled”—but the narrative quickly resumes its forward movement. This creates a characteristic blend of sentiment, comedy, and nursery-tale brutality.

The prose is accessible but carries an old-fashioned literary flavour. Formal constructions such as “enquired,” “pray show me,” “in this wise,” “befall me,” and “his Majesty” coexist with plain storytelling language. Contractions are printed in the period style with separated apostrophes, as in “I ’m,” “does n’t,” and “she ’s.” Descriptions favour bright, ornamental imagery: silvery brooks, waving grain, golden turrets, diamond-like windows, gorgeous rooms, ivory thrones, and richly dressed courtiers. Adjectives are plentiful but conventional, making scenes immediately picturesque rather than psychologically complex.

Sentence structure alternates between flowing descriptive sentences and short, functional dialogue. Many narrative sentences are linked by “and,” “but,” “so,” or “then,” giving the tale an oral, cumulative rhythm. Transitional formulas—“When she had gone,” “They were now,” “Now it happened,” and “And thus”—guide the reader plainly from episode to episode. Repetition reinforces the storybook cadence, particularly in exchanges of question and answer and in recurring reminders that the travellers must avoid breaking their shells.

Dialogue is frequent and carries much of the characterisation. Humpty speaks with pride, curiosity, confidence, and comic pomposity; Coutchie-Coulou is timid and compliant; the Princess is kindly but unsentimental; and the King is ceremonious, shrewd, and theatrical. Speech tags are simple and unobtrusive, while exclamations, direct questions, commands, and formal addresses keep scenes lively. Characters are defined through a few dominant traits rather than developed interior lives.

Structurally, the story is episodic and strongly causal. It begins with the hidden nest, moves through Humpty’s accidental expulsion and voluntary quest, introduces a companion whose death demonstrates the world’s danger, and culminates in the familiar fall from the wall. A second, courtly plot then converts Humpty’s fate into a riddle that resolves the Princess’s marriage dilemma. This final section gives retrospective purpose to the adventure and reframes the nursery rhyme as an origin story. The ending is explicitly providential: Humpty’s death allows him to repay the Princess’s kindness.

The tone continually balances innocence with irony. Beautiful settings and childlike wonder sit beside blunt reminders of mortality, while grief is softened by jokes and rapid consolation. The tale does not dwell on moral instruction, but it implies lessons about pride, recklessness, curiosity, kindness, and reciprocal reward. Overall, the style is imaginative, decorative, lightly satirical, and narratively economical, using nursery-rhyme familiarity, fairy-tale conventions, comic literalism, and cheerful fatalism to create a playful yet faintly macabre children’s fantasy.

The story uses a brisk, urbane late-Victorian comic-adventure style, combining romantic melodrama with persistent irony. Its settings—Lake Como, the Stelvio Pass, alpine hotels, precipices and glaciers—supply conventional picturesque grandeur, but the narrator repeatedly undercuts that grandeur with jokes about hotel rates, social pretension, national stereotypes and commercial self-interest. Romance is treated as both genuine emotion and an occasion for satire: Tina’s attachments are described with amused scepticism, Standish imagines converting a pension into an ancestral palace, and even mortal danger is punctuated by practical concerns about clothing, luggage and transportation.

The narration is third-person omniscient, with a highly visible, intrusive narrator. This narrator comments directly on characters, generalises about nationalities and professions, anticipates the reader’s disbelief, and pretends to be recording “a narrative of fact.” Mock-apologetic formulas such as “With deep regret it must be here admitted” and “It must be confessed” create a conversational relationship with the reader. The voice often adopts solemn or elevated language only to overturn it with a comic qualification. Aphoristic observations—about lies, artists, Englishmen or hotel-keepers—regularly interrupt the action and give the prose the air of a witty raconteur telling an improbable story with complete confidence.

The diction mixes formal, literary phrasing with colloquial expressions and commercial vocabulary. Romantic terms such as “golden evening light,” “purple haze,” “fairy-like” and “sylph-like” coexist with “beggarly,” “skin the wealthy Englishman,” “calm cheek,” and “the hotel business wasn’t in it.” Foreign words, place names and multilingual references add continental colour: francs, centimes, piastras, stiletto, vendetta, Signor Inglese, Maloga, Stelvio, Ortler and Franzenshoehe. These details lend geographical specificity while also supporting broad comic contrasts among Swiss thrift, Italian passion and English self-assurance.

Sentences are generally expansive and syntactically balanced, often carrying an observation through several clauses before arriving at a humorous sting. Parentheses, qualifications, lists and delayed punch lines are frequent. Parallel constructions intensify comic escalation, as in the progression from “serious” to “disastrous” to “absolute destruction.” Similes are vivid but deliberately extravagant: a mouth closes “like a steel trap,” Pietro falls “like a log,” and romantic helplessness is compared to losing one’s footing on the Matterhorn. Hyperbole is central to the style, though it is delivered in a dry, matter-of-fact tone.

Characterisation is broad, economical and caricatural. Old Lenz is defined by corpulence, greed and hotel-keeping pragmatism; Pietro by beauty, jealousy, knives and theatrical passion; Standish by artistic vanity, English privilege and physical resourcefulness; Tina by flirtatiousness, adaptability and tactical intelligence. Psychological depth is secondary to sharply drawn behavioural traits. Tina, however, anchors the plot through her ability to control what others know: she silences her father, conceals Pietro’s identity, manipulates Standish’s attention and preserves her marriage by tact. The final sentence turns this sustained deception into a lightly cynical domestic virtue.

Dialogue is direct, lively and functional. It establishes power relations quickly, moves the plot, and preserves comic contrast even during threats. Pietro speaks in operatic, revenge-driven images; Standish responds with bluff understatement and practical indignation; Tina uses sweetness, insult, fainting and calculated omission as circumstances require. Speech is often followed by narratorial commentary that reframes its meaning or exposes motives the speaker does not state.

The plot is linear and tightly causal, beginning as a comic love triangle, passing through courtship and attempted murder, and culminating in a mountain-carriage set piece. Early passages devote substantial space to exposition and satire; the pace accelerates sharply once Pietro attacks Standish and becomes nearly cinematic during the descent from the pass. The climax uses short turns of action, visual shocks, shouted dialogue and precise spatial hazards. Suspense is nevertheless moderated by humour: Standish escapes through the “short cuts,” defeats Pietro with a kick, and remains comically ignorant of the full romantic history.

The dominant tonal blend is affectionate cynicism, mock solemnity and sensational excitement. Violence is real but not gruesomely dwelt upon, and danger is repeatedly converted into farce. The story relies on coincidence, concealed identity, the returning rejected lover, the mad carriage ride and the resourceful hero—all conventions of melodrama—but handles them with self-aware levity. To reproduce the style, use an assured intrusive narrator, picturesque European scenery, socially stereotyped but vivid characters, elaborate sentences ending in dry jokes, mock-authorial apologies, practical details amid romance, and a steadily escalating adventure plot whose sensational elements are never allowed to lose their comic edge.

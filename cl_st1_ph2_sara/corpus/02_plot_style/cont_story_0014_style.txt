The story uses a close first-person voice that is confessional, anxious, self-conscious, and darkly comic. Its narrator moves fluidly between immediate observation, private memory, artistic insecurity, and supernatural perception, so that the hospital visit remains grounded in ordinary experience even as it becomes increasingly unstable. The retrospective narration occasionally reveals future knowledge—most importantly the father’s cancer—creating dramatic irony and placing dread beneath an episode that initially seems routine. The narrator is perceptive but unreliable in a deliberate, metafictional sense: he does not merely interpret reality but may be writing, revising, and damaging it as events unfold.

The prose combines plainspoken, contemporary diction with sudden lyrical or grotesque intensification. Much of the narration is built from short declarative sentences—“It was a basement,” “I got up,” “I was happy”—which give emotional distress a flat, controlled surface. These are interspersed with longer accumulative passages, especially when fear, imagination, or supernatural power takes over. Sentence fragments and isolated observations reproduce the narrator’s distracted attention, while repetition creates pressure and fatalism: people remain “still there,” the patients “groaned and groaned,” and the disastrous miracle “kept happening.” Parenthetical qualifications and casual phrases such as “I guess,” “pretty good,” “kind of,” and “It is what it is” keep the voice conversational and prevent its visionary material from becoming conventionally grand.

The diction repeatedly collides the mundane with the mythic. Hot plates, oatmeal, parking meters, insurance cards, plastic chairs, leaking water, and hospital paperwork coexist with divine pacts, severed tongues, living sentences, prophetic visions, and reality-altering narration. This contrast is central to the style: supernatural power is not presented in elevated fantasy language alone but embedded in poverty, bureaucracy, resentment, and artistic ambition. The Twelve-tongued God is at once muse, demon, editor, tempter, and embodiment of narrative power. Its Roman-numeral brand, changing faces, exchanged tongues, and commands not to be “boring” turn the creative process into visceral body horror.

Imagery is concrete, bodily, and often cruelly comic. Illness appears through tubes, puddles, strained joints, hospital gowns, depleted blood cells, chemotherapy, diapers, and failing bodies. The narrator’s comparisons are intentionally strange and sometimes ethically uncomfortable: an old patient seems to be used as a doorstop or hat rack; a grieving family resembles a football huddle; an elderly couple are indistinguishable as sufferer and support. Such images expose the narrator’s habit of converting people into material. The story later makes that habit explicit when imagined figures accuse him of prolonging suffering for no clear reason. This creates a tension between empathy and exploitation: he notices vulnerable people because he does not want to forget them, yet his language continually aestheticizes or instrumentalizes their pain.

The humour is sardonic, absurdist, and defensive. It arises from hostile hospital exchanges, the narrator’s envy of another writer, his description of his own sentimental kitten story, and incongruous gestures such as performing a cartwheel beside a grieving family. Comic phrasing frequently sits beside terror, preventing a stable emotional register. The narrator’s quips—such as defining adulthood as paying the meter on time—are funny but also reveal his effort to control fear through formulation. The story’s most exaggerated jokes often become accusations against the storyteller himself, particularly when his attempt to impose a universally happy ending produces grotesque physical consequences.

Dialogue is terse, socially precise, and adversarial. Hospital workers speak in clipped, dismissive, bureaucratic rhythms; the father’s speech conveys pride, irritation, practicality, and emotional reserve; the god alternates between seduction, ridicule, and editorial command. Repeated misunderstandings and failed exchanges reinforce the setting’s institutional confusion. Against this, the father’s simple curiosity about what his son writes becomes unusually intimate. Their emotional bond is conveyed obliquely through practical acts, evasive answers, coats, food, driving, and paperwork rather than explicit declarations.

Structurally, the narrative begins as realist domestic fiction, expands into a remembered supernatural origin story, and then turns into overt metafiction. The hospital’s corridors and repeated landmarks create a looping, almost purgatorial design. Each journey through radiology revisits the same patients and grieving family, but their symbolic weight increases. The embedded account of the pact explains the narrator’s creative compulsion while delaying the medical outcome. In the final movement, narration becomes performative: revisions occur openly, thoughts alter events, and clichés of healing are literalized. The title is folded into the narrator’s declarations—“the hospital where sickness ends,” then “the hospital where the affliction is flight”—showing language struggling to determine what kind of story this will be.

The pacing alternates between slow observational wandering and bursts of surreal escalation. Early scenes dwell on domestic deprivation and bureaucratic delay; the middle pauses for literary reflection and backstory; the ending accelerates through proclamations, running, levitation, falls, and bodily collapse. Recurring motifs—tongues, heat, fire, marks, coats, writing materials, thresholds, waiting, and flight—bind these shifts together. Thresholds are especially important: doors, hospital entrances, and transitions between inside and outside become places where language changes bodies.

Thematically, the style explores artistic ambition, class resentment, filial fear, illness, guilt, and the moral limits of fiction. Writing promises escape from powerlessness, but the narrator’s desire to “make what you want to see” becomes authoritarian. His attempt to rescue everyone produces an implausible, “cheap” ending whose metaphorical beauty cannot support real bodies. Flight therefore functions both as transcendence and affliction: imagination lifts people beyond suffering, but only briefly, before gravity injures them again. The final exchange with the father strips away the narrator’s aesthetic explanation. “It’s about a hospital where people can fly” is answered by “What have you done?”, leaving the story poised between creative achievement and moral catastrophe.

Overall, the style is best characterised as contemporary literary surrealism with strong metafictional, magical-realist, absurdist, and body-horror elements. Its signature effect comes from balancing emotional directness with narrative instability: intimate family realism is filtered through a voice that is funny, resentful, tender, frightened, and dangerously eager to transform suffering into art.

The story adopts an antiquarian mode of historical and cosmic horror, presenting its supernatural material through the densely documented surface of a Roman provincial chronicle. Its first-person narrator speaks with educated, bureaucratic authority, recalling events as though he were both participant and historian. Roman names, offices, military ranks, calendar formulas, legal precedents and geographical references create an elaborate impression of authenticity. This learned specificity is deliberately juxtaposed with repeated admissions of uncertainty—“must have been,” “seemed,” “rumour told”—so that the narrator appears lucid and informed while remaining unable to explain the central horror.

The diction is elevated, archaic and Latinate. Forms such as “prætorium,” “ædile,” “dæmonic,” “whilst,” “amidst” and “aught,” together with untranslated or partly translated Latin, give the prose a consciously old-fashioned, scholarly texture. Long formal words and abstract constructions—“conscientiously dogged opposition,” “provisional capacity,” “cryptic towns,” “shocking dooms”—make even practical discussion sound ceremonial and ominous. The text frequently relies on capitalised or quasi-technical labels such as “Terrible Season,” “Roman People” and “Sabbath,” turning rumours and institutions alike into mythic forces. Its descriptions of ethnic groups use the racialised physiognomic shorthand typical of older imperial adventure and weird fiction, reinforcing the narrator’s Roman colonial outlook while marking the mountain people as alien and threatening.

Sentences are often long, cumulative and heavily subordinated, carrying lists of names, qualifications and contextual details before reaching their main point. Parentheses, semicolons and dashes allow the narrator to keep adding historical or interpretive information. Enumerations are especially prominent: crowds are divided by ancestry and status, officials are introduced with titles and physical attributes, and occult learning is traced across Syria, Egypt, Etruria and Italy. This syntactic abundance produces a stately, burdened rhythm. At moments of crisis, however, the prose becomes more abrupt and emphatic. The horses “screamed, not neighed, but screamed,” the stars are successively blotted out, and ellipses reproduce panic and broken perception. The shift from elaborate exposition to sharp sensory fragments accelerates the climax.

The opening moves slowly through setting, political context and administrative dispute. Much of the early story resembles a report or historical deposition, with the protagonist arguing policy in measured rhetorical periods. This delay is central to the suspense: the threat remains indirect while practical men debate whether it deserves official attention. Recurrent references to nameless rites, disappearances, drumming and an ominous seasonal date gradually accumulate pressure. Once the cohort enters the hills, the narrative becomes progressively more sensory and immediate. Sunset gives way to dusk, torchlight, darkness and finally the extinction of the stars. At the same time, ordinary sounds are displaced by unnatural ones: distant drums, screaming horses, shrieking soldiers and the implied beating of enormous wings.

Atmosphere is created through colour, light and landscape. The scarlet-and-gold sunset initially lends the provincial town a “mystic” glamour, but its beauty soon becomes malign. The hills loom over the Roman settlement as a repository of prehuman forces, while narrow wooded paths trap the disciplined cohort inside an environment it cannot master. Light repeatedly diminishes or turns hostile: the setting sun reddens the town, the moon trembles, torches dim, stars vanish, and only the red altar fires remain. Cold is personified as an intelligent assault, and the wind possesses “sentience and deliberateness.” These effects transform nature from backdrop into an active manifestation of the supernatural.

The horror is characteristically indirect. The “very old folk” are glimpsed only through rumour, obscure messengers and ritual traces, while the climactic creatures are described as “colossal forms” and “nameless beasts” rather than anatomically defined. Repeated words such as “nameless,” “horror,” “terrible,” “hideous,” “frightful” and “forbidden” insist on emotional magnitude while withholding concrete explanation. Classical allusions supply analogies without resolving the mystery: Laocoön evokes constriction and helplessness, and references to Phrygian priests, the Bacchanalia and Diana Aricina place the terror within a deep Mediterranean history of prohibited ritual. The supernatural is thus represented as older than Rome’s institutions and beyond the reach of its categories.

A central stylistic contrast opposes Roman order to archaic chaos. The Roman characters rely on law, military discipline, civic duty, administrative correspondence and imperial prestige. The protagonist’s speeches are structured like forensic arguments, balancing duty against policy and citizens against provincials. Against this rational apparatus stand drums, Sabbaths, mountain fires and forces that cannot be subdued by a pilum. The cohort’s destruction reverses the imagery of empire: disciplined soldiers become a screaming mass, official rank ceases to matter, and the landscape isolates and attacks each man. The Latin phrase “Malitia vetus”—ancient evil—compresses the story’s governing idea that organised civilisation is only a recent and fragile layer over primordial malignity.

Characterisation is economical and often caricatural. Scribonius is grave, elderly and ultimately resigned; Balbutius is conscientious but obstinate; Asellius is young, contemptuous and complacent. Their physical details—bald head, hawk face, polished armour, compressed lips, superior sneer—serve as quick emblems of temperament. The narrator is defined less by appearance than by occult scholarship, eloquence and certainty. His forceful advocacy also creates dramatic irony: the man most aware of the danger is the one who insists on confronting it, and his learned confidence cannot protect the expedition.

The ending reframes the historical narrative as a vivid dream. This device preserves ambiguity while suggesting access to submerged memory, reincarnation or ancestral consciousness. The dry observation that no record of the cohort survives, followed by an encyclopaedic confirmation that the town endured, blends documentary fact with speculative nightmare. The playful mock-Roman signature and “Gothick Supremacy” sign-off add an epistolary, antiquarian flourish after the climax. Overall, the style combines scholarly pastiche, imperial historical detail, elaborate periodic prose and escalating cosmic dread, producing horror through the collapse of rational confidence before something immeasurably older than human civilisation.

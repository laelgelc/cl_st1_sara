The story uses a polished, early twentieth-century realist style enriched by social comedy, sentiment, and touches of romance. Its central method is to place private emotional vulnerability against the observant, often amused scrutiny of a small town. The prose treats humiliation and longing seriously while allowing the surrounding social world to appear vain, provincial, and faintly absurd. This produces a tonal mixture of tenderness, irony, pathos, and restrained satire.

The narration is third-person omniscient, moving freely between outward description, communal opinion, and the inner experience of individual characters. Ariel receives the closest emotional attention in the first section, while Joe becomes the principal focal consciousness in the second. The narrator does not disappear behind the characters but intervenes with judgments, qualifications, and lightly comic explanations, as in the accounts of Canaan’s customs or the claim that Ariel’s social position can best be defined by what others say about her. Such commentary creates the sense of an intelligent storyteller who understands both the characters’ pain and the ridiculous conventions governing their lives. The narrator frequently speaks inclusively or conversationally—using formulations such as “we can find”—which gives the narrative a genial, authorial presence.

The diction is formal and literary but generally accessible. It combines precise material detail with elevated emotional phrasing. Concrete objects—artificial roses, clumsily resoled shoes, ribbon rosettes, a cheval-glass, a ruby necklace, Japanese lanterns, a parasol—carry substantial social meaning. Clothing in particular functions as a language of class, taste, exclusion, and transformation. At the dance, Ariel’s improvised finery reveals poverty and inexperience; years later, the perfect harmony of her lavender dress, hat, gloves, and parasol announces sophistication and social power. The vocabulary occasionally has an old-fashioned or cosmopolitan flavor, with words such as “toilet,” “vraillery,” “duenna,” “junketings,” “viands,” and “preëminently.” These choices reinforce the period setting and the narrator’s cultivated voice.

Sentence structure is varied but tends toward long, balanced, clause-rich sentences. Semicolons, dashes, parentheses, and cumulative phrases allow the prose to gather observations and emotional qualifications in layers. Descriptive passages often proceed by accretion, adding one embarrassing or beautiful detail after another until the character’s emotional state becomes unavoidable. Short sentences then provide emphasis at moments of isolation or reversal: “She went away from the mirror” and “Ariel was grateful for him” are powerful because of their simplicity. Repetition is used for emotional pressure, especially in Ariel’s speech—“always, always, always” and “I do, I do”—and in narrative accounts of waiting, exclusion, or disbelief.

The story’s imagery is strongly visual and frequently organized around contrast. Mirrors are a major motif in the dance sequence. Ariel first sees herself through desire in her small mirror at home, then through social comparison in the dressing-room cheval-glass, and finally through a public, devastating reflection while dancing. Each mirror strips away another layer of hopeful self-deception. In the later section, visual imagery shifts from distortion and exposure to harmony and radiance. Ariel’s lavender clothing echoes the haze over the landscape, and her appearance is described through ivory, flowers, dawn, perfume, and summer light. Her transformation is therefore not merely stated; it is embedded in a changed sensory world.

The treatment of setting is both realistic and symbolic. Canaan is a recognizable Midwestern small town with strict hierarchies, shared gossip, limited novelty, and intense communal surveillance. Its dances, church crowds, large houses, bridges, streets, and social rituals form a complete miniature society. At the same time, interiors and exteriors reflect emotional states. The crowded, brightly lit dressing room exposes Ariel to judgment; the chair against the ballroom wall becomes an emblem of exclusion; the shadowed veranda allows grief and friendship to emerge privately. Years later, the open summer landscape accompanies Ariel’s return as a confident, worldly woman. The town itself behaves almost like a collective character, murmuring, staring, classifying, and rapidly transmitting news.

Characterization relies heavily on gesture, clothing, social behavior, and free indirect access to feeling. Ariel’s repeated laughter at the dance is especially important: outwardly it signals good humor, but inwardly it is a desperate defense against shame. Her fixed smile, aching facial muscles, hot eyes, and compulsive laughter render humiliation physically rather than abstractly. Joe is characterized through rough sympathy, embarrassment, and loyalty; his blunt speech contrasts with the narrator’s refinement. Norbert Flitcroft is drawn more caricaturally through his size, self-pity, reluctant compliance, and comic solemnity. Even minor figures receive memorable identifying details, such as Mrs. Pike’s frightened appearance and ruby necklace or the waiter’s ceremonious generosity.

Dialogue is socially revealing and sharply individualized. Ariel’s attempted sprightliness with Norbert exposes her wish to perform sophistication, while his plaintive replies reveal resentment at being treated as the obliging “fat boy.” Joe’s language is terse, colloquial, and emotionally evasive, yet his actions demonstrate concern. The dialogue often contains a painful discrepancy between what is said and what is meant. Ariel insists that she does not care and was not crying while the narrative makes the opposite unmistakable. Humor also arises through deadpan exchanges, abrupt retorts, and literal-minded responses. The speech is theatrical enough to sharpen character but remains grounded in the social idiom of the setting.

The tonal control is one of the story’s defining features. The dance scene is deeply painful, but the narration repeatedly introduces comedy: Norbert’s mournful dancing, his “burlesque face of suffering,” his increasing speed as he escapes Ariel, and his later bottle-shaped appearance. This comedy does not erase Ariel’s suffering; instead, it intensifies it by showing how easily private anguish becomes public entertainment. The later homecoming reverses the social comedy. The same town that once ignored Ariel now stops to stare, and Norbert, formerly reluctant to dance with her, falls into a trance and follows her. The reversal is satisfying but presented with enough irony to expose the shallowness of collective judgment.

Structurally, the excerpt is built around a dramatic before-and-after contrast. The first section traces Ariel’s movement from hopeful preparation to self-consciousness, humiliation, emotional collapse, and a sudden change in fortune. The revelation of her inheritance closes the section with a decisive narrative hinge. The second section jumps forward several years and stages her return through Joe’s inability to recognize her. Delaying recognition allows the reader to experience Ariel first as a mysterious vision and only afterward connect her with the rejected girl. Repeated characters, especially Joe and Norbert, provide continuity and make the reversal socially legible.

The story’s themes include exclusion, class, beauty, self-fashioning, provincial conformity, and the instability of social value. Ariel’s essential nature changes less than the town’s perception of her, yet wealth, travel, education, and cultivated appearance grant her access to the admiration she once craved. The narrative sympathizes with that craving while questioning the society that makes appearance and status prerequisites for kindness. Friendship between outsiders provides an emotional counterweight to public judgment. The story also explores the painful formation of identity: Ariel wants not simply to be admired, but to know the unwritten codes that permit a person to belong.

Overall, the style may be described as omniscient social realism with romantic transformation, comic caricature, and sentimental emotional intensity. Its most characteristic techniques are detailed descriptions of dress and gesture, intrusive but genial narrative commentary, ironic contrast between appearance and feeling, psychologically charged objects and settings, lively socially diagnostic dialogue, and carefully staged reversals of status.

The story adopts the manner of a dark folkloric legend, combining historical violence, Christian symbolism and Irish supernatural tradition. Its narrative voice is omniscient, impersonal and authoritative, recounting extraordinary events as though they belong to established local history. References to recognisable places and retrospective formulations such as “what is now called” and “the abyss that is now called” give the tale the character of an oral place-name legend, in which a haunted landscape preserves the memory of an atrocity.

The prose is formal, archaic and biblical in colouring. Elevated words such as “smite,” “woe,” “dwell” and “unto,” along with the abbot’s prophetic cadence, lend the narrative a scriptural solemnity. Christian vocabulary—altar, crucifix, holy candles, saints, martyrs, purgatory—is interwoven with Gaelic folklore, including the Sidhe, the crowned supernatural creature, the washer of the dead and the demonic or otherworldly piper. This blending creates a world in which religious conversion has not erased older fears. The troopers’ instinctive reverence for the crucifix and their half-suppressed impulse to cross themselves expose the fragility of their professed beliefs.

Sentences are often long, cumulative and joined by repeated conjunctions, especially “and,” giving the narration the flowing rhythm of oral storytelling or a chronicle. Actions and perceptions unfold in linked sequences rather than through abrupt fragments. Repetition provides both musicality and structural emphasis: the troopers try “again and again”; the moaning becomes “louder and louder”; the slope grows “more and more” severe; and the piper cries that his wife “was washing! she was washing!” Parallel constructions similarly reinforce the fated quality of events. Dialogue is sparse, direct and ceremonial, serving chiefly to issue commands, express fear, invoke belief or announce supernatural warnings.

Visual imagery dominates, particularly contrasts among white, red, yellow, grey and black. White initially belongs to sacred things—the friars’ habits, altar light and holy candles—but later migrates into the uncanny moonlight, stars and “moon-fires.” Red marks violence and destructive energy through blood, flames, the piper’s cap and hair, and the burning town. Fire, light and shadow form the central motif. The shadows in the abbey perform a “fantastic dance,” which is replaced by the “dance of the fires”; later moonlight dances grotesquely among the trees, while the stars are mistaken for holy candles. These recurring images enact the abbot’s curse that the murderers will wander among “ungovernable shadows” and follow “ungovernable fires.”

The natural landscape is animated and hostile. Trees hide the moon, branches strike steel caps, treetops moan like the dead, moonlight moves like flame, and horses sense danger before the men do. Frequent personification makes the environment appear to participate in supernatural judgment. Sensory details—crashing doors, musket smoke, wet branches, crackling ivy, rushing water, bagpipe music and the final dull impact—create an atmosphere that is at once vivid and dreamlike. The story repeatedly limits vision through darkness, smoke, clouds and flickering light, making perception uncertain until apparitions reveal an unmistakable destiny.

The plot follows a clear pattern of transgression, prophecy and punishment. The abbey massacre establishes the crime; the abbot’s dying curse states the governing law; and the ride through the woods fulfils that curse image by image. Suspense develops episodically through escalating omens: the silver-crowned worm, the washer displaying each man’s corpse, the impassable ford, the disorienting forest, the eerie piper and the apparent holy candles in the sky. Each warning offers the troopers an opportunity to recognise their doom, but martial pride and religious self-justification drive them onward. Their intended pursuit of two human messengers becomes a supernatural hunt in which they themselves are the quarry.

Characterisation is economical and collective. The five troopers are differentiated only by a few domestic details—a wife, a dead wife and waiting wine, a sweetheart—which briefly humanise them and sharpen the pathos of their deaths. More importantly, they function as a guilty group, specifically identified with the first shots and the lighting of the sacrilegious fire. The oldest trooper embodies arrogant certainty, repeatedly interpreting supernatural signs as Satanic deception and trusting his sword and doctrine. The youngest is more perceptive and fearful, but cannot break away from the group. Sir Frederick Hamilton remains a remote source of violent commands rather than a psychologically developed figure.

The pacing moves from the compressed brutality of the massacre into a slower, increasingly oppressive journey, then accelerates sharply during the final ascent. Recurrent temporal markers such as “suddenly,” “presently,” “in a moment,” “for a time” and “gradually” regulate the tale’s movement in a traditional storytelling fashion. The conclusion is swift and unsentimental: ecstatic motion, song and pursuit culminate in five screams and a “dull crash.” Overall, the style is lyrical, symbolic and fatalistic, using patterned imagery, folkloric repetition and formal narration to turn sectarian violence into a supernatural moral reckoning.

The story uses a conversational, retrospective first-person frame around a predominantly third-person narrative. The narrator speaks as an informed raconteur, opening with a playful admission that the tale is “blood-and-thundery” and closing by asserting personal knowledge of Tom. Frequent asides—“for, you see,” “as has been said,” and “that is neither here nor there”—create the impression of an oral story told to a familiar audience. This voice is genial, confident and mildly ironic, often pretending to minimise or qualify what it is actually emphasising.

The tone combines frontier adventure, dry comedy, sentiment and sudden brutality. Mundane inconveniences such as damp fuel, late supper, awkward cowboy clothing and Alfred’s short legs are treated with humorous precision, while murder, pursuit and self-sacrifice are narrated with notable restraint. The resulting tonal contrast makes the violence feel abrupt and the final pathos more forceful. Heroism is deliberately presented without grandiloquence: the men make fatal decisions calmly, practically and “without heroics.” The narrator’s repeated mockery of the idea that an ordinary cow-puncher is “incapable of the higher feelings” supplies a sustained ironic undertone, culminating in the revelation of Tom’s sacrifice.

The diction mixes polished literary narration with Western vocabulary and period slang. Terms such as “vaqueros,” “round-up,” “night-herd,” “arroya,” “chaparajos,” “quirt,” “six-shooter,” “mosey” and “pot us” establish the occupational and regional setting. Dialogue is rendered through eye dialect, dropped consonants and colloquial grammar—“Yo’ laigs is longer,” “they’s goin’,” and “jest nat’rally plugs us”—to distinguish frontier speech from the narrator’s more elaborate prose. The language also contains dated racial labels, ethnic stereotyping and caricatured speech, especially in its depiction of Mexican characters and Black Sam; these are prominent features of the story’s period style and social assumptions.

Sentence structure varies according to narrative purpose. Descriptive and explanatory passages favour long, balanced sentences with multiple clauses, parenthetical qualifications and semicolon-linked details. Action scenes shift toward shorter, sequential statements and direct verbs: men draw, fire, run, fall and climb. Comic passages often use elaborate accumulation, as in the inventory of scattered cookware or the list of clothing that makes a cowboy’s running gait ridiculous. Repetition is important both rhythmically and thematically. “Passing the love of woman” recurs like an incantation, while repeated references to Tom’s supposed ordinariness steadily prepare the final reversal.

The landscape is described on both epic and practical scales. A long geological digression reconstructs the vanished prehistoric river before narrowing its focus to the dry riverbed and clay cliff that determine the chase. This gives the setting an almost mythic antiquity while also functioning as tactical exposition. Visual description is strongly composed: silhouettes against sunset, a black ribbon of cliff, ghostly mules, white dust, moonlit clay and the gray dawn all produce stark, high-contrast scenes. The hostile environment is not merely atmospheric; topography, light, unstable ground and distance control the characters’ options.

Characterisation is economical and behavioural. Alfred is defined by competence, taciturnity and physical limitations; Tom by brooding memory, practical courage, carving and understated loyalty; Sam by expressive posture and his anxious absorption in the fire. Interior analysis is used selectively, chiefly for Tom’s melancholy and Sam’s comic distress. In moments of crisis, personality emerges through action rather than explanation: Alfred shoots the injured horse without hesitation, Tom rigs the card game, and both discuss likely death as a problem in “division of labour.”

The plot follows a clear escalation from unease to inconvenience, provocation, murder, flight, siege, sacrifice and aftermath. Early details that appear incidental—the preacher’s phrase, Tom’s love affair, the cards, the men’s different physiques and the geography of the bluff—become structurally decisive later. The rigged jack of diamonds is the central dramatic device: Tom disguises self-sacrifice as chance so that Alfred’s code of fair play will force him to accept escape. The ending then reinterprets Tom’s earlier abstraction and the repeated phrase, turning an apparently meaningless verbal obsession into an epitaph.

Overall, the style belongs to the tradition of the frontier yarn: vigorous, scenic and action-driven, but shaped by an intrusive storyteller’s humour and literary irony. Its central effect comes from placing profound loyalty inside rough, unsentimental men and allowing emotion to appear indirectly—through practical gestures, understatement and a final piece of carved language rather than through explicit declarations.

At a villa in Cannes, an aged Italian count debates justice with an American judge and an Englishman, arguing that legal principles can seem absurd across cultures. He recounts a case from an American coal-mining community populated by European immigrants.

A powerful, fearless officer arrests a criminal and escorts him toward jail. An Italian accomplice follows, asking the prisoner in Italian whether he should shoot the officer immediately or wait until they reach a ravine. At the ravine, the accomplice shoots the officer twice in the back, inflicting unquestionably fatal wounds. Knowing he will die and determined not to suffer helplessly, the officer shoots himself in the head.

The assassin is captured, identified by numerous witnesses, convicted of premeditated murder, and sentenced to hang. On appeal, however, the conviction is overturned because the officer’s suicide technically accelerated his death: under the applicable law, the “intervening responsible agent” who directly caused death was the officer himself, not the man who mortally wounded him. The assassin therefore escapes execution, and the count—who is related to him—must continue paying him a family allowance from his diminished estates.

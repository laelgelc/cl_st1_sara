The story is written as a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century frontier tale, combining regional realism, adventure narrative, and a lightly moralized character study. Its central stylistic contrast is between the regulated, socially conventional East and the physically free, “primitive” West. The West is romanticized as a place of authenticity, self-reliance, natural abundance, and release from inherited expectations, while Eastern life is associated with education, family duty, business, marriage, and conformity. The narrative ultimately treats Alexander Wells’s attraction to Texas not as a temporary adventure but as an instinctive return to a more congenial mode of existence.

The narrator is third-person, omniscient, and overtly authoritative. Rather than remaining invisible, the narrative voice opens with a broad claim about human nature and periodically interprets events, supplies historical background, or comments directly on characters and customs. Phrases such as “It might be in perfect order to remark here” and “The most peculiar feature of the whole affair” create the manner of an informed storyteller addressing a general readership. The voice moves easily between panoramic social observation and close attention to Wells’s motives, often explaining his conduct in plain causal terms. Its tone is assured, observant, and mildly amused, with occasional irony directed at Wells’s evasions, especially his fabricated illness and his efforts to prolong his stay.

The prose is formal, expansive, and documentary in texture. Sentences are frequently long and clause-rich, using semicolons, participial phrases, balanced constructions, and sequences of explanatory detail. Shorter sentences appear for emphasis—“He liked it. The romance was real; it was not an imitation”—and give moments of emotional or thematic force to an otherwise measured narrative flow. Paragraphs commonly begin with a general statement and then develop it through examples, background information, or practical description. The syntax reflects an older literary idiom, as do constructions such as “there graduated,” “the latter,” “on reaching,” and “being assured on both points.”

The diction mixes elevated abstraction with concrete frontier vocabulary. Terms such as “laudable desire,” “parental demands,” “imperative orders,” and “remorse of conscience” belong to a decorous, educated register, while words including “jacal,” “ranchito,” “pasear,” “monte,” “javeline,” “specie,” “case-keeper,” and “norther” establish regional specificity. Spanish and frontier expressions are generally explained through context or direct exposition, allowing the story to function partly as an introduction to unfamiliar customs. The terminology of hunting, transport, ranching, weather, terrain, and animal behavior lends the narrative a practical, almost ethnographic realism.

Description favors usable, material detail over lyrical ornament. Cabins are described through logs, clay chinking, floors, roofs, fireplaces, and chimneys; travel through distances, routes, stages, wagons, teams, and cargoes; hunting through tracks, wind direction, weapons, dogs, ranges, and methods of approach. Landscapes are picturesque but rarely rendered through elaborate metaphor. Their appeal emerges from accumulation: pecan groves, creek bottoms, open prairie, game herds, campfires, and horseback movement. This concrete abundance supports the story’s ideal of the West as a complete and satisfying world.

The pacing is largely chronological and episodic. The early sections proceed slowly, detailing Wells’s background, journey, arrival, and gradual installation in the cabin. Once his frontier life is established, months and years are compressed into summaries of repeated hunting seasons, letters home, and attempts to avoid returning East. The arrival of his mother abruptly shifts the narrative from leisurely accumulation to dramatic confrontation. A final time jump then shows the failure of Wells’s reintegration into Eastern society and prepares his decisive return to Texas. This structure gives the story the shape of departure, initiation, forced return, and eventual self-recognition.

Characterization is achieved more through conduct, habits, and contrast than through psychological introspection. Wells is initially an overworked but privileged graduate; he becomes healthy, bronzed, bewhiskered, linguistically capable, and increasingly independent. His transformation is shown through what he learns, where he chooses to live, how little money matters to him, and how strongly he resists social obligations. Tiburcio is characterized through mastery of woodcraft and animal signs rather than through interior thought. Their friendship rests on complementary forms of knowledge: Wells possesses formal education and modern equipment, while Tiburcio possesses embodied, experiential wisdom. The statement that Wells prefers “to sit at his feet” reverses conventional assumptions about cultural superiority, although the portrayal still retains the paternalistic and racialized language of its period.

Dialogue is sparse and strategically placed. Most information is conveyed through narration or reported speech, so direct dialogue gains prominence when it appears. Frontier speech is concise, colloquial, and dryly humorous, as in the description of a twelve-hundred-mile journey as “a little pasear.” The mother’s quiet request to Alexander is restrained rather than melodramatic, and his halting answer exposes his sudden embarrassment more effectively than explanation would. The closing speech is the fullest statement of his philosophy, explicitly opposing standardized Eastern society to the freedom of the “boundless West.”

Humor is understated and often produced by discrepancy. Wells claims ill health while thriving physically; a doctor’s professional advice is purchased; a dangerous and exhausting stage journey is casually minimized; and the mother discovers her supposedly invalid son gambling on a cowhide floor. The narrative does not condemn him harshly. Instead, its irony is affectionate, presenting his deception as evidence that the West has awakened a nature incompatible with the life planned for him.

The story’s imagery repeatedly associates vitality with movement and sound: galloping horses, pursuing hounds, falling pecans, flowing creeks, stages rattling into town, and animals breaking cover. The East, by contrast, is represented less sensuously and more institutionally, through college, family, business, marriage, and the railway depot. The final “siren” image gathers the sensory attractions of Texas into an irresistible summons. Although the allusion suggests danger or enchantment, the ending endorses rather than repudiates the call.

Overall, the style is leisurely, masculine, practical, and regionally descriptive, with a strong faith in experience over convention. It blends historical exposition, travel writing, hunting lore, gentle comedy, and romantic frontier ideology. Its dominant effect comes from the contrast between the narrator’s formal, composed manner and the increasingly unconventional life being celebrated: a life presented as rough in material circumstances but natural, vigorous, and spiritually liberating.

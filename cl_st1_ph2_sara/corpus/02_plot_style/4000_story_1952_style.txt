The story is a comic maritime tall tale presented as an intimate oral confession. A minimal frame introduces the night watchman as a reluctant raconteur who claims that the incident is both true and previously suppressed for fear of ridicule. This appeal to eyewitness authority deliberately contrasts with the increasing absurdity of the events. The first-person narrator is observant but only partially perceptive: he notices practical details and eventually detects Joe’s deception, yet closes with a solemn, simplistic moral that ignores the full implications of what he has narrated. This gap between his interpretation and the reader’s understanding creates sustained dramatic irony.

The narrative voice is strongly colloquial and shaped to resemble working-class seafaring speech. Eye dialect and phonetic spellings—such as “ses,” “arter,” “fust,” “sarpint,” “’ead,” and “agin”—establish accent, social setting, and oral immediacy. Grammatical irregularities, including double negatives, unconventional verb forms, and constructions such as “a chap what we called,” reinforce the impression of spontaneous speech. Malapropisms and comic pronunciations, including “eggstraordinary,” “symperthizing,” and “puffected,” add humour without interrupting the narrator’s fluency. Nautical vocabulary—“aft,” “aloft,” “quarter,” “eight bells,” “Sandy Hook,” “fo’c’s’le,” and “bosen”—grounds the improbable adventure in a convincingly practical shipboard world.

The syntax is predominantly loose, additive, and paratactic. Events are linked through repeated conjunctions, especially “and,” “but,” and “well,” as though the speaker is remembering and extending the story in real time. Long anecdotal sentences alternate with brisk exchanges of dialogue. The narrator frequently uses oral transitions such as “Well,” “Anyway,” and “as luck would have it,” which keep the account moving while preserving the rhythm of a spoken yarn. Speech tags are repetitive and unobtrusive—most often variations of “ses”—allowing timing, character, and punchlines to emerge from the dialogue itself. Occasional capitalization, as in “LAST” and “NOT KNOWING WHAT HE WAS DOING,” reproduces emphatic vocal stress and sharpens the irony.

Dialogue drives most of the characterization. The captain is cowardly, bullying, self-interested, and capable of disguising self-preservation as official duty. The first mate responds to danger with dry practical humour, while Stuttering Sam’s speech defect becomes part of the opening comic routine and then disappears the moment genuine fear demands speed. Joe Cooper is introduced through an extended grotesque joke about his ugliness, but the captain’s cruelty toward him creates the plot’s decisive reversal. Characters are rendered as compact comic types rather than psychologically elaborate individuals, yet their motives remain clear through speech, gesture, and action.

The humour depends heavily on understatement, incongruity, reversal, and literal-minded reasoning. A monstrous sea serpent is treated first as a joke, then as a nervous pet, and finally as a commercial opportunity. Terror is expressed through comic parallelism: “The second mate was holding the wheel, the skipper was holding his breath, and the first mate was holding me.” Cowardice is repeatedly translated into practical policy, as when the captain proposes summoning all hands because it is his duty to leave the ship last. Familiar objects and domestic comparisons reduce the fantastic creature to manageable absurdity: it approaches “like the swans in Victoria Park” and later plays beside the ship “like a kitten.” Conversely, Joe’s ordinary resentment becomes more consequential than the supernatural monster.

Structurally, the story follows a clear escalation. The serpent is first reported and mocked, then seen and feared, then fed, domesticated, and incorporated into a speculative money-making scheme. Once the crew becomes accustomed to the marvel, the narrative shifts its focus from the creature to Joe’s grievance. His apparently trauma-induced fits form a secondary comic mystery, with the narrator’s discovery that he is chewing soap revealing them as calculated performances. The final fit at the wheel activates the forbidden foghorn and drives the serpent away just before its planned capture. This delayed act of revenge supplies the climax and retrospectively makes the captain’s insult the pivotal event.

The pacing is brisk but episodic. Each scene develops around a distinct comic beat: Sam’s stutter, the officers’ panic, the feeding of the serpent, the ban on shipboard noises, Joe’s humiliation, the supposed breakdown, and the climactic foghorn incident. Repetition creates momentum, particularly in the serpent’s continued following, Joe’s recurring phrase “Frightened the sea-sarpint!,” and the sequence of increasingly implausible fits. Exaggeration is cumulative rather than lyrical; concrete details such as loaves, pork, shark hooks, wire rope, and the signaling cannon make each new absurdity seem temporarily reasonable.

The tone remains genial, deadpan, and mock-serious. Even cruelty, danger, and possible madness are filtered through practical shipboard comedy rather than sentiment or horror. The narrator rarely signals a joke explicitly; instead, he reports absurd behaviour with apparent sincerity. The ending adopts the form of a moral lesson—people should not be rude “even to the humblest”—but the moral is comically inadequate. Joe has deliberately sabotaged an extraordinary discovery, the captain’s greed has helped create the disaster, and the narrator reduces the whole affair to a lesson in politeness. The result is a style built on oral authenticity, social comedy, controlled exaggeration, economical characterization, and a final ironic mismatch between what the storyteller says and what the story reveals.

A father tells his children a familiar seasonal tale about two pumpkin seeds. The obedient seed wants only to grow into a fine pumpkin for Thanksgiving pies, while the proud, ambitious seed dreams of becoming a “pumpkin-glory” displayed among the morning-glories.

The good vine remains in its patch and devotes all its strength to producing one enormous pumpkin, which wins a prize and is later baked into pies. The ambitious vine struggles through weeds to reach and climb a fence, where it produces a single oddly shaped, long-necked pumpkin. A farm boy carves it into a jack-o’-lantern and displays it during a large Thanksgiving gathering.

The strange lantern delights the family until its mouth suddenly splits wider, frightening the elderly grandmother. In the resulting commotion, the lantern falls and breaks, and an escaped pig devours it, candle and all. The children question the story’s supposed warning against ambition, pointing out that the good pumpkin was eaten too, though their father and sister insist that being eaten by people is preferable to being eaten by a pig.

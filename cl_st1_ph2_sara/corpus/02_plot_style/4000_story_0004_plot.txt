Vasantasena, a princess enslaved after her father is killed in battle, is brought before the young King Vikramavati. Captivated by her beauty, the king frees her, showers her with riches and devotion, and expects his love to win hers. But Vasantasena remains unmoved, believing his self-sacrificing passion is ultimately selfish because he never considers whether she can return it.

Instead, she loves Madusadan, the seasoned captain of horse who rescued her during the battle. His patience, restraint, and understanding awakened her desire. When Vikramavati discovers them embracing, he condemns both to a painful death. Madusadan argues that love can accomplish anything except create love where none exists, and that death cannot erase the fulfillment they have shared.

The elderly prime minister proposes a subtler punishment: chain the lovers together and isolate them for three days in a luxurious room, predicting that constant proximity and monotony will turn passion into disgust. The king agrees, but after three days he and the minister overhear Vasantasena and Madusadan declaring their love as strongly as ever.

Their bond survives because the physical chain reflects the deeper links sustaining their love: passion, patience, fulfillment, friendship, tolerance, understanding, tenderness, forgiveness, service, and humor. The tale concludes that enduring love also requires uncertainty and eternal vigilance rather than complacent security.

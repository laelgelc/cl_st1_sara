A young warrior seeks a love potion from the wise Jehan Tugluk Khan, who answers with a cautionary tale about the nature of enduring love.

Vasantasena, a princess captured after her father’s defeat, is brought as a slave before King Vikramavati. Enraptured by her beauty, the king frees her, lavishes wealth and tenderness upon her, and offers her his entire heart. Yet she cannot return his love. Instead, she is drawn to Madusadan, the older captain of horse who rescued her during battle and won her desire through patience, restraint, and understanding.

When Vikramavati discovers the lovers together, he condemns them to death. Madusadan argues that love can accomplish almost anything except create love where none exists, and that death cannot erase what the couple has shared. The king’s prime minister proposes a subtler punishment: chain them together and isolate them in a luxurious room for three days, expecting constant proximity and monotony to turn passion into loathing.

After three days, however, the king and prime minister overhear Vasantasena and Madusadan still declaring their love. Jehan explains that the physical chain did not burden them because their love itself was a stronger chain, forged from passion, patience, fulfillment, friendship, tolerance, understanding, tenderness, forgiveness, service, and humor. He adds that lasting love also requires freedom from complacency, because it demands eternal vigilance.

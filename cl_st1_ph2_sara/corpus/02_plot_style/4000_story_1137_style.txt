The story uses a brisk, urbane comic-realistic style shaped by social observation, affectionate satire, and a final sting of irony. Its third-person narration stays close to Effie Bauer while repeatedly adopting the manner of a witty conversational raconteur. The narrator speaks directly to the reader, makes knowing generalisations, withholds information playfully, and occasionally comments on the machinery of romance itself: “So much for Effie. Enter the hero.” This intrusive voice creates intimacy while preventing the story from becoming wholly sentimental. Even when the subject is loneliness, ageing, or economic insecurity, the prose retains a raised eyebrow.

Characterisation depends heavily on material and occupational detail. Effie is rendered through clothes, cosmetics, posture, salary, shopping habits, and specialist knowledge of corsetry and fashion. Prices, brand-like product names, restaurant customs, sales departments, and consumer goods function as social shorthand. Such details locate her precisely within an urban mercantile world and show that taste, appearance, and financial independence are central to her identity. Physical description is similarly selective and comic: youthful clothing is contrasted with revealing feet, Gabe’s baldness with his romantic status, and Effie’s “kimono nature” with her “straight-front job.” Metaphors frequently draw on retail, bookkeeping, tailoring, food, and salesmanship, making the characters’ emotional lives inseparable from the commercial culture they inhabit.

The diction alternates between polished narrative language and highly idiomatic speech. The narration can be elaborately balanced and mock-formal, as in its invocation of Webster’s definition of a hero, but the dialogue is colloquial, compressed, and full of period slang: “Nix,” “dead one,” “girlie,” “live wires,” and “looka here.” Nonstandard grammar and phonetic touches distinguish Gabe’s speech and establish class, region, and ethnic milieu. Yiddish and German-derived expressions and references—such as “Ma selig,” “Goy,” and “pfannkuchen”—help create the social texture of early twentieth-century Jewish urban life, though they are filtered through conventions typical of the period. The dialogue is the story’s principal vehicle for personality: Effie speaks in sales-floor comparisons, hard financial calculations, and quick jokes, while Gabe’s bluntness, repetition, and awkward tenderness reveal a less articulate but genuine emotional steadiness.

Sentence structure is varied for comic timing. Long cumulative sentences pile up hardships, purchases, or qualifications until they culminate in a pointed observation; short fragments and one-line paragraphs then deliver punch lines or reversals. Parentheses and dashes imitate spontaneous speech and permit the narrator to insert sly asides. Lists are especially important, whether cataloguing the privations of working women, Effie’s luxuries, Gabe’s liabilities, or the imagined attentions of a husband. Repetition also shapes the rhythm: the quarterly visits, the twenty-fifth “anniversary,” Effie’s insistence that they are twenty years late, and Gabe’s renewed proposal all turn emotional hesitation into a patterned comic structure.

The plot follows a compact magazine-story design. An expository opening establishes Effie as an apparent exception to the stereotype of the impoverished working girl. The restaurant proposal then brings the central conflict—companionship versus autonomy—into explicit debate. Effie’s refusal is not coy but economically reasoned, giving the courtship scene the flavor of a negotiation or audit. Typhoid fever creates the reversal by stripping away her professional appearance, routine, and self-sufficiency. Her long account of an imaginary husband temporarily slows the pace and moves the story into a more openly lyrical and sentimental register. Domestic life is evoked through humble sensory details: cold cheeks, a wet mustache, noisy footsteps, misplaced shirts, children at supper. This imagined scene supplies the emotional home that Effie’s successful public life lacks.

Sentiment is continually checked by comedy. Effie’s illness produces real vulnerability, but she describes her appearance as “a hunk of Roquefort”; Gabe’s compassionate lie is clumsy enough to be funny; and the renewed proposal concludes not with elevated romantic language but with a request for “a couple of fool names.” The story’s tenderness therefore grows out of ordinary gestures rather than idealised passion. Love is represented as fussing, worrying, tiptoeing, and belonging to someone, while marriage is simultaneously a financial arrangement that threatens consumption and personal freedom.

The ending restores the retail setting and overturns the apparent resolution. Once Effie regains her health, professional vigor, and appetite for fashionable goods, the prospect of economic restraint becomes newly intolerable. Her decision to cancel the “arrangement” with Gabe over an embroidered blouse is deliberately disproportionate and comic. It also complicates the preceding sentiment: her longing for marriage was sincere, but so is her attachment to independence, luxury, and the identity she has built through work. The final question about the blouse size lands as a punch line, returning the story to the language of merchandise and leaving Effie caught between incompatible desires. This circular ending is characteristic of the story’s method: emotional revelation briefly promises transformation, but habit, social circumstance, and comic human inconsistency reassert themselves.

Overall, the style combines realist detail, vernacular dialogue, narrative wit, controlled sentimentality, and twist-ending construction. Its tone is amused rather than cruel, sympathetic without idealising its characters. The governing tension is between romance and economics, with clothing and commerce serving not merely as background but as the story’s central symbolic vocabulary.

The story adopts the style of a traditional sentimental fairy tale or moral folktale, with a pastoral setting, a simple linear plot, and an explicit emotional lesson about love, loneliness, and chosen family. Its world is deliberately timeless and lightly sketched: the river, mill, fields, shady lanes, and great house establish an idealised rural landscape rather than a historically precise location. Stock social figures—the solitary miller, the innocent foundling, the young Squire, and the observant country folk—reinforce the folktale quality.

The narration is third-person omniscient and warmly intrusive. The narrator explains motives, judges character, anticipates consequences, and occasionally addresses the reader directly, as in “Do you think the miller was angry?” This creates the intimate manner of an oral storyteller speaking to children. Moral interpretation is frequently supplied rather than left implicit: the miller is described as neither sour nor bad, his longing for affection is attributed to “the nature of all good men,” and Farmer Dobson plainly states the paradox that nobody cares for him because he will not allow them to do so.

The diction is clear, accessible, and gently old-fashioned. Expressions such as “behold,” “quoth he,” “as was his wont,” “waif,” “maiden,” and “pay court” lend the prose an archaic storybook flavour without making it difficult to read. Sentences often use coordinating conjunctions—especially “and,” “but,” “for,” and “so”—to produce an accumulative, oral rhythm. Repeated explanatory clauses make causes and feelings unmistakable, while balanced formulations such as “not an old man, nor a sour man, nor a bad man” give the narration a measured, almost proverbial cadence.

Repetition is the central stylistic device. The mill wheel’s onomatopoeic “Cree-e-eekety-cruck-crick!” turns machinery into rustic music, while the miller’s recurring song functions as a refrain and an index of his emotional state. Its words first express defensive isolation, then become ironically obsolete after Nathalie’s arrival, and finally return when she marries. Each alteration of the song marks a stage in his emotional education. The repetitive daily routines of grinding, singing, feeding the child, and Nathalie’s later visits also create stability and reinforce the tale’s reassuring pattern.

Dialogue is brief, direct, and emotionally transparent. Characters generally say exactly what they feel, and exchanges are designed to reveal moral or emotional turning points rather than social complexity. Nathalie’s question about why the miller claims that nobody loves him corrects his habitual self-deception; the Squire’s proposal introduces the fear of loss; and Nathalie’s final assurance resolves the false opposition between romantic and filial love. Speech tags and descriptive modifiers such as “anxiously,” “with a happy laugh,” and “with a heavy heart” guide the reader’s response closely.

Characterisation is archetypal but affectionate. The miller is defined by a contradiction between his professed indifference and his suppressed need for love. His change is shown through practical tenderness—feeding the baby, soothing her, allowing her to pull his whiskers—as well as through the changing song. Nathalie is idealised as beautiful, innocent, loving, and grateful, while the Squire is a courteous fairy-tale suitor rather than a disruptive antagonist. Conflict remains mild and domestic: even the miller’s possessiveness arises from fear of renewed solitude, not cruelty.

The tone moves from quaint comedy and rustic charm to tenderness, anxiety, and gentle pathos, but it never becomes tragic. Diminutives and tactile details—“soft little noises,” “tiny arms,” “silken ringlets,” and “little dimpled hand”—heighten the sentimentality of the foundling episode. Emotional states are openly named, and scenes are shaped to invite sympathy rather than ambiguity. The river-box motif faintly recalls legendary foundling narratives, but danger is quickly converted into domestic security.

Structurally, the story proceeds episodically through decisive stages: the miller’s isolation, the rescue of the baby, Nathalie’s childhood, her correction of his song, her maturation and courtship, the marriage, and the final reassurance. Time passes rapidly through summary phrases such as “year after year” and “the years passed by,” while key emotional moments are slowed into dialogue and close observation. The ending restores harmony without restoring the old household: Nathalie forms a new family but continues to love the miller, teaching him that affection is not diminished by being shared.

Overall, the style is earnest, melodious, didactic, and emotionally uncomplicated. It relies on refrain, direct narration, idealised characters, pastoral imagery, and explicit moral reassurance. Its governing idea is that self-protective isolation creates the very neglect it fears, whereas love transforms both identity and perception; the miller’s songs make that lesson audible throughout the tale.

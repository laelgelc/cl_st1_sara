The story uses an intimate first-person voice that confines the reader to the country doctor’s anxious, increasingly unreliable perception. The narration combines retrospective storytelling with abrupt shifts into the present tense, making events feel immediate and unstable. The doctor continuously reports not only what he sees but also his rapid calculations, grievances, rationalisations and self-reproaches. This creates a voice that is lucid at the sentence level yet powerless to make coherent sense of the situation as a whole.

The prose begins in a plain, practical register, with short declarative sentences establishing distance, weather, transport and professional obligation. Concrete details—large carriage wheels, a bag of instruments, a lantern, harnesses and a fur coat—lend procedural realism to the opening. That realism is gradually invaded by impossibilities which are narrated with almost no change in tone: horses emerge from an abandoned pigsty, a ten-mile journey collapses into an instant, animals watch through windows, and a village ritualistically strips the doctor. The restrained acceptance of these violations of reality gives the story its dreamlike, absurd and claustrophobic quality.

Sentence structure alternates between clipped statements and long, accumulating passages. The short sentences convey urgency, shock and resignation, while longer sentences reproduce the doctor’s spiralling thoughts through clauses, qualifications, repetitions and self-interruptions. Dashes and parenthetical turns allow the narration to leap rapidly between the patient, Rosa, the horses and the doctor’s professional frustrations. Repetition is emphatic and incantatory, particularly in phrases such as “the horse—the horse,” “Betrayed! Betrayed!” and the recurring concern with duty, sacrifice and return. Parallel constructions and lists also reinforce the sense of mechanical obligation and entrapment.

The diction is mostly direct and accessible, but it periodically becomes clinical, grotesque or lyrical. Medical observation supplies a detached vocabulary of circulation, fever, instruments and diagnosis, while the wound is described with vivid colour, texture and depth. It is simultaneously anatomical, floral and geological: a “flower,” a “mining pit,” and a living cavity filled with worms. This blending of beauty and bodily horror is central to the style. Ordinary domestic objects likewise acquire symbolic force, especially doors, windows, chains, clothing, the carriage and the unreachable fur coat.

Dialogue is brief, compressed and often menacing. Characters speak in commands, demands, ritual formulas and bleak paradoxes rather than in naturalistic conversation. The groom’s laconic “Giddy up,” the patient’s request to die and the children’s songs all reduce complicated moral situations to chillingly simple phrases. The songs are especially effective because their childish rhythm contrasts with their threat of violence. Conversations do not produce mutual understanding; instead, they expose the gulf between doctor and patient, professional and community, rescuer and victim.

The tone moves through urgency, bewilderment, dark comedy, disgust, guilt and despair without clean transitions. Comic incongruities—the miraculous horses, the doctor’s bureaucratic complaints, the thought of travelling with pigs—sit beside sexual violence, bodily corruption and existential terror. The doctor’s dry comments and professional maxims provide ironic distance, but this detachment repeatedly collapses under his guilt about Rosa and his awareness that he cannot fulfil the impossible expectations placed upon him.

Pacing is asymmetrical and dream governed. The outward journey occurs instantaneously, while the return becomes painfully slow and apparently endless. Early obstacles are resolved by uncanny intervention, but every solution creates a worse form of captivity. The plot follows an escalating sequence of reversals: the missing horse is replaced by supernatural horses; the apparently healthy patient reveals a fatal wound; the healer becomes the one placed in bed; and the rapid rescue journey turns into permanent exile. Causal logic remains tenuous, yet emotional and symbolic logic is strong.

The imagery repeatedly opposes heat and cold, enclosure and exposure, movement and paralysis, the earthly and the unearthly. Snow covers and immobilises the doctor; the sickroom suffocates with heat and smoke; the wound opens toward the light; and the ending leaves him naked in a “snowy desert.” Animals possess an uncanny agency lacking in the human characters, while thresholds—gates, sty doors, house doors and windows—mark unstable passages between ordinary life and nightmare.

Overall, the style is surreal, grotesque and darkly ironic, but delivered through sober, exact narration. Its distinctive effect comes from treating irrational events as administrative facts, combining professional realism with nightmare transformations, and allowing the narrator’s orderly language to reveal rather than control a world governed by coercion, guilt and irreversible error. The unresolved ending preserves this logic of entrapment: the story does not conclude so much as condemn its narrator to continue indefinitely.

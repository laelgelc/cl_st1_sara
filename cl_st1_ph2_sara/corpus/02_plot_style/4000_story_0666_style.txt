The story adopts the form of a private letter addressed to an intimate friend, creating the impression of a recovered personal document rather than a conventional tale. Its first-person narrator recounts extraordinary events retrospectively, with the measured confidence of someone offering testimony. Direct address—“you ask me,” “as you know,” “good friend”—establishes a knowledgeable recipient and allows background information to be supplied without abandoning the epistolary illusion. The narrator is intelligent, articulate and broadly reliable about external events, but his uncertain identity, involuntary impulses, lost intervals of consciousness and imperfect recollection introduce psychological instability.

The prose is formal, polished and late-nineteenth-century in character. Its diction combines legalistic precision, philosophical qualification and Gothic intensity. The narrator repeatedly limits or corrects his assertions through phrases such as “I believe,” “it seemed,” “as nearly as” and “without assignable motive,” lending his account an evidentiary tone while preserving uncertainty. Long, balanced sentences carry explanation and reflection, whereas short declarations and abrupt exclamations mark moments of revelation or terror. Colons, semicolons, parenthetical qualifications and dashes are used heavily to control rhythm, defer information and insert ironic afterthoughts. Dialogue is comparatively concise and socially decorous, though it often conceals threat, shame or emotional crisis beneath restrained language.

The dominant tonal blend is urbane irony and supernatural dread. The early treatment of the twins’ indistinguishability is comic: mistaken names, exchanged clothes, failed tattoos and the collective name “Jehnry” turn identity into a family joke. Dryly paradoxical phrases such as being “heartlessly accepted” in marriage maintain a sardonic narrative voice. As the plot advances, however, the same confusion of identities becomes ominous. Humour gradually gives way to moral unease, fatalism and horror, making the tonal transition feel like the dark fulfilment of an initially harmless premise.

The narrative is tightly patterned around doubling, mistaken identity and unexplained sympathetic knowledge. The narrator knows Margovan’s name without knowing the man, while his brother independently asks for Margovan’s address; he follows Julia and her lover under an irrational compulsion; and he hears his twin’s death cry at a distance before moving almost unconsciously to the correct house. These incidents escalate from curious coincidence to apparent psychic connection. The story never provides a definitive explanation, instead framing the supernatural as something that may belong to natural laws not yet understood. This produces an ambiguity characteristic of psychological Gothic fiction: telepathy, intuition, coincidence, dissociation and paranormal influence remain competing possibilities.

Structurally, the story proceeds as a chronological confession interrupted by reflective commentary. It begins with a philosophical challenge, supplies the twins’ history, introduces a chain of increasingly consequential impulses, climaxes in the simultaneous deaths of John and Julia, and then adds a delayed coda. That coda mirrors the earlier Union Square encounter and supplies retributive closure without solving the central mystery. The unidentified lover mistakes the surviving twin for the dead John and dies in terror, so physical resemblance functions almost as supernatural vengeance. The final aphorism—“To know of a man that he is dead should be enough”—ends the account with bleak, dismissive finality.

Pacing is controlled through selective summary and sharply dramatized scenes. Years and repeated social visits are compressed into a sentence, while encounters involving recognition, suspicion or death unfold through dialogue, gesture and precise sensory detail. Suspense frequently depends on delayed disclosure: the narrator follows strangers before learning who the woman is, confronts Julia without fully stating what he suspects, and reaches the Margovan house before the deaths are explained. The narrator also foreshadows catastrophe through retrospective phrases such as “fateful assignation” and through repeated declarations of inexplicable impulse.

Characterization is economical and filtered almost entirely through conduct. John remains comparatively indistinct, which reinforces the theme that the brothers are interchangeable. Julia is rendered through beauty, composure, pallor and guarded speech; her willingness to “accept” the narrator’s terms suggests guilt or coercion but leaves her motives unresolved. The lover is first merely dissipated and later physically ruined, his moral decline externalized in his appearance. The narrator’s own character emerges through self-justification, ironic candour, compassion and questionable curiosity. He admits to impersonation and spying while carefully defending or qualifying both actions, creating a subtle tension between confession and self-exoneration.

The settings are urban, specific and realistic—Market Street, Kearney Street, Union Square and other San Francisco locations—but they acquire Gothic force through atmosphere. Fog, wet pavement, moonlight, shadows, deserted public spaces and wan street lamps turn the modern city into a stage for secrecy and haunting. Domestic interiors likewise shift from sociable drawing rooms to chambers containing poisoned and wounded bodies. Concrete geographical detail anchors the uncanny events in an otherwise credible world.

Imagery intensifies at moments of psychic disturbance. The brother’s cry cuts through thought “as a tense cord is parted by the stroke of steel”; the street lamp casts a “wan and ghastly light”; and the narrator distinguishes physical shivering from inward shuddering. Such images favour coldness, pallor, wounds, shadows and bodily collapse. Repetition and mirroring are equally important: two brothers, two names, two lovers, two cries, two encounters in Union Square and two deaths at the Margovan house create a formal network of doubles.

Overall, the style is best described as an epistolary psychological-Gothic narrative with a sardonic, controlled surface. Its characteristic method is to place an impossible or uncanny sequence inside a precise, rationally argued testimony, using wit to establish credibility, ambiguity to sustain unease and a final macabre reversal to convert resemblance into fate.

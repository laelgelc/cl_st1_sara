The story uses a clear, controlled late-Victorian/Edwardian realist style, combining social observation with dry moral comedy. Its prose is formal but accessible, with an omniscient narrator who moves easily between outward description, compressed biography, psychological analysis, and ironic commentary. The opening is economical and visual: two men, their contrasting clothes and postures, and a table covered with work immediately establish the unequal relationship. Material details such as the cigarette, wooden penholder, sixpenny piece, papers, advertisements, and shabby lodging carry much of the social meaning. Poverty and status are rendered through concrete objects rather than elaborate scenic description.

The narration is strongly character-centred. It frequently explains motives and contradictions in compact, balanced sentences, especially when describing Starkey’s self-deception: his laziness is recast in his own mind as unrewarded industry, and his selfishness as unworldliness. This technique creates irony by placing a character’s flattering self-image beside the narrator’s blunt judgement. Aphoristic formulations—such as the observation that Starkey “did everything by halves and snarl[ed] over the ineffectual results”—give the prose satirical sharpness. The narrator is authoritative and morally discriminating, but not solemn; the final remark, “Such thoughts are very consoling,” quietly exposes Topham’s rationalisation without explicitly condemning him.

Sentence structure varies according to function. Expository passages use long, carefully subordinated sentences to trace changes in feeling, working conditions, or social circumstances. Moments of conflict and decision shift into shorter clauses, abrupt dialogue, exclamations, and fragments. Parallel constructions and antitheses are common: well dressed versus seedy, idler versus labourer, moral fitness versus practical deception, gentleman versus shopkeeper. These oppositions reinforce the story’s concern with the disparity between appearance and reality. Punctuation is expressive but controlled, with dashes marking hesitation or evasion, semicolons organising analytical prose, and exclamation marks conveying indignation, delight, or theatrical self-presentation.

Dialogue is the principal means of dramatisation and social characterisation. Starkey speaks in commands, accusations, and manipulative euphemisms; his earlier promise of partnership and improved terms is exposed as calculated exploitation. Topham’s speech changes with circumstance. He begins terse and resentful, but when impersonating Starkey before Wigmore he adopts expansive professional assurances—“my dear sir,” “competent aid,” and “no doubt whatever”—that mimic the bland commercial rhetoric he despises. Wigmore’s speech is deferential, repetitive, hesitant, and emotionally transparent. His frequent use of “sir,” his self-corrections, and his pauses reveal both social insecurity and fundamental sincerity. The slight dialect marker “yoursel” and references to his west-country accent localise him without turning him into caricature.

The tonal movement is from grim domestic realism to comic opportunity and finally to morally ambiguous farce. Topham is initially presented as an exploited, humiliated worker, and the sixpence becomes a concentrated symbol of his dependence. Wigmore’s arrival transforms the plot: his naïve ambition offers Topham an escape, but also tempts him into impersonation and fraud. The comedy depends on dramatic irony, especially the contrast between Wigmore’s reverence for “Mr. Starkey” and the reader’s knowledge that he is speaking to the assistant. It also arises from Topham’s rapid adaptation to the role of the very charlatan he resents. His internal cry of “Ass! I always undervalue myself” turns a potentially solemn negotiation into self-mocking social comedy.

Characterisation is mixed rather than purely schematic. Starkey is treated with open satirical hostility, yet Topham is not idealised as his innocent opposite. He is industrious, educated, and genuinely capable of teaching Wigmore, but also opportunistic, deceptive, and increasingly adept at self-exculpation. Wigmore is comic in his aspiration to become a curate for the sake of family respectability, yet his kindness, perseverance, and wounded paternal pride give the comedy pathos. This balance prevents the story from becoming either a simple revenge fantasy or a harsh exposure of social pretension.

The central thematic vocabulary concerns work, money, education, respectability, gentility, and professional identity. Social rank is shown as performative and unstable: Starkey’s degree and advertised authority conceal incompetence; Topham’s damaged reputation conceals real ability; Wigmore’s successful trade seems shameful to him beside the prestige of the Church. Education functions both as a genuine means of self-improvement and as a commodity sold through inflated promises. Clerical aspiration is likewise treated ambivalently, at once sincere, socially vain, and commercially exploitable.

Structurally, the story is linear and tightly causal. The first two chapters establish oppression and desperation; the third introduces the accidental opportunity; the fourth completes the negotiation and supplies a brisk retrospective resolution. Backstory is inserted only where it clarifies present motives. The decisive encounter unfolds largely as a dialogue scene, allowing suspense to grow through Wigmore’s reluctance to name his ambition and Topham’s gradual recognition of its value. The ending accelerates through summary, letters, confession, and retrospective explanation, then closes on an ironic epigram rather than a dramatic climax.

Overall, the style is best described as socially observant realism sharpened by satirical omniscience. Its distinguishing features are economical physical detail, psychologically analytical narration, class-marked dialogue, balanced syntax, understated irony, and an ending that leaves moral judgement suspended between justice and fraud. The prose invites sympathy for weakness and hardship while remaining alert to vanity, hypocrisy, and the human talent for turning self-interest into a consoling moral narrative.

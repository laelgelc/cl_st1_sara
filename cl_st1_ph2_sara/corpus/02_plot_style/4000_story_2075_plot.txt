On a beautiful June afternoon, the narrator and companions drive through the rural New England countryside toward Ogunquit. Along roads lined with woods, farms, flowers, and white rosebushes, they observe wildlife, working families, a young girl tending her own garden, and signs of both agricultural endurance and gradual decline.

They visit an old hilltop farmhouse where a war-wounded farmer, now disabled, lives with two orphaned grandchildren. The narrator recalls his late wife, who endured years of illness and the deaths of her children with extraordinary patience, and reflects on the family’s generations of hardship. After leaving, the travelers continue past gardens and historic landmarks, recalling a similarly devoted gardener in Virginia.

On the homeward journey, they encounter people returning from a funeral and learn of other deaths and illnesses nearby. Yet the day’s beauty keeps grief from overwhelming them. They follow part of an old Indigenous trail beside the river and lament the disappearance of fish caused by industrial pollution. As evening falls and they return to the village, the narrator’s frustration gives way to hope that people may eventually care more wisely for the land. The journey remains in memory as a deeply pleasurable but gently sorrowful vision of rural life, shaped by beauty, labor, loss, and resilience.

The story adopts the manner of a nineteenth-century historical romance while continually undercutting its grandeur with dry comedy. Its dominant mode is the mock chronicle: events are presented as though they were part of a sober historical record, complete with dates, political context, hearsay, appeals to precedent, and professions of documentary caution. The narrator repeatedly assumes the dignity of “a grave chronicler,” yet reports drunken dancing, dubious trade goods, and a glass eye with ceremonious seriousness. This discrepancy between elevated presentation and absurd subject matter supplies much of the humour.

The narrator is omniscient, intrusive, and overtly self-conscious. He pauses to interpret history, qualify rumours, address the reader, defend characters, and distinguish alleged events from supposedly reliable facts. Phrases such as “I have said,” “it becomes me not,” “it is further alleged,” and “when the reader reflects” imitate the language of an antiquarian historian. These interventions create ironic distance: the narrator’s claims to impartiality are plainly playful, and his ostensible respect for religious authority, colonial discipline, and Yankee honesty often signals the opposite. Verbal irony is especially prominent in passages praising the “stringent discipline” of an almost undefended fort or the “proverbial indisposition” of traders to deceive simple people.

The diction is formal, Latinate, and deliberately old-fashioned. Words such as “albeit,” “vouchsafed,” “aught,” “divers,” “seemly,” “baleful,” and “prescience” lend the narration an archaic, ceremonial quality. Spanish titles, Catholic terminology, nautical language, and historical references provide local colour and reinforce the colonial setting. Elevated phrases are frequently placed beside homely or comic details—the missing Toledo sword has become a child’s toy, the foreign captain smells of codfish, and supposedly valuable cargo consists of hard nutmegs and shoe pegs. This mingling of the stately and the mundane is a defining stylistic device.

The syntax is expansive and carefully balanced. Long, multi-clause sentences accumulate description, qualifications, contrasts, and parenthetical observations, often arriving at a comic reversal near the end. Semicolons and dashes control these elaborations, while parallel constructions give the prose a rhetorical cadence. Shorter sentences are reserved for turns in the action or emphatic revelations: “When the day broke the ship was gone”; “There was no mistake”; “It was gone.” The resulting rhythm alternates between leisurely historical narration and brisk, theatrical incident.

Description is sensuous and atmospheric, particularly in the opening storm and the later drought. The prose makes frequent use of personification and symbolic correspondences: sea foam clings and quivers, the wind gives colour to the Commander’s cheeks, Nature acquires his “dry, hard glare,” and the tearless sky mirrors his artificial eye. Seasonal imagery also structures the historical background. Spanish rule is figured as an indolent autumn, Mexican independence as winter, and American conquest as spring. This picturesque language gives the setting romantic breadth, even when the narrative is treating its inhabitants satirically.

Characterisation proceeds through emblematic details, social contrasts, and comic action rather than extended psychological analysis. Salvatierra initially appears paternal, ceremonious, kindly, and slightly ridiculous: he admires children’s handwriting, possesses an unusable fort, and cannot find his sword. Peleg Scudder is drawn as a recognisable Yankee trader—practical, irreverent, loquacious, commercially alert, and morally slippery. Their encounter juxtaposes Spanish formality and pastoral inertia with American mobility, commerce, and opportunism. Paquita functions differently: her simple speech cuts through adult superstition and policy, restoring emotional sincerity to a narrative otherwise dominated by irony.

Dialogue is relatively sparse and stylised. Salvatierra’s speech is courteous, devotional, and fond of affectionate diminutives, while Peleg’s character is conveyed mainly through reported conversation rather than direct quotation. The child’s dialogue is plain, repetitive, and emotionally direct, especially in the contrast between the Commander’s “old voice” and the alien eye. This simplicity gives her scene unusual pathos and marks the moral turning point of the story.

The plot has the compact shape of a comic supernatural folktale. A storm introduces the disruptive stranger; a mysterious gift produces apparent benefit; the benefit becomes a curse; social and natural disorder intensify; and the loss of the object restores harmony. The glass eye is both a literal comic prop and the story’s governing symbol. It represents suspicion, surveillance, scepticism, and an imported hardness that gradually reshapes the Commander’s personality. The repeated motif of seeing—gazing, supervision, winking, searching looks, blindness, and false vision—binds the narrative together. The central paradox is that the artificial eye cannot see, yet causes others to feel completely seen.

The tone shifts without abandoning its comic foundation. The opening is picturesque and gently satirical; the meeting with Peleg becomes farcical; the eye’s influence introduces unease and moral allegory; and the attempted assassination briefly moves into melodrama. Even at its darkest, however, the story retains a controlled, amused detachment. The ending restores pastoral order in deliberately comprehensive fashion—rain, grass, church, children, soldiers, and priests all return to harmony—before a final ironic glance at Peleg trafficking in “glass eyes, wooden legs, and other Boston notions.” This coda converts the supernatural episode back into commercial satire.

Overall, the style combines historical pastiche, romantic landscape writing, tall-tale invention, Gothic suggestion, and satirical realism. Its signature effect comes from narrating implausible events in dignified, quasi-historical prose while allowing concrete comic details to expose the vanity, credulity, and cultural misunderstandings beneath official appearances.

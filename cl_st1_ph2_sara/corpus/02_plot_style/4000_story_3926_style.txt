The story adopts a comic, satirical mode built around irony, exaggeration, and the repeated collision between literary theory and lived experience. Its central conceit is that Severne’s fanatical commitment to realism produces events so extraordinary that everyone interprets his truthful account as romantic invention. The narrative continually exposes the absurdity of rigid artistic principles, while also mocking editorial dogma, romantic self-dramatisation, Western conventions, and the solemnity of writers who take themselves too seriously.

The narrator is omniscient, intrusive, and conversational. Rather than remaining invisible, the narrative voice comments directly on characters and events, offers mock generalisations about editors, writers, lovers, girls, and serious-minded youths, and occasionally confesses uncertainty about minor details. Remarks such as uncertainty over whether the setting is a “dark gorge” or “narrow canon” deliberately puncture narrative authority and turn storytelling itself into part of the joke. The narrator often addresses the reader through phrases such as “you must see” and “conceive,” creating the atmosphere of an amused raconteur sharing a comic anecdote. This voice remains detached enough to recognise every character’s folly, but it is genial rather than cruel.

The prose alternates between brisk, declarative sentences and longer passages of mock-elevated exposition. Short sequences—“He glanced the story through. Then he read it carefully.”—create comic timing and imitate the “short, crisp sentences” praised within the story. Elsewhere, elaborate syntax parodies sentimental, philosophical, or melodramatic writing. Elevated formulations about martyrdom, shattered lives, noble sacrifice, and applauding angels are placed beside mundane realities such as bad hotel breakfasts, office clutter, fountain-pen maintenance, and sticky oatmeal. This contrast between grand rhetoric and trivial fact is one of the style’s principal comic mechanisms.

The diction is eclectic. Plain narration and colloquial phrasing coexist with deliberately pompous abstractions, literary terminology, Western slang, and mock-scientific comparisons. Words and phrases such as “poetic connotation,” “symbolical truth,” “Nebular Hypothesis,” and “perperoid lucubrations” caricature intellectual pretension, while dialect forms such as “pards,” “cayuse,” “ketched,” and “yore” establish the Western setting and sharpen the cultural contrast between Severne and the cowboys. Adjectives and descriptive formulas are frequently repeated—especially “short and crisp,” “served hot,” “serious-minded,” and “no sense of humour”—so that they become comic refrains.

Characterisation is largely caricatural and organised around governing traits. Brown is the dogmatic editor who worships realism; Severne is seriousness without perspective; Lucy is an equally doctrinaire romanticist; and the Westerners operate according to their own practical but mistaken assumptions. Psychological subtlety is less important than the escalating consequences of these fixed dispositions. Severne’s lack of humour is repeated so insistently that it becomes both the explanation for the plot and a running joke. His inward life is presented through mock-heroic fantasies in which ordinary disappointment becomes martyrdom, cosmic sacrifice, or the wreckage from which a monumental work of art will someday arise.

Dialogue is lively, theatrical, and strongly differentiated by social type. Brown speaks in editorial manifestos, Lucy in impassioned romantic declarations, Severne in grave abstractions, and the cowboys in compressed vernacular. Conversations often proceed as comic debates in which no participant truly understands the others. The dialogue also generates dramatic irony: readers recognise the absurd implications of Severne’s statements while the characters interpret them according to their own prejudices. Exclamation marks, interruptions, emphatic repetitions, and quick exchanges give confrontations a farcical energy.

The plot is tightly constructed through escalation and reversal. It begins as literary satire, shifts into romantic disagreement, expands into a parody of the Western adventure, and returns to the publishing world for the final ironic resolution. Recurring phrases and ideas provide cohesion: realism, imagination, crisp sentences, hot adjectives, poetic connotation, and Severne’s solemnity reappear in altered contexts. The staged pursuit becomes real; the resulting truthful story is rejected as implausible by the realist editor; it is accepted elsewhere as fiction; and Lucy reads it as proof that Severne has abandoned realism for her sake. Each reversal grows logically from the characters’ misconceptions.

Pacing varies according to comic purpose. Expository passages rapidly summarise Severne’s career, beliefs, and courtship, while the chase and attempted hanging are narrated in more immediate, sequential detail. Even during danger, the narrator sustains a light tone through understatement and incongruity. Hanging is treated with grotesque casualness, and Severne’s near death is framed as an unusually rich supply of “realistic experience.” Violence therefore functions less as tragedy than as farcical escalation, though its genuine peril intensifies the absurdity.

The story is highly metafictional. It discusses how stories should be written, edited, judged, typed, sold, and interpreted while simultaneously demonstrating competing styles. Its realism is itself stylised, its Western episode uses recognisable adventure conventions, and its romantic conclusion depends on a misunderstanding about genre. The text repeatedly suggests that “reality” in fiction is determined not simply by factual truth but by plausibility, expectation, and the reader’s interpretive frame. Art and life mirror and distort one another until a perfectly accurate account appears imaginary.

The ending condenses the story’s method into a final comic surrender. Severne briefly sees an obligation to defend the truth and preserve his artistic principles, but romantic opportunity defeats theoretical consistency. The closing question—“And Art; oh, where was it?”—is mock-tragic, rhetorical, and self-consciously literary. It leaves the story on an ironic note: artistic dogma has vanished not through reasoned conversion, but through desire, timing, and a kiss. Overall, the style is urbane, playful, episodic, and self-aware, with humour arising from deadpan narration, mock grandeur, verbal repetition, social caricature, and carefully prepared reversals.

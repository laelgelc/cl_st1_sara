Wealthy financier Arnold Thorndike is asked to write a character reference for Henry Spear, a former stenographer awaiting sentencing for stealing his employer’s money while drunk. Thorndike initially offers only a lukewarm endorsement, but on the morning of the sentencing, memories of Spear’s kindness to his mother prompt him to visit the courthouse and intervene personally.

Accustomed to privilege and efficiency, Thorndike is irritated and humbled by the court’s delays, unfamiliar procedures, and indifference to his status. He nearly leaves, but the anxious gaze of Spear’s mother and Spear’s sudden hope upon seeing him persuade Thorndike to stay. As he watches other defendants receive judgment, he becomes absorbed in their struggles and discovers a genuine desire to help them.

Spear is ultimately released into probation because his employers and other supporters testify to his good character, on the condition that he abstain from alcohol. Although Thorndike never formally speaks for him, the judge publicly praises his presence as an act of civic generosity. Deeply gratified, Thorndike comforts Spear’s mother, encourages Spear, and offers him future employment. Transformed by the experience, he ignores urgent business matters and spends the rest of the day with the assistant district attorney, deciding that whatever his lost time cost, helping Spear was worth it.

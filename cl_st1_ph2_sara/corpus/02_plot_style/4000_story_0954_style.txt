The story uses a brisk, comic, omniscient narrative style associated with early twentieth-century children’s fiction. The narrator remains close to William’s point of view while retaining an amused adult perspective, presenting his appetites, fantasies, faulty reasoning and moral evasions with mock seriousness. William’s experiences are treated as momentous adventures, but the narration continually exposes the gap between his heroic self-image and the disorder he creates. Occasional direct addresses to the reader—most notably the rhetorical appeal near the ending—strengthen the confidential, storytelling tone.

The prose is clear, energetic and largely chronological. Short narrative passages alternate with extended stretches of rapid dialogue, giving scenes the timing of a stage comedy. Sentences are generally compact, although longer, more elaborately balanced sentences appear when the narrator comments ironically or heightens a comic description. Repetition is an important device: William repeatedly adds strained formulas such as “if you don’t mind me sayin’ so” to insults and lies, while phrases like “quite all right” acquire increasing comic force as circumstances become less and less all right. Lists and accumulations—especially the names of sweets and the catalogue of William’s depredations—create abundance, momentum and sensory excess.

Dialogue is the chief means of characterization. William’s speech is rendered phonetically through dropped consonants, contractions, non-standard grammar and childish mispronunciations: “goin’,” “wun’t,” “p’lite,” “menshun” and similar forms. Mr. Moss’s working-class pronunciation is represented in the same way, while the other characters’ speech varies according to age, temperament and social position. The thin customer speaks with rigid correctness and indignation; William’s family responds with suspicion, irony or teasing; his friends communicate in terse, excited phrases. This contrast between formal politeness and colloquial speech drives much of the humour.

The dominant tone is affectionate satire. William is greedy, dishonest, destructive and self-important, but he is not presented as malicious. His misconduct grows from literal-mindedness, impulsiveness and a limitless ability to believe his own inventions. The narrator describes these faults in elevated language, turning childish absurdity into mock epic: the resolution becomes “a kind of god,” the sweet-shop resembles “Aladdin’s Cave,” and the sweet-throwing brawl becomes a “noble fight against heavy odds.” Such mock-heroic phrasing comically dignifies trivial events while preserving sympathy for the protagonist.

Character description often depends on exaggerated contrast. William’s rough, freckled, noisy physicality is set against the little girl’s idealized white clothes, golden curls, blue eyes and “silvery” voice. His infatuation briefly shifts the prose into sentimental romantic parody, complete with melting emotion, imagined rescues and ceremonious generosity. The elevated language remains filtered through childish fantasy, however, and is punctured by physical comedy as William continues stuffing sweets into his mouth. Adults are likewise reduced to sharply defined comic types: the lugubrious shopkeeper, the suspicious father, the anxious mother and the censorious customer.

The humour arises primarily from incongruity, escalation and dramatic irony. William interprets politeness as a set of detachable verbal formulas rather than a mode of considerate behaviour, so courtesy is mechanically appended to rudeness, fraud and aggression. Each episode enlarges the consequences of this misunderstanding: a modest New Year’s resolution leads to shopkeeping, overeating, false pricing, reckless generosity, vandalism and street battle. Readers understand the likely consequences long before William does, while he repeatedly recasts accidents and unauthorized privileges as proof of his own importance.

The story’s structure is tightly comic and circular. It begins with William obtaining sweets through verbal ingenuity, establishes politeness as the central premise, then places him in a sweet-shop where appetite, fantasy and opportunity combine. A succession of visitors tests the premise in different ways: the messenger creates the practical crisis, the hostile customer exposes William’s dishonesty, the little girl prompts romantic generosity, his friends encourage excess, and the older boy triggers violence. The climax arrives with Mr. Moss’s return, but expected punishment is cancelled by the shopkeeper’s romantic happiness. The final consequence is therefore bodily rather than moral: William escapes blame but cannot escape nausea.

Sensory detail is concentrated around food. Brand-like names such as “Cokernut Kisses,” “Pineapple Crisp,” “Nutty Footballs” and “Mixed Dew Drops” give the shop a vivid, almost magical allure. Their playful capitalisation and extravagant variety recreate a child’s fascinated perception of confectionery. As consumption continues, the imagery shifts subtly from temptation to surfeit, culminating in William’s pallor, unsteadiness and aversion to lunch. This physical arc provides an understated comic punishment without imposing an explicit moral lesson.

Overall, the style combines colloquial immediacy, polished narrative irony, affectionate caricature and carefully controlled escalation. It views childhood neither sentimentally nor didactically, but as a self-contained world governed by appetite, imagination and ingenious misinterpretation. The closing mock-archaic phrase “I trow not” epitomizes the method: an adult narrator applies ceremonious language to a universal childhood catastrophe, ending the tale with sympathy, amusement and ironic grandeur.

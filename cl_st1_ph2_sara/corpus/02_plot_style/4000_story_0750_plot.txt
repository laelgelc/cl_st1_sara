In old Boston, a poor butcher’s apprentice idolizes the Judge’s handsome, arrogant son, David. After witnessing David mischievously exchange his wealthy family’s market basket with a poor neighbor’s meager one, the boy accepts punishment rather than expose his hero. When David later runs away to sea, the butcher boy follows him.

For decades, David’s family receives restrained letters, increasingly lavish Oriental gifts, and signs of prosperity from abroad. Long after David’s supposed departure, the narrator and his wife welcome the elderly, taciturn “Uncle David” into their home. Though disappointingly ordinary, he proves gentle, generous, and affectionate, especially toward their baby.

The deception is exposed when David’s sister Lucretia visits and reveals that the real David had lost an arm. Their guest admits he is Tommy Biggs, David’s former schoolmate and lifelong companion. Tommy explains that the selfish, reckless David refused to write home, squandered money, drank, and pursued women. Out of devotion, Tommy supported him, sent letters and gifts in his name, concealed his faults, and continued the pretense for thirty-one years after nursing David through his fatal fever.

The narrator and his wife assure Tommy that his loyalty and love make him their true uncle, but shame over his deception drives him away. He returns to China, sends one final shipment and a letter announcing his failing health, and leaves them his estate. They never hear from him again, but his gifts—and the fragrance filling their house—remain enduring reminders of his selfless love.

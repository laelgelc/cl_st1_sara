The story uses a plain, economical, morally focused style associated with the realist parable or religious folktale. Its diction is accessible and largely unornamented, favouring concrete facts, familiar actions, and direct emotional statements over elaborate description. Characters are introduced through a few socially and physically defining details—occupation, property, appearance, habits—and settings are sketched only as fully as the plot requires. Specific cultural terms such as “samovar,” “troika,” “Czar,” and “rubles” establish the Russian milieu without interrupting the narrative to explain it.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, but remains closely aligned with Aksionov. It reports external events briskly while entering his thoughts at decisive moments, especially when suspicion, despair, anger, and forgiveness transform him. Psychological states are expressed directly rather than ambiguously: he is frightened, unhappy, vengeful, or spiritually relieved. Interior reflection often takes the form of quoted thought, giving moral conflict an immediate, almost dramatic clarity. The narrator is restrained and rarely comments explicitly, allowing the sequence of suffering and ethical choice to embody the story’s judgement.

The prose relies on chronological progression and strong causal links. Events are usually connected by simple transitional phrases such as “one day,” “then,” “when,” “at last,” and “that night.” This creates the feeling of an orally transmitted tale in which every incident advances the moral design. The opening moves rapidly from domestic normality to ominous prediction, accusation, and imprisonment. Twenty-six years are then compressed into a short summary, while the confrontation with Makar Semyonich unfolds in slower, scene-based detail. This shift in pacing gives greatest weight to recognition, temptation, confession, and forgiveness rather than to prison routine itself.

Sentence structure is predominantly straightforward, with coordinated clauses and repeated constructions. Longer sentences often accumulate actions or memories in sequence, particularly when Aksionov reviews his lost life. Repetition is an important expressive device: references to twenty-six years, the hidden knife, grey hair, prayer, truth, and God bind the narrative together. Parallel exchanges and recurring questions heighten the sense that Aksionov is being tested repeatedly—first by the law, then by his wife’s doubt, then by despair, and finally by the opportunity for revenge. Occasional proverbs and proverbial formulations reinforce the folktale quality.

Dialogue is frequent, direct, and functional. It reveals character, delivers information, and stages moral choices without much conversational ornament. Aksionov’s early speech is cheerful and confident; later it becomes sparse, humble, and spiritually resigned. Makar’s language is more colloquial, evasive, and boastful at first, before becoming broken and penitential. Forms of address such as “Gran’dad,” “your honour,” and “Ivan Dmitrich” mark status, intimacy, and changing emotional relations. Exclamations, pauses, ellipses, and stammering communicate fear or remorse in a simple theatrical manner.

Characterisation is archetypal but emotionally effective. Aksionov develops from a pleasure-loving young merchant into an innocent sufferer, respected elder, and saintly figure. His physical ageing externalises his spiritual ordeal: the wife’s dream of grey hair becomes literal, and his stoop, silence, and white hair signify the destruction of his worldly life. Makar functions first as the concealed wrongdoer and object of vengeance, then as a penitent whose confession is prompted by undeserved mercy. Secondary figures—the wife, police officer, governor, soldiers, and convicts—are defined mainly by their roles in the ethical drama.

The imagery is sparse and symbolic. Grey and white hair represent foreboding, suffering, and premature age; chains, flogging, and the prison embody worldly injustice; light is associated with sacred reading and inward knowledge. The knife is both physical evidence and the mechanism by which false appearance defeats truth. Tears recur at moments of helplessness, separation, repentance, and shared forgiveness. The contrast between legal judgement and divine knowledge is central: human institutions rely on appearances and punish the innocent, while God alone knows the truth.

The tone changes from lightly domestic and good-humoured to ominous, tragic, contemplative, and finally redemptive. Foreshadowing is explicit, especially in the wife’s dream, but its meaning deepens over time: the grey hair initially treated as a lucky sign anticipates not merely age but the annihilation of Aksionov’s former identity. Irony is equally important. He is convicted because fear makes him appear guilty; the real murderer is later imprisoned for an offence he claims not to have committed; and release arrives only after Aksionov has relinquished every desire to be free.

The story’s structure is symmetrical and morally purposeful. An innocent man is ruined by another’s concealment, then gains power over that same man by discovering the tunnel. By refusing to betray Makar, Aksionov reverses the original act: where Makar preserved himself by sacrificing Aksionov, Aksionov risks himself to spare Makar. This mercy produces the confession that punishment could not compel. The ending is abrupt and understated, withholding worldly compensation. Aksionov dies before release, so vindication matters less than his inward liberation from hatred and attachment.

Overall, the style is lucid, compressed, emotionally direct, and spiritually didactic without becoming heavily sermonising. Its principal effects come from narrative simplicity, sharp moral contrasts, symbolic repetition, controlled shifts in pace, and a final paradox: although justice is fatally delayed, forgiveness frees Aksionov before any official order can do so.

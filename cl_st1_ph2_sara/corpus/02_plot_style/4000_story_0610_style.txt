The story is a third-person social comedy told by an omniscient, conspicuously interventionist narrator. The viewpoint moves freely among George, Mary, Samuel Peel, and occasionally the infant Georgie, exposing motives and self-deceptions that the characters themselves would prefer to conceal. The narrator often steps beyond the immediate scene to supply family history, financial context, social explanation, or a general observation about human behaviour. Direct addresses and corrective asides—such as the assurance that the Tiger is respectable or the remark that men have not gasped from astonishment for two centuries—make the narrative voice feel urbane, conversational, and knowingly theatrical.

The dominant tone is dry, ironic, and gently satirical. Characters’ pretensions are inflated in formal language and then punctured by practical facts. Georgie’s ordinary appetite becomes an “astounding disregard for the decencies of life”; George’s fashionable late breakfast is measured against the millions already at work; Samuel’s encounter with a perambulator is treated as a public and political catastrophe. The comedy depends on this mock-heroic disproportion, in which domestic inconvenience, money trouble, champagne, hotel accommodation, and childcare acquire the gravity of historic events. The satire is amused rather than savage: George is vain, manipulative, and financially irresponsible, but also clever and energetic; Samuel is pompous and parsimonious, yet not wholly ungenerous.

Diction alternates between polished, Latinate narration and brisk colloquial speech. The narrative favours socially precise words such as “necessaries,” “personage,” “benefactor,” “ostentatious,” “indubitable,” and “ceremonious,” while dialogue includes idioms such as “the old cock,” “up a gum-tree,” “don’t care twopence,” and “hang on to this pram a sec.” This contrast distinguishes public dignity from private conduct and lets class, region, and temperament emerge naturally. Capitalized titles, monetary sums, professional distinctions, newspaper quotations, letters, and institutional names lend the story a documentary exactness rooted in its social world.

Sentences are often long, balanced, and cumulative, with clauses that successively qualify or undermine one another. Parentheses and dashes are frequent and usually carry the sharpest ironic correction. Repetition is used for comic emphasis: George is repeatedly described as a “rising young sculptor”; Samuel repeatedly calls the situation “perfectly ridiculous”; questions accumulate as his panic grows. Short sentences then deliver reversals or punch lines: “His fears were justified.” “They were far more than justified.” “Whereupon Georgie yelled.” This alternation between elaborate exposition and abrupt comic closure gives the prose both amplitude and snap.

Characterization proceeds chiefly through contradiction between self-image and material reality. George considers extravagance a professional strategy and dependence a mark of superiority until Samuel’s letters reveal how precarious his status is. Samuel sees himself as guardian of morality and the Peel name, but fear of gossip makes him easy to manoeuvre. Mary is quieter and more practical, often understanding both men while allowing George’s scheme to unfold. Georgie functions as an innocent comic observer and disruptive physical presence: his porridge, spoon, crying, and perambulator repeatedly expose the absurdity of adult dignity. Servants, officials, passers-by, and local worthies form a social audience whose imagined judgment governs the principal characters.

Dialogue is economical, adversarial, and rich in subtext. Characters commonly pretend calm while betraying anxiety through repetition, curtness, evasions, or misplaced blame. George’s conversations are tactical performances: he reframes failure as realism, manipulation as necessity, and Samuel’s withdrawal of support as evidence that Samuel must be “hard up.” Much of the humour comes from the gap between the literal words and the strategic purpose behind them. The narrator regularly makes that gap explicit, noting, for example, that George condemns an argument whose truth he privately recognizes.

The story is structured in four numbered movements. The opening breakfast scene establishes the Peels’ luxury and introduces the financial threat through letters. The second section pauses for retrospective exposition, explaining how reputation, inheritance, artistic ambition, and calculated display produced the crisis. The third intensifies the conflict and announces George’s plan. The fourth shifts from London to Bursley and converts the financial dispute into public farce. This structure moves from private unease to social spectacle, with exposition concentrated before the final comic action.

Pacing is deliberately controlled. The early sections linger over possessions, income, family history, and professional standing because the comedy depends on understanding the exact relation between money and prestige. The final section accelerates through entrances, misunderstandings, public observation, and escalating embarrassment. Suspense arises less from uncertainty about physical events than from anticipation of Samuel’s social discomfort. The ending resolves the practical problem swiftly, then extends the joke through the town’s long memory and the barmaid’s retelling.

Setting is rendered through selective, status-bearing detail. Regent’s Park, the studio, silver, casts, wet clay, fine clothes, servants, the Town Hall, election posters, spats, and the Tiger all function as social symbols. Objects are rarely neutral: they advertise prosperity, expose debt, embody respectability, or threaten reputation. The contrast between cosmopolitan artistic London and provincial Bursley is especially important, though both environments are shown to operate through appearances, gossip, and calculated display.

The central stylistic method is sustained dramatic irony. Readers know that George’s “principle” masks extravagance, that Samuel’s moral firmness is vulnerable to scandal, and that the Tiger is chosen precisely because it will offend him. Public reputation repeatedly diverges from private economics: a gold medal cannot pay rent, press coverage implies success while revealing champagne consumption, and an allowance masquerades as artistic independence. The prose turns these discrepancies into comedy while also offering a clear social critique of class aspiration, patronage, masculinity, artistic commerce, temperance respectability, and the power of communal opinion.

Overall, the style is polished, observant, and materially specific, combining realist social detail with farcical plotting and mock-solemn narration. Its signature effect comes from describing vanity and embarrassment with grand rhetorical seriousness, then grounding them in money, food, babies, servants, hotel bars, and the relentless awareness of what other people may say.

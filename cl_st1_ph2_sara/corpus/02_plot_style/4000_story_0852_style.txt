The story uses a lyrical, regionalist mode, combining atmospheric description, oral storytelling and tragic social realism. It opens slowly and sensuously, establishing the Louisiana setting through heat, stillness, darkness, distant lights, water and faint music. Images such as the “slow, lazy motion” of the lugger and the song floating across the bayou create a languorous rhythm. This calm frame contrasts sharply with the cruelty and emotional devastation of the embedded tale.

Its structure is that of a framed oral narrative. A third-person narrator first presents Manna Loulou attending Madame Delisle, then yields most of the story to Manna Loulou’s voice as she recounts the fate of Zoraïde. The closing exchange returns to the bedroom and finally restates the conversation in Creole, reminding the reader that the English narrative is effectively a translation of a spoken performance. This gives the piece the intimacy of a bedtime story while allowing its tragic implications to undermine that comforting form.

The prose is ornate but generally controlled, with long, flowing sentences, balanced clauses and gently cumulative description. Semicolons, dashes and repetitions regulate the pace and reproduce the movement of speech. Descriptions frequently rely on simile and visual contrast: hair is “soft and shining as satin,” Mézor is “straight as a cypress-tree,” and his body resembles “a column of ebony.” Colour imagery—black night, white feet, café-au-lait skin, ebony flesh and white flagging—is especially prominent, reflecting both the visual richness of the setting and the society’s racial classifications.

The diction blends formal English narration with French and Louisiana Creole terms such as “marais,” “nénaine,” “la belle,” “Bamboula,” “Calinda” and “piti.” French titles and place names reinforce the colonial Creole milieu, while untranslated or partially translated dialogue preserves local cadence and cultural specificity. The narrator sometimes glosses Creole expressions, but the final passage refuses full assimilation into standard English by presenting the exchange as it was “really” spoken. Speech is therefore not merely decorative local colour; it marks community, intimacy, hierarchy and the distance between lived experience and literary translation.

Characterisation is compressed and emblematic. Zoraïde is introduced through idealised beauty, elegance and desirability, while Mézor is rendered with statuesque, almost heroic physical imagery. Madame Delarivière is characterised through possessive affection, racial prejudice and decisive action. Her tenderness toward Zoraïde coexists with her willingness to control Zoraïde’s marriage, separate her from Mézor and steal her child. This contradiction is conveyed with restrained irony rather than overt authorial denunciation. Phrases such as “dear Zoraïde” and claims that Madame acts for her welfare become increasingly bitter as the consequences of that supposed care emerge.

The narrative voice is sympathetic and morally lucid. It openly labels the lie about the baby a “wicked falsehood,” invokes divine judgment and repeatedly uses compassionate epithets such as “poor Zoraïde” and “the poor thing.” At the same time, the story’s emotional force comes chiefly from concrete acts and recurring phrases rather than abstract argument. Zoraïde’s repeated “Li mouri” becomes a lament, then a sign of psychic collapse. The rag bundle functions as the central tragic symbol: an invented child that protects her from unbearable loss but ultimately prevents her from recognising the living child when she is returned.

The tone moves from dreamy and affectionate to romantic, oppressive and finally elegiac. Early references to beauty, dance and forbidden love carry the cadence of a folk romance, but the romance is destroyed by slavery’s power to sell lovers, dictate marriages and sever maternal bonds. The understated treatment of these events intensifies their horror. Even the ending avoids restoration: remorse comes too late, the real child remains rejected, and Zoraïde’s new name, “la folle,” reduces her enduring trauma to a social label. The final response—“better had she died”—leaves the story in unresolved sorrow.

Overall, the style is musical, sensuous and compassionate, with strong local colour and a deliberate tension between the beauty of the telling and the brutality of what is told. Repetition, translated speech, layered narration and symbolic objects give the story the quality of an inherited lament: personal in voice, folkloric in shape and sharply critical of paternalism, racial hierarchy and the destructive presumption that ownership can masquerade as love.

The story uses a restrained regional-realist style, combining an unobtrusive third-person narrator with close attention to the speech, customs, hierarchies, and physical textures of rural Louisiana plantation life. The narration moves chiefly through ’Polyte’s perspective, especially as his irritation turns into obsessive love, but it retains enough distance to expose the contradictions in his judgments. His belief that Azélie lacks moral sense, for example, is presented alongside his own increasingly coercive and “shameful” conduct. This creates a quiet irony: characters explain themselves in moral terms, while their actions reveal motives and assumptions they do not fully understand.

The prose is clear, measured, and concrete. Sentences tend to be moderately long and syntactically balanced, often coordinating physical description with psychological observation. Material details—a faded calico dress, a large tin pail, whitewashed pillars, closed shutters, salt meat, coal oil, a ramshackle wagon—establish social position and economic dependence without extensive exposition. Description is selective but sensuous, drawing particularly on smell, light, heat, and movement. The pungent store, the warm air in the corridor, the moon rising like “a keen, curved blade,” and the blooming cotton field give the setting a strong atmospheric presence. Objects also carry structural weight: the pail marks Azélie’s dependence on plantation credit, the store embodies control over necessities, the key represents access and authority, and the ring expresses ’Polyte’s mistaken belief that material generosity can create emotional obligation.

Dialogue is central and sharply differentiated from the more polished narrative voice. French phrases and Louisiana vernacular—“Eh bien,” “que voulez-vous,” “Bonté,” “après tout”—locate the story within a multilingual Creole and Cajun environment. Phonetic spellings, elisions, nonstandard grammar, and rhythmic repetitions reproduce local speech and distinguish social identity: “w’at,” “yere,” “fo’,” “don’,” and “Li’le river.” The dialect is dense enough to shape cadence and characterization but generally remains intelligible through context. Azélie’s speech is repetitive, literal, and emotionally uninflected; ’Polyte’s is more volatile, shifting from brusque commands to pleading repetition. Mr. Mathurin’s mixture of geniality, French expressions, and paternalistic language reflects his social authority. Historically specific racial terminology appears within this regional register and helps reveal the plantation’s racial and class hierarchy, rather than functioning as neutral contemporary diction.

Characterization proceeds through gesture, habit, speech, and contradiction rather than direct analysis alone. Azélie is introduced through hesitant movement, plain clothing, and a “gentle, inoffensive face,” yet she later acts with practical boldness. Her emotional opacity is crucial: she neither flirts nor reciprocates ’Polyte’s passion, and the narrator does not open her inner life to the reader. She remains literal, family-bound, and largely indifferent to the romantic meanings he imposes on her. ’Polyte, by contrast, is rendered from within. His swagger, industry, irritation, physical attraction, shame, longing, and self-deception form the story’s psychological arc. Repeated phrases such as “I wish she would go ’way f’om yere” and “I love you” dramatize feelings that become compulsive before he can understand them.

The pacing is initially slow and observational, dwelling on Azélie’s visit to the store and the small negotiations over provisions. This accumulation of ordinary requests establishes the economic system before the nighttime break-in turns the narrative. The burglary scene tightens the rhythm through darkness, faint sounds, brief flashes of light, and abrupt physical action. Afterward, time accelerates through summary as ’Polyte’s obsession deepens, then slows again around the proposal, Azélie’s departure, and his final decision. The structure is linear and causally compact: irritation leads to confrontation, confrontation to desire, desire to humiliation and pursuit. The ending is understated and open, stopping at the moment when ’Polyte chooses to follow Azélie rather than showing the consequences.

The tone blends sympathy, irony, melancholy, and social criticism. Humor appears in Mr. Mathurin’s genial remarks and ’Polyte’s self-important manner, but it is shadowed by poverty, dependency, racial hierarchy, and the unequal distribution of power. Romantic feeling is not idealized. ’Polyte experiences love as degradation and loss of self-command, while Azélie treats his passion as incomprehensible or merely inconvenient. The story’s emotional effect comes from this asymmetry: he constructs a redemptive romance in which he will “rescue” and reform her, whereas she remains loyal to her father and returns to Little River. The final reversal—’Polyte abandoning his stable place to follow the family he once despised—gives the narrative a compact, ironic finish.

Overall, the style is naturalistic and psychologically acute, with a strong sense of place and an economical symbolic pattern. It avoids overt moral pronouncement, allowing social arrangements and private delusions to emerge through scenes of exchange, refusal, trespass, and departure. Its most distinctive qualities are the contrast between polished narration and vernacular dialogue, the atmospheric Louisiana setting, the disciplined use of concrete detail, and the coolly ironic portrayal of desire as a force that overturns judgment, status, and self-respect.

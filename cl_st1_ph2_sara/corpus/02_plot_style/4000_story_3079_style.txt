The story uses a framed first-person narration in which a relatively unobtrusive, educated observer introduces the mining setting and then yields the narrative almost entirely to Bunt, an oral storyteller. The opening frame is concrete and atmospheric: the mine is rendered through movement, machinery, dampness, night air, and sound. Once Bunt begins his anecdote, the prose shifts from standard literary English into an extended dramatic monologue. The narrator intervenes only briefly to prompt or question him, so the tale retains the immediacy of speech rather than the polish of a formally written account.

Its dominant stylistic feature is heavily marked Western vernacular. Bunt’s speech is represented through eye dialect, dropped consonants, nonstandard grammar, phonetic spellings, and regional slang: “y’ought,” “ain’t,” “hisself,” “mout,” “bime-by,” and similar forms. Mining, gambling, ranching, and frontier vocabulary gives the voice an occupational and geographical identity. Colourful compounds and extravagant labels—such as “cow-rustlin’,” “hair-liftin’,” and “hell-roarin’ maverick”—make Bunt sound inventive, theatrical, and larger than life. His repeated conversational formulas, including “say, lemme tell you,” “well, sir,” and “y’see,” continually address an implied listener and reproduce the rhythms of a performed campfire or saloon story.

The syntax is loose, cumulative, and digressive. Sentences often accumulate clauses linked by “and,” moving through correction, qualification, repetition, and parenthetical explanation as a speaker might while improvising. Bunt frequently delays the main action in order to supply background, defend his conduct, elaborate a comparison, or anticipate objections. Repetition supplies both rhythm and emphasis: “I’m skeered. I don’t want…” and the recurring “an’” propel the narration while heightening comic urgency. In contrast, short pieces of dialogue and abrupt revelations create sharp beats, particularly the final sentence. The prose also makes frequent use of mock-formal constructions. Bunt’s proposed bargain with Peg-leg parodies legal language through phrases such as “agree an’ covenant,” “party o’ the first part,” and “bindin’ an’ obligatory,” comically mixing frontier slang with inflated bureaucratic diction.

The imagery is energetic, physical, and often drawn from animals, machinery, gambling, and ranch life. The stamp mill starts like horses struggling on a cattle deck before settling into a “roaring gallop”; Peg-leg moves like a kangaroo and falls like a rickety table; danger is measured through cards, guns, and livestock. Such similes are vivid but intentionally homespun, revealing the teller’s world as much as the objects described. Sound is especially prominent: machinery bumps and roars, the sick cough “barks,” Peg-leg bellows, and characters holler across streets. The result is a noisy, kinetic narrative dominated by collisions, pursuits, threats, and bodily comedy.

The tone combines tall-tale exuberance, grotesque humour, deadpan irony, and moral unease. Bunt routinely understates violence or describes it in jaunty euphemisms, calling killing “doing for” someone and a murderous confrontation a “little turn-up.” Illness, disability, and death are likewise converted into games or punch lines. The consumptives’ thermometer contest is built around morbid incongruity, while Peg-leg’s wooden limb becomes both a comic prop and the source of the power reversal. Bunt’s solemn insistence on his own fear and reasonableness makes the absurdity more effective because he narrates extraordinary events as practical frontier problems.

Characterisation is achieved primarily through voice and contradiction. Bunt presents himself as no hero, yet acts decisively; he professes loyalty and common sense while displaying a ruthless appetite for violent comedy. Peg-leg is constructed as a grotesque frontier villain whose campaign against profanity is absurdly at odds with his brutality. This mismatch drives the central satire: conventional verbal sin is treated as intolerable by a man who commits far greater moral crimes. The story repeatedly exposes the gap between professed principle and actual conduct, though it does so through anecdote and caricature rather than explicit reflection.

Structurally, the tale escalates through a sequence of comic reversals. A casual observation about the coughing car-boy triggers the story; the thermometer game leads to Peg-leg’s attack; removing his leg transforms a feared gunman into a helpless pursuer; the threatened narrator then turns Peg-leg’s obsession into a binding bargain. After a brief return to the framing conversation, Bunt begins a second movement concerning Peg-leg’s death. Suspense is deliberately prolonged around the hotel fire, and the narrator’s question sets up the final reveal. The last line—“they hadn’t any blanket”—is extremely compressed in comparison with Bunt’s expansive narration, functioning as a cruel, blackly comic snap ending.

Overall, the style belongs to the frontier tall-tale tradition: orally inflected, hyperbolic, episodic, masculine, and built around a raconteur’s distinctive voice. Its humour depends on exaggeration, incongruity, mock solemnity, delayed disclosure, and the collision of polished narrative description with rough dialect. The period voice also contains racial terminology and stereotypes now recognised as offensive; these are embedded in the historical vernacular and social attitudes represented by the speaker.

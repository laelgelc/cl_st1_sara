The story uses a late nineteenth-century American domestic-realist style, strongly colored by New England local detail. Its diction alternates between a clear, conventional narrative voice and highly marked regional speech. The narration is precise and gently formal, while dialogue relies on contractions, phonetic spellings, and colloquialisms such as “goin’,” “ain’t,” “allers,” “ruther,” “jest,” and “for the land sakes.” These forms establish place, period, age, and social background without requiring explicit exposition. Repeated domestic terms—pies, pudding, turkey, pantry, stove, shawls, sleighs, and church meetings—create a self-contained rural world governed by household routine, seasonal custom, and communal expectations.

The prose favors moderately long, balanced sentences interspersed with short declarative statements that sharpen emotional or comic moments. Descriptions often accumulate concrete details: clothing, furniture, food, weather, bodily gestures, and household arrangements. Parenthetical qualifications and explanatory clauses give the narrator a patient, observant manner, as though carefully interpreting the characters’ conduct for the reader. Repetition is an important device, especially in dialogue and in the recurring search for “the turkey and the plum-pudding.” It lends the story an oral, almost folktale-like rhythm while also developing the central comic mystery.

The narration is in the third person, with a largely omniscient perspective that remains closely aligned with Ann Mary. The narrator moves freely between outward observation and the child’s private reactions, reporting her disappointment, loneliness, pride, anxiety, and practical reasoning. This focalization allows ordinary events to acquire great emotional weight: a passing stagecoach destroys a hope, an empty house becomes frightening, and a white cat provides genuine consolation. At the same time, the narrator maintains enough distance to expose the adults’ foibles, particularly Mrs. Little’s absent-mindedness, pride, and reflexive resistance to correction.

Characterization is built through habitual behavior rather than extensive psychological analysis. Mrs. Little’s gold beads, rigid housekeeping standards, sharp corrections, and “humble and resentful air” reveal a domineering but fundamentally responsible woman. Ann Mary is defined by posture, restraint, vigilance, and competence: her dangling feet remain disciplined, her shoulders are held straight, and even while crying she makes sensible plans. The resemblance between grandmother and granddaughter is chiefly one of manner, giving their relationship both tension and affection. Mr. Little’s gentleness and understated amusement provide a comic counterweight to his wife’s severity.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization and humor. Exchanges are often built around correction, denial, qualification, and reluctant concession. Mrs. Little’s speech is assertive even when she is plainly wrong, while Ann Mary’s questions are timid in form but corrective in purpose. The humor is therefore dry and situational rather than joke-driven. It arises from disproportion: a grand domestic celebration is undermined by forgetfulness; an emergency journey proves unnecessary; and the family discovers that it has celebrated Thanksgiving a week early. The narrator treats these mishaps with affectionate irony rather than ridicule.

The story’s structure is linear and episodic, organized around preparation, apparent celebration, loss, isolation, revelation, and restoration. Early incidents involving omitted nutmeg, sugar, and plums foreshadow the larger disappearance of the turkey and pudding. Suspense grows from mundane errors rather than danger in the conventional sense. The central section slows down to dwell on Ann Mary’s solitary walk, the locked houses, the winter dusk, and the noises heard from bed. This temporary shift toward domestic suspense makes the grandparents’ return emotionally satisfying. The later revelation that the holiday itself was mistaken introduces a second comic reversal, and the final discovery under the sofa resolves the remaining mystery.

Sensory imagery is abundant but restrained. Food is evoked through smell, warmth, spice, and anticipation; winter through deep snow, red sunset, bare birches, cold rooms, and sleigh-bells. Interiors are rendered with memorable period objects such as the parlor organ, yellow-posted bedstead, satin-scroll wallpaper, air-tight stove, and snow-white napkins. These details create atmosphere while reinforcing contrasts between warmth and cold, company and solitude, order and confusion. The simile comparing the turkeyless meal to “a great flourish of accompaniment without any song” is especially characteristic: accessible, domestic, and emotionally exact.

The tone blends tenderness, melancholy, gentle satire, and eventual reassurance. Much of the pathos comes from the seriousness with which the story treats a child’s loneliness. Yet the sentiment remains controlled by practical action and comic observation. The ending restores family, friendship, food, and festivity, converting earlier disappointments into a lesson of hopeful endurance. Its final idea—that troubles may be followed by thanksgivings—states the story’s moral in a simple, child-centered form. Overall, the style is warm, minutely observant, regionally grounded, and quietly humorous, using domestic realism to turn small household mistakes into a narrative of fear, resilience, and communal happiness.

The story uses a layered, antiquarian mode, combining a restrained modern frame narrative with an intensely sensory historical tale. It begins in the manner of a local chronicle: the parish, abbey ruins, landscape and vicar are described with geographical precision and an air of documentary authority. This factual surface gradually opens into folklore, conjecture and visionary experience. The ruined east window serves as the central image and structural hinge, connecting the present-day conversation, the medieval monastic record and the imagined life concealed behind that record.

The narrative perspective is first person, but the framing narrator remains relatively unobtrusive. His chief function is to listen, question and occasionally challenge the vicar. Within the vicar’s reconstruction, the voice shifts into the first-person testimony of a medieval serf. This embedded speaker is immediate, bodily and limited in understanding: he interprets stained glass as supernatural light and monks as strange, many-faced beings. The resulting dramatic irony is central to the style. Readers recognize natural or historical realities that the characters understand as devils, saints, magic and faery, while the story preserves the emotional truth of those beliefs rather than merely mocking them.

The outer narration is elegant, compressed and faintly ironic. Descriptions such as the hyacinths shining “like water” beneath the oaks or ivy “weighting” the abbey wall combine visual delicacy with unusual physical force. Characterization is similarly economical and paradoxical. Mr Colbran’s twisted face becomes an outward analogue for his sardonic manner, yet his irony coexists with tolerance, sadness and generosity. The narrator frequently balances opposing impressions—pathetic and shrewd, satiric and humane, disliked yet blameless—giving the prose psychological complexity without extended explanation.

The embedded narrative is more archaic and incantatory. Its diction includes historical vocabulary and deliberately old-fashioned constructions such as “lanthorn,” “wherein,” “’twas,” “nay,” “dare not,” and “nor heeded not.” The archaism is selective rather than philologically exact: it creates a plausible oral past while remaining intelligible. Biblical and ballad-like cadences appear throughout, especially in repeated syntactic forms and emphatic inversions. Phrases such as “fought it and fought,” “night after night,” and “Bring me a knife” build rhythm through recurrence. Repetition conveys obsession, exhaustion and primitive resolve, making the voice sound as though it is remembering through bodily shocks rather than arranging events analytically.

Sentence structure varies according to emotional pressure. The descriptive frame favors balanced, controlled sentences with carefully placed qualifications. The medieval tale alternates long, accumulating sentences with abrupt declarations and fragments. During flight, fear or physical torment, clauses pile up through coordination, reproducing breathlessness and disorientation. At moments of decision, the prose contracts into blunt exchanges: “They will kill you.” “No.” Dialogue is spare, repetitive and often indirect in emotional meaning. Characters speak about knives, food, blood, cold and escape when the deeper subjects are fear, dependence, erotic attachment and mortality.

The style is strongly corporeal. Experience is registered through wounds, hunger, cold, milk, blood, breath, aching muscles, hair, skin and physical pressure. Abstract feelings are translated into bodily actions: loneliness becomes the pressing of the chest against the earth; terror stiffens the body until it resembles frozen ground; tenderness is expressed through warmth, shelter and enclosure. Similes are drawn largely from animals, plants and rural labor. Martha runs like a fawn, the lovers lie like a nut within its shell, hair resembles fire or beech leaves, and a condemned man resembles a young bullock roped for slaughter. These comparisons make human consciousness appear inseparable from the natural and animal worlds.

Landscape is not passive scenery but an active psychological force. Frost creaks and whoops, darkness pursues the fugitives “from trunk to trunk,” trees reel, the moon flees, and the larchwood hisses. Such personification keeps the boundary between natural phenomenon and supernatural agency unstable. The forest is at once materially exact and mythically charged. Snow, fire, ice and darkness recur as elemental motifs, with warmth and color offering temporary resistance to a world of coldness, violence and social persecution.

Color imagery is especially prominent. The outer tale begins with blue hyacinths and dark, tawny interiors, while the embedded narrative repeatedly invokes red: flame, blood, Martha’s hair and the stained glass. Red signifies destruction, desire, sacred radiance and life itself. The fragment of stained glass changes according to light—visionary and jewel-like at night, black and rough by day, then shining again—so that perception becomes transformative. The “bloodstone” condenses the story’s religious, erotic and violent symbolism into a single object.

The tone moves between dry skepticism, horror, tenderness and ecstatic wonder. Mr Colbran’s sing-song, sardonic delivery initially presents the reconstruction as an intellectual game, but the imagined account quickly acquires grave emotional authority. Violence is described directly yet not sensationally; its force comes from sensory accumulation and the fugitive’s vulnerable consciousness. The love story is likewise unsentimental. Affection grows through fear, hunger, bodily need and mutual protection, giving its eroticism an elemental rather than decorative quality.

Structurally, the story withholds explanation and builds toward reinterpretation. The monastic manuscript describes a demonic assault on sacred glass; the serf’s tale reveals that “the fiend” was probably a terrified fugitive climbing the window to obtain colored light for his lover. Sacred history is thereby recast from below, through the experience of an oppressed man misrecognized as monstrous. The abrupt final exchange—Mr Colbran’s insistence that the lovers “lived happily ever after” and the narrator’s flat “No”—refuses sentimental closure. It leaves the wolves, the lovers’ probable fate and the limits of historical recovery unresolved, ending on a characteristic mixture of irony, compassion and darkness.

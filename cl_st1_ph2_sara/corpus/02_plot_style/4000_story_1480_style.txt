The story adopts a formal, nineteenth-century Gothic-romantic style, shaped by moral allegory, psychological commentary, and an antiquarian sense of legend. Its first-person frame presents the episode as an inherited family narrative attached to an old New York church, lending the tale both historical distance and oral authority. After establishing this frame, the narrator becomes effectively omniscient, moving freely into the motives, memories, and emotional histories of the central figures. The voice is cultivated, reflective, and intrusive: it does not merely report events but interprets character, weighs moral responsibility, anticipates objections, and directs the reader toward symbolic meanings.

The prose is syntactically elaborate. Long, balanced sentences accumulate qualifications, contrasts, semicolon-linked clauses, and parenthetical reflections. Antithesis is especially prominent: youth is set against age, marriage against burial, brilliant clothing against the shroud, worldly prudence against spiritual feeling, and earthly time against eternity. Periodic constructions often delay the main point while building context or suspense, while shorter exclamations and direct statements appear at moments of revelation and emotional crisis. The cadence is elevated and oratorical, particularly in the bridegroom’s speeches, which proceed through parallel phrases, rhetorical questions, repetitions, and climactic declarations.

The diction is high-register and period-conscious, drawing on religious, ceremonial, philosophical, and funerary vocabularies. Words and phrases associated with decay, mortality, sepulchres, shrouds, knells, coffins, and graves recur insistently. This lexical field is opposed by the language of brightness and display—gold, velvet, satin, embroidery, sunshine, roses, glitter, and pageantry. The resulting contrast makes the wedding appear visually sumptuous but spiritually hollow, while death, though grotesque, comes to represent honesty. Archaic or ceremonious expressions such as “solemnized,” “divers nations,” “chiefest business,” and “bespoken” strengthen the historical atmosphere.

Imagery is pictorial, emblematic, and theatrical. Characters frequently appear less as naturalistic individuals than as figures in a moral tableau. The bridal party resembles a bright-colored painting or a bunch of flowers; the aged bride becomes a withered rose among young buds; the mourners form a spectral procession; and the shrouded bridegroom enters as the embodied truth concealed beneath wedding finery. Light and darkness are manipulated almost scenically: sunshine fades, visible gloom fills the church, glitter emerges from mist, and the bridal spectacle is gradually converted into a funeral pageant. Sound is equally structural. The repeated bell stroke governs the pacing, interrupts gaiety, foretells the revelation, and creates a solemn auditory refrain. At the conclusion, the organ rises against and finally overcomes the death-knell, translating terror into spiritual exaltation.

The tone combines irony, morbidity, compassion, and solemn moral intensity. Early descriptions of the elderly lovers contain satirical sharpness, especially in the treatment of the widow’s resistance to aging and the social absurdity of the match. The narrator can be epigrammatic and severe, as in generalizations about selfishness, aged folly, or the emotional damage caused by worldly marriages. Yet the mockery gradually gives way to sympathy. The grotesque spectacle is not sustained merely for horror; it becomes the means by which both characters recover sincerity. This tonal progression—from social comedy through Gothic dread to religious reconciliation—is central to the story’s effect.

Characterization is analytical rather than behaviorally subtle. Before the action begins, the narrator supplies extended moral and psychological portraits of Mr. Ellenwood and Mrs. Dabney. Each is defined through paradox. He is shy but publicly eccentric, generous yet self-absorbed, scholarly yet aimless, and apparently mad because his life has lacked purpose. She is worldly, resilient, emotionally depleted, philosophically practical, and ridiculous in her refusal to acknowledge age. Their histories function as explanations of their symbolic roles: he represents frustrated idealism hardened into morbidity, while she represents feeling compromised by expediency and vanity. Their eventual union depends on each abandoning a defensive posture—his theatrical bitterness and her worldly self-deception.

Dialogue is stylized and rhetorically purposeful. Minor speakers provide social commentary, nervous wit, or theological framing, while the central dialogue turns the ceremony into a public moral debate. The bridegroom’s language is accusatory, cadenced, and consciously theatrical; the widow’s replies become increasingly direct and emotionally authentic. Repetition—particularly of words such as “cruel,” “bride,” and “wed”—intensifies the confrontation. The speech is not designed to reproduce ordinary conversation but to articulate the tale’s governing oppositions in dramatic form.

Structurally, the narrative moves from leisurely exposition to a tightly staged procession. A substantial preface establishes the church, the lovers’ past, and society’s judgment of the marriage. The arrival of the bride initiates the action, after which successive bell strokes produce escalating disturbances. Each repetition darkens the scene: omen becomes unease, unease becomes funeral imagery, and funeral imagery becomes literal spectacle when the bridegroom enters in his shroud. The climax consists first of public accusation and then of mutual recognition. The conclusion resolves the external opposition between wedding and funeral by transforming both into a single sacramental event: earthly hope dies, but affection is elevated into an eternal bond.

The story’s dominant mode is allegorical. Concrete objects repeatedly carry moral significance: the bride’s ornate dress signifies vanity and denial; the bridegroom’s shroud signifies mortality and brutal truth; the bell voices judgment; the organ signifies spiritual transcendence; and the church itself serves as the threshold between social ceremony and eternity. The symbolism is explicit rather than understated, and the narrator openly names the “moral” embodied by the scene. The ending therefore does not dispel the Gothic machinery as trickery. Instead, it spiritualizes it, allowing grotesque spectacle to become a vehicle for repentance, forgiveness, and redemption.

Overall, the style is ornate, meditative, and symbolically concentrated. It blends social satire with Gothic spectacle, psychological diagnosis with Christian moralism, and visual pageantry with funeral ritual. Its characteristic movement is from surfaces to essences: from fashionable dress to bodily decay, from public ridicule to private suffering, from belated marriage to imminent death, and finally from lost earthly time to imagined eternity.

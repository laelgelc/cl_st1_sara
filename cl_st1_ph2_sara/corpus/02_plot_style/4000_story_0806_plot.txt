Sir Aaron Armstrong, a famously cheerful philanthropist and temperance advocate, is found dead beneath his house, apparently murdered after being thrown from an attic window. His servant Magnus initially appears guilty after fleeing with Armstrong’s money, but he has merely taken it to the police for safekeeping. He instead accuses Armstrong’s daughter, Alice, who was found unconscious with a bloody knife.

Patrick Royce, Armstrong’s secretary and Alice’s forbidden lover, then confesses, claiming that while drunk he attacked Armstrong with a revolver and rope. Father Brown rejects the confession because the evidence makes no sense: the revolver’s bullets were fired into the floor, the rope ended around Armstrong’s leg, and a supposed alcoholic would not abandon a half-full whisky bottle.

Father Brown deduces that Armstrong’s relentless public optimism concealed despair, renewed drinking, and suicidal madness. Armstrong had gathered several means of killing himself. Royce entered and tried to save him, throwing away the knife, emptying the revolver harmlessly into the floor, and attempting to restrain him with the rope. Alice misunderstood the struggle and cut the rope, accidentally freeing her father to plunge from the window. Royce falsely confessed to protect her from learning that her intervention had caused the fatal fall. Father Brown advises telling Alice the truth, arguing that a tragic mistake will poison her life less than imagined guilt or concealed sin.

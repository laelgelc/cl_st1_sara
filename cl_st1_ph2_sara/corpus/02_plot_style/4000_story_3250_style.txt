The story adopts the manner of an Edwardian children’s animal tale, combining domestic realism, nursery-story simplicity, comic menace, and a lightly moralising purpose. Its world is anthropomorphic but materially concrete: cats bake, wear clothes, borrow yeast, employ tradesmen, and worry about unruly children, while still hunting rats and behaving according to animal instinct. This overlap between genteel human society and animal nature produces much of the story’s distinctive humour.

The narration is warm, conversational, and predominantly omniscient. It begins with the traditional fairy-tale formula “Once upon a time,” then moves between Mrs. Tabitha’s search and Tom Kitten’s hidden adventure. The narrator freely explains causes, supplies background information, and occasionally addresses the reader with cautionary observations, such as the warning about climbing chimneys in old houses. Near the end, the voice shifts into the first person, claiming to have witnessed the rats’ escape and linking the fantasy to a recognisable local world. This creates the impression of an intimate storyteller recounting an incident known personally.

The diction is accessible and child-oriented, but it includes period domestic vocabulary and architectural detail: “wainscot,” “fender,” “flue,” “rafters,” “haymow,” and “skirting-board.” Concrete nouns, household objects, foods, and tactile substances recur throughout—dough, flour, butter, soot, cobwebs, bones, string, and pastry. These details make the absurd threat of turning a kitten into a pudding feel physically vivid. Descriptive phrases often use affectionate diminutives or emphatic modifiers, including “little soft paws,” “dear little muffins,” “enormous old rat,” and “very dirty dumpling.” Childlike contractions and altered pronunciations, such as “‘normous,” help distinguish the kittens’ speech.

The syntax is generally simple, linear, and easy to follow, with many short declarative paragraphs. Actions are often narrated one at a time in chronological sequence: a character enters, searches, discovers an object, and reacts. Repetition supplies rhythm and emphasis, as in “up, and up, and up,” “searched and searched,” and “rats, and rats, and rats.” The prose also uses accumulative lists to evoke the clutter and hidden complexity of the old house. Exclamation marks, dashes, italics or capitals, and abrupt sentence fragments heighten alarm, comic indignation, and oral-storytelling energy. The closing separation of “A Mouse” gives the punch line visual as well as verbal force.

Dialogue is frequent, direct, and theatrically expressive. Characters address one another by name or kinship title—“Cousin Ribby,” “Mother,” and “Anna Maria”—which reinforces both the social comedy and the clarity needed for young readers. Mrs. Tabitha’s speech is anxious and emotional; Ribby’s is practical and decisive; the kittens speak in frightened repetitions; and the rats converse with absurd formality about cooking Tom. Samuel Whiskers and Anna Maria’s polite dispute over ingredients and indigestible string creates deadpan black comedy. Their measured domestic language contrasts sharply with the violence of their intentions.

The plot is structured around concealment, parallel action, and delayed revelation. The first section presents the cats’ confused search and a series of clues: soot, missing dough, stolen butter, the rolling-pin, and noises beneath the attic floor. The narrative then rewinds to explain Tom’s disappearance and show how the rats’ actions produced those clues. This shift gives the reader retrospective satisfaction while increasing suspense. Repeated searches and thefts create an episodic pattern, while the “roly-poly” sound functions as both an auditory motif and the basis of the title.

The tone balances affection, suspense, and grotesque comedy. Tom’s danger is genuine within the story, yet it is rendered ludicrous through culinary procedure, fussy argument, and playful sound effects. The rolling-pin goes “roly-poly, roly; roly, poly, roly,” turning a threatening action into a nursery-like refrain. Similar tonal doubleness appears when the spider inspects Tom’s knots “critically” but declines to help, or when the rescued pastry is served separately with currants added to conceal the soot. Violence and predation are treated matter-of-factly rather than sentimentally: the cats eat rats, the rats attempt to cook a kitten, and the grown kittens later earn money by rat-catching.

Setting is central to the style. The “old, old house,” with thick walls, attics, chimneys, cupboards, secret passages, and jagged openings, creates a miniature Gothic environment. Queer noises and disappearing food suggest haunting, but the explanation is practical and comic: rats inhabit the concealed spaces. Spatial movement is carefully described, giving the adventure the quality of exploring a hidden domestic labyrinth. The familiar home becomes strange when viewed through chimneys, wall cavities, and spaces beneath floorboards.

Characterisation relies on names, repeated habits, and sharply defined behavioural traits rather than psychological depth. Mrs. Tabitha is an anxious but imperfectly effective mother; Tom is disobedient, curious, vain about his clothes and tail, and ultimately traumatised; Moppet and Mittens are mischievous but grow into competent rat-catchers. Samuel Whiskers is ceremonious, thieving, and complacent, while Anna Maria is practical, argumentative, and energetic. The elaborate, comic names contribute to the nursery-book atmosphere and make each figure immediately memorable.

The moral element is explicit but playful. Disobedience leads Tom into danger, and the narrator directly labels his choice unwise. Nevertheless, the ending does not impose a solemn lesson. Instead, consequences are individual and ironic: Moppet and Mittens become successful professionals, whereas Tom remains afraid even of creatures only slightly larger than a mouse. The story therefore closes with comic exaggeration rather than formal moral instruction.

Overall, the style is characterised by lucid sequential narration, vivid domestic detail, anthropomorphic social comedy, rhythmic repetition, expressive dialogue, and a mixture of cosiness and controlled peril. Its ideal effect is to amuse and mildly frighten a young audience while preserving the intimacy of a story told aloud.

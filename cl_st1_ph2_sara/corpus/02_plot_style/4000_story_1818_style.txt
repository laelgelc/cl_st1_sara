The story adopts a warm, comic, conversational style built around a framed oral narrative. A third-person narrator first presents a domestic Christmas Eve scene in which two children pressure their father into telling them a story; the father’s improvised tale about a runaway Pony Engine then occupies most of the text. Frequent returns to the frame preserve the sense of a live performance rather than a polished written fable. The children interrupt, question, correct, challenge, and emotionally react to the tale, while the father stalls, revises details, threatens to start over, and invents explanations as he goes. Storytelling itself therefore becomes as important as the adventure being narrated.

The narrative voice is gently ironic and treats the family’s reversed power dynamic with affectionate amusement. The children are said to have “brought him up” and to make him obey them, while the father behaves like an unruly child who must be kept to his task. This comic inversion continues throughout: the ostensible authority figure is evasive and unreliable, whereas the children insist on consistency, plausibility, and moral accountability. The narrator remains close to the father’s playful outlook but also registers the children’s distinct reactions, particularly the boy’s argumentative pride and the little girl’s emotional sensitivity.

The diction is informal, idiomatic, and strongly suggestive of spoken American English. Expressions such as “awfully severe,” “just perfectly boiling,” “funning,” “I guess,” “Ho!,” and “any such thing” create an intimate family-room register. Railway terminology—“cow-catcher,” “air-brakes,” “buffers,” “side tracks,” “tender,” and “up-grade”—is mixed with nursery-story language and domestic vocabulary. This juxtaposition lets industrial machinery inhabit the emotional world of a family fable. The place names tracing the journey from Boston to San Francisco add geographical specificity, even as the events become increasingly impossible.

Sentences are often long, loosely coordinated, and accumulative, with repeated conjunctions reproducing the momentum of spontaneous speech. The father’s narration frequently proceeds through strings of “and,” “but,” “well,” and “so,” giving the prose the breathless, improvisatory rhythm of a story told aloud. Dialogue is abundant and typically presented in short, quick exchanges that interrupt these longer narrative runs. Parenthetical qualifications, self-corrections, and comic evasions reinforce the sense that details are being invented under pressure. The children’s interjections also act as informal editorial commentary, exposing contradictions and forcing the father to repair—or brazenly defend—his inventions.

Repetition is a major rhythmic and comic device. The refrain “And the Express kept gaining on it” supplies suspense, structures the transcontinental chase, and parodies the recurring formulas of children’s adventure stories. The Pony Engine’s progressively shortened cry—“Pacific Express,” “Express,” “‘Press,” “‘Ess,” and finally “‘Ss”—turns exhaustion into verbal play while accelerating the narrative toward catastrophe. The father’s repeated threat to return to “Once there was a little Pony Engine” becomes another running joke, because the children fear delay more than they fear the story’s implausibility.

The embedded tale uses sustained anthropomorphism. Locomotives marry, parent children, gossip, grieve, warn, swear, eat, sleep, suffer illness, and possess ambitions. Mechanical features are converted into bodily and emotional signs: the mother wheezes from weakness in the chest, sighs with her air-brakes, and whistles dolefully; the child leans its chimney against its father’s and longs to grow a cow-catcher. The comedy depends on keeping the mechanical and human levels active at once. At the same time, the anthropomorphism produces genuine pathos, especially in the account of the drowned father locomotive, the anxious mother, and the Pony Engine’s growing homesickness.

The tone continually shifts between tenderness, absurdity, suspense, mock solemnity, and dark comedy. Sentimental conventions are invoked sincerely enough to move the little girl, then undercut by the boy’s skepticism or the father’s exaggeration. The adventure escalates from recognizable railway action to fantastic impossibilities: the engine jumps a river, flees prairie wolves, encounters a one-wheeled locomotive nightmare, and leaps the Sierras like a goat. The father insists that these events happened “truly,” making his transparent unreliability a source of humor. Even the grim ending—plunging into the Pacific and driving the mother into brain fever—is delivered with the blunt finality of an improvised cautionary tale.

Characterization is achieved chiefly through dialogue and reaction. The father is playful, affectionate, evasive, and theatrically manipulative. The boy is logical, competitive, pedantic, and eager to appear emotionally invulnerable; he challenges technical and geographical inconsistencies while quietly becoming affected by the story. The little girl is compassionate and more openly moved, yet also stubborn and unwilling to accept correction from her brother. Their overlapping questions give the family scene a convincing intimacy and reveal the children as active collaborators in the tale rather than passive listeners.

The story parodies moralistic children’s literature. The announced moral, “Children, obey your parents,” is mechanically attached to a narrative whose emotional and logical complications exceed that simple lesson. The children have already mocked the father’s habitual morals, and their questions after the ending expose the tale’s weak connection to Christmas and its impossible chronology. The father’s final suggestion that the journey may have taken a year openly sacrifices coherence to escape interrogation. Thus the text combines a cautionary plot about disobedience and overreaching ambition with a comic critique of adults who impose morals upon stories after the fact.

Pacing alternates between rapid adventure and repeated interruption. The chase grows faster through geographical compression, refrain, lists, and diminishing speech, while the frame repeatedly pauses the action for questions about biology, mechanics, logic, and vocabulary. These interruptions do not merely delay the plot; they generate much of the humor and establish the story’s central tension between imaginative freedom and literal-minded scrutiny. The ending returns fully to the domestic frame and closes on the boy’s insistence that he “didn’t put up” his lip, a final piece of comic denial that confirms he was more emotionally involved than he admits. Overall, the style blends domestic realism, tall-tale extravagance, affectionate satire, technological fantasy, and self-conscious oral storytelling.

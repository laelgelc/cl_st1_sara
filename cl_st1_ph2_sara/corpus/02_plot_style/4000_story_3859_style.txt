The story uses a late-Victorian realist style infused with scientific romance, industrial Gothic, dark comedy, and colonial satire. Its narrative voice is a confident, educated third-person omniscient narrator who moves freely between external description, social commentary, and conjecture about Azuma-zi’s mental state. The narrator often speaks collectively or directly to the reader—“we would have the noises of that shed always about the reader”—and sometimes qualifies judgments with phrases such as “perhaps,” “I fancy,” and “it is hard to say.” This creates an appearance of rational detachment while leaving room for ambiguity, especially over whether the dynamo’s “voice” is supernatural or merely the product of obsession and sensory strain.

The diction combines technical vocabulary with vivid figurative language. Terms such as “armature,” “contact rings,” “brushes,” “short circuiting,” and “Carnot’s cycle” lend material precision and establish the authority of modern engineering. Alongside them appear religious and mythic words—“shrine,” “idol,” “Lord,” “Fetich,” “sacrifice,” “martyrdom”—which transform the dynamo from a machine into an industrial deity. This sustained collision between scientific and sacred registers is the story’s central stylistic device. Mechanical processes are repeatedly personified: the dynamo breathes, roars, answers, hungers, exercises patience, and kills without wavering. Conversely, human death is reduced to engineering language as “the mere temporary deflection of a current.” The resulting irony exposes the capacity of technological civilisation to generate its own forms of superstition and brutality.

Sound dominates the sensory texture. Dense accumulations of onomatopoeia and rhythmic verbs—“buzzed and rattled,” “whoo! whoo! whoo!,” “throb, throb, throb,” “snorting, panting, and seething,” “suck and thud”—attempt to reproduce the machinery’s unremitting din. Repetition mimics rotating bands, pistons, and coils, while long, cumulative sentences layer one mechanical noise over another. Shorter sentences and isolated statements then punctuate this drone at decisive moments, particularly during warnings, deaths, and revelations. The machinery’s recurring “note” functions almost like a refrain, altering its apparent meaning as Azuma-zi’s obsession intensifies. Visual description is likewise high-contrast and Gothic: flickering purple arc light, black shadows, pale stars, blue sparks, coal grime, and singed bodies turn the dynamo shed into a modern infernal temple.

Sentence structure is varied but often expansive. Long periodic or cumulative sentences carry qualifications, parenthetical remarks, lists, and ironic afterthoughts, allowing description and commentary to coexist. Dashes frequently insert explanations or sharpen contrasts, while semicolons coordinate parallel impressions. During violent action, the prose becomes more linear and kinetic, relying on active verbs such as “gripped,” “swung,” “sprung,” “rolled,” and “darted.” This syntactic contraction accelerates the pace and gives the fights a physical immediacy that contrasts with the more meditative passages of industrial description.

The tone is sardonic, macabre, and often grotesquely comic. Holroyd is introduced through compressed contradictions: he doubts God but trusts thermodynamics, reads Shakespeare but faults his chemistry, and turns bullying into “a labour of love.” Similar epigrammatic reversals recur throughout. The official response to death is deliberately banal: experts worry about stopped trains, reporters appear, the machine is restarted, and the workplace rapidly resumes its ordinary rhythm. The final description of the cult as a religion possessing both “a Martyrdom and a Human Sacrifice” closes the story with mock-anthropological wit. Humour does not soften the violence so much as intensify the story’s bleak view of institutional indifference.

Characterisation is economical and strongly contrastive. Holroyd is rendered through bodily coarseness, dialect, violence, drunkenness, and arrogant faith in practical science. His speech uses nonstandard contractions and dropped consonants—“where’s your ’eathen idol,” “Don’t ’ee go nigh”—to mark regional and class identity. Azuma-zi is characterised primarily through silence, gesture, sensory experience, and a progressively developed interior mythology. His limited English isolates him from other characters and enables the authorities to misread events. The scientific manager, by contrast, is identified less as an individual than as a rational professional type; his calm technical reflexes persist even after attempted murder.

The treatment of race belongs conspicuously to the text’s imperial period. Racial slurs, pseudo-ethnological physiognomy, paternalistic labels such as “savage,” and broad oppositions between the “mysterious East” and European civilisation shape the narration as well as the characters’ speech. At the same time, the prose undercuts the supposed superiority of that civilisation: London exploits, impoverishes, silences, and brutalises Azuma-zi, while Holroyd’s rationalism is itself crude, violent, and idolatrous. The story therefore mixes colonial stereotyping with an ironic critique of imperial and industrial self-congratulation, rather than escaping the assumptions it examines.

Structurally, the narrative proceeds through accumulation and recurrence. It begins with compressed portraits, expands into an immersive account of the shed, traces Azuma-zi’s fascination through repeated acts of watching, touching, bowing, and praying, and then converts that fascination into sacrifice. Holroyd’s death appears to complete the arc, but the renewed attempted sacrifice creates a second climax and reveals that the cult has become self-perpetuating. Repeated images—the returning patch on the belt, spinning coils, shadows beneath the machine, changes in its note—bind psychological development to mechanical rotation. The overall effect is controlled, ironic, and claustrophobic: a modern technological setting is narrated as both precisely engineered reality and primitive temple, with the distinction between machine, monster, and god steadily collapsing.

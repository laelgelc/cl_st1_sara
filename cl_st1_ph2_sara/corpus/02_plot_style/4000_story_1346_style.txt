The story uses a retrospective first-person voice that is both communal and personal. Although the narrator occasionally speaks as an individual—“I have reason to believe,” “I have a very vivid recollection”—he frequently shifts into “we,” presenting himself as the spokesman for the rough teamsters and settlers who share in Mary’s imaginative world. This creates the effect of an affectionate local chronicle: the narrator is observant and worldly, yet emotionally involved, recalling a vanished frontier community with mingled amusement and nostalgia.

The prevailing mode is gently comic realism enriched by sentiment and whimsy. Humour arises from treating Mary’s improvised dolls and imaginary maternity with solemn adult seriousness. Ninepins become twins, a mutilated doll receives a cast-iron head, and hard-bitten men discuss wooden children’s health as though conducting genuine domestic consultations. This sustained deadpan conceit gives the story much of its charm. The comedy is rarely cruel; even when the narrator employs mock-scientific language such as “previous state of existence,” “most ordinary student of evolution,” or references to phrenological “faculties,” the irony protects rather than belittles Mary’s imagination.

The tone balances playful irony with deep tenderness. Elevated, ceremonious phrasing is applied to absurd or humble objects: Mary possesses “admirable and impartial maternity,” a blacksmith offers “artistic suggestion,” and a ninepin suffers from allegedly dissipated habits after being revived with whisky. Such mock-heroic language contrasts with the dolls’ battered materials and the men’s coarse surroundings. Beneath the comedy, however, lies genuine reverence for childhood, imaginative love, and the emotional delicacy of supposedly uncivilized men. The final movement turns increasingly elegiac as education, the railroad, social propriety, and urban refinement displace the “simple Arcadian days” of the road.

The prose is expansive, leisurely, and highly qualified. Sentences are often long and syntactically layered, using parenthetical remarks, dashes, semicolons, subordinate clauses, and delayed comic conclusions. The narrator frequently begins with a factual-sounding proposition and then complicates it through ironic clarification. Parentheses and asides create an intimate, conversational relationship with the reader while allowing the voice to move between documentary precision and humorous speculation. Short sentences are reserved for emphasis, emotional turns, or understated punch lines.

Diction alternates between polished literary narration and strongly marked frontier speech. The narrative vocabulary is formal and sometimes deliberately ornate, with words such as “inchoate,” “attenuation,” “evanescent,” “prolific,” and “colloquy.” Against this, dialogue uses phonetic spellings, contractions, grammatical irregularities, and regional expressions: “suthin’,” “outer,” “nigh,” “nuffen,” and “snivelization.” Mary’s letter extends this technique through childlike misspelling and sound-based transcription. These nonstandard forms individualize the speakers, establish locality and class, and generate humour, but they also convey warmth, ingenuity, and emotional authenticity.

Dialogue is compact, theatrical, and character-driven. Much of the humour depends on speakers accepting the premise of Mary’s motherhood without openly breaking the illusion. The men’s straight-faced concern about illness, child care, and adoption reveals affection through indirection; their tenderness is disguised as practical talk, teasing, or professional judgment. Social masculinity remains intact on the surface, while paternal devotion becomes evident through action. The understated exchanges are therefore emotionally richer than explicit declarations would be.

Description combines concrete frontier detail with fanciful transformation. Wagons, snowdrifts, saloons, blacksmith forges, mules, saddle bags, pines, and railway offices form a materially convincing setting. The dolls are described with comic physical exactness—the peeling layers, iron prongs, globular heads, canvas bodies, and stubbornly unyielding materials are all vividly tactile. At lyrical moments, the landscape participates in Mary’s imaginative vision: bees accompany her lullaby, pines release moving light and shadow, and love appears to animate Misery’s featureless iron face. Nature is personified as a benevolent “Great Mother,” supplying the maternal protection Mary herself lacks.

Characterization proceeds through anecdote rather than direct psychological analysis. Mary is defined by her actions, naming habits, mispronunciations, improvised creations, and unwavering devotion. The men are similarly revealed through what they do for her dolls: recovering them from snow, repairing them, adopting them, transporting them for visits, and preserving them during her absence. Their rough speech contrasts with their scrupulous gentleness. This inversion—uncouth men behaving with chivalric sensitivity while respectable “civilization” threatens the child’s imaginative life—is central to the story’s moral perspective.

Structurally, the narrative is episodic. It begins by introducing Mary’s five children, then gives miniature histories of individual dolls and the men connected with them. These comic anecdotes gradually accumulate emotional significance. The arrival of school and the railroad introduces a clear turning point: a loose, communal childhood must yield to institutional order. Mary’s departure, her misspelled letter, and her eventual return compress time and shift the story from comic portrait to lament. The final sentence delivers a restrained but poignant distinction between physical identity and lost imaginative identity: Mary returns, but “The Mother of Five” does not.

The style’s central contrasts are between imagination and material fact, tenderness and roughness, nature and civilization, childhood freedom and adult conformity. Its most characteristic technique is to maintain two realities simultaneously. The dolls are visibly scrap objects, yet within the emotional life of Mary and the men they are unquestionably children. The narrator never fully destroys either perspective. The resulting style is humorous, humane, nostalgic, and quietly critical of progress, presenting imagination not as mere childish error but as a shared moral power capable of drawing tenderness from an entire community.

During an evening gathering, General de G— argues that horror requires not merely death or violence, but mystery and unnatural terror. He illustrates this with two wartime experiences.

During the French retreat in 1870, exhausted soldiers seize a strange traveler they suspect of being a spy. Overcome by mob fury, they beat and repeatedly shoot the prisoner. When the general and two gendarmes search the mutilated body, they discover the victim was a woman, perhaps a mother searching for her son in the artillery. The senseless killing and the mystery of her identity make the general understand true horror.

He then recounts the fate of a desert expedition betrayed and massacred by hostile tribes. The few survivors, deprived of supplies and poisoned by supposed allies, retreat across the desert under unbearable heat. Facing starvation, they spread out to protect themselves, but one man kills another for food. The survivors divide and eat the corpse, repeating the process whenever hunger returns, until nearly all are dead. These experiences, the general concludes, reveal the true meaning of the horrible.

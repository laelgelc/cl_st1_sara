The story adopts the form of a folkloric Gothic tale framed as local history. It begins in measured, formal English with an omniscient account of the aged Reverend Soulis, presenting his haunted appearance and the ominous geography of Balweary before moving backward to the events that shaped him. The principal narrative is then delivered in dense Scots vernacular, as though recounted by an elderly parishioner over a drink. This shift gives the supernatural episode the authority, intimacy, and rough immediacy of oral testimony. The closing return to the minister’s later condition completes the circular structure and turns the entire story into an explanation of an already established mystery.

The prose contrasts elevated literary narration with strongly phonetic dialect. The framing passages use grave, precise, sometimes antiquarian diction, while the embedded tale relies on Scots vocabulary and contractions such as “guidwives,” “bairns,” “gloamin’,” “mirk,” “bogle,” and “deil.” Spellings reproduce speech rather than standardized grammar, and proverbial or communal formulations make the voice sound inherited rather than individually composed. Religious language—biblical titles, references to damnation, prayer, witches, the Accuser, and “the powers of evil”—is interwoven with domestic and regional speech. This gives theology and superstition equal linguistic weight. The repeated description of the mysterious figure as a “black man” belongs to an archaic folkloric convention in which blackness signifies the devil, darkness, and unknowability.

Sentences are often long, cumulative, and rhythmically driven, joining observations with repeated conjunctions. In the vernacular sections, “an’,” “but,” “syne,” and “weel” imitate a storyteller advancing the tale aloud, adding detail after detail while delaying decisive revelation. Parallel constructions and repetitions—especially “whiles,” “there was,” “step by step,” and sequences of increasing numbers or movements—create an incantatory momentum. These flowing sentences are punctuated by abrupt dialogue, exclamations, scriptural challenges, and short declarations at moments of danger. The resulting rhythm alternates between patient accumulation and sudden shock.

The narrative voice is communal and morally opinionated. It frequently invokes what “the folk” thought, what “them that kenned best” believed, and what witnesses later saw. Such collective testimony blurs the distinction between fact, gossip, prejudice, and legend. The narrator anticipates objections, qualifies claims, and addresses the listener directly through expressions such as “ye maun understand,” reproducing the manner of oral storytelling. This voice is not neutral: it shares the parish’s religious assumptions and treats supernatural explanations as credible, even while the young Soulis initially represents educated rationalism. The tension between his intellectual scepticism and the villagers’ inherited belief is central to the story’s dramatic irony.

Characterisation is economical and emblematic. Soulis is first shown as an austere, terrified old preacher, then as a learned but inexperienced young minister whose confidence leaves him vulnerable to realities he dismisses. Janet is constructed largely from communal suspicion, bodily distortion, silence, and uncanny gesture rather than psychological access. Her twisted neck, clicking teeth, crooked grin, and inability to utter God’s name become recurring identifying motifs. The supernatural is therefore expressed through corrupted physical detail: the body resembles a corpse before it is known to be one, and ordinary actions such as washing clothes, walking downstairs, or feeling along a wall become horrifying.

Setting is integral to the style. Place-names such as Balweary, Dule Water, Hanging Shaw, Black Hill, and the Devil’s Hag create a densely localized imaginative world while carrying ominous semantic associations. The manse, causeway, stream, moor, old graveyard, and enclosing trees form a landscape of thresholds between settlement and wilderness, Christianity and older belief, life and death. Spatial description is unusually exact, especially in the opening, allowing later supernatural movement through the house and grounds to feel physically credible. Weather functions as a Gothic pressure system: oppressive heat, breathless darkness, unnatural wind, thunder, and violent rain externalize spiritual disturbance.

Suspense develops through foreshadowing, repetition, and graduated sensory disturbance. The opening announces that Soulis has survived some terror without disclosing it. Later, the black stranger, the circling crows, Janet’s altered body, the minister’s recurring “cauld grue,” the sleepless heat, and unexplained sounds progressively narrow the range of possible explanations. Visual horror is often delayed in favour of sound: stamping feet, a bang, wind moving through the house, an unlocked door opening, and footsteps descending toward the waiting minister. The climactic apparition is revealed slowly and anatomically, after which the pace accelerates into command, lightning, combustion, thunder, rain, and flight.

The tone combines solemnity, dread, grim humour, and theological certainty. Colloquial asides and sardonic observations briefly relieve the tension, but they also strengthen the narrator’s credibility by making the account sound conversational rather than ceremonially “literary.” Horror arises from the collision between mundane village life and absolute spiritual evil. The style ultimately affirms a Calvinistic moral universe in which evil may assume bodily form, rational pride is inadequate, and survival depends upon spiritual courage. Yet the lasting emphasis falls less on triumph than trauma: Soulis defeats the possessing presence, but the encounter permanently transforms him into the wild-eyed preacher introduced at the beginning.

The story adopts a mock-chronicle style: an antiquarian, pseudo-historical narrative that combines court history, local legend, erotic fabliau, and comic biography. Its world is late-medieval and early-Renaissance France, but the historical setting is treated playfully rather than with documentary sobriety. Real rulers, offices, castles, place names, saints, and political events lend an air of authenticity, while extravagant inventions and bawdy explanations turn history into folklore. The closing insistence that the account has been restored to its “veritable, historical, and admirable beauty” is deliberately ironic, parodying the authority and patriotic bias of old chroniclers.

The narrator is omniscient, intrusive, sociable, and strongly opinionated. He repeatedly addresses the reader through phrases such as “you may be sure,” “you can believe,” and “I may tell you,” creating the atmosphere of an oral storyteller sharing a notorious local anecdote. He freely interrupts the action to offer maxims, physiological theories, gossip, moral reflections, historical corrections, and comic forecasts. His apparent certainty about private motives, secret encounters, and legendary events is part of the joke. The narrative voice is indulgent toward ingenuity, appetite, and opportunism, treating Jacques’s deception less as vice than as evidence of vitality and practical intelligence.

The prose is expansive and digressive. Long, heavily coordinated sentences accumulate genealogical information, parenthetical qualifications, physical description, comic comparisons, and narrative asides. Dashes and parentheses reproduce the rhythm of a raconteur continually remembering additional details. Periodic stretches of scene and dialogue are surrounded by explanatory commentary, so the pace alternates between leisurely elaboration and abrupt comic action. Formulaic transitions—“To be brief,” “Now,” “In fact,” and “But this is not the subject”—pretend to restore order while usually opening another digression.

The diction mixes elevated, archaic, legal, religious, and courtly registers with colloquial and bodily language. Titles, oaths, feudal terminology, invocations of saints, and phrases such as “by the rood,” “Pasques Dieu,” “liege homage,” and “allodial case” create historical colour. Against these stand earthy idioms, slang-like comparisons, and blunt references to hunger, sex, money, and physical performance. This collision of registers is central to the humour: erotic bargaining is discussed as jurisprudence, seduction as military conquest, and sexual achievement as feudal service to the Crown.

Character description is caricatural and sensuous. Clothing, posture, complexion, fragrance, movement, and bodily attractiveness receive vivid attention, but idealisation is repeatedly punctured by grotesque or comic detail. Jacques is presented as handsome, resilient, hungry, ambitious, and quick-witted; the Regent combines royal majesty with age, desire, vanity, and political cunning. Similes are concrete and often incongruously domestic or animalistic: characters are compared to greyhounds, cats, mice, fish, dogs, sponges, horses, and weather vanes. Such imagery keeps the narrative physical, animated, and irreverent.

The humour is predominantly bawdy, hyperbolic, and ironic. Sexual acts are rarely named directly; instead they are represented through extended euphemisms involving wagers, salutes, homage, assaults, sticks, nuts, trees, artillery, and “dozens.” These metaphors permit sustained double entendre while parodying chivalric, legal, theological, and political discourse. Numerical repetition gives the central erotic feat the structure of a comic ordeal, and the “empty nut” becomes both a punchline and the basis of a mock-serious legal dispute. The narrator’s mock moralising intensifies rather than restrains the ribaldry.

Dialogue is theatrical and functional, producing reversals, exposing motives, and sharpening jokes. Speakers employ ceremonious forms of address, antique oaths, and formal reasoning, yet their exchanges are driven by lust, fear, ambition, or self-preservation. Jacques’s speech is especially adaptive: he moves rapidly from feigned death to romantic declaration, filial pleading, courtly service, erotic bravado, and legal allegory. His verbal agility is the chief mechanism of his social ascent.

Structurally, the story follows a rogue’s rise through a chain of improvised opportunities. Poverty leads to pursuit, pursuit to a staged accident, the accident to intimacy with power, intimacy to patronage, and patronage to wealth and office. The central bedroom episode is framed by public consequences: restoration of the father, acquisition of land, advancement at court, and the building of a castle. A later historical coda broadens the anecdote into an origin story for Jacques’s fortune and for the Château d’Azay, while foreshadowing his eventual execution. This produces a characteristic tonal mixture of comic triumph and fatal hindsight.

The prevailing attitude is anti-solemn and appetite-centred. Religious language, aristocratic honour, female virtue, royal government, and legal deliberation are all treated as masks through which bodily desire and material interest operate. Yet the satire is genial rather than nihilistic. Intelligence, courage, sensual pleasure, local pride, and narrative exuberance are celebrated, even when the narrator pretends to defend historical truth or conventional morality. The overall effect is that of a richly ornamented Renaissance-style tale told by a learned but mischievous chronicler: erudite, digressive, theatrical, worldly, and persistently ribald.

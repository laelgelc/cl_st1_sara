The story uses a close third-person perspective largely filtered through Sun’s child consciousness. The narrator retains grammatical and descriptive control, yet the selection of details, emotional judgments and causal assumptions belong to a young child: adults become “men in black” with coat-tails “like beetles,” the piano tuner may be “the concert,” and a guest’s disappearance suggests that he has gone to fetch a puppy. This free-indirect method gives the prose both innocence and irony. Readers understand the adult party more fully than Sun does, while still experiencing its beauty, confusion and eventual ugliness with his intensity.

The diction is simple, concrete and sensuous, concentrating on colours, shapes, sounds, textures and food. Red roses, gold chairs, white clothes, shining plates, winking glasses and the pink ice-pudding house create a decorative, almost fairy-tale world. Childlike compounds and imprecise descriptions—“much-too-long-and-tight-under-the-arms,” “goldy chairs,” “funny awfully nice hats,” “a cap like a blancmange”—reproduce the way children classify unfamiliar things through appearance and analogy. Familiar household objects are animated or transformed: flower pots nod, glasses wink, and the ice pudding becomes a miniature home with snow, windows and a nut for a handle.

Sentences alternate between short, emphatic statements and cumulative sequences joined by “and” or “then.” The repeated coordination mimics excited observation as one marvel follows another: the chairs come, then the flowers, then the food, then the guests. Lists are central to the style, especially in descriptions of the feast, where abundant objects accumulate until the table seems magical and overwhelming. Repetition intensifies emotion and preserves a spoken, childlike rhythm: “Oh! Oh! Oh!”, “round and round,” “broken—broken,” and “horrid—horrid—horrid.” Exclamations, fragments, dashes and ellipses create pauses of wonder, hesitation or distress, while occasional capitalisation and emphatic phrasing lend the narration the cadence of a story told aloud.

Dialogue is frequent, brisk and sharply characterising. Moon’s impulsive questions and demands make her physical, imitative and pleasure-seeking; Sun’s quieter responses mark him as observant, serious and morally sensitive. Nurse speaks in commands and endearments, Mother in distracted social formulas, and Father shifts from hearty indulgence to abrupt authority. Adult pet names—“ducks,” “lambs,” “sweets,” “pets,” “precious babies”—reduce the children to decorative objects displayed alongside the party arrangements. The contrast between what adults say and what they do produces understated social satire.

The story’s imagery is organised around spectacle, appetite and destruction. Before the party, the dining room resembles an enchanted scene: roses become lights and napkins, a dish becomes a lake, salt cellars become drinking birds, and the pudding becomes a perfect house. After the feast, the same room is rendered through dirt, bones, peels, shells, spilled liquid and broken decorations. The repeated descriptions create a deliberate before-and-after structure, turning abundance into wreckage. This visual reversal is the story’s main stylistic and emotional device.

The plot is episodic and symmetrical. It moves from preparations, to the children’s ceremonial display before the guests, to their exclusion upstairs, and finally to their illicit return after the party. Objects introduced early—especially the nut handle and the ice house—reappear at the climax in altered form. Sun’s earlier reverent circling of the table is echoed by the guests doing the same, but their admiration leads to consumption. The pattern converts childish wonder into disillusionment.

Tone shifts gradually from comic bustle and delighted enchantment to unease, grotesquerie and grief. Much of the humour comes from child logic, exaggerated adult behaviour and the contrast between Sun and Moon. Yet the narration never mocks Sun’s response. His refusal to eat the ruined pudding is presented as an authentic reaction to violated beauty, while the adults treat it as misbehaviour. The closing movement is deliberately abrupt: Father’s joviality disappears, Mother claims vindication, and Sun is expelled. This tonal snap reinforces the emotional isolation of the child.

Overall, the style combines modernist psychological filtering with fairy-tale imagery, domestic realism and social comedy. Its apparent simplicity depends on careful contrasts: innocence and appetite, creation and consumption, beauty and waste, adult affection and adult insensitivity. The prose makes ordinary preparations appear miraculous, then allows the aftermath to become horrifying through the same uncompromisingly child-centred vision.

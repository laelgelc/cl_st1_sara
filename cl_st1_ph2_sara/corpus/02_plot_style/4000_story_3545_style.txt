The story adopts the manner of a Christian moral fable set within a historically grounded account of Russian serfdom. Its governing principle is announced by the biblical epigraph and then demonstrated through a stark opposition between retaliatory violence and patient non-resistance. The narration is earnest, didactic, and ethically unambiguous: cruelty is repeatedly identified as sin, goodness as spiritual strength, and the final outcome as evidence of divine justice. Rather than leaving the meaning implicit, the closing sentence states the lesson directly.

The narrator is third-person, omniscient, and authoritative. It begins with broad historical exposition, narrows its focus to Michael Simeonovitch and the oppressed peasants, and occasionally enters individual states of mind. Characters are commonly introduced with moral labels—“barbarous,” “tyrannical,” “good-hearted,” “peaceably-inclined”—so characterization is categorical rather than psychologically ambiguous. The narrator’s sympathies remain firmly with the serfs, who are repeatedly described as “poor,” “unfortunate,” and “overworked,” while Michael is represented through animalistic and demonic comparisons. This creates the polarized moral world typical of a parable.

The diction combines plain storytelling with archaic, biblical, and translated-Russian elements. Scriptural forms such as “ye,” “hath,” and “unto” establish a solemn religious frame, while phrases including “return evil for evil,” “peace on earth,” and “good-will to men” sustain it. Russian social and cultural terms—“moujiks,” “kopeck,” “samovar,” and “vodki”—supply local color, as do patronymics and diminutives such as “Michael Simeonovitch” and “Mishinka.” The prose otherwise favors accessible vocabulary, explicit causal links, and conventional moral expressions such as “forgotten God,” “sunk in sin,” and “devil in human form.”

Sentences are often long, balanced, and connected by coordinating or subordinating clauses. Transitional formulas—“It happened that,” “On one occasion,” “Accordingly,” “Toward the evening,” and “At length”—give the narrative the steady, sequential movement of an orally recounted tale. Exposition tends to summarize social conditions and repeated abuses, whereas major moral confrontations unfold through extended dialogue. Repetition reinforces both argument and emotion: the peasants repeatedly debate killing Michael, Peter repeatedly warns against sin, and Michael’s terrified cry, “He has conquered me,” marks the spiritual turning point.

Dialogue is direct, rhetorical, and functional. Speakers voice competing moral positions more than subtle individual psychologies. Vasili argues through urgency, indignation, analogy, and appeals to collective welfare; Peter answers with religious doctrine, warnings about the soul, and the claim that violence reproduces evil. The sparrows-and-hawk anecdote acts as a compact folk parable within the larger parable. Michael’s speech is dominated by commands, threats, interrogations, and contemptuous generalizations, revealing his obsession with control. His wife’s affectionate diminutive and pleading tone briefly introduce domestic tenderness, though even this scene chiefly serves the moral contrast.

The structure is linear and carefully escalatory. Historical background leads to a catalogue of abuses, secret meetings, a proposed murder, Peter’s opposition, the sacrilegious order to work during Easter, the miraculous candle, Michael’s collapse of confidence, his accidental death, and the liberation of the serfs. Suspense comes less from uncertainty about the moral truth than from uncertainty about how justice will occur. The candle is the central symbol: fragile in physical terms yet miraculously resistant to wind and violent movement, it embodies faith, peace, and goodness that cannot be extinguished by oppression. Peter’s calm labor contrasts with both Michael’s tyranny and the other peasants’ vengefulness.

Irony shapes the resolution. Vasili predicts that Michael will die “like a dog,” but the peasants do not kill him; instead, his death results from an ordinary accident occurring just as he is apparently setting out to reverse his command. The feared ruler dies helplessly at a village gate, while Peter—the advocate of nonviolence—is the only person courageous enough to approach and honor the body. Michael’s claim that Peter has “conquered” him identifies moral endurance, rather than physical force, as the story’s true form of power.

The tone moves from sober historical explanation through indignation and mounting menace to religious wonder and providential closure. Concrete details intensify the contrasts: the peasants plough while Easter bells ring, whereas Michael consumes an elaborate meal, drinks vodka, plays guitar, and commands the cook to sing. Such juxtaposition makes exploitation visible without elaborate imagery. Violence is described plainly rather than sensationally, and the supernatural enters in a restrained but unmistakable form through the candle.

To reproduce this style, one would use a scriptural epigraph, an omniscient narrator with explicit moral authority, a simple chronological plot, socially specific rural details, and characters organized around opposing ethical principles. The prose should alternate broad explanatory summary with long argumentative conversations, employ recurrent religious language and folk analogies, and build toward a symbolic miracle or providential reversal. The ending should resolve both the external injustice and the moral debate, then articulate the lesson in a concise, universal statement.

The story combines historical romance, revolutionary adventure, and Gothic horror. It is narrated retrospectively in the first person by a cultivated male protagonist whose intimate testimony gives the extraordinary events an air of authenticity. His voice is formal, emotionally candid, and intermittently reflective: he records practical details with apparent precision, then shifts into heightened expressions of love, dread, grief, and bewilderment. Occasional appeals to the reader, such as “You know,” create the impression of an oral confession or memoir.

The diction is elevated and distinctly nineteenth-century in flavour. Polite forms of address, French names and phrases, and period-specific political vocabulary—“citizens,” “sans-culottes,” “Cordeliers,” “pass,” and “guillotine”—establish the Revolutionary setting. Social identity is encoded in speech and manners: Solange’s instinctive use of “messieurs,” her delicate irony, and her aristocratic bearing expose the inadequacy of her laundress disguise. The prose often favours ceremonious formulations such as “I am at your disposal,” “I shall make no concealment,” and “Language has no words to express my gratitude,” lending even urgent exchanges a courtly quality.

Dialogue dominates the first half and is used to move the plot rapidly. Exchanges are composed of short questions, answers, exclamations, and repeated names, producing a brisk, theatrical rhythm. Solange’s wit and composure emerge through verbal fencing, while Albert’s direct questions reveal both his curiosity and his growing attachment. Repetition gives promises and appointments emotional weight: “to-morrow,” “at ten,” “Albert,” and “Solange” become motifs of trust and intimacy. Names themselves are symbolically important, since the lovers know each other only through improvised aliases; their private forms of address create a fragile refuge from the state’s surveillance.

The narrative structure is strongly linear and carefully escalatory. The opening begins in medias res with a scream and an arrest, followed by rescue, clandestine meetings, the father’s escape, and the lovers’ brief happiness. Summary compresses the ensuing months, but scene-by-scene narration returns as danger intensifies. The ending slows dramatically inside the abandoned chapel, where every sound, movement, and sensation is prolonged. This change from swift historical adventure to claustrophobic horror makes the final revelation especially forceful.

Foreshadowing is pervasive. The guillotine experiments, the blood trench, the drowned child, Marie Antoinette’s execution, the howling dog, the intercepted letter, the lovers’ tearful separation, and the storm all anticipate catastrophe. The narrator’s scientific access to severed heads initially appears to be a grim historical detail, but it becomes the mechanism of the climax. Romantic and macabre strands are thus tightly interwoven: the very work intended “for the good of humanity” brings Albert face to face with the mutilated body of the woman he loves.

The descriptive style becomes increasingly pictorial and sensational. Early moonlight idealises Solange through conventional romantic portraiture—pearl-like teeth, queenly hands, childlike feet—while the later landscape is dominated by rain, darkness, mud, open graves, bare trees, blood, and decay. The abandoned chapel functions as a desecrated sacred space: its empty altar and stripped walls reflect a society in which political violence has displaced religion. Placing the sack of heads before the altar turns the laboratory into a grotesque sanctuary and Solange’s severed head into a terrible relic.

Weather and setting externalise emotion. Serene moonlight accompanies attraction and hope; cold rain, wind, and premature darkness accompany suspicion and impending death. The storm’s sounds are rendered through accumulating auditory imagery—the splashing rain, moaning wind, lashing branches, swinging door, and trembling flame—until ordinary noises seem supernatural. Long, cumulative sentences reproduce Albert’s mounting panic, while abrupt declarations such as “The voice seemed to proceed from the sack!” punctuate the suspense.

The climax uses controlled repetition and delayed recognition. The disembodied calls of “Albert!” grow progressively fainter, and Albert approaches the sack through a sequence of tactile and visual sensations: warm lips, the lifted head, half-closed eyes, tears, and a final gaze. Sentence fragments, exclamation marks, physical verbs, and repeated cries of “Solange!” convey psychological collapse. The apparent post-mortem consciousness remains unexplained, preserving a supernatural ambiguity within an otherwise historically concrete narrative.

Emotion is presented in an openly melodramatic register. Love is instantaneous, absolute, and expressed through vows, tears, embraces, kisses, throbbing hearts, delirium, and self-sacrifice. Characters tend to embody clear moral qualities: Albert is generous and honour-bound, Solange is courageous and devoted, and figures such as Danton and Marceau are characterised through decisive gestures and memorable traits. Yet irony complicates the heroic surface. Albert succeeds in saving Solange’s father but cannot save Solange; the passport intended to secure her freedom helps precipitate the lovers’ refusal to part; and his scientific investigation of execution ultimately becomes a form of personal torment.

Overall, the style relies on sharp contrasts: aristocratic refinement against revolutionary brutality, private loyalty against public suspicion, rational science against supernatural terror, and romantic ecstasy against bodily mutilation. Its central effect comes from allowing a tender rescue narrative to develop into a fatalistic Gothic tragedy, while the precise historical setting makes the horror feel not merely fantastic but inseparable from institutional violence.

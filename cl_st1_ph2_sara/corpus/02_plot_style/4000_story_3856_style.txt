The story adopts the form of a framed oral adventure narrative. A brief, dialogue-led opening establishes an educated but sceptical listener, the Ethnologist, and a mildly amused raconteur, the lieutenant. The central episode then unfolds almost entirely through the lieutenant’s first-person recollection before the story returns to the framing conversation for a dry, understated conclusion. This structure makes the tale feel like an anecdote told socially rather than a formally polished heroic account, while the listener’s questions provide prompts, interruptions, and changes of emotional emphasis.

The narrative voice is conversational, self-deprecating, and distinctly British in its irony. The lieutenant habitually minimizes danger through flippant phrasing, treating a disastrous retreat, dehydration, death, and an improvised parachute as inconveniences in an entertaining yarn. Expressions such as “finis!”, “that or nothing,” and “to cut it short, I parachuted” compress moments of extreme peril into laconic statements. Heroism is therefore conveyed indirectly: the speaker avoids grand claims, jokes about his fear, and frames courage as an obligation imposed by rank and embarrassment before his men. The Ethnologist’s indignation and the lieutenant’s cheerful evasiveness create a comic contrast between academic seriousness and military anecdote.

Dialogue dominates the prose and supplies much of its characterization. Speakers are differentiated through vocabulary, rhythm, and social register: the Ethnologist uses educated, exasperated language; the lieutenant combines officer-class ease with colloquial military idiom; and the soldiers’ speech is represented through dialect spellings and blunt remarks. Short exchanges interrupt longer narrative passages, preventing the account from becoming static and preserving the sense of a live performance. Repeated conversational tags—“said the lieutenant,” “says Hooker,” “said one of the men”—are plain and unobtrusive, keeping attention on the story rather than on elaborate description of delivery.

The diction mixes formal, educated references with slang and practical military terminology. Allusions to Xenophon, Joshua, ethnology, folklore, and imperial expeditions coexist with phrases such as “funked,” “pot every one of us,” “cock-and-bull story,” and “spin much of a yarn.” References to Sepoys, Sappers, Martini rifles, matchlocks, and expeditionary command establish a specific colonial-military setting without lengthy exposition. The language also contains racial and colonial labels now recognized as derogatory or paternalistic. These mark the historical viewpoint of the narrators and the imperial culture surrounding the episode, in which local people are alternately romanticized, mocked, feared, and treated as objects of ethnographic study.

Sentence structure shifts with pace. The framing dialogue relies on short, pointed sentences and quick turns, while the lieutenant’s account uses longer cumulative sentences to trace routes, tactical decisions, and physical obstacles. During pursuit and escape, clauses are linked in rapid sequence by “and,” reproducing breathless forward movement. At moments of crisis, abrupt fragments and isolated declarations sharpen the impact: “Then, finis!” and “After a day or so …” create dramatic pauses. Parenthetical qualifications, corrections, measurements, and directional details imitate spontaneous recollection, making the speaker appear to reconstruct events aloud.

Description is selective, concrete, and strongly sensory. The landscape is rendered through cliffs, shelves, boulders, steep hills, narrow river crossings, and exposed ledges, so geography becomes the principal source of suspense. Sound imagery is especially effective: bullets come “twanging” down the slope, the river goes “giggle, giggle” below the trapped men, and water later makes a “gluck.” Heat, blackened lips, burning throats, slippery stones, moonlight, and the movement of the cliff face during the descent give the physical ordeal immediacy. Similes are vivid but economical, comparing stones to ice, the sun to a near furnace, and the opening parachute to a sail.

The pacing follows a controlled escalation. The opening treats the flying-man legend as absurd folklore; the retreat then develops through successive reversals—the ambush, pursuit, wounded soldier, apparent refuge, dead-end ledge, and thirst. The long period of entrapment slows the narrative almost to stasis, mirroring the men’s exhaustion and the “barrenness of incident.” The parachute construction and first aborted run restore momentum, after which the descent is narrated through intensified visual perception and compressed action. The ending rapidly releases tension, then adds a final grim fact about two more men jumping, followed by the lieutenant’s casual drink. This coda preserves the story’s characteristic mixture of comedy and horror.

Irony governs both plot and style. The Ethnologist complains that authentic folklore has been displaced by an implausible legend about the lieutenant, only for the lieutenant to reveal the rational event beneath the myth. Yet the explanation is almost as extraordinary as the legend, so the distinction between modern fact and traditional wonder remains unstable. The parachute appears to the local witnesses as supernatural flight, demonstrating how limited perception, cultural interpretation, and retelling transform experience into myth. The speaker’s understatement deepens this irony: the more casually he tells the story, the more severe its violence and suffering appear.

Overall, the style combines club-room anecdote, imperial adventure, black comedy, and survival suspense. It is brisk, masculine, technically attentive, and orally textured, with emotional force generated less by explicit reflection than by juxtaposition: jokes beside deaths, practical improvisation beside delirium, and cheerful sociability beside remembered thirst. The closing gesture of helping himself to whisky and soda encapsulates the method, converting trauma into a civilized ritual while quietly reminding the reader that the experience has not been forgotten.

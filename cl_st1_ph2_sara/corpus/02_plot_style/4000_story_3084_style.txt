The story adopts the form of a Western frame narrative: a reflective first-person narrator accompanies the veteran cowboy Bunt on a night watch, while Bunt supplies the embedded comic tale of Cock-eye Blacklock and Sloppy Weather. The outer narrative is quiet, atmospheric, and elegiac; the inner narrative is expansive, colloquial, violent, and farcical. This contrast gives the story its characteristic movement from campfire realism through nocturnal wonder and oral storytelling to a broad comic climax, followed by a serene return to daybreak.

The narrative voice operates in two distinct registers. The unnamed narrator speaks in polished, literary prose, with an educated vocabulary and a tendency toward historical reflection, lyrical description, and gently ironic commentary. His portrait of Bunt enlarges one cowboy into a representative of a vanishing frontier culture, particularly through the repeated account of what Bunt “had seen”: the end of gold and buffalo, the rise of cattle and wheat, and the spread of barbed wire. Bunt’s voice, by contrast, is rendered as highly stylized frontier dialect. It uses dropped endings, nonstandard grammar, phonetic spellings, compressed forms, malapropisms, and cowboy idioms: “outen camp,” “clumb,” “cu-roos,” “friction tales,” and “a’ imaginary mind.” His speech constantly translates experience into the language of cards, horses, mining, weapons, and weather. This dialect is less a naturalistic transcript than a comic literary performance, designed to establish personality, region, occupation, and oral rhythm.

Bunt’s manner of storytelling is digressive and cumulative. He delays the promised death of Blacklock through background, local anecdotes, character sketches, explanations, and repeated summaries of the villain’s wickedness. Formulaic transitions such as “Well,” “You see,” and “let me tell you” imitate spontaneous speech and allow him to keep restarting or redirecting the tale. Repetition reinforces both comedy and suspense: Blacklock is repeatedly described as lawless yet technically “within the law,” while Sloppy Weather is repeatedly called a “fool pup,” preparing the exact behavior that will bring about the outlaw’s death. The narration therefore feels loose and improvisational on the surface, but its comic mechanism is carefully planted.

The dialogue is the story’s dominant energy. Bunt’s speech is full of hyperbole, mock solemnity, elaborate insults, and incongruous sophistication. He can call bad food “a’ ingenious device for removing superfluous appetite,” describe surveillance as “keeping case,” and turn moral judgment into poker odds or mining slang. Much of the humor comes from mixing elevated abstractions with rough frontier grammar. Bunt also habitually converts ordinary action into theatrical spectacle: hunger requires chloroform, a scarce dollar becomes a museum exhibit, and dynamite turns a riverbank into an airborne confusion of “trees an’ rocks and cart-loads of dirt an’ dogs and Blacklocks and rivers an’ smoke an’ fire generally.” His narrative voice is garrulous, vivid, and confidently exaggerated.

The literary narration surrounding Bunt is strongly visual and auditory. The night sky is made intimate through metaphor: Venus becomes “a yellow lamp hung in a tree,” Mars “a red lantern in a clock-tower,” and the constellations march like Roman cohorts. The prose rejects abstract astronomical scale in favor of immediate sensory presence. At ground level, the desert is constructed from minute sounds—the distant coyote, snapping sagebrush, cooling earth, clicking horns, stamping hooves, and cattle breathing as they settle. Long, balanced sentences and catalogues slow the pace, reproducing the stillness of the night ride. Personification and simile make the landscape at once realistic and enchanted, creating a setting in which folklore, ghost stories, and improbable frontier anecdotes seem credible.

The structure is notably circular and time-bound. The story begins after supper in firelight, proceeds into darkness, occupies the middle watches with conversation, and ends at sunrise and the return to camp. Bunt’s tale fills the night so completely that dawn arrives as an interruption: “Hello, there’s the sun!” The narrator then describes night closing “like a great book,” an image that identifies the night itself with the story just completed. The final paragraph mirrors the earlier nocturnal description but replaces stars, mystery, and muffled noises with frost, dew, cattle, smoke, and morning movement. This creates a clean transition from imaginative storytelling back to practical ranch life.

Pacing alternates between stillness and acceleration. The opening ride and herd watch unfold slowly, with descriptive pauses and reflective passages. Bunt’s narrative initially proceeds by leisurely accretion, but the dynamite episode becomes increasingly rapid. Shorter actions, direct exclamations, and linked verbs reproduce the chase: the dog swims out, retrieves the explosive, follows Blacklock, and mistakes panic for play. The explosion is conveyed through a deliberately excessive catalogue rather than a precise physical account, preserving the tall-tale tone. Afterward, the pace relaxes almost instantly into burial, mock epitaph, and dawn.

The story blends elegy with burlesque. Bunt is treated affectionately as a relic of the “good old, rare old, wild old days,” but the narrative does not present frontier violence with simple heroic grandeur. Blacklock’s career is sordid, the law is ineffective, and his end comes not through a formal gunfight but through an absurd collision of criminality, dynamite, and canine obedience. The dog functions as an accidental agent of rough poetic justice. The mock epitaph and its moral—“A hook and line is good enough fish-tackle for any honest man”—parody the improving conclusions of moral anecdotes while still delivering a clear ethical verdict.

A further stylistic feature is the juxtaposition of cultural periods. An old Southern song once performed with a harp in Charleston is sung by a cowboy to calm Idaho cattle; Roman constellations hang above a modernizing West; Bunt’s memories connect soldiers, miners, gamblers, Native conflict, cattle drives, railroads, and regulatory commissions. These layered references deepen the central theme of historical transition. Bunt belongs to an oral, mobile, pre-fence world, while the narrator preserves him in literary form just as that world is disappearing.

The tone is affectionate, amused, and nostalgic rather than strictly realistic. Violence and death are filtered through exaggeration and comic distance, while the outer landscape retains genuine grandeur and melancholy. The period language includes ethnic slurs and stereotypes for Chinese, Italian, Mexican, Indigenous, and mixed-race people; these are embedded in the frontier dialect and social attitudes being represented, and they mark the work’s historical milieu as well as its dated conventions. Overall, the style combines lyrical nature writing, regional dialect, frontier reminiscence, oral tall-tale technique, and comic retributive plotting.

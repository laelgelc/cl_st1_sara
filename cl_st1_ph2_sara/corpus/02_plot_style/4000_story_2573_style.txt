The story adopts a vigorous autobiographical-adventure style, told retrospectively in the first person by a narrator who combines the authority of an eyewitness with the polish of an experienced raconteur. The voice is confident, worldly, irreverent, and self-mocking. Although the narrator presents himself as resourceful and resilient, he does not conceal his opportunism, indiscipline, or youthful selfishness. Instead, he turns these faults into comic material, describing dubious conduct with mock-heroic pride before qualifying it through hindsight. This creates a double perspective: the energetic young tramp acts within the events, while the older narrator looks back with amusement, affection, and occasional regret.

The diction mixes plain, muscular narration with period slang and the specialist vocabulary of tramp, railroad, military, and river life. Terms such as “stiff,” “push,” “coppered,” “throw my feet,” “side-door Pullman,” “profesh,” “kicks,” and “sky-pieces” establish a distinct social milieu. Much of this argot is introduced directly or made intelligible through context, allowing the prose to remain accessible while retaining authenticity. Colloquial American expressions—“fell down,” “going some,” “put it all over,” “scared stiff,” and “fixed us”—keep the tone oral and informal. Against this demotic language, the narrator occasionally places elevated or ceremonious phrases such as “mighty affirmation,” “colossal picnic,” “invincible device,” and “distinguished officer.” The contrast is a major source of humor.

Sentence structure is flexible and strongly rhythmic. Clear declarative sentences drive the action, while longer cumulative sentences convey crowds, movement, abundance, and logistical confusion. Repetition is frequently used for momentum: “another, and another,” “train after train,” and the recurring “one, two” of the boat manoeuvre reproduce the sensation of mechanical speed and coordinated action. Parallel constructions and lists are especially prominent. Catalogues of towns, railway stock, camp entertainments, provisions, and clothing give the narrative documentary density while also producing comic excess. Short sentences and fragments provide emphatic turns—“There was no first train”; “It was up to Des Moines”; “And still we went on eating”—and often function as punch lines.

The narrative is episodic and broadly chronological. It moves from the failed attempt to seize a train, through the march across Iowa and the occupation of Des Moines, into the improvised river expedition and the narrator’s eventual desertion. Within that progression, individual episodes are shaped like comic anecdotes: a problem is introduced, schemes escalate, an improvised solution appears, and the result produces a reversal or anticlimax. The railroad companies emptying their yards, the inadequately sized relief train, Des Moines financing boats to remove the hoboes, the “advance boat” stealing the best provisions, and the linked boats passing stranded police craft all follow this pattern. Transitions such as “Then,” “But,” “And still,” and “Poor General Kelly!” preserve the forward pull and reinforce the effect of an animated spoken reminiscence.

The pacing alternates between compressed historical summary and vivid scenic narration. Weeks of marching may be covered in a paragraph, while a confrontation, rapid, or comic mishap is rendered moment by moment. Dialogue is sparse but theatrical. General Kelly’s ultimatum to the committee of safety is presented as a compact dramatic scene, with call-and-response exchanges, a crowd’s collective voice, and a timed threat. Elsewhere, shouted dialogue from the police boats becomes slapstick, especially when the narrator’s crew pretends to be helplessly swept past. Such scenes interrupt exposition at strategic points and give the account immediacy.

Humor governs nearly every level of the prose. Much of it comes from understatement, exaggeration, mock logic, euphemism, and incongruity. Theft becomes “skimming the cream”; selfishness becomes “initiative and enterprise”; inability to beg because farmers have been warned becomes inability “to make an honest living.” The narrator repeatedly borrows the rhetoric of capitalism, military glory, civic duty, and technological invention to describe scavenging and insubordination. Mock-heroic language turns the hoboes into an “Army,” a boating trick into an “invincible device,” and a quarrel within one company into a “revolution.” Arithmetical detail is also comic: the calculation of six thousand meals a day transforms hunger into a municipal crisis, while the narrator’s apology for only “ten per cent” of the trouble preserves his cheerfully unrepentant persona.

Direct address strengthens the conversational relationship with the reader. Phrases such as “gentle reader,” “Do a little mental arithmetic,” and the question about climbing barbed wire with milk buckets make the narration feel like an after-dinner story or public lecture. These intrusions also allow the narrator to pause, comment, tease, and control the audience’s response. The occasional apostrophe to former companions—“here’s my hand”—adds warmth and sincerity beneath the comic bravado.

Imagery is concrete and kinetic rather than lyrical. Trains thunder past, sleet pelts the camp, boats strike snags and pivot in the current, men leap back and forth, and stranded craft pile up among boulders and shoals. Visual detail tends to focus on crowds, machinery, supplies, clothing, and bodily action. Sound is equally important: whistles toot, bugles blow, crowds shout, and boats hit obstacles with the onomatopoeic sequence “Smash!,” “Bang!,” and “Whop!” The landscape is sketched economically, often through broad regional phrases such as the “fat Iowa country” or “virgin farming country.” Human systems and improvised movement matter more than scenic contemplation.

The story’s social vision is sympathetic but unsentimental. The hoboes are represented as talented, comic, hungry, unruly, and internally divided rather than idealized victims. Farmers and townspeople can be hospitable or fearful; railway companies are obdurate but strategically effective; civic leaders act from generosity, self-interest, or desperation. The narrative admires collective endurance while repeatedly celebrating individual initiative, even when that initiative shades into exploitation. This tension between solidarity and individualism is explicit in the narrator’s description of his group as “graceless individualists.” The prose neither fully condemns nor excuses them, relying instead on candid self-exposure and comic irony.

The diary extract near the end introduces a contrasting documentary register. Its dated entries, exact times, abbreviated place names, and clipped reports strip away much of the retrospective ornament. This gives the story a factual foundation and captures the transient, fragmentary rhythm of life on the road: companions disappear, trains are caught or missed, and alliances dissolve without ceremony. The final retrospective coda returns to the older narrator’s humorous voice. Learning that the crew’s supposedly original invention had long been used in China gently punctures the story’s heroic self-image and closes on a pragmatic maxim. The ending therefore encapsulates the overall style: experiential, inventive, comic, skeptical of grand claims, and ultimately interested in whether an idea—or a person—can survive the test of action.

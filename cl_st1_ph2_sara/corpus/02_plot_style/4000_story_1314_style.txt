The story uses a first-person, retrospective narrator who is both participant and philosophical observer. His voice combines the practical knowledge of an itinerant labourer with the sensibility of a poet and the reflective authority of a memoirist. He moves freely between precise eyewitness description, autobiographical aside, social commentary, sardonic judgement and expansive meditation. Although he sometimes presents himself humorously—as an improvised accoucheur who forgets what to do next—his emotional openness gives the narrative a strong humanistic sincerity.

The prose is richly visual, sensuous and highly figurative. Landscape is rendered through saturated colours, textures, sounds and movements: gold chestnuts, turquoise and emerald hills, silver mountain tops, white foam, dark-blue water, singing streams and shifting autumn leaves. Similes are frequent, concrete and often unexpected. Leaves resemble salmon fry or amputated hands; the sea seems planed by invisible carpenters; a labouring woman writhes like birch bark in a fire; a brook talks like a maiden confiding in someone about her lover. Personification is equally prominent. The sea murmurs, bushes peer at waves, clouds dispatch shadows, and the sun contemplates humanity. Nature therefore functions not as passive scenery but as an animated presence that comments emotionally and symbolically on human events.

The dominant descriptive mode is lyrical and panoramic, but it is repeatedly grounded by coarse physical detail. Sublime accounts of the Caucasus coexist with hunger, sweat, stings, illness, blood, bodily fluids and the violence of childbirth. This juxtaposition is central to the style: beauty is not offered as an escape from suffering but as something that surrounds, contests and sometimes transforms it. The birth scene is especially naturalistic, describing pain and bodily processes with unusual directness, yet it gradually acquires religious and visionary overtones. The newborn is at once slippery, noisy and awkwardly comic, “a rosy little homunculus,” and a symbol of pristine humanity and unknowable possibility.

Sentences are often long, cumulative and syntactically layered. Participial phrases, parenthetical remarks, embedded explanations and coordinated clauses allow perception, memory and reflection to unfold simultaneously. A single sentence may begin with an observed object, branch into a historical anecdote, turn toward social commentary and conclude with an image. Repetition supplies rhetorical momentum, particularly in passages beginning with “Yes,” in reiterated appeals to divine figures, and in the closing cadence of the murmuring sea. Short sentences and exclamations appear at moments of discovery, pain or emotional climax, creating sharp contrasts with the leisurely descriptive periods.

The diction mixes elevated, archaic and literary language with colloquial speech. Terms such as “moribund,” “parturition,” “accoucheur,” “wherefore,” “delectation” and “hither” lend the narration a formal, translated-classical quality, while peasant dialogue remains plain, repetitive and idiomatic. Capitalisation, emphatic italics or typographical stress, exclamation marks, dashes and ellipses heighten the spoken and rhetorical character of the prose. Geographic names, local foods, units such as versts and culturally specific practices give the narrative ethnographic density. Historical allusions, religious formulas and references to folk customs broaden the setting beyond the immediate incident.

Dialogue is economical and strongly characterising. The displaced peasants speak in resigned formulas, while the mother’s language alternates between curses, modest requests, practical concerns and devotional expressions. Her speech preserves embarrassment about childbirth and attachment to traditional ritual, as in her concern over where the afterbirth should be buried. Exchanges with the narrator also introduce dry humour: he claims to be a medical student to explain his knowledge, and she immediately interprets his abandoned studies through the familiar explanation of drink. Such comedy never cancels suffering; instead, it makes the characters more intimate and human.

Tonally, the story ranges from rapture to bitterness, grotesque comedy, compassion and religious awe. The narrator can praise humanity’s privilege in one paragraph and wish graveyard worms upon an authoritarian acquaintance in another. His sentiment is intense but moderated by irony and material exactness. The treatment of the mother is compassionate without idealising her into delicacy: she is fearful, ashamed, physically battered, stubborn, devout and extraordinarily resilient. The narrator’s pity likewise appears through action, nervous swearing and tears rather than through detached moral pronouncement alone.

Structurally, the narrative moves from contemplation to ordeal and then to renewal. The opening establishes an Edenic yet autumnal landscape and develops a broad philosophy of beauty, suffering and imperfect humanity. The arrival of the famine migrants introduces social deprivation and displacement. The solitary birth then concentrates these themes into an immediate bodily crisis. The final walk restores the panoramic landscape, but its meaning has changed: the natural world now accompanies mother, child and narrator as a kind of secular-sacred procession. The recurring sea provides continuity throughout, shifting from distant thunder to intimate murmuring and finally becoming the story’s closing refrain.

The style is governed by strong contrasts: luxuriant Caucasian nature against famine migration, northern memories against southern abundance, modesty against bodily exposure, brutality against tenderness, despair against new life, and cosmic rhetoric against domestic necessities such as tea, string and a kettle. These contrasts support a socially conscious humanism. The story recognises poverty, uprooting and an uncertain future, yet locates dignity in mutual aid, maternal endurance and the bare fact of human birth. Its final effect is therefore neither purely tragic nor purely celebratory, but a lyrical affirmation made credible by having passed through pain, physical realism and doubt.

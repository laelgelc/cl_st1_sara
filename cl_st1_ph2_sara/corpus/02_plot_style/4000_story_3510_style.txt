The piece adopts the form of a rambling, retrospective critical essay rather than a tightly plotted short story. It begins in autobiographical reminiscence, expands into a social and cultural history of British pictorial humor, and eventually arrives at an appreciative assessment of John Leech. Its structure is deliberately digressive: memories of childhood exhibitions, books, caricatures, manners, and punishments accumulate before the ostensible subject appears. The phrase “in the consulship of Plancus” functions as a recurring comic refrain, mock-heroically translating the narrator’s youth into remote Roman history and binding the digressions together. Transitions often arise through association rather than strict argument, while the delayed announcement of the “subject in hand” makes the long preface itself part of the joke.

The narrative voice is that of a cultivated, sociable, opinionated older observer. It generally speaks in the first-person plural—“we,” “our,” and “us”—which blends personal memory with the experience of a generation and invites readers into a shared cultural community. At other moments it turns directly to “you,” asks readers to look at particular images, or imagines children interrogating their grandparents. This creates the atmosphere of an animated fireside conversation or public lecture. The narrator is authoritative without being impersonal: he confesses his age, tastes, prejudices, and sentimental attachments, and his judgments gain force from the sense that he has lived among the objects and manners he describes.

The dominant tone combines nostalgia, genial satire, mock indignation, and affectionate moralism. Childhood discomforts are exaggerated for comic effect: old pictures are terrifyingly gloomy, schoolboys wash in icy troughs, and modern children are “young villains” unjustly blessed with picture-books, baths, and towels. Yet the nostalgia is not wholly reactionary. The narrator recognizes that modern humor has become less coarse and cruel, and he welcomes its increasing kindness, domesticity, and suitability for women and children. His admiration for Cruikshank and Leech is warm and personal, while his condemnation of vulgarity, pretension, swagger, and institutional pomposity remains playful rather than severe.

The humor depends heavily on irony and incongruity. Grim or violent subjects are introduced with falsely soothing adjectives such as “pleasing,” “nice,” “amiable,” and “good-humored.” Napoleon becomes “poor Master Boney” while being kicked by every nation; drowning enemies smile amid “dimpling waters”; and a terrifying old waxwork is nostalgically praised for its murderers and dead bodies. Elevated classical language is repeatedly applied to trivial modern matters, as in the Roman dating formula, the description of watchmen as “guardians of the streets of Rome,” and the fork called a “BIDENT.” Conversely, grand people and institutions are reduced to domestic or bodily comedy. This oscillation between high and low registers is central to the style.

The prose is expansive, rhythmic, and highly enumerative. Long sentences gather lists of paintings, artists, garments, rooms, gestures, social types, and comic incidents, often using semicolons, dashes, parentheses, and repeated clauses. Anaphora is frequent: “there was,” “what,” “how,” and “now” launch successive details and create the momentum of spoken enthusiasm. These accumulations often culminate in an exclamation, rhetorical question, or comic reversal. Shorter sentences occasionally interrupt the flow for emphasis—“Miss Linwood’s was worsted-work,” for example—or deliver a crisp judgment after a lengthy catalogue.

Rhetorical questions are among the most characteristic devices. They express mock grievance, guide the reader’s attention, dramatize praise, and convert criticism into conversation: “What have these children done that they should be so much happier than we were?” and “Can we have too much of truth, and fun, and beauty, and kindness?” Exclamations likewise reproduce the narrator’s immediate emotional response. Imperatives such as “Fancy,” “Look,” “Watch,” and “Remark” turn visual criticism into a guided tour, encouraging the reader to inspect pictures alongside the speaker.

The diction mixes learned allusion, period slang, colloquial phrasing, French expressions, and domestic vocabulary. Classical references coexist with words such as “old-fogyfied,” “scapegraces,” “wags,” “young villains,” and “stunning.” Latin, Greek, and French phrases lend mock erudition and evoke a highly educated readership, while slang quoted from earlier decades provides historical color. Capitalized words—“THAT,” “OUR,” “THESE,” and “DID”—simulate emphatic speech and sharpen contrasts between generations. Proper names and topical references are densely deployed, giving the prose the texture of cultural memory and assuming familiarity with British art, literature, politics, journalism, and fashionable life.

Description is vivid, visual, and frequently grotesque. Figures are defined through posture, costume, facial expression, and bodily detail: rolling eyes, quivering fingers, glass eyes, cloven feet, greasy hands, tufts of beard, extravagant waistcoats, and trembling fingers. Verbs are energetic and often comic—people gnash, grin, howl, caper, tumble, twang, swagger, and strut. The narrator is especially attentive to material surfaces and objects: satin, worsted, steel armor, soap, boots, bonnets, furniture, food, and household fittings. This concreteness reflects the subject matter of caricature and illustration, but it also turns social history into a succession of tangible scenes.

Characterization proceeds through representative social types rather than psychological depth. The essay populates its world with grandmothers, schoolboys, dandies, watchmen, footmen, nurses, cooks, policemen, hairdressers, artists, aristocrats, and middle-class families. Individuals are often captured through one telling gesture, garment, posture, or line of dialogue. Leech’s characters are praised precisely because such small external details reveal an “inner life.” The narrator’s own imagined dialogue—especially the children questioning their grandparents—uses caricature theatrically, compressing an entire generational contrast into a comic exchange.

The treatment of art is both aesthetic and sociological. Illustrations are valued not merely for draughtsmanship but for their ability to preserve manners, interiors, fashions, class relations, gestures, and moral attitudes. The essay repeatedly argues that a successful social artist must know the world represented from within. Leech’s authenticity is therefore demonstrated through minute observations of rooms, hands, clothing, horses, servants, children, and suburban domestic life. Art becomes a historical archive, and the critic moves fluidly between formal appreciation, anecdotal memory, moral judgment, and social analysis.

Underlying the comedy is a humane ethical standard. The writer favors humor that exposes vanity and pretension without becoming brutal, obscene, or dehumanizing. Earlier caricature is admired for vigor but criticized for savagery; modern pictorial humor is celebrated as “washed, combed, clothed, and taught” good manners. Kindness toward children, sympathy across class boundaries, courage without swagger, and laughter shared by families form the essay’s moral center. Accordingly, the ending shifts from detailed criticism to a benediction, closing on the cumulative ideals of “truth, and fun, and beauty, and kindness.”

To reproduce this style, one would use a learned but intimate first-person plural narrator; build the argument through nostalgic digressions and richly detailed catalogues; mingle elevated allusion with slang and domestic observation; employ rhetorical questions, exclamations, direct addresses, and mock-heroic refrains; describe people through vivid physical gestures and social markers; and balance satire with conspicuous warmth. The result should feel simultaneously like memoir, cultural history, art criticism, comic performance, and affectionate moral essay.

The story is written as a morally instructive historical narrative, combining hagiography, chronicle, and sentimental biography. Its central concern is exemplary wifely fidelity under extreme suffering. Arria and Natalia serve as brief classical and early Christian precedents before the narrative expands into the principal account of Gertrude von der Wart. Historical events are therefore selected and arranged less for detached analysis than to illustrate courage, devotion, religious constancy, and the contrast between mercy and vindictiveness.

The narrator is omniscient, authoritative, and openly judgmental. Characters and actions are repeatedly classified in moral terms: rulers are “rude and uncouth,” revenge is “horribly savage,” the Swiss are “stout, honest men,” and Gertrude is defined by tenderness and steadfastness. The voice assumes a shared Christian ethical framework and freely explains how readers should interpret events. It distinguishes pagan heroism from a supposedly higher Christian endurance, condemns suicide, praises forgiveness, and presents suffering as spiritually meaningful. Historical uncertainty is occasionally acknowledged through cautious phrases such as “supposed to be” and “a letter ascribed to Gertrude,” but this does not weaken the narrator’s overall confidence.

The tone is elevated, grave, compassionate, and devotional, with strong melodramatic intensity. Scenes of torture and execution are described directly enough to produce horror, yet the emphasis falls on emotional and spiritual endurance rather than physical sensationalism alone. The cruelty of Agnes and the executioners is set against Gertrude’s gentleness, prayer, and practical care. Pathos accumulates through intimate details: she strokes her husband’s hair, covers his broken limbs, carries water in her shoe, moistens his lips, and remains until his final breath. Such details make abstract virtues visible through action.

The diction is formal and distinctly archaic or Victorian-historical. Expressions such as “ere,” “whilst,” “hath,” “aught,” “’tis,” “whereupon,” “bid thee farewell,” and “linger out the remaining hours” create temporal distance and lend the account scriptural or legendary dignity. Religious vocabulary is prominent: “martyrs,” “confessed,” “blessed,” “faithfulness,” “compassion,” “forgiveness,” “crown of life,” and “joys beyond.” Evaluative adjectives are frequent and emphatic—“noble,” “fearful,” “horrible,” “atrocious,” “cruel,” and “blessed”—so the prose rarely adopts a neutral register.

Sentences tend to be long, syntactically layered, and linked by coordination and subordinate clauses. They often carry a sequence of causes, actions, and judgments within a single flowing structure. Semicolons are used to balance related developments, while dashes introduce quoted poetry or moments of heightened emphasis. Shorter sentences and direct speech punctuate this expansive narration at emotional turning points. The dialogue is formal, compressed, and declamatory rather than conversational. Characters speak in polished statements that reveal moral character: Natalia’s fear is not for her husband’s safety but for his possible apostasy, while Gertrude’s “I will die with you” crystallises her absolute loyalty.

Structurally, the piece moves from concise exempla to a sustained historical episode. The opening comparison establishes a hierarchy of devotion: Arria represents pagan constancy, Natalia Christian martyrdom, and Gertrude prolonged companionship in suffering. The main narrative then broadens into political exposition about inheritance, conspiracy, assassination, and dynastic revenge before narrowing sharply to the scaffold and Gertrude’s vigil. This movement from public history to private anguish is essential to the style. Political events supply the circumstances, but domestic devotion supplies the moral centre.

Contrast is the dominant rhetorical device. Pagan and Christian virtue, innocence and guilt, vengeance and forgiveness, worldly power and spiritual strength, bodily agony and heavenly hope are placed in opposition. Agnes and Gertrude form the clearest moral antithesis: both are strong-willed women, but one is consumed by revenge while the other is sustained by love and faith. Irony also appears in the treatment of outward religion, especially when Agnes attempts to combine piety with brutality and is rebuked by the hermit. The implication is that convent-building and religious reputation are worthless without compassion.

The story incorporates quotation as a means of authentication and emotional elevation. Dialogue, the hermit’s rebuke, the priest’s scriptural exhortation, and Rudolf’s final words all function like memorable moral inscriptions. The extended lines attributed to Mrs Hemans shift the register from historical prose to lyrical lament. Their apostrophe, repetition, exclamation, and regular poetic cadence intensify the emotional ideal of inseparable marital devotion. The verse is not merely ornamental; it interprets Gertrude’s conduct for the reader and places her within a literary tradition of heroic womanhood.

Imagery is sparse but forceful. The wheel, scaffold, storm, cloak, water, fading daylight, and successive nights create a stark physical setting around the vigil. Weather and time mirror the ordeal: the howling wind mingles with prayer, while dawn, noon, evening, and renewed morning measure the painful duration. The final movement toward the convent and “true rest for ever” softens the brutality through spiritual closure. Death is framed not as defeat but as release, reunion, and the completion of faithfulness.

Overall, the style belongs to nineteenth-century Christian historical writing for a general or younger readership. It aims simultaneously to inform, move, and edify. Its characteristic features are overt moral commentary, elevated archaic diction, emotionally charged historical narration, idealised dialogue, sharp ethical contrasts, scenes of sanctified suffering, and a concluding providential resolution.

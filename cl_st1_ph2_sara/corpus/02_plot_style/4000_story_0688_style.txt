The story combines Gothic horror with dry social satire and a quasi-journalistic manner. Its narrator adopts the pose of a rational, well-informed chronicler, opening with the supposed certainty that the house is haunted and classifying the evidence in mock-logical terms. This air of objectivity is repeatedly undermined by irony: eyewitness testimony, newspaper reporting, civic respectability, legal procedure and the conventions of “chivalry” are all treated with amused distrust. The result is a tone that moves between urbane mockery and genuine dread without fully abandoning either.

The diction is formal, elevated and distinctly period-inflected. Words such as “ocular,” “demesne,” “perfunctory,” “colloquy” and “appurtenances” lend the narration legalistic or bureaucratic precision, while elaborate circumlocutions turn simple facts into comic observations. Euphemistic phrasing is especially important: murder is described as a man finding it “expedient” to cut his family’s throats and then “removing” elsewhere. Such verbal restraint intensifies the brutality by placing it beneath a polished, almost dispassionate surface. The narrator also uses aphoristic asides and mock-generalizations—particularly about noses, journalists, experts and human credibility—to establish a sardonic authorial personality.

Sentence structure is generally long, balanced and syntactically controlled, with subordinate clauses, semicolons and cumulative descriptive phrases. These expansive sentences create an assured narrative rhythm and allow irony to develop through delayed qualifications. At moments of action or revelation, however, the prose becomes shorter and more direct: doors close, darkness falls, a corpse appears and footprints are discovered. Colons, dashes and carefully placed interruptions produce suspense by postponing the decisive image. The dialogue is terse, ceremonious and antagonistic, reflecting a culture governed by rigid codes of masculine honour. Formal address—“Mr. Rosser,” “Mr. Grossmith,” “gentlemen”—contrasts sharply with oaths, insults and the primitive violence of the knife duel.

Description is concrete, visual and atmospheric. The haunted house is built from conventional details—decay, boarded windows, dust, cobwebs, weeds and damaged trees—but these are enlivened by personification and darkly comic comparison. Trees appear to be making a “concerted effort to run away,” while cobwebs hang like “rotting lace.” Light functions as a major organizing device. Candlelight makes faces grotesque and uncertain; total darkness turns familiar voices strange and releases imaginative terror; bright noon initially strips the house of supernatural gravity, only for the final evidence to become visible in daylight. This opposition between obscurity and illumination is ironic, because increased visibility does not dispel the mystery but confirms it.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, though it controls access to information with great care. It can summarize community opinion, enter a character’s anxious reasoning, withhold speech that a character “might have added,” and comment directly on the moral significance of events. The voice occasionally addresses an implied audience through phrases such as “we shall see,” reinforcing the manner of a storyteller presenting a case. Yet this apparent openness conceals crucial information: the stranger’s identity, the conspirators’ plan and the true supernatural outcome are released only in stages.

The plot is non-linear and architecturally tight. It begins with local legend and the arrival at the house, proceeds to the duel preparations, briefly diverts into a dubious supernatural report, then moves backward to explain the quarrel before advancing to the next day’s investigation. This delayed exposition converts the opening mystery into a story of calculated revenge or intimidation, after which the final discovery overturns that rational explanation. The structure therefore offers successive interpretations—haunting, human trick, psychological terror and actual supernatural visitation—each revising the one before it.

Characterization is economical and often physiognomic. Manton is rendered through mass, pallor, wrinkles, small sunken eyes, a cruel mouth and a wide jaw, so that moral ugliness appears written on the body. Minor characters are defined less by psychological depth than by social role, gesture and conduct: the hostile duellist, reluctant second, compromised deputy, practical sheriff and appalled relative. This compression suits the story’s emphasis on plot, atmosphere and moral irony. The murdered wife and children remain absent as living characters, but their absence becomes materially powerful in the final footprints.

Suspense depends less on overt supernatural spectacle than on spatial restriction and withheld perception. The sealed room, boarded windows, extinguished candle, empty floor and motionless corpse create a locked-room mystery. Sensory details—muffled footsteps, disturbed cobwebs, the concussion of a door and rigid limbs—make the horror physical while leaving its central event unseen. The supernatural is represented only through traces: a disputed white figure, inexplicable death and footprints that advance without returning. This restraint allows implication to carry more force than direct depiction.

The story’s dominant themes are guilt, retribution, the fragility of rational explanation and the barbarity concealed beneath civilized custom. The duel code is explicitly presented as brutality covered by a thin veneer of chivalry, while the conspirators’ trick exposes their own moral corruption even though their victim is a murderer. Public institutions fare little better: journalism embellishes rumours, law arrives after the fact, and supposedly unbiased observers are governed by prejudice or fear. The ending delivers poetic justice, but not moral reassurance; supernatural vengeance is precise, intimate and terrifying.

The final line exemplifies the story’s method. A minute bodily detail introduced earlier as the subject of flippant conversation—the missing middle toe—returns as incontrovertible identification. Comic triviality becomes forensic proof, and an apparently incidental anecdote becomes the mechanism of the revelation. The ending is abrupt, retrospective and epiphanic: it confirms the haunting, explains Manton’s terror and forces the reader to reinterpret the entire narrative in a single closing stroke.

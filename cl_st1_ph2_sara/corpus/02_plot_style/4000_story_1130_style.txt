The story adopts an early modernist, intellectually satirical style in which narrative serves chiefly as a framework for philosophical conversation. Its method is observational rather than conventionally dramatic: instead of building toward a decisive event, it follows two cultivated eccentrics as they examine people, social environments, and the limits of interpretation. The central tension lies between abstract classification and irreducible individual experience. This tension also shapes the prose itself, which continually moves from concrete, often sordid details to expansive claims about identity, tragedy, art, and consciousness.

The narration is third-person, controlled, and faintly ironic. It maintains enough distance from Eeldrop and Appleplex to expose their comic qualities without directly repudiating their ideas. Their solemn pursuit of the “human soul in its concrete individuality” is set against notebooks, filing systems, laundry accounts, police-station crowds, suburban gardens, and oatmeal. Such juxtapositions make their project appear simultaneously serious and absurd. The narrator occasionally imitates the dry language of biography or scholarly annotation, as when the protagonists’ contrasting intellectual dispositions are neatly summarized. This produces a mock-systematic tone particularly suited to characters who condemn classification while repeatedly indulging in it.

Dialogue dominates the story and consists largely of long, carefully articulated speeches. The exchanges resemble essays, Socratic dialogues, or intellectual duels more than naturalistic conversation. Each speaker develops propositions, tests definitions, supplies examples, and corrects the other’s terminology. Eeldrop is generally the more mystical, intuitive, and rhetorically expansive voice, while Appleplex is more empirical, procedural, and analytical. Their positions nevertheless overlap and exchange features, preventing them from becoming simple ideological opposites. Brief interruptions and understated rejoinders keep the conceptual debate lively and create dry comedy, especially when elevated reflections are punctured by domestic admissions or social appointments.

The syntax is predominantly formal and elaborate. Sentences often unfold through balanced clauses, cumulative examples, qualifications, parentheses, semicolons, and rhetorical contrasts. Parallel structures—“not the commonplace… but the too well pigeonholed”—give arguments precision and cadence. Repetition is frequently used to intensify a thought, as in meditations on what is “important,” what cannot be undone, or what happens when a person is “classed.” At other moments, catalogues generate momentum and comic excess: clothes, documents, occupations, smells, social types, and biographical episodes are amassed until the individual seems almost buried beneath evidence. Short statements such as “Edith is not unhappy” or “There is and will be no catastrophe” then acquire unusual force by interrupting this discursive flow.

The diction mixes philosophical abstraction with sharply material description. Terms such as “classification,” “individuality,” “intuition,” “instinct,” “experience,” and “detachment” recur as conceptual anchors. Against them stand tactile and socially specific details: fat fingers crumbling bread, various manners of spitting, dusty vegetation, broken blinds, stale tobacco, custard and prunes, a Pekinese, and a rejected cheque. This collision between abstract language and physical particularity embodies the characters’ aim of reaching reality beneath generalized social categories. It also exposes a paradox: their desire to preserve singularity repeatedly produces new theories and labels.

Imagery is urban, dingy, and sensuously exact. Respectable streets conceal corruption; tropical-looking suburban greenery is dusty above and mouldy below; tepid air swarms with flies. Places are treated as moral and psychological atmospheres rather than neutral backgrounds. The police station provides a theatrical threshold at which hidden violence briefly erupts into public curiosity. Interiors are read as extensions of personality, especially in the imagined room where Edith wakes among clothes, cosmetics, papers, perfume, and stale smoke. Images of surfaces and concealment—curtains, masks, labels, files, blinds, respectability—recur throughout, reinforcing the concern with whether an inner self can genuinely be apprehended.

The tone is urbane, cerebral, sardonic, and intermittently lyrical. The satire targets reformers, aesthetes, intellectual fashions, social taxonomies, and self-conscious individualists, but it rarely becomes blunt parody. Philosophical seriousness remains present beneath the comedy, especially in the discussion of murder, irreversible action, and the failure of outsiders to comprehend another person’s catastrophe. The story can therefore pass quickly from grotesque social observation to metaphysical gravity. Its humour is usually deadpan: grand theories are undercut by banal facts, and romantic poses are reduced to boarding-house food, filing errors, or predictable social arrangements.

Characterization is indirect and primarily rhetorical. Eeldrop and Appleplex are defined by how they observe, reason, catalogue, interrupt, and disagree rather than by conventional psychological exposition. Their peculiar names contribute to the stylized, semi-allegorical effect. Secondary figures are assembled from anecdote, appearance, possessions, reputation, and conjecture. Edith, in particular, is never presented directly; she is reconstructed through documents, gossip, imagined scenes, literary associations, and competing interpretations. Her absence makes her an ideal test case for the story’s central problem: whether an individual can be known without being converted into a type or an aesthetic object.

The structure is episodic and divided into two numbered sections. The first establishes the protagonists, their chosen environment, and their opposition to generalized explanations; the second applies their method to the enigmatic Edith. Rather than resolving her identity, the discussion produces successive, partly incompatible versions of her. The ending is deliberately anticlimactic: another proposed topic is postponed because of a dinner engagement. This open, deflationary close suggests that inquiry will continue indefinitely and that philosophical systems remain entangled with ordinary schedules and social life.

Intertextual and cultural references contribute to the story’s cosmopolitan intellectual texture. Theology, medieval punishment, sociology, aestheticism, Bergson, Nietzsche, Scheherazade, and the language of fin-de-siècle artistic experience are invoked with a mixture of seriousness and mockery. Such allusions assume a well-read audience but are frequently brought down to earth by mundane surroundings. The resulting style is learned without being reverent, using cultural authority as another object of scrutiny.

Overall, the prose is best characterized as a hybrid of modernist character sketch, philosophical dialogue, social satire, and essayistic fiction. Its signature technique is the ironic confrontation between the unique and the classified, the metaphysical and the trivial, the imagined soul and the documentary file. It favors intellectual movement over plot, carefully curated detail over emotional disclosure, and unresolved analysis over narrative closure.

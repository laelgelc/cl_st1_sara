The story adopts a polished late-Victorian or Edwardian literary style, combining psychological confession, pastoral description, dry social comedy and restrained supernatural suggestion. It is narrated retrospectively in the first person by an educated, urbane writer whose voice is articulate, self-conscious and mildly cynical. He frequently comments on his own reactions, weaknesses and professional habits, creating intimacy while preserving a degree of ironic distance. His rationalism makes him a useful witness to uncanny events: because he insists that he is “not a believer in ghosts,” his visions acquire ambiguity rather than becoming straightforward supernatural manifestations.

The prose is formal, rhythmical and often expansive. Long, carefully balanced sentences accumulate clauses, qualifications and parallel constructions, while shorter statements mark discoveries or emotional turns. Repetition and antithesis give the narration a rhetorical cadence, as in the opening pattern of attempting to confront work only to retreat from it. Parenthetical asides and retrospective corrections—especially the narrator’s clarification of how many years ago an event occurred—strengthen the impression of an experienced storyteller speaking directly to an audience. The vocabulary is elevated but accessible, mixing literary terms such as “importunate,” “loquacity,” “surmises” and “rhapsodies” with humorous colloquial observations. Archaic or regional forms, including “nought,” “bethought” and the carrier’s Yorkshire dialect, reinforce the period atmosphere.

Personification is central to the style. Work “presses,” “challenges” and follows the narrator; solitude becomes a “master and teacher”; hills swallow the travellers; brooks chatter, join hands and move towards life together; and the sea calls to them. This animation of landscape allows setting to reflect states of mind. The industrial town is noisy, oppressive and ghost-ridden, whereas the Wolds initially promise spaciousness, quiet and renewal. Yet the apparently idyllic village gradually reveals buried grief. The paired brooks provide a particularly sustained symbolic image: their fresh union anticipates companionship, but their eventual passage through smoke-darkened towns towards the sea foreshadows the contamination of hope by experience.

Description is sensuous and pictorial, with strong attention to light, colour, sound and texture. June roses, sweet grass, clear water, lilac cloth, black wood, faded ink, pressed flowers, dust and lamplight create a softened, nostalgic visual field. Recurrent contrasts between freshness and decay shape the story’s imagery: the beautiful young face is preserved in the miniature while the living woman has become withered; flowers survive only as flattened remnants; romantic language fades into brown ink; and an apparently timeless rural refuge contains evidence of emotional ruin. Shadows, dusk, fading light and lamplight repeatedly blur the boundary between physical perception and imaginative projection.

The tone moves gradually from comic self-deprecation to melancholy and compassion. Early passages mock the narrator’s avoidance of work and gently caricature the carrier, the lodging-house conventions and the narrator’s own romantic expectations. His initial portrait of the landlady and her daughter is sharp and uncharitable, reflecting period assumptions about age, femininity and spinsterhood. That judgement is deliberately overturned by the final revelation. The supposedly “narrow, uninteresting” woman becomes the centre of a tragedy, and the narrator’s concluding recognition—he sees her “perhaps for the first time”—provides the story’s moral and emotional correction. The ending is understated: instead of an explicit apology or sentimental reconciliation, a commonplace remark about seeing the likeness carries the weight of his transformed perception.

Structurally, the narrative is a slow-burn mystery built through linked objects and incremental disclosures. The miniature leads to the imagined figure; the figure leads to the annotated poem; the poem suggests abandonment; the landlady’s evasive history introduces death; and the diary and letters apparently confirm the inferred romance. Each clue seems to point towards a dead former lodger, encouraging both narrator and reader to accept a ghost-story framework. The final identification of the portrait as the landlady herself reverses that assumption. What appeared to be a haunting by the dead is instead a haunting by the still living woman’s former self. This twist is prepared through repeated references to her affectation of youth, her “better days,” the beauty of the apparition’s hands and the diary’s emphasis on those same hands.

Embedded documents create a second voice and sharply alter the emotional register. The diary begins in breathless, devotional romanticism, filled with exclamations, prayers, kisses and idealisation. As Christopher’s letters diminish, its language becomes anxious, then controlled, then violently candid. The contrast between the unnaturally neat entry of self-sacrificing renunciation and the vehement confession that follows exposes the conflict between social ideals of feminine dignity and the raw desire beneath them. The narrator repeatedly calls the story “conventional” and its raptures “foolish,” but these dismissive labels are progressively undermined. Familiar plots and hackneyed verses matter because they correspond to real suffering; cliché becomes evidence of shared human experience rather than artistic emptiness.

Dialogue is sparse and functional, used chiefly to reveal class, concealment and character. The carrier’s phonetic dialect establishes locality and social position, while the landlady’s restrained replies contrast with the narrator’s indirect, “fencing” questions. Much of the tension arises from what neither speaker says openly. The landlady’s faint flush and simple admission at the end communicate more than an extended explanation would. This economy in dialogue offsets the richness of the descriptive narration.

The dominant themes are memory, lost love, ageing, imaginative projection and the inadequacy of first impressions. Art preserves a youthful identity after time has made it unrecognisable, while books, annotations, letters and keepsakes act as material repositories of feeling. The narrator’s profession is also important: as a writer, he instinctively turns fragments into a coherent romantic plot, but his interpretation is only partly correct. His imagination perceives an emotional truth before he knows the factual one, yet it also objectifies the woman by preferring her beautiful past to her disappointing present. The final turn collapses that division and insists on continuity between the idealised girl and the elderly woman.

Overall, the style is ornate without being excessively obscure, sentimental but checked by irony, and Gothic in atmosphere rather than overt incident. Its characteristic method is to invite scepticism and mockery, then reveal the emotional shallowness of both. The supernatural remains unresolved: the apparition may be a ghost, a hallucination or an imaginative reconstruction. More important than its literal status is its function as the visible form of memory and neglected personhood.

The story uses an urbane, ironic realist style that gradually tightens into psychological suspense and ends in abrupt tragedy. Its tone is initially conversational and lightly amused, treating adultery, superstition, and self-deception with worldly detachment. Beneath that surface humor lies a steady fatalism: the narrator recognizes the characters’ weaknesses, allows them to rationalize their conduct, and quietly arranges those weaknesses into an inexorable chain of consequences. The final violence is presented with startling brevity, without emotional commentary, making the ending feel both shocking and retrospectively inevitable.

The narration is third-person omniscient, focused chiefly through Camillo’s consciousness but punctuated by an intrusive, self-aware storyteller. Remarks such as “Let us proceed to explain” and “But I express myself ill” draw attention to the act of narration and establish direct contact with the reader. The narrator moves freely between summary, scene, character history, moral observation, and psychological analysis. This voice is intelligent, skeptical, and gently mocking rather than openly condemnatory. It often exposes a gap between what characters believe about themselves and what their behavior reveals: Camillo claims disbelief without possessing a reasoned philosophy, seeks to preserve his honor while betraying his friend, and dismisses superstition until fear makes belief emotionally useful.

The structure is carefully retrospective and circular. It begins near the middle of the affair, with Rita recounting her visit to the fortune-teller, then pauses to reconstruct the triangle among Rita, Camillo, and Villela. After this explanatory return to the past, the narrative advances chronologically toward Camillo’s own consultation and death. The opening allusion to Hamlet supplies a thematic refrain, while the repeated location of the fortune-teller’s house and the recurrence of the phrase about “more things in heaven and earth” bind the design together. Rita’s earlier consultation prefigures Camillo’s, and the same reassurance that calms both lovers becomes the mechanism by which Camillo walks willingly into danger.

Characterization is economical and analytic. A few physical traits—Rita’s burning eyes and “piquantly inquiring” mouth, Villela’s grave bearing, the fortune-teller’s sharp eyes and dirty nails—are combined with concise judgments about temperament. Camillo is presented as morally and practically immature, easily flattered, fearful, imaginative, and susceptible to whatever interpretation offers immediate comfort. Rita is lively, sensual, anxious, and resourceful; Villela remains comparatively opaque, which preserves his menace. The fortune-teller is rendered through suggestive contradictions: her surroundings are poor and dirty, yet they enhance her “prestige”; she appears sibylline to Camillo while the narrator shows her delight in an unusually large payment. This double presentation sustains ambiguity without requiring the supernatural to be real.

The diction is formal and literary, with occasional archaic or elevated phrasing such as “selfsame,” “betokened,” “nought,” and “thence.” At the same time, dialogue is more direct and colloquial, especially in Rita’s teasing complaints and Camillo’s skeptical questions. This contrast distinguishes the polished narrator from the socially natural speech of the characters. Italian expressions, Brazilian street names, monetary references, carriages, government offices, and domestic conventions give the story a specific nineteenth-century urban setting. The translated prose sometimes favors ornate formulations and older idioms, but it remains clear and controlled.

Sentence structure varies according to dramatic function. Expository passages use long, balanced sentences, semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, and accumulative lists. Psychological agitation produces repetitions, fragments, questions, dashes, and rapidly shifting clauses: Camillo rereads Villela’s summons, imagines possible scenes, disputes his own conclusions, and hears the words as though spoken aloud. Short dialogue exchanges accelerate scenes, while aphoristic statements interrupt them with broader reflections: “Propinquity bred intimacy,” “Such is man,” and “the unknown present is the same as the future.” These maxims give the narration a philosophical polish while also compressing complicated motives into memorable formulas.

Imagery is selective but forceful. The story draws repeatedly on entrapment and predation: superstition is “parasitic vegetation,” Rita becomes a serpent winding around Camillo, mystery has “iron claws,” and the fortune-teller hovers in his imagination with ash-colored wings. Domestic and urban objects acquire symbolic force. The blocked carriage creates the pause that diverts Camillo into the fortune-teller’s house; the closed windows make that house resemble “the dwelling of indifferent Fate”; the dark, worn staircase becomes a threshold into irrational belief. Later, the clear sky, merry faces, open sea, and imagined infinite future embody Camillo’s false confidence immediately before the fatal revelation.

Suspense is generated less by concealment than by psychological oscillation. The reader recognizes the danger in Villela’s message, while Camillo alternates between dread and denial. His extended journey stretches narrative time: each street, delay, imagined explanation, and repeated phrase increases pressure. The fortune-telling scene temporarily releases that pressure by giving him the conclusion he desires. The subsequent ride transforms fear into optimism, but the reassurance is transparently fragile, creating strong dramatic irony. Camillo interprets neutral sights as proof that all is well, just as he had interpreted the same world as threatening moments earlier.

Dialogue is sparing and functional. It reveals emotional asymmetry, supplies information, and dramatizes attempts at reassurance or evasion. Rita’s speech is impulsive and intimate; Camillo’s moves from mockery to earnestness and later to panic; the fortune-teller speaks in broad, adaptable statements that acquire authority because Camillo completes their meaning for her. Villela’s final silence is especially effective. Having summoned Camillo to talk, he gives almost no verbal explanation; gesture and violence replace speech.

The governing stylistic principle is irony. The professed skeptic becomes the most consequential believer; the fortune-teller accurately identifies his fear but gives a catastrophically false prediction; Camillo’s delay, which seems dangerous, actually postpones rather than causes the already prepared outcome; and his newly acquired “faith” brings not salvation but death. Even the lush vision of sea and sky immediately before his arrival functions as ironic counterpoint. Overall, the style combines concise social realism, authorial wit, moral psychology, theatrical suspense, and a tightly engineered reversal, using supernatural suggestion mainly to expose the human appetite for comforting error.

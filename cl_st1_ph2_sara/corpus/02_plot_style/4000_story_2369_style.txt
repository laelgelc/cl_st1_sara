The story is a comic parody of aristocratic romance and melodrama, written in a mock-solemn, pseudo-Victorian manner. It imitates the conventions of tales about ancient families, threatened estates, forbidden love, hereditary pride, and concealed disgrace, but repeatedly punctures their grandeur with bathos, factual absurdity, and mercenary motives. Elevated declarations such as “Ruin had come” and references to “absolute, irretrievable ruin” create an atmosphere of catastrophe before the narrative undermines it through foolish names, dubious genealogy, and trivial explanations.

The narrator is omniscient, confident, and outwardly formal, but maintains a dry, ironic distance from every character. Narratorial intrusions often expose the artificiality of the language or clarify the obvious for comic effect. Parenthetical alternatives such as “soughed (or sogged)” parody literary scene-setting, while the statement that the dead earl “was extinct. That is to say, he was dead” reduces melodramatic tragedy to redundant explanation. The narration frequently adopts a straight face while presenting nonsense, allowing the contrast between tone and content to generate humour.

The diction mixes archaic and ceremonious language with commercial, legal, and colloquial terminology. Words and phrases such as “heedless,” “anon,” “plighted our troth,” “stately home,” “stricken face,” and “courtly grace” reproduce the idiom of historical romance. Against this appear “stocks and bonds,” “corners and margins,” “written stipulations,” “legal formalities,” and exact financial calculations. This collision of chivalric sentiment with business language reveals that both Edwin’s courtship and the Oxheads’ aristocratic values are fundamentally economic. Proper names are deliberately caricatural: Oxhead, Hardup, Mixtup, Sarcophagus Einstein, and Oshkosh turn genealogy into farce.

Sentence structure alternates between brief melodramatic pronouncements and elaborate, cumulative periods. Short openings—“It was finished. Ruin had come.”—simulate breathless crisis. Longer sentences pile up genealogical claims, heraldic terminology, qualifications, and corrections until the original point is lost. Lists are especially important to the style: the portraits of the Oxhead ancestors, the earl’s catalogue of supposed Americans, and the details of the family crest all accumulate incongruities. Interruptions, dashes, semicolons, parenthetical clauses, and hesitant revisions reproduce the characters’ inflated but uncertain thinking.

Much of the comedy depends on deliberate misuse of historical, geographical, genealogical, and heraldic knowledge. The family’s ancestors are attached to famous events only peripherally or disgracefully: they arrive too late, observe from a distance, flee battlefields, hide in trees, or are dismissed from service. The coat of arms combines authentic-sounding vocabulary with impossible elements such as “a dog intermittent” and “a plain parallelogram right centre,” while the motto is merely a sequence from a Latin grammar exercise. The earl’s confusion of Wisconsin with a noble family, of Bolivar with the United States, and of Pocahontas with an Oxhead ancestor presents ignorance as aristocratic learning.

Characterization is broad, economical, and satirical. Lord Oxhead embodies hereditary vanity, financial incompetence, and intellectual confusion. Gwendoline is presented through the clichés of the innocent romantic heroine, but the narrator repeatedly turns those clichés against her: she is a “beautiful girl of thirty-three,” and her exceptional “simplicity” strongly implies stupidity. Edwin appears as the stereotyped fortune-seeking outsider. His interest in the earl’s age, health, valuables, and estate transparently reveals calculation, even as the narration describes it as romantic generosity. Physical description is likewise mock-heroic: Edwin’s modest height, “prehensile” hand, drooping moustache, and gaze fixed on jewellery parody idealized portraits of masculine nobility.

Dialogue is formal, expository, and theatrically emotional. Characters address one another as “Father,” “girl,” and “my daughter,” repeat key phrases, and speak in polished declarations suited to melodrama rather than natural conversation. Their speeches are repeatedly undercut by logical errors, literal-mindedness, and material calculation. Euphemism and qualification are central comic devices: Pocahontas may not have married the ancestor, but “at least they loved one another”; Edwin’s proposal remains subject to stipulations and legal inquiries; and his idleness is offered as proof that he belongs among the aristocracy.

The plot is tightly organized around escalating reversals. It begins with unexplained ruin, moves to the daughter’s engagement, raises the apparent obstacle of Edwin’s American and commercial background, then dissolves that obstacle when his enormous fortune is disclosed. Edwin’s entrance produces the expected climactic shock and death, but the cause remains unresolved. The promised “awful secret” is never revealed, and the narrator dismisses it as probably too complicated to be interesting. This anti-climax parodies stories built around family mysteries while refusing the reader the conventional disclosure.

Pacing is brisk despite the ornate prose. Scene breaks marked by asterisks divide the story into three compact movements: the earl’s despair and family history, the engagement discussion, and Edwin’s arrival followed by the aftermath. Exposition is intentionally disproportionate, lingering over irrelevant ancestry and heraldry while treating death, marriage, and the disappearance of the central secret with abrupt efficiency. Repetition reinforces the parody, particularly the recurrence of ruin, family pride, “simplicity,” and the meaningless Latin motto.

The dominant humour is verbal, ironic, and structurally bathetic. Understatement coexists with extravagant emotion; literal explanation follows figurative language; noble ideals conceal greed; and solemn historical claims collapse into anachronism or incompetence. The final account of the “quiet” wedding extends the joke through suspicious omissions: Edwin rejects every expense or custom associated with celebration, a gesture framed as sensitivity but suggestive of parsimony. Overall, the style is polished, deadpan, allusive, and deliberately overinflated, using the language of romance to expose the stupidity, acquisitiveness, and hollowness beneath both aristocratic prestige and commercial courtship.

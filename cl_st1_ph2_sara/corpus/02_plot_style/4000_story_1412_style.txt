The story adopts a Gothic-Romantic mode, combining sepulchral imagery, supernatural speculation, moral allegory, and lyrical melancholy. Its atmosphere is nocturnal and ethereal rather than conventionally horrific: graves, mouldering dust, moonshine, pale starlight, tombstones, ancestral vaults, and wandering shades create an environment of subdued dread, but the dominant emotional register is wistful, contemplative, and spiritually aspirational. Horror appears chiefly as moral torment—the miser counting the sins embodied in his treasure, the murderer perpetually feeling his victim’s blood, the guilty lovers chained together in mutual loathing—while purity is associated with lightness, stars, fragrance, and upward flight.

The narrative voice is a self-conscious first-person ghost who speaks directly to the reader. This impossible narrator treats his supernatural condition as both literal reality and a device for reflecting on art, memory, guilt, love, fame, and mortality. Frequent vocatives such as “gentle reader,” “Mortal man,” and “maiden” create an intimate, theatrical relationship with the audience. The ghost alternately confides, preaches, philosophises, jokes, and pleads, producing a voice that is at once personable and elevated. Metafiction is central: the spirit claims to inspire a living author, presenting the story itself as a transmission from the dead through an imperfect mortal consciousness. This gives artistic creation a mystical origin while also allowing the narrator to lament language’s inability to capture “the finer intelligences of the soul.”

The diction is formal, archaic, and overtly literary. Words and constructions such as “aught,” “thither,” “ere,” “hath,” “methought,” “three-score years and ten,” and “till it have” establish a deliberately antique cadence. Abstract spiritual terms—“ethereal,” “celestial,” “purified,” “transgressions,” “penance,” and “immortal spirit”—are set against tactile words of weight and decay, including “clay,” “dust,” “damps,” “bones,” and “corruption.” This contrast supplies the story’s governing verbal pattern: earthly existence is gross, heavy, polluted, and imprisoning, whereas spiritual release is airy, radiant, fragrant, and upward-moving. The prose repeatedly transforms ideas into substances, so that guilt becomes a stain, grief becomes a weight, memory almost grants visible shape, and inspiration arrives as perfume, brightness, or whispered breath.

Sentences tend to be long, periodic, and richly subordinated, often carrying a thought through several qualifications before arriving at its emotional or philosophical conclusion. Semicolons, dashes, colons, exclamation marks, and rhetorical interruptions reproduce the movement of an excited speaking voice. Parallel structures and anaphora lend the prose a sermon-like rhythm: “till purified,” “till the passions,” “till it have”; or “boyhood would sport,” “youth would love,” “manhood would enjoy,” and “age would dream again.” Short exclamations—“She is gone!” or “Grave! Grave!”—puncture this elaborate syntax at moments of heightened feeling. Questions are frequently rhetorical, inviting the reader into the narrator’s metaphysical assumptions rather than encouraging genuine uncertainty.

Imagery is organised around repeated oppositions. Earth and sky, grave and stars, heaviness and buoyancy, darkness and radiance, bodily pollution and spiritual purity all structure the descriptions. Vertical motion carries moral significance: compromised spirits remain chained to graves and worldly attachments, while purified souls ascend toward the “upper stars.” Light is delicately graded rather than brilliant, appearing as glimmers, faint starlight, pale lamps, moonshine, and “misty brightness.” This preference for half-seen illumination suits a narrative concerned with mysteries that can be perceived but not fully expressed. Sensory imagery often crosses categories: thoughts gleam, remembrance gives shape, perfume bears spiritual meaning, and mortal language crushes immaterial intelligence.

The tone is notably mixed. Its prevailing solemnity is softened by whimsical supernatural details, such as ghosts summoning friends by knocking on tombstones, and by dryly comic observations about crowded cemeteries, posthumous jealousy, marital oblivion, and literary reputation. The narrator can describe himself as a “happy ghost enough” even while discussing penance and decay. This restrained humour prevents the Gothic setting from becoming uniformly oppressive and gives the afterlife the character of a social world governed by curious but intelligible rules. At the same time, apostrophe and exclamation intensify the sentimental passages, especially the ghost’s attachment to the pure maiden and his longing to follow her into heaven.

The story develops associatively rather than through a tightly plotted chain of action. It begins with a metafictional invocation, establishes a philosophy of ghosts and graves, moves through the narrator’s lost childhood expectations, surveys representative spirits and their punishments, reflects on love, family, and fame, and then narrows into the emotionally central episode of the maiden’s disappearance. The closing movement returns to artistic inspiration and direct address, first dismissing the hardened dreamer who has served as the ghost’s mortal medium and then seeking a purer receptive listener. This circular structure links haunting with storytelling: to inspire, remember, read, or write is to enter into communion with the dead.

Characterisation is emblematic. Secondary ghosts are less psychologically individualised than morally defined: the miser embodies avarice, the murderer blood-guilt, the lovers corrupted passion, and the patriot self-sacrificing civic devotion. Their supernatural punishments externalise their ruling earthly attachments. Even virtues can become chains when they remain directed toward the mortal world, as in the patriot’s inability to abandon his country. The narrator is more nuanced, combining youthful vanity, tenderness, aesthetic ambition, self-irony, loneliness, and a strong desire for purification. His development is primarily spiritual rather than practical, measured by his diminishing attraction to earthly sensations and increasing ability to rise into light.

The thematic style is meditative and didactic. The story repeatedly converts anecdote into moral reflection, often addressing the living with warnings or counsel. Earthly passions survive death only insofar as they are impure or possessive; guilt demands expiation, grief burdens the departed, fame obstructs transcendence, and attachment can delay salvation. Yet the moral scheme is not austere in a purely religious sense. Imagination, chaste affection, filial love, and artistic sensitivity retain genuine value, provided they lead beyond the self rather than fastening the spirit more firmly to earthly identity.

Overall, the prose is ornate, musical, apostrophic, and symbolically cohesive. Its characteristic effect comes from balancing dark Gothic materials with spiritual delicacy, moral seriousness with quiet irony, and narrative intimacy with elevated rhetoric. The resulting style is less one of terror than of “sweetly pensive” supernaturalism: the grave serves as a temporary chamber of reflection, while the story’s deepest imaginative impulse is always toward purification, release, and ascent.

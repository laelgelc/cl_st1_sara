The story adopts a polished late-nineteenth-century comic-gothic style, combining the conventions of a genteel ghost story with sustained social satire. Its supernatural premise is introduced through an elegant deadpan paradox—Miss Carew’s only objectionable quality is that she is dead—and the narration continues to treat haunting as though it were principally a matter of etiquette, taste, and social compatibility. The tone is urbane, amused, and lightly ironic rather than frightening. Even potentially sinister details are domesticated by references to china, embroidery, drawing-room furnishings, lavender perfume, and grammatical propriety.

The narrator is omniscient and conspicuously witty, speaking with the assurance of a socially informed observer. Frequent asides and mock-generalizations—especially claims about what is “unnecessary to explain” in good society or “superfluous to mention”—create intimacy with the reader while parodying upper-class assumptions. The narrative voice often adopts the values of its characters only to expose their absurdity. Words such as “propriety,” “gentility,” “decorum,” “good-breeding,” and “taste” recur until social refinement itself becomes comic. Philadelphia old society is portrayed as ceremonious, exclusionary, and self-important, while Iowa and “the West” are treated as culturally suspect by the Eastern characters. The story ultimately overturns that hierarchy: Western informality and ungrammatical speech prove more powerful than aristocratic tradition.

The prose is formal, balanced, and periodically elaborate. Sentences often contain parallel constructions, qualifications, parenthetical comments, and carefully delayed punch lines. Repetition is used for comic emphasis, as in the “only one possible objection” opening and the insistence on the heir being a “very, very distant cousin.” Elevated diction—“umbrageous,” “desuetude,” “patronymic,” “corporeal,” and “incorporeal”—contrasts humorously with trivial domestic concerns. Capitalization and typographical emphasis occasionally heighten a joke, turning phrases such as “Social Position” or “THAT” into miniature comic climaxes. Descriptions are precise but selective, concentrating on objects and manners that signify class: Wedgewood china, Dresden figures, daguerreotypes, Jane Austen, embroidery, watercolor painting, and stiff-backed chairs.

Characterization is economical and largely behavioral. Miss Carew is defined through her impeccable manners, antiquated tastes, and horror of aesthetic or grammatical impropriety. Her ghostly form resembles a faded daguerreotype, an image that functions both visually and symbolically: she is a surviving impression of an obsolete social order. The Boggs sisters are respectable enough to attract her approval but practical and independent enough to resent it. Their dialogue reveals a mixture of skepticism, nervousness, vanity, and common sense. Miss Prudence is the more irreverent and inventive sister, while Miss Boggs attempts to preserve rational dignity. Minor characters are rendered through social labels and characteristic speech rather than psychological depth.

Dialogue supplies much of the comedy. The characters speak in ceremonious, understated language even when confronting the impossible, so a haunting is discussed as an intrusion in “exceedingly bad taste.” The sisters repeatedly deny belief in the ghost while adjusting their conduct around her, producing a comic gap between declaration and action. The climactic Western monologue shifts into conspicuously nonstandard grammar: misplaced modifiers, a split infinitive, faulty prepositions, and a double negative become escalating attacks upon Miss Carew’s sensibilities. The final exorcism is therefore linguistic rather than religious, and the ghost departs not from terror or violence but from grammatical shock.

The structure is linear and tightly controlled. An expository opening establishes Miss Carew, her death, the dispersal of the estate, and the prejudices of Philadelphia society. The Boggs family’s arrival restores the setting and initiates the haunting. A sequence of increasingly intrusive manifestations follows: visual appearances, altered embroidery, rearranged furnishings, old-fashioned artwork, displaced books, perfume, and broken china. These episodes establish a pattern in which the sisters’ fear is gradually replaced by irritation. The narrative then introduces the visiting Westerner as the mechanism of release, and the climax rapidly converts earlier references to grammar and social approval into the solution. The brief closing—“It was the end. Miss Carew returned no more”—provides crisp, understated closure.

Imagery is predominantly domestic, visual, and olfactory. Lilac and lavender colors, rose trellises, violets, moss-roses, heartsease, and sweet lavender perfume create a faded, decorous atmosphere associated with Miss Carew. Her appearances as a figure alternately discernible and blurred give the supernatural element a distinctive period texture. The drawing-room functions as the story’s central stage and as a symbol of inherited culture: whoever controls its arrangement, reading matter, artwork, and conversation controls the household’s social identity.

The humor depends on incongruity, understatement, reversal, and personification of social codes. Death is less scandalous than bad taste; a ghost’s approval is more burdensome than her hostility; and grammatical errors become fatal weapons. Beneath the light entertainment lies a satire of cultural gatekeeping, hereditary prestige, regional snobbery, and rigid standards of femininity. The story’s characteristic effect comes from maintaining perfect narrative composure while allowing etiquette to assume supernatural force.

The story uses a retrospective first-person confessional voice, presenting an extraordinary experience as a grave personal testimony. The narrator is educated, literary, and self-consciously rational, repeatedly trying to classify sensations that resist analysis. This tension between sober recollection and delirious vision gives the narrative its credibility and unease. The opening invokes De Quincey, Baudelaire, classical myth, and exotic dream traditions, placing the account within a learned, decadent literary frame before moving into cosmic horror.

The prose is ornate, formal, and deliberately archaic. Its diction favours Latinate abstractions and antiquated or elevated terms such as “phantasms,” “firmament,” “aether,” “gloaming,” and “whilst,” alongside emphatic compounds and superlatives: “unheard-of,” “unnamable,” “immemorial,” “colossal,” and “stupendous.” Adjectives accumulate densely, especially around menace, decay, and unnatural scale. Recurrent words such as “hideous,” “ghastly,” “strange,” “black,” “sinister,” and “detestable” create a sustained vocabulary of revulsion, while the paradisal sequence temporarily replaces them with “radiant,” “ethereal,” “golden,” “amber,” “chalcedony,” and “blossomy.” This polarized lexicon sharply contrasts sublime beauty with abject horror.

Sentences are generally long, periodic, and heavily subordinated. They often begin with a measured observation, gather qualifications and sensory detail, and culminate in a revelation or emotional intensification. Semicolons, parenthetical clauses, balanced phrases, and cumulative lists produce a stately, incantatory rhythm. Shorter declarations appear at moments of crisis—“Then I opened my eyes,” “It is the end”—and gain force through contrast with the surrounding elaboration. Repetition is central to the cadence: the ocean “ate and ate,” the earth is “ever turning,” and the rift “widened and widened.” Anaphora and parallel syntax also make the apocalyptic passages sound scriptural or prophetic.

Description proceeds through sensory overload, but sound is the principal structural device. The pounding begins as a symptom in the narrator’s head, becomes the impact of breakers, persists beneath every change of scene, intrudes upon celestial music, and finally expands into planetary roaring, shrieking, and explosion. This recurring noise links bodily pain, psychological dread, the hostile sea, and cosmic destruction. Visual imagery is equally extravagant, relying on extreme colour contrasts, gigantic dimensions, strange architecture, alien vegetation, turbulent water, celestial radiance, ruins, vapour, and geological rupture. The landscapes are less realistically mapped than symbolically arranged, with incompatible seas, skies, climates, and architectural traditions suggesting dream logic.

The narrative develops through progressive enlargement. It begins with an individual body in pain, opens into a mysterious room, expands to a threatened promontory and tropical valley, rises into the upper atmosphere, and ends with the annihilation of the earth viewed against interstellar space. Each stage initially promises greater understanding or escape, but instead reveals a more comprehensive order of horror. This escalation converts private delirium into a cosmic vision. The pacing is correspondingly slow and exploratory at first, driven by cautious looking, listening, and movement, then increasingly rapid and panoramic as the earth’s destruction unfolds.

The dominant emotional mode is dread of the unknown rather than fear of a clearly defined antagonist. Threats are sensed before they are seen, and the narrator frequently stresses his inability to name, interpret, or remember them. Formulations such as “some nameless” menace or a “curse unnamed and unnamable” make linguistic failure part of the horror. The prose nevertheless describes these indescribable things at length, creating a characteristic paradox: insistence on inexpressibility accompanied by luxuriant verbal representation. Personification intensifies the effect, particularly when the sea becomes a conscious, greedy intelligence waging war upon the land.

The story also uses unstable temporal perception. Opium, fever, and delirium dissolve ordinary chronology, allowing modern writers to seem ancient and forgotten prehistoric civilizations to re-emerge. Deep time repeatedly overwhelms the human scale. Cities, nations, gods, and even geological arrangements prove temporary, while the stars remain cold spectators. The final personification of the planets as searching for their lost sister adds a mournful, almost elegiac note to the cosmic devastation, though the “humorous stars” retain an implication of remote and possibly mocking indifference.

Tonally, the piece moves from scholarly solemnity to claustrophobic apprehension, then to exotic wonder, ecstatic transcendence, apocalyptic terror, and cosmic melancholy. Beauty is never secure: the luxurious room conceals danger, the palm shelters a supernatural visitation, and celestial harmony is penetrated by the ocean’s pounding. The heavenly realm of eternal youth and pleasure functions as a fragile counterpoint to decay rather than a genuine refuge. Overall, the style combines decadent dream narrative, Gothic confession, sublime landscape writing, and cosmic apocalypse, using elevated diction, cumulative syntax, obsessive sonic motifs, and radical shifts of scale to make human reason appear helpless before immensities of time, nature, and the universe.

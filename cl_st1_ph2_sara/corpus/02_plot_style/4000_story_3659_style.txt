The story adopts a conversational first-person frame, presented as a personal reminiscence with precise historical and geographical anchoring. The opening narrator is observant, urbane and mildly ironic, describing the Riviera through balanced contrasts such as privacy against publicity and tranquillity against “pow-wow and fuss and feathers.” His voice creates an atmosphere of plausible social realism before yielding to Smith, whose extended oral narrative forms the main body of the tale. This story-within-a-story structure gives the account the intimacy of a confidence disclosed over drinks while allowing interruptions, questions and reactions to sustain suspense.

The prose is strongly shaped by oral storytelling. Sentences often include digressions, self-corrections, parenthetical qualifications and direct addresses to the listener: “phrase it to suit yourself,” “you know,” and “hold still, now.” Repetition and parallel phrasing supply rhythm and comic emphasis, while dashes reproduce pauses, interruptions and abrupt changes of thought. The syntax alternates between expansive descriptive sentences and rapid, clipped exchanges. Dialogue dominates the central narrative, with brief questions, overlapping exclamations and escalating responses creating the impression of spontaneous group conversation.

The diction mixes cultivated, occasionally formal language with colloquial idiom. Elevated phrases such as “illustrious name,” “final mournful rites” and “multitudinous and long-established facts” sit beside expressions like “ran hard aground,” “lost your mind,” “bright, conscienceless rascal” and “pork chop.” French names, monetary terms and references to European art lend specificity, but the language remains accessible and energetic. Comic euphemisms are especially important: the planned fraud is described as a “project,” Millet becomes “the moribund,” and his staged death is treated like a business campaign.

The prevailing tone is genial, deadpan and satirical. Absurd events are narrated with practical seriousness, so that the contrast between content and delivery generates much of the humour. The proposal that one artist “must die” is introduced as if it were a rational economic theorem, and the friends respond to moral and logistical outrages chiefly as commercial problems. Hyperbole intensifies the comedy: paintings multiply by the ton, publicity reaches several continents, and poverty turns almost instantly into princely travel and enormous profits. The narration rarely condemns the deception directly; instead, its cheerful matter-of-factness exposes the irrationality of the art market.

Characterisation is economical and voice-driven. Smith emerges as reflective, affectionate and amused by his younger self, while Carl is defined through quick reasoning, audacity and persuasive momentum. The artists function partly as a comic ensemble: they interrupt one another, exchange mock-medical advice and move rapidly from despair to ecstatic approval. Their warmth and loyalty soften the fraud, making it appear less like predatory crime than an ingenious rebellion against injustice. Millet himself is comparatively quiet, which preserves his status as both the object of the scheme and the final secret.

The plot follows a carefully managed comic escalation. A melancholy stranger and the parable of the neglected bird establish the theme of genius recognised too late. The narrative then moves from poverty, to an outrageous proposal, to experimental sales, manufactured publicity, a staged death and spectacular financial success. Prices and quantities recur throughout, giving the fantasy a mock-documentary concreteness. Strategic interruptions delay key information, particularly the question of who carried the coffin and what became of Millet. The final revelation circles back to the stranger seen at breakfast, closing the frame neatly and converting an apparently incidental opening detail into the punch line.

Irony governs both theme and construction. Society values the artist’s work only when scarcity, reputation and death have been manufactured around it; artistic merit remains constant while price changes according to public belief. The story satirises fashionable opinion, press manipulation, connoisseurship and the tendency to reward dead creators more generously than living ones. The bird metaphor gives this social criticism a sentimental foundation, but the tale avoids solemn moralising by resolving the problem through comic fraud. Its characteristic style can therefore be summarised as anecdotal, dialogue-heavy, digressive and theatrically paced, combining affectionate character comedy with deadpan social satire and a tightly prepared final reversal.

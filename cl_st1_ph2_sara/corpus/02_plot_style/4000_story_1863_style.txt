The story is a first-person comic mystery narrated retrospectively by an observant, self-aware traveller. The narrator is both the perceiving consciousness and the chief source of humour: feverish, lonely, and trapped indoors, he transforms scraps of ordinary information into elaborate theories about an unseen guest. His candid admissions—his nervousness, boredom, curiosity, and susceptibility to romance—make him personable but deliberately unreliable. The supposed mystery exists less in the external world than in his overactive imagination.

The tone blends melancholy atmosphere with urbane, gently satirical comedy. Rain, silence, illness, and isolation initially suggest a Gothic or supernatural tale, and the Hamlet epigraph playfully promises dangerous confrontation. Yet this ominous framing is repeatedly undercut by mundane details: rancid butter, overdone eggs, newspapers, Stilton cheese, an umbrella, and finally a pair of drab breeches. The humour is genial rather than cruel, directed at travellers, inn servants, political stereotypes, social pretensions, and above all the narrator’s own compulsive conjecturing. Mock-heroic language elevates trivial people and events, as commercial travellers become modern “knights-errant,” the landlady attacks an “enemy’s citadel,” and a visit to an adjoining room assumes the desperation of heroic romance.

The diction is richly descriptive, period-specific, and socially alert. It mixes polished literary phrasing with colloquial expressions, occupational vocabulary, obsolete terms, and comic labels such as “upper Benjamin,” “Boots,” “slammerkin,” “square-toes,” and “nightcaps.” Accumulations of adjectives—“slovenly, ragged, spongy clouds” or the “miserable, crestfallen cock”—produce vivid caricatures. People are often identified through physical or temperamental shorthand rather than psychologically rounded description: the “vinegar-faced mother,” “carroty-headed hostler,” “wall-eyed horse,” and “plethoric fellow” all belong to a world rendered through instantly legible comic types.

The syntax is expansive and rhythmically varied. Long, semicolon-linked sentences gather visual details into panoramic inventories, especially in descriptions of the stable-yard, the travellers’ room, and the inn at night. These are balanced by short exclamations, questions, and fragments that reproduce the narrator’s agitated thoughts: “Rain—rain—rain!” and “But who could he be?” Parenthetical qualifications and direct addresses create an intimate, conversational essayistic manner. Repetition is central to the rhythm, particularly the recurring rain, ringing bells, ticking clock, and footsteps. Onomatopoeic sequences such as “patter, patter, patter” and “drop—drop—drop” make the oppressive monotony audible.

Imagery is concrete, sensuous, and frequently personifying. The inn is evoked through wet straw, vapour, smoke, reeking coats, guttering candles, creaking soles, stale reading matter, cheese, and rainwater. Animals and objects acquire human attitudes: ducks are “boon companions” drinking around a puddle, a horse pokes out its “spectral head,” and even the candle appears to fall asleep. This animation of the environment sustains both atmosphere and comedy. As night approaches, ordinary coats, breathing, and footsteps become “spectral” or “ghastly,” showing how the narrator’s mood alters his perception rather than introducing any genuinely supernatural agency.

The story develops through a chain of conjecture and correction. Each sound or reported action supplies a clue from which the narrator constructs a complete identity for the Stout Gentleman: prosperous elder, alderman, radical, tavern-loving rogue, aristocrat, or even member of the royal family. New evidence then overturns the hypothesis, forcing him to repaint the imaginary portrait. This repeated pattern gives the narrative its episodic structure and comic momentum. The central figure is characterized almost entirely offstage, through bells, complaints, footsteps, food orders, servants’ reactions, and abandoned objects. His invisibility invites imaginative projection, while the capitalization of “Stout Gentleman” turns a casual inn designation into the title of a legendary personage.

Pacing is intentionally dilatory. Extended descriptions and catalogues reproduce the dragging duration of a wet Sunday, while small incidents acquire exaggerated importance because nothing else is happening. Suspense increases gradually as daylight gives way to the spectral quiet of midnight, but the anticipated revelation is continually deferred. The climax is an anti-climax: the narrator reaches the room too late, and his final opportunity yields only a rear view of coat skirts and breeches. The ending preserves the stranger’s anonymity while reducing the grand mystery to an absurdly physical glimpse.

Dialogue is sparse and generally reported or overheard. Servants’ cries, the landlady’s indignation, and coaching formulas such as “All right” lend immediacy and social texture without interrupting the narrator’s dominance. Much of the apparent dialogue is actually interior monologue, marked by quotation and rhetorical questioning. This allows the reader to follow the narrator’s speculative leaps while recognizing their weak foundations.

Overall, the style combines the familiar essay, travel sketch, character comedy, mock romance, and lightly Gothic tale. Its governing contrast is between imaginative grandeur and commonplace reality. The prose turns boredom into narrative by showing how a cultivated but feverish mind can manufacture mystery from an anonymous hotel guest. The result is atmospheric, leisurely, visually abundant, theatrically suspenseful, and finally comic in its refusal to deliver the revelation it has so elaborately prepared.

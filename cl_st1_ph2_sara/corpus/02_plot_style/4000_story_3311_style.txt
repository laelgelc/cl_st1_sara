The story uses a warm, brisk, late-nineteenth- or early-twentieth-century school-story style, combining domestic comedy, moral adventure, and regional characterization. Its tone is initially playful and gently satirical, then suspenseful and melodramatic during the rescue, before returning to celebratory humor. The narrative has a clear didactic impulse: courage, practical competence, generosity, self-command, and service to others are rewarded, while vanity, affectation, and panic are treated with amused disapproval.

The narration is third-person omniscient, moving freely between broad description, individual thoughts, and direct commentary. The narrator remains sympathetic and reassuring even at moments of danger, ensuring that the story feels suitable for young readers. Occasional exclamatory asides—such as Maine’s blessing of the little brother whose games strengthened her shoulders—heighten emotion and make the narrator’s presence more noticeable. Characters are often introduced through compact, emblematic descriptions: Massachusetts is “rosy and placid,” Virginia “graceful” and “languishing,” and Maine “lithe and breezy as a young willow.” Such descriptions establish personality immediately and encourage readers to understand each girl as a recognizable type.

A central stylistic device is the personification of American states as schoolgirls. Their names determine their temperaments, habits, and attitudes toward climate: Massachusetts is orderly and intellectual, Maine is hardy and delighted by snow, Virginia is refined and weather-sensitive, and New York is conscious of status. This conceit produces affectionate regional comedy rather than realistic psychological complexity. The characters are individualized primarily through repeated traits, verbal habits, and contrasts with one another. Maine is defined by energy, competence, cheerfulness, and physical endurance; Massachusetts by calm authority and dry humor; Virginia by languor and alarm; Miss Wayland by anxious responsibility.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization and gives the opening a lively, theatrical quality. Exchanges are quick, pointed, and frequently interrupted by exclamations or comic rebukes. Massachusetts’s repeated fines for “slang,” “vulgar colloquial,” and “personal remarks” create a running joke that later becomes structurally important. Maine’s exhausted return—“Personal—rudeness! … One cent for the missionary fund”—reprises the joke at the climax, while her later price for lending the snow-shoes supplies the final comic and moral payoff. Dialogue also differentiates social and regional speech. The schoolgirls generally speak in polished, educated sentences, whereas Benny and his mother use colloquial forms such as “can’t get nowhere,” “’cept,” “’em,” and “rest ye,” marking class and locality in a period-typical manner.

The diction alternates between plain narrative language, elevated emotional phrasing, and vivid figurative description. In the storm scenes, the wind is personified as a violent antagonist: it “clutched,” “howled,” “shrieked,” “buffeted,” and seemed filled with “angry demons.” Similes and metaphors make the weather physical and theatrical: windows rattle “like castanets,” Maine is shaken “like a leaf,” and the gale becomes a “wall of ice.” Repetition intensifies sensory experience, as in “snow, snow,” while clusters of active verbs convey effort and danger. After the storm, the imagery becomes bright and expansive. The sun “laughed,” the sky is blue and deep, and drifts resemble curling billows. This contrast transforms the blizzard from threatening chaos into a landscape of triumph and opportunity.

Sentence rhythm changes with the action. Social scenes use moderately long sentences interspersed with short dialogue, creating conversational buoyancy. The rescue sequence relies more heavily on brief declarative sentences, fragments, repeated clauses, and forceful verbs: Maine listens, struggles, falls, rises, and advances. Longer periodic sentences are used for panoramic descriptions of the storm or snow-covered village. Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, dashes, and direct interjections are frequent, reflecting the expressive conventions of children’s adventure fiction of the period. Capitalized or mythological references—such as “Boreas, Auster, Eurus, and Zephyrus”—add a lightly academic flavor appropriate to the school setting.

The plot is linear and carefully shaped around escalation and reprise. A comic dispute about snow introduces Maine’s regional expertise; the weather then grows from novelty to genuine threat. Her earlier delight in snow, her special clothing, her snow-shoes, her experience carrying her brother, and Massachusetts’s missionary fines all become functional elements in the rescue and resolution. Suspense is sustained by alternating between Maine outside and the anxious group inside. The loosened twine creates a final false catastrophe immediately before her comic re-entry. The aftermath extends the heroine’s usefulness beyond a single brave act: she obtains provisions, lends the doctor her snow-shoes, and indirectly helps save another life.

Overall, the style is energetic, picturesque, sentimental without being solemn, and openly exemplary. Heroism is presented not as grand self-dramatization but as cheerful practicality. Maine’s defining quality is that she treats danger and service as natural extensions of everyday competence. Humor repeatedly prevents the moral lesson from becoming heavy-handed, while the final enlargement of the one-cent joke into a ten-dollar charitable gift gives the story a neatly closed, uplifting finish.

The story adopts the manner of a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century comic tall tale, combining the brisk exposition of popular journalism with the elaborate, knowing voice of a literary raconteur. Its omniscient narrator speaks with confident authority, supplying backgrounds, motives, private thoughts, and occasional generalizations about human nature. This authority is repeatedly undercut by dry irony: absurd premises are explained as though they were sober facts, while fraud, vanity, romance, and commercial exploitation are treated with mock seriousness. Expressions such as “the two singular men of this narrative” give the narration a formal, self-conscious quality and remind the reader that the tale is being deliberately arranged for comic effect.

The diction alternates between ornate, Latinate narration and direct colloquial speech. Descriptive passages favor emphatic or ceremonious words—“prodigious,” “perspicacity,” “contumelious,” “effulgent,” and “insurrectionary”—whose grandeur comically exceeds the vulgarity of the dime-museum world. The narrator often piles up adjectives and parallel phrases, as in the repeated description of the “soft, pale, limpid amber” eyes. This verbal extravagance lends mock-epic dignity to deception and rivalry. At other moments the prose becomes aphoristic: “Innocence measures the strength of faith. The charm of faith is its absurdity.” Such maxims sound philosophical but are placed in an absurd context, sharpening the satire.

Sentence structure is varied but generally expansive. Long periodic sentences carry explanations, qualifications, and comic escalations, while short declarative sentences punctuate major turns: “Sampey secretly loved Zoe”; “He prepared for Bat”; “Zoe believed in Sampey.” Parallelism and antithesis are prominent, especially when the rivals are compared: “brutality against wit, strength against cunning, fury against patience.” These balanced constructions clarify the central conflict while elevating it into a mock-heroic contest. Exclamation points, dashes, parenthetical asides, rhetorical questions, and abrupt paragraph breaks produce an animated, performative rhythm resembling oral storytelling.

Dialogue is highly theatrical and character-specific. Sampey speaks in controlled, ceremonious language, using pseudo-philosophical reasoning to disguise manipulation as modest heroism. Bat’s speech is rendered in exaggerated eye dialect—“I ketcha da scound”—to mark him as an Italian immigrant and to make his anger comic. Castellani’s final speech abruptly reveals an Irish-inflected dialect, adding another layer of ethnic caricature. These representations are period-specific and rely on stereotypes that would now be considered crude. Dialogue serves less as naturalistic conversation than as comic performance, with each speaker displaying a recognizable verbal persona.

The narrative moves quickly through exposition, courtship, rivalry, deception, exposure, and reversal. Much of the opening is devoted to the grotesque origin of Bat’s tufted nose, after which the story shifts into a romantic triangle. Suspense is built around Sampey’s apparently supernatural ability to change his eye color. The explanation is withheld until the climactic dinner scene, where Bat exposes the artificial glass coverings. That revelation immediately produces a second reversal: Sampey has already married Zoe and secured a partnership in the museum, so the exposure cannot defeat him. The ending is compressed into a comic summary that restores Bat to woodcutting and rewards Sampey with love, wealth, and professional success.

Humor arises from incongruity, escalation, repetition, and deadpan treatment. The grotesque medical operation is described with technical precision, making its absurd consequence seem plausible. Zoe’s romantic idealism is contrasted with the tawdry mechanics of a sideshow illusion, while Sampey’s timidity coexists with considerable cunning. Parenthetical remarks frequently deliver secondary jokes, such as the spectator who faints upon seeing the amber eyes or the explanation that Bat’s salary is justified because visitors pull his nasal tuft. The story also uses dramatic irony: readers increasingly suspect that Sampey’s “heroism” is fraudulent, even as Zoe interprets the eye color as proof of destiny.

The principal tonal blend is affectionate whimsy, grotesque comedy, and cynical satire. The museum’s deceptions are not condemned so much as admired for their ingenuity and profitability. Commercial success repeatedly determines social worth: Castellani prefers Bat when Bat earns more, then accepts Sampey once Sampey becomes a valuable attraction and partner. Romance likewise depends on illusion. Zoe’s faith is naïve, Sampey’s courtship is manipulative, and yet the conclusion presents their marriage as happy. This moral elasticity is central to the tale’s comic worldview, in which cleverness defeats strength and a successful fraud becomes almost indistinguishable from genuine merit.

Imagery is vivid, caricatural, and oriented toward spectacle. Bat is defined by black hair, swollen muscles, a tufted nose, and violently expressive poses; Zoe by rosy cheeks, plumpness, gilt surroundings, and decorative costume; Sampey by pallor, delicacy, and eyes that shift symbolically from gray to amber to scarlet. Color functions as both motif and plot mechanism. Gray signifies ordinariness, amber romantic heroism, and scarlet menace. Repeated visual references to eyes also connect the story’s themes of spectatorship and deception: museum visitors stare at Zoe, Zoe searches their eyes for destiny, and Sampey manipulates appearances to control how others see him.

Overall, the style is exuberant, plot-driven, and knowingly artificial. It favors memorable caricature over psychological realism, ingenious reversals over plausibility, and verbal flourish over restraint. Its characteristic effect comes from presenting a ludicrous sideshow romance in the elevated language of science, heroism, destiny, and moral reflection, while allowing practical cunning and show-business opportunism to triumph.

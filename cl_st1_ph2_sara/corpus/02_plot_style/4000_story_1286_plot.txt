Senator Harrison, representing a county fiercely opposed to pardoning Alfred Williams, argues successfully that the young prisoner should remain incarcerated for murdering his father and stepmother at age eleven. Senator Dorman then recounts Williams’s childhood of abandonment, abuse, and isolation, explaining that the boy had no one to temper his despair and violence. He also describes Williams’s twelve years of exemplary conduct and intellectual growth in prison, insisting that he deserves the first real chance life has ever offered him.

As Harrison listens, his confidence in abstract justice gives way to empathy. He imagines himself in Williams’s circumstances and recognizes how thoroughly the boy was deprived of childhood, affection, freedom, and even ordinary experiences such as swimming, attending a circus, or owning a dog. Nevertheless, the pardon resolution initially fails by two votes, largely because of Harrison’s influential opposition.

Unable to bear responsibility for permanently denying Williams a place in the world, Harrison moves to reconsider the vote, even though reversing himself will likely cost him an upcoming election. He awkwardly admits that he was mistaken and now favors giving Williams his chance. The second vote passes overwhelmingly. Though others praise his action as heroic, Harrison calls it cowardice, claiming he was afraid the decision would haunt him in old age. Leaving the chamber, however, he feels at peace with the renewed life of spring and with his choice to grant another human being the same opportunity for renewal.

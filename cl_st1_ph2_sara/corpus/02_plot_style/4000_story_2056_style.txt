The story uses the manner of a late-Victorian comic character sketch: anecdotal, conversational and governed by sustained irony. A first-person narrator presents himself as a reluctant witness to the career of an incurably helpful man whose benevolent intentions invariably produce injury, confusion or misery. The narrative voice is urbane, dry and apparently reasonable. It rarely announces a joke; instead, it describes absurd events with sober precision and allows the disparity between motive and result to generate the humour. The narrator participates in the action but remains chiefly an observer and comic commentator, occasionally admitting his own foolishness in a tone of amused self-reproach.

The central comic device is inversion. Kindness becomes persecution, instruction leads to failure, mediation intensifies disputes, and social service ends in catastrophe. This governing contradiction is established immediately through childhood incidents and then repeated with escalating variations. The protagonist remains essentially static: from infancy to adulthood, he is officious, confident, generous and disastrously incompetent. Characterisation is therefore cumulative rather than psychologically developmental. Each anecdote supplies another proof of the same trait, while the settings broaden from nursery and school to sport, public transport, legal proceedings, parties, weddings, sickrooms and funerals.

The structure moves from compressed biographical summary into extended scenes and then back into a rapid catalogue of examples. Early paragraphs cover years in a few sentences, using parallel anecdotes to establish the pattern. The omnibus episode forms the principal set piece: a trivial fare calculation expands through intervention, repetition and contradiction into collective chaos. The water-rate episode gives the narrator a more active role and demonstrates how the protagonist’s enthusiasm can turn a minor administrative matter into an expensive legal principle. The final sections accelerate into shorter social disasters, ending with a concise epigram that reframes the man’s flaw as a misapplied professional talent. This alternation between summary, dramatised episode and punch-line keeps the piece brisk despite its episodic form.

The prose combines long, balanced sentences with short comic closures. Elaborate clauses often build an appearance of logic before ending in an absurd qualification: an action is described in elevated or bureaucratic terms, then punctured by its practical consequence. Parentheses, dashes and semicolons support the narrator’s digressive, after-dinner-storytelling cadence. Repetition and parallel syntax are prominent, particularly in catalogues of helpful acts. Sentences frequently employ antithesis—intention against outcome, confidence against competence, kindness against suffering—and the clean symmetry sharpens the irony.

Diction shifts between formal narrative language and colloquial speech. The narrator favours mock-serious phrases such as “inalienable rights,” “test case,” “grief-stricken relatives” and “eminently unsatisfactory,” applying elevated language to petty disputes or foolish behaviour. This inflation of register is an important source of humour. Dialogue is more socially differentiated: the conductor’s dropped consonants and slang suggest working-class London speech, while the protagonist speaks fluently and dogmatically, often in imperatives or declarations of principle. Quoted words such as “mind” and “drive” are used to expose the gap between the protagonist’s understanding of an activity and its actual effect.

The humour is predominantly deadpan, cumulative and situational, with elements of farce, bathos and hyperbole. Many punch-lines arrive as understated reversals: the boy offers classmates answers that are “invariably quite wrong”; the boxing pupils hit him successfully because they have supposedly followed his instructions incorrectly; old ladies are escorted safely into the wrong train. Hyperbole broadens these mishaps into universal laws of character, as when no quarrel is considered complete without him. The narrator’s mock objectivity strengthens the comic effect by treating obvious calamities as evidence in a reasoned case.

The pacing depends on escalation. Incidents begin with an ordinary social impulse—helping, advising, organising or defending someone—and proceed through unnecessary intervention to disproportionate damage. Dialogue accelerates the longer scenes by allowing misunderstandings to reproduce themselves in real time. In the omnibus sequence, repeated references to sixpence, fourpence and eightpence make the exchange increasingly difficult to follow; the reader experiences the same confusion as the passengers. Elsewhere, brief summary anecdotes deliver quick, epigrammatic blows.

The tone is satirical without being savage. The protagonist is ridiculous and exhausting, but not malicious. Repeated assurances of his kindness preserve a degree of affection and make the satire more pointed: good intentions are not denied, merely shown to be inadequate when joined to vanity, meddlesomeness and incompetence. The narrator’s irritation is tempered by amusement, and even his harsher remarks are framed as polished social comedy rather than moral denunciation.

Overall, the style is that of an orally inflected humorous essay or club-room anecdote. Its signature features are a genial first-person raconteur, one exaggerated but recognisable human type, formal language applied to trivial affairs, dialogue-rich comic set pieces, escalating chains of unintended consequences, and aphoristic endings. The story’s central stylistic principle is to maintain a grave, lucid surface while every example demonstrates that the world beneath it is slipping into absurdity.

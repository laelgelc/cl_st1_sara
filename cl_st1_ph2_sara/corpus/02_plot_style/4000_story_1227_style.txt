The story adopts the manner of a traditional literary fairy tale while gently parodying its conventions. It opens with the archetypal “Once there was,” establishes a pastoral world of rare animals, dutiful daughters, kings, and advantageous marriages, and follows a clear pattern of prosperity, misfortune, trial, vindication, and happy restoration. The magic is accepted without elaborate explanation: a cow has golden horns, a school building sleeps and wakes, and the missing animal eventually returns laden with treasure. This matter-of-fact treatment gives the fantasy a nursery-tale simplicity.

The narrator is omniscient, conversational, and conspicuously present. Besides reporting events and occasionally entering characters’ thoughts, the voice speaks directly to the reader through phrases such as “you ever saw,” “you must know,” and “you know.” It also offers playful judgments and explanations, often treating absurd customs as perfectly reasonable. Parenthetical qualifications and confidential asides create the impression of an adult storyteller entertaining children while sharing a joke with them.

The diction is accessible and concrete, but enriched by decorative, slightly old-fashioned vocabulary. Words associated with rural life, court ceremony, clothing, needlework, and domestic furnishings—“palfrey,” “housings,” “dimity,” “petticoat,” “tatting,” “Kensington stitch,” and “portière”—give the setting a period flavor. Repeated evaluative adjectives such as “pretty,” “beautiful,” “charming,” “exquisite,” “splendid,” and “magnificent” evoke the heightened language of children’s fantasy. Colors recur insistently, especially blue, white, pink, green, gold, and silver, producing an illustrated-storybook effect.

Description is lush, visual, and ornamental. Drusilla’s clothes, hair, meadow, cottage, cow, schoolwork, and eventual castle are presented through carefully accumulated details. Natural imagery centers on flowers, dew, grass, roses, lilies, moss, and water, while wealth is represented by gold, jewels, satin, lace, and polished objects. Comparisons are vivid and sometimes synesthetic: the cow’s milk “tasted as lilies smell.” The prettiness is deliberately excessive, however, and often shades into comic incongruity, as when a cow’s tail is tied up with a blue ribbon, rose leaves replace hay, or lace curtains decorate a stable.

The syntax is generally straightforward and suitable for oral narration, with chronological transitions such as “At length,” “After a while,” “Meantime,” and “At last.” Many sentences are coordinated strings of actions or descriptive items, giving the prose an unhurried, cumulative rhythm. Longer sentences frequently contain subordinate clauses, parenthetical comments, and lists. Repetition supplies cohesion and a fairy-tale cadence: “gold-horned cow,” “dear old father,” and the details of the blue petticoat, white gown, meadow, and roses recur as verbal motifs. Exclamation points and rhetorical questions heighten moments of alarm, wonder, or comic emphasis.

The humor is whimsical, satirical, and deadpan rather than harsh. Court etiquette is mocked through mechanical imitation: when the King reins in, every noble does the same and every horse rears. Royal vanity appears in the King’s coat and umbrella overloaded with decorations, as well as in his refusal to admit failure. The comparison of his jeweled breast to a circus-day fence is deliberately anachronistic and deflates his majesty. The seminary similarly caricatures fashionable female education: its students spend their time on decorative needlework, piano variations, complexion-preserving diets, and ornamental accomplishments. Modern elements such as electric light coexist casually with kings, palfreys, and enchanted buildings, creating comic temporal incongruity.

Dialogue is brief, courteous, and plot-driven. Drusilla speaks timidly and deferentially, repeatedly addressing the King as “your Majesty,” while the King speaks with breezy confidence and occasional peremptoriness. His affected “Pardy” and formal promises parody royal speech. Dialogue also reinforces character contrasts: Drusilla is emotionally sincere and hesitant, whereas the King is self-important, impulsive, and concerned with preserving his dignity.

Characterization relies on broad fairy-tale traits rather than psychological complexity. Drusilla is beautiful, industrious, dutiful, modest, and loyal to her father; her goodness is demonstrated through repeated domestic actions and through her refusal of rank and marriage. The King is not villainous, but vain, credulous, and comically authoritarian. The father is loving and dependent, the Queen benign, and the envious schoolmate serves chiefly to initiate the false accusation. The cow is almost emblematic: calm, beautiful, nourishing, mysterious, and ultimately the source of both vindication and prosperity.

The pacing is leisurely at the beginning, where the pastoral setting and daily routines are described in detail. It quickens when the cow disappears, then expands again during the seminary episode, which forms the main comic and satirical digression. The final movement is rapid and symmetrical: Drusilla returns home, the cow reappears, the King acknowledges her innocence, and the original pastoral life is restored in richer form. The ending preserves rather than overturns Drusilla’s values; wealth enlarges the cottage into a castle, but its thatched, mossy, rose-covered appearance deliberately imitates the old home.

The central contrast is between authentic domestic affection and artificial social elevation. Drusilla’s happiness lies in caring for her father and cow, not in courtly splendor, fashionable education, or dynastic marriage. The story praises loyalty, truthfulness, work, humility, and contentment while satirizing vanity, institutional pretension, envy, and status-seeking. Its moral is conveyed through narrative outcome rather than explicit sermonizing: Drusilla gains riches and honor precisely because she does not value them above love and home.

Overall, the style is that of an ornate Victorian or late nineteenth-century children’s fairy tale: sentimental but knowingly comic, richly visual, episodic, morally reassuring, and marked by a genial narrator who treats nonsense with solemn logic.

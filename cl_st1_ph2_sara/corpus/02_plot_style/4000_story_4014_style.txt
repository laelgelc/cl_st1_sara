The story adopts the manner of a medieval romance or saint’s legend, but tempers its spiritual grandeur with earthy comedy and quiet irony. Its setting is rendered through a few vivid, emblematic details—winter light, wooded slopes, crimson clouds, rusted armour and the ruby rose—rather than through exhaustive description. The imagined Middle Ages feel at once historical and legendary: named landscapes, feudal rivalries, saints, knights, peasants and wood-thieves give the narrative material solidity, while visions, sacred symbols and folkloric beliefs move it toward myth.

The narrator is omniscient, composed and largely unobtrusive. The voice treats mystical aspiration, violence and peasant self-interest with the same formal calm, allowing their incongruity to generate humour without explicit commentary. It occasionally rises into aphoristic or prophetic language, as in the description of dreamers and doers, but more often proceeds in the lucid manner of an oral tale or chronicle. Moral judgement is therefore present but indirect. The knight’s idealism is dignified, yet the peasants’ caution and acquisitiveness are not crudely condemned; they belong to the practical world against which his absolute faith is measured.

The prose is elevated and faintly archaic. Constructions such as “it were well,” “bid,” “hither,” “thereat” and “behold” create a scriptural and chivalric register, while invocations and capitalised abstractions—“Divine Rose,” “Most High,” “Voice,” “Powers of Corruption”—lend the knight’s beliefs ceremonial weight. This language is repeatedly juxtaposed with plain talk about pigs, crowns, breakfast, theft and fear. The contrast between sacred rhetoric and mundane concerns is central to the style: a metaphysical quest finds its fulfilment in recovering two stolen pigs.

Sentences are often long, cumulative and linked by repeated conjunctions. Actions unfold through chains of “and,” “but,” “for” and “then,” producing the forward movement of traditional storytelling. These extended sentences can carry landscape, reflection and incident together, while shorter statements punctuate decisive moments, especially in dialogue and at the knight’s death. Repetition is used rhythmically—“ran and climbed, and climbed and ran,” or the successive stages of following tracks—to give physical momentum and oral emphasis. The punctuation is formal and sometimes old-fashioned, with semicolons and colons marking logical or rhetorical turns.

Imagery is concentrated around colour, light and blood. Crimson sunset, ruby fire, the blood-coloured rose and the knight’s wounds form a coherent symbolic pattern in which spiritual illumination and sacrificial death become almost indistinguishable. The rose is both heraldic object and mystical emblem, joining the knight’s outer identity to his inward vocation. Against this luminous symbolism stand tactile details such as soft clay, leather jerkins, cloven footprints, buzzing arrows and bloody rents in armour. Such concrete observations keep the visionary material grounded.

Dialogue is strongly differentiated by social outlook. The knight speaks in formal, moral and theological categories, asking whether men are pious, true or impious and interpreting combat as sacred service. The peasants speak pragmatically of numbers, political allegiance, payment and survival. The lad’s speech is rambling, literal and candid, moving freely between cockfighting, folklore, money and curiosity. Exposition emerges through these conflicting voices rather than through detached explanation, and the knight’s long autobiographical speech functions as an embedded legend within the main tale.

The structure moves from scenic introduction to pursuit and battle, then slows into a fireside confession and death scene. The combat is narrated swiftly and clearly, with little psychological hesitation and no indulgence in graphic horror. The theological monologue deliberately expands the scale of the story, transforming a local skirmish into the culmination of a lifelong mystical mission. Yet the final movement abruptly restores the smaller, comic world: the lad appreciates the tale mainly because it contains fighting, begins the burial, hears a cock crow and abandons the grave to pursue his obsession.

Characterisation is economical and emblematic. The knight is defined by melancholy, courtesy, courage and uncompromising spiritual purpose; the peasants embody collective prudence and material need; the lad is an “innocent” whose narrow enthusiasm makes him both receptive and incapable of sustained reverence. Nevertheless, each figure is humanised by incongruous detail. The knight bargains in crowns despite his mysticism, the grateful peasants remain too frightened to return, and the lad can tend a dying man while thinking about fighting cocks.

The prevailing tone blends solemnity, wonder, melancholy and deadpan humour. Irony does not wholly invalidate the knight’s vision: his sacrifice is genuine, and the recurring radiance of the rose gives his faith imaginative authority. At the same time, the conclusion resists conventional hagiographic closure. No disciple preserves his revelation, and even his burial is left unfinished. The style thus sustains a productive tension between transcendent meaning and human distraction, presenting idealism as beautiful and heroic while acknowledging how easily ordinary appetite, fear and forgetfulness outlast it.

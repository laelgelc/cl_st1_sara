The story is written as a retrospective first-person account by an educated, professionally authoritative narrator. As a surgeon, he supplies clinical credibility, anatomical precision, and privileged access to the violence, while his repeated expressions of horror and sympathy give him the appearance of a moral witness. His reliability is nevertheless complicated by passivity: he condemns cruelty but repeatedly enables it, then observes Neranya’s final attempt rather than intervening. The temporal distance signalled by phrases such as “even after this lapse of years” frames the narrative as testimony to an extraordinary and traumatic event.

The diction is formal, elevated, and distinctly late-Victorian or early modern sensationalist. It combines medical and procedural vocabulary—“amputation,” “stump,” “arteries,” “windpipe”—with emphatic moral labels such as “fiendishness,” “diabolism,” “wretch,” and “superhuman monster.” Archaic constructions and words including “thither,” “wonted,” “contumely,” “whereabout,” and “boon” reinforce the period character. The prose also reflects colonial stereotypes, especially in its references to “cruelty purely Oriental,” racial inheritance, and supposedly fixed ethnic temperaments. These assumptions exoticise India and frame the setting through a European observer’s prejudices rather than through culturally specific detail.

Sentence structure is generally elaborate and hypotactic, with long chains of subordinate clauses used to analyse motives, reconstruct actions, and delay outcomes. Semicolons, dashes, parenthetical qualifications, and cumulative lists are frequent. During the escape sequence, this syntactic intricacy becomes almost mechanical: each bodily movement is broken into stages, obstacles, adjustments, and consequences. Shorter sentences and exclamations—“For what purpose?” and “How could he accomplish the impossible task?”—interrupt these extended descriptions, reproducing the narrator’s mounting astonishment and accelerating suspense.

The narrative is tightly linear and built around escalating mutilation, adaptation, and revenge. Early events are summarised rapidly: Neranya loses one arm, then the other, murders the prince, and loses both legs. The account then slows dramatically once he is placed in the elevated cage. A substantial architectural description establishes the spatial geometry needed for the climax, particularly the relation among cage, fountain, balcony, stairs, cot, and floor. The final night unfolds almost in real time, with repeated apparent impossibilities creating a sequence of miniature cliff-hangers. The plot briefly misdirects both narrator and reader into believing that Neranya seeks escape, before revealing that every movement has been designed as an attack.

The dominant tone blends clinical detachment, Gothic horror, melodrama, and fascinated awe. Graphic violence is often announced through evaluative language rather than described directly; the narrator sometimes retreats behind phrases such as “I care to say nothing more,” allowing omission to intensify the horror. Elsewhere, the prose dwells minutely on physical strain, bodily deformation, strangulation, blood, and crushed anatomy. Neranya’s mutilated body is repeatedly rendered in dehumanising terms—“hideous, shapeless thing,” “frightful wreck,” “unwieldy body”—yet his ingenuity and will are described with increasing admiration. This tension produces a mixture of revulsion and sympathy rather than a simple moral division.

Imagery centres on bodily fragmentation, confinement, vertical space, and predatory force. The polished palace hall, stained light, fountain, and lofty ceiling provide an elegant, cool setting that contrasts sharply with torture and imprisonment. Vertical arrangements carry dramatic meaning: the rajah first looks down upon Neranya from the balcony, but Neranya ultimately converts that height into the weapon of his revenge. The fountain partially obscures the cage during the escape and adds a soft, continuous sound beneath the clandestine action. Light imagery is concentrated in Neranya’s eyes, whose “wonderful” or “terrible” brightness externalises rage, calculation, and determination.

Characterisation is strongly polarised but gains complexity through reversals. The rajah is initially “noble” and grateful, yet also indolent, vindictive, and theatrically cruel. Neranya begins as malicious and violent, becomes an object of pity through punishment, and finally appears almost heroic in endurance while remaining murderous. The narrator’s fascination with him develops through repeated references to study, observation, deduction, and experiment. Other characters remain functional and largely voiceless; direct dialogue is almost absent, and even Neranya’s curses are conveyed through extravagant summary rather than quotation.

Repetition and accumulation are major rhetorical devices. Variations of “impossible,” “horrible,” “awful,” “frightful,” and “astonishment” continually amplify the extremity of events. The catalogue of Neranya’s curses expands from the rajah’s body and ancestry to religions, celestial bodies, landscapes, and all living things, giving his hatred a cosmic scale. Likewise, repeated accounts of effort—wriggling, bracing, tightening, sliding, pausing—turn physical action into a prolonged ordeal. Foreshadowing is explicit but effective: Neranya’s cultivation of his feet, the narrator’s fear that he may develop an “unheard-of power for mischief,” the strategic placement of the cage, and the rajah’s habit of sleeping below the balcony all prepare the final method of murder.

Overall, the style belongs to the Gothic revenge tale and the sensational adventure narrative. Its distinctive effect comes from joining lurid subject matter to controlled, quasi-scientific exposition. The climax is not merely violent; it is presented as the logical solution to a spatial and anatomical problem. That procedural exactness makes the grotesque premise feel temporarily plausible, while the final image of Neranya smiling “sweetly” in death supplies an ironic, macabre closure in which physical annihilation becomes triumphant agency.

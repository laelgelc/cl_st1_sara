The story uses an omniscient third-person narrator who stays close to Jack’s canine perceptions while freely supplying background information, social judgments, and access to other characters’ attitudes. The animal viewpoint is sustained through scent, bodily movement, instinct, and canine assumptions: the town is mapped by sausage, soap, burning fat, perfume, and the traces of other dogs rather than primarily by visual landmarks. At the same time, the dogs think and speak like socially differentiated human beings. This anthropomorphism creates a double perspective in which animal adventure becomes an allegory of human hierarchy, political power, cruelty, and freedom.

The prose is clear, chronological, and conventionally realist, with the slightly formal diction and leisurely exposition associated with older translated fiction. Sentences are often long and cumulative, combining physical description, action, and interpretive commentary. Detailed catalogues are prominent, particularly when the dogs in the cart are introduced one by one. Breed, colour, grooming, posture, health, and expression serve as compact forms of characterization. Parenthetical qualifications, explanatory clauses, ellipses, and occasional exclamations give the narration an expansive, conversational rhythm. A footnote abruptly explains the apparently supernatural capture, adding a documentary touch while preserving the comic effect of Jack’s limited understanding.

Sensory description is unusually important. Smell dominates the opening and establishes the plausibility of the canine viewpoint, while tactile and kinetic details—the jolting cart, trembling cage, tight collar, cold morning, and violent leap over the fence—make danger physically immediate. Visual details are often precise but gently caricatural: the dachshund has a “crocodile body,” a tail curls like the figure nine, and the poodle’s circus trim imitates a lion. Such comparisons combine affectionate observation with comic grotesquerie.

Dialogue occupies much of the story and functions as both characterization and philosophical debate. Each dog possesses a recognizable social voice. Jack is jaunty, gallant, impulsive, and idealistic; Arto the poodle is ceremonious, educated, ironic, and pessimistic; the greyhound is affected and delicate; Bouton is servile and eager for approval; the mouse-coloured dog is gloomy; and the violet-coloured stray is blunt, cynical, experienced, and ultimately decisive. Polite forms such as “mesdames,” “mademoiselle,” “professor,” and “dear sirs” parody class etiquette even in a cage bound for the slaughter-house. Their elevated manners contrast with their helpless physical situation, producing social satire.

The tonal movement is carefully graduated. The opening is light, fresh, and comic, presenting Jack as a confident young dog on an ordinary morning. His capture introduces bewilderment and menace; the cart scene develops into dark comedy; and Arto’s account of the slaughter-house shifts the mood toward horror and political melancholy. Humour persists through vanity, social pretension, breed stereotypes, and ironic understatement, but it increasingly coexists with images of starvation, killing, and skinning alive. The result is a tragicomic tone rather than pure sentimentality.

Structurally, the narrative proceeds as a compact journey from freedom to confinement and then to an unexpected demonstration of escape. Early confidence is repeatedly overturned: Jack believes he can find his way home, mistakes the dog-catcher’s net for a supernatural force, and assumes wit and good breeding have practical value. The cart becomes a miniature society in which the dogs discuss collective resistance, inequality, violence, and submission. Arto argues that human strength makes obedience inevitable and that canine happiness lies in finding a benevolent master. The violet dog answers not with rhetoric but with action, escaping at severe bodily cost. The closing sentence is restrained and epiphanic: the poodle silently recognizes that his philosophy was mistaken.

The story’s central technique is irony. Dogs condemn human greed, hypocrisy, censorship, inequality, and murder, thereby exposing recognizable defects in human society more sharply than a direct sermon might. Arto’s pessimism initially sounds authoritative because it is backed by age, education, and experience, yet the despised outsider possesses the courage and practical knowledge the “distinguished society” lacks. Social respectability is therefore inverted: polished speakers remain captive, while the mangy, rude, violet-stained stray proves genuinely free. The ending does not romanticize liberation, since escape tears away part of his side; freedom is possible, but costly.

Overall, the style blends realist animal observation, fable, social caricature, philosophical dialogue, and political allegory. Its signature features are scent-led focalization, elaborate physical characterization, formal and class-coded speech, darkly comic tonal contrasts, escalating confinement, and a concise final reversal in which action defeats fatalistic argument.

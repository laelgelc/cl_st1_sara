The story is a comic detective parody written in an expansive, anecdotal style. Its omniscient third-person narrator maintains a largely straight-faced manner while reporting increasingly absurd events, allowing the humour to arise from the contrast between sober narration and preposterous logic. The narrative voice occasionally adds dry, mock-sentimental commentary—particularly about Mr. Gubb’s love for Syrilla—and treats trivial or nonsensical developments with the gravity of melodrama and crime fiction.

The tone blends farce, satire, tall tale and gentle burlesque. Detective conventions are repeatedly invoked only to be undermined: clues are accidental, confessions arise from misunderstandings, and the supposedly trained detective solves a genuine theft while pursuing an invented murder. Much of the comedy depends on literal-mindedness and circular reasoning. Chi Foxy claims that perfect disguise requires behaving exactly like a tramp, while Gubb accepts the performance so thoroughly that he refuses to recognize real hunger beneath it. Similar comic logic governs the vanished clue, the detective who is also his own fugitive, and the buried “murder victim” that proves to be a stolen calf.

Diction is deliberately idiosyncratic. The narrator uses conventional, somewhat formal prose, but the dialogue is full of colloquial contractions, slang, malapropisms, phonetic spellings and grammatical distortions. Expressions such as “deteckative,” “Shermlock Hollums,” “limmy-seen,” “samwiches” and “spirit dee corpse” caricature imperfectly understood education and social aspiration. Gubb’s speech is especially marked by redundant, misapplied constructions—“the temperature is full of cold weather,” “kill you to death,” and “capturing up the criminal classes.” These errors are systematic enough to function as characterization rather than incidental dialect. Chi Foxy’s tramp vernacular is faster, more opportunistic and performative, filled with “bo,” “pard,” “cully” and extravagant claims.

The syntax alternates between long, orderly narrative sentences and rapid dialogue exchanges. Descriptive passages proceed through accumulating clauses and repeated actions, while comic conversations rely on interruptions, questions, corrections and escalating elaboration. Repetition is a major device: Chi Foxy continually invokes his intimacy with “Shermlock,” insists on the completeness of his disguises, and expands each lie whenever Gubb raises an objection. Parallel phrasing and incremental exaggeration give his monologues an oral, vaudevillian rhythm. The narrator also uses lists—especially the catalogue of sandwiches and the sequence of magnifying instruments—to prolong a joke until it reaches absurdity.

Characterization is primarily achieved through speech and comic fixation. Philo Gubb is earnest, credulous, pedantic and proud of his correspondence-school credentials. His reliance on numbered lessons parodies both formulaic detective methods and mail-order professional education. Chi Foxy is a quick-witted fabulist whose elaborate deceptions are driven by the basic need for food. His stories imitate the structure of adventure fiction and aristocratic society tales while exposing his ignorance through mangled names and titles. Minor characters are drawn in broad visual strokes, such as Gubb’s flamingo-like appearance and the calf thief’s lanky, coarse-bearded figure.

Structurally, the story uses nested comic narratives. It opens with the collapse of Gubb’s romance, shifts to Chi Foxy’s observation of a suspicious burial, then pauses for a long encounter dominated by invented reminiscences and a false murder confession. That fabrication unexpectedly leads Gubb to the real solution of the calf-theft case. The concluding return to Syrilla reconnects this episodic detective plot with the opening romantic plot. Coincidence is therefore not concealed but embraced as the chief engine of the story.

Pacing is deliberately uneven. Brief scenes of action and discovery surround an extended central dialogue in which Chi Foxy’s lies grow more elaborate. This digressive middle delays the investigation but supplies most of the verbal comedy. Once the buried bones are reached, the resolution is brisk: the bones are identified, the thief confesses, and Gubb adopts the inscrutable manner of a successful detective despite having arrived at the truth by accident.

The story’s imagery is concrete and lightly caricatural. Details such as the yellow kitchen light, fried veal chops, crushed straw, sandy soil and the chipped breakfast plate ground the farce in an ordinary Midwestern setting. Food imagery recurs throughout, linking Chi Foxy’s hunger to the mystery itself: veal is eaten, calf bones are buried, meals motivate deception, and Syrilla’s spectacular body becomes part of the final revelation. This material emphasis keeps the humour physical and immediate.

Irony controls both plot and ending. Gubb appears foolish but succeeds; Chi Foxy invents a murder yet supplies a genuine clue; the criminals confess because they overestimate the detective; and Gubb’s professional triumph coincides with personal devastation. The final sentence—“He never laughed again”—is a mock-tragic deadpan ending. Its exaggerated finality converts romantic disappointment into melodrama while darkening the otherwise genial comedy. Overall, the style is best characterized as straight-faced farce built from dialect humour, verbal inflation, parody of detective reasoning, accidental resolution and a final sting of comic pathos.

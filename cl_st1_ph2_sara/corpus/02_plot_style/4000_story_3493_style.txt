The story uses a layered first-person frame narrative. An initially sceptical narrator recounts meeting an ostentatiously learned stranger, who then becomes the principal narrator of a supernatural experience; the return to the railway setting leaves the inner tale deliberately unfinished. This structure creates distance and uncertainty, since the palace narrative reaches the reader through a raconteur whose reliability is questioned both before and after he speaks. The abrupt interruption at the promised explanation preserves mystery, while the final quarrel between the rational narrator and his theosophist kinsman adds an ironic coda.

The prose combines urbane humour, Gothic unease, romantic fantasy, and sensuous lyricism. The opening treats intellectual pretension and occult enthusiasm with dry comedy, and similar humour recurs whenever the collector contrasts his visions with his salary, reports, kerosene lamp, dog-cart, official title, and duties at the cotton market. Against this mundane colonial bureaucracy, the nocturnal palace is rendered in an elevated, dreamlike register. The tonal movement between self-mockery and rapture prevents the supernatural material from becoming wholly solemn and keeps the story poised between haunting, hallucination, and fabrication.

The diction is formal, literary, and distinctly period-specific. Archaic or elevated forms such as “methought,” “naught,” “would fain,” “wast,” and “thee” appear beside Anglo-Indian and Persianate vocabulary including “Puja,” “saheb,” “chaprasi,” “paijamas,” “attar,” “narghileh,” “Badshah,” “seraglio,” and “nahabat.” Administrative language—“collector of cotton duties,” “quarterly report,” “per mensem”—is deliberately juxtaposed with the vocabulary of enchantment and courtly luxury. Quotations and references to Shakespeare, the Vedas, Persian ghazals, the Arabian Nights, and Western poetry give the narration a cosmopolitan, allusive texture.

Sentence structure varies according to mood. Comic and expository passages tend to be controlled and declarative, whereas visionary scenes expand into long, cumulative sentences filled with parallel clauses, enumerations, and repeated sensory details. Short statements and fragments punctuate moments of compulsion or alarm: “She had maddened me”; “I saw nobody”; “All is false!” Rhetorical questions, exclamations, apostrophes, and anaphora intensify the narrator’s emotional surrender, especially when he imagines the Persian woman’s history. Repeated constructions such as “what… what… what…” and “from room to room, from path to path” create incantatory momentum.

Imagery is luxuriant and strongly multisensory. The palace is evoked through marble, coloured fabrics, jewels, fountains, moonlight, incense, spices, rose-water, henna, musical instruments, anklets, voices, and perfumed air. Sound is especially important: invisible footsteps, laughter, splashing water, guitar music, bells, sobbing, storm winds, and Meher Ali’s refrain make the unseen world seem physically present. Synesthetic combinations—fragrance carried like touch, silence filled with latent music, visions felt rather than seen—blur the boundaries between the senses and reinforce the dream state.

Personification and extended metaphor shape the supernatural atmosphere. The palace becomes a “living organism” digesting its inhabitant, later a “marble demon” or famished ogress whose stones are hungry for human life. Day falls like a theatrical curtain; the river moves like a dancing girl or nymph’s hair; the woods awaken from a dream; the tempest exposes “lightning-teeth.” These figures make the landscape and architecture active agents rather than passive settings. Recurring images of curtains, screens, doors, mirrors, dreams, and veils suggest an inaccessible past that can be glimpsed but never fully entered.

The central stylistic opposition is between day and night, modernity and the past, utility and desire. Daylight restores the collector’s bureaucratic identity and a comparatively plain, ironic mode of narration. Night dissolves that identity into ornate costume, erotic longing, historical fantasy, and increasingly fevered language. The English coat and sola hat become comic emblems of an imposed practical self, while Persian dress and palace ritual represent imaginative possession. This contrast gives the prose a double rhythm: rational recoil followed by renewed intoxication.

Characterisation is indirect and theatrical. The stranger is established through confident speech, eclectic quotations, and his pleasure in being admired. The collector emerges through his oscillation between scepticism and susceptibility, repeatedly trying to explain away his experiences with food, fatigue, or common sense before yielding to them. The spectral woman is never presented as a complete person; she appears in fragments—feet, eyes, perfume, clothing, sobs, reflected gestures—which makes her simultaneously alluring and unreachable. Meher Ali functions as a choric warning figure, his compulsive cry supplying the story’s most memorable refrain.

Pacing is gradual and recursive rather than plot-driven. Extended scenic description slows the narrative at twilight and during nocturnal wanderings, while recurring awakenings abruptly restore ordinary reality. Each episode follows a pattern of attraction, sensory intensification, interruption, and daylight disbelief, but the emotional stakes rise from playful fascination to erotic obsession, anguish, and fear of madness. The withheld history of the Persian girl denies conventional closure and makes incompletion itself part of the haunting.

Overall, the style is ornate, atmospheric, ironic, and deliberately ambiguous. It fuses Gothic architecture and supernatural suspense with Persianate romance, colonial bureaucracy, philosophical doubt, and comic framing. Its signature effect comes from making fantasy sensuously convincing while repeatedly undermining its factual credibility, so that the reader is left suspended between ghost story, psychological possession, historical memory, and a gifted traveller’s elaborate “yarn.”

The story uses a late-nineteenth-century realist style shaped by social comedy, psychological observation, and gently satirical commentary. Its central concern is the conflict between progressive ideals and involuntary human feeling: Eleanor and Faraday imagine a marriage founded on reason, freedom, and mutual trust, only to discover that jealousy survives their philosophical emancipation. The treatment is amused rather than punitive. Their unconventional principles are taken seriously, but the narrative repeatedly exposes the gap between what they profess and what they can emotionally endure.

The narrator is omniscient, intrusive, and conspicuously self-aware. Rather than remaining neutral, the narrative voice interprets motives, generalizes about men and women, anticipates objections, and occasionally addresses the reader through formulations such as “and so should we” or “It is right to say.” This creates the manner of a confident social observer telling an illustrative case history. The narrator also withholds information strategically, most notably when Eleanor’s distress is described while its cause remains unstated. Such reticence generates suspense while playfully drawing attention to the act of narration itself.

Irony is the dominant tonal device. It appears at once in the wedding notice, whose typographical insignificance makes it loom all the larger in Eleanor’s imagination, and in its placement between a holiday subscription offer and an advertisement for monuments. The latter carries a faint comic suggestion of marriage bracketed by commerce and death. Similar irony governs the couple’s symmetrical jealousies: Eleanor cannot philosophize away Faraday’s attraction to Kitty, while Faraday briefly experiences violent possessiveness when he sees Eleanor with the artist. His final thought that she is “only a woman, after all” becomes the story’s culminating irony because he has forgotten his own equivalent failure of reason.

The prose is formal, elaborate, and periodic, with long sentences built from subordinate clauses, balanced phrases, parenthetical qualifications, and abstract nouns. Expressions such as “spiritual emancipation,” “reciprocal liberty,” “psychological comprehensiveness,” and “the duality of accomplished delight and growing promise” give the narration an intellectual and sometimes mock-philosophical texture. This elevated diction reflects the couple’s habit of turning private life into a system of principles, while its occasional excess gently parodies their earnestness. Short exclamatory sentences—“It was uncalled for! / It was improper! / It was indecent!”—provide a sharp contrast, reproducing the blunt chorus of provincial outrage.

Figurative language is plentiful and often expansive. Minds and emotions are rendered through flowers, weather, warfare, chemistry, geography, and bodily sensation: Eleanor’s intelligence unfolds like petals in sunlight; her misery envelops Paris like smoke; reason enters “armed rebellion”; jealousy sends blood through Faraday’s veins “like fire.” These images externalize inner states and give psychological conflict a melodramatic vividness. The descriptions often combine admiration with comic inflation, as when Eleanor in emotional agitation becomes a “distressed goddess” or an “incipient cyclone.”

Characterization proceeds largely through narrated analysis rather than action alone. Eleanor is presented as intellectually independent, resistant to convention, and eager for self-cultivation, yet still vulnerable to inherited emotional habits. Faraday is genial, rational, optimistic, and sincerely committed to equality, but also complacent about his own assumptions. Secondary characters are sketched as social types through compressed, witty portraits. Margaret Beaton’s timid suffragism, impractical emancipatory clothing, and Kitty’s “Napoleonic grip” on domestic privileges allow the story to distinguish theoretical rebellion from instinctive power. Plymdale itself functions almost as a collective character: provincial, curious, censorious, and scandalized by deviations from established marital behavior.

The dialogue is relatively sparse and is used at turning points. It tends to be polished and economical, with hesitation, interruption, and euphemism revealing what the speakers cannot state directly. Eleanor’s repeated qualifications—“I think—I mean, don’t you think”—soften her desire to return home and contrast with her earlier ideological confidence. Dashes, fragments, and unfinished questions reproduce emotional embarrassment, while the surrounding narration supplies the psychological interpretation. French words and Parisian details, including “fiacre,” “bougie,” and “Monsieur l’Artiste,” add cosmopolitan color and distinguish Paris from provincial Plymdale.

The structure is broadly symmetrical. The opening establishes the couple’s radical theory of marriage; the middle separates them geographically and introduces Eleanor’s jealousy; the Paris episode reverses their positions by making Faraday jealous; and the ending reunites them through a mutual, though incompletely acknowledged, retreat from abstraction. Suspense depends on a benign misunderstanding: the mysterious man appears to threaten the marriage but is revealed as the painter of Eleanor’s portrait. The portrait itself is symbolically apt, since Faraday is first prepared to return home with an idealized image of his wife before Eleanor chooses to accompany him as a living, emotionally complex person.

Pacing alternates between broad summary and concentrated scenes. Months of correspondence and intellectual companionship are compressed, whereas jealousy is dramatized in detail through bodily movement, visual impressions, and interrupted conversation. The Paris street sequence slows especially effectively, filtering the city through Faraday’s souring perception: heat, shop windows, women, and art all become inadequate because his discomfort colors everything he sees. This use of free indirect shading brings the narration close to a character’s consciousness without surrendering the narrator’s ironic distance.

Overall, the style blends domestic realism with the comedy of manners and a lightly didactic fable about emotional self-knowledge. Its tone is urbane, affectionate, and skeptical. It critiques provincial convention, supports female intellectual development, and sympathizes with experiments in marital equality, yet refuses to idealize either sex. The final effect depends on poised contradiction: reason remains valuable, but love exposes the limits of pure rationalism; liberty is admirable, but its consequences are easier to endorse in theory than to bear in practice.

The story adopts the style of a nineteenth-century domestic moral tale, using a minor social dispute to illustrate a broader principle about judgment, reputation, trust, and personal character. Its tone is earnest, decorous, and openly didactic. Rather than leaving the ethical implications ambiguous, the narrative guides the reader toward a definite verdict: outwardly balanced judgments can amount to “false charity” when one party has plainly violated confidence and acted dishonestly.

The narration is omniscient and strongly editorial. The narrator speaks in the collective first person—“we heard,” “we do not think,” “we consider”—and repeatedly addresses or anticipates the reader’s judgment. General observations about human conduct frame the episode, while explicit commentary interprets characters’ motives and assigns moral weight to their actions. This creates the effect of a conversational essay joined to an illustrative anecdote. The narrator initially presents public gossip about the quarrel, then reconstructs the events in detail so that rumor can be measured against the supposedly complete truth.

Structurally, the story moves from maxim to example and finally back to maxim. It opens with a debate over the conventional belief that both sides are always partly at fault. A gossip-driven version of the disagreement follows, portraying the two women as nearly equally culpable. The narrative then reverses perspective and supplies a chronological account of the head-dress episode, revealing the asymmetry between them. The closing paragraphs deliver the moral directly. This layered arrangement creates mild suspense not through uncertainty about what happened, but through the gradual correction of social misrepresentation.

Dialogue occupies much of the story and is used both dramatically and evidentially. Exchanges are extended, orderly, and socially mannered, with characters explaining themselves at greater length than would be typical in modern realist dialogue. Their speech reveals temperament: Mrs. Tarleton’s repetitions, flattering appeals, and persistent requests convey vanity and entitlement, while Mrs. Bates’s measured refusals and carefully reasoned explanations establish restraint and principle. Gossip is reproduced through chains of conversation, showing how facts become altered as they pass “from mouth to mouth.” Brief comic touches, such as Mr. Brierly’s grumbling about dress and his dismissive “Humph!”, provide relief without disturbing the moral seriousness.

The diction is formal, evaluative, and period-specific. Words and phrases such as “due allowance,” “honorable defensive,” “indelicate pertinacity,” “ungenerous triumph,” “true delicacy of feeling,” and “violation of confidence” belong to a vocabulary of manners and moral character. Socially charged terms including “lady,” “propriety,” “esteem,” “principle,” and “honor” recur as standards by which behavior is judged. Clothing is treated not merely as decoration but as a test through which vanity, selfishness, honesty, and social refinement become visible. The prose also employs now-dated forms such as “any body,” “dress maker,” “unfrequently,” and “must have it,” along with occasional transcription or printing irregularities such as “musn’t,” “havn't,” “fnding,” and “refrered.”

Sentences are often long and balanced, built from coordinated clauses, qualifications, and contrasts. The prose favors explicit causal explanation: a character acts in a certain way “because” of a stated motive, and the narrator then clarifies the ethical meaning of that motive. Parallel constructions—especially contrasts between wounded pride and principled indignation, forgiveness and renewed intimacy, appearance and truth—give the narrative a forensic quality. Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, quoted maxims, semicolons, and em dashes or double hyphens reinforce its periodical-story manner.

Characterization is schematic but psychologically legible. Mrs. Bates is allowed a minor and excusable vanity so that she does not seem impossibly perfect, yet her defining traits are justice, delicacy, restraint, and firmness. Mrs. Tarleton is characterized through escalating breaches of decorum: importunity, misuse of borrowed property, secrecy, self-justification, and distortion of the story afterward. Supporting figures function chiefly as moral and social forces—the sister encourages wrongdoing, the milliner inadvertently exposes it, and the “mutual friends” embody careless conventional judgment.

The pacing is deliberate and repetitive. Key facts are stated first as rumor and then restaged through dialogue and narration; offers, refusals, and moral distinctions are reiterated to prevent misunderstanding. This redundancy reflects the story’s persuasive purpose. Its dominant stylistic effect is that of a social case study argued before the reader: plain in incident, expansive in explanation, confident in judgment, and designed to “hang a moral” upon the ordinary details of fashionable domestic life.

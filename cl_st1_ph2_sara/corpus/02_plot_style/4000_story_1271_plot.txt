Topham, an educated but disgraced and impoverished man, performs nearly all the work for Rudolph Starkey’s correspondence-tuition business in exchange for mere room and board. Exploited, overworked, and treated contemptuously, he remains only because he fears homelessness.

When Abraham Wigmore, a prosperous middle-aged fruiterer and one of the correspondence pupils, visits unexpectedly, Topham impersonates Starkey. Wigmore reveals that his daughters have married into a higher social class and seem ashamed of his humble occupation. Hoping to gain respectability, he wants to retire from trade, pursue an education, and eventually become a curate.

Recognizing an escape from servitude, Topham persuades Wigmore that intensive private tuition could prepare him for ordination. He accepts a position as Wigmore’s resident tutor at a generous salary, takes an advance, and leaves Starkey without warning, repaying a recently lent sixpence with ironic gratitude. He also sends Starkey a forged letter claiming that Wigmore is too ill to continue studying.

Later, Topham confesses the deception to Wigmore, who decides that Starkey deserved it. Topham diligently educates his new employer and consoles himself that his trick not only rescued him from exploitation but also protected Wigmore from a dishonest tutor and may genuinely help him enter the Church.

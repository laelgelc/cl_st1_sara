The story adopts the manner of an early children’s animal tale, combining farmyard realism, gentle comedy, natural-history instruction, and an explicit moral lesson. Its world is anthropomorphic but remains closely tied to observed animal behaviour: the chickens talk, reason, boast, disobey, and feel shame, while their feeding, roosting, growth, and vulnerability to hawks are described with practical accuracy. This creates a didactic fable in which factual knowledge and moral education reinforce one another.

The narrator is omniscient, warm, and openly intrusive. Rather than remaining invisible, the narrative voice addresses the reader directly through phrases such as “You can imagine,” “you know,” and “Just imagine,” assuming the tone of an affectionate adult storyteller speaking to children. The narrator explains unfamiliar facts, interprets behaviour, passes judgment, and guides the reader’s emotional response. Moral distinctions are clear: vanity is “foolish,” the Chicken is “naughty,” the mother is patient and devoted, and obedience grounded in experience is ultimately vindicated. This authority is softened by sympathy and humour rather than expressed as harsh condemnation.

The diction is plain, accessible, and domestic, with occasional old-fashioned or decorous phrasing such as “a sight worth seeing,” “would not waste time,” and “ever so much.” Species and farmyard terms are prominent, including “wattles,” “spurs,” “stubble,” “brood,” “gizzard,” and “roosted,” but these are either understandable from context or directly explained. Many animal names are capitalised—“Hen,” “Cock,” “Chickens,” “Hawk,” and “Grasshopper”—which treats types of creatures almost like social titles or proper names. This contributes to the formal, storybook quality of the farmyard community.

Sentence structure is generally clear and moderately expansive. The prose often uses coordinated clauses linked by “and,” producing a flowing, oral rhythm suited to reading aloud. Repetition is used for comic effect, most notably in the chain of cocks crowing because the others have crowed. Longer cumulative sentences convey bustle, as in the description of the birds fluttering, lurching, squawking, changing branches, and pushing one another before sleep. These are balanced by short, emphatic lines in dialogue, especially the Chick’s defiant “So!” and the mother’s final declaration of happiness.

Dialogue is frequent and functions as both characterisation and moral argument. The Shanghai Hen speaks anxiously but firmly; the Shanghai Cock is proud, restrained, and somewhat complacent; the young Chicken begins as petulant and self-willed, then becomes reflective and instructive. Speech is simple, direct, and lightly theatrical, allowing attitudes to be understood immediately. The Chicken’s conversion is demonstrated not merely through narration but through his advice to his brother: he repeats the lesson in his own words, showing that he has internalised it.

The plot follows a clear exemplary structure. It begins with family and social background, establishes the Shanghais’ pride in their appearance, introduces the thin Chicken as a problem, reveals his refusal to eat gravel, traces the physical and emotional consequences of his disobedience, and concludes with secret reform, confession, restored health, social acceptance, and maternal happiness. The narrative moves chronologically and uses delayed explanation effectively: the mystery of the Chicken’s thinness sustains interest until the mother identifies its cause. The ending adds a light social reward through the friendship with the Dorking Chicken, but the deeper resolution is his admission that his mother was right.

The central moral is that necessary duties may be unpleasant but cannot safely be avoided. Obedience is presented not as arbitrary submission but as trust in wiser experience; the mother eventually explains the biological reason for eating gravel, linking ethical instruction to practical cause and effect. A secondary lesson concerns humility. The Shanghais’ pride in their long, unfeathered legs establishes a pattern of valuing appearance, while the thin Chicken’s experience teaches that health, discipline, and good conduct matter more than inherited beauty. His attention to his shadow provides a child-friendly visual measure of decline and recovery.

Humour is gentle and observational. It arises from the vanity of the chickens, the solemn social hierarchy of the poultry-yard, the noisy difficulty of settling on a roost, and the absurdly circular outbreak of nighttime crowing. The animals’ concerns mirror human family and community life without losing their avian particularity. Even ridicule has a corrective narrative function: the remarks about the Chicken being a Bantam or unfit to lead a flock intensify his awareness of the consequences of his behaviour.

Emotion is handled sentimentally but with restraint. The extended account of the mother’s sacrifices—laying the egg, sitting through “weary days and nights,” sheltering the chick, teaching him, and being ready to fight for him—invites gratitude toward maternal devotion. The mother’s silent worry under the moon and stars gives the story a brief lyrical pause, while the final image of her looking lovingly after her son provides uncomplicated emotional closure. Overall, the style is genial, instructive, orderly, and reassuring: a conversational farmyard fable designed to entertain young readers while teaching natural science, obedience, perseverance, humility, and appreciation of parental care.

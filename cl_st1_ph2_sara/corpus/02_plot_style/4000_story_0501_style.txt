The story adopts the manner of a late-Victorian or Edwardian popular tale, combining commercial realism, social satire, frontier adventure and macabre poetic justice. Its narrator is omniscient, urbane and conspicuously intrusive, beginning with broad reflections on advertising, business and human nature before narrowing the focus to Danby and Strong. Frequent appeals to the reader—“when you come to ponder,” “we are apt,” “can any one imagine”—create the tone of a conversational essay or magazine sketch. The voice is confident, worldly and dryly amused, treating both English respectability and English misconceptions about Texas with sustained irony.

The diction is formal but accessible, with period-specific commercial and legal vocabulary such as “hoardings,” “testimonials,” “mandamuses,” “writ of ejectment” and “staying the proceedings.” This is offset by colloquial expressions—“hit it off,” “go out to pot some favourite enemy”—and by Americanisms in the Texas passages, including “dry goods,” “State agents,” “Colts” and “six-shooter.” The contrast between British and American usage supplies humour and reinforces the story’s transatlantic setting. Descriptions are generally concrete and economical, becoming more vivid and sombre during the murder: the bloodless lips, shattered knee, mutilated face and “short, sharp, and echoless” pistol report give the climax a stark physical immediacy.

Sentence structure is predominantly long, balanced and discursive. Clauses accumulate through qualification, parallelism and rhetorical repetition, often allowing the narrator to elaborate an observation before delivering an ironic conclusion. Parenthetical comments and digressions are characteristic, as are aphoristic generalisations such as “The law, however, is a great restrainer.” Short sentences are reserved for emphasis and narrative turns: “I am going to murder you”; “Danby was not yet dead”; and the final line, “The firm of Danby and Strong was dissolved.” That closing sentence compresses death into the language of partnership law, producing a cold commercial pun.

The structure moves from the general to the particular. An essayistic opening about famous brand names introduces the paradox that impersonal commercial identities belong to passionate, fallible men. The narrative then supplies the history of the collar business, the partners’ quarrel and bankruptcy, Strong’s emigration, his accidental discovery that Danby is trading under their old name, and his planned revenge. Advertising details that initially seem digressive ultimately function as plot machinery, since the survival of the brand brings the enemies together. The ending is tightly foreshadowed by the narrator’s reflections on concealed savagery, the restraining force of law, and the danger of discussing murderous intentions.

Pacing is leisurely at first, with substantial exposition and comic commentary, but accelerates once Strong learns Danby’s route. The prairie confrontation unfolds in a precise sequence of commands, movements and shots. After the murder, the pace slows again while Strong constructs a false tableau, creating suspense through meticulous physical detail. The reversal depends on his own ingenuity: by placing a cocked revolver in the apparently dead Danby’s hand, he creates the means of his destruction. This gives the conclusion the shape of an ironic morality tale.

Characterisation is economical and contrastive. Strong is openly bitter, violent and inventive; Danby is quieter, stubborn and initially presented as the less aggressive partner. Neither is psychologically explored at great depth, because each chiefly embodies a type within the story’s moral design. Strong’s transition from London businessman to armed cowboy externalises the savagery the narrator claims already exists beneath civilised dress. Danby’s fear and physical helplessness briefly make him sympathetic, though his final glare reveals hatred equal to Strong’s. Their mutual animosity survives bankruptcy, distance and apparent death.

Dialogue is direct, functional and differentiated by culture. The dry-goods merchant’s good-natured speech provides exposition while puncturing romantic English ideas about Texas. Strong’s language during the confrontation is theatrical and vindictive, especially when he recasts murder in legal metaphors. The dialogue becomes extremely spare at the climax, allowing the blunt exchange about murder to carry maximum force.

The dominant stylistic effects are irony, foreshadowing, rhetorical exaggeration, incongruity and grim humour. Respectable commerce is repeatedly paired with primitive violence; legal restraint with murderous desire; advertising language with personal hatred; and artistic creation with the staging of a corpse. The narrator’s comic detachment persists almost to the end, but the prairie stillness introduces a brief note of metaphysical horror. Overall, the style is polished, sardonic and plot-driven, using leisurely authorial commentary to prepare a compact revenge narrative whose final reversal is both brutal and neatly epigrammatic.

The story adopts the manner of an erudite intellectual satire, combining the club-room anecdote, the mock-scholarly essay and the fantastic cautionary tale. It is narrated retrospectively in the first person by an educated, socially assured observer whose chief function is to introduce and interpret the more eccentric Emberlin. The narrator is not neutral: he is amused, indulgent, cultivated and increasingly appalled. His frequent qualifications—“I confess,” “I rather believe,” “I have no doubt”—create the impression of spontaneous personal recollection while also allowing irony to operate at a slight distance. He initially admires Emberlin’s verbal magic and accommodates his childish vanity, but the closing passages replace affectionate mockery with disgust and moral alarm.

The prose is ornate, allusive and self-consciously literary. Its diction blends conversational English with Latinate abstractions, archaic turns and specialist vocabulary: “post-prandial,” “thaumaturge,” “unencyclopædic,” “Fons Deitatis,” “quincunxes” and “philarithmic.” References to Mallarmé, Meredith, Ben Jonson, Petronius, Giordano Bruno, Raymond Lully and Neoplatonism construct a dense atmosphere of cultivated learning. This erudition is both genuine and comic. Scholarship becomes a field of social play, obscure names are treated as common knowledge, and invented or marginal authorities are presented with solemn confidence. The exchange concerning “not the familiar Zuylerius” is characteristic: the narrator’s flippant assent is mistaken for learned agreement, exposing Emberlin’s pedantry without directly puncturing it.

Sentences are often long, balanced and elaborately subordinated. Parentheses, dashes, semicolons and qualifying clauses reproduce the rhythms of polished conversation and discursive thought. The narrator readily digresses from the immediate story into character portraiture, aesthetic reflection and comic speculation, then draws attention to the digression with phrases such as “But let that pass; I am straying from my purpose.” Such self-corrections make the prose seem leisurely and improvisatory while keeping it carefully controlled. Periodic constructions and rhetorical parallelism lend weight to absurd claims: counting is elevated into metaphysics, and an eccentric artistic movement is described in the language of philosophical history. Shorter sentences are reserved for comic emphasis or narrative turns, as when Emberlin’s fate is summarized starkly after the long embedded account.

Dialogue is highly stylized rather than naturalistic. Emberlin’s speech is fluent, expansive and lecture-like, filled with parenthetical distinctions, bibliographical explanations and speculative reconstruction. The narrator’s shorter questions and ironic prompts serve as cues for Emberlin’s monologues. Repetition supplies several comic motifs, especially “A discovery about Discoveries” and “Eupompus gave splendour to Art by Numbers.” The repeated sentence changes from an intriguing scholarly puzzle into an anthem, an obsession and finally the formula of a destructive creed. Verbal comedy also comes from disproportion: trivial information is treated with metaphysical solemnity, while madness and murder are discussed as unfortunate complications in an aesthetic history.

The structure is framed and recursive. It begins with Emberlin announcing his discovery, pauses for an extended portrait of him, returns to the conversation, and then contains a second story—the history of Eupompus—within Emberlin’s account. After that inset narrative reaches its grotesque conclusion, the frame advances four months and reveals that Emberlin has begun to repeat Eupompus’s pathology. The historical anecdote therefore functions as both exposition and prophecy. This architecture gives the story the shape of a mock case study: an obscure textual phrase produces research, research uncovers a forgotten doctrine, and the doctrine infects its discoverer. The compressed final section converts speculative comedy into a narrative of mental deterioration.

The pacing is deliberately uneven. The opening lingers over Emberlin’s personality, rooms, conversation and unpublished poetry, establishing a world in which ideas are more vivid than ordinary life. The central section slows further for detailed descriptions of imaginary paintings: thirty-three thousand black swans, geometrically arranged orchards, constellation-like crowds and a Cyclopean amphitheatre. These catalogues mimic antiquarian documentation while generating surreal visual force. The conclusion accelerates through examples of Emberlin’s new compulsions—counting tiles, steps and bodily rates—so that the grand metaphysics of Number collapses into repetitive, banal behaviour.

Tonally, the story moves from urbane amusement through whimsical fantasy to grotesque unease and finally caustic denunciation. Early images idealize intellectual conversation as intoxication, magic and a “crystal Eden.” Later images describe obsession as “mental leprosy” and reduce its sufferers to beasts, children and idiots. The narrator’s severity is itself darkly comic, particularly in his retrospective defence of Eupompus’s suicide and murders as a sane revolt against Eupompianism. This closing reversal depends on paradox: madness is presented as complacent system-building, while the flash of sanity manifests itself through violence and self-destruction.

A central stylistic device is mock elevation. Counting, one of the simplest mechanical operations, is transformed into an aesthetic and theological principle. The invented movement acquires a founder, disciples, terminology, masterpieces and a philosophy of reality. Capitalization—“Discoveries,” “Number,” “Pure Number,” “Art”—parodies the language by which abstractions are endowed with authority. Conversely, lofty speculation is repeatedly deflated by bath-time chanting, bathroom tiles, fingernail statistics and public lavatories. The oscillation between metaphysical grandeur and physical triviality generates much of the humour.

The imagery opposes two kinds of order. Emberlin’s early conversation creates a lucid, harmonious universe of ideas above the “hotch-potch” of ordinary existence; Eupompianism turns that desire for order into sterile enumeration. The imaginary paintings embody this corruption visually: identical forms, rigid groupings and numerical progressions replace human variety. Especially powerful is the amphitheatre in which thousands of one-eyed figures scrutinize the lone two-eyed creature. Its symmetry is menacing rather than beautiful, suggesting conformity, abstraction and collective fanaticism. The repeated emphasis on hard edges, planes, rows and exact quantities reinforces a movement from living complexity toward mechanical system.

The story’s humour is dry, hyperbolic and deadpan. Absurdities are not announced as jokes but embedded in precise, authoritative prose. Eupompus’s enormous fees, the reconstructed artistic movement and the elder Zuylerius’s “Dutch Latin” are narrated with pseudo-historical exactitude. Understatement sharpens the grotesquer moments: the murder of two disciples is folded into a summary of an unfinished artwork, and Eupompus’s splendour is described as “regrettably transient.” The narrator’s cultivated prejudices also contribute to the comic voice; his horror is directed less at danger than at boredom, vulgarity, bad conversation and the degradation of intelligence.

The thematic style is anti-systematic despite its scholarly surface. It distrusts ideas that claim to reduce experience to a single principle, particularly when aesthetic, religious and intellectual values are subordinated to quantification. It also satirizes the scholar’s susceptibility to the objects of his study: Emberlin does not merely interpret doctrines but compulsively reenacts them. Art, language and learning appear powerful enough to create alternative worlds, yet that power is morally ambiguous. Verbal imagination can liberate the mind from quotidian dullness, but an abstract formula can also colonize it. The result is a polished, densely allusive and fantastically comic style in which intellectual play gradually reveals the pathology concealed inside the desire for perfect order.

The story uses an early twentieth-century literary-realist style enriched by sentiment, moral commentary, travel writing, and wartime polemic. Its register is formal and cultivated but broadly accessible, combining documentary observation with the emotional architecture of a humanitarian tale. The refugee settlement is described as a real, administratively organized place, yet it is also elevated into the symbolic “City of Refuge,” a physical embodiment of Dutch compassion and of social order restored after catastrophe.

The narrative voice is omniscient, authoritative, and openly evaluative. It does not remain detached from events but directs the reader’s moral and emotional response through exclamations, rhetorical questions, and imperatives such as “think of” and “Poor, wretched creatures!” The narrator speaks with confidence about national character, human nature, and the justice of the war, often shifting from concrete observation to broad reflection. Second-person narration—“You visit,” “Then you go through,” “You read the names”—places the reader in the role of an escorted visitor and gives the middle portion the quality of a guided tour or eyewitness report. The occasional collective first person, as in “The rest we will leave,” creates intimacy and allows the narrator to withdraw tactfully at the sentimental climax.

The diction alternates between practical institutional language and emotionally charged, figurative language. Terms such as “capacity,” “barracks,” “hospitals,” “storerooms,” and “military organization” establish documentary credibility, while descriptions of the refugees’ “bleeding, ravaged land,” “cold and ruined hearthstones,” and “desolate and broken hearts” intensify the pathos. Dutch and Flemish expressions—“niet waar,” “kinderen,” “Ja, Men’eer,” “Lieve moeder”—supply local colour and a mild cosmopolitan texture. Italics emphasize foreign words, names, and emotionally significant phrases. The anti-German rhetoric is direct and propagandistic: German militarism is personified as a brutal, predatory force, while Holland is maternal, peaceful, disciplined, and humane.

Syntax is generally expansive and rhythmic. Long cumulative sentences gather places, people, or institutional details into catalogues, often using semicolons and repeated grammatical structures. The sequence beginning with farmhouses, city houses, and country mansions, for example, conveys the breadth of the Dutch response through accumulation. Dashes frequently introduce amplifications, qualifications, or sudden emotional turns. These long descriptive periods are balanced by short emphatic statements—“All doors were open,” “Something had to be done,” “Babies!”—which sharpen transitions and dramatize reactions. Repetition is used both rhetorically and comically, especially in the commandant’s outraged recurrence to the word “Babies.”

Figurative language is prominent and usually transparent rather than obscure. Holland is personified as a protective mother making “a lap for tired and frightened children.” Hospitality becomes a stream fed by a “savings-bank reservoir,” empty greenhouses are adapted to “human horticulture,” and the temporary wooden settlement is a “mushroom city.” The image of roots, branches, foliage, and blossoms extends the horticultural motif into a reflection on exposed human nature. Contrasts organize many of these figures: white magic opposes the evil of militarism, maternal shelter opposes violent displacement, and rooted national identity opposes forced exile.

Description proceeds from panoramic history to geographical setting, then to buildings, institutions, and individuals. This narrowing movement gives the story a funnel-shaped structure. It begins with hundreds of thousands of refugees and the collective response of a nation, focuses on one camp and its commandant, then concentrates on one troubled mother and two lost children. The opening sections therefore resemble an essay or report more than a conventional short story. The central narrative does not emerge until relatively late, when the chief nurse discusses Mrs. Van Orley and introduces the unidentified children. Once those strands are established, the plot moves quickly toward recognition and reunion.

Characterization relies on concise physical portraits, dominant traits, and revealing contradictions. The commandant is “portly” and “rosy,” with a military bearing and a ruling belief in discipline, but his martinet manner conceals tenderness. His emotional transformation from thunderous administrator to a man cooing over children supplies much of the humour. The description of him changing “from haircloth into silk” captures the story’s fondness for immediately legible moral contrasts. The chief nurse is calm, tactful, compassionate, and quietly effective; she guides the commandant without openly challenging his authority. Mrs. Van Orley is initially seen through communal suspicion as morose or mad, but the story reinterprets her behaviour as the consequence of grief. The children are characterized through small gestures, limited memories, and childlike speech rather than psychological explanation.

Dialogue is brisk, theatrical, and strongly individualized. The commandant speaks in commands, questions, interruptions, and explosive repetitions; the nurse answers with composed understatement and discreet humour. Their exchanges create a light comic rhythm that temporarily relieves the surrounding tragedy. Dialect spellings such as “ickle angel” and “Where was you?” render the children’s speech as innocent and emotionally immediate. The recognition scene shifts abruptly from comedy to melodrama through cries, broken syntax, repeated possessives—“mine”—physical movement, and the mother’s fainting. This is deliberately staged for maximum emotional release.

The tone blends compassion, indignation, affectionate humour, and sentimentality. Much of the story is earnest and morally didactic, but the commandant’s bluster, the sewing-room teasing, and the children’s interaction with the watch soften the institutional setting. The humour is never cynical; it reveals kindness beneath rigid appearances. The final reunion is openly sentimental, yet it has been prepared by the earlier account of Mrs. Van Orley’s supposed delusions, so the coincidence functions as both plot resolution and moral vindication. What the community interprets as madness proves to be faithful maternal memory.

The dominant themes are refuge, displacement, national identity, maternal love, and the reconciliation of compassion with discipline. Holland’s hospitality is praised, but spontaneous charity is shown as insufficient without organization. Military order is thus paradoxically reclaimed for humane purposes and contrasted with militarism used for conquest. The repeated naming of camp streets after Belgian places shows that homeland survives inwardly even when its people are physically uprooted. The story also argues against hasty social judgment: disorder, melancholy, and apparent irrationality may conceal wounds that institutions fail to understand.

Overall, the style is panoramic yet personal, morally explicit rather than ambiguous, and structured to transform public history into domestic emotion. Documentary detail gives authority to the setting; personification and patriotic rhetoric enlarge its symbolic meaning; comic dialogue humanizes authority; and the final recognition scene condenses the suffering of an exiled nation into the recovered bond between a mother and her children.

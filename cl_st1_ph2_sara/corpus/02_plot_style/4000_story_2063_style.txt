The story adopts the manner of a late-Victorian or Edwardian comic anecdote: conversational, leisurely, urbane, and gently satirical. Its principal mode is oral storytelling. An unnamed first-person listener records a tale told by Henry, who in turn recounts events he witnessed as a waiter in Paris. This layered narration creates the impression of a story being improvised in company rather than formally composed. Henry repeatedly pauses to qualify details, defend his judgements, answer interruptions, and digress into general observations about courtship, marriage, waitresses, sailors, and human vanity. Such asides give the narrative a genial, rambling rhythm while also making Henry’s personality—worldly, opinionated, observant, and faintly self-important—central to the style.

The diction blends standard literary prose with colloquial British speech. Henry uses contractions, dropped initial syllables, idioms, and popular expressions such as “leastways,” “’em,” “chap,” “scamp,” “forrarder,” “a bit,” and “panning out.” His formulations often resemble pub wisdom or comic proverbs: “A man’s a man,” “A sailor-man…makes love with his hour of departure always before his mind,” and “There’s all the makings of a good husband in him.” The language is accessible but historically marked, with references to clothing, cafés, social class, and contemporary fashions grounding it in its period. French phrases and place names add cosmopolitan colour without displacing the strongly English narrative voice.

The syntax is expansive and speech-like. Long sentences accumulate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, dashes, semicolons, and afterthoughts, reproducing the movement of a speaker remembering and revising as he talks. Henry frequently inserts phrases such as “mind you,” “as I suppose,” “when you come to think of it,” and “if you understand,” which establish intimacy with the listener. These are balanced by short, dry responses—“Not quite all,” “Maybe,” “It’s a bit complicated”—that deliver comic emphasis. Repetition is also important: words and sentence patterns recur in slightly altered forms, particularly in the exchanges between Henry and Marie, creating a controlled conversational rhythm.

Dialogue dominates the story and performs most of the characterisation. The framing listener asks practical questions, challenges inconsistencies, and prompts missing information, allowing Henry to clarify the plot while sustaining the illusion of spontaneous narration. Within the embedded story, dialogue becomes a contest of intelligence and perspective. Marie speaks sharply and economically, Tom’s sentiments are reported as earnest but elaborate, and Henry responds with deadpan common sense. Characters are distinguished less by descriptive exposition than by what they say, what they fail to understand, and how they interpret one another’s motives.

The humour is predominantly ironic, observational, and understated. Henry presents broad claims about men and women as if they were self-evident laws, then illustrates them through behaviour that complicates those claims. Comic effects arise from reversals, literal-minded remarks, euphemistic understatement, and the gap between a character’s intentions and the audience’s knowledge. Tom falls in love with his own wife without recognising her; Marie tests his fidelity by encouraging that love; and his attempt to become worthy of one woman sends him in search of the same woman under another name. The narrator rarely announces a joke. Instead, he places an absurd circumstance beside a sober comment, as when the youthful marriage is treated as administratively “quite legal,” or Tom’s extravagant farewell gifts are explained with “I take it he felt like that.”

The tone remains affectionate even when satirical. Tom is portrayed as foolish, impulsive, vain, and morally negligent, but not malicious. Marie is clever, flirtatious, wounded, and manipulative, yet her conduct is rooted in a serious desire to discover whether her husband possesses honour. Henry’s judgements similarly combine cynicism with sympathy. He makes comic generalisations about women and courtship, but recognises Marie’s pain and increasing misery beneath her performance. This mixture of warmth and scepticism prevents the story from becoming either a sentimental romance or a harsh moral satire.

Structurally, the narrative begins with a general proposition—that competition creates desire—and then turns the plot into an extended demonstration of it. The exposition is delivered retrospectively and incrementally, with interruptions delaying key facts. Once Tom enters the café, the story develops through repeated visits, escalating jealousy, Marie’s test, his departure, and a chain of letters that gradually brings him back to Paris. The correspondence compresses time while preserving suspense. The final scene concentrates the central irony into two objects, the opal brooch belonging to Marie’s assumed identity and the onyx brooch belonging to her identity as Mary Sleight.

The ending is deliberately suspended at the point of revelation. Henry’s advice that Marie wear only the onyx brooch implies the imminent recognition and reconciliation but does not narrate them. This restraint produces both emotional poignancy and comic neatness: the identifying token will tell the story more effectively than an explanation could. It also recalls the opening suggestion that there is a difference between “marrying and being married,” leaving the future of the relationship technically unresolved. Overall, the style combines anecdotal realism, theatrical dialogue, comic paradox, social observation, and a carefully delayed romantic revelation beneath an appearance of casual talk.

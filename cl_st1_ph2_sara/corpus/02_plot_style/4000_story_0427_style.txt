The story adopts an archaizing historical-romance style, evoking a Renaissance court through references to King Charles VIII, Amboise, noble ladies, artisans, courtly entertainments, costume, and ecclesiastical advancement. Its atmosphere combines ornamental pageantry with fabliau-like sexual intrigue. Elevated ideas of beauty, honour, artistic genius, and “holy” love are repeatedly placed beside poverty, lust, jealousy, deception, and violence, creating a tone that is at once romantic, bawdy, melodramatic, and satirical.

The narrator is conspicuously present and highly opinionated. Rather than recounting events neutrally, he addresses the reader indirectly, makes generalizations about women and lovers, offers moral and social commentary, corrects himself—“I am wrong”—and evaluates disputed versions of the ending. Phrases such as “as everyone knows,” “You may be sure,” and “This is the reason why” imitate oral storytelling and give the narrative the air of a worldly anecdote related to an amused audience. The professed truthfulness of the tale is playful rather than documentary, especially in the closing claim that it is “in every way true.” The final anti-moral—“This teaches us nothing worth knowing”—parodies the conventional exemplary conclusion.

The narrative voice is omniscient and moves freely between outward action and Angelo’s private fantasies. Characterization is broad, energetic, and psychologically heightened rather than subtle. Angelo is presented through a series of contradictions: gifted but impoverished, handsome but insecure, eloquent in imagination but almost mute before women, bold in solitude but timid in company, idealistic in love yet capable of calculated brutality. The lady is characterized less as an individualized consciousness than as a type: the beautiful, socially powerful court coquette who sustains desire through postponement. The narrator’s account of her is coloured by a strongly masculine and cynical view of courtship, frequently turning individual behaviour into sweeping claims about women in general.

The diction mixes courtly elevation with earthy sensuality. Formal or archaic expressions—“thereof,” “whence,” “thou,” “thy,” “lucre,” “virile majesty,” and “high-born ladies”—create the impression of an old chronicle or translated Renaissance tale. These coexist with colloquial formulations such as “did not matter a straw,” “over head and ears in love,” and “jump about outside the hedge.” The mixture allows the prose to move abruptly from lyrical idealization to comic bluntness. Erotic content is conveyed through teasing euphemism: “the garden of love,” “the skirmish of the skirt,” “that especial thing,” and “the rest.” This indirectness makes the sexuality conspicuous while preserving the mock decorum of the historical register.

Figurative language is abundant, sensuous, and often extravagant. Angelo is “in the April of his life”; his melancholy resembles that of “a dove left solitary in its nest”; his heart leaps “like a goat”; an imagined lover is compared to a prisoner glimpsing green fields through the bars of his cell. Desire is repeatedly described through heat, hunger, warfare, cultivation, and commerce. Love is a fire, a game with life as its stake, a military campaign, a garden behind a hedge, and a substance rationed like money. These recurring metaphors reinforce the tension between Angelo’s emotional excess and the lady’s calculated withholding.

The prose favours long, cumulative sentences filled with subordinate clauses, parenthetical observations, lists, and rhetorical expansions. A simple point is often developed through several parallel phrases, as when Angelo imagines how he would worship, honour, serve, obey, and entertain a mistress. Such accumulations give the narration a breathless, overflowing quality suited to his obsessive desire. Short sentences and exclamations are reserved for comic or dramatic impact, particularly in dialogue: “My husband!”, “Silence, sir!”, and “You wretch!” The contrast between elaborate narration and abrupt speech sharpens both the farce of the interrupted encounters and the violence of the climax.

The handling of tense is somewhat unstable, shifting at moments from past narration into the historical present—“Master Angelo perfumes himself,” “The Florentine has stolen”—which lends immediacy but also contributes to the feel of an older or translated text. There are occasional awkward constructions and grammatical irregularities, including unusual prepositions, tangled clauses, and minor errors such as “Your have made my life.” These features suggest prose mediated through an antiquated translation rather than modern idiomatic English.

Dialogue is stylized and theatrical. Early exchanges emphasize Angelo’s paralysis through his restricted answers of “Yes” and “No,” while later conversations become contests over power, proof, and possession. The lady’s language is imperious and evasive; Angelo’s becomes increasingly absolutist, moving from petitions for love to demands that words be validated by deeds. At the climax, his speech expands into a formal accusation full of antithesis—heaven and hell, face and lover, word and deed—before the narrative contracts into sudden physical action.

The pacing alternates between expansive summary and sharply staged scenes. Angelo’s poverty, fantasies, artistic labour, and month of frustrated visits are compressed into broad narrative passages, while the first private meeting, the interrupted seduction, the plot against the husband, and the disfigurement receive closer dramatic treatment. Repetition is central to the structure: desire rises, the husband arrives, fulfilment is deferred, and hope is revived through minor caresses. This cyclical postponement generates comic frustration before being broken by the violent final encounter.

Irony governs the story’s treatment of art and love. Angelo’s erotic frustration improves the sensual intensity of his sculpture but also distracts him enough to spoil noses. His expertise in the human form does not give him social confidence, and his ideal of perfect devotion turns into possessiveness and revenge. The lady finally appears sincere only when threatened, but Angelo interprets her submission as proof that she values beauty above love. Even the ending preserves contradiction: he becomes a cardinal and scholar while continuing to live emotionally on memories of adulterous desire. The overall style therefore combines lush romantic rhetoric with cynical narrative commentary, using exaggeration, aphorism, euphemism, and grotesque reversal to expose the unstable boundary between courtly love, appetite, vanity, and cruelty.

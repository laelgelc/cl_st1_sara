The story adopts the style of a traditional literary fairy tale or moral fable, combining courtly fantasy with explicit social instruction. Its setting is an idealized, vaguely medieval monarchy of thrones, heralds, coffers, tourneys, velvet garments, and royal proclamations. The opening nursery rhyme establishes a folkloric frame and introduces the central motif of beggars in “rags,” “tags,” and “velvet gown,” categories that the subsequent plot literalizes. The tale then proceeds in a clear, linear sequence: the compassionate prince identifies an apparent injustice, obtains temporary power to remedy it, encounters practical complications, and emerges with a revised moral understanding.

The narrative voice is third-person omniscient, confident, and openly evaluative. It moves freely between outward action, characters’ thoughts, and broad claims about human behavior. The narrator labels figures as tender-hearted, wise, lazy, greedy, deserving, or fraudulent rather than leaving such judgments entirely to the reader. Occasional direct addresses and proverbial observations—especially in the closing paragraphs—give the voice the quality of an adult storyteller instructing a younger audience. The ending makes the lesson explicit, interpreting the events and appealing to the maxim that it is “better to give than to receive.”

The diction is formal, courtly, and deliberately archaic. Expressions such as “methinks,” “of a surety,” “sire,” “hath,” “whatsoever,” “succor,” “alms,” and “so be it” create an old-world atmosphere, while titles and honorifics are repeatedly capitalized to emphasize hierarchy and ceremony. Dialogue is highly polished and rhetorical even when spoken by a twelve-year-old. Characters address one another through elaborate formulas—“Your Majesty,” “oh sire,” and “my King”—and often express arguments in balanced, carefully reasoned sentences. This makes conversation serve less as naturalistic speech than as a vehicle for debating charity, taxation, authority, and social responsibility.

The prose alternates between extended descriptive or explanatory passages and long scenes of dialogue. Sentences are often compound or complex, joined by semicolons and coordinating conjunctions, with periodic inversions such as “Very fair and sweet was little Prince Lilimond” and “Then did the Prince command.” Repetition supplies emphasis and a storybook rhythm: the King repeatedly cries “Nonsense!”, beggars arrive “singly and by twos and threes,” and the crowd is evoked through the “tramp, tramp” of feet and the barking of dogs. Formulaic transitions—“Then said,” “Now this Borland,” “And so,” and “Thus ended”—reinforce the oral-tale quality and keep the narrative easy to follow.

Characterization is broad and functional. Lilimond is initially defined by beauty, courtesy, compassion, and youthful idealism; the King by affection, complacency, and attachment to courtly pleasures; Borland by kindness without worldly judgment; and the royal officials by administrative caution. Characters chiefly embody positions in a moral argument rather than possessing deep psychological complexity. Physical details are symbolic and readily legible: rags, infirmity, velvet, rings, and silver buckles become signs by which need or deception is judged. The story’s central contrast is therefore between appearance and reality, particularly between being a beggar and being genuinely destitute.

The tone is genial, decorous, and didactic, with mild humor and irony. The prince’s noble plan appears destined for heroic success, but the crowd includes wealthy opportunists and able-bodied idlers, overturning his assumptions. The irony is gentle rather than cruel: Lilimond’s mistake arises from excessive compassion, and his brief reign functions as an educational trial. Suspense is modest and depends on whether the treasury will be emptied and whether the inexperienced child can distinguish legitimate need from fraud.

Structurally, the tale relies on symmetry and reversal. Lilimond begins by distrusting the King’s councilors and believing that widespread begging proves widespread starvation; after exercising power himself, he accepts the councilors’ wisdom and restores the crown before sunset. The one-day reign acts as a contained moral experiment. Concrete recurring images—the crown, scepter, treasury, gold pieces, clothing, and barking dogs—bind the political lesson to recognizable fairy-tale objects.

The governing style is overtly moralistic and socially conservative. It praises compassion and aid for the genuinely needy but distrusts indiscriminate charity, presenting many claimants as lazy or fraudulent and ultimately affirming the existing monarchy as prosperous and just. Rather than exposing structural injustice, the conclusion narrows the problem to discerning individual worthiness. The resulting profile is that of an accessible children’s court tale: ornamental but lucid, dialogue-driven, proverb-like in its reasoning, gently ironic in development, and explicit in its final moral.

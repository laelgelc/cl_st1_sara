The story adopts the manner of a compact nineteenth-century realist tale, combining ethnographic description, anecdotal narration and the severe logic of tragedy. Its opening is expansive and documentary: the narrator maps the Corsican landscape, explains the formation of the maquis and describes local customs with the authority of a traveller or informed observer. Direct addresses such as “If you have killed a man” and “Picture to yourself” draw the reader into this unfamiliar society while establishing the maquis as both a physical refuge and a moral world beyond ordinary law. Parenthetical explanations, geographical details, measurements and references to weapons, clothing, food and kinship give the setting an air of factual authenticity.

The narrative voice is predominantly omniscient but retains a conspicuous personal presence. The narrator briefly invokes his own visit to Corsica and presents some information as hearsay, using phrases such as “I have been told” and “rumor had it.” This mixture of eyewitness testimony, local legend and detached authority makes Mateo appear both historically credible and larger than life. The narrator’s tone is controlled, worldly and occasionally ironic. Customs that might shock an outsider are reported with dry understatement: homicide and knifing are treated as “some such trifling matter,” while male privilege is encapsulated in the observation that it is “unbecoming for a man to carry any burden but his weapons.” Such comments expose the values of the culture without overtly interrupting the story to condemn them.

The prose alternates between long, carefully subordinated descriptive sentences and short, forceful dramatic statements. Early sentences accumulate clauses, qualifications and parenthetical remarks, reproducing the winding terrain and patiently constructing social context. Once Gianetto arrives, the syntax becomes leaner and the action proceeds through rapid exchanges of dialogue. At moments of crisis, terse clauses and isolated commands dominate: “Out of my sight!”, “Say your prayers!” and “Justice.” This contraction from elaborate exposition to verbal starkness intensifies the ending. Dashes, semicolons, colons and parentheses are used frequently to insert explanations, sharpen reversals or register sudden shifts in thought.

The diction is lucid, concrete and largely formal, with specialized local terms such as “maquis,” “carchera,” “stiletto,” “voltigeurs” and “caporal” preserving the Corsican atmosphere. Physical descriptions rely on exact, memorable details: Mateo’s aquiline nose, bright eyes and dark curls; Fortunato’s heaving chest; the pale-blue dial of the watch; Gianetto emerging from the hay “drenched in blood.” Similes are vivid and often drawn from animals, hunting or rural life. Fortunato is agile “like a fawn,” Mateo stares “like a wildcat,” and the tempted child watches the silver watch like a cat confronted with a chicken. These comparisons reinforce a world governed by instinct, predation, vigilance and survival.

Characterization is economical and behavioral rather than psychologically expansive. Mateo is established through reputation, marksmanship, posture and action; his sparse speech conveys absolute authority. Fortunato is revealed through composure, ingenuity, verbal evasiveness and susceptibility to material temptation. The story does briefly name his internal conflict—greed struggling against traditional solidarity—but generally allows gestures to carry psychological meaning: his fingertips touch the watch, his thumb points towards the hay, and later his downcast eyes acknowledge his shame. Gianetto’s pride appears in his contemptuous denunciation and refusal to accept a drink from the betrayer’s household. Giuseppa’s role is more constrained, though her obedience, prayer and desperate maternal intervention dramatize the collision between familial affection and patriarchal law.

Dialogue is central to the story’s dramatic method. It is sharp, adversarial and full of repetition, evasion and bargaining. Fortunato repeats Gamba’s questions to delay him, invokes his father’s name as a shield and counters threats with mockery. The adjutant shifts progressively from interrogation to intimidation, cajolery and bribery. This verbal contest turns the concealment episode into a scene of escalating temptation. Later, Mateo’s repeated questions and commands strip language of playfulness; words become judgments that permit no negotiation. Repetition therefore changes function across the story, moving from Fortunato’s clever resistance to Mateo’s ritualized authority.

The pacing is deliberately asymmetrical. Considerable space is devoted to the landscape, Mateo’s reputation and the prolonged temptation by the watch, whereas the killing itself is narrated with brutal speed. Suspense grows through staged arrivals: first the wounded outlaw, then the soldiers, then Mateo and Giuseppa. The watch scene slows almost to a tableau, lingering on its chain, weight, polished case and movement against Fortunato’s face. By contrast, the final shot receives no sentimental elaboration: Fortunato falls “like a rock—dead.” This abruptness denies emotional release and makes the punishment feel inexorable.

The plot is tightly constructed around parallel acts of exchange and betrayal. Fortunato first hides Gianetto for a five-franc piece, then betrays him for a more desirable watch, and finally tries to return the original coin as though one transaction might cancel another. Objects carry strong symbolic weight. The maquis represents outlaw freedom and communal solidarity; the haystack becomes a fragile sanctuary; the watch embodies status, vanity and corrupting modern luxury; Mateo’s rifle represents both masculine identity and the enforcement of customary justice. His destruction of the watch symbolically rejects the bribe, but his subsequent destruction of the child extends that rejection into horrifying literal punishment.

The dominant tone moves from informative curiosity through suspense and sardonic comedy to ritual solemnity and horror. Early irony and Fortunato’s mischievous cleverness make the later severity more disturbing. The conclusion is presented almost as a religious ceremony: Mateo selects the ground, orders prayers, answers “Amen,” invokes divine forgiveness and promises a mass. Christian observance coexists uneasily with an archaic honour code, and the father frames private execution as “Justice.” The narration does not explicitly settle whether Mateo’s act is righteous or monstrous. Instead, its restrained presentation creates moral ambiguity while showing the terrible consistency of a culture in which betrayal stains an entire household.

Overall, the style is detached, economical and dramatically concentrated. Local colour and explanatory narration establish a coherent social code; vivid material details make temptation and danger palpable; tightly controlled dialogue exposes conflicts of loyalty; and the unsentimental ending converts a regional anecdote into a tragedy of honour, authority and irreversible choice.

The story is written as a didactic political allegory in the form of a biblical parable. Its central conceit turns water into a figure for essential goods and the tank called “the Market” into a simplified model of capitalist production and exchange. Capitalists, workers, economists, clergy, soldiers, and agitators appear not as psychologically complex individuals but as representative social types. This abstraction makes the economic argument easy to follow and gives the narrative the force of a moral fable.

The narrative voice is omniscient, authoritative, and openly partisan. It recounts events with the solemn assurance of scripture while repeatedly directing the reader toward a clear judgment: the people are exploited, capitalist profit produces artificial scarcity, and collective ownership offers deliverance. Character descriptions are evaluative rather than neutral, as in “dull of understanding,” “false priests,” “pestilent fellows,” and “true prophets of God.” Irony frequently creates a second layer beneath the narrator’s grave manner. Statements such as “Business is business,” “We must advertise,” and “The crisis is ended” sound absurd or cruel when placed within the life-and-death economy of water.

The diction is deliberately archaic and scriptural. It relies on words and constructions such as “ye,” “thee,” “whereof,” “howbeit,” “behold,” “unto,” “thereof,” “wherewithal,” “spake,” “hath,” “doth,” and “it came to pass.” Formulaic biblical transitions—“And after many days,” “Now,” “Therefore,” and “And it was so”—organise the action. The language closely imitates the cadence of the King James Bible, lending sacred gravity to an economic satire and allowing modern commercial terms such as “capitalists,” “Market,” “profit,” “advertise,” “overproduction,” and “lack of confidence” to appear comically incongruous.

The syntax is predominantly cumulative and paratactic. Long sentences join clauses with repeated uses of “and,” “but,” “for,” and “therefore,” producing the rhythm of oral scripture rather than modern compressed prose. Parallel and inverted constructions are common: the people cannot buy because they are not hired, while the capitalists will not hire because the people cannot buy. Such symmetrical phrasing turns economic contradiction into a memorable verbal loop. Repetition is extensive and purposeful, particularly in references to “the tank, which was the Market,” the penny paid for bringing a bucket, the two pennies charged for taking one out, and the recurring cycle of overflow, unemployment, thirst, waste, and renewed production.

Dialogue is formal, rhetorical, and expository. Characters speak in extended arguments rather than in naturalistic conversation, often using questions, commands, antitheses, and scriptural formulas: “How can we buy unless ye hire us?” and “Doth plenty breed famine?” Each social group has a distinct ideological function. The capitalists rationalise ownership and profit; the soothsayers obscure material causes with jargon; the false priests sanctify suffering; the armed men enforce property relations; and the agitators explain the system and propose collective organisation. Speech therefore serves primarily as political demonstration.

The tone combines solemnity, indignation, mockery, and prophetic optimism. The biblical register initially gives the tale an air of ancient universality, while satirical details expose the irrationality of the system. Economists become “soothsayers” learned in “dark sayings”; charity is reduced to bitter drops flicked from wet fingertips; and religious consolation promises water only after death. The satire is broad and polemical rather than subtle, with hypocrisy and contradiction made deliberately conspicuous.

Structurally, the story proceeds through repeated causal stages. It establishes scarcity, introduces private control of resources, explains wage labour and profit through the bucket-and-penny mechanism, and then traces the resulting crisis of overproduction. Successive institutional responses—advertising, economic explanation, religious pacification, charity, and force—all fail to resolve the contradiction. The cycle then repeats until agitators persuade the people to organise production for themselves. This gives the narrative a movement from bondage through revelation to collective deliverance, echoing biblical stories of exodus and redemption.

Imagery is concrete, elemental, and sustained. Thirst makes economic deprivation physically immediate, while springs, buckets, tanks, fountains, and fishponds translate abstract relations into visible objects and actions. The overflowing tank is the dominant paradoxical image: abundance exists alongside deadly want. Water also carries moral and religious associations of life, grace, and cleansing, intensifying the condemnation of those who monopolise it.

The ending shifts from satire and crisis into utopian prophecy. Collective administration abolishes masters, servants, profit, and want; social relations are recast in the familial language of “brother” and “sister”; and divine blessing validates the new order. The conclusion is absolute and idealised, consistent with the story’s purpose as persuasive socialist parable rather than realistic social fiction. Overall, the style is scriptural, repetitive, allegorical, rhetorically explicit, darkly comic, and morally uncompromising.

The story is a light comic tale built around situational irony, verbal misunderstanding, and the triumph of placid common sense over frantic dishonesty. Its tone is genial, whimsical, and gently satirical rather than morally severe. Although the plot concerns theft and retained money, the narrative treats these matters as ingredients in a farce. The central joke—the chicken thief repeatedly returning in transparent disguises to recover his lost purse—is extended through escalating encounters, while Mrs. Gratz’s refusal to abandon her “Santa Claus” interpretation turns deliberate obtuseness into a form of comic intelligence.

The narration uses a relaxed third-person omniscient perspective, staying especially close to Mrs. Gratz’s temperament and perceptions. The narrator often echoes her slow, comfortable outlook through repetitions such as “placid and fat” and through rhetorical questions about the uselessness of hurry. At times the narrative voice steps forward with amused commentary, creating an intimate storyteller-and-reader relationship. It explains characters’ motives, anticipates the joke, and occasionally adds a mock-moral aside. This produces gentle dramatic irony: the reader quickly understands that the thin visitor is the thief, while the narrative playfully presents him as a conspicuously inadequate “Santa Claus.”

Characterisation is strongly caricatural and depends on contrast. Mrs. Gratz is broad, calm, prosperous, literal-seeming, and almost impossible to unsettle; the thief is tall, thin, nervous, improvisational, and increasingly irritated. Mrs. Flannery supplies a third comic rhythm through voluble excitement and moral pronouncements. Physical traits are repeatedly tied to personality: fatness suggests security, patience, and good fortune, while thinness suggests anxiety and deprivation. The characters are drawn through a few enlarged traits rather than through psychological complexity, which suits the story’s folktale-like comic design.

Dialogue dominates the latter part of the story and carries most of the humour. Mrs. Gratz speaks in a stylised German immigrant dialect, while Mrs. Flannery speaks in a stylised Irish dialect; both are represented through altered grammar, phonetic spelling, and characteristic idioms. The thief uses colloquial American speech full of contractions, evasions, and hastily invented technical language. These differentiated speech patterns make each voice immediately recognisable, though they also reflect the period’s reliance on broad ethnic stereotypes. Mrs. Gratz’s apparent linguistic awkwardness is repeatedly set against her practical shrewdness: she may misuse words such as “clues,” but she understands the thief’s purpose perfectly and turns his own inventions against him.

Repetition is the principal comic device. Key phrases—“Santy Claus,” “come-agains,” “toober-chlosis bugs,” “bones and feathers,” “on the hoof,” and “nine hundred dollars”—return with slight variations. Each repetition accumulates new irony. The thief’s fabricated explanations are absorbed into Mrs. Gratz’s vocabulary and then used to frustrate him, so that his lies become traps of his own making. Her repeated changes in “belief” allow her to classify events according to convenience: Santa Claus accounts for gifts, tuberculosis bugs account for losses, and reindeer account for the broken padlock. This comic logic culminates in the pragmatic pleasure of putting nine hundred dollars in the bank.

The prose alternates between leisurely descriptive passages and rapid conversational exchanges. The opening is deliberately slow, dwelling on drizzle, sleep, appetite, physical comfort, and Mrs. Gratz’s lack of urgency. Once the purse is discovered, the story becomes increasingly dialogue-driven. Each return of the thief creates another compact comic scene, following a similar pattern: he presents a new identity, elaborates an implausible story, nearly exposes himself, searches unsuccessfully, and departs in frustration. This episodic structure gives the narrative the rhythm of a stage farce or repeated vaudeville routine.

Sentence style varies according to function. Narrative sentences are often balanced, repetitive, and gently expansive, mirroring Mrs. Gratz’s placidity. Dialogue is more fragmented and colloquial, with interruptions, questions, false starts, and verbal corrections. Lists and accumulations heighten comic excess, particularly in Mrs. Flannery’s breathless speeches and the thief’s increasingly elaborate account of the multiplying bugs. Dashes and repeated conjunctions help reproduce spontaneous speech, while short deadpan responses from Mrs. Gratz puncture longer speeches.

The imagery is concrete, domestic, and deliberately unromantic: bacon and eggs, rocking chairs, muddy soil, padlocks, chickens, purses, and bank deposits. Christmas imagery is comically reduced to practical exchange. Santa Claus is not a source of wonder but a name Mrs. Gratz gives to an accidental financial benefactor. The title’s metaphor transforms the emptied chicken yard into a Christmas stocking: what appears to be a scene of loss becomes the place where a valuable “present” is found.

Overall, the style combines regional and immigrant-dialect comedy, omniscient narrative irony, caricature, patterned repetition, and escalating farce. Its distinctive effect comes from the mismatch between Mrs. Gratz’s slow manner and quick understanding. She rarely confronts the thief directly; instead, she preserves a surface of innocence and lets his panic expose him. The ending maintains this comic ambiguity, presenting her “faith” as both cheerful fantasy and self-serving linguistic ingenuity.

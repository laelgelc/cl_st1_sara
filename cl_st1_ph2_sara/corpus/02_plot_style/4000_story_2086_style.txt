The story uses restrained social realism, presenting a shabby political committee room through precise physical observation, extended conversation, and understated irony. Its atmosphere is established through darkness, cold, rain, failing firelight, bare walls, wet clothes, cheap tobacco, and bottles of stout. These concrete details do more than furnish the setting: they create a mood of stagnation, poverty, and diminished political purpose. Light and heat repeatedly weaken or briefly revive, giving the room a fitful, enclosed quality that reflects the men’s uncertain loyalties and exhausted ideals.

The narration is third-person and outwardly impersonal. It rarely explains motives directly, preferring to reveal character through faces, gestures, habits, speech, and social behaviour. Descriptions are exact but often faintly grotesque or comic: moist eyes and a mechanically munching mouth, a face like “damp yellow cheese,” or another resembling that of a young ox. Such images expose bodily awkwardness and social pretension without turning into overt caricature. The narrator’s irony is generally quiet and deadpan, as in the formal explanation that Crofton is silent both because he has nothing to say and because he considers the others beneath him.

Dialogue dominates the narrative and supplies most of its movement. The plot is deliberately slight: men enter and leave, discuss canvassing and payment, gossip, drink, argue about politics, and listen to a commemorative poem. Character emerges through verbal manner rather than decisive action. The voices are sharply differentiated by rhythm, vocabulary, evasiveness, and degree of confidence. Repetition, interruption, unfinished phrases, rhetorical questions, anecdotes, mock quotations, and sudden changes of allegiance reproduce the looseness of pub and committee-room talk. Political arguments are not presented as orderly debates but as performances shaped by resentment, self-interest, drink, nostalgia, and the desire to impress an audience.

The diction mixes standard literary narration with strongly local, colloquial speech. Irish idioms and demotic expressions—such as “musha,” “wisha,” “yerra,” “boosing,” “bowsy,” “spondulics,” and “goster”—root the story in working- and lower-middle-class Dublin. Nonstandard grammar and pronunciation are preserved to suggest living speech, while titles and surnames maintain an atmosphere of guarded male formality. Oaths, slang, and comic abuse give the dialogue energy, but recurrent verbal formulas such as “my private and candid opinion” also expose pomposity and borrowed rhetoric. Political slogans and conventional phrases circulate among the men almost as automatically as gossip.

The prose rhythm alternates between compact descriptive passages and long stretches of rapid dialogue. Narrative sentences are often balanced and carefully sequenced, especially when tracing changes in light, movement, or expression. Dialogue tends to be shorter, more elliptical, and more repetitive. Pauses and silences are structurally important: they interrupt the talk, register embarrassment or distrust, and allow the cold room and weak fire to reassert themselves. Small sound effects, especially the recurring “Pok!” of corks leaving heated bottles, puncture political solemnity with physical comedy.

The structure is episodic and accumulative rather than conventionally dramatic. Successive arrivals enlarge the group and redirect the conversation, but the characters remain confined to the same room and essentially the same condition. Repeated motifs—the fire, rain, cigarettes, unpaid wages, drink, ivy leaves, political canvassing, and Parnell’s memory—bind the episodes together. The room functions as a miniature political society in which Nationalists, labour sympathisers, former Conservatives, opportunists, and nostalgic loyalists coexist uneasily. Opinions shift according to company and convenience, revealing the gap between declared principles and practical conduct.

The dominant tone combines melancholy, comedy, scepticism, and muted contempt. The men are treated neither as simple villains nor as heroic patriots. Their poverty and disappointment invite sympathy, while their hypocrisy, suspicion, laziness, and inconsistency invite irony. Henchy is especially revealing: he condemns Tierney as corrupt and miserly, then praises him as soon as the stout arrives; he can also defend welcoming the king after participating in nostalgic reverence for Parnell. The story does not explicitly condemn these contradictions. Instead, it lets them emerge through juxtaposition.

Symbolically, the ivy leaves invoke fidelity to Parnell, while the dim committee room suggests the reduced state of the political cause associated with him. The anniversary supplies an undercurrent of historical grief, but that grief survives mainly as ritual, rhetoric, and sentimental memory. The concluding recitation deliberately shifts into a florid, elevated register. Its apostrophes, patriotic personification, conventional imagery, biblical betrayal motifs, heroic abstractions, and emphatic rhyme contrast with the plain realism surrounding it. The poem is sincere but artistically conventional, and the listeners’ emotional response is genuine yet compromised by the shabby context and their earlier opportunism. The ending therefore remains characteristically ambiguous: it permits a moment of communal feeling while quietly exposing the distance between heroic language and diminished political life.

Overall, the style depends on dramatic objectivity, local speech, economical symbolism, social observation, and irony generated through contrast rather than authorial commentary. Its most characteristic method is to place grand political ideals beside cold rooms, wet boots, unpaid canvassers, petty suspicions, and bottles of stout, allowing the ordinary details to reveal the exhaustion beneath the rhetoric. Apparent misspellings and malformed phrases in the supplied text are best treated as transcription or source errors rather than intentional features of the style.

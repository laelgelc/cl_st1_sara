When the aging warship Constitution is slated for sale or destruction in 1833, Oliver Wendell Holmes writes a poem that ignites public outrage, prompting Congress to preserve and rebuild her. The narrative then explains the ship’s cherished status by tracing her history from her careful construction in the 1790s as part of America’s response to foreign attacks on its merchant vessels.

As Commodore Preble’s flagship, the Constitution helps fight the Barbary pirates and bombard Tripoli. During the War of 1812, she escapes a pursuing British squadron through ingenious kedging and clever sailing under Captain Isaac Hull. She then destroys the British frigate Guerrière, earning the nickname “Old Ironsides” when enemy cannonballs appear to bounce from her oak hull. Under Captain William Bainbridge, she defeats the Java despite Bainbridge’s wounds, and under Captain Charles Stewart, she outmaneuvers and captures both the Cyane and Levant in her final battle.

After decades of distinguished service, the Constitution becomes a naval school and receiving ship, undergoes repeated restoration, and survives as a memorial to American courage, seamanship, and the nation’s emergence as a respected naval power.

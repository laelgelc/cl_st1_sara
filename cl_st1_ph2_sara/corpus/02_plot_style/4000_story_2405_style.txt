The story adopts the form of an antiquarian folk narrative filtered through several layers of narration. A formal editor first introduces the papers of a deceased Irish parish priest, explains the local superstition behind the tale, and presents the document as collected testimony. The priest then briefly characterises the supposed teller before yielding to his highly individual first-person voice. This nested structure creates playful documentary authenticity: the supernatural episode is presented not as invented fiction but as hearsay preserved in manuscripts, family memory, and regional tradition.

Its central stylistic contrast is between the editor’s polished, Latinate prose and the narrator’s vigorous representation of rural Irish speech. The framing passages use long, carefully balanced sentences, abstract nouns, parenthetical qualifications, and mock-scholarly detail. Their tone is urbane, authoritative, and faintly ironic. By contrast, the embedded tale relies on phonetic spellings and dialect forms such as “quare,” “ould,” “sperit,” “iv,” “an’,” and “whin,” together with idiomatic constructions, repeated honorifics, blessings, curses, and appeals to the listener. The dialect is not merely decorative: it determines the rhythm, humour, characterisation, and apparent oral provenance of the narrative.

The storytelling voice is expansive, digressive, and conversational. The narrator repeatedly uses formulas such as “Well, your honour,” “as I was sayin’,” and “but that doesn’t signify,” often wandering into anecdotes or qualifications before returning to the action. Assertions of truthfulness are comically undermined by malapropisms, contradictions, evasions, and accidental admissions. The father is described as sober except for being “partial to the glass,” while his word is praised as “incredible”; similarly, the ghost calls himself “charitable and inhuman” and the father “an example of inebriety.” Such misuse of elevated vocabulary gives the speech a mock-learned quality and fulfils the frame narrator’s warning that the teller favours euphonious “big words” over accurate usage.

Syntax is predominantly cumulative and paratactic in the oral sections. Clauses are strung together with “and,” “but,” “so,” and “for,” creating the impression of breathless spontaneous narration. Repetition is frequent, especially in dialogue tags and sequences of action: characters continually say, pull, drink, protest, and qualify themselves. The formal frame, however, favours periodic sentences with embedded clauses and explanatory asides. Parentheses, dashes, semicolons, and interruptions preserve both editorial commentary and the storyteller’s habit of correcting or supplementing himself.

The tone blends Gothic suspense with broad comic realism. Conventional atmospheric devices—a decaying castle, a stormy night, a haunted portrait, sudden silence, sulphurous odours, and a spectral aristocrat—build genuine anticipation. Yet fear is repeatedly punctured by alcohol, social deference, physical farce, and the ghost’s mundane complaint about his injured leg. The supernatural world mirrors ordinary class relations: even after death, the squire expects obedience, resents familiarity, drinks heavily, threatens violence, and treats the tenant as a servant. Purgatorial suffering is rendered through practical inconveniences such as thirst, walking, weak joints, and water-carrying rather than through sublime theological terror.

Dialogue drives the main scene and gives it a theatrical, almost performable quality. Exchanges are short, repetitive, and governed by status: Terry addresses the apparition as “your honour,” flatters him, and cautiously agrees with obvious falsehoods, while the squire alternates between grandiloquence and abuse. Much of the comedy comes from this mismatch between Terry’s terror and his instinctive politeness. Religious expressions also function ambivalently. Blessings, invocations, holy water, confession, and mass coexist with drinking, curses, folk belief, and comic superstition, producing a vernacular Catholic world in which sacred and profane language intermingle naturally.

The pacing moves from leisurely exposition to concentrated suspense and then to a rapid farcical climax. Long preliminary descriptions establish Terry’s occupation, the castle’s customs, and the legend of the portrait. The vigil slows further through conversation, smoking, drinking, Larry’s sleep, and Terry’s anxious self-talk. Once the ghost emerges, the narrative accelerates through increasingly forceful commands to pull its leg, culminating in the mistaken drink of holy water, a scream, violent bodily disassembly, and Terry’s awakening with a chair leg in his hand. This final image preserves deliberate ambiguity: the event can be read as a real visitation, a drunken dream, or a comic misinterpretation of ordinary objects.

Overall, the style is characterised by framed folklore, pseudo-documentary authority, strong oral cadence, eye dialect, mock erudition, affectionate caricature, Gothic imagery, and comic deflation. Its humour depends on verbal incongruity and narrative unreliability, while its atmosphere depends on treating local superstition with enough seriousness to make the uncanny credible. The result is a comic supernatural tale in which storytelling voice matters more than psychological depth, and in which terror is continually transformed into anecdote, social satire, and communal legend.

The story adopts the form of a mock journalistic report, combining an editorial introduction, time-stamped correspondence, travel narrative, eyewitness reportage, and parodic minutes of a learned society. Its governing conceit is that trivial, foolish, or sordid events are matters of immense national and intellectual importance. The narrator reports dropped cheese, cramped berths, seasickness, petty gambling, local gossip, and absurd scientific proposals with the solemnity appropriate to political crises or historic discoveries. This sustained mismatch between elevated presentation and ridiculous subject matter is the principal source of comedy.

The narrative voice is exuberantly self-important, officious, credulous, and intrusive. Both the editorial “we” and the correspondent’s first-person voice advertise their accuracy, courage, public spirit, and extraordinary abilities in extravagantly inflated terms. The reporter repeatedly claims that scenes “baffle all description” while proceeding to describe them vividly, and he converts guesses, prejudices, and overheard remarks into authoritative intelligence. His supposed neutrality is continually compromised by emotional investments, as when he backs Professor Woodensconce in a coin toss, treats the winner’s satisfaction as evidence of moral corruption, or inflates a beadle’s routine crowd-control duties into an unconstitutional assault on British liberty. The reader is invited to perceive what the narrator cannot: his rhetoric is grossly disproportionate to reality, and his confident interpretations are often more absurd than the events themselves.

The tone is broadly satirical, mock-heroic, and farcical, with intermittent touches of deadpan grotesquerie. Physical discomfort, drunkenness, incompetence, social vanity, and minor disaster are described with ceremonious composure. Potentially alarming incidents—an old man possibly falling into the water, passengers becoming violently seasick, or a professor crashing into the ladies’ cabin—are treated as material for brisk comic observation rather than emotional realism. Sentimental and moral language is deliberately overapplied: commonplace stubbornness becomes “devotion to science,” nausea demonstrates “the triumph of mind over matter,” and a trivial administrative arrangement becomes “domination and despotism.” The humour often depends on delayed deflation, with a mysterious object or portentous development eventually revealed as something mundane, such as the scientific-looking tube that contains seasickness plaster.

The diction alternates between elaborate Latinate formality and concrete colloquial detail. Abstract nouns and superlatives accumulate heavily: “accuracy,” “authenticity,” “immeasurable superiority,” “importance,” “intellect,” and “scientific attainments” lend bogus grandeur to the proceedings. Formulae associated with newspapers, parliamentary debate, learned papers, and public controversy recur throughout: “I regret to inform you,” “I am bound to say,” “the author submitted,” “the president observed,” and “I have yet to learn.” Against this official vocabulary stand vivid material particulars—dirty sleeves, hard biscuits, mutton-chops, carpet-bags, red noses, narrow boards, unplaned forms, and knobs of cheese. Proper names are conspicuously comic and caricatural. Names such as Woodensconce, Muddlebranes, Pumpkinskull, Flummery, Crinkles, and Coppernose encode stupidity, fraudulence, physical oddity, or professional pretension before the characters speak. Place names such as the Black Boy and Stomach-ache and the Boot-jack and Countenance similarly distort plausible English inn names into grotesque verbal jokes.

The syntax is characteristically expansive. Long periodic sentences gather qualifications, parenthetical remarks, lists, subordinate clauses, and escalating claims before arriving at an anticlimax. Parallel construction and cumulative catalogues create rhetorical momentum, especially when institutions, accomplishments, or supposed threats are being inflated. Repetition is used both for emphasis and parody, as in the repeated “I have yet to learn” of the correspondent’s mock-libertarian denunciation. These elaborate passages are contrasted with abrupt time-stamped entries—“No, it isn’t”—whose brevity punctures the reporter’s appetite for sensation. Parentheses, dashes, semicolons, italics, capitals, and exclamation marks reproduce the visual and rhetorical mannerisms of excited nineteenth-century print journalism. Capitalised speaker labels in the meeting report imitate official transcripts and give nonsense the appearance of documentary authority.

The structure is episodic and accumulative rather than tightly plotted. Short observational units follow one another chronologically, with the time stamps manufacturing urgency even when nothing happens. Small mysteries, mishaps, and disputes provide local narrative arcs: the unknown object on the wharf, the contents of Mr. Slug’s tube, the coin toss, the voyage’s seasickness, and the supposed conspiracy involving the beadle. Once the association meets, the story shifts from travel farce to a sequence of mock-academic presentations. Each paper typically begins with an absurd premise, develops it through solemn reasoning, invites grave discussion, and ends by implicating government, Parliament, social fashion, or public policy. The pacing depends on alternation between verbose buildup and sharp comic reversal, while the serial succession of speakers allows the satire to broaden without requiring conventional plot development.

Characterisation is external, economical, and caricatural. Individuals are defined by names, bodily traits, habits, professional titles, luggage, appetites, and recurrent comic behaviour. Professorial status rarely corresponds to wisdom; instead, the learned characters are vain, impractical, gullible, competitive, and physically ridiculous. Bodily realities repeatedly defeat intellectual pretension: scholars cannot enter their berths, coins lodge in beams, scientific remedies fail, and elaborate theories emerge from appetite, fashion, or misunderstanding. Dialogue is less concerned with psychological individuality than with exposing modes of public speech. Characters reason fluently from false assumptions, treat puns as evidence, and dignify criminality or idleness as questions of science, economics, or reform.

The satire targets institutional pretension, journalistic sensationalism, pseudo-science, bureaucratic language, parliamentary complacency, class privilege, and indiscriminate faith in progress. Learned inquiry is represented as a machine for converting nonsense into expertise. Proposals are judged by novelty and verbal ingenuity rather than morality or usefulness, while government intervention is recommended whenever an absurdity encounters practical resistance. Respectable institutions are repeatedly aligned with disreputable conduct: a pocket-picking machine becomes a labour-saving invention, Parliament is imagined as its natural adopter, and destructive aristocratic behaviour is reframed as recreation requiring public accommodation. Puns and literalised metaphors sharpen these attacks, as with the Shakespeare twig being pronounced “a decided plant,” or bears’ grease supposedly transmitting bearish conduct to its users.

To reproduce the style, a writer would choose an unreliable reporter who treats every detail as historic, use a documentary framework with dates, headings, or formal speaker labels, and maintain complete seriousness toward increasingly absurd material. The prose should combine long, ceremonious sentences with occasional brutally short corrections; elevate banal facts through superlatives and abstract nouns; give characters transparent comic names; and allow physical detail to expose the hollowness of official rhetoric. Most importantly, the narration should not openly announce the joke. The comedy should arise from confident overstatement, false inference, excessive procedural formality, and the widening gap between what the narrator believes he is recording and what the reader can plainly see.

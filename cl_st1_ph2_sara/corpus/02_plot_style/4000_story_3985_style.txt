The story is written as a brisk comic farce, combining romantic comedy, social satire, sporting fiction, and mock melodrama. Its governing tone is light, urbane, irreverent, and knowingly artificial. Aristocratic poverty, radical politics, commercial ambition, courtship, and football fanaticism are all treated with the same exaggerated seriousness, so that trivial matters acquire epic importance while supposedly grave matters are reduced to absurdity. The humour remains genial rather than savage: the characters are caricatures, but their vanity, opportunism, and eccentricity make them entertaining rather than repellent.

The narrative voice is highly intrusive and self-conscious. The narrator repeatedly steps outside the action to discuss the supposed difficulties of short-story writing, boast about descriptive talents, lament editorial demands, and invoke “Brevity” as a refrain. These interruptions create a comic frame around the plot and parody literary convention. The narrator promises emotional, scenic, or atmospheric elaboration, then ostentatiously suppresses it with phrases such as “Let us on.” This mock struggle between expansiveness and concision becomes one of the story’s main running jokes. It also establishes an intimate relationship with the reader, who is addressed as though participating in the composition.

Characterisation is deliberately economical and theatrical. The opening adopts a playbill format, reducing the principals to comic labels such as “an angel,” “a Greek god,” and “a multi-millionaire City man and Radical politician.” These shorthand identities are subsequently complicated only enough to support the farce. Characters are defined through a few dominant traits: Clarence is handsome, impassive, privileged, and superb in goal; Rackstraw is politically aggressive but emotionally ruled by football; Lady Runnymede combines aristocratic circumstances with forceful American speech. Psychological realism is secondary to clarity, momentum, and comic contrast.

The prose mixes polished, formal narration with slang, sporting jargon, journalistic phrasing, and colloquial dialogue. Elevated expressions such as “the dewy softness of their eyes” or “fire flashing from his eyes” sit beside idioms like “hot stuff,” “had the goods,” “get out and hustle,” and “completely in the bouillon.” This clash of registers is central to the humour. Aristocratic English understatement contrasts with American directness, while the language of romance and heroic struggle is applied to charity stalls, financial inconvenience, and football matches. Period-specific vocabulary and social markers—“footer,” “pro,” “quid,” “City man,” gentlemen’s clubs, titles, dowries, and servants—give the story a distinctly early-twentieth-century British setting.

Syntax is generally clear and energetic, alternating between compact comic statements and longer cumulative sentences. Short paragraphs and rapid dialogue accelerate confrontations, while extended sentences are used for escalating absurdity. Lists frequently function as punchlines, as in the inventory of Clarence’s bazaar purchases or the catalogue of social and sporting consequences. Repetition is another important rhythmic device: “Brevity, brevity,” “Work?” and the reiterated conditional arguments around the football match build comic pressure. Parenthetical qualifications, abrupt corrections, and deadpan afterthoughts allow jokes to arrive late in the sentence.

Dialogue is stylised, economical, and strongly differentiated by social voice. Clarence speaks with courteous composure and upper-class vagueness even in crisis, producing humour through understatement and literal-minded replies. Lady Runnymede uses clipped Americanisms and businesslike imperatives. Rackstraw’s speech is forceful, metaphorical, and transactional, especially when football or money is involved. Exchanges often take the form of rapid verbal rallies, with single-word questions and answers sharpening timing. Characters frequently accept outrageous premises without surprise, which sustains the farce’s deadpan logic.

The figurative language is extravagant, incongruous, and cartoon-like. Similes compare people and actions to terriers, cats, Chinese crackers, watch springs, black beetles, steamrollers, and stuffed rats in a dogs’ home. Emotional states become physical assaults: a voice “rip[s] the air into a million shreds,” laughter strikes “like a bullet,” and bad news feels like an auger driven into the body. Sporting ability is described in mock-heroic terms—Clarence becomes a “Gibraltar among goal-keepers”—while national reactions to football are inflated into scenes of catastrophe. Hyperbole is pervasive, from the mass mortality of office boys’ fictitious grandmothers to the image of an entire city paralysed by defeat.

Irony operates at every level. The Runnymedes consider themselves impoverished on an enormous income; Clarence’s gentlemanly education leaves him unfit for work; Rackstraw’s hatred of aristocracy disappears when an aristocrat proves useful in goal; and moral objections collapse before sporting or commercial advantage. The narrator’s invented classical quotation about chickens and banana skins parodies proverbial wisdom and scholarly authority. Similar mock solemnity appears in faux-statistical claims, grand historical pronouncements, and newspaper-style commentary. The story repeatedly imitates serious forms only to fill them with comic nonsense.

The plot is tightly engineered around reversals and converging motives. The romance is obstructed by class politics, the aristocratic family is forced toward employment by financial loss, Clarence’s hidden identity enables his football career, and football eventually resolves both the romantic and social conflict. Coincidence and sudden changes of heart are embraced rather than disguised. The decisive negotiation between Clarence and Rackstraw turns apparent blackmail into evidence of business aptitude, neatly uniting love, sport, and capitalism. The concluding domestic scene supplies a compact comic coda: marriage, the treasured football relic, and the renewed attempt to corner wheat confirm that no one has fundamentally changed.

Overall, the style depends on speed, verbal precision, narrative self-mockery, extravagant comparison, and the collision of incompatible social worlds. It treats romance as negotiation, politics as posture, business as respectable piracy, and football as a national religion. The result is a buoyant, highly controlled comic mode in which the narrator’s performance is nearly as important as the events being narrated.

The story is written in the tradition of the late-Victorian or Edwardian supernatural tale, combining travel narrative, psychological unease and Gothic horror. Its atmosphere is initially picturesque rather than overtly frightening: forests, limestone ridges, moonlight, vineyards and an isolated Alpine valley are rendered with sensuous precision. Beauty steadily acquires menace, however, as remoteness, silence and shadow make the landscape feel detached from ordinary time. The prose frequently personifies nature—the wind “dropped suddenly to watch the beauty of the moonlight,” while the forest holds night “within its folds”—giving the setting an alert, almost conspiratorial presence.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, but it settles mainly into the consciousness of the unnamed “big man.” His practical, sceptical temperament provides the story’s central lens. Supernatural possibilities are therefore filtered through denial, rationalisation and reluctant sensory recognition rather than asserted immediately as fact. The narrator occasionally steps beyond strict focalisation to make general observations such as “night ever italicizes such memories,” lending the voice an assured, aphoristic quality. This produces a characteristic tension between a composed, cultivated storyteller and a protagonist whose confidence is gradually eroded.

The diction is formal, literary and slightly archaic, with phrases such as “absorption of spirit,” “in due course,” “beyond all question” and “hélas.” Descriptions favour evaluative adjectives—“queer,” “sinister,” “uncanny,” “monstrous,” “ghastly”—but the horror is also conveyed through concrete sensory motifs: shining eyes and teeth, odd-tasting milk, freshly turned earth, stealthy footsteps, crimson blood and the pin-prick wound. Repeated uncertainty markers—“perhaps,” “almost as though,” “he fancied,” “was it merely”—delay certainty and preserve ambiguity until the final revelation.

Sentence structure varies with the emotional pressure. Scenic and reflective passages use long, balanced sentences with subordinate clauses, semicolons and parenthetical qualifications. Moments of alarm shift into shorter clauses, fragments and abrupt exclamations: “Empty!” or “And night ever italicizes such memories!” Dashes are especially prominent, marking hesitation, interruption, afterthought and fearful association. Repetition also intensifies suspense, particularly when perceptions are corrected: an ordinary smell becomes turned earth, night sounds become footsteps, and a presumed sleepwalker becomes an unknown figure. The supplied text contains occasional typographical and encoding corruption, but these appear to be transmission artifacts rather than intentional stylistic features.

Dialogue is sparse, restrained and socially familiar. The men address one another as “old man” and “old chap,” while their exchanges reveal character through contrast: the older man is receptive, imaginative and increasingly entranced; the big man is protective, impatient and determined to explain away what he senses. Their speech often trails off or breaks around taboo ideas, allowing implication to carry more weight than direct statement. The vampire girl’s complete silence is especially significant. Her smiles, gaze and gliding movements replace speech, making her both seductive and unreadable.

The plot is paced through gradual escalation. It opens after the decisive visit to the chalet, then reconstructs that encounter through the big man’s sleepless recollections. This delayed flashback lets effects precede causes: the reader first notices Morton’s altered behaviour and only later learns about the milk, the girl’s touch and the deserted farmhouse. The night sequence then narrows into a chain of increasingly alarming discoveries, culminating in the cemetery confrontation. After the apparent climax, the narrative adopts a calmer, quasi-documentary mode, adding village rumours, physical evidence and the doctor’s testimony. This coda supplies successive confirmations—the vanished chalet, poisoned milk, the old accusation of vampirism, the throat wound and the bloodless heart—while retaining the manner of a reported “strange story.”

The dominant technique is the collision of rational modernity with surviving folklore. The protagonist repeatedly dismisses superstition as “rubbish” and “monstrous fiction,” yet his body registers the truth before his reason does through tingling nerves, nausea, perspiration and instinctive fear. The story’s horror lies not only in the vampire itself but in the failure of ordinary categories: buildings exist where they should not, the past intrudes physically into the present, beauty masks predation, and death is officially attributed to heart failure despite evidence of something impossible. Overall, the style is atmospheric, decorous and slow-burning, relying on landscape, psychological resistance, delayed information and accumulating sensory details rather than graphic violence.

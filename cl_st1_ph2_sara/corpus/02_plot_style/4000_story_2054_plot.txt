A narrator follows the life of a boyhood acquaintance who habitually sacrifices his own comfort to meet other people’s expectations. As a schoolboy, he forces himself to smoke and drink beer because the older boys do so. As a young man in love, he pretends to lose his appetite, secretly raiding the pantry at night, and later stages visible distress during his wife’s childbirth because those around him expect an anxious husband.

After marriage, he and his wife attend church and organize Sunday activities chiefly to avoid offending their strict cook. When an inheritance turns him into a country gentleman, he endures hunting, shooting, and other painful social obligations because he believes he must behave according to his position. Greater wealth pushes him into Parliament and yachting, both of which make him miserable, and a scandal aboard his yacht damages his reputation despite his innocence.

He then falls under the influence of a fashionable artistic circle and submits to literature, art, music, theater, and society events he neither likes nor understands. When the narrator offers him a carefree day of simple amusements he once enjoyed, he is tempted but refuses, asking what people would say. He departs for another exhausting round of fashionable obligations, still imprisoned by his need to satisfy appearances.

The story adopts the style of a traditional adventure melodrama, combining a pseudo-historical European setting with the structure of an escape narrative and the final reversal of a trap tale. Its tone is formal, controlled, and faintly theatrical. Castles, princes, brigands, secret passages, judgment halls, masked executioners, and mountain valleys create a romantic, old-world atmosphere, while precise references to dates and hours give the plot an almost procedural inevitability.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, moving freely between outward action and the thoughts of Toza, Paulo, and, more briefly, the Prince. It explains motives directly rather than relying heavily on implication: Toza’s suspicions, calculations, fear, and intention to murder Paulo are all made explicit. The narrator remains emotionally restrained, even when describing danger or cruelty, and this composure strengthens the story’s irony. Occasional authorial summaries compress long periods of hardship, allowing the narrative to dwell instead on decisive conversations, narrow escapes, and changes in trust.

Dialogue is the principal engine of characterisation and plot. Toza speaks at length, forcefully and often sardonically, especially during the trial, where his defiant rhetoric establishes him as proud, intelligent, cynical, and fatalistic. His language is filled with aphoristic comparisons—wolves devouring a fallen wolf, the powerful ruler as a larger thief—which lend him a rough philosophy of power. The Prince’s speech is quieter, shorter, and more ceremonially precise, suggesting authority and concealed malice. Paulo’s guarded, practical replies make him appear nervous and corruptible, while also preserving ambiguity about his true role. Repeated questions, whispered exchanges, and bargaining dialogue gradually expose the shifting balance of power between prisoner and gaoler.

The diction is elevated but accessible, with period colouring supplied by words such as “brigand,” “gaoler,” “miscreant,” “minions,” and “judgment-hall.” Descriptions favour conventional romantic imagery over dense sensory detail: the “profound valley,” “purling little river,” cold winter rain, roaring waterfall, torchlit execution chamber, and gleaming axe. Syntax is generally expansive and carefully subordinated, especially in explanatory passages, while moments of danger use shorter sentences, exclamations, and abrupt commands. The prose also retains older typographical habits, including double hyphens, compounds such as “alarm-bell,” and occasional constructions characteristic of late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century popular fiction.

Structurally, the story is linear and tightly causal. It begins with the sentence of death, shifts into an apparent prison-break plot, and then measures Toza’s progress against the approaching fifteenth of January. Suspense develops through withheld information, geographical obstacles, patrols, physical exhaustion, and mutual distrust. The escape occupies most of the narrative, giving the reader time to accept the tunnel as a genuine route to freedom. Temporal markers become increasingly prominent near the climax: the date is named, the journey slows, and the final approach is tracked until the clock reaches four. This produces a strong sense that Toza has not escaped his appointment but has laboriously travelled toward it.

Dramatic irony and misdirection define the story’s method. The Prince’s apparent mercy is suspicious from the outset, yet the exact mechanism of his revenge remains concealed. Paulo’s behaviour is presented in a way that supports two interpretations: he may be a frightened, greedy gaoler, or he may be guiding Toza according to a plan. His claim to have left the treasure untouched is a subtle clue, as are the suspiciously prolonged route, his careful counting of the days, and his insistence that Toza enter the tunnel first. Toza’s decision to kill Paulo creates an additional moral turn, confirming his treachery just before the trap closes. The final scene rapidly reinterprets everything that precedes it.

The ending is concise, visual, and theatrical. The sudden transition from dark tunnel to torchlit execution room presents a tableau centred on the black-draped block, masked executioner, axe, Prince, and clock. The Prince’s closing declaration—“You are just in time!”—functions as both punchline and sentence. Its grim wit completes the pattern of verbal irony established by Toza’s earlier promise to “keep the appointment.” Overall, the style depends on lucid storytelling, melodramatic imagery, calculated delay, moral symmetry, and a sharply engineered twist in which apparent escape proves to be the elaborate fulfilment of fate.

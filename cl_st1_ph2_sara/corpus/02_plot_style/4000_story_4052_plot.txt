During the Thirty Years’ War, the Protestant city of Lowenburg falls under Catholic imperial control. Its pastors are expelled, leading citizens are driven away or imprisoned, and most remaining men outwardly convert to Catholicism. Their wives, however, remain steadfast Lutherans, secretly attending Protestant services and having their children baptized outside the city.

In 1631, the unscrupulous former friar Julius persuades the town council to force the women to conform, threatening imprisonment. The council summons several prominent ladies, but instead five hundred women arrive together, led by the judge’s and burgomaster’s wives. Refusing to be separated, they declare that they will all share the same fate and will not change their faith against their consciences.

Alarmed by the women’s unity—and by the heavy household keys they carry—the councilors flee down a back staircase, lock the women inside the town hall, and abandon the confrontation. The women remain composed while their families bring them food, and they demand to know why they have been confined without trial. Attempts to portray the summons as a harmless religious conference fail, and the women firmly repeat that they will not renounce their beliefs.

Because the town cannot function well without them and the authorities cannot intimidate them, the women are released. The priest later privately pressures their three leaders to conform or falsely claim they need time to consider, but they refuse both conversion and deceit. When the other women gather again in support, the frightened magistrates compel the priest to desist. The women return home victorious, having preserved their faith through solidarity and passive resistance.

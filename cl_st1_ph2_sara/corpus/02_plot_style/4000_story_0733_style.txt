The story is written as a popular historical narrative with a strongly patriotic, commemorative purpose. It combines factual exposition, naval history, biography, and dramatized anecdote to explain why the USS Constitution became a cherished national symbol. Although presented as history, it adopts many techniques of adventure fiction, turning campaigns and battles into vivid, accessible scenes centered on courage, ingenuity, honor, and national pride.

The narrator is omniscient, authoritative, and openly sympathetic to the American side. Frequent use of “we,” “our,” and “our country” creates a collective national viewpoint and assumes an American readership: “we had a rough road to travel,” “our frigate,” and “our sailor boys.” British, French, and Barbary opponents are generally framed as threats or rivals, while American officers and sailors are characterized as gallant, heroic, resourceful, and humane. The narrator also addresses readers directly through questions and prompts such as “Why did the people care so much?” and “Think of our consul at Lisbon,” giving the piece the character of an instructive school history.

The tone is earnest, admiring, energetic, and celebratory. Moments of danger are narrated with suspense, while victories prompt exclamatory enthusiasm. Moral approval is explicit: commanders are “great-hearted,” “heroic,” “remarkable,” or “gallant,” and the ship carries the flag “with dignity and honor.” The emotional register rises particularly in descriptions of public receptions, close battles, and acts of chivalry. Humor provides occasional relief, notably when Hull’s breeches split during battle and the sailors turn his name into the pun “Hull her!” Such episodes humanize otherwise monumental historical figures without undermining their dignity.

The diction is clear, concrete, and accessible, with a mildly old-fashioned quality. Naval terminology—“broadside,” “mizzenmast,” “quarter-deck,” “kedging,” “raking fire,” and “struck her colors”—adds authenticity, but unfamiliar procedures are briefly explained. Evaluative adjectives and superlatives are common: “powerful British fleet,” “terrible broadside,” “finest frigates,” “superb seamanship,” and “precious memorial.” Expressions such as “stanch ship,” “sailor boys,” “hardest fighters,” and “went stoutly on” reflect the idiom of late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century juvenile history. The prose also employs martial and patriotic abstractions, including “freedom,” “honor,” “glory,” “prestige,” and “the affections of the American people.”

Sentence structure alternates between long explanatory sentences and short dramatic statements. The expository passages often contain dates, measurements, lists, and technical comparisons, while action scenes rely on clipped sentences and fragments: “He must get away. But how?” and “Everybody hurries to his place.” Present tense is frequently introduced during battles—“Hull knows,” “the Guerrière…pours in a broadside,” “the flag goes down”—even though the surrounding history is told in the past tense. This historical present makes distant events feel immediate and theatrical. Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, and abrupt transitions further heighten excitement.

The narrative is broadly chronological but begins with a framing episode from 1833, when Holmes’s poem supposedly saves the ship from destruction. It then moves backward to explain the Constitution’s origins and reputation before proceeding through construction, the Barbary campaigns, the War of 1812, later service, and preservation. This structure answers the opening question by accumulating evidence of the ship’s symbolic importance. Major battles form self-contained episodes, each typically organized around a threat, a display of leadership or seamanship, a decisive victory, and an aftermath demonstrating public gratitude or personal nobility.

Dialogue is plentiful in the battle narratives and is used less as documentary quotation than as vivid characterization. Officers speak in concise commands, challenges, jokes, and magnanimous gestures. Exchanges such as Hull’s refusal to take Dacres’s sword or Bainbridge’s return of Lambert’s weapon embody a code of honorable warfare. Sailors often answer commanders collectively, creating a spirited, democratic image of the crew. Much of the dialogue is polished and epigrammatic, suggesting retold legend rather than strict transcript.

The story relies on repetition and recurring epithets to build myth. The Constitution is repeatedly called “Old Ironsides,” “the old frigate,” “our frigate,” or “the stanch ship.” Her survival, strength, speed, and luck recur throughout, gradually turning the vessel into a character in her own right. Personification is central: she “won her chief honors,” “came out of the battle,” “carried her country’s flag,” and “earned a lasting place” in national affection. Physical endurance becomes a metaphor for the endurance of the nation.

Imagery is functional rather than elaborate. Battle scenes are rendered through flashes of guns, falling masts, torn rigging, blood-stained decks, whistles, drums, cheers, and crowded harbors. Similes and metaphors are simple and forceful: indignation rises “to a white heat,” public feeling “swept everything before it,” tempers move “like a flash,” and seasoned timbers are “hard as iron.” Exact statistics—numbers of guns, casualties, ships, days, miles, and measurements—lend documentary weight while also magnifying achievements.

Pacing varies sharply. Long stretches summarize years of construction or service, whereas celebrated encounters are slowed into moment-by-moment action through dialogue, commands, tactical explanation, and suspense. The escape from the British squadron and the fight with the Guerrière receive especially detailed treatment. Transitional phrases such as “A moment later,” “All day long,” “Six days later,” and “In less than five months” keep the chronology clear and propel the narrative forward.

Overall, the style belongs to didactic patriotic history for a general or younger readership. It seeks not merely to record events but to inspire admiration, teach national history, and preserve heroic legend. Its defining qualities are chronological clarity, energetic battle narration, direct reader engagement, accessible technical detail, anecdotal characterization, moral certainty, and a sustained reverence for the Constitution as both a warship and an embodiment of American independence.

The story has a distinctly modernist, impressionistic style, privileging momentary perception, private consciousness, and sensory intensity over conventional plot. Its structure is episodic and spatial rather than strongly causal: the oval flower-bed serves as a fixed centre while successive couples pass through the scene, briefly revealing memories, desires, anxieties, or social habits before dissolving back into the garden. The alternation between human figures and the snail’s minute progress creates a shifting sense of scale and places human life within a broader field of natural movement.

The narration is fluidly omniscient, moving almost imperceptibly between external description, quoted thought, dialogue, and free indirect discourse. Characters are introduced through posture, gesture, rhythm of speech, and fragments of consciousness rather than through extensive biography. Their inner lives often exceed what they can articulate: ordinary remarks about tea, money, luck, or flowers carry “heavy” emotional meanings, while memories condense into vivid objects such as a silver shoe buckle, a dragonfly, or a kiss. This produces a recurring contrast between banal speech and intense, partially unspoken experience.

The prose is highly visual and painterly. Colour dominates the descriptive vocabulary—red, blue, yellow, green, purple, silver, brown—and light continually stains, flashes across, or dissolves objects. The garden is rendered not as a stable landscape but as a shifting composition of colour, shade, transparency, and motion. Human beings, insects, petals, glass roofs, and patches of sunlight are repeatedly transformed into abstract shapes and patterns. Similes are elaborate and often defamiliarising: butterflies resemble a shattered marble column, a thrush becomes mechanical, voices resemble candle flames, and the city becomes a nest of steel Chinese boxes.

Syntax is often long, cumulative, and rhythmically mobile. Extended sentences link clauses with semicolons, conjunctions, participial phrases, and repeated qualifications, imitating the continuous movement of attention from one object to another. Lists and accumulations create density, especially in descriptions of the snail’s miniature terrain and the final panorama of heat, colour, machinery, and voices. These flowing sentences are counterpointed by short fragments—“Voices. Yes, voices.”—and by clipped dialogue, producing sudden changes in pace and emphasis. Repetition, particularly of colours, names, commodities, and conversational formulas, gives the prose a musical, incantatory quality.

The natural world is anthropomorphised without becoming sentimental. The snail deliberates, confronts obstacles, and pursues a goal; flowers appear cool and self-contained; light behaves almost like a living substance. At the same time, people are compared to butterflies, drops of water, flames, flowers, and patches of colour. This reciprocal imagery blurs the boundary between human consciousness and the environment. The garden functions as both a real public place and a symbolic field in which lives briefly emerge, intersect, and disappear.

Dialogue is socially differentiated and psychologically revealing. Simon and Eleanor speak reflectively about memory; the disturbed elderly man produces grandiose, associative monologue; the two lower-middle-class women exchange compressed names, gossip, and shopping items; the young couple speak in hesitant, commonplace phrases charged with erotic and emotional possibility. Ellipses, interruptions, repetitions, rhetorical questions, and abrupt associative transitions reproduce the incompleteness of actual thought and conversation. The story’s treatment of class can be sharply observational and occasionally satirical, but moments of wonder interrupt social categorisation.

The tone combines lyrical rapture, gentle irony, melancholy, and curiosity. The narration observes eccentricity and awkwardness with precision, yet it repeatedly discovers depth beneath apparently trivial behaviour. Memory, unrealised possibilities, desire, madness, courtship, ageing, and social routine coexist without being resolved into a single moral. Time is layered: present sensations awaken the past, while fleeting figures seem almost ghostlike as they recede among the trees.

The ending expands from the flower-bed to the whole garden and then to the surrounding city. Natural sounds, human voices, and mechanical noise merge into a complex urban-natural soundscape. Rather than offering closure, the story culminates in simultaneity: petals flash, voices cry out, traffic turns, and the city murmurs continuously. The overall effect is kaleidoscopic and immersive, presenting life as a succession of transient perceptions held together by recurring patterns of colour, movement, sound, and desire.

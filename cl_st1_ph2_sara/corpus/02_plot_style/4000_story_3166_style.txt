The story adopts a mock-Gothic, grotesquely comic style, combining plague-era horror with farce, nautical humour, and satire of aristocratic ceremony. Its atmosphere is ostensibly macabre—abandoned streets, decaying buildings, corpses, pestilential vapours, coffins, skeletons, and personifications of disease—but the horror is repeatedly undercut by drunken bravado, slapstick violence, verbal absurdity, and extravagant caricature. The prevailing tone is therefore one of comic dread: death remains materially present, yet its symbols are converted into costumes, drinking vessels, furniture, and props.

The narrative voice is conspicuously intrusive, urbane, and ironic. The narrator pauses to evaluate characters, qualify claims, and address the act of storytelling through phrases such as “I think,” “to say the truth,” and “This company I will endeavor to delineate one by one.” This creates the manner of a formal chronicler recounting ridiculous events with exaggerated seriousness. Elevated historical narration is deliberately applied to drunken escapades, while seamen are called “our friends,” “our heroes,” and “the worthy couple,” producing a sustained mock-heroic effect. Understatement and ceremonious phrasing make crude actions seem grand, and narrative dignity repeatedly clashes with physical chaos.

The diction is ornate, archaic, and Latinate. Words such as “peregrinations,” “cachinnatory,” “physiognomy,” “habiliments,” “mulct,” and “paraphernalia” lend the prose an antiquarian and pseudo-scholarly texture. Courtly formulas—“Serene Consort,” “undivided empire,” “sacred and lofty purposes”—are juxtaposed with tavern language, insults, curses, and sailor slang. The seamen speak in an extended nautical idiom, describing drinking and bodily capacity through “cargo,” “ballast,” “hold,” “stowage-room,” “bilge-water,” and similar shipboard metaphors. This contrast between royal bombast and maritime vernacular is one of the story’s principal comic engines.

The syntax is elaborate and highly periodic. Sentences often accumulate subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, parallel phrases, and delayed conclusions. Dashes are used heavily to interrupt, amplify, or redirect the prose, creating a voice that seems both ceremonious and excitable. Descriptive passages proceed through catalogues and escalating sequences, while repeated constructions—“onward—still onward,” “more noisome and more horrible,” or the chorus of “Treason!”—build rhythm and theatrical intensity. Exclamation marks, emphatic italics or quotation marks, direct interjections, and deliberately overextended speeches give the writing an oratorical, performative quality.

Characterisation is dominated by visual caricature and oppositional design. Legs is impossibly tall, thin, solemn, and angular; Hugh Tarpaulin is short, round, purple, mobile, and irrepressibly amused. The plague court is constructed on the same principle of bodily exaggeration. Each figure is associated with a single grotesquely enlarged feature or pathological condition: forehead, mouth, nose, ears, dropsy, consumption, gout, paralysis, or delirium. Bodies are compared to barrels, bladders, ship fittings, mountains, fish, and turtles. These similes are vivid but intentionally undignified, reducing supposedly exalted personages to distorted flesh and comic machinery.

The imagery is tactile, theatrical, and morbidly decorative. The plague district is rendered through mist, rank grass, broken paving stones, collapsing beams, poisonous smells, skeletons, and rotting bodies. Inside the undertaker’s shop, death becomes a stage set: skulls serve as cups, coffin-trestles as chairs, a thigh-bone as a sceptre, a pall as a cloak, a coffin as clothing, and a suspended skeleton as a lamp. Light is typically “ghastly,” “fitful,” or vapourous, heightening the Gothic chiaroscuro. Yet these details are so excessive and inventively arranged that they create spectacle rather than pure terror.

The story’s structure moves from realistic tavern comedy into forbidden urban horror, then into a carnivalesque court scene, and finally into explosive slapstick. The early passages establish the protagonists through physical description and drunken misadventure. A substantial historical digression enlarges the plague setting and generates suspense. The centre of the story slows into a tableau, introducing the six members of the Pest family one by one. Dialogue then turns the tableau into a mock trial or royal council, after which recognition of the “king” as an actor punctures the supernatural pretence. The conclusion accelerates sharply into brawling, flooding, drowning, overturned furniture, and comic escape.

Dialogue is strongly differentiated by social register. King Pest speaks in pompous, balanced proclamations filled with abstractions and royal plurals, whereas Legs and Tarpaulin answer with blunt irreverence and maritime slang. The Pest family often speaks chorally, making them resemble actors in a grotesque stage performance. Names such as “Pest-Iferous,” “Pest-Ilential,” “Tem-Pest,” and “Ana-Pest” rely on conspicuous punning, reinforcing the artificiality of the court. The revelation that its monarch is a stage-player confirms that authority here is costume, rhetoric, and performance rather than genuine sovereignty.

The humour depends on incongruity, repetition, hyperbole, and bathos. Terrifying circumstances fail to frighten the protagonists because they are too drunk to understand them; solemn speeches are interrupted by practical concerns about alcohol; royal punishments become drinking challenges; and a supposedly infernal monarch is exposed as a theatrical impostor. Violence is treated with cartoonish speed and detachment. Even drowning and brain-smashing are absorbed into the hectic comic finale rather than allowed tragic weight.

Thematically, the prose mocks pretension, hierarchy, superstition, and the ceremonial denial of bodily mortality. Disease and death form a counterfeit royal family, but sailors who treat death through familiar maritime idiom prove less vulnerable to its mystique. Aristocratic insignia are repurposed as clothing, sacred ritual becomes intoxication, and plague terror shelters theft and revelry. The style’s defining signature is this continual collision of the elevated with the debased: ornate narration describes vulgar action, Gothic horror becomes carnival, and regal authority collapses beneath laughter, drink, and physical disorder.

The story has the character of a realist social indictment shaped by an urgent moral and religious conscience. It begins in a confessional first person, with an elderly, privileged speaker acknowledging that his material life contradicts his beliefs. The voice is anguished, self-accusing, and openly didactic. Repetition—“I cannot,” “grown and grown,” “more and more”—conveys both emotional pressure and paralysis, while emphatic abstractions such as “injustice,” “cruelty,” “humiliation,” and “suffering” establish the ethical framework before the narrative proper begins. Providence is invoked as a possible justification for speaking from within the class being condemned, but the narrator carefully rejects both self-vindication and simplistic idealisation of the oppressed.

After this preface, the mode shifts into third-person omniscient narration. The narrator moves freely between external description, characters’ thoughts, and broad authorial judgment. Volgin’s consciousness is often rendered through direct thought or lightly mediated interior discourse, allowing his complacency to expose itself: he regards his banking hours as exhausting, admires the countryside, worries about an auctioned Madonna, and criticises village schooling while barely perceiving the labour and deprivation surrounding him. The resulting irony is severe but usually controlled. Rather than labeling him a hypocrite at every moment, the story lets the disproportion between his self-image and his circumstances become visible through juxtaposition.

The prose relies heavily on concrete, accumulative detail. Volgin’s possessions, clothes, grooming implements, food, newspapers, gardens, horses, and tableware are itemised at length. These catalogues create a dense material world, but their principal purpose is ethical accounting: every comfort is implicitly traced back to somebody else’s work. Parenthetical qualifications and precise figures—salaries, acreage, prices, times, ages, sums of money—give the narrative a documentary exactness. Luxurious objects are described by brand-like provenance and polished appearance, while peasant life is represented through bluntly functional objects such as birch-bark shoes, blunt spades, ragged shirts, straw, bread, soup, and potatoes. The contrast in material vocabularies embodies the class division.

Sentence structure is often long, additive, and syntactically layered. Clauses accumulate through coordination, qualification, and parenthesis, reproducing the narrator’s desire to include every causal and moral connection. Dashes frequently introduce corrections, afterthoughts, clarifications, or sharpened contrasts. At moments of emotional or ethical emphasis, however, the syntax contracts into short declarations or questions: “I cannot”; “To beg”; “Why is this?” This alternation between expansive explanation and abrupt judgment gives the prose both analytical breadth and rhetorical force.

The narrative structure is founded on contrast and parallelism. Volgin’s leisurely morning is placed against Stephen’s decades of service and the under-butler’s dawn labour; the sumptuous luncheon is placed against Mitri’s loss of his only horse and his immediate return to exhausting communal work; aristocratic charity is placed against peasant charity. Volgin gives three roubles without materially altering his life, whereas the poor household shares bread with a beggar despite fearing that there may not be enough. The abrupt transition from the luncheon to Mitri’s history is especially effective: it breaks away from privileged conversation and reconstructs the hidden labouring world that polite society treats as background.

Characterisation is social and moral rather than psychologically intricate in a modernist sense. Figures are identified through occupation, income, property, family role, political opinion, bodily appearance, and relation to labour. Volgin is not presented as exceptionally cruel; his blindness is more disturbing because he is ordinary, respectable, mildly liberal, and capable of pity. Mitri is likewise not sentimentalised. His poverty, drinking, quarrel with his wife, religious habits, weariness, and willingness to work are all included. This refusal to make the poor morally flawless supports the introductory claim that justice requires neither hatred of the rich nor romanticisation of the oppressed.

Dialogue is plain, brief, and socially revealing. Upper-class conversation is evasive, trivial, or performative, while peasant speech is economical and tied to immediate needs. Forms of address, deferential answers, and physical gestures such as kneeling or touching the forehead to the ground expose hierarchy without requiring elaborate explanation. Small moments of interior commentary—particularly the coachman’s unspoken irritation—temporarily open perspectives that privileged characters ignore.

The pacing is deliberately uneven. Mundane elite routines are narrated with almost excessive slowness, making leisure appear burdensome, wasteful, and dependent on invisible service. Peasant labour is also described minutely, but there the density communicates necessity, fatigue, reciprocity, and uninterrupted effort. Plot in the conventional sense is minimal; the story advances through observation, comparison, and moral revelation rather than suspense or decisive action.

The dominant tone combines compassion, disgust, sorrow, irony, and prophetic accusation. Images of polished silver, lacquered carriages, immaculate linen, and well-groomed bodies recur beside dirt, smoke, hunger, damaged clothing, dead animals, and strained bodies. Religious language is used ambivalently: institutional or habitual Christianity may be empty, yet the demand to recognise and help one’s neighbour remains the story’s ultimate moral standard. The closing passage abandons much of the earlier scenic restraint and becomes explicitly polemical, expanding from individual examples to whole social classes. Its final rhetorical question converts the story from representation into accusation, forcing the reader to confront not ignorance alone but the cultivated habit of “seeing and yet not seeing.”

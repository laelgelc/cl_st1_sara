In a coffee-house in Surat, travelers of many religions begin arguing over whose conception of God and form of worship is uniquely true. A Persian theologian who has reasoned himself into disbelief questions his slave, whose faith rests in a wooden idol. A Brahmin, a Jew, Catholic and Protestant Christians, a Sunni Turk, and adherents of other creeds each claim exclusive religious truth and salvation.

Asked to judge the dispute, a quiet Chinese student of Confucius tells a parable. On Sumatra, several men debate the nature and movement of the sun. A blind man denies its existence, a slave identifies it with his small lamp, and others interpret it according to their limited experience, local traditions, or travels. Finally, a knowledgeable pilot explains that the earth revolves around the sun, which illuminates far more than any single land or people.

The student compares the sun to God, arguing that pride and narrow experience cause people to imagine the divine belongs exclusively to their nation or religion. He presents the whole world as God’s temple and goodness, mercy, love, and self-sacrifice as the truest worship. He urges the listeners not to despise those who perceive only part of the truth—or even those unable to perceive it at all. Humbled by his lesson, everyone falls silent and ends the dispute.

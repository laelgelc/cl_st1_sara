The story uses a late nineteenth-century urban-naturalist style, combining close social observation with grotesque, impressionistic imagery. Its language moves between elevated, almost ceremonial narration and the phonetic slang of impoverished wanderers. This contrast is central to the effect: cheap beer becomes “dark and portentous,” a soup server presides “like a priest behind an altar,” and a shabby lodging house acquires the symbolic dimensions of a tomb, graveyard, or underworld. The grand diction does not romanticise poverty so much as expose its misery through irony, black comedy, and estrangement.

The narration is third-person and largely focalised through the unnamed youth. Although the narrator occasionally makes broad interpretive statements, the city and its inhabitants are usually rendered as the youth experiences them. External sensations gradually become psychological and social meanings: rain produces dejection, the lodging house embodies collective suffering, and the city’s buildings come to represent a nation indifferent to those at its feet. The protagonist remains socially anonymous, designated mainly as “the youth” or “the young man,” while his companion is repeatedly called “the assassin” on the basis of his appearance. Such labels give the figures an emblematic quality while also reproducing the snap judgments of street life.

Description is dense, visual, and highly figurative. The palette is dominated by steel, blue, yellow, black, purple, grey, tawny, and red, creating a nocturnal atmosphere of wet light, grime, flesh, and shadow. Light frequently cuts through darkness in sharp, active forms: lamps glitter like embroidery, dawn enters as a “lance-point,” and sunlight becomes “bright spears.” The prose also attends strongly to smell, temperature, texture, and sound. Wet cloth presses against the neck, leather cots feel like melting snow, foul odors attack like winged diseases, trains grind overhead, gongs cry, and sleepers grunt, snore, or howl. This sensory accumulation makes deprivation bodily and immediate.

Personification and zoomorphic imagery are especially prominent. The saloon is a voracious creature whose doors are “ravenous lips”; cable cars possess calm, irresistible power; the elevated station resembles a monstrous crab; sleeping men heave like stabbed fish; and the city itself becomes an aloof, regal being. Human figures are likewise compared to chickens, corpses, statues, sacrifices, chiefs, and malformed objects. These comparisons create a grotesque urban world in which boundaries between people, animals, machines, buildings, and institutions become unstable. The city appears both mechanically indifferent and monstrously alive.

Sentence structure alternates between long, accumulative descriptive periods and short, blunt exchanges of dialogue. The extended sentences often pile up participial phrases, parallel clauses, and catalogues, reproducing the crowdedness and ceaseless motion of the streets or the oppressive density of the lodging room. Short sentences puncture these passages with hard comic or emotional beats. Repetition gives key scenes rhetorical force, especially in descriptions of bodies, odors, shadows, and social suffering. At climactic moments, the prose expands into abstract, oratorical cadences, as when one sleeper’s cry is interpreted as the voice of “a whole section, a class, a people.”

Dialogue is rendered through heavy eye dialect, contractions, dropped consonants, altered spelling, and working-class vocabulary: “yeh,” “git,” “anudder,” “b’ Gawd,” and “hully.” This gives the speech speed, texture, and social specificity. The characters’ talk is often rambling, repetitive, boastful, or ritualised. They insist on one another’s respectability and virtue precisely because their social position denies them those recognitions. The assassin’s exaggerated oaths, confused reminiscences, and astonishment at a white nightshirt provide comic relief, but the humour remains edged with hunger, alcoholism, insecurity, and humiliation.

The tone continually blends pity with detachment, comedy with horror, and lyricism with satire. Poverty is not treated sentimentally: bodies are dirty, deformed, drunken, malodorous, and sometimes ridiculous. Yet the narrator also grants the outcasts moments of dignity, fellowship, and symbolic magnitude. Small acts of generosity are disguised as loans or practical bargains because open charity might threaten the men’s pride. Humour therefore becomes both a survival mechanism and a means of revealing their vulnerability.

Structurally, the story follows a loose episodic descent and partial return. The youth moves from the respectable crowd into poorer districts, is swallowed by a saloon, descends into a lodging house figured as a tomb, emerges into daylight, shares breakfast, and finally returns to City Hall Park. The movement is circular rather than progressive: food, shelter, and companionship offer temporary relief but no escape. Night briefly transforms the lodging house into a visionary image of collective doom; daylight makes it “commonplace and uninteresting,” showing how ordinary such suffering has become.

The closing style grows explicitly symbolic and socially critical. Well-dressed pedestrians form an impersonal frieze, buildings become emblems of national aspiration, and the city’s roar turns into the “clink of coin.” The youth’s exclusion is presented not merely as personal bad luck but as a condition produced by an indifferent social order. The final image internalises that exclusion: once he accepts the identity of outcast, his gaze becomes guilty and “criminal.” Overall, the style is characterised by naturalistic social determinism, impressionistic city description, grotesque metaphor, ironic elevation, vernacular dialogue, and a compassionate but unsparing portrayal of urban homelessness.

The story uses a polished, economical comic style built on irony, social satire, and the reversal of conventional moral expectations. Its tone is urbane, dry, and mischievously detached. The narrator rarely states a judgment bluntly; instead, ridicule emerges through carefully chosen modifiers such as “weakly,” “fatuously,” “lamely,” and “deplorably uninteresting.” This creates a strong contrast between the aunt’s view of herself as a responsible moral educator and the narrative evidence of her incompetence. The humour is understated rather than exuberant, with absurdities presented in a composed, matter-of-fact voice.

The narration is third-person and largely omniscient, moving lightly among the bachelor’s perceptions, the aunt’s assumptions, and the children’s reactions. The narrator maintains enough distance to treat all the characters as social types: the prim and unimaginative aunt, the observant but outwardly unsympathetic bachelor, and the relentlessly inquisitive children. Characterisation is rapid and indirect. The aunt is exposed through evasive answers and dull storytelling; the children through repetitive questions, blunt verdicts, and appetite for concrete or gruesome detail; the bachelor through silence, observation, and finally his skill at adapting a story to his audience. Even minor physical actions—the aunt bristling, the bachelor looking at the communication cord, or the girl repeating one line of a poem—function as compact comic portraits.

Dialogue dominates the story and drives both its pacing and its satire. The children’s recurring “Why?” questions create a refrain that tests the adults’ inventiveness and exposes weak reasoning. Their speech is direct, literal, and logically disruptive: they notice inconsistencies that polite adult discourse prefers to ignore. The aunt answers with clichés, distractions, and moral abstractions, whereas the bachelor responds promptly with vivid, specific inventions. His tale accommodates interruptions rather than resisting them, so the children’s questions become part of its creative development. Repetition is used extensively for rhythm and comedy, especially in phrases such as “horribly good,” in the catalogue of Bertha’s virtues, and in the recurring clinking of her medals.

The diction combines formal Edwardian prose with precise comic exaggeration. Elevated or ceremonious wording—“dissentient opinion,” “preparatory to leaving,” “unspeakable ferocity”—is often applied to trivial or ridiculous circumstances, producing mock seriousness. Adverbs and qualifying phrases sharpen the irony, while similes are vivid and sardonic, as when the family’s conversation is compared to “the attentions of a housefly that refuses to be discouraged.” Descriptive detail is selective rather than lush. Concrete colours, sounds, animals, and objects—the multicoloured pigs, the wolf’s black tongue and pale grey eyes, the spotless pinafore, and the clicking medals—give the bachelor’s embedded tale sensory force. Sound is particularly important: the repeated recitation torments the carriage, while the medals’ clinking first signifies virtue and then causes Bertha’s death.

Sentence structure alternates between balanced, elaborately coordinated narration and short, decisive dialogue. The opening sentence accumulates clauses to reproduce the oppressive length and discomfort of the journey. Lists and parallel constructions provide comic momentum, while abrupt questions interrupt adult explanations and prevent them from settling into authority. The bachelor’s story uses traditional fairy-tale formulas and cumulative description, but continually contaminates them with incongruous details: goodness is “horrible,” medals are ostentatious metal objects, a royal park contains pigs instead of flowers, and exemplary conduct leads not to rescue but to destruction.

Structurally, the narrative is a framed story that stages a contest between two kinds of storytelling. The outer railway episode establishes boredom, frustration, and failed adult control. The aunt’s embedded tale represents conventional didactic fiction: a virtuous child is rewarded and saved because others admire her goodness. The bachelor’s tale begins in apparently similar fashion, then turns the moral machinery against itself. Each emblem of Bertha’s virtue becomes a liability: her clean clothing makes her visible, her privileged access places her in danger, and her medals reveal her hiding place. The ending returns to the carriage, where the children’s delighted response validates narrative excitement over approved morality, while the aunt condemns the story precisely because it succeeds.

The pacing is carefully escalated. The first section lingers comically over repetition and irritation; the aunt’s story is compressed because its dullness is the point; the bachelor’s tale expands through questions, colourful inventories, suspense, and violent climax. The wolf’s approach slows the tempo through sniffing, hiding, trembling, and repeated clinking, before the final attack is delivered swiftly. The conclusion then adds a second punch line: the bachelor has not merely entertained the children but has furnished them with a new demand with which to torment their aunt.

Thematically, the style favours imaginative vitality, intellectual honesty, and responsiveness to an audience over rigid moral instruction. It satirises the sentimental assumption that children naturally prefer improving tales and suggests that they are more attracted to novelty, logic, danger, and poetic justice. The story’s central method is paradox: the “improper” tale proves socially effective, while the proper tale fails; the supposedly unsympathetic bachelor understands the children, while their responsible guardian does not; and goodness, conventionally a source of protection, becomes the direct cause of death. The resulting style is concise, epigrammatic, darkly comic, and controlled, with cruelty transformed into wit by narrative symmetry and ironic precision.

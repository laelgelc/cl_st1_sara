The story is written as a light Edwardian society romance with elements of comedy, melodrama, and concealed-identity intrigue. Its plot follows a tightly controlled courtship pattern: a spirited young woman rejects an apparently domineering aristocrat, becomes attracted to a seemingly uncivilised stranger, and discovers that the stranger is the same man in disguise. Social embarrassment, romantic rivalry, physical danger, and revelation steadily intensify the action, but the tone remains reassuringly playful and the ending fulfils the expectations of romantic comedy.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, moving between Merrivale, Hilary, servants, relatives, and club acquaintances while giving Hilary the greatest emotional prominence. The narrator reports outward behaviour with crisp confidence and enters characters’ thoughts selectively, often through brief free-indirect observations such as Hilary’s private judgments of the “wild man.” Emotional states are frequently conveyed through visible signs—flushing cheeks, trembling hands, steadfast eyes, abrupt movements, pauses, and changes of voice—rather than through prolonged psychological analysis. This makes the prose immediate and theatrical while preserving the characters’ emotional reticence.

The diction combines polished upper-class formality with colloquial wit. Titles, surnames, clubs, servants, house-boats, punts, and society columns establish an aristocratic English milieu, while phrases such as “poor old pater,” “what ho,” “twopence,” “Great Scotland,” and “as cool as a cucumber” create a period-specific conversational liveliness. Class and nationality are marked through speech: Reynolds uses comic nonstandard grammar, Merrivale’s assumed persona speaks careful English with a “foreign” or Western inflection, and Hilary’s language is quick, fashionable, teasing, and imperious. The prose also employs period romance vocabulary—“homage,” “courtly,” “wistfulness,” “quivering delight”—especially when the emotional register rises.

Sentence structure varies according to function. Expository passages generally use clear, balanced, moderately long sentences, whereas moments of shock or confrontation break into short clauses, fragments, exclamations, and interruptions. Dashes are especially prominent, representing hesitation, self-correction, emotional pressure, or rapid shifts of thought. Dialogue is plentiful and carries much of the characterisation and plot. Exchanges often take the form of verbal fencing: Hilary asserts her independence, Bertie teases her, Fletcher provokes her, and Merrivale answers with controlled understatement. Repetition and echoing are used for comic and dramatic effect, most notably in the rival newspaper announcements and in the recurring language of freedom, tyranny, and love.

The tone rests on dramatic irony. Readers are given enough clues to suspect Jacques’s identity well before Hilary recognises him: he is called Jack, comes from the West, possesses unusual strength, reacts knowingly to Fletcher, and laughs when Hilary complains that “Jacques” is too commonplace because so many men are called Jack. The disguise itself is deliberately broad—brown beard, brown clothes, foreign drawl, backwoodsman manners—and belongs to the conventions of popular romantic fiction rather than strict realism. Culver and Bertie function partly as amused stage-managers, sustaining the deception and turning Hilary’s prejudices into a private joke shared with the reader.

Characterisation is vivid but archetypal. Hilary is the impulsive, witty, independent heroine whose confidence conceals emotional inexperience. Her repeated insistence on freedom prepares the central reversal: she rejects social and marital constraint until she discovers love on terms that feel voluntarily chosen. Merrivale embodies controlled masculinity—physically powerful, taciturn, courteous, and emotionally steadfast. His apparent tyranny is recast as decisiveness and devotion, while his disguise allows Hilary to encounter him without the distorting influence of title or prior resentment. Fletcher serves as the sneering, possessive antagonist; Bertie and Culver supply affectionate comedy; and minor figures such as Reynolds and the clubmen create a chorus of gossip and speculation.

Setting plays a strong tonal role. London is associated with newspapers, clubs, social surveillance, titles, and formal expectations. The river, by contrast, becomes a pastoral space of loosened convention, intimacy, and transformation. Its summer light, willows, roses, moonlit water, and secluded reaches provide a sensuous romantic atmosphere. Nature is gently personified: trees whisper, roses nod, water laughs, and the world “was awake and singing.” These lyrical passages are more ornate and rhythmic than the social scenes, marking Hilary’s awakening desire and lending emotional legitimacy to her attraction.

Pacing is brisk and episodic. Asterism breaks divide the story into sharply defined scenes, each built around a fresh turn: the cancelled marriage notice, Merrivale’s counter-announcement, Hilary’s arrival on the river, the meeting with Jacques, the moonlit punt journey, Fletcher’s challenge, the fire, and the unmasking. Exposition is economical, and transitions often leap over days or weeks. Suspense depends less on whether the couple will unite than on when Hilary will discover the deception and how she will respond. The fire scene provides the melodramatic climax that exposes Merrivale’s tattooed hand, proves his courage, and converts Hilary’s attraction into conscious love.

Recurring motifs bind the narrative together. Newspaper announcements frame the story and turn private romance into public spectacle. Hands signify agency and identity: Hilary acts independently, Merrivale’s hand saves her and reveals him, and their final union is enacted through touch and embrace. Faces, beards, and hair dramatise the contrast between outward appearance and inner character. Freedom is the dominant verbal motif, initially treated as freedom from male authority and finally redefined as something less desirable than freely accepted love. The opposition between civilisation and wildness is similarly inverted: fashionable Fletcher behaves brutally, while the supposed “wild man” displays restraint, courtesy, and self-sacrifice.

Humour arises from understatement, affectionate mockery, reversals, and exaggerated social types. Merrivale responds to public humiliation by calmly drinking coffee; Reynolds measures heartbreak by the number of eggs eaten; clubmen turn scandal into sport; and Hilary’s demand for independence culminates in her embracing the very man she called a tyrant. The final phrase, “You hairless monster,” converts the revelation into intimate comedy and releases the accumulated tension. Overall, the style is elegant, fast-moving, dialogue-led, and emotionally accessible, blending drawing-room wit with river idyll, masculine adventure, and a conventionally satisfying romantic reversal.

The story is written as a rugged northern adventure tale, combining naturalistic survival fiction with an ironic contest of character. Its central stylistic tension is established through the opposition between Pierre’s physical vitality and Willard’s faith in intellect and willpower. The narrative initially presents this as a simple argument between body and mind, then tests both positions through escalating ordeals before resolving them with comic irony: each man survives through qualities he had associated with the other, while Pierre ultimately claims educated endurance for himself.

The narration is third-person and largely aligned with Willard’s perceptions, although it occasionally broadens into an authoritative, proverbial voice. Generalisations such as “he who rides with Pierre ‘Feroce’ takes chances” and “It is in such black, aimless times that thought becomes distorted” give the narrator the air of an experienced observer explaining the laws of northern life. The point of view becomes more intensely subjective as exhaustion and exposure impair Willard’s mind. Concrete observation then gives way to fractured awareness, “bleak dreams,” compulsive recollection, personified suffering, and intermittent lucidity. This movement from panoramic narration into delirious consciousness allows the prose itself to register physical and psychological collapse.

The diction is elevated, vigorous, and often archaic or melodramatic. Words such as “scintillant,” “vitiated,” “suscitating,” “maelstrom,” “efflorescent,” and “maundering” lend the narration a literary richness well above the plain speech of the characters. The prose favours forceful verbs—snow “seethed,” wind “sucked,” clouds “boiled,” and cold “smote”—which make the environment feel violently active. There is also specialised frontier vocabulary involving sledges, snow-shoes, overflows, caches, malamoots, sleeping-bags, and road houses. These practical details establish material authenticity while the heightened language transforms the journey into an elemental ordeal.

Description is highly visual and kinetic. The Arctic landscape is rendered through whiteness, glare, crystalline brightness, cutting edges, and immense empty distances. Similes repeatedly draw on weapons and machinery: sled runners whine “like the song of a cutlass,” the gale cuts “like well-honed steel,” and the wind moves like “the breath of a giant bellows.” At other moments the imagery becomes cosmic, with cold air compared to “interstellar space” and the storm imagined as descending from “voids of sunless space.” Such comparisons enlarge a regional journey into a struggle against an almost metaphysical force. The landscape is beautiful but hostile, producing exhilaration, dread, fascination, and madness in quick succession.

Nature is strongly personified. The silence “oppressed like a weight,” hunger “gnawed,” frost “crept,” and the gale possesses “full hate.” Wind is the dominant antagonist: it smells, whispers, gathers intention, distorts distance, shouts through the night, and finally attacks. The Ass’s Ears function as a folkloric warning and as a visual omen, linking Indigenous belief with the later storm. This sustained animation of the environment makes the setting more than a backdrop; it becomes the principal agent of the plot and the impartial judge of the men’s competing philosophies.

Sentence structure varies with narrative pressure. Early descriptive passages use long, balanced sentences packed with participial phrases and accumulating clauses, creating breadth and forward sweep. During storms and moments of danger, the prose becomes more compressed, with abrupt statements, exclamations, and sequential actions. Repetition is used emphatically—“always the whiteness; always the listening silence,” “wind—wind,” and “black, black eyes”—to convey obsession and sensory extremity. Semicolons and dashes frequently control long accumulations, while short paragraphs isolate shocks, reversals, and decisive discoveries. The rhythm therefore alternates between panoramic expansiveness and hard, urgent propulsion.

Dialogue sharply differentiates the protagonists. Willard speaks in polished, abstract, argumentative English, invoking mentality, education, culture, soldiers, endurance races, and Roman spectacles. Pierre’s speech is represented through heavy phonetic spelling, French interjections, altered grammar, and comic malapropism: “By Gar,” “Mon Dieu,” “the heducate man,” and “stan’ the ’ardsheep.” This dialect gives him an immediately recognisable voice and supplies much of the story’s humour, though it also reflects an older literary convention that caricatures French-Canadian speech for an Anglophone readership. Pierre’s dialogue is blunt, physical, and mocking, whereas Willard’s is analytical and self-conscious. Under extreme suffering, however, both voices deteriorate into curses, laughter, muttering, and fragmentary speech, showing language itself breaking down under bodily pressure.

Characterisation is built through contrast, action, and reversal rather than extended psychological explanation. Pierre is introduced through aggression, sensual power, cruelty, noise, and animal imagery; he casts the air “like a wild beast” and dominates his dogs through violence. Willard appears pale, refined, amused, and physically slight, yet gradually proves practical, stoical, and tenacious. Their traits are not merely opposed but interwoven. Pierre possesses knowledge, instinct, and endurance as well as strength, while Willard’s mental theory manifests through brutally physical persistence. The dogs serve as extensions of the men’s condition: initially magnificent and joyful, they become blood-footed, starving, and barely movable as the expedition deteriorates.

The plot uses cumulative escalation. A hazardous but exhilarating journey gives way to overflows, a four-day storm, frozen dogs, stolen provisions, injury, hunger, snow-blindness, another storm, madness, and apparent death. Each ordeal removes another layer of security. Food, shelter, sight, bodily sensation, rational thought, and companionship are progressively stripped away. Foreshadowing is prominent: Pierre predicts that Willard will have a chance to demonstrate his will; the Ass’s Ears warn of wind; the cached gum-boot and missing food stress dependence on fragile preparations; and the early debate is repeatedly recalled as the men fail. The storm’s approach is especially gradual and cinematic, beginning with gauzy streamers around distant peaks and ending in a sudden engulfing blast.

Tone shifts between admiration, menace, grim humour, and grotesque horror. The opening celebrates dogs, speed, health, and Arctic beauty, but this vitality steadily contracts into starvation and mutilation. Humour survives through understatement and verbal incongruity, as when Willard jokes about needing “a habeas corpus” for the sinking stove. Later, laughter becomes a symptom of madness: Pierre’s laughter is “horrible,” and Willard reaches the road house cackling at his own maxim. The final scene restores comedy, but it remains darkly grounded in frostbite and trauma. Pierre’s insistence that he and Willard are “heducate mans” turns the original argument inside out and closes the story with affectionate satire rather than a solemn philosophical verdict.

Overall, the style is muscular, ornate, and sensation-driven, characteristic of early frontier and Arctic adventure fiction. It combines documentary detail with romantic sublimity, naturalistic bodily suffering, masculine rivalry, dialect comedy, and a tightly prepared ironic ending. Its most distinctive effect comes from setting elaborate, image-rich narration against rough spoken voices, then using the hostile landscape to erode the apparent divide between intellect and physique.

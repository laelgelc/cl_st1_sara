The story adopts a nineteenth-century moral-satirical mode, combining social realism, philosophical reflection, sentimental pathos, and bitter irony. Its governing method is to begin with reassuring euphemisms about poverty—“Poor Man’s Manure,” “Poor Man’s Egg,” “Poor Man’s Pudding,” and “Rich Man’s Crumbs”—and then expose the material suffering concealed by such benevolent language. The title phrases function as recurring verbal motifs and as instruments of critique: what sounds picturesque or providential to the comfortable becomes degrading, inadequate, or repulsive when encountered by the poor themselves.

The narrative is delivered retrospectively in the first person by an educated, observant traveller. This narrator is both participant and witness, entering impoverished spaces while retaining the manners, vocabulary, and economic security of a gentleman. His distance from the poor is openly registered through his clothing, physical discomfort, inability to eat their food, and freedom to leave. At the same time, his outlook changes through direct experience. Blandmour’s optimistic abstractions are tested against the Coulters’ domestic life, while the civic guide’s praise of London charity is tested against the violent distribution of aristocratic leftovers. The narrator’s irony is therefore both outwardly satirical and inwardly corrective: he learns to distrust consoling ideas that cost their speakers nothing.

Dialogue drives much of the story. Blandmour and the London guide speak in enthusiastic, formulaic praise of Nature or charity, repeatedly offering the narrator openings for dry, understated rebuttals. His responses often mimic agreement while quietly revealing contradictions: snow is called universally beneficent even as it blows from a poor farmer’s field onto a rich man’s; royal leftovers are called generous even though the hungry receive only mutilated scraps. This Socratic pattern allows social criticism to emerge through question, answer, qualification, and ironic echo rather than through immediate authorial denunciation. By contrast, the Coulters’ speech is plain, affectionate, practical, and regionally inflected, with forms such as “ain’t,” “rheumatiz,” “nighest,” and “whimseys.” Their language humanises them without idealising their circumstances.

The diction moves between elevated Latinate abstraction and concrete physical detail. Phrases such as “blessed almoner,” “beneficent,” “fertilizingly moistened,” and “peculiar social sensibilities” evoke sermons, philanthropy, political argument, and genteel essay-writing. Against this ornate register stand soaked boards, patched boots, mossy sticks, rank pork, damaged rice, bare floors, fouled tables, and torn clothing. The clash between rhetorical grandeur and sensory ugliness is central to the satire. Euphemistic abstractions belong mainly to those who theorise about suffering; textures, smells, cold, dampness, hunger, and bodily exhaustion reveal what suffering actually is.

Syntax is frequently elaborate and periodic, with long sentences built from subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, semicolons, dashes, and accumulative lists. The narrator often suspends the main point while adding moral or psychological refinements, producing a meditative, essayistic cadence. At moments of crisis, however, the prose contracts into fragments, commands, and abrupt exclamations: “Wedge—wedge—in quick,” “hold! jam!” This shift accelerates the pace and reproduces confusion or panic. Repetition is prominent, especially in phrases such as “damp, damp, damp,” “still, still,” and “The Squire! the Squire!” It intensifies atmosphere, grief, urgency, and obsession.

The imagery is strongly elemental and bodily. Wetness dominates the rural section: melting snow, soaked ground, oozing boots, damp fuel, clouded panes, dripping roofs, and grief that “drizzles and drizzles down” on the soul. Material dampness becomes an objective correlative for poverty, illness, bereavement, and emotional enclosure. The food imagery is equally important. The pudding’s mouldy, briny taste condenses the Coulters’ deprivation into a single mouthful, while the gouged pheasants, hollow pasties, and half-consumed jellies turn aristocratic luxury into grotesque remains. Eating is never merely domestic; it exposes class relations, dependence, waste, and humiliation.

The descriptions frequently personify objects and environments. An old chair has an “enfeebled constitution,” a house is “constitutionally damp,” firewood hisses and splutters vainly, and banners appear to look down upon beggars as Dives looks upon Lazarus. Such personification gives physical settings a moral charge. Biblical allusion supplies a recurring ethical vocabulary: the Psalmist’s snow, Dives and Lazarus, the Pit and the Lost, and the narrator’s final appeal to Heaven all place social inequality within a framework of judgment. Historical and political references—to Napoleon, Waterloo, the Prince Regent, Wellington, Alexander of Russia, and the Cossacks—enlarge the critique from one household to an international order celebrating victory and splendour while hunger persists beneath it.

The story’s structure is diptych-like. “Poor Man’s Pudding” presents rural American poverty as quiet, proud, domestic, and sorrowful; “Rich Man’s Crumbs” presents metropolitan English poverty as crowded, enraged, spectacular, and nearly animalised by hunger. The two halves mirror one another through guided visits, supposedly charitable provisions, revolting food, and the narrator’s growing disillusionment. The first moves slowly inward, from a snowy road into an intimate kitchen and then into a grieving mother’s confidence. The second moves through a compressed urban crowd into Guildhall, culminating in riotous motion and escape. This contrast gives the work both emotional and dramatic range.

Tone is unstable by design. It begins with genial banter and comic scepticism, deepens into embarrassment and compassion, expands into explicit political commentary, then becomes grotesque, nightmarish, and sardonic. Humour is often verbal and deadpan, but it darkens as repeated jokes about “poor man’s” substitutes reveal their cruelty. Sentiment enters most strongly through the Coulters’ marriage and the deaths of their children, yet it is checked by restraint: the narrator often falls silent because speech would cheapen grief. The poor are accorded dignity through hospitality, loyalty, pride, and emotional delicacy, while the comfortable are criticised for converting deprivation into a picturesque theory or public spectacle.

The narration periodically leaves the scene to deliver direct social analysis. These essayistic passages articulate the story’s central claim: privileged observers misread the habits of the poor because they judge from conditions of warmth, shelter, and satiety. Even apparently imprudent behaviour, such as refusing ventilation, is reinterpreted as a rational response to cold. Poverty is thus represented not as personal failure but as an embodied condition intensified by labour relations, social hierarchy, and the humiliating forms taken by charity.

Overall, the style is rhetorically ornate yet sensuously concrete, compassionate yet caustic, and comic in a way that steadily turns accusatory. Its characteristic movement is from euphemism to actuality, abstraction to embodiment, picturesque benevolence to moral disgust. The final effect is anti-consolatory: poverty cannot be redeemed by pretty names, providential maxims, aristocratic leftovers, or admiration from those who remain comfortably outside it.

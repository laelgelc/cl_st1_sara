The story is written in the manner of a traditional oral wonder tale, combining fairy-tale simplicity with grotesque exaggeration, broad comedy, and episodic adventure. It opens with the conventional formula “Once on a time” and proceeds as though recited aloud, relying on familiar transitions such as “So,” “Well,” “At last,” and “Then.” The narrator is omniscient but unobtrusive, reporting events directly rather than exploring psychology. Characters are defined by fixed traits and social roles—the king, the farmer, the troll, Old Nick, the goodman—while Grumblegizzard is governed by two comic attributes: limitless strength and limitless appetite.

The diction is deliberately archaic, rustic, and regionally coloured. Words such as “bairn,” “brose,” “goodman,” “grange,” “grieve,” “fell,” “scrip,” “trow,” and “smithied” evoke an older rural world and preserve the flavour of a translated or adapted Scandinavian folktale. Contractions and idiomatic turns—“’Twas,” “I’ve not known,” “no mistake,” “out of house and home”—give the prose a conversational quality. Religious and folkloric euphemisms, especially “Old Nick” and “the Old One,” treat the devil as a familiar comic antagonist rather than an embodiment of metaphysical evil. The syntax alternates between long, loosely coordinated narrative sentences and short, emphatic dialogue. Clauses are often linked by “and,” “but,” and “so,” reproducing the cumulative rhythm of spoken storytelling: one feat leads immediately to the next with little pause for description or reflection. Repetition is central to the cadence. Phrases such as “sat and sat,” “drank and drank,” “change and change about,” and “both far and long” create an incantatory, childlike momentum. Parallel constructions and escalating lists likewise reinforce the tale’s oral character, as in sequences of increasingly heavy tools, larger meals, greater loads, or repeated failed attempts by horses and men.

Dialogue is brisk, formulaic, and usually comic. Speakers state their desires plainly, issue impossible commands, boast, bargain, or respond with deadpan understatement. Grumblegizzard’s speech is especially laconic: fantastic dangers are reduced to ordinary nuisances, bullets become “bilberries,” cannons become “big peashooters,” and enormous weapons are dismissed as suitable only for cracking nuts or nailing shoes. This mismatch between the scale of events and the casualness of his reactions produces much of the humour. Dialogue also serves as a refrain, most notably in the repeated demand for “Herrings and brose, porridge and milk” and the recurring question, “What shall I do now?”

The narrative structure is linear, cumulative, and highly episodic. Grumblegizzard moves from one employer or adversary to another, and each episode follows a similar pattern: he receives a task intended either as ordinary labour or as a means of destroying him; he performs it with excessive literalness and superhuman force; his success causes even greater disruption; and his frightened master sends him toward a more dangerous trial. Challenges escalate from farm work to warfare, hell, trolls, haunted castles, and a bargain with the devil. Repetition of this pattern gives the story coherence, while continuous amplification keeps it energetic.

Hyperbole is the dominant figurative device. Eggs are as large as heads, stones contain horse-loads, meals require twelve barrels of meal, clubs weigh fifteen tons, and blows shake hills or scatter armies like chaff. Numbers are symbolic rather than realistic, with folkloric quantities such as five, seven, twelve, and fifteen recurring throughout. Similes are concrete and agrarian—enemies fly “like chaff,” the milkmaid resembles a “big fir-stump,” and felled trees lie like windfalls. Violence is similarly exaggerated and emotionally flattened: men are slain, horses lose their heads, trolls are decapitated, and witches burst, yet these events are narrated without pathos. The result is comic grotesque rather than realism or horror.

The tone is exuberant, mischievous, and morally irreverent. Authority figures are cowardly, deceitful, and self-interested; the king repeatedly tries to dispose of the hero and then evade payment. Grumblegizzard is not conventionally virtuous—he is destructive, gluttonous, and casually violent—but his directness and ingenuity make him the tale’s comic champion. The story favours physical power, literal-mindedness, appetite, and trickery over refinement or institutional authority. Its ending uses the traditional tall-tale formula that an impossible condition may persist “to this very day,” closing on an image of the dishonest king suspended between heaven and earth. Overall, the style is that of a robust folk narrative: fast-moving, repetitive, orally inflected, fantastically exaggerated, and driven by deadpan humour rather than psychological depth.

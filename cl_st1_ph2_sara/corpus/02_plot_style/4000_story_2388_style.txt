The story combines a lush, Romantic frame narrative with an exuberant imitation of Irish oral storytelling. It opens in a reflective, highly pictorial register, directly inviting the reader to “fancy” the scene. The landscape is rendered through long, flowing sentences, layered visual detail, and elegiac personification: evening air is “saddened,” trees spread their arms in “everlasting love and sorrow,” and streams reflect the “funereal glories” of the sky. Colour, sound, mist, vegetation, ruins, and distant landmarks create a pastoral atmosphere tinged with melancholy. The narrator is conspicuously present, addressing the reader and explaining the circumstances in which the embedded tale is heard. This framing gives the piece the character of a story collected from a local speaker rather than an impersonal written fiction.

Once the storyteller begins, the prose shifts sharply from polished literary description to energetic vernacular speech. Irish English is represented through extensive eye dialect and phonetic spelling, including forms such as “an’,” “wid,” “ould,” “begorra,” “divarshin,” “sperits,” “Frinch,” and “convarse.” Grammatical constructions imitate colloquial speech as well as pronunciation: “it’s what he’d be wishing,” “who should he fall in wid,” “away wid himself,” and “the divil a taste of property” reproduce idiomatic rhythms associated with Hiberno-English. Recurrent expressions such as “Well,” “my dear,” “do ye mind,” “sure enough,” “bedad,” and “says he” serve as oral-performance markers, continually renewing contact with the listener. The spelling is not naturalistic in a modern linguistic sense; it is a deliberately theatrical, nineteenth-century literary representation of brogue, intended to make the narrator’s voice audible on the page.

The embedded narration is digressive, repetitive, and cumulative. Events are frequently delayed by asides, parenthetical memories, comic qualifications, inventories, and miniature arguments. The storyteller may interrupt himself to remember a former sweetheart, enumerate a bride’s fortune down to pigs and cookware, or comment generally on love, drink, marriage, and human foolishness. Repetition is central to the rhythm. Dialogue repeatedly uses the tag “says he” or “says she” after nearly every clause, and sequences such as “talkin’ an’ whisperin’, an’ whisperin’ an’ talkin’” mimic the momentum of live narration. Sentences often accumulate through strings of coordinating conjunctions, especially “and” and “an’,” producing a breathless, additive movement rather than tightly subordinated written syntax.

The dominant tone is comic, affectionate, and knowingly extravagant. Humour arises from hyperbole, antic comparison, bathos, misunderstanding, and the contrast between elevated subjects and homely details. Love is declared stronger than whisky, the sea, and death, yet is also likened to toothache and colic. Billy’s military career is inflated into mock-epic legend: Napoleon supposedly regards him as more dangerous than Wellington, entire forces are sent against him, and his apparent death results from his pausing to enjoy “Garryowen” while smoking. Historical figures and warfare are domesticated through village idiom, pub logic, and absurd strategic dialogue. The storyteller’s unreliable exaggeration is part of the entertainment; factual credibility matters less than comic conviction and narrative flourish.

The story repeatedly blends sentiment with farce. Molly’s faithful mourning and reunion with Billy are treated with genuine tenderness, supported by soft evening imagery, birdsong, moonlight, and nostalgic references to “ould times.” Yet the reunion rapidly becomes a comic concealment plot involving a crowded pigsty, a closet, drunken singing, ominous snoring, mistaken resurrection, clerical panic, and ineffective Latin exorcism. Potentially solemn material—death, war, religion, bereavement, and marriage—is continually redirected into communal comedy without wholly losing its emotional warmth. This balance reflects the frame narrator’s description of the Irish brogue as equally capable of leaving “a burden of mirth or of sorrow.”

Characterisation is broad, vivid, and performance-oriented. Figures are established through memorable physical details, verbal habits, comic obsessions, and social roles rather than sustained psychological analysis. Billy is charming, reckless, romantic, boastful, and action-driven; Molly is tender and constant; her parents are practical, superstitious, and highly responsive to money; Father O’Flaherty combines clerical authority with physical cowardice and comic profanity. Dialogue carries much of the characterisation, with each confrontation shaped as a rapid exchange of threats, evasions, blessings, curses, and formulaic repetitions.

The narrative structure resembles a folktale or fireside anecdote. A courtship is obstructed by parents, a quarrel leads to separation, the hero departs for war, false news of his death reaches home, and he returns unexpectedly to claim the faithful beloved. The final sequence turns on mistaken identity and supernatural fear before resolving in marriage, feasting, drunken mishaps, and prolific domestic continuity. Transitions are loose and formulaic—often beginning “Well” or “An’ so”—while suspense is generated through delayed revelation and escalating complications. The ending expands beyond the wedding into a communal catalogue of excess, giving the tale the rounded, celebratory closure of oral folklore.

Overall, the style is ornate and elegiac in its outer narration but colloquial, hyperbolic, and carnivalesque in its central tale. Its signature features are direct address, a framed storyteller, richly localised Irish settings, phonetic vernacular, additive syntax, repeated speech tags, humorous inventories, mock-heroic inflation, affectionate social satire, and a continual interplay between romantic pathos and broad farce.

The story uses a polished, lightly satirical realist style, combining precise social observation with the machinery of a comic anecdote. Its setting is established through concrete local detail—the platform, bookstall, newspaper, railway traffic and Five Towns manufacturers—while the narrator treats this ordinary provincial world with amused detachment. Formal, period-specific diction such as “earthly endeavour,” “artfully complete,” “comparative trifle” and “interference of fate” is deliberately grander than the petty contest it describes, creating irony through disproportion.

The narration is third-person omniscient and confidently intrusive. It moves freely between external action, compact summaries of character and direct access to thought, as when Mr Gale calculates the “net result” of his scheme. The narrator also supplies generalisations and mock-philosophical asides, commenting on friendship, human nature and destiny in a tone that is urbane, knowing and faintly patronising. Phrases such as “these two excellent individuals,” “almost miraculous” and “passed eternally out of the lives of the other two” parody the language of moral drama and romance while describing trivial financial losses and wounded vanity.

Sentences are often long, balanced and syntactically elaborate, with semicolons, parenthetical qualifications and carefully staged clauses. These are interspersed with short dialogue and blunt statements that sharpen comic timing. Repetition is used rhythmically and ironically: the precise reiteration of bets, coins and losses turns the characters’ calculations into a running joke, while repeated threats to the friendship inflate a minor disagreement into an apparent catastrophe. Numerical exactness—half-a-crown, half-a-sovereign, seven-and-six, nine and elevenpence—gives the comedy a ledger-like quality and exposes the men’s mercenary thinking.

Dialogue is brisk, colloquial and socially revealing. Expressions such as “What’s the game?”, “hook it,” “do me down” and “That’s like you” contrast with the narrator’s elevated prose. The exchanges proceed through challenge, counterchallenge, accusation and defensive accounting, allowing character to emerge through verbal behaviour rather than description alone. Mr Sandbach is patronising, competitive and self-assured; Mr Gale is ingenious, opportunistic and obstinate. Their similarity matters more than their differences: both are prosperous, mechanically minded bachelors who approach friendship as a succession of tests and life as a problem to be solved for advantage.

The humour is predominantly ironic, situational and character-based. Each clever plan produces an unforeseen reversal: the shabby man refuses the bait, accepts Gale’s bribe, appears to have no coin, then sheds the missing coin from his boot; finally, the recovered money belongs to someone else. The narrative repeatedly permits the men to believe they have mastered events before chance corrects them. Even the shabby man, initially treated as an object of experiment, leaves with the only secure profit. The young woman’s entrance supplies a final double deflation: she removes the disputed coin and briefly attracts the bachelors before mentioning her husband.

Structurally, the story resembles a sequence of escalating wagers and punch lines. Exposition quickly establishes the setting and the men’s competitive friendship; the dropped half-crown introduces the first test; Gale’s silent bargain creates the central complication; the reappearing coin seems to resolve the mystery but instead renews the quarrel; and the owner’s arrival delivers the culminating reversal. The closing exchange avoids reconciliation and preserves the characters’ essential pettiness. The final line is terse, dry and accusatory, leaving the comedy suspended in mutual resentment.

Imagery is sparing but vivid and comic. The locomotive is compared to an excitable mother dragging an inoffensive child, nostrils twitch with “the scent of battle,” and the men make a “superb and undignified rush” for the coin. Such comparisons animate the scene while gently mocking adult dignity. Overall, the style depends on the contrast between elevated narration and trivial motives, between claims of ingenuity and repeated miscalculation, and between respectable social appearance and childish competitiveness. Its governing tone is affectionate but unsparing: human vanity, greed and self-interest are exposed without bitterness, through exact observation, elegant irony and tightly controlled comic reversal.

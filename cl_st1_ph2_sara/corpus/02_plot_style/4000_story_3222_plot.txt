After the war, Sir Henry Marquis recounts the hidden truth behind the celebrated hero St. Alban. During the conflict, St. Alban devoted himself to eliminating German espionage and traveled to France to ensure that plans for the Allied offensive on the Somme remained secret. While returning home with a minor arm wound, a supposed nurse replaced his dressing with a bandage whose lining secretly contained a detailed map of Allied troop positions.

A German submarine commander, Plutonburg, intercepted St. Alban’s hospital transport after receiving a signal from an agent in Paris. Pretending to inspect the wound, he removed the bandage and revealed the stolen plans. Rather than kill St. Alban or sink the transport, Plutonburg deliberately let everyone live, expecting St. Alban to conceal his humiliating role in betraying the very secrets he had worked to protect.

Stunned and barely conscious of events, St. Alban later responded to Plutonburg’s drawn pistol with the spontaneous words, “Don’t threaten, fire if you like!” Witnesses interpreted this as extraordinary courage, and Plutonburg’s departure as an act of chivalry. The episode made St. Alban a national hero. Contrary to Plutonburg’s expectations, however, St. Alban confessed everything to the British authorities. The intelligence leak helped explain the Allied offensive’s initial failure. Crushed by guilt and despair, St. Alban eventually shot himself, while his family disguised the suicide as murder. England nevertheless preserved the false heroic legend, raising a monument to him and immortalizing the words he had spoken during the catastrophe.

Reginald Peacock, a vain and self-important singing teacher, resents his practical wife for disrupting his comfort and failing to admire his supposed artistic genius. He romanticizes his female pupils, interpreting their praise, flirtation, and emotional dependence as proof of his irresistible talent and charm, while treating ordinary household needs as attacks on his freedom.

After a day of flattering lessons, he dines with an admirer and performs successfully at an aristocratic gathering, becoming intoxicated by champagne and social attention. Returning home, he briefly longs to reconcile with his wife and share his triumphs. Yet when he tries to speak sincerely, he can only repeat the polished, insincere phrase he uses with all his female admirers, revealing his inability to form genuine intimacy with her.

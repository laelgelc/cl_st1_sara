The story uses a warm, retrospective first-person voice, with Mary narrating from a later vantage point and treating the past as a cherished, almost enchanted memory. Her perspective is affectionate and openly interpretive: she not only records Uncle Jesse’s appearance and actions but repeatedly tells the reader what his gestures reveal about his character. This produces an intimate, testimonial quality, as though the narrator were preserving the moral and emotional legacy of someone beloved. Occasional foreshadowing—especially the early statement that she will one day learn what he seeks—gives the reminiscence a gentle sense of destiny.

The prose is lyrical, sentimental, and picturesque, combining domestic realism with romanticized maritime atmosphere. Natural scenery is richly personified: winds “harp,” breakers “boom,” the harbour sparkles and dimples, and the sea laughs, sobs, calls, and carries spiritual significance. Colours, light, sound, and movement are prominent, with recurring images of gold, silver, blue, white, mist, sunrise, and restless water. The coastal setting is therefore more than a backdrop. The Golden Gate becomes the story’s governing symbol, functioning simultaneously as a harbour entrance, a passage into memory, and a threshold between earthly life and the afterlife.

The diction alternates between elevated literary narration and colloquial dialogue. Narrative passages use ornate descriptive phrasing, balanced clauses, metaphor, allusion, and occasional archaism, as in the reference to “faerie lands forlorn.” Sentences are often long and cumulative, layering physical detail with emotional or symbolic interpretation. By contrast, Uncle Jesse’s speech is represented through eye dialect, nonstandard grammar, contractions, and nautical idiom: “jest,” “yander,” “purty,” “knowed,” and “‘tweren’t.” His recurring references to God as a “Captain,” death as receiving “sailing orders,” and the afterlife as an unmapped sea extend his sailor’s vocabulary into a complete spiritual worldview. The dialect individualizes him while his aphoristic remarks give him homespun wisdom and gentle humour.

Characterization relies heavily on affectionate paradox. Uncle Jesse is physically homely but spiritually beautiful, socially simple but philosophically perceptive, humble in manner yet heroic in experience. The narrator frequently sets outer appearance against inner worth, using images of light shining through a rough exterior. His goodness is demonstrated through small acts—making tea, gathering flowers, bringing trout, defending a woman’s reputation—before the narrative reveals the adventurous scale of his former life. This gradual enlargement allows him to remain credible as both neighbourly old sailor and romantic hero.

Dialogue is central to the story’s charm and pacing. Uncle Jesse’s speeches blend comedy, humility, theology, anecdote, and pathos, often moving lightly from a joke to a serious insight. His humour is self-deprecating rather than caustic, and his religious faith is presented as instinctive, generous, and tolerant. He tends to speak in memorable comparisons and compact moral observations, making his conversation sound spontaneous while also giving it the polish of folk wisdom. The narrator’s more formal language frames these speeches admiringly and sometimes explicitly explains their effect.

Structurally, the story is linear and episodic. It begins with a portrait, moves backward to the first meeting, passes through two summers and the intervening seasons, and concludes with Uncle Jesse’s death. Early episodes establish his personality and habits; the account of lost Margaret supplies the emotional centre; the life-book plot then converts memory into art and prepares the final fulfilment. Seasonal progression carries symbolic weight: spring brings arrival and friendship, summer brings fullness and creative activity, autumn and winter accompany decline, and the final spring morning joins death with renewal.

The tone blends nostalgia, humour, reverence, and controlled pathos. Sentiment is direct rather than ironic: love survives death, goodness beautifies the plain, storytelling preserves experience, and faith transforms loss into anticipated reunion. The ending intensifies these tendencies through visual and symbolic echoes. Uncle Jesse dies with his transformed life-book clasped to his heart, while the sunrise beyond the Gate suggests that he has entered the spiritual voyage he long imagined. The narration does not present this as demonstrable fact, but its phrases—“somehow I think” and the final vision of his drifting spirit—allow emotional conviction to overtake literal certainty.

A notable feature is the story’s self-conscious interest in storytelling itself. Uncle Jesse possesses experience and oral genius but lacks literary technique; Robert possesses verbal craft but needs incidents and characters; Mary contributes emotional understanding and shapes the conclusion. The finished book is therefore presented as a collaboration between lived experience, spoken narrative, literary form, and sympathetic interpretation. This metafictional strand mirrors the short story’s own method: it “garbs” a humble life in romantic, polished prose while claiming to preserve the soul of its subject.

Overall, the style belongs to a traditional sentimental-romantic mode. It favours moral clarity, idealized characterization, evocative landscape, expressive dialogue, symbolic patterning, and a climactic ending in which physical setting and emotional meaning become one. Its most distinctive effect comes from the contrast between the narrator’s lyrical elegance and Uncle Jesse’s earthy maritime speech, both of which work toward the same vision of courage, kindness, memory, and enduring love.

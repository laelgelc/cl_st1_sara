The story uses polished nineteenth-century realist prose, filtered through a formal English translation style. Its diction is elevated but generally lucid, combining socially specific terms—“vice-governor,” “ante-rooms,” “Maecenases,” “rout,” “salon”—with occasional poetic abstractions such as “sweet oblivion” and “the approach of the god.” French and Italian phrases lend cosmopolitan colour, establish class and nationality, and reflect the multilingual culture of aristocratic St. Petersburg. Parenthetical translations and a historical footnote give the text a lightly scholarly, period-document quality.

The narration is third-person omniscient, centred chiefly on Charsky but free to offer broad social commentary. The narrator is conspicuous, urbane and ironic, sometimes addressing the reader through collective asides such as “we must confess.” General observations about poets, patrons and fashionable society interrupt the action and turn individual incidents into social satire. The humour is dry rather than farcical, relying on parenthetical qualifications, comic lists, disproportionate comparisons and understated exposure of vanity. Charsky’s supposed expertise in horses, gambling and cuisine, for example, is dismantled item by item. The narrator is amused by weakness but not cruel; irony is balanced by sympathy and moral nuance.

Characterisation proceeds through contradiction. Charsky is a genuine poet who is ashamed of being known as one, a cultivated gentleman who descends into snobbery, and a vain dandy who nevertheless possesses “a good and noble heart.” The improvisatore is likewise divided between sublime artistic power and “artless love of gain.” These contrasts are central to the story’s treatment of art: inspiration is presented as mysterious and exalted, while artistic life remains entangled with money, reputation, fashion and public performance. Social identity is often established through clothing, rooms, gestures and manners. Detailed portraits—the Italian’s worn coat and false diamond, Charsky’s luxurious dressing gown, the salon audience’s gloves and brilliant dress—make class difference immediately visible.

The prose alternates between expansive exposition and sharply staged scenes. Long, balanced sentences, often containing semicolons, parentheses and cumulative clauses, dominate the descriptive and reflective passages. These are punctuated by short questions, exclamations and repeated phrases in dialogue, which quicken the pace and heighten embarrassment or excitement. Repetition is used structurally and comically: “Haven’t you written anything new?” becomes a refrain representing Charsky’s social persecution, while the repeated “Well?” dramatises the improvisatore’s need for judgment. Ellipses frequently mark hesitation, emotional transition or suspense, and exclamation points are especially common in the Italian’s animated speech.

Dialogue is socially diagnostic. Charsky’s controlled, sometimes cutting manner contrasts with the improvisatore’s bows, stammering, exclamations and expressive gestures. Speech moves between English, French and Italian, making misunderstandings and assumptions about status part of the drama. Formal address—“signor,” “Your Excellency,” “gentlemen”—reinforces hierarchy, while Charsky’s shift from cold interrogation to warm collegiality marks his moral correction. The dialogue also stages competing conceptions of poetry: private inspiration, public entertainment, professional labour and commercial commodity.

Descriptions are selective but vivid, often built around a few telling visual contrasts. The improvisatore’s dark hair and beard are set against his pale face and white neck; the glittering salon opposes the dirty tavern corridor; artistic ecstasy is repeatedly followed by mundane calculation. Such juxtapositions provide much of the story’s structure and irony. The setting is rendered through recognisable social spaces rather than extensive landscape: studies, ante-rooms, taverns, salons and platforms form a sequence of interiors in which art is hidden, negotiated and finally exhibited.

The chapter structure gives the narrative a clean progression. The first chapter establishes Charsky’s divided identity and introduces the stranger; the second tests the improvisatore’s talent in private and exposes his commercial instincts; the third transforms the private marvel into a public spectacle. Pacing slows before moments of inspiration. Physical signs—pallor, trembling, sparkling eyes, perspiration and sudden stillness—create an almost supernatural aura around poetic composition. The final verse shifts into rhymed, elevated language, with royal imagery, music and ominous silence. Because the text breaks off during this performance, it ends at a point of maximum suspense and leaves the tension between beauty, erotic power and death unresolved.

Overall, the style combines social realism, restrained psychological insight, cosmopolitan comedy and romantic reverence for inspiration. Its characteristic tone is poised between amused scepticism and genuine wonder: society’s treatment of artists is satirised, artists’ vanity and greed are exposed, yet the creative act itself remains mysterious, transporting and sublime.

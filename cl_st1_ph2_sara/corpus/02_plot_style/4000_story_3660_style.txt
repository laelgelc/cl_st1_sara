The story adopts a first-person, mock-autobiographical voice that is confident, opinionated, and comically unreliable. The narrator presents his encounter with Italian grammar as a rational investigation, yet his reasoning repeatedly turns literal-minded, eccentric, and absurd. He speaks with the authority of an amateur reformer, treating personal irritation as objective proof and grammatical complexity as a moral or administrative failure. This collision between solemn certainty and obvious misunderstanding supplies much of the humor.

The dominant mode is comic satire, especially satire of language instruction, grammatical terminology, institutional organization, and the impatience of the monolingual learner. Grammar is transformed from an abstract system into a population of unruly beings: verbs have families, legitimacy, tails, disguises, occupations, and questionable moral characters. Regular verbs are respectable relatives, while irregular verbs are “pathetic outcasts.” The central conceit develops into an elaborate military pageant in which tenses become uniformed squads, conjugations become drills, and the verb itself becomes a weapon that must be captured, tamed, and brought under command. The narrator’s effort to simplify grammar only generates a more cumbersome spectacle, creating sustained dramatic irony.

The prose alternates between long, accumulative sentences and short, emphatic declarations. Extended passages pile up parallel clauses—often beginning with repeated phrases such as “I must”—to imitate the momentum of formal argument while pushing it toward comic excess. Lists, qualifications, interruptions, and escalating analogies make the syntax deliberately overengineered. By contrast, commands and dialogue are clipped and theatrical: “Halt!”, “Front—face!”, and “Pipe the next squad to the assault!” These abrupt lines accelerate the action and sharpen the contrast between the narrator’s expansive commentary and the regimented performance he has arranged.

Diction blends elevated, antiquated, technical, colloquial, and slang registers. Formal phrases such as “beyond peradventure,” “established the fact,” and “the right and wise course” parody scholarly and bureaucratic seriousness. Grammatical labels coexist with military terminology, nautical commands, commercial metaphors, and American vernacular expressions such as “blame’ glad,” “the ticket,” and “I don’t want any stock in a Had Trust.” This continual register-switching is central to the style: specialized terms are detached from their proper contexts and recombined as though they belonged to one coherent system. Occasional foreign words lend local color but are also used as material for jokes, mistranslations, and fabricated authority.

Figurative language is abundant and usually extended beyond reasonable limits. A verb is successively imagined as an evasive culprit, a domesticated animal, a firearm, an artillery battery, a ship, a commercial enterprise, and a military formation. Conjugational endings become “tails” by which linguistic species can be identified, like livestock. The overdevelopment of each comparison creates a burlesque effect: instead of merely clarifying an idea, the metaphor takes control of the narrative and becomes a literal scene. Hyperbole reinforces this process, as a fifty-seven-form conjugation is described as a Gatling gun “fatal at a mile and a half,” while a simple recitation becomes a dazzling ceremonial review capable of moving the narrator to tears.

Humor also depends on repetition, semantic distortion, and verbal play. Ordinary words are treated as if they possess technical or legal force, while technical distinctions are dismissed through absurdly concrete reasoning. The dispute over two forms of “had” becomes a dense comic riff built from repeated variations of “had,” culminating in images of sickly verbs watching the barometer and drawing public salaries. Capitalization gives grammatical concepts mock-personal or institutional status—“Verb,” “Termination,” “Had”—and coined expressions such as “Had Trust” turn linguistic categories into monopolies or political offices. The final footnote extends the joke by offering a confidently false definition in the guise of editorial scholarship.

Dialogue is used extensively and functions as staged comic conflict. The facchino plays the comparatively patient straight man, attempting to explain genuine grammatical distinctions, while the narrator interrupts, misinterprets, and issues new orders. Their exchanges proceed through question, correction, objection, and escalation. The narrator’s impatience often prevents explanations from being completed, allowing ignorance to masquerade as decisiveness. The conjugation drills introduce call-and-response rhythms, with Italian forms followed by English glosses and the narrator’s increasingly enthusiastic reactions. This makes the language lesson resemble both a classroom recital and a military revue.

The narrative is episodic but tightly unified by the central metaphor. It begins with a general diagnosis of the verb as the source of linguistic disorder, moves through the decision to capture and train one, expands into preparations for a theatrical-military demonstration, and culminates in practical drills using dogs and chickens as grammatical objects. Each stage raises the level of absurdity while preserving the narrator’s surface seriousness. The pacing slows during descriptive and argumentative set pieces, then quickens through commands, recitations, and interruptions. The noon gun and church bells provide an external stopping point, ending the dispute before any genuine understanding can occur.

The setting is lightly but effectively evoked through Italian occupations, rural laborers, Florence, church bells, and the long room called the Rope-Walk. These details create the frame of a traveler’s anecdote, but realism remains subordinate to comic invention. The social perspective is deliberately period-marked: servants and peasants are organized as performers under the narrator’s authority, and national or occupational types are treated broadly. The style therefore resembles a satirical travel sketch in which cultural observation is filtered through the prejudices and self-importance of the visiting speaker.

Punctuation contributes strongly to the oral, performative quality. Dashes mark sudden changes of thought, interruptions, qualifications, and shouted commands; semicolons support long chains of mock-logical reasoning; quotation marks highlight jargon and doubtful terminology. Repetition, rhetorical questions, direct address, and self-correction make the prose sound like animated speech rather than polished exposition. Minor solecisms, comic malapropisms, and questionable Italian forms are consistent with the narrator’s imperfect knowledge and reinforce the gap between his claimed mastery and actual competence.

Overall, the style is exuberant, digressive, and theatrically argumentative. Its signature technique is to apply literal logic and grand institutional machinery to a small linguistic difficulty. The narrator’s combination of pomposity, practical ingenuity, hostility to nuance, and verbal inventiveness turns grammar instruction into an extended comic campaign. Beneath the farce lies a broader satire of people who try to master complex systems by denying distinctions they find inconvenient, while remaining serenely convinced that the system—not their understanding—is at fault.

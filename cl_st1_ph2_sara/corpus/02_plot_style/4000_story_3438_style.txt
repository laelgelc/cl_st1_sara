The story uses a nineteenth-century regionalist, oral-tale style, combining a polished frame narrative with an extended vernacular monologue. The opening narration is leisurely and pictorial, establishing the barn as an idyllic, secluded storytelling place through long, balanced sentences and abundant sensory detail: fragrant hay, autumn light, murmuring hens, riverbanks, elms, and water-lilies. This pastoral calm creates a drowsy, nostalgic atmosphere and frames the ensuing legend as communal entertainment remembered from childhood. The first-person plural viewpoint—“we”—gives the frame an intimate, collective quality, while occasional first-person singular interjections make the listeners active participants in the tale.

The dominant voice is Sam Lawson’s, rendered through heavily phonetic rural New England dialect. Nonstandard spellings and contractions such as “Wal,” “jest,” “suthin’,” “arter,” “crittur,” and “goin’” reproduce pronunciation and make the prose sound performed rather than written. His speech is repetitive, digressive, and formulaic. He repeatedly begins passages with “Wal” or “Lordy massy,” addresses his audience as “boys,” and uses conversational fillers such as “ye see” and “kind o’.” These verbal habits supply rhythm, characterize him immediately, and allow the narrative to wander through anecdotes, moral reflections, local gossip, and supernatural lore. The children’s impatient questions repeatedly redirect him, creating a comic tension between their desire for plot and his pleasure in delay.

Syntax varies sharply between the two narrative levels. The frame narrator favors elaborate, grammatically controlled sentences with descriptive clauses, lists, and literary comparisons. Sam’s syntax is looser and additive, often chaining events with “and,” repeating words for emphasis, or shifting direction mid-thought. His storytelling relies on incremental phrasing—“they drawed and they drawed,” “down, down, down,” “tried and tried and tried”—which imitates spoken emphasis and heightens suspense. Dialogue is frequently nested inside his account, so that Sam not only tells the story but impersonates Mother Hokum, Primus, and the treasure hunters. This gives the episode the lively quality of a dramatic performance.

The tone blends nostalgia, rustic comedy, mock solemnity, and mild supernatural suspense. Sam continually turns sensational material into moral instruction, treating Captain Kidd’s fate as a warning against rejecting religion or “hastin’ to be rich.” Yet his moral authority is gently undermined by his evasions, laziness, self-interest, and carefully qualified involvement in the treasure hunt. He insists that his conscience kept him from participating while admitting that he accompanied the group, advised them on supernatural procedure, concealed his purpose from his wife, and conveniently slept through the decisive event. This gap between his self-presentation and his conduct is the story’s central comic irony.

The narrative is deliberately digressive. The promised account of buried treasure is postponed by a ballad, a summary of Captain Kidd’s career, the history of the Hokum family, an anecdote about an unpaid butcher’s bill, arguments about poverty and wealth, drinking, ghost stories, and a description of divining rods. These apparent detours build a dense portrait of village culture, where folklore, religious teaching, social resentment, rumor, and practical cunning coexist. The plot itself follows the shape of a folk legend: a marked rock, treasure guarded by spirits, a prescribed rule of silence, a midnight excavation, a tantalizing discovery, and a fatal spoken exclamation that causes the treasure to vanish.

Humor arises from exaggeration, incongruity, repetition, and anticlimax. Mother Hokum’s enormous family is described through comic images such as a “rabbit-pen” with a “tow-head to every crack and cranny,” while the treasure hunters loudly deny belief in spirits as they fortify themselves with alcohol. Primus is the most practically intelligent participant: he demands payment in advance, secures it before the expedition, and laughs when the others lose their imagined fortune. The final revelation that Sam did not actually see the pot because he fell asleep punctures the supernatural climax and leaves the entire event suspended between legend, drunken misperception, and comic fabrication.

Imagery shifts with the mood. The opening uses soft, graceful, pastoral imagery, while the midnight section introduces blood-red leaves, answering whippoorwills, whispering trees, hollow grave-like sounds, groans, and buried corpses. Sound is especially important: hens “craw, crawing,” Caesar’s fiddle, the lugubrious pirate ballad, the spade striking iron, the snapped rope, the descending “jink, jink, jink,” and the final unearthly laugh. These auditory details reinforce the sense that the story belongs to oral culture and is designed to be heard.

The prose also incorporates songs, proverbial expressions, biblical language, sermonizing, and literary allusion. The Captain Kidd ballad interrupts the prose with repetitive verse and supplies a miniature moral biography of rebellion, murder, and punishment. Phrases such as “vanity,” “judgment day,” “ways o’ sin,” and “the Devil’s money” reflect a community steeped in Protestant moral vocabulary, though Mother Hokum’s complaint about the prosperous parson introduces social satire and exposes the economic distance between religious instruction and poverty.

Characterization is largely achieved through voice. Sam is leisurely, evasive, superstitious, self-excusing, and theatrically moralistic. Mother Hokum is forceful, resourceful, irreverent, and driven by material hardship. The treasure hunters are credulous, lazy, and greedy, whereas Primus is skeptical and financially shrewd. The frame narrator’s amused treatment encourages readers to enjoy these figures as village types. At the same time, the portrayals of Black characters rely on period racial terminology, stereotype, caricatured dialect, and generalized racial commentary. These elements are integral to the historical style but mark the story’s perspective as paternalistic and racially dated.

Overall, the style is expansive, performative, and gently satirical. Its chief effects come less from the buried-treasure plot than from the contrast between elegant narration and rustic speech, the storyteller’s comic unreliability, the accumulation of local detail, and the transformation of village gossip into folklore. The ending remains characteristically inconclusive: rather than resolving whether the treasure existed, it returns to Sam’s amused impatience with questioning, preserving the ambiguity and sociable charm of a tale whose real subject is the act of storytelling itself.

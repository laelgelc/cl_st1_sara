A wealthy, lonely man becomes enchanted with a historic country house and immediately bonds with its elderly owner, Mel Hays, who must sell because of financial hardship and his wife’s illness. Their mutual affection for the property and its history seems to make the sale certain. But when Hays asks whether he might occasionally stay overnight after selling, the buyer hesitates before agreeing. Hurt by that momentary reluctance, Hays abruptly refuses to sell, even when offered far more than the asking price.

After Hays’s wife dies, the narrator repeatedly drives past the property and watches it deteriorate while its owner remains isolated inside. He writes increasingly frustrated letters, first seeking reconciliation, then accusing Hays of pride and obstinacy, though he stops himself from sending the cruelest one.

Now terminally ill, the narrator recognizes that both men’s pride destroyed a possible friendship and allowed the house to decay. He imagines writing a final letter that forgives Hays, accepts the impermanence of the house and their lives, and asks for his prayers. Yet his own pride still prevents him from writing it, even as he knows he is running out of time.

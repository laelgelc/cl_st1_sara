A writer who has long opposed autobiography reluctantly begins recounting the origins of her literary life. She traces her imagination and talent to a family of scholars, ministers, reformers, and successful authors: one grandfather was a renowned biblical scholar, while the other aided fugitive slaves and experienced an alleged haunting that fascinated her as a child.

Her mother, a popular writer, balances literary ambition with devoted motherhood but dies from exhaustion after giving birth while completing a book. Her father then raises the motherless children and deeply shapes the narrator’s intellectual life through his scholarship, discipline, and readings of Wordsworth and De Quincey.

Although lively and tomboyish rather than precocious, the narrator publishes her first piece at thirteen. A later contribution earns her two dollars and fifty cents, which she uses to buy art. Receiving that first payment gives her a lasting sense of dignity, independence, and personal value, marking the true beginning of her life as a professional writer.

The story is written as a dramatic monologue in which only Ellen’s side of a late-night conversation is heard. The listener’s questions and reactions are omitted, but they can be inferred from Ellen’s replies—“No, madam,” “Only the last year, madam,” and similar formulations. Ellipses mark these unheard interventions as well as pauses, hesitations and shifts of thought. This one-sided dialogue gives the narrative the intimacy of overheard speech while keeping the silent “madam” indistinct, allowing the reader to occupy her position.

Ellen’s voice is highly individualised through colloquial diction, class-marked grammar and deferential repetition. Expressions such as “ever so kind,” “something dreadful,” “as you might say,” and “would you believe it, madam?” create an eager, conversational rhythm. Non-standard forms—“the lights was out,” “where we was living,” and the self-correction “seen—saw”—suggest limited formal education without reducing Ellen to caricature. Her frequent “madam” continually reasserts the social hierarchy even during moments of emotional intimacy. Exclamations, rhetorical questions and affectionate diminutives such as “little red book,” “little nose” and “ducky little brooch” convey warmth, nervousness and a habit of making painful experience sound quaint or manageable.

The syntax imitates spontaneous speech. Sentences often proceed by accumulation, joined with “and,” interrupted by dashes, or broken into fragments: “White! he turned as white as a woman.” Parenthetical qualifications and repeated corrections show Ellen monitoring herself as she talks. She regularly begins with one subject and drifts into another through association: tea leads to prayers, prayers to her lady’s mother, the mother to Ellen’s childhood, and childhood to the donkey ride and abandoned marriage. This loose, digressive construction feels naturalistic, yet the sequence is carefully shaped to reveal her life gradually.

The narrative is episodic and retrospective. Small memories function like compressed scenes, each organised around a vivid object or sensory image: the kettle boiling during prayers, purple pansies beside a dead woman’s neck, curling tongs closing on a child’s fingers, silver-grey donkeys with red saddles, the imagined flower-shop window, and the silver bird brooch carrying a chained heart pierced by a dagger. Such objects carry emotional meanings that Ellen herself only partly recognises. Flowers, hair, hands, domestic furnishings and acts of arranging or tending recur throughout, linking beauty and care with mortality, injury and frustrated desire.

The dominant tone combines comedy, tenderness and pathos. Ellen’s literal-minded observations can be gently comic, as when she reflects that Christ did not suffer from her elderly mistress’s lumbago, or pretends to talk in her sleep so that she can confess her longing for a donkey ride. Yet these humorous details repeatedly open onto deprivation. Childhood cruelty is excused as her grandfather’s pride; a lost chance for pleasure becomes a “funny” anecdote; the renunciation of marriage is narrated as proof of devotion. The prose avoids direct authorial judgement, so the emotional force arises from the discrepancy between Ellen’s cheerful interpretation and what the reader understands about her loneliness and self-denial.

Characterisation depends heavily on dramatic irony and psychological subtext. Ellen presents herself as loyal, practical and contented, but her repetitions, abrupt evasions and compulsive storytelling reveal suppressed needs. Her insistence that she does not think about the future exposes how much she fears her mistress’s death. Likewise, her description of the sleeping lady as resembling her dead mother unconsciously merges loving care with the anticipation of loss. The final self-rebuke—“At it again—you silly girl!”—shows that she has internalised the discipline of service so completely that even reflection and private desire seem indulgent.

The story’s emotional method is restrained and indirect. Major traumas are not elaborated in elevated language; they appear amid domestic chatter and are quickly softened by euphemism, humour or apology. The absence of an external narrator prevents explicit explanation and leaves the reader to reconstruct Ellen’s history. Repeated motifs of looking, arranging and concealment reinforce this indirectness: Ellen peers from beneath a tablecloth, watches her mistress through a mirror, imagines shop windows, and disguises waking desire as a dream. The result is a subtle modernist style in which consciousness is disclosed through speech patterns, omissions and symbolic details rather than conventional exposition.

Pacing alternates between rapid summary and slowed dramatic recollection. Long stretches of a lifetime pass in a few sentences, while decisive moments—the childhood punishment, the donkey fantasy and the rejection of Harry—are recreated through direct quotation and sharply visualised gestures. The clock near the end returns the story to the present room and reveals how long Ellen has spoken. The circular close, with Ellen offering to tuck in the listener’s feet and repeating her mistress’s bedtime ritual, restores the domestic situation but leaves the central anxiety unresolved.

Overall, the style is intimate, oral and deceptively simple. Its surface is that of a servant’s rambling bedtime conversation; beneath it lies a precisely controlled portrait of class dependence, emotional repression and a life organised around care for others. Sentiment is kept from becoming sentimental by irony, concrete detail and the speaker’s incomplete self-knowledge.

The story adopts the manner of a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century maritime adventure tale, blending commercial intrigue, nautical realism, supernatural suspense, and comic irony. Its world is rendered through concrete facts—distances, dates, dimensions, legal claims, vessel types, tides, rigging, and sailing conditions—which give the narrative an air of documentary credibility. This factual groundwork makes the apparent haunting seem more plausible and heightens the eventual contrast between extraordinary appearances and an ordinary explanation.

The narrative is told retrospectively in the first person by a participant-observer. The narrator, Mr. Dixon, is close enough to witness the central events but remains secondary to the more colourful sailors. His voice is educated, measured, and generally reliable, with occasional direct addresses to the reader and self-conscious qualifications such as admissions of fear or embarrassment. Phrases like “I am told,” “you may believe,” and “if you can see what I mean” create the atmosphere of an anecdote recounted conversationally after the fact. The narrator also withholds information fairly: he describes what he knew and perceived at each moment rather than prematurely revealing the solution.

The prose alternates between expository breadth and tightly focused suspense. Early passages contain long, syntactically layered sentences that explain the island’s location, ownership, and commercial value. The haunting scenes narrow the field of attention, proceeding through incremental observations and repeated questions before unveiling the apparition detail by detail. Parallel constructions—“as before” and “we watched and waited,” for example—turn the ghost’s appearances into a ritual pattern. Short sentences and paragraph breaks then punctuate revelations: the sails have been set again, the men abandon the voyage, and the ghost is finally identified.

Diction divides sharply between the narrator’s formal descriptive English and the sailors’ heavily represented vernacular. The narration uses precise nautical language such as “crosstrees,” “tops’l,” “jib halyards,” “quarterdeck,” and “fo’c’s’le,” alongside vivid figurative descriptions: rigging is “etched against the sky,” a mast resembles the hand of an illuminated clock, and a boat can be rowed “as softly as a drifting cloud.” Dialogue is phonetic, idiomatic, and socially marked. Dropped consonants, altered vowels, contractions, maritime slang, and nonstandard grammar distinguish Ryder’s American business patter from Ally Bazan’s colonial or Cockney-inflected speech. This dialect writing supplies characterisation and humour, though it also reflects the broad, caricatural conventions of its period.

Characterisation is economical and external. Ryder is established through his repeated use of “proposition,” his commercial opportunism, and his grimy office. Hardenberg is physically powerful but superstitious; Strokher masks fear with talk and fabricated illness; Ally Bazan is comically fearful, verbose, and unknowingly responsible for the haunting. Recurrent labels such as “the Three Black Crows,” “the colonial,” and “the little fellow” give the cast the bold outlines of adventure-story types. Actions, speech habits, and physical gestures carry more characterising weight than psychological analysis.

The dominant tone moves from brisk and worldly to eerie, then increasingly comic. The supernatural episodes use Gothic imagery—a shrouded figure, fog, moonlight, gray hours after midnight, and a “horrid supercargo”—but this atmosphere is repeatedly undercut by practical sailorly reactions and retrospective humour. Fear is treated sympathetically yet satirically: the men proclaim sensible plans by daylight, refuse to carry them out, infect one another with superstition, and eventually rationalise their cowardice. The humour often arises from the discrepancy between masculine bravado and actual conduct.

Structurally, the story resembles a framed mystery or tall tale. The commercial race for Paa creates an external deadline and meaningful stakes; the haunting interrupts preparations and causes the protagonists’ defeat. Numbered sections mark clear phases: exposition, first disturbance, repeated manifestations, experimentation and abandonment, and delayed explanation. The two-year time jump before the final scene postpones the solution long enough for the sailors’ mistake to become irreversible. The denouement retrospectively reinterprets every clue: the ghost appears only when Ally sleeps forward, never appears to Ally himself, wraps itself in sheet-like material, and instinctively handles sails.

Irony is the story’s governing device. The men who pride themselves on handling danger are defeated not by a rival or a genuine ghost but by one companion’s sleepwalking. Ally, the most frightened and superstitious character, is himself the apparition. The rational explanation does not restore what was lost: Petersen has already secured the island and made his fortune. The narrator’s decision not to tell the Three Crows preserves both their dignity and the comic force of their error. Overall, the style combines realistic technical detail, theatrical dialect, carefully staged Gothic suspense, and a dryly amused narrative intelligence, producing an adventure story whose apparent supernatural threat resolves into farce and economic misfortune.

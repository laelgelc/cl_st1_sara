The story uses an elevated, nineteenth-century historical-romantic style, combining nautical realism with melodrama, philosophical reflection, and epic spectacle. Its omniscient third-person narrator moves freely between external action, technical explanation, collective emotion, and authoritative commentary. The narrative voice does not remain neutral: it interprets events, announces their significance, and repeatedly intensifies them through categorical statements such as “Nothing more terrible can happen” and “To describe the terror is impossible.” This gives the prose the character of an oral legend or historical chronicle shaped by a morally confident storyteller.

The dominant stylistic device is sustained personification. Once the carronade breaks loose, it ceases to be merely a weapon and becomes a “supernatural beast,” a “familiar monster,” a demon, an insect, a panther, and an opponent capable of thought, patience, malice, and tactical calculation. The central episode is therefore framed not as an accident but as a duel between animate and inanimate forces, “the battle of matter and intelligence.” This transformation is reinforced by antitheses: metal is opposed to wood, brute force to the human soul, the invulnerable to the frail, the mastodon to the ant, and the thunderbolt to the pygmy. Such contrasts enlarge a confined shipboard emergency into a mythic conflict.

The imagery is extravagant, kinetic, and accumulative. Similes draw on animals, natural disasters, warfare, religion, and classical spectacle: the cannon rebounds like a child’s ball, leaps like a panther, lands like a tiger, becomes a chariot of the Apocalypse, and resembles a thunderbolt rumbling over an earthquake. Long catalogues of movements—“struggles, advances, backs, strikes right, strikes left, retreats”—imitate the gun’s erratic course. Repetition and parallel construction create a hammering rhythm, while strings of active verbs produce a sense of relentless impact. The prose often piles image upon image until physical description becomes visionary or symbolic.

Sentence structure alternates sharply to control pace. Short declarative paragraphs—“Something terrible had just happened,” “The battle began,” “It was ended”—serve as dramatic blows and mark decisive turns. These are surrounded by long, semicolon-heavy sentences filled with lists, rhetorical amplification, and explanatory clauses. During moments of suspense, fragments and balanced oppositions slow the action into tableaux: “Frailty struggling against the invulnerable. The gladiator of flesh attacking the beast of brass.” Rhetorical questions also proliferate, especially when the crew appears helpless. Their repeated “What is to be done?” converts practical uncertainty into existential terror.

The diction blends specialized maritime vocabulary with grand poetic abstraction. Terms such as “carronade,” “breeching,” “cascabel,” “gun-carriage,” “mizzenmast,” “larboard,” and “companion-way” provide material authority and make the danger mechanically credible. Against this technical language stand abstractions such as “infinite,” “depravity,” “intelligence,” “soul,” “courage,” and “negligence.” The resulting style moves continually between engineering detail and metaphysical interpretation: the precise causes of the accident are explained, but the cannon is simultaneously treated as matter rebelling against human mastery.

Violence is depicted graphically but ceremonially. Bodies are crushed, severed, and reduced to fragments, while blood moves with the rolling deck. Yet the narration rarely adopts intimate psychological realism; individuals are instead arranged as figures in a vast dramatic composition. Light and darkness, the swinging lantern, the empty gun-deck, the watching crew, and the hurricane beyond the ship create a theatrical setting. Repeated references to a “scene,” “contest,” “duel,” “play,” and “sight” emphasize spectacle and make the action resemble staged tragedy.

Characterization is economical and action-based. The gunner is defined first by culpable negligence, then by courage and technical skill. The old passenger is characterized through stillness, precision, emotional opacity, and command. His immobility contrasts with the cannon’s frantic motion, marking him as a figure of superior discipline. Dialogue is sparse, formal, and highly functional. Brief exchanges interrupt the descriptive expansiveness, reveal hierarchy, and sharpen moral choices. The gunner’s intimate challenge to the cannon—“Come!”—humanizes him, while the old man’s clipped orders establish terrifying authority.

The narrative architecture depends on escalation and reversal. The crisis begins abruptly, expands through explanatory digression, reaches a prolonged duel, and resolves in apparent triumph. That resolution is then overturned: the gunner is decorated for courage and immediately condemned for negligence. The juxtaposition of reward and execution supplies the story’s final moral shock. The closing line converts the old man’s severity into political symbolism, suggesting that the preceding episode has functioned as a test of leadership.

The tone is grave, urgent, awe-struck, and morally absolutist. Courage and competence are admired, but responsibility is treated as inexorable; heroic action can redeem immediate failure without cancelling guilt. The sea is presented as permanent warfare, nature as an ambush, and command as the power to reward merit while punishing error without sentiment. This severity gives the ending the cadence of judgment rather than simple cruelty.

The prose also bears signs of an older English translation or edition. There are archaic spellings and constructions, formal titles, occasional typographical or grammatical irregularities, and phrases that sound transferred from another language rather than naturally idiomatic in modern English. These features contribute to the antique atmosphere, though they sometimes produce awkward wording. Overall, the style is maximalist and declamatory: technically concrete, rhetorically heightened, rich in personification and antithesis, and designed to turn a maritime accident into an epic parable of chaos, courage, discipline, and command.

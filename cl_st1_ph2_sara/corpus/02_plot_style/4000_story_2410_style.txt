The story adopts the style of a Victorian antiquarian ghost tale, combining Gothic horror, domestic history, moral anecdote, and quasi-legal testimony. Its supernatural events are embedded in an otherwise concrete world of inheritances, mortgages, leases, servants, clergy, and provincial estates. This accumulation of ordinary social and material detail lends credibility to the haunting while allowing the narrative to present itself as a curious family record rather than an overt fantasy.

The narration is first-person but largely indirect. The unnamed narrator recounts an experience received from his uncle, Mr. Watson, repeatedly affirming that the uncle was “truthful,” “veracious,” and “not prone to fancies.” This testimonial framing creates both authority and distance: the narrator does not claim to have witnessed the apparition, yet supplies corroborating evidence at the conclusion. Qualifying phrases such as “I think,” “I suppose,” and “I believe” give the voice a conversational, judicious air. At the same time, the narrator is openly evaluative, calling Walshawe a “scamp,” a “handsome vagabond,” and a “bloated old sinner.” The result is a voice that sounds sociable and anecdotal while retaining the pose of a careful chronicler.

The diction mixes formal, Latinate, and archaic language with colloquial satire. Phrases such as “reserved and contumacious,” “in articulo,” “beyond a cavil,” and “edax rerum” evoke an educated nineteenth-century narrator, whereas expressions such as “no end of scrapes,” “seedy and sick,” “diddled old mischief-maker,” and “what the devil” make the prose lively and socially textured. Irish dialect is represented phonetically in Molly Doyle’s curse, while Captain Walshawe’s broken, profane speech conveys drunkenness and aggression. Euphemistic dashes censor his obscenities, preserving period decorum without diminishing their force.

The syntax is expansive and digressive. Long, many-clause sentences frequently qualify, revise, or elaborate an observation, mimicking oral storytelling and the workings of memory. Parenthetical comments and ironic asides allow the narrator to step outside the action and address the reader implicitly. These elaborate constructions are balanced by abrupt declarative sentences—“He spent her income, frightened her out of her wits with oaths and threats, and broke her heart”—which deliver moral judgments with exceptional force. Lists and cumulative clauses are also prominent, especially when cataloguing Walshawe’s vices, physical decay, household objects, or the successive stages of the supernatural manifestation.

The tone shifts gradually from comic and satirical to ominous, grotesque, and horrific. The early portrait of Walshawe is broadly humorous in its account of his vanity, fortune-hunting, and self-pity, but the humour is edged with moral disgust. Even during the haunting, comic incongruities persist: the apparition’s long wink, inflated cheek, and distorted proportions would be ludicrous outside their terrifying context. This fusion of absurdity and dread is central to the story’s effect. The supernatural is not solemnly beautiful; it is physically unstable, malicious, decayed, and grotesquely theatrical.

Characterization relies on authorial summary, emblematic physical description, and revealing behaviour. Walshawe is constructed as a rake hardened into a vicious old tyrant, his bodily deterioration mirroring his moral corruption. His transformation from elegant young officer to swollen, worm-ridden corpse compresses his entire life into a visual moral allegory. Peg O’Neill is treated with pity but also with the period’s casually comic emphasis on appearance: her “potato-faced” plainness contrasts with her emotional susceptibility and unhappy devotion. Molly Doyle is austere, religious, and implacable, functioning as both loyal servant and prophetic agent. Uncle Watson is grave, honest, devout, and slightly self-important; his pointed prayer at the attorney supplies gentle satire even as his religious susceptibility makes him an apt witness to the supernatural.

The setting is rendered through tactile and acoustic detail. The old house contains presses, wainscot, four-poster curtains, heavy candlesticks, looking-glasses, concealed drawers, and antiquated furniture. These objects are not merely decorative: the candle, mirror, drawer, extinguisher, and fireplace all become mechanisms of plot and haunting. Storm sounds dominate the nocturnal scenes—rattling windows, piping wind, gravel striking glass, creaks, knocks, and “ha-ha-hooing” among trees and chimneys. Firelight produces alternating glare and shadow, while cold, darkness, and drafts mark the apparition’s power. The sensory specificity keeps the uncanny rooted in a believable physical environment.

Suspense is built incrementally. The first chapters provide extensive biography and establish the holy candle and Molly Doyle’s curse. The middle section introduces the missing leases and places Watson alone in the house. The bedroom sequence then progresses through increasingly ambiguous disturbances: moving curtains, a thump, knocks, touches, a whisper, and unexplained pressure. Each incident admits a possible natural explanation until the extinguisher is visibly lifted. The apparition itself unfolds slowly and anatomically, beginning as a miniature figure and enlarging into a full-grown man. Its subsequent aging and decomposition form a second escalation, carrying the scene from wonder through terror to abjection.

Structurally, the story is divided into short, plainly titled chapters that announce objects, places, or stages of action: “The Blessed Candle,” “The Bed-Chamber,” “The Extinguisher Is Lifted,” and “The Visitation Culminates.” This gives the tale an episodic, serial quality and makes the climax feel carefully engineered. The conclusion returns to the language of evidence, enumerating “two curious circumstances” that authenticate the vision: the identity of the candle and the discovery of the hidden papers. A final resemblance test links the apparition’s changing forms to Walshawe at different ages. The lingering speculation about whether the demolished house was reputedly haunted leaves a residue of uncertainty after the principal mystery has been explained.

Imagery centres on corruption, distortion, imprisonment, and transformation. Walshawe’s life is initially described through the agricultural metaphor of wild oats producing thorns, nettles, and thistles. The curse imagines his soul confined within the candle wick, turning a devotional object into a supernatural prison. During the apparition, proportions stretch as though reflected in a warped mirror, youth collapses into diseased age, and flesh becomes ash, worms, and sparks. These transformations externalize the story’s moral logic: vice deforms the self, and death reveals the corruption that social charm once concealed.

Thematically, the story presents supernatural justice as the completion of earthly business. Walshawe’s abuse of his wife, contempt for sacred ritual, and attempt to suppress legal documents return upon him through the very candle he desecrated. Yet the haunting is not purely punitive, since the damned figure also reveals the hidden leases and thereby repairs one of his intended wrongs. Religion is treated with a characteristic mixture of seriousness and irony. Catholic ritual supplies the cursed candle and the language of imprisoned souls, while Methodist belief shapes Watson’s interpretation of the phantom. Sectarian practices may be gently mocked, but the supernatural reality behind them is not dismissed.

Overall, the style is ornate, ironic, morally pointed, and vividly grotesque. It achieves its distinctive effect by placing an extravagant apparition inside a densely documented social world, balancing sceptical qualification with emphatic testimony, and allowing humour to sharpen rather than relieve the horror.

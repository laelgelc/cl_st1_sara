Doctor Heidegger invites four elderly friends—each ruined by the follies of earlier life—to test water supposedly drawn from the Fountain of Youth. He first demonstrates its power by restoring a fifty-five-year-old dried rose to bloom, then offers the water to his guests while declining to drink it himself.

After several glasses, the guests become young again, but instead of using their experience to behave wisely, they immediately repeat their former vices. The merchant devises reckless schemes, the politician returns to empty rhetoric and intrigue, the colonel resumes drinking and lustful behavior, and the widow becomes vain and flirtatious. The three men revive their old rivalry for her, fight, overturn the table, and shatter the vase, spilling the remaining water.

The effects soon fade, leaving all four elderly once more. Heidegger concludes that temporary youth is not worth regaining when age has brought wisdom, but his guests learn nothing: they resolve to travel to Florida, find the fountain, and drink from it continually.

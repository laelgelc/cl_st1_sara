The story uses a reflective, memoir-like frame around an embedded fairy tale. Its first-person narrator looks back on childhood with affectionate irony, contrasting the child’s instinctive faith in imaginative truth with the adult world’s demand for facts, plausibility and social explanation. The narration moves fluidly among philosophical commentary, comic recollection and direct retelling, so that the fairy tale and the rainy childhood evening continually illuminate one another. Frequent authorial digressions interrupt the plot deliberately, turning the act of storytelling itself into a central subject.

The tone is warm, playful and gently satirical, but it gradually becomes lyrical and elegiac. Humour arises from mock solemnity, especially when the narrator treats a child’s dislike of lessons as a matter of cosmic justice, or imagines modern readers interrogating a fairy tale through “scientific spectacles.” Exaggeration is affectionate rather than caustic. The adult narrator understands the absurdities that the child accepts without question, yet never patronises the child; instead, childhood credulity is presented as a deeper mode of perception, capable of reaching emotional truth more directly than rational criticism.

The diction combines plain storytelling language with elevated, occasionally ornate phrasing. Formulaic expressions such as “There was a king,” “What then?” and “Let it pass to-day” create the cadence of oral tradition, while phrases such as “the Crystal Palace of Truth,” “the ever-shut chamber of the great End” and “the still water of time” give the reflective passages poetic grandeur. The vocabulary often draws on royalty, religion and Indian social or material culture—kings, Brahmans, austerities, sandalwood, peacock-tail fans, golden ornaments and incantations—without burdening these details with explanation. The prose retains a slightly formal, old-fashioned idiom, including constructions such as “had not got any son,” “practise austerities” and “to-day.”

Sentence structure varies according to function. Comic and narrative moments often use short, emphatic sentences or paragraphs: “But, alas, nor did my teacher!” Longer sentences dominate recollection and meditation, accumulating clauses and sensory details in a measured, flowing rhythm. Parallel constructions and repetition are prominent. Repeated questions—“Which king?”, “Who are you?” and above all “What then?”—supply momentum and embody both sceptical adult inquiry and a child’s urgent hunger for continuation. Lists and balanced contrasts reinforce the story’s central oppositions: knowledge and truth, adulthood and childhood, criticism and belief, death and sleep.

Dialogue is simple, direct and theatrically placed. It preserves the rhythms of spoken storytelling and sharply characterises the child’s persistence, the mother’s indulgent practicality and the grandmother’s calm authority. The embedded fairy tale depends on archetypal characters rather than psychological complexity: king, queen, princess, Brahman’s son and magician. Its causal logic is dreamlike and absolute. A king’s impulsive command produces a marriage; years pass in a sentence; death can be reversed through ritual. The framing narrator highlights these improbabilities while defending their imaginative legitimacy.

Pacing alternates between summary and vivid scene. The opening is discursive, establishing the argument about fairy tales before settling into the concrete Calcutta evening. The tutor episode is brisk and comic; the grandmother’s tale proceeds through large temporal leaps, then slows around moments of suspense, especially the marriage, the repeated questioning and the flower-strewn bedchamber. The interruption after the boy-husband’s death shifts attention away from plot resolution and toward the emotional necessity of resolution. What matters is not precisely how the fairy tale ends, but why a child requires it to continue beyond death.

Imagery is sensuous and symbolic. Rain, dim lamplight, mosquito curtains, pillows and the grandmother’s droning voice create an enclosed atmosphere of safety and enchantment. Gold, flowers, perfume and blue fabric lend the fairy tale ceremonial richness. The rainy night outside and the river carrying the dead body blur the boundaries between the frame and the embedded story. In the conclusion, sleep becomes a metaphorical river, the child’s body becomes the resurrected fairy-tale hero, and morning becomes a return to “life and light.” This final transformation gives the prose its most lyrical quality.

Overall, the style is metafictional without being cold or technical. It comments on storytelling while simultaneously recreating its spell. Its defining qualities are intimate retrospective narration, humorous self-awareness, oral repetition, fairy-tale simplicity, culturally specific ornament, philosophical digression and a closing movement from domestic comedy into poetic meditation. The governing aesthetic is that emotional and imaginative truth need not submit to factual probability: the story’s apparent impossibilities are precisely what allow it to express childhood wonder, fear, hope and faith.

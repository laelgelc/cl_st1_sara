The story is written as a comic first-person monologue in a highly exaggerated representation of Irish speech. Its most conspicuous feature is dense eye dialect: standard spellings are altered to suggest pronunciation, as in “gintleman,” “mesilf,” “wid,” “illigant,” and “Frinchman.” Contractions, dropped consonants, phonetic substitutions, colloquialisms, and recurring Irish expressions such as “Och hon,” “mavourneen,” “by the powers,” and “at all at all” dominate the prose. The linguistic caricature is intentionally excessive rather than naturalistic, reflecting a nineteenth-century tradition of ethnic burlesque. French speech receives similar comic distortion through meaningless approximations such as “woully wou, pully wou” and the mangled title “Mounseer Maiter-di-dauns.”

The narrator’s voice is exuberant, boastful, intimate, and relentlessly self-dramatising. Sir Pathrick repeatedly insists on his aristocratic title, elegance, education, physical magnificence, and perfect politeness, while his language and conduct continually undermine those claims. He addresses the reader directly through phrases such as “ye may swear,” “you percave,” and “belave me, my jewel,” creating the effect of an animated oral performance. His storytelling is full of self-interruptions, parenthetical asides, oaths, appeals to divine truth, and invitations to admire or laugh at what he describes. Repetition functions as verbal showmanship: his full title, stock phrases, and superlatives recur until they become comic refrains.

The narration is radically unreliable. Sir Pathrick interprets every glance, smile, withdrawal, and accidental touch as proof of his irresistible charm. He even translates gestures into elaborate imagined declarations of love. The reader is expected to recognise the disparity between his interpretation and the likely reality. This sustained dramatic irony is the principal source of humour: the narrator presents himself as the triumphant romantic hero while inadvertently revealing himself to be vain, ignorant, socially inept, jealous, and physically aggressive. His habitual insistence that everything is “God’s truth” further weakens rather than strengthens his credibility.

The syntax mimics excited speech. Sentences are often long, loosely coordinated, and propelled by repeated conjunctions such as “and,” “thin,” “wid that,” and “jist.” Events accumulate in breathless chains instead of being arranged in measured literary periods. Exclamations, rhetorical questions, dashes, parentheses, and abrupt interjections reproduce the rhythms of a raconteur acting out his story before an audience. Paragraphs frequently open with transitional formulas—“And it was,” “Wid that,” “But,” or “And then”—which maintain forward momentum while reinforcing the tale’s oral quality.

The diction mixes comic vulgarity with misapplied genteel language. Words associated with rank, refinement, fashion, and courtship—“Barronitt,” “purliteness,” “gentaal,” “obaysance,” “ledd[y]ship”—are set beside bogs, potatoes, frogs, kicking, squeezing, and violent insults. Malapropisms and misunderstood terms are central to the style: musical instruments, French names, social titles, and written language are repeatedly garbled. This contrast exposes the narrator’s insecure social pretensions. His elevation from “bog-throthing” to baronetcy is treated not as genuine transformation but as a comic costume he has only recently put on.

The tone combines affectionate-seeming exuberance with broad satire and slapstick cruelty. Much of the comedy is physical: exaggerated bows, conspicuous winks, competitive seating, secret hand-squeezing, an enraged outburst, a flight down the stairs, and the final crushing of the Frenchman’s hand. Violence is rendered through playful culinary metaphors such as “raspberry jam,” keeping the conclusion within the register of farce. At the same time, the humour depends heavily on period stereotypes of Irish impulsiveness, vanity, linguistic confusion, and pretension, as well as on caricature of the French rival.

Structurally, the story begins by establishing the narrator’s inflated self-image, then introduces a romantic rivalry, stages a prolonged drawing-room contest, and ends with a reversal that explains the title’s promised injury. The central scene is extended through incremental repetition: the Frenchman speaks incomprehensible French, Sir Pathrick boasts and flirts, the widow reacts, and the mysterious hand responds with increasingly forceful squeezes. Suspense rests on a simple piece of spatial misdirection—the narrator assumes he is holding the widow’s hand behind the sofa. The revelation that both men have been squeezing each other’s hands retrospectively transforms the entire courtship scene into sexual and social embarrassment. The closing line returns neatly to the initial question, giving the rambling monologue the shape of a comic explanatory anecdote.

Overall, the style is performative rather than psychologically subtle. Character emerges almost entirely through voice, and the narrative’s energy comes from phonetic spelling, hyperbole, repetition, misunderstanding, and the widening gap between self-perception and reality. The ideal imitation would use a boastful and oblivious first-person speaker, heavily marked oral diction, cascading conjunctions, direct reader address, repeated claims of gentility, and a farcical plot whose final revelation humiliates the narrator without allowing him fully to understand his own defeat.

The story uses a detached third-person omniscient narrator who moves freely between external action, individual perceptions, and the collective psychology of the soldiers. The narrative perspective remains close enough to Sergeant Morton to expose his grievances, vanity, anger, and rapidly changing impulses, yet it never fully endorses his interpretation of events. Instead, the narrator observes him with cool, understated irony. Brief shifts toward other soldiers—the sleepy corporal, the frightened Johnston, the apologetic wounded man, or Patterson facing what he imagines as his “death-chamber”—broaden the scene into a study of group behaviour under pressure.

The prose combines plain military realism with vivid, occasionally grotesque figurative language. Concrete details dominate: packs used as pillows, pipes lit in bare rooms, cartridge belts tangled around legs, blood running down a neck, and a boot leaving a “greasy red streak” on the floor. Images are often startlingly physical and unsentimental. The fruit concealed beneath the soldiers’ shirts becomes a “horrible cancer” of bumps, while bullets “moaned, hummed, spat, burst, and sang.” Such personification makes the unseen gunfire feel animate and predatory. Similes tend to draw on ordinary civilian experience—a distant target resembles “an upright needle from the full length of a ballroom,” and gun smoke smells like burnt paper and firecrackers—showing how men understand combat through familiar, inadequate comparisons.

Sentence structure is generally direct and declarative, especially during action, but it varies to control pace. The opening uses cumulative clauses and semicolon-linked complaints to reproduce Morton’s sustained irritation. During the attack, shorter sentences, abrupt cries, and rapid transitions create confusion and momentum. Repetition intensifies emotional pressure: “When was Knowles killed?” and “Can’t you shut up?” convey the sergeant’s frantic need to impose order on chaos. At other moments, longer descriptive sentences slow the action to isolate a wound, a gesture, or a psychological response. This alternation between compressed violence and unnervingly calm observation gives the battle sequence its force.

Dialogue is central to characterization and is colloquial, repetitive, and socially differentiated without heavy dialect spelling. Morton speaks in commands, rhetorical complaints, sarcastic questions, and grand declarations. His verbal habits reveal both insecurity and a genuine capacity for command. Corporal Flagler’s respectful agreement is knowingly mechanical, while the privates grumble, curse, and offer practical excuses. Much of the humour arises from the gap between formal military hierarchy and ordinary human behaviour: soldiers pilfer fruit, complain about marching, become tangled in equipment, and continue personal conversations until shooting begins. Even in crisis, speech remains fragmentary and mundane rather than conventionally heroic.

The tone blends black comedy, irony, brutality, and restrained pathos. Before the attack, Morton appears pompous, irritable, and faintly ridiculous, inspecting the post with “a major-general’s serious scowl.” Once fighting starts, many of those same traits become functional courage. His rigidity steadies the men, and his insistence on resistance transforms a small, disorganized squad into a force the enemy mistakes for roughly one hundred soldiers. The story neither idealizes nor dismisses him. His heroism grows out of vanity, anger, professional instinct, and stubbornness rather than noble rhetoric. This moral ambiguity is a defining feature of the style.

The narration frequently juxtaposes pastoral calm with sudden violence. Bees hum, flowers scent the room, and sunshine falls across a sleeping soldier shortly before bullets enter the house. The tranquil landscape remains visually beautiful even as it becomes populated by “little fleeting figures” and filled with death. This contrast produces an anti-romantic view of warfare: nature is indifferent, combat is confusing, and death is often abrupt, arbitrary, or absurd. Corpses are treated with deliberate bluntness, as when Johnston’s hanging body is later pierced three more times “by chance.” The adverbial aside makes the horror more disturbing by presenting it as incidental.

Structurally, the story follows a sharp escalation. It begins as a comic account of military resentment and petty indiscipline, pauses in a deceptive interval of domestic ease, and then contracts into a sustained defensive action. As casualties accumulate, the occupied house changes from an unpleasant billet into a claustrophobic chamber of smoke, blood, and bodies. The pacing grows progressively faster, while the available space and number of active men diminish. Morton’s arc mirrors this progression: he moves from complaining about duty, to taking pride in fortifying the house, to exercising real authority, and finally to dying in the position he had resented being ordered to hold.

Irony supplies the story’s final organizing principle. Morton initially condemns the army and doubts the quality of his men; in battle, those supposedly inadequate soldiers mount an extraordinary defence. His superiors appear to have abandoned him, yet he continues to act as if orders and reinforcement must still belong to a coherent military system. The final perspective shift to the attacking subaltern delivers the climax through understatement. The enemy’s estimate that the defenders were “at least one hundred strong” retrospectively measures the squad’s achievement without sentimental tribute. The ending is concise, external, and bitterly validating: the defenders are recognized only through the scale of the resistance they created, after nearly all of them have been killed or wounded.

Overall, the style is realist and naturalistic, with impressionistic attention to sensation and psychology. It rejects polished heroics in favour of confusion, bodily vulnerability, accidental courage, and institutional absurdity. Its emotional power comes from restraint: heroism is revealed indirectly through behaviour, pathos is interrupted by grotesque detail or comedy, and the final judgment is entrusted to an enemy officer’s astonished miscalculation.

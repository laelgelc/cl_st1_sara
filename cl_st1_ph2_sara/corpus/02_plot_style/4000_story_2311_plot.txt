A visitor dines with Mr Colbran, the sardonic but generous vicar of Beauvale and an amateur archaeologist. Colbran reads from a fifteenth-century abbey record describing how, during a winter service, a fiery “devil” shattered the abbey’s stained-glass east window, terrifying the monks and leaving bloodlike fragments on the snow.

He then imagines the human story behind the account. A medieval serf, brutally flogged for killing a horse that attacked him, burns his master’s stables and flees into the frozen forest. Starving, wounded, and terrified, he reaches a mill where Martha, the miller’s daughter, secretly shelters and feeds him. Because searchers are hunting him, Martha gives him a knife and supplies, and the two escape together through a snowstorm, hoping to find refuge among outlaws.

Lost in the storm, they encounter the brightly illuminated abbey window. Mistaking its colored glass and the monks behind it for supernatural beings, the serf climbs the stonework to seize a piece of the wondrous light for Martha. He breaks the window, sees the frightened monks within, dislodges a religious statue, and falls into the snow with a fragment of red glass—creating the event the monks interpret as a demonic assault.

The fugitives continue into the woods, nearly freezing before making a fire. The serf gives Martha the red glass, which they regard as a magical “bloodstone” symbolizing his life and love. As evening falls, they awaken to the sound of wolves. Colbran abruptly insists that they lived happily ever after, but his listener rejects the comforting ending, recognizing that their probable fate was death.

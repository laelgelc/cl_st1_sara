The story uses a stark naturalist style, presenting poverty, domestic violence, cruelty and helplessness as ordinary features of life in an urban tenement. Its world is governed less by moral justice than by disparities of power: adults dominate children, children dominate animals, and the dog occupies the lowest and most vulnerable position. Violence is habitual and often treated by the characters as entertainment, discipline or domestic routine. This matter-of-fact social realism makes the final tragedy feel inevitable rather than exceptional.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, but it stays especially close to the dog’s presumed perceptions. Human motives are described with cool detachment, while the animal’s gestures are translated into a rich emotional language of shame, guilt, prayer, devotion and despair. The narrator knowingly anthropomorphises the dog, attributing to him ideas of crime, repentance, fidelity and social rank. At the same time, qualifying expressions such as “no doubt,” “apparently” and “it is not to be supposed” create mock-objective distance. This combination gives the narration the air of a solemn psychological report while quietly exposing the absurdity and brutality of the human world.

The tone continually blends pathos with irony. Elevated or ceremonial words are applied to shabby, violent events: the child becomes a “potentate” and “monarch,” the dog a “subject” and “retainer,” their encounters are “interviews,” and attacks become “recreation,” “carnival” or “fun.” Such euphemistic phrasing does not soften the cruelty; it intensifies it by revealing how violence can be normalised or enjoyed. The irony is often bitterly comic, as when the dog’s evasions are described as “strategic ability of a high order,” but the humour remains inseparable from pity. Near the end, comic observation survives even during the dog’s fall, making the scene more disturbing rather than less serious.

The diction alternates between plain physical description and exaggerated, quasi-formal abstraction. Concrete nouns—cobbles, dust, rope, sticks, saucepans, coal, coffee-pot, stairways and furniture—give the setting tactile realism. Against these details stand words such as “contritely,” “protestations,” “sublime,” “fidelity” and “infinite lowliness.” The disparity between lofty language and degraded circumstances is central to the style. It confers dignity on the dog’s loyalty while satirising the pretensions and moral failures of the people around him.

Sentences are generally clear, chronological and moderately paced, with frequent coordination that allows actions to accumulate. Short declarative sentences punctuate important turns—“No one was in,” “So the dog prospered,” or the final discovery—while longer sentences catalogue movement, reaction and violence. Repetition creates structural and emotional pressure. The dog repeatedly rolls onto his back, holds his paws “in a peculiar manner,” and offers a “small prayer”; the child and the adults repeatedly beat him; the dog repeatedly forgives and returns. Because the same submissive ritual first appears in relatively minor abuse and then recurs during the fatal assault, it becomes both a motif and a tragic refrain.

Characterisation is primarily behavioural rather than introspective. The child is simultaneously cruel, possessive, affectionate and brave. He first strikes the dog for amusement, later claims him as a “valuable thing,” and eventually becomes his protector. This moral inconsistency is presented without explanatory commentary, suggesting that the child imitates the violence surrounding him even as he remains capable of attachment. The father is characterised through drunkenness, arbitrary authority and gleeful aggression; the rest of the family forms a hostile collective rather than a set of distinct personalities. By contrast, the dog is individuated through bodily gestures—wagging, slinking, capering, drooping, praying—and through his unwavering capacity to forgive.

The imagery often turns physical posture into emotional meaning. The dog droops “like a scorched plant,” slinks “like an assassin,” swerves “as if caught in a tide,” and carries fear or devotion in his ears, eyes, tail and paws. Religious and feudal imagery elevates his submission: he prays, repents and serves a monarch with “perfect faith.” These metaphors cast canine loyalty as morally sublime while also showing how absolute devotion leaves him defenceless. The child’s protection is similarly rendered through romance and chivalry when he emerges “like a knight,” though his courage is powerless against adult force.

The setting is sketched economically but functions as a system of confinement. The dusty street, dark stairways, cramped room and crowded tenement establish an atmosphere of heat, dirt and restricted movement. Domestic objects repeatedly become weapons, so that the home offers no stable distinction between shelter and danger. The vertical geography of the building is especially important: the child painfully ascends the stairs with the dog early in the story, while at the end the dog is thrown five stories down and the child must descend slowly, backward, to reach him. This spatial reversal gives the plot a grim formal symmetry.

The narrative structure is linear and cumulative. It begins with a chance meeting, develops through attachment and domestic incorporation, establishes a repetitive pattern of abuse and protection, and culminates in a single extreme act. Early incidents foreshadow the ending: the dog’s dragging rope, stumbling, fear of the stairway, ritual submission and inability to understand human violence all anticipate his final helplessness. The climax briefly widens the viewpoint to show neighbours reacting to the dog’s flight, producing a grotesque public spectacle before the narrative contracts again to the child and the body.

The ending is restrained and deliberately unelaborated. Rather than supply moral commentary, consolation or an account of the child’s thoughts, the narrator closes on a static image of companionship transformed into mourning. The child’s slow descent delays the reunion and emphasises his physical smallness and impotence. The final phrase, “his dark-brown friend,” recalls the story’s recurring designation of the dog but replaces possession and hierarchy with friendship. This quiet lexical shift gives the conclusion its emotional force: only after the dog’s death is the relationship stated in its simplest and most equal form.

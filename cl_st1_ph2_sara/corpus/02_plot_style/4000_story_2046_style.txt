The story is a light social comedy built around a single exaggerated character trait: McQuae’s extraordinary absent-mindedness. Its humour is cumulative rather than plot-driven. Each anecdote enlarges the same flaw—from arriving for dinner on the wrong day, to forgetting invited guests, losing a passenger from a carriage, and finally mislaying his own wife—so that ordinary forgetfulness escalates into genial absurdity. The comic method combines repetition, increasingly serious consequences, and restrained narration. Improbable events are presented as if they were practical inconveniences requiring perfectly sober discussion.

The narrative voice is conversational, urbane, and dryly amused. It moves between second-person narration in the opening episode and first-person reminiscence thereafter, creating the atmosphere of an anecdote told among friends. The narrator is both participant and observer: fond of McQuae, irritated by him, and willing to enjoy his discomfort. His apparent reasonableness provides a comic counterweight to McQuae’s chaos. Remarks such as being “glad” that McQuae must travel to Scotland in evening dress, or noting that his prospect of reunion with his wife is not entirely pleasurable, reveal a tone of mock cruelty without genuine malice.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization and comic timing. Exchanges are brisk, idiomatic, and often structured around misunderstanding, delayed realization, or deadpan correction. Short answers create particularly effective beats: “Had an accident?” “Yes.” “Cart upset?” “No, only me.” McQuae speaks in cheerful exclamations—“Great Scott!”, “By Jove!”, “Good God!”—and tends to grasp disasters only at the last possible moment. Other characters answer with irony, impatience, or literal-minded understatement. The dialogue frequently supplies the punchline while the narration quietly prepares and then extends it.

The prose uses a mixture of compact conversational sentences and longer, carefully balanced comic periods. Parenthetical asides are prominent and often contain secondary jokes, as when the narrator explains that Leena was merely the young woman McQuae “recollected being engaged to,” before adding that he had forgotten two other fiancées. Colons, semicolons, dashes, and parentheses regulate timing, allowing the narrator to postpone crucial information until the funniest point. Lists also serve the humour, especially when a task is reduced to the supposedly impossible feat of remembering “the day, the church, and the girl, all in one morning.”

The diction is informal but polished, with a distinctly late-Victorian or Edwardian British flavour. Period expressions and social references—“foolscap,” “hansom,” “beanfeast,” “table d’hôte,” “not a sou,” the Mansion House, house-boats, clubs, first nights, and illustrated newspapers—place the comedy within an upper-middle-class metropolitan world. The language assumes familiarity with servants, dinner parties, theatres, hotels, railway travel, lodging houses, and fashionable leisure. Mild oaths and colloquialisms enliven the speech without making the overall register coarse.

Characterization is economical and behaviour-based. McQuae receives little physical description; he is constructed almost entirely through repeated acts of heedlessness, his good humour, and his bewildered responses to consequences. His absent-mindedness is treated as a force of nature rather than a psychological condition. Supporting characters are similarly defined by a few sharp reactions: the suspicious wife, the exasperated cook, the bruised and grammatically shaken Hallyard, the laughing policeman, and Maud, whose unseen response is made more formidable by being withheld.

The story’s structure resembles a linked comic sketch or club-room reminiscence. It begins with a self-contained mishap, broadens into a sequence of illustrative anecdotes, then reaches its climax after McQuae’s marriage. Transitional statements such as “Matters work out still more awkwardly” and “Another day” openly signal escalation. The marriage episode pays off earlier predictions that he could not possibly remember a bride, ceremony, and married life together. The final incident is the most elaborate because forgetfulness now threatens not merely etiquette or comfort but the continuation of his marriage.

Irony and understatement dominate the humour. The narrator habitually describes ludicrous situations in formal or euphemistic terms: a cook threatens to “wash her hands, figuratively speaking,” a violent fall “shakes” Hallyard’s grammar, and a solicitor “instituted inquiries” to reunite a man with the address he has forgotten. Mock-heroic touches heighten trivial events, most clearly when McQuae is restored to his wife “after the manner of an Adelphi hero in the last act.” The story also uses anticlimax effectively: the reunion itself is omitted, and the closing joke lies in McQuae’s vague admission that his wife said “much what I expected,” followed by the narrator’s dry observation that he never revealed what that was.

The pace is quick and episodic, with minimal scenic description except where atmosphere sharpens the absurdity. The stormy Scarborough beach briefly introduces a darker, almost melodramatic mood, only for the crouching, broken-spirited figure to prove to be McQuae, stranded because he does not know where he lives. Even here, distress is continually converted into practical comedy through hunger, lack of money, failed door-knocking, and flight from the police.

Overall, the style is genial, anecdotal, and satirical rather than psychologically realistic. It treats social embarrassment as comic catastrophe, relies on escalation and precise timing, and maintains an affectionate distance from its central eccentric. The ideal imitation would use a calm, observant narrator; brisk dialogue; period British idiom; parenthetical jokes; repeated variations on one character flaw; and a final understated line that leaves the most dramatic reaction offstage.

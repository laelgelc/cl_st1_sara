The story adopts the manner of a traditional animal folktale filtered through an early children’s literary voice. Its omniscient narrator speaks with genial authority, freely summarising events, explaining customs, and occasionally addressing the reader directly. Remarks such as “I suppose” and the assurance that a hippopotamus really does laugh “Guk-uk-uk-uk!” create the impression of an amused adult storyteller performing for children. This conversational intrusiveness makes the fantastic world feel both knowingly invented and confidently factual.

The tone is playful, whimsical, and lightly satirical, even when the action involves capture, enslavement, killing, or revenge. Violence is presented in exaggerated, storybook terms rather than with emotional or physical realism. Keo’s irrepressible laughter supplies the dominant comic motif, functioning as a refrain that marks his temperament, punctuates transitions, and turns danger into farce. The final joke—Gouie swears by the bones of a grandfather famous for having none—provides an ironic, neatly delayed punch line.

The diction combines plain narrative language with mock-ceremonial grandeur. Phrases such as “ancient and aristocratic family,” “royal tribe,” and “implore your mercy and friendly assistance” elevate hippopotamus society into a comic imitation of human monarchy. This stateliness is repeatedly undercut by mud wallowing, waddling, snorting, and Keo’s cheerful foolishness. Archaic or period-flavoured expressions, including “’tis,” “anigh,” “mankind knew it not,” and “a year and a day,” strengthen the fairy-tale atmosphere. The depiction of African characters relies on dated colonial vocabulary, physical stereotypes, and a simplified opposition between “the black people” and the animals; this is a conspicuous feature of the story’s historical register.

Sentences are generally clear, chronological, and moderately expansive. Long descriptive or explanatory sentences often use parallel clauses and cumulative lists, as in the catalogue of every river curve, pit, shallow, rock, stump, and wallow known to the hippopotamuses. Repetition gives the prose an oral rhythm: “part beast, part man, part fowl and part fish,” “part sorcerer, part wizard, part magician and part fairy,” and the recurring period of “a year and a day.” Short declarative sentences are used for emphasis, especially at turning points: “It was continual warfare,” “Keo laughed,” and “And Keo ran.” Exclamation marks, direct speech, and sound effects keep the pacing lively and make the story suitable for reading aloud.

Characterisation is broad and emblematic. Keo is defined by a single governing trait—jollity—which remains constant through hunger, captivity, supernatural transformation, and revenge. Gouie is thoughtful and cunning but ultimately dishonest; the adult hippopotamuses are dignified, fearful, and devoted to family; Glinkomok is an awe-inspiring magical helper. Characters are distinguished less through psychological depth than through repeated behaviours, speech patterns, titles, and comic physical details. The animals are anthropomorphised through language, kinship, law, honour, monarchy, and moral obligation, while retaining recognisable animal habits and bodies.

The plot follows a symmetrical folktale design. An initial capture produces a solemn bargain; the hero returns home, receives magical aid, honours his promise, and reverses the captor–captive relationship. Gouie’s own words and conditions are turned against him, creating poetic justice. Repeated riding episodes form an escalating pattern: first groups of villagers disappear, then Gouie himself is carried into danger. The structure depends on clear cause and effect, patterned recurrence, delayed fulfilment, and a final reversal rather than suspenseful complexity.

Humour arises from incongruity, hyperbole, literalism, and narrative understatement. The hippopotamuses weep enough to raise the river, Keo becomes stronger than ten elephants and faster than the wind, and Gouie calmly commercialises rides on an invulnerable captive. Verbal comedy appears in Keo’s threat to have “a piece” of Gouie’s leg and in the mirrored oaths involving tusks and bones. The narrator often describes absurdities as though they were perfectly reasonable, which intensifies their comic effect.

Overall, the style is that of a brisk, orally inflected literary fairy tale: episodic, repetitive, morally ordered, and rich in comic exaggeration. Its central values are promise-keeping, loyalty, courage, and reciprocal justice. Keo prospers because he keeps his oath despite danger, whereas Gouie gains worldly status but loses peace of mind because he breaks his. The ending therefore combines a folktale moral with a final absurdist twist, preserving the story’s buoyant tone while delivering its ethical judgment.

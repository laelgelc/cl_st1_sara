The story adopts the form of a comic urban sketch, blending personal essay, fictional encounter, social observation, and mock-documentary reporting. Its first-person narrator presents himself as a curious, cultivated, mildly eccentric observer whose exaggerated sensitivity turns an ordinary feature of London—the proliferation of advertising bills—into a source of fantasy, satire, and investigation. He speaks with an air of conscientious accuracy, yet his supposedly rational explanations are repeatedly undermined by absurdity, self-deception, and theatrical overstatement. This combination creates a genial, ironic narrative voice: urbane and alert to social detail, but also deliberately pompous, fanciful, and unreliable in comic ways.

The tone moves fluidly between mock gravity, whimsical enthusiasm, grotesque description, and affectionate social comedy. Mundane subjects are treated as though they possess epic, supernatural, or political importance. Posters become instruments of psychological vengeance; advertising vans become “fatal cars”; their drivers are “Ministers of Fate”; and an experienced tradesman becomes “His Majesty,” the King of the Bill-Stickers. The humour rests heavily on incongruity, especially the contrast between elevated language and low or ordinary material. Ceremonial, royal, military, biblical, and classical vocabulary is applied to paste, hoardings, tobacco, workplace disputes, and public-house refreshments. Conversely, moments that appear sublime are punctured by practical details, colloquial remarks, or bodily discomfort.

The prose is expansive, rhythmical, and highly elaborated. Sentences are often long and cumulative, built from parallel clauses, repeated conditional structures, parenthetical qualifications, and escalating lists. The opening fantasy repeatedly uses constructions such as “If he…” to imagine the enemy encountering accusations on houses, walls, omnibuses, bridges, pavements, and vans. This anaphoric pattern creates momentum while intensifying the joke through accumulation. Lists are a central stylistic device throughout: newspaper disasters, famous advertisers, taxes, branches of bill-sticking, districts of London, and occupational grievances are all catalogued in detail. The syntax frequently postpones the comic payoff, allowing a sentence to swell into apparent seriousness before ending in a pun, anticlimax, or absurd qualification.

Parentheses, dashes, semicolons, and intrusive asides reproduce the movement of a lively speaking mind. The narrator constantly corrects, qualifies, or digresses from his own account. Phrases such as “which Heaven forbid,” “by-the-by,” and “as nearly as I could judge” lend an air of conversational spontaneity while also parodying scrupulous factual narration. Even obvious jokes are presented with bureaucratic precision. The closing insistence that the narrator’s sickness must have been caused by arsenic in the paste or some poisonous ingredient in the ink—and certainly not by tobacco or rum-and-water—is a characteristic example of comic denial delivered in formal language.

The diction deliberately mixes registers. The narrator uses polished literary language, elaborate circumlocution, allusion, and mock-official terminology, while the King speaks in colloquial working-class English marked by nonstandard grammar and pronunciation: “wans,” “winder,” “air as following,” “up’ards,” “an’t,” and “year” for “years.” His speech includes occupational vocabulary such as “dabber,” “trampers,” “joints,” “hoarding,” and “black-wash,” giving the story a strong documentary texture. The contrast between the narrator’s rhetorical refinement and the King’s direct idiom produces humour without depriving the tradesman of authority. Indeed, the King is the expert, while the educated narrator is often naïve, physically fragile, or carried away by fantasy.

Dialogue is used both for characterization and comic timing. Exchanges are frequently structured around misunderstanding, as when the narrator calls himself a “collector” and the King anxiously assumes he collects income tax, water rates, gas rates, or sewer charges. Short questions and answers interrupt the more ornate narration, quickening the pace and creating a theatrical rhythm. The narrator’s ceremonious questions—asking permission to inscribe the man’s name “upon the tablets” of his memory—are answered with dry practicality. The King’s composure is itself a recurring joke: surrounded by lurid announcements of catastrophe and by the noise of obstructed traffic, he smokes, drinks, reclines, and calmly discusses his trade.

Characterization is economical but vivid, relying on selected physical traits, habitual gestures, and distinctive speech. The King is introduced through a rapid sequence of compact details: “a shining face, a tight head, a bright eye, a moist wink, a quick speech, and a ready air.” The parallel phrasing makes the portrait energetic and memorable. He combines the qualities of craftsman, entrepreneur, trade historian, street fighter, and genial monarch. His grand title is comic, but his experience, technical ingenuity, and command of his professional history give him genuine stature. The narrator’s own character emerges indirectly through his curiosity, love of novelty, literary associations, overactive imagination, and comic evasions.

Description is intensely visual and often grotesque. The ruined warehouse is represented through imagery of decomposition, encrustation, injury, and organic decay. It resembles “an old cheese,” a fouled ship’s keel, a wreck, and a body with “rents and gashes” beneath which apparently endless layers of posters remain. Paste and paper seem to consume the architecture they cover, until the building becomes “one adhesive heap of rottenness and poster.” Such descriptions transform the modern city into a strange material organism. Similes are plentiful and often unexpectedly concrete: a scrap of tobacco resembles a curl-paper removed from a barmaid’s head, while the last surviving poster waves like a shattered flag.

The London setting is rendered through specific streets, markets, bridges, theatres, public houses, construction hoardings, and commercial names. These details create a dense sense of urban actuality. The narrative is fascinated by the city as a field of competing messages, trades, routes, and unofficial territories. Advertising occupies every available surface and turns the environment into a form of public text. Capitalized catch-lines and brand names visually imitate the posters being described, so typography becomes part of the style. The conspicuous capitals—“SECRET KEYS,” “PROFESSOR HOLLOWAY,” “MOSES and SON”—reproduce the aggressive visual rhetoric of advertising while also making the page resemble the street.

The structure moves from imaginative speculation to observation, encounter, interview, and retrospective report. The opening establishes the central conceit that posters can persecute the conscience. The ruined warehouse then anchors that conceit in physical London. The passing advertising vans provide a transition from solitary meditation to narrative action, and the discovery of the King turns the piece into an informal ethnographic interview. A long central section records the history, practices, economics, rivalries, and terminology of bill-sticking. The conclusion returns to personal comedy, as the narrator’s intoxication or nausea dissolves the encounter and leaves the King once again elusive.

Pacing alternates between energetic comic sequences and lengthy deposits of factual material. The fantasies, descriptions, and dialogue are brisk and escalating, whereas the King’s scroll introduces a denser, more historical mode. This documentary passage retains vitality through anecdotes of territorial conflict, fines, rival companies, and physical fights. Facts are not presented neutrally; they are framed as a royal proclamation and repeatedly interrupted by the narrator’s questions and reactions. The story therefore sustains its entertainment even while conveying genuine information about an obscure urban occupation.

The style makes frequent use of allusion and parody. Biblical references such as Belshazzar’s palace lend supernatural menace to advertisements; fairy-tale material appears in the comparison with the Sleeping Beauty; royal history, Napoleon, the Astronomer Royal, the Great Wall of China, and theatrical conventions are invoked for comic enlargement. Puns and verbal echoes are also prominent, as in the association of “Drama” with “Drummer,” the idea of “posting” information like a proclamation, and the repeated exploitation of royal language. These devices assume a reader able to recognize cultural references, but the humour also works through the immediate mismatch between reference and subject.

Beneath the comedy lies a satirical interest in commerce, competition, publicity, regulation, and the transformation of public space. Advertising is represented as ubiquitous, coercive, and almost animate: walls reproach, stones speak, names pursue, and vehicles broadcast catastrophe to indifferent citizens. The story also observes the hypocrisies of authority, particularly the politician who supports restrictions on bill-sticking while expecting his own election bills to be placed everywhere. Yet the satire is not harshly condemnatory. It remains curious and humane, showing respect for practical knowledge and for the elaborate customs, hierarchies, rivalries, and pride that develop within a disregarded trade.

Overall, the style is exuberant, digressive, theatrical, and densely figurative. It derives its distinctive effect from the union of precise urban observation with extravagant imaginative transformation. The narrator elevates street life into mock epic while repeatedly exposing his own pretensions, and the story treats factual reportage as an occasion for character comedy, linguistic play, and social satire.

The story adopts the style of an early twentieth-century historical mystery or moral detective tale, combining an Edwardian frame narrative with a melodramatic account of colonial Virginia. Its atmosphere is antiquarian and ceremonious: the faded diary, fragile paper, London library and firelit reading room establish distance from the events, while the diary itself transports the reader into a vanished society governed by inherited rank, formal law and religious belief. This layered structure gives the narrative the authority of a preserved testimony and makes the central case feel at once legendary and documentary.

The point of view is multiply framed. An unnamed observer first describes Sir Henry Marquis reading the diary; within the diary, Pendleton’s daughter records a case her father related; and much of the action is therefore filtered through retrospective phrases such as “my father said” and “my father thought.” This chain of narrators creates a formal, oral-history quality. Pendleton is not given interiority in a modern psychological sense. Instead, his daughter interprets his bearing, intentions and moral stature, presenting him as an almost biblical lawgiver. The narrative voice is admiring and reverential, repeatedly emphasizing his physical dominance, composure and certainty.

The prose is elevated, archaizing and rhetorically elaborate. Its vocabulary draws heavily on law, religion and social hierarchy: “Court of Equity,” “commonwealth,” “assignment,” “conveyance,” “King of Kings,” “sacrilege” and “damnation.” Period markers such as “eagles,” “foolscap,” “stock,” “Spanish Grandee” and “justice of the peace” reinforce the historical setting. Characters speak in highly formal constructions, even during confrontation, and address one another by surname and title. The dialogue is therefore less naturalistic than theatrical, designed to reveal moral position, social identity and intellectual combat.

Sentence structure alternates between short declarations and long, cumulative passages. Brief statements—“There will be no sale” or “The sign of death!”—carry decisive dramatic force. Around them, extended sentences gather descriptive clauses, qualifications and parallel phrases, often moving toward an emphatic conclusion. Repetition is a major rhetorical device: “the very things, the very particular things,” “You are lost, Zindorf!” and the recurring language of signs, masters and reversed purposes build the effect of a sermon or courtroom summation. Exclamation, apostrophe and elevated abstraction give climactic speeches a declamatory intensity.

Characterization is strongly emblematic. Pendleton represents justice, Virginia and the providential moral order; Morrow represents sensual appetite and aristocratic frivolity; Zindorf represents avarice, apostasy and spiritual inversion. Their bodies and clothing function as visible moral signs. Zindorf’s tonsured head, monk-like movement and patched garments suggest religious humility, but each resemblance is exposed as a counterfeit produced by greed. Morrow’s beauty, imported clothes, jeweled hand and graceful gestures embody cultivated decadence. Pendleton’s “big chin,” hardened strength and iron authority make him a monumental rather than intimate figure. The young woman is depicted more idealistically, through whiteness, flowers, radiance and Spanish beauty, emphasizing both her innocence and her ignorance of the men’s scheme.

The central imaginative method is symbolic reversal. Zindorf has abandoned the Church but retains the appearance of a monk; a former church has become his house; supposed humility is actually avarice; a deed of sale becomes the means of proving freedom; and a bell rung for one dead man summons justice for another. Pendleton explicitly turns these reversals into a theology of signs. The story’s governing idea is that evil corrupts sacred forms but is ultimately exposed by the very forms it has misused. The decisive clue—the impressions made by the patches on Zindorf’s knees—is both a conventional piece of physical evidence and the completion of this symbolic pattern. What visually mimics prayer becomes the murderer’s identifying mark.

Religious imagery pervades the natural and architectural descriptions. Sunlight lies on the fields “like a benediction”; the open air cleanses the room of liquor, tobacco ash and perfume; gothic windows and ruined church walls surround the conspiracy; and the distant bell acts as warning, summons and judgment. Nature is calm, fertile and benevolent outside, while covert violence gathers within the divided church-house. This contrast gives the setting moral significance rather than merely decorative detail. Biblical allusions, especially to the Pentateuch and the judges of Israel, elevate Pendleton’s intervention into an enactment of divine law.

The pacing is deliberately slow and cumulative. The story spends substantial time establishing the proposed sale, the legal arguments concerning the girl’s status and the personalities of the three men. Suspense grows through delayed explanation: the tolling bell, the people approaching on every road, the hidden papers and Pendleton’s cryptic warnings are introduced before their meanings are disclosed. Each revelation enlarges the case—from an immoral transaction, to proof of the girl’s freedom, to Ordez’s murder, and finally to Zindorf’s identification as the killer. The last line supplies the concrete solution after a long sequence of metaphysical pronouncements, giving the ending the snap of a detective-story reveal.

The tone is grave, ominous and morally absolute, with occasional touches of sardonic wit in Morrow’s mockery and Pendleton’s controlled replies. The narrative does not cultivate ambiguity about good and evil. Its social and ethical world is hierarchical, patriarchal and providential, and its treatment of slavery is framed through legal status, personal villainy and rescue rather than sustained examination of the institution itself. Overall, the style blends legal puzzle, gothic atmosphere, biblical rhetoric and historical melodrama, producing a story in which deduction and divine justice appear to converge upon the same unmistakable sign.

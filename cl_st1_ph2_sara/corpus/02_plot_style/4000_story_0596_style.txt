The story’s style is comic-realist, briskly plotted, and gently satirical. It treats a relatively ordinary crisis—catching a train in time for a marriage—as though it were an epic struggle against fate. The comedy arises from the disproportion between Arthur’s catastrophic interpretation of every setback and the practical manageability of his circumstances. Suspense is genuine but repeatedly punctured by irony, understatement, and the eventual revelation that Arthur’s assumptions, rather than external events alone, are the chief source of his suffering.

The narration is third-person and closely aligned with Arthur’s consciousness. Although the narrator retains an amused, observant distance, events are usually filtered through Arthur’s anxious perceptions. Delays threaten his “career,” “honour,” and “life”; a missed appointment makes suicide appear “the one feasible enterprise”; and ordinary physical anxiety becomes a pair of pincers clutching his heart and an anvil resting on his stomach. The repeated pincers-and-anvil image functions as a comic leitmotif, disappearing whenever Simeon restores order and returning whenever uncertainty intrudes. This free-indirect approach lets the prose reproduce Arthur’s melodrama while simultaneously exposing its absurdity.

The narrative voice is urbane, knowing, and mock-heroic. It frequently elevates trivial actions through grand language: Simeon has “infinite calm” and “infinite resource,” his confidence is “almost god-like,” and wheeling a trunk over ice becomes a feat of gallantry. Personification contributes to the same effect. Clocks become hostile forces whose hands refuse to move; a train “obstinately” remains still; another is “in an ironical mood” and later “covers itself with glory.” Such phrasing makes machinery, timetables, weather, and bureaucracy appear to conspire against Arthur, while the narrator’s tone signals that this conspiracy exists largely in his imagination.

The diction combines formal, sometimes ornate narration with colloquial and regionally marked speech. Words such as “indubitably,” “celerity,” “sangfroid,” “immemorial,” and “personification” give the narrative a polished Edwardian flavour, while expressions such as “What’s up?”, “By Jove,” “Bosh!”, “confounded thing,” and “welly” locate the characters in a more immediate social and regional world. Dialogue is economical and strongly characterising. Arthur speaks in questions, alarms, protests, and solemn declarations; Simeon uses clipped instructions and blunt corrections. Their contrast is encapsulated in Simeon’s repeated practical imperatives—“Light the stove,” “Hurry,” “Quick!”—and in his final diagnosis that Arthur “think[s] too much.”

Sentence rhythm varies with emotional pressure. Periodic, elaborately qualified sentences establish setting and social context, while short clauses, fragments, exclamations, and rapid exchanges accelerate moments of panic. Repetition is central to the comic rhythm: Arthur is repeatedly safe and then unsafe, reassured and then terrified; clocks and exact times recur obsessively; and phrases such as “He was safe” or “the pincers and the anvil” mimic the circularity of anxious thought. Parenthetical qualifications and narratorly asides add dry humour, as when luggage is described as “almost, but not quite, indispensable” or a fellow passenger is classified as “a sort of a Levite” and another as “perhaps a cousin of a Good Samaritan.”

Timekeeping governs both the subject matter and the prose. Exact times—“sixteen minutes past six,” “twenty minutes to seven,” “one minute past two”—create documentary precision and turn the story into a comic race against the clock. The repeated calculations of walking times, railway connections, and margins produce mounting tension, while also satirising the modern belief that life can be perfectly controlled by schedules. The plot advances through a chain of reversals: the clock is wrong, the porter fails to arrive, the barrow collapses, Arthur boards the wrong section of the train, and the London express encounters mist. Each apparent disaster is followed by an improvised solution, so the structure alternates methodically between crisis and relief.

The setting is rendered through concrete details of provincial industrial life: gas stoves, green blinds, incandescent street lamps, cinder-heaps, railway signals, commercial hotels, and the teapot trade. Place names such as Bursley, Knype, Hanbridge, and the Five Towns give the story a strong regional identity. The landscape is bleak, dark, icy, and industrial, yet it is not treated sentimentally. Instead, it supplies practical obstacles and an atmospheric background for the characters’ comic ordeal. References to railway rules, station inspectors, Bradshaw, telegraph offices, registry-office law, and corporate street lighting also create a socially specific world shaped by modern institutions.

Characterisation is built through contrast rather than extensive psychological exposition. Arthur is imaginative, dependent, socially anxious, and prone to converting uncertainty into disgrace. Simeon is methodical, physically capable, adaptable, and almost aggressively calm. Yet the contrast is affectionate rather than cruel. Arthur recognises and admires Simeon’s competence, while Simeon repeatedly protects him without making a display of devotion. Simeon’s advancing the clock, packing the trunk, taking charge of the barrow, hiring a milk-cart, recovering the luggage, buying a crutch, and rearranging the ceremony form a running demonstration of practical brotherly loyalty.

The humour combines situational farce, verbal irony, caricature, and anticlimax. Arthur’s elaborate fears culminate not in tragedy but in a registry-office wedding accomplished with time to spare. Even the mysterious “small bright object,” withheld long enough to suggest something momentous, remains subordinate to the mechanics of the journey. The final sentence—“there was one bachelor the less in this vale of tears”—closes in a mock-melancholy register, reducing the romantic destination to a sardonic social fact. Overall, the style is precise, energetic, theatrically suspenseful, and warmly ironic, using the machinery of a disaster narrative to celebrate practical intelligence and expose the comic vanity of excessive worry.

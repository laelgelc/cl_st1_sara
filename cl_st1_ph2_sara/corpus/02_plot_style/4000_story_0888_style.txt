The story adopts the manner of a light comic tale or provincial farce, treating an intrinsically absurd subject—the competitive hunting of truffles with trained pigs—with the solemnity of epic sport and public ritual. Its central stylistic principle is mock grandeur: local customs, reputations, and rivalries are described as matters of national or even civilizational importance. The narration repeatedly inflates the trivial through ceremonious phrasing, formal comparisons, sporting terminology, and claims such as the truffle championship’s superiority to the Derby or the Grand Prix de Rome. This exaggerated seriousness creates a gently satirical tone rather than a harshly critical one.

The narrator is omniscient, confident, and conspicuously present as a raconteur. The voice supplies historical, culinary, social, and pseudo-technical background with the authority of a guide explaining an established culture. References to Brillat-Savarin, regional traditions, professional truffle hunting, and the supposed dueling code lend documentary plausibility to comic invention. Broad declarations—“To Perigord alone come such days” or “all Montpont hunted truffles”—give the narrative the air of folklore. The narrator also uses dry asides and sweeping generalizations, often about southern French temperament or local standards of honor, to sustain an amused distance from the characters.

The diction blends elevated, ceremonial language with concrete culinary and animal imagery. Words such as “propitious,” “inexorable,” “contention,” “reiterate,” and “mortal combat” coexist with pigs, cheese, trowels, mushrooms, and flying dirt. French names, titles, place names, food terms, and occasional Latin phrases create a stylized Perigord atmosphere. The French setting is rendered less through realistic linguistic imitation than through an English-language comic convention: repeated use of “Monsieur,” elaborate courtesies, volatile honor, café conversation, and gastronomic devotion. The result is picturesque and theatrical rather than naturalistic.

Sentence structure is generally expansive and rhythmically controlled. Long descriptive sentences accumulate clauses, qualifications, and parallel phrases, allowing the narrator to build an elaborate comic premise. Short sentences are then used for emphasis at decisive moments: “Bets were freely made”; “The gallery held its breath”; “This was too much.” Repetition creates both momentum and mock solemnity, as in the parallel actions of the pigs pointing, the rivals digging, and their hands closing on the same truffle “at the same second.” Balanced constructions and antitheses are common: weekday commerce is opposed to Sunday sport, art to science, friendship to rivalry, and extreme politeness to violent hostility.

Figurative language is vivid, caricatural, and frequently incongruous. Monsieur Bonticu resembles “an enormous green pepper,” digs like a “panic-stricken beaver,” and walks on knees turned to “meringue glace.” His eyes lie at the bottoms of wells, while his face becomes a full moon. The pigs are compared to sporting dogs and synchronized machines, and human children are dismissed as “squealing, wiggling cabbages.” Such similes convert bodies and emotions into foodstuffs, animals, machinery, or oversized objects, reinforcing the tale’s exuberantly grotesque comedy. Hyperbole is pervasive: the pigs are priceless prodigies, the truffle is a monster, a verbal insult becomes culturally unspeakable, and a local quarrel activates an extraordinarily lethal code of honor.

Characterization is broad, visual, and type-based. Bonticu is the massive, stately, outwardly peaceful undertaker; Pantan is the small, bristling, irascible veterinarian-apothecary. Their contrasting physiques, professions, complexions, and temperaments are established rapidly through caricature. Both are defined by parallel obsessions: bachelorhood, professional vanity, devotion to truffle hunting, and affection for a prized pig. The pigs, Anastasie and Clotilde, function simultaneously as sporting animals, beloved companions, status symbols, and proxies for their owners’ masculine pride. Much of the humor comes from the men treating pigs with greater tenderness and respect than they extend to human beings.

Dialogue is highly formal, symmetrical, and performative. The rivals frame accusations as exquisite courtesies—“If Monsieur will pardon me” and “With profoundest respect”—even while moving toward violence. Their repeated third-person forms of address and ceremonious circumlocutions parody aristocratic codes of conduct. As anger rises, the speeches shorten and become more direct, but neither man fully abandons rhetorical display. Insults escalate through carefully staged steps, culminating in the culturally specific “puff-ball,” whose disproportionate gravity is then explained by the narrator. The exchange has the structure of a comic duel before the literal duel is declared.

The story’s pacing moves from leisurely exposition to sporting suspense and finally to melodramatic crisis. The opening takes time to establish Montpont, truffle culture, the championship, and the protagonists. Once the contest begins, precise temporal markers, score reversals, spectators, an umpire, and the diminishing light produce the rhythm of a sports report. The shared discovery under the oak compresses the action into synchronized gestures and rapid dialogue. After the challenge, the pace slows again to dwell on Bonticu’s fear, ending on a visual cliffhanger as he imagines his own name on a coffin plate.

The dominant tone is affectionate irony. Provincial society is gently mocked for making an obscure pastime the basis of prestige, identity, and mortal honor, yet its customs are described with enough richness to make them appealing. The humor depends on incongruity, escalation, pompous understatement, literalized metaphor, and the collision of etiquette with rage. Beneath the farce lie themes of masculine vanity, rivalry, civic ritual, professional identity, and the fragility of friendship when status is threatened. Overall, the prose is urbane, highly visual, theatrically French-inflected, and driven by mock-heroic narration that magnifies a culinary competition into an affair of life and death.

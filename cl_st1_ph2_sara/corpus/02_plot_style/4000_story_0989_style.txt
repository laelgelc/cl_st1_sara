The story uses a direct, economical pulp-gothic style that combines rural realism with supernatural horror. Its prose is accessible and concrete, relying on familiar domestic details—coffee, feed bills, dinner, farm equipment, a pocket watch—to establish an ordinary world before introducing the uncanny. The Vermont setting is rendered as cold, isolated, and inhospitable, with wind, mist, rocks, ruined buildings, dead grass, and barren trees creating a persistent atmosphere of decay. Nature is not passive scenery but an animate, predatory presence: the leaf taps insistently, the wind plucks at Lori’s body, and the apple tree sways, lashes, summons, and apparently reproduces through her death.

The narration is third-person and initially stays close to Lori’s physical and emotional experience. Her attraction to the tree is conveyed through bodily sensations—pounding blood, tingling skin, hunger, shuddering, breathlessness—rather than through explanation. This limited perspective preserves mystery: neither Lori nor the reader is given a rational account of her bond with the tree. After her death, the viewpoint broadens toward Steve and Ed, producing a colder, more detached aftermath. That shift also sharpens the dramatic irony, since the men interpret events through appendicitis, distance from medical care, and grief, while the reader understands the significance of the apple Lori ate.

Sentence structure is often clipped and fragmentary, especially during moments of suspense: “Carried by the wind. Tapping faintly, insistently.” Short paragraphs, repeated words, and isolated statements create a steady pulse of anticipation. In action scenes, rapid independent clauses and active verbs accelerate the pace, while longer accumulative descriptions slow it around the ruined orchard and the living tree. Repetition is a major rhythmic device. Lori repeatedly says that this will be “the last time,” insists that she is not afraid, and announces “I came,” while the tapping, wind, pain, and tree movements recur in escalating cycles. These repetitions give the story a ritualistic, compulsive quality.

The diction is plain but strongly sensory. Visual imagery emphasizes black stalks, brown leaves, red cheeks, pale snow, and glistening fruit; tactile language centers on cutting, piercing, whipping, tugging, and gnawing; sounds include tapping, groaning, rustling, screaming, and branches rubbing together. Personification is pervasive, with windows protesting, leaves dancing, hunger gnawing, pain devouring, and the tree behaving like a jealous lover or demanding supernatural entity. The imagery often carries erotic undertones, particularly when the leaf presses against Lori’s loins, the wind touches her body, and the tree catches her around the waist. This sensuality is fused with menace, making Lori’s attraction simultaneously ecstatic, shameful, and fatal.

Dialogue is terse, functional, and socially revealing. Lori speaks in anxious pleas, evasions, and playful challenges, whereas Steve and Ed use blunt questions and practical language. Their talk of business, bills, profit, taxes, hospitals, and causes of death represents a masculine world of calculation that cannot perceive Lori’s private imaginative or supernatural life. Speech tags are simple and frequently intensified by verbs such as “demanded,” “grumbled,” “rumbled,” and “grated,” reinforcing emotional pressure without elaborate psychological commentary. The men’s reserve and Lori’s urgency create a sharp contrast between repression and desire.

The plot is tightly linear and organized around escalation, concealment, and delayed revelation. A minor disturbance at the window leads to the abandoned orchard, the apple, the fatal night, and finally the graveyard. Transitions are abrupt, particularly the cut from Lori eating the apple to waking in pain and the later jump of several months. These ellipses avoid explanation and preserve momentum. The doctor’s discussion of swallowed seeds supplies a plausible clue without making the supernatural mechanism explicit. The concluding image—a vigorous apple tree growing over Lori’s grave and bearing unnaturally early red fruit—functions as a visual twist that retrospectively completes the story’s pattern.

The overall tone moves from uneasy domestic suspense through sensual fascination and panic to clinical grief, then ends in quiet uncanny dread. Horror arises less from explicit monstrosity than from implication, transformation, and the invasion of the body by nature. The final scene is restrained rather than sensational: the impossible fruit, Steve’s memory of Lori’s flushed cheeks, and Ed’s desire to leave carry the revelation. The style therefore depends on atmospheric repetition, physical immediacy, suggestive symbolism, and a compact final reversal rather than extensive exposition or overt explanation.

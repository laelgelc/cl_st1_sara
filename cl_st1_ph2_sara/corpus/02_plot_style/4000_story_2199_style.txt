The story adopts the manner of a historical adventure chronicle, combining documentary reconstruction with the energy and suspense of popular storytelling. Its narrator speaks retrospectively, from a period later than the events, and treats the surviving records as raw material to be interpreted, clarified and occasionally questioned. Dates, tonnage, troop numbers, ranks, shipboard arrangements and quoted reports create an air of factual authority, while phrases such as “it is to be presumed,” “one record says,” and “this is not in any official record” openly reveal the process of reconstruction. The result is not detached historiography but an animated retelling in which documentary detail supports a strongly shaped moral narrative.

The narrative voice is confident, conversational and distinctly intrusive. It frequently addresses the reader directly—“as you know,” “if you can realize”—or pauses to explain technical and institutional matters. Parenthetical glosses translate specialist language into ordinary terms: starboard becomes “right-hand,” port becomes “left-hand,” and the role of the Colours is explained as representing “the very soul of a Battalion.” This produces the impression of a knowledgeable raconteur speaking to a general audience. The narrator also makes conjectures about unrecorded actions and motives, filling gaps with common-sense inference and genial speculation. That looseness gives the account warmth, though it also makes the narrator’s admiration and prejudices unmistakable.

The dominant tone is admiring but restrained. Courage is celebrated through practical conduct rather than lyrical praise: the soldiers obey orders, clear ammunition, cut away wreckage, pump water and return repeatedly to dangerous work. Heroism appears collective, disciplined and workmanlike. The narrator’s strongest approval is reserved for competence, endurance, loyalty and composure under pressure. By contrast, the mutinous sailors are described bluntly as “bad, lazy and insubordinate,” and their withdrawal is labelled infamous. This sharp moral division is characteristic of the story’s military ethos, in which discipline is not merely ceremonial but the quality that makes survival possible.

Humour continually offsets the danger. Much of it is dry, understated or incongruously practical: repeated loss of consciousness becomes “a fair amount of fainting that evening”; a desperate struggle against fire requires “some deviation from the usual military evolutions”; and running out of tobacco is presented as something many soldiers may have considered as serious as the fire. Jokes spoken by the troops at moments of extreme peril reinforce their courage, especially the exchange following the explosion. The humour does not diminish the catastrophe; instead, it functions as evidence of steadiness and group spirit. National and occupational stereotypes—such as the Scotch engineer holding his “trump-card in reserve” and the French being ceremoniously polite—add to the genial period flavour.

The diction blends nautical and military terminology with plain, vigorous English. Terms such as “bulkhead,” “bunkers,” “abaft,” “countermarched,” “rudder-head” and “rank and file” lend technical precision, but they are surrounded by concrete verbs: men drag, shovel, lash, plug, pump, cut, haul and throw. This emphasis on physical action makes the account highly tactile. Colloquial expressions—“the game was up,” “taking no chances,” “turned to,” “old woman”—prevent the prose from becoming bureaucratic. Official quotations, with their formal and sometimes awkward phrasing, are set against the narrator’s livelier idiom, allowing the story to derive humour from the contrast between administrative language and violent reality.

The syntax is expansive and cumulative. Long sentences assemble sequences of actions through semicolons, dashes and coordinating clauses, often producing the sense that many operations are happening simultaneously. Parentheses and interpolated explanations mimic spoken narration, while lists convey the scale and complexity of the work. At moments of crisis, shorter declarative sentences or abrupt clauses provide emphasis: the ship fights “for her own life alone,” the magazines explode, or the troops simply return to work. Repetition is used rhythmically, especially in strings such as “pumping, bucketing, sluicing and damping,” which turn labour into a driving verbal pattern.

Structurally, the story proceeds in broadly chronological order. It begins with logistical and historical exposition, establishes the vessel’s inadequacy and the crew’s indiscipline, then concentrates on the fire as an extended central ordeal. The crisis unfolds through successive practical problems: rescuing the Colours, removing ammunition, preventing the flames from moving forward, cooling the bulkhead, preserving steering and finally navigating to port. Each apparent reprieve reveals another danger, creating episodic escalation without abandoning documentary plausibility. The aftermath then broadens into damaged travel, hospitality, deprivation and official recognition. The closing military order supplies an explicit moral and retrospectively organizes the entire narrative around “subordination and strict obedience.”

Pacing is managed through alternation between summary and scene. Weeks of travel may pass in a sentence, whereas the night of the fire is rendered almost task by task. Exact dates and times give the emergency a measured progression, while the accumulation of setbacks maintains tension. The narrator occasionally interrupts the action to explain ship construction or military procedure, but these digressions generally heighten the reader’s understanding of the danger. Suspense depends less on mystery than on material causation: the reader is repeatedly shown what will happen if a mast falls, a magazine explodes, a bulkhead fails or the ship turns before the wind.

Characterization is economical and role-based. Most figures are identified by rank, profession or a single defining trait: the jovial captain jokes, the provident lieutenant builds a raft, the serene quartermaster-sergeant records barrels, and the Scotch engineer produces the decisive technical solution. Individual names are preserved chiefly where they attach to exemplary deeds, as with Richard Richmond and W. Wiles. Even the unnamed man with the hatchet is memorialized through his action. This method keeps the regiment, rather than any single protagonist, at the centre. The collective hero is presented as an organized body whose identity is embodied in its Colours and expressed through coordinated labour.

The imagery is vivid, elemental and often theatrical. Fire turns the stern into a “floating volcano” and the sea into blood; red-hot iron, bluish vapour, black water and green waves create strong colour contrasts. The gutted ship is compared to a burned theatre, while cartridges splutter like crackers and loose tanks thunder against the hull. Sharks circling beneath the illuminated wreck add a repeated emblem of impersonal menace. Yet the imagery remains anchored in machinery, heat, water and damaged structure rather than becoming fantastical. The ship is lightly personified as an “old woman” fighting for her life, which gives emotional unity to the technical account.

Dialogue is sparse and strategically placed. Brief exchanges crystallize attitude rather than develop psychology: “We’ll fight till we’re driven overboard” states the soldiers’ resolve, while “Lights out” turns imminent death into barrack-room comedy. Quoted records and official orders form a second kind of dialogue, allowing institutional voices to enter the narrative. Their formality is often gently mocked, but the final order is accepted as a valid summation of the event.

Overall, the style is that of a patriotic imperial-era tale shaped from archival evidence: technically informed, orally animated, humorous under pressure and morally explicit. Its chief stylistic signature lies in the fusion of factual specificity with conversational commentary, cumulative action, vivid disaster imagery and admiration for disciplined collective courage. The story’s governing value is not individual self-expression but duty made visible through orderly work in terrifying circumstances.

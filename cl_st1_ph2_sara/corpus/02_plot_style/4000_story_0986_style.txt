The story uses a spare, direct form of third-person past-tense narration. Its viewpoint is largely external and observational: characters are revealed through speech, gestures, and physical reactions rather than through extended access to their thoughts. Brief focal shifts attach the reader mainly to Captain Franco and Peterson, but the narrator remains emotionally restrained, allowing the moral implications to emerge without explicit commentary. The opening aphorism establishes the governing irony by contrasting philosophical speech with foolish conduct.

The prose is economical and concrete. Sentences tend to be short or medium-length, built around simple actions: characters walk, stare, pull, eat, and raise weapons. Description is selective rather than elaborate, with memorable physical details concentrated on the wub’s bulk, moist eyes, laboured movement, and piglike body. Recurring references to sagging flesh, panting, jowls, food, and digestion create a deliberately material vocabulary that clashes with the wub’s intellectual sophistication. The spaceship and Martian setting are sketched through functional terms—gangplank, hold, ballast, jets, frozen-food locker—so the speculative world feels ordinary and workmanlike rather than grandiose.

Dialogue drives most of the narrative. Exchanges are quick, plainly punctuated, and frequently broken by terse action beats. Human speech is colloquial, impatient, and utilitarian, especially Franco’s commands and dismissals. By contrast, the wub speaks in courteous, measured, discursive sentences, using abstract language drawn from philosophy, ethics, psychology, mythology, and political thought. Its repetitions—“tolerant, eclectic, catholic,” for example—and polite qualifications give it a genial, professorial voice. This contrast supplies both comedy and characterization: the physically ungainly animal is the most articulate and civilized presence, while the technologically advanced humans sound crude and predatory.

The tone blends deadpan humour, grotesque comedy, moral unease, and dark satire. Comic effects arise from incongruity: a huge piglike creature discusses democracy, telepathy, Odysseus, and individuation while belching, wheezing, and struggling through narrow corridors. The narration does not signal jokes heavily; absurd events are presented with a straight face. As the story progresses, the humour becomes increasingly sinister. Franco’s fixation on the wub’s taste turns practical hunger into callous appetite, and the crew’s discomfort provides a subdued moral counterpoint without producing effective resistance.

Pacing is brisk and strongly scene-based. A series of compact dramatic episodes moves from acquisition, to revelation, to philosophical confrontation, to execution, and finally to the dinner-table reversal. Asterisks mark abrupt transitions, often skipping routine action and preserving momentum. Suspense is created less through physical danger than through delayed disclosure of the wub’s abilities. Its telepathy is introduced early, its power to immobilize Franco is shown but not fully explained, and its ultimate capacity is withheld until the final lines. The ending retrospectively transforms the preceding meal and conversation, using repetition of the Odysseus discussion as the decisive clue.

Characterization is schematic but effective. Franco embodies appetite, authority, anti-intellectualism, and the reduction of life to utility. Peterson is sympathetic but passive, capable of recognizing the wub’s personhood yet unable to protect it. The other crew members function largely as a fearful and guilty chorus. The wub combines comic physical helplessness with moral and intellectual authority. Its gentleness may initially appear naïve, but the ending reveals that its philosophy of return, continuity, and survival has a literal dimension. This makes its earlier remarks about the “life essence,” separation, and the wanderer’s return operate as foreshadowing.

The story’s central method is reversal. Surface categories repeatedly prove false: the “animal” is rational, the humans are barbaric, physical weakness conceals mental power, and death becomes a form of continuation. Eating works as both plot mechanism and metaphor, joining consumption, identity, and assimilation. The final irony is especially sharp because Franco believes he has reduced the wub to “organic matter,” while his speech and appetites reveal that the wub has instead absorbed or replaced him. The conclusion is concise, uncanny, and circular, returning to mythology while resolving the conflict through a darkly comic transfer of voice and consciousness.

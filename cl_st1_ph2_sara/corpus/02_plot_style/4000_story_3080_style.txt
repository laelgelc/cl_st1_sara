The story uses a late-nineteenth-century American realist-naturalist style, combining documentary precision with melodramatic economic conflict. Its subject is treated as both a human crisis and an impersonal struggle between market forces. Farmers, speculators and brokers appear to exercise individual will, but their fortunes are governed by systems larger than themselves: commodity prices, storage costs, telegraphic information, monopolistic control and the collective behaviour of the trading pit. The recurring bull-and-bear metaphor turns commerce into a form of predatory combat.

The narrative is divided into titled sections that announce changes in price, location and power: the ruined wheat grower at sixty-two cents is set against the triumphant bull market at a dollar ten, followed by the immediate action of “The Pit.” This structure is strongly contrastive and cinematic. It moves from the quiet Kansas ranch to a private Chicago office and then into the crowded exchange, widening the story’s scale while revealing the connections between rural suffering and financial speculation. Exposition often compresses months of market history into authoritative summary, whereas decisive encounters are rendered scene by scene.

The narrator is third-person and broadly omniscient, with access to characters’ calculations, fears and uncertainties. The voice frequently explains technical circumstances directly, supplying prices, quantities, contracts and market positions with journalistic confidence. Such factual detail gives the fiction an almost reportorial texture. At the same time, the narrator heightens events through evaluative and dramatic phrasing: reversals are “tremendous,” enemies are “invincible” or “unassailable,” and financial defeat becomes actual or figurative death.

Diction alternates between formal commercial language, specialised market jargon and colloquial American speech. Legalistic formulations such as “Party of the First Part,” precise references to certified checks and warehouse receipts, and terms including “bull clique,” “cornered,” “short,” “contracts” and “support the market” establish professional authenticity. Dialogue is terser and more idiomatic, using contractions, clipped phrases and expressions such as “clear up over a hundred thou’,” “Blamed if I know,” and “busted.” This contrast distinguishes institutional mechanisms from the people operating within them.

Sentence structure varies according to pace. Rural scenes employ measured, extended sentences and pauses that convey exhaustion, silence and apprehension. The exchange scene shifts to long cumulative sentences packed with coordinated actions: voices roar, bodies surge, feet grind and hands rise almost simultaneously. Parenthetical comments, semicolons, dashes and repeated clauses reproduce the overload of the trading floor. These expansive action sentences are punctuated by abrupt commands and bids—“Sold,” “Wire the Chief,” “Support the market”—which give the prose a military rhythm.

Imagery is physical, violent and animalistic. Traders “hammer” prices, “stick the knife” into opponents, “gore” enemies and try to smash them “dead.” The market is conceived as a battlefield, while Truslow’s identity as the “Great Bear” and Hornung’s role as the dominant bull make economic competition resemble a contest between beasts. In the pit, the crowd behaves like a single organism: a hundred voices, feet and hands merge into one convulsive mass. Repetition of “a hundred” and “Sold!” intensifies this collective force.

Characterisation is economical and socially representative. Sam and Emma Lewiston are introduced through practical gestures, restrained dialogue and material circumstances rather than extensive psychological analysis; they stand for a “vast population” of threatened wheat growers. Hornung and Truslow are more archetypal, defined by strategic power and animal epithets. Minor physical details—the velvet skull-cap, Pittsburg stogie, Gladstone bag and rolled apron—quickly locate class, occupation and period. Characters are therefore both individuals and embodiments of positions within the economic system.

The tone progresses from grave resignation through calculating menace to feverish excitement. Pathos in the opening is controlled rather than sentimental: the Lewistons’ ruin is expressed through silence, vague gazes and limp hands. Suspense later arises from incomplete knowledge. Hornung believes that no wheat remains outside his control, yet an unknown seller appears; repeated questions about where the grain came from create a mystery within the financial action. Foreshadowing is explicit, especially in warnings that the desperate Bear remains dangerous.

Overall, the style is muscular, panoramic and socially conscious. It turns abstract market operations into visible drama through exact figures, technical vocabulary, sharp contrasts and violent metaphor. Its signature effect comes from placing restrained domestic suffering beside the spectacular energy of finance, showing how a shouted price in Chicago can determine the fate of distant lives.

The story uses a lucid, restrained literary realism enriched by vivid sensory detail, dry humor, and understated social criticism. Its prose is accessible but carefully textured, combining plain narrative exposition with striking figurative language and culturally specific vocabulary. Bengali terms and references—such as “almari,” “zamindar,” “durwan,” saris, attars, and carom boards—are introduced without explanatory interruption, allowing the Calcutta setting to feel inhabited rather than translated for an outsider.

The narration is third-person and largely centered on Boori Ma, though it moves freely into the thoughts, assumptions, and collective judgments of the building’s residents. This flexible omniscience creates a communal perspective: individuals are briefly distinguished, but the residents often merge into social groups such as “the wives” or “the residents.” The narrator maintains a measured emotional distance, seldom announcing how the reader should feel. Instead, sympathy emerges through concrete observations of Boori Ma’s body, habits, possessions, and precarious place in the building. Her vulnerability is conveyed through details such as her swollen knee, walnut-sized knot of hair, newspaper bed, cheap sari, reed broom, and useless skeleton keys.

The tone balances compassion with irony. Boori Ma’s grandiose accounts of her former wealth are treated as doubtful, contradictory, and theatrical, yet never simply mocked. The narration openly notes that she garbles facts and embellishes, while also recognizing that these stories may be a form of mourning. This ambiguity is central to the style: literal truth matters less than the emotional truth of dispossession. The repeated phrase “Believe me, don’t believe me” expresses both defiance and helplessness, gradually changing from a comic verbal signature into a tragic plea. By the ending, “Believe me, believe me” no longer carries the protection of performance.

Characterization relies heavily on recurring gestures, objects, and verbal refrains. Boori Ma shakes the loose end of her sari to rattle her keys, carries her broom everywhere, patrols the building, and repeats catalogues of lost luxuries. Mr. Chatterjee delivers formulaic pronouncements about “changing times,” while Mr. Dalal’s aspirations are embodied by the new basins. These repetitions give each character a compact dramatic identity and allow meanings to shift as circumstances change. The skeleton keys initially suggest a possibly magnificent lost household; at the end, when nothing rattles, they become an economical image of complete dispossession.

Dialogue is concise, stylized, and socially revealing. Boori Ma’s speech tends toward incantation and enumeration: rosewater rice, pewter bowls, marble floors, muslin linen, silk mosquito nets. Her lists create an oral, performative rhythm and contrast abundance in memory with scarcity in the present. The residents’ dialogue is more practical, competitive, and accusatory, revolving around possessions, renovations, status, and blame. Questions frequently function as complaints or challenges—“Is it beyond us to buy sinks of our own?”—rather than requests for information. This gives the communal speech a mounting, contagious quality.

The syntax alternates between straightforward declarative sentences and longer cumulative constructions. Short statements provide emphasis—“So she garbled facts. She contradicted herself. She embellished almost everything.”—while extended sentences gather inventories of objects, actions, or rumors. Parallel structures and repeated openings produce a controlled rhythm, especially when depicting collective behavior. The prose often progresses through accumulation: the contents of Boori Ma’s alleged former home, the services she performs, the gifts supposedly bought for Mrs. Dalal, and the renovations commissioned by jealous neighbors. Such lists are both descriptive and thematic, showing a society increasingly preoccupied with ownership and display.

Imagery is concrete, tactile, and frequently domestic. The story emphasizes bedding, bathwater, food, cleaning implements, plumbing, walls, shutters, and stairwells. Similes are fresh and slightly abrasive: Boori Ma’s voice is “tart as curds” and sharp enough to grate coconut; rain comes “like a boy in slippers too big for him”; pain “chewed” through her knee. These comparisons draw on ordinary materials and bodily experience, keeping the figurative language rooted in the story’s physical and cultural environment. Weather is similarly sensuous, rendered through slapping rain, wet mango leaves, damp winds, peppers, yogurt, and newsprint ink.

The setting is constructed as a small social ecosystem. The decaying apartment building, with its glassless windows, stored bathwater, brick privies, crooked stairs, and collapsible gate, functions almost as a character. Its vertical arrangement is important: Boori Ma moves continually from bottom to top and back again, occupying thresholds, landings, the roof, and the space beneath the letter boxes rather than a private room. Her marginal social position is therefore made spatially visible. The stairwell is both her workplace and the community’s shared artery; when renovations obstruct it, her role is displaced before she is physically expelled.

The story’s structure is tightly causal and quietly foreshadowed. It begins with mites, sleeplessness, and the destruction of Boori Ma’s bedding, then introduces Mrs. Dalal’s promise to replace it. Mr. Dalal’s promotion brings the basin, which sparks envy, renovation, uncontrolled traffic, and the collapse of Boori Ma’s guarding function. Her anticipated blanket never arrives before the theft gives the residents an excuse to remove her. Ordinary improvements therefore generate the conditions for disaster. The pacing is initially patient and observational, then accelerates through rumors, purchases, construction, and accusation.

Irony governs the plot and the title. Boori Ma already performs the work of a “real durwan” despite lacking the official status, gender, uniform, or wages associated with the position. Once the building’s appearance improves, the residents decide that they need a “real” gatekeeper and discard the person who has guarded them for years. Their modernization produces greater insecurity, while their desire for valuables makes them imagine themselves as newly vulnerable. The stolen basin becomes less important as an object than as a symbol of aspiration, rivalry, and the community’s readiness to scapegoat someone powerless.

The emotional method is deliberately indirect. The narration does not dwell on Boori Ma’s inner grief at the climax; instead, it shows her silence, her grip on the broom, the residents speaking about rather than to her, and the final absence of rattling keys. This restraint prevents sentimentality and makes the ending sharper. The final image condenses the story’s major concerns—memory, credibility, belonging, labor, and loss—into a simple physical gesture.

Overall, the style is marked by economical characterization, communal narration, rhythmic repetition, sensuous domestic imagery, culturally embedded diction, and an understated but severe use of irony. Its social critique operates through objects and behavior rather than overt commentary: sinks, blankets, keys, brooms, gates, and freshly painted shutters reveal how material aspiration and collective self-interest can erase gratitude, distort truth, and turn a tolerated outsider into a convenient scapegoat.

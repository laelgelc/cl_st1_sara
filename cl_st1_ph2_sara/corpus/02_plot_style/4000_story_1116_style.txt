The story uses a late-nineteenth-century local-colour style, combining sentimental realism with the atmosphere and social texture of Catholic New Orleans. Its setting is established through specific urban and religious references—Chartres Street, Canal Street, Jackson Square, the Cathedral, vespers, rosaries, vestments and High Mass—while French phrases and French-inflected English create a culturally mixed linguistic environment. Expressions such as “cher Seigneur,” “ma mère” and “c’est ma très grande faute” are woven naturally into the narration and dialogue rather than translated at length. Period spellings and punctuation, including “to-night,” frequent semicolons, exclamations and paired dashes, reinforce the historical register.

The narration is third-person omniscient but remains closely aligned with Sister Josepha’s perceptions. It moves readily from external observation into her private complaints, fantasies and fears, often approaching free indirect discourse. The narrator also intervenes with reflective or moralising commentary, as in the meditation on rainbows, earthly pleasure and “bonbons and glaces.” These interventions create a gently ironic distance from Josepha’s naïveté while preserving strong sympathy for her. The prevailing tone is tender, wistful and emotionally heightened, with touches of humour in the descriptions of the squeaky organ, badly trimmed wicks and Josepha’s irritation with the other sisters.

Diction is ornate, sensuous and strongly visual. The prose repeatedly contrasts the convent’s “brown walls,” grayness, coarse food and ugly clothing with the colour, perfume, sweetness and movement of the secular world. The cathedral scene becomes a lavish tableau of purple, crimson, gold, lace, flowers, candles and military ornament. Sound is equally important: bells toll, organs creak or peal, censers clink, choirs rise and military equipment seems to echo in Josepha’s imagination. Smell, touch and taste—roses, magnolias, perfume, hard beds, bonbons and glaces—complete the sensory richness. Inanimate things are frequently personified: sunbeams laugh, flowers smile, lamps are “greedy for oil,” and the censer appears to take up a musical note.

Sentence structure alternates between expansive, cumulative description and short emotional statements. Long sentences gather details through parallel clauses, lists and repeated conjunctions, especially during ceremonial or psychological climaxes. Short sentences such as “Briefly, Sister Josepha was in love” puncture the ornament with directness and mild narrative wit. Repetition supplies rhythm and emphasis: “Camille this, Camille that,” “the days glided into weeks, the weeks into months,” and the recurring references to the young soldier’s “tender, pitying brown eyes.” Dialogue is more abrupt and colloquial than the narration, revealing Josepha’s youth, impatience and impulsiveness, while the Mother Superior’s formal, French-inflected speech conveys institutional authority.

The characterization depends heavily on physical detail, gesture and recurring diminutives. Josepha is repeatedly “little,” fragile, tremulous and childlike, with small brown hands, a red mouth and large black eyes. This vocabulary emphasises the disparity between her youth and the permanent discipline imposed upon her. Her former self, Camille, is described through energetic animal similes—she flees “like a frightened fawn”—and through language of tropical beauty, vivacity and instinct. Such descriptions carry the exoticising and racialised assumptions of the period, particularly in references to her unknown “blood,” early maturity and uncertain identity.

Structurally, the story opens with Josepha already weary of convent life, then moves backward into an extended account of her childhood and entry into the order. Once the background has caught up with the opening situation, the plot proceeds chronologically through the cathedral celebration, her romantic awakening, her plan to escape and her final renunciation. The drama is primarily inward: there is little direct action, and the young soldier remains less a developed character than a symbolic glimpse of tenderness, freedom and worldly possibility. Recurrent motifs—beads, lamps, eyes, doors, walls and processions—bind the narrative together. Beads signify both mechanical devotion and tears; lamps suggest repetitive duty and contained vitality; eyes awaken desire; doors and streets embody the possibility of escape.

The ending is restrained but melodramatically symbolic. Josepha pauses at the convent entrance, looks toward Chartres Street, then returns behind the “heavy door.” This reprises the story’s central contrast between the enclosed brown convent and the dangerous, alluring city. The close offers no liberation or uncomplicated spiritual reconciliation; instead, it presents submission as the product of fear, social vulnerability and lack of identity. Overall, the style blends decorative religious spectacle, intimate psychological narration, authorial reflection and sentimental social critique, producing a story whose emotional force comes from the tension between vivid worldly desire and institutional confinement.

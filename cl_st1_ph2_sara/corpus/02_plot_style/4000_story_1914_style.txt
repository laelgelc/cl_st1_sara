The story uses a brisk, economical comic-realistic style rooted in early twentieth-century urban working-class life. Its setting is conveyed through a few functional details—lodgings, public houses, trams, small gardens and modest domestic rooms—rather than through extended description. This creates a compact social world governed by money, marriage, respectability and neighbourly suspicion. Material details such as pint pots, twopenny cigars, sovereigns, cheap hats, mustard tins and tram fares establish both period and class while also supplying comic weight.

The narration is third-person and largely external, with occasional access to Mr. Teak’s thoughts and motives. The narrator maintains an amused, dryly detached attitude, rarely explaining the joke directly. Humour instead arises from the discrepancy between the characters’ professed motives and their obvious self-interest. Teak describes the planned theft as an attempt to rescue his wife from “the vice of avarice,” and the narrator echoes such hypocrisy in mock-serious phrases like “assistant fortune-hunter” and “the veracious Mr. Chase.” This elevated wording applied to petty greed produces sustained irony.

Dialogue dominates the story and drives both characterization and plot. Speech is strongly individualized through colloquial grammar, dropped initial consonants and phonetic spellings such as “’ead,” “’ouse,” “’ave” and “chimbley.” Expressions including “play the giddy goat,” “mistook” and “the leastest bit” give the exchanges a vivid vernacular rhythm. The dialect is broad but consistently legible, and it marks social position without requiring explanatory commentary. Teak’s scheming self-justification, Chase’s blunt bargaining and Mrs. Teak’s apparently plain domestic speech establish personality almost entirely through verbal interaction.

The prose alternates between rapid conversational exchanges and concise narrative summaries. Sentences in dialogue are often short, interrupted and repetitive, especially when characters are lying or under pressure. Dashes, hesitations and verbal corrections—most notably Chase’s “it flowed—I mean flew”—turn failed improvisation into comedy. Narrative sentences are generally more balanced and formal, often ending on an understated ironic phrase. This contrast between polished narration and rough speech sharpens the comic effect.

Characterization depends on physical gesture as much as language. Winks, side-glances, grunts, ear-pulling, clenched fists and exaggerated expressions repeatedly expose intentions that words attempt to conceal. Several gestures recur as comic motifs, particularly the conspirators’ increasingly conspicuous winking. Bodies are also treated farcically: Teak is pushed into the bar, Chase emerges backwards from a chimney, Teak performs a silent hornpipe, and the final quarrel overturns the supper table. These visual actions give the story the pace and clarity of stage comedy.

The plot follows a tightly controlled escalation. An apparently simple scheme—to locate hidden savings—produces a sequence of increasingly expensive and humiliating complications: gardening, the zoo excursion, the ruined room, the supposed robbery and the fight between the conspirators. Each episode grows logically from the previous one while frustrating Teak’s expectations. The rain-shortened outing and Chase’s discovery in the chimney function as comic reversals, but the decisive reversal is delayed until the closing conversation, when Teak learns that his wife moved the money herself. The ending retrospectively reinterprets the entire “robbery” and confirms that the schemers have been defeated by the person they underestimated.

The humour combines verbal irony, domestic farce, situational reversal and moral comedy. Teak and Chase are ridiculous rather than wholly villainous: their greed is softened by incompetence, vanity and mutual mistrust. Mrs. Teak, initially treated by the men as passive and easily deceived, proves more observant and practical than either of them. Her apparent distress over the missing money becomes an effective performance, mirroring and surpassing the men’s own deceptions. The story therefore derives much of its satisfaction from comic justice: greed defeats itself, male confidence collapses into suspicion, and domestic intelligence quietly outmanoeuvres elaborate plotting.

The overall tone is genial, sardonic and unsentimental. Violence, dishonesty and marital hostility are stylized into farce rather than presented as serious menace. The final remark—“If you was a married man, Alf, you’d understand it better”—restores the conversational, pub-centred atmosphere and turns Teak’s defeat into a rueful generalization about marriage. Stylistically, the story’s chief signatures are compressed plotting, dialogue-led characterization, readable eye dialect, mock-elevated narration, visual slapstick and a carefully prepared final twist.

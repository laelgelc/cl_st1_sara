The story adopts a nineteenth-century realist style marked by patient observation, moral seriousness, and an unsentimental preoccupation with death. Its four-part structure sets different forms of mortality beside one another: the prolonged decline of an aristocratic woman, the neglected death of a poor post-driver, the ceremonial treatment of the wealthy woman’s corpse, and the felling of a living tree. These parallel episodes create meaning through juxtaposition rather than direct authorial argument. Social rank determines the outward circumstances of death—medical attention, religious rites, family grief, monuments—but does not alter its essential finality. The closing movement into the forest broadens the human drama into a natural cycle of destruction, renewal, and impersonal continuity.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, moving freely between external description, dialogue, reported thought, and brief passages of interior monologue. The perspective often remains detached and observational, yet it can enter a character’s mind at decisive moments, especially the invalid’s resentful reflections on her husband, servants, and illness. This access does not produce full sympathy or idealisation. Instead, the narrator exposes contradictions between what characters say, what they believe, and what their bodies or actions reveal. The dying woman professes religious resignation while continuing to blame others and cling to imagined cures; her husband displays genuine distress but also appetite, helplessness, and avoidance; the post-station workers speak brutally about Theodore’s impending death while still showing intermittent practical kindness. Characterisation therefore emerges through behaviour, physical detail, and self-deception rather than explanatory psychological analysis.

The descriptive method is highly concrete and physiognomic. Bodies are rendered through complexion, hair, hands, clothing, posture, breath, coughing, and small involuntary movements. The maid’s “florid” health, swelling bosom, quick eyes, and sturdy legs are placed against her mistress’s wasted hand, shrunken chest, dry lips, and death-like parting. Similar attention is given to Theodore’s red beard, sunken eyes, hairy wasted hand, and laboured breathing. Such details make physical decline unavoidable and establish life and death as bodily facts. Objects also carry social and symbolic weight: the lap-dog, carriage, crinoline, boots, headstone, wax candles, bier, axe, and wooden cross all reveal class, custom, need, or mortality. The boots are especially effective as a material emblem: they pass from a dying man to a living worker before death has formally occurred, demonstrating the practical economy by which life continues around the doomed.

The prose frequently uses long, cumulative sentences, particularly in scenic descriptions. Clauses gather visual, auditory, tactile, and olfactory information into a densely layered environment: mud, mist, carriage springs, window-glass, eau de cologne, cabbage, bread, sheepskins, coughing, snoring, birdsong, and the rhythm of an axe. These extended sentences are balanced by abrupt dialogue and short declarative statements at moments of finality, such as the announcement that Theodore is dead or that the invalid has become a corpse. Repetition reinforces both emotional pressure and thematic inevitability. Characters repeatedly say “My God,” repeatedly ask what can be done, and repeatedly speak of promised cures, journeys, boots, stones, and crosses. This circularity conveys denial and powerlessness: language continues, but circumstances do not change.

Dialogue is socially differentiated and strongly dramatic. The aristocratic exchanges are polite, euphemistic, and evasive, with terms such as “my friend” and “my angel” softening conflict without resolving it. The post-drivers and cook speak more bluntly, colloquially, and materially, discussing death in terms of boots, hospital space, burial, and work. Their apparent harshness is not treated as simple cruelty; it reflects poverty, familiarity with death, and the pressure of immediate necessity. Religious speech forms another register, ranging from conventional consolation and talk of divine mercy to the elevated language of the Psalms. The story repeatedly tests these formulas against bodily suffering. Prayer may calm a person temporarily, but it does not alter the grey road, restore damaged lungs, or stop death.

Imagery is organised around seasonal and environmental contrasts. Autumn accompanies the two sick travellers and establishes greyness, cold, mud, enclosure, and exhaustion. Spring surrounds the aristocratic woman’s final hours with thawing streams, bursting buds, bright clothes, sparrows, and renewed movement. The irony is pronounced: nature revives while the woman dies indoors. In the final section, dawn and early sunlight transform the forest immediately after the tree is felled. The surviving trees appear more beautiful in the newly opened space, and birds continue to move and sing. Nature is often personified—the leaves whisper, the tree trembles, the branches rustle over the fallen body—but the effect is not conventionally sentimental. The natural world seems animated yet indifferent, absorbing individual death into a larger process.

The story’s tone combines compassion with irony. It recognises fear, grief, and suffering while refusing to flatter the dying or sentimentalise the living. Irony often arises from proximity: the husband eats while worrying about his wife; curious girls inspect the consumptive woman as a spectacle; children play noisily near their mother’s corpse; a psalm about renewal is recited by a clerk who does not understand it; a marble monument quickly marks the noblewoman’s grave while the poor driver’s promised stone remains unbought. The narrator rarely comments directly on these contrasts, allowing arrangement and detail to produce the judgement. Occasional rhetorical questions—most notably whether the corpse understands the sacred words—briefly intensify the philosophical dimension while preserving ambiguity.

Pacing is deliberately uneven. The carriage journey and deathbed scenes unfold through minute gestures, repeated conversations, and pauses, reproducing the oppressive duration of illness. Theodore’s death is quieter and more compressed: he becomes still while ordinary station life continues around him. The tree-felling sequence slows down again, describing each blow, tremor, crack, and movement of the crown. This close temporal attention gives the tree a death scene analogous to the human ones, but without theology, social ceremony, or psychological resistance. The final return to sunlight, birds, and rustling leaves gives the ending a grave, impersonal lyricism.

The diction has an elevated, somewhat archaic translated quality, with formal constructions, repeated honorifics, culturally specific terms, and occasional foreign-language references. Words for Russian garments, offices, religious objects, and burial markers preserve the setting’s social texture. At the same time, the supplied text contains typographical irregularities, awkward spacing, and probable transcription or edition errors; these are incidental to the underlying style rather than deliberate aesthetic features. Beneath them, the dominant idiom is lucid realist narration enriched by sensory accumulation, restrained symbolism, and carefully staged contrasts.

Overall, the style is observational, corporeal, contrapuntal, and ethically incisive. It treats mortality through parallel lives rather than abstract exposition, contrasting privilege with poverty, denial with acceptance, ritual with material necessity, and individual extinction with natural renewal. Its strongest effects come from understatement: people continue eating, working, playing, bargaining, praying, and singing while others die, and the world becomes neither more nor less beautiful because of their passing.

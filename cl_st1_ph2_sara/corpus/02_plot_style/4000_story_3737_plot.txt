A cheerful sixty-year-old Black servant is asked how she has lived without trouble. Her manner turns grave as she recounts being born enslaved, lovingly raising seven children, and then being sold at auction when her enslaver became impoverished. Her husband and children are sold away from her despite her desperate resistance. She never sees six of the children again, and for twenty-two years carries the pain of their loss.

During the Civil War, Union officers occupy the house where she works as a cook. She asks whether they have encountered her youngest son, Henry, whom she remembers as a small boy with scars on his wrist and forehead. Unknown to her, Henry escaped north, became a barber, and later joined the Union forces in hopes of searching the South for his mother.

At a soldiers’ dance in her kitchen, she angrily repeats a proud expression often used by her own mother. A young Black soldier hears it and becomes suspicious that she may be the mother he has sought. The next morning he approaches her as she cooks. She recognizes him, confirms his identity by the scars on his wrist and forehead, and rejoices that Henry has finally been restored to her. She ends with bitter irony, saying that she has known neither trouble nor joy.

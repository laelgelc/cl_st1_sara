An elderly club guest recalls traveling with his wife and five children from wintry Cambridge to Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, during Easter. On Easter morning, he witnesses the Moravians greeting sunrise in their cemetery with horns and a powerful hymn, an experience of profound religious beauty.

Later, he tells his children about the Easter customs of his youth, especially colored eggs, and jokingly claims that Easter eggs can hatch rabbits and other marvelous creatures. The children become obsessed with buying decorated eggs. After Easter Sunday keeps the shops closed, they purchase six eggs on Monday, but the oldest boy persuades the others to fight them against one another until only one remains.

Back home, the children place the surviving boiled egg beneath a reluctant pullet, pretending it might hatch. When the shell is later found broken and empty, their father realizes that they never truly expected the cooked egg to hatch; they had willingly shared his make-believe. He then proposes convincing their mother that a “chick” did emerge.

With the two oldest children, he buys the Easter bonnet his wife had admired but refused because of the family’s travel expenses. Since the children object to its decorative dead bird, they replace it with forget-me-nots and present the bonnet as the creature hatched from the Easter egg. His wife appears delighted and surprised, but later admits that the children had already revealed the plan while saying their prayers. The affectionate deception becomes a fond memory of family love, childhood imagination, and parental tenderness.

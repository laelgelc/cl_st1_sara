Orphaned distant cousins Edward Mills and George Benton are raised by the Brants, who preach that purity, honesty, sobriety, industry, and consideration guarantee success and friendship. Edward faithfully follows these principles, while George behaves selfishly and irresponsibly. Yet George continually receives extra attention, indulgence, and rescue, whereas Edward’s goodness is taken for granted.

As adults, Edward becomes a hardworking businessman, while George repeatedly runs away, steals, drinks, gambles, and causes trouble. When the Brants die, they leave their property to George because he “needs it,” requiring Edward to make him a business partner and continue protecting him. George then wins the woman Edward loves because she believes marrying him will reform him. He later ruins their business through gambling and debt, leaving both families impoverished.

Edward struggles honestly through hardship and eventually becomes a trusted bank cashier, receiving little help from the charitable organizations he once supported. George, meanwhile, repeatedly falls into drunkenness and crime but is celebrated each time benevolent citizens “reform” him. His relapses bring him publicity, employment, sympathy, and eventually a career lecturing as a reformed drunkard. Even after committing forgery, he receives a pardon and further assistance.

During a bank robbery, Edward refuses to betray his employers by revealing the safe’s combination and is murdered. The chief robber proves to be George. Edward’s heroism brings his destitute family only a meager donation, while his bank even tries to discredit him. Public sympathy instead centers on George, with widespread efforts to save him from execution. After he experiences a last-minute religious conversion, he is mourned and honored as a redeemed sinner.

George’s grave is decorated and inscribed as though he lived nobly. Edward’s family remains poor, while admirers raise a large sum not for them but to build a memorial church. Edward’s unfinished gravestone inscription—repeating the moral rule that supposedly assured success—underscores the irony that society rewards dramatic vice and repentance while neglecting quiet, lifelong virtue.

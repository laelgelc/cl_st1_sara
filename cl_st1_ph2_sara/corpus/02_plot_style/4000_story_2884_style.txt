The story adopts the style of a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century maritime adventure narrative, combining documentary realism with overt admiration for institutional heroism. Its prose is formal, expansive, and authoritative, with period vocabulary and spellings such as “succor,” “endeavor,” “mayhap,” “life-preservers,” and “beleagured.” Nautical and life-saving terminology—“close-hauled,” “leeway,” “breeches buoy,” “shot-line,” “whip,” “hawser,” and “sand-anchor”—is used densely and confidently. This specialist diction gives the rescue procedural authenticity, while occasional explanations ensure that a general reader can still follow the operation. The narrative repeatedly shifts from technical description to elevated moral language, presenting the rescuers as “Spartan heroes” whose disciplined sense of duty carries them into mortal danger.

The narration is third-person omniscient, although it remains chiefly aligned with Captain Litchfield, the station crews, and the collective spectators on shore. The narrator freely supplies background information, summarizes previous events, explains equipment and procedures, and comments on the meaning of the action. This produces the impression of an informed chronicler rather than an invisible dramatic observer. Authorial intrusions are conspicuous: phrases such as “In a word” and direct reflections on what distant newspaper readers could understand turn the wreck into a broader meditation on courage, public service, and the limits of second-hand knowledge. The closing rhetorical question is especially characteristic, directly challenging comfortable readers who reduce catastrophe to “inanimate lines” in a newspaper.

Sentence structure is generally long, cumulative, and clause-rich. Semicolons, dashes, participial phrases, appositions, and extended lists allow the prose to pile detail upon detail, particularly in descriptions of the storm, wreckage, and rescue apparatus. The opening paragraphs use this accumulation to create physical pressure: surf, foam, snow, debris, darkness, and wind seem to advance simultaneously. Short sentences and fragments are then used sparingly for dramatic contrast. “The signal!” abruptly interrupts the slower account of waiting, while orders such as “Open boat-room doors—man the beach-cart!” and “Man weather whip—haul ashore!” convert the prose into urgent action. Exclamation marks and rhetorical questions heighten emotional intensity without displacing the predominantly reportorial manner.

Imagery is strongly sensory and often violent. The sea is rendered through sound, weight, texture, and motion: it “thundered,” arrived like a “solid wall,” and broke into a “seething flood of foam.” The unusual comparison of storm foam to stiffly beaten egg white gives the setting tactile specificity rather than conventional picturesque beauty. Darkness becomes “almost tangible,” wind cuts faces, spray blinds eyes, and the undertow makes pebbles emit a “peculiar musical roar.” The ocean is repeatedly personified as an active antagonist that flings, seizes, swallows, buffets, and destroys. When a wave engulfs the first rescued sailor, it appears to make “a last attempt to seize its intended victim.” This personification creates a clear dramatic contest between elemental violence and organized human skill.

The tone moves among foreboding, urgency, suspense, pathos, and solemn admiration. From the outset, the narrator announces that the night is of the kind on which wrecks and acts of heroism occur, so suspense does not depend on whether disaster will happen. Instead, it arises from when the brig will strike, whether each line will reach it, whether the hawser will hold, and how many sailors can be saved before the vessel breaks apart. Repeated failures—the lost first line, the missed second shot, the parted third line, and finally the fallen mast—create an escalating pattern of hope and reversal. Emotional responses are often presented collectively through groans, cheers, trembling, and the crowd’s movements, while individual suffering is conveyed through restrained physical details such as stiffened fingers, drenched clothing, exhaustion, and the relief of standing on firm ground.

Pacing alternates between broad scenic narration and closely sequenced procedural action. The opening lingers over the storm and the station’s watchfulness, establishing atmosphere and inevitability. Once the signal appears, the narrative accelerates through commands and coordinated tasks, yet slows again to explain each stage of the breeches-buoy system. This technical delay intensifies suspense because every practical operation consumes time while the wreck deteriorates offshore. Temporal markers—“a few minutes,” “a second more,” “scarce fifteen minutes,” and “for several hours”—keep the rescue measurable and emphasize the narrow margins between survival and death. The repeated trips of the buoy give the central episode a rhythmic structure, with each successful landing temporarily releasing tension before the final mechanical collapse.

Characterization is economical and largely functional. Captain Litchfield is defined through vigilance, experience, authority, and composure rather than personal history or introspection. The surfmen behave as a disciplined collective, each performing an “allotted part,” and the absence of confusion is stressed as a sign of professionalism. Individual sailors are identified through nationality, build, rank, endurance, or physical condition: the Scandinavian is large and stunned, the mate small and wiry, the final sailor Spanish. Dialogue is minimal and practical, consisting chiefly of commands, signals, thanks, and factual reports. This restraint supports the story’s emphasis on deeds rather than personality and makes the rescue service itself, rather than any single individual, the true protagonist.

The structure is linear and tightly organized around a movement from warning to wreck, rescue, partial failure, aftermath, and moral reckoning. The opening establishes the hostile environment; the next section explains why the station expects a disaster; the central portion gives a detailed account of the rescue; the deaths of the captain and Spanish sailor provide a tragic limit to the rescuers’ success; and the calm morning reveals the material scale of destruction. The final widening of perspective—from one brig to wrecks along the coast, and then to newspaper readers across the country—transforms a local incident into a national tribute to the Life-Saving Service. The contrast between the violent night and clear morning, as well as between heroic exertion and the public’s rapid forgetfulness, gives the conclusion its elegiac and admonitory force.

Overall, the style blends sensational maritime spectacle, procedural nonfiction, moralized adventure, and civic commemoration. It seeks both to immerse the reader in a storm and to instruct them in the labor behind a coastal rescue. Its defining qualities are densely observed natural description, technically precise action, restrained characterization, conspicuous authorial commentary, escalating mechanical suspense, and an earnest conviction that disciplined duty under extreme conditions deserves remembrance.

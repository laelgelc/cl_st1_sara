The story uses close third-person narration, moving chiefly through Lucy’s perceptions while occasionally entering Willy’s consciousness and offering broader social commentary. The narrator is observant but restrained, interpreting character through physical behaviour, domestic habits, speech, and bodily response rather than through extensive abstract analysis. Brief explanatory intrusions—such as the gloss on “Sorry” as a corruption of “Sirrah”—give the voice an ethnographic quality, placing local customs and language within a wider frame.

The prose is realist, tactile, and strongly attentive to bodies. Willy is repeatedly defined by compactness, muscularity, animal vitality, and his “bullet” head, while Lucy is associated with fairness, quietness, cleanliness, and control. Contrasts between black and white, dirt and cleanliness, vigour and vulnerability recur throughout. The mine enters the home through coal dust, clothing, smell, injury, and routine, so that industrial labour is rendered as a physical force rather than merely a social background. Images are concrete and sensuous: the damp, exhausted odour of the pit, the scarlet mouth in a blackened face, warmed work clothes, heavy boots, and the shaking of houses by the traction engine. Similes are sparing but vivid, often animalistic, as when Willy resembles a mating bird, a vigorous animal, or a lion.

Standard literary English governs the narration, but dialogue is rendered in dense regional working-class dialect. Phonetic spellings, altered grammar, contractions, and local vocabulary—“tha,” “thee,” “wesh,” “peen,” “clammed,” “snap,” and “clommaxed”—create a pronounced oral texture. The dialect is not ornamental: it establishes class, locality, intimacy, and communal identity. It also sharpens emotional scenes, since Willy’s pain and madness emerge through repetitions and broken cries rather than polished speech. Lucy’s language moves between standard and dialect forms, reflecting both her social position and her adaptation to her husband’s world.

Sentence structure alternates between measured descriptive periods and short, forceful statements. Early exposition is economical, compressing courtship, marriage, housing, work, debt, strike, and injury into a clear chronological sequence. Participial constructions such as “Having seen her” and “Being a good worker” contribute to a slightly formal, early twentieth-century narrative cadence. During moments of crisis, syntax fragments and repetition intensifies: “the peen, the peen,” “Kill her! Kill her!” The frequent dialogue interruptions, exclamations, and dashes reproduce agitation and overlapping voices. Crowd scenes are built from snippets of shouted speech, creating a choral soundscape and allowing collective excitement to infect the domestic drama.

Characterisation depends on contradiction. Willy is proud, affectionate, industrious, childish, domineering, physically attractive, and frightening. His authority in the home is presented through small rituals and commands, yet his confidence collapses under invisible internal pain. Lucy is tender but wary, practical but emotionally strained, dutiful yet capable of coldness. Their marriage is neither idealised nor reduced to simple oppression: happiness coexists with fear, tenderness with hierarchy, and loyalty with social anxiety. The repeated claim that Willy is a “good husband” is tested against his dependence on Lucy and his violent delirium.

The tone begins as calm social realism with touches of dry irony, especially around Willy’s self-satisfaction and domestic lordliness. It gradually darkens into psychological and bodily unease. Violence is conveyed without melodramatic embellishment; the most disturbing moments arise from the gap between Willy’s ordinary affection and his sudden murderous speech. The aftermath is subdued and compassionate, with three people crying in silence. Even then, the story turns quickly from private anguish to practical fear: Lucy worries that gossip about madness may endanger compensation. This creates a final tone of precariousness rather than resolution.

The setting is a tightly observed mining community organised around work, strikes, union payments, football, neighbours, and public reputation. Domestic space and street life constantly overlap. Curtains, windows, adjoining houses, and listening neighbours make privacy fragile, while the crowded street functions almost like a collective character. Social pressures are conveyed through ordinary details rather than explicit political argument. Economic insecurity, labour solidarity, institutional indifference, masculinity, illness, and the fear of destitution remain embedded in the action.

Overall, the style combines regional realism, psychological intensity, and sensuous physical description. Its emotional power comes from juxtaposition: the clean home against the contaminating pit, masculine vitality against helpless pain, marital tenderness against violence, and private suffering against the scrutiny of the community. The ending remains open and anxious, closing not on recovery but on Lucy’s unfinished question about survival.

The story is a comic frontier-and-seafaring yarn framed as an oral reminiscence. A relatively conventional first-person narrator introduces Bunt McBride, then largely withdraws while Bunt recounts the adventure in his own highly distinctive voice. This layered narration gives the tale the air of an anecdote heard at first hand while preserving a contrast between the literate, composed framing voice and Bunt’s exuberant vernacular. Frequent addresses such as “Mister Man” and “Mr. Dixon” keep the implied listener present and make the story feel performed rather than merely written.

Its most conspicuous stylistic feature is eye dialect. Bunt’s speech is represented through clipped endings, altered spellings, nonstandard grammar, and colloquial constructions: “ain’t,” “kin,” “gits,” “wot,” “along o’,” and “I’m a-goin’ for to relate.” The dialect combines cowboy slang, gambling language, nautical terminology, comic pseudo-formality, and deliberately mangled Spanish. Bunt repeatedly dresses simple ideas in elaborate phrases—calling lungs “leathern valves,” a journey a “pasear,” and an uprising a “reel live Revolution-Thing”—so that his voice sounds at once unschooled, inventive, and rhetorically accomplished. Grammatical irregularities such as “Which I puts it up” and “they is” function less as realism alone than as a consistent comic mannerism.

The syntax is accumulative and energetic. Sentences often run through strings of coordinated clauses joined by “an’,” reproducing the breathless momentum of spontaneous storytelling. Long enumerations, parenthetical interruptions, digressions, and self-corrections imitate a speaker discovering the best version of the story as he tells it. These sprawling passages alternate with short dialogue exchanges and emphatic declarations, giving the prose a lively stop-start rhythm. Repetition is central to the cadence: words and structures such as “friendly-like,” “jiggered an’ jiggered,” and “they sure did” intensify both the oral quality and the comedy.

The humour depends heavily on hyperbole, incongruous comparison, and mock-heroic inflation. Bunt compares nervous men to “philly-loo birds on a rail,” romantic rivals to “two he-hens on a hot plate,” and seasickness to the condition of a prairie dog that has eaten a horned toad backwards. Such similes are concrete, grotesque, regionally flavoured, and intentionally excessive. Elevated or solemn language is repeatedly applied to foolish conduct: the courtship is treated as a grave campaign, the boxing match as an epic trial, and Bunt’s fear of women as hard-earned philosophy. Conversely, danger, revolution, smuggling, and violence are described with breezy understatement. This mismatch between register and subject sustains the tall-tale tone.

The narrative is strongly dialogue-driven. Characters reveal themselves through boasts, insults, ritual politeness, and competitive repartee rather than through psychological analysis. Hardenberg and Strokher are broad comic types—the swaggering American and the proud Englishman—whose national rivalry amplifies their romantic jealousy. Their speech is differentiated somewhat by Strokher’s English colouring, but both inhabit the same world of masculine bravado. Bunt acts as chorus and commentator, continually insisting that they are fools while admitting that he followed them into the affair. His supposed prudence is itself part of the joke.

Structurally, the tale proceeds through escalation and delayed revelation. The opening announces a bare-knuckle fight caused by a woman, creating curiosity about both the contest and its cause. The revolutionary gun-running plot supplies adventure, but its main function is to bring the men into contact with Esperanza and sustain the misunderstanding. Romantic infatuation becomes national insult, then a prolonged boxing match, after which the apparently female passenger returns at the precise moment when both suitors are visibly battered. The final disclosure—that “Esperanza” is the disguised revolutionary Palachi—retroactively converts their rivalry, courtship, and injuries into an elaborate humiliation. The closing frame adds a final comic beat by having Bunt present his refusal to mention the incident as evidence of rare wisdom.

Pacing is expansive in the setup and compressed around transitions. Bunt lingers over waiting in the lodging house, the men’s lovesickness, his seasickness, and the absurdities of the fight, because these episodes offer opportunities for verbal comedy. By contrast, political background and logistical details are dispatched briskly. Even the boxing match is not narrated blow by blow; instead, its duration and ferocity are conveyed through summary, exaggeration, and the failure of bell and referee to stop it. Suspense comes less from physical danger than from anticipating the collapse of the men’s self-importance.

The setting blends the American Southwest, San Francisco Bay, the Pacific coast, and a fictionalized Central American revolutionary landscape. Cowboy diction is carried into maritime surroundings, so the schooner is understood through comparisons with horses, fences, trails, and livestock. This deliberate transplantation of range language to the sea helps maintain Bunt’s perspective and produces comic estrangement. Nautical and revolutionary details lend the yarn an adventurous surface, while the social world remains one of male companionship, gambling, weapons, smuggling, and contests of nerve.

Thematically, the story satirizes masculine vanity, romantic projection, and competitive honour. Hardenberg and Strokher fall in love largely with an appearance, transform desire into a national contest, and injure each other without learning anything about the supposed woman. Their eventual reconciliation after the fight shows that male rivalry and male affection are closely connected: the contest strengthens their bond even as its premise proves absurd. Bunt’s refrain that women are “weather-breeders” casts femininity as a source of disorder, but the plot ultimately exposes the men’s own fantasies as the true cause. The gender disguise sharpens that irony by revealing that the desired woman never existed as they imagined her.

The characterization is intentionally caricatural rather than inward. Physical tags—Hardenberg’s freckles, Strokher’s yellow moustache and buck tooth, Palachi’s disguise—serve as memorable comic emblems. Emotion is externalized through theatrical gestures: knees jiggle, moustaches twirl, rivals sigh extravagantly, and battered men leap overboard in synchronized panic. The prose favours visible action and performative speech over introspection, consistent with the conventions of the tall tale and magazine adventure story.

The tonal mixture is jovial, sardonic, and mock-philosophical. Bunt repeatedly pauses to generalize about human nature, especially the “foolishness o’ male humans,” but his aphorisms are comic rather than genuinely analytical. Beneath the farce lies admiration for courage, loyalty, and physical endurance: the fight is ridiculous, yet each man respects the other for refusing to yield. The story therefore mocks its adventurers without wholly diminishing them.

Historically dated ethnic, national, and gender stereotypes are embedded in the voice and comic conventions. Terms used for Latin American characters, the treatment of Spanish as comic linguistic material, the caricature of the Englishman, and broad claims about women reflect the period’s popular adventure fiction rather than a neutral narrative register. Because nearly all of this passes through Bunt’s speech, it contributes to characterization, but the story also relies on those stereotypes to generate humour and plot.

Overall, the style is best described as vernacular tall-tale realism with mock-epic adventure elements. Its signature qualities are a strongly performed raconteur’s voice, dense eye dialect, rolling paratactic syntax, extravagant similes, masculine banter, escalating farce, and a carefully prepared reversal that punctures heroic and romantic pretensions.

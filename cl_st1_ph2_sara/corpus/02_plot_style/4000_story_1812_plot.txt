After the Revolutionary War, the United States gains control of the territory west of the original states and establishes the Northwest Territory as a region free from slavery. White settlers rapidly cross the Ohio River, often occupying land without legal authority and despite Indigenous nations’ insistence that the river remains the boundary between them. Disputed treaties, land speculation, and continual settlement intensify warfare between the settlers and the Ohio tribes.

The Indigenous confederacy defeats two American expeditions. General Harmar’s retreat is ambushed in 1790, and in 1791 Little Turtle leads a devastating surprise attack against General Arthur St. Clair’s poorly supplied and undisciplined army. St. Clair fights bravely despite illness, but roughly half his force is killed, and the wounded are massacred. Survivors recount desperate escapes and acts of courage, including William Kennan rescuing the wounded General Madison, while other incidents reveal the battle’s cruelty and human cost.

General Anthony Wayne then advances cautiously through Indigenous territory, building forts along the way. In 1794 he decisively defeats the confederated tribes near Fort Defiance, breaking their military resistance for sixteen years. The narrative closes by confronting the horrific aftermath of St. Clair’s defeat, where Wayne’s soldiers later find and bury hundreds of skulls, emphasizing the brutality of war and the violent dispossession underlying Ohio’s settlement.

The story adopts the manner of a late nineteenth-century comic-sentimental reminiscence. It is narrated retrospectively in the first person by an urbane, conversational observer who reconstructs events partly from family tradition and partly from personal experience. The narrator repeatedly admits uncertainty—“as far as I can find out,” “perhaps,” “from all I can gather”—which gives the account the air of an anecdote honestly pieced together while also allowing delays, digressions, and comic qualifications. His direct addresses to the reader and references to the process of telling make the narration genial and self-conscious rather than invisible.

The prevailing tone combines affectionate irony, social satire, and restrained pathos. Boston dignity, inherited status, family pride, moral duty, and theatrical displays of feeling are treated with amused skepticism. The narrator’s jokes are often produced by understatement or mock solemnity, as when a disastrous dinner is dismissed because he does not wish to make the story “any more pathetic than is necessary.” Similar humor comes from disproportion: minor domestic inconveniences are described in elevated language, while emotionally serious facts are sometimes delivered almost casually. The comedy never entirely cancels the story’s tenderness, however. Once Tommy Biggs’s lifelong devotion is revealed, earlier comic details acquire emotional weight, and the ending turns the recurring aromatic joke into a sincere metaphor for love and memory.

The diction is polished, expansive, and recognizably period-specific. It mixes formal words such as “munificence,” “perfunctory,” “vituperation,” and “personage” with colloquial expressions such as “can’t lick anybody,” “hot in the collar,” and “ain’t changed a mite.” This contrast helps distinguish the narrator’s cultivated voice from Tommy’s compressed speech and Aunt Lucretia’s blunt aggression. Capitalization, commercial formulas, old-fashioned greetings, and place names in the inserted letters imitate nineteenth-century business correspondence. Occasional typographical emphasis through capitals—“OTHER,” “WAS,” “ANYWAY”—reproduces vocal stress and sharpens comic timing.

The syntax is generally elaborate and leisurely. Long, clause-rich sentences accumulate qualifications, parenthetical remarks, balanced contrasts, and humorous afterthoughts. Dashes are used frequently to interrupt a statement, revise it, or insert a sly aside. At moments of revelation or confrontation, the prose abruptly shortens. Aunt Lucretia’s “That ain’t HIM!” and Tommy’s “I’m Tommy Biggs, Miss Lucretia” gain force from their brevity after the narrator’s extended preparations. Tommy’s eventual explanation is rendered in fragments—“Peculiarities. Had ’em. Peculiarities.”—which embodies both his taciturnity and his emotional difficulty. His language compresses an entire life of loyalty into disconnected factual units, leaving the narrator to expand their moral significance.

The story is carefully structured around delayed disclosure. It begins with the childhood basket-switching episode, where the butcher-boy accepts punishment rather than betray his idol. This incident initially seems like a picturesque family anecdote, but it later proves to be the miniature pattern of Tommy’s whole life: he repeatedly takes responsibility for David’s wrongdoing and preserves David’s reputation at personal cost. The long middle section builds an idealized absent figure through gifts, letters, exotic objects, and fragrance. When the supposed uncle finally appears, the contrast between expectation and reality creates comedy, but the deeper mystery remains concealed. Aunt Lucretia’s arrival triggers the recognition scene, the daguerreotype provides tangible evidence, and Tommy’s confession retrospectively reinterprets every earlier event.

Characterization relies heavily on recurring habits, physical details, and comic caricature. The narrator defines the Judge through pompous severity, Aunt Lucretia through angularity, intrusion, and moral aggression, and Tommy through silence, pipe-smoking, overcoats, routine, and unobtrusive kindness. Aunt Lucretia is deliberately exaggerated: she points a “long bony finger,” prepares for a theatrical reunion, and marches “like a grenadier.” Tommy, by contrast, is anti-picturesque. His commonplace appearance and cheap patchouli undermine the family’s romantic fantasy, but his ordinary exterior conceals the story’s true heroism. David remains mostly absent and is constructed through other people’s worship, his “peculiarities,” and the consequences of his selfishness. This indirect characterization preserves the contrast between glittering charm and quiet goodness.

Dialogue is economical and highly individualized. The narrator and his wife ask socially conventional questions; Aunt Lucretia speaks in accusations and emphatic declarations; Tommy answers with minimal, factual phrases. His silence is not merely a comic trait but an ethical one: he does not dramatize his sacrifices, justify himself at length, or condemn the dead man he served. Even his harsh disclosures are softened by euphemism. David drank and pursued women, but Tommy calls these failings “peculiarities” and continues to insist that he was the “finest man in the world.” The gap between Tommy’s judgment and the evidence creates both irony and pathos.

Sensory imagery, especially scent, provides the central unifying motif. Oriental furniture, jade, ivory, sandalwood, teak, rugs, china, ginger, and preserved oranges establish a richly material atmosphere associated with distant travel and family generosity. At first the aroma seems exotic and romantic; then the returning uncle’s personal scent proves disappointingly cheap; finally fragrance becomes detached from exoticism and converted into an emblem of enduring affection. The closing statement that the house “still smells of his love” completes this transformation. Material gifts function similarly: what might have been mere decorative abundance becomes the physical residue of Tommy’s unspoken devotion.

The setting is presented through nostalgic social history rather than dense realism. Old Boston is evoked as provincial, orderly, status-conscious, and secure enough for market baskets to remain unattended. China and other distant places are less fully represented as actual cultures than as the remote source of commodities, wealth, and mystery, reflecting the period’s commercial and Orientalist imagination. The domestic interiors are more important than the foreign locations: the emotional action centers on houses, family rooms, inherited objects, meals, letters, and visits.

Overall, the style depends upon the controlled fusion of farce and moral sentiment. The narrator’s wit prevents the tale from becoming mawkish, while the final revelation prevents its eccentric characters and domestic mishaps from remaining merely comic. Repetition, foreshadowing, ironic reversal, inserted documents, and a strong closing image give the story a polished anecdotal shape. Its characteristic movement is from romantic appearance to humble reality, from family mythology to moral truth, and from laughter at an impostor to reverence for a self-effacing substitute whose conduct makes him more authentically an uncle than the man whose identity he assumed.

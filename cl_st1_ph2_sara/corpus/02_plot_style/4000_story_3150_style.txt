The story uses a warm, gently didactic children’s-fable style in which woodland animals are anthropomorphised without losing their recognisable natural habits. The narrative combines an account of a young Red-headed Woodpecker’s development with a simple moral lesson about vanity, self-awareness, and the influence of social criticism. Its intended audience is clearly young readers: unfamiliar aspects of bird life are explained in accessible terms, potentially frightening dangers are softened, and correction is delivered through humour rather than punishment.

The narrator is omniscient, affectionate, and conspicuously present. Rather than merely reporting events, the voice comments on them, explains motives, asks rhetorical questions, and speaks directly to the reader through phrases such as “you can imagine” and observations about what “many people would like to know.” This creates the feeling of an adult storyteller addressing children aloud. The narrator confidently enters the minds of both parent and young birds, while occasionally pretending uncertainty—“who can tell” what the unhatched chicks were thinking?—as a way of encouraging wonder. Generalisations about mothers, children, birds, and family life give the voice a proverbial, reassuring authority.

The diction is clear, domestic, and mildly old-fashioned. Words such as “sunshiny,” “morsel,” “plumage,” “behold,” and “barn-yard” contribute to a period storybook register, while affectionate diminutives and evaluative adjectives—“tiny bird,” “tired little fellow,” “dear, dry old tree,” “juicy and delicious morsel”—keep the tone intimate. Animal species are capitalised as though they were social identities or family names: “Woodpecker,” “Tree Frog,” “Gray Squirrel,” and “Blue Jay.” This strengthens the anthropomorphic world by making each kind of animal resemble a recognised community or household.

Sentences are generally fluent and moderately long, often linked by coordinating conjunctions and explanatory clauses. The prose frequently moves from a concrete action to a broader lesson: the mother lets the chick break its own shell, for example, and the narrator immediately explains that self-help “makes them strong and fits them for life.” Parenthetical qualifications and conversational turns such as “to be sure,” “of course,” “however that may be,” and “at any rate” reproduce the rhythms of oral storytelling. Short exclamations—“No indeed!” and “Ah, but they must leave it”—add emphasis and animation. Repetition is used for comic and emotional effect, especially in “tap-tap-tap,” “Ker-r-ruck! Ker-r-ruck!,” and the young bird’s troubled repetition of “a silly fellow.”

Dialogue is frequent, simple, and character-revealing. The parents speak with practical wisdom and mild irony, while the young Woodpecker’s literal questions expose his immaturity. Much of the humour comes from his inability to understand figurative language: after being told that he sees the world through his own feathers, he asks whose feathers he ought to look through. The father’s answer turns the metaphor into a playful anatomical joke about where the bird’s eyes would have been placed if he were meant to admire himself continually. The adults correct the child indirectly, preferring anecdotes, teasing, and common sense to severe rebuke.

The setting is presented through small, concrete details rather than extensive scenic description. The hollow tree, wood chips, bark, branches, orchard, eggshells, feathers, and fruit create a compact natural world centred on nest and family. Descriptions also carry unobtrusive natural-history instruction. The reader learns how Woodpeckers excavate nests, remove shells to avoid predators, support themselves with stiff tails, search bark for food, and preen their feathers. Facts are woven into the action and often transformed into fanciful explanations, such as birds keeping their “waterproof” in a pocket under the tail or guarding the ability to recognise ripe fruit as a “forest secret.”

Structurally, the story follows a chronological coming-of-age sequence. It begins with hatching, moves through feeding, sight, feather growth, play, and flying lessons, then narrows its focus to the eldest chick’s vanity. The moral conflict develops through repeated examples of his self-absorption before reaching a comic turning point in his conversation with the Gray Squirrel. Resolution comes when an outsider’s judgment succeeds where parental advice has failed. The mother’s final observation supplies a wry domestic coda: children may ignore repeated family counsel yet immediately heed the same criticism from someone else.

The moral is explicit but lightly handled. Vanity is called a “great fault,” and the narrative repeatedly contrasts the brother’s anxious self-scrutiny with his sisters’ generous admiration of one another. His vanity is shown as both foolish and self-defeating: he worries about natural growth, pulls at his feathers, makes himself sore, and overlooks the “wonderful great world.” The preferred virtues are moderation, attention to others, practical usefulness, independence, and cheerful acceptance of natural development. The final reform is behavioural rather than dramatic; he is permitted to care for his appearance, but only at sensible intervals.

Overall, the style is that of an early twentieth-century animal story blending fable, family comedy, natural-history lesson, and moral instruction. Its distinguishing features are a companionable adult narrator, direct engagement with child readers, personified but biologically grounded animals, gently archaic diction, explanatory asides, rhetorical questions, playful literalisation of idioms, and an affectionate closing lesson.

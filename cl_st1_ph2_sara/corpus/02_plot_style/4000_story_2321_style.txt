The story uses a psychologically intense, early modernist realism in which external landscape and inward emotion continually reflect, disturb, and reinterpret one another. Its third-person narration remains closely aligned with Syson for most of the story, moving freely between observable action, subjective perception, memory, and direct thought. The perspective is intimate but not wholly sympathetic: Syson’s sensitivity, vanity, nostalgia, self-contempt, and habit of idealising others are all exposed. At significant moments the narration also enters Hilda’s consciousness, revealing motives and insights unavailable to him. This subtle mobility of viewpoint turns the encounter into a contest between incompatible inner realities rather than a simple romantic confrontation.

The prose is richly sensory and strongly visual, especially in its treatment of the countryside. Colour is unusually prominent: bluebells become “azure shallows,” primroses appear in scarlet, pink, and pale purple, pine trunks are brick-red and purplish, and marigolds cast gold onto brown water. Landscape is rarely neutral description. It is animated through metaphor and personification: hazel branches spread “glad little hands,” daffodils resemble “yellow, ruffled birds,” hills wear woods like bearskin garments, and birdsong seems to condense from sunshine. Such images often fuse precise natural observation with lyrical transformation. Botanical and ornithological specificity—woodruff, dog-mercury, hyacinth, milkwort, lousewort, linnets, buntings, kingfishers—gives the setting material authority, while figurative language makes it visionary and emotionally charged.

A central stylistic tension exists between permanence and change. Repetition of phrases such as “still,” “unaltered,” and “back” initially presents the landscape as an “eternal” world preserved from Syson’s youth. Yet the apparent continuity of place throws human change into sharper relief. The familiar countryside survives, but the emotional meaning Syson once assigned to it collapses. Natural beauty therefore produces both exaltation and pain. Even at the end, when the world remains “marvellous, for ever new,” Syson experiences it as a kind of “monotone hell.” This characteristic doubleness allows beauty and anguish to occupy the same image.

The diction moves between lyrical refinement, philosophical abstraction, plain rural speech, and occasional literary allusion. The narrator’s vocabulary is often elevated and exact, using words such as “diapered,” “sublimation,” “phosphorescence,” and “truncheon,” while local characters speak in contracted dialect forms such as “lodgin’,” “goin’,” “allus,” “a’ready,” and “ter.” Dialect is rendered lightly rather than phonetically throughout, enough to establish regional and class difference without making the dialogue difficult to read. Syson’s cultivated register, quotations, poetry books, French verse, and references to Dante, Botticelli, William Morris, and classical gods mark both his education and his tendency to filter experience through art. These allusions are not merely decorative: they expose his habit of turning Hilda into an imagined Beatrice, nun, angel, or poetic symbol instead of recognising her autonomous identity.

Sentence structure alternates between expansive descriptive passages and abrupt dramatic exchanges. Landscape sentences frequently accumulate clauses, colours, textures, and comparisons, creating a flowing, immersive rhythm. Emotional shocks are conveyed through short statements, fragments, exclamations, and sudden questions: “You?—Addy!”, “I’m married myself,” and “I was married the same night as you.” Dashes, semicolons, ellipses, and pauses reproduce hesitation, withheld feeling, and shifts in power. Repeated silences are especially important. Characters often fail to answer directly, and the narrative records pauses as active moments in which pride, jealousy, fear, and calculation operate beneath speech.

Dialogue is adversarial and oblique. Conversations proceed through challenges, evasions, ironic repetitions, and statements whose implications are more important than their literal content. The encounter with Pilbeam begins as a dispute over trespass but quickly becomes a struggle over sexual and social territory. Syson and Hilda similarly debate love through abstractions about freedom, selfhood, gods, soil, and personal development. Their speech often wounds indirectly. Irony functions as a defence, especially for Syson, whose “brilliant” laughter and cultivated wit repeatedly conceal humiliation. Hilda’s quieter, more assured speech gradually gains dominance, and her use of the shared language once created with Syson demonstrates that she has absorbed and appropriated what he believed belonged to their relationship.

Character description is physical, evaluative, and psychologically suggestive. Pilbeam’s thrusting chest and balanced body make him seem “taut with animal life,” while Hilda’s arms, throat, hair, and posture are described through images of plants, kernels, crowns, and fur. Bodies disclose forms of being: Pilbeam is instinctive and embodied; Hilda is rooted, self-contained, and increasingly sensual; Syson is mobile, analytical, luminous, and divided against himself. The story repeatedly opposes intellectual or aesthetic possession to bodily and local belonging. Hilda’s declaration that she is “like a plant” able to grow only in her own soil encapsulates this organic conception of identity.

The three-part structure traces a progressive dismantling of illusion. The first section restores Syson’s past through landscape, then disrupts it through his meeting with Pilbeam. The second brings him into Hilda’s domestic and natural world, where familiarity gives way to estrangement. The third reveals the keeper’s hut, Hilda’s sexual independence, and the final collapse of Syson’s idealised history. Spatial movement carries symbolic force: Syson passes from open views into increasingly enclosed and shadowed paths, reaches the secret inner room of the hut, and then returns toward open country with painful knowledge. Thresholds, gates, woodland paths, windows, and concealed compartments repeatedly mark transitions between memory and actuality, public identity and private desire.

The dominant motifs are trespass, ownership, rootedness, selfhood, and mistaken perception. Syson trespasses physically in the wood and emotionally in Hilda’s life; Pilbeam guards both territory and relationship; Hilda resists being treated as an extension of another person’s imagination. The story’s deepest irony is that Syson, who prides himself on perception, has failed to see Hilda as she is. Its style therefore combines lyrical naturalism with a severe critique of romantic idealisation. The concluding episode, observed covertly through bushes, replaces Syson’s literary fantasy with the physical intimacy of Hilda and Pilbeam. Yet the ending offers no simple victory: Hilda retains distance even from her lover, Pilbeam remains anxious, and Syson leaves wounded but newly aware. The overall tone is elegiac, sensuous, ironic, and emotionally unsparing.

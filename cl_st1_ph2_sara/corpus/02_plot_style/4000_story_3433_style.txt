The story adopts a late-Victorian Gothic mode, combining hereditary decadence, sexual scandal, murder, supernatural revenge and the oppressive atmosphere of an ancient country house. Its dominant mood is ominous and fatalistic: characters appear governed by inherited passions and moral defects, while places and physical objects preserve the consequences of concealed wrongdoing. The ruined farm, the elevated ancestral mansion, the cracked hearth-stone and the repeatedly emerging golden hair all function as outward manifestations of corruption, guilt and inevitable exposure.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, with an authoritative, socially observant voice. It moves freely between family history, communal opinion and individual psychology, often interpreting events through generalisations about class, sex, temperament and human behaviour. The narrator’s tone mixes moral seriousness with dry irony, as in the comparisons between declining families and exhausted vegetation or in detached comments on scandal, domestic conflict and provincial respectability. This creates distance from the characters while also framing their actions as examples of larger social and hereditary forces.

The prose is formal, elaborate and period-inflected. Its vocabulary favours abstract moral and psychological terms such as “decadence,” “dissipation,” “voluptuousness,” “recklessness,” “vengeance” and “degradation.” Long, carefully subordinated sentences are common, especially in the opening exposition, where genealogies, class distinctions and causes of decline are accumulated through parallel clauses. Parenthetical qualifications, balanced contrasts and rhetorical comparisons give the narrative a discursive, almost historical texture. Dashes, semicolons and enumerations allow sentences to expand while preserving a deliberate rhythm. At moments of crisis, however, the syntax contracts into shorter statements, exclamations and fragments, accelerating the pace and heightening alarm.

The story begins with panoramic exposition rather than immediate action. It establishes the reputations of the Brents and Delandres, contrasts aristocratic and yeoman forms of degeneration, and presents the central figures as products of worn-out bloodlines. This extensive social and hereditary background gives the later violence a sense of inevitability. The structure then narrows progressively: from neighbourhood scandal to family quarrel, from reported accident to private revenge, and finally from the whole estate to the sealed hall and the crack in a single stone. That spatial contraction intensifies the claustrophobia.

Pacing relies on alternation between compressed summary and fully dramatized scenes. Months and journeys are passed over rapidly, while decisive encounters—Margaret’s return, Geoffrey’s nocturnal labour, the discovery of the hair and the final vigil—are rendered in close sensory detail. Suspense is built through delayed explanation and incremental clues: Geoffrey’s survival at the precipice, Margaret’s scarred reappearance, the missing lime, the cracked hearth, his secretive behaviour and the first strands of hair. The reader understands the probable crime before every character does, producing dramatic irony and sustained dread.

Characterisation is strongly typological and physiognomic. Geoffrey is introduced as the embodiment of an exhausted aristocratic race, simultaneously handsome, cultivated, cruel and potentially monstrous. Margaret represents the female form of the Delandre inheritance: passionate, sensual, defiant and implacably vengeful. Wykham is shaped by resentment, wounded pride and drink, while Geoffrey’s Italian wife is idealised through beauty, sweetness, emotional intensity and linguistic delicacy. Physical appearance repeatedly signals moral or psychological condition: aquiline beauty suggests dominance, scars register betrayal and suffering, pallor betrays guilt, and facial expressions expose terror. Characters are therefore less nuanced individuals than concentrated Gothic forces—lust, pride, vengeance, innocence and remorse.

Dialogue is theatrical, confrontational and emotionally heightened. Threats, curses, rhetorical questions and exclamations dominate, with frequent reporting clauses that specify whispers, hisses, shrieks and hoarse voices. Margaret’s speech is especially melodramatic and prophetic, turning her suffering into a declaration of revenge. The Italian wife’s slightly archaic or non-native phrasing—“What dost thou?” and “I know not where to turn”—marks her foreignness and adds lyrical vulnerability. Social dialect is used more broadly for the builder’s foreman, whose dropped consonants and colloquial grammar distinguish working-class speech from the narrator’s elevated register.

The supernatural is handled through strategic ambiguity before becoming visually undeniable. Margaret first appears “like” a ghost, but her testimony establishes that she survived the attempted murder. The returning hair, by contrast, exceeds ordinary explanation: it grows through stone, survives repeated burning and finally entangles Geoffrey’s feet. The narrative does not explain the mechanism, allowing the image to operate simultaneously as supernatural vengeance, material evidence and the physical embodiment of guilty memory. Repetition turns the hair into the central symbol. Once associated with Margaret’s beauty and sexual power, it becomes scarred survival, accusation, revenant presence and ultimately an instrument of doom.

Sensory description concentrates on darkness, moonlight, sound and tactile material. Clicks of locks, muffled blows, scraping stone, dragging noises and smothered cries make the hidden murder audible before it is visible. Lime, scaffolding, ice, torrent, cracked masonry and hair provide concrete textures against the story’s abstract language of passion and degeneration. Light is used selectively: lamplight reveals Geoffrey’s pallor, moonlight advances across the hall like an active force, and golden hair shines against stone and shadow. This contrast between beauty and horror is central to the tale’s aesthetic.

The style repeatedly employs foreshadowing and symbolic doubling. Both families are decayed in different class forms; both Geoffrey and Margaret combine attractiveness with destructive passion; the first precipice murder is echoed by the later concealment beneath the hearth; and Margaret’s lost beauty returns through the one feature that survived, her hair. Domestic spaces are inverted: the hearth, conventionally a symbol of family warmth and continuity, becomes a grave, while the anticipated heir and renewed household are overshadowed by an earlier illicit union. The conclusion completes this inversion by placing husband and wife together beside the corrupted hearth, innocence dying alongside guilt.

Overall, the style is ornate, moralising, sensational and highly visual. It blends social chronicle with melodrama and supernatural horror, using hereditary explanation to establish doom, procedural clues to sustain suspense, and a single grotesque image to deliver poetic justice. The emotional register remains elevated throughout, and the ending favours a stark tableau rather than explanation: the peaceful dead wife, the horrified guilty husband and the grey-streaked golden hair rising from the broken stone.

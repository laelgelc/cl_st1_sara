The story uses a genial first-person comic voice, combining conversational ease with mock-serious reflection. The narrator presents himself as sensible, restrained, and long-suffering, but his exaggerated irritation and fantasies of violence make him part of the joke. He describes absurd events in the measured language of reasonable observation, allowing the contrast between tone and subject to generate much of the humour. Parenthetical asides, qualifications, and self-conscious admissions—such as conceding that he may be prejudiced—create an intimate relationship with the reader while sustaining an appearance of fairness.

The narrative is episodic and character-centred rather than tightly plotted. It follows Begglely through a succession of obsessive enthusiasms: tennis, photography, golf, and finally whist. Each craze is developed through escalating anecdotes until its comic possibilities are exhausted, after which the story moves briskly to the next fixation. Repetition provides structural unity: Begglely does not fundamentally change but transfers the same relentless zeal from one hobby to another. The ending is deliberately understated and circular, showing him absorbed in yet another pastime and implying that the cycle will continue indefinitely.

The humour depends heavily on escalation, incongruity, hyperbole, and deadpan understatement. A tennis enthusiast becomes so tiresome that the narrator contemplates eliminating an entire sporting family; an inept photographer’s portraits destroy romances, reputations, and self-esteem; a supposedly mild game of golf leaves elderly players physically ruined and “full of evil thoughts.” Small inconveniences expand into social catastrophes, while extreme consequences are reported as though they were ordinary and inevitable. Comic reversals are frequent: Begglely’s friends first suffer from his hobbies, then deliberately encourage him to pursue photography abroad in the hope that foreign violence will solve their problem.

The prose alternates between compact dialogue and longer, elaborately balanced sentences. Dialogue is brisk, direct, and often arranged as a sequence of short exchanges that sharpen timing and deliver punchlines cleanly. Narrative passages favour accumulative syntax, parallel constructions, trios, and catalogues. Phrases such as “playing tennis, or practising tennis, or reading about tennis” reproduce the oppressive repetitiveness of Begglely’s obsession. Longer descriptions pile example upon example, particularly in the account of his photographs, where each new grotesque result intensifies the central joke.

Diction is accessible but distinctly period-inflected, with expressions such as “by Jove,” “mater,” “beast of a hole,” and “Great Scott,” alongside references to boarding establishments, vicars, maiden ladies, servants, and amateur sporting culture. The narrator occasionally adopts elevated, legalistic, scientific, or moral language for comic effect. Claims about perspective being governed by no law “either human or divine,” or about a photographer being merely “an instrument in the hands of science,” parody authoritative forms of explanation. Formal phrasing is repeatedly applied to trivial matters, lending ridiculous hobbies the gravity of public crises.

Characterisation is achieved mainly through habitual behaviour and reported speech. Begglely is less a psychologically complex individual than a durable comic type: the monomaniacal enthusiast who cannot recognise the inconvenience he causes. His cheerfulness and geniality prevent him from becoming malicious, while his total lack of proportion makes him socially destructive. Secondary characters appear briefly as victims, witnesses, or accomplices, often defined by one vivid reaction. The narrator himself is characterised through dry commentary, cultivated exasperation, and a tendency to turn private annoyance into elaborate comic argument.

The descriptive method is selective and caricatural. Physical details are introduced when they establish a visual joke—the red-haired player beneath the bedroom window, the oversized camera resembling a missionary box, or the apparently gigantic nephew seated on his aunt’s knee. Images frequently distort scale and perspective, mirroring the defects attributed to Begglely’s camera. Personification also plays an important role: the camera behaves like an “evil sprite” or a malicious critic, actively seeking defects and exposing humanity at its worst.

The story’s pacing is lively despite its anecdotal form. It begins in medias res with onomatopoeic impacts—“Bump. Bump. Bump-bump. Bump.”—then quickly turns a possible burglary into a trivial sporting nuisance. Thereafter, summary passages cover long periods rapidly, while representative scenes slow down for dialogue and comic demonstration. Transitions between hobbies are abrupt, which reinforces the sense that Begglely’s enthusiasm is indiscriminate and inexhaustible.

Overall, the style is that of a polished comic reminiscence: urbane, digressive, observant, and affectionately satirical. It mocks obsessive leisure pursuits, amateur technology, social vanity, and the discomforts produced by overenthusiasm, but it avoids bitterness. Its characteristic effect comes from presenting absurdity in composed, reasonable prose and allowing repetition, disproportion, and impeccably timed understatement to reveal the joke.

The story uses an omniscient third-person narrator who moves fluidly between scenic description, external action, and intimate psychological reflection. The narrative voice is poised, literary, and compassionate, but it also possesses a strong satirical edge. It treats the lovers’ suffering with lyrical seriousness while exposing caste orthodoxy, patriarchal authority, and revenge-driven moral reasoning through irony. The narrator occasionally steps beyond neutral observation to offer aphoristic commentary, as in the lament that earthly beauty is easily “twisted out of shape.” This creates the sense of a morally engaged storyteller rather than an invisible recorder.

The prose combines romantic naturalism with social realism. Moonlight, spring breezes, blossoms, birdsong, trees, darkness, and stars are not merely background details; they echo or counterpoint the characters’ emotional states. The opening full-moon night is sensuous and fertile, filled with fragrance, music, and playful intimacy, while the final waning-moon night is silent, dark, and nearly abstract. Repeated natural motifs—the papiya’s song, the south wind, the open window, and the changing moon—bind the story together and dramatize the passage from apparent harmony to crisis. Nature often remains beautiful and indifferent while human custom deforms private happiness, producing a characteristically poignant contrast.

Imagery is lush, figurative, and frequently personified. A breeze plays with a flowering shrub; moonlight sleeps like an exhausted heroine; time stands like a hushed ocean; fate becomes a painter arranging judge and accused within an eternal tableau. Emotional experiences are translated into physical ordeals or substances: Kusum “waded barefooted through fire,” love crumbles into dust, and Hemanta’s rage burns only himself. Similes are extended enough to give scenes a fable-like or emblematic quality, yet they remain tied to immediate feeling. The style often enlarges a domestic incident into something cosmic through references to endless space, eternal night, annihilation, fate, and future births.

Sentence structure varies with narrative purpose. Descriptive and reflective passages favour long, balanced sentences with accumulating clauses, repetitions, and rhetorical parallelism. Kusum’s meditation on love, for example, builds through a sequence of relative clauses before collapsing into a bitter conclusion. Moments of confrontation shift to short questions, answers, commands, and declarations: “Is it true?” “It is.” “I don’t care for caste.” This contrast makes emotional turning points feel abrupt and decisive. Dashes, semicolons, direct exclamations, and parenthetical glosses contribute to an older translated-literary texture.

Dialogue is central and sharply differentiated by character. Hemanta begins playful and affectionate, using fanciful exaggeration and literary allusion, but becomes broken, furious, and finally calm in resistance. Kusum speaks sparingly, slowly, and gravely; her restraint conveys dread more powerfully than overt melodrama. Harihar’s language is authoritarian and imperative, often accompanied by roaring and the ominous sound of his slippers. Peari Sankar speaks at greatest length, delivering the story’s explanatory core as an embedded oral narrative. His manner is composed, sardonic, and chillingly courteous. He frames vengeance as duty, jokes about turning events into a romance, and offers refreshments after describing the destruction he has engineered. The discrepancy between his polite tone and his cruelty generates much of the story’s black humour.

The plot is constructed through delayed revelation. It opens at the instant before confession, interrupts that confession with paternal judgment, then reconstructs the hidden past through Kusum’s brief account and Peari Sankar’s much fuller monologue. This retrospective structure converts what first appears to be a private marital secret into the outcome of an intergenerational feud. The final scene returns to the bedroom and repeats several elements from the opening—the open window, the night, the approaching slippers—but reverses their emotional meaning. This circular composition gives Hemanta’s closing refusal greater force: the setting that witnessed threatened intimacy becomes the setting of ethical choice.

The prevailing tone moves from lyrical tenderness through foreboding, anguish, bitter irony, and near-tragic solemnity. Humour appears throughout, but it is rarely carefree. Early marital teasing establishes warmth, while later humour exposes hypocrisy and moral self-deception. Peari Sankar’s elaborate account is especially marked by deadpan sarcasm, euphemism, and mock-reasonableness. The story’s pathos is controlled rather than sentimental: Kusum’s suffering is conveyed through stillness, silence, physical gestures, and metaphors of scorching, emptiness, and emotional desiccation.

Characterization relies on revealing actions and verbal mannerisms rather than extensive physical portraiture. Hemanta’s playful handling of Kusum’s hair and ornaments establishes intimacy; Kusum’s fixed gaze and eventual clasping of his feet embody fear, shame, and social vulnerability; Harihar is announced by his aggressive footwear before he appears; Peari Sankar’s composure becomes a sign of calculated vindictiveness. Repeated gestures acquire symbolic force, particularly Kusum’s attempts to withdraw or disappear and Hemanta’s movement from private confusion to public defiance.

The diction is elevated but accessible, combining poetic English with culturally specific Bengali and Hindu terms such as *Phalgun*, *mantra*, *prayaschittam*, *pan*, and caste designations. Parenthetical explanations translate unfamiliar references for an Anglophone readership, sometimes in a conspicuously editorial voice. Mythological and literary allusions—to Jayadeva, Parbati, Brahminical rites, and rebirth—situate the domestic plot within a larger cultural world. Archaic or formal expressions such as “betook myself,” “remained obdurate,” “bidding good-bye,” and “auspicious moment” reinforce the period character. Occasional typographical or transcription irregularities are present, but the underlying style remains ornate, rhythmic, and distinctly that of an older literary translation.

Thematically, the story sets spontaneous human love against inherited systems of caste, honour, ritual purity, and patriarchal control. Its irony repeatedly exposes “duty” as a word used to disguise vengeance or cruelty. Social law is shown to be theatrical and unstable: caste can be supposedly preserved, contaminated, weaponized, or renounced according to the interests of powerful men. Kusum bears the consequences of decisions made by guardians, fathers, and avengers, while her own assent remains deliberately uncertain. The conclusion does not undo her humiliation, but it converts the romantic bond into a moral stand. Hemanta’s final plain sentence rejects the elaborate sophistries that have governed the preceding events.

Overall, the style is lyrical, ironic, psychologically observant, and structurally economical. Its signature method is to surround a harsh social conflict with sensuous natural beauty, use recurrent imagery to transform mood, reveal the past through dramatically voiced narration, and end with a concise act of resistance after passages of ornate description and moral complexity.

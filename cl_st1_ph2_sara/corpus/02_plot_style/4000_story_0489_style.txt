The story uses a clear, accessible late-Victorian popular-fiction style, combining domestic realism, social comedy, and suspense. Its tone begins ominously, with the father’s “bowed head and angry brow” establishing the prospect of a family confrontation, but gradually shifts toward genial irony and ultimately reconciliation. The narration treats the characters sympathetically while retaining a lightly comic distance, especially from Mr. Saunders’s self-important notions of authority and duty. Expressions such as “nailed his flag to the mast,” “the parting shot,” and “on the war-path” humorously cast an ordinary domestic dispute as a military campaign.

The narrative is told in the third person by an omniscient, intrusive narrator. The viewpoint moves principally among Mr. Saunders, Mrs. Saunders, and Dick, revealing motives and emotional histories that the characters cannot openly communicate. The narrator occasionally offers generalising commentary—“as a man with a good conscience is entitled to do” and “as every woman must who lives with a cantankerous man”—which creates the voice of a confident storyteller addressing a broad readership. This voice is morally observant but not solemn: it judges the father’s brutality, admires the mother’s loyalty, and recognises Dick’s resentment, yet allows each character redeeming qualities.

Characterisation is economical and strongly contrastive. Mr. Saunders is rigid, domineering, suspicious, and physically associated with heaviness: his “heavy footfall,” stout stick, grim posture, and forceful stride embody patriarchal authority. Mrs. Saunders is gentle, anxious, self-effacing, and protective, functioning as a lifelong “buffer between father and son.” Dick is more modern in speech and outlook, answering parental alarm with wit, slang, and affectionate irreverence. Banker Hammond is expansive, worldly, and comic, his evening dress, rose, enthusiasm for whisky, betting, and billiards marking him as the opposite of the austere father. These contrasts also dramatise a generational divide between older ideas of obedience and respectability and a newer urban culture of clubs, leisure, and less formal employer–employee relations.

Dialogue carries much of the story. Exchanges are brisk, naturalistic, and often structured around incomplete knowledge. Mrs. Saunders hesitates and evades; her husband interrogates in clipped questions; Dick speaks in playful, colloquial phrases such as “Right for once,” “on the duty tack,” and “a plunger from Plungerville.” Dashes, repetitions, interrupted sentences, and exclamations reproduce anxiety and emotional pressure. The dialogue also differentiates social attitudes without requiring extensive description. The father’s stern literalism, the mother’s euphemistic caution, and Dick’s teasing modernity are immediately audible.

The prose generally uses medium-length, balanced sentences, alternating with short declarative lines at moments of conflict. Descriptive passages become more elaborate when the setting changes from the modest household to the opulent club. The club is rendered through electric lighting, leather chairs, sporting pictures, evening dress, and the silver cup, giving it theatrical splendour while preserving the father’s bewildered perspective. During the billiard match, the syntax grows controlled and sequential, mirroring Dick’s concentration. Repeated sounds—particularly the “click-click of the balls”—heighten the tense silence and make the game sensorially vivid.

The plot is built around misunderstanding and dramatic irony. Mr. Saunders interprets Dick’s late nights and club membership as evidence of moral decline, while the reader gradually learns that Dick neither drinks nor gambles and has joined at his employer’s request. The telegram produces the central comic complication: Hammond assumes that the father already knows about the billiard match, so his statements about Dick “going from bad to worse,” having little hope of “recovering his lost ground,” and losing ten thousand are understood morally and financially by Mr. Saunders but refer only to the state of the game and Hammond’s wager. This sustained double meaning briefly transforms a comic misunderstanding into apparent catastrophe before the billiard room reveals the truth.

Suspense develops in successive stages: the threatened confrontation at home, Dick’s departure, the father’s march to the club, the mother’s telegram, the ominous conversation with Hammond, and finally the match itself. The billiard sequence functions as the climax and is narrated like a sporting contest, with silence, crowd reactions, score changes, tactical decisions, and sudden applause. Although the technical details remain intelligible to a non-specialist, they are sufficiently precise to make Dick’s skill credible. His decision to scatter the balls after being taunted demonstrates both mastery and personal honour, turning the match into a test of “skill and pluck and nerve.”

The story’s humour rests chiefly on reversal. The allegedly corrupt club proves imposing and respectable; the banker is himself an enthusiastic gambler; the father who comes to discipline his son becomes his fascinated admirer; and the supposed evidence of dissipation reveals exceptional talent and self-control. Yet the humour does not erase the underlying pathos. The history of fear and estrangement between father and son is presented seriously, and Mrs. Saunders’s distress gives emotional weight to what might otherwise be a simple farce. The concluding reconciliation is restrained rather than sentimental: the father says he is proud, and Dick asks him to wait so they can walk home together. That final proposed walk quietly reverses the father’s solitary, angry journey and suggests renewed companionship without claiming that all past damage has been repaired.

Overall, the style is plot-driven, warmly ironic, dialogue-rich, and designed for rapid readability. It blends melodramatic anticipation with comic misapprehension, uses a socially observant omniscient narrator, and resolves family conflict through a public demonstration of the son’s competence. Its dominant stylistic effect is the transformation of moral suspicion into paternal pride through carefully managed irony and suspense.

The story adopts the form of a retrospective first-person oral confession framed by a brief third-person introduction. Lloyd Merriwether recounts his ordeal as though speaking informally to companions, repeatedly addressing an implied listener with phrases such as “you know,” “remember,” “I’ll tell you,” and “pass the bottle.” This conversational staging creates the impression of a clubroom anecdote or traveller’s yarn rather than a polished written memoir. The narrator’s frequent self-corrections, rhetorical questions, digressions, emphatic repetitions, and references to details learned later reproduce the loose, improvisatory movement of speech. At the same time, the opening frame distances the author from Merriwether and presents his account as a story often retold “in after years.”

The diction mixes colloquial early-twentieth-century American slang with ornate descriptive prose. Expressions such as “You bet,” “Sure,” “truck,” “as easy as pie,” “kicked the bucket,” and “get his goat” establish Merriwether as a brash, commercially minded New Englander. These coexist with heightened words and phrases such as “adamantine dust,” “illimitable distances,” “lasciviously scented,” and “entangled, exuberant commingling.” This contrast is central to the style: the narrator tries to contain an uncanny experience within wisecracks, business language, and masculine bravado, while the narration repeatedly expands into melodramatic and feverishly Gothic description. His racist slurs and dehumanising depictions of Africans are integral to the story’s colonial-era voice, revealing a prejudiced white trader who treats both people and landscape primarily in terms of utility, danger, and profit.

The syntax alternates between long, heavily coordinated sentences and abrupt fragments. Extended sentences accumulate clauses, sensory details, and parallel constructions until they mimic physical exhaustion, delirium, or the oppressive density of the jungle. Short units—“Three days. Four. Five—”, “A house!”, “Dying! Sure.”—interrupt that flow and sharpen moments of panic. Dashes, exclamation marks, ellipses, semicolons, and parenthetical asides are used profusely, giving the prose a breathless, performative rhythm. Repetition often charts psychological escalation: “home,” “fear,” “hatred,” “loneliness,” and “myself” recur until ordinary thoughts become obsessions. The occasional capitalised opening words and broken transitions imitate magazine or pulp-story typography and help divide the narrative into dramatic episodes.

The tone is a volatile blend of comic self-deprecation, colonial swagger, psychological horror, and grotesque irony. Merriwether repeatedly calls his behaviour ludicrous or melodramatic, commenting on his own story even while intensifying it. Humorous similes—mangroves sitting back “like hair on the brow of a professional patriot,” for example—briefly relieve the tension, while the narrator’s commercial cynicism makes even suffering and exploitation material for jokes. This humour does not cancel the horror; it makes the horror less stable by leaving the reader uncertain whether an incident is ridiculous, terrifying, or both. The final revelation likewise operates as a macabre punchline: the invisible enemy is Merriwether’s own reflection, rendered unrecognisable by fever, isolation, and the loss of trivial grooming objects.

The setting is described through dense sensory imagery, especially heat, smell, texture, and bodily discomfort. Africa is characterised as “crawling,” “stinking,” “putrid,” “acrid,” wet, thorny, and fever-ridden. The natural environment is persistently personified: it broods, hates, leers, swallows, bars the way, and reaches toward the narrator. Rivers, mangroves, flowers, rain, and heat become parts of a single hostile organism. This Gothic animation reflects Merriwether’s deteriorating mind more than an objective landscape; his fear and racial hostility are projected outward until the continent appears to possess human malice. Civilization receives an opposing cluster of images—razors, talc, advertisements, paved streets, saloons, policemen, ordinary dinners—but it is treated with deliberate bathos. Safety is represented not by lofty ideals but by consumer goods, routine, grooming, and familiar urban vulgarity.

Structurally, the story is built around foreshadowing and delayed explanation. The opening announces that small comforts become vital in a crisis, reveals that Merriwether went mad, and mentions both his missing toilet kit and Morrison’s rescue. Suspense therefore comes less from what happened than from discovering how these apparently trivial details caused the breakdown. The river journey then forms a progressive descent from irritation to unease, obsession, exhaustion, fever, hallucination, and violence. The abandoned suburban house appears to offer rescue but instead becomes an uncanny enclosure in which Merriwether’s diffuse fear acquires a seemingly physical form. The mirror climax gathers the story’s motifs—self-scrutiny, missing grooming equipment, fear of oneself, and civilization’s fragile rituals—into a literal image of failed self-recognition.

The central stylistic effect is ironic psychological exposure. Merriwether presents himself as practical, experienced, unimaginative, and self-sufficient, yet his narration continually disproves those claims. He is highly imaginative, superstitious under pressure, dependent on social and material routines, and terrified by his own altered appearance. His habitual language of mastery—over workers, trade, wilderness, and his emotions—collapses as the story proceeds. The mirror twist is therefore more than a surprise ending: it reveals that the monster he has attributed to Africa is also a projection of his own fevered consciousness. Overall, the style combines the colonial adventure tale, the tall tale, pulp Gothic horror, and the comic monologue, using flamboyant description and vernacular speech to turn a minor deprivation into a study of isolation, delirium, and the precariousness of civilized identity.

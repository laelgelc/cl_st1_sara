The story is a comic satirical tale built around an absurd technological invention and a rigorously prepared reversal. Its manner combines social realism, mock-scientific exposition, bureaucratic parody, and farce. The setting is initially rendered with composed, observational precision: the crowded provincial hall, the bright dresses and uniforms, the attentive silence, and even the buzzing fly create a credible public occasion before the grotesque machine comes into focus. This sober framing makes the increasingly outrageous premise seem temporarily plausible.

The narration is third-person, omniscient, and outwardly restrained. The narrator generally reports events with the calm authority of a public chronicler, allowing the characters’ words and actions to expose their own folly. Irony is pervasive but rarely explained. Elevated phrases such as “mechanical justice,” “most respected audience,” and “beloved fatherland” are placed beside brutality, vanity, and incompetence, producing a strong gap between official language and moral reality. The narrator occasionally intervenes with ceremonious formulations—“It is difficult to describe” or “it should be said”—which imitate sober historical reporting while sharpening the comedy.

Much of the story consists of the lecturer’s extended monologue. His speech is verbose, repetitive, self-important, and carefully graduated from pedagogical argument to nationalist enthusiasm and utopian prophecy. He relies on rhetorical questions, enumerations, scriptural and literary allusions, patriotic commonplaces, technical terminology, and constant direct address to “ladies and gentlemen.” His argument parodies intellectual rationalisation: cruelty is not rejected but modernised, depersonalised, quantified, and presented as humane progress. The formal eloquence and apparent logic conceal a grotesque premise, so the speech becomes a sustained exercise in dramatic irony.

The diction alternates between ornate public rhetoric and exact mechanical description. Words associated with education, administration, engineering, punishment, and measurement dominate: “celerity,” “indicator,” “scale of severity,” “statistical and revisionary purposes,” “equipoise,” and numbered degrees and strokes. Lists of institutions, components, punishments, and social benefits create a bureaucratic texture. Quantities, weights, coins, keys, springs, rods, and counters give the fantasy material specificity. This excessive precision is itself comic, suggesting a mentality that can calculate suffering without comprehending it. Period details and Russian institutional terms contribute to the historical and provincial atmosphere, while occasional explanatory footnotes reinforce the pseudo-documentary effect.

Sentence structure shifts according to function. Descriptive passages use balanced, moderately long sentences with accumulating clauses. The lecture favours long periodic constructions, parallelism, catalogues, qualifications, and emphatic conclusions. During the catastrophe, the prose becomes shorter and more fragmented. Exclamations, cries, commands, ellipses, onomatopoeic sounds, and isolated counter readings accelerate the action: “3180, 3181, 3182….” The repeated numbers act as a mechanical refrain, registering time, pain, and the machine’s indifference. This contrast between discursive slowness and frantic action is central to the story’s pacing.

Dialogue is sharply individualised through comic motifs. The deaf governor repeatedly proposes excessive punishment, while his pug-dog supplies a barking echo. The attendant combines devotion with ludicrous practical concern about the lecturer’s new trousers. The inventor’s polished abstractions collapse into cries, insults, and pleas once he becomes the victim. This change in register exposes the difference between discussing violence and experiencing it. Minor characters are sketched economically through a single behaviour, accessory, or verbal habit, giving the tale the crowded quality of a staged comic scene.

Structurally, the story proceeds as a demonstration with an inevitable trap. The concealed apparatus creates initial curiosity; the long explanation delays its operation while planting every mechanism required for the climax. The lecturer repeatedly stresses that the machine cannot be stopped, that any round object can activate it, and that heavier objects produce more strokes. His display of the heavy chronometer therefore triggers a reversal that is surprising in timing but fully prepared in retrospect. The inventor is literally subjected to the impersonal system he praises, making the ending a form of poetic justice and a physical enactment of the phrase “hoist with his own petard.”

The humour escalates from dry irony to grotesque slapstick. The lecturer’s accidental capture, the fruitless search for keys, the concern over his trousers, the shattered sword, the injured dignitary, and the eventual arrival of the fire brigade form a sequence of increasingly chaotic attempts at rescue. Beneath the comedy lies genuine cruelty: the victim’s suffering is prolonged, public, numerically recorded, and met by institutional helplessness. The machine is frightening precisely because it performs violence without anger, judgment, or mercy. Its “mathematical precision” turns moral responsibility into procedure.

The ending returns to a concise, retrospective narrative mode. The machine and its “idea” perish together, while the chastened inventor becomes gentle, melancholy, and popular with his pupils. This conclusion has the compact symmetry of a moral fable, but its lesson is delivered through irony rather than explicit sermonising. Overall, the style is theatrical, technically detailed, rhetorically inflated, and darkly comic. Its central method is to let an ideology of rationalised punishment elaborate itself at great length, then destroy its pretensions through an exact, bodily, and farcical reversal.

The story adopts a nineteenth-century antiquarian Gothic style, combining personal reminiscence, local history, family tradition, and orally transmitted supernatural legend. Its narrator presents himself as a careful collector of testimony rather than as an omniscient storyteller. He begins with memories of childhood visits to Lough Guir, describes the people, objects, buildings, and landscape associated with the place, and then relays stories attributed to Miss Anne Baily. This layered transmission—events remembered by the narrator, told by Miss Baily, or reported by servants, relatives, and a governess—gives the narrative the character of a folkloric record while preserving uncertainty about the truth of its marvels.

The narrative voice is cultivated, reflective, and gently digressive. It frequently pauses to add genealogical, historical, architectural, or social context, creating an atmosphere of inherited memory. The narrator’s authority rests on personal acquaintance and material detail: he recalls handling old glasses, seeing the castle, hearing named witnesses, and learning the customs of the household. Parenthetical qualifications such as “I think,” “as I supposed,” and “I forget which” create an impression of scrupulous honesty. At the same time, retrospective phrases such as “in my young days” and references to vanished lakes, altered buildings, and extinct customs lend the prose an elegiac quality. The past is treated as something partially lost but still perceptible through relics, landscapes, and stories.

The diction is formal, ornate, and distinctly period-specific. It draws on the vocabularies of genealogy, antiquarianism, architecture, hospitality, folklore, and the supernatural. Words and phrases such as “retinue,” “convivialities,” “stronghold,” “apparition,” “scathe,” “servitude of a strong spell,” and “ghastly succession” raise the register, while local names and customs—Teigue O’Neill, Moll Rial, beetling, the Munster fairies, the banshee—anchor the material in Irish regional culture. Occasional colloquial expressions, including “grand-looking,” “hurry-scurry gallop,” and “good people,” preserve traces of oral narration within the more polished literary voice.

The syntax is generally elaborate, with long, balanced sentences containing subordinate clauses, parenthetical remarks, semicolons, and cumulative descriptive phrases. These sentences often delay the principal action while establishing provenance, qualification, or atmosphere. Shorter declarations are reserved for moments of revelation or supernatural shock: “Alas! it was empty,” “There was no answer,” or “Here had been the earl himself.” This contrast gives climactic events additional force. Repetition is also used rhythmically, especially in sequences of transformation, measurement, pursuit, or escalating fear.

Structurally, the work is episodic and framed as a collection of connected legends. A long introductory section establishes Miss Anne Baily, Lough Guir, the family house, its relics, and the surrounding landscape. The succeeding titled episodes narrow the focus into discrete supernatural anecdotes: the enchanted Earl of Desmond, Moll Rial’s encounter, the banshee music, the governess’s recurring dream, and the apparition in the Earl’s Hall. These episodes are linked by place, family memory, and recurring motifs rather than by a single continuous plot. The overall movement is from genial reminiscence and picturesque description toward increasingly intimate and ominous encounters with the supernatural.

The pacing is slow and circumstantial before moments of crisis. Domestic routines, physical settings, and practical actions are described at length: the operation of beetling clothes, the measurements in the castle hall, the architecture of rooms and stairs, or the smith’s work at night. Such concrete preparation makes the uncanny intrusion feel more credible. Once an apparition appears, the pace accelerates through sequential verbs, transformations, repeated gestures, and rapidly intensifying sensory effects. Suspense often depends on a prohibition or threshold: the countess must remain silent, witnesses must not address the enchanted earl, the governess must decide whether to follow the figure, and characters repeatedly approach doors, stairs, lakeshores, or concealed chambers.

Description is highly visual and atmospheric, with strong contrasts between light and darkness, domestic comfort and ancient ruin, beauty and horror. The landscape is rendered through dark vivid verdure, moonlit roads, reflective water, wooded islands, and overhanging hills. Interiors rely on Gothic chiaroscuro: lofty halls, deep window recesses, immensely thick walls, narrow arched doors, spiral stairs, and weak shafts of light. Sound is equally important. Hammer blows, hoofbeats, cathedral-like music, cavernous echoes, wingbeats, screams, and sudden silence often announce or authenticate supernatural events. Colour imagery becomes especially intense at moments of danger, as in the red-clad phantom, the silver horseshoes, and the lake transformed into an “estuary of blood.”

The supernatural imagery is grotesque but controlled. Transformations are presented through precise physical details: sprouting feathers, hooked features, yellow hanging skin, enormous eyes, foaming mouths, quivering tongues, unnaturally extended limbs, and menacing gestures. Horror frequently emerges from the corruption of something courtly or familiar. A gracious gentleman becomes an ensnaring spirit; a family castle is both ancestral monument and enchanted prison; beautiful music signifies approaching death; and a promised treasure leaves only the impression of an absent vessel. This tension between refinement and menace is central to the story’s Gothic effect.

Characterisation is economical and often based on appearance, manners, and social role. Miss Anne Baily is established through her refined bearing, side-long glance, uplifted finger, and talent for storytelling. Legendary figures are defined by emblematic traits: the magician earl is accomplished, lordly, and dangerous; his bride is curious and imperious; Moll Rial is cheerful, practical, and shy; the governess is grave, conscientious, solitary, and susceptible to visionary experience. Direct dialogue is sparse. Communication more often occurs through gestures, looks, reported speech, warnings, and silence, which heightens the atmosphere of taboo and uncertainty.

The tone balances affection, wonder, melancholy, and restrained terror. The narrator does not simply endorse every supernatural claim, but neither does he dismiss it. Rational alternatives are introduced after the uncanny account has achieved its full effect: nervous exhaustion may explain the banshee music, fever may have produced the governess’s apparition, and the treasure may have been removed by ordinary means. These explanations remain tentative and are usually placed at the end of an episode, preserving ambiguity rather than resolving it. The result is a style in which sceptical courtesy coexists with imaginative sympathy.

A further stylistic hallmark is the treatment of objects and places as repositories of historical memory. Old glasses, silver horseshoes, flagstones, towers, portraits, and castle rooms are not merely decorative details; they connect the narrator’s present to political, familial, and legendary time. The prose repeatedly moves from tangible evidence to imaginative reconstruction. This produces a palimpsestic sense of place in which contemporary domestic life overlays aristocratic history, rebellion, enchantment, death, and folklore. Overall, the story’s style may be described as leisurely, erudite, locally grounded, ceremonially Gothic, and deliberately poised between oral legend, memoir, antiquarian record, and supernatural fiction.

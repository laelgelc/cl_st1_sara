The story uses a polished first-person memoir voice that is urbane, self-mocking and knowingly unreliable. The narrator frames the episode from a future vantage point, joking that age will transform youthful idleness and vice into respectable industry and temperance. This opening establishes the governing tone: sophisticated comic irony delivered with an air of perfect composure. The narrator presents himself as the sober observer of Lykeham’s extravagance, but his aphoristic remarks—such as his inability to sympathize with other people’s pleasures—make him almost as theatrical as his friend. His retrospective authority is therefore continually qualified by vanity, cynicism and playful exaggeration.

The diction combines educated conversational English with classical, literary and antiquarian references. Greek and Roman mythology, Latin tags, Old English etymology, Marlowe, Lemprière and Oxford customs are treated as part of the characters’ ordinary intellectual furniture. This creates a distinctly collegiate atmosphere in which learning is both genuine and absurdly misapplied. Elevated words such as “cataclysmic,” “basso profondo,” “Olympian,” “profane” and “embracements” coexist with slangy or blunt expressions such as “damned women,” “fool,” “flopped” and “GOAT-FOOT.” The contrast between high cultural language and bodily or farcical reality is a principal source of humour. Period spellings and forms such as “to-night,” “Phœbus” and “Hephæstus” reinforce the early-twentieth-century character of the prose.

Sentences are generally balanced, fluent and syntactically elaborate, with long periodic constructions used for scene-setting, reflection and comic accumulation. These are offset by clipped dialogue, abrupt questions and dry interjections. Parentheses and dashes allow the narrator to insert qualifications or deflate Lykeham’s rhetoric at exactly the right moment. Lists often build rhythmically toward a comic conclusion, as in the rapid sequence of hedges, fields, roads and mud during the pursuit. The prose also makes conspicuous use of antithesis and paradox: Lykeham’s beloved is “burning ice,” “virginally passionate,” and at once pale, cold and ardent. Such formulations parody the language of romantic idealization while preserving a faint aura of genuine enchantment.

Dialogue dominates the central portion and is shaped as a comic contest between Lykeham’s rhapsodic self-dramatization and the narrator’s sceptical interruptions. Lykeham speaks in breathless superlatives and mythological analogies, repeatedly insisting that the woman is a goddess and that he must be a god. The narrator punctures each lyrical ascent with literal-minded, indecorous or formally critical remarks: perspiration replaces passion, palm-sweat replaces ecstasy, and the supposed lover’s account is corrected as though it were a badly composed literary work. Repetition becomes a comic refrain, especially in the reversal between “passionately virginal” and “virginally passionate.” The exchanges are epigrammatic and carefully timed, resembling a drawing-room comedy embedded within a supernatural anecdote.

The descriptive mode changes markedly in the later episode. Oxford and its surroundings are first evoked with lyrical delicacy: twilight is tranquil, stars are “lighted,” peewits cry over invisible meadows, and the weir supplies a continuous auditory burden. Sound imagery is especially prominent throughout the story, from Great Tom’s deep tolling and the guitar’s shrill discord to the quieter nocturnal noises of the countryside. The language briefly permits the setting to become genuinely numinous when the moonlit woman enters the glade. Yet this romantic atmosphere is undercut by slapstick physical action—galloping, crashing through hedges, landing in mud and cursing in the undergrowth. The style repeatedly places beauty and bathos side by side.

Structurally, the narrative moves from comic memoir framing, through an extended reported amorous adventure, to apparent supernatural confirmation and a final learned punchline. Information is withheld with care. Lykeham proposes glamorous identities such as Apollo, while the story supplies clues through his looks, vanity, sexuality and the woodland setting. The revelation that his divine identity is goat-footed rather than Olympian in the heroic sense retrospectively reorganizes the whole anecdote. The capitalized final insult functions as both mythological solution and comic coup de grâce. The ending does not explain the reference at length; it assumes a classically literate reader and derives extra humour from the narrator’s delighted recognition.

Overall, the style blends Oxford reminiscence, classical burlesque, romantic fantasy and social comedy. Its central method is deflation: lofty passion becomes an anonymous theatrical flirtation, gods behave like impulsive undergraduates, and supernatural revelation culminates in ridicule. Nevertheless, the story does not eliminate wonder entirely. The moonlit encounter is narrated with enough seriousness to leave the magical event intact. The resulting style is light, erudite and irreverent, with precise comic pacing and a persistent tension between lyrical enchantment and worldly scepticism.

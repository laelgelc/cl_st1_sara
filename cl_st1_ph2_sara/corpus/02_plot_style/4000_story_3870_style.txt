The story uses a brisk, lucid late-Victorian prose style that combines scientific speculation, suspense, social comedy, and farce. Its premise is presented with enough technical detail—microscope slides, bacterial cultures, water supplies, staining, and cultivation—to lend plausibility to an exaggerated comic plot. Scientific language sits alongside vivid, theatrical rhetoric, especially when the Bacteriologist personifies cholera as a predatory force that creeps through pipes, wells, food, and public fountains. This movement from factual explanation into melodramatic fantasy is characteristic of the story’s playful treatment of science: technical knowledge creates both genuine anxiety and opportunities for vanity, misunderstanding, and absurdity.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, moving freely among the perspectives of the Bacteriologist, the Anarchist, Minnie, and the street spectators. The narrator often stays close to a character’s thoughts through free indirect discourse, allowing delusions and assumptions to appear temporarily convincing while preserving ironic distance. The Anarchist’s fantasies of notoriety, Minnie’s certainty that her husband has gone mad, and the Bacteriologist’s belated amusement are all conveyed in language coloured by their respective outlooks. This shifting focalisation builds dramatic irony: each participant interprets the chase differently, while the reader gradually sees how their misconceptions overlap.

Dialogue is frequent and strongly characterising. The Bacteriologist speaks in educated, measured, technically informed sentences, although his taste for grand rhetoric exposes his vanity. The pale visitor uses hesitant pauses and loaded questions before erupting into revolutionary grandiosity. Minnie’s speech is practical, domestic, and concerned with propriety, sharply contrasting with the supposed threat to London. The cabmen and bystanders speak in heavily marked colloquial dialect, with dropped consonants and phonetic spellings such as “keb,” “’at,” and “bloomin’.” Their lively chorus transforms an apparently apocalyptic pursuit into a public sporting spectacle. Social distinctions are therefore registered through vocabulary, pronunciation, and conversational rhythm rather than through extended explanation.

Sentence structure varies with pace and mood. The laboratory opening employs controlled exchanges, precise observations, and measured exposition. The Bacteriologist’s imagined account of an epidemic expands into a long, cumulative sentence filled with repetition, parallel clauses, and geographical movement. The repeated word “death” and the recurring participles—“creeping,” “picking,” “getting,” “lying”—give the passage the force of an incantation. Once the theft is discovered, the prose accelerates through short exclamations, abrupt transitions, energetic verbs, and rapid changes of scene. The cab chase is rendered through clattering sounds, shouted remarks, and physical mishaps. By contrast, the Anarchist’s internal monologue slows the action briefly so that his fear, resentment, and self-dramatisation can be exposed.

The descriptive style is selective and economical. Characters are sketched through a few emphatic physical details: the visitor is pale, haggard, lank-haired, nervous, and morbidly intent, while the Bacteriologist is defined more by professional manner and absent-minded enthusiasm than by appearance. Dynamic visual details dominate the chase—the lost slipper, the hatless scientist, swaying cabs, gesticulating figures, and Minnie following with clothes in hand. Sound imagery is equally important: doors slam, whips swish, hooves clatter, glass cracks, and crowds roar. These concrete sensory details give the action a cinematic clarity and intensify its comic momentum.

The prevailing tone is ironic. Potential mass death is treated seriously enough to generate suspense, yet almost every elevated gesture is undercut. The Bacteriologist’s apocalyptic eloquence is punctured by the observation that “rhetoric was his weakness.” The Anarchist imagines himself as a historic martyr, but his revolutionary act is founded on a lie and culminates in his drinking a harmless organism. His solemn pose and cry of “Vive l’Anarchie!” become ridiculous because the Bacteriologist already knows the truth. The final revelation converts terror into grotesque comedy: instead of spreading cholera, the stolen culture may turn its victim blue. The closing concern about replacing the sample and wearing a coat for social respectability completes the deflation.

Characterisation is satirical rather than psychologically elaborate. The Anarchist is driven less by a coherent political programme than by wounded vanity and a craving for recognition. His repeated fantasies about teaching the world his importance expose terrorism as a form of narcissistic theatre. The Bacteriologist is intelligent but careless, pleased by his own eloquence, and insufficiently alert to the effect of his words. Minnie supplies practical domestic reason, though her interpretation of events is comically limited. Even the crowd functions as a collective character, converting danger into entertainment without understanding it. No one possesses the full picture until the end, and this distribution of partial knowledge powers both the suspense and the humour.

Structurally, the narrative follows a compact setup, pursuit, apparent catastrophe, and reversal. The laboratory scene plants the dangerous object and establishes the visitor’s fascination. A brief interruption enables the theft. The chase enlarges the action from a private scientific room to the streets of London, while alternating viewpoints sustain momentum. The broken tube appears to fulfil the threat and leads the Anarchist to embrace martyrdom. The final disclosure then reinterprets everything that preceded it. This twist ending is carefully foreshadowed by the Bacteriologist’s showmanship and by his unexplained smile when the Anarchist announces that he has swallowed the culture.

Overall, the style depends on contrasts: microscopic organisms and metropolitan consequences, sober science and sensational rhetoric, political violence and domestic inconvenience, inner grandeur and outward absurdity. The prose is accessible, energetic, and visually immediate, but it also carries a satirical intelligence. Its distinctive effect comes from treating a speculative scientific danger with the machinery of a thriller, then allowing social observation, dramatic irony, and farcical misunderstanding to overturn the thriller at the moment of climax.

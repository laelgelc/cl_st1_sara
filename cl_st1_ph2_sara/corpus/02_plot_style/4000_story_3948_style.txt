The story adopts a polished Edwardian comic style, combining social satire, romantic farce, and the brisk mechanics of a drawing-room comedy. Its narrative voice is urbane, conversational, and knowingly intrusive. The narrator frequently steps outside the immediate action to offer mock-generalizations about human behaviour, social types, and modern life, addressing the reader directly or speaking in a genial collective voice: “we all have our greyer moments” and “You have been through it, dear reader, and so have I.” This creates intimacy while allowing the narration to treat private absurdities as universal laws.

The humour depends heavily on ironic contrast. Characters understand themselves in ways sharply at odds with the reader’s view of them. Rollo regards his courtship schedule as a brilliant scientific system, while the narration steadily reveals his vanity, dimness, and lack of practical judgment. Wilson, ostensibly the deferential and emotionally impassive servant, proves more competent in love than his master. Andrew Galloway’s solemn renunciation of romance collapses into infatuation with a chorus girl, while Rollo’s elaborate campaign unintentionally reunites her with Wilson. The plot is built around this reversal: the supposedly passive servant acts effectively, and the self-proclaimed strategist becomes merely the accidental facilitator.

Characterization is economical and caricatural, usually established through one dominant trait and then enriched by comic detail. Rollo is cheerful, wealthy, confidently foolish, and “not marred by any superfluity of brain.” Wilson is defined by professional restraint, laconic replies, and hidden emotional experience. Galloway is physically and vocally excessive, his appetite for food mirroring his renewed appetite for romance. These figures are recognizable social types rather than psychologically intricate portraits, but their repeated verbal habits give them vivid individuality.

Dialogue drives much of the story. Exchanges are rapid, cleanly punctuated, and structured around repetition, misunderstanding, and disparities in tone. Rollo’s expansive slang—“old scout,” “by George,” “see what I mean?”—meets Wilson’s minimal, ceremonious “Indeed, sir?” and “Just so, sir.” Wilson’s formal composure acts as a comic straight line against Rollo’s enthusiasm. Repeated phrases become motifs: the “schedule,” “Market Bumpstead,” and “see what I mean?” accumulate comic force and return neatly in the conclusion. The dialogue also encodes class differences without making them entirely rigid; Wilson’s verbal deference masks greater emotional intelligence and practical ability.

The prose alternates between brisk scene work and elaborate comic commentary. Sentences are generally clear and rhythmically controlled, but the narrator often expands a simple observation into an inflated analogy or mock-philosophical digression. Rollo’s sudden need for haste is compared at length to a suburban commuter discovering that his watch is slow. Galloway’s waistcoat advances “as Birnam Woods preceded the army of Macduff,” and a gloomy club resembles, by turns, a cathedral, a Turkish bath, and a prosperous dentist’s waiting room. Such similes are deliberately disproportionate: mundane situations are described with epic, literary, or scientific grandeur.

A major stylistic device is mock elevation. Courtship becomes a campaign governed by schedules, strategic advances, and “forlorn hopes”; inheritance becomes a specialized profession; commercial braces become an emblem of emotional self-control. The narrator repeatedly applies grand rhetoric to trivial appetites and social inconveniences. The extended metaphor linking Galloway’s braces to his resistance to love is especially characteristic: “the trousers of passion” are held in place by “the tough, masterful braces of self-control.” The absurdity comes from sustaining the metaphor with complete rhetorical seriousness.

The diction mixes formal narrative language with contemporary colloquial speech. The narration uses phrases such as “the present unsatisfactory state of the law,” “human benefactor,” and “feast of reason and a flow of soul,” often for parodic effect. Against this stand Rollo’s fashionable slang and clipped expressions: “topping,” “dashed tricky,” “deuced brainy,” and “cut loose.” American commercial energy enters through Galloway’s Pittsburgh background, his booming manner, and the repeated branding of “Tried and Proven.” French culinary terms, clubland references, theatre vocabulary, and details of dress and dining create a socially specific metropolitan setting.

The story’s satire is affectionate rather than savage. It mocks inherited wealth, idle young men, theatrical courtship, millionaire appetites, class conventions, and masculine theories about women, but it does not dwell on cruelty or moral condemnation. Even Rollo’s mercenary concern about his inheritance is rendered as amiable foolishness. The narrative voice cushions ridicule with warmth, allowing every major character to finish happily or at least advantageously.

Structurally, the plot is tightly organized around setup, complication, acceleration, and reversal. The opening conversation plants both competing courtship methods: Rollo’s elaborate London schedule and Wilson’s direct Market Bumpstead approach. The middle introduces the threat of Galloway as a romantic rival and forces Rollo to abandon his own system. The apparent failure at the Carlton leads to the final domestic revelation, where all earlier details converge. The conclusion reprises the initial debate and gives Wilson’s method the last word, producing a symmetrical comic closure.

The pacing varies strategically. Dialogue scenes move quickly, while narrative interludes pause to establish character, social context, or an extended analogy. Section breaks mark clear changes in phase and help the story resemble a sequence of comic set pieces. Suspense remains light but effective, resting on the reader’s anticipation of crossed intentions and incomplete information rather than on danger.

The overall tone is buoyant, elegant, and theatrically controlled. Emotional stakes such as love, disappointment, and financial insecurity are translated into wit rather than pathos. The ideal imitation would use a confident omniscient narrator, lightly archaic British diction, sharply differentiated dialogue, recurring verbal motifs, extravagant analogies, and a plot whose final reversal exposes the hero’s elaborate cleverness as inferior to ordinary human directness.

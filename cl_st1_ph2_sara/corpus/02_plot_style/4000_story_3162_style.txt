The story’s style is elevated, ornate, and unmistakably Gothic-Romantic, blending psychological confession, mystical speculation, idealized love, and luxuriant natural description. Its emotional register is consistently intense: love becomes worship, grief transforms the physical world, and possible madness is treated not merely as illness but as a heightened mode of perception. The atmosphere moves from enchanted seclusion and sensuous abundance to bereavement, decay, temptation, and supernatural absolution.

The narration is first-person, retrospective, and deliberately unreliable. The speaker begins by questioning the boundary between madness and profound insight, then divides his life into an earlier “lucid” epoch and a later period clouded by doubt. This framing gives the narrative the character of a confession while preserving ambiguity about the reality of its supernatural events. The narrator speaks directly to the reader, asking for belief, disbelief, or interpretation, and presents his memories with solemn self-consciousness. His voice is erudite, ceremonious, introspective, and prone to emotional extremity.

The diction is formal, archaic, and highly literary. Words and constructions such as “thenceforward,” “recreant,” “mine eyes,” “ye cannot,” and “reigneth and ruleth” create a scriptural or antique tone. Abstract concepts—including Love, Death, Humanity, Life, Heaven, and Spirit—are frequently capitalized and personified, giving private experience a cosmic dimension. Classical, biblical, Arabic, and Persian references lend the prose an air of scholarship and mythic universality. Proper names and repeated place names, particularly “Eleonora,” “Ermengarde,” “River of Silence,” and “Valley of the Many-Colored Grass,” carry symbolic as well as descriptive force.

The syntax favors long, elaborate, cumulative sentences built from parallel clauses, qualifications, interruptions, and rhythmic repetitions. Semicolons, dashes, exclamation marks, and parenthetical remarks regulate the narrator’s surging thought and emotional agitation. Sentences often progress through accumulation: one image is intensified by another until the description reaches a rhetorical climax. Repeated structures such as “one by one,” “ten by ten,” “more divine than all save,” and “once—but once” create incantatory rhythm. Shorter declarations appear at moments of transition or revelation, gaining force from their contrast with the otherwise expansive prose.

Description is sensuous, symbolic, and densely chromatic. The natural setting is rendered through saturated colors, perfumes, textures, sounds, and light: green grass, ruby-red asphodels, silver and ebony trees, crimson-and-gold clouds, angelic fragrances, and musical waters. Nature does not function as neutral scenery; it mirrors and participates in the lovers’ emotional lives. When love awakens, flowers bloom, birds arrive, colors deepen, and the silent river acquires music. After Eleonora’s death, the same effects reverse: flowers withdraw, colors fade, animals depart, music diminishes, and the cloud of glory recedes. This carefully patterned correspondence between landscape and feeling is central to the style.

Figurative language is abundant and hyperbolic. Eleonora and Ermengarde are repeatedly compared to seraphs, angels, and divine beings, while beauty is described as surpassing nearly everything in nature. Metaphor, personification, simile, and religious imagery elevate romantic attachment into sacred devotion. Recurring motifs include dreams, eyes, silence, perfume, breath, music, flowers, enclosed spaces, and thresholds between life and death. The secluded valley resembles both Eden and a “magic prison-house,” embodying innocence, erotic awakening, isolation, and the impossibility of remaining outside time.

The narrative structure is symmetrical and epochal rather than scene-driven. It moves from a philosophical preamble to an idyllic childhood, the awakening of love, a deathbed vow, prolonged mourning, entry into the worldly court, renewed passion, and final absolution. Changes in the landscape mark the major emotional transitions. Pacing is slow and meditative through the descriptive and elegiac passages, then becomes compressed when years pass or decisive events occur. Dialogue is extremely sparse; important speech is summarized in the narrator’s ornate idiom until the final supernatural message is quoted directly, allowing the ending to arrive as a concise oracle.

The story’s dominant tonal qualities are dreamlike, melancholic, reverent, feverish, and ultimately consolatory. It cultivates suspense not through external danger but through the anticipated consequences of a sacred vow. The ending resolves that tension through a mysterious voice rather than rational explanation, preserving the supernatural ambiguity while replacing guilt and dread with spiritual permission. Overall, the style depends on musical prose, rhetorical excess, symbolic landscapes, idealized feminine beauty, divided consciousness, and the fusion of erotic passion with religious awe.

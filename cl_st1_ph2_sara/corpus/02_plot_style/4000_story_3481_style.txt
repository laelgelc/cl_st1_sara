The story uses an omniscient, authorial third-person narrator who moves freely between external action, characters’ thoughts, and broad reflections on love, death, gender, society, and human behaviour. The narrator is not invisible: interventions such as “At first, I fancy” and “I do not know why” create the impression of an intimate storyteller who can interpret events while still admitting uncertainty. Individual consciousness is central, especially Kadambini’s unstable sense of whether she belongs among the living or the dead, but the narrative also shifts into the perspectives of servants, friends, relatives, and the wider community.

The tone combines Gothic unease, psychological pathos, social comedy, and philosophical irony. Darkness, torrential rain, the cremation ground, the extinguished lamp, frogs, crickets, lightning, and the deserted hut establish a supernatural atmosphere. Yet the apparently ghostly premise is grounded in a plausible revival from suspended animation. Much of the horror consequently arises not from an actual ghost but from belief, social reaction, and Kadambini’s internalisation of other people’s assumptions. Comic passages—particularly the frightened servants’ excuses and the marital argument between Sripati and Jogmaya—temporarily relieve the tension while sharpening the story’s satire.

The plot is linear and tightly causal, though the narrator occasionally pauses for commentary or brief retrospective explanation. It proceeds through a sequence of reversals: Kadambini is declared dead but awakens; she is physically alive but believes herself a ghost; others initially accept her as alive, then conclude that she must be dead; finally, she recognises her own life only to find that society will not recognise it. Repeated thresholds and transitions structure the narrative: night and dawn, cremation ground and village, outdoors and household, living society and imagined eternity. The ending delivers the central paradox in a concise epigram: Kadambini proves that she had been alive only by actually dying.

The prose alternates between plain narrative exposition and heightened lyrical meditation. Concrete actions are generally related clearly and economically, while emotional or metaphysical moments expand into figurative language. Consciousness disappearing is compared to ink spilling across an exercise book; memory becomes “the letters of the world’s book”; affection is imagined as “passage-money for the silent land”; living people and ghosts occupy opposite banks of “the river of death.” These metaphors make abstract states tangible and often draw on writing, travel, water, shadow, and boundaries. Nature frequently mirrors the psychological atmosphere: the monsoon rain creates enclosure and uncertainty, darkness erases ordinary distinctions, and daylight restores the social world only to make Kadambini more afraid.

Sentence structure is varied. Short declarative sentences and abrupt exchanges accelerate scenes of fear or confrontation, while longer, balanced sentences carry philosophical reflection. Parallel constructions, antithesis, and repetition are prominent: living is set against death, people against ghosts, daylight against darkness, social identity against private feeling. Repeated cries such as “I am not dead” and the recurring question “whither shall I go?” intensify emotional pressure. The prose also uses proverbial generalisations—“Men and ghosts dread each other,” for example—which lend the tale the authority and cadence of oral wisdom while often carrying ironic force.

Dialogue is direct, economical, and strongly functional. It reveals fear, domestic friction, affection, and social convention rather than individualised speech patterns in great detail. Kadambini’s language becomes increasingly solemn and existential, whereas Jogmaya’s is practical, impatient, and socially anxious. Sripati speaks with mild rationality, the cremation attendants with nervous evasiveness, and the child with simple emotional directness. The child’s requests and questions are especially powerful because their unadorned language cuts through the adults’ elaborate beliefs.

Characterisation relies less on physical description than on relationships, habits, and contrasting responses. Kadambini is defined first through dispossession—she has neither husband, son, nor natal kin—and then through her intense, unofficial maternal love for the child. Her lack of an acknowledged social claim anticipates her later lack of an acknowledged claim to life itself. Jogmaya represents domestic order and concern for reputation; Sripati represents benevolence constrained by uncertainty; Saradasankar embodies the authority of familial and ritual belief. Minor characters are sketched quickly through behaviour, often with gentle mockery.

A major stylistic feature is the blending of sympathy with satire. The narrator treats Kadambini’s suffering with grave tenderness, yet comments wryly on cowardly servants, marital pride, male partiality, and household attitudes toward mystery. Some generalisations about men and women reflect the social assumptions of the period and are delivered in a playful, aphoristic manner. This humour does not cancel the tragedy; instead, it shows how ordinary vanity, convention, and practical reasoning contribute to Kadambini’s isolation.

The cultural setting is conveyed through specific Bengali social and religious details: the zemindar household, the zenana, Brahmin servants, funeral rites, the burning ground, the invocation of Ram, the House of Yama, and the concept of maya. These elements are usually embedded in the action, though occasional parenthetical explanations clarify them for an outside readership. The English bears signs of an older translation, including formal phrasing, period vocabulary, explanatory glosses, and occasional typographical or transcriptional irregularities. The overall register is therefore literary and accessible but somewhat archaic.

At its core, the style turns a supernatural folktale pattern into a psychological and social parable. The ghost motif expresses Kadambini’s existing condition as a childless widow: emotionally necessary to the household but deprived of a secure identity and recognised claim. The narrative’s dominant devices—paradox, dramatic irony, symbolic weather, antithesis, intrusive commentary, and a final epigrammatic reversal—produce a story that is simultaneously eerie, compassionate, humorous, and devastating.

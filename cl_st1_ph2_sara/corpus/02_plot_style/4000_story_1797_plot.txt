As settlers push into Kentucky’s shared Indigenous hunting grounds, violent conflict intensifies between pioneers and Ohio tribes. Daniel Boone explores the region, survives early attacks and isolation, then establishes Boonesborough with his family and other settlers. During the Revolutionary War, he is captured while making salt and adopted by an Indigenous family at Old Chillicothe. Though treated well and valued so highly that his captors reject a British ransom, Boone secretly worries about his family. Learning of a planned attack on Boonesborough, he escapes and travels 160 miles through the wilderness, only to find that his family, believing him dead, has returned to North Carolina. He later continues hunting and fighting before moving west to Missouri.

Boone’s friend Simon Kenton, living under an assumed name after believing he killed a man in Virginia, becomes a renowned frontier scout and fighter. After stealing Indigenous horses during a reconnaissance mission, he is captured, brutally beaten, forced to run multiple gantlets, and repeatedly sentenced to be burned alive. He briefly escapes but is recaptured. Simon Girty, an old friend who recognizes him, persuades a council to spare him, but Kenton is condemned again when a defeated war party demands vengeance. The chief Logan tries to intercede, and at Sandusky a British agent finally takes custody of Kenton, preventing his execution.

Held at Detroit under relatively lenient conditions, Kenton recovers from his injuries and escapes with two fellow prisoners. After a month-long journey through the wilderness, they reach Louisville safely. Kenton continues fighting until Indigenous resistance is defeated in 1794, then settles in Ohio—eventually living on the site where he was once meant to be executed—and dies peacefully at eighty-one.

Mrs. Kearney, an ambitious and socially conscious Dublin mother, secures a contract for her daughter Kathleen to accompany four concerts organized by an Irish cultural society. She takes charge of much of the planning, spending her own money and using her knowledge and contacts to improve the events.

The concerts are poorly organized and badly attended, and the society cancels the third performance. Fearing that Kathleen will not receive the contracted eight guineas, Mrs. Kearney repeatedly demands assurances from the evasive organizers. Before the final concert, she refuses to let Kathleen perform until she is paid in full. With the audience growing restless, the organizers give her half the money and promise the remainder later, allowing the first half of the concert to proceed successfully.

At intermission, however, the committee refuses immediate full payment and threatens to void the contract if Kathleen does not continue. Mrs. Kearney stands firm, so another performer takes over the accompaniments. Public opinion among the artists and organizers turns against her, despite the society’s poor management and failure to honor the agreement. Furious, Mrs. Kearney removes Kathleen from the concert and vows to pursue the matter, while the organizers congratulate themselves for resisting her.

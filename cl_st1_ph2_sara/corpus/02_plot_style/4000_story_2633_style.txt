The story uses a highly ornate, archaic mode of fantasy prose, shaped like a legend, prose poem, or sorrowful folktale. Its elevated diction—“whence,” “whereof,” “thou,” “lieth,” “peradventure,” “whilst”—places the narrative outside ordinary historical time and gives it a scriptural, ceremonial quality. Invented place names such as Teloth, Aira, Oonai, Nithra, and Sidrak are introduced without explanation, creating the impression of an ancient and extensive mythical geography. Recurrent epithets, including “the granite city,” “the city of lutes and dancing,” and “the city of marble and beryl,” make these locations feel emblematic rather than realistically mapped.

The narration is omniscient, emotionally restrained, and largely aligned with Iranon’s romantic imagination. It relates extraordinary events in the calm, authoritative voice of oral tradition, often using formulas such as “So Iranon went,” “Then one night,” and “It is not known how long.” Characters are treated more as symbolic figures than as psychologically intricate individuals: Iranon embodies beauty, memory, and dream; Teloth represents joyless utility; Oonai represents sensual distraction; and Romnod illustrates the corruption or abandonment of youthful longing. The narrator allows irony to accumulate quietly, especially through the contrast between Iranon’s unwavering self-conception and the final revelation about his origins.

Sentences are frequently long, cumulative, and coordinated with repeated conjunctions, particularly “and,” “but,” “for,” and “so.” This paratactic structure produces a flowing, incantatory rhythm reminiscent of scripture and traditional storytelling. In descriptive or lyrical passages, clauses gather image after image—moonlight, marble, flowers, rivers, domes, groves—so that syntax itself conveys abundance and rapture. Shorter declarative sentences appear at moments of judgment or transition, giving statements such as “All here must serve, and song is folly” the force of maxims. Inversion and unusual word order—“Into the granite city of Teloth wandered the youth”—further heighten the antique, ceremonial register.

The vocabulary is sensuous and strongly colour-oriented. Purple robes, yellow or golden hair, green vines, cerulean pools, crystal fountains, tinted marble, and rose-wreathed revellers form a jewel-like visual palette. Beauty is also conveyed through scent, sound, texture, and light: myrrh, fragrant groves, soft songs, lutes, smooth stone, moonbeams, and golden flame. Favoured materials such as marble, beryl, onyx, jade, ivory, crystal, satin, and cloth-of-gold give the imagined world a decorative, dreamlike richness. By contrast, hostile reality is rendered through granite, grey domes, sluggish water, stony slopes, dirt, wine-reddened faces, and quicksand.

Repetition is central to the style. Iranon repeatedly names Aira and its defining features—the marble and beryl, the hyaline Nithra, the tiny Kra, the groves and gardens—turning memory into a refrain. The recurrence creates musical cohesion while also revealing the rigidity of his dream: his language remains unchanged even as companions age, audiences lose interest, and landscapes deteriorate. Parallel scenes reinforce this pattern. Iranon arrives in a city, sings, briefly hopes to be understood, encounters indifference or degradation, and moves on. Repeated images of stars appearing one by one, moonlight on a floor, green branches, vine crowns, and tattered purple bind the ending to the beginning.

Dialogue is formal, extended, and rhetorical rather than conversational. Characters often speak in complete, balanced arguments or lyrical monologues. Iranon’s speech relies on rhetorical questions, aphoristic contrasts, and apostrophe: toil is opposed to song, utility to beauty, death to joyless existence, and present disappointment to remembered perfection. His songs blur direct speech, recollection, and poetic description, allowing exposition to emerge through emotional vision rather than factual explanation. The inhabitants’ speech is similarly stylised, but their blunt doctrines and prohibitions contrast with Iranon’s luxuriant imagery.

The pacing is episodic and dreamlike. Individual scenes in Teloth and Oonai receive substantial attention, while many years and entire journeys are compressed into a sentence or paragraph. Time behaves symbolically: Romnod grows old and corrupted, yet Iranon remains supernaturally youthful as long as he sustains his belief in Aira. When that belief is destroyed, his true age appears instantaneously. This temporal distortion makes youth less a biological condition than a manifestation of imagination and hope.

The tonal movement is from wistful wonder through melancholy disillusionment to fatal elegy. Even moments of splendour carry foreboding, since every real city is measured against an impossible ideal. Teloth’s austerity and Oonai’s decadence are presented as different failures: one rejects beauty as useless, while the other consumes it as passing entertainment. The ending completes the story’s pattern of contrasts by setting luminous moonlight against lethal mud and visionary youth against sudden decrepitude. Its final sentence broadens Iranon’s death into a mythic cultural loss, making him representative of “youth and beauty” in an ageing world.

The governing style is therefore lyrical, antiquarian, repetitive, and symbolist. It favours atmosphere over realism, archetype over detailed characterisation, and musical cadence over colloquial naturalism. Its signature effect comes from the tension between sumptuous language and bleak narrative logic: the prose continually creates the beauty that the story’s world cannot preserve.

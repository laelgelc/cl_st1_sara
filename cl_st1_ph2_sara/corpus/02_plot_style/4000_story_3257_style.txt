The story adopts the manner of a historical anecdote or orally transmitted chronicle, combining documentary authority with the colour and momentum of a brigand legend. Its first-person narrator remains mostly outside the action, presenting himself as a collector of testimony: he admits gaps in his knowledge, refers to eyewitnesses, recalls conversations with an official, and occasionally addresses the reader directly. Phrases such as “I do not know,” “Imagine,” and “Perhaps you do not know” create an informal, conversational relationship with the audience while also giving the narrative an appearance of scrupulous honesty. Historical names, dates, places, military details and explanatory footnotes strengthen this quasi-documentary effect, though the final episodes increasingly assume the shape of folklore.

The prose is lucid, economical and strongly narrative-driven. Sentences are generally declarative and chronological, with concise statements carrying much of the dramatic force: villages are attacked, armies collapse, prisoners are transferred and escapes are executed with little ornamental delay. Longer sentences tend to appear in military descriptions, ethnographic digressions and crowd scenes, where coordinated clauses accumulate details. The diction belongs to a formal nineteenth-century register, visible in expressions such as “in virtue of treaty stipulations,” “stood in no need whatever,” and “not being bound to look upon brigands from their romantic side.” At the same time, concrete regional vocabulary—“karoutsa,” “yataghans,” “beshliks,” “dolman,” “Arnouts”—gives the setting cultural specificity. Explanations of unfamiliar objects and customs turn the narrator briefly into a travel writer or ethnographer.

The structure moves from broad historical background to increasingly concentrated personal drama. Kirdjali is first introduced through reputation and a brutal exploit, then placed within the failed Greek revolt, then observed as a refugee and prisoner, and finally restored to legendary freedom through a trickster-like escape. Several embedded narratives shape the story: the narrator recounts history, reports an official’s eyewitness account and later reproduces that same official’s account of Kirdjali’s fate. This layering of testimony makes the story feel passed from speaker to speaker, while allowing scenes to become more vivid and theatrical as they approach the climax. Digressions—especially the account of the battle and the description of the Moldavian vehicle—temporarily slow the plot but enlarge the social and historical world around the protagonist.

The tone is notably mixed. It is cool, ironic and sometimes comic when treating military incompetence, official pomposity or human credulity, yet grave and admiring before courage, endurance and proud self-possession. The major threatening insurgents with his finger, the shabby official reading solemnly through pewter spectacles and the seven guards persuaded to arm their prisoner are presented with understated ridicule. The narrator rarely announces the joke; humour arises from incongruity and deadpan phrasing. Against this comedy stand abrupt scenes of violence and pathos, such as Kantagoni driving a lance farther into his own body to reach his killer, or Kirdjali falling in tears at the official’s feet because he fears for his family. The transitions between brutality, absurdity and compassion are swift, producing an emotionally complex rather than conventionally heroic portrait.

Characterization is external, selective and compressed. Kirdjali is defined through action, reputation, physical bearing, speech and a few decisive gestures rather than psychological exposition. His “proud and calm” appearance, physical strength, eloquent defence before the Russian authorities, concern for his wife and child, and audacious manipulation of the guards complicate his initial identity as a ferocious brigand. He is neither absolved nor reduced to a villain. Instead, the narrative sustains a romantic fascination with his courage and independence while repeatedly acknowledging his violence. Secondary figures are often captured by one memorable feature or action: a purple, knob-like nose; dangling buttons; a trembling voice; a threatening finger; a greed-driven consultation. This caricatural economy keeps the cast vivid without interrupting the tale’s pace.

Dialogue is sparse but strategically important. Most historical information is summarized, whereas direct speech appears at moments when character and power are being tested. Kirdjali’s appeal to the Russians is rhetorically controlled and morally pointed; his request concerning his family reveals vulnerability; and his conversation with the Turkish guards displays calculated fraternity, patience and cunning. Repetition such as “Brothers!” helps him manufacture intimacy, while short exchanges during the digging scene create suspense. The dialogue often carries dramatic irony, since the reader can anticipate danger before the guards recognize it.

Description is visual and socially observant rather than lyrical. Clothing, weapons, vehicles, bodily features and public gatherings are rendered through sharply chosen details. The account of the refugee insurgents in worn jackets, red slippers and tilted skullcaps creates a tableau of faded martial splendour, while the prison-gate crowd supplies local colour through costume, ethnicity and gesture. Similes are infrequent and concrete—perspiration rolls “like hail”—so they stand out when used. Ellipses and exclamations mark heightened feeling, hesitation or wonder, particularly around Kirdjali’s appearance and sudden liberation.

The pacing alternates between panoramic summary and sharply staged scenes. Years of rebellion and dispersal may pass within a paragraph, but the departure from prison and the nocturnal treasure hunt unfold moment by moment. The final escape accelerates through short actions—untying, arming, stabbing, seizing pistols, flight—and ends with a brisk report of Kirdjali’s renewed extortion. The concluding exclamation, “Such is Kirdjali!”, has the force of a storyteller’s flourish: it does not offer moral resolution but seals the protagonist’s legendary identity.

Overall, the style blends historical realism, ethnographic observation, romantic outlaw narrative, black comedy and laconic irony. Its central effect comes from the tension between official history and popular legend, and between bureaucratic judgment and imaginative sympathy. The narrator recognizes Kirdjali as murderer, brigand, refugee, family man, charismatic storyteller and folk hero all at once. That refusal to simplify him gives the story its distinctive moral ambiguity and enables the closing triumph to feel simultaneously alarming, comic and exhilarating.

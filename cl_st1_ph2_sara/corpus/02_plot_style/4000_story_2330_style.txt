The story uses a sardonic, socially critical mode of Australian bush realism. Its setting is established with brisk, economical strokes—the grey plain, mulga scrub, rough shanty and nearby nameless graves—and then treated as a closed moral environment in which alcohol, money and isolation govern behaviour. Landscape description is sparse but symbolically forceful: the plain’s emptiness, the “dry stretch,” moonlight and ghostly dawn amplify the characters’ degradation and loneliness. Grey, red and moonlit imagery gives the setting a bleak, death-haunted visual palette.

The narrative voice is omniscient, conversational and knowingly intrusive. It frequently speaks as “we” or addresses the reader through phrases such as “they do say,” “all we can say,” “let us draw the curtain,” and “to put the matter decently.” This creates the manner of a communal bush yarn while allowing the narrator to pass moral judgement. The voice alternates between deadpan understatement, caustic irony and explicit social commentary. Euphemism and strategic reticence—“no matter what it was,” “better left alone,” and the metaphorical closing of the curtain—suggest indecency without describing it directly, often making the satire sharper.

The diction mixes standard literary narration with Australian vernacular and occupational slang: “shearer,” “jackeroo,” “swagman,” “cocky,” “chequemen,” “shout,” “bob,” “billy” and “git.” Quotation marks often distance the narrator from clichés, commercial pretences or locally accepted labels. Colloquial contractions, dropped endings and nonstandard constructions give dialogue a strong oral quality, while the narration itself retains a more formal, late nineteenth-century cadence. Occasional theatrical language—Alice’s “stage attitude,” her pointed finger and elongated “Go-o-o!”—makes social performances and hypocrisies visible.

Sentence structure is varied. Long, cumulative sentences catalogue people, habits and movements, often building towards an ironic reversal or dismissive punch line. Short declarative statements then deliver judgements with force: “And no one missed him”; “Stiffner was always drunk”; “HE’S AS GOOD AS YOU!” Parallel phrasing and repetition are important rhythmic devices, especially in descriptions of Danny’s false generosity and the travellers’ movement through the landscape. Dashes, semicolons, parentheses, ellipses and quoted expressions reproduce the pauses and digressions of spoken storytelling.

Characterisation is compressed, external and caricatural at first, relying on nicknames, physical features, habitual gestures and social roles. Stiffner is defined by drink and predation; his wife is a “hard-featured Amazon”; Alice is presented through cold colourlessness and calculated desirability; Danny through contradictory generosity and degradation; Yankee Jack through physical composure and moral bluntness. These broad types gradually acquire pathos. Danny’s humiliation exposes the conditional nature of companionship, while the jackeroo’s silent departure turns a minor observer into the story’s loneliest figure. Physical descriptions can be grotesque and dehumanising: drunken bodies become “heaps,” Danny becomes “an object,” and Stiffner’s beard resembles that of a frilled lizard. This objectification reflects both comic observation and the destructive effects of drink.

Dialogue is relatively sparse and therefore emphatic. It reveals the shanty’s hierarchy more than it advances plot: respect depends on possession of a cheque, and moral standards change when money runs out. Yankee Jack’s rebuke is typographically isolated and capitalised, creating the story’s sharpest dramatic rupture. Much of the surrounding speech is indirect or summarised, preserving narrative speed and reinforcing the sense that these exchanges are recurring social patterns rather than unique events.

The structure moves from panoramic exposition to a concentrated Sunday incident, then to a nocturnal aftermath and dawn departure. Early passages assemble the shanty’s cast and explain its economic machinery; the central humiliation exposes that machinery in action; the moonlit sequence slows the pace into close visual observation. Repeated gestures bind the final section together: Alice sighs over Jack, while the jackeroo later breathes with a groan before leaving. The ending avoids explicit explanation, using Danny’s half-heard question and the men’s dismissive response to imply concealed sexual or emotional distress. This withholding gives the conclusion ambiguity and bitterness.

Humour is dark, laconic and often founded on bathos. Comic descriptions of swaggering, drunkenness and pretension repeatedly shade into cruelty, exploitation and despair. The governing irony is that the shanty’s apparent fellowship is entirely transactional: men are indulged while their cheques last and discarded afterward. The style therefore combines yarn-like entertainment with moral indictment, presenting alcohol not as convivial excess but as a “selfish, barren, and useless” social curse. Its most characteristic effect comes from placing rough comedy beside sudden dignity and pathos, allowing the laughter to expose rather than soften the brutality of bush life.

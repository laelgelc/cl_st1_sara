The story adopts an urbane, polished comic style shaped by social satire, psychological observation, and steadily intensifying absurdity. Its surface manner is controlled and elegant, while its underlying vision is mischievously cruel: Oliver’s harmless preference for imagined experience develops by plausible increments into monomania and death. The comedy depends less on overt jokes than on incongruity, ironic understatement, exact physical detail, and the narrator’s composed treatment of increasingly irrational behaviour.

The narration is third-person omniscient but closely aligned with Oliver’s consciousness. It moves freely between external description, explanatory commentary, and passages coloured by his fantasies. The narrator understands Oliver more clearly than Oliver understands himself and often classifies his behaviour with mock-clinical detachment, eventually calling his condition an “agreeable insanity” and presenting him as a “harmless monomaniac.” This creates sustained dramatic irony: Oliver experiences his imaginative life as liberation and enrichment, while the reader sees his autonomy gradually disappearing. The narrator occasionally broadens into aphoristic generalisation—such as the comment about destiny depending on small accidents—which gives the tale the air of a sophisticated moral fable.

Characterisation is built through systematic contrasts. Oliver is soft-voiced, indoor-loving, fastidious, passive, aesthetic, and devoted to mediated experience; Alice is physically strong, practical, energetic, direct, and contemptuous of second-hand experience. Their preferences are embodied in domestic objects: his embroidery, shaded lamps, hot milk, and idealised rustic scenes oppose her walnuts, port, cigarettes, and turning-lathe. The gender inversion is treated in the period’s comic vocabulary, but it also functions as a broader opposition between imagination and action. Neither character is rendered through extensive introspection at first; instead, habits, gestures, possessions, food, speech, and décor reveal temperament.

Dialogue is crisp, balanced, and epigrammatic. The siblings speak in compact antitheses—doing versus reading, experience versus imagination, masculinity versus femininity—and their exchanges often resemble a drawing-room comedy. Oliver’s remarks are refined and self-conscious, with petulant qualifications and polished formulations, while Alice’s are blunt, practical, and bodily grounded. Dialogue usually performs several tasks at once: it establishes character, states the story’s governing conflict, and plants ironies that later return in altered form. Alice’s prediction that Oliver may one day play golf, for example, is fulfilled only within his delusional life.

The diction combines cultivated literary language with precise references to consumer goods, urban geography, fashions, foods, and shop departments. French phrases, literary allusions, brand-like names, and references to Conrad, Pierre Loti, Leonardo, Pygmalion, Byron, and Pater give the prose a cosmopolitan, upper-middle-class texture. Elevated words such as “peregrinations,” “scintillated,” “inclemencies,” and “perfidy” are repeatedly applied to trivial or ridiculous circumstances. This mock-heroic mismatch is central to the humour: browsing shop windows becomes adventure, commercial displays become portals to romance, and a department store becomes an entire universe.

Sentence structure is generally expansive and carefully balanced. Long periodic sentences accumulate clauses, lists, qualifications, and parenthetical asides, allowing fantasies to unfold with luxuriant momentum. Parallel constructions and catalogues convey the abundance of Oliver’s suggestibility: sights, smells, fabrics, jewels, sporting equipment, stationery, food, and feminine clothing produce rapid chains of imagined identities. Short sentences are reserved for emphasis and reversal—“She was not there,” “He was dead”—and therefore strike with exceptional force. The final sentence’s bluntness sharply punctures the ornate imaginative world that precedes it.

Imagery is strongly sensory, especially olfactory and visual. Smells of fish, lemons, medicines, coffee, perfume, and food act as triggers that transform London into distant seas, casinos, sickrooms, ballrooms, and exotic landscapes. Colour is similarly prominent: orange dresses, blue lakes, white flowers, black mourning clothes, red mouths, golden hair, and glittering gems make Oliver’s imagined world vivid and decorative. These sensuous details are often artificial or commercial—wax figures, paper blossoms, shop displays, embroidery, bottled scent—reinforcing the theme of counterfeit experience. The story repeatedly opposes the dirt, discomfort, danger, and unpredictability of reality to the clean, curated substitutes offered by art and retail.

The structure is cumulative and circular. It begins with a domestic debate about whether life should be experienced or imagined, then follows Oliver’s discovery of a theory that validates his avoidance of reality. His imagination first enriches books and individual shop windows, then produces original fantasies, then fixes obsessively on the mannequin, and finally becomes wholly governed by external stimuli. The orange-dressed figure provides a miniature rise and fall: romantic attachment temporarily makes him healthier and more active, but the revelation that his beloved is merely a reproducible commercial display wounds him back into lighter fantasies. The department store then concentrates the whole external world beneath one roof, accelerating his condition until the funeral department supplies the fatal suggestion.

Pacing follows this psychological escalation. Early scenes are leisurely and domestic, with extended description and conversational sparring. The middle broadens into episodic urban wandering, using montage-like transitions as one sensory cue abruptly replaces another. In the department-store section, the catalogues become denser and the imagined activities more rapid, suggesting both exhilaration and loss of control. The ending narrows suddenly to a single black door, a coffin, an undertaker, and a terse collapse. This compression gives the conclusion the inevitability and neatness of a macabre punchline.

The tonal range blends affectionate amusement, satire, fantasy, and morbidity. Oliver is ridiculous, but the prose also conveys the genuine pleasure and creative richness of his inner life. His aversion to reality is mocked, yet the commercial city’s ability to stimulate endless identities is presented with considerable imaginative delight. The story therefore sustains an ambivalence between imagination as art and imagination as pathology. Its governing irony is that Oliver seeks experiences without consequences, only to discover that imagined experience can produce the most absolute consequence of all. Overall, the style is best described as elegant Edwardian comic fantasy: socially observant, verbally ornate, sensuously detailed, structurally controlled, and capped by a darkly economical ending.

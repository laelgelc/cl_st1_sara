The story’s style is a sustained comic parody of sentimental nature-writing. It adopts the elevated language, emotional intensity, and moral earnestness of a tragedy while applying them to the ordinary growth and harvesting of a tomato. This disproportion between subject and treatment creates the central humour: horticultural processes become “warnings of destiny,” ripening becomes a passage into perilous maturity, and preparation for a salad becomes a violent, sorrowful death. The satire is affectionate but pointed, exposing how easily imaginative sympathy can turn into extravagant anthropomorphism.

The narrative is framed by a dry, urbane first-person introduction concerning Uncle Peter and a literary controversy. This opening establishes an ironic distance from the inset tale. Peter’s succession of lofty justifications for following the dispute—philosophical, philological, patriotic, and finally personal—gently mocks intellectual self-importance. His proposed role as an undetectable “peace-maker” further signals that the ensuing story is not neutral mediation but a mischievous intervention. The abrupt manuscript note at the end completes the frame and prevents the parody from resolving into a genuine sermon.

Within the inset story, the narrator assumes the voice of an omniscient moral commentator. The narration repeatedly treats conjecture as certainty, insisting that an “imaginative observer” can infer the tomato’s lessons, feelings, and family relationships from its behaviour. Rhetorical questions such as “Who can trace the subtle avenues” and “Who could think that such a creature could be cruel?” imitate the persuasive gestures of earnest moral prose while highlighting the absurdity of its claims. Direct addresses to the reader create a mock-confidential, didactic tone, inviting sympathy and then exaggerating that sympathy until it becomes comic.

The diction mixes lyrical, biblical, philosophical, and melodramatic registers. Phrases such as “a late-comer at the feast of life,” “the cruel hand of fate,” “the unknown,” and “the perils of life” lend grandeur to mundane events. Colour imagery is especially prominent: Tom passes from green through pale intermediate shades to a “bright crimson hue,” becoming “glowing red as a ruby.” The language of family affection—“mother-love,” “mother-heart,” “my Tommykin”—intensifies the sentimentality, while violent verbs such as “torn,” “snatched,” “tore,” and “dropped” redescribe gardening and eating as acts of remorseless brutality.

The prose generally uses long, balanced, carefully punctuated sentences, often built through accumulation and parallelism. Lists of motives, plants, dangers, and foods give the writing an expansive, essayistic rhythm. Shorter sentences and exclamations are reserved for comic emphasis: “Simple process!”; “A golden afternoon—the last!”; “Not a bit of it.” Repetition also heightens mock pathos, particularly in the girl’s approach through the garden and in the sequence of incredulous questions about her apparent innocence. Dashes, italics, quotation marks, and isolated signal words such as “Droop!” and “Snuggle!” provide theatrical emphasis.

Figurative language is abundant and deliberately overworked. The garden becomes a miniature society filled with “leguminous comrades,” aspirations, dreams, philosophy, schemes, and resentments. Much of the wordplay depends on plant names and characteristics: beans aspire to climb, corn possesses “high spirits,” sage embodies philosophy, eggplants hatch schemes, peppers have hot feelings, and raspberries “rasp” their neighbours. Literary allusion is likewise comic; the Shakespearean phrase “sicklied o’er with a pale cast of thought” is repurposed to describe a tomato changing colour. Such devices make the narrator sound learned and poetic while repeatedly drawing attention to the artificiality of the personification.

Dialogue is simple, affectionate, and instructional. Tom’s mother speaks like an idealised Victorian parent, combining tenderness with maxims about obedience, modesty, patience, and self-development. Her lessons translate natural processes into moral discipline: Tom must grow before he reddens, remain true to himself, endure rain, and obey signals of danger. The dialogue therefore parodies both animal fables and improving literature for children. Its repetitions and diminutives give it a nursery-tale quality that contrasts sharply with the threatened “crimson tragedy.”

Structurally, the inset narrative follows the arc of a miniature tragic biography: auspicious birth, sheltered childhood, education, maturation, warnings of danger, narrow escapes, a final moment of happiness, catastrophe, and moral reflection. Foreshadowing is overt from the beginning, so suspense comes not from whether Tom will die but from how the ordinary event will be inflated into tragedy. The three warning signs create a folktale pattern, but the third remains untaught, making Tom’s destruction feel both inevitable and mock-tragic. The final revelation that he is wanted for tomato salad punctures the elevated mood with domestic bathos.

The ending broadens the satire by cataloguing the meal’s other “victims”—wheat, fish, grouse, lettuce—and applying the same rhetoric of innocence and suffering to all food. This reductio ad absurdum exposes the difficulty of maintaining absolute sentimental identification with every living thing. The unfinished moral is crucial to the tone: it imitates didactic fables but withholds the promised lesson, leaving the satire suspended between compassion and ridicule. Overall, the style is polished, whimsical, rhetorically elaborate, and deadpan, using mock solemnity, anthropomorphism, bathos, verbal wit, and an intrusive moralising narrator to parody excessively sentimental representations of nature.

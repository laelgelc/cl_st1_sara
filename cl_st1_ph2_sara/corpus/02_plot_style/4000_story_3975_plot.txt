Owen Bentley, a poorly paid bank clerk, asks permission to marry Audrey Sheppherd, but her father refuses because Owen has neither money nor prospects and forbids them to meet. Audrey continues their relationship by telephone and encourages Owen to seek a fortune. She recalls that years earlier he had dramatized Edith Butler’s sentimental novel and submitted the script without receiving a reply.

While holidaying at a Shropshire farm, Owen meets the irascible writer Prosser, who angrily throws a loaf at him for disturbing his work. The farmer’s fortune-telling mother predicts that Prosser will soon marry and that Owen will acquire “pots of money.” Back in London, Owen’s lovesick absent-mindedness leads him to send unstamped letters to Prosser’s fiancée. Prosser complains to the bank, but the two recognize each other, reconcile, and arrange to have supper after Owen attends the newly staged adaptation of the novel.

At the theatre, Owen realizes that the production contains a character and dialogue he invented, proving that it is based on his lost manuscript. Over supper he tells Prosser that Edith Butler stole his work, only for Prosser to reveal that “Edith Butler” is his pseudonym. Prosser explains that Owen’s covering letter blew away when the manuscript arrived, leaving him unable to identify its author. Owen correctly names the hotel from which he sent it, convincing Prosser of his claim.

Owen is initially crushed to learn that the London production has failed, but Prosser then reveals that the play was already a major success in America, with long runs and touring companies. Owen is therefore entitled to substantial earnings, fulfilling the fortune-teller’s prediction and removing the financial obstacle to his marriage.

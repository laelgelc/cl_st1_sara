The story uses a formal, late-nineteenth-century realist style that develops into psychological and gothic horror. It begins with a composed social tableau—the villa, the drawing room, cigars and liqueur glasses—before moving into accounts of war, mob violence, starvation and cannibalism. This contrast between cultivated society and human brutality is central to its effect: atrocity is presented not as something supernatural, but as a possibility latent within ordinary people when authority, security and social order collapse.

The narrative is framed as an after-dinner conversation, with an external narrator briefly establishing the scene before yielding almost entirely to General de G----’s first-person testimony. The General speaks as both witness and theorist. His opening distinction between what is “terrible” and what is “horrible” supplies the story with an explicit thesis: horror requires not merely suffering or death, but mystery, abnormality and a destabilising encounter with the inexplicable. The two embedded narratives then function as evidence for this definition, while the final return to the framing voice gives the whole piece the quality of a disturbing anecdote preserved by listeners.

The prose is controlled, declarative and observational. It often proceeds through accumulations of concrete details: snow, stiffened limbs, bloodied footprints, matchboxes, clothing, pocket contents, springs, camels and measured distances between marchers. Such documentary specificity lends credibility to extreme events and prevents the horror from becoming melodramatically abstract. Lists and catalogues are especially important. The inventory taken from the murdered stranger’s pockets delays the revelation of her sex, while the procedural account of the desert retreat turns cannibalism into a grim system of survival. Bureaucratic, military and geographical language further strengthens the reportorial tone.

Sentence structure alternates between short statements that establish facts and long, cumulative sentences that convey exhaustion, panic or oppressive scale. Repetition creates rhythm and intensification: soldiers “fired,” “reloaded,” and “fired again”; the desert survivors repeatedly separate, kill, divide and resume marching. Parallel phrasing and anaphora—particularly repeated references to snow, darkness, cold, hunger and distance—produce a sense of inescapability. The frequent use of semicolons and coordinated clauses allows scenes to unfold as continuous chains of action, while abrupt dialogue and exclamations such as “To the wall!” and “The Prussians!” reproduce the contagious violence of the crowd.

Imagery is stark and physical. Light and darkness provide a recurring visual structure, from cigars shining “like eyes” to matches briefly illuminating a mutilated body. Cold dominates the first account and heat the second, making the episodes elemental opposites: one landscape freezes human mechanisms into immobility, while the other burns away restraint through thirst and hunger. Bodies are repeatedly dehumanised through mechanical, animal and material metaphors. Men become an “overworked human mechanism,” a corpse becomes a “bundle,” “mass” or “pulp of flesh,” and soldiers act with the energy of “brutes.” These metaphors show both bodily degradation and the erosion of moral identity.

The tone remains restrained even at moments of extreme violence. Emotional commentary is sparing, so the General’s eventual admission that he cried carries unusual weight. Irony also sharpens the horror. The soldiers murder a supposed spy, panic at their own gunfire, and flee from a threat they have invented; the victim may have been a mother searching for her son. Similarly, the desert survivors remain “irreconcilable allies,” preserving a grotesque form of cooperation by sharing the bodies of those they kill. Religious imagery is used bitterly when the firing squad circles the corpse as mourners might sprinkle holy water before a coffin, converting a ritual of reverence into collective desecration.

Characterisation is economical and functional. The General is defined through rank, experience and reflective authority, yet he is not presented as fully in control: he cannot restrain the mob, explain the woman’s presence or master his own emotional response. Most other figures appear collectively—the routed army, the gendarmes, the survivors—because the story is less interested in individual psychology than in group behaviour under extreme pressure. Anonymous or partially named victims reinforce the atmosphere of uncertainty and moral dislocation.

The pacing depends on delayed revelation and escalation. The opening philosophical discussion creates anticipation; the first episode moves from military misery to suspicion, mob execution and the discovery that the “spy” was a woman. The second broadens the scale from betrayal and poisoning to organised cannibalism and repeated murder. Crucial moments are slowed through methodical description, particularly the striking of successive matches and the survivors’ regulated movement across the desert. The story ends with a rhetorical question rather than consolation or resolution, returning the reader to the General’s original definition and leaving horror as an intellectual and emotional problem.

Overall, the style combines social realism, military reportage, oral storytelling and philosophical horror. Its most distinctive qualities are formal diction, nested narration, factual precision, cumulative syntax, restrained emotional delivery, elemental landscapes and revelations built through procedural detail. The horror arises from ambiguity, helplessness and the collapse of civilisation, not from the supernatural.

The story’s style blends urbane romantic comedy, psychological fantasy, and pagan supernaturalism. It begins in a socially observant, lightly ironic mode, treating an insignificant accident—the rolling olive—with elaborate attention. This disproportionate focus turns the object into a comic emblem, a romantic token, and eventually a magical summons. Mundane hotel life on the Italian Riviera is rendered with dry wit: strikes, exchange rates, unwanted purchases, guidebook enthusiasm, and the protagonist’s dutiful reading of Freud all establish a recognisably modern, post-war world whose habits and explanations will be displaced by something older, freer, and irrational.

The narration is third-person and closely aligned with the unnamed male protagonist. It moves easily between external description, free indirect thought, and direct access to his desires and bewilderment. The narrator remains knowing and occasionally teasing, particularly when exposing the hero’s loneliness, suggestibility, and instinct to romanticise coincidence. Brief authorial intrusions—such as “Heaven alone knows why”—strengthen the conversational, amused tone. At the same time, strategic information is withheld: the girl’s nature, the reality of the nocturnal adventure, and the exact meaning of being “half-and-half” or “altogether” remain suggestive rather than fully explained.

The prose is sensuous, rhythmic, and image-rich. Colour, texture, movement, scent, and sound recur insistently: shiny parquet, silvery leaves, rose light, purple sea, fragrant grass, singing sailors, whispering wind, and the distant pipe. The olive is described through repeated tactile and visual epithets—“moist,” “shiny,” “lob-sided,” “juicy,” “succulent”—which make it increasingly vivid and animate. Repetition gives the object talismanic force, while verbs such as “rolled,” “plopped,” “hesitating,” and “settled” personify it as an agent with intentions of its own. Later, this animation spreads to the landscape: the sky descends, olive-tree spirits dance, dawn arrives as a presence, and hills and ground respond ecstatically to the returning god.

Sentence structure varies with mood. The realistic opening favours balanced, explanatory sentences, parenthetical qualifications, semicolons, and comic accumulations. Lists convey the hero’s repetitive habits and the clutter of ordinary life. During the dream or vision, the prose becomes faster and more lyrical, with exclamations, imperatives, fragments, parallel clauses, and rapidly chained actions. Dashes and ellipses register interruption, breathlessness, hesitation, and altered consciousness. Long flowing sentences accompany running, dancing, kissing, and the dissolution of rational identity, while short declarations—“It was a message,” “The Dawn is here”—give supernatural moments ritual emphasis.

Dialogue is economical but strongly characterising. The hotel staff and residents speak in conventional, explanatory formulas that embody the tedious social world the protagonist wishes to escape. The girl’s speech is playful, peremptory, elliptical, and riddling. She issues commands, anticipates his thoughts, and repeatedly tests whether he understands how to “play.” Her recurring phrases—“half-and-half,” “altogether,” and references to the olive as a “message”—function like private incantations. The lovers’ exchanges often slide from flirtation into mythic recognition, allowing ordinary courtship language and supernatural initiation to coexist.

The dominant tonal movement is from comic self-consciousness to ecstatic abandon, with a brief but potent undercurrent of terror. The girl is at once an English daughter, a mischievous romantic heroine, and a nymph-like intermediary. Her beauty is repeatedly described as bright, soft, swift, and elusive, yet moments of remoteness make her uncanny. Likewise, the pagan god is “benign yet terrible”: joy and panic are inseparable before him. This mixture of attraction and dread gives the fantasy more force than a simple pastoral idyll. The supernatural is not presented as gloomy or morbid, but as an eruption of overwhelming vitality.

The setting is integral to the style. The omnipresent olive groves first become a comic obsession, then a threshold into an archaic Mediterranean world. Local topography, dawn light, the sea, mimosa, abandoned villages, and earthquake ruins are gradually mythologised. Bussana’s ruined church becomes the central symbolic site, where institutional Christianity is displaced by a shaggy pagan divinity associated with music, sexuality, animal life, laughter, and seismic power. The treatment is deliberately provocative: formal religion appears lifeless and unprepared, while pagan worship is embodied, communal, dangerous, and joyous.

Structurally, the story uses circularity and doubling. It opens with the olive crossing the dining-room floor and ends with the heroine confirming that she understood the hero through the way he picked it up. Gestures and phrases from the vision recur in waking life: she appraises his eyes and ears, calls him “half-and-half,” and tweaks his ears. These repetitions blur the distinction between dream, prophecy, shared memory, and supernatural encounter. The apparent awakening does not cancel the fantasy; instead, the final courtship retroactively validates it while preserving ambiguity.

Thematically, the prose opposes repression to instinct, isolation to communion, and modern self-consciousness to ancient play. Freud is invoked humorously as an inadequate written theory of “suppressed desires,” whereas the olive initiates an immediate, lived release of those desires. The protagonist’s transformation is expressed less as intellectual discovery than as recovered memory: he has been ready “for five thousand years.” Falling in love thus becomes both a social event and a return to an older, pre-rational identity. The overall signature is playful yet mystical, richly sensuous, symbolically repetitive, and poised between sophisticated irony and wholehearted romantic enchantment.

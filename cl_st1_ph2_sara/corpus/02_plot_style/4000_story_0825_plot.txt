Eleanor Gail and Charles Faraday marry with the unconventional understanding that each will preserve complete personal freedom, relying on mutual love and trust rather than traditional marital expectations. To further Eleanor’s education, Charles leaves her in Paris to master French while he returns to his university post in Plymdale. They maintain their intimacy through frequent letters, although Eleanor briefly becomes cold and distressed after Charles innocently writes about his attraction to the youthful beauty of Kitty Beaton.

When Charles reunites with Eleanor in Paris, she behaves secretively around a handsome male visitor and later spends an afternoon with him. Seeing them cheerfully riding together, Charles becomes intensely jealous, despite his professed belief in marital liberty. He soon discovers that the man is an artist whom Eleanor secretly commissioned to paint her portrait as a surprise for him.

Reassured, the couple decide that Eleanor has learned enough French and should return home with Charles. Eleanor then admits that her earlier coldness arose from jealousy over Kitty, confessing that some emotions cannot be overcome by philosophy. Charles concludes affectionately that she is “only a woman,” failing to recognize that his own jealousy over the portrait has exposed the same gap between his ideals and his feelings.

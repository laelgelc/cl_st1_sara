The story is a brisk comic tale told by an assured third-person omniscient narrator. The narrative voice remains outwardly formal and composed while describing foolish, undignified behavior, creating a strong contrast between elevated narration and low comedy. It moves freely between external action, character background, social observation, and Mr. Braddy’s thoughts, but maintains enough ironic distance to expose the characters’ self-deceptions. Mock-heroic phrasing repeatedly enlarges trivial events into epic ones: deciding to get drunk becomes a Rubicon-like act, entering a Turkish bath opens “the land of adventure and achievement,” and overcoming embarrassment is treated as a battle with the “demon Timidity.”

The dominant tone is genial, satirical, and farcical rather than cruel. The characters are caricatured through exaggerated physical descriptions, habitual phrases, and comic analogies. Mr. Braddy is a vast, timid, placid man whose “moon face” resembles a giant Kewpie, while Mr. Lum is thin and “waspy,” concealed “in ambush behind a fierce pair of mustachios.” Such descriptions are vivid, economical, and deliberately incongruous. Bodies are repeatedly rendered as objects, landscapes, or substances: bathers become “pink mountains,” Braddy moves like a “flabby pink bear,” and after his bath he feels like “a cauliflower cooled after boiling.” The humor is visual and kinetic, with particular emphasis on bulk, awkward movement, collisions, repetition, and bodily discomfort.

The diction combines polished narrative prose with period American slang, commercial language, and urban vernacular. The narrator uses words such as “adipose,” “diffidence,” “bellicosely,” and “avoirdupois,” often for comic inflation, while the characters speak in contractions, malapropisms, dropped consonants, and phonetic spellings: “Wadda pity,” “N’Yawk,” “thoity-six,” and “Didja enjoy the trip?” Speech strongly marks class, region, intoxication, and personality. Braddy’s recurring “You don’t tell me!” initially establishes his passivity, while Lum’s slogans and imperatives—“Get busy! Act! Do something!”—display his dependence on popular self-help rhetoric. As Braddy changes, his speech becomes longer, firmer, and more theatrical, culminating in the confident bluff delivered to his manager.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization and comic rhythm. Exchanges are fast, repetitive, and built around misunderstanding, overstatement, and verbal catchphrases. Drunken speech is represented through clipped pronunciation rather than lengthy explanation, allowing sound alone to signal the altered state. Repeated lines acquire structural importance: “I can and I will,” “fresh as a li’l’ daisy,” and “A man can do what he is a-mind to” pass from motivational cliché into action, then become ironic confirmations of Braddy’s success. The story treats clichés as both ridiculous and unexpectedly effective.

Sentence style varies according to function. Expository passages favor long, balanced sentences containing parenthetical asides, qualifications, and comic digressions. Action scenes use shorter clauses, fragments, lists, exclamations, and sound effects. The steam-room sequence accelerates through shouted protests—“Lay off me, guy!” and “Ouch, me ankle!”—while repeated impacts and returns create the rhythm of slapstick. Onomatopoeia is conspicuous: “Pat, pat, pat,” “Wop, wop, wop,” and “Zizzzzzz!” turn physical action into a kind of comic percussion. Dashes, italics, quotation marks, and abrupt paragraph breaks further control timing and emphasize punch lines.

The plot follows a clean comic escalation. It begins with the discovery of a supposedly unique bottle, expands into a debate about courage and self-confidence, sends Braddy on a drunken nighttime adventure, subjects him to a prolonged Turkish-bath ordeal, and concludes with his successful demand for a doubled salary. Each stage literalizes something mentioned earlier: his fear of drink, his lifelong wish to take a Turkish bath, and his inability to request a raise. This careful setup-and-payoff construction gives the farce cohesion. The bath sequence forms the comic centerpiece, using spatial confusion and repeated failed attempts to cross the steam room as a cumulative running gag.

Pacing is leisurely during the cellar conversation, where background and personality are established, then increasingly rapid once the men drink. The Broadway episode is episodic and absurd, while the Turkish bath slows into an extended set piece whose repetitions deliberately prolong Braddy’s ordeal. The final workplace scene becomes tauter and more controlled, mirroring his new decisiveness. Suspense is mild but effective: the reader waits to discover whether his newfound confidence will end in humiliation or reward. The ending lands as a concise reversal, validating behavior that the story has spent much of its length making ridiculous.

The social setting is distinctly early twentieth-century metropolitan and lower-middle-class. Long Island City cottages, the subway, Broadway, an Automat, Turkish baths, department-store hierarchies, salary envelopes, cigars, and sales departments create a dense urban-commercial world. References to Prohibition, wartime sacrifice, self-improvement pamphlets, industrial magnates, and the prestige of managerial salaries locate the comedy within a culture fascinated by success, masculine confidence, and upward mobility. Brand-like specifics, prices, wages, and consumer objects lend the story material texture and provide social measurement: status is expressed through the difference between a six-cent and an eleven-cent cigar, a ten-cent breakfast and a club breakfast, or thirty dollars and sixty dollars a week.

The satire focuses especially on self-help ideology and performative masculinity. Lum repeats formulas about nerve, decisiveness, and famous businessmen without recognizing his own vulnerabilities, particularly his fear of his wife. Braddy, meanwhile, adopts these formulas almost mechanically. The irony is that shallow motivational language actually works for him—not because it reveals a profound truth, but because it supplies a script for assertive behavior. His transformation is both comic and genuine. The story therefore balances mockery with optimism: courage may be theatrical, borrowed, alcohol-assisted, and founded on bluff, yet acting courageous can still produce material results.

Characterization is built through sharp contrasts. Lum presents himself as bold and successful but quickly retreats when domestic consequences loom. Braddy begins as timid, repetitive, and deferential, yet proves capable of persistence once he accepts a new identity. His progress is dramatized through posture and movement: he shifts from waddling, shuffling, and listening to striding, interrupting, smoking defiantly, and making others wait. Angelica and Mr. Berger remain largely offstage or functional, representing the domestic and professional authorities he fears. Minor figures—the bath clerk, Al, Gawge, subway guard, newsboy, salesmen, and bathers—are rapidly sketched through a single visual trait, vocal mannerism, or comic role.

The story’s imagery is concrete, cartoonlike, and often grotesquely affectionate. Similes draw on toys, animals, food, machinery, commerce, and popular advertising. Metaphors turn suburban houses into “municipal measles,” a corridor into a “rabbit warren,” and the steam bath into hell. Elevated cultural allusions—to Caesar, biblical promises, “master of his fate,” and heroic struggle—are placed beside frankfurters, underwear advertisements, cigar coupons, and rug sales. This collision of high and low registers is one of the story’s defining stylistic signatures.

Overall, the style is that of an urban comic farce with a satirical, mock-heroic narrator; strongly individualized phonetic dialogue; exaggerated physical characterization; cumulative slapstick; and a tightly engineered sequence of setups and payoffs. Its central effect comes from treating an ordinary timid salesman’s drunken bath and salary negotiation as a grand campaign of personal liberation, while preserving enough warmth to let his absurd triumph feel deserved.

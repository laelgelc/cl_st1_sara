The story uses a brisk, polished comic style characteristic of early twentieth-century British children’s fiction. Its third-person narrator stays close to William’s perspective while retaining an amused adult distance. William’s inconveniences are rendered as epic ordeals: taking out a baby becomes “the horrible thing demanded of him,” his family closes around him like the Fates, and his embarrassment is compared with martyrdom and Medusa’s horror. This mock-heroic inflation is central to the humour, elevating ordinary domestic events into melodrama while allowing the narrator to expose the gulf between William’s romantic self-image and the mundane reality of his actions.

The tone is affectionate, ironic, and deadpan rather than moralising. The narrator treats childish schemes with apparent seriousness, then punctures them through precise qualification or understatement. “Cooked,” for example, is declared “too euphemistic a term” for sausages merely blackened over smoke. Similar effects come from formal descriptions of absurd behaviour, such as the baby “repenting of its condescension” and spitting out cake. The narration frequently borrows elevated vocabulary—“culinary operations,” “righteous indignation,” “unassailable,” “judicially”—to describe grubby, incompetent play. This contrast between dignified language and physical chaos gives the prose much of its comic energy.

The diction alternates sharply between urbane narration and colloquial dialogue. The narrative voice is grammatically controlled, literate, and allusive, invoking the Fates, Medusa, martyrs, and the Goddess of Danger. The boys speak in clipped, non-standard forms that reproduce pronunciation and social idiom: “goin’,” “holdin’,” “p’raps,” “wotcher,” “s’pose,” and “Garn.” Their speech is full of challenges, repetitions, rhetorical questions, and improvised logic. William’s repeated “I’m simply statin’ a fact,” copied from his brother, becomes a comic verbal refrain. Its growing irrelevance shows how children appropriate adult phrases for authority without fully understanding their use.

Sentence structure is varied but generally clear and propulsive. Longer narrative sentences establish circumstances, list preparations, or build complications, while short dialogue exchanges accelerate arguments and sharpen punchlines. Parentheses supply dry corrections and explanatory asides, as in the provenance of the stolen cake or the description of the sausages. Dashes, italics, exclamations, elongated syllables, and phonetic spellings convey performance and emotion, especially in the mother’s baby talk and the infant’s babbling. Repetition is heavily used for comic rhythm: “Me? Me? Me take a baby out in a pram?” and “Dad—dad—dad—dad—dad.” The dialogue often proceeds through echoed phrases, with one child turning another’s words against him.

Characterisation is economical and behaviour-based. William is proud, theatrical, resourceful, resentful of adult control, and intensely concerned with his standing among the Outlaws. His imagination instantly converts forced childcare into a kidnapping, but his grand concept collapses under practical questions about ransom notes and returning the baby. The other boys are distinguished through rivalry and argumentative habits rather than detailed physical description: Henry competes for leadership, Ginger supplies blunt logic, and Douglas enjoys the baby’s supposed recognition of him as “Dad.” Adults remain mostly at the edges and are filtered through William’s frustration. His mother’s gentle emotional pressure and Robert’s “crushing superiority of eighteen” are captured through their characteristic speech.

The comedy is cumulative and increasingly physical. The plot begins with a simple obstruction to William’s plans, then escalates through a chain of plausible but absurd consequences: the baby is redefined as a kidnap victim, ruins the food, covers itself in paraffin and potato, is washed with licorice water, encounters a stolen cow, loses its hat, and returns in a battered pram. Each solution creates a worse problem. Objects function as recurring comic props—the cake, oil, handkerchief, hat, dog biscuit, cow, and pram—and their progressive destruction gives the story structural cohesion. The baby itself acts as an agent of chaos while remaining serenely indifferent to the boys’ schemes.

The setting is a lightly sketched English domestic and rural world of half-holidays, larders, servants, country lanes, fields, stiles, farms, and disused barns. Social detail is conveyed incidentally rather than descriptively. The respectable household contrasts with the barn, where the boys try to create a private realm of adventure. Yet their outlawry consists largely of pilfered food, badly lit fires, trespassing, and borrowed ideas from adventure books. This discrepancy gently parodies both romantic outlaw narratives and children’s attempts to imitate them.

The story’s structure is linear and tightly controlled. It opens with William preparing for freedom, interrupts that plan with the baby, briefly restores his desired adventure by converting childcare into outlawry, and then allows disorder to accumulate until he must return home. Recurring phrases and motifs bind the episodes together, particularly disputes over ownership and responsibility: “You brought it,” “It’s your kidnap,” and “It’s Henry’s cow.” No one willingly accepts consequences, although each insists on assigning them to someone else. The final line delivers a delayed visual punchline by separating the mother’s gratitude from her sight of the transformed baby. Its emphatic capitalisation breaks the otherwise composed narrative surface and closes the story at the exact moment before adult reaction, leaving the anticipated explosion to the reader’s imagination.

Overall, the style combines close child-centred perspective, sophisticated narrative irony, energetic colloquial dialogue, mock-heroic allusion, escalating farce, and a sharply timed final reversal. Its humour is affectionate because William’s pretensions are exposed without malice: the prose recognises the ingenuity and intensity of childhood imagination even as it shows that imagination colliding disastrously with babies, cows, food, and adult expectations.

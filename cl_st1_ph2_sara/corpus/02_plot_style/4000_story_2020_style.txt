The story adopts the manner of an antiquarian ghost tale: formally narrated, historically situated, and grounded in precise architectural, geographical, and scholarly detail before the supernatural is allowed to emerge. Its horror is restrained rather than sensational. Ordinary domestic life, academic learning, and the apparent security of an English country house create a credible surface beneath which occult violence gradually becomes visible. The prevailing tone is sober, urbane, and faintly ironic, with melancholy developing into unease and finally into macabre revelation.

The narrator is an educated first-person commentator who remains largely outside the action. Phrases such as “as far as I can ascertain” give the account the air of a reconstructed historical case, while occasional personal observations lend authority to descriptions of extraordinary events. The narrator generally maintains emotional distance, preferring qualifications, documentary evidence, and measured judgments to overt expressions of fear. This composure makes the supernatural details more disturbing: ghosts and occult murders are related in much the same exact manner as architectural alterations, dates, books, or coroner’s findings.

The prose is formal, periodic, and syntactically elaborate. Long sentences accumulate subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, lists, and carefully balanced observations. Description often proceeds from broad setting to increasingly specific detail, as in the survey of the Hall and its grounds. Semicolons and dashes regulate the flow of information, while occasional short statements provide emphasis at moments of recognition or danger. The vocabulary is elevated and historically inflected, combining country-house terminology, theological and classical references, legalistic phrasing, and antiquarian scholarship. Latin expressions and references to obscure ancient authorities help establish intellectual credibility while also characterising learning as something potentially sterile, obsessive, and morally corrupt.

Dialogue supplies a marked contrast to the narrator’s polished prose. Mr Abney speaks with nervous repetitions, abrupt self-corrections, false joviality, and conspicuous questions about Stephen’s age and birthday. These verbal irregularities plant suspicion before his intentions are known. Mrs Bunch and Mr Parkes speak in strongly rendered regional and lower-class idiom, with nonstandard grammar, dropped consonants, phonetic spellings, and proverbial turns of phrase. Their speech provides warmth and mild comedy, but it also becomes a vehicle for folklore, household memory, and intuitions that educated characters might dismiss. The distinction between polished narration and colloquial dialogue reinforces the story’s social and historical setting.

Characterisation is economical and indirect. Stephen is defined by curiosity, courage, and a child’s trust in adults; Mrs Bunch by kindness, practical knowledge, and imperfect understanding; Mr Abney by scholarly prestige, reclusiveness, and a hospitality that gradually appears calculated. The repeated epithet “the tall, the thin, the austere” gives Abney a slightly theatrical outline, while his excessive interest in dates and ages functions as an early clue. His ultimate depravity is conveyed not through confession in dialogue but through his own clinical written memorandum, whose euphemisms expose his moral monstrosity more effectively than emotional language would.

The story’s pacing is deliberately incremental. A leisurely opening establishes place, period, and social circumstances. Apparently incidental oddities then accumulate: missing children, a dream of a corpse, scratches on a nightdress and door, voices in the cellar, warnings about the equinox, and spectral figures outside the window. Each incident is partially rationalised or left unresolved, allowing tension to build through implication. Dates and anniversaries quietly organise the plot, while repeated motifs—the heart, the left side of the chest, long nails, concealed rooms, and questions about age—prepare the final explanation. The climax itself is brief and partly withheld: the reader does not witness Abney’s death directly but hears a choked cry and then encounters its aftermath.

Atmosphere depends heavily on contrasts between beauty and menace. Evening light, autumn melancholy, moonlit woods, domestic fires, and the stately Hall initially suggest tranquillity, but enclosed or neglected spaces—the disused bathroom, wine cellar, locked doors, and scholarly study—harbour the story’s horrors. Wind, distant cries, moonlight, and unseen movement animate the landscape without fully personifying it. Sensory effects remain selective: glowing windows, cold floorboards, dry gravel, transparent hands, and an almost inaudible moan are isolated with enough precision to make them memorable.

The supernatural is handled through delayed confirmation. Dreams, sounds, scratches, and apparitions initially permit natural explanations, but their correspondences eventually form an unmistakable pattern. The ghosts are simultaneously pathetic and terrifying: physically wasted, wounded, hungry, and unable to communicate except through gestures and cries. Their revenge is presented as morally intelligible, although its exact mechanism remains unexplained. This balance between evidential clarity and supernatural mystery is central to the tale’s method.

The concluding documents change the mode from atmospheric narrative to archival disclosure. Abney’s papers retrospectively explain nearly every earlier detail, producing a tightly controlled effect of recognition. Their cold, pseudo-scientific language—especially the use of experimental and philosophical terms for murder—creates grim irony. The final coroner’s verdict supplies a conventional public explanation, while Stephen’s reading establishes the private truth. The ending therefore preserves the outward appearance of rational order even as it confirms a supernatural act of retribution.

Overall, the style is characterised by antiquarian realism, documentary framing, restrained horror, learned allusion, class-differentiated dialogue, cumulative foreshadowing, and an ironic opposition between scholarship and morality. Its deepest stylistic effect comes from treating the impossible with scrupulous factual calm: the more exact and reasonable the narration appears, the more credible—and unsettling—the haunting becomes.

A successful humorist approaching fifty reflects bitterly on why his early literary promise never developed into greater fame. He calls his countless distractions “goat-feathers”: public offices, charitable committees, telephone calls, visitors, side projects, and even trivial tasks that repeatedly pull him away from writing. Although some patriotic service is worthwhile, most of these obligations consume his time, deepen his debts, and prevent him from building on the success of his famous humorous story.

He recalls abandoning stories to clean his typewriter, attend meetings, travel, construct toys, and pursue unrelated ideas while more disciplined writers steadily surpass him. Concluding that achievement requires sustained concentration on one’s central purpose, he warns that unnecessary side pursuits can turn potential success into mediocrity or failure. Yet even as he hopes this self-reproach will reform him, he doubts he can overcome his lifelong habit of chasing distractions.

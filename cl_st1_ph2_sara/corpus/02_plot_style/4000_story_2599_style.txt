The story adopts a retrospective first-person confessional mode, with an unnamed physician recounting Herbert West’s career from a position of belated terror. The narrator is both witness and accomplice: medically educated, initially fascinated by West’s work, yet increasingly appalled by its consequences. This divided perspective creates tension between clinical observation and moral horror. Frequent references to West’s later disappearance, the narrator’s present shuddering, and facts not yet fully disclosed give the narration a fatalistic cast, as though the entire account were evidence leading toward an inevitable catastrophe.

The prose is formal, ornate, and deliberately archaic, using period spellings and diction such as “shewn,” “daemoniac,” “revellers,” and “licenced.” Sentences are often long and syntactically layered, with semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, subordinate clauses, and explanatory digressions. Abstract Latinate vocabulary—“revivification,” “decomposition,” “mechanistic,” “interdicted,” “inexpressibly”—lends the narrative a scholarly surface. This intellectual register is repeatedly invaded by highly emotive Gothic adjectives, including “hideous,” “ghastly,” “sinister,” “loathsome,” “unnatural,” and “soul-petrifying.” The resulting style combines the language of a medical case history with the rhetoric of supernatural horror.

The narrative proceeds episodically. Each section begins by restating the central situation, summarising earlier experiments, and re-establishing West’s appearance, theories, and need for exceptionally fresh corpses. This recurrence gives the story a serial structure and makes each episode a variation on the same pattern: a body is obtained, scientific preparations are described, the experiment appears to fail or only partly succeeds, and a delayed revelation exposes a grotesque consequence. Foreshadowing is heavy and explicit. Phrases such as “the first horrible incident,” “one terrible and unforgettable occasion,” and “it would have been better if we could have known” announce disaster in advance, shifting suspense away from whether something dreadful will occur and toward its precise form.

Pacing alternates between methodical exposition and sudden violence. Long passages explain West’s materialist theory of life, the condition of specimens, laboratory equipment, burial practices, and chemical procedures. These practical details make the fantastic premise feel systematic and quasi-scientific. Climactic moments then arrive abruptly through cries, clawing, attacks, fires, escaped bodies, or shocking recognitions. The prose becomes more rhythmically intense at these points, accumulating images and superlatives before ending on a brief, often sardonic line of dialogue or revelation. The episode concerning Dr. Halsey, for example, culminates in West’s cold complaint that the corpse “wasn’t quite fresh enough,” compressing scientific frustration, moral blindness, and black humour into a single sentence.

Characterisation is strongly contrastive. West is repeatedly rendered as physically slight, pale, blond, blue-eyed, spectacled, and soft-spoken, while his mind is described as cold, fanatical, logical, and almost diabolical. The contrast between delicate appearance and ruthless purpose makes him uncanny. He is less a psychologically developing character than the embodiment of scientific obsession without ethical restraint. The narrator is more emotionally responsive but remains morally compromised: his revulsion rarely prevents his participation. His habit of speaking collectively—“we decided,” “we equipped,” “we followed”—quietly establishes his guilt even when he attributes the driving ambition to West.

Atmosphere depends on the collision of recognisable New England settings with Gothic secrecy. Universities, hospitals, boarding houses, factories, newspapers, cemeteries, deserted farms, and village streets are described concretely, while invented names such as Arkham, Miskatonic, Sefton, and Meadow Hill create a coherent regional mythology. Puritan respectability, institutional authority, and provincial routine form the daylight world; laboratories, potter’s fields, cellar graves, epidemics, and nocturnal journeys form its hidden underside. Darkness is especially functional: midnight, moonlight, curtained rooms, dark lanterns, isolated houses, and “black small hours” provide both concealment and symbolic descent.

The imagery is excessive and theatrical in the tradition of pulp Gothic horror. Hell, Tartarus, Eblis, demons, ghouls, bat-wings, anthropomorphic fiends, and the “agony of the damned” supply a mythic vocabulary for events that West insists are purely chemical. Sound often carries the horror before sight does: appalling cries, scratching, howling, whispered reports, and imagined footsteps allow the creature or threat to remain partly unseen. Bodies are represented through fragments and signs—stiff forms, vacant faces, opened eyes, claw marks, blood trails, damaged tissue—so that horror emerges through incomplete apprehension rather than sustained visual description.

A central stylistic tension lies between rational explanation and language that repeatedly exceeds rational control. West interprets every failure in terms of tissue freshness, species difference, or defective formulae, while the narrator reaches for infernal and supernatural metaphors. The story does not require literal supernaturalism; instead, scientific materialism itself becomes the source of the uncanny. Death is treated as a mechanical interruption, yet attempts to reverse it produce beings stripped of intellect, identity, speech, and restraint. Repetition of the word “fresh,” often in increasingly macabre contexts, turns an apparently neutral technical criterion into a grotesque motif.

The tone mixes dread, disgust, sensationalism, and mordant irony. Moral judgement is usually indirect: the narrator calls the work “loathsome” and “sinister,” but the detailed attention given to logistics also conveys fascination. Understatement frequently sharpens the horror, especially when atrocities are described as “inconvenient,” “anticlimactic,” or scientifically disappointing. Conversely, moments of panic employ extravagant hyperbole. This alternation between restraint and excess is central to the story’s pulp energy.

The text also reflects historically racist and xenophobic modes of representation. Black and immigrant characters are described through dehumanising physical caricature, animal comparison, exoticised imagery, and ethnic stereotyping. Terms, associations, and narrative descriptions connect racial difference with primitiveness, criminality, or bodily monstrosity. These elements are not incidental to the diction; they contribute to the story’s strategy of producing disgust and otherness, while marking the work’s cultural prejudices and period context.

Overall, the style may be characterised as pseudo-documentary Gothic horror: retrospective and confessional in viewpoint, ornate and archaic in language, repetitive and episodic in structure, scientifically detailed in exposition, and sensational in climax. Its signature effect comes from presenting grave robbery and reanimation with procedural seriousness, then allowing the controlled medical vocabulary to collapse into images of animality, madness, infernal sound, and bodily violation.

The story adopts a warm, morally purposeful mode of historical domestic realism, shaped as a children’s tale set in rural New England. Its atmosphere is deliberately old-fashioned, with gambrel-roofed houses, homespun clothing, chimney ovens, spinning wheels, pewter dishes and Revolutionary relics establishing the period through concrete household detail rather than extended exposition. Thanksgiving preparations supply the narrative with warmth, colour and sensory richness: gray November weather and dry grass outside contrast with kitchens full of “warm, spicy odours,” pies, apples, cranberries and nuts. The prefatory note has the voice of an editorial recommendation, advertising the tale’s appeal to both girls and boys and signalling its combination of domestic subject matter, humour and historical adventure.

The narrator is third-person omniscient, genial but morally authoritative. The viewpoint moves most closely between Submit and Sarah, though Submit’s temptation, deception and remorse form the psychological centre. The narrator explains childish motives with sympathetic precision, treating them as serious without losing adult perspective. Phrases such as “naughty covetousness” and the comparison of temptation to Satan entering a “godly New England home” place the children’s conduct within a traditional moral framework, yet the tone is more affectionate than punitive. Gentle irony often softens the instruction: Sarah agrees to the wager while trying to justify herself to her doll, and Submit’s revolutionary ancestry is invoked first to help her endure a cut thumb and later to dramatize her confession.

Characterization relies on vivid physical miniatures, repeated gestures and emblematic objects. Sarah is thin, prim and delicate, with a high forehead and sharp chin that reveal her hidden resolution; Submit is associated with black, sparkling eyes, a red hood and energetic movements. Their nodding, head-shaking, hugging and staring turn an argument about turkeys into a small but intense contest of wills. Thankful, the work-box and the bullets are more than props: the doll embodies affection and temptation, the work-box represents exchange and rivalry, and the Revolutionary bullets comically transform inherited heroism into the instrument of a childish fraud. The objects also bind private domestic life to collective New England history.

The prose alternates between descriptive passages of moderate length and brisk, highly patterned dialogue. Sentences are generally clear and chronological, but visual descriptions can become more figurative and rhythmical. Submit on the wall, surrounded by red petticoats and blackberry vines, resembles “some strange late flower,” while her movement into the house is likened to “a red robin into a nest.” Parallel constructions and repetition create emphasis, especially during emotional or moral turning points. The narration frequently records small sequential actions—climbing, fetching, sewing, weighing and replacing—which builds suspense around the cheating episode with almost procedural exactness.

Dialogue is central to the style and reproduces regional, period-inflected speech through contractions, nonstandard grammar and domestic forms of address: “ain’t,” “marm,” “mebbe,” “as you are a mind to” and “ours weighed the most.” The girls repeatedly use one another’s full names, giving their quarrels a ceremonious gravity that is both comic and convincing. Adults speak more firmly and sententiously, issuing commands or moral judgments, while Grandfather Thompson’s biblical diction—“like unto the brawlers in the taverns and ale-houses”—parodies, without wholly undermining, Puritan solemnity. The contrast between the children’s short, stubborn exchanges and the elders’ formal pronouncements reinforces the gap between childish feeling and adult codes of conduct.

The plot is tightly constructed around a wager, temptation, concealment, exposure, confession and reconciliation. Early comparisons between the turkeys introduce rivalry; domestic chores delay resolution and intensify anticipation; the bullets provide the central reversal; and Sarah’s delivery of the doll forces Submit’s private guilt into public view. The paired bedroom windows create a symmetrical final scene in which both girls discover that they have been punished for the same offence. Their mutual sympathy replaces competition, and the promised second doll resolves the material conflict without rewarding dishonesty. This structure gives the tale the clarity of a moral fable while preserving the texture of everyday family life.

The dominant tone blends tenderness, humour, suspense and mild didacticism. Wrongdoing is described in explicitly religious and ethical terms, but confession is treated as cleansing rather than merely humiliating. The comparison between truth-telling and balm smarting on a wound neatly condenses the story’s moral method: correction hurts, yet heals. Mercy ultimately “tempers justice,” embodied by the kindly grandmother and Aunt Rose. The ending is harmonious and socially restorative, returning both children to family, friendship and the Thanksgiving meal.

Overall, the style is picturesque, accessible and emotionally reassuring. It combines period dialect, domestic detail, child-centred psychology, symbolic objects and an overt lesson about covetousness, gambling, honesty and forgiveness. Its distinctive effect comes from granting a small childhood transgression the weight of a moral crisis while surrounding that crisis with humour, affection and the secure rhythms of household and community life.

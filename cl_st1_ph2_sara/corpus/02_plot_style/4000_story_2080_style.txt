The story uses a restrained, realist style shaped by psychological precision, social observation and muted tragedy. Its tone is initially cool, austere and faintly ironic, reflecting Mr. Duffy’s rigidly ordered existence. As the narrative progresses, that composure gives way to disgust, guilt, grief and desolation, but the prose remains controlled rather than openly sentimental. Emotional force arises through gradual accumulation and belated recognition.

The narration is third-person and closely aligned with Mr. Duffy’s consciousness. It moves freely between external description, reported thought and free indirect discourse, often presenting his judgements without immediately distinguishing them from the narrator’s perspective. This creates a subtle critical distance: his self-image as disciplined and intellectually superior is reproduced in the prose, while his vanity, emotional sterility and capacity for self-deception are quietly exposed. Aphoristic formulations such as “every bond…is a bond to sorrow” reveal his habit of converting difficult feelings into abstract principles.

Characterisation proceeds through material detail, routine and environment. The inventory of Mr. Duffy’s room—iron furniture, white shelves, carefully ordered books, sparse decoration—functions as an external portrait of his controlled and joyless mind. His repetitive meals, tram journeys, solitary walks and limited recreations similarly establish him through habitual action rather than direct explanation. Physical details are selective but interpretive: his harsh features, alert yet disappointed eyes and sense of living “at a little distance from his body” embody his alienation from both physical life and emotional intimacy.

The diction is formal, exact and often Latinate, with occasional archaic or ecclesiastical touches. Concrete nouns and carefully chosen adjectives produce a sombre urban texture: disused buildings, shallow water, cold streets, gaunt trees and failing light. The vocabulary of order, judgement and moral classification dominates Mr. Duffy’s world, while warmer language enters during his intimacy with Mrs. Sinico. Her companionship is figured as “warm soil about an exotic,” and music, darkness and touch briefly soften the prose. After her death, the language becomes increasingly sensory and bodily—cold grease, stomach sickness, trembling nerves, breath in winter air—showing the physical life he has denied returning in involuntary form.

Sentence structure alternates between measured descriptive catalogues and longer, layered psychological sentences. Parallel constructions and repetition reinforce routine, rigidity and inevitability. Short sentences become prominent at moments of shock or final recognition, especially in the closing sequence: “He listened again: perfectly silent. He felt that he was alone.” This narrowing of syntax strips away his intellectual defences and leaves the story with a stark emotional conclusion. Repeated words and sentence patterns also create a fatal rhythm, as when existence is reduced to dying, ceasing to exist and becoming a memory.

Dialogue is sparse, socially plausible and subordinate to narration. It usually reveals asymmetry rather than mutual understanding: Mrs. Sinico invites intimacy, whereas Mr. Duffy answers through theories and carefully controlled scorn. The story’s longest direct-speech section appears in the embedded newspaper report. Its bureaucratic testimony, legal formulas and conventional expressions of sympathy contrast sharply with the private emotional history behind the death. The impersonal report reduces Mrs. Sinico to evidence, injuries and social embarrassment, while also triggering Mr. Duffy’s movement from moral revulsion to self-accusation.

The structure is linear but highly selective. It begins with an extended static portrait, moves through the compressed development and abrupt termination of the relationship, leaps forward four years, and then slows dramatically around the newspaper report and its aftermath. This pacing mirrors Mr. Duffy’s life: years of uneventful repetition are condensed, while the single evening of recognition receives intense attention. The newspaper article forms a structural rupture, introducing a different register and forcing an excluded reality into his ordered private world.

Setting carries symbolic and psychological weight. Dublin is presented through specific streets, suburbs, parks, transport routes and public venues, giving the story a strong topographical realism. At the same time, these places reflect states of mind. Empty concert halls, lonely roads, disused industrial spaces and bleak parkland echo failed connection and emotional barrenness. The distant city lights appear “redly and hospitably,” making communal life visible but inaccessible. The train is both the literal instrument of Mrs. Sinico’s death and a recurring acoustic symbol: its mechanical rhythm becomes the syllables of her name before fading into silence.

Imagery depends heavily on contrasts between order and disorder, warmth and cold, communion and isolation, motion and stasis. Mr. Duffy initially fears physical and mental disorder, yet his excessive rectitude proves destructive. Touch is especially important: Mrs. Sinico’s pressing of his hand to her cheek precipitates the rupture, and later imagined touches become signs of guilt and lost possibility. Sound also charts his emotional passage, from music and shared conversation to the train’s pounding rhythm and finally “perfect” silence.

Irony is pervasive but understated. Mr. Duffy considers himself morally exact, yet his moral certainty prevents compassion; he condemns conventional society while remaining trapped by its constraints; he believes in the soul’s incurable loneliness and then discovers that he has helped make that belief true. The narrative does not simply absolve or condemn him. Instead, it exposes the tension between legitimate practical obstacles and his deeper fear of embodied, reciprocal life. The tragic effect comes from his recognition arriving only when action is impossible.

Overall, the style combines documentary realism, symbolic patterning and close psychological narration. Its defining qualities are restraint, exactness, tonal irony, urban specificity, carefully controlled shifts in pace, and a final movement from detached observation into existential isolation. The closing silence is characteristic of the story’s method: rather than explaining the moral, it converts emotional absence into an immediate sensory experience.

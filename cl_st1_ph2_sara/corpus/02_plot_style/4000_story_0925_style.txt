The story adopts a nineteenth-century literary-historical style that blends regional legend, travel narrative, comic character sketch, supernatural tale, and overt political allegory. It begins in the manner of a local chronicle, describing the “Wandering Jew” and the “Lake Gun” as established curiosities of Seneca Lake. This documentary opening, with its geographical particulars, accumulated eyewitness testimony, and cautious discussion of possible natural causes, gives the fiction an air of antiquarian authenticity. The later movement into Fuller’s quest and the Seneca legend gradually converts this factual-seeming account into a moral fable. The editorial glosses in braces are supplementary annotations rather than part of the story’s original narrative voice.

The narrator is omniscient, conspicuous, and interpretive. Rather than presenting events neutrally, the voice comments on motives, evaluates beliefs, generalizes about human nature, and guides the reader toward the intended political meaning. Phrases such as “It must be confessed,” “for the obvious reason,” and “as often happens in such cases” create a conversational relationship with the audience, while occasional irony keeps the narrator slightly detached from the characters. Fuller’s assumptions, Peter’s exaggerations, and the Seneca’s divided relation to tradition are all subjected to gentle scrutiny. The narration often moves from concrete observation to broad moral reflection, so that landscape, folklore, and character become evidence in an argument.

The prose is formal, elevated, and syntactically expansive. Long periodic or cumulative sentences use semicolons, dashes, balanced clauses, inversions, and parenthetical qualifications. Archaic or Latinate diction—“lustrums,” “broached,” “hazarded,” “beneficent,” “desultory,” “mien,” and “conspired”—contributes to the cultivated register. Explanations are frequently delayed by subordinate clauses, allowing suspense or emphasis to build before the principal statement arrives. Rhetorical antitheses recur throughout: red man and pale-face, fathers and sons, tradition and innovation, superstition and reason, flattery and truth. Repetition and parallelism become especially prominent in the moral passages, as in the Seneca’s catalogue of the demagogue’s conduct and the final sequence concerning clouds, darkness, sunlight, and trust in God.

Description is picturesque and often personifying. The floating tree has a “bald head,” “features,” a “countenance,” and an apparent will of its own; it battles ice, shifts its helm, and travels like a ship. The lake likewise “speaks” to the hills, which answer with echoes. Nautical vocabulary—“berth,” “unmooring,” “sheets,” “helm,” “cutwater,” and “foresheet”—grounds the legendary material in practical observation and supports the story’s central image of perpetual wandering. Landscape description follows the conventions of the picturesque and sublime: cultivated northern shores are “smiling pictures,” while the southern hill country possesses a sober dignity and the “elements that characterize the noble in nature.” Visual detail is repeatedly transformed into symbolic meaning, most notably when the broken trunk resembles the selfish, indecisive face of a demagogue.

Dialogue differentiates character sharply. Peter speaks in heavily marked regional dialect, with eye-dialect and colloquial forms such as “crittur,” “seed,” “sich,” “ag’in,” and “a-goin’.” His speech is animated by sailor’s idiom, proverbial expressions, comic exaggeration, and unwavering credulity. Fuller uses educated, conventional English and functions as the curious outsider whose questions elicit explanation. The young Seneca speaks in controlled, aphoristic, elevated language. His short early replies—especially “My FATHER’S country”—carry emotional and historical weight, while his later speeches expand into polished moral discourse. His diction combines references to Indigenous tradition with biblical and civic rhetoric, producing a stylized voice that is less naturalistic than emblematic. The portrayal reflects period conventions: the Seneca is idealized through classical comparisons, melancholy dignity, physical nobility, and an assumed conflict between education and “primitive” inheritance.

The pacing is deliberately gradual. The opening exposition establishes the two mysteries at length; the unsuccessful cruise then creates delay through repeated reports, digressions, and comic episodes. The encounter on the promontory slows the action further through visual description and ceremonious dialogue. Once the floating tree is sighted, physical discovery becomes secondary to the Seneca’s embedded legend of See-wise. This tale-within-the-tale supplies the story’s ideological center, after which the Lake Gun and the tree provide apparent confirmation. The climax is therefore interpretive rather than action-driven: the mysteries are never scientifically solved but are assigned a political and providential meaning.

The dominant tone shifts from wonder and genial curiosity to satire, solemnity, and admonition. Humor surrounds Peter and the improbable behavior attributed to the tree, while the Seneca’s account is grave and prophetic. Supernatural ambiguity is preserved: currents, light, and accidental weathering may explain the phenomena, yet the timing of the boom and the tree’s disappearance encourages awe. The story neither wholly endorses nor dismisses folklore. Instead, it suggests that a legend may convey moral truth regardless of literal fact, a point crystallized in the claim that whether the object is merely a tree or a punished man, “it tells the same story.”

The governing mode is didactic allegory. See-wise is a transparent caricature of a contemporary political demagogue, and his violation of fishing laws represents the rejection of inherited restraints in the name of popular liberty. Demagoguery is defined as reciprocal flattery between leader and crowd: the leader praises the people’s wisdom, the people elevate him, and both become hostile to law, tradition, and limits. The story’s political outlook is conservative, paternal, and providential. It distrusts mass enthusiasm, reformist claims to humanity, generational revolt, and projects of social perfection, while valuing ancestral counsel, established law, humility, restraint, and religious faith. Its closing image converts the local curiosities into a universal moral formula: just as the lake contains and punishes See-wise, divine order will eventually expose and restrain every demagogue.

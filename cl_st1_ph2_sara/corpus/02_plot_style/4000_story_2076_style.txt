The story uses a polished modern realist style shaped by close third-person narration, social observation and sustained dramatic irony. The narrator remains formally detached but gradually moves closer to Jimmy Doyle’s perceptions, reproducing his excitement, vanity and increasingly impaired judgment without fully endorsing them. Free indirect discourse is especially important: phrases such as “pots of money,” “this was seeing life,” and “What jovial fellows!” sound like Jimmy’s own thoughts while remaining within the narrative voice. This allows the story to expose the gap between his glamorous interpretation of events and the financial and social exploitation visible to the reader.

The diction combines journalistic precision with lyrical and symbolic phrasing. Concrete names, nationalities, streets, institutions and means of transport give the action a strongly localized Dublin setting, while references to France, England, Hungary, Canada and the United States create a cosmopolitan surface. Against this factual texture, the prose introduces vivid figurative language: cars “scudding” like pellets, machinery becoming a “swift blue animal,” the harbour resembling a “darkened mirror,” and daybreak entering as a “shaft of grey light.” Technology, money and motion form a recurring vocabulary, lending the narrative an energetic modernity while also suggesting impersonal economic power.

Sentence structure is varied and carefully responsive to mood. The opening uses long, balanced sentences to contrast the speed and wealth of continental motor racing with Dublin’s “poverty and inaction.” Expository passages employ extended, clause-heavy sentences to summarize Jimmy’s family history, education and social aspirations. During scenes of excitement, the prose becomes quicker and more fragmented, using exclamations, repetitions and abrupt transitions: “Cards! cards!”, “What merriment!”, and “What excitement!” These compressed expressions simulate intoxication and breathless enthusiasm. Toward the end, shorter, darker statements slow the narrative and register Jimmy’s exhaustion and dawning shame.

The tone is urbane, controlled and quietly satirical. Social distinctions are conveyed through apparently casual details: Jimmy’s father is proud of foreign connections, Segouin possesses the “unmistakable air of wealth,” and Villona is admired for his accomplishments but diminished by the qualification that he is “very poor.” The narrator repeatedly allows characters’ pretensions to reveal themselves. Jimmy believes proximity to wealthy Continentals confers status, yet he struggles to hear their speech, guesses at meanings, and remains socially peripheral. His father’s nationalism has softened as his wealth and commercial interests have grown, while Dublin’s spectators cheer foreign cars passing through their own impoverished city. Such contrasts generate irony without overt authorial judgment.

Characterization is economical and socially diagnostic. Characters are introduced through nationality, profession, wealth, appetite or cultural accomplishment rather than through extensive psychological description. Segouin is defined by commercial assurance and social dexterity; Riviere by technical ambition; Villona by music, hunger and benign detachment; Routh by English firmness; Farley by American wealth and exuberance. Jimmy receives the greatest inward attention, but even his psychology is presented through material motives: notoriety, money, speed and the desire for association with prestigious foreigners. His “innocent-looking” eyes and inability to calculate his own losses make him appear inexperienced beneath his fashionable grooming.

Dialogue is sparse, cosmopolitan and functional. Brief exchanges establish sociability and national mixture, while songs, toasts and multilingual names create an atmosphere of international fellowship. Yet this fellowship is shallow. The men drink to nations and “Humanity,” but their relationships ultimately revolve around competition, money and display. Villona’s final announcement—“Daybreak, gentlemen!”—is simple and literal, but it also carries symbolic force: daylight exposes the folly concealed by nocturnal excitement.

The narrative structure traces a continuous movement from public spectacle to private consequence. It begins with racing cars entering Dublin, proceeds through dressing, dining, promenading, travel and entertainment, and culminates in gambling aboard a yacht. Each stage seems to raise Jimmy into a more exclusive social sphere, but also removes him further from security and clear judgment. The sequence accelerates through linked vehicles—the motorcar, street car, train, rowboat and yacht—so that physical motion mirrors Jimmy’s surrender of control. The yacht appears to represent freedom and cosmopolitan luxury, yet its enclosed cabin becomes the site of entrapment and loss.

Pacing depends on this contrast between exhilaration and aftermath. Early movement is smooth, rapid and sensuous; the middle expands into convivial scenes of dinner, politics, music and drinking; the card game is rendered in increasingly disjointed bursts. The narrative withholds exact financial figures, keeping Jimmy and the reader in a state of uncertainty. His losses become fully meaningful not through calculation but through the ominous emotional descent from excitement to “dark stupor.”

The dominant imagery opposes light and darkness, speed and inertia, appearance and reality. Dublin’s pale lights and temporary “mask of a capital” suggest borrowed glamour rather than genuine power. The blue racing cars symbolize modern wealth and continental vitality, while the Irish crowd remains stationary and admiring. Night permits Jimmy’s fantasies of status and fellowship; grey dawn brings recognition. The ending is deliberately understated and unresolved, stopping at the instant of exposure rather than narrating the practical consequences.

Overall, the style is compressed, ironic and symbolically organized. Its realism rests on precise social detail, but its deeper effect comes from patterns of motion, money, nationality, light and machinery. The prose captures the intoxication of modern glamour while steadily revealing its cost, turning an apparently festive adventure into a critique of social aspiration, economic dependence and self-deception.

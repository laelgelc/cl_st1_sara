The story adopts the manner of a late nineteenth-century Gothic or supernatural memoir, presented as a sober first-person account of events the narrator cannot fully explain. The retrospective opening—“The night that I have in memory”—immediately places the narrative between testimony and unstable recollection. Although the narrator repeatedly insists upon facts, he also admits to gaps, distortions and second-hand knowledge. This combination gives the prose an air of documentary credibility while quietly establishing him as an unreliable witness.

The narrative voice is educated, formal and self-conscious, with a dry, sardonic edge. Elevated words such as “prestidigitateurs,” “thaumaturgists,” “peculiarly forbidding” and “prudish purveyance” locate the prose in a cultivated literary register. Periodic flashes of bluntness—“That was not altogether true” or “The devil you are!”—cut through that formality and reveal the narrator’s fear, jealousy and hostility. He often comments upon his own storytelling, disclaims certainty and distinguishes carefully between what he remembers, what he inferred and what he was later told. These qualifications imitate the language of evidence and make the impossible events sound like a case history.

Sentence structure alternates between long, balanced descriptive periods and short statements of shock or revelation. The extended sentences map landscapes, mental states and chains of perception with careful precision; abrupt clauses then deliver supernatural turns. Parentheses, semicolons, dashes and qualifying phrases allow the narrator to correct or complicate himself in mid-sentence. The result is measured and analytical on the surface, yet increasingly destabilised beneath it. Repetition of formulations such as “I recall,” “I do not recollect,” “I cannot remember” and “I know” makes memory itself a structural motif.

The story is divided into five numbered sections and proceeds through controlled disclosure. It begins with an ominous reunion, moves backward to explain the narrator’s original encounter with Dorrimore, then returns to Auburn for the apparent courtship, assault and final explanation. Each section adds a new layer of unease while withholding decisive interpretation. The closing newspaper extract functions as a retrospective key: it rationalises the supernatural events through hypnosis without entirely restoring security, since its final suggestion expands the possible duration and reach of the delusion. The ending therefore explains the mechanism while preserving existential uncertainty.

Pacing is deliberate. The opening landscape is established through practical geographical detail before fear enters, and scenes of menace often slow down around visual perception: moonlight, bodies, weapons, shadows and facial expressions are registered one element at a time. Sudden reversals—the corpse sharing Dorrimore’s face, the dead man standing, Margaret’s apparent presence, the hotel clerk’s denial—arrive sharply after this accumulation. Ellipsis is equally important. The narrator omits Dorrimore’s travel narrative, cannot recount the cemetery struggle and compresses days of delirium, allowing gaps in consciousness to become part of the horror.

Dialogue is concise, ceremonious and adversarial. Polite formulas conceal suspicion: invitations are offered insincerely, civil replies carry threats, and Dorrimore’s light laughter repeatedly undermines social courtesy. His speech is controlled and economical, reinforcing his power, while the narrator’s speech becomes increasingly emotional and accusatory. The contrast between cultivated manners and psychological violation is central to Dorrimore’s “disagreeably engaging” presence.

The settings externalise the narrator’s mental condition. The flooded ravine, chill fog, empty town, moonlit hill and ruined cemetery are liminal spaces marked by darkness, obscured vision and unstable boundaries. Descriptions combine concrete topography with moralised Gothic imagery. The cemetery is not merely decayed but “a dishonour to the living, a calumny on the dead, a blasphemy against God”; shadows become “conspiracies,” roots commit “unspeakable sin,” and the town appears “cheerless, forbidding, and desolate.” Such personification turns environment into an accomplice of dread.

Character description is similarly symbolic. Dorrimore is rendered through darkness, glittering eyes, disordered black hair and cobra-like soullessness. The quasi-Orientalist association with India, jugglery and occult mastery reflects period conventions in which foreign knowledge is treated as seductive, sophisticated and threatening. His combination of gentlemanly manners, melodious voice and predatory power creates a charismatic antagonist whose danger lies less in physical violence than in control over perception.

The prevailing tone is ominous, ironic and psychologically claustrophobic. Terror is moderated by wit, especially in the Bohemian Club conversation, the aside about reporters being exempt from mass hypnosis and the satirical interruption concerning literary censorship by the “Young Girl.” That digression briefly shifts into mock-polemical comedy, using inflated rhetoric and verse to parody Victorian prudery. Even so, the humour does not dissolve the menace; it sharpens the story’s sceptical attitude toward testimony, journalism, social convention and professed rationality.

The central stylistic method is ambiguity managed through apparent precision. The prose supplies exact roads, hotels, cities and social settings, but the narrator’s lived experience proves radically false. Seeing is repeatedly presented as unreliable, while language—Dorrimore’s suggestions, the narrator’s reconstruction and the newspaper’s explanation—determines what can be accepted as real. The style therefore joins Gothic atmosphere to proto-psychological horror: its deepest fear is not the apparition itself, but the possibility that consciousness can be occupied, edited and sustained inside an illusion without knowing it.

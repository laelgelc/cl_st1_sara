The story adopts the form of a panoramic Western animal biography, following a line-backed steer from birth to death while treating his life as an episodic picaresque adventure. Its structure is chronological and largely linear, with seasons, round-ups, cattle drives, poker games, and changes of ownership marking successive stages. The opening is intimate and pastoral, dwelling closely on birth, maternal care, and sensory discovery; the narrative then steadily widens into the commercial and territorial world of the cattle industry. Time is handled flexibly: decisive experiences such as branding, defeat in love, joining the trail herd, and the final killing are dramatized at length, while uneventful months or years are compressed into transitional summaries. This produces the expansive, anecdotal movement of an oral frontier chronicle.

The narrator is third-person omniscient, authoritative, and conspicuously humanizing. The steer’s sensations, motives, pride, jealousy, humiliation, curiosity, and resolutions are presented as if they formed a coherent human psychology. This anthropomorphism is sustained but not wholly sentimental: the animal remains governed by bodily needs, instinct, weather, grazing, and herd behavior. The narrative often moves between close identification with him and a broader historical or economic viewpoint. Phrases such as “the subject of this sketch” create the manner of an old-fashioned biographical narrator, while ironic observations about profit, ownership, civilization, and destiny remind the reader that the steer is also livestock within a commercial system.

The diction combines elevated literary narration with precise ranching vocabulary. Lyrical descriptions of moonlight, grass, water, weather, and open country sit beside terms such as “dam,” “out­rider,” “cutting out,” “branding-pen,” “night-herd,” “road brand,” “winter drift,” and “beef round-up.” Spanish and regional expressions, including “ranchero” and “pasear,” reinforce the South Texas setting. The prose also uses the idiom of gambling and ownership—“poker steer,” “chips,” “high card,” and “last trump”—as a recurring lexical pattern that turns the animal’s fate into a game of hazard. Dialogue is sparse but vivid, dominated by laconic cowboy speech, boasts, jokes, practical calculations, and occupational shorthand.

Sentences are generally long, balanced, and syntactically elaborate, often linked by coordinating conjunctions and participial phrases. They accumulate physical action and contextual detail in a measured, nineteenth-century narrative cadence. Shorter sentences appear at emotional or decisive moments—“He wanted to die,” “He was a beauty,” “It was a momentous day in his life”—and give those moments blunt emphasis. Transitional formulas such as “But springtime came,” “One day,” “The next spring,” and “By the middle of summer” maintain the storytelling flow and make the narrative resemble a remembered saga.

Description is concrete and strongly sensory. The story repeatedly evokes heat, cold, thirst, pain, scent, grass, mud, dust, blood, and water. Natural imagery is especially prominent in the early sections: the moon resembles “a hunter’s horn,” the calf collapses “like a limp rag,” and his playmate trembles “like a fawn.” Personification and figurative elevation grow more ambitious during the trail-drive passages, where cattle become “brigades of a king’s army” and cowboys “the vikings of the Plain.” Such comparisons enlarge an individual animal’s journey into a myth of westward movement, though the grand language is undercut by the steer’s status as stray property and eventual meat.

The tone shifts repeatedly between tenderness, humor, brutality, irony, and elegy. Maternal scenes and the calf’s early discoveries are affectionate; the failed romance with the speckled heifer is comic in its human emotional framing; branding and slaughter are rendered with graphic physical intensity. Dry frontier humor appears in the ownership disputes and poker games, where legal title, rustling, and personal honor coexist uneasily. The narrator sometimes adopts the prejudices and paternalistic language of the historical frontier milieu, particularly in references to Indigenous people and “our Christian nation.” This creates a satirical edge toward official civilization, but the representation remains rooted in period stereotypes and colonial attitudes.

Characterization is achieved through action rather than extended analysis. The steer is defined by obedience, endurance, pride, determination, adaptability, and a growing appetite for freedom. His decision to join the trail herd functions as the central assertion of agency, yet every later episode demonstrates how limited that agency is. Human figures are sketched economically through speech, occupation, and conduct: cowboys are practical, competitive, humorous, evasive about ownership, and loyal to their informal code. Even named characters remain secondary to the animal and to the collective machinery of ranching culture.

The central thematic tension is between self-determination and imposed destiny. The steer repeatedly appears to choose his course—leaving home, entering the herd, surviving inspection, changing ranges—yet brands, markets, contracts, gambling, and violence continually redefine his identity and destination. His altered brand is both a literal mark of ownership and a symbol of unstable social identity. Chance governs his life through poker hands and accidental encounters, while commerce converts him from calf to steer, stray, gambling stake, marketable beef, and contractual allotment. The final phrase about a destiny beyond his control makes explicit the fatalism that has shadowed the narrative from the beginning.

Overall, the style blends naturalistic animal observation, sentimental anthropomorphism, cowboy anecdote, regional realism, and frontier myth. Its characteristic effect comes from treating a steer’s life simultaneously as an intimate consciousness, a comic adventure, an economic object lesson, and a tragic epic. The ending completes that mixture: the steer dies violently but defiantly, “playing his last trump,” so that the gambling metaphor, the frontier code of admiration, and the story’s fatalistic grandeur converge in a final image of resistance.

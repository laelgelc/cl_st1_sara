The story adopts the manner of a nineteenth-century moral and social sketch, combining domestic realism, religious didacticism, and restrained satire. Its central purpose is exemplary rather than purely dramatic: the events in Brother Adkin’s week are arranged as a practical demonstration of the incompatibility between his worldly conduct and his assumed spiritual authority. The narrative moves from character portrait to illustrative episodes and finally to explicit moral commentary, asking readers to apply the lesson beyond the individual case.

The narrator is omniscient, conspicuously present, and ethically authoritative. Frequent first-person plural constructions—such as “we can say,” “our lay preacher,” and “we may hope”—create the voice of a communal observer addressing a morally receptive readership. The narrator moves freely between external action and Adkin’s private thoughts, interpreting his motives and measuring his conduct against Christian principles. Irony often arises from the distance between Adkin’s self-conception and the narrator’s clearer understanding of him: he regards himself as conscientious and dutiful, while his evasiveness, irritability, commercial self-interest, and lack of preparation steadily disqualify him from the pulpit. Although critical, the narration is not wholly condemnatory. Adkin remains “well-meaning and conscientious,” and his discomfort indicates that his conscience is active even when his behaviour is defective.

The tone is sober, admonitory, and gently satirical. Humour comes chiefly from verbal understatement, religious quotation, and the exposure of contradiction. Expressions such as Adkin’s preaching “in his feeble way,” his refusal to “take counsel of Jonah,” and the description of his “well-affected surprise” quietly puncture his pretensions. The satire becomes sharper in the church scene, where the accusing faces of his customers turn the congregation into a visible embodiment of conscience. Even so, the story avoids caricaturing him as a villain. Its dominant tone is one of moral concern: Adkin’s failures are treated as symptoms of a broader structural problem in combining commercial life with occasional ministry.

The diction blends formal literary language with biblical, clerical, and mercantile vocabularies. Elevated phrases such as “spiritual intuitions,” “the influx of intelligence,” and “practical observance thereof” coexist with commercial terms including “market rate,” “ledger,” “bill,” “profit and loss,” and “summons.” This lexical contrast reinforces the story’s governing opposition between heavenly and worldly concerns. Biblical and devotional idioms—“call to preach,” “bread of life,” “adversary,” “house of God,” and “hungry sheep”—place Adkin’s actions under a scriptural standard. Quotation marks around expressions such as “hold forth,” “Brother Adkin,” and “free utterance” often signal distance or scepticism, inviting readers to question conventional religious language when it is unsupported by knowledge or conduct.

The prose is syntactically elaborate and periodically structured. Sentences frequently contain multiple subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, balanced contrasts, and semicolon-linked reflections. Antithesis is especially important: Sabbath is opposed to weekday, love to self-interest, minister to tradesman, profession to practice, and spiritual clarity to mental obscurity. The narrator also uses accumulative phrasing—“Clearness of discrimination, grasp of thought, orderly arrangement”—to give arguments rhetorical weight. Some constructions and punctuation are historically marked, including long explanatory sentences, frequent commas, doubled hyphens, and formulations such as “not unfrequently,” “ere giving utterance,” and “we cannot apprize the reader.”

Dialogue is direct, functional, and socially revealing. The commercial disputes unfold through terse exchanges in which repetition and evasion expose character more effectively than abstract description alone. Adkin rarely tells an outright lie; instead, he answers questions narrowly, resorts to forced politeness, or asserts his rights in language that conceals self-interest. His customers speak more colloquially, using contractions and dialectal forms such as “D’ye” and “a’n’t,” which distinguish their speech from the narrator’s formal register. The dialogue grows increasingly confrontational, often turning on repeated denials and accusations. Short exclamations—“Certain!” and “I never had it!”—create immediacy within an otherwise discursive narrative.

Structurally, the story is linear and episodic. An expository opening establishes Adkin’s double role and central weakness; the succeeding weekday incidents accumulate evidence against him; Saturday night introduces the unavoidable preaching request; and Sunday brings all previous conflicts together in a climactic scene of public humiliation. The week functions as both chronological frame and moral architecture. Monday’s deceptive corn purchase, the lawsuit against Giles, and the quarrel with Mrs. Smith reappear visually in the congregation, so that secular actions literally invade sacred space. The anonymous note—“The hungry sheep look up, and are not fed”—serves as the concise verdict toward which the entire narrative has been moving.

Characterisation is primarily ethical and psychological rather than physical. Adkin is defined through habitual thought, commercial decisions, self-justification, emotional reactions, and bodily signs of unease: his voice falters, his hands tremble, his head aches, and he struggles for utterance. Secondary characters are sketched quickly through sharp gestures and facial expressions—a sarcastic curl of the lip, knit brows, compressed lips, or a “perked head.” They function partly as individuals and partly as witnesses whose judgments externalise Adkin’s conscience.

The story makes extensive use of dramatic irony, foreshadowing, repetition, and symbolic contrast. Adkin preaches that love fulfils the law and then immediately pursues gain; he fears that he does “more harm than good,” and the week proves the fear justified. His thoughts are repeatedly described as a “current” flowing through a “channel,” suggesting that sustained mental habits cannot be reversed at will. The pulpit becomes a place of judgment rather than authority, while his inability to speak coherently symbolises the disjunction between sacred office and daily life. The watching congregation acts almost as a moral tribunal, and each familiar face triggers a phrase or memory that fractures his sermon.

The ending shifts decisively from narrated example to essayistic argument. Rhetorical questions generalise the case, and the narrator states the lesson directly: spiritual teaching requires knowledge, preparation, disciplined attention, and credible conduct. This overt explanation is characteristic of didactic fiction, in which ambiguity is subordinate to moral usefulness. The final address to “those whom it may concern” turns the reader from spectator into potential subject of the warning.

Overall, the style is best described as formal, omniscient, morally didactic, biblically inflected, socially observant, and mildly satirical. Its characteristic effects come from the contrast between elevated religious profession and ordinary commercial behaviour, from cumulative episodes arranged as evidence, and from a narrator who interprets events with confidence while preserving enough sympathy to make correction and reform seem possible.

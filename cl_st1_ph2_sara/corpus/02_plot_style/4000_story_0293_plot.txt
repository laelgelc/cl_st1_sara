During a citywide uprising, a man is awakened by his wife, who tells him that revolutionaries are building barricades in their street and that his brother has already joined them. As the couple listens to the workers’ axes and hammers in the strangely silent, smoke-free spring night, fear gives way to exhilaration. Both feel transformed, as though ordinary time, domestic routine, and the city’s oppressive past are dissolving.

The wife declares that her husband and children, though dearly loved, are only part of a temporary life, while the struggle for freedom offers a rare, transcendent purpose. She wants to join the barricades even at the risk of death, but agrees to remain until the next day so she can arrange care for the children. The couple begin calling each other “comrade,” recognizing one another anew through their shared commitment.

After eating and reflecting on their former life, the man says farewell to his sleeping children. His nine-year-old son, already aware of what is happening, reminds him to take his hidden gun and asks whether he will be killed. The man promises to return, parts calmly from his wife, and descends the dark stairway, overwhelmed not by dread but by joy at entering an unknown new life.

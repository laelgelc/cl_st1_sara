The story adopts the manner of a nineteenth-century children’s animal fable, combining domestic realism, social comedy, and gentle moral satire. Its barnyard society is anthropomorphic: hens, cocks, and ducks conduct marriages, exchange gossip, consult physicians, raise families, and worry about reputation, while still retaining recognisable animal instincts and behaviour. This double perspective makes the narrative accessible as a comic animal tale while allowing it to parody human conventions surrounding parenthood, medicine, education, gender, and social status.

The narrator is omniscient, conversational, and conspicuously present. Phrases such as “we will call,” “I can’t say,” and “to do Master Fred justice” create the impression of an adult storyteller speaking directly and informally to a young audience. The voice is affectionate but ironic: it sympathises with Mrs. Feathertop’s anxieties while inviting the reader to recognise truths that the characters cannot. Much of the humour depends on dramatic irony, since the audience quickly understands that the supposed deformed chicks are ducklings, whereas the barnyard authorities interpret every natural difference as illness or failure.

The diction mixes plain storytelling language with genteel, ceremonious, and mock-learned vocabulary. Domestic phrases such as “bring up a family,” “make a morning call,” and “get on in life” transfer human social habits into the poultry yard. Elevated descriptions—“gloomy grandeur,” “a proud and happy mother,” and “a serious time in your family”—comically dignify ordinary animal conduct. Dr. Peppercorn’s jargon, including elaborate diagnoses and absurd remedies, parodies professional pretension by using polysyllabic medical language to conceal ignorance. Colloquial contractions and idioms—“ain’t,” “folks,” “that’s all,” and “Bless me”—give the gossips a vivid, provincial voice. Some human descriptions, especially the racial terminology applied to old Sam, reflect dated and offensive conventions of the historical period.

Sentence structure alternates between expansive narrative sentences and brisk dialogue. The longer sentences often accumulate descriptive or qualifying clauses, producing a leisurely oral cadence suited to a story told aloud. Dialogue speeds the action and dominates many scenes, with frequent interruptions, exclamations, rhetorical questions, dashes, and emphatic capitalisation. These features make conversations lively and theatrical. Repetition is important: the neighbours repeatedly predict disaster, the doctors repeatedly misdiagnose natural traits, and the ducklings repeatedly seek water despite efforts to restrain them. Parallel phrasing and comic escalation reinforce the fable-like pattern.

Characterisation is economical and caricatural. Mrs. Feathertop develops from a vain, inexperienced young hen into an anxious but capable mother. Gray Cock is self-important, inattentive, and susceptible to flattery. Mrs. Scratchard and Goody Kertarkut embody censorious gossip and retrospective moralising, while Dr. Peppercorn represents pompous, incompetent authority. Dr. Partlett functions as the corrective figure whose fresh observation dissolves the collective delusion. Names such as Feathertop, Scratchard, Peppercorn, Red Comb, and Kertarkut are playful, descriptive, and immediately suggest temperament or species.

The tone remains warm, comic, and lightly didactic rather than severe. Satire is aimed at people who mistake difference for deformity, rely on social prejudice instead of observation, or claim credit after success becomes undeniable. The ducklings’ spoon-bills, webbed feet, and love of water are treated as alarming defects only because the hens assume that every child ought to resemble a hen. Their eventual swim provides both the narrative climax and the central revelation: characteristics condemned as abnormalities are natural strengths in the proper environment.

Structurally, the story proceeds in clear episodic stages: the introduction of Mrs. Feathertop and her critics, Fred’s substitution of duck eggs, the hatching, the community’s alarm, the failed medical treatment, the ducklings’ apparent drowning, and the final recognition of their identity. Foreshadowing is transparent rather than suspenseful, because readers know about the eggs from the beginning. The real narrative interest lies in watching mistaken assumptions multiply. The ending restores harmony but preserves the satire through comic self-exoneration: Dr. Peppercorn claims responsibility for the ducks’ infancy, while the adoptive parents attribute the outcome to their educational system.

Imagery is generally concrete, bright, and pastoral. The henhouse, sunny bank, bushes, river, mud, reeds, azaleas, and gleaming green-and-gold feathers create an idyllic miniature world. Personification animates the landscape as the river “danc[es] and dimpl[es] and wink[s],” matching the ducklings’ exhilaration. Hyperbole, mock solemnity, punning animal references, and incongruous comparisons—such as bills resembling saucepans—sustain the comic effect. Overall, the style is that of an orally inflected moral tale: picturesque, dialogue-rich, socially observant, sentimentally sympathetic, and driven by irony at the expense of conformity, gossip, and false expertise.

In a drought-stricken land, a small group of capitalists controls all water sources. They hire the people to collect water, paying one penny per bucket, then charge them two pennies to buy each bucket back. Because workers cannot afford all they produce, the market tank overflows while they remain thirsty. The capitalists stop hiring them, causing a recurring economic crisis: without wages the people cannot buy water, and without buyers the capitalists will not employ them.

Economists offer hollow explanations such as overproduction and lack of confidence, false priests urge patient suffering, and the capitalists dispense token charity. When the people grow rebellious, the capitalists recruit armed men to defend the tank. After the stored water is wasted on the capitalists’ luxuries, work resumes, only for the same cycle to repeat.

Eventually, agitators explain that profit and private control—not scarcity—cause the people’s misery. They persuade the workers to organize production themselves, electing accountable coordinators and sharing the water equally rather than supporting masters. The people abolish the capitalist system, collectively manage their labor and resources, and create a society in which no one lacks water or other necessities.

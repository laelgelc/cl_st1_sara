The story adopts a mock-antiquarian, pseudo-chronicle style that blends historical narration, clerical anecdote, fabliau, and bawdy satire. Its narrator presents scandalous material with the solemnity of a scholar consulting proverbs, cartularies, and monastic records, thereby creating a persistent contrast between documentary authority and comic improbability. Formulae such as “the said abbey,” “the above-mentioned flea,” and “here are the facts of the case” parody legal, theological, and historiographical language. The narrator is conspicuously intrusive, addressing the reader, commenting on the reliability of traditions, digressing into explanations, and claiming to translate or modernise an old source. This cultivated pose of learned authenticity gives license to extravagant invention.

The tone is exuberant, irreverent, conspiratorial, and frequently obscene without relying on bluntly explicit vocabulary. Sexual and bodily matters are conveyed through euphemism, allegory, circumlocution, and comic substitution: keys, gates, books, fleas, breeches, instruments, relics, and religious offices repeatedly acquire erotic meanings. Much of the humour arises from the collision of sacred and profane registers. Confession, baptism, holy water, canonisation, monastic rules, divine providence, and ecclesiastical hierarchy are applied to lust, flatulence, constipation, venereal disease, and clothing. The narration does not reject religion wholesale; rather, it satirises institutional hypocrisy, casuistry, credulity, and the effort to impose pious interpretations upon ordinary physical appetites.

The diction is deliberately archaic and ceremonious. Terms such as “whence,” “thereupon,” “varlet,” “jade,” “harlot,” “chemise,” “orison,” and “Monseigneur” establish a late-medieval or Renaissance atmosphere, while French phrases, ecclesiastical terminology, place names, and references to saints and offices lend local colour. Elevated abstractions sit beside earthy colloquialisms, producing abrupt comic deflation. People are often described through humorous epithets—“merry abbot,” “good recluses,” “virtuous maidens,” “holy troop”—whose ostensible praise is undermined by context. Repeated adjectives such as “good,” “holy,” “virtuous,” “blessed,” and “innocent” function ironically.

The prose favours long, accumulative sentences packed with subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, parallel constructions, and catalogues. Lists are a signature device: the elaborate enumeration of romantic “preliminaries,” the inventory of female figures, and the sequence of a flea’s imagined organs all turn excess itself into comedy. Synonym chains—“gate, door, wicket, opening, or road”—linger over suggestive images while pretending to clarify them. Rhythmic repetition, balanced phrasing, escalation, and anticlimax give the narration an oral, performative quality. Short exclamations and blunt punch lines then interrupt these expansive passages, sharpening the comic effect.

Dialogue is lively, rapid, and theatrically staged. The nuns speak through questions, literal misunderstandings, teasing insinuations, and apparently innocent replies that expose unexpected experience. Much of the comedy depends on dramatic irony: naïve characters fail to understand double meanings, while others pretend not to understand them. Group laughter, blushing, whispering, bodily accidents, and interruptions create the atmosphere of a communal storytelling performance. Individual characters are lightly sketched rather than psychologically developed; they function primarily as comic voices, foils, dupes, or agents of verbal mischief.

The narrative structure is episodic and digressive. It begins with an antiquarian survey of Poissy’s scandalous reputation, moves into a framed account of nocturnal convent gatherings, expands into extended set pieces about flea-catching and Sister Petronille, and concludes with a separate anecdote about the archbishop’s breeches. Transitions such as “to return,” “but to continue,” and “I cannot leave them without relating” openly advertise the narrator’s loose control of the tale. Each episode develops from an ambiguous word or object, elaborates the misunderstanding to grotesque proportions, and ends with a maxim, retort, or comic moral.

Imagery is physical, abundant, and often grotesque. Bodies are described through food, animals, landscapes, mechanical objects, and architecture: laughter resembles ripe figs, nuns gather like ants, flesh contains valleys and mountains, and clothing becomes a habitation or abyss. Onomatopoeia and sensory detail intensify the farce, particularly in scenes involving eating, smell, disease, bodily functions, or material textures. Hyperbole is constant: rumours encompass every amorous invention, fleas undergo full religious conversion, and a repaired garment becomes an ornate ceremonial centrepiece.

The implied audience is treated as worldly and complicit. The narrator assumes that readers can decode innuendo and enjoy seeing solemn institutions overturned by appetite and accident. Moral conclusions are intentionally perfunctory or inverted. The closing claim that the bridegroom’s joke supplies all the moral required parodies the exemplary endings of traditional tales: matrimony, monastic chastity, charity, and episcopal dignity are finally compressed into the same bawdy emblem. Overall, the style is learned burlesque—ornate, archaic, digressive, hyperbolic, and cheerfully indecorous, with elaborate verbal wit transforming religious history into carnivalesque comedy.

The story uses an ornate, observant realist style that combines sensuous description, social satire, psychological irony and a tightly controlled anecdotal structure. Its opening is deliberately spectacular: the women appear “buried in a bed of flowers,” while their carriage becomes a “strange, rolling, flowery beast.” Dense inventories of flowers, colours, fabrics and decorative objects create an atmosphere of luxury and excess. The imagery repeatedly transforms material scenes into living tableaux: carriages form an endless ribbon, flowers fly like missiles, and distant warships resemble armoured apocalyptic animals. Beauty is therefore vivid but rarely innocent; beneath the floral softness lie violence, class division, erotic danger and humiliation.

The narration is third-person and largely external at first, moving like a camera across the festival crowd before narrowing its attention to the two women. It has an omniscient, socially knowing quality, evident in concise judgments such as the observation that the police keep the poor from mingling with the rich. Such remarks give the prose a satirical edge without interrupting the narrative for extended moral commentary. Once Marguerite begins her story, the mode changes into a first-person embedded narrative. This frame-tale construction shifts the story from public spectacle to private confession while preserving suspense: a casual conversation about servants’ love gradually leads to the revelation that the maid Rose is an escaped male convict.

The prose alternates between long, cumulative descriptive sentences and short exchanges of dialogue. Descriptive passages favour coordination, enumeration and layered modifiers, often delaying the main impression while details accumulate. The dialogue, by contrast, is quick, lightly provocative and full of questions, denials and interruptions. Dashes, repetitions and hesitations reproduce embarrassment and evasiveness, especially when the women approach erotic or socially improper subjects. The diction is formal and occasionally old-fashioned, with class-marked terms such as “landau,” “equipages,” “livery,” “concierge” and “domestic.” Some constructions have the stiffness of a period translation, but this also reinforces the story’s nineteenth-century social atmosphere.

Characterisation is achieved less through biography than through speech, gesture and reaction. Simone’s romantic selectiveness is opposed to Marguerite’s worldly claim that any man’s love is flattering. Marguerite’s anecdote then complicates her sophistication: she can calmly describe being bathed, dressed and massaged by Rose, yet feels profoundly humiliated when Rose is revealed as a man convicted of sexual violence. Her emotional response is presented with psychological precision and moral ambiguity. She is less troubled by physical intimacy or danger than by the implication that this particular man, supposedly sexually predatory, did not reveal desire for her. The story does not explain this bluntly; instead, it lets her broken phrasing and Simone’s final silence expose the wounded vanity beneath her account.

Irony governs both plot and tone. The women begin by debating whether affection from a servant could matter, and the tale ends with Simone contemplating the coachman’s shining buttons. Rose is described as the perfect maid precisely because the disguise overturns assumptions about gendered competence, touch and intimacy. The police revelation functions as a sharp reversal, but the deeper twist lies in Marguerite’s reaction. The narrative continually contrasts appearances with concealed realities: flowers mask bodies, social polish masks brutality, a maid conceals a man, and indignation conceals vanity or desire.

The tone moves from festive and languorous to comic, unsettling and suggestive. Humour appears in the mock warfare of the flower festival, the women’s amused condescension toward enamoured servants and the bureaucratic absurdity of the police search. Yet the comedy is edged with menace through references to criminality, assault and coercive class relations. The final “sphinx’s smile” avoids resolution and leaves Simone’s thoughts deliberately unreadable. This elliptical ending is characteristic of the story’s method: rather than stating a moral, it closes on a charged visual detail that echoes the earlier conversation and invites the reader to infer a shift in desire.

Overall, the style is pictorial, ironic and psychologically economical. It begins with luxuriant surfaces, passes through polished conversational comedy, and ends by revealing the unstable desires hidden beneath social convention. Its most distinctive effects come from sensuous catalogues, abrupt reversals, class-conscious observation, suggestive omissions and a final image whose ambiguity carries more force than explicit explanation.

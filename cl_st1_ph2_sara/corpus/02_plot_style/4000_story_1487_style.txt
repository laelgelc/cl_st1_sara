The story adopts the manner of an antiquarian supernatural romance, presenting itself as the retelling of an old Chinese legend. Its narrative voice is omniscient, ceremonious, and faintly oral: it opens with a poetic citation, addresses the reader directly through rhetorical questions, invokes traditional storytellers and books as sources, and then withdraws into a largely uninterrupted historical narration. This framing gives the tale the authority of folklore while preserving a sense of mystery. The narrator frequently anticipates later events or interprets them with gentle hindsight, creating the fatalistic impression that Ming-Y’s enchantment and loss have been ordained from the beginning.

The diction is elevated, archaic, and highly ornamental. Forms such as “thither,” “hither,” “shouldst,” “beheld,” “ere,” and “suffer him to go” create the atmosphere of an old translation or romance. Polite speech is elaborately formal, especially in dialogue, where characters refer to “my honored visitor,” “your excellent patron,” and “my poor family.” Chinese names, titles, customs, objects, festivals, literary allusions, and translated maxims are densely incorporated into the prose. These details produce cultural specificity, although they are filtered through a consciously exoticizing, late-nineteenth-century literary idiom that emphasizes remoteness, refinement, and strangeness.

Sentences are often long, cumulative, and rhythmically balanced. They gather clauses through semicolons, dashes, repeated conjunctions, and parallel constructions. Repetition is a major musical device: “with the lisping of the leaves; with the quivering of many-fingered boughs; with the fluttering of lights and shadows” turns description into incantation. Periodic sentences delay their central action while surrounding it with atmospheric qualifications. Shorter exclamations and direct statements appear at moments of revelation or emotional intensity, giving the climactic discoveries greater force.

The prose is intensely sensory. Color is especially prominent: violet skies, azure roofs, crimson flowers, purple wine, phantom gold, pale silk, yellow jade, and green moss give the setting a jewel-like richness. Smell and sound are equally important, with blossom perfume, incense, bird-song, whispering leaves, silken-stringed music, and the “silver-sweet” voice of Sië creating a dreamlike environment. Natural phenomena are repeatedly personified: flowers open their eyes, shadows dream, stars watch, trees remember, and the forest whispers the dead woman’s name. Similes draw on flowers, butterflies, brooks, silk, lacquer, gems, incense, and changing light, making beauty seem at once delicate, artistic, and unreal.

The dominant mood is languorous, sensual, and elegiac. Sië’s world is marked by softness, perfume, silence, music, wine, twilight, and secluded woodland. Erotic feeling is conveyed through enchantment rather than explicit physical detail: voices blend, vision grows luminous, time dissolves, and the lovers’ senses are overcome. Pleasure is continually shadowed by mortality. Autumn, fading light, old songs, buried objects, ruined architecture, and dying flowers gradually disclose that the romance belongs to the past and cannot endure in ordinary time.

Structurally, the story follows a clear folkloric progression: introductory legend, moral warning, chance encounter, invitation into an enchanted dwelling, secret love, discovery by elders, supernatural revelation, and retrospective epilogue. Foreshadowing is explicit but effective. The parental warning against female beauty, Sië’s knowledge of ancient art, her mastery of obsolete songs, the antiquity of her possessions, and her cryptic account of her identity all prepare the final disclosure. The disappearance of the mansion converts earlier decorative details into evidence of haunting, while the return to the opening line about peach blossoms over Sië-Thao’s tomb gives the narrative a circular form.

Dialogue serves both characterization and exposition. Ming-Y speaks with youthful reverence and emotional excess; Sië combines formal modesty with intimate confidence; the father and commissioner speak in the language of patriarchal authority, law, reason, and interpretation. Sië’s speech is musical and evasive, often containing riddles or concealed truths. The final explanatory speech decodes her names, possessions, literary preferences, and historical associations in the manner of a traditional supernatural tale, where rational inquiry does not disprove the ghost but identifies it.

The pacing is expansive during scenes of beauty and intimacy and compressed across longer spans of time. Individual journeys, rooms, songs, and farewells receive luxuriant description, whereas an entire summer passes in a few summarizing paragraphs. This contrast reproduces Ming-Y’s enchanted consciousness: nights with Sië seem timeless, while the external world advances rapidly toward exposure and separation. The climax is not violent but visual and interpretive—the vanished house, the ancient tomb, and the recognition of Sië-Thao’s identity.

Thematically, the style links love with memory, art, and haunting. Poetry, music, calligraphy, and crafted objects survive the bodies and dynasties that produced them; Sië herself resembles such an artwork, preserved beyond death through desire and recollection. The tale also opposes youthful passion to filial duty and social order, yet it does not treat the supernatural beloved as simply evil. She deceives Ming-Y, but she is tender, cultured, and resigned to loss. The closing image of the jade lion and agate brush-case quietly preserves the affair without breaking Ming-Y’s lifelong silence, leaving material beauty to bear witness where speech cannot.

The story adopts an ornate, antiquarian Gothic-horror style, told retrospectively by an educated first-person narrator. The voice is formal, self-conscious and analytical, presenting terror through the vocabulary of reason, philosophy and natural history rather than through colloquial immediacy. Elevated or archaic terms such as “sepulchre,” “betwixt,” “whilst,” “naught” and “despatching” create a distinctly nineteenth-century register, while repeated references to composure, reflection and rational inference establish a narrator who prides himself on intellectual control. His eventual collapse into shouting, gibbering and horror supplies an ironic contrast to that cultivated reserve.

The prose is highly Latinate and elaborative. Sentences are often long, periodic and syntactically layered, with subordinate clauses delaying the main point while qualifications and deductions accumulate. Dense adjective clusters—“vast and labyrinthine,” “total and almost palpable blackness,” “hideous and fearsome shapes”—intensify the atmosphere. Abstract nouns such as “tranquillity,” “realisation,” “apprehensions,” “locomotion” and “extinction” give even violent or emotional events a clinical formality. This creates a characteristic tension between detached explanation and mounting dread: the narrator repeatedly classifies, hypothesises and rationalises phenomena that are becoming increasingly irrational.

Atmosphere is built through confinement, darkness and sensory deprivation. The cave is treated almost as a living cosmic environment: immense, silent, subterranean and indifferent, with its “bowels,” “ramparts,” “inky confines” and “interminable recesses.” Vision disappears when the torch fails, so sound becomes the principal mechanism of suspense. Soft steps, irregular patterns of movement, breathing, impacts, metallic clicks and finally speech are introduced in carefully graded stages. Repetition and incremental phrasing—“Nearer, nearer,” “now close at hand; now very close”—slow narrative time and reproduce the narrator’s strained attention. Darkness also licenses imaginative projection, allowing “disordered fancy” to populate the unseen space with grotesque possibilities.

The narrative structure is linear and tightly organised around escalating revelation. It opens after the central predicament has already become certain, then moves from resignation to a faint hope of rescue, from hope to animal threat, from confrontation to apparent escape, and finally from investigation to the climactic discovery. Several false interpretations control the suspense: the footsteps are first imagined to belong to the guide, then to a mountain lion, then to an unknown cave-adapted ape. Each explanation is plausible within the narrator’s limited knowledge but is displaced by something more disturbing. The rescue therefore functions as a false resolution; safety and restored light do not end the horror but make its true nature visible.

The creature is described through pseudo-scientific observation. Its pigmentation, hair, eyes, gait, claws and facial structure are catalogued as adaptations to prolonged cave life. This taxonomic language lends credibility to the fantastic premise and postpones recognition by encouraging both narrator and reader to regard the figure as zoological. The grotesque effect depends on unstable boundaries between human and animal: hands become “paws,” movement alternates between quadrupedal and bipedal, and the face is measured against that of an ape. The final human utterance abruptly overturns this classification.

Emotion is amplified through melodramatic and superlative language: conclusions are “horrible,” certainty is “awful,” darkness is “sinister,” and the monster is the strangest ever beheld. Exclamation is generally withheld until moments of extreme release, making the final capitalised “MAN!!!” deliberately theatrical. The ending operates as a compressed shock revelation, explicitly stating the truth after a long sequence of clues. Its horror is complicated by a sudden shift from fear to “wonder, awe, compassion, and reverence,” transforming the supposed monster into a victim and the narrator into an unwitting killer.

Overall, the style combines Gothic sensationalism, antiquarian diction, psychological narration and speculative naturalism. Its signature effect comes from filtering primal fear through an excessively rational, ceremonious voice: the more methodically the narrator explains the unknown, the more forcefully the unknown defeats his categories.

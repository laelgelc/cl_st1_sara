The story uses a restrained, morally earnest realist style, centred on public debate but driven by private psychological change. Its narrative voice is third-person omniscient with a close and increasingly sustained focus on Senator Harrison. The narrator can report the external business of the Senate chamber, summarise Dorman’s argument, and enter Harrison’s thoughts with equal ease. Much of the drama therefore occurs inwardly: Harrison’s fixed confidence gradually gives way to doubt, imaginative sympathy, guilt, and decisive action. Free indirect discourse blends his reflections with the narrator’s voice, allowing his self-justifications and changing moral perceptions to unfold without heavy authorial explanation.

The prose is formal, lucid, and characteristic of early twentieth-century social fiction. Its vocabulary combines political and legal language—“resolution,” “pardon,” “moral responsibility,” “constituents,” “reconsideration”—with accessible domestic details and lyrical natural imagery. Sentences are often long and cumulative when tracing thought, balancing clauses through repetition, qualification, and rhetorical questions. Short sentences and fragments are reserved for emphasis, especially at turning points: “The whole thing was very confusing,” or “The boy in the penitentiary was denied his chance.” Dashes frequently mark hesitation, revision, emotional pressure, or the movement from abstraction to concrete feeling. Repeated constructions such as “He wondered,” “He was thinking,” and “another chance” create rhythm while charting Harrison’s moral awakening.

Dialogue is used selectively and theatrically. Dorman’s speech is polished, expansive, and persuasive, relying on parallelism, direct appeals, controlled repetition, and vivid hypothetical comparisons. Harrison’s climactic speech, by contrast, is halting and inarticulate. His stammered declaration carries greater moral force precisely because it lacks rhetorical finish. This contrast between eloquence and sincerity is central to the style: public language may initiate change, but the deepest conviction appears as awkward, almost embarrassed action. The chamber’s observers—reporters, senators, spectators—also create a self-conscious atmosphere in which every gesture may become performance, political calculation, or newspaper copy.

The story is structured as a conversion narrative. It begins after Harrison has completed a confident argument against the pardon, then allows Dorman’s speech to provide the factual history of Alfred Williams while Harrison’s thoughts supply the emotional and ethical response. The movement is gradual and carefully staged: intellectual unease leads to personal comparison, personal comparison to imaginative identification, and identification to fear of lifelong guilt. A first, narrowly defeated vote creates a false ending before Harrison’s motion to reconsider produces the reversal. The second vote resolves the public conflict, while the final conversation and outdoor scene resolve Harrison’s private understanding of what he has done.

Irony shapes both characterisation and moral effect. Harrison initially prides himself on being logical, dispassionate, unpopular, and sacrificial, yet his supposed objectivity is exposed as vanity and emotional distance. After performing an act everyone else calls heroic, he insists that it was cowardice because he feared being haunted in old age. This self-deprecating interpretation complicates the triumph and prevents him from becoming a simple moral hero. There is also situational irony in the fact that his strongest opposition makes the pardon’s defeat possible, while his abrupt reversal gives the cause an overwhelming victory. The story treats moral courage as something that may feel humiliating from within even when it looks magnificent from outside.

The emotional method depends on translating abstract justice into concrete deprivation. Alfred Williams never appears directly; he is constructed through testimony, imagined scenes, and emblematic absences. The story moves from broad concepts such as responsibility, environment, punishment, and social science to the sensory particulars of a lost childhood: the unused swimming hole, the uncaught fish, the unattended ball game, the unseen circus, and especially the unowned dog. These modest details are more affecting than grand declarations because they make the boy’s exclusion from ordinary life tangible. Likewise, the prison is represented through denied experiences—the sunrise, sunset, stars, and “voices of the night”—rather than through physical brutality alone.

Nature imagery forms the story’s principal symbolic pattern. Harrison repeatedly looks toward the “budding things” on the State-house grounds while considering whether Alfred should receive another chance. Spring growth, birdsong, sunlight, night, and the stars stand for participation in life and the universe. The imagery initially remains outside Harrison, something he merely observes; by the close, it becomes the basis of his moral reconciliation. Trees are permitted to bud again, birds to sing again, and the earth to yield again, so pardon is aligned with the regenerative order of nature. This symbolism is explicit and sentimental, but it is anchored by recurring visual details and by the seasonal setting of an April afternoon.

The tone develops from coolly observant and faintly satirical to compassionate, elegiac, and finally affirmative. Early descriptions of reporters sharpening pencils and senators pretending to read the Journal gently mock political ceremony and public self-consciousness. As Harrison imagines the prisoner shut away from childhood, nature, and companionship, the prose becomes more lyrical and emotionally heightened. The ending avoids unqualified celebration: Harrison has sacrificed his political future and still feels exposed before his constituents. Nevertheless, the final “tranquil sense of unison with Life” gives the story a redemptive close.

Overall, the style joins political realism, psychological narration, social protest, and humane sentiment. Its governing technique is moral perspective-taking: the repeated effort to “put yourself in his place.” It argues that justice without imaginative sympathy becomes mechanical and that genuine ethical understanding begins when categories such as criminal, constituent, and senator give way to recognition of a fellow living creature.

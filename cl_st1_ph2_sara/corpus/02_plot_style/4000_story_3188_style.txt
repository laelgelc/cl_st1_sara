The story uses a retrospective first-person confessional voice. Its narrator speaks with controlled intelligence and absolute moral certainty, addressing an implied listener who supposedly knows “the nature of my soul.” This creates intimacy without trust: the reader is admitted into the narrator’s reasoning but given no independent confirmation that the original grievance justified his revenge. The long interval between crime and narration further intensifies his coldness, since the account remains proud, lucid, and largely unrepentant after fifty years.

The diction is formal, elevated, and faintly archaic, drawing on words such as “impunity,” “immolation,” “retribution,” “flambeaux,” and “hearkened.” Italian and French cultural terms, heraldic language, wine terminology, and Latin phrases give the prose an aristocratic, Old World character. This cultivated vocabulary contrasts with the primitive brutality of the act being described. The narrator often uses balanced maxims and abstract formulations—especially in the opening discussion of punishment and redress—to present revenge as if it were a logical or philosophical principle rather than an emotional obsession.

Sentence structure alternates between elaborate, carefully subordinated narration and terse dramatic statements. Longer sentences explain plans, surroundings, and psychological calculations with measured precision; short clauses such as “No answer” or “The Amontillado!” punctuate the account at moments of tension. Repetition is prominent, particularly in the dialogue, where the wine’s name functions as lure, refrain, and grim joke. Exclamations, interrupted phrases, dashes, and transcribed sounds—the cough, screams, laughter, chains, and jingling bells—bring a theatrical, almost audible quality to the scene.

The tone is urbane, ironic, and increasingly macabre. Montresor’s surface courtesy never disappears, even when he is leading Fortunato to death. Terms such as “my dear Fortunato,” “my friend,” and “my poor friend” therefore acquire a sadistic double meaning. The narrator repeatedly urges his victim to turn back while knowing that Fortunato’s vanity will make retreat impossible. This sustained dramatic irony is the story’s chief stylistic mechanism: nearly every expression of concern conceals manipulation, and innocuous conversational remarks become threats understood only by the narrator and reader.

Dialogue drives much of the action. The exchanges are brief, repetitive, and strategically asymmetrical: Montresor supplies suggestions, while Fortunato responds through pride, intoxication, and fixation. The absent rival Luchresi is used as a psychological lever, and the Masonic exchange introduces a compact pun that also foreshadows the murder weapon. Fortunato means membership in a brotherhood; Montresor answers by producing a mason’s trowel. Such wordplay is not comic relief so much as an extension of the narrator’s cruel control.

The descriptive style is strongly Gothic, relying on enclosed spaces, darkness, decay, bodily remains, dampness, and failing light. The movement from carnival streets to palazzo, vaults, catacombs, crypt, and final recess creates progressive spatial compression. Visual imagery—white nitre, black silk, piled skeletons, glowing torches—is reinforced by tactile dampness and an especially rich soundscape of coughing, bells, clanking chains, screams, and silence. The carnival’s colour and noise stand in sharp contrast to the underground world, while Fortunato’s jester costume makes his fate both grotesque and symbolically humiliating.

Pacing is deliberate and processional. The opening reveals the intention to take revenge, so suspense comes not from whether violence will occur but from how the plan will be executed and when Fortunato will understand it. The repeated descents, pauses for wine, warnings about the cough, and references to the nitre delay the climax while steadily narrowing the victim’s options. Once Fortunato is chained, the narration becomes methodical: the wall rises tier by tier, turning construction into a countdown. Brief pauses to hear the chains or answer the screams prolong the cruelty and expose the narrator’s pleasure.

The structure is tightly unified through foreshadowing and recurring motifs. The family motto, “Nemo me impune lacessit,” restates the opening doctrine of revenge; the coat of arms depicts injury and retaliation; the trowel anticipates the method of execution; the cough prompts Fortunato’s unwitting prophecy that he “shall not die of a cough.” Wine, bells, masonry, dampness, and burial recur with altered significance. Even the victim’s name is ironic: Fortunato is anything but fortunate, while his carnival identity as a fool externalizes the vanity that destroys him.

Psychological characterization is indirect and concentrated. Fortunato is defined chiefly by pride in his connoisseurship, susceptibility to rivalry, drunkenness, and delayed recognition of danger. Montresor is characterized through the flawless correspondence between his stated theory and his actions. His insistence on total impunity, his enjoyment of the victim’s terror, and his final attribution of sickness to “the dampness” reveal repression or calculated self-deception. The narrative never explains the “insult,” leaving the disproportion between grievance and punishment unresolved and making the narrator’s reliability deeply suspect.

The ending is compressed, quiet, and ceremonially final. After screams, bargaining, and invocation of God, silence and the faint bells replace direct human speech. The restoration of the bones conceals both the crime and the newly built wall, completing the narrator’s demand for secrecy and impunity. The closing Latin blessing, “In pace requiescat,” sounds solemn and pious but remains bitterly ironic when spoken by the murderer. Overall, the style combines formal confession, Gothic sensory detail, tightly controlled dramatic irony, symbolic patterning, and ritualistic pacing to produce an atmosphere of claustrophobic inevitability.

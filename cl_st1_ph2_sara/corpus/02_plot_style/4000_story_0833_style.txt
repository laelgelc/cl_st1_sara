The story uses a richly localised regional-realist style, rooted in the social world of Louisiana’s Acadian prairie. Its setting is rendered through concrete cultural details—rice and cane fields, plantations, galleries, Catholic observances, gumbo, quadrilles, and communal balls—rather than through lengthy scenic exposition. French and Creole expressions are woven into the English, while dialogue is represented phonetically to distinguish Acadian and African American speech. This code-switching gives the narrative a strong oral and communal texture, establishes class and ethnic identities, and makes language itself part of the setting. The racial terminology and some character descriptions reflect the prejudices and conventions of the historical period.

The narrator is third-person omniscient but highly mobile, moving among Bobint’s jealousy, Alcee’s frustration, Clarisse’s anxiety, and Calixta’s volatile desires. Free indirect discourse frequently blurs narration with a character’s thoughts, as in Bobint’s rhetorical questioning and Alcee’s sudden revaluation of his ruined crop. The narrative voice can be intimate and sympathetic, yet it also maintains an amused, lightly ironic distance. Characters often misunderstand themselves or act from impulses they cannot fully articulate, while the narrator quietly exposes the contrast between their declared intentions and their actual behaviour.

The prose alternates between sensuous description and brisk storytelling. Physical appearance is conveyed through accumulative lists, parallel phrases, and vivid comparisons: eyes are “bluest” and “drowsiest,” a voice resembles a contralto song, and Clarisse is successively likened to a lily, sunflower, and marsh reed. Such imagery is tactile, musical, and bodily, with particular attention to eyes, hair, mouths, feet, clothing, touch, heat, moonlight, and movement. Nature also carries emotional force. The cyclone externalises Alcee’s devastation, while darkness, dawn, mosquitoes, wet grass, and the changing light accompany shifts in desire and decision.

Sentence rhythms are varied. Long, flowing sentences accumulate impressions or reproduce emotional agitation, while short declarative statements deliver judgments, reversals, and comic effects: “That was Alcee”; “They were acting like fools.” Rhetorical questions, exclamations, repetitions, fragments, and dashes create an energetic, speech-inflected cadence. Dialogue is often rapid and confrontational, revealing character through teasing, evasion, commands, and verbal sparring rather than through explicit explanation. Repeated questions—especially Clarisse’s demand to know where Alcee has gone—build tension, while the phonetic dialogue slows the reader into hearing each speaker’s distinctive voice.

Structurally, the story proceeds through intercut romantic trajectories. Bobint’s anxious pursuit of Calixta is set against Alcee’s frustrated passion for Clarisse; the ball brings these lines together, then separates and resolves them through paired reversals. Calixta turns to Bobint after Alcee abandons her, while Clarisse’s confession transforms Alcee’s despair into exhilaration. The parallel phrasing around the two men’s sudden happiness reinforces the symmetry. Scene changes are cinematic, shifting from fields and plantation rooms to moonlit galleries, the crowded dance hall, the dawn prairie, and finally the woods. Typographical rows of dots create clear pauses between the concluding movements.

The pacing expands around charged encounters and compresses transitional action. Exposition about crops, family, reputation, and custom is delivered economically, but moments of erotic or emotional tension receive close attention: a held hand, an earring touched, lips brushing an ear, a hidden face against a saddle. Desire is generally conveyed through gesture, proximity, and sensory detail rather than explicit statement. The overall tone combines sensuality, comedy, social observation, and irony. Although the ending supplies romantic pairings, it is not conventionally sentimental: Calixta’s proposal is practical and emotionally compromised, Bobint accepts limited affection, and Alcee instantly relegates Calixta to “a myth.” The style therefore presents love as impulsive, unstable, socially conditioned, and capable of transforming catastrophe into apparent insignificance.

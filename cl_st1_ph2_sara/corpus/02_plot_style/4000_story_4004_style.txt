The story adopts a witty, polemical, modernist style that blends social satire, mock inquiry, fantasy, and conversational comedy. Its surface manner is light, urbane, and deliberately ingenuous, while its underlying argument is sharply critical of patriarchy, masculine institutions, literary authority, imperialism, and war. Serious questions about education, maternity, intellectual prestige, and women’s autonomy are approached through absurd situations and comic reversals rather than direct exposition.

The narrative is told retrospectively in the first-person plural, with Cassandra acting as the individual narrator within a collective female “we.” This communal perspective gives the story the air of an anecdote, a society’s minutes, and a shared intellectual awakening at once. Cassandra’s voice is intimate and apparently artless, often qualifying events with phrases such as “so far as I can remember” or “I have forgotten,” but this casual imprecision is strategic: it contrasts with the women’s increasingly systematic investigations and mocks conventional claims to scholarly certainty. Direct addresses to the reader create the atmosphere of an informal oral account.

The dominant tone is ironic, playful, and deadpan. Extraordinary premises are narrated as though entirely reasonable, while accepted social beliefs are treated as strange hypotheses requiring evidence. Men’s greatness, women’s duty to bear children, chastity, honour, scholarship, and literary reputation are all subjected to literal-minded scrutiny. The story’s central satirical method is reversal: women become investigators and judges, men become specimens, and institutions ordinarily associated with reason and dignity reveal themselves as irrational, sterile, or theatrical. Understatement frequently intensifies the comedy, especially when humiliating, violent, or historically momentous events are described in decorous social language.

The prose alternates between long, accumulating sentences and quick exchanges of dialogue. Extended sentences often pile up examples, qualifications, institutional names, or escalating absurdities, reproducing the momentum of animated conversation and collective discovery. Short questions and exclamations—“Why?”, “The truth!”, “What war?”—interrupt these passages and create a brisk dramatic rhythm. Parenthetical asides, sudden corrections, digressions, and remembered details make the narration feel spontaneous, while carefully controlled repetition supplies rhetorical force. Lists are especially important: sequences of authors, professions, public institutions, statistics, imperial possessions, and masculine achievements build an impression of overwhelming civilisation before exposing its moral incoherence.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization and argument. The women have distinct comic tendencies: Poll is blunt and book-saturated, Jane procedural, Castalia impulsive and paradoxical, while the investigators report in voices coloured by their subjects. Conversations repeatedly turn into miniature debates, but logic is destabilized by jokes, interruptions, evasions, and physical comedy. Official or prestigious language is mimicked and deflated. Euphemisms and abstract ideals such as “honour,” “chastity,” “civilisation,” and “intellect” are tested against bodily realities, economic motives, hunger, childbirth, sexuality, and death.

The diction combines cultivated literary language with colloquial bluntness. Elevated phrases such as “intensity of desolation,” “the objects of life,” and “the marvels of civilisation” coexist with “rubbish,” “fiddlesticks,” “damn,” and “oh, bother.” This mixture prevents the philosophical argument from becoming solemn and repeatedly punctures inflated rhetoric. Proper names and recognizable institutions lend topical specificity, while comic names such as Lord Bunkum and Sir Harley Tightboots openly signal caricature. Literary quotations are jumbled into gibberish, showing how cultural prestige can become mechanical repetition rather than genuine understanding.

Structurally, the story moves through a series of episodic reports held together by a mock-scientific enquiry. It begins with domestic leisure and naïve praise of men, expands into investigations of libraries, universities, courts, the navy, galleries, business, literature, empire, and science, and then encounters the catastrophic interruption of war. This widening scale allows the satire to move from private assumptions to national and civilizational systems. The time jump from the declaration of war to the peace treaty darkens the comedy and makes the earlier games of inquiry appear both prophetic and inadequate. The ending returns to the domestic sphere and passes the unfinished investigation to a child, creating a circular but forward-looking conclusion.

The story’s humour depends heavily on incongruity. Naval honour is reduced to calculations about caning; judges resemble trained animals; scholarship becomes a monumental defence of Sappho’s chastity centred on an object like a hairpin; literary reviewing becomes a refusal to judge literature; and technological achievement is set beside poverty, imprisonment, maternal mortality, and imperial violence. These comic set pieces are not detachable jokes but enact the story’s central suspicion that intellectual systems can conceal absurdity and brutality behind ceremony and expertise.

Beneath the exuberance lies an increasingly melancholy tonal current. Early laughter gives way to uncertainty, war, exhaustion, rain, failed fireworks, and anxiety about what children can be taught to believe. The conclusion does not offer a stable doctrine. Even faith in masculine intellect is exposed as a manufactured social arrangement, while literacy is both a burden and the basis of liberation. The final emphasis on teaching a girl to believe in herself preserves a qualified hope, but the child’s tears keep the ending characteristically ambivalent: emancipation is necessary, yet knowledge brings responsibility, disillusionment, and pain.

The story is a first-person comic satire told in the manner of an anecdotal tall tale. Its narrator is educated enough to be a lawyer and businessman, but he is vain, irritable, acquisitive, and largely blind to his own absurdity. He presents himself as the reasonable party even while fantasising about murdering a friend over radio equipment, exploiting cemetery lots, and accepting increasingly ludicrous events as ordinary. This gap between his self-image and his behaviour creates much of the humour. Direct addresses such as “Yes, sir” and “You know the sort of program” give the narrative the conversational air of a story being told aloud to a familiar listener.

The tone combines deadpan seriousness, genial exaggeration, and escalating farce. Absurd ideas are described in sober legal, commercial, and technical language: a radio-equipped tomb, perpetual battery maintenance, exclusive loud-speaking rights, and a cemetery’s profitability are all discussed as practical business matters. The comedy depends on treating the grotesque and impossible as administratively routine. Hyperbole is frequent, from acquaintances fleeing across vacant lots to every corpse being removed from the cemetery. Violent impulses, death, religious uplift, and commercial opportunism are rendered lightly rather than grimly, producing a macabre but buoyant humour.

The plot is tightly constructed around ironic reversal. It begins with enthusiasm for a fashionable new technology, introduces Solander’s grand scheme, shows the scheme becoming a spectacular commercial success, and then destroys it through an unforeseen regulatory change. The will’s meticulous safeguards, intended to make Solander’s wishes permanent, become the precise reason the mistake cannot be corrected. The ending completes the reversal: the solemn “Talking Tomb” becomes a “Jazz Tomb,” the fashionable cemetery becomes deserted, and the supposedly unfortunate dead man is declared “the lucky man.” Foreshadowing appears in the opening editorial-like paragraph, which warns against putting radio provisions into a will, and in the narrator’s repeated emphasis on the speed and unpredictability of technological change.

The prose is plain, brisk, and highly accessible, with long narrative sentences broken up by short emphatic statements and dialogue. Repetition supplies rhythm and comic insistence: “I told him I was,” “That was so, too,” and repeated references to the fourteen volumes, the fixed wavelength, and the cemetery’s new addition keep the central absurdities prominent. Lists are used for accumulation, especially when cataloguing the will’s restrictions, the broadcast material, or the effects of the disaster. Parenthetical qualifications and self-justifying asides make the narrator sound spontaneous and defensive. Dashes, exclamations, italics or verbal emphasis, and abrupt turns such as “and then the thing happened!” imitate animated oral storytelling.

Dialogue is central to characterisation. The opening argument uses extravagant comic abuse—“lunkheaded noodle,” “pin-headed idiot,” and “half-baked lunatic”—to establish the obsessive temperament of radio enthusiasts. Solander speaks in solemn, repetitive, ceremonious phrases, contrasting his lofty ideals with the impracticality of his plan. His slow delivery is caricatured through vivid similes: his words “drip down his long chin like cold molasses,” while his eyes resemble those of “a salt mackerel.” Later, snippets of popular songs and vaudeville-style patter sharply contrast with the hymns, sermons, missionary reports, and religious poems that were meant to issue from the tomb.

The diction mixes colloquial American speech with the vocabularies of law, commerce, religion, and early radio. Expressions such as “smell a rat,” “sell like hot cakes,” “old codger,” and “went right up in the air” reinforce the informal period voice. Technical details—crystal and tube sets, “A” and “B” batteries, wavelengths, call letters, hook-ups, and loud speakers—lend plausibility while also dating the story to the early broadcasting era. Legal phrases such as “last will and testament,” “provided,” and “perpetual care and maintenance” are repeated until their solemnity becomes comic. The period style also includes topical references to prohibition, jazz, flappers, popular novelty songs, and ethnic vaudeville humour.

The story satirises technological faith, attempts to control the future, moral self-importance, commercial greed, and the instability of fashion. Solander believes radio is “imperishable,” yet its standards change almost immediately. His effort to impose uplift on posterity instead broadcasts the popular culture he despises. The cemetery trustees publicly endorse solemnity while privately calculating lot prices, publicity, and expansion. Religion, death, and memorialisation are therefore continually entangled with advertising and profit. The satire is not delivered through explicit moral commentary; it emerges from the narrator’s candid calculations and his failure to recognise how mercenary he sounds.

Imagery is concrete, caricatural, and deliberately incongruous. Solander’s “sheeplike face,” molasses-like speech, mackerel eyes, and monumental granite tomb turn him into a comic type rather than a psychologically complex figure. The final image of the emptied cemetery as a battlefield full of shell-holes pushes the consequences into surreal exaggeration. Overall, the style is that of an early twentieth-century American magazine humour piece: fast-moving, plot-driven, orally inflected, rich in comic repetition and caricature, and organised around a single ingenious premise whose logic is pursued to a disastrous punch line.

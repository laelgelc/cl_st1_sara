The story is a first-person comic anecdote framed by a chance encounter on a railway journey. The narrator is an observant, mildly self-mocking gentleman who functions chiefly as listener and straight man. His opening description is concrete and economical, establishing character through small physical actions and conspicuous objects: weekly papers, a silk handkerchief, a horseshoe and a bald head. The narrative then passes almost entirely to the unlucky traveller’s embedded monologue, before returning to the railway carriage for a brief closing image. This circular frame gives the story a polished anecdotal shape: the horseshoe initiates the discussion of luck and supplies the final, visible proof of its malign effects.

The dominant tone is dry, urbane and deadpan. Absurd incidents are reported with composure, while emotional reactions are understated. Remarks such as the narrator’s admission that asking whether the horseshoe hurt was “a foolish question” create humour through self-correction and obviousness. The unlucky man likewise treats disasters as ordinary inconveniences, saying that the falling horseshoe hurt only “a little” even as the swelling grows to the size of an egg. This contrast between calm delivery and escalating calamity is central to the comic method.

The humour depends heavily on irony and reversal. Objects conventionally associated with good fortune—a horseshoe, a black cat, a piebald horse and an unexpected Christmas gift—produce injury, romantic loss, unemployment, drunkenness or arrest. Luck is therefore treated not merely as unreliable but as perversely literal: every apparent blessing contains the mechanism of disaster. The story repeatedly allows a hopeful interpretation to arise, only to overturn it. The comic pattern becomes cumulative, with each new example encouraging the reader to anticipate a worse consequence.

Characterisation is indirect and action-based. The unlucky traveller is patient, credulous, passive and faintly self-pitying, yet his own poor judgement plainly contributes to his troubles. He blames fate, although the goose episode reveals vanity, irritability, indecision and a susceptibility to drink. His insistence that he is “unaccustomed” to alcohol grows funnier as he visits one public house after another. The narrator rarely challenges him directly; instead, brief questions expose the gaps in his reasoning. This preserves the speaker’s dignity while letting the reader perceive his comic unreliability.

Dialogue drives the prose. Exchanges in the railway carriage are short, courteous and rhythmically timed, often following a question-and-answer structure. Within the goose narrative, quoted speech creates a succession of miniature scenes involving landlords, drinkers, a poulterer, policemen and an inspector. Each secondary character is sketched through a line or two of idiomatic speech. Colloquial contractions and period expressions—“big ’un,” “chap,” “crib,” “governor,” “beggar,” “Bobby”—give the story a late-Victorian or Edwardian English social texture. The diction alternates between the narrator’s formal phrasing and the traveller’s conversational vernacular.

The syntax is generally clear and chronological, suited to oral storytelling. Short sentences deliver reversals and punch lines, while longer compound sentences track increasingly tangled actions. Repetition provides both momentum and comic structure: the traveller tries to sell the goose, raffle it, give it away, abandon it and finally throw it into the canal, only for each solution to fail. Recurrent formulations such as “I thought,” “I went,” “I offered” and “I explained” mimic a man patiently presenting what he regards as a reasonable chain of conduct. Parenthetical qualifications and sober afterthoughts sharpen the irony.

Pacing begins briskly, slows into a series of compact examples, and then expands dramatically for the goose episode. That episode is organised as an escalating ordeal. Each attempt to dispose of the bird introduces a new location, social encounter and humiliation. Public houses serve as transitional stations, while the goose becomes progressively muddier, heavier and more sinister. The rising sequence culminates in the abrupt revelation that the recovered parcel contains a dead baby. This is a strong instance of black comedy: the narrative leaps from farce into grotesque criminal suspicion without abandoning its matter-of-fact tone.

The setting is rendered through precise English place names and everyday institutions rather than lyrical description. Ipswich, Manningtree, Liverpool Street and numerous London roads create geographical specificity and lend plausibility to an improbable tale. Railway compartments, clerks’ offices, public houses, shops, streets and police stations form a recognisable urban world governed by social embarrassment, class assumptions and bureaucratic procedure. Description remains functional; places matter mainly as stages on which the next mishap occurs.

The prose uses restrained figurative language. Most comic force comes from exact physical detail, incongruous measurement and mock precision: the horseshoe weighs “three pounds at the least,” the goose seems to reach thirty-six pounds, and the mysterious offer is sevenpence halfpenny. Such specificity makes exaggeration sound factual. Elevated phrases—including references to Providence, Fate, “apostrophising the universe” and fighting for a “heavenly crown”—are juxtaposed with mundane objects and low social comedy, creating a mild mock-heroic effect.

The story’s structure relies on delayed explanation. The traveller first states the consequence of each lucky object, then reveals the chain of events that produced it. The black-cat anecdote is elliptical, allowing the reader to infer that the cat killed the young woman’s canary and thereby ruined the romance. The piebald-horse episode is more fully narrated, and the goose episode becomes an extended set piece. This progressive enlargement prevents monotony and gives the story a sense of mounting comic ambition.

The ending is concise and visually controlled. After the long narrative, the action returns to the carriage, where the man cannot put on his hat because of the horseshoe-induced swelling. His quiet declaration that he does not believe much in luck is therefore unnecessary in literal terms but effective as character comedy: the physical gag has already made the point. Overall, the style combines conversational narration, social observation, cumulative farce, ironic understatement and a carefully engineered escalation from plausible inconvenience to extravagant disaster.

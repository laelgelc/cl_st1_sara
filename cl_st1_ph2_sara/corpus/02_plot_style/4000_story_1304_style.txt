The story uses a retrospective first-person narration framed by a brief hospital conversation. Most of the narrative is delivered as an apparently autobiographical recollection, but the final return to the hospital hammock places the tale at a distance and complicates its moral authority. The narrator is observant, articulate and self-consciously ironic. He admits his own predatory impulses without straightforward remorse, often disguising brutality beneath urbane qualifications, mock modesty and philosophical generalisation. This creates an unreliable, morally compromised voice whose intelligence makes his callousness more disturbing rather than less.

The diction combines literary description with the rough vocabulary of tramps, soldiers and outcasts. Elevated or archaic phrases—such as “whither,” “peradventure,” “selfsame instant” and “four and twenty hours”—sit beside curses, slang, religious scraps and blunt talk of theft, hunger and violence. This mixture gives the prose a distinctly nineteenth-century, translated quality. Formal euphemisms about “infringing the rights of property” or “the progress in morals” are used satirically, exposing the hypocrisy of respectable society while also allowing the narrator to evade direct moral judgment. Occasional explanatory footnotes and regional terms lend the setting ethnographic specificity.

The syntax is frequently expansive and cumulative. Long sentences gather physical details, qualifications and sardonic asides, particularly when the narrator describes character, social injustice or bodily suffering. These are balanced by abrupt fragments and short exchanges at moments of tension: “A man?”; “All right!”; “Catch!” Repetition is prominent. Phrases such as “further on,” “we went,” “my brothers,” and “we ate” evoke the monotony of wandering, the obsessive pressure of hunger and the unstable fellowship among the men. Dashes, ellipses and exclamations reproduce interrupted thought, exhausted speech and sudden emotional shifts.

Sensory description is intense and bodily. Hunger is rendered through dry mouths, contracting stomachs, weakened muscles, hallucinated meat and the almost ecstatic passage of bread down the throat. Food acquires a near-sacramental significance, temporarily restoring warmth, calm and humanity, yet immediately provoking further greed. The landscape is equally vivid: the steppe appears as a black platter beneath a blue cupola, the road as a burning ribbon, the stars as “bright flowers of heaven.” Such broad, lyrical images contrast sharply with the characters’ sordid conduct. The spacious, radiant natural world remains indifferent to human misery, and the bright morning makes the murder seem almost metaphysically impossible even while the corpse lies in view.

The tone moves between bleak realism, grotesque comedy, lyricism and bitter social satire. Comic details—the student’s improvised footwear, the soldier’s liturgical singing, the fantasy of clouds made from cranberry vinegar and milk—do not relieve the misery so much as sharpen it. The humour is dark and unstable, repeatedly turning into threat or cruelty. Religious language is similarly degraded: hymns, appeals “for Christ’s sake,” anathemas and invocations of God coexist with robbery and murder. This ironic use of Christian vocabulary exposes the gulf between conventional moral speech and behaviour driven by deprivation.

Characterisation relies on compressed physical portraits, habitual gestures and distinctive dialogue. The soldier is muscular, impulsive, superstitious, sentimental and violent; his hoarse bass, curses and distorted moral reasoning dominate the conversation. The student is physically shrivelled and verbally sparse, his pressed lips and greenish eyes suggesting calculation before his final betrayal confirms it. The narrator presents himself as reflective but proves equally willing to attack the carpenter. None is reduced to a single trait: fellowship, humour and responsiveness to beauty coexist with selfishness and brutality. The carpenter, by contrast, is described through tremors, emaciation and anguished appeals, becoming both an individual with a family and a vulnerable object upon whom the others project contempt.

Dialogue drives much of the action and exposes moral psychology more directly than authorial explanation. The men rationalise theft as natural redistribution—“you had food and we had none”—and treat fear, sickness and grief as irritating weaknesses. Their repeated use of “brother” and “comrade” is deeply ironic: the language of solidarity accompanies coercion, abandonment and murder. Yet the irony is not wholly simple, because temporary companionship does exist among them. Their solidarity is genuine only while their needs coincide, confirming the narrator’s claim that misfortune is a powerful but precarious “cement.”

Structurally, the story progresses as a tightening moral descent. It begins with purposeless movement across an empty landscape, narrows around the discovery of the armed carpenter, pauses in the temporary communion of shared food and fire, and culminates in murder and flight. Suspense arises less from uncertainty about whether the men are dangerous than from how far hunger and resentment will carry them. The student’s disappearance and the carpenter’s death are revealed after the narrator wakes, making the decisive violence occur offstage. This omission preserves ambiguity for a moment while emphasising the narrator’s passivity and the group’s fragmented responsibility.

The governing style is naturalistic and anti-sentimental. Material conditions—hunger, homelessness, police suspicion and social exclusion—shape behaviour with almost deterministic force. At the same time, the story refuses to turn deprivation into moral innocence. Its central paradox is that the men are both victims and predators. The closing assertion that nobody is individually to blame because everyone is “beasts of the same kidney” condenses the narrator’s fatalistic worldview, but it is presented as a troubling rationalisation rather than an uncontested truth. Overall, the style joins panoramic lyricism, physiological realism, sardonic commentary and morally abrasive dialogue to portray freedom, degradation and human fellowship as inseparably entangled.

A young narrator accompanies his elderly uncle and the servant Yorpy ten miles upriver to a secluded island, where the uncle secretly tests an elaborate machine he has spent ten years developing. He believes the invention will rapidly drain swamps, make him rich, and secure his fame.

Despite careful preparation, the apparatus produces no effect. Enraged and devastated, the uncle tears it apart, then makes one final attempt to rebuild and test part of it. When that also fails, he nearly collapses, and the others take him home.

During the return journey, the uncle accepts his failure and advises the boy never to invent anything but happiness. He decides the ruined apparatus can still serve ordinary purposes and, touched by Yorpy’s loyalty, regains his kindness and humility. Declaring that failure has made him a better man, he becomes peacefully content for the remainder of his life. The narrator, witnessing this transformation, learns wisdom from his uncle’s example.

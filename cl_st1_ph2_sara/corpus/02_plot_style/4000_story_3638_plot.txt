An American observer in Vienna reflects on reports that members of the Paris Peace Commission received $100,000 each for six weeks’ work. He hopes this generous payment will establish a precedent for adequately compensating American diplomats, who are expected to represent a wealthy and powerful nation abroad while receiving meager salaries.

He attacks the tradition of “Republican Simplicity,” which forces civilian diplomats to wear conspicuous black evening clothes at European state functions rather than dignified official uniforms. He argues that this custom, inherited from Benjamin Franklin, is discourteous abroad and misrepresents America. He proposes granting diplomats temporary military rank so they can wear appropriate uniforms.

He then compares low American diplomatic salaries with Britain’s generous pay, housing, and entertainment allowances. Because American representatives must fund residences and official hospitality themselves, only rich men can properly fill the posts, while poorer diplomats cannot cultivate the social relationships needed to advance American commerce. The nation’s supposed economy therefore damages both its dignity and its business interests.

As America has entered world society, he argues, it must abandon its outdated pretenses and pay ambassadors enough to perform their duties properly. In a postscript, he learns that he will not become ambassador to Vienna and humorously insists that he never wanted the position—while declaring that he would refuse any ambassadorship paying less than $75,000. He closes by ridiculing the contradiction between America’s professed simplicity and its actual wealth, luxury, extravagance, and love of display.

A narrator experienced in mesmerism is summoned to the bedside of Mr. Vankirk, a terminally ill man whose suffering has previously been eased through hypnotic trances. Vankirk, long skeptical of the soul’s immortality, believes that mesmerism has given him an intuitive grasp of truths inaccessible in ordinary consciousness. He asks to be mesmerized and questioned about God, death, and the afterlife.

In a trance, Vankirk predicts his own death and describes God as an indivisible, all-pervading form of “unparticled matter” whose motion constitutes thought and creation. He explains that individual beings are portions of the divine mind embodied in matter. Death, he claims, is not annihilation but a painful metamorphosis from a temporary, organic body into an immortal, unorganized form with vastly expanded perception. Earthly life and suffering are necessary preparations that make heavenly bliss possible by contrast.

As Vankirk finishes describing the ultimate state of existence, his voice weakens and his expression alarms the narrator. The narrator awakens him, whereupon Vankirk smiles, falls back, and dies. His body becomes unnaturally cold and rigid almost immediately, leaving the narrator to wonder whether Vankirk had already been speaking from beyond death during the final part of their conversation.

The story adopts the manner of a Renaissance fabliau or bawdy chronicle, presented through a conspicuously talkative, worldly narrator. The voice is omniscient but far from neutral: it addresses the reader directly, pauses to explain geography and custom, offers mock-moral judgments, and repeatedly intrudes with asides such as “Do you thoroughly understand?” This creates the impression of an oral raconteur telling a well-known local anecdote to a complicit audience. Assertions like “as everyone knows” and references to other stories simulate a larger body of communal history, while hearsay formulas—“Some people say”—blur the boundary between record, gossip, and legend.

The diction is pseudo-archaic and strongly marked by an older English translation style. Titles, place names, occupational labels, and legal or religious terms give the narrative historical texture, while words such as “wench,” “bodkin,” “burthening,” “Monseigneur,” and “mischief” evoke an antiquated social world. Elevated phrases are continually mixed with colloquial idioms, coarse insinuation, and commercial language. Sex, honour, justice, and money are discussed in the same transactional vocabulary, producing much of the story’s comic irreverence. The prose also delights in proverbial formulations and sweeping claims about women, men, law, and virtue, often framed as if they were self-evident wisdom.

Sentences tend to be long, cumulative, and loosely jointed, with clauses connected by commas, semicolons, and repeated conjunctions. This syntax supports the narrator’s digressive, improvisatory manner: geographical explanations, character judgments, and comic qualifications accumulate before the action moves forward. Against this expansive narration, the dialogue is brisk and theatrical. Short exchanges, repetitions, misunderstandings, and verbal reversals give scenes the rhythm of comic performance. Puns and double meanings are central, especially where ordinary objects or legal expressions acquire sexual significance.

The tone is exuberantly bawdy, satirical, and mock-judicial. Serious subjects—sexual coercion, class privilege, prostitution, and institutional injustice—are filtered through ribald comedy and a period-specific, deeply misogynistic skepticism about female testimony. The narrator’s supposed defence of the heroine is unstable: he calls for indulgence and seeks to “cleanse” her reputation, yet also labels her a “giddy wench” and “little hussy.” This mixture of sympathy, voyeurism, and moral condescension is characteristic of the tale’s deliberately indecorous social satire. Nobles, judges, clergy, kings, and respectable ladies are all implicated in hypocrisy, so no institution retains uncomplicated moral authority.

The central comic episode is constructed as an extended physical analogy. The judge’s needle-and-thread test turns legal reasoning into slapstick and sexual allegory. Repetition of the judge’s evasive movements builds farcical suspense, while the heroine’s eventual imitation of persuasion and persistence reverses the experiment and allows her to outwit him. Euphemism, personification, and double entendre intensify the scene: the bodkin remains “virgin,” the thread seeks “marriage,” and penetration becomes both literal demonstration and comic proof. The woman’s wit, rather than abstract legal principle, finally drives the judgment.

Structurally, the narrative moves from local and biographical exposition to seduction or assault, legal complaint, comic trial, financial resolution, and moralizing aftermath. The pacing is uneven by design: the opening dwells on geography and reputation, the courtroom demonstration occupies the greatest space, and the conclusion rapidly summarizes later lovers, repentance, charity, marriage, and adultery. This telescoped ending imitates a chronicle while also multiplying ironies. The heroine’s temporary sexual commerce is described as the route by which she can stop laundering, and her later religious penance becomes another kind of “polishing” or cleansing.

Imagery and motifs repeatedly connect laundering, staining, colour, cleanliness, and moral reputation. Virtue is something that may be contaminated, polished, washed, priced, or restored. These motifs unify the heroine’s profession with the tale’s concern for public scandal and retrospective absolution. Monetary enumeration—one hundred crowns, one thousand crowns, ten lords becoming a hundred in gossip—adds the precision of an account book to an ethically chaotic situation and exposes how reputation is exaggerated by rumour.

The ending supplies a mock exemplum rather than a sincere moral. Its concluding maxim—that “with strength and patience justice itself can be violated”—compresses the story’s sexual, legal, and political meanings into a final paradox. Throughout, apparent moral lessons are undercut by the events meant to prove them. The resulting style is expansive, anecdotal, theatrical, sexually punning, and aggressively ironic: a pastiche of historical chronicle, popular tale, courtroom farce, and mock sermon.

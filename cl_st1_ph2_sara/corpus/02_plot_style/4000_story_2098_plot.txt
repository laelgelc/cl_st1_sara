A mason and former supervisor reflects on the construction of an immense defensive wall, built in disconnected sections rather than continuously. He explains that this seemingly impractical method kept skilled workers motivated: after completing a manageable section, they returned home as celebrated participants in a national undertaking, then departed renewed to work elsewhere. Yet he suspects the distant, unknowable leadership deliberately chose this fragmented plan for deeper purposes, perhaps to unify the population rather than merely repel northern nomads.

His inquiry broadens into a meditation on imperial authority. In remote southern villages, the emperor is revered but so distant that news, commands, and even knowledge of the reigning dynasty arrive late, distorted, or not at all. A legend illustrates this separation: an imperial messenger bearing a dying emperor’s personal message can never escape the endless palace and capital to reach the intended subject. The people therefore remain intensely loyal to an emperor they cannot know, confusing past rulers with present ones and treating current wars and rebellions as ancient history. The narrator concludes that this failure to make imperial power immediate is both a weakness and, paradoxically, a force that unifies the country—an unsettling idea he refuses to investigate further.

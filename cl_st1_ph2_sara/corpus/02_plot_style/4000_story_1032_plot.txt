Returning to the site of his childhood school, the narrator finds it largely demolished by a railway. This discovery prompts affectionate but critical memories of his earliest school and, more vividly, the later institution where he became the top pupil.

He recalls a brutal, ignorant headmaster who cared more for money and corporal punishment than education, contrasted with overworked but capable ushers and eccentric visiting teachers. He describes privileged and mysterious pupils whose backgrounds inspired elaborate legends, along with the boys’ own society: slate pencil used as currency, birds and trained white mice kept in desks, theatrical performances, rumors, pranks, and rivalries. Among the staff, a kindly usher suffers an unhappy romance, an exhausted Latin master is humiliated by the headmaster, and the gruff handyman Phil reveals unexpected compassion by nursing boys through scarlet fever.

The recollections end with the narrator contemplating how the school, flawed and unworthy of pride though it was, has vanished beneath the railway, becoming another piece of the past erased by change.

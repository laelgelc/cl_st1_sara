A narrator living outside Chicago often encounters a lonely old man whom villagers consider insane. Unable to connect with those around him, the old man invents elaborate family relationships with public figures he reads about in newspapers.

The papers are dominated by the trial of a bicycle-factory foreman who inexplicably murdered his pregnant wife. The foreman had idealized a young office worker, regarding her as pure and unattainable, while growing increasingly alienated from the ordinary intimacy of his wife, children, and mother-in-law. After a picture show, he suddenly stabbed his wife to death in their dark apartment hallway, fabricated a story about an attacker, disposed of the knife, and then confessed without explaining his motive.

During a foggy walk, the old man insists that the murderer is his brother and describes an imaginary childhood they shared. The narrator senses that, through this delusion, the old man is expressing both the murderer’s unspoken anguish and humanity’s broader loneliness and longing for unattainable beauty. Overcome, the old man squeezes his small dog so tightly that the narrator must tear it from his arms. Later, the old man passes the narrator’s house without the dog, suggesting he may have killed his only companion just as the foreman killed his wife. The story closes with rain brutally beating autumn leaves to the ground, mirroring lives denied beauty, freedom, and escape.

The story adopts the manner of a mock historical chronicle: ceremonious, antiquarian, and outwardly respectful, but governed throughout by comic irony. An intrusive first-person narrator presents himself as a conscientious historian who has gathered scattered “biographical anecdotes,” weighed their authenticity, and reluctantly omitted doubtful material. This claim to scholarly care is part of the joke. The narrator repeatedly praises Van Twiller as “renowned,” “illustrious,” “sage,” and “excellent,” while supplying evidence of his inertia, obtuseness, gluttony, and incompetence. The dominant tonal mechanism is therefore sustained deadpan incongruity: solemn language and historical authority are applied to ridiculous behavior.

The diction is elevated, Latinate, and deliberately old-fashioned. Formal phrases such as “under the commission and control,” “indubitable sign,” “legal acumen,” and “final opinion of the court” coexist with Dutch titles and names, including “Mynheer,” “burgomasters,” “stadtholder,” and “timmerman.” Biblical, classical, political, and Oriental references—“the year of our Lord,” Apollo, Providence, Haroun Alraschid, the Barbary powers, and King Log—lend the narrative a grand cultural range while comically magnifying trivial events. The language also imitates legal and administrative prose through formulae such as “the said Wandle,” “inasmuch as,” and repeated “therefore.” Concrete domestic terms—pipes, pudding, tobacco-boxes, jack-knives, account books—continually puncture this lofty register.

The syntax is expansive and leisurely, favoring long periodic or cumulative sentences filled with subordinate clauses, appositions, parenthetical qualifications, and balanced contrasts. Sentences often delay their comic conclusion, allowing dignified description to accumulate before arriving at an absurd image or judgment. Parallel structures are especially prominent, as in the contrast between men who talk without thinking and men who remain silent without thinking. Semicolons, dashes, and extended lists support the narrator’s digressive manner. Shorter statements and quoted remarks appear sparingly, so they land with greater comic force, particularly Van Twiller’s flat declaration, “Well, I see nothing in all that to laugh about.”

The humor relies heavily on verbal irony, hyperbole, understatement, false inference, and mock logic. Van Twiller’s silence is interpreted as wisdom, indecision as intellectual grandeur, sleep as profound deliberation, and the absence of prosecutions as proof of merciful government. The narrator habitually offers two possible explanations for an action—both unflattering—while pretending not to decide between them. He also makes conspicuous disclaimers, insisting that a satirical observation should not be applied to Van Twiller immediately before or after making the application unmistakable. This technique preserves the pose of fairness while sharpening the ridicule.

Characterization is caricatural rather than psychologically realistic. Van Twiller is built from a cluster of repeated traits: corpulence, silence, smoking, sleepiness, solemnity, and chronic doubt. His body becomes an emblem of his government. Geometrical and architectural descriptions turn him into a static object: his head is a sphere, his body an oblong, and his overall figure resembles “a beer barrel on skids.” Similes are vivid, extended, and frequently deflationary. He is compared to an oyster, an owl, a hazy firmament, an apple, and a barrel. Even his daily routine is reduced to comic arithmetic: four hours eating, eight hours smoking and doubting, and twelve hours sleeping. These precise measurements mimic documentary fact while pushing the portrait toward grotesque absurdity.

The narrative is organized as a comic character sketch that gradually develops into an exemplary anecdote. It begins with historical placement and an idyllic seasonal overture, moves through ancestry, temperament, appearance, routine, and conduct in council, and culminates in the lawsuit between Wandle Schoonhoven and Barent Bleecker. That episode serves as a miniature demonstration of all Van Twiller’s established qualities. Faced with incomprehensible accounts, he substitutes the books’ physical weight and thickness for their financial contents, then declares the accounts balanced. The climax is an anti-climax: his only recorded decision is nonsensical, yet it is celebrated as jurisprudential brilliance.

Pacing is intentionally slow and digressive. The narrator lingers over physical details, qualifications, pseudo-etymology, historical allusions, and incidental maxims. This dilatory movement formally mirrors Van Twiller’s own indolence and inability to decide. Action is scarce; smoking, eating, sitting, staring, and sleeping dominate. When events do occur, they are narrated with disproportionate ceremonial weight. The result is a style in which description and commentary matter more than plot, and delay itself becomes comic.

Dialogue is limited and functions mainly as characterization. Van Twiller speaks rarely, usually in monosyllables, vague expressions of doubt, or ponderous judgments. His restrained speech contrasts with the narrator’s abundant eloquence. Gestures often replace words: shaking his head, closing his eyes, laying a finger beside his nose, emitting grunts, and puffing smoke. Such repeated physical business gives scenes a theatrical, almost cartoon-like quality.

The setting blends recognizable colonial history with comic legend. Place names, institutions, dates, political offices, and ethnic details create a surface of historical specificity, but realism is subordinated to satirical mythmaking. New Amsterdam appears as a quaint, self-contained community whose inhabitants enthusiastically misread incompetence as wisdom. The satire consequently extends beyond one governor to magistrates, admirers, bureaucratic institutions, historical chroniclers, and the public appetite for venerating authority.

The prose is sensuous and pictorial despite its mock-scholarly posture. The opening uses pastoral imagery, personification, birdsong, flowers, and celestial movement to establish an exuberant contrast with the inert governor. Later passages emphasize shape, weight, color, sound, and texture: the mottled cheeks, carved oak chair, amber pipe, curling smoke, guttural noises, thick account books, and heavy pudding. Recurring motifs of bulk, balance, smoke, and stillness unify the portrait. “Balance” is especially important, shifting from bodily proportion to mental hesitation and finally to the absurd balancing of financial accounts by weighing their books.

The thematic emphasis falls on the confusion of appearance with substance. Gravity passes for intelligence, inactivity for benevolence, indecision for caution, and procedural solemnity for justice. The narrator exposes how reputations are manufactured through flattering interpretation and institutional language. Yet the satire remains genial rather than savage. Van Twiller is rendered ridiculous, but not vicious; his rule is peaceful largely because he does almost nothing. The overall style combines affectionate burlesque, elaborate caricature, pseudo-erudition, and urbane narrative irony, producing comedy through the persistent mismatch between majestic presentation and trivial reality.

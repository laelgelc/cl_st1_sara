The story adopts the style of a nineteenth-century popular history or moralizing historical chronicle. Its narrator is omniscient, authoritative, and openly interpretive, recounting Rome’s sack by the Gauls while supplying extensive explanations of Roman geography, government, warfare, religion, and social customs. The opening proceeds panoramically rather than dramatically, establishing the Tiber, the Campagna, the Forum, the Senate, the Capitol, and the legions before narrowing into the conflict with Brennus. This creates the sense that individual deeds emerge from the larger life of a nation.

The prose is formal, elevated, and distinctly antiquarian. Its vocabulary includes historical and institutional terms such as “patricians,” “lictors,” “tribunes,” “legions,” “Dictator,” and “curule chair,” usually explained for a general or youthful readership. Archaic or literary words and constructions—“succor,” “durst,” “thither,” “beheld,” “waxing sharp,” and “gainsaid”—lend the account legendary gravity. Rome is frequently personified as a feminine civic being, while phrases such as “Father Tiber,” “the Roman State,” and “the honor of Rome” intensify the patriotic and mythic atmosphere. Latin expressions, classical references, quoted poetry, and editorial footnotes reinforce the learned, documentary surface.

Sentence structure is expansive and cumulative. Long, clause-heavy sentences gather landscape, historical background, explanation, and moral judgment into a continuous narrative flow. Semicolons, dashes, and coordinated clauses are common, while shorter declarative sentences appear at moments of crisis or resolution. Paragraphs are generally long and densely expository, though pivotal scenes—the silent senators in the Forum, Pontius Cominius climbing the Capitol, the geese raising the alarm, and Camillus interrupting the ransom—receive slower, more visual narration. Repetition and parallel phrasing help build ceremony and suspense, especially in descriptions of stillness, ascent, danger, and collective action.

The narrative voice is not neutral. It judges conduct according to courage, duty, reverence, self-sacrifice, lawful authority, and fidelity to the state. The Fabii are “foolish” covenant-breakers; their father’s intervention spreads their guilt to the nation; the elderly senators, Pontius, Manlius, and Camillus embody different forms of public heroism. Events are repeatedly interpreted as moral consequences, so Rome’s defeat follows its breach of sacred law, while deliverance follows endurance, discipline, and renewed civic unity. The narrator often pauses to state the lesson directly, as in observations about “a sinful heart” producing a “failing hand” or about brave contempt of life deserving perpetual remembrance.

A pronounced Christian framework is superimposed on the pagan material. Roman religious sincerity is respected, but described as partial or anticipatory: the senators’ sacrificial deaths become a “presage” of Christian atonement, and Roman gods are treated as objects of belief rather than accepted realities. Terms such as “heathens” and “heathen festivals” establish the narrator’s religious distance. Providence is implied through punishment and restoration, even when events are presented through dreams, omens, ritual obligations, or the sacred geese of Juno.

Characterization is economical and exemplary rather than psychologically complex. Figures are defined by dominant moral qualities: Brennus is able, fierce, and insulting; Camillus is proud, stern, and militarily brilliant; Pontius is resolute and self-endangering; Manlius is alert and forceful; the senators are grave and self-sacrificing. Collective identities matter as much as individuals. The Romans are steadfast, disciplined, religious, and civic-minded, whereas the Gauls are repeatedly rendered as physically imposing, wild, curious, and “barbarian.” Their red hair, brawny bodies, broad swords, and mountain hardiness provide vivid visual contrast with the white robes, ivory staffs, and solemn composure of Roman authority.

Description is painterly and theatrical. The author favors emblematic tableaux: amethyst-colored hills around the Campagna, magistrates in purple-bordered togas, deserted streets leading to the Forum, motionless white-haired senators seated like statues, blackened ruins beneath the Capitol, and armored Roman horsemen arriving at the climactic instant. Color, costume, posture, and civic architecture carry symbolic weight. Rome is represented through stone heights, robes, ritual objects, and formal assemblies; the Gauls through bodily scale, weapons, animal energy, and destructive movement.

The story’s pacing alternates between broad historical summary and concentrated adventure. Centuries of Roman expansion are compressed into a few paragraphs, while dangerous missions and famous legendary episodes unfold step by step. Suspense is often generated through rhetorical questions—how can the decree reach Camillus, and who can relieve the Capitol?—and through the audience’s awareness of imminent discovery or attack. Exclamations and speculative comments such as “A wondrous scene it must have been” invite emotional participation and make the narrator resemble an oral storyteller as well as a historian.

Dialogue is sparse, formal, and aphoristic. Speeches are included chiefly when they crystallize political or moral conflict: Brennus’s doctrine of power, “Voe victis!”, and Camillus’s declaration that Rome is guarded “with iron, not gold.” Such lines function as memorable historical emblems rather than naturalistic conversation. Direct quotation is surrounded by explanatory narration that controls its interpretation.

The ending introduces historiographical caution without surrendering the legend’s value. The narrator acknowledges that the narrative’s truth is “greatly doubted,” distinguishes uncertain detail from a possibly authentic outline, and admits that Roman tradition may have exaggerated or concealed events. Yet factual uncertainty is subordinated to moral and imaginative truth: even if the episode is “romance” rather than literal fact, it remains worthy of preservation as a tradition of heroic deeds. The overall style therefore blends historical exposition, classical legend, patriotic adventure, and explicit ethical instruction, presenting the past as a storehouse of exemplary conduct rather than as an object of detached scholarly analysis.

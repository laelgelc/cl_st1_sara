The story is a comic character piece told by an assured third-person omniscient narrator who stays close to Monsieur Pettipon’s thoughts while retaining enough distance to expose their disproportion and absurdity. The narrative voice is genial, urbane, and lightly satirical rather than cruel. It treats Pettipon’s devotion to cleanliness with mock-epic seriousness, elevating routine cabin work into religious and artistic ceremony: he is imagined as a “high priest,” a “Shelley of the dustpan,” and a “Keats of the sheets.” This grand framing establishes the central comic method, in which a trivial incident acquires the emotional and rhetorical scale of catastrophe.

The diction combines polished literary narration with theatrical, French-inflected speech. The narrator uses elevated or ceremonious words such as “sacerdotal,” “estimable,” “damnatory,” and “ignominiously,” often in deliberately incongruous relation to dust, bedding, tips, and a tiny insect. Pettipon’s language is florid, repetitive, and melodramatic, full of honorifics, exclamations, endearments, and literal translations or comic approximations of French idiom: “old turnip,” “little cabbage,” “old oyster,” “Name of a name,” and “Holy Blue.” Repeated French markers—including “monsieur,” “alas,” “helas,” and “oo la la”—create a stylized rather than realistic Frenchness typical of period magazine comedy. The passengers and officers are also rendered through broad verbal and physical caricature.

Sentences in the descriptive passages are often long, balanced, and elaborately qualified, allowing details to accumulate until they become comic. Lists are especially important: Pettipon inventories cabin occupants, estimates their tips, enumerates his precautions, and repeatedly cites his “twenty-two years, three months and a day” and his 127 crossings. These exact figures parody bureaucratic testimony and reveal a mind that treats professional pride as a legal and moral record. Shorter sentences and fragments appear at moments of alarm—“It was alive”; “It was perfection”—giving punch to revelations or judgments. Exclamation marks, dashes, rhetorical questions, and interrupted speeches make the dialogue energetic and stage-like.

Characterization is predominantly external and caricatural, built from recurring physical motifs and signature behavior. Pettipon is repeatedly described through his plumpness, pinkness, perspiration, double chins, wheezing movements, delicate gestures, and emotional volatility. His body functions almost like a comic instrument: cheeks quiver “like spilled jelly,” his face becomes “pink as a peony,” and sobs shake his huge frame. Yet the exaggeration is balanced by genuine affection. His love of his work, concern for his son’s violin, loyalty to his family, and fear of professional dishonor give emotional substance to what could otherwise be a one-note farce. Supporting characters are sketched economically through a single governing trait: Thérèse is practical, the head steward irritable, the chief steward ponderously jocular, the captain brisk, and the company president imposing but perceptive.

Dialogue drives much of the story and is structured around misunderstanding. Pettipon assumes that every superior already knows of his disgrace and interprets impatience, irony, or indifference as concealed condemnation. The officers, meanwhile, cannot understand why he treats one small insect as a disaster. This dramatic irony creates a repeating comic pattern: Pettipon displays the specimen, invokes his spotless record, blames the passenger, begins the anecdote about Georges Prunier, and is interrupted. Each repetition varies slightly while escalating the rank of the listener—from passenger to head steward, chief steward, captain, and finally company president. The repeated formula acts as both running gag and structural spine.

The plot has a clean farcical escalation. It begins with an extended portrait of perfect order, introduces Pettipon’s modest desire to buy his son a violin, and then overturns his world with the discovery of the unnamed insect. His increasingly desperate appeals carry him upward through the ship’s hierarchy, making the social structure of the liner part of the comic machinery. Suspense depends less on what will happen than on how far Pettipon will magnify the incident. The climax reverses his expectations: the very fuss that he believes proves his guilt demonstrates his conscientiousness and earns him promotion. The final line completes the reversal by transforming the hated specimen into a treasured watch charm.

The humor rests on incongruity, hyperbole, repetition, bathos, and dramatic irony. Pettipon compares his predicament to artistic failure, aristocratic disgrace, Waterloo, criminal judgment, and even death, while everyone else regards the insect as beneath notice. Nautical dangers such as leaks, fires, icebergs, and sinking are repeatedly invoked only to be displaced by his microscopic crisis. The narrator also uses mock-heroic imagery, comic similes, and exaggerated physical descriptions to maintain a buoyant tone. Even Pettipon’s fleeting contemplation of jumping overboard is quickly converted into another step in the escalating appeal process.

The setting is vividly functional rather than atmospherically elaborate. Specific cabin numbers, class divisions, staff ranks, routes, tips, berths, corridors, and offices give the ship a credible social geography. The liner is both workplace and miniature hierarchy, with Pettipon beginning in second class and moving symbolically toward the luxurious first-class suite. Period details—steamship travel, formal service, watch charms, pince-nez, francs, and rigid class distinctions—place the piece in an early twentieth-century comic tradition.

The story’s underlying concerns are professional pride, dignity in service, class aspiration, and the difference between objective importance and subjective meaning. Pettipon’s crisis is ridiculous in scale but sincere in origin: he identifies himself completely with the quality of his work. The ending rewards not worldly cleverness but excessive conscientiousness. Overall, the style is warm, polished, highly performative, and mock-heroic, using a cumulative farce to turn a meticulous working man’s minor embarrassment into an affectionate comedy of honor.

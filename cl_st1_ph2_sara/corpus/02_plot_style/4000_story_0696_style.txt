The story uses a formal, nineteenth-century prose style marked by precision, irony, and emotional restraint. Its omniscient third-person narrator reports military procedures and physical details with documentary exactness, while periodically intruding through sardonic generalisations, such as the remarks about physiognomists, promotion, and “the various visible forms of nature.” These asides create a dry, sceptical narrative personality and keep the account from becoming conventionally heroic or sentimental.

The diction combines elevated Latinate language—“circumjacent,” “discretionary,” “incriminating,” “magnanimity”—with specialised military vocabulary and colloquial regional speech. Descriptions are exact and often densely qualified, using long, balanced sentences to establish setting, rank, motive, and circumstance. Shorter declarative sentences dominate moments of recognition or command, giving lines such as “The pass is a forgery” and “you shall not die until to-morrow morning” stark dramatic force. Punctuation, especially semicolons, dashes, and parenthetical clauses, supports a rhythm that moves between analytical exposition and abrupt action.

Dialogue sharply distinguishes the principal figures. Brune’s dialect initially marks his disguise and social appearance, but once exposed he speaks in controlled, dignified, almost ceremonial language. Hartroy’s speech is similarly formal, though it becomes broken and repetitive when emotion defeats his military composure. Both men are characterised through restraint rather than explanation: Brune’s tranquillity, Hartroy’s cold professionalism, their bows, silences, and refusals to plead or excuse themselves convey a severe code of honour. The contrast between outward calm and inward moral crisis is central to the style.

The narrative is tightly constructed in four numbered sections. It begins with a minor checkpoint encounter, turns that encounter into a pursuit and arrest, then supplies strategic context before revealing the deeper personal history shared by captor and captive. Information is deliberately withheld and released through successive recognitions: Hartroy identifies Brune, Brune reveals that he has already identified Hartroy, and the past finally transforms a routine execution into an insoluble moral debt. This delayed exposition gives the story the mechanism of a tragic revelation while preserving suspense.

Imagery is sparse but symbolically pointed. The forged pass rolls into the dust “as in humiliation for the lie that it bore”; the bugle interrupts confession with the impersonal demand of duty; the execution volley is paired with Hartroy’s nearer, solitary pistol shot; and the mountain’s “solemn shadow” reduces both military conflict and personal anguish to silence. Objects and sounds—the cocked pistol, official papers, bugle calls, drums, musketry—carry much of the dramatic meaning. Personification and ironic phrasing occasionally animate the landscape or objects, but the dominant sensory register is austere and martial.

The tone combines grim irony, fatalism, and suppressed pathos. The narrator recognises courage and compassion yet refuses to let either cancel law, treason, shame, or consequence. Hartroy cannot spare the man who once spared him without violating his own command, but after enforcing the sentence he cannot morally retain the life Brune gave him. This symmetry produces the story’s characteristic bitterness: noble conduct does not redeem the characters but binds them more tightly to catastrophe. The ending is concise, balanced, and anti-romantic, denying military honours to both men and placing deserter, spy, officer, and suicide together in forgotten graves.

The final paragraph beginning “That was a rough story” departs noticeably from the story’s established voice. It adopts an informal, explanatory, classroom-like register and explicitly names “Bitter Bierce,” whereas the preceding narrative relies on implication and tonal control. It functions as editorial commentary rather than as part of the fictional style proper.

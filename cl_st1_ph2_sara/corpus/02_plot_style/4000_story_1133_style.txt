The story uses a brisk, comic, highly conversational style associated with early twentieth-century American magazine fiction. Its first-person narrator is not a central participant but a knowing local observer who speaks directly to the reader, anticipates expectations, digresses at will, and openly comments on the mechanics of storytelling. Remarks such as “Now for the story,” “another dash or two of local color,” and “as our best talent would put it” make the narration self-conscious and gently parody the formulas of popular fiction. The opening’s insistence that this is “not a baseball story” is playfully misleading: baseball supplies the setting, vocabulary, social world, and governing metaphor, while the actual plot is a romantic comedy of disillusionment.

The tone is buoyant, sardonic, and affectionate. Small-town behavior is mocked without becoming cruel, and almost every character is treated as both ridiculous and recognizably human. The narrator habitually inflates trivial matters through grand comparisons, mock-heroic language, and comic hyperbole: the local ballpark is likened to Ascot and the Grand Prix, an attractive pitcher becomes an “Adonis” or “blonde god,” and ordinary players are described as “knights” and “heroes.” These exalted descriptions are repeatedly punctured by mundane realities such as dirty uniforms, low salaries, boarding houses, winter jobs, and shoe fitting. The central stylistic movement is therefore from glamour to bathos, culminating in the revelation of Rudie not as a heroic athlete but as a deferential shoe clerk.

The diction mixes educated allusion with vernacular American speech. References to Jean Valjean, Saint Cecilia, Little Eva, Madame de Staël, Adonis, and fashionable society coexist with baseball slang, commercial language, colloquialisms, and period expressions such as “dub,” “got the goat,” “bush leaguer,” “piffle,” “bum pitcher,” and “h’ist one over.” This social and linguistic mixture generates much of the humor. Elevated or sentimental phrases are often immediately undercut by something bluntly practical, while deliberately nonstandard forms—“ain’t,” “Yessir,” “How-do,” “Good eye,” and “Give ’em a dark one”—give dialogue regional and class texture. Parenthetical qualifications and comic uncertainty, as in exaggerated guesses about salaries or stadium capacity, further establish the narrator’s teasing informality.

Sentences vary between long, accumulating comic catalogues and short punch lines. Descriptive passages pile up clothing details, physical traits, civic titles, baseball habits, and social observations through coordination and repetition. These lists create momentum while exposing vanity and pretension. By contrast, brief sentences such as “But it didn’t” or “Then he saw” control timing and sharpen reversals. Repeated structures are especially important: the sequence of Rudie’s visits, each provoking a stronger parental response, compresses time into a comic pattern, while Ivy’s breathless chain of questions at her first game dramatizes her instantaneous conversion to fandom. Dashes, parentheses, exclamation marks, direct address, and interrupted speech give the prose the rhythm of an animated oral performance.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization. Pa Keller speaks in practical, paternal, colloquial bursts and treats family conflict as though it were a civic negotiation. Ivy initially adopts the languid diction and poses of a cultivated schoolgirl, but her speech becomes rapid, technical, and emphatic once she discovers baseball. Rudie’s romantic clichés repeatedly fail because Ivy redirects every conversation toward pitching, fielding, and league performance. This mismatch creates dramatic irony: Rudie wants to discuss “us,” while Ivy has fallen in love less with the man than with the heroic image produced by the game. Her final denunciation of him as a poor pitcher completes the transformation of her language and reveals that the romantic illusion has collapsed into hard fan judgment.

Characterization is caricatural but precise, built through selected social and physical details rather than psychological exposition. Pa Keller’s many civic positions, Ivy’s school emblems and literary poses, Undine’s conspicuous sensuality, Pug’s orangutan arms, and Rudie’s clerkly hand-rubbing all function as comic shorthand. Clothing is particularly significant: baseball uniforms, fashionable hats, shirtwaists, and shop coats indicate status and shape perception. Rudie appears magnificent in uniform because Ivy sees him within baseball’s theatrical frame; in ordinary work clothes, performing a servile commercial task, he loses that aura immediately.

The plot is episodic and tightly organized around contrast. An expansive opening establishes the town’s baseball culture; Ivy’s conversion leads into courtship; parental opposition produces the agreement to wait; and the visit to the shoe shop delivers the decisive anticlimax. A six-month jump then allows the final domestic scene to serve as a compact comic coda. The ending does not explain Ivy’s change of heart directly. Instead, her newly contemptuous baseball analysis supplies the punch line and lets the reader infer how thoroughly the shoe-shop encounter destroyed her idealized vision.

Overall, the style combines journalistic observation, vaudevillian timing, social satire, romantic parody, and warm regional comedy. Its most distinctive features are the intrusive raconteur narrator, the collision of high and low registers, energetic colloquial dialogue, exaggerated local color, mock-heroic imagery, and a carefully delayed deflation of fantasy by ordinary economic reality.

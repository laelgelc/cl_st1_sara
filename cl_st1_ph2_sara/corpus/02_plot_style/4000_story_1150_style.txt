The story uses a highly self-conscious, conversational narrative voice that openly comments on the conventions of popular fiction. It begins with an extended comic digression about the supposed obligation to describe a heroine’s clothes, directly addressing the reader through “you” and referring knowingly to writers, publishers, illustrations and stock romantic scenes. This metafictional opening is not merely decorative: it establishes clothing as the story’s governing image and prepares the reader to interpret Sophy’s dress as a sign of poverty, aspiration, vulnerability and social identity. The narrator repeatedly interrupts the action with asides, qualifications and mock explanations, creating the impression of an amused, opinionated storyteller speaking informally to an audience.

The point of view is third-person omniscient, but it is strongly mediated by this intrusive narrator. The narration moves freely between external description, social commentary and glimpses into Louie’s and Sophy’s thoughts. It can summarize an entire social class or urban phenomenon, then narrow its attention to a handkerchief, a washbowl or a scrap of lace. Although the voice frequently jokes at the characters’ expense, it remains sympathetic to them, especially when exposing the pressures created by low wages, exhausting work and the need to maintain appearances. The narrator’s irony is therefore double-edged: it mocks vanity and literary cliché while also revealing the material hardship beneath apparently foolish choices.

The tonal range combines comedy, satire, sentiment and social realism. Much of the humour comes from exaggeration, mock solemnity and incongruous comparisons, as in the treatment of shoe selling as a religion or curiosity as a theatrical force that “plays leads” in life’s dramas. Elevated phrases are often placed beside slang, shabby surroundings or practical worries, producing a comic contrast between romantic language and working-class reality. The story can turn abruptly from flippancy to discomfort or tenderness, particularly in the account of the boss’s unwanted kiss, Louie’s private washing and Sophy’s lunchless sacrifices for jewellery. Its sentiment is carefully protected by wit: emotional moments are usually followed by a joke, a quarrel or a defiant gesture before they can become openly melodramatic.

The diction mixes polished literary narration with period colloquialism, commercial vocabulary, fashion terminology and urban slang. French-derived fashion expressions such as “au fait,” “robe de nuit” and “La Valliere” coexist with “get-up,” “tacky,” “rube,” “fade” and “ferget it.” Brand names, prices, garment details and shoe sizes give the setting material specificity. The language is historically marked by early twentieth-century attitudes and stereotypes, including ethnicized descriptions and broad generalizations about gender and class. Such expressions help locate the piece in its period, though they may strike a modern reader as dated or prejudicial.

Sentence structure is varied and performance-oriented. Long, accumulative sentences are used for comic catalogues, social observation and mounting emphasis, while short sentences deliver punch lines or emotional reversals: “I quit her cold,” “Sophy continued to wear the black dress,” and “Louie did learn.” Parallel constructions and repeated phrasing create rhetorical momentum. The narrator often builds a sequence through anaphora—“He learned … he learned …”—or through escalating alternatives and qualifications. Parentheses, dashes, semicolons, rhetorical questions and direct interjections reproduce the rhythms of spoken storytelling. The prose also uses deliberately formal or grand language to describe ordinary events, turning the contrast itself into humour.

Dialogue is central to characterization and is sharply differentiated from the narrator’s prose. Sophy speaks in compressed, phonetic urban vernacular, with dropped consonants, nonstandard grammar and theatrical slang. Her speech is quick, defensive, mocking and full of verbal feints; humour functions as both social performance and emotional armour. Louie’s language is plainer, more grammatical and more earnest, reflecting his small-town upbringing and moral directness. The boss’s broken syntax and commercial brusqueness establish him immediately as coarse and exploitative. Exchanges frequently operate as contests in which characters negotiate status, attraction and shame. Banter advances the relationship more effectively than explicit declarations would.

Characterization relies on emblematic physical details, social habits and contrasts. Sophy is constructed through paradox: she is “daringly demure,” worldly yet young, brazen in manner yet capable of shame and tenderness. Her revealing black dress initially appears to signify impropriety, but the narrative gradually reframes it as a practical response to poverty and a calculated means of attracting attention in an overcrowded city. Louie is likewise shaped by contrast. He is naïve but professionally knowledgeable, morally conventional but not weak, and comic in his provincial innocence without being treated as contemptible. Their developing attachment is conveyed through changes in behaviour rather than romantic confession, culminating in Sophy’s decision to sew in the lace yoke.

The structure is circular and symbolically concentrated. The opening discussion of costume leads into a detailed account of Sophy’s dress; the central conflict repeatedly returns to its low-cut V; and the conclusion transforms that same garment through the makeshift yoke. This gives the story a clear progression from appearance, through dispute and mutual recognition, to a modest act of change. The ending remains indirect. Sophy refuses to admit that Louie influenced her and invents practical and fashionable excuses, while her defiance preserves her pride. The emotional resolution lies in the discrepancy between what she says and what the reader knows.

Imagery is predominantly concrete and urban. Shop windows, elevators, display cases, mosaic floors, cramped rooms, cheap jewellery and piles of shoes create a commercial city organized around display and exchange. Clothes and footwear function as an extended social metaphor: they reveal class, aspiration and employability, but they can also disguise need. The shoe shop is especially effective as a setting because it joins appearance to deception; inferior goods are made to look valuable just as workers must market themselves through dress, charm and performance. References to the stage, actors, balconies and “stage managers” reinforce the idea that city life requires continuous self-presentation.

The story’s thematic style depends on exposing the economic realities hidden beneath judgments about taste and morality. It examines the conflict between provincial standards and urban survival, the vulnerability of working women, the humiliations of male and female poverty, and the emotional significance of courtesy. Louie initially treats Sophy’s dress as a moral failing, while Sophy forces him to understand its practical purpose. In turn, his respectful treatment awakens in her a nearly forgotten desire to be regarded differently. The narrative does not wholly endorse either character’s position; instead, it lets their argument reveal how poverty complicates respectability.

Overall, the style is witty, intrusive, socially observant and emotionally warm. Its defining qualities are direct reader address, comic digression, ironic contrast, vivid material detail, dialect-heavy dialogue and a recurring symbolic object. The prose moves between caricature and compassion, using humour to make social criticism palatable and withholding overt romance in favour of gesture, implication and a final defensive joke.

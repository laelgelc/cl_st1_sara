The story adopts the style of a comic literary fairy tale transplanted into a modern urban setting. Its central conceit—the magical animation of a department-store mannequin—is presented with cheerful matter-of-factness rather than elaborate wonder. Fairyland, enchantment and mischievous elves coexist naturally with plate-glass windows, lunch counters, streetcars, police stations and commercial prices. This collision between fantasy and everyday city life drives both the plot and the humour.

The narration is third-person omniscient, linear and strongly authorial. The narrator freely explains motives, supplies background information and interprets events for the audience. Direct addresses such as “For you must consider, dear reader” create the intimacy of an oral storyteller and mark the work as children’s fiction. The narrative voice is affectionate toward the wax lady, repeatedly calling her “the poor thing,” “the poor creature” or “the inexperienced lady,” while treating pompous or unimaginative adults with gentle mockery. It maintains a tone of amused sympathy even when the heroine is damaged or imprisoned.

The prose is clear, accessible and moderately formal, with an early twentieth-century flavour. Sentences are often long and sequential, joined by conjunctions and temporal markers such as “then,” “when,” “before,” “so” and “whereupon.” These constructions give the story the steady forward movement of a narrated adventure. Explanatory clauses frequently clarify the logic of the fantasy: the heroine does not need food because she is mostly wood, and she possesses patience because of her former existence as an inanimate display. Such rational-sounding explanations lend comic plausibility to absurd events. Occasional inversions and elevated phrases—“In all Fairyland there is no more mischievous a person”—add a storybook formality.

The diction alternates between whimsical fantasy language and concrete commercial vocabulary. Names such as “Tanko-Mankie the Yellow Ryl” and “Inspector Mugg” are playful, rhythmic and caricatural, while exact prices such as “$19.98,” “$4.23” and “76-cent” evoke department-store advertising and mercantile bookkeeping. The persistent repetition of prices becomes a comic refrain, particularly in Mr. Floman’s complaints. Colloquial expressions including “whuckered,” “hurry-up wagon” and “funny bone” reinforce the period setting and informal humour.

Dialogue is frequent, brisk and theatrically staged. Most encounters follow a compact pattern: an ordinary person uses a familiar expression, the wax lady interprets it literally or fails to understand its social meaning, and the resulting exchange exposes her unnatural identity. “Cab, ma’am?” answered by “No … I’m wax” is characteristic of the story’s punning wordplay. Questions about fares, money, reading, death and criminal responsibility similarly produce comedy through semantic misunderstanding. Speech is socially differentiated: newsboys and conductors use compressed dialect spellings such as “Wot’ll ye ’ave?” and “yer mornin’ paper,” whereas officials and shopkeepers speak in more conventional prose. This creates a lively urban soundscape and quickly sketches class and occupation.

Characterization is broad and economical. Tanko-Mankie is a fairy-tale trickster defined almost entirely by his delight in mischief. The wax lady is an innocent outsider whose mind is compared to that of a newborn child. She learns by imitation rather than understanding, copying hats, gloves, restaurant orders and public behaviour from the people around her. Her literal-minded innocence gives her both comic appeal and pathos. Secondary characters are occupational types—the janitor, waiter, conductor, policeman, storekeeper and inspector—rather than psychologically complex individuals. Inspector Mugg is a comic embodiment of complacent rationalism: he rejects evidence, praises his own level-headedness and confidently states the false moral that dummies are only wood and wax.

The humour combines incongruity, repetition, literalism, slapstick and satire. Physical comedy appears in the janitor’s collapse, the conductor’s terrified flight and the policeman’s knocking knees. The wax lady’s near-indestructibility allows the streetcar accident to be described grotesquely but not realistically: she loses an ear and has her head caved in, yet immediately asks for her hat. This softens violence into cartoon-like absurdity. Institutional satire emerges when the police debate whether laws against theft can apply to a dummy and when property values matter more to Mr. Floman than the possibility that a new form of life has appeared. The story also mocks social conformity, since the heroine assumes that successful participation in society consists of wearing the right accessories and mechanically copying others.

Structurally, the story follows a cumulative episodic pattern. A brief magical opening establishes the cause; the heroine awakens and equips herself inside the store; she then passes through a sequence of increasingly disruptive encounters in public; the action culminates in arrest and official confusion; and a final magical reversal restores her to lifelessness. Each episode tests another social convention—fashion, eating, payment, literacy, transport, mortality, law or ownership. The escalation is controlled: private imitation becomes public embarrassment, then physical catastrophe, then institutional crisis. Tanko-Mankie’s return closes the frame and resolves the fantasy abruptly.

Imagery is selective rather than lush. Visual details concentrate on costume, colour and material: fluffy yellow hair, pink cheeks, pea-green gloves, wax skin, wood, glass and damaged clothing. The heroine is repeatedly viewed as an object on display, first behind a window and later by crowds in the street. Glass, mirrors, placards and price tags emphasize appearance, spectatorship and commodification. The narrative is more interested in action and comic logic than in atmospheric description, so settings are established through recognizable objects and occupations rather than extended sensory passages.

Beneath its light tone, the story explores identity, social learning and the uncertain boundary between person and object. The wax lady believes that resemblance and imitation should make her human, while society defines her through material composition and commercial ownership. Her brief life has a faintly melancholy arc: she gains consciousness, encounters only superficial customs and suspicion, and is returned to objecthood without anyone fully recognizing her experience. The closing exchange preserves this tension. Inspector Mugg asserts a tidy rational rule, but the policeman’s ungrammatical, whispered observation—“this one were a dummy as lived”—gives the final word to wonder and lived evidence rather than official certainty.

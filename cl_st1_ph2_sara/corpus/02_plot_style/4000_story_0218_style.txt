The story adopts the style of a nineteenth-century didactic fairy tale for children, combining domestic realism with light fantasy and an explicit moral purpose. Its world is familiar and orderly—home, school, errands, meals, charitable visits—but a single magical device, the enchanted “skipping shoes,” transforms ordinary duties into comic adventures. The fantasy is playful rather than threatening: the shoes discipline Kitty by physically compelling her to obey, while also rewarding kindness and willing cooperation with graceful dancing, extraordinary leaps, and access to the voices of woodland creatures. This gives the narrative the character of a moral lesson disguised as an entertaining day-long escapade.

The narrator is third-person omniscient and strongly authoritative, speaking with the confidence of an adult storyteller addressing young readers. Kitty’s actions and thoughts are freely reported, while the narrator occasionally offers general moral commentary, as in the observation that people feel happy after doing kind things. The opening immediately defines Kitty by her fault—she habitually says “I won’t” and “I can’t”—and contrasts her with “obliging children,” establishing the behavioral standard against which she will be judged. The tone remains affectionate rather than severe. Kitty is called naughty, cross, and disobedient, but her flaws are treated as correctable childish habits, and her punishment is humorous, proportionate, and ultimately beneficial.

The prose is clear, linear, and accessible, with moderately long sentences linked by semicolons, conjunctions, and sequential clauses. The diction is simple but distinctly period-flavored, using terms such as “mamma,” “Nursey,” “victuals,” “piazza,” “cosey,” and “oblige.” Repeated temporal and causal formulas—“One day,” “The words were hardly out of her mouth,” “The minute she gave up,” and “When all the errands were done”—create the smooth forward motion of oral storytelling. Repetition is central to the comic pattern: Kitty begins a refusal, the shoes instantly act, and she is carried to the place where duty requires her to go. Variations on “I won’t,” “I can’t,” “away went the shoes,” and “at one jump” give the story a refrain-like quality and reinforce its lesson without complicated explanation.

Dialogue is frequent, brief, and vividly characterizing. Kitty’s complaints, unfinished protests, sighs, and private remarks convey impulsiveness and self-dramatization, while her mother speaks calmly and persuasively. The adults generally guide rather than threaten her, and the mother’s decision to let Kitty “choose” whether to perform the charitable errands marks the decisive stage in her reform. Much of the humor comes from the discrepancy between Kitty’s words and her body: she declares that she cannot or will not move, while the shoes leap, trot, dance, or drag her onward. Exclamation marks, abrupt interruptions, and broken-off phrases such as “I’m too ti—” heighten the speed and physical comedy.

The plot has an episodic, cumulative structure. Each episode tests a related failing: reluctance to help, temptation to steal cake, distraction, lateness, anger, selfishness, or resistance to duty. At first the shoes operate as an external corrective, preventing forbidden actions and forcing obedience. Kitty then discovers their positive power when she offers to help another child and is rewarded with a marvelous leap. The emotional turning point comes when she voluntarily agrees to carry supplies to poor families. From then on, movement becomes easy and harmonious, indicating that her will has aligned with the lesson represented by the shoes. The day closes when the magic expires at sunset, restoring ordinary reality while preserving the habits and sympathies Kitty has acquired.

Imagery is concrete, bright, and animated. The shoes wink with button-like eyes, tap tunes with their heels, and seem to possess a mischievous personality. Movement verbs—“skipped,” “trotted,” “whisked,” “dragged,” “pranced,” and “waltzing”—sustain a buoyant kinetic energy. The Fairyland passage shifts into more lyrical nature writing, with fern arches, moss, wood flowers, squirrels, butterflies, frogs, and birds forming a miniature enchanted society. Similes are child-friendly and visual: Kitty moves like a grasshopper or an india-rubber ball; hungry children gather like young birds; the setting sun resembles the wink of a sleepy eye. Nature is both magical and educational, encouraging sympathy for “innocent and interesting little creatures.”

The governing themes are willing obedience, self-control, usefulness, kindness, and the transformation of duty into pleasure. The story distinguishes coerced goodness from chosen goodness: Kitty initially behaves properly only because the shoes compel her, but gradually learns that cheerful service brings freedom, delight, and social approval. Its moral is overt yet softened by comedy, sensory charm, and wish-fulfillment. Overall, the style is warm, brisk, whimsical, and gently corrective, with a strong domestic ethic and a child-centered fantasy mechanism that makes moral education visible through physical action.

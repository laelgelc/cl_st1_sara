The story adopts the manner of a nineteenth-century regional romance, combining an omniscient historical narrator with comedy of manners, maritime adventure, and an undercurrent of fatalism. Its opening is retrospective and elegiac: the couple’s deaths and joint funeral establish the end of the story before the narrative returns fifty years to explain how their marriage began. This circular structure gives the courtship the quality of an anecdotal local legend while creating gentle irony around the funeral text, “in their death they were not divided.” The narrator immediately qualifies the minister’s sentimental account of a marriage “made in Heaven,” hinting that human determination—especially Nance’s—had at least as much to do with it.

The narrative voice is urbane, companionable, and lightly intrusive. It moves freely between panoramic social observation, intimate access to characters’ thoughts, and direct commentary such as “the truth may be told” or “I, who tell the tale, will not dogmatise.” These interventions create the impression of a practiced storyteller addressing an audience familiar with the conventions of provincial life. The narrator is amused rather than satirical in a harsh sense: Troy’s snobbery, the congregation’s vigilance, the young women’s matrimonial calculations, and Samuel’s vanity are exposed with affectionate irony. Statements are frequently balanced by qualifications, producing a tone that is simultaneously authoritative and playfully noncommittal.

The prose alternates between expansive descriptive passages and brisk dramatic scenes. Sentences are often long, syntactically balanced, and rich in semicolons, subordinate clauses, and parenthetical qualifications. This measured syntax supports the historical, reflective tone, while short declarative lines—“There’s no such place”; “I told you a lie”—deliver major reversals with comic force. Lists of precise details are used to characterize communities and individuals: the congregation counts Samuel’s desk-thumps and notices his frayed cuff, while Nance observes the shape of his head and the ring on his finger. Such accumulation conveys the scrutiny of a small town and turns apparently trivial details into evidence of desire, status, and judgment.

The diction blends polished literary English with regional Cornish-inflected speech. The narrator uses formal or slightly archaic vocabulary—“dithyrambic,” “comely,” “lissom,” “cogent,” “dogmatise”—as well as nautical terminology such as “fore-sheet,” “tiller,” “close-hauled,” “sweeps,” and “painter.” Dialogue is strongly marked by dialect forms including “iss,” “you’m,” “thee,” “’ee,” “hissel’,” and “we’m.” This contrast does more than provide local colour: it encodes differences of class, education, and competence. Samuel speaks in correct, dignified standard English but is helpless at sea; Nance’s nonstandard speech accompanies practical authority, physical confidence, and decisive action. As the emotional intensity rises, her dialect also acquires a lyrical, almost ballad-like directness.

Setting is central to both atmosphere and plot. Troy and Ruan are defined through their narrow harbour, ferry, fishing economy, chapel culture, and mutual social prejudice. The geography becomes a map of hierarchy: Troy “looks down upon” Ruan literally and socially, though the towns are separated only by a penny ferry. The night voyage then removes Samuel from the ordered, judgmental world of chapel and town into an elemental space of wind, tide, frost, moonlight, and stars. Maritime description is concrete enough to make the journey credible, but it is also symbolic. Samuel believes his future is self-directed, yet his “destiny” is already “launched” on the tide; once aboard Nance’s boat, he has neither navigational knowledge nor practical control. The imagery repeatedly contrasts his abstract ambition with her command of physical reality.

Characterization proceeds through social observation, bodily detail, action, and irony rather than sustained psychological analysis. Samuel is youthful, zealous, ambitious, respectable, and somewhat vain. His fear of scandal is comic but historically plausible, since his livelihood depends on communal approval. Nance is introduced as socially inferior in Troy’s eyes but is progressively established as the stronger character: observant, physically capable, sexually direct, strategically bold, and willing to risk disgrace. Her proposal reverses conventional gender roles so completely that it becomes a comic abduction narrative. Yet the story avoids reducing her to caricature. Her trembling offer to release Samuel after bringing him home reveals vulnerability and moral awareness, while his answer turns coerced attraction into a choice, albeit one made under extraordinary circumstances.

Dialogue supplies much of the humour and momentum. The chapel girls’ gossip functions like a local chorus, explaining social rivalries and provoking Nance’s action. During the voyage, the exchange becomes a contest of incompatible vocabularies: Samuel invokes propriety, duty, ruin, and command, while Nance invokes love, bodily fitness, labour, and irreversible risk. Her blunt confession transforms what initially resembles a nocturnal pastoral call into a courtship ultimatum. Repetition—especially “ruin,” “look at me,” and “I’m glad you’ve come”—tracks shifts in power and emotion. The comedy depends on reversal: the minister summoned to guide a supposedly dying woman is instead carried off by a woman who intends to determine his life.

The pacing is carefully staged. The opening rapidly compresses fifty years and several deaths; the account of Samuel’s trial sermon broadens into social comedy; his arrival slows into scenic description and foreshadowing; and the nocturnal voyage unfolds almost in real time. Suspense develops through incremental unease: the closed ferry, the unexplained distance, Nance’s test of Samuel’s nautical ignorance, and the boat’s course away from Ruan. After the revelation, external action gives way to a prolonged emotional standoff beneath the stars. The kiss acts as the climax, after which the return journey is compressed and subdued. The final paragraph again accelerates through marriage and fifty years of companionship, restoring the frame established at the beginning.

The dominant tone is warm, ironic, and romantic, with brief touches of Gothic suspense. Nance’s moonlit appearance at the garden door initially resembles an apparition, and the false deathbed summons lends the episode a faintly uncanny edge. That atmosphere is soon redirected into farce and then into sensuous romance. Celestial imagery enlarges the emotional turning point: the crowded constellations, breathing firmament, shooting star, and moonlit body make Samuel’s surrender seem both physically immediate and cosmically ordained. This elevated imagery is knowingly counterbalanced by practical boat-handling and by Nance’s immediate command to unfasten the sheet and duck.

The story’s themes include love as an active force, the conflict between social convention and individual desire, gendered power, class prejudice, fate, and retrospective moral judgment. It treats Nance’s deception as genuinely improper but refuses a simple condemnation, measuring one audacious lie against fifty years of fidelity. The conclusion preserves this ambiguity through comic understatement: Samuel may not have chosen so much as been chosen, and the narrator declines to pronounce a final verdict. Overall, the style is distinguished by polished omniscient narration, affectionate regionalism, dialect-rich dialogue, precise maritime realism, comic reversals, lyrical night imagery, and a framing irony that turns an unconventional kidnapping into the origin story of an enduring marriage.

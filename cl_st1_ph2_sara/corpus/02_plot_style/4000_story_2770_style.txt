The story uses a framed, retrospective narrative characteristic of nineteenth-century realism. It begins in a sociable masculine setting—the smoking room after dinner—where an apparently casual discussion prompts a lawyer to recount a private tragedy. The opening collective “we” quickly yields to Le Brument’s first-person testimony, which then encloses the dying woman’s much longer first-person confession. This layered arrangement lends the tale the air of an authenticated case history: the reader receives the central drama through a witness whose professional status implies credibility, yet its emotional substance remains dominated by the mother’s subjective account.

The prose is formal, lucid, and largely chronological. Its diction combines legal and social precision—“will,” “heir,” “fee,” “worldly code of honor”—with strongly emotive language such as “atrocious confusion,” “perpetual laceration,” “frightful suffering,” and “abominable, endless waiting.” Material description is sparse but socially significant. The wealthy woman’s apartment, with its “elegant simplicity” and thick upholstery, establishes refinement and privilege before attention turns almost entirely to moral and psychological distress. The translated prose retains an older idiom and punctuation style, including honorifics, French titles, long dashes, emphatic repetitions, and occasional typographical irregularities.

Sentence structure alternates between composed exposition and agitated, cumulative speech. Le Brument’s framing narration is economical and controlled, whereas the woman’s confession relies on long periodic sentences, parallel clauses, rhetorical questions, exclamations, interruptions, and self-corrections. Her language repeatedly circles around guilt and justification: “What could I do?”, “Can any one explain such things?”, and “Do you believe…?” These appeals make her confession feel directed not only toward the lawyer but toward an imagined moral tribunal. Repetition—especially of “my son,” “my dear child,” “I waited,” and “cruel”—imitates obsession and gives the monologue an incantatory, increasingly desperate rhythm. Dashes and ellipses register hesitation, shame, breathlessness, and emotional collapse.

Dialogue carries nearly all of the dramatic weight. Rather than staging many scenes directly, the story compresses decades into a deathbed monologue, then sharply expands a few decisive moments: the discovered kiss, the son’s silent departure, the night of waiting, and the mother’s final plea. Pacing slows markedly around suspense and anguish. Repeated time markers—“an hour, two hours,” “all night,” “every day for the last twenty years”—turn waiting into the central temporal experience of the story. The son’s near-total silence and physical disappearance create a powerful negative presence; he becomes important through absence, while the mother’s torrent of speech attempts unsuccessfully to bridge that absence.

The tonal movement is from urbane curiosity to intimate horror, pity, and moral condemnation. Although the narrative presents adultery as the initiating transgression, its emotional emphasis falls less on scandal than on punishment, maternal suffering, and the disproportionate cruelty of inflexible judgment. The woman is portrayed through a mixture of self-accusation and self-defense: she calls herself weak and cowardly, yet describes passion as almost irresistible and emphasizes the harshness of women’s social position. Le Brument’s concluding label, “that criminal son,” supplies a forceful verdict, but the story retains some moral ambiguity because the son’s motives, fate, and perspective are withheld.

Overall, the style is confessional, melodramatic, and psychologically realist. It achieves intensity through narrative enclosure, restricted perspective, rhetorical accumulation, strategic silence, and emotional repetition rather than through elaborate imagery or external action. The ending returns abruptly to the smoking room and broadens one woman’s tragedy into a general observation about hidden dramas occurring in ordinary life, giving the tale the quality of both a legal anecdote and a moral exemplum.

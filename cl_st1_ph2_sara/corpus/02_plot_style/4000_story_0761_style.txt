The story is a comic small-town detective farce built around inversion, misunderstanding, and verbal literalism. It borrows the machinery of a mystery—an eccentric client, a threatened murder, surveillance, concealed motives, and a final killing—but continually subordinates suspense to humor. Its central joke is that Philo Gubb treats detective work as a set of mechanically learned procedures while lacking the judgment needed to interpret events. The plot repeatedly reverses expectations: the detective pays the client for the privilege of investigating, a “slow murder” by pea-shooter initially proves to be a pretext for free surveillance, and the apparently absurd murder claim is then vindicated in a startlingly literal fashion.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, with a genial, lightly mocking attitude toward every character. The narrator supplies compact background information, moves freely between outward action and private motives, and occasionally intrudes with ironic observations, as when an idle townsman criticizes another man for having too much spare time. Descriptions tend toward comic caricature rather than realism. Uncle Gabriel is assembled from memorable visual signs—pointed white beard, octagonal spectacles, ancient silk hat, faded frock coat—and his stinginess is exaggerated through the folkloric image of squeezing a silver dollar until the eagle squawks. Such hyperbole establishes character immediately and keeps the prose buoyant.

Dialogue dominates the story and provides most of its characterization, pacing, and humor. Speech is written in a stylized rural or small-town American vernacular, using forms such as “ain’t,” “goin’,” “feller,” “knowed,” “quatter,” and “deteckative.” Philo’s especially conspicuous malapropisms and awkward formulations—“What did Miss Syrilla convey the remark of?” and “If you insist to wish to know”—make his speech sound simultaneously formal, uneducated, and imitative. He has learned the outward vocabulary of professional detection without mastering ordinary idiom, just as he has learned investigative “Lessons” without acquiring practical insight. Uncle Gabriel’s language is plainer but strategically repetitive and argumentative, reflecting his ability to bargain, rationalize, and manipulate.

The comic rhythm often depends on extended exchanges in which a ridiculous premise is developed with solemn logic. The negotiation over the detective’s daily payment proceeds in tiny monetary increments, dramatizing Gabriel’s miserliness while reversing the expected client–detective relationship. Likewise, the discussion of cannonballs, rifle bullets, small-caliber pistols, and peas turns a transparent absurdity into an elaborate pseudo-logical proof. Repetition is crucial: phrases such as “shootin’ is shootin’,” the recurrent insistence on being murdered “by inches,” and the escalating numbers of shots mimic rigorous reasoning while exposing its foolishness. The characters rarely recognize the absurdity of their own arguments, so the humor is presented with a deadpan surface.

The prose alternates between relatively standard narrative English and heavily marked colloquial dialogue. Narrative sentences are clear, chronological, and moderately elaborate, often accumulating concrete details or extending a comic comparison. Dialogue sentences are shorter, more repetitive, and frequently organized as question-and-answer volleys. Paragraphs are generally brief, allowing rapid shifts between speakers and producing a brisk, theatrical pace. Parenthetical asides, emphatic italics, capitalized display lines, and labels such as “the lovelorn paper-hanger detective” or “the correspondence school detective” reinforce the story’s serialized, popular-fiction flavor.

Characterization is economical and type-based. Philo is earnest, gullible, vain about his credentials, and oddly admirable in his willingness to work “hard and faithful.” His dual occupation as detective and paper-hanger creates a persistent contrast between grand aspiration and mundane reality. Gabriel is the archetypal miser and trickster, calculating even when frightened. Mr. Medderbrook’s mercenary treatment of his daughter’s telegram echoes Gabriel’s bargaining and establishes money as a recurring source of comic exploitation. Secondary townspeople function as a gossip chorus: their casual conversation reveals the hidden divorce scheme, changes Philo’s interpretation of the case, and prepares the final reversal.

The setting is a socially intimate American town in which the bank, cigar store, office block, and private homes form a compact network of observation and rumor. Reputation travels quickly, idlers exchange intelligence, and professional roles are informal enough for a paper-hanger to advertise himself as a detective. Period details—telegrams, correspondence-school instruction, gold-mine stock, cigar-store loafers, formal frock coats, and the small local bank—give the story an early twentieth-century comic atmosphere without requiring extensive exposition.

Structurally, the story moves through a chain of transactions and reinterpretations. The opening episode involving Syrilla is largely detachable from the murder plot, but it establishes Philo’s romantic gullibility and the pattern of others extracting money from him. Gabriel’s arrival initiates the main case; the long interview gradually reveals the absurd weapon and suspected culprit; the surveillance scene exposes Gabriel’s deception; and the closing report overturns that exposure by announcing a real murder. The ending is a punch line rather than a resolution. Instead of solving the crime, Philo preserves his earlier theory and insists that the death was merely the culmination of a prolonged pea-shooter murder. His inability to produce a retort leaves him suspended between accidental correctness and social ridicule.

The prevailing tone is affectionate satire rather than cruelty. The story mocks pretension, greed, literal-mindedness, professional vanity, and amateur logic, yet it avoids psychological darkness even when murder occurs. Violence remains verbally mediated and almost cartoon-like. Overall, the style combines dialect comedy, caricature, ironic narration, escalating pseudo-reasoning, and a sharply engineered final reversal, producing a light, conversational parody of detective fiction.

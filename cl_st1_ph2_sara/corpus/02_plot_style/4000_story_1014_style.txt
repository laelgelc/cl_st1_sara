The story uses a first-person observational narrator who combines the roles of participant, reporter, social investigator, and comic essayist. His voice is educated, urbane, curious, and theatrically self-aware, frequently turning factual experience into imaginative spectacle. Although he travels with the police and records practical details, he remains a literary presence rather than an invisible witness: he digresses, jokes about his own eloquence, addresses the reader indirectly, and filters the river through a highly subjective sensibility.

The dominant atmosphere is nocturnal, wintry, and Gothic. Darkness is established through emphatic repetition—“very, very dark,” “bitter, bitter cold”—and then expanded into an almost total symbolic condition. Air, water, vessels, buildings, shadows, and even sounds become black. The Thames is figured as an image of death running through the living city, while noises of chains, engines, and turbulent water suggest drowning, imprisonment, and industrial menace. This morbidity is repeatedly offset by humour, however, so the piece never becomes unrelievedly grim. Suicide, crime, poverty, and violence coexist with verbal play, eccentric anecdote, and comic understatement.

The descriptive method is expansive and associative. A local east wind prompts a mock-global catalogue of mummy dust, temples, caravans, crocodiles, sphinxes, jungles, and Himalayan snow. Such passages enlarge physical sensation through fantasy and hyperbole. Personification is especially prominent: the wind has a “determined temper,” bridges frown, the night searches, and Waterloo Bridge becomes “Waterloo,” a speaking social character embodied by its toll-taker. The effect is to animate the city’s infrastructure and make London itself seem like a vast cast of personalities.

Sentences vary between terse exchanges and long, accumulating constructions. Extended sentences often proceed through semicolon-linked clauses, parenthetical interruptions, enumerations, and repeated conjunctions. Catalogues are a signature device, used both lyrically and journalistically: rivers, shades of black, criminal specialities, pieces of police equipment, and stages in a pursuit are all presented through rhythmic accumulation. Parallel structures such as “how Waterloo… and how…” reproduce the momentum of oral storytelling. Dashes, parentheses, colons, exclamation marks, and strategic italics or capitals create a performative rhythm, marking sudden turns, vocal emphasis, ironic commentary, and changes of pace.

The diction deliberately mixes registers. The narrator employs formal, Latinate, and antiquated phrasing—“component parts,” “felony,” “aforesaid,” “execution of his duty”—alongside nautical terminology, police language, occupational jargon, and demotic slang. Expressions such as “Cove,” “cut into,” “bilking the toll,” and “Three, ten” preserve the speech of working London. Dialect is represented through nonstandard grammar and eye dialect, including “giv’,” “know’d,” “wery,” “heerd,” and “picter.” These speech patterns individualise Waterloo and Pea while giving the narrative a documentary sense of overheard authenticity.

Dialogue is central and character-led. The narrator’s polished, figurative speech contrasts comically with Pea’s patience and practical economy, especially when Pea coughs to stop him “holding forth.” Waterloo’s monologues are anecdotal, repetitive, colloquial, and full of professional expertise. Questions from the narrator serve as unobtrusive prompts that open new branches of testimony. Much of the exposition is therefore dramatised as conversation rather than delivered as neutral background, allowing information about suicide, tolls, assaults, policing, and river crime to emerge through personality.

The humour is dry, humane, and often parenthetical. It depends on mock solemnity, exaggerated logic, euphemism, anticlimax, and the collision of elevated language with mundane facts. A damaged head may suffer little because it was “wrong in it afore”; Parliament commemorates Waterloo by renaming a bridge and saving the expense of a monument; crowds cry “Fire!” and “Murder!” in the hope that one may apply. Even the narrator’s educational aside about rowing becomes a comic story about a tutor consuming rum and egg for an alleged liver weakness. The humour generally exposes absurdity without stripping ordinary people of dignity.

Structurally, the story resembles a piece of literary reportage or a nocturnal urban sketch. It begins with atmospheric immersion, moves into a police stakeout, pauses for a long encounter with the personified Waterloo, returns to the river for a systematic account of criminal types, and ends with an inspection of the Wapping station. The journey down the Thames supplies a loose linear spine, while conversations and digressions create an episodic form. Transitions often follow physical movement—embarking, landing, passing under bridges, entering the tiers of shipping—or a new question addressed to an informant.

The treatment of factual material is vivid rather than clinical. Statistics, institutional procedures, criminal classifications, and inventories are incorporated into scenes rich in metaphor and comic characterisation. Tier-rangers, Lumpers, Truckers, and Dredgermen are explained with the precision of a social survey, but their methods are rendered as miniature dramas. Technical information is made memorable through concrete examples, occupational vocabulary, and comparisons to pantomime, warfare, streets, factories, or foundlings.

The social perspective is reform-minded but not overtly polemical. The narrative is fascinated by hidden labour, marginal lives, petty crime, institutional ingenuity, and the mechanisms by which a metropolis regulates itself. It acknowledges trouble, abusive husbands, desperation, drunkenness, and theft while admiring competence, courage, and preventive policing. Working figures are allowed extensive speech and practical authority; their knowledge often corrects or grounds the narrator’s romantic imagination.

Overall, the style is characterised by atmospheric urban realism enriched with Gothic imagery, comic personification, elaborate catalogues, energetic digression, socially attentive reportage, and sharply differentiated voices. Its most distinctive movement is between darkness and amusement, lyricism and statistics, cultivated narration and vernacular testimony. The city is presented simultaneously as a real system of bridges, boats, tolls, trades, and crimes, and as an animate, theatrical world whose objects, institutions, and inhabitants continually turn into stories.

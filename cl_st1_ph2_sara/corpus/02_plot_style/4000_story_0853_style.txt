The story uses an elegant, late-nineteenth-century realist style enriched by sentiment, irony, and French local colour. Its diction is formal and decorous, with recurrent French titles and expressions—“Mme.,” “ma tante,” “bonne,” “ma foi”—and with religious terms such as “angelus,” “bénitiers,” and “Sacré-Coeur.” These details establish a specifically French social world while contrasting the ordered seclusion of the convent with the fashionable, theatrical disorder of Paris. The vocabulary is often sensuous and gently ornate, especially in descriptions of spring, interiors, clothing, and devotional objects, but remains concrete enough to anchor the story in everyday life.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, moving freely between external observation, characters’ perceptions, and occasional authorial summary. It is especially close to Adrienne and Sister Agathe, allowing their emotional attachment to emerge through gestures, remembered sensations, and intimate thoughts rather than direct explanation. At other moments the narrator draws back into a lightly ironic social perspective, as in the brisk transition, “Paris had engulfed her,” or in the comic treatment of Sophie’s suspicions. This combination produces a tone that is affectionate without being naïve: the convent is peaceful and genuinely restorative, but also rigid, hierarchical, and capable of cruelty.

Sentence structure alternates between long, flowing descriptive periods and short, emphatic statements. Extended sentences accumulate sensory particulars and subordinate clauses, creating a leisurely rhythm suited to recollection, pastoral calm, and close observation. Brief declarations and fragments—“And then the commotion began,” “Yes, Adrienne was at home,” and “It was Sister Agathe”—mark shifts in scene or emotional emphasis. Exclamations, rhetorical questions, dashes, and repeated phrases give both narration and dialogue a lively, oral quality. The prose often uses cumulative listing to convey abundance or agitation, whether cataloguing convent furnishings, Parisian clutter, or Sophie’s grievances.

Dialogue is a major instrument of characterization. Sister Agathe speaks in tender, pious formulas; the Mother Superior’s speech is restrained and administrative; Sophie’s is torrential, theatrical, repetitive, and comic; Adrienne’s is impulsive, teasing, and commanding. Long reported anecdotes and monologues allow characters to expose themselves indirectly. Sophie, in particular, supplies crucial information while misunderstanding its significance, creating dramatic irony. Her belief that Adrienne’s annual flight is “insanity” is comic to the reader, who understands that the lilacs awaken a deep need for refuge and renewal. The dialogue also reveals the social ambiguity surrounding Adrienne: the convent knows her as a respectable widow and former pupil, whereas the Paris scenes disclose that she is a celebrated performer with lovers or admirers, a truth that ultimately leads to her exclusion.

Imagery is strongly sensory, with scent functioning as the central mnemonic and structural device. Lilac perfume collapses time and distance, replacing the noise and crowds of Paris with grass, sunlight, birds, insects, and convent walls. The lilacs symbolize spring, memory, innocence, and Adrienne’s recurring return to an earlier self. Their meaning darkens at the end: flowers once crushed in loving embraces fall from her arms and are finally swept away. Other recurring sensory motifs include polished white surfaces, soft natural sounds, bells, running water, and birdsong. These create the convent’s atmosphere of purity and repose, while the Paris apartment is defined by colour, luxury, disorder, chatter, cigarettes, theatrical garments, and hot-house roses.

The story’s settings are arranged as a sustained contrast. The convent is white, bare, rhythmic, pastoral, and communal; Paris is gilded, crowded, artificial, indulgent, and socially exposed. Adrienne herself bridges these worlds. Her brown dress, cultivated music, generosity, and childlike delight make her appear almost conventual during her visits, yet her buoyancy, sensual responsiveness, fashionable clothes, and playful violence belong to the stage and salon. Characterization therefore depends on duality rather than simple moral opposition. She is neither hypocritical nor wholly innocent: she sincerely loves the convent, but partitions her life into incompatible identities. Sister Agathe likewise combines genuine affection with submission to institutional authority.

The pacing is deliberately asymmetrical. The first visit unfolds slowly through arrival, inspection, walking, conversation, prayer, bedtime, and departure, allowing the reader to inhabit the annual ritual in detail. A rapid cut to Paris then recontextualizes everything that preceded it. The following year is compressed until the repeated lilac stimulus sets the pattern in motion again. Because the second journey echoes the first, the reader anticipates another joyful welcome; the locked door therefore arrives as a sharp reversal. This mirrored construction gives the ending its force: nearly every earlier emblem of welcome is inverted into rejection.

The dominant tones are pastoral tenderness, domestic comedy, restrained sensuality, and final pathos. Humour emerges from affectionate exaggeration, social incongruity, and verbal performance rather than from ridicule alone. The ending becomes stark and melodramatic but remains controlled by concrete actions: the cautious opening of the door, the returned devotional gifts, the dropped lilacs, the locked key, Sister Agathe’s muffled sobs, and the lay sister’s broom. The final image is especially economical and symbolic. By sweeping away the blossoms, the institution erases the visible trace of Adrienne’s love and annual pilgrimage, turning an ordinary household act into a quiet emblem of judgment, repression, and emotional betrayal.

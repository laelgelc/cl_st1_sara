The story uses a warm, sentimental, domestic style associated with late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century children’s and family fiction. Its omniscient third-person narrator remains close to the Golden household, observing the characters with affection and mild humour. The narrative voice is morally assured but rarely intrusive: values such as Christian charity, familial duty, compassion, and loyalty emerge chiefly through the family’s decisions rather than through explicit authorial preaching.

Dialogue dominates the storytelling and provides most of the characterization. Each family member is distinguished by recurrent habits of speech: Mother Golden’s exclamation “My heart!”, Father Golden’s measured “saying and supposing,” the children’s impulsive choruses, and Benjamin’s breathless, colloquial energy. Rural or small-town vernacular appears in forms such as “’twas,” “ain’t,” “swallering,” “most,” and “if so’t had been.” These usages create a plainspoken social setting and convey homeliness rather than harsh realism. Biblical language and references—“for Christ’s sake,” “Amen,” the naming of Benjamin, and the idea of being “nearest heaven”—give the prose a gentle Protestant moral framework.

The diction is accessible, concrete, and emotionally direct. Domestic objects and familiar details—the leather Bible, cabinet organ, tidies, warm milk, geraniums, and leather chair—establish an ordinary, comfortable household. Descriptions become more lyrical at moments of tenderness, particularly in the tableau of the baby on Mother Golden’s knee, where light, colour, and texture converge in phrases such as “yellow flossy curls” and “rosy, dimpled creature.” Diminutives and affectionate expressions, including “little precious man,” “Ruthie,” and “Benny,” reinforce the intimate family atmosphere.

Sentence structure alternates between short, animated exchanges and longer, balanced narrative passages. Exclamations, questions, repetitions, interruptions, and collective responses create lively comic rhythm in the family scenes. In contrast, Father Golden’s climactic speech uses deliberate parallel constructions and carefully weighted alternatives—Benjamin must either “go” or “stay”—to slow the pace and lend ceremonial gravity to the decision. Repetition is a major device: “A baby!” passes through the household as an echo, while the final sequence of individual declarations turns acceptance into a family ritual.

Characterization relies on speech, gesture, and contrast rather than psychological analysis. Mother Golden is practical, nurturing, decisive, and instinctively charitable; Father Golden is slower, formal, benevolent, and quietly strategic. The older children are sober and dutiful, while Benjamin embodies disorder, vitality, and spontaneous affection. Humour comes from naïve logic and incongruity: Adam assumes speech depends on teeth, Joseph cites a toothless uncle who “talks like a buzz-saw,” and the foundling’s later mischief is explained through games, excuses, and April Fool’s tricks. The narrator treats these comic moments indulgently, never undermining the characters’ dignity.

The two-part structure is symmetrical and strongly shaped. The first part presents Benjamin’s arrival and provisional acceptance; the second, set ten years later, tests whether that acceptance has become permanent love. Mother Golden’s absence adds elegiac feeling, while her portrait and remembered principles continue to govern the household. The central conflict is less about whether Benjamin will truly be expelled than about forcing the siblings to recognize and articulate the bond they already feel. This gives the plot the form of a moral revelation rather than a suspense-driven crisis.

The emotional register blends pathos with comedy. Death, abandonment, unfulfilled love, and the prospect of separation are handled gently, without graphic suffering. Potential melodrama is moderated by practical domestic action and humorous dialogue. The ending releases the solemnity through Benjamin’s exuberant entrance and the revelation of his April Fool’s prank. The unanimous final response—everyone volunteering to fly his kite—translates their formal promise into immediate companionship, producing a neat, optimistic, and emotionally satisfying closure.

Overall, the style is wholesome, affectionate, theatrically dialogic, and didactic without being austere. Its signature effect comes from combining vernacular family comedy, sentimental domestic imagery, biblical morality, and a carefully staged affirmation that kinship is created by sustained love and chosen responsibility rather than blood alone.

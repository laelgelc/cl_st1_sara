The story uses a framed first-person narration that quickly yields to an extended oral performance. An initially unobtrusive observer describes a social gathering, but the narrative centre is soon occupied by Osip Mihalitch Polzunkov, whose confession is delivered directly to a noisy, mocking audience. This layered arrangement makes storytelling itself part of the drama: Polzunkov is not merely recounting his humiliation but compulsively reenacting it before people who may humiliate him again. Frequent interruptions, questions, heckling and bursts of laughter preserve the atmosphere of a live anecdote rather than a polished written memoir.

The dominant voice is voluble, digressive and emotionally unstable. Polzunkov repeatedly announces, delays, qualifies and restarts his story, moving between boastfulness, shame, indignation, self-reproach and theatrical excitement. His manner is intensely self-conscious: he comments on his own bad jokes, rhetorical excesses and weakness even while continuing to indulge them. Repetition—“I melted,” “speak, speak,” “talking and talking and talking”—conveys agitation and compulsion. Exclamations, ellipses, abrupt questions, parenthetical asides and broken constructions imitate breathless speech and create the impression that emotion is outrunning control.

The diction mixes bureaucratic formality, sentimental cliché, colloquial phrasing and comic grandiloquence. Official language about reports, ranks, accounts, petitions and dismissal sits beside idioms such as “feather his nest,” “taken his hook” and “kick up your heels.” Polzunkov habitually elevates trivial or sordid events through literary expressions, mock-heroic comparisons and fashionable phrases, only to undercut himself or be corrected by his listeners. References to epics, Penates, prodigal sons, Homeric laughter and patriotic verse lend his account a parody of cultivated eloquence. The translated nineteenth-century register is also visible in formal modes of address, patronymics, French expressions such as “tête-à-tête” and “bon voyage,” and socially marked terms connected with provincial Russian officialdom.

The syntax is expansive and cumulative, especially in Polzunkov’s monologues. Long sentences gather clauses, qualifications and emotional afterthoughts until they verge on incoherence; these are contrasted with short audience interjections such as “Tell it,” “How so?” and “Well?” Dialogue therefore governs both rhythm and pacing. The audience’s clipped remarks accelerate the tale, expose evasions and puncture inflated language, while Polzunkov’s digressions repeatedly retard the revelation. Important information is disclosed piecemeal, often through correction or reinterpretation, so that apparent reconciliation, engagement and moral redemption gradually reveal themselves as stages in a calculated fraud.

The tone combines farce, social satire and pathos. Comic effects arise from physical exaggeration, bad puns, bathos, melodramatic tears and Polzunkov’s disproportionate responses. Yet the laughter is persistently uncomfortable because it depends on a vulnerable man’s degradation. His audience treats him as entertainment, just as Fedosey Nikolaitch treats him as a useful instrument. The story thus shifts from comic anecdote toward tragicomic exposure without abandoning humour. Even the climax—the official use of an April Fools’ resignation letter—is both an ingenious joke and an act of bureaucratic cruelty.

Characterization is achieved mainly through speech and performance rather than descriptive exposition. Polzunkov appears vain, foolish, sentimental and morally compromised, but also painfully candid and eager for affection. His recurring inability to defend himself is central to his character: he recognizes exploitation after the fact, narrates it lucidly, and still cannot call his exploiter a scoundrel to his face. Fedosey Nikolaitch is characterized through polished paternal language that conceals calculation. His emotional displays, religious appeals and talk of forgiveness invert the actual moral situation, allowing the corrupt superior to lecture and “bless” the subordinate he is cheating. The surrounding guests function collectively as a chorus, encouraging disclosure while reinforcing Polzunkov’s status as a comic spectacle.

Structurally, the narrative is built on reversals and delayed ironies. The initial bribe turns out to be payment for incriminating papers; the apparent restoration to a family circle becomes entrapment; the engagement becomes leverage; the returned money becomes self-ruin; and Polzunkov’s own April Fools’ joke becomes the legal means of dismissing him. The date of 1 April is therefore both a comic motif and a structural principle: the supposed joker is the true dupe. Objects and documents—the roll of notes, resignation letter, report, sealed envelope, bird-cage and returned starling—carry decisive force, emphasizing a world in which written bureaucracy overrides intention and sentiment.

The style’s central effect comes from the clash between self-dramatization and self-exposure. Polzunkov tries to shape his experience into an “exquisite” comic story, but his language continually reveals loneliness, shame and a desperate need to please. The closing anticlimax is especially characteristic: after preparing to denounce Fedosey Nikolaitch, he admits that he could not bring himself to do it. This subdued final confession retrospectively deepens the entire performance, leaving the reader with an uneasy mixture of amusement, pity and moral disgust.

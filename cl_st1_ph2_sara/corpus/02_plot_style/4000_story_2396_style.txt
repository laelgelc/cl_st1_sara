The story adopts the form of a nineteenth-century antiquarian or literary memoir, framed as one instalment from the papers of a deceased Irish parish priest. Its governing voice is educated, reflective, and mildly pedantic: the narrator addresses a “dear friend,” advances a cultural argument, supplies examples, recalls personal encounters, and comments on the evidence as though preparing an informal scholarly essay. This creates a layered, documentary quality in which criticism, anecdote, oral history, and imaginative literature are presented together.

The prose is formal, expansive, and highly periodic. Sentences often unfold through several subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, balanced phrases, and enumerations before arriving at their main point. The narrator favours emphatic repetition and rhetorical contrast—beauty against absurdity, native feeling against caricature, oral tradition against written history—and frequently strengthens assertions through paired or tripled adjectives. Elevated Latinate diction such as “misconceptions,” “pretensions,” “perverseness,” and “illustration” gives the commentary a learned tone, while proverbial expressions, conversational asides, and restrained irony prevent it from becoming wholly academic. The voice is earnest in its defence of Irish culture but also capable of affectionate amusement at eccentricity and human vanity.

A central stylistic feature is the alternation between standard literary English and represented Irish vernacular. The narrator’s own language is polished and metropolitan, whereas Finley’s speech and ballads use phonetic spellings and dialect forms such as “an’,” “iv,” “wor,” “thrembled,” “sthream,” and “undher.” Grammar, idiom, and pronunciation are rendered conspicuously to suggest oral performance. This dialect is not used solely for comedy: it conveys social identity, emotional force, local colour, and the rhythms of communal storytelling. Humour does arise from verbal turns—most notably Finley’s comparison of a written poem to a surveyed pigsty—but the surrounding argument insists that vernacular expression can possess dignity and genuine poetic power.

The narrative structure is discursive and cumulative rather than conventionally plot-driven. It begins as a polemic against stereotypes of Irish poetry, moves through lyrical and political specimens, develops into a character sketch of Michael Finley, and culminates in the long ballad of Phaudhrig Crohoore. Transitional remarks repeatedly explain why each extract is being introduced, producing the impression of curated evidence. The narrator also authenticates the material through claims of memory, transcription, eyewitness experience, and personal difficulty in obtaining it. Such devices simulate editorial reliability even while admitting fragmentation, imperfect preservation, and questionable methods.

The inserted poems create marked tonal and generic variety. The first lyric is mournful, polished, and conventionally Romantic, using moonlight, rivers, hawthorn, night winds, fading years, and seasonal decay to associate natural scenery with bereavement. Its smooth metre, recurrent sounds, and restrained melancholy support the narrator’s claim that Irish writers can achieve formal correctness. The political verses are harsher and more declamatory, relying on curses, patriotic colour symbolism, religious invocation, and direct praise of heroic sacrifice. The comic stanza about supper displays deliberately loose rhyme, serving as a foil to the more accomplished examples.

The ballad of Phaudhrig Crohoore shifts fully into vigorous oral narrative. Its verse uses rhyming couplets, repeated formulas, hyperbole, parallel constructions, and incremental action. Phaudhrig is drawn in heroic, almost legendary proportions: his height and strength are exaggerated, his voice is compared to thunder, and his eye to lightning. Repeated lines function like a refrain and would help an oral performer maintain momentum and audience recognition. Lists of surnames, festivities, physical actions, and crowd noises produce abundance and communal energy. The wedding sequence accelerates through strings of participles—roaring, jumping, jigging, flinging, joking, blessing, kissing, singing—before sudden silence and Crohoore’s theatrical entrance create a strong dramatic reversal.

Dialogue in the ballad is direct, confrontational, and ceremonially emphatic. Characters announce motives and loyalties in public rather than revealing them through subtle psychological narration. Emotion is externalised through gestures, pallor, tears, looks, swelling hearts, loud voices, and decisive violence. The action follows folk-ballad logic: love is absolute, hatred equally strong, villains and heroes are plainly distinguished, a single blow resolves the rivalry, and the lovers escape before the crowd can react. The closing reference to the rebellion of 1798 enlarges the romantic adventure into patriotic legend, converting the hero’s private courage into national sacrifice.

Imagery throughout draws heavily on Irish landscape and Romantic nature. Rivers, mountains, mists, moonlight, wild flowers, caverns, hawthorn, storms, autumn leaves, and green fields provide both atmosphere and symbolic resonance. Similes are vivid and accessible rather than intricate: years pass like dead leaves on a current, a man enters like a black cloud among stars, a crowd rises like a swarm of bees, and voices resemble thunder or an eagle’s call. Colour carries emotional and political meaning, especially green, red, black, pale moonlight, and wintry imagery.

The prevailing tone combines cultural advocacy, nostalgia, humour, melancholy, and heroic excitement. The narrator treats oral tradition with respect while acknowledging its roughness, absurdities, and instability. His irony is generally genial, though the concluding comparison with Scott’s “Lochinvar” adds a wry scholarly note about originality and literary precedence. Overall, the style seeks to elevate indigenous Irish popular culture without smoothing away its dialect, exaggeration, performative energy, or communal roots.

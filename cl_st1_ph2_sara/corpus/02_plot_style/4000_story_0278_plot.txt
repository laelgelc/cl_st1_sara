Wing Biddlebaum, a timid and isolated old man living outside Winesburg, anxiously hopes for a visit from his only friend, young reporter George Willard. Around George, Wing briefly overcomes his fear and speaks passionately about individuality, dreams, and resisting society’s influence. His expressive hands are central to his communication, yet he is terrified of them and usually keeps them hidden.

Wing was once Adolph Myers, a beloved schoolteacher in Pennsylvania whose gentle habit of touching his pupils’ shoulders and hair helped inspire them. After a troubled student falsely described imagined sexual misconduct as fact, townspeople interpreted the teacher’s affectionate gestures as evidence of abuse. He was beaten, nearly lynched, and driven from town.

Taking a new name, he settled in Winesburg and spent twenty years in fearful solitude, believing his hands were somehow responsible for his suffering. When he instinctively reaches toward George with the same affectionate gesture, the memory of his persecution overwhelms him and he flees. Back at home, he waits in vain for George, eats alone, and prepares for bed, his rapidly moving fingers resembling those of a priest counting a rosary—a final image of his suppressed tenderness, trauma, and loneliness.

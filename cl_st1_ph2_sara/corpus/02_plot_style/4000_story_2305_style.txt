The story is shaped as a first-person dramatic monologue delivered by a small-town barber to an almost entirely silent customer. Its conversational frame creates the impression of spontaneous talk rather than formal narration: the speaker digresses, repeats himself, explains local relationships, points to objects in the shop, and periodically returns to the practical business of barbering. The final question, “Comb it wet or dry?”, abruptly restores the everyday setting after the account of a death, giving the story a circular, understated close.

The narrative voice is strongly vernacular. Nonstandard grammar, double negatives, regional contractions, dropped final consonants, and phonetic spellings—such as “ain’t,” “would of,” “nothin’,” “settin’,” and “w’ile”—reproduce oral speech and establish the barber’s social and geographical milieu. Sentences often accumulate through loose coordination with “and,” “but,” “well,” and “so,” while habitual fillers such as “as I say,” “for instance,” and “I guess” mimic the rhythms of an anecdotal storyteller thinking aloud. Repetition is central to the voice: stock judgments like “He certainly was a card!” recur as comic refrains, even as the incidents being described become increasingly cruel.

The diction is plain, concrete, and rooted in local life. Barbershop chairs, shaving mugs, spittoons, poolrooms, circuses, picture shows, store credit, cheap gin, and nearby towns furnish a socially specific world. Characters are introduced through gossip, physical peculiarities, occupations, debts, romances, and community reputations rather than through sustained psychological description. The town emerges as a network of stories in which everyone watches, judges, and talks about everyone else. Explanatory asides give the speech an intimate, locally knowledgeable quality, while also showing that the narrator assumes his listener is an outsider.

The story’s humour relies on anecdote, mimicry, wordplay, practical jokes, deadpan phrasing, and repeated comic formulas. Early episodes are structured like barbershop jokes, with a setup, quoted exchange, and punch line. As the narrative continues, however, the same comic machinery is applied to humiliation, domestic neglect, intimidation, and sexual aggression. This produces a dark tonal shift without any corresponding change in the narrator’s manner. He continues to describe Jim as mischievous, amusing, and “good at heart,” so the widening gap between his genial language and the brutality of the events generates the story’s strongest irony.

The barber is therefore a limited and morally unreliable narrator, though not necessarily a consciously deceptive one. He supplies enough evidence for the reader to recognize Jim as abusive and dangerous, yet repeatedly minimizes his conduct as joking or “mischief.” Euphemisms such as “a little affair,” “the rough stuff,” and “a kind of a raw thing” reveal the community’s tolerance of male cruelty. His sympathy is inconsistent: he pities Julie, Paul, and Jim’s family, but also reproduces insulting labels, laughs at humiliations, and remains nostalgic for the entertainment Jim provided. The style asks the reader to judge more severely than the narrator does.

Structurally, the story progresses through associative digression. The barber moves from Jim’s empty shaving mug to his employment, marriage, jokes, enemies, Julie’s infatuation with the doctor, and finally the shooting. Apparently incidental stories gradually become functional clues. Jim’s talent for imitating voices prepares for the deception of Julie; his mistreatment of Paul helps explain Paul’s vulnerability; Doc Stair’s role as coroner becomes crucial after the death; and the doctor’s remark that such a man “ought not to be let live” casts retrospective suspicion over the official verdict. The accumulation is leisurely, but the plotting is controlled beneath the narrator’s rambling surface.

Dialogue is embedded frequently and rendered in the same colloquial register as the narration. It individualizes characters only lightly; its greater purpose is to dramatize the public, performative nature of town life. Jim’s identity depends on having an audience, while the crowd’s laughter enables his behaviour. The unheard customer is also important: because the barber continually addresses “you,” the reader is placed in the barber chair and made into a captive listener, exposed to the town’s assumptions and invited to infer what the speaker cannot or will not see.

The tone combines geniality, nostalgia, gossip, cruelty, pathos, and menace. Its emotional restraint is especially effective near the end. The death is not narrated with melodramatic intensity but reported through practical details and official conclusions. Ambiguity remains over whether the shooting was truly accidental, whether Paul acted on Doc Stair’s words, or whether the doctor deliberately suppressed further investigation. The style does not resolve these possibilities; instead, it lets suggestion arise from juxtaposition and omission.

Overall, the prose uses colloquial realism and comic storytelling to expose communal callousness. Its signature effect is dramatic irony: an apparently friendly barber tells what he considers an entertaining local reminiscence, while the reader gradually perceives a much darker story about bullying, misogyny, complicity, revenge, and possibly concealed murder. The casual closing line intensifies that effect by showing ordinary life continuing unchanged after everything that has been disclosed.

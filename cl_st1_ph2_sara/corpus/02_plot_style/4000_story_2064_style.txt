The story adopts a retrospective first-person voice in which an urbane adult narrator revisits the follies of his adolescence. The persona is confessional without being solemn: he exposes his younger self’s vanity, credulity and appetite for forbidden pleasures while allowing the older voice to supply ironic qualifications. This double perspective is central to the comedy. The boy experiences the music hall as a site of moral danger and intoxicating glamour, whereas the adult recognises the absurdity both of youthful dissipation and of the moral panic surrounding it. Occasional references to later life broaden the satire, as when the narrator links a childhood friend’s polished hypocrisy to his eventual legal career.

The prevailing tone is genial, amused and mock-serious. Minor incidents are narrated in the elevated language of temptation, ruin, treaty, battle and divine punishment, creating a sustained contrast between style and subject. A decision to visit a music hall becomes a fall from grace; an agreement between nephew and uncle is presented as a diplomatic compact; and an exchange of insults is magnified into heroic combat. The narrator rarely states a joke bluntly. Instead, he builds a reasonable-sounding argument and lets its logic turn quietly absurd, often ending a paragraph or episode with a dry corrective or delayed punchline.

The diction mixes formal, Latinate narration with colloquial speech. Phrases such as “subsequent investigation,” “ethical relapse,” “singularly unresponsive landed proprietor” and “the necessary qualities for this part of his duty” give disreputable or trivial behaviour a decorous surface. Against this appear slang, contractions, dropped initial consonants and working-class pronunciations: “sprung half a dollar,” “that rot,” “‘Ear, ‘ear,” “Blow yer zither,” and “Gawd’s sake.” Social distinctions are therefore heard directly in the language. The narrator’s polished prose, the chairman’s aspirational malapropisms and the audience’s vigorous vernacular all contribute to the comedy, though the popular audience is portrayed with affectionate fascination rather than simple contempt.

Sentences are often long, balanced and elaborately qualified. Parentheses, dashes and subordinate clauses allow the narrator to interrupt himself, revise a claim or insert an afterthought that undercuts the main statement. Repetition of “I think” and “whether” constructions creates comic uncertainty, while exact-sounding details—“five and three-quarter minutes by the clock”—lend mock-documentary authority to obvious exaggeration. Short sentences are strategically placed after extended passages for emphasis, as in “Then she began.” Such abrupt transitions control pace and announce a comic escalation.

The humour depends heavily on verbal inversion, literal interpretation and zeugma-like ambiguity. The “most satisfactory” cigar is satisfactory only because it makes the smoker want no more; the call to suppress music halls is confused with a call to suppress short skirts; and a heavenly city promising “Rest and Peace” is crowded with angels noisily blowing enormous trumpets. Moral categories are repeatedly destabilised by practical observation. The improving Shakespearean entertainment is rejected for the supposedly corrupt music hall, but respectable adults prove hypocritical, moral literature proves grotesque, and the unruly performer ultimately displays genuine artistry and professional force.

Structurally, the narrative is episodic and digressive. It begins as a youthful initiation story, moves through remorse and satirical commentary on temperance literature, then jumps forward to later visits and develops into an extended music-hall anecdote. Transitions arise through memory rather than through a tightly plotted causal sequence. The early sections establish the narrator’s attraction to compromise, especially through the symbolic “little bridge” between the broad and narrow ways. The later music-hall scene then becomes the story’s comic set piece, occupying increasingly more space and shifting the emphasis from autobiography to theatrical observation.

Dialogue is used both dramatically and sociologically. The chairman’s repeated attempts to introduce the performer are continually broken by heckling, so the scene acquires the rhythm of live performance. Interruptions, callbacks and variations on the disputed word “zithern” create accumulating comic momentum. The narrator sometimes pauses the scene for an aside, but the exchanges themselves carry much of the characterisation. Speech reveals Skegson’s duplicity, the uncle’s convenient morality, the chairman’s weakness, the crowd’s hostility and the performer’s combative authority.

The final confrontation is rendered through extravagant metaphor and mock-epic imagery. The performer’s language becomes a physical weapon: it strikes, coils like a whip, tosses, blinds, tramples and surrounds its victim. Biblical, classical and heroic registers mingle with the low setting, comparing the antagonists to eagle and lamb or invoking gods, lightning and prophetic curses. Anaphoric sentence openings—especially repeated uses of “It”—produce a rapid, cumulative assault that imitates the torrent of abuse without reproducing its actual obscenities. This avoidance is itself a comic technique: the narrator describes the insults with grand aesthetic admiration, leaving their precise content to the imagination.

Characterisation is caricatural but vivid, relying on a few decisive traits. Skegson is prematurely lawyerly; the uncle is severe until his own presence requires concealment; the chairman is mild, sleepy and unequal to his office; the coalheaver is physically imposing but verbally defeated; and the performer changes from a falsely exotic “Signorina” into a commanding popular artiste. Names and professional labels—“Monarch of Mirth,” “world-renowned performer,” “Man About Town”—are deliberately inflated, exposing the self-advertising culture of entertainment and journalism.

The prose repeatedly treats popular entertainment as both ridiculous and artistically vital. Its satire ranges across middle-class respectability, temperance propaganda, legal solemnity, family hypocrisy, theatrical pretension and class etiquette. Yet the narrative’s ultimate energy comes from admiration for performance, timing and verbal ingenuity. The closing reversal, in which the unsuccessful zithern player discovers fame by turning her authentic street language into a stage speciality, crystallises the story’s governing style: polished comic narration celebrating the anarchic force of vernacular speech.

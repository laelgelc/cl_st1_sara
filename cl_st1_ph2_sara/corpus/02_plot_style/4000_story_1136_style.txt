The story uses a highly intrusive, conversational third-person narrator who repeatedly addresses the reader, anticipates objections, asks rhetorical questions, and comments on the mechanics of storytelling itself. Its opening is overtly metafictional: the narrator admits that the “hungry girl in a big city” is a familiar subject, jokes about plagiarism and literary precedent, and promises only a new “slant” on an old plot. This self-conscious framing creates the manner of an oral performance or vaudeville routine, with digressions, mock disclaimers, direct appeals, and comic asides continually interrupting the narrative.

The dominant tone is comic-satirical, but it rests on genuine social indignation and pathos. Hunger, unemployment, urban indifference, and class inequality are treated seriously, yet described through wit, exaggeration, and bitter irony. The luxurious fruit display becomes “a breeder of anarchism” and “a second feast of Tantalus”; the department-store grocery is at once a delicatessen, market, and theatrical spectacle. Humour rarely cancels the suffering. Instead, it sharpens the contrast between abundance and deprivation, making the city’s plentiful food seem actively cruel to someone who cannot buy it.

The diction mixes literate, allusive narration with early twentieth-century American colloquial speech. Biblical references, classical allusions, and phrases such as “piece de resistance” sit beside vaudeville slang, advertising language, commercial jargon, and demotic expressions such as “Beat it,” “Search me,” and “Surest thing you know.” Dialogue is rendered phonetically and idiomatically to distinguish social types, using contractions, nonstandard grammar, clipped syntax, and period slang. The narrative also contains dated ethnic and racial labels characteristic of its historical setting. Chicago is evoked through specific streets, department stores, public transport, policemen, shop displays, and prices, giving the story a strong urban and period texture.

Sentences vary markedly in rhythm. Long, cumulative catalogues reproduce the overwhelming profusion of food, piling up fruits, cheeses, meats, smells, colours, and textures until the prose itself feels excessive. These are balanced by short emphatic fragments—“Maymeys from Cuba,” “The wantonness of it!”—and by abrupt questions and interjections. Parenthetical remarks, dashes, colons, italics in effect, and exclamations give the narration a restless, improvisatory cadence. Repetition is important: the recurring phrase “maymeys from Cuba” changes from an absurd luxury into the key to the final misunderstanding, while repeated lists of restaurants and delicacies intensify Jennie’s obsessive hunger.

Description is sensuous and hyperbolic, especially where food is concerned. Colour, scent, texture, temperature, and imagined taste are rendered with almost grotesque richness: peaches are “dewy and golden,” sausages form “great rosy mounds,” and baked apples swim in “lakes of golden syrup.” Food is frequently personified as mocking, tempting, or taunting the hungry. The imagery therefore makes material inequality physical. Windows, counters, and glass cases function as barriers separating spectacle from possession, while the city becomes a gigantic restaurant through which Jennie must move without being permitted to eat.

Characterisation is economical and often caricatural. Minor figures are introduced through one or two sharply observed physical details, occupational signs, or verbal habits: the stout theatre-goer, the lime-covered workman, the sniffing errand boy, the monumental crossing policeman, and the unseeing floorwalker. The itemised description of the crowd resembles a ledger or stage cast, reducing city dwellers to representative social types. Jennie, by contrast, is developed through bodily sensation and increasingly narrowed perception. Her history is deliberately withheld; the narrator insists that the fact of her hunger matters more than a sentimental explanation for it. This avoidance of elaborate backstory resists the convention that poverty must be morally justified before it deserves sympathy.

The narrative movement is episodic and accumulative. Jennie passes from the fruit window to the streets, the policeman, prospective benefactors, the floorwalker, and finally the grocery department. Each encounter offers the possibility of help but ends in indifference, misunderstanding, or humiliation. As hunger intensifies, the focus tightens from broad social satire to bodily crisis. The sampling sequence briefly creates hope, then makes appetite more painful, culminating in the attempted theft of a scone and Jennie’s collapse. Pacing accelerates through fragments and compressed sensory impressions at the climax: “a closing in upon her—a whirl of faces.”

Irony governs both structure and ending. The exotic maymeys first symbolize pointless luxury: goods have been transported from Cuba for wealthy consumers while Jennie lacks a quarter. In her fainting murmur, however, “Jennie” and “maymeys from Cuba” are misheard as “Mamie” and a claim that she herself is Cuban. The bystanders respond not to her starvation but to the supposed oddity of a light-complexioned Cuban woman. This comic twist is deliberately cruel, returning the story to its opening concern with fresh “slants” and surprise endings while exposing the crowd’s failure to understand the obvious human tragedy before it.

Overall, the style combines journalistic social observation, theatrical comedy, vernacular realism, extravagant food writing, and sentimental urban protest. Its characteristic effect comes from tonal doubleness: the narrator is jaunty, knowing, and entertaining, while the events reveal systemic callousness. The prose invites laughter at verbal incongruities and social absurdities, then turns that laughter into discomfort by showing abundance displayed before hunger and human need repeatedly converted into spectacle.

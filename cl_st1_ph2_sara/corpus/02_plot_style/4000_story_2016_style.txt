The story adopts the manner of a restrained antiquarian ghost tale, presenting supernatural events through educated, conversational recollection rather than dramatic omniscient narration. It begins with a casual smoking-room discussion that acts as a frame narrative, then moves into a long first-person account of schooldays. This structure gives the tale the air of oral testimony: the narrator is recalling an old, imperfectly understood experience for a sceptical but interested listener. His frequent qualifications—“if I remember right,” “I really don’t know,” “I think,” and “I suppose”—create an impression of honesty and fallible memory. They also make the supernatural material more credible by avoiding absolute claims.

The narrative voice is urbane, understated, and mildly ironic. Horrific possibilities are described in the same composed register as school routines, Latin exercises, and dormitory arrangements. Even the climactic disappearance is reported with remarkable economy: “And next day Mr. Sampson was gone.” This emotional restraint is central to the effect. The narrator does not explain what happened, moralise heavily, or indulge in overt terror; instead, he assembles circumstantial evidence and leaves the listener to infer a buried crime and supernatural vengeance. Colloquial phrases such as “some rot,” “jolly odd,” “bagged the extra paper,” and “very cheap next day” preserve the remembered idiom of schoolboys, while the adult narrator’s more measured language frames their reactions retrospectively.

Dialogue carries much of the story. The opening exchange establishes its intellectual premise—the folklore of private schools—and distinguishes conventional, second-hand ghost stories from the apparently authentic case that follows. Within the embedded narrative, dialogue reproduces the boys’ curiosity, teasing, uncertainty, and fear. Speech is naturalistic and often digressive, with interruptions, self-corrections, rhetorical questions, and remembered verbal tics. Exposition is therefore made to feel incidental rather than authorially imposed. The listener’s occasional interruption also reminds the reader that this is a story being told in company and encourages the sense that physical evidence might still exist.

The prose combines long, loosely cumulative sentences with short statements placed for emphasis. Extended sentences are used for reminiscence, scene-setting, and chains of cautious inference; abrupt clauses mark uncanny discoveries or irreversible developments. Parentheses, dashes, semicolons, and qualifying phrases reproduce the rhythms of thoughtful speech. Lists are also important: the opening catalogue of grotesque school legends introduces familiar horror images, while later sequences of small factual details—the number of papers, the red ink, the finger-mark, the vanished writing—build a more disciplined and persuasive mystery. The Latin phrases lend the haunting an academic and ritualistic quality, while their plain translations turn classroom exercises into warnings.

The pacing is gradual and evidential. A substantial opening delays the principal narrative and establishes an atmosphere of amused scepticism. The school, its grounds, McLeod, and Sampson are then introduced in ordinary detail before anything supernatural occurs. Suspense develops through three escalating incidents: McLeod’s involuntary sentence about the well among four yews, the extra paper bearing a threat, and the wet, emaciated figure at Sampson’s window. Each episode is separated by a return to normal school life, preventing the story from becoming continuously sensational and allowing unease to accumulate. The final event is not shown directly; the possible apparition disappears from sight, and Sampson’s fate is inferred from his unexplained absence.

Characterisation is selective and functional. McLeod is deliberately described as an ordinary boy, making his involuntary visions harder to dismiss as products of an eccentric personality. Sampson initially appears sociable, well travelled, and competent, but a few precise details—the black beard, pale complexion, Byzantine coin, engraved initials, and exact date—suggest a concealed history. His guilt is conveyed almost entirely through bodily reactions: silence, turning his back, illness, a noise in his throat, sudden flight, and gingerly movement toward the desk. The narrator and McLeod are likewise characterised through behaviour rather than introspection. Their boyish inquisitiveness leads them toward the mystery, but their final inability to speak about it suggests supernatural coercion or profound psychological shock.

The setting juxtaposes institutional security with hidden menace. The private school is a large old house near London, surrounded by cedars, ancient elms, fields, and later the implicitly significant yews and well. Classrooms, lockers, desks, corridors, dormitories, and windows provide familiar, regulated spaces in which inexplicable intrusions become especially disturbing. The bright full moon gives the final sighting visual clarity without resolving what is seen. Windows and thresholds recur as points of invasion: the threatening presence moves from an involuntary mental image, to an anomalous written message, to a figure poised at Sampson’s window.

The horror depends on suggestion, omission, and delayed connection. No explicit account is given of Sampson’s past, the importance of 24 July 1865, the well, or the identity of the wet figure. Nevertheless, the details imply that someone may have died or been concealed in a well among four yews and has returned to summon or punish him. Water imagery, emaciation, the command to remember, and the conditional threat form a coherent pattern without being explained. This controlled incompleteness invites the reader to reconstruct the crime while preserving uncertainty about the mechanics of the haunting.

Overall, the style is formal but conversational, precise but reticent, and eerie without melodrama. Its characteristic method is to embed a supernatural revenge narrative inside apparently reliable social testimony, support it with mundane and quasi-documentary details, and conclude just before certainty can be reached. The resulting atmosphere is one of quiet, cumulative dread: ordinary memory becomes evidence, schoolroom language becomes prophecy, and the most frightening meanings remain unstated.

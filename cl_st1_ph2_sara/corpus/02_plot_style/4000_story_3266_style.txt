The story adopts the form of a traditional literary ghost tale, but its dominant register is affectionate, domestic, and gently comic rather than horrific. A brief fireside frame introduces Miss Le Petyt as an amiable raconteuse speaking to an intimate audience; her first-person reminiscence then occupies nearly the whole narrative. This oral setting shapes the prose throughout. She frequently addresses her listeners as “my dears,” anticipates their objections, pauses to explain practical details, and inserts retrospective qualifications such as “as I now believe.” The result is a voice at once conversational and carefully composed: socially poised, self-deprecating, observant, and quietly ironic.

The diction is formal and period-inflected, with words such as “coombe,” “yeoman,” “hind,” “incubus,” and “obsequiousness,” alongside colloquial idioms and regional speech. Miss Le Petyt’s polished language contrasts with Mrs. Carkeek’s Cornish grammar and pronunciation—“she’ve,” “they was,” “gel,” “the brave she was”—and Farmer Hosking’s plain rural manner. These dialect touches establish class, locality, and character without making the dialogue difficult to follow. Literary and cultural allusions, including Mrs. Gummidge, Lord Lytton, and the Queen of Spain, reinforce the cultivated narrator’s social world and contribute to the story’s light, urbane humour.

Sentences tend to be long, balanced, and syntactically layered, often moving through qualification, parenthesis, and correction before reaching their point. Semicolons, dashes, parentheses, and explanatory clauses reproduce the rhythms of remembered speech. At moments of supernatural revelation, however, the prose becomes markedly simpler and more fragmented: “Over the porcelain basin… I saw two hands. That was all—two small hands, a child’s hands.” This contraction of syntax isolates the image and gives it force. Repetition is similarly controlled, especially around “hands,” “washing,” “little,” and “nothing,” turning an ordinary domestic action into an uncanny motif.

Dialogue drives much of the narrative. The opening exchange creates curiosity through teasing interruptions, while later conversations reveal information incrementally and dramatize changes in the narrator’s understanding. Humour often arises through understatement, social tact, and literal-minded replies, as in the question whether “little” describes the story or the ghost. Even fear is filtered through practical concerns: confronted by the apparition, Miss Le Petyt remains preoccupied with turning off the tap. This comic pragmatism makes her credible and prevents the supernatural material from becoming melodramatic.

The structure is a gradual reclassification of apparently mundane details. Tresillack is first presented as an attractive but suspiciously “secluded” property; its history of disreputable tenants suggests one kind of danger, while Mrs. Carkeek’s evasiveness suggests another. The mysteriously perfect housekeeping, the blocked cistern, the running tap, and the housekeeper’s alarm form a chain of clues. Each incident is slight in itself, but their accumulation creates suspense. A detailed architectural explanation of the house and pantry slows the action immediately before the manifestation, grounding the ghost in a precisely mapped physical setting. The supernatural therefore emerges not from a vague Gothic darkness but from plumbing, crockery, dusting, flowers, and household routine.

The setting blends pastoral beauty with restrained uncanniness. The Cornish coombe, hidden sea, isolated cottage, roses, moonlight, and distant waves provide sensuous atmosphere, yet the house is bright, clean, and welcoming rather than decayed or oppressive. Descriptions rely strongly on sound, scent, and touch: the chattering stream, the plash of waves, the fragrance of roses, trickling water, and the final pressure of small hands. Water becomes the central sensory and symbolic element. It first signals mystery, then reveals the ghost’s compulsive cleanliness and service, and finally connects innocence, care, and purification.

Characterization is economical but nuanced. Miss Le Petyt combines independence, social confidence, romantic susceptibility, and moral courage. Her initial class assumptions about servants and country people are exposed and gently corrected by experience. Mrs. Carkeek is practical, loyal, emotionally reserved, and protective of the dead child; her concealment of the pantry’s water supply is understandable rather than sinister. Farmer Hosking appears suspicious at first but is later revealed as honest and repeatedly victimized. Margaret herself is characterized almost entirely through effects and testimony: immaculate rooms, anticipated wishes, small washing hands, and acts of protection. Her invisibility makes her less an individualized spectre than an embodiment of tireless, unrequited care.

The supernatural is handled through restraint and partial visibility. Margaret never appears as a complete figure, speaks no words, and performs no spectacular feat. The one visual manifestation is anatomically incomplete but physically convincing: water splashes over the hands rather than passing through them. This precise, limited impossibility is more effective than elaborate description. The story also avoids rational explanation; instead, it normalizes the haunting through folklore about piskies and through the women’s acceptance of Margaret as another member of the household.

Tonally, the narrative moves from drawing-room amusement to mild suspicion, concentrated dread, compassion, and finally elegiac tenderness. The ghost is initially advertised as “harmless,” but the deeper reversal is that she becomes beneficent: the living tenants, not the dead child, have brought corruption and cruelty into the house. Horror survives chiefly in the reported abuse committed by previous occupants, while the haunting offers resistance to that abuse. Domestic labour, ordinarily unnoticed, becomes the visible language of love.

The ending is quiet, tactile, and emotionally conclusive. After an extended account filled with explanation and conversation, the final encounter is brief and almost silent. Miss Le Petyt closes her eyes and reaches into uncertainty; Margaret’s hands answer by resting in hers “for a moment only.” The image resolves the recurring hand motif, transforms fear into mutual recognition, and supplies restrained pathos without sentimentally overexplaining it. Overall, the style may be described as an elegant period fireside narrative: leisurely, ironic, socially observant, domestically detailed, carefully suspenseful, and ultimately more concerned with tenderness, loyalty, and moral sympathy than with terror.

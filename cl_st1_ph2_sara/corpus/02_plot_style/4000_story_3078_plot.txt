Caliph Harun al-Rashid, traveling Baghdad at night with his vizier Jaafar and servant Mesroor, encounters an impoverished fisherman. He pays the man to cast his net in the Tigris, and the fisherman retrieves a locked chest containing the dismembered body of a young woman. Enraged that such a murder has occurred under his rule, the caliph gives Jaafar three days to find the killer, threatening to crucify him and forty relatives if he fails.

As Jaafar is about to be executed, a young man confesses to the murder. An old man also claims responsibility, hoping to sacrifice himself for the youth. The young man proves that he is the killer and explains that the victim was his wife and cousin, while the old man is her father. During her illness, she asked for an apple, so her devoted husband traveled to Basra and bought three rare apples from the caliph’s garden. Later, he saw a slave carrying one of them. The slave claimed that his lover had received three such apples from her unsuspecting husband. Consumed by jealousy, the young man returned home, found one apple missing, murdered and dismembered his wife, and threw her body into the river.

Afterward, his son revealed that he had taken the missing apple outside to play and that a slave had stolen it after hearing where it came from. Realizing that the slave had lied and that his wife was innocent, the husband was overwhelmed with remorse and offered his life in retribution. The caliph spared him, judging the lying slave responsible for the tragedy, but then ordered Jaafar to find that slave within three days or die in his place.

Unable to identify him, Jaafar prepared for execution. While bidding farewell to his favorite daughter, however, he discovered an apple in her pocket. She said their slave Reyhan had sold it to her. Questioned, Reyhan admitted stealing it from a child whose account matched the murdered woman’s son. Jaafar brought him before the caliph, thereby solving the case, and the extraordinary chain of events was recorded and made public.

The story is written as a nineteenth-century didactic social tale, combining domestic realism, professional anecdote, mild satire, and an explicit moral lesson. Its prose is formal and measured, with period vocabulary and constructions such as “a’n't,” “any thing,” “practised eye,” “paid for your attentions,” and “called into requisition.” Long, clause-heavy sentences alternate with brisk dialogue, while emphatic punctuation—dashes, exclamation marks, italics, and repeated questions—heightens anxiety, indignation, and comic misunderstanding. Medical terminology such as “febrile symptoms,” “mucous membrane,” “deglutition,” and “narcotic sleep” lends professional authority and contrasts with the patients’ emotional, colloquial speech.

The narration is third-person omniscient and openly evaluative. It moves freely between outward action, Dr. Elton’s private thoughts, broad social commentary, and direct judgments about the characters’ conduct. The narrator is sympathetic to the physician and treats his grievances as representative of the profession as a whole. Rather than maintaining modern dramatic neutrality, the voice explains causes, summarizes patterns, anticipates conclusions, and finally addresses the reader through proverbial and moralizing language. Phrases such as “it is not to be wondered at,” “it is not necessary to trace,” and “a word to the wise is sufficient” reveal an intrusive storyteller guiding interpretation.

Characterization is economical and functional. Dr. Elton is established through conduct, restrained irritation, conscientious anxiety, and ledger entries; he is both a caring professional and a practical tradesman who must earn a living. The Marvels are characterized largely through repetitive alarm, self-contradiction, and indignation over fees. Their speech makes them comic examples of hypochondriacal dependence and financial ingratitude. Mr. Smith initially appears more dignified and grateful, but his eventual accusation repeats the same pattern, broadening the satire from one foolish household to a general social failing. Characters are therefore less psychologically complex individuals than representative moral types.

Dialogue drives much of the story. Exchanges are rapid, plainly attributed, and often built around repetition: anxious relatives repeatedly ask what the doctor thinks, reject his reassurance, demand more attention, and later dispute the resulting charge. This cyclical pattern creates situational irony. The very people who insist on frequent visits later claim that little illness occurred and accuse the doctor of exploitation. Repeated phrases and parallel scenes reinforce the central contradiction between the high value assigned to medical care during danger and the low value assigned to it after recovery.

The structure is episodic and cumulative. It begins with a comic midnight false alarm, develops into a pattern of needless treatment and mounting charges, then shifts to a genuinely dangerous illness that displays the doctor’s skill, emotional investment, and sense of responsibility. Despite the contrast between trivial and serious cases, both episodes end in complaints about payment. Ledger entries, itemized visits, exact sums, and remembered calls provide a recurring economic motif. These concrete details turn the abstract theme of professional ingratitude into an accounting dispute and give the narrative a documentary, almost case-history quality.

The tone moves between humorous exasperation, sober medical seriousness, sentiment, and moral indignation. The Marvel episode is lightly satirical, especially when parental panic survives every reassurance and even a sleeping, recovering child is suspected of dying. The typhus episode becomes earnest and melodramatic, emphasizing the physician’s sleepless anxiety and the father’s tears. The final section abandons narrative subtlety for direct advocacy, presenting the doctor as a “ministering angel” whose study, labor, discomfort, and risk deserve proper compensation.

Stylistically, the story relies on contrast, repetition, irony, rhetorical questions, exclamatory dialogue, authorial generalization, and sentimental appeal. Descriptions are usually practical rather than sensuous, though storm, warmth, fatigue, pallor, fever, and bodily symptoms are used to create immediate physical stakes. The central thematic oppositions are fear versus fact, gratitude versus self-interest, service versus payment, and professional responsibility versus public entitlement. The ending states the lesson explicitly: society eagerly demands the physician’s skill in moments of crisis but forgets its value when health returns.

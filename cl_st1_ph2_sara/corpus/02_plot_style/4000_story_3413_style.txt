The story uses a clear, orderly style characteristic of early scientific romance, combining practical engineering detail with speculative wonder. Its narrative voice is third-person omniscient but remains closely aligned with Roland Clewe, reporting both his actions and the fluctuations of his thoughts. The narrator is composed and authoritative, presenting even the most extraordinary discoveries in a matter-of-fact manner. This sober delivery lends plausibility to the fantastic premise and creates a contrast between technological confidence and the unknowable environment beneath the earth.

The diction is formal, precise, and somewhat archaic. Technical terms such as “windlasses,” “indicator,” “electric bells,” “strata,” and “automatic shell” appear alongside elevated phrases such as “region of unreality,” “measureless depth,” and “an awful terror shot into my soul.” Scientific and mechanical language grounds the expedition, while language of sublimity conveys the psychological impact of the underground realm. Explanations are generally accessible rather than densely technical: machinery and geological ideas are described by their function, enabling a general reader to follow the speculation.

Sentence structure tends toward long, balanced constructions linked by conjunctions and semicolons, with shorter sentences introduced at moments of discovery or alarm. Repetition is a prominent rhythmic device. “Down, down he went” measures the prolonged descent, while “Never, never, never again” gives emotional finality to Clewe’s refusal to return. Lists of equipment and preparations create an impression of comprehensiveness and control; later, repeated questions and abrupt exclamations reveal the collapse of that confidence. The prose also frequently qualifies observations with phrases such as “it seemed,” “he thought,” and “he believed,” preserving uncertainty until Clewe formulates his theory.

The opening proceeds chronologically and methodically through preparation, departure, and descent. Early pacing is deliberate, with extensive attention to logistics and safety arrangements. During the descent, the narration alternates between physical observation, mundane thought, and growing unease. Clewe’s concern about flower-beds and factory inventions humanises him and provides comic contrast with the magnitude of his journey. Once he enters the transparent cavern, the pace slows into close sensory description: small movements, glances, and changes of footing are narrated step by step. The return to the surface is comparatively compressed, after which dialogue accelerates the revelation of the central idea.

Suspense arises less from overt action than from spatial disorientation and delayed comprehension. The illuminated shaft first appears measurable and controlled, but the cave below defeats ordinary perception. Paradoxical images—an immense shell apparently resting on air, shadows descending through transparent solidity, and a surface resembling “petrified air”—produce an uncanny effect. Vision becomes unreliable: intense light reveals limitless depth while concealing the material immediately underfoot. Tactile sensations therefore become crucial, with Clewe testing surfaces through his hands and feet. This emphasis turns a scientific expedition into an encounter with the sublime, in which knowledge and terror are inseparable.

Characterisation is economical and action-based. Clewe is established as methodical, inventive, self-controlled, and intensely curious. His practical habits persist under extreme conditions, yet his cigars, nervous silence, hunger, and sudden panic expose his vulnerability. Bryce functions as a competent, loyal intermediary and as the voice of caution from the surface. Margaret provides the emotional frame for the discovery and acts as a sceptical audience whose questions allow Clewe to explain his theory. Their relationship is affectionate but expressed through restraint, urgency, and shared intellectual excitement rather than elaborate romantic description.

Dialogue is direct, formal, and expository. Exchanges during the descent are brief and functional, reinforcing the expedition’s procedural character. The final conversation employs rapid questions, interruptions, exclamations, and repeated assertions. Margaret’s incredulity gives the reader permission to doubt the theory, while Clewe’s calm answers preserve his authority. The dialogue also introduces the economic consequences of the discovery: unlimited diamonds would destroy rather than create conventional wealth. Thus the speculative conceit expands from geology into a paradox about scarcity, value, and secrecy.

The dominant tonal progression moves from confidence and industrious preparation through monotony, unease, awe, triumph, and lingering dread. The conclusion deliberately tempers success with trauma. Clewe has apparently found limitless material wealth and a new theory of the planet’s formation, but the emotional climax is his memory of almost slipping across an invisible slope. The story therefore treats scientific discovery as both exhilarating and destabilising. Human ingenuity can penetrate the earth, but it cannot make the resulting experience psychologically safe or fully comprehensible.

The imagery repeatedly opposes darkness and light, solidity and emptiness, knowledge and illusion. Electric illumination symbolises technological mastery, yet its passage through diamond creates the terrifying impression of infinite space. The earth’s centre is imagined not as a conventional fiery core but as a former cometary diamond enclosed in layers of ash, rock, and metal. This grand cosmological explanation is delivered with confident internal logic rather than rigorous demonstration, a hallmark of speculative adventure fiction.

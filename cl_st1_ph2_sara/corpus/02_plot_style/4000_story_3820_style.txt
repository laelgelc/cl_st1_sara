The story adopts a polished first-person memoir style, combining outdoor adventure with reflective essay and comic anecdote. The narrator is educated, self-aware, and conversationally authoritative, presenting himself as both an experienced angler and a fallible enthusiast. He frequently pauses to examine his own temperament, especially his tendency toward impatience, despondency, and irrational hope. This self-mockery keeps the voice genial rather than boastful: even genuine danger is filtered through urbane understatement, as when concern for a “large family dependent upon him” becomes a mock-serious justification for continuing toward the fish.

The opening is philosophical and aphoristic. It begins with broad observations about chance, habit, and crisis before narrowing to one fishing expedition. The conclusion reverses that movement, returning from the particular adventure to a general claim about life. This creates a framed, circular structure: the initial idea of the “turning-point” is embodied by the grasshopper and then reconsidered in the final paragraph. The story’s apparent climax—the landing of the salmon—is deliberately displaced. The narrator identifies the quieter moment of catching the bait as the true crisis, reinforcing the theme that decisive events often appear trivial while they are occurring.

The diction mixes elevated, literary language with sporting terminology and colloquial comedy. Words such as “precipitation,” “deliberation,” “predestined,” and “psychological” coexist with the practical vocabulary of leaders, flies, rods, nets, pools, and casting. Specialized angling knowledge lends authenticity, while playful descriptions keep it accessible. The mock-technical phrase about the “reflex action” of the narrator’s and grasshopper’s “neural ganglia” humorously magnifies a simple grab into a contest of biological destiny. French names and exclamations, along with Quebec place references, provide regional texture, while phrases such as “horse-cars” and the detailed catalogue of artificial flies give the prose a distinctly late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century character.

The syntax is generally expansive and balanced, suited to leisurely reflection, but it becomes shorter and more kinetic during moments of action. Long sentences accumulate qualifications, examples, and parallel constructions when the narrator meditates or explains angling theory. By contrast, the jump onto the log is rendered through a rapid chain of verbs: he “jumped,” “landed,” “felt,” “ran,” and “leaped.” This rhythmic acceleration reproduces physical movement. Short standalone statements—“It was the only chance,” “So I jumped,” and “All seemed to be lost”—serve as pivots that sharpen suspense without abandoning the story’s light tone.

Humour is the dominant stylistic colouring. Much of it arises from incongruity: a recreational disappointment is treated as existential tragedy, fly selection becomes a battle between conservative and radical theory, and a runaway log resembles “a playful hippopotamus.” The narrator repeatedly inflates minor difficulties through mock-heroic language, then punctures the grandeur with practical or absurd detail. Antithesis and epigram are common, as in the observation that “Precipitation is a fault. But deliberation, for a person of precipitate disposition, is a vice.” Comic personification also appears throughout. The fish is imagined as reasoning about the end of grasshopper season, the river is “furious and headstrong,” and the grasshopper resists fulfilling its “destiny.”

The imagery is vivid but selective, emphasizing movement, colour, and physical character. The autumn landscape glows crimson, the flooded river gurgles, eddies, and rushes, and the hooked fish leaps with “silvery sides.” Similes are concrete and often whimsical: opportunities swarm like gnats, a crisis stands out like a salmon on a rapid, the log wallows like a hippopotamus, and Ferdinand swings the net like a mower’s scythe. Natural description therefore serves both scenic beauty and comic animation. Literary allusion performs a similar double function. The reference to Tennyson’s “Flower in the crannied wall” signals the narrator’s cultural sophistication but is immediately converted into a joke about falling from a cliff.

Pacing alternates between digression and concentrated action. The narrator is willing to suspend the main plot for reflections on childhood skating, tackle shopping, fly-selection theories, or proper net technique. These digressions delay the catch and increase anticipation, but they also establish the essayistic character of the piece. Suspense develops through a sequence of obstacles—the flood, the cliff, the unstable log, the fish’s refusal of artificial flies, the inadequate tackle, and the broken net. Each apparent resolution generates a fresh complication, giving the anecdote a gently escalating structure.

Dialogue is sparse and functional. Most of the narrative is controlled by the retrospective voice, while the principal direct speech is the urgent multilingual cry for Ferdinand and the net. Capitalisation heightens its suddenness and contrasts with the narrator’s usual composure. Elsewhere, quoted titles, fly names, poems, and onomatopoeic birdlike sounds enrich the page visually and aurally. The repeated “KRI-KAREE-KAREE-KRI” turns the grasshopper into a memorable comic character and makes its arrival feel like an audible intervention by fate.

Overall, the style is leisurely, cultivated, humorous, and humane. It treats angling as both an absorbing physical pursuit and a miniature model of life, governed by chance, perception, patience, and absurdity. The prose maintains affection for its subject without demanding that the reader share the narrator’s obsession. Its signature effect comes from balancing genuine excitement with amused hindsight: danger and triumph remain real, but both are continually moderated by irony, self-knowledge, and the recognition that the grand crises of life may depend upon something as small as getting bait.

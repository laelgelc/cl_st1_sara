The story uses a grave, realist narrative style that gradually develops into psychological and supernatural allegory. It begins with detached, report-like exposition, describing military movements, discipline and summary executions in plain chronological order. This restrained presentation makes the violence feel bureaucratic and routine rather than sensational. The narrator is third-person omniscient but remains closely aligned with Captain Markof, moving from external descriptions of his conduct and appearance into his fevered sensations, memories and moral rationalisations.

The diction is formal, literary and slightly archaic, with military titles, historical place names, biblical phrasing and period expressions such as “your honour,” “punitive expedition” and “three-fourths of the globe.” Concrete details—the sleet, the hollow room, the dying fire, the ticking watch and the crackle of burnt fuel—create a bleak Gothic atmosphere. Adjectives frequently appear in cumulative groups, as in descriptions of weariness, terror or cruelty, giving the prose an emphatic, heightened quality. Repeated contrasts between outward hardness and inward weakness are central: Markof’s “firm, obstinate chin” and habitual authority conceal fever, exhaustion, fear and moral instability.

Sentence structure alternates between measured descriptive passages and long, rhetorically expansive speeches. The descriptive sentences often accumulate clauses and participial phrases, producing a deliberate, observant rhythm. Dialogue is sharper and more fragmented. Markof’s commands are curt, interrupted and authoritarian, while his feverish speech becomes broken by ellipses, dashes, repetitions and unfinished phrases. The old man speaks in balanced, sermon-like periods, using historical catalogues, parallel constructions and solemn repetition. This difference in verbal texture establishes the conflict between temporary military power and an apparently timeless moral judgment.

The tone moves through several registers: coldly documentary at first, ominous and claustrophobic in the farm scene, philosophically confrontational during the dialogue, and finally prophetic and biblical. Irony pervades the portrayal of Markof. He claims obedience, patriotism and historical necessity as excuses for murder, yet his logic is exposed as self-serving; notably, he argues that sparing one innocent man would be “unfair” after killing others. His physical fever functions both literally and symbolically, blurring dream and reality while externalising the sickness of his conscience.

The plot is tightly structured around a nocturnal visitation. Initial references to the condemned old man create suspense before his unexplained appearance at the bedside. The encounter then broadens from one execution to a panoramic history of human violence, moving through empires, massacres, religious persecution and warfare before arriving at Cain and Abel. The old man is thus transformed from prisoner into archetype: Cain, the first murderer, condemned to witness every subsequent shedding of blood. Historical enumeration gives the story epic scope while the confined room preserves dramatic intensity.

Imagery is dominated by darkness, blood, fire, cold and bodily weakness. The red firelight initially sharpens Markof’s martial severity, while the extinguished fire and pale dawn accompany his defeat. Blood imagery expands hyperbolically until it seems sufficient to “drown the whole world.” Sound is also important: ticking, crackling, hollow voices, whispered pleas and the abrupt waking command intensify the border between hallucination and revelation. Repetition—especially “Awake!” and Markof’s pleas for the visitor to leave—creates incantatory force and prepares the transition from dream to waking.

The ending is restrained rather than melodramatic. Markof awakens too late to prevent the executions, and his resignation from command is attributed outwardly to fever, leaving the depth and durability of his repentance uncertain. The sergeant-major’s calm obedience mirrors the impersonal machinery established at the beginning. Overall, the style combines military realism, Gothic psychological fiction, moral debate and biblical parable, using a controlled escalation from ordinary brutality to universal condemnation.

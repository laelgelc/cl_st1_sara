“Roses” is written as a nineteenth-century sentimental moral tale, combining domestic realism, melodrama, and a fairy-tale pattern of kindness rewarded. Its emotional register is openly tender and didactic: poverty, temptation, remorse, charity, suffering, gratitude, and eventual friendship are presented in ways designed to awaken sympathy and encourage benevolent conduct. The moral is explicit but embedded in the action—the smallest compassionate gesture may transform a life, while true charity lies as much in “kind looks” and “gentle words” as in material gifts.

The story uses an omniscient third-person narrator who moves freely between Lizzie’s and Belle’s thoughts while maintaining an affectionate, morally evaluative presence. Characters are repeatedly described through sympathetic labels such as “poor Lizzie,” “the little errand girl,” “pretty Belle,” and “a kind little soul.” The narrator sometimes addresses readers through general observations, as in the comment that anyone who has tried to find something in a hurry can imagine Belle’s frustration. This creates an intimate storyteller–audience relationship and supports the instructive purpose of the narrative.

The diction is accessible, emotive, and strongly contrastive. Lizzie’s world is rendered through words such as “forlorn,” “worn-out,” “faded,” “dilapidated,” “bruised,” and “tearful,” whereas Belle’s surroundings are “handsome,” “cosy,” “lovely,” “delicate,” and “homelike.” Material details carry social meaning: wet boots, a thin cotton dress, rich carpets, sofa cushions, cake, flowers, and an elaborate ball gown make class inequality immediately visible. The language is frequently intensified by exclamations, superlatives, and emotional adverbs, giving the story a heightened, melodramatic quality without making its prose difficult.

Sentence structure alternates between long, flowing descriptive sentences and brisk dialogue. The longer sentences often accumulate parallel details to establish atmosphere or hardship, while shorter clauses accelerate moments of distress, discovery, or generosity. Semicolons and coordinated phrases are common, reflecting the balanced, expansive rhythm of period prose. Dialogue is lively and character-defining: Lizzie speaks in modest, colloquial forms such as “I haven’t got any” and “I’d like ’em ever so much,” while Belle’s speech is more privileged, impulsive, and initially petulant. Marie’s exaggerated French-influenced exclamations provide mild comic relief and theatrical flourish.

Imagery is predominantly visual, tactile, and floral. The opening storm externalizes Lizzie’s misery through mud, sleet, wet clothes, and “draggle-tailed” sparrows. By contrast, the indoor flowers introduce warmth, fragrance, color, and imaginative possibility. Nature is lightly personified: pigeons wear “little red boots,” sparrows behave derisively, and flowers become “household spirits.” Similes are simple and picturesque, as when Lizzie is drawn to the plants like “a butterfly or bee,” or the rose resembles “a rosy face smiling out from the green leaves.” Such imagery gives the prose a child-friendly vividness and softens its social criticism with charm.

The two-part structure is carefully symmetrical. “Roses” establishes Belle’s spontaneous kindness, Lizzie’s theft and confession, and the accident that changes her life. “Forget-me-Nots,” set five years later, reverses their positions: Belle now needs flowers, and Lizzie is able to repay an old kindness through her acquired skill. The first section ends in apparent catastrophe, while the second converts suffering into vocation, gratitude, and friendship. Coincidence is central to the plot, but it functions symbolically rather than realistically, creating a providential sense that good deeds return at the moment they are most needed.

Flowers organize the story’s symbolism. The stolen rose represents beauty, temptation, conscience, and the first bond between the girls. Artificial flower-making turns Lizzie’s aesthetic longing into useful labor and independence. The forget-me-not wreath embodies memory and reciprocated kindness, while its name supplies the final moral injunction. The conclusion gathers these motifs into a compact metaphor: friendship blooms “out of the dead roses,” and the faded garland continues to preserve the lesson of compassion.

Characterization relies on moral contrasts that gradually become more nuanced. Lizzie is poor but sensitive, industrious, aesthetically gifted, and quick to repent; her theft is treated as a moment of desperate temptation rather than evidence of bad character. Belle begins as sheltered, vain, and capable of trivial dissatisfaction, yet she is naturally generous and able to learn. Her development consists not in abandoning privilege but in becoming conscious of lives beyond it. Both girls are idealized, and secondary characters serve clear functions: Madame represents harsh economic authority, Estelle practical self-interest, Marie comic vanity, and Belle’s mother secure domestic affection.

The tone moves through carefully staged transitions: bleak and compassionate in the opening, warm and intimate during the girls’ first conversation, briefly tragic after Lizzie’s fall, comic and anxious during the search for the wreath, and finally celebratory and morally reflective. Pathos is created through tears, illness, loneliness, lameness, and unequal fortune, but the overall effect remains reassuring. Suffering is not explored with psychological ambiguity; instead, it becomes the means through which virtue, talent, and human connection are revealed.

The prose belongs to a tradition of domestic literature for younger readers. It favors clear ethical distinctions, emotional immediacy, picturesque description, providential plotting, and a closing maxim that explains the story’s significance. Capitalized words such as “NOT,” “SHE,” and “WON’T” reproduce emphatic stress, while direct exclamations heighten the spoken quality. The missing spaces and occasional forms such as “Marnma” appear to be transcription or OCR artifacts rather than features of the intended style. Overall, the style is warm, ornamental, compassionate, and overtly instructive, using floral symbolism and mirrored incidents to show how a seemingly minor act of mercy can produce lasting moral and practical consequences.

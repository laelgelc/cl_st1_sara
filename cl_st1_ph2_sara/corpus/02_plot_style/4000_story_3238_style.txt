The story uses the manner of a traditional children’s animal tale, combining domestic comedy, fairy-tale peril, and a gently macabre sense of adventure. Its opening formula, “Once upon a time,” establishes an oral-storytelling atmosphere, while the old house, hidden passages, thick walls, attics, chimneys, and mysterious noises add elements of the miniature Gothic. The danger is real enough to generate suspense—Tom Kitten is tied up and nearly cooked—but it is rendered absurd rather than traumatic through the rats’ culinary disagreements, exaggerated politeness, and practical concern about indigestible string.

The narrative voice is warm, assured, and playfully intrusive. It begins mainly as a third-person omniscient account, moving between Mrs. Tabitha’s search, the kittens’ mischief, and Tom’s concealed predicament. Near the end, however, the narrator enters the story in the first person, mentioning a trip to the post and recognizing the stolen wheelbarrow. This creates the impression of a storyteller who belongs to the same village world and can personally certify its improbable events. Direct addresses and moralizing transitions—such as the observation that Tom’s experience shows how unwise it is to climb an unfamiliar chimney—imitate adult instruction, but the lessons are delivered with comic affection rather than severity.

The diction blends simplicity with quaint, precise, and occasionally formal vocabulary. Child-friendly words and diminutives such as “little,” “paws,” “kitten,” and “mewed” coexist with terms like “wainscot,” “rafters,” “counterpane,” “indigestible,” and “persuaded.” Domestic and architectural details are unusually concrete: dough pans, flour barrels, milk pans, rolling-pins, skirting-boards, dust sheets, flues, lath, plaster, and haymows give the fantasy a materially convincing setting. Archaic or regional touches, including “sit ye down,” “durst,” and “’normous,” strengthen the old-fashioned rural tone. Repeated intensifiers—“old, old house,” “more and more distracted,” “up, and up, and up”—make the prose rhythmic and readily understandable to children.

Sentence structure alternates between short, decisive statements and longer descriptive or explanatory passages. Short paragraphs often isolate individual actions, revelations, or punch lines, giving the narrative a page-turning cadence. Coordination is more common than syntactic complexity: characters go, look, hear, fetch, scramble, and hide in clear sequence. Repetition supplies momentum, particularly in search scenes and physical action. Sound-patterning is prominent in phrases such as “stuffy fusty room,” “pattering noise,” “bit and spat, and mewed and wriggled,” and especially the recurring “roly-poly” of the rolling-pin. Exclamation marks, dashes, italics or capitalization for emphasis, and abrupt paragraph breaks reproduce the animation of oral performance.

Dialogue is frequent and strongly characterizing. Mrs. Tabitha speaks in anxious exclamations and repetitive lamentations; Ribby is brisk, practical, and confident; the kittens report events in frightened, childlike language; and Samuel Whiskers and Anna Maria speak with incongruously formal domestic seriousness. Much of the humor comes from this contrast between civilized manners and grotesque intentions. The rats discuss turning a kitten into pudding as though resolving an ordinary recipe dispute, and Samuel’s elaborate phrasing—“whatever you may urge to the contrary”—makes him sound pompous even during his hurried escape. Anna Maria’s continued arguing after the danger has passed turns marital bickering into a recurring comic trait.

Characterization depends on vivid habits rather than psychological depth. Mrs. Tabitha is the overwhelmed parent, Moppet and Mittens are mischievous but educable children, Tom is curious and disobedient yet vulnerable, Ribby is the capable neighbor, and the rats are thieves whose domestic partnership mirrors that of respectable human adults. The animals remain recognizably animal in their instincts—cats fear or hunt rats, rats steal food, a dog sniffs and scratches—while also wearing clothes, using tools, keeping house, borrowing ingredients, and conducting business. This balance between animal behavior and human social custom is central to the story’s comic world.

The plot is tightly organized around concealment, parallel action, and delayed explanation. The first section follows the cats’ unsuccessful search and reveals clues: soot, missing dough, stolen butter, the rolling-pin, and noises beneath the floor. The narrative then turns back in time to explain Tom’s route through the chimney and his capture. This retrospective shift lets the reader reconstruct the mystery while increasing suspense. The two strands converge when the sawing above interrupts the rats’ preparations. The conclusion then expands beyond the rescue into a chain of consequences: the rats relocate, Farmer Potatoes inherits the infestation, Moppet and Mittens become professional rat-catchers, and Tom retains a lifelong fear.

Humor is produced through understatement, exaggeration, incongruity, and cumulative repetition. The account of the family eating seven young rats for dinner is delivered matter-of-factly; the nearly fatal dumpling is later served separately with currants “to hide the smuts”; and the rat population grows into “children and grand-children and great great grand-children” with “no end to them.” Practical details repeatedly deflate danger. Even John Joiner’s rescue is followed by talk of wheelbarrows and hen-coops, while the stolen wheelbarrow becomes a source of personal indignation for the narrator.

The imagery is tactile, spatial, and sensory rather than lyrical. Soot, smoke, butter, dough, cobwebs, dust, cramped passages, strange smells, and scratching noises create a world that can almost be touched and smelled. The old house functions as both domestic home and hidden labyrinth, with ordinary cupboards and chimneys opening into secret animal territories. Scale is especially important: tiny doors, passages behind skirting-boards, and rooms under attic floors transform familiar household architecture into a perilous landscape.

The moral framework is clear but light-handed. Disobedience and reckless curiosity lead Tom into danger, while Moppet and Mittens eventually become useful and industrious. Yet the story avoids solemn punishment: Tom is rescued, fed, bathed, and left only with a comic limitation. Its ending uses a delayed punch line, separating “bigger than—” from “A Mouse” to emphasize Tom’s diminished courage. Overall, the style is affectionate, whimsical, suspenseful, and dryly comic, with nursery-tale simplicity enriched by precise domestic realism, mock-formal dialogue, and an undercurrent of controlled grotesquerie.

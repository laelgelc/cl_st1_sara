As winter begins, cowboys Baugh, Stubb, and Arab Ab are laid off under new cattle-company rules that also deny unemployed range riders the customary food and shelter at ranch camps. They travel to a Kansas county seat intending to enjoy themselves until spring, but gambling and horse racing soon consume nearly all their money.

To survive the rest of the winter, Baugh devises a confidence scheme targeting Louie Seigerman, a German hotel-and-bar owner eager to hold public office. The three cowboys pose as influential representatives of major cattle companies and persuade him that cattlemen, angered by rampant theft, unanimously want him to run for sheriff. They promise vast political support, campaign funds, hundreds of imported votes, and even the prospect of eventually becoming a U.S. marshal.

Flattered and convinced, Seigerman declares his candidacy. Believing the cowboys to be his campaign managers, he provides them with free lodging, meals, liquor, and travel money for several months. When spring returns and ranch work is again available, the trio invents reports of political trouble, obtains final expense money, and disappears, leaving a letter claiming that urgent cattle business has called each of them away while assuring Seigerman of victory.

At the county convention, Seigerman receives only one vote on each ballot. The cowboys turn his humiliating result into the range expression “Seigerman’s Per Cent,” meaning the smallest or worst possible return.

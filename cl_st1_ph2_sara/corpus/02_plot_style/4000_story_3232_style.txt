The story uses an elevated, early-twentieth-century mystery style that combines social melodrama, courtroom detection and modern fairy tale. Its structure is strongly linear: an opening frame introduces the episode as one story among several, after which the narrative moves from the lawyer’s office, through the nocturnal city and opera house, to the courtroom revelation. The plot is arranged around an extreme reversal of fortune. The accused man’s wife descends into helplessness, is rescued by an almost magical stranger, and returns the next morning to see apparent doom transformed into vindication. Suspense depends less on concealing the relevant facts than on withholding the significance of conspicuous details—the diagram, the question about a missing arm, the dropped glove and the rosebud—until they converge in the final deduction.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, though it often stays close to the wife’s bewildered perceptions. Her movement from despair into the singer’s luxurious world is rendered as a dreamlike passage, allowing the detective plot to acquire the atmosphere of enchantment. The narrator also moves outward when necessary to explain motives, histories and legal implications, as in the account of the singer’s Swiss childhood or the analysis of the corrupt lawyer’s courtroom speech. Characters are frequently presented through authoritative moral description: the lawyer is brutal and self-serving, Thompson dissipated and frightened, the judge honorable and weary, and the singer compassionate, intelligent and radiant. Psychological complexity is secondary to ethical clarity.

The diction is formal, literary and slightly archaic. Words and phrases such as “ambassadress,” “decedent,” “obliquity,” “volition,” “prodigious memory” and “no vestige of injury” create a cultivated register, while legal and investigative terminology lends procedural authority. French expressions—“Ma pauvre femme,” “Vous êtes malade,” “monsieur,” and “juge d’instruction”—mark the singer’s foreignness and reinforce her aura of sophistication. The prose often uses abstract nouns to interpret feeling and conduct, describing “profound misery,” “mental coma,” “enthusiastic purpose,” “overpowering vitality” and “exclusive dominance.” This produces a narrator who not only depicts events but continually assigns them emotional and moral meaning.

Sentence structure alternates between blunt dramatic statements and long, cumulative explanatory periods. Short sentences—“Night had now arrived,” “The girl rose,” “Panic enveloped the cornered man”—punctuate decisive moments. Longer sentences accumulate subordinate clauses, qualifications and parallel phrases, especially in legal exposition and psychological commentary. Repetition is a major rhythmic device. The wife “remained” in the street; the lawyer reiterates his demand; the singer repeatedly says that a detail “has everything to do with it.” Such recurrence emphasizes helplessness, insistence or dawning revelation. Ellipses, exclamation marks and interrupted phrasing heighten emotional intensity, while occasional period inconsistencies in punctuation and typography contribute to the text’s historical feel.

Dialogue is direct, theatrical and sharply differentiated by social role. The lawyer speaks in threats and sarcasm; the wife uses plain, repetitive language; the prosecutor adopts formal procedural objections; and the singer speaks with courteous hesitation, accented by French forms of address. Her repeated formula—“Why, yes, monsieur, if that is the way”—makes her seem ingenuous while she strategically directs the courtroom. Dialogue frequently functions as staged action rather than naturalistic conversation: each exchange advances the mystery, exposes character or prepares a reveal. The climactic courtroom sequence is especially performative, with objections, questions, gestures and cries arranged to maximize public recognition.

Description relies on strong visual and tactile contrasts. Sordid interiors, frayed cuffs, fog, shabby hotels and the smell of a cleaner’s mop represent poverty and institutional despair. Against them stand furs, silver dishes, motorcars, jewels, flowers and theatrical light. The singer is repeatedly described through luminous, pastoral and celestial imagery: her hair resembles ripe corn, her voice is a “friendly arm put around the heart,” and her presence evokes sunlight, the sea, heaven and an angel. Crowd imagery is more impersonal and naturalistic—the “human tide” and “human eddy” carry the wife through the city—emphasizing her social invisibility. Similes are abundant and emotionally explicit, comparing her to a frightened child, a trapped woodland creature and a dreamer transported into an Arabian tale.

The pacing expands and contracts according to dramatic need. The confrontation with the lawyer is brisk; the wife’s wandering and rescue slow into atmospheric description; the murder circumstances are presented in methodical detail; and the courtroom climax unfolds through incremental questions that make the inference appear inevitable. Exposition is sometimes extensive, but it serves either to establish the geometry of the crime or to connect the singer’s detective ability with her father’s profession. The eventual solution is a classic physical deduction based on handedness, spatial arrangement and wound position, supplemented by evidence obtained through the glove and flower stratagems.

Tonally, the story moves among pathos, menace, wonder and triumphant moral irony. It treats evil as possessing an intimidating appearance of reality and power, while benevolence initially seems miraculous or unreal. The conclusion overturns that hierarchy. The lawyer’s sneer about “Cinderella in a pumpkin coach” becomes literalized by the glamorous rescuer and her waiting motorcar, and his confidence in money, cunning and institutional control is shaken. The dominant themes are justice restored through observation, compassion allied with intelligence, the vulnerability of the poor before legal power, and the defeat of cynical materialism by an improbable but rational intervention. Overall, the style is ornate, morally emphatic and theatrically plotted, blending Golden Age detective mechanics with sentimental romance and fairy-tale symbolism.

The story uses an intimate second-person point of view, addressing the protagonist as “you” while remaining tightly confined to her memories, sensations, and withheld knowledge. This creates the impression of a divided consciousness: an adult self revisits and interrogates the actions of a ten-year-old self, yet speaks with enough distance to make that younger identity feel almost estranged. The narration shifts between past tense for the remembered summer and present tense for the return to Grandmama’s house, allowing the past to intrude upon the present rather than remain safely contained. The voice is controlled, lucid, and psychologically acute, with intense feeling usually conveyed indirectly through physical reactions, sensory associations, and omissions.

The structure is nonlinear and revelation-driven. It begins by naming the central catastrophe—“It was the summer Nonso died”—but postpones the truth of how he died. Memory proceeds associatively through fruit, trees, family rituals, jealousy, gendered exclusions, and the adults’ responses to death. The recurring formula “It was the summer…” gives the opening a ritual, incantatory rhythm while accumulating apparently ordinary details around the concealed trauma. Later sections return to the funeral, the protagonist’s lie to her mother, and finally the actual fall. Each return revises what the reader previously understood. Suspense therefore arises less from discovering that Nonso died than from uncovering the protagonist’s agency, motive, and continuing self-deception.

The prose combines relatively long, flowing sentences with short declarative statements placed for force. Lists and parallel constructions create an accumulating pressure, while abrupt sentences such as “It was the summer Nonso died” function as emotional and structural shocks. Repetition is prominent: words such as “summer,” “hate,” and “you remember” mimic obsessive recollection, while repeated phrases expose the mind circling what it cannot comfortably confront. Parentheses, dashes, qualifications, and alternative explanations reproduce the instability of memory and guilt. The narrator repeatedly offers competing possibilities—“Or maybe…”—so that factual uncertainty becomes part of the moral texture.

The diction is accessible but richly sensuous. Heat, damp soil, insects, fruit, rain, bark, rust, blood, and bodily smells make the Nigerian setting tactile and immediate. Images often join beauty to menace or decay: branches embrace one another but can also release a child; fruit rains generously but echoes the sound of a falling body; the snake’s transparent molt is delicate yet signifies sudden death. Similes are concrete and sometimes unsettling, comparing eyes to a poodle’s, a sob to a dog’s bark, and the impact of the fall to many fruits dropping at once. Nature is not merely decorative scenery but a symbolic system through which desire, danger, memory, and culpability are expressed.

The style incorporates Igbo words and culturally specific concepts without heavy exposition. Terms such as “nwadiana,” “echi eteka,” and “i laputago m” are translated or made intelligible through context, preserving the specificity of family hierarchy and language. Nigerian seasonal terms, food, kinship structures, and ritual practices coexist with American settings and racial-cultural tensions. This bicultural frame sharpens the conflict between Grandmama and the protagonist’s mother, but neither country is treated as a simple moral opposite. Instead, the story emphasizes competing forms of belonging, exclusion, and interpretation.

Dialogue is sparse and usually embedded within narration rather than staged as extended conversation. What people say matters chiefly for what it reveals about hierarchy or denial: Grandmama’s insistence on lineage, the mother’s repeated questions, the father’s warning to be careful, and Dozie’s final admission that only the protagonist’s desires mattered to him. Silence is equally important. Dozie’s lifelong quietness, the mother’s refusal to discuss Nonso, and the protagonist’s withholding all create an atmosphere in which unsaid things exert more pressure than spoken ones.

The emotional register is restrained but disturbing. Childhood sexuality, sibling hatred, favoritism, and death are narrated without sentimental cushioning. The child’s jealousy is presented with unnerving clarity: Nonso’s death emerges from a wish to reduce him, to make room for herself within a family that privileges him as male and as bearer of the family name. Yet the prose neither excuses nor melodramatically condemns her. Its moral complexity comes from holding structural injustice, childish cruelty, adult manipulation, and irreversible consequence together.

Trees, fruit, bodies, and space form the story’s central motifs. Nonso “takes up too much space,” while the protagonist wants room to be seen; Grandmama’s yard is lush and crowded; memories are “flattened” and tucked away until they expand again. The avocado tree serves simultaneously as crime scene, memorial, witness, and source of physical punishment when the protagonist presses her hands into its bark. The title’s poisonous snake is both literal folklore and an emblem of delayed consequence: although its bite supposedly kills within minutes, the figurative poison of the summer survives for eighteen years. The ending remains deliberately unresolved, closing not with confession or forgiveness but with Dozie walking away and the protagonist weeping beneath the tree.

The story is written in a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century realist mode, combining social observation, religious moralism, humour, and restrained sentiment. Its central stylistic device is contrast: rural tradition is set against urban reform, emotional preaching against practical ministry, inherited authority against education, and public performance against ethical action. These oppositions are embodied in the father and son rather than treated abstractly, allowing the story’s argument to emerge through encounters, dialogue, and changes of perception.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, moving freely between external description and the private thoughts of Abram and Robert Dixon. It is especially attentive to Robert’s tact, anxiety, and self-discipline, but it also grants Abram sympathy and dignity even when exposing his prejudices. The narrator occasionally interprets motives directly—explaining, for example, the father’s reliance on emotion or the son’s reluctance to challenge his parents—and thus guides the reader’s moral understanding. This produces an authoritative, humane narrative voice rather than an impersonal or radically ambiguous one.

The prose is generally clear, measured, and moderately formal. Expository passages use balanced syntax and rhetorical parallelism, as in the descriptions of Abram’s education under “the birds and the trees and the winds of heaven” and his closeness to “nature’s God.” Such phrasing gives the narration a gently elevated, almost sermonic cadence. Concrete sensory details—the hum of bees, wild honeysuckle, wharves, doorsills, the chintz-covered sofa—anchor the moral conflict in recognisable settings. Description is selective rather than luxuriant: details usually establish atmosphere, social conditions, or differences in outlook.

Dialogue carries much of the characterisation. Abram and the townspeople speak in a heavily phonetic representation of African American Southern dialect, while Robert generally uses standard English, marking the effects of education, migration, and professional training. The contrast is social and generational, but it does not simply make Robert superior: Abram’s speech contains wit, warmth, force, and biblical resonance. His remark about the devil not taking a vacation is a typical example of the story’s affectionate humour. The dialect spellings are historically characteristic of the period’s literary conventions, though both the terminology and the transcription of Black speech may appear dated or stereotyped to modern readers.

The story’s tone is sympathetic, gently ironic, and ultimately conciliatory. Abram’s assumptions are treated with mild comedy—the scandal of football, his suspicion of a vacation, and his awe before a chintz-covered sofa—but he is never reduced to a caricature. Robert is likewise not presented as smugly enlightened. His humility, uncertainty, and refusal to declare his own way categorically better preserve the emotional balance. The humour softens the ideological conflict, while scenes involving neglected children, unequal justice, and the father’s awakening supply pathos without becoming excessively melodramatic.

Structurally, the narrative follows a symmetrical conversion pattern. Robert first returns to his father’s world and fails its public test; Abram later enters Robert’s world and discovers the practical meaning of his son’s ministry. The two church services mirror one another. In the first, Robert’s quiet sermon disappoints Abram’s congregation; in the second, Abram preaches in Robert’s church after his own outlook has changed. This reversal gives the story a compact, parable-like shape. The movement from Danvers to the city also shifts the argument from doctrine to lived evidence: Abram is not persuaded by debate but by seeing homelessness, discriminatory punishment, rescue work, and Robert’s influence on children.

Biblical language and allusion unify the piece. Phrases such as “Feed my lambs” and the concluding text, “A little child shall lead them,” interpret social service as an authentic form of Christian ministry. The religious vocabulary is familiar and emotionally accessible rather than theological or intellectually elaborate. Repetition—“On trial,” “I didn’t understan’,” and “dey’s some’p’n in it”—marks moments of pressure and revelation, giving them the cadence of oral testimony or preaching.

The pacing is deliberate and linear. The opening supplies background efficiently, the homecoming and failed sermon establish the conflict, and the later city visit resolves it through a sequence of illustrative scenes. Time is compressed between the two visits, while decisive moments are dramatised at greater length. Dialogue alternates with explanatory narration, keeping the story accessible and giving it the feel of an instructive social tale.

Overall, the style is realist but openly didactic. It advocates a practical Christianity concerned with clean living, charity, education, and protection of the vulnerable, yet it avoids resolving the conflict through outright repudiation of the older tradition. Instead, the ending blends Abram’s emotional preaching with Robert’s humane social gospel. The final smile is understated, closing the story on reconciliation, mutual affection, and moral renewal rather than triumph.

The story uses a conversational, digressive first-person style that combines urban observation, comic anecdote, and explicit moral reflection. The narrator speaks directly and informally, often seeming to think aloud as he moves between the shabby terrace opposite his balcony, his pleasure in manual work, and the elaborate preparations for his bath. Asides such as “you know,” self-corrections, parenthetical remarks, and abrupt qualifications create the impression of spontaneous speech rather than tightly controlled literary narration. The voice is personable and self-deprecating, but also sharply observant, socially conscious, and prone to turning ordinary details into broader reflections on human life.

The tone blends dry humour, melancholy, affection, and irony. Poverty is depicted through grotesque comedy—the terrace resembles a row of skulls, with doors like tomahawk wounds—but the residents themselves are not treated with contempt. Repeated references to unpaid rent, improvised curtains, sudden removals, and anxious scrutiny of the rent collector establish deprivation without overt sentimentality. The narrator’s humour protects the material from becoming solemn while allowing an undercurrent of compassion to remain. His own failed bath is treated as farce, yet the final analogy transforms that farce into a sympathetic meditation on people whose lives have gone wrong, particularly those burdened by physical disadvantages.

The structure is deliberately loose and apparently disproportionate. A long opening description of “Skull Terrace” is openly declared to have “nothing whatever to do with the story,” after which the narrative shifts into an increasingly meticulous account of the narrator’s bath preparations. This apparent rambling is purposeful: the terrace supplies the social and moral frame, while the bath anecdote provides the comic emblem. Suspense arises not from danger but from exhaustive practical detail. Every item is arranged—the canvas, gas-pipe, towels, reading matter, tobacco, coffee, toast, toothbrush, and nail knife—before the revelation that the most basic necessity, the plug, has been forgotten. The final paragraphs then return to Skull Terrace and reinterpret the whole anecdote as an allegory of failed lives.

The prose alternates between long, accumulating sentences and short comic blows. Extended sentences pile up observations, objects, alternatives, and qualifications, mimicking both the narrator’s busy mind and his obsession with practical arrangements. Dashes and parentheses frequently interrupt the grammatical flow, adding jokes, corrections, or supplementary thoughts. Sentence fragments such as “Labours of love,” “Van of the ‘bottle ho’ variety,” and “But I was a carpenter and housepainter first” give the narration a spoken, anecdotal rhythm. Repetition is prominent: “ragged,” “casual,” “old,” and variations on vacancy recur until they become both comic motifs and social diagnoses. The climactic sentence, “I’d forgotten to put in the plug,” is extremely plain and brief, gaining force from its contrast with the elaborate preparation preceding it.

The diction mixes colloquial Australian speech, practical trade vocabulary, and mock-literary phrasing. Terms connected with building and household labour—“matchboard,” “galvanized iron,” “gas-pipe,” “limewashing,” “copper,” and nail sizes—lend tactile realism and establish the narrator as a working man who values competent manual craft. Informal expressions such as “pome,” “bottle ho,” and “knocked up” strengthen the vernacular voice. Against this plain diction, elevated phrases such as “pure and unadulterated joy,” “full glare of the day,” and “the sleep of utter happy weariness” are used partly sincerely and partly for comic inflation. The capitalised opening declaration, “THE MORAL should be revived,” parodies the conventions of openly didactic storytelling even as the narrative ultimately embraces them.

Imagery is concrete, visual, and often grotesquely personifying. The central image of the terrace as a skull gives the setting a deathly, hollow appearance, while its slit-like doors and furtive candlelight suggest precarious, half-visible lives. Domestic objects—old tablecloths, ragged blinds, buckets, canvas, string, towels, and scraps of wood—define a world of improvisation and scarcity. This material emphasis also supports the story’s moral logic: life depends upon small, practical details, and one missing component can render all other care useless. The bath plug consequently becomes a simple but flexible symbol for overlooked necessities, bad luck, human fallibility, and the hidden handicap that can undo otherwise intelligent effort.

Characterisation is achieved primarily through habits, objects, and gestures rather than psychological exposition. The terrace residents are sketched collectively through their furtive movements and makeshift possessions. The landlady is characterised through her peculiar thrift about firewood, her practical cooperation, and her excessive sympathy after the mishap. The narrator reveals himself through his deafness, pride in craftsmanship, delight in contrivance, appetite for comfort, and tendency to overprepare. His admission of physical impairment quietly complicates the joke and prepares the final reference to people “handicapped physically.”

Overall, the style is realist, vernacular, humorous, and morally reflective. It turns humble urban scenery and a trivial domestic failure into a social parable without abandoning the texture of ordinary life. Its characteristic movement is from comic particularity to humane generalisation: the reader first laughs at excessive preparation defeated by one omission, then is invited to see the same pattern in lives shaped by poverty, disability, circumstance, or mischance.

The story uses a framed first-person narrative: an unnamed hotel guest discovers a former occupant’s diary and transcribes it verbatim, then returns briefly at the end to deliver the punch line. This manuscript-within-a-story device creates an air of authenticity while allowing the central narrator—the diarist—to reveal himself indirectly. His dated entries impose a clear chronological structure, and the contrast between the neutral outer frame and the highly opinionated diary produces much of the comedy. The final sentence neatly reverses the apparent subject of the account: the later guest’s experience differs chiefly because he failed to encounter the two widows.

The narrative voice is urbane, observant, self-satisfied, and knowingly irreverent. The diarist presents himself as a sophisticated man of the world, but his vanity, sensuality, and moral flexibility are continually exposed through his own remarks. He moves readily from concrete observation to sweeping social pronouncement, often treating personal prejudice as universal wisdom. This makes him both engaging and unreliable. His attraction to the widows transforms his judgment of the resort from “sad” to “delightful,” while their departure returns him to boredom, sanitation complaints, and obsessive attention to his treatment. The discrepancy between what he claims to assess objectively and what actually governs his reactions is central to the characterization.

The dominant tone is comic and satirical, tempered by passages of genuine lyrical appreciation. The health resort is initially portrayed through deadpan exaggeration as a place where silence, solemnity, and medical routine have extinguished pleasure. Invalids drink mineral water with ritual gravity, hotel guests dine without conversation, and even the casino orchestra resembles “a foolish bird” singing alone in a desert. Society, respectability, medicine, morality, and masculine vanity are all treated ironically. The humor frequently comes from aphoristic reversals, mock logic, incongruous comparisons, and the narrator’s elaborate attempts to justify selfish or libertine impulses as natural principles.

The diction alternates between polished descriptive language and brisk diary shorthand. Scenic passages employ elevated, sensuous vocabulary—“billows” of mountains, bluish vapor, gleaming water, green valleys, and volcanic craters—while uneventful days collapse into fragments such as “Nothing,” “The treatment,” and “Ditto. Ditto.” This alternation gives the prose rhythmic variety and makes emotional priorities visible: landscape, women, and amusing anecdotes receive expansive treatment, whereas routine is reduced to clipped notation. Period expressions such as “by Jove,” “watering place,” “fair companions,” and “good breeding” lend the story a formal, late-nineteenth-century social register.

Sentence structure is similarly variable. Long, balanced sentences accumulate visual details or develop comic arguments through clauses, qualifications, and rhetorical escalation. Short sentences and fragments punctuate these passages, especially in the dated entries, producing abrupt changes of pace. Exclamations, rhetorical questions, parenthetical asides, and direct addresses convey spontaneity and conversational intimacy. Repetition is used for comic emphasis: the recurring records of “Nothing” and “treatment” imitate the monotony of the cure, while repeated praise of the resort rises and falls according to the diarist’s romantic circumstances.

Description is strongly visual and spatial. Landscapes are arranged panoramically through contrasts between mountain and plain, enclosure and openness, green woods and gray lava, clear water and hazy distance. Similes are concrete and memorable: the plain is “infinite as the sea,” the lake shines like a silver coin, and lanterns move like will-o’-the-wisps. Other sensory details—still air, barking dogs, orchestral music, dust scented with vanilla and cattle—give the setting physical immediacy. Nature is often serene and beautiful, while human institutions appear absurd, artificial, or oppressively solemn.

The characterization is economical and largely indirect. The widows remain deliberately mysterious and are defined more by the diarist’s fascination than by individual psychology. Their narrative function is to enliven the resort, flatter his vanity, and expose the instability of his judgments. Minor figures—the silent invalids, the custodian of the spring, respectable hotel guests, villagers, and the ingenious priest—are rendered as social types. The diarist is the only fully developed personality, assembled cumulatively through his habits of noticing, rationalizing, boasting, and contradicting himself.

Pacing follows the irregular logic of a diary. Early entries establish the resort and its rituals at a measured pace; the appearance of the women accelerates the narrative and prompts longer, more animated reflections. After their departure, the entries contract sharply, dramatizing emptiness through form rather than explicit lament. The extended anecdote about the “Sins of the Cure” temporarily interrupts the diary’s routine and functions as a comic tale within the embedded tale. The ending is rapid and epigrammatic, closing both the diary and the outer frame without explanation.

The thematic profile centers on boredom, desire, vanity, social performance, and the relativity of moral judgment. Health treatment is presented as labor rather than leisure, and the diarist’s supposed physical cure competes with his appetite for diversion. His reflections on being seen with attractive women satirize the treatment of beauty as status and possession. His moral arguments parody intellectual consistency: he constructs elaborate theories chiefly to authorize whatever pleases him. Beneath the lightness lies a skeptical view of respectable society, whose members disguise desire beneath etiquette, duty, medical discipline, and public reputation.

Overall, the style combines realist observation, travel writing, social satire, comic memoir, and anecdotal storytelling. Its signature effect comes from the tension between beautiful, precise description and a narrator whose shallow motives repeatedly undermine his claims to wisdom. The prose remains elegant and accessible, with irony emerging less from explicit authorial judgment than from arrangement, contrast, repetition, and the diarist’s unwitting self-exposure.

The story is a comic Western tall tale transplanted into an urban labour conflict. Its governing mode is expansive, boastful and deliberately improbable: an ordinary journey to Chicago becomes, in Billings’s account, a one-man campaign against corruption, mob violence and industrial paralysis. The narrative treats danger as farce and violence as rough entertainment, building toward the absurdly literal “arbitration” of a strike leader by lasso, near-drowning and forced labour. The humour depends on exaggeration, incongruity and reversal, especially the collision between cowboy customs and metropolitan institutions.

The narration uses a first-person frame in which an unnamed, relatively educated observer introduces Billings and records his story. This framing voice is dry, urbane and mock-philosophical, as in its explanation of frontier etiquette and curiosity. Once Billings begins speaking, he dominates almost the entire narrative through a long embedded monologue. His perspective is energetic, self-dramatising and unreliable in degree rather than basic fact: the newspaper clippings eventually provide comic confirmation that his outlandish adventure did occur. Interruptions from the ranch audience preserve the oral-storytelling setting, punctuate the monologue and allow listeners to voice disbelief while encouraging further escalation.

Diction is the story’s most distinctive feature. Billings speaks in a heavily marked vernacular filled with contractions, nonstandard grammar, phonetic spellings and frontier idioms: “I enters,” “I follers,” “drawed,” “figgered,” “plumb,” “ain’t” and “’em.” This cowboy speech is mixed with technical, journalistic and pseudo-learned language such as “municipal economies,” “perihelion,” “tensile strength,” “paradox” and “compulsory arbitration.” He repeatedly misapplies elevated vocabulary or forces it into frontier metaphors, producing comic malapropism and bathos. Formal public language—law, politics, labour organisation and newspaper rhetoric—is translated into the practical terms of drinking, poker, cattle handling, fighting and rope work.

The syntax reproduces breathless oral narration. Sentences often accumulate clauses linked by “and,” “but,” “then” and “while,” creating the impression of improvised speech. Parenthetical explanations, digressions and direct addresses to the porch audience make the tale feel performed rather than written. Billings frequently pauses to reinterpret events through jokes or analogies, so exposition rarely remains neutral. Dialogue is brisk and confrontational, built from challenges, interruptions, insults and punch lines. The frame narrator’s more balanced sentences contrast with Billings’s loose, additive syntax and help distinguish cultivated irony from vernacular exuberance.

Figurative language is dense, hyperbolic and grotesquely concrete. People are compared to landscapes, animals, machinery and food: a thirsty man is a “human blotter,” Heegan resembles a black bass and a toadfish, and the blood-and-tomato-smeared Badrich looks like a fancy ice-cream drink. Industrial action becomes warfare, navigation becomes a cavalry charge, and the city is interpreted through ranch experience. Billings’s metaphors frequently combine incompatible registers—nautical terminology, mining language, medical jargon and cowboy slang—giving the prose its comic inventiveness. Sound effects and action verbs intensify the physicality: crowds roar, missiles clatter, boats scrape and boil, and bodies are soaked, dragged, battered and reeled in like fish.

The humour is largely verbal but reinforced by situational irony. The strike begins, in Billings’s simplified account, with an employer’s indigestion; a court’s offer of the “privilege” of working off a fine leads eventually to the union leader being compelled to work; and arbitration, conventionally a process of negotiation, becomes coercion by rope. Repeated phrases such as “ten dollars and costs” operate as comic refrains and supply Billings with a personal motive beneath his claims of public heroism. Mock-heroic phrasing elevates barroom accidents and street fights into epic events, while bathos abruptly reduces politics and justice to appetite, bodily discomfort or revenge.

Pacing follows a staged escalation. The quiet porch opening creates curiosity around the press clippings, after which Billings moves through drinking, robbery, arrest, imprisonment, employment, riot and triumph. Expository passages about the strike are enlivened by caricature and interruption; the river journey then accelerates through increasingly large obstacles until the sight of Heegan triggers the climax. The closing newspaper quotation acts as both corroboration and polished punch line, returning the sprawling oral tale to the “press notices” that initiated it.

Characterisation is broad, theatrical and action-based. Billings is fearless, impulsive, vain, verbally inventive and governed by a rough personal code. He is less an introspective individual than a comic frontier type whose directness exposes the pretensions and failures of Eastern systems. Heegan is caricatured as a hypocritical demagogue, Badrich as an irascible capitalist revived by danger, and the ranch listeners as a chorus of sceptical insiders. Moral distinctions are uncomplicated: courage, loyalty, work and physical competence are admired, while legal formalism, rhetorical politics, idleness and deceit are mocked.

The story reflects the attitudes and conventions of early popular frontier fiction. Its masculinity is performative and combative, its social world almost entirely male, and its treatment of labour conflict strongly anti-union and individualist. Ethnic and racial labels, stereotypes and slurs are used casually as period comic shorthand; these elements date the prose and are integral to its rough vernacular register, though they are offensive by contemporary standards. Overall, the style combines the Western yarn, dialect comedy, mock epic and urban adventure, with narrative pleasure arising from the exuberant conversion of modern industrial conflict into a cowboy’s language of ropes, fights and personal settlement.

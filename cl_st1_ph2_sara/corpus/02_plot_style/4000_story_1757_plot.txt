A man examining a small painting recalls how the artist Blakey gave it to him as a wedding present because it depicted the Florentine villa where he had first met his future wife. While transporting it to her family’s home, he stopped at a florist to buy flowers, then boarded a horse-car. After arriving with only the flowers, he became convinced that he had left the painting on the car.

He repeatedly and vividly described placing the picture beside him, keeping a hand on it while speaking with an acquaintance, and forgetting it when he got off. Transit employees assured him it would be recovered, but searches produced nothing. Distressed, he confessed the loss to his fiancée, while concealing the painting’s sentimental subject for fear that forgetting it would imply he did not truly love her.

The painting was finally delivered from the florist’s shop, where he had actually left it before boarding the car. He realizes that his detailed memory of guarding and losing it on the second horse-car was entirely invented—a projection of what he had genuinely done on an earlier part of the journey. His wife later interprets this false memory as an unconscious premonition born from his dread of losing the gift, while his brother jokingly concludes that he had simply gone temporarily mad.

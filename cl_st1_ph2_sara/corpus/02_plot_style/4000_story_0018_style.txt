The story uses a genial, anecdotal Western style, combining frontier realism with the shape and timing of a tall tale. Its third-person narrator is broadly omniscient but stays close to the outlook of the cattle range, explaining customs such as “chuck-line riding” and treating cowboy values as the natural frame of reference. The narrative voice is confident, sociable, and mildly ironic. It rarely condemns the central trio outright; instead, their fraud is presented as roguish ingenuity, while the impersonal cattle companies, political ambition, and local credulity form the wider objects of satire.

The diction mixes plain standard narration with period-specific range vocabulary: “pay-roll,” “brand,” “round-up,” “rustling,” “branding-chute,” and “sinews of war.” Biblical, political, military, and commercial phrases are humorously transplanted into cowboy life, as in “cast bread upon the waters,” “central committee,” and Baugh’s “tuition account.” This produces a comic contrast between the men’s precarious circumstances and the grand language with which they describe themselves. The prose also preserves archaic forms and spellings such as “towards,” “cheque,” “some one,” and “to-day,” giving it a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century texture.

Dialogue dominates much of the story and carries both characterization and plot. Baugh speaks in expansive, theatrical boasts and strategic instructions; Stubb adopts a drier, skeptical manner; Arab amplifies every lie through extravagant statistics and practical claims about delivering votes. Their banter is affectionate but abrasive, filled with mock insults, oaths, gambling idioms, and comic undercutting. Repetition helps establish the trio’s rhythm: Baugh invents the scheme, Stubb lends it respectability, and Arab inflates it beyond plausibility. Seigerman’s speech is rendered through heavy phonetic German-accent spelling—“Dot ish my name” and “I accep”—a period caricatural convention that sharply distinguishes his voice but also makes ethnicity part of the joke.

Sentence structure varies according to function. Expository passages use measured, medium-to-long sentences to explain range life and economic conditions, while comic scenes proceed through brisk exchanges. The fraudulent campaign speeches are deliberately long, cumulative, and overloaded with plausible detail. Lists of cattle losses, political connections, company names, vote totals, and promised funds mimic the rhetoric of business and electioneering, allowing fabrication to acquire authority through specificity. Parenthetical comments and qualifying phrases add an oral, reminiscing quality, as though the narrator were retelling a well-known camp story.

The pacing is episodic and linear. It begins with social and economic background, narrows to the trio’s dismissal, follows their gambling losses, and then devotes the largest section to the escalating confidence trick. Repeated visits to Seigerman’s back room create a patterned comic progression: each man performs a role, the supposed political coalition grows, and Seigerman becomes steadily more committed. Several months are then compressed into a brief summary before the trio withdraws as spring returns. This seasonal structure is important: winter creates their need, the bogus campaign sustains them, and new grass releases them back into legitimate range work.

Humour arises chiefly from dramatic irony, exaggeration, incongruity, and delayed payoff. The reader understands that the political movement is fictitious while Seigerman interprets every refusal of drink, private conference, and invented credential as proof of professionalism. The trio’s temporary temperance is especially comic because it is purely tactical. Their increasingly grand coalition is marked through the mock-diplomatic sequence “Zweibund,” “Dreibund,” and “Fierbund,” elevating a bar-room swindle into an international alliance. The final revelation—that Seigerman receives one vote—provides a concise punch line, followed by a folkloric coda in which “Seigerman’s Per Cent” becomes range slang for the worst possible return.

Characterization is economical and behavioral rather than introspective. Baugh is defined by confidence, verbal control, physical presence, and improvisational intelligence. Stubb is the “conservative” whose apparent caution makes the deception credible, while Arab is the campaign “heeler,” expert in boasting about mass organization and electoral manipulation. Seigerman is characterized through hospitality, vanity, credulity, and political ambition. The narrator occasionally labels or summarizes these traits, but the men are chiefly revealed by how they talk, gamble, scheme, and respond to one another.

The underlying tone is amused rather than moralizing. The deception is ethically dubious, yet the story frames it as a survival strategy prompted by corporate stinginess and winter unemployment. Hospitality is treated as a central frontier virtue: the cattle companies’ withdrawal of food and shelter violates range custom, whereas Seigerman’s generosity—though exploited—keeps the men alive. At the same time, the tale satirizes electoral corruption, manufactured public opinion, inflated commercial authority, and the ease with which personal ambition can be manipulated. Overall, the style is colloquial, masculine, dialogue-heavy, regionally grounded, and structurally oriented toward a memorable comic ending, with realism supplying the setting and tall-tale exaggeration supplying the energy.

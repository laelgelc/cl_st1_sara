The story uses restrained literary realism, combining close psychological observation with a carefully rendered urban setting. Its narrative voice is third-person and largely limited to Little Chandler’s perspective, but it frequently shades into free indirect discourse. His aspirations, prejudices, self-justifications and fantasies enter the narration without always being explicitly introduced as thoughts. Consequently, descriptions that initially sound authoritative often reveal Chandler’s own sentimental or distorted way of seeing: Dublin’s houses become “a band of tramps,” his melancholy becomes evidence of a poetic temperament, and his friend’s success appears both admirable and unjust. The narration maintains enough distance, however, to expose the vanity and weakness within these perceptions. This produces a quiet, sustained irony rather than overt authorial judgement.

The prose alternates between lucid, economical narration and passages of lyrical reverie. Sentence structure is generally balanced and controlled, with parallel clauses, accumulative lists and measured repetitions used to trace Chandler’s changing emotions. Short declarative sentences punctuate moments of certainty or defeat—“It was useless”—while longer sentences accompany fantasy, self-analysis and mounting agitation. Parenthetical qualifications and hesitant constructions reproduce his timid, self-conscious temperament. Repetition is especially important: ideas of littleness, melancholy, escape, success and imprisonment recur until they acquire symbolic force. Exclamation marks, ellipses and broken syntax become more prominent when the narrative moves from reflective composure to intoxication, resentment and panic.

The diction shifts according to social and psychological context. Narrative passages employ precise, sometimes elevated vocabulary—“punctiliously,” “necessitous,” “equipoise,” “connubial”—which suits Chandler’s cultivated self-image and the story’s slightly formal period register. Poetic and classical allusions, such as the comparison to “alarmed Atalantas” and the quotation from Byron, reflect his literary ambitions, though they also expose their derivative quality. Gallaher’s speech is brasher and more idiomatic, filled with slang, journalistic energy, boasts, rhetorical questions and cosmopolitan fragments of French or Irish. Chandler’s dialogue is quieter, more tentative and punctuated by modest qualifications. The contrast in their voices dramatises the opposition between apparent worldly confidence and inhibited domestic respectability.

Description is concrete but symbolically charged. Dublin is presented through named streets, institutions, bridges, shops and public houses, giving the story a strong sense of geographical and social specificity. Light and colour provide a recurring visual pattern: the “kindly golden dust” of sunset encourages Chandler’s poetic hopes, while Corless’s glitters with red and green glasses and Gallaher is marked by his vivid orange tie and slate-blue eyes. The domestic ending contracts this broader urban world into a small lamplit room filled with hire-purchase furniture, a framed photograph and an unpaid-for life. Spatial movement therefore mirrors psychological movement: Chandler walks toward Corless’s imagining that each step brings him closer to London, but the story ultimately returns him to confinement at home.

Imagery repeatedly reduces or diminishes. Chandler is physically small and childlike, the poor children in the street are likened to mice and vermin, Dublin’s houses are stunted, and his poetic hope takes life “like an infant hope.” These motifs culminate in the actual infant whose crying defeats his attempt to read and write. The child functions both realistically and symbolically: it embodies Chandler’s responsibilities, but also exposes his own emotional immaturity. Images of enclosure reinforce this effect. Marriage is described by Gallaher as putting one’s “head in the sack”; Chandler sees himself as a “prisoner for life”; Byron’s “narrow cell” appears just before his frustration erupts. Such motifs are woven into ordinary detail rather than announced as symbols.

Dialogue occupies the central social scene and is used for indirect characterisation. Gallaher dominates through rapid questions, anecdotes, teasing and self-advertisement, while Chandler mostly prompts, listens or offers cautious challenges. Their conversation is less a genuine reunion than a contest of identities. Gallaher performs the worldly journalist; Chandler performs the refined, serious man while covertly seeking validation. The narration quietly destabilises both performances: Gallaher’s glamour appears vulgar and tawdry, but Chandler’s supposed superiority is founded on fantasy and resentment. The bar scene thus develops through subtle changes in Chandler’s response—from admiration to envy, disillusionment, defensiveness and wounded pride.

The overall structure is progressive and tightly patterned. It begins with anticipation, expands into urban reverie, reaches its social centre in the meeting with Gallaher, and then contracts into domestic crisis. Chandler’s imagined future as a poet is repeatedly raised but never converted into action. His “epiphany” is not liberating; it is a painful recognition of his own paralysis and moral failure. The final movement replaces grand thoughts of London, Paris, art and escape with the immediate consequences of shouting at a frightened child. The ending is deliberately subdued: shame, exclusion from the lamplight and tears of remorse take the place of explanation or resolution.

The tone blends melancholy, compassion and irony. Chandler is neither simply mocked nor fully vindicated. His sensitivity and frustration are treated as real, but his self-romanticisation, snobbery and passive resentment are exposed. This tonal complexity is central to the style: emotional force arises through understatement, patterned detail and the gap between how the protagonist imagines himself and what his conduct reveals. The result is an intimate psychological portrait in which a commonplace evening becomes a study of thwarted ambition, envy, domestic entrapment and self-recognition.

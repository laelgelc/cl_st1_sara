The story uses a controlled realist style shaped by social observation, understated satire and sustained dramatic irony. Its third-person narrator moves freely among characters while remaining coolly detached, often adopting the assumptions or vocabulary of the person under scrutiny. This flexible focalisation allows the narration to expose self-deception without overt commentary: Mrs. Kearney’s belief in her own refinement, prudence and maternal righteousness is presented close to her perspective, yet the phrasing continually reveals calculation, vanity and class anxiety. The narrator’s judgments are usually embedded in apparently neutral statements, such as the claim that she married “out of spite” or respected her husband as she respected the General Post Office, “as something large, secure and fixed.” Such comparisons are concise, comic and psychologically exact.

The diction is lucid, concrete and largely unornamented, with occasional elevated or figurative turns that sharpen the satire. Physical details carry moral and social significance: Mr. Fitzpatrick chews a programme “into a moist pulp”; Madam Glynn’s faded dress is exposed by a “revengeful” shadow; O’Madden Burke balances himself on both a literal umbrella and the “moral umbrella” of his impressive name. Clothing, accents, manners, gestures and small breaches of decorum operate as markers of class and character. Descriptions frequently combine precision with caricature, but the caricature remains plausible rather than grotesque. Repeated adjectives and motifs—“vacant,” “flat,” “little,” “friendly,” “ladylike,” “respectable”—gradually accumulate ironic force.

Sentence structure is generally balanced and sequential, favouring coordinated clauses that record actions, impressions and consequences in an orderly progression. Longer sentences catalogue social behaviour or trace a character’s reasoning, while short declarative sentences deliver comic verdicts or moments of conflict. Parallelism and repetition are prominent: Mrs. Kearney “advised and dissuaded”; the artistes “rolled and unrolled their music”; the audience “clapped and stamped”; and the central insistence that Kathleen “won’t go on” becomes a verbal refrain. The prose often moves from a broad summary of habits into a sharply observed scene, producing an economical alternation between compressed biography and real-time drama.

Dialogue is naturalistic, socially coded and increasingly confrontational. Forms of address, idiomatic phrasing, evasions and grammatical peculiarities reproduce Dublin speech and reveal distinctions of education, confidence and status. The committee’s representatives speak vaguely and defer responsibility—“You’d better speak to Mr. Fitzpatrick”—whereas Mrs. Kearney repeats the concrete language of contracts, payment and rights. Her speech shifts from controlled politeness to legalistic insistence, sarcasm and open anger. Interruptions, repeated questions and curt exchanges accelerate the final dispute. The dialogue is framed by gestures—staring, stroking a beard, moving a shoe, glancing around the room—which expose emotions that convention prevents the characters from stating directly.

Characterisation is indirect and cumulative. People are defined through habits, possessions, bodily features, social aspirations and their behaviour under pressure. Minor figures receive compact miniature portraits that give the crowded social world unusual density: the nervous tenor, the self-protective baritone, the faded soprano, the flirtatious contralto and the suavely opportunistic journalist all embody different varieties of insecurity or pretension. Names and titles contribute to the comedy, especially the repeated use of “artiste,” “committee,” and ceremonious honorifics. Mrs. Kearney is the dominant figure, presented neither as wholly wrong nor wholly sympathetic. Her contractual grievance has substance, but her rigidity, pride and inability to accommodate social realities turn justified suspicion into public defeat. The committee, meanwhile, is disorganised and evasive, yet collective opinion finally protects it by recasting her resistance as indecency.

The narrative is structured as a gradual tightening of conflict. An expository opening establishes Mrs. Kearney’s temperament, marriage, ambitions and investment in her daughter. Preparations for the concerts then demonstrate her competence and create the contractual basis of the dispute. Each performance supplies a further warning: the empty hall, poor artistes, cancelled concert, elusive officials and delayed payment. On the final night, backstage space becomes increasingly crowded and claustrophobic while the impatient audience supplies an audible pressure through clapping, stamping and whistling. The climactic refusal to perform is followed by a temporary compromise, renewed confrontation at the interval and a decisive departure. The closing exchange—“I’m not done with you yet” / “But I’m done with you”—ends the story on a blunt reversal, after which male communal approval seals Mrs. Kearney’s social exclusion.

The treatment of the Irish cultural revival is sceptical and anti-romantic. Nationalist language, patriotic performance and cultural prestige appear entangled with postcard fashion, personal advancement, amateur administration and journalistic publicity. Lofty public ideals contrast with shabby practical arrangements, mediocre performances and disputes over money. This does not amount to direct denunciation; rather, the story exposes the gap between ceremonial enthusiasm and institutional competence. The concert itself becomes a miniature social system governed by reputation, gender, class, patronage and the fear of embarrassment.

The overall tone is dry, observant and faintly merciless. Humour arises from incongruity, bathos and the narrator’s exact placement of details rather than from explicit jokes. Moments of pathos—Madam Glynn’s humiliation, Kathleen’s meekness, Mrs. Kearney’s isolation—are present but restrained. The style avoids sentimental resolution and offers no authoritative moral judgment. Instead, it lets competing forms of vanity, cowardice, pragmatism and injured dignity reveal themselves through action, leaving the reader to recognise both the legitimacy of Mrs. Kearney’s demand and the social destructiveness of the manner in which she pursues it.

In 1859, forty-two-year-old Procopio accepts a well-paid position as attendant to Colonel Felisbert, a wealthy, gravely ill, and notoriously abusive man. After a brief period of cordiality, the colonel subjects him to constant insults, humiliation, sleeplessness, and occasional violence. Procopio repeatedly considers leaving, but pleas from the local vicar and the colonel himself persuade him to remain.

One night, after the colonel throws a water bottle at his face, Procopio loses control, attacks him, and strangles him. Horrified by what he has done, he conceals the marks on the body and reports that the colonel died naturally from his illnesses. No one suspects him; his trembling and distress are interpreted as grief and devotion. He returns to Rio de Janeiro tormented by guilt, nightmares, and fear of discovery.

A week later, Procopio learns that the colonel named him sole heir to his considerable fortune. Initially, he wants to refuse the inheritance or give it all away, both to avoid profiting from the killing and to redeem himself. He accepts it, however, reasoning that refusal might arouse suspicion. Back in the village, he is praised for his patience and loyalty, while the townspeople recount the colonel’s lifelong cruelty. Their condemnation gives Procopio a secret sense of relief and helps him recast the killing as self-defense, an accident, or merely the hastening of an inevitable death.

As time passes, his remorse fades. He donates only a small portion of the inheritance to charity and religious causes, erects a monument over the colonel’s grave, and keeps the rest. Near death years later, he confesses the truth while bitterly concluding that wealth, rather than virtue, brings consolation.

The story is written as an antiquarian ghost tale in a polished, late-Victorian or Edwardian literary register. Its world is one of auction rooms, manuscript collections, country houses, scholarly societies and obscure local histories. Precise references to places, dates, prices, institutions and domestic arrangements give the narrative the texture of a factual memoir. This documentary realism makes the supernatural episode seem less like fantasy than an anomalous event embedded in an otherwise orderly social world.

The narrator is retrospective, urbane and only indirectly involved. He presents the events as having been related to him by the principal sufferer, creating a layered testimony rather than an omniscient account. He frequently addresses the reader, comments on his own methods and decides what may be summarized or omitted. Phrases such as “it is no part of my plan,” “I need not trouble you” and “that is all we need know” produce the manner of an educated raconteur shaping evidence for an attentive audience. The voice is authoritative but not solemn: dry asides, qualifications and mock-apologies establish a companionable relationship with the reader while preserving uncertainty around the central horror.

The prose is formal, measured and syntactically elaborate. Long sentences carry subordinate clauses, parenthetical observations, lists and careful qualifications, often delaying the main point. Latinate diction and antiquarian terminology—“intimation,” “reprehensibly,” “cursory,” “prolific,” “topography,” “quartos”—reinforce the narrator’s scholarly milieu. This formality is balanced by conversational phrasing and precise comic timing. The prose often begins with administrative or material detail and only gradually admits disturbing implications, so that the supernatural appears within the same controlled syntax used to discuss catalogues, curtains and social engagements.

Humour is central to the style rather than incidental to it. Much of the opening is a comedy of manners involving Denton’s absent-minded collecting, his aunt’s domestic tyranny, tedious acquaintances and the pretensions of tradesmen. The narrator’s wit is typically parenthetical and understated, as in the comment on where Denton could have stamped his foot or the observation that quarrelling was not uncommon in Hearne’s career. Miss Denton’s complaints expand into comic monologues, while Denton’s mildness and scholarly distraction make him an amiably ineffectual protagonist. The humour lowers the reader’s guard and establishes normality before the story turns toward terror.

Dialogue is strongly individuated. Educated characters speak in decorous, socially restrained exchanges, though Miss Denton’s repetitive grievances reveal her dominance. Mr. Cattell’s dropped aspirates, misquotations, clichés and self-important circumlocutions are rendered phonetically, creating a comic social portrait. His prolix speech also becomes an indirect vehicle for warning: the block-cutter’s instinctive disgust with the design is disclosed amid professional chatter. This allows ominous information to surface without a conspicuous change in narrative register.

The structure follows a gradual progression from curiosity to acquisition, reproduction, installation and manifestation. An apparently harmless antiquarian discovery passes through a chain of mundane decisions until it becomes physically present throughout the house. The repeated handling and copying of the fabric pattern gives the plot a logic of transmission: danger is not merely uncovered but recreated and multiplied. Foreshadowing is cumulative and initially dismissible. The pattern resembles hair; its bands converge into suggestive forms; the craftsman senses evil in it; the curtains seem to contain peeping figures; they rustle on a still night. Each detail acquires its full significance only retrospectively.

Pacing is deliberately slow for most of the story. Considerable space is devoted to auctions, furnishings, manufacturing delays and social obligations. This procedural density creates plausibility and suspense through postponement. The actual apparition is brief, sudden and intensely sensory. At that point the prose becomes more immediate and kinetic, moving from the tentative touch of hair to the realization of a prone human shape, then to flight, impact and pursuit. Even here the creature is not anatomically explained. Its facelessness, stillness and incomplete materiality preserve ambiguity and concentrate fear in texture, posture and movement.

The horror depends on suggestion rather than spectacle. Hair, usually a decorative or bodily detail, becomes an uncanny material that crosses boundaries between person, corpse, pattern and furnishing. The tactile sequence is especially important: Denton first mistakes the thing for his dog, strokes it and only then recognizes its unnatural stillness. This corruption of a familiar domestic gesture intensifies the shock. The entity remains “shapeless,” and its grasp is described as something potentially “worse than a hand,” allowing implication to exceed visual description. The narrator’s admission that he has merely “tried to put into words” the horror further suggests that the experience resists complete representation.

The embedded diary supplies the explanatory climax in a pastiche of early-eighteenth-century prose. Archaic spelling, capitalization, abbreviations and period vocabulary give the passage the appearance of an authentic primary source. Its account connects the design with a beautiful but depraved young man, his violently removed hair, a coffin supernaturally filled with hair and hangings made as a memorial to it. The explanation is substantial enough to complete the pattern of clues but remains incomplete in metaphysical terms. The story identifies the origin of the haunting without explaining precisely how the fabric preserves or releases it.

Characterization is economical and socially grounded. Denton is defined through habits rather than psychological exposition: antiquarian enthusiasm, absent-mindedness, politeness, aversion to social duties and a tendency to defer to his aunt. Miss Denton combines domestic authority, moral pressure and aesthetic enthusiasm; her fascination with the pattern drives its reproduction, although she does not install it in her own room. Secondary figures are sharply caricatured through one dominant mannerism. Even the historical villain, Sir Everard Charlett, is built from suggestive biographical fragments—beauty, vanity, dissipation and luxuriant hair—rather than a full portrait.

Thematically, the story explores the danger of reviving what should remain buried. Antiquarian curiosity, aesthetic taste and modern manufacture combine to restore a malignant remnant of the past. The domestic interior, supposedly improved by tasteful decoration, becomes the site of invasion. The narrative also plays on failures of interpretation: the aunt sees beauty, Denton sees an innocuous pattern, the craftsman feels unexplained revulsion, and the original owner hints at a “subtlety” in the design without discovering it. Knowledge arrives only after experience, when the concealed manuscript leaves are separated and read.

The ending is concise, sardonic and controlled. The destruction of the curtains is reported almost as an accounting matter, while the final Shakespearean allusion converts the episode into a familiar cultural tag. This refusal to end on an elaborate supernatural explanation is characteristic of the story’s restraint. The dominant stylistic effect comes from the contrast between genial scholarship and concentrated terror: leisurely social comedy, documentary exactitude and narrative self-consciousness prepare a world in which one briefly glimpsed, inadequately described figure becomes overwhelmingly disturbing.

Nabendu Sekhar inherits his father’s obsessive ambition to gain British official favor and hopes to be rewarded with the title of Rai Bahadur. After marrying Arunlekha, however, he enters a strongly nationalist family whose members despise colonial servility. His clever sisters-in-law, led by the beautiful Labanyalekha, repeatedly mock his reverence for Englishmen, even treating English boots and common British names as objects of worship.

Eager for Labanya’s approval, Nabendu gradually hides and then disavows his pursuit of British favor. During a visit to Labanya and her husband Nilratan, he is maneuvered into donating a thousand rupees to the Indian National Congress. Public letters alternately praise him as a patriot and defend him as a loyal opponent of Congress. Provoked by Labanya’s teasing, he publishes a forceful anti–Anglo-Indian reply, largely composed by Labanya and Nilratan, which establishes his public reputation as a nationalist.

Still anxious about British approval, Nabendu is tricked into believing that the District Magistrate has called on him. He rushes to return the visit, bribes the servants, and is humiliated when the Magistrate denies ever having come. Meanwhile, Congress leaders embrace Nabendu as an important supporter, and he finds himself celebrated as a national leader almost by accident.

When the Queen’s Birthday honors are announced, Nabendu’s name is absent from the list of Rai Bahadurs. Instead, his sisters-in-law ceremonially crown him their “King,” garlanding and honoring him as a patriot, while his wife privately prepares her own affectionate tribute. Yet the ending ironically suggests that Nabendu’s old hunger for colonial recognition may not be permanently extinguished.

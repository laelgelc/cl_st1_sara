The story adopts a polished comic-realist style with the flavour of an early twentieth-century urban conte. Its setting is rendered through precise Parisian geography, commercial detail, and local social customs, but this realism is filtered through a playful, conspicuously literary narrator. The avenue de la Grande Armée, the Métropolitain entrances, cafés, shopfronts, products, prices, and trades establish a concrete social world in which status is measured by décor, clientele, cleanliness, and visible prosperity.

The narration is third-person omniscient and strongly intrusive. The narrator moves freely among the motives of Alexandrine, Espérance, Hippolyte, and others, while repeatedly stepping back to interpret events through aphorisms, ironic summaries, and mock-solemn judgments. Phrases such as “the veritable root of all evil,” “there is a serpent in every Eden,” and “He was magnificent, was Hippolyte!” inflate a petty neighbourhood quarrel to biblical or heroic proportions. This discrepancy between elevated narration and trivial subject matter is the principal source of humour. The narrator also uses anticipatory remarks—especially the repeated mystery of “and above all, now!”—to guide the reader toward the eventual revelation.

The tone is urbane, amused, and satirical rather than cruel. Human vanity, jealousy, commercial ambition, sentimentality, and self-interest are exposed, but the characters remain vivid and broadly sympathetic comic types. Alexandrine is defined by envy, wounded pride, theatrical rage, and excessive devotion to her cat; Espérance by commercial shrewdness, placidity, and a latent appetite for retaliation; Hippolyte by amiable ambition and sudden domestic heroics; Flique by genial opportunism backed by institutional power. Zut functions simultaneously as a character, a comic emblem of indifference, and the plot’s unwitting agent of chaos. Her serene unconcern contrasts with the exaggerated passions projected onto her by the humans.

The diction combines formal, sometimes archaic English with French vocabulary and idiom. Expressions such as “the which,” “cares naught,” “thereby,” “wherewith,” and “vouchsafed” give the prose an old-fashioned mock-ceremonial quality. French terms—including *épicerie*, *salle de coiffure*, *quartier*, *dot*, *patronne*, *matou*, and *brillantine*—are generally left untranslated because their meaning is apparent from context. Dialogue employs heavier code-switching, exclamations, compressed colloquialisms, and literal-seeming French constructions: “Do I know?”, “What has she?”, “your affairs march to a marvel,” and “Name of a name.” This creates a stylised impression of French speech in English rather than strict linguistic naturalism. Repeated invocations such as “Mon Dieu,” “c’est tout,” and “au r’voir” reinforce place, temperament, and comic rhythm.

Sentences are often long, balanced, and accumulative, with several subordinate clauses and parenthetical qualifications. Lists are used to build both atmosphere and comic excess, as in the elaborate catalogue of the salon’s mirrors, nickel apparatus, soaps, perfumes, sponges, signs, uniforms, and furnishings. Parallel phrasing and incremental repetition sharpen contrasts: possible custom must become “probable,” then “permanent”; Alexandrine suffers from “accumulating debts and decreasing earnings”; Zut’s visits progress from short to long and finally to overnight stays. Parenthetical corrections—“which was exaggeration,” “which was doubtful,” “which was wholly untrue”—allow the narrator to puncture characters’ claims without interrupting the narrative flow.

Description is sensuous but selective, relying especially on visual display and smell. The salon glitters with gilt, glass, enamel, and nickel and smells of violet and lilac, whereas the grocery is dusty, dull, and heavy with vegetables, cheese, dried fish, sardines, and biscuits. These contrasting sensory fields externalise the women’s fortunes and explain Alexandrine’s resentment more effectively than abstract commentary alone. Physical caricature is similarly economical: Alexandrine’s former “fifty” kilos have become ninety; Flique is ruddy-faced; Zut is prodigiously large, sapphire-eyed, and majestically inert.

Dialogue is highly theatrical and often arranged as escalating verbal combat. Courteous formulas become weapons, minor remarks turn into insults, and climactic exchanges are reduced to sharp cries such as “Mal élevée!” and “Gueuse!” Gestures—shrugs, fists, flouncing exits, pursed lips, and eggs nearly hurled across a counter—carry as much meaning as speech. Formal commercial politeness is repeatedly juxtaposed with open hostility, culminating in Espérance’s mock-civil letter, whose “distinguished consideration” makes the theft of the white kitten more pointedly insulting.

The plot is tightly constructed as a chain of comic escalation. It begins with the feud already established, then supplies its economic and emotional causes, introduces Abel’s defection, identifies Zut as the catalytic object, stages failed reconciliation, intensifies the cat’s visits, and resolves everything through the birth of the kittens. Foreshadowing is conspicuous but playful: the opening declares Zut the “root of all evil,” and Alexandrine’s recurring “above all, now!” delays the information that the cat is pregnant. The final white kitten converts a domestic accident into a legal, commercial, and social victory for the Sergeots.

Irony governs the story at every level. Alexandrine adores an animal that barely acknowledges her; her attempts to preserve customers through inferior goods drive them away; her accusations of theft help produce the circumstances in which a kitten is actually appropriated; and the policeman’s supposed impartial authority is openly shaped by friendship, free grooming, perfume, and sardines. The legalistic claim over the kittens parodies serious disputes over property, while Flique’s final contrast between the “superior kitten” and “inferior eggs” neatly joins the story’s two main strands.

The ending is circular and epigrammatic. Alexandrine’s “Oh, Zut!” refers at once to the cat, her defeat, and the French exclamation of disgust or resignation. The narrator then repeats the earlier explanation that the word means “everything and nothing,” turning the title character’s name into a summary of the tale’s comic philosophy. The overall style is therefore ornate yet controlled, cosmopolitan, mock-heroic, and driven by social observation, verbal irony, symmetrical plotting, and affectionate caricature.

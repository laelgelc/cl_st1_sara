The story uses a late-Victorian or Edwardian supernatural-horror style, combining the conventions of a traditional ghost story with the methodical suspense of a psychological thriller. Its setting is emphatically urban and wintry: the empty floors of a gaunt Bloomsbury house, the darkness of a December night, sleet striking the windows, and wind roaring among black roofs create an atmosphere of isolation within a crowded city. This bleak London interior is contrasted with Johnson’s anticipated Alpine holiday, whose sunshine, snowfields, skating, dancing, and “rosy-cheeked girls” represent the ordinary, healthy world he is increasingly unable to reach.

The narration is third-person and largely restricted to Johnson’s perceptions, though the narrator occasionally steps back to analyse fear in general. Passages such as the comparison of accumulating impressions to ice forming “film by film” on still water give the narrative a reflective, quasi-psychological authority. The supernatural is therefore approached through the gradual alteration of consciousness rather than introduced abruptly. Johnson hears, doubts, investigates, rationalises, and only then sees. This close alignment with his mind allows the story to preserve uncertainty for much of its length: footsteps may be the wind, a figure may be peripheral vision, and the moving bag may be a lapse of memory. At the same time, the narrator steadily undermines these explanations by noting that Johnson does not truly believe them.

Suspense is cumulative and carefully graded. The story explicitly traces a progression from uneasiness to nervousness, fear, terror, and finally “pure horror.” Repetition is central to this escalation. The stealthy tread returns several times, always nearer; the human sigh is heard first without a source and then beside Johnson’s face; the bag repeatedly appears to have shifted; and the murderer’s white face, low black fringe, and evil eyes recur first in memory, then in accidental shapes, and finally as an apparition. Each repetition adds one concrete detail and reduces the possibility of a natural explanation. The pacing slows sharply around doors, staircases, curtains, switches, and acts of looking, stretching moments of hesitation before releasing them in sudden movements such as a bound, a lurch, a slammed door, or the restoration of electric light.

The prose alternates between long, elaborately subordinated sentences and short exclamations or blunt statements. The longer sentences reproduce Johnson’s anxious reasoning, qualify what he sees, and delay revelation; the shorter ones mark shocks and decisive recognitions. Parenthetical qualifications and self-corrections—“a shadow—or was it a travel stain?”—dramatise uncertainty in real time. Intensifying adverbs and paired adjectives are frequent: movements are “soft and stealthy,” the weather “cheerless” and “dismal,” and the murder “brutal and cold-blooded.” The language is formal but accessible, with period expressions such as “By Jove,” “in the name of Heaven,” “pure nerves,” and “real pluck.” The occasional direct address of the reader, as in references to “the other things this story has to tell,” belongs to the conversational tradition of the fireside ghost tale.

Sensory description is dominated by sound and touch before sight becomes decisive. Footsteps, rattling windows, volleying sleet, echoing voices, the roar of the gale, a resounding bang, and the repeated sigh make the house acoustically alive. Cold is both meteorological and bodily: Johnson shudders, turns white, feels his legs weaken, experiences crawling flesh, and senses something “cold about his heart.” Visual imagery is withheld or made indistinct until the climax. Shadows, folds of canvas, empty stairs, swaying curtains, and peripheral glimpses keep forms unstable, whereas the apparition is revealed “clear as life” under the “merciless glare” of electric light. Light is not automatically reassuring; darkness briefly becomes a “merciful screen,” while illumination forces Johnson to confront what he fears.

The kit-bag functions as the story’s central object and symbolic focus. At first it is an ordinary practical item whose shapelessness and unlimited capacity are convenient. It gradually becomes anthropomorphic: its folded canvas resembles a face, its brass rings become eyes, its sides appear stretched, it lurches, and it shuffles across the floor. The mundane act of packing is thus transformed into an unwitting repetition of the murderer’s disposal of the dismembered body. The bag also embodies suppressed evidence. Its dirt, wear, bloodstain, and bulging shape carry the physical memory of the crime, while Johnson’s own mind carries the visual memory of the trial. Object and consciousness reinforce each other until the past becomes materially present.

Characterisation is economical and functional. Johnson’s delicate, almost feminine appearance initially suggests vulnerability, but the narrator repeatedly stresses his courage and self-control. His habit of speaking aloud, laughing forcibly, humming too loudly, and inventing rational explanations reveals fear through failed attempts to conceal it. Wilbraham is presented as professionally composed and emotionally restrained, while his warning that disturbing pictures return when least wanted foreshadows the entire episode. Mrs Monks and the barrister’s servant are rendered partly through class-marked dialogue, including dropped initial consonants and non-standard grammar. Their practical, casual voices contrast with Johnson’s elevated inner crisis and help return the conclusion to the everyday social world.

Dialogue is brief, naturalistic, and strategically placed. The opening legal conversation supplies the murder, the insanity verdict, Johnson’s fixation on Turk’s face, and the request for a bag without making the exposition feel detached from the action. Johnson’s later speech is mainly defensive self-talk, exposing the difference between his outward insistence on reason and his inward certainty of an invisible presence. The apparition’s only words—“It’s my bag. And I want it”—are plain, possessive, and almost banal. Their simplicity makes them more disturbing than an ornate supernatural pronouncement would be.

Structurally, the story is circular and tightly foreshadowed. It begins with Turk escaping execution and Johnson borrowing a kit-bag; it ends with the revelation that Turk has killed himself and requested burial in that same bag. The final explanation confirms both a mundane error—the wrong bag was delivered—and a supernatural claim: Turk died at ten o’clock, the time when the disturbances began. This ending does not explain the ghost away. Instead, practical details authenticate it, linking the apparition to a precise object, motive, and hour of death. The closing twist is delivered through the servant’s unemotional report, whose understatement intensifies the macabre effect.

The overall tone moves from professional detachment through unease and claustrophobic dread to explicit horror, then ends in grim irony. Its chief stylistic strengths are slow accumulation, repeated sensory motifs, psychological qualification, the corruption of an ordinary domestic task, and the delayed convergence of courtroom evidence, haunted memory, and supernatural possession. The result is a controlled, atmospheric ghost story in which rational modern surroundings—legal chambers, electric lights, rented rooms, and travel preparations—offer no protection against the violent return of the past.

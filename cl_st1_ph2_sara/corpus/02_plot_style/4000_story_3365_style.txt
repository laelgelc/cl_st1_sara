The story uses a plain, realist style focused on working-class life, economic insecurity, and moral choice. Its narrative is linear and tightly organised: Gerasim’s unsuccessful search for work establishes the problem, Yegor’s intervention creates an apparent solution, and the overheard conversation reveals the human cost of that solution. The numbered sections divide the plot into clear dramatic stages and give the story the measured progression of a moral tale.

The narration is third-person and largely aligned with Gerasim’s experience. It reports his circumstances in an economical, matter-of-fact manner, occasionally entering his thoughts and feelings through phrases such as “it disheartened him,” “it stabbed him like a dagger,” and “it made him sick at heart.” This limited psychological access keeps Gerasim sympathetic while avoiding extensive introspection. Character is shown primarily through conduct: his desperation, gratitude, hesitation, and final refusal communicate his nature more strongly than direct authorial description.

The diction is accessible and concrete, with an older, translated quality. Expressions such as “betook himself,” “held him in high favour,” “man of all work,” and “had no allurements” create a formal, slightly archaic register, while references to kopeks, kvas, military duty, coachmen, merchants, and the almshouse establish the Russian social setting. The prose often uses proverbial or idiomatic formulations—“not a stone did he leave unturned,” “wag his tongue,” and “sick at heart”—which reinforce its folktale-like moral clarity. Occasional awkward constructions and irregularities suggest an older English translation rather than deliberately ornate prose.

Sentences are generally moderate in length and syntactically straightforward. Expository passages compress years of background efficiently, whereas moments of conflict are rendered through short exchanges and rapid alternation between speakers. Repetition is used for emphasis, particularly in the servants’ speech: “We work and work,” “ruined us, ruined us,” and Gerasim’s broken “and—and” reproduce agitation and emotional pressure. Dashes, interruptions, exclamations, and fragments give the dialogue an oral, spontaneous rhythm.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization and social commentary. Yegor’s boastful, patronising speech reveals vanity, self-interest, and loyalty to hierarchy; Sharov’s hesitant replies show a limited but genuine conscience; Polikarpych and his wife voice the vulnerability of ageing workers; and Gerasim’s deferential language marks his precarious social position. The speakers are distinguished less by dialect than by tone, rhetorical habits, and relative power. Forms of address such as “Yegor Danilych,” “my boy,” “my lad,” and “sir” continually signal status.

The story’s irony is structural. Gerasim’s longed-for employment becomes morally unacceptable once he discovers that it depends on another servant’s dismissal. Yegor presents himself as an exemplary worker, yet his security rests on informing, manipulation, and the displacement of someone weaker. Sharov appears to hold the authority, but Yegor’s insinuations guide his decision. The overheard conversation functions as the turning point, transforming abstract good fortune into visible suffering.

The emotional treatment is restrained but openly sympathetic. Poverty is presented through practical details—hunger, dependence on acquaintances, cold work, old age, and the threat of the almshouse—rather than elaborate description. Metaphors are sparse and conventional, so the few that appear carry force. The ending depends on ethical paradox: Gerasim leaves without work, yet feels “happy and lighthearted.” Material loss becomes moral liberation, giving the conclusion the compact force of a parable.

Overall, the style combines social realism with didactic simplicity. It exposes the cruelty produced by insecurity and competition without lengthy authorial preaching, allowing dialogue, contrast, and Gerasim’s final action to express the central values of compassion, dignity, and solidarity among the poor.

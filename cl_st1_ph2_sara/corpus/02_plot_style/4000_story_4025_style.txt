The story adopts a deliberately archaic, legendary mode, blending medieval Christian hagiography with the atmosphere of an oral folktale. Its remote monastic setting is established through concrete details—wicker cells, wooden chapels, white habits, censers, querns, illuminated books and jewelled reliquaries—while names such as Brother Dove, Brother Bald Fox and Brother Bittern lend the community a gentle, emblematic quality. The narrative world is austere but enchanted: wolves move through snowy woods, stars appear through roof smoke, birds perch without fear, and miracles emerge naturally from everyday labour.

The prose is formal, rhythmic and highly cumulative. Long sentences are built from coordinated clauses, repeated conjunctions and extended lists of people, objects and actions. This paratactic structure creates a scriptural or chronicle-like cadence, while recurring constructions such as “one… one… one…” and “and… and… and…” give the narration the measured flow of oral storytelling. These expansive periods are balanced by short, lucid exchanges in dialogue, particularly the child’s questions about the stars, God, fire and nothingness. Repetition reinforces the tale’s theological ideas and gives abstract concepts the simplicity of doctrine or parable.

The diction mixes plain physical vocabulary with elevated religious language. Bread, water, snow, mud, grass, tools and manual work coexist with phrases concerning crystalline spheres, divine breath, angelic orders, martyrdom and the love of God. Archaic or ecclesiastical terms such as “succour,” “vespers,” “acolytes,” “crozier” and “penance” sustain the historical register. Characters often speak in ceremonious invocations—“O blessed abbot” and “O Thou Who dwellest beyond the stars”—which heighten the devotional tone without removing the story’s warmth or occasional mild humour.

The narrator is omniscient, composed and sympathetic, presenting supernatural beliefs without irony. Characterisation is economical and archetypal: the abbot embodies authority, Brother Dove imperfect learning and suspicion, Olioll contemplative innocence, and the beggar radical humility and sanctity. Descriptive epithets and small behavioural details replace psychological analysis. Olioll’s supposed stupidity is defined as a mind drawn to “every wandering sound” and “every wandering light,” subtly recasting inattentiveness as spiritual receptivity. The saint’s holiness is likewise shown through abstinence, cheerful labour, pity and communion with animals rather than through self-proclamation.

Imagery is sensuous yet symbolic. Fire signifies divine love and annihilation of the worldly self; the ruby concentrates that fire into a sacred object; snow and raggedness express poverty and mortification; roses and supernatural light mark miraculous grace. The opposition between interior warmth and exterior winter initially structures the scene, but the arrival of the beggar brings wild nature into the monastery and eventually erases the division between them. Wolves are first feared as devourers, then become peaceful companions in the saint’s presence.

The pacing moves from a densely described communal tableau into a sequence of questions, an ominous arrival, a seasonal transition and a retrospective revelation. Important events are often narrated through reported observation rather than staged directly: Brother Dove’s long account of following Olioll becomes the dramatic centre of the story. This embedded testimony delays recognition of the beggar’s identity and allows wonder to build through footprints, tears, tame animals, prayer, light and the scent of roses.

The dominant tone is reverent, tranquil and marvellous, with tension kept gentle even when the story invokes wolves, witchcraft or punishment. Its central irony is that worldly categories are repeatedly overturned: the stupid child possesses spiritual openness, the ragged beggar is a great saint, the hardest labour becomes joyful song, and divine fullness is found in “nothing.” Overall, the style is lyrical, ceremonial and symbolically unified, using archaic cadence, luminous natural imagery and the structure of a saint’s legend to explore humility, innocence, compassion and self-emptying devotion.

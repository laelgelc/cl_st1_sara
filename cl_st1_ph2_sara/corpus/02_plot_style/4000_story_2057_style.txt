The story adopts the manner of a late-Victorian comic romance, combining sentimental melodrama, supernatural suggestion, and dry social satire. Its governing tone is genial, urbane, and knowingly ironic. Although the plot involves separation, presumed death, haunting, and remorse, the narrator repeatedly prevents it from becoming solemn for long. Elevated emotions are punctured by practical explanations, domestic details, or amused commentary: youthful lovers dream of heroic sacrifices but settle on giving up smoking; a seemingly spectral reunion is exposed by a dropped meerschaum pipe; and the final crisis turns partly on the mundane need for brandy and the propriety of entering a woman’s bedroom. The humour is affectionate rather than savage, treating human vanity, immaturity, and self-deception as ridiculous but forgivable.

The narration is strongly intrusive and self-conscious. The opening anticipates objections to the tale’s improbability and discusses the writer’s inevitable tendency to “adorn and embroider” reality. Throughout, the narrator steps outside the action to qualify claims, offer generalisations, correct expectations, and address the reader as a conversational equal. Parenthetical remarks and aphoristic asides—about theologians, lovers, ghosts, women, work, dreams, and growing older—create the impression of an experienced raconteur telling a curious anecdote over a drink. This voice claims worldly common sense while taking playful liberties with omniscience. It can enter both Charles’s and Mivanway’s minds, then withdraw to judge their behaviour with amused hindsight.

The prose alternates between long, elaborately balanced sentences and short statements used for comic or emotional emphasis. Extended sentences accumulate qualifications, subordinate clauses, and parenthetical interruptions, giving the narration a leisurely, discursive rhythm. By contrast, abrupt declarations such as “That was a mistake to begin with” or “The end came one evening after dinner” impose crisp judgments and sharpen transitions. Repetition is often used structurally and humorously: successive choices are labelled “mistakes,” “the next morning” arrives too late, and each supposed apparition reenacts the same twilight encounter until the misunderstanding can no longer be sustained.

The diction mixes literary elevation with colloquial plainness. Romantic or Gothic language appears in descriptions of cliffs, twilight, dreams, spirits, and the Atlantic, while ordinary phrases such as “bad tactics,” “knock their heads together,” and “feeling more bitter” undercut the grandeur. Classical and historical allusions—Aphrodite, Hercules, dragons, Marie Antoinette—are employed less to dignify the lovers than to expose the comic disparity between their romantic imaginations and their limited domestic experience. Period-specific vocabulary and institutions, including table d’hôte lunches, civil engineering firms, Presbyterian respectability, colonial work, and meerschaum pipes, give the narrative a distinctly nineteenth-century social texture.

The setting is rendered economically but atmospherically. Cornwall supplies a liminal landscape of black rocks, white water, steep paths, gathering twilight, and suggestively named places such as the Witches’ Cauldron. The sea is personified in lyrical terms: breakers roar pompously while a smaller wave seems to laugh and listen. This creates a fairy-tale or ghost-story ambience without fully abandoning comic realism. Newcastle, by contrast, is associated with isolation, middle-aged society, dingy offices, damp weather, and the constriction of unhappy domestic life. The South African veldt broadens the scale and marks Charles’s maturation, while also making him more receptive to dreams and supernatural interpretations.

Characterisation relies on authorial summary, comic typification, and selective psychological insight rather than exhaustive realism. Charles and Mivanway initially appear as recognisable types: the poetically inclined young man and the pretty, indulged, petulant girl. Their youth is repeatedly emphasised through diminutives, exaggerated gestures, and mock-heroic language. Yet the narrator gradually grants them emotional depth. Separation turns Charles into a disciplined adult and reveals Mivanway’s love only after she believes him dead. Neither is presented as wholly guilty; their tragedy arises from inexperience, pride, bad timing, physical discomfort, hunger, loneliness, and the absence of sensible friends. This practical psychology is central to the story’s comic worldview.

Dialogue is sparse but decisive. Early marital conflict is mostly summarised, allowing the narrator to control its comic interpretation, whereas the cliffside reunion unfolds through short, ceremonious exchanges. Because each participant believes the other to be a ghost, ordinary declarations of regret acquire an awed, theatrical formality. Dramatic irony governs the entire sequence: the reader is told that both are alive while each character interprets the other through grief and superstition. Their dialogue therefore carries two meanings at once, one supernatural to the speakers and one comic to the audience.

Structurally, the story is framed as an orally transmitted local tale. It moves from a leisurely inn-side introduction into chronological biography, then narrows into a carefully staged three-night climax. Foreshadowing is clear but unobtrusive: dreams, the return to the original meeting place, the widow’s evening walk, and Charles’s smoking all prepare the resolution. The plot depends on symmetrical reversals. The lovers first meet when Mivanway rises from the sea and Charles watches unseen; years later she approaches by the same path and mistakes him for a spirit. Each believes the other dead, each seeks forgiveness, and the object once blamed for marital discord becomes the evidence that restores them to one another.

The dominant stylistic effect is a balance of pathos and bathos. Genuine feeling is allowed to emerge, but it is protected from sentimentality by jokes, qualifications, and material interruptions. The story ultimately treats romantic love as real while distrusting the theatrical ideas young people attach to it. Maturity consists not in losing sentiment but in combining it with patience, work, self-knowledge, and humour. The ending preserves this blend: the lovers’ reconciliation is emotionally satisfying, yet the final perspective belongs to the bewildered elder sister, whose concern with scandal and sanity returns the extraordinary event to the comic world of ordinary social life.

An elderly aristocratic narrator, tormented by the injustice of living comfortably on the labor of the poor, resolves to expose the social order he feels unable to escape.

He describes Alexander Volgin, a well-paid Moscow bank clerk visiting his wealthy landowning cousin. Volgin enjoys an elaborate routine sustained by numerous servants, strolls through the estate, and regards the surrounding peasants with shallow, patronizing concern. When Mitri Sudarikov, an impoverished peasant whose only horse has died, comes begging for help, Volgin gives him three roubles before writing a cheque for sixty times that amount to purchase a painting.

The narrative then follows Mitri. Burdened with five children and deprived of the horse essential to his livelihood, he receives a few roubles from Volgin and the landowner, gives the money to his wife, and immediately returns to exhausting communal farm labor. Despite their poverty and hunger, Mitri and the other peasants still share bread with an even poorer beggar.

The story contrasts the peasants’ relentless work and generosity with the privileged classes’ luxury, idleness, political chatter, and moral blindness. It ends by asking how otherwise kind and educated people can live amid such suffering without truly seeing the workers and children whose deprivation supports their lives.

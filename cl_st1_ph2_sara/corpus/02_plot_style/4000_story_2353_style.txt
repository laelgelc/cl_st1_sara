The story uses a first-person retrospective narrator who is both participant and close observer. His voice is informal, worldly and self-effacing, with the air of a yarn told among working men rather than a formally plotted literary narrative. Although he reports events plainly, he is highly attentive to gestures, faces and unspoken suffering. His occasional comments—such as wishing he did not notice so much—reveal an emotional sensitivity concealed beneath laconic humour. Mitchell serves as a complementary figure: outwardly sardonic and evasive, but quietly compassionate in action.

The diction is strongly vernacular and rooted in late nineteenth-century Australian working-class life. Colloquialisms and regional terms such as “stumped,” “graft,” “billy,” “togs,” “cocky,” “yarning,” “ratty” and “Jimmy Woodser” establish the social world of miners, rovers and itinerant labourers. Dialogue reproduces nonstandard pronunciation and grammar through forms such as “ag’in’,” “arterwards,” “knowed,” “give” and dropped initial consonants. This speech is not used merely for local colour: it conveys class, comradeship and emotional reserve, allowing characters to discuss grief in language that remains practical and unadorned.

The prose alternates between long, loosely cumulative sentences and short statements of emotional weight. The opening comic meditation expands through qualifications, repetitions and digressions, mimicking conversational storytelling. Later passages slow into detailed observation, often using parallel constructions and repeated words—“up and down, up and down” or “thinking, thinking, thinking”—to evoke mental fixation and prolonged pain. Dashes, semicolons, ellipses and interrupted dialogue reproduce hesitation, afterthought and feelings that cannot be spoken directly. Lists are also prominent, compressing a broad social history into terse losses: money, years of life, limbs, souls and loved ones.

Structurally, the narrative moves from communal comedy into private tragedy. It begins with a wry reversal of the success story: men leave home with money and return impoverished. The steerage setting then gathers together a defeated but resilient community, from which the bereaved digger gradually emerges as the central figure. The narrator imaginatively reconstructs the moment when the man received the telegram, shifts into direct conversation with him, and finally stages the painful reunion on the wharf. This progressive narrowing—from a class of unsuccessful wanderers to one man, then to his children’s faces and minute bodily reactions—intensifies the emotional effect.

Characterisation depends more on conduct and physical detail than on explicit psychological explanation. The digger’s grief appears in his quiet voice, folded arms, fixed gaze, clenched hands, working throat and sudden retreat below deck. His children are distinguished through similarly economical visual details: the eldest daughter’s strained mechanical smile, the boy’s discomfort in an ill-fitting black suit, and the little girl’s unwavering, tear-filled gaze. Mitchell’s goodness is conveyed indirectly through giving up his berth, following the digger below, carrying the baby and helping the family ashore. His final claim that he merely wanted a drink comically disguises his kindness and preserves the masculine code against open sentimentality.

The dominant tone is compassionate but restrained. Pathos is intense, especially during the arrival scene, yet the story avoids melodramatic declarations. Emotion is displaced into pauses, practical offers, jokes and acts of service. Humour repeatedly protects the characters from exposure: the Oracle’s rambling anecdotes comfort the digger, while Mitchell’s final joke deflects attention from his sympathy. This mixture of dry comedy and sorrow prevents the narrative from becoming uniformly bleak and reflects a culture in which tenderness is most acceptable when hidden beneath rough speech.

Imagery is sparse but vivid. The Western landscape is evoked through the “brassy sky,” desert plain, gloaming and brilliant star clusters, producing a sense of isolation and vast distance from home. On the ship and wharf, imagery becomes claustrophobic and temporal: the boat takes a “cruel long long time” to come alongside, extending the family’s separation even when they are physically close. Domestic and labour imagery overlap throughout. Marriage is described as a shared struggle, life keeps a man’s “nose…to the grindstone,” and grief is borne with the same endurance demanded by mining and manual work.

The central themes are loss, failed aspiration, distance, masculine stoicism and working-class solidarity. Economic hardship separates families and delays affection until it is too late; the digger’s hope of finally giving his wife comfort arrives only after her death. Against this harshness, the story presents an understated ethic of mateship. The men cannot repair his loss, but they offer money, clothes, food, a berth, conversation and practical help. The style therefore derives much of its force from contrast: rough men perform delicate acts, jokes surround grief, apparent indifference conceals close attention, and emotional truth emerges most strongly through what the characters cannot bring themselves to say.

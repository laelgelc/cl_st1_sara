The story uses a restrained, reflective realism shaped by hindsight, emotional reticence, and gradual revelation. It is narrated in the first person by an adult looking back on childhood, so the voice combines the child’s limited understanding with the adult’s sharper awareness of desire, class, guilt, and betrayal. This double perspective generates much of the story’s tension: the younger Okenwa experiences his attachment to Raphael as exhilarating but confusing, while the older narrator can recognize its intimacy and the cruelty produced by jealousy. The retrospective voice is lucid and controlled rather than openly confessional, allowing remorse to emerge through precise recollection and through attention to moments when the narrator could have acted differently.

The structure moves from the present into an extended memory. The opening portrayal of Okenwa’s elderly parents establishes aging, mortality, and the instability of identity before Raphael’s name triggers the central flashback. This transition is rendered as a sudden recovery of suppressed experience: the parents’ leisurely storytelling gives way to the “sharp awakening of memory.” The past then unfolds largely chronologically, with kung-fu practice, Apollo infection, physical closeness, jealousy, and the final lie forming a steadily tightening causal sequence. Exposition is embedded naturally in remembered scenes rather than delivered as detached background. Small incidents acquire cumulative force, and the narrative delays explicit interpretation, trusting the reader to understand the emotional stakes before the narrator fully names them.

The prose is clear, polished, and sensuous, with mostly conventional syntax varied by short emphatic sentences. Longer sentences often accumulate observations or qualifications, reproducing the movement of reflective thought, while brief statements—“But I remembered. Of course I remembered Raphael”—mark emotional pivots. Repetition is used for emphasis and moral pressure, especially in the ending’s sequence of “I could have,” which stretches the decisive silence and foregrounds the narrator’s culpability. Parallel constructions, lists, and balanced clauses give the prose a quiet rhythmic authority. Figurative language is selective and exact: the parents’ features “blend and bleed,” Raphael’s muscles run “wiry like ropes,” and admiration resembles a maize shoot growing toward light. Such images usually arise from concrete surroundings and intensify emotion without making the style ornate.

Description is built from sharply chosen domestic and bodily details. The overfurnished flat, Vicks VapoRub, Mateus rosé, Russian dolls, boiled yam, akara, Milo, Lucozade, eye drops, and the bare boys’ quarters establish place, generation, class, and household hierarchy. Objects are rarely neutral. The parents’ books make Okenwa feel displaced in his own bedroom; Raphael’s improvised nunchaku embodies creativity and companionship; the unopened medicine bottle exposes vulnerability; and the contrast between Okenwa’s well-provisioned sickroom and Raphael’s sparse quarters quietly reveals inequality. Bodily sensations are equally important, especially the burning eyes, warmth, touch, breath, and physical movement of kung fu. These details give indirect form to feelings the young narrator cannot articulate.

Dialogue is economical, socially precise, and often carries more meaning than the accompanying explanation. Nigerian English and occasional Igbo expressions—such as “Ifukwa?,” “Ndo,” and “Kedu?”—are incorporated without translation-heavy interruption, making multilingual speech feel ordinary and lived-in. Forms of address such as “Madam” and “Okenwa” register status and intimacy. The parents’ crisp questions and commands reveal their intellectual confidence and domestic authority, while Raphael’s simpler, guarded speech reflects both his position as a servant and his emotional caution. Repeated phrases such as “Watch it!” become private signals of shared enthusiasm, whereas later ordinary questions about plantain or yam painfully expose the mismatch between Okenwa’s intense expectations and Raphael’s outward detachment.

The characterization is indirect and morally layered. The parents are affectionate yet intimidating, intellectually sophisticated yet capable of class prejudice and rash judgment. Their tenderness toward their sick son is placed beside their suspicion and dismissal of Raphael, exposing a hierarchy of care without reducing them to villains. Raphael is presented through action, gesture, competence, and the narrator’s fascinated gaze rather than through extensive access to his inner life. This limitation is central to the style: because Raphael remains partly unknowable, the narrative also dramatizes Okenwa’s tendency to imagine that their bond belongs to him. Okenwa himself is rendered with unusual candor. His loneliness, longing, vanity, jealousy, and dependence are understandable, but the prose does not excuse the lie that weaponizes his parents’ power against Raphael.

A strong current of irony runs beneath the understated narration. The educated parents dismiss superstition in youth but embrace sensational stories in old age; they fail to perceive the intimacy developing inside their own household; and Apollo, treated as an unwanted contagion, becomes the means through which Okenwa experiences care, touch, and emotional awakening. Illness therefore operates both literally and symbolically. It breaches boundaries of class and propriety, creates a private space between the boys, and then becomes the source of accusation and separation. Kung fu performs a similar symbolic function: it gives Okenwa and Raphael a shared language of discipline, grace, masculinity, and imagined power, while the actual power in the household remains with Okenwa and his parents.

The emotional tone is controlled, wistful, and increasingly ominous. Humor appears in the parents’ credulity, academic competitiveness, and domestic habits, but it is edged with sadness. Nostalgia is never uncomplicated; pleasurable memories are contaminated by the adult narrator’s knowledge of their consequences. The story avoids melodramatic declarations, instead locating emotional intensity in pauses, gestures, withheld visits, and slight changes in tone. The final scene exemplifies this method. Time seems to slow between the lie and its consequences, and the narrator’s silence becomes an action in itself. The unfinished moral interval—when he could still have corrected the accusation—creates the story’s deepest sense of shame.

Overall, the style is intimate but unsentimental, culturally specific but unobtrusively contextualized, and psychologically acute without excessive analysis. Its principal techniques are retrospective first-person narration, concrete domestic detail, understated symbolism, socially revealing dialogue, controlled shifts in pace, and the gradual exposure of a buried moral wound. The result is a quiet, lucid narrative in which private desire intersects with class privilege, and in which the most devastating event arises not from dramatic malice but from a child’s brief jealousy reinforced by an unequal household’s existing structures of power.

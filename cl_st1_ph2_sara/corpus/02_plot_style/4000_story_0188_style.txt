The story is written in the mode of a nineteenth-century children’s moral tale, combining domestic realism, sentimental fiction, and Christmas fantasy. Its prose is warm, accessible, and openly didactic, designed to entertain young readers while guiding them from selfish dissatisfaction toward sympathy, gratitude, and charitable action. The moral is not concealed or left ambiguous: Effie’s transformation demonstrates that happiness comes less from receiving material gifts than from giving comfort and pleasure to others.

The narration uses a kindly, omniscient third-person voice focused closely on Effie. The narrator freely explains her moods, motives, and character, describing her as spoiled, fanciful, listless, or touched by what she sees. This voice has the affectionate authority of an adult storyteller: it occasionally generalises about children, dreams, pleasure, and proper conduct, and it interprets events for the reader rather than maintaining psychological distance. Mild humour softens the judgement, especially when Effie dramatizes her boredom, mamma dances with excitement, or Nursey reacts theatrically to the secret preparations.

Dialogue occupies a large part of the story and gives it an animated, almost theatrical quality. Effie’s speech is impulsive, emphatic, and full of childish absolutes—she is “so tired” of Christmas, wants “something new,” and imagines poor children must be happier than rich ones. Her naïve logic exposes both her privilege and her imaginative liveliness. Mamma’s responses are patient, gently corrective, and practical, while Nursey speaks in an affectionate colloquial register marked by expressions such as “my deary,” “your blessed ma,” and “my precious.” Exclamations, direct questions, repetitions, and commands create energetic exchanges and preserve the sound of oral storytelling.

The diction is generally plain but richly embellished with affectionate diminutives, festive objects, sensory details, and emotionally charged adjectives. Words such as “little,” “pretty,” “dear,” “sweet,” “merry,” “splendid,” and “happy” recur throughout, producing an intimate, child-centred atmosphere. Lists are a prominent stylistic device: toys, sweets, clothing, decorations, foods, and charitable gifts accumulate in catalogues that convey abundance and wonder. These pleasurable inventories are later contrasted with parallel lists of poverty—cold rooms, empty pockets, thin blankets, hunger, drunkenness, and neglected children. The contrast gives material objects a moral meaning: luxury is initially stale and excessive, but becomes beautiful when redistributed.

Sentence structure varies between brisk dialogue and long, flowing descriptive sentences. The fantasy passages often use cumulative coordination, rhythmic repetition, and parallel clauses to create momentum: snow falls faster, wind blows colder, and night grows darker; elsewhere fires are kindled, tables spread, clothes wrapped around shivering bodies, and sad hearts consoled. Repeated negative constructions—“no nice dinners,” “no gay trees,” “no little stockings,” “no happy sounds”—intensify the depiction of deprivation. Exclamatory punctuation and rhetorical amplification heighten excitement, particularly during the dream and the final orphanage celebration.

The story is carefully framed and symmetrical. It begins with Effie rejecting Christmas because its pleasures have become repetitive, moves into a dream that externalises her selfish wishes and teaches her the meaning of charity, and then converts that dream into a real social action. Images and details recur in transformed form: the imagined Christmas spirit becomes Effie’s costume; the “grove of Christmas trees” becomes the orphan girls’ celebration; her request for one splendid gift and one dear little gift is fulfilled through the charitable event and Katy’s humble doll. This pattern of anticipation and fulfilment gives the ending a satisfying fairy-tale inevitability.

Fantasy is presented as an extension of ordinary childhood consciousness rather than as an entirely separate supernatural realm. The narrator explicitly notes that Effie’s dream mixes together what she has thought, read, heard, and seen during the day. The dream therefore functions as both enchantment and moral psychology. Magical transformations—a cloak made from snowflakes, soup sailing to Effie’s lips, flying checks, living dolls, and multiple Santa Clauses—are playful and visually vivid, but every marvel illustrates a practical virtue such as generosity, discipline, compassion, or service.

The style is strongly intertextual and Dickensian. The opening invokes Scrooge and *A Christmas Carol*, while Tiny Tim, the Cratchits, Christmas spirits, urban poverty, supernatural instruction, and moral conversion all shape the tale. Yet the story adapts this model to a young female protagonist and a domestic sphere in which a mother turns revelation into organised philanthropy. Fairy-tale motifs—Cinderella, good fairies, magical guides, hidden preparations, costumes, and sudden spectacles—are similarly redirected toward social benevolence.

The emotional register is sentimental but controlled by humour and concrete action. Tears, kisses, wistful faces, tender children, and grateful orphans are used deliberately to awaken pity. The climax involving lame Katy and her doll is especially sentimental: the poorest child becomes the only person to give Effie a personal Christmas present, completing the heroine’s moral education through reciprocal generosity. The closing sentence extends Effie’s change across her whole life, giving the narrative the exemplary finality of a fable.

Overall, the style is affectionate, festive, visually abundant, morally explicit, and emotionally persuasive. It relies on sharp contrasts—wealth and poverty, boredom and wonder, receiving and giving, dream and reality—while using repetition, catalogues, lively dialogue, fairy-tale imagery, and sentimental pathos to make charitable conduct appear not merely dutiful but magical and joyful.

The story adopts the manner of a bawdy historical fabliau or Renaissance court anecdote, combining romantic intrigue, sexual farce, social satire, and a final mock-moral. Its setting is evoked through references to Parisian streets, courts, trades, estates, royal households, and legal offices rather than through sustained physical description. The narrative moves episodically from the arranged marriage to the failed wedding night, the bride’s life as the king’s mistress, and the husband’s humiliating attempt to reclaim her. Coincidence, disguise, bribery, substitution, and sexual deception drive the plot, giving it the structure of a comic cautionary tale.

The narrator is highly conspicuous, conversational, and knowingly amused. Rather than maintaining neutral distance, the voice addresses the audience directly, comments on motives, generalises about women, lawyers, lovers, and worldly behaviour, and occasionally claims limited knowledge for comic effect: “where from I know not, seeing I was not there.” Phrases such as “And now behold,” “our advocate,” and “you can as well imagine” create the atmosphere of an oral storyteller performing for listeners. The narrator’s stance is mock-genial and morally elastic: cruelty, coercion, adultery, corruption, and humiliation are treated less as occasions for solemn judgment than as material for wit and worldly observation.

The diction is deliberately archaic and pseudo-historical. Expressions such as “the which,” “nothing loth,” “’twas,” “wherein,” “unto death,” “behold,” and “sire” imitate an older storytelling idiom. This elevated or antiquarian vocabulary is repeatedly mixed with earthy colloquialisms, legal jargon, commercial language, and blunt bodily comedy. Love and marriage are described through the language of bargains, ownership, warfare, hunting, law, and property: the daughter is “sold,” the husband tries to take possession of “his own property,” sexual pursuit becomes a “passage of arms,” and the woman marks out the “confines of the king’s domain.” This sustained collision between lofty courtly language and material appetite is central to the humour.

The prose tends toward long, cumulative sentences joined by commas, semicolons, participial phrases, and strings of subordinate clauses. These sentences often gather momentum through lists of actions or increasingly extravagant qualifications. At moments of confrontation, however, the syntax contracts into rapid dialogue and short declarations—“Everything,” “Who?” “The real one”—which sharpen comic timing. Repetition and parallel construction intensify obsession, especially in the advocate’s lamentations and vows. The grammar and punctuation are occasionally irregular, producing the feel of an old or imperfect translation: some clauses are awkwardly attached, tense usage shifts, articles or words appear to be missing, and exclamation marks and dashes are used freely.

Dialogue is theatrical, pointed, and functional. Characters speak in compact challenges, bargains, threats, and double meanings rather than in psychologically subtle conversation. The bride’s speech is bold and absolute, announcing her refusal from the outset and repeatedly turning male claims of ownership against their speakers. The advocate’s language oscillates between legal calculation, erotic pleading, and self-deluding confidence. The king speaks through courtly compliment, while servants and courtiers use practical, conspiratorial language. Much of the comedy comes from characters stating their intentions plainly and still being misunderstood, particularly when the advocate interprets explicit rejection as flirtatious artifice.

Characterisation is caricatural and socially typed. The advocate is old, ugly, foul-smelling, avaricious, crafty in law, and foolish in love; his professional cunning fails whenever desire governs him. The bride is defined by beauty, sexual intelligence, defiance, adaptability, and control over access to her body. The father is mercenary, the king lustful, the maid opportunistic, and the courtiers transactional. Physical descriptions are exaggerated and often grotesque: the advocate has “the features of an ape,” while the woman’s body is idealised through conventional images of whiteness, firmness, flowers, jewels, and precious commodities. Psychological depth is secondary to comic function, though the husband’s obsessive misery receives enough attention to lend the farce a streak of pathos.

Figurative language is profuse, concrete, and hyperbolic. Similes compare wealth to fleas on a dog, entrapment to a fly in glue, coins to nuns going to vespers, and exhaustion to an empty pocket. Metaphors transform sex into jousting, siege, hunting, eating, commerce, and royal territorial conflict. The woman is successively a jewel, a bird, a flower, game, merchandise, and a fortified realm. These figures are rarely delicate; they are designed to be vivid, comic, and bodily. Hyperbole is equally important: fortunes are enormous, beauty is beyond price, desire is fatal, and humiliation is socially annihilating.

The humour is broad, cynical, and frequently cruel. It relies on grotesque description, sexual innuendo, anticlimax, reversals of power, mock-heroic phrasing, and the gap between what the advocate believes and what the audience knows. Bodily functions and smells puncture aristocratic or romantic pretensions, while legal and religious language is irreverently repurposed to justify lust, bribery, and deception. The maid’s claim that a plucked hair “dies and discolours” exemplifies the story’s taste for absurd sophistry: a patently ridiculous explanation succeeds because the victim is desperate to believe it.

Thematically, the story treats marriage, sex, law, and courtly favour as forms of exchange. Land, crowns, jewels, status, and bodies circulate through bargains, while supposed moral institutions—marriage, law, confession, and monarchy—offer little protection from appetite or fraud. Male authority is repeatedly undermined: the father can compel a wedding but not desire, the husband possesses legal rights but no practical power, and even the king is manipulated by his mistress. At the same time, the woman’s agency is presented through a strongly male, objectifying narrative gaze, so her resistance and ingenuity coexist with persistent commodification of her beauty and sexuality.

The pacing is brisk despite the story’s length. Scenes are linked by transitional formulas such as “Meanwhile,” “Thereupon,” “One day,” and “Now behold,” creating a chronicle-like forward movement. The central episodes—the threatened wedding-night assault, the public encounter, and the bed substitution—are expanded for suspense and comic detail, while years of courtly life are compressed into summary. The ending rapidly supplies names, later history, death, and social consequences before closing with a proverbial lesson.

Overall, the style is exuberant, archaic, digressive, and performative. It imitates an old French comic tale through ornate narration, worldly asides, bawdy metaphor, and mock morality. Its dominant effect comes from the tension between ceremonious language and sordid motives: love is commerce, marriage is litigation, erotic conquest is warfare, and the ostensible moral arrives only after the narrator has delighted in every scandalous detail.

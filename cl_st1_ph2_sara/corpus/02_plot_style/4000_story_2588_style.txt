The story adopts the form of a first-person journal or retrospective record, divided into short, chronologically advancing entries. Its narrator is an educated, socially awkward assistant professor whose cultivated language, wounded pride, and dry self-mockery strongly shape the prose. Because every event is filtered through his limited perspective, the narration remains intimate but unreliable: he records precise observations while repeatedly misinterpreting their significance. His compulsive interest in the pale guest gives the narrative a narrow, claustrophobic focus.

The diction is formal, slightly archaic, and often academic. Expressions such as “make his acquaintance,” “inveigle him into conversation,” and “I can not but believe” establish a period voice, while spellings such as “surprized” and constructions such as “if there be one” reinforce its old-fashioned character. The narrator habitually uses elaborate phrasing to describe mundane situations, producing understated comedy. His disdainful portraits of the “venerable bell-boy,” the clerk with a “Hitler mustache,” the supposed quack doctor, and the provincial townspeople reveal a mixture of fastidiousness, insecurity, and frustrated superiority.

Sentence structure is generally clear and measured, with medium-length declarative sentences interspersed with rhetorical questions, exclamations, and speculative qualifications. Repeated phrases—especially “I wonder,” “It is odd,” and references to the “pale man”—imitate the narrator’s circular thinking. Paragraphs often move from observation to conjecture and then to comic or self-pitying reflection. As the danger approaches, entries become more emphatic and fragmented, with openings such as “HE HAS done it again!” and “I KNOW now.” The capitalized entry beginnings create a serialized, diary-like rhythm and mark successive stages in the mystery.

The tone blends macabre suspense with sardonic humor. Much of the comedy arises from dramatic irony: the reader is likely to understand the pale man’s progress before the narrator does, while the narrator treats an ominous supernatural pattern as a matter of etiquette, hotel administration, and intellectual curiosity. His remarks about academic disappointment, bachelorhood, illness, and death are wryly aphoristic, giving the voice a brittle charm. This humor does not dissolve the horror; instead, it delays and sharpens it by making the narrator’s blindness both amusing and pitiable.

Structurally, the story is built around a simple numerical countdown. The pale man moves from room to room along the corridor, approaching the narrator’s room at the front of the hotel. Each relocation forms a repeated narrative beat: the narrator notices the new room, invents possible explanations, and anticipates the next move. The death of the elderly guest, the pale man’s sudden leap forward during the narrator’s collapse, and his final occupation of the adjoining room progressively clarify the pattern. The spatial arrangement of the hotel therefore functions as both setting and plot mechanism, turning the corridor into a visible measure of approaching death.

The setting is rendered through a compact Gothic vocabulary: the hotel is “dreary,” the corridor “long and drab,” its wallpaper musty, and its electric bulbs dim “as from a tomb.” The decaying building, sparse population, oppressive summer heat, and social isolation create an atmosphere of stagnation and entrapment. Mundane details—room numbers, restaurant habits, hotel staff, medical fees—anchor the supernatural premise in ordinary life. This contrast between bureaucratic realism and impossible events is central to the story’s effect.

Characterization is indirect and voice-driven. The narrator presents himself as rational and observant, but his vanity, passivity, loneliness, and tendency to rationalize emerge through his own remarks. His lifelong failure to assert himself parallels his inability to confront the pale man. The stranger, by contrast, is almost purely emblematic: tall, healthy, ivory-pale, black-eyed, silent, and marked by an ambiguous smile. His lack of dialogue or conventional biography preserves him as a personification rather than a psychologically developed character.

Dialogue is sparse and reserved for the climactic confirmation of the mystery. The exchange with the room clerk uses plain, direct speech that contrasts with the narrator’s ornate internal monologue. The clerk’s insistence that the rooms are empty abruptly removes any realistic explanation and validates the supernatural reading. Exposition is otherwise delivered through gradual inference rather than authorial explanation.

The ending compresses the established pattern into a final image. The narrator reaches calm recognition, bolts the door despite knowing it cannot protect him, and waits for the inevitable visitor. The isolated final sentence—“The door swings slowly open....”—uses present tense, ellipsis, and visual spacing to suspend the moment of death rather than depict it. Overall, the style is that of a concise supernatural conte or pulp-era weird tale: formally voiced, tightly patterned, darkly comic, and organized around a delayed but increasingly unavoidable revelation.

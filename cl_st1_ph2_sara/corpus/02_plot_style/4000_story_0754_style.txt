The story adopts the manner of an oral comic folktale, presented by a highly visible storyteller whose voice is intimate, performative, and knowingly exaggerated. The narrator repeatedly addresses an implied audience through affirmations such as “Yas, sah” and “no mistake,” creating the rhythm of a tale told aloud. The narration blends mock solemnity with playful incredulity, treating supernatural absurdities as though they were matters of common knowledge and strict logic.

Its most conspicuous feature is an eye-dialect representation of African American vernacular speech. Pronunciation is rendered through altered spelling, elision, and nonstandard grammar: “dey,” “whut,” “outen,” “gwine,” “ain’t,” “’ca’se,” and similar forms dominate both narration and dialogue. Syntax relies on habitual constructions, double negatives, omitted auxiliaries, shifted verb forms, and redundant identification, especially the recurring phrase “dat li’l’ black boy whut he name is Mose.” This dialect is not confined to individual characters but structures the whole narrative voice. It is historically marked and heavily caricatured; together with repeated racial labels and slurs, it reflects a minstrel-era literary convention and would now be regarded as racially stereotyped and offensive.

The diction combines rustic colloquialism with incongruously elaborate or misapplied Latinate vocabulary. Words such as “cogitate,” “speculate,” “perambulate,” “confabulate,” “locality,” and “unanermous” give the storytelling a mock-learned quality. Much of the comedy comes from this collision between vernacular phrasing and inflated formality, particularly when the ghosts hold a convention, pass resolutions, and conduct themselves like a civic or parliamentary body.

The prose is strongly repetitive and cumulative. Phrases, descriptions, and entire situations return with small variations: Mose repeatedly encounters ghosts, accidentally takes or steps on one of their missing body parts, apologises, and is solemnly informed that “Dey ain’t no ghosts.” Each repetition raises the scale—from one headless ghost to groups, officers, a king, and finally millions of supernatural beings. This folktale pattern produces momentum through escalation while making the central contradiction progressively more comic.

Sentence structure is predominantly additive and paratactic. Clauses are linked by repeated “an’,” “so,” “but,” and “’ca’se,” giving the prose a breathless, spoken cadence. Long chains of actions alternate with short refrain-like lines, especially during Mose’s journey. Paragraphs often function as oral beats, with sound effects or dialogue isolated for emphasis. The repeated cries of the rain-doves, owls, and wind—“Oo-oo-o-o-o,” “Whut-whoo-o-o-o,” and “You-you-o-o-o”—form an acoustic refrain that sustains the eerie atmosphere while also becoming comic through overuse and occasional reassignment.

Imagery is concrete, grotesque, and hyperbolic. Mose’s widening eyes are compared successively to butter-pats, saucers, a china plate, and the bottom of a wash-tub. Ghostly hands feel like wet rags, toadstools, lizards, or cellar wind. Numbers and dimensions are deliberately impossible: the ghosts number “about leben millium an’ a few lift over,” and one ghost swells until he is as large as a house. Such exaggeration keeps the horror cartoonish rather than psychologically realistic.

The tone balances mock horror, slapstick, and deadpan irony. The graveyards, darkness, disembodied body parts, and spectral assembly provide Gothic material, but every frightening development is undercut by literal-minded reasoning, bureaucratic behaviour, or verbal contradiction. The ghosts’ insistence that ghosts do not exist is the story’s governing joke. The narrator maintains a straight face, repeatedly certifying absurd claims as “logical” or true, which intensifies the irony.

Characterisation is broad and archetypal rather than subtle. Mose is defined chiefly by fear, politeness, literal reasoning, and the desire to accept reassurance. Supporting figures are identified through repeated tags rather than developed psychology: the schoolteacher always boards at Uncle Silas Diggs’s house, Sally Ann always lives up the road, and Zack Badget is both superstitious and eager to impress the teacher. These formulaic epithets resemble oral storytelling aids and become running jokes.

The narrative structure is circular. It begins with Mose’s terror of ghosts, sends him through a sequence of encounters that supposedly proves their nonexistence, and returns him home still uneasy. His experience changes the terms of his fear rather than eliminating it: he is no longer afraid of “ghosts whut am” but of “the ghosts whut ain’t.” The final refrain, “Jes lak white folks!”, turns the ending into a satirical aside, extending Mose’s irrational rationalisation to human behaviour more generally. The result is a comic paradox in which direct supernatural evidence becomes proof that the supernatural does not exist.

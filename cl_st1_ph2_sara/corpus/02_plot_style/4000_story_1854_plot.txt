During a voyage from England, the narrator’s ship turns south after weeks of storms and passes the tranquil, fertile Bermudas. Their beauty prompts him to investigate whether the islands’ history and legends influenced Shakespeare’s *The Tempest*.

He recounts how, in 1609, Sir George Somers’s Virginia-bound ship was crippled by a hurricane and driven toward the Bermudas, then feared as haunted “islands of devils.” The ship lodged safely between rocks, allowing everyone aboard to reach shore. The castaways discovered abundant food, a pleasant climate, and fertile land. After an attempted voyage for help ended with the relief boat disappearing, quarrels divided the survivors into rival factions. They eventually built two cedar vessels and sailed to Virginia, where they found the colony starving.

Somers returned to Bermuda to gather supplies but died from the hardships of the voyage. Although he instructed his crew to take provisions back to Virginia, they instead carried his embalmed body to England. Reports of Bermuda’s riches soon inspired a permanent colony.

Meanwhile, three sailors—Christopher Carter, Edward Waters, and Edward Chard—had remained behind and briefly ruled the islands as self-styled kings. They lived harmoniously until they discovered an immensely valuable mass of ambergris. Greed led to rivalry and threats of violence, though Carter prevented bloodshed by hiding their weapons. When Governor Richard More arrived with settlers, their rule ended, and the colonizing company claimed the ambergris.

The narrator concludes that accounts of the shipwreck, Bermuda’s reputation for enchantment, its unexpected fertility, and the three men’s comic struggle for power may have supplied Shakespeare with imagery, atmosphere, and character ideas while he was composing *The Tempest*.

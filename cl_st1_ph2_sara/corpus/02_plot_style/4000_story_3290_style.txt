The story adopts the form of an orally transmitted historical anecdote, framed by a carefully observed domestic scene. It begins almost like a stage direction, placing the reader in a Dartmoor farmhouse kitchen through precise descriptions of granite, whitewash, lime-ash flooring, peat fire, hanging bacon and window geraniums. This solid, sensuous realism gives the subsequent tale the authority of local memory. The immediate narrator remains largely unobtrusive: he listens while the farmer’s sister recounts a story inherited from her grandmother. Within that account, letters and remembered speech introduce further layers of testimony. The result is a nested narrative whose events feel less invented than preserved and retold.

The narrative voice combines literary polish with the rhythms of fireside storytelling. The storyteller repeatedly uses conversational transitions such as “I must tell you,” “as I said,” “what do you suppose,” and “you may guess,” directly acknowledging an audience and sustaining the impression of spontaneous speech. Information is sometimes delayed, qualified or repeated in the manner of recollection. Parenthetical remarks provide historical background, family context or dry commentary without seriously interrupting the forward movement. This blend of oral informality and controlled narration allows the story to be expansive while remaining clear.

Diction is strongly regional and historical. The descriptive passages use exact or old-fashioned vocabulary—“asperities,” “planching,” “ingle,” “biddick,” “high-lows,” “dimmety”—while the dialogue employs West Country grammar, contractions and phonetic forms such as “we’m,” “mun,” “crep’,” “taty-patch,” and “bein’.” Dialect is used not merely as decoration but to establish social identity, geography and intimacy. The American prisoner’s speech is lightly marked, while Nathan’s dialogue is rendered through an exaggerated written lisp. That latter portrayal, together with repeated references to “the Jew” and stereotyped emphasis on acquisitiveness, reflects period ethnic caricature and gives the comedy an element that reads as prejudicial to a modern audience.

The syntax alternates between long, cumulative descriptive sentences and short, pointed exchanges. Opening sentences gather physical details through clauses, dashes and parentheses, producing a measured antiquarian texture. During comic or dangerous action, the prose becomes more direct and sequential, with repeated conjunctions and rapid verbs: characters run, halt, shout, seize and pursue one another. Dialogue is often built around compact question-and-answer patterns, allowing the humour to emerge from understatement, misunderstanding and reversals rather than elaborate authorial explanation.

Characterisation is economical and behaviour-based. The grandmother is defined by competence, fairness and impulsive compassion; the grandfather by practical intelligence, loyalty, dry humour and an uneasy conscience; the escaped prisoner by youth, vulnerability and a passionate hunger for freedom. Even minor figures are distinguished through characteristic gestures or verbal habits: Captain Sharpland screws in his eyeglass, the sentry hides his grin, and Nathan alternates between alarm, calculation and supplication. Moral complexity is conveyed through small reactions. The grandfather enjoys his joke but feels ashamed of deceiving a decent official; the grandparents help a fugitive despite the danger; and the prisoner’s disappearance is both an apparent betrayal and an understandable act of self-preservation.

The plot is constructed as an escalating chain of comic accidents. A serious initial transgression—the grandmother’s rescue of a prisoner—creates danger, but that danger is repeatedly diverted through disguise, mistaken identity and coincidence. The grandfather pretends to be an escaped American, is arrested for his own joke and is rewarded rather than punished. Nathan is first the mistaken captor and later the victim of another visual mistake when he approaches the occupied scarecrow. These mirrored episodes give the story strong structural symmetry: both depend on someone confidently misreading an appearance, and both turn avarice or opportunism into humiliation.

Suspense and humour remain closely interwoven. The possibility of discovery is genuine, yet the narration usually treats peril with genial detachment. Comic understatement is especially prominent: hoeing stubble is described as “a new kind of agriculture,” imprisonment as a joke that has gone too far, and the soldiers’ assault as “a pleasant little revenge.” The grandfather’s literal-minded objections and deliberate sulks create deadpan comedy, while delayed explanations preserve the force of the final revelation. The scarecrow episode functions as the climactic punchline, resolving the prisoner’s disappearance, Nathan’s condition and an apparently incidental detail planted much earlier.

Pacing is leisurely but purposeful. The opening establishes place and provenance; the escape is recounted quickly; the false capture occupies the broad comic centre; and the final nocturnal episode is withheld until after Nathan’s unexplained return. Foreshadowing is conspicuous in retrospect but unobtrusive on first reading. The grandfather’s concern about the scarecrow appears to be a practical anxiety about a search party, yet it prepares the decisive encounter. Likewise, Nathan’s trade in old clothes and hats anticipates both his temptation and the prisoner’s improvised disguise.

The setting is integral to the mechanics and mood of the tale. Dartmoor is presented as open yet surveilled, with houses visible from distant tors, busy roads leading to the prison, and mist capable of turning familiar fields into disorienting spaces. The farmhouse represents warmth, food and domestic protection, while the prison and exposed moor signify confinement, pursuit and uncertainty. In the prisoner’s letter, however, the dark moor becomes a paradoxical space of freedom. His repeated cry of “Liberty! Liberty!” briefly lifts the story beyond comic anecdote and gives emotional weight to his refusal to return indoors.

The overall tone is genial, ironic and humane, though shaded by period-specific social assumptions. Violence, fear and legal danger are softened by retrospective distance and by the teller’s confidence that the episode has become family legend. The style favours anecdotal charm, local colour, narrative symmetry and comic justice. Beneath the farce lies a sympathetic concern with mercy and freedom: respectable people break the law out of compassion, official authority is more humane than expected, and the fugitive survives through improvisation. The governing aesthetic is that of a polished folk tale—rooted in a particular landscape, animated by dialect, and shaped so that every apparently casual detail contributes to the final turn.

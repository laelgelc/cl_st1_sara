The story adopts the form of a philosophical parable or moral fable, framed by an encounter in a cosmopolitan coffee-house. Its structure is carefully nested: a detached narrator introduces a religious dispute among travellers, after which the Confucian student tells an illustrative story about competing explanations of the sun. The inner tale mirrors the outer debate, allowing the final moral to emerge through analogy rather than through direct authorial argument. The narrative proceeds cumulatively, with each speaker claiming greater knowledge than the last, until a calm, comparatively enlightened figure broadens the discussion beyond local experience and sectarian certainty.

The narrative voice is omniscient, composed, and didactic. It presents characters largely as representative types—the Persian theologian, Brahmin, Jewish broker, Catholic missionary, Protestant minister, Turk, fisherman, sailor, and pilot—rather than as psychologically complex individuals. Nationality, religion, occupation, and physical condition function as shorthand for each person’s limited perspective. The narrator occasionally supplies explicit interpretations, especially concerning pride, intellectual confusion, and false certainty, but generally allows the sequence of speeches to demonstrate the argument. The final address makes the lesson unmistakable: humility, tolerance, and ethical conduct offer a higher conception of religion than dogmatic exclusivity.

Dialogue dominates the story and is formal, declamatory, and argumentative. Speakers often begin by rejecting the preceding claim—“No,” “You also are wrong,” or “What you are saying is untrue”—before setting out their own doctrine. Their speeches rely on assertions, rhetorical questions, appeals to tradition, personal experience, historical endurance, geographical reach, or institutional authority. Repetition creates a ritual rhythm: “So spoke” repeatedly closes an intervention, while parallel claims that one faith, nation, island, or explanation alone possesses the truth reinforce the satire of human self-importance. The quiet Chinaman and the wise pilot contrast with the louder disputants; silence, reflection, travel, and observation are presented as signs of wisdom.

The diction is elevated but accessible, with a nineteenth-century moral and religious register. Words such as “Deity,” “First Cause,” “bosom,” “sway,” “vain,” “nay,” and “therefore” lend the prose an archaic, scriptural gravity. Long, balanced sentences are interspersed with short emphatic declarations, especially in dialogue. Parallel constructions, catalogues, antitheses, and carefully staged rhetorical questions give the concluding passages the cadence of a sermon. Capitalisation of divine pronouns and phrases such as “the one true God” heighten the scriptural tone, while older spellings and usages—“splendor,” “cocoanut,” “every one,” and “near by”—place the prose in an older translated-literary tradition.

Description is economical and functional. Concrete details—the divan, opium, wooden idol, pipe, cocoanut shell, crutches, stick drawing circles in the sand—give the allegory physical clarity without slowing its progress. Settings are geographically expansive, moving in reference across India, Persia, China, Sumatra, Europe, Africa, and the Americas. This breadth supports the central contrast between narrow local conviction and a wider, universal viewpoint. Characters’ knowledge increases in proportion to their experience of the world: the islander sees only the island, the fisherman knows the sea, the captain knows many coasts, and the pilot understands the planetary system.

The pacing is sequential and controlled. Each new voice interrupts, corrects, or enlarges the previous account, producing an ascending ladder of perspectives. Both the outer and inner debates begin in confusion, gather force through repeated contradiction, and culminate in a speech by a restrained observer. The final movement slows into extended metaphor, comparing the world to a universal temple whose font is the ocean, whose roof is the heavens, and whose lamps are the celestial bodies. This imagery shifts the story from satire to reverent lyricism and gives the conclusion emotional as well as intellectual force.

The dominant stylistic qualities are clarity, symmetry, repetition, allegorical economy, and moral explicitness. Irony arises from the fact that nearly every disputant mistakes partial knowledge for complete truth. The story does not dismiss religion itself; instead, it criticises pride, sectarianism, literalism, and the urge to monopolise the divine. Its preferred values are intellectual humility, broad experience, compassion, mutual aid, mercy, and recognition that differing beliefs may contain partial rays of a larger truth. The closing silence provides a conventional fable-like resolution, signalling that the parable has succeeded where direct disputation failed.

The story adopts the manner of an early twentieth-century psychological and supernatural thriller, combining a realistic walking-tour setting with an increasingly uncanny sense of predestination. Its central technique is to begin in ordinary physical detail—the map, sign-post, knapsack, fading light and moorland road—and then allow small discrepancies to accumulate until the familiar landscape seems governed by hidden forces. The supernatural is presented less as an apparition than as a disturbance of perception and interpretation: names shift, impulses acquire alien authority, language arrives intuitively, and a possible future is experienced as though it were present.

The narration is in the third person but remains closely aligned with Martin’s consciousness. External description repeatedly gives way to free indirect thought, rhetorical questions and quoted fragments of inner speech, so that the reader experiences his uncertainty from within. The narrator occasionally steps back to make aphoristic or explanatory observations, as in the comment that the “short-cut snare is old as human nature,” but the dominant perspective is Martin’s increasingly unreliable awareness. His profession as an accountant shapes the characterization and imagery: he values accuracy, order and moral balance, while the uncanny experience “dislocate[s] the machinery of his just and accurate soul.” This contrast between a methodical temperament and irrational phenomena intensifies the horror.

The prose is formal, literary and period-specific, with British spellings, leisurely descriptive passages and a vocabulary of psychology, morality and spiritualism. Words such as “bewilderment,” “interpretation,” “subconsciously,” “psychic,” “interpolated” and “locus standi” give the narration an analytical cast. The story often attempts to reason through the irrational, piling abstract nouns and qualifications around experiences that resist explanation. Repetition is central: “unexplained,” “another,” “warning,” “no right,” and the conviction that events are “not really meant for him” recur like obsessive refrains. Italics, quotation marks and dashes isolate charged concepts and emphasize Martin’s effort to name what is happening.

Sentence structure varies with tension. Early sentences are measured and descriptive, often using balanced clauses and semicolons. As Martin’s confusion deepens, sentences become accumulative, filled with lists, parenthetical qualifications and repeated questions. During the attack, syntax contracts and accelerates through abrupt clauses: events happen “at once and in a heap,” blows rain down, and impressions arrive in rapid succession. Ellipses reproduce lapses in consciousness and the compression of the vision, while short standalone statements—“There were two tramps,” “They were disguised,” “These messages were warnings!”—deliver shocks with emphatic finality. The occasional awkward or archaic construction contributes to the period voice, while apparent textual blemishes such as “per-son,” “Peeping Torn” and “togoupon” look like transcription or printing errors rather than deliberate stylistic features.

Landscape functions symbolically as well as descriptively. The white road curves “like a white river running into space,” heather spreads through twilight, solitary pines appear “unexplained,” and a green field shines “like a thought of daylight.” Such similes turn the moor into a mental or psychic territory. Maps, roads, cross-roads, thresholds and wrong turnings form a sustained motif of moral and metaphysical orientation. Martin has not merely lost his geographical way; he has entered “another’s map of life.” The wireless receiver and telephone analogies modernize the supernatural premise, presenting clairvoyance as accidental interception or faulty tuning rather than magic.

The treatment of fear is gradual and somatic. Bewilderment becomes unease, then fear, then terror, with each stage registered through bodily sensations: thickened thought, weak knees, difficult breath, sweat, chattering teeth and alternating heat and ice. The imagined murder is visceral but not excessively graphic. Its strongest details—the shining object, fire entering the throat and a “thick sweet thing” pouring from the mouth—are filtered through sensory disorientation. This restraint preserves the dreamlike quality while making the violence immediate.

Dialogue is sparse and functional. Short exchanges, especially the repeated “T’ank you; much opliged,” operate as ominous signals rather than as vehicles for characterization. German words and accents provide a recurring clue, while the mistaken social identity of the well-dressed men and their tramp disguises reinforces the story’s concern with concealed meanings. The references to Germans and a possible “Teutonic invasion” reflect the espionage anxieties and national stereotypes of the period; they supply a contemporary political undertone to what is otherwise a psychic horror story.

Structurally, the narrative uses foreshadowing, repetition and delayed confirmation. The first apparent sequence culminates in murder, then abruptly resets at the sign-post and is revealed as a prophetic vision. Later details reenact elements of that vision in altered form: the German men appear undisguised, the true intended victim asks for Litacy Hill, and a tramp again requests the time. The ending confirms the prophecy through a concise report of the tourist’s murder. Its effect depends on tragic irony: Martin possesses enough knowledge to foresee the crime but not enough certainty or moral freedom to prevent it.

The governing mood is one of loneliness, dislocation and fatalism. The horror arises from the possibility that individual lives occupy overlapping but normally separate patterns, and that crossing into another person’s pattern produces both knowledge and paralysis. Martin’s ethical dilemma—whether information gained through supernatural “eavesdropping” may legitimately be used—adds moral anguish to the suspense. The final style is therefore not merely sensational; it is introspective and metaphysical, using a compact mystery plot to explore intuition, responsibility, conscience and the terrifying opacity of causation.

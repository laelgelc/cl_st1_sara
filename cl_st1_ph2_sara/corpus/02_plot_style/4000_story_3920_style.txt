The story adopts the style of a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century Western adventure tale, combining frontier realism, dry comedy, and sudden brutality. Its omniscient third-person narrator speaks with confident authority, moving freely between character description, social generalisation, practical plains knowledge, and direct explanation of tactics. The voice is conversational and knowingly ironic: Alfred is introduced through the apparent contradiction between his diminutive, apologetic manner and his extraordinary competence. Repetition—“very little and very bashful”—and comic understatement establish him as an eccentric folk hero whose outward diffidence conceals courage, decisiveness, and lethal skill.

Characterisation depends heavily on contrasts. Alfred’s blushes, soft voice, narrow shoulders, and “perpetual sidling apology” are repeatedly set against his ability to track, ride, shoot, and fight. This incongruity drives both the humour and the action. His bashfulness functions almost like a recurring comic refrain, disappearing when danger demands authority and returning at moments of social or bodily embarrassment. Miss Caldwell and Allen are initially characterised as privileged amateurs whose romantic notion of “roughing it” leaves them unable to recognise genuine danger. Their teasing of Alfred creates dramatic irony because the reader is made aware of his expertise before they appreciate it.

The prose alternates between brisk, plainly coordinated sentences and longer explanatory passages. Short declarative statements—such as “But Alfred could read the prairie like a book”—mark decisive shifts and give the narration the cadence of oral storytelling. Longer sentences accumulate qualifications, examples, and comic asides, often using semicolons, dashes, and parenthetical remarks. The narrator frequently pauses the action to explain tracking signs, frontier customs, weapons, Indigenous battle tactics, or the psychology of a mounted charge. These digressions create an impression of documentary authenticity while also controlling suspense by delaying the next event.

The diction mixes polished literary narration with colloquial frontier speech. Descriptive passages use vivid comparisons drawn from weather, animals, and landscape: bullets sound like wind through bare branches, hoofbeats resemble increasing rain on a roof, and mounted warriors divide like terns in flight. Dialogue is differentiated through dialect and social background. Alfred’s speech includes contractions and phonetic forms such as “a-goin’,” “fer,” “nor-rard,” and “we-all,” while the Eastern characters speak more conventionally. His ordinary “soft Southern burr” becomes clear and incisive in crisis, so changes in speech register signal changes in power and temperament.

Landscape is treated both lyrically and practically. The prairie can appear peaceful, empty, and almost pastoral, yet small details—a hoofprint, a blue bead, the absence of teepee-pole furrows—reveal concealed danger. This makes perception a central motif: inexperienced characters see monotony and freedom, whereas Alfred reads the land as evidence. The prairie-dog town is similarly double-coded, first as a place of harmless amusement and then as a tactical trap whose holes obstruct horses and alter the course of the fight.

The plot is tightly shaped around foreshadowing and reversal. Early references to Alfred killing men “diffidently,” his refusal to let travellers leave the train, and Miss Caldwell’s craving for solitude all prepare the later crisis. The action escalates from comic teasing to disobedience, tracking, discovery, encirclement, and apparent attack. Suspense peaks when Alfred’s defensive bluff succeeds, only to be overturned by the abrupt revelation that Miss Caldwell has already shot herself. The ending is deliberately terse and anti-sentimental. Alfred’s simple explanation, his modest adjustment of her skirt, and his three precise retaliatory shots express grief through action rather than introspection. The wagon train’s arrival in the final sentence closes the tale with stark factual economy.

The humour is dark and often based on understatement. Statements such as Alfred’s hypothetical willingness to “bashfully” kill Allen treat violence as an ordinary frontier competency, while his apology for swearing in the middle of an attack juxtaposes etiquette with mortal danger. This tonal mixture prevents Alfred from becoming a conventional swaggering gunfighter; his heroism is defined by restraint, practical intelligence, and responsibility rather than display.

The narrative also reflects the racial and gender assumptions of its period. It makes broad claims about women as comfort-seeking and psychologically constituted, depicts Miss Caldwell’s independence as naïve, and presents Indigenous people chiefly as a threatening collective. Terms such as “savages” and “Injins,” along with generalisations about Indigenous weapons, discipline, and behaviour, belong to the colonial conventions of historical Western fiction rather than a neutral perspective. These assumptions are embedded both in character speech and in the supposedly authoritative narration.

Overall, the style is that of a compact frontier yarn: anecdotal in framing, ironic in characterisation, technically detailed in action, visually animated in description, and ruthless in resolution. Its signature effect comes from pairing Alfred’s exaggerated social timidity with absolute competence under pressure, then turning an apparently successful defence into a tragic misunderstanding.

The story adopts an antiquarian Gothic-horror style, combining elaborate scene-setting, historical erudition and a gradually intensifying atmosphere of disgust. Its first-person narrator is educated, rational and socially restrained, with the sensibility of a late nineteenth-century scholar. He recounts events retrospectively and survives only through a providential catastrophe, giving the narrative the form of a testimonial whose composure is repeatedly strained by memories of the encounter.

The opening is panoramic and essay-like rather than immediately dramatic. It begins with broad claims about seekers of horror, then narrows from exotic ruins and distant countries to isolated New England farmhouses, from regional history to a particular building, and finally from that building to one room, one book, one image and one drop of blood. This funnel-shaped structure makes the horror increasingly intimate. The extended preamble also supplies a theory of place: remoteness, religious repression, inherited brutality and isolation from civilisation are presented as forces capable of preserving archaic forms of evil.

The diction is elevated, archaic and strongly evaluative. Polysyllabic words such as “copiousness,” “redolent,” “antediluvian,” “abhorrent” and “unutterable” create a learned, ceremonious register. Adjectives accumulate around objects before anything explicitly supernatural occurs: the house is “antique and repellent,” the windows are “bleared,” and the atmosphere suggests “unhallowed age” and “unpleasant crudeness.” Recurrent terms—including “ancient,” “strange,” “hideous,” “hateful,” “sinister,” “ghastly” and “horrible”—continually instruct the reader’s emotional response. Superlatives and absolutes amplify the tone, while words such as “somehow,” “seemed,” “almost” and “scarcely” preserve uncertainty during the early stages.

Sentences are often long, periodic and clause-heavy, with semicolons, balanced constructions and parenthetical qualifications. They delay their principal point while layering observation, historical interpretation and emotional reaction. Lists and parallel phrases give the prose an oratorical rhythm, as in the catalogues of haunted places and the combination of “strength, solitude, grotesqueness and ignorance.” Shorter statements appear at decisive moments—most notably “but rain is not red”—and gain force from their contrast with the surrounding verbal density. The syntax therefore contributes directly to suspense: elaborate speculation slows perception, while compressed declarations mark revelation.

The setting is animated through sustained personification. Houses “squat,” “lean,” “stare,” “blink,” conceal secrets and dream; vines crawl, shadows become shrouds, and windows appear capable of memory and madness. This makes architecture seem sentient before the inhabitant is introduced. The physical environment is correspondingly tactile and decayed: damp grass, mossy stone, falling plaster, dirty panes, rotting books, ragged clothing and a hateful odour create a sensory field of neglect. Weather functions as both atmospheric accompaniment and plot mechanism. Rain traps the narrator inside, thunder punctuates the old man’s confession, and the final lightning strike destroys the evidence while saving the narrator’s sanity.

Horror develops through foreshadowing and incremental corroboration rather than sudden action. The house looks inhabited despite its neglect; the rare book repeatedly falls open at an illustration of cannibalism; historical dates do not align; former visitors have disappeared; the host’s fascination advances from pictures to slaughter, appetite and possible life extension. Each clue can initially be rationalised, but together they form an increasingly unmistakable pattern. The climax does not show an act of violence directly. Instead, a small drop of blood falls onto the cannibalistic engraving, and the narrator follows it to a spreading stain on the ceiling. The restrained scale of the sign makes it more effective than graphic disclosure, because it compels the reader to infer the unseen body overhead.

The narrator’s language is sharply contrasted with the old man’s phonetic Yankee dialect. The narrator speaks in polished, Latinate prose; the host uses forms such as “haouse,” “Afriky,” “picters,” “calc’late” and “suthin’.” This dialect establishes regional isolation and apparent rustic simplicity, but it also makes the confession immediate and bodily. His speech begins with ingratiating hospitality, becomes rambling and feverishly enthusiastic, then sinks into an intimate whisper. Repetition, broken clauses and euphemistic phrases such as “more the same” dramatise a mind circling around forbidden desires without naming them outright. The mismatch between his weak, deferential voice and his powerful, filthy body produces further unease.

The dominant tonal movement is from cultivated curiosity to instinctive aversion, temporary reassurance, mounting disgust and finally mental collapse. The narrator repeatedly tries to discipline fear through manners, scholarship and rational explanation. He suppresses exclamations out of “good breeding,” studies the dialect, inventories the furnishings and identifies the book bibliographically. These civilised habits delay his recognition that scholarship has placed him beside something primitive and predatory. Historical specificity—including dates, editions, printers, religious works and genealogical references—gives the improbable episode a documentary texture and makes the horror appear embedded in recoverable history.

The story’s imagery repeatedly joins looking with appetite. Windows stare, the engraving looks upward, the old man gazes lovingly at mutilated bodies, and visual contemplation awakens physical craving. Books are not protective emblems of culture here; an old volume transmits and intensifies savagery. Blood finally lands on the printed representation of bloodshed, collapsing the distance between image and reality. Other central oppositions include civilisation and atavism, hospitality and predation, religious severity and concealed sin, scholarly knowledge and forbidden knowledge, apparent human longevity and cannibalistic renewal.

Overall, the style is ornate, atmospheric and cumulative. It favours indirect revelation, antiquarian detail, personified landscapes, elaborate syntax and emphatic emotional vocabulary. Its most characteristic technique is to surround a simple grotesque discovery with layers of history, decay, intuition and rational hesitation, allowing dread to gather long before the final physical evidence appears.

The story adopts the manner of a comic oral yarn, framed by a night-watchman who addresses an implied listener and uses a personal anecdote to illustrate a supposedly general truth. His opening reflections on superstition lead casually into the embedded tale, and the frame gives the narrative the air of an improvised dockside performance rather than a polished literary account. The speaker’s questionable logic, suppressed irritation with the cat, and confident moralising immediately establish him as a humorous and not entirely reliable raconteur.

The dominant linguistic feature is heavily marked working-class vernacular. Pronunciation is represented through eye dialect and dropped consonants—“’ad,” “’im,” “arter,” “ses,” “wot,” “agin,” and “fust”—along with colloquial contractions, nonstandard grammar, nautical expressions, and idioms such as “three sheets in the wind.” This phonetic spelling creates a strong speaking voice, locates the tale socially, and sustains the impression that it is being told aloud. The narration and dialogue share much of the same idiom because the entire embedded story is filtered through the watchman’s voice. Repetition, self-correction, loose coordination, and phrases such as “as I said afore” further imitate spontaneous speech.

Dialogue drives most of the action. Exchanges are brisk, plainly phrased, and built around repetition with small comic variations. Bill’s terrified “Good-by,” Silas’s increasingly elaborate ghostly instructions, and Mrs. Burtenshaw’s practical stage-management of the groans all gain force from timing rather than descriptive complexity. Speech tags are deliberately repetitive—especially “ses”—which reinforces the storyteller’s oral rhythm and keeps attention on the verbal contest between characters. Interrupted phrases, stammering, emphatic questions, and dashes reproduce fear, hesitation, and comic escalation.

The humour is dry, ironic, and frequently deadpan. Absurd or cruel details are delivered as though they were perfectly reasonable: a ghost dislodges a drowned cabin-boy from his leg, Silas is asked to vary his groans like a performer, and Bill’s alcoholism is described as the natural consequence of earning enough to support only a wife. Much of the comedy comes from incongruity between solemn supernatural language and mundane material interests. The apparent haunting begins as a scheme to reform Bill, is hijacked by Silas to obtain fifteen shillings a week, and is then reclaimed by Mrs. Burtenshaw through a second fraudulent visitation. The narrator seldom explains the joke; understatement and literal-minded phrasing allow the irony to emerge on its own.

Characterisation is economical and behavioural. Bill is drunken, credulous, selfish, and cowardly, but his fear makes him obedient. Silas is introduced as gloomy and superstitious, then revealed as opportunistic beneath his pose of charitable concern. Mrs. Burtenshaw initially appears long-suffering and vulnerable, yet proves the most adaptable and strategically intelligent character. Their motives are shown through actions, bargaining, and speech rather than psychological analysis. Recurring physical details—the bedclothes, hidden head, luminous paint, groans, poker, and weekly money order—function as comic props and make the domestic setting theatrical.

The plot is tightly constructed around reversal and escalating deception. A preliminary chain of anecdotes establishes superstition as the comic premise. The central scheme then unfolds in clear stages: Silas proposes the false haunting, Bill is difficult to wake, the performance finally succeeds, the moral warning becomes financial extortion, and Mrs. Burtenshaw discovers that her intended cure has cost her money. The final counter-trick mirrors the first haunting but reverses control. She imitates a conversation with the invisible ghost, exploits Bill’s inability to look, and redirects the weekly payment to herself. This symmetrical structure gives the ending the neatness of a practical joke or folktale.

Pacing alternates between compressed summary and extended scene. Years of drinking and marital difficulty are covered quickly, while the bedroom haunting is rendered almost moment by moment. The prolonged failure to wake Bill delays the expected confrontation and turns suspense into farce. Later, his repeated retreats beneath the bedclothes provide a visual rhythm, while Silas’s increasingly specific demands steadily reveal the true object of the deception. The second haunting is shorter because it repeats an already established pattern, allowing the conclusion to arrive sharply.

The prose uses simple syntax, concrete detail, and cumulative clauses suited to spoken narration. Descriptions are brief but vivid, particularly Silas’s luminous face, wet hair, and resonant groans. Figurative language is mostly colloquial and exaggerated—noise that “froze the marrer” in the bones, a room-shaking groan, and a warning “fifty million times worse than pink rats.” Hyperbole is treated as ordinary speech, intensifying both the narrator’s personality and the farcical atmosphere.

The thematic treatment of superstition is deliberately paradoxical. Superstition is irrational, yet it produces practical results: Bill stops drinking, becomes healthier, and supports his household, albeit under false pretences. Deception is similarly ambiguous. Every major character manipulates someone else, but the final arrangement is presented less as a moral scandal than as rough domestic justice. The closing sentence delivers the punch line by translating the supernatural plot into the everyday reward of hats and clothes. Overall, the style combines vernacular realism, oral storytelling, domestic farce, restrained satire, and a precisely engineered comic twist.

At fourteen, the narrator is given money by his aunt to attend an improving theatrical performance with his seemingly respectable friend Skegson. Instead, Skegson persuades him to visit a music hall, where they smoke cheap cigars and drink whisky. The narrator returns home late and ill, confesses to his mother, and becomes excessively remorseful, obsessing over moralistic illustrations that depict smoking, drinking, and music halls as steps toward ruin. His reform proves temporary.

Nearly three years later, he returns to a music hall under the pretense of broadening his experience as a journalist, only to encounter his uncle. Each threatens the other’s secrecy, so they agree never to mention the meeting, with the uncle paying their expenses.

The narrator then recalls an incident at a rowdy music hall where the popular comic Joss Jessop fails to appear. His replacement, Signorina Ballatino, a zithern player, is jeered by the audience, especially a coalheaver demanding Joss. The timid chairman fails to restore order, so Ballatino confronts the heckler herself and overwhelms him with an extraordinary torrent of insults. Her verbal performance wins the entire audience’s admiration and launches her career. She abandons her foreign stage name and instrument, becoming famous instead for portraying working-class coster characters.

The story uses a reflective first-person narrator who acts chiefly as witness, listener, and interpreter rather than as the central subject. His voice is intimate but philosophically assertive: he observes physical details, records other people’s confessions, and repeatedly attempts to turn individual suffering into general truths about human nature. His confidence is also questioned within the story. Whenever he offers a neat explanation, another character resists it, so the narrative creates tension between the desire to understand people and the impossibility of fully entering another life.

The structure is framed and recursive. An opening encounter with a weary psychoanalyst establishes the story’s central language and ideas; this gives way to LeRoy’s account of the Iowa woman; the closing walk with LeRoy then echoes the first encounter almost word for word. Recurrent gestures, vocal changes, and images link the two men: voices grow shrill, neck cords tighten, sneers appear, and speakers turn or walk away before returning. This circular construction makes LeRoy gradually resemble the doctor and turns the woman’s predicament into a universal pattern. The story advances less through conventional plot than through anecdote, recollection, dialogue, commentary, and the repetition of motifs.

The prose is plain, direct, and largely unornamented at the sentence level, but it carries strong emotional and symbolic weight. The vocabulary is accessible, with frequent use of elemental words such as “life,” “love,” “weariness,” “clean,” “illness,” and “desire.” Simple declarative sentences often state psychological judgments with almost biblical force. These are interspersed with longer explanatory passages that follow the movement of thought, qualifying claims and circling difficult ideas. Repetition is central to the rhythm: words, sentence patterns, and complete formulations recur until they acquire the quality of refrains. Dashes, exclamations, abrupt fragments, and doubled adjectives such as “creeping crawling” reproduce agitation and emotional pressure.

Dialogue is intense, theatrical, and confessional. Characters speak in bursts, using accusations, rhetorical questions, paradoxes, and emphatic repetitions. Their speech often moves from ordinary conversation into heightened philosophical declaration. Emotional states are conveyed through physical action as much as through exposition: trembling, staring, spitting out words, gripping shoulders, kneeling, and walking away all externalize inner conflict. The narrator frequently pauses to interpret changes in voice or gesture, giving the dialogue the quality of a psychological case study while preserving ambiguity.

Characterization is selective and expressionistic. People are introduced through a few memorable traits—a taut neck, a limp, a lean body, a wounded-bird gait—and these details become signs of psychic strain. The characters are presented as “grotesques”: not caricatures, but human beings distorted by loneliness, inherited beliefs, repression, excessive thought, or frustrated desire. The Iowa woman is viewed from several imperfect perspectives: as an object of gossip, a source of fear, a sexual enigma, and finally a person whose deeper need is patient love. The narration reflects the gender assumptions of its period, especially in its broad claims about “women’s affairs” and sexuality, yet it also exposes the cruelty and inadequacy of the men’s reductive judgments.

The dominant imagery is organic and elemental. Human lives are compared to young trees choked by old vines; inherited ideas become seeds planted by the dead; psychic burdens become crawling things covering the body and soul. Against this imagery of entanglement stands the repeated fantasy of becoming a dead leaf blown freely by wind over hills or water. Death here signifies not merely extinction but release, purification, and possible rebirth. Roads, parks, forests, hills, and the distant lake provide sparse symbolic landscapes in which the characters articulate their inward crises.

The tone combines sympathy, melancholy, unease, and passionate earnestness. Moments of irony arise when the narrator’s supposedly penetrating insights are thrown back at him, especially in the repeated phrase about being “smart” and putting things “aptly.” The story distrusts clever formulations and clinical solutions, insisting that life contains something “vague and fine” that definitive explanations miss. Its central method is therefore paradoxical: it offers sustained psychological analysis while dramatizing the limits of analysis itself.

Pacing is measured and meditative, with short scenes expanded by interpretation and philosophical reflection. The most dramatic incident—the woman’s appeal to LeRoy—is narrated plainly and then reconsidered at length, so its significance lies less in immediate action than in its aftereffects. The ending does not resolve the woman’s fate or LeRoy’s dilemma. Instead, it returns to the opening imagery of exhaustion, contamination, and the longing to be made clean. The overall style is modern psychological realism shaped by oral storytelling, symbolic repetition, and lyrical natural imagery, producing a story that feels at once anecdotal, confessional, and parabolic.

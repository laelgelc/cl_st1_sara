The story is written as a first-person retrospective anecdote, with the narrator acting both as participant and comic observer. His voice is genial, practical, and morally receptive, inviting the reader to share his sympathy for the supposed stowaway while limiting information to what he himself sees and understands. This restricted viewpoint is essential to the deception: the reader accepts the stranger’s identity largely because the narrator does. Once the disguise is discovered, the same perspective allows the narrator to reverse the trick and turn an intended humiliation back upon its author.

The prose is clear, brisk, and characteristic of late nineteenth-century popular fiction. Its diction is mostly plain and conversational, though it includes nautical terms such as “stern,” “bulwark,” “companion-way,” “state-room,” and “aft wheelhouse” to establish the shipboard setting. Concrete details—the luminous wake, folding steamer chairs, tarpaulin, damp deck, steward’s tray, and narrow passages—give the episode physical credibility and provide practical machinery for the plot. Period expressions such as “stood treat all round,” “pitch in,” “square meal,” and “work it pretty slick” lend the narration an informal historical flavour.

Dialogue dominates the story and performs several functions at once: it supplies exposition, distinguishes character, accelerates the action, and creates comedy. The stowaway speaks with emotional urgency, deference, and rhetorical force, repeatedly using “sir” while presenting poverty as a social injustice. The steward’s clipped “Yessir” establishes his role economically. Cupples speaks in the expansive, self-important manner of a lawyer, using elaborate legal phrasing and invented precedents to intimidate the narrator. His mock statute is a comic parody of legal language, inflated through exhaustive lists—“vessel, barge, brig, schooner, bark, clipper, steamship or other craft”—and ponderous qualifications. The narrator’s comparatively direct replies make Cupples’s verbosity appear even more absurd.

Sentence structure varies according to dramatic purpose. Descriptive and reflective passages use moderately long, coordinated sentences, while moments of danger or discovery shift toward short statements, interruptions, exclamations, and rapid exchanges. Dashes frequently mark hesitation, interruption, or a sudden change in thought. Repetition heightens emotion and comic effect, as in “Oh, no, no, no” and the insistence on acting “before witnesses.” The narration also uses lightly exaggerated formulations—“with much the air of a man who is stealing fowls,” for example—to keep the tone playful even while the apparent subject is hunger and persecution.

The plot is tightly constructed as a comic confidence trick followed by a counter-trick. Its opening creates atmosphere and sympathy; the middle increases pressure through the threat of discovery and Cupples’s fabricated legal warnings; the revelation in the state-room abruptly reinterprets everything that came before. After that recognition, the pace quickens into farce, with sailors surrounding the wheelhouse, chairs rattling, Cupples being seized by the collar, and his attempted escape ending in a trip. The closing disclosure that he is an actor completes the explanation and retrospectively validates his persuasive performance. His later recital of “The Stowaway” forms an ironic coda, turning the private deception into public theatrical art.

The tone blends sentiment, suspense, satire, and broad comedy. For much of the story, genuine social pathos appears to be central: the stowaway’s claim that “poverty is the great crime” evokes inequality, waste, hunger, and the harsh treatment of migrants. Yet this moral seriousness is ultimately revealed as part of an actor’s performance. The story does not entirely erase the social argument, since the speech remains emotionally plausible, but it subordinates it to comic plotting and theatrical illusion. Cupples’s legal pretensions are also satirised: his supposed expertise becomes the instrument of his embarrassment when the narrator reports him according to his own advice.

Humour arises chiefly from dramatic irony, reversal, incongruity, and deadpan understatement. The narrator’s solemn acceptance of a wildly overdrawn statute is funny in retrospect, while Cupples’s capture turns his impersonation into an unintended reality. Verbal jokes puncture the action, notably the bystander’s literal response, “You can’t both hold on and let go.” The narrator’s feigned astonishment when Cupples is unmasked is another calculated comic performance, mirroring the actor’s earlier deception. Thus acting, disguise, and role-playing operate both as subject matter and as structural principles: Cupples plays a stowaway and lawyer, the narrator plays the dupe and then the innocent informer, and the final shipboard entertainment openly reproduces the original fiction.

Overall, the style is that of a compact comic tale designed for oral readability and immediate entertainment. It relies on an accessible narrator, vivid but economical setting, strongly differentiated speech, escalating complications, and a decisive twist. Its apparent melodrama is deliberately converted into farce, while its polished symmetry—performance answered by performance—gives the anecdote a satisfying finish.

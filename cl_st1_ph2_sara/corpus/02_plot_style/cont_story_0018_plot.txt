Akunna, a young Nigerian woman, wins the American visa lottery and moves to Maine to live with a family acquaintance she calls her uncle. Although his home initially feels welcoming, she leaves after he sexually assaults her and implies that submitting to him is the price of his support.

She settles alone in a small Connecticut town, working illegally for low wages as a waitress and abandoning her plans for college. She sends money home without revealing her whereabouts or writing letters, ashamed that she cannot fulfill her family’s expectations. Isolated and alienated by Americans’ ignorance, racism, and wastefulness, she feels an invisible pressure tightening around her neck each night.

Akunna begins dating a well-traveled, affluent white university student who is knowledgeable about Africa. Their relationship eases her loneliness, but differences in race, class, privilege, and experience repeatedly divide them. He often fails to understand the humiliations and inequalities that shape her life, while she resents his freedom, wealth, and tendency to romanticize poverty. Despite these tensions and the scrutiny their interracial relationship attracts, they grow close, and her feeling of suffocation begins to subside.

Akunna finally writes to her parents and learns that her father died five months earlier. Devastated by having been absent and uninformed, she decides to return to Nigeria alone. Her boyfriend offers to pay and accompany her, then asks whether she will return to him. She gives no answer, embraces him tightly at the airport, and lets him go.

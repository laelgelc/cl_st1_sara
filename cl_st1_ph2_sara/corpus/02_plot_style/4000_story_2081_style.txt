The story uses a retrospective first-person voice that remains closely aligned with the perceptions of an unnamed boy. The narrator’s mature command of language gives shape to the child’s limited understanding without wholly dissolving it: intense emotions are recalled with clarity, but their causes often remain obscure to the experiencing self. This double perspective creates gentle irony. The boy treats his infatuation as a sacred quest, while the narrative gradually exposes its naivety, vanity, and susceptibility to fantasy.

The prose is lyrical, precise, and sensuous, combining realistic observation with symbolic and poetic elevation. Concrete details—damp books, muddy lanes, kitchen windows, a rusty bicycle-pump, a silver bracelet, ticking clocks, coins, and closing stalls—anchor the narrative in ordinary urban life. These particulars are frequently transformed by metaphor and personification. Houses “gaze” at one another, lamps lift “feeble lanterns,” the boy’s body becomes a harp, and the bazaar’s jars resemble “eastern guards.” The style therefore moves continually between material drabness and imaginative enchantment.

Diction is formal and often literary, with occasional archaic or devotional colouring. Words associated with religion—“chalice,” “prayers,” “adoration,” “soul,” “church,” and “service”—frame adolescent desire as a kind of private worship. Romantic and exotic language similarly gathers around the girl and the name “Araby,” which acquires an “Eastern enchantment.” This elevated vocabulary contrasts sharply with the mundane speech and commercial realities of the adult world. The contrast is central to the story’s effect: spiritualized longing is set against gossip, lateness, money, fatigue, flirtation, and the practical business of closing a market.

Sentence structure alternates between measured description and long, accumulating sequences. Extended sentences often carry the reader through layers of sensory experience, as in the passage through lanes, gardens, ashpits, and stables, or through the noisy market streets. Repetition and parallel phrasing create rhythm while also conveying obsession: the girl’s image recurs morning and night, and delays accumulate as the boy waits for his uncle. Shorter sentences and fragments appear at moments of emotional pressure, especially in dialogue and at the conclusion, where the prose contracts into a stark recognition.

The imagery is dominated by light and darkness. Half-open doors, lamp glow, shaded windows, dim rooms, brown houses, and the darkening bazaar establish a chiaroscuro effect. Light initially idealizes the girl by isolating her neck, hair, hand, and clothing, making her seem like an icon or vision. As the story progresses, light becomes weaker and more explicitly commercial: gaslit streets lead to coloured lamps, counted coins, extinguished lights, and final darkness. This visual pattern traces the collapse of the boy’s fantasy. Colour is restrained—brown, violet, white, silver, and yellow—and is used symbolically rather than decoratively.

The setting is rendered as enclosed, stagnant, and faintly decayed. The “blind” street, the dead priest’s house, musty rooms, curled pages, dripping gardens, and ruinous buildings produce an atmosphere of confinement. Movement is repeatedly obstructed by darkness, schoolwork, social obligations, the uncle’s delay, the slow train, and the bazaar’s closing hour. Spatial enclosure thus mirrors emotional and social frustration. Even the apparently exotic destination proves to be another dim interior governed by money and indifference.

Sound and smell contribute strongly to the atmosphere. Children’s shouts echo in silence; harness produces “music”; rain strikes the earth like needles; clocks tick; coins fall; and distant cries arrive weakened through glass. Odours rise from ashpits and stables, while the bazaar is marked by an almost ecclesiastical hush. These sensory contrasts allow environments to register as emotional states. The street can feel alive during play yet sombre at dusk, while the bazaar’s silence signals exhaustion and disenchantment.

Characterization is indirect and economical. The girl is not developed as an autonomous personality but appears through the boy’s selective gaze: a “brown figure,” moving hair, a curved neck, a bracelet, and fragments of speech. This deliberate limitation reveals more about his idealization than about her. Adults are similarly sketched through gestures and habits—the aunt’s practicality, the uncle’s forgetfulness and drinking, Mrs Mercer’s gossip, and the stallholder’s dutiful indifference. Dialogue is sparse, colloquial, and socially revealing. Its plainness punctures the narrator’s ornate inward language.

The narrative structure is a controlled movement from enclosure to anticipated escape and then to disillusionment. Early descriptive passages establish a world of paralysis; the boy’s infatuation gives that world apparent purpose; the promise to bring back a gift converts desire into a quest. Suspense is then generated through delay rather than action. The uncle’s lateness, the slow journey, and the closing stalls steadily drain the bazaar of its imagined splendour. The climax is not an external event but an epiphany: the boy suddenly sees his quest as self-deception.

The prevailing tone blends melancholy, tenderness, irony, and suppressed bitterness. The narration does not crudely mock the boy’s feelings; it preserves their intensity while revealing the disproportion between fantasy and reality. The ending becomes severe and self-condemning, with “anguish and anger” replacing enchantment. Its abruptness gives the final insight exceptional force.

Overall, the style is modernist and epiphanic: plot is slight, atmosphere and consciousness carry the narrative, symbols arise from ordinary objects, and the decisive transformation occurs within perception. Its signature qualities are restrained realism, musical prose, religious and romantic symbolism, close sensory detail, recurring darkness, and a final moment in which idealized desire is exposed by the banal pressures of commerce, class, time, and adult indifference.

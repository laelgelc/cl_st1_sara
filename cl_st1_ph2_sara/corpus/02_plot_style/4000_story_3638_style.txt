The piece adopts the form of a topical, first-person public essay rather than a conventionally plotted short story. It opens with a dateline and a report from the morning papers, giving the prose the immediacy of journalism, then expands a specific item—the alleged payment of Peace Commissioners—into a broad satirical argument about diplomatic salaries, official dress, national dignity, commerce, and the hypocrisy of “Republican Simplicity.” Its structure is digressive but controlled: each apparent tangent supplies another example or analogy, while repeated returns to the original precedent keep the argument coherent. The postscript provides a comic reversal, retrospectively suggesting that the narrator’s lofty civic concern may also conceal personal ambition.

The narrative voice is confident, conversational, and theatrically self-aware. The speaker presents himself as a patriotic common-sense observer offering “gratis suggestions,” yet repeatedly undermines his own dignity through mock modesty, exaggerated disavowals, and hints of self-interest. He addresses readers as members of a shared national community through “we,” “our,” and appeals to what “we all know,” creating intimacy while recruiting the audience into his judgment. The persona moves fluidly between sober commentator, indignant reformer, homespun philosopher, businessman, and comic performer. Claims of calmness, impartiality, or lack of personal concern are transparently contradicted by the emotional and rhetorical energy with which they are expressed.

The dominant mode is satirical persuasion. The prose makes a serious case—that American representatives abroad are underpaid, badly dressed, and commercially handicapped—but advances it through irony, ridicule, caricature, and comic exaggeration. Lofty abstractions are continually punctured by mundane objects and bodily imagery. An ambassador’s swallow-tail is compared to a night-shirt and an undertaker’s outfit; inadequate diplomacy becomes “ham and lemonade”; national parsimony produces ambassadors living in garrets, serving sardines in return for whale, and decorating a stove with the American flag. These deliberately absurd reductions expose the mismatch between America’s international ambitions and its material support for those who represent it.

The humour depends heavily on incongruity. Elevated political language is placed beside colloquial or domestic detail, as in the contrast between “the dignity of the United States of America” and “bacon and doughnuts.” Grand diplomatic ceremonies become scenes of social embarrassment, while the ambassador appears “like a mislaid Presbyterian in perdition.” The closing sequence intensifies this technique through parallel grotesque images: “a billionaire in a paper collar, a king in a breechclout, an archangel in a tin halo.” Hyperbole is frequent, but it is usually anchored in a recognizable contradiction, allowing extravagance to sharpen rather than replace the argument.

The diction ranges from formal, Latinate political vocabulary—“precedent,” “proprieties,” “dignity,” “civilisation,” “commercially”—to idiomatic American speech. Phrases such as “it butters no commercial parsnips,” “papa has to stand it,” and “the hired girl” give the essay a vernacular, democratic energy. Capitalized abstractions such as “Republican Simplicity,” “Business,” and “Great Republic” are treated almost as comic characters or official slogans. Repetition turns these phrases into ironic refrains: each recurrence further separates the ideal they advertise from the vanity, wealth, and luxury the nation actually displays.

Sentence structure is highly varied. Long cumulative sentences carry chains of examples, qualifications, and parallel clauses, building momentum toward a comic climax. Short sentences—“No—not once”—interrupt these expanses with emphatic verdicts. Semicolons, colons, dashes, parentheses, and parenthetical corrections reproduce the movements of an agile speaking voice. Dashes are especially important in the postscript, where broken sentences, false starts, and self-interruptions dramatize wounded vanity: the speaker insists that the failed appointment is “of no consequence” while visibly struggling to contain his reaction. Lists and catalogues are another signature device, often escalating from plausible evidence into comic profusion.

The argument repeatedly uses analogy and personification to translate statecraft into ordinary experience. Precedent is imagined as something that can be born, die, travel, or establish a career. Custom becomes a “petrifaction” requiring dynamite. The Republic becomes an eighteen-year-old girl who lengthens her skirts, puts up her hair, enters society, and becomes “a thundering expense.” Diplomacy is compared with salesmanship: the ambassador is a business agent, and the experienced commercial “drummer” understands that entertainment is an investment. Such analogies make abstract policy accessible while exposing public institutions to the standards of practical everyday reasoning.

The tone shifts between mock solemnity, indignation, amusement, and patriotic exhortation. Although the speaker ridicules American policy, the satire is framed as loyal criticism. Repeated references to the flag, national pride, and the representative as an embodiment of the Republic lend genuine force to the reformist case. At the same time, patriotism itself is scrutinized whenever it hardens into empty symbolism. The central tonal tension lies between sincere concern for national dignity and delight in demolishing pompous national myths.

The piece’s most characteristic rhetorical strategy is accumulation. An initial proposition is illustrated, expanded, contrasted, and restated until it becomes difficult to resist, while every new example offers another opportunity for comedy. Numerical salary comparisons provide factual ballast; hypothetical scenes make the facts vivid; rhetorical questions anticipate objections; and aphoristic statements lend authority, as in “custom is a petrifaction” and “there is no economy in ham and lemonade.” The result is prose that feels spontaneous and rambling on the surface but is carefully arranged to convert repetition into pressure.

Overall, the style is that of an expansive American comic polemic: journalistic in its occasion, essayistic in structure, oral in rhythm, and satirical in execution. Its defining qualities are a shrewd first-person persona, strategic digression, mock seriousness, vernacular wit, escalating lists, vivid incongruous comparisons, and a final self-revealing twist. Beneath the comedy lies a sustained critique of institutional stinginess, social hypocrisy, national self-deception, and the gap between republican ideals and the realities of wealth, prestige, and international power.

A narrator asks his Uncle Peter about the difference between honesty and literal truth. Peter argues that people often distort the past unintentionally, especially when recalling the books and loves of their youth; autobiography tends to portray life as it ought to have been rather than exactly as it was.

Pressed to give an honest account of his own childhood reading, Peter admits that he preferred fishing, outdoor adventure, and time with his father to any book. Yet city life and long winters left him ample time to read. He progressed from fairy tales, adventure stories, and miscellaneous religious and historical works to the novels of Scott and Dickens, gradually developing an appreciation for literary style. Poetry and Shakespeare further deepened his attachment to literature, while certain celebrated authors never appealed to him.

Peter identifies his reading of *Henry Esmond* in college as the moment when his boyish enjoyment of books became an adult love of literature. He still considers it the greatest English novel and names several other mature favorites. When the narrator teases that liking one of them means he is growing old, Peter replies that he is merely growing up.

The story uses a warm, retrospective first-person voice framed as an intimate conversation between an older woman and a younger female relative. Direct addresses such as “my dear,” references to mending and tea, and interruptions from the domestic present give the narrative the ease of an oral family anecdote. The opening question immediately introduces the central object—the green satin gown—while the closing revelation connects that object to courtship, inheritance, and family history, creating a neatly circular structure.

The narrator’s voice combines mature amusement with close sympathy for her seventeen-year-old self. She gently mocks youthful vanity and emotional extravagance without dismissing their intensity. Exclamations, rhetorical questions, self-corrections, and conversational asides reproduce spontaneous speech: “Was this a jest?” and “A perfect guy? Well, perhaps—and yet—” convey the girl’s agitation, while phrases such as “as one does suffer at seventeen” belong to the older narrator’s wiser perspective. This dual consciousness allows embarrassment to feel serious in the moment while remaining comic and proportionate in retrospect.

The diction is polished, decorous, and distinctly period-inflected. Words and references such as “courtesy,” “bombazine,” “mantua-maker,” “toilet necessaries,” and “young gentlewoman” establish an old-fashioned social world governed by manners, dress, and class expectations. The prose is nevertheless accessible because formal description is balanced by colloquial remarks, affectionate commentary, and lively hyperbole. Comparisons such as “The Sultan is on the front door-step,” “bowing like pump-handles,” and Jessop behaving “like a jack-in-the-box” add playful energy and prevent the stately setting from becoming solemn.

Sentences vary between flowing, detail-rich recollection and abrupt bursts of emotional reaction. Long sentences accumulate social and visual information through semicolons, dashes, parentheses, and lists, often imitating the movement of memory. Short fragments and questions intensify key moments: “Cry? No, I did not cry” and “Would silk or muslin be prettier?” The frequent em dashes create pauses, qualifications, and shifts between the younger self’s sensations and the older self’s interpretation. Dialogue is plentiful and strongly characterising: Miss Persis speaks in breathless excitement, Madam Le Baron in measured and gracious formulations, and the narrator alternates between youthful candour and retrospective composure.

Description is selective but sensuous. The Le Baron house is rendered through cool dimness, family portraits, silk rustling, sandalwood, damask, silver, brocade, and candlelight. These repeated textures and subdued sensory details create an atmosphere of faded aristocratic elegance. Clothing and jewellery carry much of the visual symbolism: the supposedly ridiculous green gown first embodies social humiliation, then becomes a means of imaginative identification with Madam Le Baron’s youth, and finally serves as a material legacy of affection. The chrysoprase necklace, gold pieces, slippers, fan, and tortoise-shell comb lend the tale a restrained fairy-tale quality without breaking its domestic realism.

The pacing follows a clear emotional arc. The brisk summons to the mansion and hurried preparations generate anticipation; the unveiling of the unfashionable gown produces the central crisis; the remembered injunction to “maintain a sense of proportion” creates the moral and psychological turn; and the portrait, comb, and necklace transform reluctant compliance into a graceful tribute. The party itself is deliberately compressed because its social cruelty matters less than the narrator’s successful self-command. A later summary and final revelation then convert the episode from an embarrassing ordeal into a story of generosity, inheritance, and romance.

The tone blends humour, tenderness, nostalgia, and mild moral instruction. Social satire is gentle rather than caustic: fashionable young people are capable of thoughtless ridicule, but Effie Gay’s behaviour is partly excused by the repeated observation that she “had no mother.” The narrative’s governing ethic is consideration for others, especially the ability to set vanity against kindness and see both “in proportion.” Its lesson is conveyed through action rather than explicit preaching: the heroine’s reward follows not fashionable attractiveness but tact, courage, and empathy.

Overall, the style belongs to the genteel domestic tale with elements of sentimental fiction, comedy of manners, and fairy tale. It idealises courtesy, intergenerational affection, moral poise, and the emotional continuity preserved by treasured objects. Its most characteristic effect comes from placing a small social ordeal inside an affectionate oral frame, allowing the green satin gown to appear successively absurd, beautiful, precious, and ultimately romantic.

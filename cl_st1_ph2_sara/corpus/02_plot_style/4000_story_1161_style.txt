The story adopts a flamboyant mock-historical style, combining Elizabethan adventure, literary satire, physical comedy, and a darker atmosphere of sexual threat and violence. Its narration is omniscient, conspicuously verbal, and often playfully intrusive. The narrator moves freely between immediate action, retrospective biography, antiquarian commentary, and comic judgment, treating the period with affectionate irreverence rather than strict realism. Historical London is rendered less as a documentary setting than as a theatrical, moonlit arena of alleys, overhanging houses, fog, blades, plumes, watchmen, poets, and printers.

The opening is fast, cinematic, and deliberately depersonalized. Pursuer and pursued are initially reduced to metonymic labels—“Soft Shoes” and “Flowing Boots”—so that costume, sound, and movement temporarily replace identity. Present-tense verbs such as “flashes,” “darts,” “leaps,” and “crackles” create breathless immediacy, while abrupt fragments and participial constructions imitate the rhythm of the chase. The repeated pattern of moonlight and darkness gives the action a flickering, almost silent-film quality. Space is stylized rather than mapped precisely: streets contract, houses lean inward, and darkness behaves like a physical agent, cutting off the moon or swallowing a fugitive “at a mouthful.”

The prose is highly figurative and animated by extravagant personification. Night is “hungry,” moonlight is a “pale caress,” lanes enfold the runners, and the city seems complicit in pursuit and ambush. Similes are vivid, surprising, and often comic: the hidden man becomes “a grotesque god,” a climber flaps “like a huge bird,” and an exhausted body pours onto a couch “like stuff from a suddenly inverted bottle.” Images frequently combine menace with absurdity, preventing the suspense from settling into solemn melodrama. Hyperbole serves the same purpose, as when security would supposedly require battalions, blunderbusses, and Armadas.

The diction mixes archaic color with modern wit. Period terms and constructions—“gallants,” “jerkin,” “pike-staff,” “an you give me,” “hue and cry”—establish historical flavor, but the narrative voice repeatedly introduces deliberately anachronistic idioms and attitudes. References to literary magazines, reactionary drama, “very large” printings, cultural fashions, and charges of plagiarism turn Elizabethan literary culture into a comic analogue of a modern artistic scene. This selective anachronism is central to the style: the past is made lively by allowing its poets, actors, intellectuals, and fashionable young men to behave like recognizable modern bohemians.

Sentence structure alternates sharply according to pace. Chase passages use fragments, accumulations, repetitions, and long rushing sentences threaded with movement. Interior scenes slow into fuller descriptive periods, parenthetical asides, and comic qualifications. Dashes are frequent and expressive, producing sudden shifts in thought or inserting sardonic commentary. Colons often introduce theatrical revelations, while semicolons and layered clauses allow description to swell into baroque patterns. Repetition is used rhythmically—especially in sequences of pursuit, concealment, and resumed pursuit—and parallel phrasing gives some passages the quality of staged choreography.

Dialogue is brisk, epigrammatic, and character-defining. Wessel’s speech is dry, cultivated, and defensively ironic; Soft Shoes answers with impudence, verbal agility, and self-dramatizing humor. Their exchanges depend on insults, reversals, deliberate misunderstanding, and understated punch lines. The pursuers, by contrast, speak in broken, emotionally charged phrases that reveal genuine anguish beneath their aristocratic violence. This tonal contrast complicates the comic surface: the fugitive’s charm is set against the possibility of serious wrongdoing, and Wessel’s amusement turns abruptly to moral disgust.

Characterization is largely external and caricatural, built from bodily detail, gesture, clothing, and verbal mannerism. Wessel is introduced through dim eyes, an obvious stomach, indolence, and bookish sociability; Soft Shoes through agility, an “elfish” face, irony, and almost supernatural physical control. The gallants are defined by rich dress, wounded bodies, and “furious horror.” Such figures are initially types—the reader, the rogue, the avenging brothers—but acquire ambiguity through rapid tonal shifts. Soft Shoes in particular remains unstable: comic acrobat, possible libertine, frightened fugitive, and inspired writer.

The story’s humor is urbane, satirical, and self-conscious. It mocks literary pretension, bad verse, fashionable enthusiasm, antiquarian solemnity, and the gap between artistic ideals and bodily reality. Parenthetical comments and exaggerated comparisons repeatedly puncture grandeur. Yet the satire coexists with genuine delight in books and imaginative creation. The recurring presence of *The Faerie Queene*, together with the final emergence of the opening lines of *The Rape of Lucrece*, frames literary production as something born from appetite, danger, guilt, sleeplessness, and transmutation. The final reveal is handled indirectly: the story turns the night’s questionable “entertainment” into the legendary origin of a canonical poem.

Structurally, the narrative moves from anonymous pursuit to domestic concealment, then from comic interrogation to nocturnal authorship. This progression steadily converts physical action into text. The frame-like antiquarian reference to Wessel’s tomb, the mock-biographical interlude, and the concluding quotation give the tale the form of a playful literary-origin myth. Overall, the style is energetic, ornate, ironic, and theatrical, balancing swashbuckling momentum with sophisticated literary parody and allowing comedy, menace, and artistic wonder to remain simultaneously present.

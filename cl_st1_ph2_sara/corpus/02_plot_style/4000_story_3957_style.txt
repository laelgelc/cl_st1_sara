The story is a brisk first-person comic narrative told by Reggie Pepper, an affluent, leisured bachelor whose genial self-confidence is undercut by limited insight. He presents himself as a worldly adviser and fascinated observer, yet his schemes repeatedly complicate the situations he hopes to solve. This gap between his self-image and his actual competence creates sustained dramatic irony. His direct addresses to the reader—“don’t you know,” “you must know,” “believe me”—give the narration the easy intimacy of a club anecdote told aloud.

The diction combines upper-class Edwardian slang with exuberant colloquialism. Recurrent expressions such as “old top,” “old scout,” “chappie,” “dashed,” “rummy,” “by Jove,” and “all to the velvet” establish a socially privileged, cheerfully frivolous milieu. Formal or elevated language is frequently placed beside slang for comic effect: people are “excrescences on the social system,” yet someone also “legged it”; a legal dispute involves a man being “non compos,” while Reggie imagines him going “straight into the nut factory.” The narrator favors comic euphemism, indirect phrasing, and invented idioms over plain statement, making even anxiety and disaster sound buoyant.

The prose is highly figurative and relies on extravagant, incongruous comparisons. A painting looks as though summer “had stubbed its toe on a bomb”; a silence is “chunky”; a newspaper headline “jumped out and bit”; and an embrace resembles someone charging toward soup at a railway buffet. These similes deliberately bring lofty emotions, artistic pretensions, and romantic crises down to the level of food, sport, bodily mishap, or mechanical accident. Personification and hyperbole are similarly prominent, while metaphors are often treated as if they were literal physical objects: trouble has stuffing hammered out of it, a scheme is examined from every angle, and social esteem can be cornered like a commodity.

Sentence structure alternates between rapid, clipped dialogue and longer anecdotal sentences loaded with qualifications, asides, repetitions, and comic afterthoughts. Dashes frequently mark interruption, hesitation, or abrupt changes of direction. Repetition supplies rhythm and emphasis, especially in openings and lists: “lots of money and lots of time and lots of curiosity.” Paragraphs are generally short, keeping the pace lively. Even exposition is filtered through Reggie’s commentary, so background information rarely remains neutral for long before being capped by a joke.

Dialogue is central and sharply differentiated. Reggie’s speech is loose, slangy, and reassuring; Archie’s becomes agitated, evasive, or melodramatic; Eunice initially speaks in solemn language about genius and artistic purity; and Renshaw Liggett delivers an extended, pompous legal monologue. The comedy often arises from collisions between these registers. Interruptions, unfinished sentences, pauses, and repeated “Halloa?” exchanges give conversations a staged, farcical timing. Important revelations are usually delayed until the most embarrassing possible moment.

The narrative follows a carefully escalated comic plot. An initial secret produces a lie; the lie requires an invented explanation; that explanation becomes public; and publicity summons an external authority who exposes the deception. Section breaks mark successive complications and small jumps in time. Each apparent solution contains the mechanism of the next disaster, creating a chain of reversals. The climax resolves the central conflict through a symmetrical double revelation: the supposedly pure artist secretly draws popular cartoons, while the supposedly spiritual poet secretly writes commercial advertisements. This structural mirroring turns impending marital rupture into mutual relief.

The humour is affectionate rather than cruel. Modern art, spiritual pretension, legal solemnity, advertising, marriage, journalism, finance, and class manners are all satirized, but the characters remain likable. Reggie’s casual judgments are comic because they reveal his own shallowness as much as anyone else’s absurdity. Running motifs of food, drink, money, comfort, and popular entertainment keep puncturing claims to aesthetic transcendence. The story’s governing contrast is between prestigious self-presentation and practical livelihood: “high” artists survive by producing “low” commercial work, and happiness arrives only when both partners admit the compromise.

The tone remains light even during financial ruin, marital deception, and threatened scandal. Emotional intensity is translated into physical comedy—faces lengthen, people goggle, simmer, gibber, wince, or become white about the gills. The ending preserves the narrator’s comic persona: after tactfully withdrawing from the reconciliation, Reggie closes with a mildly self-pitying aside about nobody noticing him. This final deflation is characteristic of the story’s style, which repeatedly allows sentiment to emerge and then trims it with an understated joke.

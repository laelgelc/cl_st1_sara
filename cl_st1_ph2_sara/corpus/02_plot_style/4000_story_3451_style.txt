The story adopts the style of a nineteenth-century moral animal fable blended with domestic comedy and social satire. Its forest society is an anthropomorphic miniature of human communal life: birds and beasts have marriages, professions, parishes, fashions, committees, concerts, and reputations, while retaining species-specific habits such as nesting, perching, croaking, and living among cat-tails. This interplay supplies much of the humour. The narrator states the lesson at the outset by distinguishing mere busyness from genuine industry, then demonstrates it through a chain of comic but increasingly harmful examples.

The narrative voice is omniscient, conversational, and openly judgmental. It addresses the reader directly—“you must know”—and frequently pauses to explain motives, assess conduct, or generalise about human nature. Phrases such as “to say the truth,” “I confess,” and “you may be sure” create the impression of an intimate storyteller speaking to a familiar audience. The narrator is morally authoritative but not severe: even flawed characters such as Tommy Oriole are treated with amused qualification, while Mother Magpie is condemned through irony and accumulated evidence rather than abstract denunciation.

The diction mixes plain storytelling language with period social vocabulary, including “industrious,” “parsonage,” “gratis,” “constitution,” “nervous system,” “established merit,” and “courses unbefitting his age and standing.” Formal or pretentious phrases are often placed in characters’ mouths to expose vanity and self-deception. Tommy Oriole’s claim that Nature has marked out for him a path “beyond the range of the commonplace,” for example, parodies the grand language used to excuse selfish behaviour. Mother Magpie likewise borrows the language of medicine, morality, and expertise to make her interference sound authoritative. Colloquial expressions—“A fiddle on your constitution,” “It’s enough to make a bird swear,” and “Let me alone”—keep the tone lively and theatrical.

Sentence structure alternates between long, loosely cumulative narrative sentences and brisk dialogue. The longer sentences pile up parallel phrases and lists, as in Mother Magpie’s movement “up and down, here and there,” reproducing her restless, indiscriminate activity. Dashes, semicolons, exclamations, interruptions, and emphatic capitals give the prose an oral, animated quality. Dialogue dominates the central scenes and is used as a form of characterization: Mrs. Oriole speaks timidly before becoming indignant, Tommy is self-dramatising, Dr. Bullfrog is bewildered and vulnerable, and Mother Magpie is relentlessly confident. Her speech follows a recurring pattern of unsolicited diagnosis, appeal to dubious authority, refusal to hear objections, and later denial of responsibility.

Characterization is broad, comic, and type-based. Names such as Parson Too-Whit, Tommy Oriole, Dr. Bullfrog, and Nancy Nibble combine nursery-story playfulness with species allusion. Mother Magpie is a personification of officious gossip: energetic, intrusive, certain of her own infallibility, and incapable of recognising the damage she causes. Her repeated assertions that she understands everything and never gives offence produce sustained dramatic irony, since the reader can see that she misunderstands situations and provokes precisely the outcomes she claims to prevent.

The humour rests on incongruity, caricature, verbal irony, and parody. A swinging oriole’s nest becomes the subject of fashionable medical advice; a frog is persuaded to abandon the water and practise Italian scales; and woodland disagreements are described in the language of respectable human institutions. Sound effects such as “Paddy-go-donk” and the sudden “THUMP” introduce comic vividness, though the Bullfrog episode darkens into pathos. This tonal progression is significant: the story begins with harmless bustle, moves through marital disruption and public humiliation, and ends with physical danger and lasting melancholy.

The plot is episodic and cumulative rather than tightly suspenseful. Each episode repeats the same causal pattern: Mother Magpie enters another household, magnifies an insecurity, offers unsuitable advice, creates disorder, and then disclaims responsibility. The Oriole episode presents private domestic harm; the Bullfrog episode expands the pattern into professional and social ruin. Transitions such as “But old Mother Magpie had now got a new business in hand” reinforce the sense of a roaming busybody moving from victim to victim.

The governing themes are meddling, gossip, vanity, misplaced expertise, resistance to social pressure, and the danger of abandoning one’s natural strengths to imitate fashion. The story also satirises domestic double standards, generational conflict, cultural modernisation, and communities that allow mockery to overturn established worth. Its moral is embodied in the final renaming of the protagonist as “OLD MOTHER MISCHIEF”: interference disguised as helpfulness is not merely irritating but destructive, especially when combined with certainty, gossip, and refusal to accept responsibility.

The story uses the style of a nineteenth-century moral tale for children, blending domestic realism, pastoral idyll, gentle comedy, and overt ethical instruction. Its tone is warm, sentimental, compassionate, and reassuring. Suffering is present—wounded soldiers, injured animals, bereaved mothers—but it is softened through childlike play, picturesque natural settings, and the belief that attentive love can produce practical good.

The narration is third-person omniscient but remains closely aligned with Nelly’s perceptions. The narrator moves easily between external description, access to characters’ thoughts, explanatory background, and direct moral commentary. Nelly’s innocent interpretations—seeing animals as soldiers, rebels, patients, or fairy creatures—shape much of the narrative world. At times the voice steps back to state general truths, as in the maxim-like observation that love makes friends. This creates an affectionate, authoritative storyteller who both delights the child reader and guides that reader toward the intended lesson.

The diction is accessible but distinctly period-inflected. Domestic and rural terms such as “mamma,” “lint-picking,” “summer-house,” “rushes,” and “watering-cans” coexist with Civil War vocabulary including “ambulance,” “contrabands,” “Sanitary Commission,” “rebel,” “wards,” and “surgeon.” Nelly’s shortened or mispronounced words, such as “amb’lance” and “venti-some-thing,” characterize her age without making her foolish. Tony’s speech is plainer and more colloquial, marked by contractions and practical phrasing, while the mother speaks with composed, instructive precision. The contrast among these registers establishes character and social position gently rather than critically.

Sentence structure alternates between brisk dialogue and long, flowing descriptive or explanatory sentences. The longer sentences often accumulate details through lists, coordinated clauses, and semicolons, producing an abundant, storybook rhythm. Catalogues of creatures, plants, tools, foods, and miniature medical supplies create a sense of fullness and imaginative play. Repetition is used for emphasis and cadence, especially in parallel constructions such as the description of Nelly’s daily routine. The dialogue is frequent, naturalistic within the period style, and often carries exposition, humor, and moral debate.

Nature is richly personified and presented as responsive to human kindness. Flowers kiss Nelly, roses peer through windows, the wind has fragrant breath, and the sun draws rosy curtains before saying good night. Birds, insects, and animals are likewise anthropomorphized, though enough naturalistic detail is retained to make their care educational. This combination gives the setting a mild fairy-tale quality while supporting the story’s central claim that all living things belong to one moral community. Color imagery—golden buttercups, green rushes, white doves, emerald beetles, rosy sunlight—contributes to the bright, pastoral atmosphere.

Humor is affectionate and visual. It arises from incongruous miniaturization and imitation: butterflies tucked into beds, worms lost in bedclothes, a turtle named “Commodore Waddle,” and an invalid butterfly carried by spiders. Nelly’s seriousness makes these images amusing, but the narration avoids ridiculing her compassion. Adults and older children may smile at her schemes, yet the plot repeatedly validates her intentions. Comedy therefore supports, rather than undermines, the moral purpose.

The story is episodic and cumulative. It begins with Nelly’s proposal, proceeds through the preparation of the hospital, follows her collection and treatment of individual patients, broadens to show the project’s influence on the neighborhood, and ends with Will’s farewell gift and the motto. Each episode presents a small ethical or practical challenge: pity must be joined with knowledge; natural enemies still require care; even an unattractive or frightening creature deserves mercy; responsibility transforms play into useful work. The ending gathers these lessons into a formal statement through the quotation from Coleridge’s “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner.”

Characterization is idealized but functional. Nelly embodies tenderness, imagination, diligence, and moral growth. Her mother represents wise encouragement tempered by practicality; Tony supplies rustic knowledge and unobtrusive kindness; Will links the child’s miniature hospital to the national experience of war. Will’s recovery from listlessness gives the project a second curative function: Nelly not only helps animals but restores purpose and cheer to her wounded brother. Secondary characters are sketched through a few defining traits rather than developed psychologically.

The moral framework is explicitly Christian and humanitarian. Compassion is treated as a duty extending beyond personal preference, even toward creatures associated with fear or enmity. Biblical language and concepts—charity, the Samaritan, loving one’s enemies, God’s care for creation—appear alongside civic ideals of service associated with wartime nursing. The story’s didacticism is direct, but it is embedded in action, dialogue, humor, and emotionally rewarding consequences rather than delivered only as abstract sermonizing.

For imitation, the key features are a benevolent omniscient narrator; close alignment with an earnest child protagonist; frequent dialogue; period domestic and rural vocabulary; lush personification of nature; detailed lists of small objects and creatures; gentle comic incongruity; a plot built from successive acts of care; and a concluding moral crystallized in a memorable maxim or literary quotation.

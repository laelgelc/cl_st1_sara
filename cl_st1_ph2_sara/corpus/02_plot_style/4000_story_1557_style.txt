The story adopts a comic, romantic Western style, combining frontier realism with urbane narrative wit. Its setting is established through recognisable cattle-country details—ranches, corrals, trail drives, branding irons, mesquite grass, chaparral, revolvers and Stetsons—but these are repeatedly reframed through the language of monarchy, card games and mock-heroic ceremony. The Nopalito becomes a “kingdom,” its owner a “cattle-queen,” its workers subjects and vassals, and Webb a disgruntled “prince-consort.” This sustained analogy gives the story its central stylistic unity while playfully elevating a domestic quarrel into a crisis of royal succession.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, with a conspicuous, personable narrator who frequently comments on events, addresses the reader and withholds information for comic or sentimental effect. Asides such as “but this is not Baldy’s story,” “Wonder, if you will,” and “waste not your pity” make the telling feel like an oral performance by a sophisticated raconteur. The narrator moves freely between close observation, aphorism, parody and direct audience manipulation. Emotional facts are sometimes concealed behind a theatrical “veil,” allowing actions and symbols to reveal what characters will not openly confess.

A major feature is the contrast between the narrator’s learned, elaborate vocabulary and the characters’ colloquial frontier speech. The narrative voice uses Latinate and mock-scholarly expressions such as “tonsorial recourse,” “ex consuetudine,” “hebdomadal squads” and “auricular nerves,” often to describe simple or comic actions. By contrast, the cowboys speak in contractions, nonstandard grammar and ranch idiom: “If I was you,” “we knowed it,” “I seen,” and “don’t amount to a knot in a stake rope.” Baldy’s misuse of elevated terms—“interregnum,” “habeus corpus,” “pseudonym”—creates malapropistic humour and shows frontier culture comically appropriating the language of European rank and formal learning.

The dialogue is vigorous, character-defining and often organised as comic repartee. Speakers express emotion indirectly through metaphors drawn from cards, livestock and ranch work. Webb’s subordinate status is described as being a “two-spot,” an “eight-spot in a queen-high flush,” or a prince-consort who “don’t take no tricks.” Baldy’s advice that Webb should “be king” becomes a refrain that drives the plot. Exchanges are typically concise, but longer anecdotal speeches reproduce the loose rhythms of oral storytelling and embed earlier events inside the present narrative.

Syntax alternates between brisk action sentences and ornate, balanced periods. Physical confrontations and riding scenes are rendered through rapid verbs—characters gallop, plunge, dash, stumble and speed—while transitions and narrator commentary often expand into ceremonious lists or extended conceits. The passage of time, for example, is extravagantly personified as days, weeks and months forming military units under banners marked “Tempus fugit.” Such rhetorical excess is deliberately disproportionate, generating a mock-epic tone.

Figurative language is abundant and eclectic. Similes draw on frontier life, animals, scripture, music, warfare and European court culture. Talk “gushed” like water struck by Aaron’s rod; a rider’s departure sounds like a rising covey of quail; Santa moves “like a panther” and later “like a lapwing.” Characters are compared to coyotes, turtle-doves and other animals, while cattle are assigned aristocratic consciousness. Personification and hyperbole enliven the landscape and keep even descriptive passages playful. Biblical and classical allusions coexist with slang, reinforcing the mixture of high and low registers.

The humour is affectionate rather than cruel. It arises from verbal incongruity, exaggerated ceremony, drunken logic, cowboy solidarity and the narrator’s deadpan qualifications. Even minor figures receive comic treatment: the sheep-man is dismissed as “of little account,” and an exhausted horse is mock-solemnly defended from the reader’s pity. Yet the comedy gradually admits genuine tenderness. Pride, marital hurt and longing remain understated until Santa’s secret midnight branding and Webb’s return, where brief dialogue carries more emotional weight than explicit psychological explanation.

Structurally, the story is tightly patterned around repetition, reversal and delayed revelation. An opening conversation establishes the king-and-consort motif; a public dispute over the destination of cattle causes the separation; a formal business transaction creates the means of reconciliation; and the final return repeats the earlier question about where the beeves should be sent. The repeated phrase “You hear your boss” changes meaning after the infant is proclaimed king. The heart-and-cross emblem is introduced in recollection, disappears into the plot, and returns as the decisive symbolic message branded onto the white heifer. The closing question leaves the emblem unexplained within the characters’ world but fully legible to the reader.

The emotional method is therefore symbolic and indirect. Santa and Webb are proud, competent figures who cannot readily verbalise vulnerability. Their reconciliation is accomplished through coded signs, practical ranch actions and comic titles rather than confession. The heart enclosing a cross condenses the story’s romantic idea of love joined with suffering, while the newborn “king” resolves the struggle for authority by transforming marriage from a contest for sovereignty into a family hierarchy treated with affectionate absurdity.

Overall, the style is marked by a polished storyteller’s voice, dialect-rich dialogue, sustained comic conceits, ironic erudition, vivid Western detail and a sentimental core carefully protected by humour. Its characteristic movement is from farce toward feeling: elevated language makes ranch life comically royal, but the same metaphorical system ultimately gives dignity to reconciliation, devotion and domestic love.

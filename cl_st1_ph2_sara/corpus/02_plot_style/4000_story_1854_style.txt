The story adopts the style of a nineteenth-century literary-historical essay, combining travel sketch, antiquarian research, maritime adventure, and lightly comic anecdote. Rather than presenting a wholly invented plot, it begins with the narrator’s personal experience of sailing near Bermuda and uses that picturesque encounter as a frame for an inquiry into possible historical sources for Shakespeare’s *The Tempest*. The resulting voice is at once scholarly and imaginative: documentary evidence is quoted and weighed, but history is repeatedly animated through scenic reconstruction, poetic association, and narrative speculation.

The narration is first-person and retrospective, delivered by a cultivated, companionable essayist. The narrator openly records his impressions—“I was reminded,” “I could hardly realize,” “I think”—and presents his thesis modestly as a “surmise” rather than a definitive discovery. He addresses the reader as an intelligent participant who will ultimately “determine” the strength of the argument. This creates a conversational form of scholarship, less systematic than academic criticism and more concerned with suggestive correspondences, atmosphere, and imaginative probability.

The prose is elevated, expansive, and strongly periodic. Sentences are often long and syntactically layered, with subordinate clauses, balanced phrases, and cumulative lists. Semicolons frequently organize sequences of action or description, while colons introduce explanations, examples, and quoted evidence. The diction alternates between formal Latinate terms—“misprision,” “self-gratulation,” “assiduity,” “consternation,” “appurtenances”—and vivid nautical vocabulary such as “aft,” “forecastle,” “hold,” “rigging,” and “foundering wreck.” Archaic spellings and expressions appear mainly within historical quotations, lending documentary texture and evoking the early seventeenth century.

The opening sea passage is written in a lush Romantic-picturesque mode. It proceeds through a dramatic contrast between northern turbulence and southern serenity: the “dark and angry sea” gives way to calm water, warm air, moonlight, dolphins, nautiluses, flying fish, music, and softly swelling sails. Personification and enchantment imagery transform the natural world into something theatrical and supernatural. The ship makes a “whispering way,” sounds are “charmed to silence,” and moonlight spreads a “pale, mysterious light.” Such descriptions favor visual richness, musical cadence, and sensory abundance, preparing the connection with Shakespearean romance before the historical argument has been stated.

Quotation is central to the style. The narrative is framed and punctuated by passages from early pamphlets, Shakespeare, Fletcher, Waller, and other literary or historical sources. These excerpts serve several functions: they authenticate the account, reproduce period language, heighten atmosphere, and allow the narrator to construct his argument through verbal echoes. Shakespearean phrases such as “sea change” and the “still vexed Bermoothes” are woven into the narrator’s own prose, so that literary allusion becomes part of the narrative texture rather than remaining external evidence. Footnotes and named pamphlets reinforce the antiquarian pose, though the method remains interpretive and associative rather than rigorously analytical.

The historical sections use a more direct chronicle style while retaining dramatic color. Dates, names, ships, companies, and legal arrangements are carefully supplied, but crucial events are staged as scenes: sailors stand waist-deep in the flooded hold, drink farewell to one another, hear the sudden cry of “land,” and rush on deck “in a frenzy of joy.” Sir George Somers is characterized in heroic and elegiac terms as gallant, generous, dutiful, and physically steadfast. His death is treated with solemn pathos, while the crew’s decision to carry his body to England instead of obeying his final command is judged through restrained moral irony.

The interpolated episode “The Three Kings of Bermuda” shifts into a more comic, satirical register. Its three criminals are mock-heroically described as monarchs, potentates, and rulers of a “tripartite sovereignty,” although they are merely isolated fugitives without subjects. Their discovery of ambergris turns an island idyll into a miniature political history of greed, rivalry, civil war, and dispossession. Grand political language is deliberately applied to petty quarrels, creating bathos and exposing the corrupting effect of wealth. The episode has the structure of a moral fable: scarcity shared in common produces harmony, while valuable property produces envy, ambition, and violence.

Characterization is economical and largely archetypal. Somers embodies patriotic duty and maritime courage; his nephew and crew illustrate grief compromised by misguided ceremony; the three fugitives represent appetite, vanity, and covetousness. Individuals are defined less by psychological depth than by conduct, social role, and moral implication. Dialogue is sparse and usually appears as quoted exclamation or Shakespearean parallel rather than sustained dramatic exchange. The narrator’s own interpretive commentary therefore remains the dominant voice.

The pacing is broad and episodic. It begins slowly with sensuous travel description, accelerates through shipwreck and survival, pauses for ecological and climatic detail, then moves through dissension, shipbuilding, voyage, death, and colonization. The ambergris episode forms a compact comic narrative inside the larger historical account. The conclusion becomes explicitly argumentative, revisiting earlier details and aligning them with elements of *The Tempest*: the islands’ reputation for enchantment, their unexpected fertility, the fantasy of a natural commonwealth, and the three would-be rulers. Repetition here is purposeful, converting narrative material into literary evidence.

The prevailing tones are wonder, nostalgia, cultivated curiosity, moral reflection, and genial irony. The narrator admires natural beauty and heroic endurance, delights in old chronicles and poetic correspondences, and treats human folly with amused rather than savage satire. A recurrent structural and thematic device is reversal: winter becomes summer, a feared inferno becomes paradise, shipwreck becomes rescue, abundance gives rise to dissension, criminals become “kings,” and treasure destroys the happiness that poverty permitted. Throughout, Bermuda occupies a liminal space between geography and imagination, history and romance, civilized ambition and pastoral escape.

To reproduce the style, the writing should employ a reflective first-person essayist, elaborate but controlled sentences, rich maritime and natural imagery, formal diction, historical specificity, embedded literary quotations, and occasional archaic flavor. Description should readily pass into allusion, factual narration into moral commentary, and sober history into gentle mock-heroic comedy. The essential effect is that of an urbane antiquarian storyteller who finds poetry latent in documentary history and treats landscape as the meeting place of fact, legend, and literary creation.

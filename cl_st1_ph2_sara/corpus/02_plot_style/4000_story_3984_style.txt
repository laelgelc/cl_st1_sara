The story is a light comic narrative built around an elaborate anecdote-within-a-frame. It opens in a clubhouse, where a disillusioned young golfer challenges the venerable Oldest Member to prove that golf has practical value. The Oldest Member’s tale supplies the main narrative, and the return to the clubhouse delivers the final joke: the listener, converted before the moral is even finished, rushes to reclaim his clubs. This circular structure gives the story the air of a humorous parable, with the Oldest Member functioning as both sage and club raconteur.

The narrative voice is urbane, conversational, and conspicuously intrusive. Although the narrator tells the story with mock gravity, he frequently pauses to comment, qualify, generalize, or address the reader directly: “I will skip lightly,” “I do not know if you have had any experience,” and “One could moralize on this at considerable length, but better not, perhaps.” These interventions create intimacy while parodying the stately habits of traditional storytelling. Elevated formulations—“the incidents occurred which I am about to relate,” “the feast of reason and flow of soul,” “the tender emotion”—are repeatedly undercut by slang, sporting language, or blunt colloquialisms. The characteristic tonal movement is from ceremonious dignity to comic bathos.

The humour depends heavily on incongruity. Golf is treated with the solemnity of religion, philosophy, and heroic endeavour, while supposedly serious literary culture is exposed as pretentious, faddish, and socially competitive. The rivalry between “the Golfers and the Cultured” becomes a “Schism”; a golf ball interrupts a lecture; a novelist hides in a coal cellar; and a revered Russian intellectual turns out to care more about famous golfers than about literature. Mundane or ridiculous events are narrated in inflated language, while grave subjects such as revolution, assassination, artistic reputation, and romantic anguish are reduced to breezy jokes. Recurrent comic comparisons sharpen this effect: Adeline stands out “like a jewel in a pile of coke,” Cuthbert hides like a piece of furniture or a sardine, and the excluded intellectuals resemble “cats at a dog-show.”

The diction mixes formal literary vocabulary, biblical and classical allusion, contemporary slang, commercial jargon, and specialist golfing terminology. Phrases such as “summum bonum,” “pity and terror,” and “the stone which the builders had rejected” coexist with “golly,” “guff,” “hot potato,” “sell them a pup,” and “pretty nifty tomb.” Golf words such as “foozled,” “stymied,” “niblick,” “mashie,” and “approach-putts” are used both literally and metaphorically, making the sport the story’s governing idiom. Financial and market language also recurs in the collapse of Raymond’s reputation: his “stock” falls, there is a “rapid slump,” and his metaphorical preferred shares end up “in the cellar with no takers.” Such lexical collisions are central to the comic style.

Sentences are generally fluent, expansive, and carefully balanced, often accumulating details until the final phrase overturns the apparent seriousness. Long periodic sentences support descriptive exaggeration and narrative digression, while short statements deliver punch lines: “Dead?”; “Sovietski no good!”; “And is that good?” Parentheses, dashes, lists, and qualifying clauses reproduce the rhythms of an accomplished oral storyteller. Parallel constructions are frequently used for escalation, as when Adeline looks at Raymond while he is speaking, while he is not speaking, and while sitting beside him. Repetition similarly drives jokes forward: Brusiloff’s successive denunciations of Sovietski and Nastikoff turn literary judgment into a ritual of comic demolition.

Dialogue is brisk, adversarial, and rich in reversals. Characters often speak past one another because they inhabit incompatible value systems: Cuthbert interprets achievement through golf, Adeline through celebrity and Culture, and the literary circle through fashionable critical opinion. Their exchanges rely on literal-minded replies, abrupt deflations, and mismatched registers. Cuthbert’s plain slang punctures Adeline’s lofty abstractions, while Brusiloff’s fractured English gives him a bluntness that destroys the room’s elaborate pretence. His speech is rendered through comic phonetic spelling, malformed grammar, invented Russianized words such as “pipovitch,” “gameovitch,” and “niblicksky,” and sudden borrowings from English slang. The accent is highly caricatured rather than naturalistic, serving rhythm and farce.

Characterization is economical and typological. The Oldest Member is the serene guardian of golfing wisdom; Cuthbert is the decent, literal, athletically gifted lover; Adeline is susceptible to reflected prestige; Raymond Parsloe Devine is a self-important literary poseur; Mrs. Smethurst is an energetic apostle of suburban Culture; and Brusiloff is the unpredictable celebrity whose verdict reorganizes the social hierarchy. Characters are defined less through psychological depth than through habits of speech, social ambition, and comic obsession. Even emotion is treated playfully: Cuthbert’s love improves his score, literary lectures weaken his golf swing, and jealousy causes his waistcoats to require alteration.

The setting is a satirical version of prosperous English suburbia. The catalogue of amenities—gravel soil, drainage, electric light, baths, and company water—mimics promotional property prose and establishes a materially comfortable community anxious to acquire spiritual distinction. “Culture” is capitalized and personified as a social project rather than represented as genuine intellectual engagement. The literary society’s members defer to reputations, foreignness, critical fashion, and celebrity endorsement; their opinions change instantly when a greater authority speaks. Golf culture is also exaggerated, but it is treated more affectionately. It provides genuine expertise, fellowship, pleasure, and ultimately romantic success.

The plot follows a farcical pattern of humiliation followed by spectacular reversal. Cuthbert enters the literary world as a despised outsider and endures escalating discomfort while Raymond dominates it. Brusiloff’s arrival first destroys Raymond’s prestige, then unexpectedly reveals Cuthbert to be the only person in the room with knowledge the celebrity values. The rejected “nonentity” becomes the gathering’s central figure, and Adeline’s affections turn with almost mechanical speed. Coincidence is embraced rather than concealed; plausibility matters less than the elegance and comic satisfaction of the reversal.

Overall, the style is polished, buoyant, hyperbolic, and affectionate in its satire. It combines club-story narration, romantic farce, social comedy, and parody of high-cultural seriousness. Its signature technique is to narrate trivial passions as epic matters and supposedly profound matters as social absurdities. Beneath the effortless surface lies tightly controlled comic engineering: motifs recur, status shifts are carefully staged, dialogue prepares later payoffs, and nearly every descriptive passage bends toward a punch line.

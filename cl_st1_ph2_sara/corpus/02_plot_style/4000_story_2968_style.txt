The story combines Edwardian social comedy with supernatural horror, treating both in a polished, understated, and persistently ironic manner. Its central stylistic effect comes from the collision between an orderly, self-satisfied rural world and a predatory force drawn from folklore. Respectable institutions—country houses, parish councils, Sunday schools, newspapers, and church memorials—are unable to comprehend the danger and instead absorb it into reassuring social narratives. The fantastic is therefore presented not only as frightening but as a means of exposing complacency, vanity, and conventional sentiment.

The narration is third-person and largely focalised through Van Cheele. This perspective keeps the reader close to his limited reasoning while allowing the narrator to reveal its inadequacy. Van Cheele regards himself as observant and authoritative, yet his “naturalism” chiefly supplies him with conversational material, and he repeatedly translates unmistakable warnings into manageable social problems such as trespass, poaching, liability, and improper behaviour. The narrator’s dry intrusions sharpen this characterization, often qualifying Van Cheele’s assumptions with mockingly precise explanations. Much of the humour depends on the gap between what Van Cheele believes he is doing and what the reader can see him failing to understand.

The prose is urbane, controlled, and economical. Sentences are generally balanced and lucid, but they often contain delayed qualifications or understated reversals that deliver the joke at the end. Parenthetical remarks are used as miniature satirical asides, as when the narration questions the appropriateness of “talking through your hat” to a naked boy. The vocabulary is formal without becoming ornate, mixing genteel social language—“communicative,” “customary,” “suitable,” “decided”—with animalistic and ominous terms such as “tigerish,” “snarl,” “fangs,” and “flesh.” This lexical contrast reproduces the story’s larger opposition between civilized surfaces and feral appetite.

Dialogue is brisk, pointed, and structurally important. Exchanges consist largely of short questions, evasions, and literal-minded replies. Gabriel-Ernest speaks with calm confidence, answering ordinary inquiries in ways that are both candid and incredible. His composed manner makes his threats more disturbing, while Van Cheele’s increasingly irritated questioning reveals his dependence on familiar categories. The boy’s literal response to being ordered out of the woods—appearing in the house instead—illustrates the story’s fondness for maliciously exact interpretations. Miss Van Cheele’s dialogue supplies another kind of comedy: her charitable clichés and practical concern with clothing prevent her from recognising what is directly before her.

Characterization is compressed and caricatural but highly efficient. Van Cheele is defined by loquacity, social importance, superficial knowledge, and belated understanding. His aunt embodies indiscriminate benevolence and sentimental propriety. Cunningham, the artist, functions as the reluctant witness whose imaginative perception allows him to see what conventional minds dismiss. Gabriel-Ernest is characterized through physical imagery, animal movement, laconic speech, and an unsettling mixture of beauty and menace. His languor in sunlight, “tigerish” eyes, low laugh, and sudden speed make him seem simultaneously like an attractive adolescent, a pagan faun, and a carnivore.

The descriptive writing is selective and sensuous. Natural settings are rendered through colour, texture, posture, and changing light: wet limbs, smooth stone, weed-grown banks, orange and pink sunset, and the sudden arrival of cold grey. Sunset functions as both atmospheric motif and plot mechanism. The draining of colour marks the transition from human form to wolf and from comic uncertainty to immediate danger. Pagan imagery also contributes to the story’s atmosphere, placing Gabriel-Ernest outside Christian and domestic order even before his nature is confirmed.

The pacing moves from conversational hint to uncanny encounter, domestic farce, explicit revelation, and abrupt tragedy. Early warnings are planted casually and then repeated with increasing significance: the wild beast, the missing child, hunting on four feet, the fear of dogs, and the importance of nightfall. Suspense arises through dramatic irony, because the reader is encouraged to accept the supernatural explanation before Van Cheele does. Once Cunningham confirms the transformation, the prose accelerates into urgent movement, shortening the distance between recognition and disaster. The climactic event itself remains offstage; a scream, discarded clothes, and absence are enough. This restraint makes the implication more chilling.

The ending returns sharply to satire. The community converts a probable act of predation into a story of heroic self-sacrifice, commemorating the werewolf as a virtuous child. The memorial brass is a final emblem of society’s ability to misunderstand reality in a flattering and ceremonious way. Van Cheele’s refusal to contribute provides the closing deadpan joke: he alone knows the memorial’s grotesque error, but his knowledge produces no public revelation or restoration of order. The overall style is therefore elegant, epigrammatic, macabre, and comic, with horror conveyed through implication and social absurdity rather than graphic description.

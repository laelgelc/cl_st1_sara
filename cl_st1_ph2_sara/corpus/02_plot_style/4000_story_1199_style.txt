The story uses a gently comic, sentimental mode of domestic realism, rooted in small-town life and the social manners of an earlier period. Its scale is deliberately intimate: adjoining houses, household servants, church attendance, afternoon calls, tea and sponge cake, cats, gardens and Thanksgiving provide the framework for exploring pride, affection and reconciliation. The central conflict is trivial in origin but emotionally serious in consequence, allowing the narrative to treat the sisters’ stubbornness with amused irony while preserving sympathy for their suffering.

The narrator is third-person omniscient, moving freely among the thoughts and feelings of the sisters, Mira, the servants and the surrounding community. The voice is warm, observant and mildly intrusive, often supplying general reflections on human behaviour, as in the image of loved ones fighting with “two-edged swords.” It maintains an affectionate distance from the characters: their vanity and obstinacy are exposed, but they are never treated cruelly. Dramatic irony is central. The servants and, eventually, the reader understand the likely truth about the kittens before the sisters do, while the sisters’ intimate knowledge of each other merely enables them to prolong their mutual misery.

The prose combines orderly exposition with vivid domestic detail. Sentences are generally clear and moderately long, often built from coordinated clauses that accumulate background, description or emotional consequences. Repetition is used for comic and structural effect: both sisters watch each other, both refuse invitations, both receive would-be peacemakers with tea and sponge cake, both leave on visits, and both independently decide to surrender. Parallel constructions reinforce their essential likeness even while they insist on opposition. Short sentences and fragments become more prominent in dialogue, sharpening moments of confrontation and giving the quarrel a brisk theatrical rhythm.

Diction mixes formal narrative language—“peace and concord,” “estrangement,” “importunities,” “dissension”—with colloquial rural speech. Expressions such as “Good land!,” “awful set,” “yeller cats,” and “Cat’s hind leg!” establish region, class and personality without making the dialogue difficult to follow. Speech is strongly individualized: Sarah is firm, sarcastic and practical; Melissa is tearful, sentimental and equally obstinate; Mira speaks wistfully and apologetically; Abby and Maria use earthy vernacular and quietly sensible humour. The dialogue often depends on repetition and contradiction, particularly in the exchange of “Billy” and “Susy,” reproducing the circular logic of a quarrel in which neither side is willing to yield.

Characterization is economical and frequently conveyed through gesture, habitual action and domestic choices. Sarah’s temper appears in her “awful firmness,” clipped commands and haughty refusal to listen. Melissa’s temperament emerges through tears, crooning and wounded sentiment. Their shared pride is revealed less by direct description than by their symmetrical conduct. Minor figures function as witnesses, mediators and foils: the servants possess the practical knowledge their mistresses refuse to hear, while Mira’s romantic sorrow echoes the sisters’ non-romantic but equally genuine grief. Physical descriptions are selective and picturesque, often linking appearance to emotional state, as in Mira’s drooping head beneath a rose-trimmed spring hat.

The pacing alternates between scenic dialogue and compressed summary. The initial quarrel is dramatized at length, while the following weeks of estrangement are condensed through repeated routines and reports from the village. A sequence of failed reconciliation attempts creates comic escalation: Mira, her mother, other women, the minister’s wife and finally the minister all consume the same refreshments and achieve nothing. The sisters’ separate journeys then create a pause before the climactic homecoming, where the revelation that both cats are female dissolves the conflict quickly. The final Thanksgiving scene serves as a warm coda, restoring domestic light, food, sociability and communal harmony.

Imagery is homely, seasonal and symbolic. The adjoining houses embody both closeness and separation; closed curtains make emotional estrangement visible. Spring flowers accompany the arrival of the kittens and the beginning of conflict, autumn wind and threatened frost mark the sisters’ loneliness, and Thanksgiving completes the movement toward reunion. Food repeatedly expresses social duty and suppressed feeling: tea, sponge cake, biscuits and chicken pies allow characters to offer care even when direct emotional speech is impossible. The identical yellow cats symbolize mistaken certainty, while their abundant kittens turn the original source of discord into a gift for the reconciled young couple.

The tone moves between affectionate satire, pathos and festive warmth. Comedy arises from disproportion—the immense consequences of an argument over indistinguishable kittens—and from the sisters’ mirrored behaviour. Pathos comes from the genuine deprivation caused by their pride and from Mira’s concealed heartbreak. The resolution avoids harsh moralizing: the sisters recognize that they have been “a pair of fools,” laugh at themselves and resume their accustomed generosity. The ending draws the older sisters’ devoted, non-romantic bond into harmony with Mira and Harry’s renewed courtship, making mutual love, humility and shared domestic life the story’s final emotional key.

The story adopts a first-person confessional mode framed as a courtroom address. Its opening declaration—“Yes, gentlemen, I killed him!”—immediately establishes both the crime and the speaker’s defiant attitude. The narrator speaks directly to judges or listeners, presenting his testimony less as a legal defence than as a satirical justification. Because his grievance expands from one victim into an entire social type, the voice is deliberately unreliable: psychologically obsessive and grotesquely disproportionate, yet acutely observant about everyday vulgarity, hypocrisy and intrusion.

The dominant tone is comic, bitter and hyperbolic. Murder is treated with mock solemnity, while trivial irritations are catalogued as though they constituted unbearable persecution. The humour depends on this mismatch of scale, as bad manners, clichés, unwanted conversation and cultural philistinism accumulate into a capital offence. The narrator’s exasperation repeatedly rises into melodrama, but the concrete accuracy of his complaints makes the satire recognisable. Beneath the farce lies genuine disgust at the corruption of privacy, beauty and intellectual life by self-important mediocrity.

Structurally, the story moves from startling confession to an extended indictment, then narrows into the railway-carriage incident that precipitates the killing. Much of the middle is episodic and accumulative: encounters in homes, trains, galleries, theatres, landscapes, books and historical sites all demonstrate the persecutor’s ubiquity. Repetition creates the impression that countless individuals are manifestations of one indestructible being. The delayed naming of this being as “the Russian intelligent” functions as the central satirical revelation. The final railway episode converts the social caricature into a particular antagonist, while the closing concern about the victim’s son supplies a darkly comic twist: the narrator fears not remorse or punishment, but the reproduction of the hated type.

The prose alternates between forceful declarative sentences and long, escalating lists. Short assertions such as “I killed him” and “I do not repent” give the confession rhetorical bluntness, whereas catalogues of professions, inventions, litter, inscriptions and offences produce a breathless sense of saturation. Parallel constructions and repeated formulas reinforce the narrator’s obsession. Exclamations, rhetorical questions, abrupt paragraphing, italics of emphasis in spirit, and broken or elongated words—such as “Intel-li-gent!”—lend the narration the energy of an oral performance. Parenthetical comments and comic qualifications sharpen the caricature by adding apparently incidental but revealing details.

Dialogue is central to the style. The persecutor speaks almost entirely in platitudes, intrusive questions, second-hand opinions and pompous moral maxims. His drawled syllables, repeated tags, empty cultural judgments and officious threats individualise his speech while also making him representative of a class. These quoted fragments often appear without much narrative mediation, allowing cliché to satirise itself. Physical details—hand-crushing, shoulder-slapping, coat-grabbing, humming, tapping and breathing into another person’s face—give the annoyance a tactile immediacy and turn social overfamiliarity into bodily assault.

The diction combines formal legal and moral language with colloquial irritation. Elevated phrases about conscience, execution, sentence and the “best minutes” of life are placed beside comic references to sugar lumps, orange peel, dog-eared pages and gramophone records. This contrast sustains the mock-heroic effect. Cultural references to Russian writers, art, opera and celebrated landscapes broaden the satire: the target is not ignorance alone, but shallow, performative appreciation that reduces art and nature to anecdotes, quotations and fashionable opinions. Some spellings, constructions and explanatory footnoting reflect an older English translation, giving the prose a period character while preserving its Russian social setting.

Overall, the style is that of a satirical monologue driven by accumulation, caricature and escalating indignation. It turns an etiquette grievance into a fantasy of persecution and then into a mock tragedy. Its distinctive effect comes from the unstable balance between plausibility and absurdity: the narrator is plainly deranged in his conclusion, but the story carefully ensures that the social irritations producing his rage remain vivid, specific and uncomfortably familiar.

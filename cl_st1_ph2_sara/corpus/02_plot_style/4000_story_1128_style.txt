The story adopts the form of a retrospective first-person testimony, narrated by an educated, rational-minded gentleman who insists upon the literal truth of an apparently supernatural experience. His direct address to the reader, his reluctance to tell the story, and his refusal to accept sceptical explanations establish a confessional yet argumentative tone. The twenty-year distance between event and narration lends the account reflective authority, while repeated appeals to memory and sensory evidence create the conventions of an eyewitness deposition. This measured credibility is essential to the tale’s effect: the narrator is presented not as naturally credulous, but as a practical barrister and sportsman whose confidence in ordinary reality is gradually overwhelmed.

The prose is formal, polished, and characteristically Victorian in diction and cadence. It uses elevated vocabulary, balanced clauses, emphatic repetitions, and occasional archaic or literary turns of phrase. Long, syntactically elaborate sentences dominate descriptive and philosophical passages, often accumulating details through parallel constructions and catalogues. These are offset by short exclamations, fragments, and rhetorical questions at moments of danger: “Death!”, “Ceased!”, and “What accident?” Such changes in sentence length regulate tension, moving from composed recollection to immediate emotional alarm. Intensifiers such as “strange,” “dreadful,” “ghastly,” “infinite,” and “terrible” openly heighten the Gothic register, while recurring negatives—no smoke, no stars, no sound, no reply—make absence itself threatening.

Description is highly visual and spatial, with settings revealed through successive acts of looking. The moor is rendered in broad, desolate expanses of purple heather, low hills, snow, darkness, and silence; the farmhouse is then explored through dense inventories of objects; finally, the coach interior is examined detail by decaying detail. This progressive scrutiny gives the narrative a quasi-investigative quality. Material specificity—the iron-studded door, telescope, philosophical instruments, mouldering leather, broken sash, and rotten floor—grounds the supernatural plot in tangible reality. The imagery depends heavily on contrasts between darkness and unstable light: lanterns, firelight, carriage lamps, moonlight, phosphorescence, and the flash-like final vision illuminate only enough to disclose danger. Cold, dampness, silence, smell, and physical nausea extend the atmosphere beyond the visual, making horror bodily as well as imaginative.

The setting belongs firmly to the Gothic tradition, but its Gothicism is rural and scientific rather than conventionally medieval. The bleak northern moor functions as an isolating threshold outside ordinary social life. The remote farmhouse resembles a hybrid of barn, laboratory, library, and occult retreat, while its owner combines the roles of philosopher, rejected scientist, visionary, and hermit. His intellectual monologue links contemporary science with mesmerism, mysticism, ghosts, and ancient philosophy. This produces a central tension between empirical scepticism and forms of knowledge that resist laboratory proof. The story does not resolve that tension objectively; instead, it places legal testimony, sensory evidence, medical diagnosis, and supernatural conviction in competition.

Dialogue is sharply differentiated by class and temperament. The narrator speaks briskly and confidently, often in the compressed language of a man accustomed to asserting himself: “Meat, drink, and sleep.” The master’s speech is formal, eloquent, and rhetorically structured, especially in his defence of apparitions. Jacob’s dialect—marked by forms such as “fra’,” “muir,” “gude,” and “nowt”—gives him a rough northern identity and contributes local colour. His evasiveness, grim humour, and warnings also make him a conventional threshold figure who knows more than he explains. In the climactic coach sequence, dialogue fails altogether: the passengers answer only with silence and fixed looks. The transition from talk to muteness signals the narrator’s passage from the human world into the realm of the dead.

The narrative is carefully staged as a slow escalation. It begins with a credibility frame, moves into realistic physical peril, offers temporary refuge, pauses for an extended philosophical debate, and then returns the narrator to the night with his imagination primed for supernatural interpretation. Foreshadowing is explicit but controlled: the master’s defence of ghosts, Jacob’s account of the fatal mail-coach accident, the broken parapet, and the unnatural cold all prepare the revelation. Suspense arises through delayed recognition. The approaching lights first promise rescue; the coach initially appears fortunate; its silence seems merely discourteous; its disrepair appears negligent; only gradually do its passengers become recognisable as corpses. This sequence repeatedly converts reassurance into menace.

At the climax, the style becomes rapid, fragmented, and cinematic. Anaphoric constructions such as “Then … Then … and then” reproduce the speed and disorientation of the crash, while a compressed series of images—the moon, sign-post, parapet, horses, and gulf—creates the effect of a final flash of perception. The aftermath abruptly returns to sober summary and medical explanation. This tonal recoil restores the language of ordinary causation without cancelling the supernatural account. The surgeon’s diagnosis of fever and delirium provides a plausible rational interpretation, but the narrator’s final declaration decisively reasserts personal certainty.

Overall, the style combines documentary realism, sensation fiction, philosophical Gothicism, and the oral intimacy of a fireside ghost story. Its characteristic method is to establish credibility through concrete detail and a socially respectable narrator, surround him with an atmosphere of isolation and sensory unease, and then force rational discourse into confrontation with an experience it cannot conclusively explain. The ending is emphatic rather than ambiguous from the narrator’s perspective, but it preserves ambiguity for the reader by allowing hallucination and haunting to remain equally available explanations.

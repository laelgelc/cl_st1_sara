The story is written as a darkly comic moral folktale, combining the Faustian bargain, colonial legend, supernatural cautionary tale, and social satire. Its world is recognisably historical—early eighteenth-century New England—but history is filtered through oral tradition, local superstition, and comic exaggeration. Pirate treasure, Indian forts, witches, religious persecution, speculative finance, and the devil coexist within the same legendary landscape. The narrative ultimately functions as an exemplum: Tom Walker’s fate demonstrates that avarice, exploitation, and hypocritical piety lead to destruction.

The narrator is third-person, omniscient, and strongly intrusive. Rather than presenting the events as verified fact, the voice repeatedly attributes them to “old stories,” popular report, unnamed historians, and competing local versions. Phrases such as “it is said,” “some asserted,” and “the most current and probable story” imitate the methods of folklore while playfully undermining their reliability. The narrator alternates between scepticism and mock solemnity, questioning improbable details only to endorse the legend’s moral and supernatural truth. This creates a companionable storyteller’s voice: worldly, amused, ironic, and conscious of addressing an audience familiar with traditional tales.

The tone blends Gothic foreboding with robust humour. Swamps, dusk, stagnant pools, rotting trees, skulls, carrion birds, thunder, and fire establish an atmosphere of corruption and supernatural menace. Yet horror is repeatedly deflated by deadpan jokes, domestic satire, and comic understatement. Tom fears his wife less than neither danger nor the devil; he values her apron and household property more than her life; and her presumed struggle with Old Scratch is treated like an especially violent marital quarrel. The resulting comedy is mordant rather than lighthearted, exposing the grotesque logic of greed.

Characterisation is broad, emblematic, and caricatural. Tom is defined by miserliness, hardness, stubbornness, and calculating self-interest. His wife is rendered as an exaggerated termagant whose greed and physical aggression rival his own. The devil is not an abstract metaphysical presence but a shrewd, sardonic businessman, woodsman, tempter, and collector of corrupt souls. Secondary figures—the hypocritical deacon, boastful buccaneer, desperate speculator, and respectable churchgoers—represent social types rather than psychologically complex individuals. Repetition of dominant traits makes the characters memorable in the manner of oral storytelling.

The prose uses elevated nineteenth-century literary diction mixed with colloquial idioms and proverbial expressions. Formal words such as “propitiated,” “parsimony,” “expediency,” and “superfluous” sit beside phrases like “Old Scratch,” “hard times,” “hand over hand,” “play his cards,” and “Egad.” This contrast supports the comic mode: the narrator can describe absurd or sordid conduct with mock-historical dignity, while the dialogue remains brisk and earthy. Some terminology and racialised descriptions reflect the colonial setting and the period in which the story was written; they function within its historical voice but are dated and offensive by contemporary standards.

Sentences are often long, balanced, and cumulative, particularly in descriptions of landscape, history, and public behaviour. Semicolons coordinate successive observations, while parallel clauses and catalogues build rhythmic momentum. The description of the swamp, for example, moves through trees, pits, mud, pools, animals, and decaying trunks, progressively thickening the atmosphere. By contrast, supernatural encounters and negotiations use short exchanges, emphatic exclamations, and rapid verbal thrusts. The bargain between Tom and the devil becomes almost a comic business transaction, with each speaker attempting to outdo the other in ruthlessness.

Imagery is highly concrete and frequently symbolic. The Walker household externalises the couple’s spiritual barrenness: the starving house, sterile savin-trees, smokeless chimney, exposed stones, and skeletal horse all embody miserliness. In the swamp, flourishing trees that are rotten at the core represent outwardly respectable but inwardly corrupt public figures. Their names are scored into the bark, and the devil’s axe anticipates their deaths. Tom’s unfinished mansion, underfed carriage horses, cindering financial documents, and chest of worthless shavings similarly turn moral corruption into visible objects. Material wealth repeatedly proves hollow, decayed, or illusory.

The setting operates as a moral landscape. Boston represents commerce, credit, speculation, and institutional hypocrisy, while the swamp is the hidden realm in which the true spiritual ownership of corrupt lives is revealed. The journey into the swamp therefore resembles a descent into both the wilderness and Tom’s own moral nature. Darkness, twilight, black mud, thunderclouds, and fire recur around moments of temptation, judgment, and damnation. The final gallop returns Tom from the commercial city to the supernatural wilderness where his bargain began, giving the plot a circular shape.

Dialogue is economical and character-revealing. Tom’s replies are sceptical, blunt, and commercially minded even when he is speaking to the devil. The devil’s speech combines menace with dry wit, and their negotiation resembles haggling between traders. Ironically, Tom rejects participation in the slave trade on moral grounds but enthusiastically embraces usury, even proposing harsher terms than the devil requests. This selective scruple deepens the satire by showing that his conscience is arbitrary and self-serving rather than genuinely ethical.

The plot is linear and episodic, moving from local legend to domestic introduction, supernatural encounter, bargain, worldly success, false repentance, and punishment. Descriptive passages slow the opening and create an ominous legendary setting; thereafter, compact anecdotes and summary accelerate Tom’s rise. The final scene becomes more immediate and dramatic through direct dialogue, exact physical details, repeated knocks, violent movement, and the approaching thunderstorm. The aftermath returns to summary and folklore, transforming Tom’s individual fate into communal proverb.

Irony is the governing rhetorical device. Tom’s “friendship” with borrowers ruins them; his churchgoing intensifies alongside his exploitation; his Bible marks his place while he conducts usurious business; and his attempt to secure salvation becomes another form of account-keeping. Religious and financial vocabularies continually overlap, as sins become entries in a ledger and piety becomes a speculative investment. The story also uses antithesis, hyperbole, personification, grotesque simile, symbolic foreshadowing, and mock-biblical phrasing. The newspaper declaration that “A great man had fallen in Israel,” applied to a corrupt rich man, exemplifies its satirical treatment of public respectability.

The thematic centre is greed, but the satire extends to marriage, commerce, speculative bubbles, religious zealotry, social reputation, and the exploitation of distress. Tom’s damnation arises not merely from wanting wealth but from converting other people’s desperation into profit. His late religiosity is condemned because it is performative and transactional: he treats heaven as another party to be manipulated. The closing moral is explicit and proverbial, yet the tale’s chief persuasive force comes from comic spectacle. Its distinctive signature is therefore a fusion of Gothic atmosphere, folkloric narration, historical local colour, symbolic landscape, broad caricature, and urbane irony, all directed toward a sharply entertaining moral satire.

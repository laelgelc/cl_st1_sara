The story uses a restrained, classical realist style shaped by retrospective first-person narration. Its narrator is both participant and witness: he introduces Silvio through his own youthful fascination, records the judgments of the officers around him, and later supplies the conclusion through the Count’s embedded account. Because the narrator does not directly witness the decisive events, the story preserves mystery while allowing the same conflict to be viewed from several perspectives. His older, reflective voice also creates a mild irony toward his younger self and toward the officers’ simplistic code of honor.

The structure is notably symmetrical and economical. Chapter I establishes military life, Silvio’s exceptional marksmanship, his apparent failure to answer an insult, and his explanation of the unfinished duel. Chapter II shifts forward several years and into a contrasting rural and aristocratic setting, where the narrator encounters the Count and hears how the deferred shot was finally used. Objects and actions recur with altered significance: pistols, measured paces, lots, a pierced cap, a shot-marked picture, and the adversary’s composure before death. The delayed revelation converts what first appears to be cowardice into obsessive self-preservation and then turns revenge from a physical act into a psychological one.

The prose is lucid, controlled, and largely unornamented. Descriptive passages rely on a few precise, memorable details rather than elaborate imagery: Silvio’s shabby black overcoat, bullet-riddled walls, champagne flowing at his modest table, cherries in the rival’s cap, and the two bullets in the Swiss landscape. Such concrete details often carry symbolic weight. The cherries embody the Count’s youthful indifference to death, while the nearly superimposed bullet holes demonstrate both Silvio’s technical mastery and the exactness of his revenge. Similes are infrequent but forceful, as when the walls are “as full of holes as a honeycomb” or Silvio paces “like a tiger in his cage.”

Sentence structure is generally balanced and formal, reflecting an older translated literary register. Longer sentences organize background, social observation, and psychological qualification; shorter statements mark reversals or intensify suspense: “Silvio did not fight,” “I stopped behind,” and “I will not.” The diction is polished and somewhat ceremonious, with phrases such as “demand satisfaction,” “have the goodness,” and “Your Excellency.” Period-specific military and social vocabulary—“Hussars,” “faro,” “punter,” “seconds,” “telega,” and “versts”—grounds the narrative historically. Initials and dashes conceal names and places, giving the tale the appearance of a discreetly reported true incident. Occasional explanatory footnotes and foreign expressions reinforce the texture of a historical translation.

Dialogue is central to both characterization and dramatic tension. Silvio speaks tersely, formally, and with caustic precision; his controlled language makes his anger more threatening than an outburst would. The Count’s later narration is more emotional and fragmented, revealing fear that Silvio’s own account suppressed. Repeated formulations—especially references to jokes, firing, and a “shot due”—turn polite language into menace. Much of the climactic action is presented as quoted speech within quoted recollection, producing an oral, anecdotal quality while keeping the crucial encounter vividly immediate.

Characterization proceeds indirectly through habits, possessions, reputation, gestures, and reactions. Silvio’s contradictions dominate the portrait: he lives poorly yet serves champagne, appears indifferent to opinion yet is ruled by wounded pride, seems fearless yet refuses avoidable danger, and seeks revenge but finally refrains from killing. The story avoids explicit moral judgment, allowing these contradictions to remain unresolved. The Count likewise changes from a carefree favorite of fortune into a man made vulnerable by love and domestic happiness. The Countess functions chiefly as the embodiment of what he now has to lose, and her presence transforms the duel’s emotional stakes.

Pacing alternates between compressed summary and carefully staged scenes. Years of routine or waiting pass in a few sentences, while games, duels, pauses, glances, and shots are slowed almost moment by moment. Suspense grows through postponement: Silvio delays retaliation against the lieutenant, delays explaining himself to the narrator, delays taking his original shot, and delays firing when he confronts the married Count. Ellipses, pauses, silence, and interrupted action repeatedly suspend the expected violence. The actual climax is not a death but Silvio’s recognition that fear and humiliation are sufficient.

The tone combines social comedy, romantic mystery, and psychological severity. Dry observations about provincial boredom, military drinking, and country neighbors lighten the narrative, while the central plot retains the atmosphere of a dark tale of honor and revenge. Romantic elements—the enigmatic outsider, exceptional skill, diabolical appearance, long-nursed vengeance, and reported death in a foreign revolt—are disciplined by realistic settings and concise narration. The final report of Silvio’s death is brief and hearsay-based, closing his life at a distance and preserving his legendary quality.

Overall, the style is defined by narrative economy, formal clarity, nested storytelling, ironic social observation, concrete symbolic detail, and suspense created through delay. Its central aesthetic effect comes from restraint: violent passions are conveyed through controlled prose, and the deepest moral questions—whether Silvio triumphs, relents, or remains enslaved to pride—are left for the reader to decide.

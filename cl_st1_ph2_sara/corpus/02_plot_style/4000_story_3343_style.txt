The story is written in an urbane, early-twentieth-century society-fiction style, combining drawing-room comedy with romantic melodrama and a final mystery revelation. Its setting is affluent and cultivated: bridge tables, white flannels, jeweled purses, French windows, formal gardens, European travel, country clubs, and marble busts establish a leisured upper-class world. Social sophistication is conveyed less through exposition than through manners, possessions, and references to fashionable places such as Capri, Rome, and the Riviera.

The narration uses a controlled third-person perspective that initially surveys the ensemble but gradually aligns itself with Caroline and Kingsley. Characters are introduced through compact, evaluative descriptions—Mrs. Baxter is “a languid shadow of some brilliant and devastating yesterday,” while Caroline is slender, poised, and outwardly cold. Such phrases blend physical observation with social and psychological judgment. The narrator generally remains unobtrusive, allowing glances, gestures, silences, and overheard remarks to disclose emotional states.

Dialogue dominates the story and is the principal means of characterization. The older women speak in polished epigrams, teasing paradoxes, insinuations, and lightly cruel observations. Their conversation is theatrical and self-aware, often turning suffering into entertainment: gossip is explicitly compared with masculine humor because both depend on other people’s misfortunes. Mrs. Baxter’s wit is especially aphoristic, while Kingsley’s stiff, earnest speech marks him as less socially agile but more emotionally sincere. Caroline’s dialogue is lyrical and hesitant, moving between whimsy, confession, and urgent questioning. Contrasts between brittle social comedy and grave private feeling generate much of the story’s tension.

The prose favors long, balanced sentences punctuated by semicolons, dashes, parenthetical qualifications, and accumulative lists. These are interspersed with short declarations at moments of emotional emphasis: “That he is dead!” and “I will try.” Dashes frequently represent interruption, hesitation, or a sudden change in thought, while ellipsis is conveyed through broken syntax rather than typographical dots. The diction is formal but readable, with period expressions such as “the mater,” “en tour,” “sitting-out,” “funked it,” and “make lost the rubber.” The characters’ speech assumes familiarity with social conventions and makes irony more important than literal statement.

Description is selective and pictorial. Light, shadow, whiteness, coldness, and stillness recur throughout. Caroline is repeatedly associated with whiteness, slenderness, frost, and composure, while Kingsley is linked with warmth: he encloses her cold hand in his “warm, brown ones.” The landscape beyond the veranda offers a visual counterpoint to the enclosed, gossip-filled music room. Gardens, hills, clouds, and the distant hay wagon create a pastoral spaciousness, but Caroline experiences that openness as uncertainty, asking of the horizon, “Which way? Which way?” Interior objects also acquire symbolic force. The unused music room suggests arrested harmony; the discordant guitar and piano notes externalize Kingsley’s frustration; the marble composers reinforce motifs of death, memory, and emotional petrification.

The story’s structure is carefully staged. It begins with apparently casual gossip about Olive Nettleton, shifts to Caroline’s analogous predicament, and then returns to the gossip as an embedded narrative whose details become decisive evidence. Much of the central action consists of overhearing: the drawing-room conversation supplies a public commentary on the private drama unfolding outside. Information is delayed in increments, and the anecdote about Gregory Nettleton is allowed to seem merely thematic before the Egyptian ring connects it directly to Caroline’s vanished bridegroom. The ending is abrupt and epiphanic. Rather than explain every circumstance, it relies on Caroline and the reader making the same inference: the ring indicates that her missing lover lived during the years Nettleton cannot remember, most likely under another identity. The final line, “I—have buried my dead,” converts uncertainty into symbolic closure.

Emotion is handled through restraint rather than sustained introspection. Caroline’s grief appears in the corners of her mouth, her fixed gaze, her cold hand, and her possessive clutching of the ring. The story repeatedly contrasts outward poise with concealed intensity, using images such as “frozen fire,” “husk,” “shadows,” “ghost,” and an unhealed wound. Its governing themes are ambiguous loss, fidelity, memory, identity, conscience, and the need for certainty before mourning can begin. The Puritan conscience functions both as character shorthand and as a moral pressure that prevents Caroline from accepting a new love while the old one remains unresolved.

The tone moves fluidly between languor, satire, sentiment, and suspense. Early exchanges are arch and amused; the veranda scene becomes earnest and romantic; the Nettleton anecdote introduces sensational mystery; and the final revelation is solemn but economical. A style emulating the story would therefore use elegant social dialogue, lightly ironic narration, symbolic visual details, delayed disclosure, parallel personal histories, and a concrete object that transforms gossip into revelation. Apparent anomalies such as “ages to Bee,” “four yeas,” “beslde,” and inconsistent capitalization are likely transcription or OCR errors rather than intentional features of the prose.

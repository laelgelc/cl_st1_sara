The story uses a late-nineteenth-century regionalist style, combining gently formal narration with vividly rendered local speech. Its setting is established through concrete details of the Louisiana bayou and pine hills: pine needles, cotton seed, garden produce, tobacco plants, pumpkins, corn, and couche-couche. These domestic and agricultural particulars create a strongly localized social world without lengthy exposition. French-derived names and expressions such as “Allons donc,” “Ti Démon,” “grosse bête,” and “’Cadian” further define the cultural setting.

The narrator is third-person omniscient and moves freely among Herminia, Solistan, and Ti Démon. The most distinctive feature is the treatment of the pony’s consciousness. Ti Démon understands human speech, remembers his youth, resents comparisons with Raymond’s mule, swears inwardly in the idiom of his former owner, and reasons like a cantankerous old man. His thoughts are presented through direct quotation and free indirect discourse, often without any sharp tonal break from the human characters’ perspectives. This anthropomorphism supplies much of the comedy while also giving the animal an independent personality and a melancholy inner life.

Narratorial diction is polished, measured, and occasionally elevated. Phrases such as “cherished the certainty,” “beyond further hesitancy,” “the menace of some dreadful and unknown fate,” and “with philosophic resignation” give ordinary rural incidents a mock-serious dignity. The syntax is generally clear but often expansive, using balanced clauses and cumulative detail. Shorter declarative sentences interrupt these longer constructions at decisive moments, as in the recognition that the horse can go no farther or the discovery that he has vanished. This variation keeps the leisurely storytelling from becoming static.

Dialogue is heavily marked by phonetic dialect. Contractions, omitted consonants, altered spellings, and nonstandard grammar—“w’at,” “yo’,” “lef’,” “ho’se,” “mus’,” and “I would walked myse’f”—represent the English of the Cajun community. French phrases are folded naturally into the speech rather than translated or explained. Ti Démon’s inner voice uses a different vernacular, derived from an older Western or frontier idiom: “A thousand dollars to a doughnut,” “whole shootin’ match,” and “pullin’ my freight.” The contrast between the narrator’s formal prose, the Cajun dialogue, and the pony’s frontier slang differentiates voices sharply and contributes to the story’s humor.

Description is selective and picturesque. Physical characterization often depends on a few memorable visual details: Herminia’s vivid red calico dress and white sunbonnet, Ti Démon’s patchy coat and scant tail, Solistan’s blue checked shirt and earth-heavy boots. Nature is rendered through quiet sensory touches rather than elaborate lyricism. Patches of sunlight, crawling ants, mockingbirds, and the “drowsy noises” of the forest create a peaceful atmosphere against which Ti Démon’s grievances become comically disproportionate. Similes are accessible and drawn from familiar life, as when Herminia’s legs are “sturdy as steel,” her eyes are “quick as a squirrel’s,” and the pony resembles “a wise old philosopher.”

The tone blends affection, irony, comedy, and subdued pathos. Herminia’s romantic hopes concerning Prospere are gently exposed as fantasies, while Solistan’s unpolished devotion proves genuine. The story repeatedly sets appearance against reality: Herminia imagines an elegant rescue but is ignored at the planter’s house; Ti Démon appears to be a suffering martyr but soon escapes; Solistan fears a disaster and instead finds Herminia merely humiliated and stranded. The narrator does not ridicule the characters cruelly. Their vanity, anxiety, and misunderstandings are treated with sympathetic amusement.

Structurally, the narrative follows a comic chain of misinterpretations. Ti Démon’s temporary lameness leads Herminia to abandon him; his return home makes Solistan believe she has been injured; Solistan’s rescue reveals his love; and his joking suggestion that the old pony be shot creates the fear that eventually drives Ti Démon away. The plot is therefore tightly causal despite its relaxed pace. Parallel viewpoints generate dramatic irony because the reader knows where both Herminia and Ti Démon are while the characters do not. The courtship emerges indirectly from the animal’s mishap, allowing romance to develop through action rather than declaration.

The ending shifts the comedy toward elegy while preserving irony. Ti Démon’s fear of being shot causes him to flee the home where Solistan had intended to support him for life, and he dies while apparently trying to return to the “Indian Nation” of his youth. Herminia remembers him as “good an’ faithful,” although the story has shown his stubbornness, self-interest, and flight. This contradiction is affectionate rather than corrective. Overall, the style is that of a compact regional tale: locally textured, dialect-rich, omniscient, anthropomorphic, lightly satirical, and emotionally restrained, with humor arising from mismatched perspectives and pathos arriving through understatement.

The story has the polished, urbane style of an early twentieth-century romantic comedy, blending sentimental adventure with gentle social satire and a final touch of the supernatural. Its tone is affectionate and amused rather than sharply ironic. Ainsley’s suffering is treated as emotionally sincere, yet his extravagant declarations, impulsive journeys, packed trunks, false alarms, and literal-minded search for a “sign” make him a comic figure. This balance allows the narrative to mock romantic excess without dismissing the depth of romantic feeling.

The narration is third-person omniscient, focused chiefly through Ainsley’s perceptions while retaining enough distance to expose his self-dramatisation. The narrator frequently offers dry explanatory asides, such as the distinction between Ainsley’s ostensible life as a gentleman farmer and his real purpose in waiting for Polly. Similar comments convert emotion into social comedy, especially when the ordinary life of New York continues with “unfeeling regularity” after Polly’s departure. The narrative voice is confident, cultivated, and lightly intrusive, often summarising motives or interpreting behaviour before moving into a vividly staged scene.

The diction is formal and literary, with period-specific references to taxicabs, wireless messages, cablegrams, steamers, clubs, dahabiyehs, and the social rituals of wealthy Americans. Elevated words such as “execrable,” “unremunerative,” “conducive,” and “inestimable” coexist with colloquial exclamations and comic modernisms. Ainsley’s language is especially hyperbolic: he will “plain DIE,” drown himself, demand the captain’s cabin, or cross the world at once. Capitalised words reproduce vocal stress and intensify both the comedy and theatricality of his speech. The prose also uses occasional idioms and aphoristic formulations, most notably Polly’s distinction between “the man you can live with” and “the man you can’t live without.”

Sentence structure alternates between long, balanced descriptive sentences and short, energetic bursts. The longer sentences establish landscape, psychology, and social context through parallel clauses, qualifications, and accumulative detail. During emotional or physical action, the syntax becomes faster and more fragmented: Ainsley slides down the rock, clears the flower-bed, mounts the terrace, and begins issuing orders in a rapid chain. Dialogue is plentiful and dramatically organised, often relying on repetition, interruption, rhetorical questions, and escalating absurdity. These exchanges give the story the quality of a stage comedy while also revealing character directly.

The settings are rendered through precise, pictorial description. Lone Lake is personified as the “eye” of the farm, with oaks for eyebrows and reeds for lashes, and its changing surface reflects emotional moods. Nature is not merely decorative; it externalises Ainsley’s loneliness and later becomes the medium through which chance, providence, or fate acts. The six white birds are described in repeated visual and rhythmic phrases—“drooping head,” “dragging wings,” “utter weariness, abject fatigue”—which lend them pathos, mystery, and symbolic weight. Their whiteness against sombre clouds and sunset light creates a nearly visionary tableau.

Structurally, the story proceeds in clear phases: comic exposition, failed courtship, separation, prolonged expectation, apparent revelation, impulsive journey, romantic reunion, and ironic supernatural coda. Early details are carefully planted and later recalled. Polly’s deliberately ambiguous promise of a sign generates the central misunderstanding, while Ainsley’s abandoned travel trunks make his sudden departure both plausible and comic. The false cable and intaglio ring establish a pattern of mistaken signs, preparing the reader to question the birds even as Ainsley accepts them without hesitation. The ending then delivers a double reversal: Polly never sent the birds, yet the same birds appear in Egypt, suggesting that Ainsley’s mistake may have been guided by something genuinely inexplicable.

Pacing moves between broad narrative summary and fully dramatised set pieces. Weeks of waiting can pass in a paragraph, while the farewell on the ship, the birds’ arrival at Lone Lake, and the reunion on the Nile unfold moment by moment. Suspense is mild but carefully sustained through delayed recognition: the birds are first observed aesthetically, then debated as natural specimens, and only the mention of the sacred ibis triggers Ainsley’s interpretation. The final recurrence is similarly delayed until after the lovers have clarified—or failed to clarify—the misunderstanding.

Characterisation is economical and comic. Ainsley is wealthy, impractical, generous, emotionally transparent, and prone to converting love into melodrama and action. His inability to trust intuition contrasts with Polly’s demand that genuine love should require no explicit instruction. Polly is poised, elusive, and verbally adroit, but her ambiguity drives much of the plot. Secondary characters function as a social chorus: they provide scepticism, naturalistic explanations, and a contrast to Ainsley’s romantic conviction.

The dominant devices are irony, hyperbole, personification, foreshadowing, repetition, and symbolic coincidence. The deepest irony is that Ainsley reaches the correct destination for the wrong reason, while Polly interprets his mistaken response as proof that he understood her perfectly. The birds embody the story’s deliberate tension between coincidence and providence. Consequently, the style remains comic and worldly on the surface but admits an undercurrent of wonder. Its characteristic effect comes from joining brisk social comedy, lush natural description, sincere romantic yearning, and an ending that preserves mystery rather than explaining it.

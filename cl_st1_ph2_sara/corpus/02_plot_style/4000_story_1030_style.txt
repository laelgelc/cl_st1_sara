The story is written as a comic, discursive travel sketch in which observation, anecdote, social commentary, and affectionate portraiture matter more than conventional plot. Its structure is cumulative and panoramic: the narrator moves from the humiliations of arrival to the town’s streets, markets, fishing community, notable residents, entertainments, and expatriate population. Transitions often arise through association rather than strict chronology, giving the piece the air of a guided walk conducted by a talkative, highly opinionated companion. Recurring figures and motifs—especially Bilkins, M. Loyal, national manners, and the phrase “our French watering-place”—provide cohesion.

The narrative voice uses the editorial or communal first-person plural, “we,” even when recounting plainly personal experiences. This creates a mock-public authority while preserving conversational intimacy. The narrator is self-dramatising but also self-mocking, presenting seasickness, weak legs, confusion, and tourist behaviour as comic spectacles. Direct addresses to the reader, invitations such as “Permit us,” and parenthetical confidences make the narration feel performative and sociable. The voice repeatedly adopts tones of ceremonial gravity only to apply them to trivial matters, producing mock-heroic humour.

The tone combines exuberance, irony, satire, sentiment, and genuine admiration. British travellers, bureaucratic procedures, cultural tastemakers, patriotic reflexes, boarding-house bores, and romanticised tourism are ridiculed through exaggeration. Yet the satire is usually softened by warmth. Working people, children, fishermen, market women, and genial local figures are represented with pleasure and sympathy. The portrait of M. Loyal shifts especially smoothly from caricature to moral tribute: his expansive claims and military pride are comic, but his hospitality, generosity, and instinctive gentility are treated sincerely. More sombre passages, such as the dwindling group of old men on the ramparts, introduce restrained pathos without abandoning the observational mode.

The syntax is characteristically long, elastic, and accumulative. Sentences gather subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, interruptions, lists, and delayed conclusions, often mimicking the narrator’s overflowing perceptions. Enumeration is a major rhythmic device, as in the descriptions of market goods, fishermen’s clothes, public amusements, and urban nuisances. These catalogues build momentum through repetition and parallel phrasing before culminating in a punch line or emphatic judgment. Short sentences and fragments are then used for comic contrast, abrupt emphasis, or affectionate apostrophe: “Good M. Loyal!” The punctuation is highly expressive, with dashes, semicolons, parentheses, exclamation marks, and quotation marks controlling shifts of voice and pace.

The diction mixes elevated, Latinate, and quasi-official language with colloquial English, foreign phrases, comic mistranscriptions, and vivid concrete nouns. Bureaucratic events are described in the vocabulary of captivity, military assault, lunacy, and national crisis. Ordinary people become “military creatures,” passport control becomes imprisonment, and a rough landing becomes a public degradation. French words and names lend local colour, while distorted English expressions such as “Mingle” and “Nokemdon” create linguistic comedy. Capitalisation and typographical emphasis intensify spoken stress and theatricality.

Figurative language is energetic and often sustained. The market becomes a stream that slips through a gateway, brawls downhill, disappears, sparkles, and finally dries up. Houses “burst” into a second existence on the ramparts; the sea enjoys itself on an “instrument of torture”; fishermen’s clothing appears made from tarred sails; and medals require a providentially large body on which to hang. Such metaphors animate settings and turn description into miniature narrative. Hyperbole is constant but precise: maps enlarge tiny estates to national proportions, cupboards could hold the equipment of a regiment, and local dancers possess joints unknown to anatomy.

Characterisation depends on sharply selected physical details, habitual gestures, repeated sayings, and comic contradictions. Minor figures are often captured in a few strokes: crumpled faces, floating hair, threadbare coats, bright caps, enormous boots, or solemn riders on a roundabout. M. Loyal receives fuller treatment through his appearance, speech, possessions, domestic arrangements, civic role, and characteristic optimism. Dialogue is brief, theatrical, and shaped around verbal misunderstanding or self-revelation. Accents and malapropisms are rendered selectively rather than through exhaustive phonetic spelling.

The prose is strongly visual but engages all the senses. Colour is especially prominent—red caps, blue blouses, white caps, striped petticoats, bright produce—while smells, cooking, bells, market cries, music, wind, sea noise, and physical movement create a crowded sensory environment. Scenes are often composed as animated tableaux, with figures arranged by costume, occupation, age, and gesture. The result resembles both reportage and caricature.

At the thematic level, the story contrasts authentic pleasure with prescribed taste, humane conduct with official systems, and lived cultural exchange with national prejudice. It distrusts guidebook authority and fashionable enthusiasm, embodied by the invented arbiter Bilkins, preferring spontaneous appreciation. Social class is judged morally rather than ceremonially: true gentility appears in generosity, courtesy, work, and hospitality. The final outlook is cosmopolitan and reformist, praising ordinary French life while satirising both English insularity and oppressive continental government. Overall, the style is expansive, theatrical, humane, and comic, using verbal abundance and caricature to turn travel observation into social portraiture.

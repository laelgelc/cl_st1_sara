A skeptical narrator listens as his idealistic friend Blandmour praises nature’s supposed provisions for the poor, including “poor man’s manure,” “poor man’s egg,” and a simple rice pudding. To test Blandmour’s belief that poverty can yield comfort, the narrator visits the impoverished Coulter family. He finds William laboring for a wealthy landowner and his pregnant wife Martha living in a cold, damp house, serving spoiled pork and a pudding made from damaged rice and old brine. Martha recounts the deaths of their two children and her profound loneliness, revealing that the supposedly comforting pudding is inseparable from deprivation and grief. The experience convinces the narrator that comfortable people romanticize hardships they do not endure.

The following year, while visiting London, the narrator is taken to a celebrated charity event at Guildhall. After a lavish royal banquet, starving people are admitted to fight over the aristocrats’ mangled leftovers. Officials praise this distribution as magnificent generosity, but the narrator sees only humiliation, hunger, and contempt disguised as charity. When the scraps run out, the desperate crowd riots, and he narrowly escapes, bruised and with torn clothes. Reflecting on both experiences, he rejects alike the sentimental glorification of poor people’s meager food and the degrading crumbs offered by the rich.

Wealthy young Eleanor Stuart is frustrated by the obligation to buy Christmas presents for friends and relatives who already possess everything they need. Her aunt suggests that, instead of exchanging costly but meaningless gifts, Eleanor use her money to help impoverished neighbors.

The aunt describes several struggling households: a consumptive shoemaker craving fruit; his overworked wife and children; a generous young mother who longs for a purple cap for her baby; and two nearly ninety-year-old women living in a cold garret with little food or fuel. Moved by their circumstances, Eleanor plans gifts of oranges, grapes, clothing, tea, coal, warm shawls, and a new stove. Her aunt explains that meaningful presents arise from love and genuine need, and that Christmas, commemorating Christ’s humble birth, is especially suited to remembering the poor and forsaken.

When Eleanor’s charitable purchases arrive, her cousins initially mock the unusual parcels. After learning their purpose, however, one cousin praises her idea and contributes ten dollars. Eleanor embraces her new role as a “good fairy,” anticipating that her practical gifts will bring rare happiness and laughter to several needy families on Christmas morning.

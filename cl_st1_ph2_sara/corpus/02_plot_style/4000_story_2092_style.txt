The story uses a cool, formal, parable-like mode to present an increasingly grotesque situation as if it were an ordinary matter of public administration. Its third-person narrator remains close to the hunger artist’s consciousness while retaining an impersonal, faintly judicial authority. The narration moves freely between external description, the artist’s private grievances, public opinion, and broad generalisation. This produces a shifting perspective in which the protagonist is psychologically exposed but never fully explained. Assertions are repeatedly qualified by phrases such as “of course,” “in fact,” “perhaps,” and “at any rate,” giving the prose the air of careful reasoning even as its logic becomes circular or absurd.

The diction is largely plain, precise, and restrained. Concrete details—the barred cage, scattered straw, clock, flashlights, flowers, photographs, dirty signs, and rotting bedding—anchor the allegorical material in a drab physical world. Alongside these objects appears the language of commerce, entertainment, publicity, contracts, supervision, and crowd management. Art is therefore treated as both a spiritual vocation and a marketable spectacle. Elevated terms such as “honour,” “art,” “martyr,” and “self-denial” coexist with practical references to ticket sales, advertising, breakfast, measurements, and staffing. This collision between the sublime and the bureaucratic is central to the story’s style.

Sentence structure alternates between blunt declarations and long, intricately subordinated sentences. Short sentences often deliver bleak conclusions—“They depressed him,” “It was all futile,” or “People went straight past him”—while extended sentences accumulate qualifications, reversals, motives, and physical details. The longer passages reproduce the hunger artist’s obsessive reasoning and the suffocating complexity of social misunderstanding. Parentheses, dashes, and embedded clauses continually interrupt apparently straightforward statements, revising or undermining them. Repetition is prominent, especially around “fasting,” “understanding,” “admiration,” and the question of why the performance must stop. Rhetorical questions intensify the artist’s sense of injustice without offering genuine dialogue or resolution.

The tone combines melancholy, irony, grotesque comedy, and emotional detachment. Humiliating scenes are described with exactness but little overt pity: the ceremonial end of the fast becomes a piece of slapstick in which the weakened artist is shaken, dragged, displayed, and fed while the audience laughs. The narrator’s irony is usually understated. Public concern repeatedly turns out to be vanity, suspicion, commercial calculation, or habit, while the artist’s apparent heroism is finally revealed to arise from compulsion rather than sacrifice. This irony does not reduce him to a fraud; instead, it makes his sincerity more tragic, because he is utterly committed to an achievement whose basis even he has misunderstood.

Characterisation is built through fixation rather than broad biography. The hunger artist has almost no identity outside his profession. His body, vocation, pride, and grievance merge until fasting becomes his entire mode of existence. The impresario is characterised through theatrical gestures and manipulative explanations, the public through changing collective appetites, and the circus staff through institutional indifference. Most secondary figures function as social types—the butchers, doctors, young women, spectators, attendants, and supervisor—rather than as individuated personalities. This reinforces the story’s allegorical quality and isolates the protagonist within systems of entertainment and judgment.

The pacing is expansive at first and increasingly compressed. Early sections dwell on the customs of the performance, the watchers, and the fortieth-day ceremony, creating a sense of ritual repetition. The narrative then accelerates through the decline of public interest and the move to the circus. Once the artist is forgotten, time becomes unmeasured: the day counter stops, “many days” pass, and his supreme achievement becomes indistinguishable from neglect. The structure thus traces a movement from celebrity to marginality, from obsessive observation to complete invisibility, and from strictly regulated fasting to an unlimited fast that no one notices.

Dialogue is sparse until the death scene. Most of the story is mediated by narration, reported thought, and generalised social commentary. The final exchange with the supervisor is consequently stark and intimate. Its simple questions and answers strip away the elaborate public mythology surrounding the artist. The revelation that he fasted because he could find no food he liked functions as an anti-climax, confession, and existential key at once. Even here the language remains ambiguous: his “art” may represent genuine artistic purity, alienation, incapacity for ordinary pleasure, spiritual hunger, or an involuntary condition mistaken for achievement.

Imagery is organised around opposed states: hunger and appetite, emaciation and bodily abundance, confinement and freedom, artifice and instinct, visibility and neglect. The cage remains constant while its meaning changes from stage to forgotten storage space and finally to the panther’s enclosure. The hunger artist’s pale, skeletal, folded body contrasts with the panther’s vigorous body “almost to the point of bursting.” The artist must explain and defend his vocation, whereas the animal’s vitality communicates itself immediately through its teeth, throat, movement, and appetite. The closing image therefore supplies a forceful symbolic reversal: the crowd that could not sustain interest in self-denial is irresistibly drawn to embodied life.

The ending is characteristically unsentimental. The artist is buried with the straw, and the institution immediately reuses his cage. No moral is explicitly stated, but the juxtaposition with the panther crystallises the story’s central tensions. The style favours unresolved paradox over direct instruction: the hunger artist is authentic yet misguided, disciplined yet helpless, admired yet misunderstood, and finally liberated from imposed limits only through abandonment. Overall, the prose is lucid on the surface but structurally labyrinthine, combining realistic detail, bureaucratic logic, symbolic contrast, and deadpan absurdity to create a bleak meditation on art, recognition, self-deception, and the public’s changing appetite.

After the Civil War leaves her once-wealthy Southern family impoverished, Mary Myrover reluctantly takes a position teaching Black children. Despite inherited racial prejudices and strict social conventions, she grows devoted to her work and earns her pupils’ affection. One pupil, a poor Black girl named Sophy, becomes especially devoted to Mary, constantly helping her and bringing her flowers. Mary responds kindly but maintains the racial boundaries expected by her family and community.

Mary dies after a brief illness, and her embittered mother blames the strain of teaching Black children. She forbids Black people from entering the house or attending the funeral. Sophy brings yellow roses tied with a ribbon Mary once gave her, hoping to place them on her teacher’s coffin, but she is turned away from the house, effectively barred from the church, and excluded from the whites-only cemetery.

After watching the burial from outside the fence, Sophy sees Mary’s dog, Prince, beside the grave. She pushes the fading bouquet through the bars and asks him to take it to Mary. Prince carries the roses to the grave and lays them among the other flowers, allowing Sophy’s final tribute to reach the teacher she loved.

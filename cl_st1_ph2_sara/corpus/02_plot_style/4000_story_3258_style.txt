The story uses a lucid, economical nineteenth-century realist style that gradually shades into the comic-gothic. Its narration is controlled and largely chronological, moving from the coffin-maker’s relocation, through the neighbour’s feast and the nocturnal visitation, to the final awakening. Concrete domestic and occupational details—the sacred images, crockery, funeral clothes, coffins, signs, prices and business arrangements—give the setting a solid everyday reality. This practical foundation makes the supernatural episode more vivid while preserving the story’s dry humour.

The narrator is omniscient, urbane and conspicuously present. Direct addresses and literary asides, such as the appeal to the “enlightened reader” and the refusal to describe clothing according to “the usual custom of modern novelists,” create a playful relationship with the audience. Such interventions parody literary conventions and prevent the narrative from becoming solemn or melodramatic. The narrator frequently reports absurd, mercenary or morally questionable behaviour in an even, decorous manner, allowing irony to emerge through understatement rather than overt judgment.

The diction is formal but accessible, with an old-fashioned social vocabulary tied to trades, ranks, clothing, religion and urban life. Polite formulas such as “respected neighbour,” “little father” and “do me the favour” establish the manners of the period and often become comic when applied to grotesque situations. Death is described through a mixture of professional euphemism and blunt physical detail. The coffin-maker thinks of funerals primarily as transactions, while the dead are rendered through waxen skin, sunken mouths, rotten cloth and rattling bones. This contrast between ceremonious language and bodily decay is central to the story’s black comedy.

Sentences tend to be moderately long, balanced and syntactically orderly, often linking action, description and explanation in a single flowing movement. Lists are used to create social and material density: furnishings are placed in their proper corners, funeral goods are inventoried, toasts accumulate, and the dead appear in an assortment of caps, uniforms and holiday caftans. Repetition and escalation give several scenes their comic rhythm, particularly the succession of drinks at the wedding feast and the increasingly alarming arrival of Adrian’s former customers. Shorter sentences and exclamations appear at moments of surprise or panic, accelerating the pace during the supernatural climax.

Dialogue is brisk, socially revealing and often built around occupational jokes. Characters speak less to disclose deep interiority than to expose habits, prejudices and economic interests. Schultz’s geniality, Yourko’s coarse joke, the servant’s common sense and Adrian’s wounded pride are all established efficiently through speech. Adrian’s own dialogue shifts from taciturn calculation to drunken indignation and finally to relieved practicality, tracing his emotional movement without extended psychological analysis.

Characterization is concise and satirical. Adrian is not presented as evil, but as a tradesman whose gloomy temperament and commercial instincts perfectly suit his profession. His concern over damaged funeral garments and his hope that a dying woman will restore his profits reveal his self-interest through matter-of-fact observation. Minor figures are drawn with a few memorable strokes, sometimes through comic similes: tradespeople gather “like ravens,” a bookbinder’s face resembles red morocco, and the skeleton’s boots rattle “like pestles in mortars.” These images are vivid, slightly grotesque and tonally playful.

The tonal range combines social comedy, irony, macabre fantasy and mild moral satire. The wedding feast is boisterous and farcical; Adrian’s resentment introduces darker emotional pressure; the gathering of corpses briefly becomes genuinely uncanny; and the awakening dissolves the terror into domestic relief. Even at the height of horror, grotesque details remain comic, especially the shabby skeleton who complains about having been sold a deal coffin instead of an oak one. The supernatural scene functions as an exaggerated reckoning with Adrian’s professional dishonesty, yet the ending avoids a heavy moral pronouncement. His immediate request for tea restores ordinary life and leaves any reform uncertain.

Structurally, the story relies on symmetry and reversal. Adrian feels insulted when invited to drink to his “customers,” retaliates by inviting the dead to his own house, and then appears to receive precisely the company he requested. The final revelation that the funeral and visitation were part of a drunken dream retrospectively collapses the apparent sequence of events. This twist is prepared by alcohol, sleep and Adrian’s preoccupation with Trukhina’s expected death, so it feels psychologically motivated rather than arbitrary. The ending is concise and anti-climactic in a deliberate way, exchanging gothic catastrophe for tea, daughters and morning bells.

Overall, the style is restrained, polished and ironic. It draws its effects from contrasts: ordinary commerce against mortality, polite hospitality against supernatural invasion, realistic detail against dream logic, and apparent moral seriousness against comic deflation. The prose avoids elaborate emotional rhetoric, preferring precise observation, narrative wit and rapid scene construction. The result is a compact comic tale in which bourgeois routine and death coexist with unsettling—and amused—familiarity.

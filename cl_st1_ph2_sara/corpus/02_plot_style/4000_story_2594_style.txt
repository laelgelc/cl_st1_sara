The story adopts a first-person confessional mode, presented as a hastily written final testimony by a psychologically shattered narrator. Its opening announces his intended suicide and morphine dependence, creating a fatalistic frame in which the outcome is known but the cause remains concealed. This retrospective structure gives the narrative the quality of a found document, while the closing return to the moment of writing collapses past and present into an immediate final crisis. The narrator is educated, observant and broadly rational, yet his extreme distress makes his reliability uncertain. His repeated efforts to supply natural explanations for what he encounters create tension between empirical reasoning, hallucination and supernatural truth.

The dominant mode is cosmic and maritime horror. Terror arises less from conventional violence than from immensities of space and time, the fragility of human reason, and the possibility that ancient nonhuman civilisations exist beneath the sea. The landscape is repeatedly described as empty, monotonous, silent and immeasurable, making scale itself oppressive. Humanity is characterised as “puny,” recent and vulnerable when set against geological ages and submerged powers. The grotesque creature provides the narrative’s clearest shock, but the deeper horror lies in what it implies: a whole primordial order that predates human history and may eventually overwhelm it.

The diction is elevated, archaic and intensely Latinate. Words such as “prodigious,” “declivity,” “surcease,” “thither” and “whilst” give the account a formal, old-fashioned register, while biblical and mythological terms lend grandeur to the horror. The prose relies heavily on charged adjectives—“hellish,” “putrid,” “Stygian,” “Cyclopean,” “loathsome,” “detestable”—and on superlative or absolute formulations such as “unending plain,” “fathomless chaos” and “eternal night.” This lexical excess conveys a mind struggling to describe sights beyond ordinary categories. Strategic vagueness is equally important: phrases such as “less describable things,” “sensations I cannot express” and “features less pleasant to recall” withhold precise detail, encouraging the reader’s imagination to supply what language allegedly cannot.

Sentences are often long, periodic and clause-heavy, with semicolons, subordinate phrases and cumulative descriptions delaying their main point. This syntax produces a stately, antiquarian cadence during exposition and landscape description. Parallel constructions and repeated negatives—“nothing within hearing, and nothing in sight”—reinforce emptiness and monotony. At moments of revelation, however, the rhythm tightens dramatically. “Then suddenly I saw it” is blunt and isolated compared with the surrounding prose, and the final fragments—“God, that hand! The window! The window!”—represent the collapse of elaborate narration into panic. Punctuation therefore helps trace the narrator’s movement from controlled testimony to mental disintegration.

The imagery is overwhelmingly sensory but weighted toward sight, texture and smell. Black slime, rotting carcasses, scorching light, sticky soil, flabby lips and a slippery body create a vocabulary of decay, contamination and bodily disgust. Darkness and whiteness are placed in sharp contrast: the black mire and abyss form a backdrop against which the moonlit monolith appears with uncanny clarity. The sea is transformed from an open route of escape into a concealed underworld, while the risen ocean floor violates the expected boundary between visible land and hidden depth. Silence is treated almost as a physical force, and the absence of birds, waves and ordinary life makes the setting feel cosmically abandoned.

Allusion enlarges the story’s scale and gives the narrator’s terror a learned frame of reference. References to *Paradise Lost*, Satan, Styx, Polyphemus, Doré, Poe, Bulwer, Neanderthal man and the Philistine god Dagon connect the experience with epic, classical mythology, archaeology, anthropology and Gothic literature. Scientific language concerning volcanic upheaval, ocean floors, ethnology and extinct species initially offers rational authority, but it ultimately confirms rather than dispels the impossible. The fusion of scholarly speculation with myth is central to the style: modern knowledge is shown to be partial, conventional and incapable of containing primordial reality.

The narrative develops through controlled escalation. It moves from wartime capture and solitary drifting to an impossible environmental transformation, then to a journey across the exposed seabed, the discovery of archaeological evidence, the sighting of a living being, rescue, addiction and final persecution. Each stage widens the implications of the previous one. The carved images prepare for the creature itself, while the narrator’s observation that the figures are badly proportioned foreshadows the revelation that they may not be imaginary gods but life-sized representations. The climactic apparition is described only briefly, preserving its force and avoiding prolonged explanation. Afterward, the story shifts from physical adventure to recurring trauma and apocalyptic speculation.

The tone combines dread, revulsion, awe and intellectual fascination. Even at the height of fear, the narrator experiences “the scientist’s or archaeologist’s delight,” producing a characteristic conflict between the desire to know and the danger of knowledge. Frequent anticipatory statements—“I dare not speak,” “the mere remembrance makes me grow faint,” and “I think I went mad then”—continually signal that revelation will damage the mind. The ending remains deliberately ambiguous: the noise at the door may be a real creature, a hallucination, or the final manifestation of guilt and addiction. Nevertheless, the abrupt ending strongly privileges immediate terror over rational closure.

Overall, the style is ornate, claustrophobic and cumulative, despite the vastness of its settings. It uses a cultivated but unstable narrator, antiquarian vocabulary, geological and mythological scale, grotesque marine imagery, strategic indescribability and a frame of doomed testimony to produce cosmic horror. Its signature movement is from reasoned explanation toward linguistic and psychological breakdown: the narrator begins by arranging evidence for posterity and ends with repeated exclamations as the incomprehensible seemingly enters the room.

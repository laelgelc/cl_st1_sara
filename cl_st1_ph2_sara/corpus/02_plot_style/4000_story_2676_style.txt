The story uses a restrained modernist realism in which an ordinary office visit gradually opens onto grief, repression, cruelty, and mortality. Its setting is rendered through a few sharply selected material details—the green leather armchair, bright patterned carpet, elaborate furniture, glowing electric heaters, photograph, inkpot, and blotting paper. These objects do more than establish place: they expose the boss’s self-satisfaction, preserve traces of the dead son, and eventually provide the instruments for the central symbolic action. The polished comfort of the office contrasts with bodily frailty, wartime graves, and the fly’s violent death.

The narration is third-person but mobile and intimate, moving chiefly into the boss’s consciousness while briefly adopting Woodifield’s and the office messenger’s perspectives. Free indirect discourse allows thoughts to merge with narration without formal attribution, as in judgments about Woodifield being “on his last pins” or the fly being a “plucky little devil.” This produces psychological closeness while retaining ironic distance. The narrator can reproduce a character’s assumptions and idiom yet quietly reveal their vanity, evasiveness, or limited self-knowledge. Occasional generalising statements, such as the comparison between human attachment and a tree clinging to its last leaves, broaden the scene into a meditation on ageing and loss.

The diction mixes plain conversational English with vivid, sometimes grotesque figurative language. Woodifield’s speech is marked by contractions, colloquialisms, repetitions, and phonetic touches—“Y’are,” “ain’t,” “have yer”—which convey age, class, and a piping, weakened manner. The boss’s language is brisker and more authoritative, filled with clichés of business, masculinity, and endurance: “still at the helm,” “learning the ropes,” “Never say die.” These stock phrases become ironic because the story steadily undermines the emotional competence and resilience they claim. Descriptions frequently reduce people to bodies or animals: Woodifield peers like a baby from a pram, the messenger behaves like an expectant dog, and the boss is represented through his “fat body,” “thick wrist,” and “firm heavy steps.” Such imagery unsettles social hierarchy by placing human beings within a vulnerable animal world.

Sentence structure shifts with the emotional movement. Dialogue and social observation use short, lively exchanges, interruptions, exclamations, and pauses. Ellipses and dashes register failing memory, hesitation, and subjects that cannot be spoken directly. The boss’s recollection of his son expands into longer, cumulative sentences and rhetorical questions, mimicking an old, rehearsed narrative of grief. By contrast, the fly episode is narrated through precise, sequential clauses that slow time and magnify tiny physical actions. Repetition—“over and under,” successive cleanings, repeated drops of ink—creates a ritualistic rhythm and steadily increases tension. Sentence fragments and abrupt declarations, especially “It was” and “The fly was dead,” give the climax a stark finality.

The characterisation is indirect and psychologically ironic. Woodifield appears diminished and childlike, controlled by “the wife and the girls,” yet his wandering conversation inadvertently destabilises the more powerful boss. His movement from graves to the price of jam combines bathos with emotional realism: commercial irritation becomes a manageable substitute for confronting mass death. The boss is defined by possessiveness, status, and control. He enjoys displaying his office, dispensing forbidden whisky, ordering subordinates, and imagining his son as the continuation of his business. His grief is sincere but also bound up with inheritance, identity, and thwarted ambition. Most importantly, he attempts to command grief itself: he schedules solitude, arranges to weep, and is disturbed when the expected tears do not come.

The story’s structure is tightly patterned around memory and forgetting. Woodifield initially cannot remember what he came to say, then recalls the graves; the boss tries to recover his grief, becomes distracted by the fly, and finally forgets what he had been thinking about. This circular design makes forgetting both a symptom of age and a defence against suffering. The narrative also moves from large public catastrophe to minute private violence. The son’s death in war remains offstage and mediated through a photograph, telegram, and grave, while the fly’s death occurs in painstaking close-up. The miniature scene condenses the larger themes of arbitrary power, endurance, trauma, and destruction.

The fly functions as the story’s governing symbol without being reduced to one fixed meaning. Its repeated recovery recalls soldiers surviving ordeals, the boss’s ideal of masculine perseverance, and his own claim that he could never recover from bereavement. At the same time, the boss occupies the position of an indifferent power that repeatedly inflicts suffering merely to test resilience. His admiration for the fly does not prevent him from killing it; indeed, admiration becomes the justification for further torment. This creates a disturbing parallel between sentimental praise for courage and the systems that demand, exploit, or destroy that courage. The ink may also recall the telegram and bureaucratic machinery through which wartime death entered the office.

The tone moves from comic and gently satirical to elegiac, sinister, and finally blank. Early humour arises from Woodifield’s infantilisation, the whisky episode, and the absurd complaint about jam. That humour never disappears completely, but it darkens as the boss’s emotional failure and casual sadism emerge. Pathos is controlled rather than openly sentimental: the narrative allows the boss’s memories their force while exposing the self-dramatising quality of his grief. The ending refuses explanation or moral commentary. His demand for fresh blotting paper is an attempt to erase the physical evidence and restore office order, while his inability to remember his son leaves the story on a note of repression, emotional exhaustion, and chilling irresolution.

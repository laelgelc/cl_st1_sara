Mr. Saunders learns that his son Dick frequently returns home late and belongs to a gambling club. Convinced that Dick is falling into vice—and angry that his wife has concealed the situation—he orders Dick to wait at home for a confrontation. Dick tells his mother that although the club permits gambling, he neither gambles nor drinks; his employer, banker Julius Hammond, actually arranged and paid for his membership. Nevertheless, Dick must attend an important appointment at the club and leaves despite his father’s demand.

When Dick fails to return, Mr. Saunders sets out for the club, while his frightened wife sends a telegram warning Dick that his father is coming. Hammond intercepts the message and welcomes Mr. Saunders. Speaking ominously about Dick’s poor prospects and the ten thousand he stands to lose, Hammond leads the father to believe that his son has disgraced himself financially. The misunderstanding is resolved when Mr. Saunders discovers that Dick is competing in the deciding match of a major amateur billiards tournament and that Hammond has wagered ten thousand on him.

Dick initially appears certain to lose, but he stages a remarkable recovery, demonstrating exceptional skill, composure, and sportsmanship before defeating his opponent and retaining the club’s cup. Seeing his son’s courage and talent transforms Mr. Saunders’s anger into admiration. He tells Dick that he is proud of him and intends to report the victory to his mother, while Dick asks to walk home with him, signaling a reconciliation between father and son.

The story is a comic first-person character narrative built around an unreliable, self-justifying voice. Jane tells the tale in a confidential, conversational manner, repeatedly appealing to the reader with phrases such as “I put it to you” and “if you’ll believe me.” She presents herself as practical, sensible and economically clear-sighted, but her own account steadily exposes her as jealous, calculating and dishonest. Much of the humour arises from this gap between her moral self-image and her plainly described behaviour. Even after locking Mattie in the church, fabricating a rejection and attempting to take Jack for herself, she insists that she acted “for the best.” The narration therefore delivers its judgement indirectly: Jane condemns herself without understanding that she is doing so.

The diction is colloquial, rural and historically marked. Non-standard grammar—“if they was,” “when the letter come,” “I says,” “leave me go,” “he don’t”—helps establish Jane’s social background and gives the prose the quality of spoken reminiscence. Idioms, clichés and proverbial expressions are frequent, including “put his foot down,” “all’s well that ends well,” “one wife is as good as another,” and “Satan to find some mischief still for idle hands to do.” Jane also relies on domestic and material comparisons: a face is “as long as a fiddle,” Jack frowns “like a July thunderstorm,” and she describes matching expressions to intentions as one might match ribbon to cloth. This figurative language is concrete rather than lyrical and reflects her practical, household-centred outlook.

The syntax imitates spontaneous speech. Sentences often proceed through long chains of clauses joined by “and,” “but,” “so” and “for,” with parenthetical qualifications, afterthoughts and comic corrections. Jane regularly weakens or revises incriminating statements—locking Mattie in was “a sort of joke,” “at least, I think so”—and such hesitations reveal motives she would rather conceal. Repetition gives the narration oral momentum, especially in constructions such as “and she… and she…” or “says I… says he.” Short sentences and abrupt exclamations punctuate moments of discovery or reversal, while rhetorical questions allow Jane to recruit the reader into her warped reasoning.

Dialogue occupies a large proportion of the story and is brisk, idiomatic and socially revealing. Speakers are differentiated less through elaborate description than through what they value and how directly they speak. Mattie’s language is emotionally simple and sincere; her father’s is paternal and proverbial; Jack’s becomes forceful and impatient under pressure; Jane’s is evasive, insinuating and strategically incomplete. She often avoids explicit lies at first, instead claiming to infer Mattie’s “mind” or withholding information until Jack supplies the conclusion she wants. This conversational manipulation is central to both the plot and the character comedy.

The structure is linear and anecdotal, beginning with Jane’s declaration that romantic love is nonsense and then furnishing a story that disproves her. Early exposition establishes the opposition between Mattie’s constancy and Jane’s materialism. Jack’s letter acts as the principal catalyst, and the narrative then accelerates through the secret meeting, Jane’s impersonation, the proposal and the forgotten imprisonment. Suspense is generated by practical objects and spatial arrangements: the church key, the prayer-book note, the ash copse, the locked door and the bell rope. The climax is theatrically visual and audible, with Mattie ringing the church bell until the community arrives. The final reconciliation is swift, while Jane’s exclusion from supper supplies a compact comic punishment.

Material detail is used as a form of characterization and moral contrast. Jane notices savings, prospects, bank deposits, dresses, bonnets, gloves, jewellery and Jack’s gold chain and diamond ring. Mattie, by contrast, scarcely registers Jack’s fortune and wears the old dress associated with their courtship rather than her fashionable new clothes. These details dramatize the difference between acquisitiveness and affection without requiring overt authorial commentary. Domestic work—jam-making, berry-picking, butter-selling and preparing tea—grounds the action in everyday rural life, while the church, farm, village and copse create a close communal setting in which secrecy is difficult to maintain.

The dominant tone is dryly comic, satirical and ironic rather than sentimental, even though the plot centres on faithful love. Jane dismisses Mattie as foolish, yet Mattie’s emotional directness repeatedly appears generous and perceptive beside Jane’s scheming. The story also uses comic disproportion: Jane treats attempted romantic theft as reasonable household management, describes deception as “acting for the best,” and responds to exposure by complaining that others cannot take a joke. Understatement sharpens the effect, especially in the closing lines, where “I had had all the supper I wanted” compresses humiliation, resentment and defeat into a seemingly practical observation.

The principal themes are love versus economic calculation, self-deception, envy, fidelity and moral blindness. The story initially appears to invite debate over whether marriage should be based on affection or financial security, but its deeper interest lies in the psychology of rationalization. Jane can interpret every event in a way that preserves her sense of virtue, while the reader sees that her supposed practicality masks covetousness. The ending does not reform her: she remains incapable of honest self-recognition. This unresolved blindness gives the conclusion its final ironic force and preserves the consistency of the narrative voice.

The story adopts the manner of a late nineteenth-century comic folk tale, combining an urbane, literate narrator with heavily phoneticized African American dialogue. The third-person narrator is omniscient and socially observant, moving freely between external action, communal opinion, and individual motives. The narration often maintains a pose of calm objectivity while quietly exposing vanity, resentment, self-interest, and faulty reasoning. Formal constructions such as “as might have been expected” and “strange to say” lend the account a mock-chronicle quality: absurd events are reported with the composure of serious local history.

The dominant tone is genial, ironic, and satirical. Humor arises less from overt jokes than from incongruities: an illiterate preacher expounds a Bible he cannot read; a domestic quarrel becomes doctrine; a congregation tries to settle theology through arithmetic; and an indefensible claim is preserved through an ingenious logical trick. The narrator’s understatement sharpens the comedy, especially when describing outrage, social rivalry, and threats of disorder in ceremonious language. Characters are treated with amused distance rather than psychological intensity, although the humor can also be patronizing because it is filtered through the racial and social conventions of its period.

Narrative prose is expansive, balanced, and syntactically polished. Sentences are often long and multi-clausal, using qualifications, parenthetical comments, and carefully staged contrasts. The diction is formal and mildly elevated, particularly when trivial motives are described in abstract terms such as “fundamental doctrine,” “biblical authority,” or “deep-seated resentment.” This mock-serious register magnifies small village disputes into institutional crises. Transitional phrases guide the reader clearly through shifts in time and collective feeling, giving the story the orderly progression of an anecdotal case history.

Dialogue provides the strongest stylistic contrast. Speech is rendered through extensive eye dialect, altered spelling, elision, nonstandard grammar, and recurring oral formulas. Brother Peter’s sermons use repetition, direct address, rhetorical questions, parallel clauses, biblical references, and cumulative storytelling. His repeated appeals to “brethren” establish a persuasive cadence, while his improvisational scriptural interpretations reproduce the energy of oral preaching. Other speakers are differentiated chiefly by temperament and social position rather than by radically distinct verbal patterns: Susan Henry is forceful and argumentative, Aunt Rebecca laconic and cutting, and Peter ceremonious, evasive, and verbally resourceful. This representation of Black speech is historically marked and stereotyped; the narrator’s standard English remains the implicit prestige form, creating a hierarchy between narrating voice and represented speakers.

The story is structured around escalation and reversal. It begins with concise exposition of Peter’s social role and domestic circumstances, introduces the provocative sermon, then widens into collective reaction, debate, and threatened expulsion. The middle section delays the confrontation through community meetings and Peter’s absence, allowing indignation and curiosity to accumulate. The final church scene narrows the focus again and proceeds through a sequence of apparently harmless questions. The punch line arrives as a syllogistic reversal: because Scripture explicitly says that seven devils were cast out of one woman and does not say the same of others, Peter concludes that all other women still possess them. The congregation’s silence, followed by the request for a hymn, supplies a dry, economical ending and confirms that rhetorical agility has defeated literal scrutiny.

Characterization is comic and socially functional. Peter is earnest, stubborn, opportunistic, and gifted at improvisation; his theology grows from personal grievance, but his authority survives because he understands performance and audience psychology. Aunt Rebecca acts as both the cause of the sermon and a figure strong enough to make its misogyny self-defeating. Susan’s opposition is partly principled and partly rooted in wounded pride, which lets the satire encompass both preacher and protesters. The congregation operates almost as a collective character, with shifts in naming—especially from the respectful clerical title to the familiar “Uncle Pete”—serving as a compact indicator of status and approval.

The thematic method is comic rather than didactic. The story satirizes scriptural literalism, improvised authority, gender antagonism, crowd behavior, and the ease with which private grievances can be disguised as universal truths. It also shows that communal authority depends as much on confidence, verbal performance, and clever framing as on knowledge. Biblical material is localized through references to familiar crops, prices, work, and regional apples, turning sacred history into rural folklore. Overall, the style blends polished narrative irony, broad characterization, oral-performance rhythms, dialect comedy, and a tightly prepared final paradox.

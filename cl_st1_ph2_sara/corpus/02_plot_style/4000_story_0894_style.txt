The story adopts a brisk, comic, early twentieth-century magazine style, combining workplace satire, romantic farce, and affectionate character study. Its omniscient narrator is conspicuously present: confident, knowing, and amused by both the characters and the social world they inhabit. The narration frequently supplies judgments before the characters can reveal themselves, as in the mock-official “blue prints and specifications” that introduce Tidbury Epps through clipped categories such as “Weight: feather. Nose: stub. Eyes: apologetic.” This pseudo-bureaucratic opening mirrors Tidbury’s clerical occupation while reducing him, humorously, to an inventory of negligible qualities. The comedy is sharp but not cruel. Tidbury is presented as absurdly meek and undistinguished, yet his kindness, diligence, and emotional vulnerability keep the reader’s sympathy firmly with him.

The prose alternates between rapid, fragmentary description and longer, rhythmically accumulated sentences. Short declarative units create caricature and comic emphasis: “Nature: kind. Disposition: amiable but subdued.” Elsewhere, parallel phrases and lists build momentum, particularly when Mr. Spingle dismisses Tidbury as having “No spirit. No nerve. No fire. No zip.” Repetition is a major structural device. Tidbury’s “high stool and low salary,” his “little figures” and “big black books,” and his pepper-and-salt suit recur as verbal motifs, fixing his static existence in the reader’s mind. Balanced contrasts—high and low, little and big, good man and good fellow—give the narration a polished epigrammatic quality.

The diction mixes formal narrative language with commercial slang, colloquial speech, journalistic shorthand, and mock-scientific terminology. Elevated or technical expressions such as “sartorial grandeur,” “inferiority complex,” “Puritanical complex,” and “latent fire” are placed beside phrases like “regular fella,” “knew the ropes,” “dud,” and “zip.” The disparity produces much of the humor. Psychoanalysis is treated playfully rather than seriously: Tidbury’s inhibitions are diagnosed with fashionable terminology, then traced to Aunt Elvira’s switches, cat-o’-nine-tails, and punitive moral creed. Likewise, grand historical and literary comparisons are deliberately attached to trivial subjects. Mr. Spingle becomes the “Napoleon of Hatdom,” while the mundane straw-hat business acquires mock-imperial importance.

Figurative language is abundant, concrete, and intentionally extravagant. Characters are captured through comic similes and metaphors: Tidbury is “as inconspicuous as an oyster in a fifteen-cent stew,” has no more personality than a parsnip, and leads a life as empty as that of a Hubbard squash. Mr. Spingle’s face covers an “acre,” while his silk shirts suggest “an explosion in a paint factory” and the sunset in the Grand Canyon. Love “laid him violently by the heels,” jealousy becomes a monster with “torturing talons,” and Hydeman’s grooming advertises sophistication through a cluster of glittering surfaces. These images are vivid, accessible, and slightly cartoonish, supporting a style based on social types rather than psychological realism.

Characterization proceeds through compressed physical detail, habitual behavior, clothing, and speech. Tidbury’s plain shirts, dark ties, apologetic eyes, and drooping shoulders embody his diffidence. Spingle’s expansive body, gaudy silk, and commercial self-importance make him a caricature of prosperous vulgarity. Hydeman’s slick hair, brilliant tie, diamond ring, cigarette gesture, and modish suit immediately identify him as a flashy urban rival. Martha is introduced through her round eyes, tilted head, and metropolitan aspirations. Minor details carry social meaning: clothes indicate confidence and rank, entertainment reveals sophistication, and a person’s ability to “show a buyer Broadway” matters more in business than conscientious work.

Dialogue is economical, idiomatic, and strongly differentiated by social role. The exchange between Spingle and Blatter resembles comic vaudeville patter, with candidates proposed and rejected in quick succession: “Too young,” “Too old,” “Too careless,” “Too inexperienced.” Spingle’s contractions, slang, rhetorical questions, and sales-oriented maxims establish his breezy business persona. The dialogue also exposes the story’s central social irony: Tidbury possesses conventional virtues, but those virtues are treated as professional defects because success depends on sociability, display, and controlled dissipation. The house motto, “Our business friends are our personal friends,” is presented with understated skepticism, revealing friendship as a commercial technique.

The narrator often relies on ironic aphorisms and comic reversals. Tidbury “may be a good man, but he’s not a good fellow”; Aunt Elvira dies from “an excess of virtue”; and Tidbury knows that feeling happy must be wicked, only for the narrator to declare that “Love laughs at complexes.” Such lines condense the story’s themes into memorable formulas. The satire targets Puritan repression, corporate favoritism, masculine expectations, consumer culture, and the mythology of metropolitan sophistication. Yet it remains buoyant rather than bitter, using exaggeration and verbal sparkle to keep criticism entertaining.

The setting is evoked through selective period detail rather than extensive description. Brooklyn boarding-house life, wholesale offices, time clocks, Broadway entertainment, the Winter Garden, Central Park concerts, the Aquarium, trade papers, and fashionable men’s clothing establish an urban commercial world. References to “Nifty Straw Hattings,” advertising, expense accounts, and entertaining buyers give the workplace a specifically modern, sales-driven atmosphere. Rural Maine and Ohio function as restrained provincial backgrounds from which Tidbury and Martha have been drawn toward New York’s promise of advancement and excitement.

The narrative structure is linear but heavily shaped by explanatory backstory. It begins with a dossier-like introduction, moves into the office decision that denies Tidbury promotion, pauses to explain his childhood conditioning, and then develops the romantic conflict with Martha and Hydeman. Exposition is unusually direct, but the narrator’s wit prevents it from feeling inert. Pacing accelerates through summary—“He saw her home; he called on her; he brought her salted peanuts”—and slows around decisive scenes of humiliation, jealousy, or confrontation. The supplied passage ends on a small cliffhanger, with Hydeman thrusting two tickets under Tidbury’s nose, suggesting a serialized or magazine-fiction method of sustaining suspense.

Overall, the style is light, urbane, hyperbolic, and theatrically observant. It treats characters as comic social types while preserving enough tenderness to make Tidbury’s predicament emotionally engaging. Its most distinctive features are the intrusive humorous narrator, mock-formal cataloguing, rhythmic lists, recurring verbal motifs, slang-inflected dialogue, flamboyant similes, and ironic elevation of ordinary office and romantic troubles into miniature epic conflict.

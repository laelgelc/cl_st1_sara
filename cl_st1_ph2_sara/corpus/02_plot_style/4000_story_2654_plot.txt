James Madison rises from a studious, modest Virginia planter’s son to a leading architect of the United States government through discipline, integrity, and sustained hard work. After entering public service, he champions religious liberty, stronger national finances, and commercial reform. He helps convene the Constitutional Convention, plays a major role in drafting and defending the Constitution, and promotes its ratification through essays in *The Federalist*.

Madison later breaks with Alexander Hamilton’s expansive financial policies, joins Jefferson’s Republicans, and authors the Virginia Resolutions opposing the Alien and Sedition Acts. After serving as Jefferson’s secretary of state, he is elected president in 1808. His presidency is dominated by escalating conflicts with Britain over trade restrictions and the impressment of American sailors. Though reluctant to fight, he accepts the War of 1812, survives political opposition and the British burning of Washington, and welcomes the peace concluded at Ghent.

After approving measures to stabilize the postwar economy, Madison retires to Montpelier in 1817. He spends his final years in private life, making one last public appearance at Virginia’s 1829 constitutional convention. The narrative concludes that his success came less from extraordinary natural talent than from intense study, perseverance, self-mastery, and unwavering public virtue.

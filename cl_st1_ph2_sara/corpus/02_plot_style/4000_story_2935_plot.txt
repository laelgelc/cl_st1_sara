After discovering that his wife and closest friend, Henry Stockton, have betrayed him, prominent lawyer Clarke Randolph resolves to kill them both and then himself. He buys a revolver, prepares his estate, and imagines exposing and disgracing the guilty pair before taking revenge.

As he waits for them, however, Randolph’s rage gradually yields to reflection. He first spares his wife because killing her would be cowardly, then abandons plans to publicly shame or impoverish her, recognizing compassion, mercy, and self-control as marks of true humanity. He ultimately reaches the same conclusion about Stockton: killing him would be mere animal vengeance and would only deepen his wife’s suffering.

Determined to preserve their reputations and allow them a chance at happiness, Randolph fabricates an incurable illness as the reason for his suicide. He writes affectionate letters asking Stockton to protect his wife, revises his will to leave them his fortune jointly if they marry within two years, and then calmly shoots himself.

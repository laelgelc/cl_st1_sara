At a rowdy mining-camp dance, an idealistic Easterner mistakes the lively young woman Bismarck Anne for an innocent girl in danger of corruption. His Western companion explains that she is actually a notorious reveler. Anne drinks heavily, loses herself in the chaotic festivities, and wanders out of town after violence erupts in the dance hall.

She sleeps in a mountain dell and awakens feeling purified by the beauty of nature. When a handsome young rider approaches, however, his apparent goodness makes her suddenly conscious of her degraded life, and she collapses in remorse. Hearing an itinerant preacher recite scripture, she begs him to tell her how to reform. He distractedly quotes an instruction to work with one’s hands, which Anne takes as a divine answer.

Anne spends a week washing dishes at a hotel, but her initial sense of redemption gives way to boredom and drudgery. On the eighth day she abandons the work, returns to the monthly dance, and becomes the drunkest reveler there. Outwardly nothing has changed, but because she has glimpsed innocence and knowingly rejected her chance to change, her old behavior now carries a deeper moral guilt.

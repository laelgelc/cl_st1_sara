The story adopts the manner of a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century historical tale for children, combining patriotic instruction, domestic sentiment, and Christmas fantasy. Its narrator is overtly present, genial, and authoritative, addressing young readers directly as “Children” and repeatedly drawing them into the scene through questions, explanations, and invitations to imagine: “Can you not see her…?” This creates the effect of an adult storyteller speaking aloud to an attentive group. The narrator assumes a shared moral and national outlook, treating the Revolution as inherited family history and linking the hardships of 1776 to the freedoms enjoyed by later generations.

The point of view is broadly omniscient but remains emotionally close to Kitty, Harry, and Mrs. Tracy. Historical events are filtered through the limited understanding of children and the anxieties of a household separated from its father. The narration moves easily between factual exposition about geography, troop positions, and Washington’s campaign and intimate domestic details such as knitting stockings, gathering nuts, talking to a doll, and saving a turkey. This juxtaposition makes large-scale history accessible by translating it into disruptions of family ritual: war matters because it may prevent a father from coming home for Christmas.

The diction is warm, decorous, and deliberately old-fashioned. Words and phrases such as “bosom,” “valiantly,” “merrymaking,” “goodly store,” “in a trice,” and “from whence” give the prose an elevated period flavour, while affectionate diminutives—“little people,” “dollie,” “small folks,” and “pretty little Fido”—soften it for children. Archaic spellings and historical terms, including “woollen,” “neighbours,” “shellbarks,” “Hessians,” and “red-jackets,” reinforce the setting. Child speech is represented through contractions and playful mispronunciations such as “‘sprised” and “mis’able,” giving Kitty a quaint, idealised childishness rather than strict realism.

The syntax alternates between long, expansive narrative sentences and short, lively dialogue. Expository passages often use layered clauses, parenthetical qualifications, and balanced constructions, lending the narrator a leisurely, conversational cadence. Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, and direct appeals maintain animation and intimacy. Dialogue is simple and character-revealing: Kitty is imaginative and sentimental, Harry practical and food-minded, Mrs. Tracy reassuring, and Captain Tracy morally serious. Repetition around the absent father, the postponed Christmas, the stockings, and the turkey creates a clear emotional pattern that young readers can follow.

Structurally, the story begins with a broad historical address, narrows to Bordentown and the Tracy household, builds suspense around Captain Tracy’s absence, and then reconnects the domestic plot to Washington’s victory at Trenton. The Christmas preparations provide the narrative clock. Anticipation gives way to disappointment, distant gunfire introduces the military climax, and the father’s return supplies the emotional resolution. Fido’s arrival adds a final sentimental episode in which compassion crosses enemy lines. The dead British officer is described as “a real gentleman,” allowing the story to condemn war’s suffering without complicating its patriotic celebration of the American cause.

The tone is affectionate, reassuring, sentimental, and openly didactic. Hardship is present—bleeding soldiers’ feet, anxious families, a dying officer—but violence is restrained and framed by courage, prayer, kindness, and reunion. Humour appears gently in Kitty’s plan to marry a candy-shop keeper, Harry’s fixation on turkey, and the mother’s answer that Santa Claus is on “the right side.” Santa is treated with playful narrative literalness while also appearing “in the form of” a farmer uncle, so fantasy and domestic reality coexist without friction.

Imagery centres on warmth and cold, home and battlefield. Roaring logs, rosy faces, sugar-plums, woollen stockings, and the waiting turkey contrast with snow, sleet, bleeding feet, damp clothing, and the night march. This contrast reinforces the home as a place of safety and reward while casting patriotic service as the sacrifice required to preserve it. The ending states the moral explicitly: the privations of the Revolutionary generation enabled their descendants to live as citizens of a free nation. Overall, the style is that of a fireside historical lesson—picturesque, accessible, emotionally demonstrative, nationally celebratory, and designed to teach history through the experiences of an idealised family.

The story is written as comic social realism, combining close observation of provincial life with genial satire, irony, and escalating farce. Its central perspective belongs largely to Mr Cowlishaw, an inexperienced outsider whose anxious, “finicking” sensibility makes the industrial Five Towns appear strange and overwhelming. At the same time, an assured third-person narrator repeatedly steps beyond Cowlishaw’s viewpoint to explain local habits, institutions, and hierarchies. This narrator is conspicuously present, addressing the reader through generalizations such as “one may say,” supplying mock-authoritative judgments, and treating the Five Towns as a distinctive civilization whose peculiarities require interpretation.

The narrative voice is urbane, knowing, and affectionately mocking. It creates humour by describing ordinary things in disproportionately grand language. Tramcars become “dreadful engines,” their vibrations become earthquakes and “seismic phenomena,” factory whistles are “steam muezzins,” an aching tooth is “colossal” and “monumental,” and a routine extraction resembles the charge of the Light Brigade. Such mock-heroic inflation is the story’s most characteristic device. The narrator also uses solemn formulations to deliver absurd observations, as when the excellence of a hotel is “proved” by its proprietor being mayor, or when a new dentist’s arrival supposedly cures every toothache for miles. The comedy is generally indulgent rather than cruel: Cowlishaw’s vanity, fear, and professional awkwardness are exposed, but his youth and earnestness keep him sympathetic.

The prose blends formal literary diction with colloquial speech and commercial or technical language. Elevated words such as “surcease,” “envisage,” “colloquy,” “veritably,” and “uncanniness” coexist with slang, dialect, newspaper rhetoric, dental terminology, football vocabulary, and the language of advertising. Institutional names are often given in full—“Five Towns Electric Traction Company, Limited”—to comic effect, suggesting a world proud of modern companies, civic offices, professional status, and public display. Parenthetical remarks, ironic qualifications, and precise but unnecessary statistics reinforce the narrator’s amused pedantry. Exact times, passenger capacities, family totals, and the estimated length of a tooth root lend pseudo-documentary solidity to increasingly ridiculous events.

Sentence structure is varied but often expansive. Long, cumulative sentences build crowded inventories of sounds, people, objects, and civic details, while brief sentences deliver comic reversals: “Then silence”; “It was not the last”; “There was no three.” Repetition is used rhythmically, especially in the sleepless-night sequence, where each fresh disturbance restarts Cowlishaw’s frustration. Parallel constructions and incremental lists create a sense of mechanical accumulation: trams, laughter, boots, furnaces, carts, clogs, whistles, and an alarm clock arrive one after another. The short emphatic sentences interrupting these passages function as punchlines and control the comic timing.

Dialogue is economical, strongly characterized, and socially revealing. Cowlishaw speaks in hesitant, polite, professional phrases, frequently struggling to maintain dignity. Rannoch is blunt, practical, and commercially shrewd, while Mrs Clowes is commanding, literal-minded, and impervious to professional advice. Regional and class identity are suggested through light phonetic rendering—“th’ fut-baw,” “Beg yer pardon,” “ye”—without allowing dialect to overwhelm readability. Verbal exchanges often become contests between professional etiquette and local common sense. Characters talk past one another, and Cowlishaw’s attempts at technical authority repeatedly collide with the patient’s certainty about what should be done.

Characterization depends on concise physical caricature, social placement, and behaviour under pressure. Cowlishaw’s fair hair, weak moustache, bachelorhood, and cathedral-town background immediately mark him as young, genteel, and insufficiently robust for Hanbridge. Rannoch’s missing teeth, durable clothes, and bargaining instinct establish him as both sporting hero and canny professional. Mrs Clowes is introduced through an accumulating catalogue of family connections and civic influence, making her almost an institution in herself. Her large personality is mirrored physically in her “august” mouth and monumental tooth. Minor figures—the barmaid, boots, commercial travellers, actors, and hotel staff—are sketched through occupational roles, adding to the crowded social texture.

The setting is not passive background but a major comic force. The Five Towns is presented as noisy, industrial, commercially vigorous, football-obsessed, and socially self-confident. Cowlishaw’s first night stages an initiation into its physical rhythms: trams shake the hotel, blast furnaces breathe, factory whistles summon workers, and traffic begins before dawn. What terrifies the outsider is scarcely noticed by the inhabitants. This contrast between metropolitan or southern gentility and industrial provincial resilience shapes much of the humour. Local pride is also satirized through football journalism, mayoral prestige, fashionable patronage, and the belief that civic importance extends to every aspect of life.

The plot is tightly organized as a sequence of linked comic trials occurring within one day. The opening night establishes Cowlishaw’s exhaustion and nervous fragility; the empty surgery then introduces his professional insecurity; Rannoch tests his commitment to etiquette; and Mrs Clowes tests his courage and practical competence. These episodes escalate toward the extraction scene, where the dentist succeeds technically but faints emotionally. Earlier details become causal elements in the climax: his sleeplessness, fearfulness, ambition, concern for reputation, and deference to local power all converge. The ending resolves the apparent disaster through an ironic social triumph. Mrs Clowes interprets his collapse not as weakness but as proof of her own overwhelming impressiveness, and this flattering misunderstanding promises to establish his career.

Recurring contrasts give the story thematic coherence: outsider versus community, professional theory versus practical demand, dignity versus bodily reality, and private anxiety versus public reputation. Modernity appears through electric transport, dental equipment, newspapers, professional journals, and commercial advertising, yet it remains unruly and faintly absurd. Professional ethics are treated with mock solemnity, while success ultimately depends less on expertise than on managing personalities and securing influential patronage. The style therefore combines regional portraiture, comedy of manners, and farce, using an intrusive ironic narrator, mock-epic diction, cumulative sensory detail, socially precise dialogue, and carefully delayed reversals to turn an ordinary dentist’s first day into a miniature civic adventure.

The story adopts a restrained Gothic mode in which supernatural dread is set against the precise, practical world of railway technology. Its atmosphere is claustrophobic, sombre, and increasingly fatalistic. The deep cutting, wet stone walls, black tunnel, red danger-light, cold wind, telegraph wires, and restricted strip of sky turn an industrial workplace into an underworld-like space cut off from ordinary nature. Concrete sensory details—oozing rock, earthy smells, vibrations, metallic signals, lamenting wind—make the setting physically credible while also giving it symbolic force. The tunnel suggests concealed danger and death, while the red light becomes a recurring emblem of warning, uncertainty, and doom.

The narrative is delivered retrospectively in the first person by an educated, observant visitor. His voice is formal, measured, and rational, often qualifying its own impressions through phrases that stress uncertainty, recollection, or conscientious accuracy. This creates the manner of a sober witness statement rather than an overtly sensational ghost story. The narrator repeatedly tries to explain events through coincidence, nervous illness, optical disturbance, and imagination, yet he candidly records his own fear and bodily reactions. The tension between his rational vocabulary and his involuntary dread sustains ambiguity until the conclusion. Even after the apparent supernatural pattern is completed, the narrator avoids expansive interpretation and closes by identifying a “coincidence,” preserving an outward tone of evidential restraint.

The diction is elevated and distinctly nineteenth-century, combining Latinate abstraction with sharply visual description. Words associated with attention and judgment—“observed,” “detected,” “speculated,” “question,” “circumstance,” and “impression”—reinforce the narrator’s analytical stance. By contrast, the signal-man’s haunting is expressed through a small set of urgent, plain phrases: “Halloa! Below there!”, “Look out!”, and “For God’s sake, clear the way!” Their simplicity and repetition make them incantatory. Railway terminology such as “Line,” “Danger-light,” “telegraphic instrument,” and “signal” grounds the uncanny events in a regulated technical system. The capitalization of terms connected with the apparition and railway duties lends them an ominous, almost allegorical importance.

Sentence structure alternates between long, carefully subordinated descriptive sentences and short bursts of dialogue. The longer sentences reproduce processes of observation and reasoning, often moving through several qualifications before reaching a judgment. They also slow the reader’s progress through the landscape, especially during the descent into the cutting. The dialogue is spare, interrogative, and frequently arranged as rapid exchanges of question and answer. This gives conversations the rhythm of an examination or deposition, with information disclosed in controlled stages. Dashes, parentheses, semicolons, and interruptions register hesitation, correction, and suppressed anxiety. Repeated constructions such as “There was…” and “I saw…” create a cumulative, almost ceremonial emphasis at moments of heightened tension.

The story’s structure is tightly patterned around repetition, delay, and return. It begins with an unexplained call, proceeds through a first encounter, deepens through a second nocturnal visit and the signal-man’s account of two earlier warnings, and culminates in a final return that reveals his death. Each repetition slightly alters the meaning of what came before. The opening words initially seem casual, then become associated with the spectre, and finally prove to be the engine-driver’s fatal warning. Likewise, the arm gesture passes from apparition to signal-man’s imitation to the driver’s real movement. This circular construction transforms ordinary details into retrospectively charged signs. Foreshadowing is therefore both verbal and visual, and the ending achieves its effect through exact correspondence rather than surprise alone.

Suspense develops through strategic withholding. The signal-man’s initial reluctance, unexplained glances toward the red light, reactions to a bell that the narrator cannot hear, and postponed confession all suggest danger without defining it. The narrative repeatedly offers temporary rational relief, only to undermine it with another detail. Even the climax briefly reverses itself: the narrator sees what appears to be the spectre, recognizes it as a living man, and only then learns that the signal-man has been killed. This oscillation between supernatural expectation and mundane explanation makes the final convergence more disturbing.

Characterization is economical and largely behavioral. The signal-man is established through his dark appearance, low voice, fixed gaze, exact performance of duty, and controlled but mounting distress. He is neither credulous nor incompetent; his intelligence, education, vigilance, and conscientiousness are repeatedly emphasized. This makes his testimony harder to dismiss and turns the haunting into a moral torment. His central fear is not merely personal death but failure in his responsibility for other lives. The narrator is characterized by curiosity, social confidence, rationalism, and ethical hesitation. His delayed decision to seek medical help introduces a subdued note of guilt, though the story does not make him directly culpable.

The dominant themes are failed communication, the limits of knowledge, and the burden of responsibility. The railway is a network designed to transmit warnings accurately, yet the supernatural warning is fatally incomplete: it announces danger without identifying its location, cause, or remedy. Bells, telegraphs, flags, voices, and gestures fill the narrative, but meaning repeatedly arrives too late. The signal-man’s predicament is especially cruel because he occupies a profession devoted to interpreting signs, yet confronts a sign he cannot use. The story also contrasts human systems of rational control with forces that remain obscure, suggesting that technological precision cannot abolish uncertainty, coincidence, or death.

Overall, the style is controlled rather than flamboyant, atmospheric without abandoning realism, and psychologically unsettling rather than graphically horrific. Its chief effects come from formal narration, exact physical detail, repetitive verbal motifs, incremental revelation, and the fusion of industrial modernity with Gothic fatalism.

The story uses a collective first-person voice, speaking predominantly through “we” rather than an individuated narrator. This communal perspective makes the twenty-six workers seem like a single consciousness shaped by confinement, exhaustion and resentment. Individual men rarely emerge from the group, and even the narrator remains anonymous. The effect is deliberately double-edged: the voice initially invites sympathy for the workers as exploited “living machines,” but later exposes their collective possessiveness and cruelty. Occasional shifts into general reflection—such as observations about silence, love, degradation and the human need to worship—give the narration a philosophical, authorial quality beyond ordinary eyewitness testimony.

Its dominant mode is social realism intensified by naturalistic and allegorical elements. Material conditions are rendered insistently: flour dust, tainted food, sweat, mud, smoke, low ceilings, stale heat and punishing hours of repetitive labor. The cellar functions both as a literal workplace and as an underworld or prison. Its heavy architecture appears to press physically upon the men, while the absence of sunlight symbolizes their exclusion from freedom, dignity and ordinary human fellowship. Industrial labor is presented as dehumanizing and mechanically repetitive, reducing the workers to “dull oxen,” statues and slaves.

The prose is highly sensory, with particular emphasis on oppressive sight, sound, smell and touch. Recurring noises—the scraping shovel, purring kettle and trembling fire—form a mechanical refrain that reproduces the monotony of the bakery. Personification turns the environment into an active tormentor: the oven becomes a fairy-tale monster with a fiery mouth and pitiless eyes, while its reflected flame seems to mock the men. These grotesque images coexist with lyrical similes, especially in descriptions of song, which rises like a wave or resembles a distant sunlit road. The contrast between sordid physical detail and elevated poetic imagery expresses the persistence of imagination within degradation.

Light and darkness supply the central symbolic contrast. The workers inhabit a damp, smoke-blackened “ditch,” whereas Tanya is associated with brightness, freshness and celestial imagery. Her face appears at the window like a brief visitation, and she “flashed” before them like a falling star. She becomes a substitute for the sun and eventually an idol onto whom the men project everything they believe remains pure in themselves. The religious vocabulary—“offering,” “idol,” “sacred custom,” “sanctity” and “godling”—makes their affection resemble worship, but the narration steadily reveals that this worship is founded on appropriation. They regard Tanya as “our Tanya,” and their supposed reverence depends upon her conforming to the role they have invented for her.

The diction moves between plain, concrete description and emotionally heightened abstraction. Words connected with heaviness, dampness, filth, suffocation and imprisonment recur throughout, building a claustrophobic lexical field. Repetition is conspicuous: “twenty-six,” “morning till night,” “as before,” and repeated references to walls, mud, heat and silence emphasize cyclical existence. At moments of emotional intensity, the language becomes incantatory, accumulating parallel phrases and repeated clauses. Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions and ellipses create an agitated, oral quality, while emphatic contrasts—sun and cellar, purity and filth, love and hatred, individual and crowd—organize the story’s moral structure.

Sentence construction alternates between long, cumulative descriptive sentences and abrupt dialogue. The longer sentences often join multiple clauses with repeated conjunctions, producing a breathless, crowded rhythm suited to the cellar and its unending labor. Parenthetical explanations and reflective digressions slow the action to examine motives or formulate broad claims about human behavior. In contrast, confrontations are rendered through short fragments, interruptions and repeated challenges. The soldier’s wager with the baker is especially clipped, accelerating the narrative and conveying masculine rivalry more than genuine concern for Tanya. Ellipses frequently indicate hesitation, emotional pressure, unfinished thought or ominous suspension; their unusually heavy use also gives the prose the character of an older translation.

Dialogue is socially marked and dramatically functional. The workers speak in rough, collective bursts, often laughing, jeering or reinforcing one another’s judgments. Tanya’s language is initially light and affectionate—“Little prisoners!”—but becomes terse and devastating at the climax. The soldier’s speech is boastful, self-satisfied and fixated on bodily strength, fashionable appearance and sexual conquest. His repeated claims about women expose a shallow identity based almost entirely on virility. The baker’s taunts turn Tanya into the object of a contest, while the workers’ subsequent discussions reveal how readily their devotion becomes voyeuristic curiosity.

The narrative structure is built around reversal. It begins by establishing the men as victims of economic oppression, then introduces Tanya as their sole source of tenderness and symbolic hope. The soldier’s arrival creates a test of the ideal they have imposed upon her. Suspense develops slowly through the wager, the passing of two weeks and the workers’ increasingly obsessive anticipation. The climactic revelation is visually staged through cracks in a wall, making the men literal spectators. When Tanya emerges happy, their ideal collapses, and they redirect the violence of their own humiliation toward her. The ending reverses the moral balance: the supposedly degraded prisoners become persecutors, while Tanya, whom they accuse of falling, displays the greatest dignity and freedom.

The story’s irony is severe and tragic. The men believe their love represents the best in them, yet it proves inseparable from ownership, pride and collective vanity. Their claim that Tanya has “robbed” them exposes their refusal to recognize her as an autonomous person. Even their curiosity about whether she will “resist” is portrayed as cold and knife-like, showing that they have already sacrificed her humanity to the preservation of their idol. Their abuse of her echoes the proprietor’s abuse of them: oppressed people reproduce the logic of domination when given a vulnerable target. The story therefore avoids sentimentalizing poverty or suffering; degradation may explain cruelty, but it does not absolve it.

Tone shifts are central to the effect. The opening is bleak, compassionate and claustrophobic; the passages about communal singing become mournfully lyrical; Tanya’s appearances bring warmth and gentle humor; the soldier introduces comic bravado edged with menace; and the wager produces nervous, almost feverish suspense. The climax turns savage and shame-filled, followed by a stark, subdued conclusion. The final return to the cellar restores the original setting but strips it of its only symbolic light. Tanya’s absence completes the circular structure: materially nothing has changed, yet the men have revealed and destroyed what they believed was best in themselves.

Overall, the style combines documentary realism, grotesque personification, lyrical symbolism, psychological analysis and moral parable. It is emotionally forceful without offering a simple division between innocence and guilt. Its most characteristic technique is to establish a collective identity through repetition and shared suffering, then expose the moral danger of that same collectivity when reverence hardens into possession and wounded pride erupts into mob cruelty.

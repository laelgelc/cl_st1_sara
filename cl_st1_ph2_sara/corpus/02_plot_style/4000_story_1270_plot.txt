Rose Whiston, a shy woman nearing thirty, lives a narrow, isolated life governed by her affectionate but rigidly class-conscious father. During their annual seaside holiday, they encounter Charles Burroughs, a cheerful red-haired clerk whom Mr. Whiston dismisses as coarse and vulgar because he talks about beer and tobacco. Charles courteously returns some wildflowers Rose has forgotten, prompting her to question her father’s prejudice against strangers.

On the train home, Charles and Rose become quietly attracted to each other. When her father briefly leaves the carriage, they exchange names and addresses. Rose initially feels liberated by this act but later suffers intense shame, torn between her upbringing’s strict rules of propriety and her conviction that Charles is honest and decent.

Rather than write secretly to Rose, Charles sends respectful letters to Mr. Whiston, asking to become acquainted with him so that he may know Rose. To protect her, he falsely claims to have learned their address by following them home. Outraged, Mr. Whiston tells Rose about the letters as proof that strangers must be kept at a distance. Rose then confesses that she voluntarily gave Charles their address and reveals that he was the person who returned her flowers.

Her admission forces Mr. Whiston to reconsider both his assumptions and his daughter’s independence. Though reluctant, he investigates Charles and allows him to explain himself. Rose patiently refrains from pressing her wishes, and her father gradually recognizes that Charles’s lie showed delicacy rather than dishonesty. At last, he sends Charles a formal invitation, opening the way for the young couple’s relationship.

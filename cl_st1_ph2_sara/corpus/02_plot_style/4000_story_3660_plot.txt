A foreign-language learner decides that mastering Italian verbs is essential because their shifting tenses cause confusion. He hires his versatile servant, a former horse doctor, to organize the conjugations as a colorfully uniformed military company. After rejecting the enormous verb “to love,” he chooses the supposedly manageable auxiliary “to have,” only to discover that it also has fifty-seven forms.

During an elaborate drill, squads representing different tenses parade and recite their conjugations. Finding the bare forms lifeless, the learner gives them objects: a dog produces no enthusiasm, but unprotected chickens inspire the peasants to perform the past definite with passion. When the servant introduces the imperfect—another form of “had,” used for prolonged or vaguely timed actions—the learner angrily dismisses the distinction as needless grammatical bureaucracy and orders that tense abolished. Before the next squad can appear, the noon gun and church bells sound, and labor rules abruptly end the lesson.

The story is written in a lyrical realist mode, combining close psychological observation with sensuous, often romantic description. Its prevailing mood is elegiac: age, mortality and vanished youth are treated with sadness, but the conclusion transforms that sadness into a gentle affirmation of continuity and renewal. The emotional register moves from scepticism and spiritual weariness through nostalgia and erotic memory toward reconciliation. Sentiment is strong and openly expressed, yet it is moderated by irony, social observation and concrete physical detail.

The narration is third-person and largely centred on Voznitsin’s consciousness. An omniscient narrator explains his condition with confident psychological generalisations, moving freely between external action, remembered experience and reflective commentary. The narrator sometimes distances himself from the protagonist through mild irony, as when Voznitsin’s observations are identified as conventional or “by no means original.” At other moments, the narrative enters his perceptions so closely that landscape, scent and bodily sensation become extensions of his emotional state. This flexible viewpoint gives the work both the breadth of an authorial tale and the intimacy of remembered experience.

The prose favours long, cumulative sentences built from parallel clauses, qualifications and enumerations. Semicolons and dashes allow perceptions and reflections to unfold gradually, while repeated conjunctions create a flowing, meditative rhythm. Short sentences and fragments are used selectively for emphasis, especially in moments of recognition, distress or dialogue. Repetition is an important device: phrases such as “old age” and “I love you” reproduce obsessive thought and emotional urgency. Ellipses frequently suggest hesitation, embarrassment, fading recollection or feeling too intense to be stated directly.

The diction is formal, literary and somewhat old-fashioned, with words and constructions associated with late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century translated prose. Social titles, patronymics, diminutives and place names preserve a specifically Russian setting. Affectionate forms such as “Kolya” and “Lenotchka” carry much of the emotional history between the characters. Some phrasing has the texture of translation, including elaborate abstractions, conspicuous explanatory clauses and occasional lexical oddities, but this contributes to the period character of the narrative.

Description is highly sensory. Smell is especially prominent: varnish, apples, fish, pitch, seaweed, fallen leaves, poplar buds and black-currant shoots act as triggers of memory and emotional recognition. Light and colour are equally important, with milky rose mist, golden sunlight, purple shores, sapphire eggs and flaming skies giving the natural world a luminous, almost sacramental quality. Such details are rarely decorative alone; they bind the present voyage to childhood, adolescent desire and Easter’s imagery of resurrection. The story repeatedly associates memory with bodily sensation rather than with abstract recollection.

Figurative language is abundant but accessible. Similes draw upon animals, plants, religion and natural cycles. Young people “carried on together like young puppies,” feminine coquetry is compared to a kitten sharpening its claws, and the Easter turkeys resemble antediluvian beasts. The more elevated metaphors describe youth as flowering, memory as a wound, age as spiritual blight and life as an eternal rising from the dead. Contrasts between comic and sublime imagery prevent the prose from becoming uniformly solemn.

Characterisation proceeds through accumulated physical detail, habitual gesture, dialogue and retrospective summary. Voznitsin is introduced not through dramatic action but through an extended diagnosis of middle age: his narrowing pleasures, suspicion, weakened responsiveness to beauty and newly personal fear of death. Elena is first seen as an ordinary, ageing woman, then gradually overlaid with the image of the lively girl she once was. The daughter functions as a visible continuation of the mother, allowing the protagonist to recognise recurrence without confusing it with literal restoration. Physical ageing is treated frankly—grey hair, freckles, faded colouring and white hair—but without cruelty.

Dialogue is naturalistic in its hesitations and social courtesies, though it also serves an overt thematic purpose. Interruptions, diminutives, repeated phrases and playful reproaches establish intimacy more effectively than exposition alone. Elena’s revival of “Nasty, horrid boy” links present conversation directly to the remembered courtship. Much of the speech is restrained and lightly humorous, while Voznitsin’s final declaration becomes aphoristic and philosophical. This movement from casual reminiscence to explicit meditation is characteristic of the story’s design.

The structure is circular and carefully patterned. It begins with a failed attempt to recover the past by revisiting physical places; those altered locations leave Voznitsin emotionally cold. The genuine recovery occurs unexpectedly during travel, through a living person rather than a preserved setting. The sea voyage provides a transitional, liminal space between old age and renewed feeling. The Easter setting reinforces this pattern: the adolescent kiss took place on Easter night, and the present reunion occurs during the same festival, turning private memory into a secular version of death and resurrection.

Pacing is initially leisurely and expository, with substantial attention to Voznitsin’s mental condition, itinerary and surroundings. The recognition scene introduces a mild narrative suspense, after which the story expands into a long retrospective sequence. This embedded memory is the emotional centre of the work and slows time through close descriptions of touch, scent, embarrassment and desire. The return to the present is comparatively brief, producing a quiet resolution rather than a dramatic climax.

The treatment of adolescent sexuality is sensuous but idealised. Bodily contact, breasts, waist, perfume, trembling and perspiration are described directly, yet always through the confused reverence of first desire. The narration preserves an awareness of awkwardness and unequal feeling: the girl permits affection only ambiguously and later sets a boundary. The remembered episode therefore combines rapture, shame, comedy and loss rather than presenting youthful love as wholly reciprocal.

The dominant motifs are age and youth, death and renewal, memory and alteration, recognition and mistaken estrangement. The story initially questions whether nostalgia can restore emotional life, then answers that the past survives not in unchanged buildings or exact repetition but in relationships, descendants and influence. Its final philosophy is organic and interconnected: individuals disappear, while their bodies, thoughts, actions and talents continue in others. The resulting style may be characterised as psychologically observant, nostalgic, sensuous, gently ironic and ultimately humanistic, with realism gradually opening into symbolic and spiritual affirmation.

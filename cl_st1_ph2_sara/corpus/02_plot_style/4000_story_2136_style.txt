The story uses a clubbable, anecdotal first-person style in which an observant male insider recounts a colonial social mishap while repeatedly admitting that his knowledge is partial. The narrator speaks for a collective “we” as often as for himself, presenting the isolated Anglo-Indian Station as a small, gossip-driven community with its own assumptions, rituals, and slang. His tone combines worldly confidence with self-mockery: he offers maxims about courtship and women, then undercuts his authority by insisting that men are blind in such matters and that the “woman’s version” remains inaccessible. Direct addresses such as “Would you believe it” and “You must not laugh at this” make the tale resemble an after-dinner story told to a familiar audience.

The narrative voice is ironic, aphoristic, and socially satirical. Characters are introduced through brisk verdicts rather than extended psychological analysis: Saumarez is vain, efficient, and “summary” by nature; the sisters are assessed according to the Station’s marriage-market judgments. Bureaucratic language is humorously transferred to romance when Saumarez “drafted his Resolution” and formed a “Select Committee of One.” Similar incongruities animate the comparisons with ponies, polo, official councils, and military staff. The narrator’s generalisations about gender are deliberately sweeping and period-bound, reflecting the masculine conventions and prejudices of the colonial milieu while also exposing their limitations.

The diction shifts effectively between conversational plainness and heightened descriptive prose. Social passages rely on colloquial expressions, parenthetical qualifications, dry understatement, and local or institutional terms such as “Civilian,” “Station,” “Hills,” “pagri,” and “rupees.” In the storm sequence, the language becomes sensory, kinetic, and near-apocalyptic. Verbs such as “lashing,” “roaring,” “whirling,” “thrashing,” and “spurting” give the weather violent agency, while images of the Day of Judgment, the Pit, lost souls, and the end of the world enlarge a romantic misunderstanding into a moment of cosmic disorder. Heat, dust, lightning, sweat, darkness, and noise are rendered with physical intensity.

Sentence structure follows this tonal movement. The social exposition often uses balanced, elaborately qualified sentences whose clauses reproduce the narrator’s habit of revising or contradicting himself. Aphorisms and short judgments punctuate these longer constructions. During the storm and pursuit, coordination and accumulation accelerate the rhythm: repeated “and” clauses convey confusion, breathlessness, and sensory overload. Capitalisation in dialogue—“I WANT to go home,” “HER,” and “WOULD”—records shouted emphasis and emotional extremity, while dashes, semicolons, italics, and exclamation marks preserve the energy of oral narration.

The plot is tightly constructed around concealment, mistaken identity, and reversal. The opening establishes that Saumarez is expected to propose to the elder sister; the sisters’ physical and vocal resemblance makes the error plausible; the moonlight picnic supplies a socially sanctioned courtship setting; and the dust-storm removes sight, privacy, and ordinary restraint at exactly the crucial moment. The narrative then changes from comedy of manners to elemental melodrama and finally returns to social comedy. This progression is mirrored by the weather: oppressive heat corresponds to emotional tension, the storm precipitates confession and confusion, and the cleared dawn restores conventional identities and behaviour.

Dialogue is sparse but dramatically concentrated. Most of the story is mediated through the narrator’s commentary, so the sudden cries and shouted exchanges in the darkness have exceptional force. Edith’s repeated wish to go home initially appears to be fear of the storm, then acquires its true emotional meaning. Saumarez’s blunt confession—“I’ve proposed to the wrong one!”—compresses the central reversal into a comic line. The narrator’s failure to produce an adequate response reinforces the sense that the storm has temporarily suspended reason and etiquette.

Characterisation is largely external and social. People are defined by reputation, appearance, income, manner, and conduct rather than by sustained interior monologue. Edith’s hidden vulnerability is revealed through action and breakdown; Maud’s happiness is captured in a sudden lyrical image; and Saumarez’s emotional confusion appears physically in his shaking, clawing grip, and drawn face. The narrator himself becomes an accidental “Providence,” meddling out of a confused desire to make events proceed “decently and in order.” His retrospective embarrassment prevents him from assuming the status of an uncomplicated hero.

The dominant effects are comic irony, atmospheric suspense, and emotional release. The proposal to the wrong sister is farcical, but Edith’s flight gives the mistake genuine pain and urgency. The theatrical reunion, complete with ghostlike spectators applauding beneath the trees, is both moving and faintly absurd. Repeated claims that the experience was dreamlike, unnatural, or “out of the world” frame the night as a temporary suspension of English reserve. By dawn, the participants return to ordinary social roles, and the heightened episode recedes into communal legend.

The story’s frame is self-conscious and metafictional. It begins by declaring that the exact truth cannot be known because the decisive female perspective is unavailable to the male narrator, and it ends by returning to that absence. This circular structure casts doubt on everything between: the account is vivid and authoritative in manner, yet explicitly “from the outside” and therefore potentially “all wrong.” The verse epigraph establishes an elevated mood of earthly expectation and shared disturbance, which the prose then translates into the smaller but emotionally charged world of courtship. Overall, the style blends oral anecdote, colonial comedy of manners, masculine self-satire, romantic melodrama, and powerful meteorological description.

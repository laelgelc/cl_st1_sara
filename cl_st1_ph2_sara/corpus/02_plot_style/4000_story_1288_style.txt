The story uses a romantic, folkloric mode that blends pastoral village life with Gothic supernatural suggestion. Its setting is rendered through lyrical, highly atmospheric description: evening “dreamily” embraces the heavens, song flows like a stream, darkness broods, and moonlight transforms the landscape. Nature is persistently personified and made responsive to human feeling. The gloaming, stars, pool, willows, pine wood, and ruined house do more than establish place; they mirror the lovers’ tenderness, Hanna’s melancholy, and the growing sense of unease. Soft celestial and floral imagery surrounds the courtship, while shadows, moss, closed shutters, and dark water foreshadow danger.

The narrative voice is omniscient and conspicuously literary, with the cadence of a nineteenth-century tale or an English translation of one. Its diction includes elevated and somewhat archaic expressions such as “without surcease,” “hard by,” “begirt,” “menial,” and “whereupon,” alongside colloquial speech like “old curmudgeon” and “no use.” This contrast separates the narrator’s ornamental storytelling voice from the characters’ intimate, rustic conversation. Repeated diminutives—“little stars,” “bright little homes,” “tiny feet”—give the lovers’ exchange an innocent, tender quality, while references to Cossacks, village customs, Ascension Day, witches, angels, and evil spirits root the story in a Christianised folk culture.

The prose generally favours long, flowing, clause-rich sentences, especially in scenic description, punctuated by shorter dramatic statements and exclamations in dialogue. Similes are frequent and accessible: song is “stream-like,” eyes shine “like little stars,” the water moves “like a babe in a cradle,” and the girl’s face is “as white as snow.” Repetition intensifies emotion and gives the speech an oral, ballad-like rhythm: “far, far away,” “long, long ago,” “very, very little,” and “thinking, thinking all the night through.” Exclamations, rhetorical questions, affectionate apostrophes, and broken phrases make the dialogue emotionally immediate and faintly theatrical.

The narration shifts from the retrospective past of the opening into a vivid historical present when Leoko approaches Hanna’s house, then returns largely to the past. This tense movement lends the courtship scene the quality of a tableau unfolding before the reader. The story also uses a framed-tale structure: a tranquil village evening leads into the lovers’ conversation, which contains Leoko’s embedded legend of the bewitched stepdaughter. That inner tale is more compressed and action-driven than the surrounding scene, relying on patterned escalation—the successive nights and numbered days—to reproduce the formal simplicity of oral folklore.

Characterisation is conveyed primarily through speech, gesture, and conventional romantic types rather than psychological analysis. Leoko is ardent, playful, protective, and boastfully confident; Hanna is affectionate, anxious, imaginative, and curious. Their dialogue moves naturally from flirtation and marriage worries to cosmology and ghost lore, allowing domestic concerns to shade gradually into supernatural apprehension. The lovers are idealised through stock physical details such as rosy fingers, dark brows, hazel eyes, shining eyes, and flushed cheeks.

Tonally, the passage progresses from communal merriment to private tenderness, then to melancholy and foreboding. Happiness is never entirely free of sadness: even the opening songs carry a mournful strain, and Hanna’s declarations of love are interrupted by fears about separation and social hostility. The supernatural remains formally ambiguous. Leoko dismisses the legend as an “old woman’s tale,” but the spectral appearance of the house, Hanna’s fixed gaze, the dark pool, and the fiery rising moon preserve an ominous possibility. The ending does not deliver an explicit apparition; instead, it closes on silence, distance, and the bolting of a door, using restraint to leave the menace latent.

Overall, the style is richly descriptive, sentimental, melodious, and folk-Gothic. Its key effects come from the contrast between warm human intimacy and an enchanted, potentially hostile landscape; between Christian belief and pagan superstition; and between the security of village courtship and the dark history embedded in the surrounding countryside. Editorial or translational traces—such as the intrusive explanation of “Hahn” and occasional awkward wording—further contribute to the impression of an older tale carried into English from another literary tradition.

The story adopts a polished late-Victorian or Edwardian comic style, combining social satire with the first-person reminiscence of a naïve young literary man. The narrator speaks retrospectively, allowing his youthful vanity and credulity to expose themselves while his older, wiser perspective supplies the irony. He rarely condemns anyone directly. Instead, the humour arises from the discrepancy between what the young narrator once believed, what the reader quickly suspects, and what subsequent events reveal.

The tone is urbane, amused, and gently cynical rather than bitter. Romantic and sentimental language is repeatedly inflated and then punctured by mundane facts. The woman’s voice “crept round one like a warm caress,” her touch nearly drives the narrator to theatrical acts of devotion, and her glance seems to communicate spiritual weariness; yet she leaves with the supposedly “hollow Society man” and later repeats the same intimate performance for other admirers. Parenthetical asides and dry qualifications sharpen this deflation, as when the narrator estimates her real age, admits that he was “foolish for my age,” or observes that a crowded reception offers accommodation for eight people but contains eighty.

Characterisation is achieved primarily through speech, gesture, and repetition. Mrs. Clifton Courtenay is never analytically explained. Her adaptable personality emerges through a repertoire of flattering questions, soft tones, calculated touches, apparent confessions, and borrowed phrases. She makes each man feel uniquely understood while mirroring his interests and convictions back to him. The repeated claims that she longs to reveal her “real self,” together with the recycled image of a woman’s arms as a heavenly “lifebelt,” turn verbal repetition into the central comic device. Her apparent sincerity is persuasive at every individual encounter but becomes transparently performative when the encounters are placed side by side.

The dialogue is lively, socially coded, and highly revealing. Mrs. Courtenay’s speech is intimate, breathless, and strategically deferential, filled with flattering adjectives such as “clever,” “brilliant,” and “beautiful.” The men, by contrast, speak in the specialised rhetoric of their chosen identities. The clergyman uses the language of earnest Protestant morality, the Abbé speaks in devotional metaphors, the Theosophist favours abstract claims about truth and wisdom, the Colonel adopts patriotic bluster, and the radical Jocelyn invokes emancipation and a “coming dawn.” Each voice is distinct, but all share self-importance and the assumption that they alone have reached the woman’s authentic inner being.

Structurally, the story moves from personal anecdote to cumulative social panorama. Its opening section follows a compact comic arc: enchantment, anticipated intimacy, interruption, and disillusioning evidence. The encounter with the narrator’s literary friend then repeats the pattern and reveals that the supposed intimacy is formulaic. After the typographical break, the narrative broadens into a sequence of reported testimonies from religious and political figures. This catalogue-like arrangement accelerates the satire, with each new speaker contradicting the last while unknowingly reproducing the same vanity. The closing sentence—“But they all agreed she was a charming woman”—provides a concise ironic summation: they disagree about everything she believes because she has reflected each belief back to its advocate, yet all remain united by the pleasure she gives their egos.

The prose alternates between elegant descriptive sentences, quick conversational exchanges, and extended rhetorical monologues. Sentence length varies accordingly. Short lines deliver comic reversals, while longer periodic sentences imitate the grandiosity of ideological speech. Dashes, semicolons, parentheses, and interruptions preserve the rhythm of spoken anecdote and permit the narrator to insert sly afterthoughts. Occasionally formal or period-specific constructions—“from myself,” “as yet,” “artizan,” “it behoves,” and “damme, sir”—anchor the piece in an upper-middle-class metropolitan world of salons, literary journals, Church factions, and political societies.

The humour depends heavily on dramatic irony, parallelism, escalation, and bathos. The narrator’s early fantasy of kneeling or standing on his head at Mrs. Courtenay’s feet comically joins romantic surrender with physical absurdity. Repeated encounters create parallel scenes in which men mistake standardised flattery for singular intimacy. The later testimonies escalate this private comedy into a broader satire of institutional vanity: every spokesman believes that his eloquence has converted her, and each treats her receptiveness as proof of his own persuasive power. Even their supposedly compassionate interpretations are forms of self-congratulation.

Although Mrs. Courtenay is the apparent target, the satire falls at least as strongly on the men around her. She is socially manipulative, but her success depends upon their vanity, possessiveness, ideological certainty, and hunger for admiration. They hear what confirms their preferred image of themselves: the author wants to be recognised as brilliant, the priest as a spiritual guide, the lecturer as a profound thinker, the soldier as a defender of the nation, and the radical as the confidant of an oppressed soul. The story’s governing style is therefore one of comic indirection. It presents a sequence of confident interpretations without supplying an authoritative account of the woman’s “real self,” leaving the reader to infer that her most consistent talent is making other people believe they have discovered it.

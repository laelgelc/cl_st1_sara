The story adopts the form of a historical-biographical sketch enclosing an autobiographical confession. It opens with a detached account of Duclos and his manuscripts, narrows its attention to Mademoiselle Gautier, and then yields the narrative almost entirely to her first-person testimony before returning to an editorial voice for the conclusion. This framed structure creates two distinct but complementary perspectives: the outer narrator supplies historical context, interpretation, and gently sceptical commentary, while Gautier’s memoir provides immediacy, personality, and emotional detail. The transitions are explicit and orderly, often announcing what is significant and instructing the reader how to understand the material.

The outer narration is urbane, measured, and essayistic. It resembles nineteenth-century literary journalism or antiquarian biography, addressing an educated general readership with an interest in unusual historical characters. The narrator maintains an appearance of impartiality but frequently guides judgment through qualifications, ironic contrasts, and direct appeals to “the reader” or “the student of human nature.” His irony is genial rather than destructive. Gautier’s omissions, vanities, and religious excesses are exposed, yet the final assessment remains compassionate, emphasizing her sincerity, generosity, endurance, and essential goodness. The result is a tone poised between curiosity, amusement, moral reflection, and sympathy.

Gautier’s first-person voice is more animated, theatrical, and emotionally volatile. Her former identity as an actress remains audible in the dramatic arrangement of scenes, emphatic exclamations, vivid gestures, and reported inner speeches. She repeatedly turns private conflict into miniature drama: conscience speaks aloud, friends make ominously apt jokes, the crack of a whip provokes a shriek, and the prospect of flagellation produces an impassioned debate between vanity and obedience. Her language moves rapidly between penitential solemnity and worldly self-awareness. Even while condemning her former life, she remembers her beauty, fashionable appearance, plump figure, admired shoulders, and social importance. These touches generate much of the narrative’s comedy and preserve the lively personality that religious discipline is supposed to subdue.

The diction is formal, polished, and historically inflected. Long Latinate terms—such as “justification,” “flagellation,” “submission,” “tranquillity,” and “dissipations”—coexist with concrete domestic details involving carp, nuts, saucepans, brooms, rope shoes, clocks, and pitchers. Religious vocabulary is pervasive, including “Providence,” “state of grace,” “general confession,” “penitence,” “discipline,” and “the sacrifice of liberty.” Elevated expressions are often placed beside mundane or bodily facts, creating comic incongruity: an act of humility substitutes for a meal, mystical tranquillity is confirmed by successful digestion, and spiritual zeal ends with the penitent flat on her nose. The prose also retains period manners through titles, place names, ecclesiastical offices, and precise dates.

Sentence structure is predominantly expansive and carefully balanced. Long sentences accumulate clauses, qualifications, and explanatory phrases, often tracing a chain of motives or presenting opposing considerations. Parenthetical remarks, dashes, semicolons, and appositions allow the narrator to add context without breaking the flow. Gautier’s moments of agitation, by contrast, are rendered through shorter declarations, rhetorical questions, exclamations, repetitions, and direct quotation. Parallel constructions are common, especially in summaries of conduct: “always received,” “always talked,” “always assisted,” and “always willingly wrote.” This rhythmic repetition reinforces moral conclusions and gives the closing passages a commemorative cadence.

Dialogue is used sparingly but effectively. Most speech appears as memorable quotation rather than extended conversation, and each exchange reveals character or sharpens irony. The internal dialogue with conscience gives conversion a dramatic origin; the farewell dinner lets innocent jokes acquire painful significance; and Father Deveaux’s prediction that Gautier will request the whip “on your bended knees” supplies a neat comic reversal when it comes true. Gautier’s own quoted thoughts are especially important, since they expose the tension between her anger, vanity, religious fear, and desire for obedience.

The narrative proceeds chronologically, but it is selective and episodic rather than exhaustive. Precise dates and elapsed periods lend documentary authority, while representative scenes carry the emotional and comic burden. The main movement is from public display to enclosure, from theatrical performance to religious ritual, and from social distinction to menial labour. Yet the story avoids presenting conversion as a simple erasure of identity. Gautier’s passion, pride, physical vigour, theatricality, and sociability survive in altered forms: romantic ardour becomes devotional zeal, bodily strength becomes convent labour, and worldly influence becomes charitable advocacy.

A persistent stylistic feature is the gap between Gautier’s interpretation of events and the outer narrator’s. She attributes her conversion to sudden heavenly inspiration, whereas the framing account points to the emotional devastation caused by her lover’s rejection. The narrator does not aggressively resolve this discrepancy; instead, he allows psychological and religious explanations to coexist. Similar ambiguity surrounds the austerities of convent life. They can appear sincere and spiritually meaningful while also seeming comic, excessive, or manipulated by clerical authority. This restraint gives the piece its characteristic double vision.

The dominant mode is therefore documentary realism softened by humane irony. Dates, institutions, names, money, meals, illnesses, chores, and physical sensations ground the account in tangible experience, while moral commentary elevates it into a study of character. Humour arises chiefly from incongruity and self-revelation rather than from overt jokes. The ending captures the governing balance particularly well: Gautier’s lifelong perseverance is treated with admiration, but her papal dispensation to appear unveiled before visitors preserves one final, affectionate note of vanity. Overall, the style is ceremonious yet accessible, psychologically observant, gently satirical, and reluctant to let either religious devotion or human weakness cancel the other.

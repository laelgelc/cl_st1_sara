The story adopts the manner of a nineteenth-century literary romance, blending domestic realism, fairy-tale symbolism, and moral allegory. Its governing effect comes from placing “the wild and wonderful” within an ordinary New England village: prophetic signs, visionary figures, and intimations of destiny are not disproved so much as translated into homely realities. The result is gently ironic rather than purely fantastical. Grand expectations are fulfilled in reduced but meaningful forms—a buried treasure becomes cultivated land, imperial rule becomes teaching children, and an idealized maiden becomes a faithful childhood companion.

The narrator is overt, reflective, and authoritative. Rather than remaining invisible, the narrative voice introduces the artistic design, comments on characters’ beliefs, controls transitions, and occasionally addresses the reader through formulations such as “we say not,” “Pass we over,” and the concluding “Would all who cherish such wild wishes.” This creates the atmosphere of an oral moral tale while also maintaining ironic distance from Ralph Cranfield’s romantic self-conception. The narrator treats his delusions sympathetically, recognizing their imaginative beauty while quietly exposing their vanity and impracticality.

The diction is elevated, antiquarian, and Latinate. Words and phrases such as “faëry,” “futurity,” “threefold fate,” “venerable sage,” “extended empire,” and “Heaven-instructed message” lend the prose ceremonial weight. Archaic turns of phrase—“of yore,” “whereas,” “hath,” “mayhap”-like constructions, and inversions such as “Pass we over”—reinforce the legendary register. This elevated vocabulary is repeatedly set against plain provincial details: moss-grown houses, a millstone doorstep, village selectmen, cows by the roadside, and a quartz brooch. The stylistic contrast supplies much of the humor and clarifies the story’s moral movement from abstraction toward useful, affectionate life.

Sentences are generally long, periodic, and elaborately subordinated. They often accumulate alternatives, qualifications, and parallel clauses before reaching their main point. The descriptions of the three signs are especially expansive, using repeated “whether” constructions to display the breadth of Ralph’s fantasy. Parenthetical interruptions and dashes imitate a meditative voice that tests several interpretations at once. Balanced antitheses are frequent: foreign versus familiar, dream versus waking, grandeur versus humility, youth versus weary manhood, and imagined destiny versus providential duty. Short exclamations and direct statements are reserved for emotional or revelatory moments, giving lines such as “The youth returns not” and “you have interpreted my wild and weary dream” particular force.

The imagery is strongly symbolic and repeatedly organized around visible signs. The heart-shaped jewel represents ideal love transformed into enduring human fidelity; the pointing hand and the word “Effode” redirect fantasies of sudden wealth toward labor in native soil; the sage’s wand becomes a village squire’s flourished cane. Shadows, twilight, mist, dreams, and half-seen forms create an atmosphere of uncertainty in which common objects can acquire supernatural significance. Light is similarly transitional: dusk encourages projection, morning revives the dream, and sunset accompanies Ralph’s movement toward recognition. The recurring imagery of sleep and awakening supplies the story’s central metaphor for moral correction.

Characterization is built through contrast and emblematic detail rather than psychological realism alone. Ralph is presented as “tall, dark, stately,” marked by exotic travel and inward abstraction; Faith is “neat, comely and quiet,” rooted in continuity, restraint, and domestic feeling. Squire Hawkwood is rendered through comic physical particulars—his three-cornered hat, silver-headed cane, pompous speech, and “little-great” importance. Even minor figures and settings carry thematic value. The largely unchanged village embodies the stable reality Ralph has overlooked, while his mother’s bustle represents the ordinary obligations of love.

Dialogue is relatively sparse and stylized. Ralph’s crucial speeches follow the predetermined formulas of prophecy, allowing ordinary conversation to become ritual fulfillment. By contrast, the mother and the selectmen speak in socially recognizable voices: the mother is affectionate and practical, while the squire’s inflated official rhetoric comically magnifies the modest schoolmaster’s post. Dialogue therefore helps distinguish visionary language from communal language, even as the plot ultimately reconciles them.

The pacing is leisurely and contemplative. Exposition occupies substantial space, especially in the formal enumeration of Ralph’s three anticipated destinies. The return to the village then unfolds through a sequence of recognitions, each corresponding to one of the original signs. This triadic architecture gives the story the symmetry of folklore or parable. Coincidences are deliberately transparent: the reader recognizes the meaning of the tree, the three selectmen, and Faith’s brooch before Ralph fully accepts it. Suspense lies less in what will happen than in whether he will interpret what happens correctly.

The tone combines melancholy, tenderness, whimsical irony, and moral seriousness. Ralph’s wasted years and disappointed hopes are treated with genuine pathos, but his grandiosity is gently deflated through comic juxtapositions. The story never becomes harshly satirical; its aim is restorative. Imagination is not condemned, but disciplined and redirected toward work, community, family, and reciprocal love. The ending makes the allegorical lesson explicit, presenting happiness as something often available within one’s appointed circumstances rather than at the end of a heroic world-search.

Overall, the style is ornate, symbolic, and self-consciously literary. It derives its distinctive character from the fusion of fairy legend with regional domestic detail, from a narrator who is both sympathetic and ironic, and from a pattern in which supernatural-seeming omens are fulfilled through ordinary responsibilities. The prose moves from exotic amplitude toward local simplicity, formally enacting the protagonist’s awakening from romantic ambition into a humbler but more substantial destiny.

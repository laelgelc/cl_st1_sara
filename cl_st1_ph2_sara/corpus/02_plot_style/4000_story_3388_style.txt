The piece adopts the form of a reflective moral essay rather than a conventionally plotted short story. Its voice is personal, cultivated, and conversationally authoritative: the first-person speaker addresses an implied community through “we,” “us,” and occasional direct imperatives, turning private self-examination into a shared meditation on conduct, failure, happiness, and death. The tone combines solemnity with genial scepticism. Moral claims are advanced earnestly, but moral self-importance is repeatedly punctured by irony, wit, and humane qualification. The speaker sounds learned without becoming wholly academic, moving easily between biblical teaching, classical history, literary anecdote, proverbial wisdom, and everyday domestic experience.

The diction is elevated and distinctly late-Victorian. It includes Latinate abstractions such as “incredulity,” “transcendental,” “capitis diminutio,” and “self-examination,” alongside archaic or formal constructions such as “it were,” “thence springs,” “shall we not,” and “of such an one.” Latin quotations, biblical phrases, and references to Tacitus, Germanicus, Bacon, Paul, Marcus Aurelius, and Christ establish a broad humanistic and religious frame. This learned vocabulary is balanced by concrete, colloquial, and sometimes comic expressions: losing one’s teeth on camp bread, being “unconscionably long of getting hanged,” “twopenny concerns,” the dinner-call, and the old soldier laying down his arms. The juxtaposition of high and low registers keeps the prose intellectually serious but socially intimate.

The syntax is expansive, periodic, and rhythmically controlled. Long sentences accumulate clauses through semicolons, dashes, colons, and coordinated phrases, often delaying the principal conclusion until a sequence of distinctions has been made. Parenthetical remarks create the impression of a mind revising itself in real time, while balanced clauses give arguments a memorable aphoristic finish. Short declarations appear at strategic moments—“One person I have to make good: myself”—and gain force from contrast with the surrounding elaboration. Parallelism and anaphora are frequent, especially in passages beginning with repeated formulations such as “to be,” “we may,” “how far,” or “it is.” Enumerations enlarge the moral field while producing a sermon-like cadence: “envy, malice, the mean lie, the mean silence, the calumnious truth.” Antithesis is the governing rhetorical habit, setting positive against negative morality, kindness against interference, cheerfulness against dreariness, self-forgiveness against judgment, and failure against dishonour.

The argument develops associatively but with clear architectural control. Four numbered sections move from mortality and imperfect service, through Christmas cheerfulness and tolerance, to the relation between goodness and happiness, and finally to resignation before death. Each section begins from a seasonal or ethical occasion and broadens into general reflection. The method is dialectical: the speaker proposes a claim, imagines an objection, qualifies both positions, and arrives at a humane middle course. Rhetorical questions draw the reader into this reasoning, while repeated concessions—“it may be argued,” “no doubt,” “perhaps”—soften dogmatism. Despite its moral purpose, the essay distrusts rigid moralism; its own style therefore models flexibility, self-correction, and sympathetic judgment.

Imagery is drawn chiefly from warfare, labour, travel, judgment, and the close of day. Human life becomes military service, conduct a long campaign, temptation an enemy, moral preparation a “clearing of the decks,” and death the defeated but undishonoured soldier’s recall from the field. Other recurring metaphors present life as a knotted thread that must be patiently unravelled, a task for which wages must not be demanded, or a day’s work ending at sunset. These images make abstract ethical propositions tangible while sustaining the central idea of effort without guaranteed success. The final borrowed poem extends the day-and-labour metaphor into a serene natural tableau of lark, sunset, stars, and sleep, shifting the close from argumentative prose to lyrical benediction.

Humour is dry, paradoxical, and often directed against spiritual vanity. The speaker exposes the secret pleasure contained in denunciation, the disguised envy of those who condemn pleasures they cannot share, and the absurd ambition of people who prefer suppressing heresy to the humble work of being kind. Epigrams condense these reversals: morality that produces dreariness is declared suspect; self-contempt becomes “greed of hire”; failure is treated as the common destiny rather than an exceptional disgrace. Such paradoxes are not merely decorative. They overturn conventional ethical assumptions and replace punitive morality with patience, cheerfulness, active kindness, and humility.

Characterisation and scene are minimal, since the piece relies on illustrative types rather than dramatised individuals. Charles II, mutinous veterans, the Pharisee, the reforming lady who opposes dolls, and the hypothetical moral busybody function as compressed exempla. Dialogue is limited to quotation, and there is no sustained narrative action. Momentum comes instead from the unfolding argument, changes in emotional register, and the recurrent return to the opening phrase about being “an unconscionable time a-dying.” That refrain gives the essay circular cohesion.

The emotional movement is from rueful mortality toward acceptance. The prose acknowledges weakness, loneliness, illness, failed friendships, and inevitable defeat, yet refuses bitterness. Its dominant sensibility is stoic-Christian but heterodox: duty matters, reward is uncertain, conscience offers no simple peace, and religious teaching must be interpreted through sympathy rather than prohibition. The concluding ideal is the “Faithful Failure,” a phrase that crystallises the style’s characteristic fusion of contradiction, martial dignity, modesty, and hope. Overall, the writing is ornate but lucid, rhetorically elaborate but conversational, morally serious but anti-puritanical, and elegiac without surrendering warmth or wit.

The story adopts an elegant late-Victorian society style, combining a cosmopolitan frame narrative with a romantic mystery and an ironic psychological conclusion. It begins in a leisurely, observant first-person voice amid Parisian café life, where “splendour and shabbiness” establish an interest in attractive contrasts. The narrator is urbane, socially assured and lightly epigrammatic. His polished detachment balances Lord Murchison’s emotional intensity, allowing the story to treat the affair both as a personal tragedy and as an object of amused psychological analysis.

Its structure is strongly framed. A chance meeting in Paris leads to Murchison’s long retrospective account, within which scenes, conversations and quoted dialogue are embedded. This layered narration creates intimacy while preserving uncertainty: Lady Alroy is never given her own narrative perspective, so she remains an image interpreted by other people. The photograph serves as the transition into the central tale and as a recurring emblem of her unreadability. At the end, the narrative returns to the frame, closing on Murchison’s unresolved “I wonder?” rather than on absolute confirmation. The shape is therefore circular and deliberately inconclusive, even though the narrator offers a neat explanation.

The prose alternates between descriptive narration and dialogue-heavy dramatic scenes. Exposition is economical, rapidly establishing social rank, shared history and character through selective details. Dialogue carries much of the action and is often composed of short, balanced exchanges: “women are meant to be loved, not to be understood” and “I cannot love where I cannot trust.” Such lines have an aphoristic, quotable quality. They compress broad attitudes into symmetrical formulations and frequently introduce paradox, especially the opposition between love and understanding, truth and appearance, or secrecy and emptiness. The narrator’s wit is dry and socially sophisticated, as in the joke about Murchison’s truthfulness or the “survival of the matrimonially fittest.”

The diction is cultivated and associated with aristocratic European society. References to Oxford, the House of Peers, the Bois, Bond Street, Park Lane, clubs, drawing rooms, the Opera and fashionable dinners create an affluent metropolitan world. French phrases such as “ma belle inconnue,” art-historical allusions such as “the Gioconda in sables,” and objects including morocco cases, moonstones, lace, furs and silver tissue give the prose a decorative, aesthetic finish. Beauty is represented less through precise physical anatomy than through clothing, atmosphere, posture and cultural analogy.

Lady Alroy is described in painterly and symbolic language. Her “large vague eyes,” loosened hair, rich furs, veil, pale face and low musical voice make her seem more like an artwork or occult figure than an ordinary person. Repeated comparisons—to a clairvoyante, the Mona Lisa, a moonbeam, a strange crystal and finally a Sphinx—construct her as an embodiment of mystery. The imagery favours moonlight, greyness, pallor, veils, clouds and reflective surfaces, all of which suggest obscurity and unstable perception. Her beauty is called “psychological, not plastic,” showing the story’s fascination with the imaginative meanings projected onto appearance.

Murchison’s narration is more impassioned than the frame narrator’s. His language intensifies as curiosity becomes obsession: he is “passionately, stupidly in love,” then “sick and tired,” “maddened,” “mad” and “frantic.” Repetition and exclamation convey emotional excess, most notably in “I had loved her so much, I had loved her so madly.” His straightforward moral nature makes him unable to tolerate ambiguity. Consequently, his tragedy arises not from proven betrayal but from his demand that every appearance have a definite, truthful explanation.

Suspense is generated through a sequence of suggestive but inconclusive clues: the yellow brougham, Lady Alroy’s fear of being overheard, the false postal name, the secret address, the veil, the latch-key and the dropped handkerchief. These details employ the machinery of sensation fiction or detective fiction, encouraging both Murchison and the reader to imagine adultery, blackmail or danger. The eventual revelation deliberately frustrates those expectations. The secret rooms contain no lover and no crime; they exist merely as a stage on which Lady Alroy can perform secrecy. The climax is therefore an anti-climax, converting external mystery into a study of self-dramatisation.

The prevailing tone shifts among amused, romantic, suspenseful and melancholy registers. Social comedy dominates the opening, gothic suggestiveness surrounds Lady Alroy, and melodrama enters during the confrontation and her sudden death. Yet irony governs the whole. Murchison falls in love partly because of the mystery, then destroys the relationship because he cannot endure it. Lady Alroy cultivates the appearance of a hidden life but apparently has nothing to conceal. The final phrase, “a Sphinx without a secret,” encapsulates the central stylistic device: a paradox that is at once witty, memorable and psychologically revealing.

Sentences are generally fluent and moderately elaborate, often using coordination, semicolons and balanced clauses to sustain a polished conversational rhythm. Longer descriptive passages are interspersed with rapid dialogue, preventing the narrative from becoming static. Parenthetical qualifications and self-corrections reproduce the movement of thought, as when Murchison claims he loved Lady Alroy “in spite of the mystery,” then admits it may have been “in consequence of it.” Repeated colour motifs—yellow, dark green, grey and silver—and precise times and locations lend concrete realism to an otherwise dreamlike story.

Overall, the style is aesthetic, epigrammatic and psychologically ironic. It treats identity as performance and suggests that imagination can create a more compelling mystery than facts can sustain. The central effect depends on the contrast between sumptuous surfaces and narrative emptiness: elaborate secrecy surrounds no substantive secret, while the absence of an answer proves more haunting than any conventional revelation.

The story adopts the form of a comic, discursive social essay rather than a conventionally plotted short story. Its governing device is an extended analogy between theatrical pantomime and everyday life: stock figures such as the clown, pantaloon, harlequin, and supernumerary become satirical types through which the narrator examines commerce, fashionable society, fraud, public credulity, and parliamentary politics. The piece develops associatively, moving from a general thesis to illustrative street scenes, character sketches, anecdotes, and political commentary. Transitions often imitate spontaneous thought—“The mention of this latter…reminds us” or “a new branch of our subject flashed suddenly upon us”—although the apparent improvisation conceals a carefully sustained metaphorical scheme.

The narrative voice is that of a sociable, urbane periodical essayist. It speaks predominantly in the editorial first-person plural—“we maintain,” “we consider,” “we take it”—and repeatedly addresses an imagined readership. Inclusive phrases such as “let us take a slight example” invite the reader into the argument, while direct questions, anticipated objections, and invented interruptions create the impression of a lively public conversation. The narrator stages imaginary responses from readers and playgoers, then corrects them with mock courtesy. This produces a self-aware, performative voice that is simultaneously companionable, authoritative, and mischievously superior.

The dominant tone is satirical, exuberant, and mock-serious. Moral indignation is expressed through the language of sober judgment, but its ceremonious excess makes the judgment comic. Petty misconduct is described as though it were a grave philosophical or legal matter, while serious corruption is translated into slapstick. Phrases such as “with becoming respect,” “on a more mature consideration,” and “we will frankly own” parody the measured language of respectable commentary. The humour depends heavily on ironic incongruity: cruelty, swindling, political opportunism, and sexual impropriety are treated as pantomimic performances, while theatrical routines are analyzed as if they were evidence in a moral inquiry. Beneath the playfulness lies a cynical view of human behaviour, especially the public appetite for humiliation, the hypocrisy of spectators, and the ease with which wealth, rank, or office can suspend ordinary judgment.

The prose is expansive and highly periodic. Sentences frequently accumulate subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, parallel phrases, and catalogues before arriving at their principal point. Repetition provides rhythm and emphasis, as in sequences built around “not because,” “we might have,” or “the same.” Dashes and semicolons are prominent, allowing the narrator to insert afterthoughts, intensify contrasts, and reproduce the momentum of animated speech. Exclamation marks punctuate scenes of mock excitement, while rhetorical questions propel the argument and pressure the reader toward agreement. Short exclamatory sentences—“How the audience roar!” or “The pantaloon to the life!”—interrupt longer passages and imitate the cries and beats of stage comedy.

The diction combines elevated Latinate abstraction with colloquial vigour. Formal expressions such as “amorous propensities,” “indecorum and temerity,” and “propitiatory address” sit beside physical verbs such as “buffet,” “cuff,” “hobble,” “chuck,” “strut,” “swagger,” “twist,” and “tumble.” This contrast is central to the comic effect: bodily farce is narrated in inflated intellectual or bureaucratic language. The vocabulary is theatrical, commercial, legal, and political, reinforcing the idea that social life is both performance and transaction. Period-specific references to London streets, theatres, trades, clothing, Parliament, and popular entertainers give the prose a distinctly metropolitan and topical character.

Characterization is caricatural and external. People are introduced through conspicuous physical details, clothing, gestures, facial expressions, and habitual movements: the prosperous gentleman’s “broad, red cheek,” the old libertine’s cane and “toothless chuckle,” or the supernumerary’s “broad stolid simper” and “dull leaden eye.” Names such as Fitz-Whisker Fiercy and Do’em are comic labels that reveal function rather than psychological depth. Individuals matter chiefly as embodiments of recognizable social types. The narrator often directs the reader’s gaze with imperative verbs—“See,” “Mark,” “Take”—giving scenes the immediacy of stage directions or illustrated tableaux.

Dialogue is used sparingly and theatrically. Brief quoted exchanges represent imagined readers, stock audience members, or formulaic public speech rather than intimate conversation. Voices are distinguished through social markers such as dress, pronunciation, theatrical allegiance, and slang. Capitalization and typographic emphasis—particularly words such as “NOT,” “DO,” “THE CHANGE,” and the clown’s “Here we are!”—simulate vocal stress and sharpen the comic timing.

The pacing alternates between leisurely exposition and rapid visual action. Argumentative passages broaden the social analogy, while miniature scenes compress whole comic narratives into sequences of falls, blows, pursuits, deceptions, and reversals. Lists accelerate the prose and create a sense of mounting absurdity. Each example generally follows the same movement: a familiar pantomime convention is described, its real-life equivalent is revealed, and the resulting parallel is driven toward a satirical punch line.

Overall, the style is that of a nineteenth-century metropolitan sketch: digressive, theatrical, observant, rhetorically elaborate, and morally satirical. Its most distinctive features are the communal “we,” mock-judicial seriousness, direct engagement with an imagined audience, energetic catalogues, long clause-rich sentences, grotesque physical detail, topical allusion, and a single extended metaphor capable of organizing many areas of social life. The comedy remains buoyant on the surface, but its underlying vision is sharply critical: society is a stage on which vanity, fraud, cruelty, and political obedience recur as familiar routines, with spectators laughing until they themselves become the victims.

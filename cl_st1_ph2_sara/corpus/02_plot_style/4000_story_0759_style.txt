The story adopts the style of an early twentieth-century comic detective burlesque. Its central method is sustained dramatic irony: Philo Gubb treats a trivial chicken theft with solemn professional gravity, while chance places him in contact with a genuine murderer whose references to “the Chicken” he persistently interprets as references to poultry. The humor depends on neither man recognizing the other’s meaning, even as their conversation appears coherent enough to produce an accidental arrest, confession, and payment. This carefully prolonged misunderstanding gives the plot the mechanical neatness of farce while parodying detective fiction’s faith in inference, clues, disguises, and professional expertise.

The narrator writes in a genial, dryly amused third-person voice, staying close to Gubb’s limited understanding without fully adopting it. Commentary such as “A detective must be cautious” and the observation that the chickens’ tails brush his hat, which “needed brushing,” delivers jokes with deliberate understatement. Gubb’s mistakes are rarely announced directly; instead, the narrator presents his reasoning in formally logical stages and lets its disproportion or false premise expose the absurdity. His theory about the thief returning to the coop, for example, is internally plausible, but the grand seriousness with which it is developed comically enlarges an insignificant case. The narrative voice remains affectionate rather than cruel: Gubb is foolish, literal-minded, and professionally pretentious, yet also patient, earnest, brave, and unexpectedly successful.

The prose alternates between expansive narrative sentences and brisk dialogue. Long sentences accumulate qualifications, lists, and chains of cause and effect, often using syntactic excess for comic inflation. The opening account of Gubb’s finances and Syrilla’s telegram piles one absurd detail upon another, while the catalogue of soups turns repetition into a set piece. By contrast, confrontations are built from short exchanges, recurring phrases, interruptions, and verbal corrections. Dashes, exclamation marks, italics, and repeated words give the dialogue a lively, stage-comedy rhythm. Transitional exclamations such as “Nothing—absolutely nothing—happened!” sharpen changes in pace and mimic the sensational language of popular adventure stories.

Diction is divided sharply between the narrator’s relatively formal, sometimes mock-technical language and the characters’ colloquial speech. Gubb uses malformed professional terms such as “deteckating” and “deteckative,” reflecting an education acquired through a correspondence course and an imperfect imitation of detective-story rhetoric. Mrs. Smith speaks in practical rural vernacular, while Wixy and Sandlot use criminal slang, nonstandard grammar, dropped consonants, and phonetic spellings such as “goin’,” “follerin’,” and “ain’t.” Their voices are differentiated further through temperament: Sandlot is anxious and argumentative, Wixy drunkenly dismissive, and Mrs. Smith blunt and economically minded. The dialect is broad and comic, functioning both as characterization and as a marker of the story’s period.

Characterization proceeds primarily through speech, physical action, and repeated comic traits rather than psychological depth. Gubb’s “bird-like face,” paper-hanging equipment, false whiskers, dark lantern, oversized revolvers, and rigid professional formulas create a visual caricature. His behavior repeatedly combines ordinary incompetence with elaborate preparation: he searches for the “usual” fingerprint and conveniently torn button, wears conspicuous disguises while hiding in darkness, and accidentally captures Wixy by falling on him. The criminals are similarly drawn in bold strokes, but their conversation supplies enough concrete detail about their crime and fear of pursuit to create genuine narrative stakes beneath the comedy.

The plot is episodic but tightly constructed. It begins with unrelated comic business concerning Syrilla and Gubb’s debts, moves into the apparently minor chicken case, expands through two nights of surveillance, and introduces the murderers while Gubb sleeps directly above them. The extended criminal dialogue gives the reader privileged knowledge and establishes the crucial double meaning of “Chicken” and “Mother Smith.” From that point onward, each exchange compounds the misunderstanding: “killed the Chicken,” “Mother Smith,” the reward, the five hundred dollars, and the description of the victim as a “tough old rooster.” The climax is therefore verbal rather than investigative. Gubb succeeds not through deduction but through coincidence, literalism, physical accident, and Wixy’s guilty assumptions. The closing scene refuses to resolve the confusion for him, ending instead with Mrs. Smith’s irritated door-slam, an abrupt domestic anticlimax after talk of murder and blackmail.

Pacing relies on contrast. Detailed accounts of waiting, hiding, and procedural thought slow the narrative and parody methodical detection; sudden physical events—the breaking bottle, Gubb’s drop through the roof, Wixy’s revival—produce bursts of slapstick action. The dream sequence is a comic digression that transforms legal language about trusts and “restraint of trade” into surreal literalism, with Gubb ordered to “dissolve himself” and searching for chemical solvents. This episode reinforces the story’s fascination with words interpreted too literally, anticipating the central confusion over “Chicken.”

The setting is rendered through practical, concrete description rather than atmosphere for its own sake. Cherry Street, the chicken coop, the brick-yard, abandoned kilns, loose plank roofs, pond, straw, and drying-floor create a small-town working environment and provide the physical machinery of the farce. Explanatory passages about brick-kiln construction have a quasi-documentary quality, but they also prepare Gubb’s hiding place and accidental descent. Mundane objects repeatedly become comic detective props: paste pails, false beards, rusty cans, dark lanterns, horseshoes, broken pipes, and revolvers coexist without hierarchy.

Overall, the style combines vernacular realism, mock-heroic narration, slapstick, linguistic ambiguity, and affectionate satire. Its most distinctive feature is the elevation of trivial events into detective melodrama while a serious crime passes almost unnoticed. Repetition, literal-minded reasoning, disproportionate detail, and double meanings sustain the comedy, producing a world in which incompetence resembles expertise closely enough to achieve results by accident.

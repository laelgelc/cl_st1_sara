The story has the character of a philosophical parable shaped by dark satire, tragic irony, and psychological allegory. Its central figure is deliberately presented less as a fully individualised person than as a representative human type. The substitution of “Mr. N+1” for Max Z. turns him into one term in an endless sequence of lovers, idealists, martyrs, and prisoners. The recurrent assertion that he has “no special peculiarities” reinforces this universalising method: although Max considers every new passion or belief unprecedented, the narrator repeatedly places him within familiar patterns of human vanity, disillusionment, and endurance.

The narrator is conspicuous, intrusive, and intellectually playful. He openly chooses the protagonist’s name, comments on his motives, addresses “my reader,” asks rhetorical questions, and occasionally admits uncertainty about dates or elapsed time. This creates the atmosphere of an oral philosophical tale in which narration and commentary continually mingle. The narrator’s attitude is double-edged. Max is treated with sympathy because his suffering is real, but his self-importance and absolutism are gently mocked. His conviction that no one has ever loved as intensely as he has, or understood humanity as clearly, is undercut by comparisons with Adam, previous lovers, political preachers, and the reader. The voice therefore remains compassionate without becoming sentimental.

The tone moves between comic detachment and severe existential horror. Early passages use urbane irony, mock logic, and exaggerated generalisation, especially in the classifications of how lovers “perpetuate” love through history, literature, newspapers, or birth records. Bureaucratic and scientific language is repeatedly applied to emotional or spiritual matters: Max conducts a “liquidation of his spiritual property,” proves himself alive through “psycho-physiological acts,” and is tested by medical experts. Such phrasing reduces profound suffering to administrative or clinical evidence, producing grim comedy while also exposing the inadequacy of rational systems before inner experience. As the story progresses, the humour recedes and the imagery becomes claustrophobic, culminating in the stark physical and metaphysical torment of the cell.

The prose tends toward long, cumulative sentences containing parallel clauses, enumerations, qualifications, and parenthetical interruptions. These elaborate constructions give the narrator room to reason, generalise, and reverse expectations. They are balanced by short, emphatic sentences at structural turning points: “But he was a man.” and, most decisively, “He hoped.” These abrupt statements acquire exceptional force because they follow extended analytical passages. Repetition also provides rhythm and cohesion. Variations on “he believed,” “his soul was dead,” and “Max did not have any special peculiarities” mark recurring cycles of conviction, collapse, and renewal.

Figurative language is broad, symbolic, and often ironic. Love appears as an inscription written on a woman’s face and erased by betrayal. The world’s evil is condensed into a “red flower,” while society becomes a panic-stricken crowd trapped by an imaginary cry of fire. Max’s inner life is treated as property that can be lost, restored, inventoried, paralysed, buried, or put to sleep. Cosmic personification enlarges the satire: the earth turns “light-mindedly” around the sun and behaves coquettishly toward the moon, indifferent to human catastrophe. The final prison cell externalises Max’s condition through white light, geometric confinement, silence, and bodily distortion. The contrast between relentless outward illumination and “impenetrable” inward darkness is especially important, reversing the conventional association of light with hope or knowledge.

The narrative is episodic and cyclical rather than conventionally dramatic. Max passes through successive absolute commitments—romantic love, friendship, faith in humanity—and each is destroyed by betrayal or misunderstanding. These phases are separated by “intermissions,” a theatrical metaphor suggesting that identity consists of roles and costume changes. Vague weekday references—“Monday or Tuesday,” “Wednesday or Thursday,” “Friday or Saturday”—create a mock-chronological framework. Time is at once ordinary, uncertain, and archetypal, making Max’s experience seem repeatable rather than historically unique.

The diction alternates between elevated biblical or prophetic language and mundane, comic detail. Phrases such as “as strong as death,” “heaven will be established on earth,” and “bearer of fire and sword” give Max’s beliefs religious intensity, while references to borrowing money, newspapers, medical tests, and feet hanging from chairs pull the prose back toward material life. Historical, literary, mythological, and biblical allusions widen the frame without grounding the story in a specific social setting. This mixture gives the work an intellectually cosmopolitan but deliberately timeless quality.

Overall, the style depends on paradox. Max is generic yet intensely human, ridiculous yet heroic, spiritually dead yet biologically alive, imprisoned in constant light yet surrounded inwardly by darkness. The narrator dismantles each of his grand beliefs, but does not reduce him to nihilism. The final one-sentence revelation transforms hope from an optimistic doctrine into a primitive, inexplicable instinct. Stylistically, the story’s deepest effect comes from this movement away from satirical abstractions and toward a compressed existential conclusion: after love, friendship, ideology, freedom, and even ordinary sensory life have been stripped away, the bare human impulse to continue remains.

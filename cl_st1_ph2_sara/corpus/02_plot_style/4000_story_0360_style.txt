The story adopts the form of a compact philosophical drama or dramatic dialogue, combining myth, biblical legend, and eschatological fantasy. It opens with a sparse stage direction that establishes an austere, symbolic setting—the end of time, a deserted earth, a rock, a waning day, and two circling eagles—before allowing dialogue to carry almost the entire narrative. Character names precede each speech as in a play, while occasional parenthetical directions mark physical actions and reveal, crucially, that Ahasverus is dreaming. The setting is less a realistically rendered place than a metaphysical stage on which opposing attitudes toward life, suffering, mortality, and hope can debate one another.

The prose is elevated, archaic, and declamatory. Expressions such as “of a surety,” “thenceforth,” “’tis,” “jot,” and “beheld” lend the dialogue a scriptural or classical resonance. Both speakers regularly use apostrophe, invocation, rhetorical questions, exclamations, and ceremonious repetitions: Ahasverus bids farewell to the sky, clouds, roses, waters, and earth, while Prometheus speaks in prophetic cadences about a perfected future humanity. The diction draws heavily on religious, mythological, and historical reference, juxtaposing Christ, Moses, Abraham, David, and Jericho with Prometheus, Jupiter, Vulcan, Hercules, Thebes, and Sardanapalus. This syncretic allusiveness gives the story a timeless, cosmopolitan intellectual texture and permits Christian and classical creation myths to confront one another directly.

Sentence structure alternates between concise dramatic exchanges and expansive, rhythmically cumulative monologues. Short replies—“No,” “You?” or “My name is Prometheus”—create moments of recognition and theatrical tension, whereas Ahasverus’s accounts of his wandering unfold through long parallel clauses, catalogues, and reiterated phrases. Lists such as “the race, the creed, the tongue; suns and snows, barbarous and civilized peoples, islands, continents” convey the breadth and monotony of his experience. Repetition is central to the style: “century after century,” “dawn upon dawn, sunset upon sunset,” and repeated commands to “speak on” dramatize both temporal exhaustion and the sudden revival of desire. Antithesis likewise structures the language and thought—life and death, prosperity and desolation, old and new humanity, the ephemeral and the eternal, punishment and remission.

The principal mode is dialectical. Ahasverus initially regards death as consolation after endless wandering, while Prometheus argues that prolonged life confers knowledge, continuity, and privileged witness. Their exchange repeatedly reverses moral positions. Prometheus first appears as a hopeful prophet, then becomes in Ahasverus’s eyes the culpable creator of human misery; Ahasverus shifts from despairing victim to furious accuser and would-be punisher, then from eager executioner to credulous disciple. The debate is animated not by neutral philosophical reasoning but by volatile passions: fatigue, resentment, pride, pity, vanity, and hope. Abstract ideas are therefore embodied in legendary figures whose immortality has made them extreme test cases for the value of existence.

The dominant imagery is cosmic and elemental. The horizon, sun, sky, mountains, rocks, iron chains, flowers, thistles, swans, swallows, and eagles turn philosophical claims into vivid emblems. Time is personified as a force that gnaws at all things; life is imagined as a many-gated city and as a book whose chapters are generations; humanity becomes a tree whose dead branches may be cut away. The eagles are especially important as framing symbols. At first they appear as distant creatures associated with hunger, divine punishment, and death. At the close they approach and speak, revealing a perspective superior to that of the dreamer and converting the entire dialogue into a darkly comic commentary on human self-deception.

The tone moves through solemnity, weariness, intellectual combat, menace, exaltation, and finally ironic pathos. Ahasverus’s initial longing for extinction is presented with grave lyricism, but the ending exposes the instability of that longing: once Prometheus offers him kingship, distinction, and renewed life, he embraces existence with mounting rapture. His repeated disbelief—“I myself?”—betrays vanity as much as hope. Prometheus’s prophecy therefore reads less as reliable revelation than as strategic improvisation designed to escape the chains. The final exchange between the eagles supplies the story’s epigrammatic judgment: Ahasverus claimed to hate life only because he loved it too intensely to endure its disappointments. This ending produces a characteristic blend of compassion and satire, treating his illusion as both ridiculous and profoundly human.

Pacing is controlled by successive recognitions and reversals rather than external action. The revelation of Ahasverus’s identity leads to the revelation of Prometheus’s; accusation leads to attempted retribution; promised death gives way to promised renewal; binding gives way to unbinding. As Ahasverus becomes more hopeful, his speeches shorten, fragment, and fill with questions and exclamations, reproducing emotional excitement in the syntax. The final stage direction abruptly withdraws from his exalted vision and restores the physical reality of a dying man asleep beneath approaching birds. Overall, the style is theatrical, allegorical, erudite, and rhetorically ornate, using grand mythic discourse and a sharply ironic frame to explore the persistence of hope, the seductions of self-importance, and humanity’s contradictory attachment to life.

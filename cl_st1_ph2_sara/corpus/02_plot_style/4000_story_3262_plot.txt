A young officer stationed in a dull provincial town befriends Silvio, a mysterious former hussar and extraordinarily skilled pistol shot. Silvio’s reputation for courage is damaged when he refuses to challenge a drunken lieutenant who throws a candlestick at him. Before leaving town, however, Silvio explains that he cannot risk his life because he has an unfinished duel.

Years earlier, Silvio had envied a wealthy, charming young count who displaced him as the most admired officer in their regiment. After Silvio provoked him and received a slap, they dueled. The count fired first and shot through Silvio’s cap, then calmly ate cherries while awaiting Silvio’s return shot. Enraged by the count’s indifference to death, Silvio withheld his bullet, reserving the right to fire whenever the count valued his life more. Learning that the count is about to marry, Silvio departs to claim his shot.

Several years later, the narrator meets the count and discovers what happened. Silvio confronted him during his honeymoon, but instead of simply shooting an unarmed man, he arranged another drawing of lots. The count again fired first and missed, striking a painting. As Silvio prepared to shoot, the count’s terrified wife entered and begged for her husband’s life. Satisfied that he had finally made the once-carefree count fear death and experience humiliation, Silvio spared him. Before leaving, he effortlessly fired into the painting, placing his bullet directly above the count’s. The narrator never sees Silvio again and later hears that he was killed while fighting in a Greek revolutionary uprising.

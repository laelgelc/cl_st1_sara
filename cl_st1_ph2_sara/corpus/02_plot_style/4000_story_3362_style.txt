The story uses a playful, orally driven fairy-tale style that combines homespun American speech with exuberant nonsense. Its narrator sounds like a practiced storyteller addressing children directly: confident, conversational, and willing to treat impossibilities as ordinary facts. The opening relies on plain, categorical statements—chimneys let smoke out, doors open, windows are open or shut—before gradually carrying that matter-of-fact logic into a surreal world. This contrast between simple explanation and wild invention is central to the story’s comic effect.

The diction mixes everyday rural and mechanical vocabulary with whimsical coinages. Pigs, pastures, pitchforks, ragbags, railroad stations, whistles, bells, and cash belong to a recognizable folk-Americana setting, while phrases such as “blue spanch,” “steam hog,” “balloon pickers,” and “forty ways farther yet” make the world strange. Names such as Gimme the Ax, Please Gimme, and Ax Me No Questions are built from fragments of colloquial speech, giving the characters the quality of figures in a tall tale or nursery rhyme. Concrete details are preferred to abstract description, but those details are freely recombined into fantastic images: balloons grow like crops, clowns are baked in ovens, and pigs wear patterned bibs.

The syntax is mostly simple, additive, and repetitive. Sentences frequently begin with “And,” “Then,” “Sometimes,” or “When,” creating the forward rhythm of spoken narration. Repeated formulas—“the train ran on and on,” “everything is the same as it always was,” and the full description of the “long slick yellow leather slab ticket with a blue spanch across it”—function like refrains. They make the prose easy to remember, give it ceremonial momentum, and allow small variations to become humorous. Parallel structures and incremental lists are common, as in the catalogues of destinations, balloons, ovens, and patterned pigs.

Sound is especially important. Alliteration appears in clusters such as “pigs, pastures, pepper pickers, pitchforks,” while internal rhyme, repeated consonants, and rhythmic triads give many lines the bounce of verse. Onomatopoeic expressions such as “chick-a-chick” and “pfisty-pfoost” imitate the machinery of the train while turning it into a creature with its own comic personality. Questions often come in threes—“Who’s who? How much? And what’s the answer?” or “So far? So early? So soon?”—and counting patterns such as “once, twice, three times” reinforce the folktale cadence.

The imagery is vivid, primary-coloured, and deliberately illogical. Human features are described through landscape and weather: the children have “shadows of valleys by night” in their eyes, sunrise on their foreheads, and hair like dark wild grass. Personification gives the wind fingers and the train a hog-like nose. Similes remain accessible and visual, comparing zigzagging tracks to repeated letters Z or a clown to a large doll. The fantasy therefore feels tactile and visible rather than ethereal.

Dialogue serves less to create psychologically distinct characters than to establish rhythms, rules, and comic explanations. Children ask literal questions, and the father answers with serene absurdity. His explanations do not resolve the world’s strangeness; they extend it according to dream logic. The deadpan delivery is crucial: no character expresses sustained disbelief when trains pass over and under one another or when songs make a man light enough to float. The narration treats each marvel as part of a coherent local custom.

Structurally, the story moves from confinement to journey. It begins in a house defined by sameness, then uses the railroad as an episodic passage through increasingly fantastic countries. Each episode has a compact pattern: arrival, visual spectacle, a child’s question, and the father’s explanatory answer. The repeated motion of the train links these self-contained scenes. The ending shifts into direct second-person instruction, turning the adventure into practical folklore and inviting the reader to repeat it. It also circles back through the story’s recurring objects and phrases rather than providing conventional closure.

The tone is warm, mischievous, and wonder-filled, with an undercurrent of dissatisfaction with fixed routines. Sameness is answered not by argument but by imaginative departure. Yet the story does not reject rules altogether; it replaces ordinary rules with more delightful ones. Its distinctive style arises from that combination of plainspoken certainty, musical repetition, tall-tale exaggeration, childlike linguistic play, and surreal invention presented as common sense.

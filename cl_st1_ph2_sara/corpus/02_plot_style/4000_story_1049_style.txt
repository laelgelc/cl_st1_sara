The story adopts a comic Gothic mode, using the language and machinery of supernatural terror to satirise artistic convention. Its first-person narrator is a lonely, dreamy, self-deprecating barrister whose cultivated manner and anxious imagination make him both an articulate observer and an unreliable interpreter of events. He addresses the reader directly, offers confidences and explanatory asides, and presents his account as sober testimony even while its absurdity steadily increases. This tension between the narrator’s solemn conviction and the reader’s awareness of the ridiculous is the principal source of humour.

The narrative voice is urbane, conversational and mock-formal. Polished phrases such as “the subject of my present recollections” and “can never be effaced from my remembrance” imitate memoir, legal evidence and sensational confession. The narrator repeatedly insists upon the truth of what he saw and heard, borrowing the language of affidavits, proof and professional credibility. Yet his profession is itself treated ironically: he is “in the Law, but not of it,” spends idle days in court and cannot tell whether he is standing on his wig or his boots. Parenthetical qualifications, confidential remarks and courteous appeals to the reader create intimacy while also exposing his vanity, passivity and susceptibility to melodrama.

The tone moves fluidly among whimsical autobiography, social satire, farce and mock horror. Dreary chambers, dim lamps, midnight thunder, ghostly steam and a mysterious recurring figure establish a Gothic atmosphere, but each ominous development culminates in something mundane or ludicrous. The “terrible Being” is a poorly paid artist’s model; his threatening promise to “grow one” refers to a beard; and the “Ghost of Art” is not an apparition but an unemployed man adapting himself to fashion. Grand emotional declarations—“My blood ran cold,” “I stood transfixed,” “More dead than alive”—deliberately exaggerate the danger and magnify trivial incidents into supernatural crises.

The prose alternates between expansive, carefully balanced sentences and short dramatic statements. Longer passages accumulate descriptive details, subordinate clauses and parenthetical interruptions, reproducing the narrator’s wandering mind and building comic suspense. These are punctuated by abrupt questions, exclamations and isolated declarations such as “The beard did everything.” Repetition supplies rhythm and emphasis, especially in the model’s prophecy—“I’ll grow one”—and in the catalogue of emotions performed by manipulating his beard. Capitalisation intensifies the parody of melodramatic revelation and gives ordinary statements the force of curses or prophetic warnings.

Dialogue is central to the characterisation and social comedy. The narrator speaks in educated, ceremonious standard English, while the model uses a strongly marked colloquial dialect: “I sets,” “wery,” “warn’t,” “warses,” and “table-kivers.” Phonetic spellings, nonstandard grammar and comic plural formations reproduce speech while signalling class difference. His language is energetic, aggrieved and materially specific, full of wages, costumes, furniture, borrowed plate and stock studio accessories. The contrast between his practical complaints and the narrator’s elevated responses repeatedly punctures the latter’s romantic interpretation. Malapropism-like distortions and extravagant constructions, particularly the lengthening of “Davenportseseses,” give the spoken passages a performative, almost theatrical rhythm.

Visual catalogues are another defining feature. The narrator initially recognises the model through a rapid series of literary, biblical and historical associations, as though the man were a composite of every conventional figure in popular painting. Later, the model lists the familiar props of exhibition art—armour, antique cabinets, table coverings, tambourines, Italian scenery and smoking Vesuvius. These accumulations create comic abundance while exposing artistic formula. The story’s satire rests on the idea that painters manufacture expression by isolating and exaggerating reusable physical “points”: first the model’s throat and legs, then his beard. Human character is reduced to a set of detachable visual signs, and the same beard can signify severity, benevolence, death, jealousy, despair, avarice or rage.

The structure resembles a supernatural encounter tale in miniature. An introductory section establishes the narrator’s temperament, profession and knowledge of art; a storm-bound meeting introduces the mysterious model and his prophecy; a two-year interval heightens expectation; and a second storm brings the apparent fulfilment of the threat. The final paragraph expands the private haunting into a continuing cultural affliction visible throughout public exhibitions. Parallel weather conditions bind the two encounters together, while delayed explanations and repeated questions sustain suspense. Each revelation is staged theatrically through gestures, poses and changes of appearance, fitting a story concerned with models, representation and visual display.

The descriptive imagery is vivid but intentionally overcharged. The Temple is imagined as a dry well, the upper chambers belong to “tiles and sparrows,” rain tears down from rooftops like mountain torrents, and steam turns the model into a shifting spectre. Personification and hyperbole transform ordinary urban spaces into settings of portent. At the same time, concrete comic details—rain wrung from a coat-cuff, a carpet-broom serving as a staff, a missing candle caused by a negligent laundress—keep interrupting the sublime. This continual collision between elevated atmosphere and prosaic reality is characteristic of the piece.

Overall, the style is exuberant, theatrical and densely ironic. It combines direct reader address, caricature, class-conscious dialogue, elaborate lists, recurring catchphrases, mock-heroic diction and Gothic parody. Beneath the farce lies a focused critique of fashionable art: formulaic poses and exaggerated anatomy substitute for observation, inherited symbols replace genuine emotion, and artists follow market taste rather than individual vision. The story turns that criticism into a memorable comic emblem—the endlessly adaptable beard that “does everything” and consequently expresses nothing with sincerity.

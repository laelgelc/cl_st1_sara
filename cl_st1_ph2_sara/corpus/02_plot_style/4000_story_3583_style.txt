The story adopts a nineteenth-century realist mode, combining social observation, psychological intimacy, and restrained melodrama. Its structure is that of a framed oral confession: a cultivated first-person narrator briefly establishes the setting, then yields the narrative almost entirely to the district doctor, whose account becomes the emotional centre of the piece. This layered narration creates both immediacy and distance. The doctor appears to speak spontaneously, while the outer narrator quietly shapes and authenticates his testimony through physical observations, parenthetical comments, and occasional interventions.

The prose is conversational and deliberately digressive. The doctor’s speech is crowded with fillers and self-corrections—“you know,” “well,” “suddenly,” “to tell the truth,” “how should I say?”—as well as repetitions, hesitations, unfinished clauses, and ellipses. These mannerisms individualise his voice and suggest embarrassment, nervousness, and imperfect command over painful memories. His account repeatedly wanders into practical matters such as payment, roads, professional reputation, medicine, and social standing before returning to the central tragedy. Such digressions make the confession feel unpremeditated and reveal character indirectly: he is compassionate but financially anxious, self-conscious, modest, observant, and inclined to protect himself with humour.

Sentence structure alternates between long, accumulating passages and short emotional bursts. Extended sentences reproduce the doctor’s breathless storytelling, especially when he recounts medical uncertainty or Aleksandra’s declarations. Rapid questions, exclamations, interruptions, and fragments intensify scenes of crisis. Parentheses and dashes frequently insert qualifications or gestures, creating the impression of speech being performed before the listener. Repetition—“worse and worse,” “day after day,” “cruel… cruel,” “I don’t understand”—conveys emotional pressure more effectively than polished eloquence would.

The diction mixes plain colloquial language with occasional formal or elevated phrasing. Everyday expressions such as “bread and butter,” “my heart sinks into my boots,” and “by Jove” coexist with religious appeals, professional terminology, and sentimental declarations. This contrast reflects the doctor’s position between educated society and provincial practicality. Concrete details—the shaggy horses, broken dyke, thatched house, samovar, rum, mustard-plaster, snuff, holy image, and ring—anchor the emotional drama in a materially specific rural world. Social class is registered through manners, names, dowries, literacy, forms of address, and assumptions about fees.

Dialogue dominates the narrative and is used primarily for psychological revelation. Aleksandra’s speech becomes increasingly urgent, repetitive, and absolute as she approaches death, while the doctor’s responses remain evasive and professionally reassuring until emotion overwhelms restraint. The dialogue often contains dramatic irony: both doctor and reader recognise that her love is inseparable from fever, isolation, fear, and the belief that death releases her from social consequences. The doctor’s later interpretation of her feelings is hesitant and self-diminishing, leaving their exact nature unresolved.

The pacing begins leisurely, with comic observations about the doctor accepting his fee and the conventions of sudden intimacy between strangers. It then slows around the sickroom, where repeated nights, medical setbacks, whispered conversations, and pauses create claustrophobic suspense. Key disclosures are delayed by the doctor’s embarrassment and circumlocution. After Aleksandra’s death, the conclusion contracts sharply and returns to mundane life: marriage, dowry, an ill-tempered wife, and a small-stakes card game. This anticlimax is purposeful, placing ordinary necessity beside irrecoverable private grief.

The tone is compassionate but unsentimental. Pathos arises from helplessness, missed possibilities, and the discrepancy between intense feeling and the ordinary life that continues afterward. Yet the story repeatedly checks emotional excess with irony. The doctor belittles his appearance, mocks his unfashionable first name, worries about fees, and ends by winning two and a half rubles. The outer narrator does not judge him; understated details allow tenderness, vanity, guilt, professional insecurity, and self-deception to coexist.

Characterisation is indirect and psychologically nuanced. Trifon Ivanich emerges through verbal habits, bodily gestures, contradictions, and changes in tempo rather than through explicit analysis. His snuff-taking, coughing, blushing, pauses, and abrupt proposal to play cards function as emotional defences. Aleksandra is seen almost wholly through his memory and therefore retains ambiguity: she is at once an individual, an idealised beauty, a desperate dying woman, and the object of a love he can neither fully claim nor dismiss.

Overall, the style depends on the tension between confession and concealment, romance and realism, exalted emotion and provincial routine. Its emotional power comes less from overt authorial commentary than from the imperfect spoken voice of a man trying to narrate an experience that still exceeds his ability to explain it.

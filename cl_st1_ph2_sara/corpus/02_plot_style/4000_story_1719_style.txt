The story uses a comic first-person voice in which an inanimate object—a ten-dollar bill—narrates its own circulation through New York. This personification gives the narrator an unusual combination of innocence, worldly experience and financial self-importance. The bill understands human behaviour largely through transactions, yet repeatedly exposes truths that its human owners overlook. Its direct addresses to the reader create the air of a confidential, improvised monologue, while self-corrections such as “My references is—or are—” make the voice deliberately loose and conversational.

The diction is highly colloquial, saturated with period slang, urban idiom and money-related nicknames: “ten-spot,” “fiver,” “wad,” “one-spot” and “bison plaster.” Social groups and locations are described through comic circumlocution rather than neutral labels. Waiters become a “hod-carriers’ union in jackets and aprons,” while hungry hangers-on are “things with cold noses and hot gullets.” Elevated vocabulary and literary phrasing are continually mixed with street speech, producing humorous incongruity. Foreign expressions, bureaucratic language, advertising slogans, biblical phrases and classical or fashionable references sit beside dropped consonants and expressions such as “cut out yer kicks.” The resulting register is densely allusive but intentionally irreverent.

The prose moves quickly through short assertions, abrupt asides, rhetorical questions and bursts of dialogue. Dashes frequently interrupt sentences, allowing jokes, qualifications and changes of direction to enter the narration. Longer sentences often accumulate parallel clauses, escalating a situation through lists until it becomes absurd. Dialogue is compact and competitive: characters trade insults, puns and wisecracks rather than speaking naturalistically at length. Even exposition is compressed into anecdotal snapshots, giving the story the brisk momentum of the circulating bill itself.

Wordplay is central to the style. The narration repeatedly builds jokes from financial terminology, literalized idioms and verbal associations: money “talks,” bills are “tainted,” gold certificates have “a streak of yellow,” and the legal language printed on currency becomes the basis of the conclusion. Names, slogans and familiar quotations are twisted into comic forms. Many descriptions operate as caricatures, using exaggerated similes and improbable cultural comparisons. This humour depends on speed and density; jokes are often delivered without explanation, presuming a reader familiar with contemporary New York, popular entertainment, politics, sport and commerce.

The setting is rendered as a socially mixed urban panorama. Rather than dwelling on physical scenery, the story maps New York through places where money changes hands: banks, bars, gambling rooms, restaurants, shops, tenements and bakeries. The mobile narrator passes rapidly between classes and occupations, allowing the narrative to juxtapose wealth, vice, labour and poverty. Minor figures are introduced through one vivid detail or satirical label, so the city feels crowded and energetic without requiring extended characterization.

The prevailing tone is jaunty, cynical and mock-sophisticated, but it periodically opens into restrained pathos. Much of the story ridicules moral pretension, especially the notion that money can be intrinsically corrupted by its source. The church rejects a gambler’s donation while accepting money generated by dubious church-fair practices; respectable institutions appear no cleaner than gambling houses or cafes. Against this satire, the starving woman and sick child introduce genuine suffering. Their episode is sentimental in subject but controlled by the bill’s wisecracking narration and by the one-dollar note’s deliberately unsentimental slang.

Structurally, the story begins with a paradoxical premise, develops through episodic circulation, and narrows into a single moral test. Early adventures establish the narrator’s voice and the social reach of money; the gambler’s rejected donation then supplies the central question of “taint.” The poor woman’s use of the bill provides the practical answer, and the final exchange turns that answer into a punch line. The ending has the force of an epigram: the official inscription on the note defeats the abstract distinction between clean and unclean money. This produces a characteristic reversal in which socially disreputable money performs an unmistakably charitable function.

Overall, the style combines urban vernacular, anthropomorphic fantasy, social satire, compressed anecdote and a neatly engineered ironic ending. Its moral argument is conveyed less through explicit preaching than through contrasts between what characters profess and what money actually does. Beneath the exuberant slang and comic bravado lies a humane skepticism about respectability, class judgment and institutional virtue.

The story uses a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century Gothic-realist style, combining close psychological observation with supernatural suggestion. Its setting is materially precise—a deserted New York boarding house, oppressive summer heat, narrow yards, muffled street noise—but these realistic details gradually acquire the qualities of a nightmare. The hot, stagnant atmosphere, deepening darkness and empty house externalize the protagonist’s grief and fear, turning an ordinary deathbed vigil into a scene of claustrophobic horror.

The narration is third-person and closely focalized through the wife. Although the narrator occasionally makes broad, authoritative statements about grief, death and the soul, most of the narrative follows her fluctuating sensations and thoughts. Free indirect discourse merges her consciousness with the narrative voice, especially in the rapid succession of questions: where is the soul, why does her husband not respond, where is Death, and from what direction will he come? This proximity allows perception, speculation and hallucination to blur. The reader experiences sounds and shadows as she does, while remaining aware that terror may be distorting them.

The diction is elevated, formal and emotionally intense. Abstract nouns such as “Death,” “Ego,” “Soul,” “Personality,” “Intelligence” and “EAR” are capitalized to transform concepts into palpable forces. The vocabulary alternates between metaphysical language and physical detail: spiritual union and disintegrating matter, the soul and its “putrefying tenement,” loving memory and the cold, yellow hands of the dying man. This collision of idealism with bodily decay is central to the story’s effect. Archaic or ceremonious constructions—“Did she light the gas,” “the Creator had vouchsafed,” “the while”—reinforce the period style and lend solemnity to the narration.

Sentence structure shifts with the protagonist’s mental state. Long, layered sentences dominate the descriptive and philosophical passages, often accumulating clauses, qualifications and parallel phrases. Under stress, the prose contracts into fragments, exclamations and repeated questions: “Where was Death?”; “He still lived”; “gone alone!” Dashes interrupt the flow and imitate broken thought, while semicolons support measured, rhetorical development. Repetition is a major rhythmic device. Words and structures such as “waiting,” “slowly,” “one—two—three,” “louder, louder,” and “How could he” reproduce obsessive thought and stretch subjective time.

The imagery is tactile, auditory and funereal. Heat is rendered as a “hot suspended ocean,” darkness as a “pall,” the husband’s face as white sculpture, and the approaching presence through iron feet, vibrating walls and thunderous echoes. Personification drives the horror: Death becomes a deliberate male intruder who creeps, loiters, hunts and knocks. The wife imagines resisting him physically, exposing both her helplessness and her desire to convert an incomprehensible process into an antagonist she might fight. Recurring contrasts—heat and icy shock, silence and crashing sound, bodily stillness and mental agitation, intimate love and impersonal mortality—heighten the emotional pressure.

Pacing is deliberately constricted and suspenseful. The narrative covers a brief vigil but expands it through microscopic attention to breathing, heartbeats, darkness and intermittent noises. Early passages dwell on grief and metaphysical bewilderment; the middle converts grief into fear; the final section organizes that fear around the sound of approaching footsteps. Each landing and stair becomes a stage in a countdown. The story withholds the mundane identity of the visitor until the final sentence, allowing the wife’s conviction that Death is approaching to dominate the scene.

The tone moves from elegiac desolation through philosophical anguish into hysteria and Gothic terror. Fleeting irony appears in the comparison between Death’s arrival and theatrical entrance music, but even this moment of self-conscious levity becomes part of the terror. The ending uses concise dramatic irony: the supposedly supernatural footsteps belong to Mary, while the wife’s fear has itself become fatal. The final image of “a dead woman lying across a dead man” is stark, symmetrical and emotionally compressed, replacing the earlier ornate introspection with blunt closure.

Overall, the style is characterized by psychological intensity, rhetorical repetition, personified abstraction, sensuous morbidity and a gradual transformation of realist detail into subjective Gothic horror. Its central method is to make an internal experience—anticipatory grief and terror of death—seem physically present in the room, while preserving enough ambiguity for the climax to function both as supernatural apparition and fatal misperception.

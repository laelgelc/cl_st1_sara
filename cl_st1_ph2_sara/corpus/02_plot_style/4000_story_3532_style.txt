The story uses a formal, realist mode associated with nineteenth-century psychological and moral fiction. Its prose is clear, sober, and largely unornamented, with an authoritative narrator who presents social circumstances, personal history, and inner motivation as parts of a coherent moral inquiry. The diction is elevated but accessible, incorporating period-specific military, aristocratic, and religious vocabulary—such as “aide-de-camp,” “maid of honour,” “starets,” “tonsure,” and “hermitage”—without making the narrative densely archaic. Occasional French dialogue reinforces the cosmopolitan manners and social pretensions of the Russian upper classes. The apparent missing spaces between some words are transcription artefacts rather than stylistic features.

The narration is predominantly third-person omniscient, but it stays closely aligned with Kasatsky’s consciousness. The narrator moves freely between external summary, psychological explanation, remembered experience, and brief passages of direct interior thought. Characters’ motives are often stated explicitly, then complicated by distinctions between what they believe about themselves and what actually drives them. Kasatsky’s religious vocation, for example, is presented as sincere while also being entangled with pride, wounded vanity, ambition, and the desire for superiority. This creates a diagnostic narrative voice: compassionate enough to recognise mixed motives, but unsparing in exposing self-deception.

The narrator also intervenes with generalisations and parenthetical judgments, sometimes speaking in the first person: “I think,” for instance, introduces reflections on social classes or changing attitudes toward women. These intrusions give the prose the quality of moral and social commentary rather than neutral observation. Broad classifications—such as the division of high society into four groups—reduce complicated institutions to lucid, memorable schemes. The tone in such passages is confident, analytical, and faintly ironic. Social prestige, courtly glamour, monastic reputation, and public displays of virtue are all treated with suspicion.

Sentence structure alternates between long, cumulative analytical sentences and short, emphatic statements. The longer sentences frequently assemble motives through coordination, qualification, repetition, and contrast, mirroring the layered nature of the protagonist’s psychology. Recurrent constructions such as “not merely … but,” “as soon as,” and “whether … or” establish logical relationships with unusual explicitness. Short sentences then mark decisions, reversals, or revelations: Kasatsky learns the truth about his fiancée, leaves the service, enters a monastery, or becomes a hermit. Dashes, semicolons, parentheses, and ellipses reproduce hesitation, correction, emotional interruption, and the narrator’s habit of refining an initial claim.

Characterisation is built through repeated behavioural patterns rather than isolated description. Kasatsky’s pursuit of perfection appears successively in study, military discipline, social accomplishment, romantic aspiration, monastic obedience, and ascetic withdrawal. Parallel episodes reveal that the object of his striving changes while its underlying structure remains constant. This repetition gives the story a strongly patterned moral design: worldly ambition survives beneath religious renunciation, and humility can itself become a form of pride. Physical details are selected economically but tellingly. Nicholas I’s erect figure and sonorous voice embody imperial charisma; the Abbot’s plump hands, protruding stomach, and satisfied smile expose worldly complacency; Kasatsky’s pallor, trembling jaw, and clenched fist make suppressed violence visible.

Dialogue is concise, socially coded, and often dramatic. Polite formulas conceal status calculations, while broken phrases and silences carry emotional force. In the engagement scene, hesitation, incomplete confession, and unanswered questions gradually reveal the scandal. During confrontations, speech becomes abrupt and imperative—“Don’t touch me!” or “You may go!”—allowing restrained narration to give way to sudden intensity. Internal monologue is similarly direct and less formal than the surrounding prose, especially when temptation, boredom, fear, or vanity breaks through a character’s composed exterior.

The pacing combines large-scale biographical summary with sharply dramatised scenes. Years of education, military advancement, or monastic life may be compressed into a few paragraphs, while decisive moral crises unfold through dialogue, gesture, sensory detail, and moment-by-moment thought. This produces an episodic structure organised around successive stations in Kasatsky’s attempt at self-mastery. Roman-numeral sections mark major phases, and transitions often have the plain force of chronicle: he enters the monastery, receives ordination, moves to another monastery, becomes a hermit, and encounters a new temptation. Each apparent ascent prepares a more searching test.

Description is restrained and functional rather than luxuriant. Natural imagery briefly intensifies emotional atmosphere—the shaded linden alley, trilling nightingales, rustling leaves, smooth snow, tinkling bells—but does not become symbolically obscure. Sensory details are especially prominent when exposing worldliness or temptation: wine on the breath, cigar smoke in whiskers, glittering uniforms, fine clothes, fur, candlelight, and bodily beauty. Contrasts between sacred settings and material sensations sharpen the story’s central conflicts.

The overall tone is grave, lucid, and morally investigative, with intermittent irony and flashes of melodrama at moments of humiliation or temptation. The style depends on antithesis: appearance versus motive, worldly rank versus spiritual rank, obedience versus self-will, purity versus sexuality, humility versus vanity, and faith versus doubt. Its central technique is to reinterpret every outward victory as a possible inward defeat. The result is a psychologically penetrating style in which plot serves as a sequence of ethical experiments, and the protagonist’s greatest danger lies not simply in desire or disbelief but in his persistent need to regard himself as exceptional.

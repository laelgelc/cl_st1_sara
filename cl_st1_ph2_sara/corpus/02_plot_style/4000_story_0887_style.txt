The story is a light comic satire shaped by affectionate irony. Its central method is to contrast Chester’s inflated romantic imagination with mundane physical reality, especially appetite, family life, money, grooming and adolescent awkwardness. The narrator treats youthful love as both sincere and ridiculous: Chester’s emotions are never entirely dismissed, but his idealisation of Mildred is steadily punctured by her vigorous eating and practical self-interest. The tone remains genial rather than cruel, moving from amused observation through farce to a neatly ironic conclusion.

The narration is third-person omniscient, though it stays chiefly aligned with Chester’s perspective. Free indirect touches allow his assumptions to colour the prose—Mildred eating beans becomes “an outrage, a sacrilege”—while the narrator’s more mature voice quietly exposes those assumptions. This voice is urbane, knowing and mock-serious, often applying elevated language to trivial events. Chester is overcome by “reverie,” performs “ablutions of appalling thoroughness,” and imagines a boxing match ending in a “coup de grâce.” Such grand diction comically magnifies teenage dressing, daydreaming and posturing. Parenthetical qualifications and dry explanatory asides reinforce the narrator’s amused superiority.

The prose is visually precise and rich in concrete period detail. Clothing, toiletries, domestic furnishings, railway times, candy boxes, menus and public buildings are catalogued with comic exactness. Brand-like phrases and advertising language—such as the underwear label proclaiming that “No Human Hand Touches It”—help locate the story in an early twentieth-century consumer culture while gently mocking Chester’s faith in appearances. Colours are especially prominent: maroon, lustrous blue, purple, red, yellow, green and black give the scenes a bright, almost cartoon-like vividness. Food descriptions become increasingly elaborate and chromatic as Chester’s fantasy collapses.

Sentence structure alternates between long, ceremonious descriptive sentences and brisk dialogue. The longer sentences accumulate qualifications, lists and mock-logical explanations, creating a leisurely comic rhythm. Shorter clauses and exchanges sharpen embarrassment, family teasing and romantic hesitation. Dialogue is strongly characterising: Chester speaks in evasive circumlocutions when confessing love, Mildred relies on childish intensifiers such as “heavenly,” “horrid” and “I could just DIE,” and the parents use practical, deflating language. Dialect is used sparingly for the New York official—“foist,” “Satiddy,” “Thoisday”—to add social texture and heighten the couple’s bewilderment.

Repetition provides the main structural and comic motif. Mildred’s claim that she could “just die” eating various foods recurs until it becomes an absurd refrain, even seeming to sound in the rhythm of the train wheels. Her repeated insistence that she feels “weak and funny” without nourishment delays the elopement in an escalating sequence of sweets, griddle cakes, sausage and lunch. The escalation is carefully engineered: Chester initially imagines her subsisting on tiny, ethereal portions, but each new scene reveals a larger appetite. The plot therefore operates as a running reversal of his idealised menu, culminating in physical sickness and emotional disillusionment.

The comedy also depends on dramatic irony, incongruity and delayed payoff. Readers recognise Mildred’s appetite long before Chester fully accepts it, and apparently incidental food references return in altered form at the end. The final domestic menu reproduces the very dishes associated with the disastrous lunch, transforming Chester—formerly eager for food—into the person who insists that there are “other things in life besides food.” The closing hope that Mildred “gets enough to eat” is concise, bitterly comic and structurally satisfying, since it converts his former worship into weary sarcasm.

Characterisation is economical and behaviour-based. Chester is romantic, vain, inexperienced and theatrically masculine: his mirror-boxing fantasy, rehearsed “nonchalant” saunter and lowered adult-sounding voice reveal a personality assembled from newspapers, popular culture and adolescent daydreams. Mildred’s birdlike voice and sentimental vocabulary initially support his image of delicacy, while her actions contradict it. The family acts as a chorus of worldly realism, repeatedly puncturing Chester’s pretensions through grammatical correction, teasing and observations about appetite. Overall, the style combines social comedy, adolescent farce, mock-heroic narration and motif-driven plotting in polished, accessible prose.

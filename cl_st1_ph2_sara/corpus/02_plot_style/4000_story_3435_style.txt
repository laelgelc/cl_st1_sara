The story adopts the form of a regional fireside anecdote, framed by a cultivated first-person narrator who visits an elderly Irish farming couple and invites them to explain the secret of their long marriage. The narrator is observant, courteous and mildly self-deprecating. His standard, relatively formal English contrasts sharply with the couple’s vernacular speech, creating social and linguistic distance while also allowing him to act as the reader’s interpreter. Brief parenthetical explanations of local words and customs reinforce the impression of an outsider recording a distinctive rural community.

Dialogue dominates the narrative. Michael and Katty speak in heavily phoneticised Hiberno-English, with spellings such as “iv,” “wid,” “whin,” “shtand” and “undhershtand,” alongside Irish terms of affection and local vocabulary including “acushla,” “boreen,” “caubeen” and “blackthorn.” Their grammar, idioms, repetitions and proverbial turns are as important as pronunciation. The dialect is highly performative rather than unobtrusively realistic: it supplies humour, marks regional identity and gives oral energy to long stretches of reported storytelling. Katty’s voice is especially expansive and dramatic, moving freely between narration, quoted dialogue, commentary and retrospective wisdom. Michael speaks less, but his compact observations have the rhythm of maxims.

The prose alternates between polished narrative exposition and animated oral narration. Descriptive passages are economical but sensuous, using domestic details such as the glowing hearth, aromatic turf, cool dairy and clean crockery to establish warmth, order and rural solidity. Material objects—the rack-comb, razor, coat, pipe and blackthorn—also serve as concrete anchors for the central anecdote. The syntax outside dialogue is often balanced and elaborately qualified, while the spoken passages rely on short challenges, parallel exchanges and cumulative clauses. Repetition, particularly in the argument over the fair and the comb, creates both comic timing and mounting tension.

Structurally, the story proceeds by delay and redirection. The narrator repeatedly asks for a simple formula for marital happiness, but the couple answer through digressions, jokes, memories and metaphors. The image of happiness as a road, high-road or private boreen becomes the principal organising motif. This pattern gives the tale a conversational, seemingly spontaneous shape while steadily preparing the revelation about the morning after the wedding. The comb episode is presented almost as a miniature stage scene: opposing declarations are repeated, physical action abruptly intervenes, and Katty pauses at the crucial moment before supplying the outcome. A final qualification—Michael’s return from the road to the fair—complicates the apparent moral by showing tenderness after coercion.

The tone blends affection, comedy, nostalgia and didacticism. Humour arises from dialect, marital teasing, the narrator’s embarrassment about his engagement, and the old couple’s habit of interrupting or correcting one another. Beneath that genial surface, however, lies a patriarchal conception of domestic order in which peace depends upon an acknowledged household “master” and physical punishment is treated as corrective instruction. The story presents this attitude through the couple’s own retrospective approval rather than through overt authorial condemnation. From a modern perspective, the slap and the closing analogy between women and children produce a disturbing tension with the otherwise sentimental portrayal of devotion.

Characterisation is largely indirect. Katty emerges through her vivid memory, quick inference, emotional candour and capacity to turn experience into domestic philosophy. Michael is taciturn, physically authoritative and fond of proverbial wisdom, but is softened by humour, affection and his decision not to attend the fair. Their mutual praise—each crediting the other for the prosperity of the farmhouse—helps establish enduring loyalty before the more troubling origin story is disclosed. The narrator remains a lightly comic intermediary whose persistent questions draw out the tale without making him its centre.

Overall, the style is oral, theatrical and strongly regional, combining ethnographic observation with comic dialogue and a moral fable. Its chief signatures are the contrast between standard narration and dense phonetic dialect, the use of homely objects to dramatise abstract questions, the gradual unfolding of an anecdotal “secret,” proverbial imagery, affectionate irony and a concluding piece of folk wisdom whose historical assumptions are more unsettling than its genial delivery initially suggests.

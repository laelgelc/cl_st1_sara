The story adopts the style of an early twentieth-century romantic adventure set in colonial Australia, combining domestic realism, frontier danger, outlaw mythology, and restrained melodrama. Its plot is built around a concealed-identity twist: the stranger whom Dot assumes to be Inspector Hill is actually Buckskin Bill. The narration carefully withholds explicit confirmation while planting conspicuous clues—the revolver, the stranger’s physical confidence, his guarded answers, his knowledge of animals, and his proposed “bargain.” This creates dramatic irony once the real inspector arrives and allows the romance and the escape plot to resolve simultaneously.

The narrative voice is a conventional third-person omniscient narrator, though it remains closely aligned with Dot’s perceptions and emotions. The reader sees the stranger largely as she does: first as a source of rescue, then as an attractive but morally questionable policeman, and finally as the fugitive to whom she owes her life. The narrator occasionally steps back to explain circumstances, as in the summary of Jack’s cattle losses and the police pursuit, or to make lightly humorous generalizations about gender and inexperience. Phrases such as “things which a mere ignorant male would never have dreamt of” give the narration a playful, socially knowing tone.

The diction is lucid, accessible, and period-specific. Australian colonial expressions such as “new chum,” “slouch hat,” “scrub,” “blue gum trees,” and “a few head of cattle” establish place without heavy descriptive exposition. Colloquial contractions and pronunciations—“p’r’aps,” “Halloa,” “missis”—differentiate spoken language from the narrator’s more polished prose. The vocabulary of pursuit repeatedly casts the outlaw as an animal: he is “wily,” “tracked down like a wild beast,” “in a trap,” and said to have “broken cover.” This imagery reinforces the frontier setting while also supporting Dot’s objection to the unequal hunt.

Dialogue dominates the story and carries most of the characterization. Exchanges are brisk, teasing, and often lightly combative. Dot’s speech is impulsive and emphatic, full of exclamations, protests, rhetorical questions, repetitions, and abrupt reversals. Jack’s dialogue is affectionate but paternal, while the stranger’s is laconic, humorous, and strategically evasive. His answers frequently redirect questions rather than resolve them, allowing the deception to remain plausible. The flirtation develops through verbal sparring: Dot condemns the policeman’s profession, while the outlaw, posing as that policeman, tests her sympathy and invites her to judge him “without prejudice.” The repeated language of bargains, fairness, odds, and professions gives their conversation the form of a moral negotiation as well as a courtship.

Sentence structure varies with pace. Domestic and expository passages use moderately long, balanced sentences, often accumulating details of work, landscape, or background. Moments of danger shift into shorter clauses and rapid sequencing: the grass moves, the snake rises, Dot cries out, and Robin runs toward the house. Repetition heightens sensation and emotional intensity, as in “sucked and sucked,” “she thrilled her—they thrilled her,” and “very, very slowly.” Dashes are especially prominent. They reproduce hesitation, interruption, shock, and unspoken implications, making the prose feel emotionally immediate while preserving the period’s decorum.

Description is selective and functional rather than lavish. The Australian landscape is rendered through heat, glare, pasture, scrub, grass, barns, lambs, and blue gums. These concrete details provide both atmosphere and plot machinery: the oppressive heat contributes to Dot’s dizziness and sleep, the long grass conceals the snake, and the open country suits the fugitive’s escape. Colour imagery is unusually strong. Dot’s blue eyes, the stranger’s “extraordinarily blue eyes,” the vividly green snake, the sun-baked yard, and the golden evening pastures create a bright, almost heightened visual world. The stranger’s resemblance to a “young wild animal” links physical attractiveness with danger and freedom.

Characterization relies on physical gesture and emblematic traits. Dot is repeatedly described as boyish, sunburnt, candid, energetic, and indomitable. Her combination of domestic competence and declared willingness to shoot an outlaw places her between traditional femininity and frontier independence. At the same time, her vulnerability to the snake, alcohol, sleep, and romantic fascination returns her to the conventions of the endangered and enchanted heroine. The stranger is defined through size, strength, blue eyes, white teeth, a “magnetic” smile, and gentleness controlled by latent force. Inspector Hill, by contrast, is “lantern-jawed,” sardonic, keen-eyed, and emotionally austere. These compressed physical contrasts immediately guide the reader’s sympathies.

The emotional style is romantic and sensuous but euphemistic. Attraction is conveyed through eye contact, flushed faces, lowered voices, trembling, touch, and bodily awareness rather than direct declarations. The treatment of the snake-bite permits unusually intimate contact under the cover of medical necessity. The later kiss on the wound transforms the site of danger into a romantic symbol, while Dot’s continued touching of the red mark makes it a private token of the encounter. Expressions such as “the force of the man throbbing behind” his touch and “one burning moment” intensify desire without violating the story’s restrained social register.

The story also uses fairy-tale and enchantment imagery to elevate a brief frontier episode into romance. Dot sleeps “like the enchanted princess of old,” the evening landscape becomes a “magic atmosphere,” and the stranger’s arrival seems governed by “lucky chance.” The outlaw therefore functions partly as a dangerous romantic rescuer, while Dot’s awakening marks a transition from innocence to secret emotional knowledge. This dreamlike imagery contrasts with the practical details of bread, mending, cattle, whisky, police work, and snake-bite treatment.

Humour softens both suspense and sentiment. Jack’s warnings not to shoot a policeman, Dot’s disdain for “mere ignorant” males, the stranger’s claim that man-hunting is hungry work, and Robin’s uncomplicated approval of the visitor all create comic relief. The dog also serves as an ironic guarantor of character: Robin accepts the outlaw instinctively even though human institutions label him a criminal. The final exchange between Jack and Hill continues this understated humour, converting confrontation into tacit mutual understanding.

The principal themes are justice versus mercy, institutional law versus personal honour, gratitude, loyalty, and the romantic appeal of the outsider. Dot begins with a simplistic willingness to shoot Buckskin Bill but changes once the supposed villain behaves courageously and compassionately. The story distinguishes legal guilt from moral worth without denying that Bill is a thief. Jack’s final decision to protect him rests on a personal debt and a code of reciprocity, while Hill’s muted response suggests reluctant comprehension. The conclusion avoids explicit moral resolution: Bill escapes, Dot keeps his secret, Jack shields her, and Hill effectively accepts defeat. This produces a warm, conspiratorial ending in which human sympathy temporarily overrides the machinery of justice.

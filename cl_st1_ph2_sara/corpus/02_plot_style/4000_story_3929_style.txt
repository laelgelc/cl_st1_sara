The story uses a close, unstable third-person perspective that repeatedly slips into first-person thought, remembered speech, and direct address. The narration remains attached to the protagonist’s disordered consciousness, so distinctions between narrator, thought, spoken remark, memory, and hallucinated conversation are deliberately blurred. Refrains such as “he kept saying” and “he kept thinking” frame the prose as compulsive self-narration, while abrupt addresses to “Madam,” “Doctor,” and an absent “old friend” make the monologue feel simultaneously private and performed. Other characters are reduced largely to detached sales phrases, which enter the protagonist’s thoughts without meaningful response and sharpen his isolation.

The syntax is long, recursive, and accumulative. Sentences expand through parentheses, qualifications, substitutions, lists, dashes, and repeated conjunctions, often revising their own claims as they proceed. Assertions are immediately destabilised: a wall becomes a doorway, glass is “not quite glass,” and touching becomes “almost” touching. This continual correction gives the prose a circling, obsessive rhythm and formally imitates a mind unable to settle upon a reliable perception. Parentheses are especially prominent, serving as intrusive afterthoughts, alternate labels, private glosses, and sudden changes in logical direction. Shorter statements—“Nobody can not jump”—occasionally interrupt the flow with aphoristic force, but even these tend to contain paradox or verbal distortion.

The diction combines ordinary speech with scientific, psychological, architectural, commercial, and pseudo-technological language. Words such as “prefrontal lobe,” “amoeba,” “viable,” and “filterable air” coexist with synthetic product names such as “flexsan,” “duroid,” “Minipiano,” and “ugli-proof.” These fabricated terms parody modern advertising and industrial design, creating a world of hygienic, prefabricated surfaces whose names promise precision while making reality less intelligible. Sales language—“washable,” “crease-resistant,” “tested,” “maximum of openness”—is treated as eerie and dehumanising. The contrast between this sterile vocabulary and lyrical phrases about spring, love, poetry, and natural growth produces a persistent tension between emotional longing and manufactured modernity.

Repetition supplies the story’s main structural principle. Doors, walls, circles, rats, jumping, blows to the nose, food, convulsions, exhaustion, and willingness recur with altered meanings. The laboratory rat becomes the governing metaphor for human existence: people are trained to trust religious belief, scientific knowledge, money, romantic love, domestic achievement, or social convention, only to find that the promised “door” no longer opens. The metaphor is not presented once and explained; it spreads through the entire narrative until architecture, memory, bodily movement, and language itself all become parts of the experiment. The protagonist’s compulsive return to the man in Jersey similarly turns a minor anecdote into an emblem of breakdown under insoluble pressures.

The tone mixes existential dread, black comedy, pathos, and satire. Logical absurdities, awkward neologisms, and literalised clichés can be comic, yet the humour remains inseparable from fear and exhaustion. The protagonist is both ridiculous and deeply vulnerable. His heightened self-awareness allows him to analyse his own collapse, but that analysis becomes another circuit in the collapse. The story therefore avoids a clean division between sanity and madness: the environment is genuinely confusing, manipulative, and impersonal, while his response merely intensifies those qualities into a total explanatory system.

Imagery repeatedly converts mental distress into bodily and spatial experience. Psychological frustration becomes jumping, bumping, bleeding, racing, falling, or meeting a floor that rises toward the foot. Transparent and reflective surfaces create doubles and reversals, especially when the protagonist sees his own eyes and recognises the defeated expression of the experimental rats. Doors promise transcendence but become barriers; houses promise stability but become things to dismantle; moving stairs carry the protagonist passively downward. The ending offers no firm release: the automatic door opens correctly, yet the ground still seems to rise to meet him, preserving the perceptual disturbance in a quiet final gesture.

Overall, the style is modernist and absurdist, built from stream-of-consciousness effects, syntactic recursion, destabilised reference, satirical jargon, and obsessive motifs. Its form enacts its subject: the prose keeps “jumping” from one verbal or conceptual door to another, searching for coherence in a world whose meanings and rules are continually being changed.

The story adopts the style of a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century maritime adventure: orderly, descriptive, morally earnest, and grounded in practical seamanship. Its narration combines documentary realism with steadily intensifying peril. Nautical procedures, geographical references, cargo properties, shipboard routines, and technical vocabulary—“testing-rod,” “holystoned,” “oakum,” “scuppers,” “braces,” and “squared yards”—lend the account an air of authority. Expository passages explain unfamiliar matters directly, such as the spontaneous combustion of Cardiff coal, the behavior of pilot-fish, and the customs of deep-water ships. These explanations slow the action temporarily but make the emergency feel plausible and instructional rather than sensationally contrived.

The point of view is third-person omniscient, with the narrative moving among Captain Stafford, Mrs. Stafford, Howard, and occasionally the crew. Captain Stafford supplies the story’s professional perspective; his knowledge allows danger to be recognized from an almost imperceptible smell and translated into decisive action. Mrs. Stafford provides the emotional and domestic perspective, particularly through her anxiety for her child and her grief over possessions laden with personal associations. Howard’s innocent understanding creates both humor and pathos. The narrator also speaks with broad authority, offering general observations such as “Anxiety and suspense destroy appetite” and explaining maritime life directly to the reader. This produces the confident, instructive voice characteristic of period adventure fiction.

The prose is generally formal and lucid, with a moderately elevated vocabulary and a preference for complete, carefully balanced sentences. Long descriptive and explanatory sentences often accumulate details through lists, semicolons, dashes, and subordinate clauses. Shorter statements appear at moments of discovery or crisis—“Again that slight smell of gas,” “Dawn put an end to suspense,” and “Then came the order ‘Abandon ship!’”—giving key developments greater force. The syntax therefore participates in the pacing: expansive sentences establish atmosphere and procedure, while compressed declarations mark irreversible turns.

Description is vivid, visual, and frequently chromatic. The sea is populated by “deep orange,” “steel-blue,” “pale lemon,” and pink forms; the sunset contains an almost catalogic range of colors; and the final fire transforms the water into “a sea of blood.” Similes and metaphors are accessible rather than obscure: the ocean resembles “a vast mirror,” the boats are “frail cockle-shells,” the ship becomes a volcano, and the internal fire is a “demon of destruction.” Personification reinforces the conflict, with the rudder-chains seeming to remind the crew of the calm, the ship appearing to flee its hidden enemy, and the flames writhing eagerly up the rigging.

A central stylistic device is contrast. The tranquil, crystal-clear Pacific conceals a shark beneath the ship, just as the handsome, apparently secure Lochleven conceals fire inside its cargo. The procession of marine creatures is enchanting before the crisis but becomes maddeningly monotonous afterward. The sublime sunset and tropical storm are set against suffocating gas and mortal danger. Most importantly, the majestic exterior of the ship remains intact until its decks burst open, dramatizing the discrepancy between appearance and reality. Repeated volcanic imagery makes this concealed destruction especially vivid.

The pacing follows a controlled escalation. The opening is leisurely and observational, devoting substantial space to marine life, the captured turtle, and Howard’s play. Danger enters through a minute sensory detail, after which the narrative proceeds in stages: suspicion, investigation, attempted containment, temporary hope, confirmation of fire, preparations for abandonment, a brief reprieve supplied by wind, and final evacuation. Time markers—noon, evening, dawn, Sabbath services, midnight, and eight bells—organize the action and create a shipboard chronology. Alternations between crisis and respite prolong suspense without breaking the story’s realism.

Dialogue is sparse, functional, and socially differentiated. The captain’s commands are concise and technical, reflecting discipline and competence. Exchanges between husband and wife are restrained, with emotion conveyed more through implication and observation than open declaration. Howard’s speech is simple, literal, and comic: he wonders whether his father has “preached all the sermons” he knows and worries about Sunday pudding. His childish concerns repeatedly reduce the vast disaster to immediate, personal terms. This reaches its climax when the loss of the toy Lord Nelson becomes, for him, more emotionally comprehensible than the destruction of the ship itself.

Characterization relies on conduct rather than extensive psychological analysis. Captain Stafford is vigilant, practical, religious, and unwilling to abandon his vessel prematurely. Mrs. Stafford is dignified and emotionally controlled, struggling against despair rather than yielding to it. Howard is affectionate, energetic, mischievous, and innocent. Minor figures are sketched economically through telling details, as with the apprentice grieving over the sea-chest prepared by his widowed mother. Such touches broaden the emotional stakes beyond the captain’s family without distracting from the main action.

The tone evolves from wonder to unease, suspense, sublimity, and elegy. Humor appears gently in the turtle ride, the one-armed toy sailor, and Howard’s religious questions, but it never becomes satirical. The treatment of danger is grave and restrained; even the ship’s destruction, though spectacular, is described with awe rather than melodramatic panic. Religious feeling is explicit but practical. Prayer and Sabbath observance matter, yet the captain insists that human effort must precede appeals for deliverance. Providence is implied through the timely squalls and eventual survival, though the story avoids presenting rescue as effortless divine intervention.

The ending deliberately shifts from public catastrophe to private grief. The burning of a large vessel is rendered on a grand, almost apocalyptic scale, but the final emotional focus is Howard’s forgotten toy. This sentimental close is characteristic of family-oriented adventure fiction: material loss becomes poignant through an intimate object, and childhood sorrow provides the most memorable measure of disaster. The final projection that Howard will remember those tears even at “four score” gives the episode the retrospective weight of a lifelong trauma.

Overall, the style blends nautical realism, natural-history observation, moral instruction, domestic sentiment, and adventure suspense. It is best imitated through an authoritative omniscient narrator, precise procedural detail, richly colored but intelligible imagery, gradual escalation from calm to catastrophe, restrained adult emotion, and a concluding human detail that makes a large-scale disaster personally affecting.

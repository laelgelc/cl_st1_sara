The story adopts a polished nineteenth-century comic-gothic style, combining psychological observation, supernatural suggestion, sentiment, and dry social satire. It is narrated retrospectively in the first person by a practical civil engineer whose professed distrust of anything that cannot be mathematically demonstrated establishes him as a rational, mildly sceptical observer. His curiosity about “odd people,” however, makes him receptive to Mr. Jaffrey’s fantasy. This tension between professional rationalism and imaginative susceptibility drives the narrative voice: the narrator analyses Jaffrey as a specimen, yet gradually begins to think and speak of the imaginary Andy as if he were real.

The diction is formal, cultivated, and period-specific, with words such as “supposititious,” “celerity,” “perspicacious,” “enfantillage,” and “atmospherical.” French and classical expressions, literary references, and allusions to figures such as Benvenuto Cellini, Richard III, Dante, and the witch of Endor give the prose an urbane, bookish quality. This elevated narration is set against the plainer, colloquial speech of Mr. Sewell, whose clipped phrases, dialect spellings, and blunt idioms—“shilly-shallied,” “struck all of a heap,” “jest at that point”—mark him as practical, provincial, and emotionally unsympathetic. Mr. Jaffrey’s speech occupies a middle ground: genial, fanciful, digressive, and increasingly absorbed in its own invented reality.

Sentences are often long, balanced, and syntactically elaborate, using semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, dashes, and accumulative lists. The opening sentence is representative, building a series of parallel examples before arriving at the narrator’s own taste for unusual people. This expansive syntax supports both leisurely storytelling and comic escalation. Short sentences and fragments are reserved for emphasis, especially in dialogue: “A boy!”, “Got a tooth!”, and “He’s gone!” produce abrupt turns in mood and sharpen the contrast with the narrator’s more ceremonious prose. Repetition, delayed clarification, and carefully placed qualifications frequently create irony.

The humour is predominantly deadpan and character-based. The narrator treats absurd circumstances with mock seriousness, as when he remarks that assisting at imaginary births is not “strictly in my line,” or worries that Andy will soon be too old to spank because of his fantastic rate of growth. Literal-minded observations collide with impossible events, while elevated language is applied to trivial or grotesque subjects. The story also uses comic similes and metaphors: Jaffrey’s hair resembles theatrical footlights, Sewell “poniards” parsnips, and the innkeeper’s mind is finally described as a vacant room “to let. Unfurnished!” The narrator occasionally addresses the reader directly, especially when refusing to recount tedious details of childhood, creating a confidential, knowingly performative relationship with the audience.

Beneath the comedy lies a strong current of pathos. Jaffrey’s imaginary son grows out of unresolved grief over Mehetabel and embodies an entire domestic life that never occurred. The prose moves delicately between ridicule and sympathy, never reducing him entirely to a joke. Tender images—Jaffrey bending over an invisible cradle, drawing the shade so Andy can sleep, or mourning the child’s supposed death—temporarily suspend the satire. The narrator’s attitude evolves accordingly: curiosity becomes amusement, amusement becomes complicity, and complicity becomes genuine grief for “the poor little phantom.” This emotional progression gives weight to what might otherwise be merely an eccentric anecdote.

The setting contributes a restrained gothic atmosphere. The decaying tavern, empty rooms, rattling windows, spectral footsteps, rusty machinery, neglected graveyard, and violent autumn storm make hallucination seem locally plausible. Yet these gothic elements are repeatedly undercut by grotesque humour, as in the fantasy of thin family ghosts keeping warm by passing one another through a mangle. Weather functions symbolically and psychologically: Jaffrey is compared to a barometer, becoming hopeful in sunshine and despondent under storm clouds. The climactic gale externalises his agitation and accompanies Andy’s imaginary death, while the clear morning corresponds to Jaffrey’s emotional reset.

Structurally, the story unfolds as a framed case study or anecdotal mystery. The opening identifies Jaffrey as an “unfamiliar species,” after which the narrator gathers background information, observes him in private, develops a provisional psychological explanation, and records the rapid life of the invented child. Time is manipulated for comic and uncanny effect: Andy ages roughly a year each night, while illnesses and incidents that appear immediate may already belong to the previous year. This accelerated imaginary chronology creates a sequence of episodic scenes—birth, infancy, schooling, misbehaviour, punishment, and death—within the narrator’s two-week stay. The final exchange with Sewell supplies a twist: the apparently spontaneous fantasy is a fixed cycle that Jaffrey repeatedly performs with any available listener.

Dialogue carries much of the characterisation and pacing. Jaffrey’s excited declarations make his fantasy vivid without requiring direct access to his thoughts, while the narrator’s cautious questions allow the delusion to elaborate itself. Sewell’s interruptions and hostile glances foreshadow his prior knowledge. The ending retrospectively alters the whole narrative, revealing that the narrator has not witnessed a unique psychological development but has unknowingly taken a familiar role in a recurring script. Its final punch line is concise, cruel, and memorable, closing the story on comic deflation after the preceding tenderness.

Overall, the style is genteel, ironic, allusive, and rhythmically controlled. It blends realism of manners with whimsical fantasy and mild gothic horror, maintaining ambiguity about how fully the narrator is drawn into Jaffrey’s world. Its distinctive effect comes from treating an absurd delusion with both comic precision and humane melancholy, so that the imaginary child becomes simultaneously a running joke, a ghostly presence, and the embodiment of a life lost to hesitation.

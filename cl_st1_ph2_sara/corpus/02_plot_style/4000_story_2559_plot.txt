While fishing off Waihee, the skeptical narrator listens irritably as the elderly Hawaiian fisherman Kohokumu chants legends of Maui. When challenged, Kohokumu argues that Maui’s feats are no less reasonable than biblical creation and notes that traditional myths echo truths recognized by modern science.

Their fishing interrupts the debate. Kohokumu, despite being over seventy, dives forty feet, wrestles a huge octopus from a coral crevice, kills it with his teeth, and returns triumphantly. He attributes his vigor to the sea, which he regards as his mother and believes will someday receive and rebirth him. This leads to a philosophical exchange about dreams, truth, identity, and whether reality itself might be Maui’s dream.

To spare the narrator more singing, Kohokumu tells a supposedly factual tale about Keikiwai, an eleven-year-old “Water Baby” who understood the language of fish. When a visiting king demanded lobsters, the people could not gather them because forty sharks infested the lagoon. Keikiwai heard the sharks plotting to eat him and repeatedly announced that the shortest-tailed shark was his protector. Each time he distracted them by throwing a lava rock into the water, dove safely, and returned with a lobster. The suspicious sharks then measured their tails and devoured whichever among them had the shortest. This continued until the final shark, swollen from eating the others, burst and died.

Kohokumu insists the story is true, citing distant family testimony, the actual diving place, and thirty-nine lava rocks he claims still lie on the seabed. Before the narrator can object, hooked fish begin tugging their bamboo poles, and Kohokumu returns to fishing while confidently repeating that he knows the tale for a fact.

The story uses a retrospective first-person voice that combines the emotional intensity of childhood with the verbal sophistication of an adult memoirist. Events are filtered through a child’s imagination, but narrated in polished, highly literary prose. This double perspective creates much of the humour: childish hopes and grievances are treated with mock-epic seriousness, while adult behaviour is viewed as careless, dull, and faintly absurd. The narrator remains sympathetic to his younger self rather than condescending to him, presenting disappointment over the circus as a genuine emotional catastrophe.

The tone is whimsical, nostalgic, ironic, and exuberantly comic. It moves readily between melancholy and rapture, magnifying ordinary childhood experiences into cosmic events. A broken promise makes the earth seem flat and lifeless; the restoration of hope gives the mare wings of fire and sends the children through purple clouds. Elevated language is repeatedly applied to trivial or unruly behaviour, as when Harold’s tantrum becomes a professional “mission” or his repetitive chant is described as a hymn to the timeless glory of the circus. This mock-heroic treatment is affectionate rather than satirical in a harsh sense.

The diction is ornate, cultivated, and distinctly old-fashioned. It draws on classical mythology, religion, finance, warfare, romance, and imperial adventure: the gods reward Harold, expectation becomes a bank, a clown possesses a “panoply,” and circus performers appear as princesses or Cleopatras. Formal phrases such as “needless to say,” “thereupon,” “inexorably written,” and “vouch-safed” coexist with colloquial speech, childish contractions, and comic directness. Adults are often reduced to the distancing pronoun “Them,” reinforcing the child’s sense that they form an alien and oppressive class.

Sentences are frequently long, balanced, and rhythmically elaborate, with semicolons, dashes, parallel clauses, cumulative lists, and carefully delayed conclusions. The prose accelerates through repetition and enumeration at moments of excitement: circuses multiply across countries, Harold threatens to “yell, and yell himself into a fit,” and the children scout, lurk, creep, and ambuscade. Short statements are then used for comic finality or emotional impact, as in “Blood, however, was not to be had.” Exclamations, rhetorical questions, and emphatic repetition reproduce the extremity of childhood feeling.

Imagery is vivid, kinetic, and sensuous. The story repeatedly contrasts confinement with release, flatness with circular motion, and dull domestic order with the colour and velocity of the circus. Before the disappointment, the globe itself seems to spin like a circus ring; afterward, the world becomes “ditch-riddled, stagnant, and deadly flat.” At the performance, sound, smell, colour, and movement combine in a near-hallucinatory rush of hoofbeats, cymbals, bells, tan, gauze, spangles, and whip-cracks. Metaphors are often extended beyond realistic proportion, translating emotion into a transformed physical landscape.

Personification is a major device. Nature can be prim, friendly, sulky, or conspiratorial; roads crawl, elms dress themselves, suspense broods, and beauty rides out of the ring. Abstract states are similarly embodied: hope dies, ambition asserts itself, and the orange’s “soul and body” pass into Harold. Such animation reflects the narrator’s childhood consciousness, in which the external world appears to participate directly in private joy or misery.

Dialogue is sparse but sharply characterised. Adult speech is casual, distracted, and evasive, especially when the circus is mentioned alongside clothes and social engagements. Harold speaks loudly, concretely, and imperiously, while the “funny man” uses solemn understatement and treats the children as equals. His dry suggestion that they are suffering from “satiety and world-weariness” exemplifies the story’s characteristic incongruity between adult phrasing and childish circumstances. Dialect is lightly indicated through forms such as “tellin’” and the chant “We’re-goin’-to-the-circus,” without overwhelming readability.

The narrative structure follows a strong emotional arc: promise, anticipation, betrayal, despair, comic violence, rescue, fulfilment, and enchanted aftermath. Pacing slows during introspection and descriptive reverie, then quickens in scenes of quarrelling, travel, and performance. Transitions are often associative rather than strictly event-driven, following the narrator’s rapidly changing fantasies. At the circus, love for Coralie is swiftly displaced by ambition to become a clown, then by devotion to Zephyrine, accurately capturing the volatility and totality of childhood desire.

Humour arises from hyperbole, incongruity, understatement, and shifts in register. The children’s fantasies of bloodshed are presented as reasonable responses to frustration; a squashed orange becomes a “golden fraud”; sibling combat is described in legal and military terms. The narrator also gently exposes his younger self’s egotism, particularly in his confidence that Harold must surrender Coralie because age establishes an unquestionable hierarchy. These jokes preserve the internal logic of childhood while allowing the adult reader to perceive its absurdity.

The thematic centre is the moral seriousness of promises made to children. Adults possess alternatives and compensations, whereas children build entire imaginative worlds upon casual words. The story also contrasts conventional adulthood with a rarer form of sympathetic maturity. The “funny man” succeeds because he does not perform childishness or analyse the children’s feelings; he recognises their imaginative reality, acts decisively, and afterward leaves their enchantment undisturbed. Overall, the style is lyrical, mock-epic, sensuous, and comic, using ornate adult narration to vindicate the scale and authenticity of childhood emotion.

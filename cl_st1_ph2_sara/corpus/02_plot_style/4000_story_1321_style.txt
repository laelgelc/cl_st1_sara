The story adopts a retrospective first-person plural perspective, with an adult narrator recalling childhood adventures from within the children’s collective “we.” This double viewpoint is central to the style: events are presented with the immediacy and unquestioning logic of childhood, while the language carries an adult’s sophistication, irony, and literary awareness. The narrator seldom breaks the spell by explicitly contrasting past and present; instead, mature commentary is woven into the children’s assumptions, producing a comic incongruity between their minor escapades and the elevated terms in which they understand them.

The prose is ornate, leisurely, and distinctly literary. Its diction mixes formal, archaic, and Latinate language—“dubiety,” “fastnesses,” “admonishment,” “debarcation,” “imprecation”—with colloquial childhood expressions such as “rot,” “real brick,” “old dragon,” and “jolly.” This continual movement between registers creates much of the humour. Borrowing a farmer’s boat becomes “piracy”; evading punishment requires mastery of “the stern facts of life”; a disapproving aunt is classified as a hostile “she-creature.” Mythic, military, political, and legal vocabulary enlarges the children’s world without undermining its emotional reality.

Sentences are often long, balanced, and syntactically elaborate, shaped by semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, parallel clauses, and delayed comic conclusions. The narrator favours mock-authoritative formulations such as “the public voice” and “morally considered,” treating impulsive childhood decisions as though they were matters of statecraft. Shorter dialogue and action sentences interrupt these expansive passages, keeping the narrative lively. The rhythm therefore alternates between reflective, ceremonious narration and brisk scenes of argument, trespass, pursuit, and escape.

The dominant tone is affectionate, ironic, and gently satirical. The children’s self-importance, prejudices, and casual destructiveness are exposed without cruelty. Adult authority, meanwhile, appears restrictive, unimaginative, or faintly absurd: rules are obstacles to adventure, a nursemaid is an agent of retrieval, the aunt embodies joyless supervision, and Farmer Larkin becomes an enemy commander. The humour depends on understatement, euphemism, mock heroism, and deadpan moral inversion. Chasing calves leaves the narrator with “a conscience at peace with all men,” stealing a boat is “capturing” it, and Edward’s persecution of a cat is presented as an attempt to “teach it manners.”

Classical and literary allusion forms the story’s imaginative framework. Jason, Medea, the Argo, Ulysses, Perseus, Psyche, Atalanta, the Symplegades, and the sirens are not decorative references but templates through which the children transform their surroundings. A pig-trough becomes the Argo, a narrow stream becomes the Orinoco and a mythic sea, and an isolated young woman becomes Medea. References to fairy tales, *Pilgrim’s Progress*, *Westward Ho!*, Alfred and the Danes, and Borough English further create a densely intertextual voice. Yet the allusions remain playful and accessible because they arise from imaginative games rather than scholarly display.

Description is sensuous and atmospheric, especially in the garden episode. The quiet water-gate, overgrown flowers, heavy heliotrope, drawn blinds, and grey sundial establish an enchanted, suspended environment. Personification and metaphor give the setting emotional pressure: quiet “laps” the garden, scent seems hung in “solid festoons,” and the sundial “dominates” the lawn. The imagery shifts here from comic adventure to lyrical melancholy. Lucy’s emergence is described in flowing, almost mythic terms, and her temporary revival through play evokes Persephone returning from the underworld. This tonal deepening allows genuine sadness to surface without fully explaining it.

Characterization is economical and comic, built through action, speech, and recurring traits. Harold is imaginative, vain, persistent, and emotionally predictable; Edward is practical, bold, sceptical of pretending, and adept at evasion; the narrator occupies a middle position between imaginative immersion and observational irony. Adults are often rendered as types from the children’s perspective—the aunt, the farmer, the wrathful village woman—whereas Lucy briefly acquires emotional complexity. Dialogue is natural, brisk, and individualized, preserving childish directness while revealing each speaker’s logic. The children’s arguments often imitate adult reasoning but are designed to justify desires already settled.

Structurally, the story moves from a reflective account of the children’s acceptance of classical mythology into a concrete adventure that reenacts it. The boat expedition supplies a loose episodic shape: conception of the plan, exclusion of the girls, theft of the boat, voyage, discovery of “Medea,” interruption by authority, Edward’s comic pursuit, and tactical return home. The central garden scene slows the pace and introduces mystery and pathos; the surrounding river episodes restore comic momentum. The ending is characteristically understated, resolving the practical danger through Edward’s ingenuity and closing on another classical comparison, with Ulysses serving as the authority capable of persuading him where ordinary advice cannot.

Overall, the style blends Edwardian nostalgia, mock-epic comedy, pastoral adventure, and lyrical fantasy. Its defining effect comes from treating childhood imagination as both ridiculous and profound: the material world remains stubbornly ordinary, but the children’s literary vision genuinely transforms it. The narration smiles at their illusions while granting those illusions dignity, emotional force, and temporary sovereignty.

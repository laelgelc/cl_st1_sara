The story adopts the manner of a late-Victorian or Edwardian comic ghost tale, combining maritime adventure, supernatural recurrence, and satire of corporate respectability. Its narrator is conversational, omniscient, and conspicuously knowing, speaking directly to the reader and freely commenting on law, journalism, officialdom, and human motives. This voice often pretends to be sober and reasonable while delivering barbed ironies. Understatements such as describing a fatal accident as “of small importance to the general public” create a dry, macabre humour, while broad generalisations about officials, justice, and corporations give the narration the flavour of an urbane anecdote told by a worldly observer.

The tone moves between mock solemnity, genial cynicism, bureaucratic comedy, and restrained horror. Death is treated with deliberate casualness, while commercial procedures are described as though they were more consequential than the dead man himself. The company’s supposed generosity is repeatedly undercut by the narrator’s explanation of its self-interest, and the sentimental newspaper editorial is parodied through exaggerated praise and the absurd claim that it brought tears to the Stock Exchange. The supernatural element is handled in the same matter-of-fact register as tickets, cabin allocations, and compensation claims. This refusal to change tone makes Keeling’s return both eerie and comic: the ghost behaves less like a traditional spectre than an aggrieved customer insisting that a contract be honoured.

The narrative is tightly constructed around delayed explanation and escalating repetition. It begins by revealing Keeling’s death and the company’s eventual payment of £2,100, then asks how that unexpected outcome came about. The rest of the story supplies the answer through successive voyages. Keeling first reappears as an anomalous passenger whose name is absent from the list, dies again in an impossible repetition of the original accident, and then returns once more to state his terms explicitly. Each recurrence intensifies the pressure on the company while preserving the same basic pattern. The ending circles back to the opening fact, converting what initially looked like corporate benevolence into reluctant submission to supernatural enforcement. This frame gives the story the shape of a comic mystery or explanatory anecdote.

The prose mixes moderately long expository sentences with brisk dialogue and short paragraphs at moments of surprise. Parenthetical qualifications, balanced clauses, and ironic concessions are common: both Mrs. Keeling and the company are allowed to be “probably correct,” though the imbalance of power is unmistakable. The syntax often imitates legal or official reasoning, only to expose its absurdity. Repetition is used for comic and ominous effect, especially in Keeling’s promise to return “again and again.” Dashes, emphatic interruptions, rhetorical questions, and occasional exclamations enliven the dialogue and reflect period conventions of printed popular fiction.

The diction is accessible but lightly formal, with nautical and commercial vocabulary—“purser,” “hold,” “hatch,” “passenger list,” “legal liability,” and “managing director”—providing a practical framework for the fantastic events. Polite formulas such as “Quite so” and “What can I do for you?” contrast with colloquial phrases like “cut for it,” “pooh-poohed,” and “what the—,” helping the story move between respectable institutional surfaces and blunt human alarm. The invented name “Hot Cross Bun Line” immediately establishes a comic, faintly ridiculous corporate world, while the grand ship name “Gibrontus” parodies the imposing names associated with major liners.

Characterisation is economical and functional. Keeling is defined by his wan, cadaverous appearance, listless manner, “cold and fishy eyes,” and implacable logic. He does not rage or threaten violence; his calm insistence makes him more unsettling. The purser is officious, polite, practical, and gradually terrified, serving as the principal witness to the impossible. The captain and managing director represent institutional authority, while Mrs. Keeling remains largely offstage as the powerless victim of legal and financial inequality. Characters are presented less through psychological depth than through their roles in the machinery of commerce, transport, publicity, and liability.

A central stylistic device is the literalisation of figurative moral debt. The company treats Keeling’s passage as a contract and his death as a liability to be minimised; Keeling returns on precisely those terms, demanding either delivery to New York or compensation for breach. Justice, ineffective when pursued through courts, becomes mechanically enforceable through haunting. The increasing price of £100 per voyage gives supernatural vengeance the structure of a commercial penalty. The story’s governing irony is therefore that the ghost understands business logic better than the businessmen do.

Overall, the style is concise, plot-driven, and anecdotal, with horror subordinated to wit and social criticism. Suspense comes not from uncertainty about whether the apparition is real, but from watching respectable officials attempt to absorb an impossible event into routine procedure. The resulting profile is one of deadpan supernatural satire: polished, reader-conscious narration; economical description; dialogue-led scenes; escalating repetition; anti-corporate irony; and a final reversal that delivers both comic satisfaction and rough moral justice.

The story is written in a mode of late-nineteenth-century regional realism, combining affectionate social observation with restrained elegy. Its focus is deliberately local: a tiny rural community, its farms, store, church, graveyards, roads and scattered households become the frame through which national history is remembered. The setting is rendered with precise visual and geographical detail—elm trees, budding thickets, mountain ranges, old-fashioned houses, the church belfry and weather vane—so that Barlow feels both isolated and fully inhabited. Landscape is not merely decorative; its quietness and seasonal renewal heighten the pathos of the aging veterans and their dwindling numbers.

The narrative voice is third-person omniscient, calm, humane and gently authoritative. It moves easily between panoramic description, communal judgment and the private feelings of individual characters. At times it speaks with retrospective historical awareness, as in the observation that such veterans’ processions grow “more pathetic year by year.” This creates dramatic distance: the characters experience a modest local parade, while the narrator recognizes it as part of a vanishing national generation. The voice is sympathetic without becoming uncritical, acknowledging poverty, neglect, resentment, weakness and hypocrisy while consistently looking for the human motives beneath them.

A central stylistic feature is the contrast between the narrator’s polished standard English and the strongly marked rural dialect of the dialogue. The men speak in contractions, dropped consonants, nonstandard grammar and regional vocabulary: “’tain’t,” “wan’t,” “git,” “betwixt,” “ary one,” “public sperit,” and “rigiment.” Apostrophes densely reproduce elision, while idiomatic phrases such as “rum clinched with him an’ threw him” give the speech physical force and local color. The dialect distinguishes the characters socially but does not caricature them. Their language is plain, indirect and sometimes repetitive, yet capable of moral subtlety, humor and emotional power. Serious feelings are often approached through practical talk about planting, pensions, flags, routes, uniforms and expenses rather than through explicit confession.

The syntax varies according to narrative function. Descriptive passages often use long, cumulative sentences filled with coordinates, qualifications and spatial details, creating a leisurely surveying motion. Dialogue tends toward loosely connected clauses that imitate spontaneous rural conversation, with digressions, self-corrections, remembered quotations and anecdotal expansions. Short declarative sentences and isolated observations provide emphasis: “There was a long pause”; “The other men made no answer”; “It was one of the great moments of the day.” These pauses are essential to the emotional method, allowing silence and implication to carry what the characters cannot comfortably articulate.

Characterization is communal and conversational rather than plot-driven. John Stover, Henry Merrill and Asa Brown emerge through small differences in temperament: Stover is initially reserved but becomes an organizer; Merrill is reflective and emotionally receptive; Asa is talkative, practical and concerned with public recognition. Their shared history matters more than sharply opposed personalities. Secondary figures are presented through revealing social contradictions. Martin Tighe is treated as burdensome during the year but honored on Decoration Day; Eb Munson is remembered as both alcoholic and brave; Martha Down is first condemned for abandoning him, then quietly reinterpreted through her secret flowers and offer of a gravestone. The story repeatedly replaces easy judgment with belated compassion.

The structure is tripartite and gently dramatic. The first section begins with an ordinary Saturday-evening conversation and develops the idea of holding a Decoration Day observance. The second stages the procession itself, expanding from the three friends to the whole community and supplying the emotional climax. The final section returns to the store and to intimate conversation, allowing the men to interpret what has happened and revealing Martha’s private act of remembrance. This circular return gives the story formal balance: the same place and speakers reappear, but their sense of themselves and of Barlow has changed.

Pacing is unhurried. The opening devotes considerable space to weather, agriculture and village topography before the commemorative subject fully emerges. This slowness establishes ordinary life as the medium through which historical meaning surfaces. The parade is similarly described step by step, with stops at houses, graves and the poor farm. Rather than relying on a sudden twist, the story accumulates emotional force through modest incidents: old uniforms, a toy flag in a window, Grandmother Dexter standing at her door, flowers on paupers’ graves and an imperfect rendition of taps.

The tone blends nostalgia, tenderness, irony and melancholy. Humor appears in the men’s self-deprecation, their worries about making the procession look longer and their recognition of their own stiffness. The irony is rarely cruel; it exposes discrepancies between professed community values and everyday behavior. The sentimental elements—flags, tears, graves, martial music—are controlled by concrete detail and social candor. Emotion is strongest when understated: the veterans cannot look at one another during taps, Martha leaves flowers without seeking credit, and Stover speaks wistfully of memorial stones because his own brother lies in a nameless grave.

Symbolism remains accessible and rooted in physical objects. The flag represents national loyalty, but it also changes meaning according to scale: it is a grand borrowed banner, a small grave marker, a child’s toy and a sign of family connection to sacrifice. Faded uniforms and damaged bodies embody the erosion of time, while spring flowers and budding fields suggest renewal through remembrance. The procession transforms familiar men into representatives of a larger history, temporarily overcoming the community’s habit of measuring them by wealth, productivity or social usefulness. Taps functions as an auditory bridge between present-day Barlow and remembered army camps, producing the story’s most intense movement from local reality into collective memory.

The thematic style is ethically revisionary. Again and again, the story asks characters and readers to “view it all round”: Southern soldiers may also have died for their country; an alcoholic pauper may remain a courageous veteran; an apparently heartless woman may have carried lifelong remorse; an unimpressive parade may awaken genuine public spirit. Patriotism is therefore presented not as abstract rhetoric but as an enlarged capacity for memory, gratitude and mercy. The overall effect is intimate, reflective and quietly ceremonial—a regional tale in which plain speech and humble communal acts recover the moral magnitude of events that had once seemed too near, and later seemed in danger of being forgotten.

The story is written as a comic social satire, combining an omniscient narrative voice with irony, caricature and flashes of lyrical description. Its principal target is colonial status-seeking: the desire for British approval, official titles and social access is represented as a quasi-religious form of servility. At the same time, nationalism is not treated with complete solemnity. Public reputation, political conversion and patriotic celebrity are all shown to be vulnerable to vanity, accident and theatrical manipulation. The prevailing tone is amused, urbane and mischievously censorious rather than bitterly polemical.

The narrator is conspicuously present and frequently addresses characters, readers and abstractions directly. Exclamations such as “Alas, father Purnendu Sekhar!” imitate tragic lament while actually intensifying the comedy. Editorial intrusions—“we greatly doubt it,” “our Motherland,” and similar formulations—create the impression of a storyteller sharing a joke with an informed audience. This voice freely interprets motives, anticipates consequences and comments on the moral absurdity of events. It often assumes mock solemnity, treating trivial embarrassments as catastrophes and social farce as epic or sacred drama.

Irony operates at nearly every level. Honorifics and elevated phrases are repeatedly used in contexts that expose their emptiness: Rai Bahadurship becomes a “desert shore,” advancement a “ladder to Paradise,” and English acquaintances a pantheon of “gods.” The story repeatedly borrows the language of religion, mythology, science, warfare and state ceremony to describe vanity and social climbing. Salaaming is imagined as a transferable physical force; English boots are ceremonially worshipped; common English surnames become a devotional namavoli; and competing social factions attempt to “hook” Nabendu like a fish. These incongruous analogies give the prose its distinctive satirical energy.

The diction mixes formal, literary English with Anglo-Indian and Bengali cultural vocabulary. Terms such as “sahib,” “Burra Sahib,” “Rai Bahadur,” “bakshish,” “chuprassi,” “Poojah” and “Congresswalla” remain embedded in the prose, locating the narrative firmly within colonial Indian society. Explanatory glosses occasionally clarify culturally specific objects, as with the namavoli. The formal register is often deliberately inflated, full of phrases such as “by an irony of fate,” “auspicious day,” “with great pomp and ceremony” and “the Goddess of Fortune.” Against this ceremonious diction, blunt dialogue and colloquial insults—most notably “you are a howling idiot”—produce sharp comic contrast.

Sentences tend to be moderately long and syntactically balanced, with coordinated clauses, rhetorical qualifications and carefully staged reversals. Many paragraphs begin by establishing a dignified or sentimental premise and end by puncturing it. Parallelism and accumulation are common, especially in lists of honours, entertainments, officials, imagined dangers and humiliations. Repetition is used structurally as well as verbally: Nabendu is repeatedly tempted by prestige, mocked, provoked into a patriotic gesture, frightened by its consequences and then reassured by a new form of attention. Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions and dashes give the prose an animated, performative rhythm.

Figurative language is abundant and often grotesquely comic. Human beings are compared to pumpkins, donkeys, butterflies, fish, owls, lizard tails and helpless infants. These similes externalise psychological states with exaggerated physical vividness: Nabendu’s head moves “like a pumpkin swayed by the wind,” he twitches “like a koi-fish,” and his wounded heart convulses like a severed lizard’s tail. Metaphors of agriculture and commerce are also prominent. Social relationships become investments, honours grow in cultivated soil, marriage is a bargain, and politeness is judged by whether it yields a return. Such imagery exposes the calculations hidden beneath respectable behaviour.

The comic method depends heavily on dramatic irony. Nabendu rarely understands the full situation, while the narrator, Labanya and the reader do. Labanya’s mock concern is especially important: she appears to protect Nabendu’s prospects while deliberately goading him into ever more public acts of defiance. Her literal words are solicitous, but their pragmatic meaning is taunting. Nabendu’s vanity then completes each trap. He can be manipulated through ridicule because he wants simultaneously to retain British favour, appear courageous and impress his attractive sister-in-law. His political identity therefore emerges less from conviction than from embarrassment, flirtation and momentum.

Dialogue is brisk, pointed and theatrical. Speakers are distinguished less through idiosyncratic dialect than through rhetorical role: Labanya uses feigned innocence and insinuation; Nilratan speaks in dry, principled aphorisms; Nabendu alternates between boasting, evasion and nervous submission; officials use curt, humiliating formulas. Exchanges often follow a pattern of provocation, denial and comic exposure. The dialogue advances the plot rapidly and allows social power to be displayed through forms of address, politeness, silence and mock ceremony.

Although satire dominates, the prose periodically shifts into a sensuous lyrical mode. Seasonal landscapes, river light, flowers, dew and the warmth of the winter sun accompany Nabendu’s stay with Labanya. These passages use romantic imagery and softer rhythms, temporarily suspending the public comedy in favour of domestic pleasure and half-articulated attraction. The lyrical descriptions are not wholly detached from irony, however: Nabendu’s political reform is linked to comfort, beauty, appetite and flirtation. The contrast between expansive nature writing and scenes of petty status anxiety also broadens the story’s emotional texture.

Characterisation is largely typological and comic. Nabendu is a vain but not vicious social climber, susceptible to affection, praise and shame. His contradictions make him ridiculous while preserving a degree of sympathy. Labanya functions as wit, strategist and satirical agent; her beauty and intelligence give her influence over him. Pramathanath supplies a more serious moral precedent, undergoing a genuine awakening after recognising that apparent respect is merely respect for his European disguise. Purnendu, though dead, survives as the embodiment of inherited servility. Characters are thus built around attitudes toward colonial authority, but domestic relationships keep them from becoming purely abstract symbols.

The structure is episodic and cumulative. It begins with family history, establishes opposing traditions, and then develops through a chain of comic tests: ritual mockery, false explanations, the Congress donation, newspaper controversy, the invented official visit, the humiliating call on the Magistrate and eventual public elevation by the nationalist party. Each episode raises the stakes and makes retreat more difficult. Coincidence, misinformation and practical jokes are used openly as mechanisms of farce. The ending refuses a simple conversion narrative: Nabendu is ceremonially crowned by his family, yet the narrator predicts that he may still become a Rai Bahadur. This circular conclusion preserves his inconsistency and denies the satire an overly neat moral resolution.

Overall, the style is distinguished by genial but penetrating irony, mock-heroic elevation, culturally hybrid vocabulary, intrusive narration, vivid comic similes and rapid theatrical dialogue. It treats colonial mimicry as both political degradation and social comedy, while portraying identity as unstable, performative and strongly shaped by the desire to be admired. The final cheer reprises the borrowed idiom of imperial public ceremony and turns it back upon the story’s colonial loyalists, closing on a note of deliberately ambiguous, exuberant ridicule.

The story has the character of a folkloric prose romance shaped by Irish oral tradition, Celtic mythology and visionary supernaturalism. Its world is rural, archaic and geographically specific: named villages, lakes, bogs, hills and townlands give the narrative a firm local foundation, while the Sidhe, mysterious voices and symbolic apparitions open that landscape onto an unseen realm. The supernatural is not treated as a rupture in ordinary reality but as something latent within woods, water, mist, fire and darkness. This creates an atmosphere that is at once homely and numinous, with potatoes, turf, rags and blackthorn sticks existing naturally beside fairy powers and deathly visions.

The narration resembles a traditional storyteller’s voice. It is omniscient but closely aligned with Hanrahan’s perceptions, so that events are often presented through uncertainty: things “seemed” to him, a track is “maybe a road and maybe the dry bed of a stream,” and he cannot always tell whether he is asleep or awake. Such qualification preserves ambiguity between supernatural revelation, delirium and dream. The narrator also uses communal formulations such as “he had often heard” and proverbial turns of phrase, suggesting that the story belongs to a body of shared local memory rather than to a private psychological account.

The syntax is predominantly long, cumulative and paratactic. Clauses are linked repeatedly by “and,” “but” and “for,” producing a flowing, incantatory rhythm that recalls spoken storytelling and biblical prose. Sentences often proceed by accretion, moving through landscape, sensation, memory and vision without sharp structural breaks. In moments of revelation or speech, this expansive movement is interrupted by shorter declarations and ritual questions—“Who are you?” or “The Cauldron, the Stone, the Sword, the Spear”—which acquire ceremonial force through their compression. Repetition is central to the style, especially in Winny’s cry of “I am beautiful, I am beautiful,” the recurring music, the pale hands and the image of the burned-out candle. These refrains bind the story together and make the prose resemble a chant, lament or ballad.

The diction combines plain rural vocabulary with elevated mystical language. Concrete terms such as “turf,” “heather,” “rushes,” “bog drain,” “oaten cake” and “blackthorn” establish physical texture and social setting. Alongside them appear phrases such as “the lasting unwearied Voices,” “the heart of the music of Heaven” and “some world out of sight and misty.” The contrast allows transcendence to emerge through poverty and decay rather than apart from them. The prose also preserves Irish-inflected idioms and constructions, including “it is not…,” “he was after my death,” “the way they were” and “that had no window.” These patterns give the English a distinctive Hiberno-Irish cadence and reinforce the impression of oral transmission.

Imagery is elemental, symbolic and strongly colour-based. Silver water, brown bog pools, grey flesh, black earth, pale mist and white arms recur throughout. Water and mist mark thresholds between worlds; fire signifies both domestic shelter and supernatural illumination; candles can belong equally to weddings and funerals. The dominant imagery repeatedly joins opposites—youth and age, beauty and ugliness, marriage and death, divine love and divine hate. Similes draw mostly on the natural or household world: a silence is “like the silence in the heart of a lake,” the world burns out like a candle, and supernatural arms are white “as the foam on a river.” This makes even the most visionary passages sensuous and locally grounded.

Characterisation is emblematic rather than extensively psychological. Hanrahan is the wandering poet whose age, bodily weakness and imaginative susceptibility prepare him to cross into the other world. Winny is simultaneously a destitute, weather-beaten beggar and an embodiment of immortal beauty. Her outward degradation conceals a supernatural identity, making her the story’s central paradox. The four card-playing women and the shadowy sword-bearers function less as individual characters than as ritual figures or manifestations of cosmic powers. Even ordinary objects become archetypal: the pot, stone, knife and stick are transformed into the Cauldron, Stone, Sword and Spear, linking the cabin to mythic regalia.

Dialogue is sparse and stylised. Characters do not generally speak in naturalistic conversation; instead, their words take the form of cries, formulas, prophecies, invocations and riddles. Winny’s repeated proclamation has the structure of a spell or song, while Hanrahan’s final utterances resemble ritual recognition. Direct speech therefore heightens rather than relaxes the story’s solemnity. It also allows contradictory meanings to coexist: Winny’s claim to youth and beauty sounds like madness when judged by appearances, but the ending reveals it as a supernatural truth.

The pacing is slow, repetitive and processional. The opening lingers over Hanrahan’s wandering and contemplation, then gradually narrows from the broad landscape to the lake, the hillside, the cabin and finally the deathbed. Repeated days of weakness and recurring sounds create a suspended sense of time. Near the climax, perceptions shift rapidly between mist, swords, darkness, fire and symbolic objects, imitating fever or visionary initiation. The final return to daylight, communal discovery and burial restores ordinary chronology, but Winny’s disappearance leaves the supernatural explanation intact.

Tonally, the story blends melancholy, awe, dread and tenderness. Bodily decay is described without sentimentality, yet the broken and dying are presented as possible vessels of immortal powers. Death is frightening but also figured as a wedding, possession and passage into a lasting world. The overall style is therefore elegiac and mythopoeic: it turns the death of an old poet into a symbolic union between mortal imagination and an eternal, terrible beauty concealed within madness, age and ruin.

The story adopts a first-person, retrospective voice that is observant, urbane, and quietly ironic. The narrator participates in the action but functions chiefly as a foil and witness to Oberon’s emotional extravagance. His dry asides—often undercutting his friend’s lofty claims—create distance between what the characters profess and what the reader is invited to perceive. This produces a characteristic tonal mixture of sympathy and mockery: artistic despair is treated as psychologically real, yet also as theatrical, self-indulgent, and faintly absurd.

The prose is formal, literary, and distinctly nineteenth-century in cadence. Its diction combines elevated abstractions such as “retribution,” “conflagration,” and “incorporeal” with colloquial exclamations including “Pooh! Pish! Pshaw!” Sentences are often long and intricately balanced, using embedded clauses, cumulative lists, semicolons, dashes, and periodic constructions. These expansive sentences allow description, commentary, and comic qualification to coexist. In moments of excitement, however, the syntax contracts into fragments, repetitions, imperatives, and exclamations, accelerating the pace and dramatizing Oberon’s agitation.

Description is vivid, sensuous, and highly figurative. The opening winter scene is rendered through tactile cold, violent wind, frosted glass, hard pavements, and thundering wheels, while nautical metaphors turn the narrator’s wind-driven progress into a comic voyage. Fire becomes the dominant image and organizing symbol. It shifts from domestic comfort to purgation, spectacle, artistic vision, demonic agency, and finally public catastrophe. Cold and flame form a sustained contrast: the frozen town surrounds a heated interior in which imagination, alcohol, shame, and ambition intensify toward combustion.

The dialogue is stylized and rhetorically polished rather than strictly naturalistic. Oberon speaks in elaborate metaphors, literary allusions, apostrophes, and melodramatic self-condemnation, revealing a mind that instinctively converts experience into art even while renouncing authorship. The narrator replies with brevity, understatement, teasing literalism, and mock indignation. Their exchanges create much of the comedy, particularly through reversals: rejected manuscripts are imagined as demonic objects, bad writing as a spiritual curse, and commercial publishing as a field of villainy.

The narrative follows a carefully escalating comic-gothic structure. It begins in realistic social satire, moves into a chamber scene of confession and debate, intensifies through the ritual burning of the manuscripts, and culminates in an apparently supernatural consequence. Foreshadowing is embedded in the repeated language of devils, flames, enchantment, and possession. The final reversal transforms artistic failure into spectacular efficacy: the tales that could not attract a publisher literally set the town on fire. This ending depends on a pun made physical and gives Oberon the recognition he craves in grotesquely displaced form.

The dominant mode is comic Gothic, blended with literary satire and metafiction. Supernatural suggestion remains playfully ambiguous: the “fiend” may be an actual apparition, an imaginative projection, or simply the burning papers carried up the chimney. The story is self-conscious about composition, inspiration, revision, rejection, authorship, and the unstable value of literary work. It repeatedly dramatizes the gap between the ecstasy of creation and the disappointment of rereading, as well as the conflict between private imaginative conviction and public commercial judgment.

The characterization relies on contrast and caricatural amplification. Oberon is volatile, ambitious, self-lacerating, visionary, and addicted to grand gestures. His despair rapidly becomes rage, ecstasy, repentance, and triumph. The narrator is steadier and more skeptical, though he too is susceptible to imagination and spectacle. Secondary figures, especially the publishers, appear only through satirical summary and are reduced to comic types representing caution, philistinism, and commercial self-interest.

Allusion and analogy elevate and parody the situation simultaneously. References to Dante, Gray, Nebuchadnezzar, the Alexandrian manuscripts, witchcraft, fairy coin, and martyrdom lend cultural density while comically exaggerating the fate of an unpublished manuscript. Similes are frequent and expansive: ideas become buried jewels, inspiration becomes desert water, creative blockage becomes a wall of ice, and the manuscripts resemble a deformed infant. Such comparisons make inward mental states concrete and theatrical.

Overall, the style is ornate yet controlled, combining romantic imagery with skeptical humor. Its signature effect comes from allowing imaginative language to grow increasingly feverish while maintaining an ironic narrative frame. The result is a richly figurative, dialogue-driven satire in which artistic temperament is at once honored for its visionary power and exposed as vanity, obsession, and combustible excess.

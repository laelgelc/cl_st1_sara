The story uses a plain, direct first-person style that presents an extraordinary encounter as a factual personal recollection. The narrator reports events chronologically and with little overt self-dramatisation, creating the authority of eyewitness testimony. The opening editorial note frames the episode as autobiographical and adds retrospective ethical context, but the narrative itself remains focused on practical action rather than moral commentary. Its tone is sober, candid and understated, even when the narrator describes exhaustion, fear, injury and the possibility of death.

The diction is concrete, accessible and largely unornamented. It draws heavily on the material vocabulary of hunting and winter travel—tracks, snowshoes, guns, sledges, fir thickets, beaters and hoar-frost—while explanations of tracking techniques make unfamiliar practices easy to follow. Repetition of basic words such as “bear,” “snow,” “track” and “forest” reinforces the tale’s oral, report-like quality. Occasional similes are simple and physical: Damian glides “as if in a boat,” the narrator is “as wet as a drowned rat,” and the bear approaches “like a whirlwind.” These comparisons clarify immediate sensation rather than displaying verbal ingenuity.

The syntax alternates between moderately long, cumulative sentences during travel and observation and short, abrupt clauses at moments of danger. Sequential connectors—“then,” “when,” “suddenly,” “all at once”—keep the action moving and make cause and effect exceptionally clear. Parallel constructions and lists reproduce the persistence of physical obstacles, as when the narrator repeatedly catches, slips or stumbles in the snow. During the attack, sentences tighten around bodily perceptions and rapid actions, producing urgency without abandoning clarity.

Dialogue is frequent, economical and functional. It conveys decisions, practical knowledge and differences in experience rather than providing elaborate psychological exposition. Damian’s speech is confident and rooted in close observation of the landscape, establishing him as the most capable figure in the story. His urgent cries during the attack retain colloquial simplicity and introduce a faintly comic note into an otherwise terrifying scene: he addresses the bear almost as though rebuking a badly behaved animal. Social distinctions are lightly registered through forms such as “sir” and “master,” yet Damian’s competence and courage give him the narrative’s strongest moral presence.

Description is selective but vivid. Most landscape details have practical significance: the softness of the snow determines movement, tracks reveal the bear’s strategy, a magpie indicates its location, and the shape of the forest controls visibility and shooting. A notable lyrical passage occurs when the narrator wakes beneath frost-covered trees and initially imagines a glittering hall with white pillars and a star-studded black vault. This moment briefly transforms the forest into a scene of wonder, but the narrator soon identifies each visual effect in literal terms. The pattern typifies the style: poetic perception is admitted, then grounded in physical reality.

Characterisation emerges through conduct rather than analysis. The narrator is determined, impulsive and conscious of his own physical limitations; his mistakes are admitted without excuse. Damian is defined by endurance, woodcraft, decisiveness and selfless bravery. The bear is not sentimentalised, yet it is presented as intelligent, deceptive and driven largely by fear before becoming wounded and ferocious. The narrator’s repeated access to his own thoughts—often in short quoted phrases—creates immediacy while revealing the limits of his judgment. His predictions repeatedly prove wrong, building suspense through reversals.

Structurally, the story proceeds as a linear adventure narrative: the wounded bear escapes; the hunters track and encircle it; they return with beaters; the narrator misses his first shot; the bear charges and mauls him; Damian intervenes; and a later hunt supplies closure. Tension develops gradually from patient procedural detail. The lengthy account of tracking establishes space, weather and competence, making the sudden violence credible. During the hunt, repeated missed opportunities and mistaken conclusions create escalating suspense. The climax is intensely tactile, centred on pressure, heat, smell, blood and the sensation of teeth cutting the face “with knives.” The brief aftermath returns quickly to restraint, medical fact and final outcome.

A key stylistic effect is the contrast between emotional extremity and narrative composure. Fear is conveyed through bodily signs—a beating heart, trembling, heat, exhaustion and pain—rather than through abstract declarations. Even the thought “my end has come” is delivered without elaboration. The ending is similarly matter-of-fact: the bear is killed, its damaged jaw explains the earlier violence, it is stuffed, and the narrator’s scars become barely visible. This cool closure gives the tale the character of a remembered anecdote while leaving an implicit tension between admiration for the animal, dependence on peasant expertise, the violence of hunting and the narrator’s later renunciation of the practice.

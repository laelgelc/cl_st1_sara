The story uses a strongly naturalistic, psychologically charged realist style, enriched by lyrical landscape description and symbolic contrasts. Its prose alternates between expansive, atmospheric narration and coarse, energetic dialogue, placing private moral conflict against the immense physical presence of the Volga. The result is both socially observant and melodramatically intense: the characters belong to a specific peasant and working world, but their dispute is framed as a larger struggle between bodily vitality, spiritual conscience, custom, desire, and power.

The narrative voice is third-person and broadly omniscient. It moves freely from external observation into interpretive commentary, explaining not only what characters do but what their voices, gestures, and silences reveal about them. The narrator frequently assigns emotional or metaphysical significance to physical details: Silan’s shouting expresses his surplus of strength, Sergei’s need to talk is presented as resistance to the oppressive silence, and Mitia’s physical weakness mirrors his inward torment. Characterization is therefore overt rather than reticent. Epithets and compact descriptive labels—“fair, weak, melancholy-looking,” “bluff, healthy, red-bearded,” “energetic, tall, mocking”—establish moral and temperamental contrasts immediately.

Landscape is the dominant stylistic instrument. The river, fog, clouds, darkness, moonlight, and eventual sunrise are repeatedly personified and made responsive to the human drama. Water “wails,” stillness “suffocates,” clouds “bar the way,” the night possesses a “hidden meditative silence,” and nature later “thrilled, quickened, and smiled.” The opening’s gray, oppressive world externalizes Mitia’s entrapment and shame, while the clearing sky and richly colored dawn accompany Silan and Marka’s confident affirmation of desire. This correspondence is not simply decorative: nature acts as an emotional chorus. Recurring images of walls, crushing vapor, fathomless water, wolves, fire, and strangling nooses give the story a symbolic vocabulary of obstruction, predation, torment, and doom.

The descriptive diction is elevated, sensuous, and often painterly. Color is carefully controlled, progressing from “ragged gray,” “gray-black,” “humid,” and “leaden” to yellow moonlight, polished steel, purple-gold waves, emerald fields, and “dew diamonds.” Sound is equally important. Onomatopoeic and auditory phrases such as “wistful wailing swish,” “thud-thud,” “groaned and scraped,” “gurgled musically,” and repeated cries of “Ahoy!” make the raft’s world physically immediate. Silence is treated almost as a substance, repeatedly broken by engines, laughter, curses, sobbing, or powerful male voices. The opposition between silence and sound parallels the opposition between Mitia’s inwardness and the other men’s forceful appetite for life.

Sentence structure varies according to mode. Landscape passages favor long, cumulative sentences with participial phrases, coordinated clauses, semicolons, dashes, and repeated modifiers. These sentences create a slow, drifting rhythm analogous to clouds, fog, and the raft’s movement. Short declarative sentences—“The raft floats onward,” “Day broke gradually,” “The water murmured”—punctuate the flow and give scenic transitions a grave simplicity. Dialogue, by contrast, is rapid, fragmented, and exclamatory. It contains interruptions, repetitions, rhetorical questions, curses, imperatives, colloquialisms, and abrupt tonal shifts. Sergei’s speech in particular sprawls through taunts and improvised arguments, while Mitia’s language moves from hesitant, conciliatory phrasing into biblical denunciation and ecstatic religious cadence.

The dialogue is the chief means of social and psychological revelation. Sergei speaks in a rough, mocking vernacular full of insults, proverbial formulations, and bodily metaphors: a man must have “teeth and claws,” Mitia is “neither fish nor fowl,” and his thoughts are treated as a useless “maggot.” His teasing is repetitive and performative; he talks partly to dominate Mitia and partly to fend off silence and loneliness. Mitia’s speech is quieter, abstract, and religious, centered on “soul,” “law,” “God,” “sin,” and “Christ.” At emotional peaks it becomes sermon-like, using parallel clauses, anaphora, apostrophe, and scriptural imagery: the others are “wolves,” “beasts,” and men “rending each other’s flesh.” Silan’s speech is blunt, possessive, and decisive, combining awareness of sin with an unapologetic ethic of strength and passionate living. Marka speaks less and is rendered more through looks, posture, smiles, and physical responsiveness than through extended argument.

The characterization is organized through stark contrasts. Mitia is pale, sickly, passive, introspective, and spiritually absolutist; Silan is physically powerful, sexually vigorous, socially authoritative, and pleased with his own vitality. Sergei occupies an unstable middle position: he admires strength and ridicules weakness, yet he is also lonely, envious, curious, and eventually unsettled by the suffering he has provoked. Bodies carry ideological meaning throughout. Expanded chests, hairy arms, powerful throats, strong teeth, bare feet, and steel-spring muscles signify fitness for the harsh material world, whereas coughing, panting, thin hands, sobbing, and collapse identify Mitia’s inability to inhabit that world on its terms. This emphasis gives the prose a pronounced naturalistic determinism, even while Mitia argues for spiritual freedom.

The pacing alternates between suspended scenic description and escalating verbal confrontation. The raft’s steady downstream movement provides continuity, but the action itself is comparatively limited: steering, passing a steamer, talking, embracing, and watching dawn arrive. Dramatic momentum comes from gradual disclosure. Sergei’s teasing first reveals the father’s relationship with Marka, then Mitia explains his rejection of marriage, then his religious plan to flee, and finally his shame erupts into a breakdown. The chapter division shifts perspective forward to Silan and Marka, replaying the same moral conflict from the lovers’ side. This creates dramatic irony and complicates judgment: Silan and Marka know they are sinning, yet regard their love as vital and authentic, while Mitia’s moral purity has also helped create the situation.

Repetition is a major rhetorical feature. Words and concepts such as “soul,” “sin,” “law,” “strength,” “silence,” “darkness,” and “life” recur insistently. Characters repeat questions and commands—“How did it happen?”, “Get married!”, “Give her up!”, “Look ahead!”—until they become emotional refrains. “Look ahead” has both a practical and symbolic resonance: the men must navigate the river, but each is also trying, or failing, to imagine a future. The raft itself functions as a confined dramatic stage on which incompatible people are bound together and carried onward by a force larger than themselves.

The tone combines tragic seriousness, harsh comedy, sensual admiration, and moral ambiguity. Sergei’s mockery can be comic in rhythm and phrasing, but it repeatedly crosses into cruelty. Silan and Marka’s erotic warmth is rendered with sympathy and physical vividness, yet their wish for Mitia’s death introduces menace. Mitia is treated compassionately, though his idealism is also shown as impractical, self-denying, and implicated in the suffering of others. The prose does not offer a simple moral verdict. Instead, it sets competing laws against one another: religious conscience, social custom, masculine power, sexual need, paternal authority, and individual love.

The story bears the marks of an older English translation. There are formal or archaic constructions, heavy punctuation, conspicuous explanatory narration, occasional typographical or grammatical irregularities, and idioms such as “rum un,” “bad ’un,” “I’m blest,” and “king of trumps,” which give Russian peasant speech a distinctly period English coloring. Exclamation marks and dashes are abundant, and emotions are often named explicitly. The editorial footnote supplies sociological and historical explanation from outside the fictional scene, a practice characteristic of older translated editions. Overall, the style is robust, elemental, and high-contrast: lyrical nature writing surrounds a raw spoken drama, while physical detail and symbolic atmosphere transform a peasant family scandal into a conflict between life-force and conscience.

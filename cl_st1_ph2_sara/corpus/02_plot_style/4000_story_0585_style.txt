The story is written in a vigorous, early twentieth-century adventure-naturalist mode, combining frontier realism with melodrama, moral fable, and survival narrative. Its dominant atmosphere is harsh, masculine, and fatalistic. The Arctic environment is not merely a backdrop but an active pressure upon the characters: cold, hunger, disease, distance, and physical labour determine behaviour and expose moral worth. Practical competence is treated as a cardinal virtue, while ignorance, idleness, vanity, and collective prejudice lead directly to suffering. The narrative’s central irony is that the supposedly experienced and self-assured camp is helpless, while the ostracised Klusky possesses the means to save it but withholds them out of hatred.

The narration uses a flexible third-person omniscient perspective. It generally follows Captain, but it can move outward to describe the camp collectively, enter another character’s motives, or pause for authoritative explanations of scurvy and sled travel. These expository passages have a documentary quality, presenting frontier knowledge as hard-earned fact: the symptoms of disease, the mechanics of hauling a sled, the dangers of perspiration and frost, and the value of fresh food are all described with clinical confidence. The narrator frequently generalises from the action in aphoristic statements, such as the maxim about cabining by a “grub-pile” or observations about idle communities and human endurance. This gives the prose the air of a seasoned raconteur interpreting events as well as recounting them.

The structure is linear and tightly causal. The opening establishes the camp’s fatal mistakes; the onset of scurvy confirms the warnings of Captain and George; George’s illness creates the rescue mission; Klusky’s attempted murder reveals concealed treachery; his own illness forces a delay; and his confession redirects Captain toward the hidden potatoes. Suspense repeatedly turns on time, bodily deterioration, and incomplete knowledge. The final return is prolonged through false signals—the missing smoke, the untrodden snow, the apparently motionless figure—before ending in an emotional reversal when George speaks. Foreshadowing is prominent: Klusky’s threats of revenge, George’s prediction that he may commit murder, the rumour of missing potatoes, and the Indian left to guard the cabin all prepare the revelation.

The prose alternates between expansive descriptive passages and rapid dramatic scenes. Long, cumulative sentences are used to convey physical ordeal, particularly during the sled journey, where clauses pile up images of dragging runners, freezing clothes, overflows, deep snow, wind, and numb limbs. Repetition reinforces monotony and strain, especially constructions such as “always there is” and the reiterated “lingered, lingered maddeningly.” During moments of danger or discovery, the syntax contracts into short statements, exclamations, commands, and fragments: “Dead!”, “Get up!”, “Too late!” This rhythmic contrast accelerates confrontations and gives emotional shocks immediate force.

The diction mixes elevated narration with frontier slang and occupational vocabulary. Terms such as “sourdoughs,” “chechakos,” “grub,” “parka,” “go-devil,” “paystreak,” “malamoot,” and “snow-shoes” root the story in a specific northern culture. The narrator’s language can be formal and literary—“implacable progress,” “hopeless fatalism,” “oily modulation”—but it also favours muscular verbs and tactile detail. Bodies “wallow,” frost “bites,” the rope “galls,” and the disease makes flesh soft and gums livid. Figurative language is concrete and often animalistic: the settlers live like marmots, Klusky fights with “rat-like fury,” George snorts like a sea lion, and stars resemble wolves’ eyes beyond a fire. Personification makes both nature and disease predatory, with cold, scurvy, and the landscape appearing to watch, seize, or consume human beings.

Dialogue carries much of the characterisation. George speaks in heavily marked frontier dialect, with dropped consonants, nonstandard grammar, comic malapropisms, and exuberant comparisons. His humour is macabre and self-deprecating, especially when he imagines himself as an angel with a halo and harp. Captain’s speech is comparatively direct and standard, reflecting his practical, restrained temperament. Klusky’s dialogue is rendered through exaggerated phonetic spelling and recurrent verbal tics such as “Oi! Oi!” and “Vat,” signalling his foreignness and agitation. This portrayal, along with repeated references to him by ethnicity and stereotyped descriptions of his appearance and behaviour, reflects period antisemitic caricature. The Frenchmen are likewise represented through comic eye dialect. These devices create sharply differentiated voices, but they also rely on ethnic stereotypes that are conspicuous to a modern reader.

Characterisation is broad, external, and morally polarised. Captain embodies loyalty, endurance, and duty; George combines physical strength, practical wisdom, humour, and generosity; the camp functions largely as a cowardly and foolish collective; and Klusky is shaped into a grotesque antagonist whose cruelty is partly explained, though not excused, by sustained persecution. There is some psychological complication in Captain’s treatment of Klusky. He beats and dehumanises him, then recoils on discovering that the man’s weakness is caused by scurvy. His refusal to abandon Klusky after promising to remain creates the story’s central moral trial: honour delays the rescue and may cost George his life. The prose therefore admires toughness but distinguishes moral strength from mere physical dominance.

Tone shifts between sardonic humour, horror, suspense, and sentiment. The camp’s laziness and conceit are mocked through hyperbole and ironic phrasing, while the deaths from scurvy are treated with grim, sometimes grotesque humour. Menard dying immediately after boasting that he will win a survival bet exemplifies the story’s taste for black irony. As the rescue journey begins, the tone becomes increasingly claustrophobic and ominous. The conclusion turns openly sentimental, but the emotion has been earned through prolonged restraint: Captain, previously defined by action and determination, collapses into childlike tears when he discovers that George is alive.

The thematic style is strongly didactic. The story advocates preparedness, exercise, fresh food, practical experience, mutual aid, and loyalty. It condemns complacency, mob cruelty, prejudice, and vindictive selfishness. At the same time, it presents civilisation as fragile under extreme conditions: isolated people regress into pack behaviour, persecuting an outsider and then submitting passively to disease. Its governing worldview is naturalistic in the pressure it assigns to environment and physiology, yet romantic in its belief that courage, friendship, and personal honour can preserve human dignity. The final effect is that of a rugged northern yarn shaped into a cautionary moral drama, with technical realism lending credibility to sensational action and an emotionally emphatic ending.

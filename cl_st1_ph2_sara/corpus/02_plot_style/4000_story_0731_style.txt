The story uses a retrospective first-person narrator whose educated, observant voice lends credibility to an increasingly supernatural experience. He begins as a practical traveller, attentive to food, maps, walking distances and payment, but proves unusually receptive to atmosphere and mystical suggestion. His repeated qualifications—“I thought,” “if I remember rightly,” “it seemed to me”—create the manner of a carefully recorded personal testimony. This measured uncertainty preserves ambiguity even when the narrative openly presents ghosts, visions and altered states of time.

The prose is ornate, lyrical and distinctly late-Victorian or Edwardian in character. Its elevated vocabulary—“protean appearances,” “impersonal benevolence,” “ineffable scents,” “momentary glimpse of a mightier universe”—combines with older idioms and formal constructions such as “altered no whit,” “thereupon,” and “for his part.” Long, balanced sentences dominate, often carrying several subordinate clauses and cumulative descriptive phrases. Shorter statements appear at moments of revelation or threat, giving them exceptional force: “He comes before a death,” “I have come for someone,” and “HE had called her by her own name.” Dashes, semicolons, ellipses and periodic sentence structures reproduce hesitation, trance, suspended thought and the gradual disclosure of meaning.

Landscape is central rather than decorative. Somerset and North Devon are rendered through saturated colour, scent, sound and texture: golden light, luminous mist, blossoming orchards, sea air, birdsong, daisies, roses and whispering pines. The imagery is strongly synaesthetic, allowing voices to become “waves of sound,” shadows to weave a “tissue of dreams,” and flowers to laugh. Nature is repeatedly personified and treated as spiritually animate. The pastoral setting initially suggests ease, innocence and abundance, but its beauty gradually acquires an uncanny intensity. Sunshine dazzles and vanishes, shadows pause, and the pine wood becomes both a real grove and a threshold between life and death.

The tonal movement is from leisurely pastoral reverie through curiosity and enchantment into metaphysical awe, quiet dread and elegiac confirmation. The supernatural is not handled primarily as grotesque horror. Instead, fear mingles with yearning, beauty, tenderness and spiritual exaltation. Death is figured as movement, sleep, recall and transition rather than annihilation. This produces a characteristic emotional doubleness: the narrator’s highest joy contains all possible pain, while the ghost’s summons is frightening yet compassionate. The final revelation is therefore melancholy rather than sensational.

Pacing is deliberately gradual. The opening lingers over luncheon, weather, clothing and the orchard before allowing the old man to speak. Suspense develops through small irregularities: the private room’s unexplained occupant, the daughter’s failure to notice him, his refined voice, his references to having “moved,” and his disappearance. A long embedded village history then delays the promised midnight encounter while furnishing the haunting with communal memory and folklore. The nocturnal journey again proceeds slowly through silence, darkness and sensory detail, but the vision inside the wood accelerates through abrupt temporal transformations. The brief closing return one year later supplies restrained factual confirmation and completes the prophecy.

Dialogue is formal, economical and highly suggestive. The old man speaks in gentle, poetic riddles, frequently using euphemism and repetition: “move,” “come,” “journey,” “sleep.” His language carries authority without aggression. The narrator’s replies are more hesitant, broken by dashes and unfinished formulations, showing that ordinary speech cannot contain what he apprehends. The innkeeper’s daughter speaks simply and colloquially, grounding the visionary material in village belief. Her anxious repetition of “come for someone” also foreshadows that she is the intended victim.

Characterisation relies on contrast and emblematic detail. The old man’s bent body, wrinkled face, corduroys, faded jacket and stick mark him as an ordinary rustic, while his noble head, silvery hair, luminous eyes and cultivated voice suggest spiritual distinction. The daughter is first presented as an ideal of country beauty, then momentarily appears dull beside the narrator’s transcendent vision, and finally returns within that vision as a lively haymaker. This repetition of her appearance in altered contexts links pastoral youth and mortality. Secondary figures remain types—the benevolent yeoman, devoted wife, village maiden and robust young swain—appropriate to the story’s folktale structure.

The narrative blends several modes: travel sketch, pastoral prose poem, ghost story, village legend and mystical vision. Its supernatural machinery draws upon folklore—the apparition that precedes a death, the haunted grove, collective oral testimony—but the philosophical treatment extends beyond conventional superstition. Time becomes an “immense Present,” identity passes among changing appearances, and death belongs to a recurring journey. The wood consequently functions as a liminal symbol: geographically ordinary, imaginatively haunted, and metaphysically outside linear time.

A notable stylistic technique is the collision of practical detail with visionary language. Knapsack travel, luncheon, small change, parish monuments and a medical cause of death coexist with ecstasy, prophetic sight and temporal collapse. The mundane details authenticate the marvellous, while the supernatural interpretation transforms ordinary events such as sunstroke into the visible surface of a deeper process. Even the ending maintains this dual framework: the innkeeper offers a natural explanation, but its timing exactly fulfils the narrator’s vision.

Overall, the style is lush, contemplative, atmospheric and spiritually uncanny. It favours cumulative sensory description, formal first-person testimony, symbolic landscape, delayed revelation and emotional ambiguity. Its most distinctive effect comes from making death appear simultaneously ominous and beautiful: a rupture in everyday reality, but also a tender summons into a larger and more mysterious order.

The story uses a dry, colloquial realist style shaped by the Australian bush-yarn tradition. Its narrative voice is third-person and broadly omniscient, moving freely between outward observation, social commentary, and partial access to Steelman’s intentions. The narrator often appears to stand beside the reader as a knowing raconteur, inserting sardonic generalisations such as the aside about the tone used by a wife in an argument. These digressions create an informal bond with the audience while giving the prose a distinctly opinionated, period-specific character.

The diction combines plain narration with Australian and working-class slang. Terms such as “bushies,” “graft,” “stumped,” “quid,” “bob,” “blued,” “stoush,” “stuff,” and “chew his lug” establish class, place, and social identity without explanatory framing. Money is repeatedly specified in pounds, sovereigns, guineas, and shillings, lending the confidence trick material weight and making financial calculation part of the story’s texture. The dialogue also contains historically characteristic racialised expressions, used as contemporary idioms for brutal labour; these mark the period and social milieu rather than a neutral modern narrative register.

Dialogue dominates the story and performs most of its characterisation and plotting. Steelman speaks expansively, rapidly, and theatrically, while Smith answers with monosyllables, hesitant questions, and apparent dullness. Their contrasting rhythms create a comic double act: Steelman is volatile, inventive, and verbally agile; Smith is impassive, literal-seeming, and strategically unreadable. Gentleman Sharper’s polished confidence is conveyed through smoother, more economical speech until his unease produces awkward pauses and disjointed phrasing. Character emerges less through description than through verbal habits, gestures, and reactions.

The syntax alternates between long, accumulating sentences and short comic beats. Extended sentences are used for scene-setting, social observation, and Steelman’s elaborate arguments; brief exchanges such as “Yes?” and “Thank yer, Steely” puncture his rhetoric. Parentheses, dashes, ellipses, repetitions, and italicised emphasis reproduce hesitation, interruption, covert signalling, and shifts in conversational power. The prose often delays a sentence’s decisive information, creating suspense while preserving the sound of oral storytelling.

Humour is understated, ironic, and strongly character-based. It comes from the discrepancy between appearance and reality: the supposedly green bushmen are staging a performance for the professional swindler, while the man who believes himself an observer is being deliberately fed information. Steelman’s exaggerated disgust at Smith’s “suspicion,” Smith’s studied vacancy, and absurd details such as counting flies or requesting “some fruit, and a tract” keep the deception comic without turning it into farce. The narrator’s mock-solemn formulations and inflated descriptions of ordinary gestures add another layer of humour.

The story is structured as a reversal confidence trick. It begins after the scheme is already underway, with Steelman and Smith established “in the dress and character” of bushmen, then lets the reader watch them stage an overheard conversation. The first small investment serves as bait: the sharper returns a conspicuous profit in order to secure a larger sum. Steelman then refuses the expected escalation and reveals, principally to Smith and the reader, that he has trapped the trapper. This structure depends on dramatic irony. The reader gradually understands the plan before the sharper does, but Steelman’s emotional response to the stolen money introduces ambiguity about whether the act is merely clever, morally troubling, or both.

Pacing is deliberate and scene-based. Balcony conversations, walks, pauses, glances, nudges, and small movements are described with unusual precision because physical positioning controls who can overhear whom and who believes himself unseen. Suspense is generated through silence as much as action: “pregnant” pauses, evasive weather talk, and the sharper’s delayed request all signal changing advantage. The climactic moment is not violent or loudly dramatic but a single “hard, tight, cold wink,” after which the balance of power is settled.

The setting is rendered economically but atmospherically. The hotel balcony, harbour lights, frosty moonlight, misty hills, private bar, and shared bedroom create a recognisable colonial urban environment at the meeting point between bush labour and metropolitan leisure. Scenic description is occasionally lyrical—especially the “beautiful death of day”—but it is usually subordinated to action. The refined landscape and hotel manners also contrast with the rough slang and practical cunning of the protagonists.

Characterisation relies heavily on performance. Steelman is simultaneously actor, director, confidence man, protector, and uneasy moralist. His furtive glances and rehearsed indignation reveal technical control, while the pocketbook containing the portrait of a woman and child suggests a private motive or obligation that remains unexplained. Smith’s passivity is likewise tactical: his blankness makes him an ideal accomplice because others underestimate him and because he does not disrupt Steelman’s improvisations. The sharper is deliberately less individualised; his title-like designation reduces him to a social type and makes his defeat emblematic.

Thematically, the story explores trust, deception, class prejudice, and the unstable distinction between honesty and fraud. Steelman wins because the sharper assumes that rough rural workers must be naïve. The tale therefore celebrates practical intelligence over polished appearance and turns social condescension against the person who holds it. At the same time, Steelman’s troubled pacing and self-justifying speech complicate the triumph. He constructs a moral narrative in which robbing a robber becomes justice, repeatedly insisting that the public version of events is what matters. His statement that he has “driven a nail” home suggests both satisfaction and irreversible guilt.

Overall, the style is conversational, theatrical, and slyly moral without becoming openly didactic. It combines vernacular comedy, close observation of behaviour, controlled suspense, and a compact trickster plot. Its signature effect comes from making talk itself the principal form of action: identities are improvised, beliefs are planted, and power changes hands through overheard words, strategic pauses, and precisely judged performances.

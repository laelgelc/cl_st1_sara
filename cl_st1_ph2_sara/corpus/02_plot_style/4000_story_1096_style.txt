The story adopts the manner of a late-Victorian colonial adventure tale, blending a fireside yarn, a supernatural mystery, and a comic treasure hunt. It is framed as an anecdote told aloud by Jack Turnbull to an unnamed listener over drinks and cigars. Direct addresses such as “sir,” conversational prompts, and repeated assurances that the events are true create the impression of spontaneous oral narration. The opening begins with the mystery of Tom Donahue’s nickname, while the ending returns to the storytelling present and briefly accounts for the later fortunes of the principal characters. This circular frame gives the tale the intimacy and retrospective warmth of a personal reminiscence.

The first-person narrator presents himself as practical, experienced, and somewhat roughened by colonial life, though his former legal education occasionally shows through in his precise vocabulary and orderly explanations. His voice mixes educated phrasing with colloquial speech: formal terms such as “sobriquet,” “apparition,” and “protuberances” sit beside expressions like “knocked about,” “old fellow,” “made tracks,” and “put two and two together.” Frequent interjections—“Well,” “Why,” “By Jove,” “Hollo!”—reinforce the spoken quality. Repetition, self-correction, parenthetical remarks, and comments about the length of the story make the narrator sound as though he is recalling events in real time rather than delivering a polished written account.

The prose alternates between long, elaborately coordinated sentences and short exclamatory statements. Extended sentences are used for background, landscape, technical procedures, and accumulating suspense, while abrupt lines such as “Ghosts,” “Only one,” and “Rock-salt!” deliver shocks or comic reversals. Semicolons, dashes, and embedded qualifications reproduce the rhythm of animated conversation. Descriptive passages favour vivid but conventional Gothic imagery: the valley resembles “the gate of Hades,” its depths are “dark as Erebus,” and the mysterious light is “ruddy,” “lurid,” “flickering,” and “baleful.” Against this heightened language, the careful descriptions of sighting sticks, cords, angles, distances, and leverage introduce a rational, almost procedural register.

Dialogue occupies a large proportion of the story and drives both characterisation and plot. Jack is loyal, sceptical, and comparatively steady; Tom is impulsive, imaginative, technically resourceful, and prone to violent swings between despair and exhilaration; Dick Wharton is a bluff, credible witness whose fear lends authority to the legend. Their speech is informal and energetic, full of teasing, commands, questions, and exclamations. Tom’s feverish repetitions and rapid deductions convey his obsessive excitement, while Jack’s calmer responses anchor the reader. The dialogue also permits information to emerge naturally: the legend, the optical conditions of the light, Tom’s theory, and the later correction of their mistake are all disclosed through conversation rather than authorial exposition.

Structurally, the narrative is built around delayed explanation and repeated reversal. It begins by promising the origin of “Lucky Tom,” supplies financial hardship and a haunted-valley legend, then transforms the apparent supernatural threat into a material clue. The expedition to locate the glowing point unfolds through incremental physical actions, sustaining suspense while withholding Tom’s reasoning. Discovery of the supposed diamond appears to resolve the mystery, but Madison’s identification of it as rock salt turns triumph into farce. Tom’s renewed deduction restores hope, only for the marked area to appear empty; the final discovery that the accidentally dislodged fulcrum contains the true gem completes a pattern of expectation, disappointment, and recovery. This double false ending makes luck seem inseparable from observation, persistence, and accident.

The pacing is correspondingly variable. The early history of the men’s emigration and poverty is compressed into summary, while the stormy night, the search for the light, and the daylight triangulation are narrated slowly and minutely. Repeated references to distances, positions, and movements make the investigation visually intelligible and invite the reader to participate in solving it. The return journeys are accelerated, except where Tom’s changing emotional state matters. Comic scenes are delivered briskly, particularly Madison’s deadpan “Rock-salt!” and the immediate collapse of Tom’s confidence.

Tonally, the story moves between convivial nostalgia, Gothic unease, adventurous excitement, and broad humour. The supernatural atmosphere is genuine enough to produce suspense, but it is ultimately subordinated to empirical reasoning. The “demon’s eye” is not dismissed as pure fantasy; instead, folklore preserves a misunderstood natural phenomenon. The result is characteristic of rational mystery fiction: an eerie event is decoded by prior knowledge, close observation, and improvised technology. Yet chance remains crucial. Tom recognises the significance of the light because he once read an obscure pamphlet, the wrong mineral happens to lie beside the right one, and the genuine diamond is detached unintentionally. His nickname is therefore both ironic and justified.

The story’s imagery repeatedly opposes darkness and illumination, ignorance and insight, superstition and knowledge. The single glowing “eye” functions first as a demonic sign, then as an optical target, and finally as a precious object. Games and fortune supply a second metaphorical field: the men believe they have played all their “small cards” before the “trumps” appear, and Tom continually interprets his life through luck. Physical looking is equally important. Characters peer, sight, align, mark, and inspect, but they also repeatedly misread what they see. The tale thus suggests that perception alone is unreliable unless joined to memory, inference, and persistence.

The setting is rendered through the conventions of colonial adventure writing: isolated cabins, stockaded farms, dangerous border country, rocky passes, revolvers, and prospects of sudden mineral wealth. The landscape is treated as both hostile wilderness and field of opportunity. Period-specific racial terminology and broad representations of African belief reflect the prejudices and paternalism of the era; Indigenous people remain largely offstage, serving chiefly as sources of the supernatural legend and as part of the perceived danger of the frontier. The narrative focus stays firmly on the camaraderie, enterprise, and advancement of the European adventurers.

Overall, the style is expansive, masculine, anecdotal, and plot-centred. Its chief effects come from an engaging raconteur’s voice, lively dialogue, Gothic scenic colouring, lucid practical detail, and a succession of suspenseful and comic reversals. The final note is affectionate and elegiac: Jack’s memories of hardship have mellowed into pleasure, and the adventure becomes not merely an explanation of wealth but a celebration of friendship, resourcefulness, and the “rough old days.”

An American businessman first visits London’s Temple district, a secluded haven inhabited by prosperous unmarried lawyers. Invited to an elaborate dinner with nine bachelors, he enjoys abundant food, wine, cultivated conversation, and carefree fellowship. Impressed by their comfortable lives, free from wives, children, pain, or apparent responsibility, he regards the Temple as a paradise.

Later, needing large quantities of paper for his seed envelopes, he travels through a bleak, frozen New England mountain pass to an isolated paper mill. There he finds rows of pale, exhausted young women silently tending relentless machines, their individuality and vitality reduced by monotonous labor. As he tours the mill and watches rags transformed into blank paper, he becomes increasingly disturbed by the contrast between industrial efficiency and the workers’ misery. He completes his business but leaves haunted by the unequal worlds he has witnessed: privileged bachelors enjoying ease and fellowship, and unmarried women trapped in cold, dehumanizing toil.

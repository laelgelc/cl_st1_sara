The story uses a lyrical, compassionate mode of social realism, combining close attention to a vulnerable individual with criticism of the customs that determine her fate. Its emotional centre is Subha’s inability to speak, but silence is treated not as an absence of consciousness: it becomes an alternative language expressed through eyes, gestures, tears, animals, and the natural world. The mood is predominantly elegiac and tender, gradually darkening into quiet tragedy. Pathos is strong but controlled, arising less from melodramatic action than from the contrast between Subha’s rich inner life and other people’s failure to recognise it.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, moving freely between Subha’s feelings, her parents’ anxieties, communal attitudes, and general reflections on human nature. At moments the narrator becomes conspicuously present through phrases such as “I know not” and “I think,” or addresses the reader with rhetorical turns such as “Only think.” This creates the voice of a sympathetic storyteller: intimate, reflective, and morally alert rather than impersonal. The narrator frequently pauses the action to offer aphorisms, analogies, and social observations, giving the prose the quality of a fable or oral tale while retaining psychological depth.

The diction is elevated but accessible, with a slightly archaic, formal cadence. Words and constructions such as “wherefore,” “vouchsafe,” “nay,” “whom else but,” and “on the morrow” lend the English a period or translated-literary flavour. This high register is mixed with plain domestic details—fish-curry, betel, cows, fishing, marriage negotiations—which ground the story in village life. Occasional humorous or ironic phrasing briefly lightens the sadness, particularly in the description of Pratap as a socially useful idler and in the narrator’s mock-grand account of his fishing.

Sentence structure varies between concise emotional statements and long, rhythmically cumulative passages. Parallel clauses, repeated openings, and lists are common, especially in descriptions of nature: “the murmur of the brook, the voice of the village folk, the songs of the boatmen…” Such accumulation creates a flowing, incantatory movement. Repetition is also used structurally and emotionally—“Gradually she grew up. Gradually she began to find herself”—to suggest slow, inevitable change. Semicolons and extended periodic sentences allow perceptions to unfold in waves, while short declarative sentences sharpen moments of social cruelty or irreversible consequence.

Figurative language is abundant and central to the style. Similes repeatedly associate Subha with natural or vulnerable things: her lips tremble “like a leaf,” her fear resembles that of a “stricken doe,” and her sorrow is “like a mist-wrapt dawn.” Nature is extensively personified. The river behaves like a dutiful middle-class daughter, becomes a household deity, and offers endless benediction; the earth becomes Subha’s “strong silent mother.” Conversely, human society is rendered through sacrificial and commercial imagery: the prospective husband appears as a god selecting a beast for sacrifice, while Subha’s tears are treated like pearls that increase her matrimonial value. These metaphors expose the dehumanising logic of arranged marriage and social reputation.

A governing pattern of oppositions shapes the prose: speech and silence, human society and nature, usefulness and burden, inner feeling and outward judgment, village intimacy and urban estrangement. Ironically, those who possess speech repeatedly fail to communicate, whereas Subha, the animals, and the landscape understand one another without words. Her expressive eyes form a recurring motif and are described as immediate conduits of thought, free from the distortions of verbal “translation.” Sound imagery—murmurs, songs, cries, rustling, sighing—is therefore paradoxically prominent in a story about muteness. Silence itself acquires texture, scale, and emotional force.

The setting is rendered symbolically as well as realistically. Chandipur’s riverbank is a protective, almost sacred space in which Subha’s interior life harmonises with the environment. Calcutta, by contrast, is called a “strange country” and appears chiefly as a site of confinement, display, inspection, and exchange. The movement from village to city parallels the loss of Subha’s natural community. Her childhood companions—the cows, kitten, river, and idle fisherman—are characterised through affectionate detail, while the institutions surrounding marriage are depicted through cold calculation and bitter irony.

Characterisation relies more on habitual action, gesture, and narrated insight than on dialogue. Subha is developed through repeated visits to the cows, her silent watching, her physical closeness to the earth, and her desire to prove herself useful. Pratap is lightly sketched through his idleness, fishing, teasing, and limited affection. Dialogue is sparse and consequential; because Subha cannot answer, spoken remarks often dramatise her exclusion. Other characters speak about, around, or at her, while her response is conveyed through looks, tears, and bodily movement.

The narrative proceeds episodically and chronologically, beginning with family background, dwelling at length on Subha’s affinities with nature and animals, then accelerating as she reaches marriageable age. The early and middle sections are expansive and contemplative; the final movement becomes increasingly compressed and inexorable. The conclusion refuses consolation. Its final image of “endless, voiceless weeping” gathers the story’s motifs of silence, incomprehension, and spiritual witness into a subdued but devastating close. Overall, the style is poetic, humane, symbolically rich, and gently didactic, using lyrical beauty and understated irony to reveal the cruelty hidden within ordinary social convention.

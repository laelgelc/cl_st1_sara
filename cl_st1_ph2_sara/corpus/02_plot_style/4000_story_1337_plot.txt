Jonathan Chapman devotes his life to carrying apple seeds from Pennsylvania into the wilderness of Ohio and Indiana, planting nurseries ahead of advancing settlements so future pioneers can establish orchards. Traveling mostly barefoot and dressed in discarded clothing, he becomes known as Johnny Appleseed. His eccentric appearance, Swedenborgian faith, eloquent preaching, generosity, and extraordinary compassion for children, settlers, Indigenous people, and animals earn him widespread affection and respect.

During the War of 1812, his friendly relations with Indigenous communities allow him to travel unharmed and warn frontier families of approaching attacks. He continues planting trees, distributing religious writings, aiding impoverished families, rescuing neglected horses, and refusing to harm even insects or dangerous animals. He sells or gives away his seedlings but cares little for payment, using any surplus money to help others.

As civilization overtakes Ohio, he bids farewell to the families he has known and moves westward to continue his mission in Indiana. In 1847, after decades of wandering and planting orchards across a vast region, he arrives at a settler’s home, shares religious teachings, and dies peacefully the following morning at the age of seventy-two. His orchards and reputation for humility, benevolence, and reverence for life become his enduring legacy.

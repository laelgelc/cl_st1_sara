The story has the profile of a late nineteenth-century French society tale: urbane, psychologically observant, ironic, and organized around an elegant reversal. Its setting is the aristocratic world of salons, clubs, country estates, and railway travel, where reputation and “good form” govern conduct more powerfully than affection or morality. Adultery is treated less as a source of melodrama than as a contest involving vanity, social appearances, legal authority, and sexual power. The prevailing tone is cool and worldly, with an undercurrent of melancholy about aging and desire, while the conclusion sharpens into comic cruelty and unresolved irony.

The narration is third-person omniscient but remains closely aligned with the baron’s perceptions. External action repeatedly gives way to free-ranging accounts of his thoughts, memories, calculations, and changes of feeling. The narrator can also step back to make generalized observations about age, identity, and human transformation, as in the reflections on how the body and heart renew themselves over time. These aphoristic digressions broaden a private marital encounter into a meditation on mutability, but they retain a lightly skeptical, conversational quality rather than becoming solemnly philosophical. The narrator’s irony often arises from the gap between the baron’s belief that he is “master of himself” and the ease with which vanity, nostalgia, and renewed desire unsettle him.

The plot is symmetrical and tightly framed by two accidental—or apparently accidental—encounters. In the first, a mirror reveals the wife’s infidelity; in the second, a railway carriage forces the separated couple into renewed intimacy. The six-year interval is compressed into brisk summary, while both encounters are rendered scene by scene. This produces a clear dramatic architecture: discovery, controlled separation, passage of time, unexpected reunion, attempted reassertion of marital power, and the wife’s final countermove. Suspense depends less on what physically happens than on shifting interpretations: whether the woman is truly the baroness, whether she has recognized him, whether reconciliation is possible, and whether her pregnancy is real. The ending deliberately refuses to resolve the final question, preserving the wife’s victory as one of narrative control as much as social strategy.

Description is selective, sensuous, and symbolically charged. The princess’s bedroom is presented almost theatrically, with its azure-and-gold bed, dark drapery, and mirror “like a lake seen at a distance.” The bed resembles a catafalque in which love is buried, while the mirror becomes an accomplice that stores erotic memories and exposes hidden conduct. Later, the enclosed railway carriage functions as another intimate stage, but one marked by movement, dawn, bodily exposure, and changed identities. Light frequently governs revelation: the dark bedroom yields its secret through reflection, and a slanting ray of morning sun reveals the transformed baroness. Physical appearance is central throughout, especially the contrast between youthful freshness and masculine deterioration. Clothing, wraps, hair, skin, weight, and facial condition are not decorative details; they register status, desire, age, and control.

The prose alternates between relatively long, balanced sentences of description or psychological analysis and short declarative exchanges in dialogue. Repetition supplies emphasis—“an accident, an accident pure and simple,” “It was she and yet not she”—while parallel constructions clarify contrasts between past and present, youth and age, appearance and reality. Rhetorical questions reproduce the baron’s uncertainty and self-conscious strategizing. Lists are used to sketch character rapidly, particularly in the portrait of the baroness as “clever, spoiled, elegant, coquettish, witty.” The diction is formal but accessible, with period markers such as “medical man,” “good form,” “amicable separation,” and aristocratic titles. Occasional awkward punctuation, typographical irregularities, and phrases such as “tete-d-tete” suggest an older English translation or transcription rather than a contemporary idiom.

Dialogue is polished, adversarial, and socially coded. The baron speaks ceremoniously, relying on titles, legal rights, diplomacy, and controlled phrasing; the baroness answers with calm brevity and factual cruelty. Her restraint is more destabilizing than open anger would be. Their conversation develops like a duel in which each line adjusts the balance of power. His compliments conceal possession, and his appeal to marital law reveals desire disguised as authority. Her final explanation converts his own demand that she preserve appearances into a weapon against him. Much of the story’s wit comes from this reversal: the husband who once dictated the rules of discretion is trapped by their successful observance.

Characterization is built through contradiction. The baron is dignified yet vain, sexually unfaithful yet offended by his wife’s infidelity, apparently rational yet governed by wounded pride and revived appetite. He avoids scandal not from generosity but from fear of ridicule. The baroness initially appears through his patronizing image of a slight “Parisian doll,” but after the time jump she possesses composure, physical maturity, and strategic intelligence. Her development is conveyed largely through his astonishment, allowing the narrative to expose how little he understood her. The gender politics remain rooted in the period’s assumptions about male legal authority and female reputational vulnerability, yet the story subverts those assumptions by giving the wife superior command of performance, witnesses, and ambiguity.

The overall effect is economical despite the substantial psychological commentary. Long stretches of time are summarized quickly, while decisive moments are slowed through visual detail, interior speculation, and exact dialogue. The story’s signature blend is therefore social realism, erotic psychology, aphoristic reflection, and sardonic twist-ending construction. Its most distinctive stylistic principle is the conversion of surfaces—mirrors, faces, clothes, manners, public appearances—into instruments that both disclose and conceal the truth.

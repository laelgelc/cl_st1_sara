The story is written in an ornate, late-Victorian mode that combines domestic romance, psychological melodrama, and supernatural Gothic fiction. Its atmosphere moves gradually from social realism and lightly ironic marital comedy into dreamlike menace and metaphysical horror. The central stylistic tension is between the civilized world of courtship, money, churchgoing, travel, and fashionable society, and the primitive language of ownership, willpower, death, and possession.

The narration is third-person omniscient but remains closely aligned with Fanny Gilbert’s perceptions. It freely reports her thoughts, rationalizations, desires, and fears while occasionally stepping back into a knowing, socially observant voice. Phrases such as “It is true” and “on the other hand” give parts of the narrative the air of a carefully balanced character study, while wry remarks about widowhood, gossip, and respectable marriage introduce restrained irony. The narrator does not fully resolve whether Fanny’s experiences are dreams, psychological compulsions, or literal supernatural visitations; instead, the point of view progressively validates her subjective reality until the final apparition is also perceived by John Mowbray.

The prose is elevated, expansive, and highly figurative. Its vocabulary draws on the sea, classical mythology, medieval romance, Scripture, astronomy, flowers, jewels, and spiritualism. Captain Gilbert rides “like a centaur,” Fanny resembles both a woman and a rose, and the stars stand as sentinels over “the great courts of space beyond space.” Maritime imagery is especially important: Fanny’s resistance is a wave breaking against a rock, Gilbert’s authority resembles command over a ship, and emotional life is figured as storm, depth, voyage, and overpowering force. Images of flowers, velvet, pearls, jewels, and changing color construct Fanny as delicate, ornamental, and sensuous, whereas Gilbert is repeatedly associated with rock, sunlight, heroic stature, and elemental power.

Sentence structure varies between long, cumulative descriptive periods and abrupt emphatic statements. Parallel constructions and repetition establish Gilbert’s dominance from the opening: Fanny declares that she will not act, “but she did,” or wishes to act, “but she didn’t.” Later repetitions—“mine, mine, mine,” “I am afraid,” “He has come,” and “slipping, slipping”—intensify obsession and terror. Dashes, exclamations, rhetorical questions, and interrupted speech give the dialogue an emotionally breathless quality. The syntax becomes increasingly fragmented as the supernatural pressure grows, culminating in broken declarations, unfinished sentences, and perceptions that seem to bypass ordinary sensory experience.

Characterization depends heavily on contrast. Captain Gilbert is idealized as physically magnificent, morally generous, intellectually visionary, and erotically compelling, yet he is also jealous, tyrannical, and possessive. This contradiction is not softened; it is the source of his fascination and menace. Fanny is presented as affectionate, impressionable, socially charming, and responsive to strong personalities. Her apparent freedom after Gilbert’s death proves psychologically incomplete, and her repeated rejection of suitors establishes a pattern in which his will survives inside her before it becomes an explicitly supernatural force. John Mowbray initially appears to offer a healthier, broader life through music, travel, courtesy, and trust, but he too wins her by exerting decisive authority. His protective firmness mirrors Gilbert’s mastery, making the struggle between the two men partly a contest of wills enacted through Fanny.

Dialogue is theatrical and emotionally heightened rather than naturalistic. It serves to dramatize power relations: Gilbert speaks in commands, declarations, extravagant compliments, and metaphysical certainties; Fanny answers with protests, questions, hesitation, and repeated fear; Mowbray shifts from tenderness to stern insistence and final defiance. Endearments such as “little woman,” “dear child,” and “my wife” convey affection while also emphasizing paternalism and possession. The supernatural speeches retain this proprietary language, so that death does not alter Gilbert’s conception of marriage as eternal ownership.

Structurally, the story develops through escalation and recurrence. The opening summarizes the first marriage and establishes Gilbert’s unconquerable will. A sequence of rejected proposals demonstrates his continuing influence after death. Mowbray’s courtship then offers an apparent release, but the dream visitation reverses that hope. Marriage and travel create a temporary interval of happiness before recurring nightly encounters weaken Fanny physically and dissolve the boundary between dream and waking life. The conclusion transforms the domestic love triangle into a metaphysical confrontation, ending with Gilbert carrying Fanny into an “intenser light” while Mowbray is left in darkness.

The tone combines admiration, irony, sensuality, foreboding, and fatalism. Beauty is repeatedly shadowed by danger: roses accompany both courtship and haunting, warmth is invaded by supernatural cold, and sunlight becomes the medium through which the dead captain appears. The story’s dominant concerns are erotic submission, the persistence of marital bonds beyond death, the instability of female autonomy, and the destructive potential of charisma mistaken for love. Its final effect is deliberately ambiguous in moral terms. Gilbert’s return can be read as proof of transcendent devotion, but the language of conquest and destruction makes it equally legible as the ultimate victory of coercive possession.

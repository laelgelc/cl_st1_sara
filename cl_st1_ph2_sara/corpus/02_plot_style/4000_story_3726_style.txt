The story adopts a genial, mock-serious comic voice rooted in the American tall-tale tradition. A restrained frame narrator briefly introduces Mr. McWilliams, then yields almost the entire narrative to his extended first-person monologue. McWilliams speaks as an aggrieved but methodical raconteur: he insists upon the factual accuracy of events while recounting increasingly impossible disasters. His composure is central to the humor. Burglars are treated as guests or business partners, injuries as administrative inconveniences, and spectacular failures as matters to be corrected by another paid visit from an expert.

The prose combines colloquial speech with inflated, formal diction. Plain expressions such as “first rate,” “mighty well,” “a hitch,” and “had enough of that kind of pie” sit beside ceremonious phrases such as “hallowed conventionalities of our Christian civilization” and “nothing extenuated, and naught set down in malice.” This contrast repeatedly deflates pretension. Characters use legal, commercial, religious, and technical language in absurd circumstances, lending trivial matters an air of solemn authority. Euphemistic phrasing also sharpens the comedy: an impulse to strike a burglar becomes an “attention,” gunfire becomes “sudden music,” and financial exploitation is concealed behind phrases such as “charged accordingly” or “a remunerative figure.”

The humor is primarily deadpan, ironic, and hyperbolic. The narrator seldom marks a joke as a joke; instead, he presents irrational conduct as perfectly reasonable. Logical forms are preserved while their premises become absurd. A burglar apologizes for failing to ring the alarm, stolen property is “redeemed” at negotiated rates, and a stranger awakened from death collects his life insurance. Literal-minded interpretations, reversed priorities, and mock causality recur throughout. The alarm protects burglars rather than residents, attempts to improve security make the house progressively less secure, and the final practical solution—trading the machine for a dog and shooting the dog—delivers an abrupt piece of anti-logic.

Structurally, the narrative is cumulative and episodic. Each sequence follows a repeating pattern: the alarm fails, an expert is summoned, an expensive modification is installed, and the modification creates a worse problem. Repetition supplies rhythm and expectation, while escalation supplies surprise. The defects expand from an uncovered floor to deafening daily alarms, resident burglars, armed accidents, hundreds of false warnings, theft of the alarm itself, and clocks that reverse its intended schedule. Transitional phrases such as “by and by,” “well,” “next morning,” “at length,” and “finally” keep the monologue moving with the loose fluency of oral storytelling. They also make years of calamity feel like one uninterrupted anecdotal performance.

Sentences vary between short comic blows and long, clause-heavy accumulations. The longer sentences often pile consequence upon consequence through semicolons, dashes, parenthetical qualifications, and coordinating conjunctions. This creates the impression of a speaker improvising while remaining determined to document every relevant absurdity. Short declarative lines—“Through a second-story window,” “It was even so,” or “So I paid the bill”—then puncture the buildup. Lists and numerical specifics provide mock precision: prices, percentages, hours, floors, tags, miles of wire, and periods of time make the exaggerations sound documented and therefore comically credible.

Dialogue is stylized rather than naturalistic. Exchanges are built around elaborate politeness, rhetorical formality, and incongruous etiquette. Even the burglar speaks like an orator, while McWilliams answers with social courtesy and practical criticism of his metaphor. Direct address—“Mr. Twain,” “mind you,” “you see,” and “you already foresee the result”—maintains an intimate storyteller-listener relationship. It allows the speaker to anticipate objections, emphasize punch lines, and enlist the audience as a witness to his supposed reasonableness.

Characterization is economical and comic. McWilliams emerges through his language as patient, henpecked, financially exploited, self-dramatizing, and convinced of his own common sense. Mrs. McWilliams is defined largely through the recurring joke about “compromise,” in which her preference invariably prevails. Thomas the butler embodies unreliable human operation, the coachman becomes an overzealous armed ally, and the expert functions less as an individual than as the personification of profitable technical incompetence. The burglars, by contrast, are calm, adaptive, and almost domesticated, which intensifies the reversal between respectable institutions and criminal competence.

The tonal register remains amiable despite violence, theft, exhaustion, and financial loss. Injuries have cartoon-like consequences rather than emotional weight, and no calamity permanently interrupts the anecdote. The narration treats domestic chaos as social comedy, avoiding psychological depth in favor of timing, escalation, and verbal ingenuity. Satire is directed at technological overconfidence, costly specialists, defective systems, commercial opportunism, domestic power arrangements, and the human tendency to keep repairing a fundamentally bad idea.

The ending compresses the story’s method into a final sequence of reversals. McWilliams summarizes nine years of maintaining the device “for” the burglars, moves from exasperation to a brutally concise remedy, and closes with an extravagant comparison of the alarm to “a fire, a riot, and a harem.” The sudden everyday departure—“Good-by: I get off here”—cuts off the grand rhetoric and returns the tale to the casual conversation from which it arose. Overall, the style is conversational yet rhetorically elaborate, realistic in surface detail yet wildly implausible in event, and driven by deadpan narration, recursive structure, mock precision, formal-colloquial contrast, and steadily intensifying absurdity.

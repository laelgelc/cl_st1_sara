The story adopts the manner of a literary fairy tale combined with a satirical fable. Its setting is an idealised royal world of princes, castles, banquets, roses and fireworks, but conventional fairy-tale splendour is repeatedly punctured by worldly jokes about status, bureaucracy and vanity. The doubled salary that does not exist, the Court Gazette’s pompous announcements and the courtiers’ automatic praise of the King’s bad flute-playing establish a social comedy in which rank matters more than truth. The magical element—fireworks, flowers and animals that speak—is presented without explanation, giving the narrative the effortless unreality of an oral tale.

The narration is third-person omniscient, confident and lightly intrusive. The narrator supplies concise judgments about characters, often in aphoristic or mock-definitional form, such as the observation that some people believe repetition will make a claim true. These comments create complicity with the reader, who is invited to recognise follies that the characters themselves cannot see. The narrative voice remains polished and outwardly composed even when describing absurd behaviour, producing a dry, deadpan comic effect.

The dominant tone is witty, urbane and ironic. Praise frequently means its opposite, while high-minded language conceals vanity, selfishness or stupidity. The Remarkable Rocket calls himself sensitive, sympathetic and imaginative, yet every claim is disproved by his conduct. His “sympathy” means expecting everyone to think about him; his “imagination” consists of refusing to see reality; and his tears, intended as proof of emotional refinement, make him too damp to perform. This systematic gap between self-image and actuality drives the satire. Comic bathos is especially important: grand declarations about genius, destiny and public life culminate in mud, a kettle fire, an unnoticed explosion and a stick falling on a goose.

The diction alternates between decorative fairy-tale language and the vocabulary of fashionable society. Descriptions of the Princess and the wedding use luminous, ornamental terms—gold, silver, ermine, crystal, velvet, pearls, roses and stars—creating a jewel-like visual richness. By contrast, the Rocket speaks in inflated abstractions about “emotional nature,” “sympathy,” “genius,” “public life” and “the higher things.” Parliamentary, journalistic and social language is transferred to fireworks and animals, creating comic incongruity. Formal phrases such as “distinguished visitor,” “lower orders” and “becoming dignity” parody aristocratic pretension.

The syntax is generally clear, balanced and highly readable. Descriptive passages favour flowing compound and complex sentences, often linked by “and,” which gives the opening the cadence of a traditional tale. Dialogue is built from crisp exchanges, rhetorical questions, reversals and parallel constructions. The Rocket’s speeches are longer and more elaborate than those of the other characters, reflecting his self-importance and desire to monopolise attention. Repetition is used both musically and satirically: “White rose, Red rose,” “Romance is dead,” the Rocket’s repeated coughs, and his escalating claims of flying higher than the stars, moon and sun. These recurring patterns give the prose an oral, performative quality.

Dialogue carries most of the characterisation. Each speaker embodies a recognisable social type: the Rocket is the narcissistic aesthete and failed celebrity; the Roman Candle is practical and sceptical; the Catherine Wheel cultivates fashionable melancholy; the Cracker imitates a politician; the Frog is a complacent conversational monopolist; and the Duck represents usefulness, domesticity and common sense. Their exchanges often take the form of miniature debates in which words are twisted to preserve vanity. The Rocket repeatedly mishears insults as compliments—“bad” as “grand,” and “old” as “gold”—turning verbal misunderstanding into a symbol of wilful self-deception.

Figurative language is vivid but accessible. Similes dominate the ceremonial opening: the Princess is compared to the Snow Palace and to white and red roses, while the moon resembles a silver shield and fireworks become golden flowers with blossoms of fire. Personification extends beyond the speaking objects to the natural setting, where lilies watch the dance and poppies keep time. These images give the story sensuous beauty while also intensifying the contrast between genuine spectacle and the Rocket’s imaginary greatness. Onomatopoeia—“Whizz,” “Boom,” “Bang” and “Fizz”—accelerates the firework scenes and gives them the sound and rhythm of a staged performance.

The structure is strongly contrastive and episodic. A lavish royal frame introduces the fireworks, after which the story narrows around the Rocket’s conversations and decline. Each encounter repeats the same comic pattern: he asserts his superiority, another character exposes or ignores him, and he reinterprets the failure as evidence of his distinction. His physical descent—from ceremonial firework stand to ditch, mud, fire and discarded stick—mirrors his social and symbolic collapse. At the same time, his self-estimation rises, making the irony progressively sharper.

The ending is concise and circular. The Rocket finally explodes, but nobody sees or hears him; his predicted world-changing sensation is reduced to startling a goose. Even then he interprets the accident as confirmation of his greatness. The final verb, “went out,” refers literally to the extinguished firework but also suggests death, disappearance and the extinction of his pretensions. The style therefore leaves its moral unstated but unmistakable: vanity can absorb any contradiction, and complete self-delusion may survive even the most decisive failure.

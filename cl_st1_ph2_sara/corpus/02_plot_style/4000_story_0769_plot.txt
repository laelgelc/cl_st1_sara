A Boston man learns that his Aunt Georgiana is coming from rural Nebraska to settle a small inheritance. Once a gifted music teacher in Boston, she abandoned her career decades earlier to marry his uncle and endure an isolated, laborious life on a frontier homestead. The narrator remembers how, despite exhaustion and hardship, she educated him, taught him music, and helped him escape the narrow world that had consumed her own gifts.

When she arrives, Georgiana is physically worn, awkwardly dressed, and preoccupied with farm chores she left unfinished. Hoping to revive something of her former life, her nephew takes her to a Wagner concert. At first she seems detached, but the orchestra breaks through thirty years of deprivation and silence. Familiar melodies awaken her buried love of music, and she weeps as she realizes that her nephew has enjoyed such beauty ever since leaving Nebraska while she has remained cut off from it.

After the concert, Georgiana refuses to leave, sobbing that she does not want to go. Her nephew understands that departure means returning not merely from the concert hall, but to the bleak farm, relentless work, and spiritual isolation that have defined her life.

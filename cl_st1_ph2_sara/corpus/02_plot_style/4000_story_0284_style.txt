The story uses restrained, psychologically focused realism to portray emotional estrangement within an ordinary Midwestern household. Its setting is rendered through plain, concrete details—college rooms, loose shutters, cornfields, snow, gardens, domestic furniture—while the central drama occurs largely inside Hugh Walker’s mind. The provincial environment appears stable and commonplace, but this surface normality is repeatedly unsettled by feelings of imprisonment, unreality, distance, and suppressed desire.

The narration is third-person and closely aligned with Hugh’s consciousness. It moves freely between external observation, indirect thought, and sentences that personify his inner voice, often without sharply marked transitions. Although the narrator occasionally makes broad declarative judgments, the perspective remains limited by Hugh’s confusion and self-deception. His interpretations of Winifred, Mary, the children, and the household are therefore psychologically revealing rather than reliably objective. Other characters are generally seen from outside and acquire symbolic functions in his mind.

The diction is simple, direct, and deliberately repetitive. Short statements such as “Marriage was marriage to her” or “Nothing happened” carry more weight than their literal content suggests. Familiar words—“fact,” “life,” “belong,” “walk,” “understand,” “prison”—recur until they become thematic motifs. Abstract reflection is grounded in ordinary physical objects and actions: banging shutters suggest a damaged inner shell, walking becomes an attempted cure for spiritual paralysis, and reading stands for detached thought in opposition to lived experience. The story also uses racial terminology and stereotyped descriptions characteristic of its historical period; the Black domestic worker is treated less as a fully developed individual than as an intuitive, symbolic presence.

Sentence structure alternates between blunt declarative clauses and longer, accumulating passages that follow Hugh’s drifting associations. Fragments and isolated questions—especially “For what?”—interrupt the narrative like intrusive thoughts. Repetition reproduces the circular motion of his mind: he returns continually to the same questions without resolving them. Parallel constructions, lists of domestic facts, and phrases such as “on and on” reinforce the sense of monotony and entrapment. At moments of agitation, the prose becomes more rapid and forceful, with clustered verbs, oaths, and sudden outbursts.

Dialogue is sparse, banal, and emotionally indirect. Exchanges about health, shutters, books, or headaches conceal what the characters cannot articulate. Hugh’s most extensive conversations are effectively with himself, often framed as if an internal “third person” were speaking. Spoken language repeatedly becomes a form of evasion: Hugh lies about his motives, Winifred fills silence with domestic observations, and Mary invents a headache after the kiss. The disparity between speech and feeling is one of the story’s principal stylistic tensions.

Imagery is subdued but symbolically persistent. Houses, rooms, doors, bars, shells, walls, and locks form an enclosure motif associated with marriage and possession. By contrast, roads, fields, hills, darkness, and distant figures suggest freedom, though that freedom remains elusive and indistinct. Natural comparisons are especially important: people appear as trees, children as unformed growth, and an unanswered question as a hungry animal. Light is usually faint, dim, or quivering, allowing fleeting moments of recognition to emerge from surrounding darkness.

The pacing is slow and recursive rather than conventionally plot-driven. Large spans of married life are compressed into a few sentences, while small gestures—a hand touching a cheek, a woman crossing toward a barn, a girl kissing a sleeping child—receive intense attention. The narrative develops through repeated domestic scenes and walking episodes, then sharpens around Hugh’s fixation on Mary. Abrupt incidents of anger or physical contact puncture the prevailing stillness, but they do not produce genuine resolution.

The tone is sober, melancholy, and quietly disturbing. It combines sympathy for Hugh’s alienation with an increasingly clear exposure of his narcissism, possessiveness, and rationalization. Irony arises from the gap between his claim that he wishes to preserve Mary’s freedom and his coercive act of kissing and banishing her. The ending adopts his language of liberation and imprisonment while revealing that he has himself initiated the very loss of innocence he claims not to cause. Overall, the style depends on plain language, symbolic repetition, psychological proximity, emotional understatement, and unresolved moral ambiguity.

The story is written as a fast-moving military adventure, combining early technological science fiction with the conventions of naval war fiction. Its tone is grave, urgent, and increasingly claustrophobic. Technical procedures—submerging, steering by compass, managing oxygen, firing torpedoes, pumping out water—are presented with enough specificity to lend the action mechanical credibility, but the machinery is also romanticised through images of immense, almost supernatural power. Horsepower becomes “hundreds of demons,” shells are “steel-clad demons,” and the sea battle resembles “a multitude of sea-devils” roaring at one another. This fusion of practical detail and extravagant personification gives the submarine campaign both documentary texture and mythic intensity.

The narration uses a largely omniscient third-person perspective, though it frequently moves close to Captain Andrey’s physical sensations and immediate thoughts. Brief internal phrases such as “‘A mine!’ flashed across Andrey’s mind” and “‘Bad!’ thought Andrey” compress his mental response into instinctive flashes suited to crisis. The narrator occasionally withdraws to provide explanatory information, as in the observation about seawater’s transparency from a great height. These authorial interventions resemble the explanatory voice of early popular science fiction and adventure writing, where the reader is guided through unfamiliar technology and tactical circumstances.

The prose is predominantly chronological and event-driven. It proceeds through a chain of hazards—minefield, oxygen shortage, aerial attack, encirclement, concealment, attempted breakout, torpedo assault, flooding, and apparent rescue—with relatively little retrospective material. Transitions such as “At nightfall,” “At dawn,” “Suddenly,” “Presently,” “Two weeks,” and “At the end of that time” keep the narrative advancing clearly. Pacing alternates between condensed summaries of prolonged activity and sharply dramatised moments of danger. Two weeks of concealment are covered in a few sentences, while the approach of a mine, the failure of the periscope, or the opening of the final hatch is narrated nearly moment by moment. Short commands and exclamations accelerate the action: “Stop! Stop!”, “Full speed forward!”, and “All hands below!”

Sentence structure tends toward long, coordinated constructions filled with successive actions, observations, and consequences. Semicolons often connect simultaneous duties or rapidly changing tactical conditions. In moments of greatest danger, however, the syntax contracts into short declarations and commands. This rhythmic contrast reproduces the alternation between sustained pressure and abrupt emergency. Repetition is used emphatically, especially in shouted orders such as “Forward! forward!” and in enumerations like “in front of her, on both sides, everywhere,” which create the sensation of total encirclement.

The diction mixes formal, sometimes old-fashioned phrasing with specialised naval and mechanical vocabulary. Expressions such as “in that quarter,” “at the end of which time,” “had blundered into,” and “some action was inevitable” give the narrative an early twentieth-century translated or period-adventure quality. Terms including “ballast tanks,” “conning tower,” “mine net,” “torpedo tubes,” “sextant,” “logarithmic tables,” and “lead cylinders” establish the professional environment. Some words prefixed with an anomalous “v,” such as “vdistilling,” “vhatchways,” and “vhydroplanes,” appear to be transcription or OCR artefacts rather than meaningful stylistic features. The later shift between “Andrey” and “Audrey” likewise appears accidental.

Sensory imagery is dense and strongly colour-based. The underwater world is rendered in “diluted, greenish light,” “eery darkness,” and “greenish twilight,” while the surface world appears in pale blue, orange, purple, and molten-glass tones. Light repeatedly marks the boundary between imprisonment and freedom: the submarine descends into darkness, the periscope screen comes alive, candles fail, and the final hatch admits “light and air.” Sound is equally prominent. Motors pulsate, pinions crack, guns crackle, bombs hiss, waves froth, and leaking water falls with a monotonous splash. Onomatopoeic phrases such as “rat-a-tat” and “F-r-r-r” give the combat scenes an immediate, almost serial-adventure vividness.

The story repeatedly contrasts the beauty and tranquillity of nature with mechanised violence. The sea is “quiet and peaceful,” the sunset warms the clouds, and the submarine cuts through orange water “as if cutting through molten glass,” even while aircraft and warships close in. This juxtaposition heightens the unnatural brutality of warfare. Similes are direct and visually forceful: the submarine circles “like a run-down hare,” a hydroplane moves “like a bat,” men gulp air “like a fish,” and an explosion rises as a “mountainous mass of fire and water.” Personification turns the Kate into an active female presence that sinks, gropes, trembles, dives upward, and picks her way through danger.

Characterisation is subordinated to action but gains emotional force through physical gesture. Andrey is defined by command presence, endurance, and protective tenderness: he is “everywhere at once,” sustains the crew with his voice, gives oxygen to himself only when necessary, revives Bylopolsky with brandy, and kisses his “childlike forehead.” Under pressure, however, courage mutates into destructive intoxication. The crew’s final decision to re-enter battle arises not from duty but from “greediness” and “a yearning for destruction.” This explicit psychological turn complicates the heroic mode, showing combat as both exhilarating and irrational. Faces recur as symbols of shared vulnerability—livid, pale, childlike, filmed with approaching death—humanising men otherwise identified mainly by rank and function.

Dialogue is sparse, practical, and usually tied to command. Naval hierarchy shapes most exchanges, yet moments of colloquial understatement reveal character, as when a suffocating sailor says, “I’m a bit unwell.” Such restraint intensifies pathos because terror is expressed indirectly. The final dialogue is similarly simple and emotionally effective: the expected confrontation becomes recognition, and “Russians, Russians” releases the accumulated tension. Kuritzyn’s “Thank God!” supplies an abrupt rescue ending without prolonged explanation.

Structurally, the story relies on serial escalation and repeated reversals. Apparent escape from the minefield leads to discovery; fresh air leads to aerial attack; concealment leads to starvation; an opportunity to return home becomes a reckless attack; apparent destruction is followed by partial resurfacing; and anticipated capture or self-destruction ends in rescue. The closing scene mirrors the earlier motif of sealed hatches: throughout the story, opening a hatch risks death, but at the end it brings both air and salvation. Overall, the style is cinematic, melodramatic, technically furnished, and physically immersive, with heroic adventure complicated by claustrophobia, bodily suffering, and the destabilising exhilaration of mechanised war.

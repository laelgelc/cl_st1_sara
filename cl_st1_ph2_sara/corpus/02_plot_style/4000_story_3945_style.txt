The story uses a gentle, intimate fairy-tale mode, opening with the traditional “There was once” and combining domestic realism with nursery magic. Its narrative voice is warm, assured, and quietly omniscient, moving freely between external description and the Rabbit’s private thoughts. Although the narration remains in the third person, it is closely aligned with the Rabbit’s limited understanding: living animals are interpreted as unusually sophisticated toys, movement is attributed to clockwork, and physical transformation is initially imagined in terms of seams, sawdust, and mechanisms. This childlike perspective creates mild dramatic irony while preserving emotional sincerity.

The diction is clear, accessible, and slightly old-fashioned, with nursery vocabulary and period details such as “sateen,” “fender,” “Nana,” “picture-books,” and “fowl-house.” Capitalisation gives archetypal significance to figures and concepts—“the Boy,” “the Rabbit,” “Real,” and “Fairy”—so that ordinary childhood experiences take on the quality of myth. Evaluative words such as “splendid,” “charming,” “wonderful,” “lovely,” and “delightful” recur, reflecting a child-centred emotional scale. Repetition and intensification are prominent: the Rabbit becomes “shabbier and shabbier,” the Boy loves him “for a long, long time,” and Rabbitland is promised “for ever and ever.” These patterns make the prose soothing, memorable, and suitable for reading aloud.

Sentences often unfold through coordinated clauses joined by “and,” reproducing the cumulative rhythm of oral storytelling. Longer descriptive passages gather sensory details, actions, and feelings in a flowing sequence, while short sentences mark revelations and emotional turning points: “He was Real,” “And then, one day, the Boy was ill,” and “He was a Real Rabbit at last.” Parenthetical explanations and gently explanatory transitions guide younger readers without sounding overtly instructional. The recurring openings “And so,” “And then,” “One evening,” and “Weeks passed” give the narrative a measured, episodic progression through seasons and stages of the Rabbit’s life.

Dialogue is simple, direct, and character-revealing. The philosophical exchange with the Skin Horse uses a question-and-answer pattern resembling a child’s catechism, turning an abstract idea into emotionally comprehensible language. The Skin Horse speaks with plain, aphoristic wisdom: becoming Real is not a matter of manufacture but of being loved, enduring change, and ceasing to fear damage. Elsewhere, dialogue creates social contrast. The mechanical toys are boastful and status-conscious, Nana is brisk and practical, the doctor is abrupt, the wild rabbits are teasing and literal-minded, and the Fairy speaks with serene authority. Capitalised emphasis in spoken lines—especially “REAL”—captures childish insistence and gives the story’s central word symbolic force.

The tone blends tenderness, humour, melancholy, and reassurance. Comic passages arise from the toys’ vanity, the Rabbit’s misconceptions, and Nana’s sweeping approach to tidying, but the humour is never cruel. The story gradually deepens into pathos as love wears away the Rabbit’s beauty, the Boy falls ill, and the Rabbit is discarded for burning. Sentiment is expressed openly through tears, longing, loneliness, and devotion, yet concrete physical details—rubbed fur, missing whiskers, loosened seams, faded spots—keep the emotion grounded. The final magical rescue converts loss into fulfilment, producing a consolatory rather than tragic ending.

Personification is the central technique. Toys possess consciousness, social hierarchies, embarrassment, vanity, loyalty, and wisdom, while their material construction remains continuously visible. This tension between object and person supports the story’s governing metaphor: love makes identity real, even as it causes physical wear. Shabbiness therefore becomes an emblem of intimacy and experience rather than decay. The contrast between expensive mechanical toys and the worn Skin Horse also establishes an ethical opposition between novelty and endurance, pretension and authenticity, appearance and inward worth.

Imagery is tactile and domestic at first, dwelling on velveteen, sateen, sawdust, thread whiskers, button eyes, bedclothes, warmth, and hugging. As the story moves outdoors, its imagery becomes pastoral and luminous: raspberry canes resemble a jungle, bracken shines like frosted silver, moths flutter at dusk, and rabbits dance on velvet grass. The magical climax uses jewel-like colours and delicate materials—emerald leaves, a golden cup, pearl, flowers, and dewdrops—creating a visual register distinct from the worn nursery world. Seasonal movement from Christmas through spring, summer, illness, autumn, winter, and a renewed spring mirrors the Rabbit’s passage from creation to apparent destruction and rebirth.

Structurally, the narrative follows a transformation arc built around two meanings of “Real.” First, the Rabbit becomes real within the Boy’s imaginative and emotional world; later, the Fairy makes him biologically real and visible as such to everyone. The encounter with the wild rabbits foreshadows this distinction and introduces the Rabbit’s deepest insecurity: he is emotionally real but not yet physically alive. The threatened bonfire forms the darkest reversal, while the genuine tear functions as the threshold into fairy-tale transformation. The concluding return to the Boy creates a circular, bittersweet closure: the Rabbit remembers the child who loved him, while the Boy recognises only a trace of his former toy.

Overall, the style is that of a literary children’s tale designed to speak simultaneously to children and adults. Its surface is lucid, rhythmic, and comforting, while its emotional and symbolic structure addresses love, mortality, ageing, sacrifice, memory, and the creation of identity through relationship. The prevailing effect is wistful but hopeful: genuine love leaves visible marks, involves pain and loss, and grants a form of permanence that outward beauty and fashionable novelty cannot provide.

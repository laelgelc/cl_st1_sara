The story adopts the voice of a traditional literary fairy tale, combining antiquarian realism, folklore, Christian morality, sentimental romance, and gentle supernaturalism. Its narrator is omniscient, companionable, and openly instructive, frequently addressing the reader through phrases such as “we shall hear,” “it is easily understood,” and “this must be remembered.” Rather than remaining invisible, the narrator explains customs, interprets emotions, anticipates questions, and closes with an explicit warning. The tone moves between conversational humour, historical curiosity, wistful nostalgia, pathos, and solemn moral reflection.

The opening is deliberately discursive. Before the central narrative begins, the text supplies an etymological explanation of Hauschen or Hysken Street, an account of German merchants in Copenhagen, and the origin of the Danish expression “pepper gentry.” This documentary material gives the tale a pseudo-historical foundation and makes the later supernatural event seem rooted in communal tradition. The exposition is leisurely and digressive, with customs, clothing, architecture, trade, and urban conditions described at length. Lists of commodities and garments—beer, saffron, aniseed, ginger, pepper, hats, collars, cloaks, knives, and shoes—create a densely furnished historical setting.

The prose generally uses long, syntactically layered sentences joined by semicolons, commas, dashes, and coordinating conjunctions. Paragraphs often progress through accumulation: a physical detail leads to social commentary, which leads to a general observation or moral reflection. The diction is formal and slightly archaic, with expressions such as “olden times,” “fain,” “tutelar saint,” “four-and-twenty hours,” and “dwelt,” but it remains accessible through direct explanations and familiar analogies. Repetition is common, especially in emotional passages, where words such as “tears,” “thoughts,” “memory,” “old,” and “nightcap” acquire symbolic weight.

Humour is mild, verbal, and often parenthetical. Anthony has “two, nightcaps I mean, not heads,” while the narrator playfully corrects linguistic assumptions and comments on social habits. Such moments soften the story’s melancholy and establish an oral-storytelling intimacy. The humour gradually recedes as Anthony’s loneliness, disappointed love, poverty, ageing, and death become dominant. Even then, the sentiment is controlled by proverbial formulations and concrete images rather than by psychological analysis alone.

Characterisation relies heavily on visual description, habitual action, and emblematic objects. Anthony is introduced through his thin body, wrinkles, bony fingers, eyebrows, tuft of hair, clothes, and ever-present nightcap. His anxious nightly checking of the fire and shutters conveys loneliness, insecurity, and compulsive care without requiring direct exposition. Molly is drawn more lightly, through bold speech, teasing behaviour, dark hair, and her childhood power over Anthony. The characters have the simplified clarity of folktale figures, yet Anthony receives substantial emotional depth through memory, regret, resentment, resignation, and religious consolation.

The narrative structure is framed and retrospective. It begins with the cultural meaning of the bachelor’s nightcap, narrows to Anthony’s solitary life in Copenhagen, and then enters an extended sequence of memories covering childhood, first love, rejection, family ruin, migration, and old age. These memories do not initially appear in strict order; the narrator explicitly compares them to pictures illuminated within a tear. The story later returns to Anthony’s deathbed and finally extends beyond his death, when others wear the cap and inherit his visions. This circular design transforms a comic social object into a vessel of preserved suffering.

Dialogue is sparse but strategically important. Molly’s childhood instructions about planting the apple pip reveal confidence and practical intelligence, while her adult rejection is direct, composed, and emotionally decisive. Anthony’s spoken lines tend to be brief and emblematic, expressing bitterness, resignation, or faith. Embedded songs, legends, reported sayings, and rhetorical questions broaden the tale’s oral and folkloric texture, although some quoted songs are introduced without their full verses in the surviving text.

Imagery is strongly symbolic and repeatedly drawn from nature. The apple tree is the central symbol: planted jointly by the children, it represents their shared affection, growth, separation, endurance, and the persistence of memory. Its blossoms and fruit correspond to youth and possibility; its broken branches and exposed position mirror Anthony’s damaged, neglected life. Tears are repeatedly described as pearls, turning private grief into something beautiful, durable, and ultimately magical. The nightcap becomes a material archive that absorbs those tears and transmits Anthony’s emotional history to later wearers. Roses, snow, darkness, fire, moonlight, birdsong, and changing seasons reinforce the movement between love, loss, hardship, grace, and death.

The supernatural is introduced gradually rather than through abrupt spectacle. Anthony’s final vision of Saint Elizabeth, roses, and the apple tree can first be read as a dying hallucination or religious consolation. Only after his death does the nightcap’s power become externally verifiable, when its wearers experience his memories and pearls fall from it. This delayed confirmation preserves a dreamlike ambiguity before resolving the tale into a moral fantasy.

The story’s emotional method is openly sentimental. Sorrow is externalised through scalding tears, broken heartstrings, ruined horses, barren winter rooms, and the neglected apple tree. Contrasts intensify the pathos: childhood warmth against adult isolation, spring blossoms against snow, remembered abundance against present poverty, and a loving household against the abandoned bachelor’s death. Religious imagery prevents the ending from being wholly bleak. Saint Elizabeth’s miracle and Anthony’s peaceful final vision frame death as release and spiritual refreshment, even though his material loneliness remains a social reproach.

Its thematic concerns include the persistence of memory, the pain of unfulfilled love, the vulnerability of strangers and unmarried old men, the instability of wealth, and the moral value of compassion. The narrator also challenges casual ridicule: the mocked bachelor and his comic nightcap conceal an entire history of love, labour, disappointment, and endurance. The final imperative—do not wish for a bachelor’s nightcap—functions both literally and metaphorically. It warns against coveting another person’s apparent possessions or romanticising their inner life, because inherited thoughts and dreams may also mean inherited suffering.

To reproduce this style, a writer would use an intrusive but kindly storyteller, begin with cultural or historical explanation, build the setting through concrete inventories, and allow digressions to carry moral and emotional significance. The prose should alternate long descriptive sentences with short proverbial declarations, mingle gentle jokes with grave reflection, and centre the plot on ordinary objects that gradually become symbols. Folklore, local legend, Christian imagery, seasonal nature, embedded anecdote, and direct reader address should converge in a melancholy ending capped by an explicit moral.

The story uses a warm, romantic, late-Victorian or Edwardian narrative style, combining pastoral realism with fairy-tale wonder. Its third-person omniscient narrator moves chiefly between Miss Trevor’s and Paul’s perceptions, but occasionally enters Stephen’s concealed emotional life. The narrative voice is affectionate, assured, and gently interpretive, openly characterising Paul as charming and imaginative while allowing understated ironies to emerge around Miss Trevor’s confidence that wealth and education must improve his life.

Description is lush, painterly, and strongly colour-saturated. Seascapes are rendered through layered shades—“daffodil and rose,” “fiery golden,” “purple,” “violet,” “silver”—and through delicate motion such as waves “crisping and silvering” or masts “nodding” against the horizon. Light repeatedly carries emotional and spiritual significance: sunset opens a path into Paul’s imagination, his smile appears as an inner lamp, and dawn marks his restoration to home. The natural setting is frequently personified, with the sea throbbing, the night becoming kind, and the confined town wind resembling a “fettered, cringing thing.” These techniques make the landscape more than a backdrop: it becomes an extension of identity and feeling.

The prose favours long, flowing, carefully balanced sentences in descriptive and reflective passages, often accumulating clauses and sensory details before arriving at an emotional conclusion. Shorter sentences are used at moments of discovery or decision—“Then she saw Paul,” “In the end Paul ‘liked,’” and “He had to.” Repetition intensifies emotional climaxes, especially in Paul’s homesickness: “never—never,” “are you glad—are you glad?” Dashes, semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, and rhetorical questions reproduce shifts in thought and lend the narration a conversational flexibility. The diction is polished and literary, including words such as “elusive,” “meditative,” “capricious,” and “untrammelled,” but it remains accessible through concrete imagery and clear emotional movement.

Figurative language is abundant and idealising. Paul looks “as young princes should—and seldom do”; his imagination is compared to a prism turning every ray into rainbows; the cottage resembles a seashell cast up by the tide. Similes often unite the ordinary shore world with enchantment, blurring the line between realistic observation and Paul’s invented mythology. His imagined figures—the Twin Sailors, Nora, the Golden Lady, the enchanted boat, and Sunset Land—are presented with vivid specificity before being humorously punctured by his insistence that none of them is true. The effect is not to discredit fantasy but to establish imagination as a consciously exercised creative gift.

Dialogue is a major vehicle of characterisation. Paul’s speech blends childish grammar and pronunciation—“prob’bly,” “b’lieve,” “’normous,” “don’t hardly”—with unexpectedly elaborate storytelling and moral delicacy. His repeated warnings that his tales are not true create gentle comedy and reveal his literal honesty. Miss Trevor speaks in a playful, cultivated, faintly theatrical manner, asking about “tiresome items” and “how long” Paul has been himself. Stephen’s language is sparse, dialectal, and emotionally guarded. His laconic phrases contrast with both Miss Trevor’s sophistication and Paul’s imaginative abundance; the restraint of “I reckon I am” carries more force because his love is rarely verbalised.

The tone shifts gradually from enchanted delight to emotional unease, homesickness, and quiet affirmation. Early scenes are leisurely and episodic, built around meetings, stories, and growing affection. The adoption proposal introduces a conflict between opportunity and belonging. Miss Trevor’s plan is benevolent, but the narration subtly exposes its limitations: she initially views Paul through class assumptions, calls him a “shore boy,” and speaks patronisingly to Stephen. The town then becomes claustrophobic and artificial, while Noel’s Cove remains spacious, animate, and sustaining. This contrast supports the story’s central preference for organic attachment over imposed advancement.

Character emotion is often conveyed indirectly. Stephen’s expressionless face, the hand gripping his pipe in his pocket, his refusal to influence Paul, and his momentary flash of exultation all reveal love through repression. Paul’s emotions, by contrast, are immediate and bodily: his throat tightens, he chokes with tears, runs until breathless, and feels gladness like an “upspringing fountain.” Miss Trevor is represented through reflective commentary and affectionate self-dramatisation. This variation gives each character a distinct emotional register.

Structurally, the story is circular. It begins at sunset with Paul seated in his native landscape and ends at dawn after his return, restoring his bond with Stephen and his “rock people.” Sunset initially signifies imaginative departure; dawn signifies recognition and homecoming. The foolscap book functions as a symbol of Paul’s creative talent and of Miss Trevor’s claims upon him. By leaving it behind, he relinquishes a physical record of fantasy while returning to the place that nourishes fantasy itself. The final line, in which he runs to see Nora, confirms that his imaginary world is emotionally real even though he knows it is factually invented.

Overall, the style is sentimental without being wholly saccharine, whimsical without abandoning realism, and ornate without losing narrative clarity. Its defining features are lyrical maritime description, affectionate omniscience, idealised child characterisation, humorous yet credible dialogue, restrained pathos, symbolic use of landscape and light, and a strong thematic contrast between material opportunity and the deeper claims of home, love, imagination, and belonging.

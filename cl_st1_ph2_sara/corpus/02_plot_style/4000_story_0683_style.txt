The story is written as a first-person frontier tale that blends Gothic mystery, grotesque comedy, social satire, and supernatural ambiguity. Its narrator is educated, observant, and self-consciously rational, but also fanciful and susceptible to dread. He recounts events retrospectively, often pausing to interpret his own reactions or mock his attempts at logical explanation. This creates a double perspective: the experiencing narrator feels fear and curiosity, while the narrating voice subjects those feelings to dry, ironic scrutiny.

The prose alternates between elaborate literary narration and heavily marked regional dialogue. Descriptive passages use formal, sometimes Latinate diction, long periodic sentences, balanced clauses, and carefully developed metaphors. By contrast, Jo Dunfer and Gopher speak in compressed frontier vernacular, with dropped consonants, phonetic spellings, slang, malapropisms, and comic coinages. Expressions such as “justifiably homicided” and the distorted legal verdict turn brutality into absurd bureaucratic language. This sharp linguistic contrast distinguishes the narrator from the local characters while also allowing supposedly respectable institutions—law, religion, politics, and racial ideology—to be satirised through the speech of their adherents.

The dominant tone is sardonic. Nearly every solemn subject is undercut by verbal irony, comic qualification, or grotesque comparison. Jo’s religious conversion, patriotism, and racial hostility are presented as hypocritical performances masking drunkenness, jealousy, and violence. Biblical allusions are repeatedly debased for comic effect: Jo uses scripture to excuse cruelty, while the narrator describes Gopher’s stick as a rod that neither blossoms nor becomes a serpent. Elevated phrasing is similarly applied to trivial or sordid acts, producing mock-heroic humour. The narrator’s battle against superstition, for example, becomes an extravagant military campaign of “troops and squadrons,” “mental guns,” and “irresistible conclusions,” only for dread to defeat him “bag and baggage.”

The imagery persistently animates the landscape. The ravine appears “half-confidential,” as though withholding a secret, and later “unbosoms itself” like a guilty friend. Paths flow, stones establish independent lives, winds finger treetops, trees whisper, and decay arbitrates between cultures. This personification makes the natural setting feel sentient without confirming anything supernatural. The ravine functions as both a physical location and a symbolic repository of concealed guilt. Its coolness, shadow, silence, and enclosure contrast with the exposed heat of the surrounding country, marking entry into it as a descent from ordinary social reality into buried history.

Descriptions are vivid, tactile, and frequently grotesque. Jo is rendered through knotted, violent physical details: a “corded face,” “gnarled arm,” and hand like “a bunch of prison-keys.” His stoop suggests a beast preparing to spring and tear its prey. Violence is often conveyed through startling similes involving animals, machinery, or bodily damage: Jo recoils like a cannon and collapses like slaughtered beef. Such comparisons create an atmosphere in which comic exaggeration and genuine menace are inseparable.

Dialogue is highly theatrical. Jo’s story is repeatedly interrupted by drinking, gestures, demonstrations, and the narrator’s caustic commentary. His intoxication is shown indirectly through repetition, unstable vision, and increasingly erratic performance. Gopher’s later narration is even more dramatic: his manner shifts from clownish to tender, then snaps back into mockery. He is described as a “consummate actor,” and his emotional transformations control both the narrator’s sympathy and the reader’s interpretation. Speech therefore serves not merely to convey information but to stage competing versions of the past.

The narrative structure is delayed and recursive. The opening establishes the ravine as a mystery before introducing Jo and his incomplete account of Ah Wee. The eye at the knothole provides a brief Gothic shock, after which the narrator discovers the grave and an apparently explanatory epitaph. That seeming solution is then withheld from full interpretation until the second section, set four years later. The return to the same landscape introduces a second grave, a second inscription, and Gopher’s corrective narrative. Each revelation revises the preceding one rather than simply completing it: Ah Wee’s supposed incompetence becomes a cover story; Jo’s hatred conceals attachment and guilt; Gopher’s comic persona conceals bereavement and madness; and the narrator’s assumed innocence is destabilised by the accusation that he poisoned Jo’s whisky.

Pacing follows this logic of postponement. Expansive description and comic digression slow the approach to each revelation, while moments of terror arrive abruptly—the eye in the wall, the graves, the accusation, and Gopher’s final explanation. Section breaks and repeated visits create temporal depth, allowing physical decay to become part of the storytelling. The changed clearing reflects the erosion of official distinctions and public lies, even as memory remains embedded in inscriptions, graves, and obsessive speech.

The story’s humour depends heavily on incongruity. High style meets low subject matter; sentiment collides with insult; murder is absorbed into legal farce; and epitaphs combine memorial tenderness with racial abuse and cheap wordplay. The grave inscription’s shift from “Chinaman” to “She Was a Good Egg” is initially treated as lunatic inconsistency but later becomes evidence of Ah Wee’s concealed identity and Jo’s conflicted grief. Comedy thus operates as camouflage: jokes disclose truths that the characters cannot state plainly.

The treatment of race is intentionally saturated with period slurs and stereotypes, but these belong chiefly to prejudiced characters and to the social order being exposed. Jo’s anti-Chinese rhetoric is depicted as self-serving, incoherent, and performative. The coroner, church, electorate, and political system all participate in converting murder into acceptable racial ideology. At the same time, the story’s own historical vocabulary remains dehumanising by modern standards, and its satire works through caricature rather than through a consistently humane representational mode.

A central stylistic feature is unstable interpretation. The narrator repeatedly proposes rational explanations—drunken hallucination, delirium tremens, madness—but the story never wholly secures them. Gopher is identified as insane, yet he knows details that connect him to the eye at the knothole, the burial, and the epitaphs. His fear of meeting the dead Jo preserves a supernatural possibility, while his final offstage beating of the oxen restores the comic surface. The ending therefore refuses emotional or epistemic closure. Its last line is an anticlimactic frontier command, but the absurdly renamed ox and the unseen “vigorous thumps” leave the reader with a characteristic mixture of laughter, cruelty, unease, and unresolved mystery.

The story uses close third-person narration, moving chiefly through Frances’s unstable emotional perspective while occasionally entering Anne’s and Tom’s consciousness. The narrator combines psychological intimacy with an assured, sometimes aphoristic overview of character: Anne is practical and “unvexed,” Frances whimsical and wounded, and Tom instinctive but emotionally inarticulate. Feelings are rarely explained in purely abstract terms; instead, they are registered through bodily reactions, glances, gestures, changes of attention, and the shifting appearance of the physical world.

The prose is sensuous, richly visual, and strongly rooted in the rural landscape. Colour terms recur with painterly precision—brown, scarlet, blonde, khaki, ruby, blackish—and natural objects are rendered through unusual textures and comparisons. The countryside is not decorative background but an emotional medium. Heat, glare, scorched vegetation, stillness, fruit, stubble, and dry earth reflect Frances’s exhaustion and estrangement. As her emotional condition changes, the landscape changes in value: objects first possess an intense, almost hostile poignancy, then become “trifling” after the mole’s death. Nature is therefore both vividly material and symbolically responsive.

Sentence structure alternates between compact dramatic statements and long, cumulative descriptive passages. Dialogue and moments of violence are handled with short clauses, abrupt turns, and precise physical verbs, while landscape descriptions expand through lists, parallel constructions, semicolons, and layered spatial perspectives. Repetition is used to intensify sensation, especially in the mole episode, where its blindness, frantic movement, small hands, and gaping mouth are described several times from slightly altered angles. This creates a slow, almost unbearable scrutiny before the sudden simplicity of its death.

The diction mixes literary description with colloquial, regionally marked speech. Forms such as “appen,” “s’ll,” “wi’,” “yon,” “nay,” and “moudiwarp” establish a working rural community and expose differences of class, education, and aspiration. Anne’s speech is direct, comic, and practical; Tom’s dialect conveys both social rootedness and vulnerability; Frances’s more standard speech reflects refinement, self-consciousness, and her attraction to gentility. Speech itself becomes part of courtship when Frances asks Tom to “talk nicely,” superficially correcting him while actually inviting intimacy.

Dialogue is highly subtextual. Characters speak about rabbits, engagements, dialect, and moles while negotiating jealousy, sexual attraction, pride, class, and emotional power. Exchanges often proceed through teasing contradiction, evasive questions, and sudden tonal shifts. Frances habitually protects herself with sarcasm, coldness, or flippancy, whereas Anne’s literal-minded interventions provide comic relief and expose what the adults are avoiding. Tom’s courtship is hesitant and oblique, expressed through verbal awkwardness and physical agitation rather than polished declaration.

Characterization depends upon contrast. Anne is younger but behaves maternally; she is vigorous, affectionate, complacent, and capable of swift practical violence. Frances is older yet more fragile, divided between longing, pride, cruelty, and the desire to surrender. Tom is socially less refined than Frances’s idealized Jimmy, but he possesses a physical and emotional force that Jimmy’s distant “half-measures” lack. These oppositions—practicality and sensitivity, refinement and instinct, detachment and desire—generate the story’s tension.

The mole functions as the central symbolic image and structural hinge. Its ecstatic but blind exposure to heat and touch parallels Frances’s emotional vulnerability, while its killing dramatizes the destruction of helpless feeling. Frances’s pity gives way to indifference, and she subsequently repeats the act herself. Bringing Tom the second dead mole becomes a displaced erotic offering and proof that she can accept his harsher, earthbound world. The symbol remains deliberately ambivalent: killing the mole suggests emotional deadening and compromised choice, but also decision, agency, and entry into adult sexuality.

The tone shifts fluidly among rustic comedy, lyrical pastoral description, psychological unease, cruelty, and restrained erotic intensity. Humour arises from Anne’s bluntness, Tom’s dialect, and the sisters’ teasing, but it never cancels the underlying sadness. Violence is presented without melodramatic commentary; its emotional force comes from exact observation and Frances’s changing response. The final courtship scene is similarly unsentimental. Desire appears as something bodily, frightening, coercive, and tender at once.

Structurally, the story moves from fatigue and romantic disappointment through symbolic death to a substitute attachment. The progression is quiet but tightly controlled: Frances hears of Jimmy’s engagement, witnesses Anne kill a mole, tests Tom through the language of killing, then kills another mole and offers it to him. Recurring images and phrases bind these stages together. The ending preserves emotional ambiguity through paradox: Frances answers in a “dead voice,” yet experiences “a thrill of pleasure in this death.” This fusion of loss and gratification typifies the story’s style, which treats love not as uncomplicated fulfilment but as a troubling mixture of surrender, displacement, pride, violence, and renewed vitality.

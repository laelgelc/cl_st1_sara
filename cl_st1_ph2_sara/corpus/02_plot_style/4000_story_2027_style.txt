The story adopts the manner of an antiquarian ghost tale: urbane, scholarly and outwardly restrained, with supernatural horror emerging from an environment of catalogues, museums, college rooms, guidebooks and historical records. Its world is socially narrow and highly particularised, inhabited by university Fellows, museum officials, dealers and servants. Mundane institutional detail lends the narrative documentary credibility, so that the impossible event appears not in a romantic wilderness but among recognisable routines of tea, Hall, golf, whist, correspondence and collection management.

The narrative voice is that of an educated raconteur recounting events at second or third hand. It speaks directly and companionably to the reader, frequently using formulations such as “I believe,” “I suppose” and “you may be sure.” These qualifications create an air of modest accuracy rather than omniscience. The narrator often pauses to explain the limits of his knowledge, compress irrelevant events or make dry comments about university customs. This produces a clubbable, conversational authority: the voice seems amused by social trivia but scrupulous about evidence. Its self-conscious references to what a “conscientious writer” should or should not include also give the storytelling a lightly performative, oral quality.

The tone depends upon a controlled tension between dry humour and grave unease. Academic habits, golfing conversation, college etiquette and professional rivalries are treated with gentle irony. Parenthetical asides and mock-apologies repeatedly soften the narration, while understated phrases make alarming events more effective. The supernatural is rarely announced in sensational language; instead, it is described as “rankly impossible” but “absolutely certain.” This combination of rational phrasing and impossible content is central to the story’s effect. Even moments of fear retain social decorum, and comic remarks about whisky, breakfast or the price of the engraving sit beside images of predation and death.

The diction is formal, precise and period-specific. It includes institutional and antiquarian vocabulary—“mezzotint,” “gazetteer,” “topographical,” “Bursar,” “MSS.,” “sculpsit”—as well as the idioms of collegiate life, such as “Hall,” “common-room,” “sport the door” and “skip.” Long Latinate words and elaborate polite constructions dominate the narration, while dialogue is more direct and idiomatic. The servant’s speech is rendered through eye dialect and dropped consonants, marking class and accent, whereas the Fellows speak in educated but relaxed conversational English. Brief Latin and literary references reinforce the cultured setting without turning the story into overt display.

Sentence structure is generally expansive and periodic. Long sentences accumulate subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications and balanced contrasts, often postponing the principal point. This creates a leisurely rhythm suited to anecdote and scholarly exposition. At moments of revelation, however, the prose becomes shorter, firmer and more visual: “There was no figure, and there was the open window.” Such syntactic tightening sharply registers each supernatural change. Dashes, semicolons and parentheses are frequent, allowing the narrator to digress while retaining control of the main line of action.

The plot is linear and investigative. It begins with contextual exposition, introduces an apparently unremarkable object, and then develops through a sequence of incremental alterations. Each new viewing of the engraving advances a silent narrative: first a distant head, then a crawling figure, an open window, and finally the figure carrying away a child. This serial progression functions like frames in an animation, allowing a static work of art to reveal a crime over time. The characters respond by observing, comparing testimony, signing statements, taking a photograph and consulting reference books. Their rational procedures do not dispel the haunting; instead, they authenticate it.

Description is selective but concrete. The manor house is rendered through architectural features, dimensions, light and composition, establishing a stable visual field against which minute changes become disturbing. The apparition is disclosed gradually and incompletely. Its black garment, white cross, hidden face, thin legs and clasped burden are more suggestive than anatomically explicit. The refusal to provide a full view preserves ambiguity and encourages the reader to imagine what the observers cannot clearly see. Moonlight, drifting clouds and windows provide recurring visual motifs, but the prose avoids lush Gothic ornament.

Dialogue serves both evidentiary and dramatic purposes. Different witnesses independently describe what they see, and their questions focus attention on discrepancies in the picture. Repetition—especially questions about figures, moonlight and windows—turns perception itself into the source of suspense. The dialogue also individualises the social milieu: Williams is anxious but methodical, Nisbet practical, Green anecdotal, and the servant instinctively horrified. Exposition is often embedded in conversation, particularly in Green’s account of the historical feud, so that the background arrives as remembered local tradition rather than as authoritative narration.

The story’s pacing moves from leisurely social observation to concentrated suspense and then back to documentary closure. Considerable space is devoted to ordinary routines before and between the picture’s changes. These delays prevent melodrama and heighten anticipation. The sentence “We may give them a respite until five o’clock” openly manipulates narrative time, reminding the reader of the storyteller’s presence while postponing the next revelation. The historical explanation comes only after the visual drama has completed itself, retrospectively converting the apparition’s movements into the reenactment of an old revenge.

Thematically, the story explores the intrusion of buried violence into respectable modern life. An apparently worthless collectible becomes a medium through which an unresolved crime repeats itself. It also plays on the limits of empirical inquiry: witnesses, photographs, signed accounts and chemical tests establish that something happened but cannot explain how. Antiquarian knowledge is therefore double-edged. Collecting, cataloguing and identifying the past promise intellectual mastery, yet they also expose the investigator to forces preserved within objects. The final explanation remains suggestive rather than complete, linking the apparition to Gawdy’s execution and the extinction of the Francis line without reducing the haunting to a fully solved mechanism.

The ending returns to institutional sobriety. The picture is placed in a museum, tested and carefully watched, but never changes again. This anticlimactic report imitates the conclusion of a case history and preserves the mystery rather than resolving it. A final sceptical academic remark supplies comic resistance, while the narrator’s disapproval keeps the reader aligned with the witnesses. Overall, the style achieves horror through restraint, social realism, scholarly specificity, gradual visual alteration and the collision between rational documentation and an inexplicable survival of the past.

The story is written as a comic Irish oral tale, presented less as polished literary narration than as a performance delivered by an expansive local storyteller. Its framing as an “Extract” from the legacy of a parish priest gives it a mock-documentary pedigree, but the narrative voice immediately replaces formal authority with colloquial intimacy. Repeated addresses such as “your honour” imply a listener within the scene of narration, while formulas including “Well,” “sure enough,” “by my sowl,” and “at all at all” reproduce the rhythm of speech, keep the story moving, and create the impression of spontaneous recollection.

The most conspicuous stylistic feature is the dense phonetic rendering of Hiberno-English pronunciation. Spellings such as “dacent,” “illigant,” “plisint,” “ould,” “throuble,” “dhropped,” “wid,” and “sperit” encode accent on the page, while elisions such as “an’,” “iv,” and “afther” sustain a rapid conversational flow. The dialect is also grammatical and idiomatic rather than merely orthographic: constructions such as “it’s Mick Hanlon that’s in it,” “the man an’ the woman beyant,” “it’s too late you are already,” and “divil a taste” give the narration a strongly local syntax. Archaic rural vocabulary—“borheen,” “gossoon,” “bosthoon,” “spalpeen,” “wake,” and “Shrovetide”—further anchors the tale in an Irish Catholic peasant community. The representation is highly theatrical and belongs to a historical tradition of literary dialect; its exaggeration can therefore feel both affectionate and caricatural.

Sentences are typically long, additive, and loosely coordinated. Clauses accumulate through repeated “an’,” “but,” “so,” and “for,” imitating a teller who continually appends qualifications, consequences, and incidental details. Paragraphs often begin with “Well,” resetting the oral rhythm before the next episode. Balanced literary description is less important than momentum: events are chained together by coincidence, misunderstanding, and escalation. Repetition is central to the cadence, particularly intensifiers such as “mighty,” “intirely,” “complate,” “sure enough,” and “tin times,” as well as recurring speech tags in the form “‘says he’” and “‘says she.’” These tags are retained even when the speaker is obvious, because they function rhythmically, like beats in a performed anecdote.

Dialogue dominates the story and carries most of its characterization. Exchanges are rapid, combative, and built around literal misunderstanding. Nell mistakes Jim for a ghost; Jim mistakes her supernatural interpretation for drunken nonsense; the priest initially accepts the profitable version of events and then panics when the supposed spirit appears. Characters speak in emphatic formulas, comic curses, blessings, invocations, and mock-solemn oaths. Sacred and profane diction mingle continually: “God rest his soul,” holy water, masses, and the sign of the cross coexist with “the divil,” “by jabers,” threats of murder, and opportunistic bargaining. This mixture gives the dialogue its irreverent energy without wholly rejecting the community’s religious worldview.

The tone is broadly farcical, genial, and deadpan. Violence, death, exposure, mistaken burial, and domestic abuse are treated as materials for grotesque comedy rather than psychological tragedy. Jim’s injuries are described through homely comparisons—his nose becomes “as big as a pittaty”—and the unidentified corpse has a face like an “over-boiled pitaty” or a pancake. Such similes are concrete, rustic, and deliberately undignified. Hyperbole is constant: roars threaten to lift roofs, blows improve by “tin times,” snow nearly freezes and smothers travellers at once, and the priest promises to subdue the strangest ghost in seven parishes. The narrator’s matter-of-fact acceptance of these excesses intensifies the humour.

Characterization is schematic and comic rather than inward or nuanced. Jim is a good-natured but unlucky husband; Nell is beautiful, violent, self-pitying, credulous, and astonishingly quick to remarry; Andy is opportunistic and timid; and the priest is worldly, avaricious, and protective of his authority. Their traits emerge through repeated actions and verbal habits, not through introspection. Even grief is dramatized as egotistical performance: Nell laments that she, rather than the supposedly dead Jim, is the truly unfortunate party. The narrator maintains amused distance from everyone, allowing no character complete moral superiority.

Structurally, the tale follows a cumulative misunderstanding plot. An initial domestic quarrel drives Jim from home; bad weather traps him away; a disfigured body is misidentified and buried; Nell remarries; Jim returns and is taken for his own ghost; and the priest’s exorcism produces the physical recognition scene. Each stage is plausible enough within the story’s comic world, yet each compounds the absurdity of the previous one. Delays, digressions, and apparently incidental anecdotes—such as the piper who plays at the wedding and then dies in the snow—enlarge the social world while reinforcing the narrator’s rambling manner. Suspense comes not from uncertainty about Jim’s survival, which the reader already knows, but from anticipation of the collision between his knowledge and the community’s false belief.

The ending uses comic restoration rather than strict realism. Jim is made to remarry his own wife, Andy receives a minor penance, the priest profits from both the exorcism and the wedding, and the neighbours enjoy the entertainment of a wake and two marriages. The final irony lies in communal silence: no one mentions the priest’s twelve-pound fee because he has forbidden discussion of the affair. This closes the story with an anti-clerical wink, exposing clerical greed and social hypocrisy while preserving the priest’s practical authority. Overall, the style combines dialect performance, episodic plotting, grotesque physical humour, religious satire, verbal repetition, and the communal perspective of folklore. Its chief effect is that of a lively fireside anecdote whose pleasure lies as much in the teller’s voice and comic timing as in the mistaken-death plot itself.

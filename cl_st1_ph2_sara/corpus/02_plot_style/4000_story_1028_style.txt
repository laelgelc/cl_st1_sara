The story adopts the form of a comic character sketch rather than a conventionally plotted narrative. It builds a social type—the inveterate bore—through a cumulative series of anecdotes, habits, conversational routines, and exaggerated claims. Each episode is largely self-contained, but all repeat the same underlying pattern: an ordinary topic prompts the bore to seize control of the conversation, magnify his own importance, and overwhelm his listeners with irrelevant detail. The structure is therefore episodic and accretive, moving through travel, politics, public events, illness, privileged information, argument, and social affinity before ending with a neat personal joke at the narrator’s expense.

The narrative voice is first-person plural, repeatedly referring to “our bore” and presenting the speaker as the representative of a weary social community. This inclusive “we” creates intimacy with the reader while also turning observation into mock collective testimony. The narrator’s tone is urbane, conversational, and dryly satirical. He usually maintains a surface of courtesy—calling the bore good-hearted, distinguished, or well informed—while allowing the context to reverse the apparent praise. This sustained mock politeness is central to the humour: irritation is expressed through ceremonious understatement rather than direct abuse.

Characterisation is external and performative. The bore is not explored psychologically so much as reconstructed from his speech, mannerisms, repetitions, and effects on other people. His fixed smile, unvarying voice, false modesty, compulsive precision, and inability to perceive others’ discomfort make him instantly recognisable. He is also treated as both an individual and a specimen of a larger “bore family,” giving the sketch the air of comic social taxonomy. Secondary figures are lightly caricatured through expressive names such as Callow, Moon, Clatter, Snugglewood, and Jilkins; they function less as independent characters than as components in the bore’s inexhaustible store of anecdotes.

The prose alternates between compact aphoristic observations and long, winding imitations of the bore’s monologues. Short sentences often deliver satirical judgments with epigrammatic force: he “may put fifty people out of temper, but he keeps his own.” By contrast, the reported anecdotes expand through qualifications, corrections, parentheses, digressions, and redundant particulars. Sentences repeatedly interrupt themselves—“the second—no, the third—stay—yes, the third turning”—to reproduce the pedantry and self-dramatisation of speech in real time. The syntax thus performs the very tediousness being mocked: it makes the reader experience, in controlled comic form, the verbal entrapment suffered by the bore’s listeners.

The diction mixes polished literary narration with colloquial address, fashionable clichés, bureaucratic formulas, travel talk, medical terminology, and fragments of French and Italian. Foreign phrases are conspicuously capitalised and immediately translated, parodying the traveller who displays cosmopolitan knowledge while assuming his audience needs instruction. Recurrent forms of address such as “sir,” “you understand,” and “perhaps you know” simulate direct conversation and give the anecdotes an intrusive, buttonholing quality. Stock patriotic phrases and received opinions reveal a mind composed largely of social commonplaces.

Repetition is the principal rhetorical engine. The anaphoric phrase “Our bore” opens many sections and gives the piece the rhythm of an inventory or mock natural history. Recurring constructions—“Our bore has travelled,” “Our bore is great in secret information,” “Our bore is also great in argument”—continually announce new varieties of the same vice. Repeated corrections of times, directions, diagnoses, and circumstances parody a fetish for accuracy that adds no useful meaning. Lists and catalogues intensify the comic excess, especially when scenery, symptoms, political subjects, or conversational objections are piled up until the hearer seems figuratively buried beneath them.

The humour relies heavily on irony, hyperbole, anticlimax, and free indirect mimicry. The bore’s experiences are always unique, definitive, or historically significant: he discovers the finest unknown picture in Italy, an incomparable Swiss valley, the precise moment before Parliament burns, and voyages that are invariably the best or worst ever known. These claims grow absurd through accumulation rather than explicit refutation. Hyperbolic descriptions of their social effects—men are paralysed, strangled, enervated, or beaten out of confidence—translate conversational discomfort into mock physical violence. Anticlimax punctures grandeur, as when an apparently heroic Alpine discovery culminates in six weeks of illness, or an elaborate medical history ends with mutton chops and sherry.

Dialogue is stylised for comic rhythm rather than strict realism. The bore reconstructs conversations theatrically, preserving exact words, gestures, translations, and dramatic pauses, even when such precision is implausible. Questions and answers are frequently embedded in narration so that the reader is cast as the trapped interlocutor: “You DON’T know that statue?” This second-person address is important to the story’s immediacy. It converts description into a simulated social encounter and makes the bore’s pressure upon his audience directly felt.

The pacing is deliberately paradoxical. At the macro level, the sketch moves briskly from one example to another; within each example, however, the pace slows under the weight of corrections and circumstantial detail. That alternation prevents the piece from becoming as monotonous as its subject while preserving an impression of suffocating prolixity. Escalation also controls the pacing: each new section demonstrates a broader or more extravagant form of self-importance, from travel reminiscence to historical coincidence, medical martyrdom, political omniscience, and mysterious private knowledge.

The dominant satirical targets are conversational egotism, social vanity, false expertise, name-dropping, patriotic cliché, tourism, medical fashion, and the desire to turn coincidence into personal distinction. The bore’s defining flaw is not malice but a total absence of imaginative sympathy: he cannot recognise that other people have experiences, knowledge, time, or conversational rights of their own. His apparent modesty only strengthens his self-importance, since he attributes authority to eminent acquaintances, his Eastern brother, physicians, guides, artists, and public figures while ensuring that every story remains centred on himself.

The ending supplies a compact reversal. After observing that bores instinctively find and praise one another as exceptionally intelligent, the narrator notes that “our bore” has never paid the narrating group that compliment. The final line converts an apparent slight into reassurance: not being admired by a bore becomes proof that one is not part of his species. This closing stroke typifies the story’s style as a whole—socially polished, structurally cumulative, verbally exuberant, and sharpened by understated irony.

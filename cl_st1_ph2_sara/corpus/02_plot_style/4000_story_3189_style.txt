The story adopts the manner of a mock historical chronicle, presenting an absurd folktale with exaggerated scholarly seriousness. Its first-person narrator claims to be a scrupulous, impartial historian, invoking manuscripts, inscriptions, authorities, derivations, and archival evidence. This pose is deliberately undermined by the fanciful subject matter, comically unverifiable claims, and invented scholars and treatises. The result is sustained deadpan irony: the narrator’s formal confidence makes the borough’s irrational customs and impossible events appear all the more ridiculous.

The narrative voice is intrusive, theatrical, and openly conscious of its audience. It repeatedly addresses “the reader,” announces what will be explained later, comments on the adequacy of its own descriptions, and uses exclamations and rhetorical questions to direct emotional response. Although the narrator initially affects restraint and objectivity, his language grows increasingly partisan. The foreign intruder is called a “rascal,” “scoundrel,” “villain,” and “nincompoop,” while the conclusion becomes an explicit appeal to restore the old order. This shift exposes the supposed historian as emotionally aligned with the borough’s values and prejudices.

The diction combines ornate nineteenth-century prose with comic colloquialism. Elevated phrases such as “rigid impartiality,” “diligent collation of authorities,” and “remotest period of antiquity” sit beside homely references to cabbages, pigs, pipes, shoe buckles, and sauerkraut. Mock-scholarly Latin, pseudo-German names, bibliographical minutiae, French dance terms, and phonetic dialect all add to the linguistic density. The invented proper names are especially playful, relying on heavy consonants, reduplication, and grotesque sound patterns to suggest Dutch or German speech while functioning as verbal caricature.

Sentence structure is generally elaborate and cumulative. Long periodic sentences gather qualifications, parenthetical remarks, lists, and subordinate clauses before reaching their main point. Dashes are frequent and create comic pauses, abrupt corrections, emphatic afterthoughts, and changes of direction. Semicolons and colons support catalogues and formal exposition, while exclamation marks become more common as order collapses. Short sentences are reserved for emphatic reversals, especially the clock’s numbered strokes and the shocked response to “Thirteen!” The movement from expansive description to clipped call-and-response heightens the climactic disruption.

Repetition is the principal organising device. The borough consists of sixty identical houses; each garden has the same path, sundial, and twenty-four cabbages; each household contains nearly identical people, furnishings, foods, animals, and watches. Clocks and cabbages recur as visual motifs, civic emblems, and comic obsessions. Parallel phrasing reinforces the inhabitants’ mechanical conformity: they sit alike, dress alike, smoke alike, and repeat the clock’s strokes in unison. Even the council’s resolutions reduce civic philosophy to resistance to change, hostility toward the outside world, and devotion to “clocks and cabbages.” This patterned repetition gives the prose a clockwork rhythm while satirising social uniformity.

Description is highly visual, concrete, and grotesquely miniaturised. The setting resembles a toy village or animated clock: circular, enclosed, symmetrical, brightly coloured, and populated by tiny, round figures. Physical traits are exaggerated into caricature—large eyes, enormous chins, swollen stomachs, oversized buckles, and absurdly long coat-tails. Colour is used lavishly and incongruously, with orange clothing, green stockings, pink shoes, purple ribbons, red bricks, and black trim. Precise measurements and quantities lend counterfeit realism to an obviously artificial world.

The humour depends on escalation and incongruity. Minor details are treated as matters of immense civic importance, while genuine violence and supernatural disturbance are rendered as slapstick. The belfry-man’s beating becomes a percussion performance; animals with watches tied to their tails add to the chaos; clocks dance and pendulums writhe; overcooked cabbage and extinguished pipes are lamented as catastrophes. Lists lengthen as the disorder spreads, and polysyndeton—particularly strings of “and”—creates a breathless sense of accumulating noise and motion.

Dialogue is sparse until the climax, where it becomes rhythmic, communal, and phonetic. The numerical exchange between the great bell and the smaller watches resembles a ritual antiphony. Dialect spellings such as “von,” “doo,” “dvelf,” and “dirteen” produce broad comic characterisation and make the citizens sound as uniform in speech as they are in behaviour. Their simultaneous cries further erase individuality, turning the population into a chorus. Onomatopoeic and sound-oriented language—ticking, striking, puffing, scraping, squeaking, screeching, and caterwauling—makes the final scene acoustically crowded.

Structurally, the story proceeds from static order to explosive disorder. Most of the narrative patiently constructs the borough as an enclosed, changeless system. The stranger then crosses the forbidden hills shortly before noon, and the countdown creates temporal suspense. His irregular dancing, untimely music, and thirteenth bell stroke attack the community’s central principle: exact temporal coordination. The conclusion does not resolve the disturbance but converts the tale into a mock public appeal, preserving the narrator’s pseudo-documentary frame.

The dominant tone is whimsical, satirical, and burlesque, with an undercurrent of menace. The borough is picturesque but also narrow, complacent, xenophobic, and absurdly resistant to alteration. The intruder appears sinister, yet he also embodies spontaneity, asymmetry, music, and disorder in opposition to mechanical routine. The style therefore supports a satire of provincialism, unquestioned tradition, bureaucratic dignity, and obsessive regularity. Its distinctive effect comes from narrating cartoonish fantasy in the solemn language of scholarship, history, and civic emergency.

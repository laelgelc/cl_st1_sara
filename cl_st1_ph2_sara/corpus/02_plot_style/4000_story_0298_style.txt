The story combines social realism with lyrical symbolism and psychological intensity. Its world is materially harsh and precisely observed: cold water filmed with ice, patched clothes, soot-stained walls, vodka, hunger, illness, cramped rooms and institutional punishment establish an atmosphere of poverty and humiliation. Against this drab, coercive setting, the wax angel appears as an object of almost supernatural beauty. The central stylistic movement is therefore contrastive, passing from grime, violence and bitter comedy into visionary tenderness, then ending in quiet devastation when the angel melts.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, but it stays especially close to Sashka’s perceptions before widening to include his father’s memories and emotions. Free indirect colouring allows the prose to adopt the boy’s resentful, combative view of adults and privilege without wholly surrendering the narrator’s compassionate distance. Character psychology is conveyed through bodily habits and involuntary gestures—Sashka’s grimaces, clenched teeth, harsh voice, crouching posture and unaccustomed tears—rather than through abstract diagnosis. The father’s inward life is treated more explicitly and lyrically, his response to the angel opening into recollections of lost love, dignity and possibility. Father and son remain emotionally separate even at their moment of communion, which gives the scene a restrained pathos rather than easy sentimentality.

The prose often uses long, syntactically layered sentences, cumulative clauses and parallel constructions. Repetition gives emotional pressure to key passages: Sashka’s endless prospect of school and punishment, his whispered “Dear—dear little angel,” and the recurring descriptions of the angel’s trembling wings all create a ritual, incantatory effect. Elevated formulations—“the injustice and sorrow of human life,” “a breath of human happiness,” “a soft divine radiance”—coexist with concrete domestic detail and rough colloquial speech. The slightly formal, sometimes archaic English diction, including expressions such as “was fain,” “withal” and “whence,” reflects the character of an older translation and contributes to the tale’s solemn, literary cadence.

Dialogue is terse, socially marked and strongly individualized. Sashka speaks in insults, blunt refusals and defensive bravado; his mother’s language is repetitive, abusive and fuelled by resentment; his father whispers, hesitates and resorts to weak persuasion; the wealthy adults use polite, didactic formulas that conceal indifference or condescension. Child speech is represented phonetically in Kolya’s “’oo” and “dood,” emphasizing his sheltered innocence and sharpening the class contrast with Sashka. Comic dialogue and caricature prevent the opening from becoming uniformly tragic, but the humour is abrasive and often rooted in cruelty, embarrassment or social mismatch.

Imagery is dominated by oppositions of light and darkness, warmth and cold, purity and contamination, flight and entrapment. Artificial lights repeatedly define emotional spaces: the reddish street lamp, the dazzling Christmas tree, the smoky cooking lamp and the grey-blue dawn. The rich household’s tree casts a “strong, shadowless light” that Sashka initially experiences as hostile and insolent, while the angel hides in darker branches and possesses a softer, inward radiance. Cold signifies deprivation and chronic suffering, especially through the father’s incessant shivering, whereas heat is dangerously ambivalent: the stove offers bodily comfort but destroys the cherished angel. Flight imagery makes the ornament seem alive and spiritually free, yet its wax body remains vulnerable to the material world.

The angel functions as the story’s governing symbol. It embodies beauty, compassion, lost innocence, spiritual recognition and the possibility of connection across otherwise unbridgeable isolation. Its expression is deliberately described as beyond language and rational definition, making aesthetic intuition more powerful than doctrine or social morality. Sashka’s response reveals tenderness beneath his cultivated savagery, while his father sees in the figure the purified world and lost woman from which he feels exiled. Their shared contemplation temporarily collapses the distance between them, but the symbolism remains tragic rather than redemptive: beauty can illuminate damaged lives, yet it cannot survive their conditions.

The narrative is arranged in three clear movements. The first establishes Sashka’s rebellious character, family history and social circumstances; the second places him amid the affluent Christmas celebration and stages his encounter with the angel; the third creates a hushed domestic tableau of father-son communion before delivering the final reversal. Pacing slows dramatically around the angel. Earlier events are summarized briskly or handled through rapid exchanges, whereas perception becomes prolonged, repetitive and almost suspended once Sashka sees the ornament. The ending then returns to detached physical observation, describing the melting wax, the cockroach and the approaching workday with chilling precision.

Irony is pervasive but controlled. Sashka’s “liberty” after expulsion merely exposes him to hunger; the respectable benefactors’ charity carries humiliation; the mother grows physically stronger as she becomes morally and emotionally more destructive; and the stove that preserves the sick father’s life destroys the object that briefly restores his spirit. The hostess advises Sashka to kneel only before God, unaware that his desperate abasement is directed toward an object carrying precisely the sacred meaning absent from her conventional piety. The final cockroach crawling over the melted remains reduces the transcendent vision to matter without entirely cancelling its emotional truth.

Overall, the style is compassionate, tragic and unsentimental, with flashes of grotesque humour and melodramatic intensity. It treats aggression as the defensive language of a wounded child and places private longing against entrenched class inequality, domestic violence and social failure. Its most distinctive effect comes from joining exact realist detail to concentrated symbolic imagery: the angel’s brief radiance does not transform the world, but exposes both the depth of the characters’ deprivation and their still-living capacity for love, beauty and mutual recognition.

The story adopts the style of an early eighteenth-century documentary account, presenting a supernatural event not primarily as imaginative fiction but as a “matter of fact” supported by testimony, reputation, chronology, and corroborating evidence. Its governing voice is sober, earnest, and argumentative. The first-person narrator stands largely outside the central incident and acts as an investigator or guarantor, repeatedly stressing personal acquaintance with Mrs. Bargrave, the credit of witnesses, and the consistency of her report. Direct assertions such as “I can avouch,” “I asked,” and “I am as well satisfied” create an impression of firsthand verification. This posture lends the narrative the character of a deposition, pamphlet, or case history and deliberately counters the reader’s likely scepticism.

The diction combines plain domestic language with legal, theological, and evidentiary vocabulary. Words and phrases such as “authority,” “reputation,” “matter of fact,” “avouched,” “demonstration of truth,” “undoubted credit,” and “confute that pretence” frame belief as a question to be decided through rational inquiry. At the same time, terms including “Providence,” “afflictions,” “edification,” “future happiness,” and “God’s favor” establish a devout Protestant moral atmosphere. The supernatural is therefore interpreted through both empirical and religious modes: the apparition is validated by circumstantial proof, while her message is understood as spiritual consolation and moral instruction. Archaic expressions—“hath,” “spake,” “methinks,” “insomuch,” “twelvemonth,” and “waived it”—reinforce the period character, as do formal modes of address and recurrent honorifics.

The prose is expansive and cumulative. Sentences are often long, loosely coordinated, and filled with qualifications, subordinate clauses, parenthetical explanations, and chains of circumstantial detail. Frequent conjunctions such as “and,” “but,” “for,” “though,” and “so” reproduce the movement of oral testimony, in which one recollection prompts another. Digressions and delayed additions are openly acknowledged in phrases like “I should have told you before” and “one material thing more,” giving the narrative an apparently unpolished spontaneity. This mild disorder functions rhetorically: instead of seeming artistically contrived, the account appears to grow naturally from memory and inquiry.

The narration repeatedly uses exact particulars to manufacture credibility. Names, kinship relations, books, sums of money, garments, locations, dates, clock times, and lengths of conversations are carefully recorded. The apparition arrives on 8 September 1705 at noon, leaves at a specified quarter-hour, wears a newly made scoured-silk riding habit, and refers to a purse of gold, rings, and two broad pieces. Such mundane details anchor the marvellous event in an ordinary social world. Especially important are details that can supposedly be verified independently, such as the secretly scoured gown, the allowance from Mr. Bretton, the servant who overhears conversation, and the relatives arriving as Mrs. Veal dies. Repetition of this evidence creates a cumulative case rather than a single dramatic revelation.

Dialogue occupies much of the central episode and is rendered in a conversational, formulaic manner through repeated tags such as “says Mrs. Veal” and “says Mrs. Bargrave.” The persistent use of the historical present in these tags gives the reconstructed exchange immediacy, even when the surrounding narration uses past tense. The dialogue also individualizes the women modestly: Mrs. Bargrave sounds practical, hesitant, and courteous, while Mrs. Veal speaks with unusual earnestness and spiritual elevation. Her repeated gestures—drawing a hand across her eyes, refusing to lower her head, clapping her hand on her knee—serve both as physical characterization and as clues retrospectively interpreted in support of her ghostly identity.

The story’s structure moves from authentication to background, then to the apparition itself, its discovery as an apparition, and an extended defence against objections. The opening anticipates controversy by affirming Mrs. Bargrave’s character and identifying hostile detractors. A retrospective account of the women’s friendship supplies emotional and religious context. The long central visitation unfolds in almost real time through conversation and small domestic actions, while the later sections resemble cross-examination: alternative explanations are raised, disputed, and answered one by one. The conclusion broadens the case into an epistemological argument that inexplicable events should not be rejected merely because their causes cannot be demonstrated.

The tone remains controlled despite the sensational subject. There is little Gothic terror, atmospheric darkness, or overt suspense. The apparition comes at midday, behaves like a familiar visitor, discusses books and clothing, and departs through a busy market street. Fear is displaced by intimacy and decorum. This daylight domestic setting makes the encounter stranger while allowing the narrator to insist that Mrs. Bargrave was neither frightened nor deluded. The emotional high point lies not in horror but in reconciliation, consolation, and the promise that earthly suffering will be rewarded.

Characterization is largely reputational rather than psychological. Individuals are defined through piety, sobriety, cheerfulness, social standing, and credibility. Mrs. Bargrave’s composure under marital abuse is repeatedly offered as evidence of mental stability and moral worth. Mrs. Veal is characterized through past hardship, religious reading, remorse over neglected friendship, and concern for her friend. Mr. Veal functions chiefly as an opposing voice whose attempts to suppress the story are treated as suspicious. Social rank also matters: “gentlemen,” “persons of quality,” neighbours, servants, and reputable witnesses collectively form a public tribunal before which the event is assessed.

A notable stylistic tension exists between professed factual plainness and careful persuasive design. The narrator claims merely to transmit testimony, yet selects and interprets each circumstance so as to guide the reader toward belief. Objections are included, but generally in forms that can be rebutted; hostile witnesses are undermined by attacks on their reputation, whereas supporting witnesses are described as credible and disinterested. The narrator’s accumulation of qualifications—Mrs. Bargrave gains no money, never varies her account, speaks modestly, and wishes to avoid publicity—constructs sincerity as the central proof.

Overall, the style blends spiritual exemplum, journalistic report, legal testimony, and realistic domestic narrative. Its defining features are testimonial authority, circumstantial abundance, long accretive syntax, direct conversational reconstruction, pious reflection, and sustained apologetic reasoning. The result is a deliberately credible supernatural tale in which ordinary detail and rational argument are used to make an extraordinary event appear socially, morally, and empirically persuasive.

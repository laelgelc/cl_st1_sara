The story adopts a formal, late-nineteenth-century literary style that combines military realism, supernatural mystery, and sardonic irony. Its diction is elevated and occasionally antiquarian, using precise terms such as “caisson,” “cannoneers,” “allocution,” and “immemorial,” together with technical references to surveying, medicine, warfare, and acoustic phenomena. Concrete descriptions of terrain, weather, troop movements, and bodily details give the narrative an air of factual authority, even while the events become increasingly uncanny. The vocabulary frequently shifts between plain physical observation and abstract reflection, allowing ordinary details—a moonlit road, a hand passed through the hair, a pool of water—to acquire ominous significance.

The narration is third-person and largely aligned with the disoriented protagonist, but it remains intellectually detached from him. The narrator sometimes enters his thoughts directly, yet also stands outside them to comment with dry superiority, as in phrases such as “the man of whom we write” and “the hero of this tale.” This distance creates dramatic irony: the reader is encouraged to suspect that the protagonist’s interpretation of events is unreliable before he understands it himself. Occasional generalisations about human behaviour and references to history or professional knowledge further establish an authoritative, essay-like narrative voice.

Sentence structure is varied but often elaborate. Long, carefully balanced sentences accumulate visual and contextual detail through subordinate clauses, parallel constructions, and semicolon-linked observations. These are interrupted by short declarations, exclamations, and fragments at moments of fear or revelation. Repetition is used rhythmically, especially in the description of the spectral army: “another regiment,” “another and another,” and the recurring movement from obscurity into obscurity create the sense of an endless, mechanical procession. Dashes and colons frequently introduce qualifications, reversals, or sudden clarifications, reflecting both the protagonist’s unstable reasoning and the narrator’s ironic control.

The atmosphere is established through contrasts between visibility and silence, familiarity and estrangement, life and death. Moonlight, mist, haze, shadows, and dawn make the landscape simultaneously distinct and uncertain. The troops can be seen but not heard; the protagonist hears his own voice but finds it unfamiliar; cultivated fields appear peaceful despite their history of violence. This sensory disjunction drives the uncanny effect. Silence is not merely an absence of sound but an active pressure, while the gradual return of daylight fails to produce intellectual illumination. Images of obscurity therefore operate both literally and psychologically.

The story’s structure is tightly controlled and retrospective in effect. It is divided into three numbered sections with mock-instructional titles. These headings—presenting a supernatural encounter as a “welcome,” recommending a physician after one has “lost” one’s life, and warning against looking into water—supply a darkly comic commentary on the action. Each section advances the protagonist through a new stage of recognition: first military and spatial confusion, then social and temporal confusion, and finally bodily recognition. Information is withheld so that the final revelation reorganises everything that precedes it. Repeated gestures, especially the protagonist’s examination of his hand after touching his head, function as clues whose meaning becomes clear only later.

Dialogue is formal, courteous, and adversarial. The exchange with the physician proceeds through evasions, professional scrutiny, restrained insults, and misunderstandings. The doctor’s measured curiosity contrasts with the lieutenant’s urgency and growing impatience, generating black comedy while prolonging the mystery. Speech also reveals historical and social distance: characters use titles, elaborate politeness, and indirect formulations even when frightened or suspicious. The protagonist’s private exclamations are more immediate, exposing the anxiety beneath his military self-command.

The pacing is gradual and suspenseful. The opening dwells on landscape and the silent procession, creating a dreamlike stillness. The medical encounter slows the narrative through questioning and delayed answers, while the final section accelerates through a chain of discoveries: fatigue, aged hands, a weathered face, the date on the monument, and the reflected image. The climax is brief and violent, ending almost immediately after recognition. This abrupt closure preserves the shock and avoids explanatory aftermath.

The dominant tone is grave, eerie, and fatalistic, but it is repeatedly edged with intellectual mockery. The narrator treats confusion, injury, aging, and death with an almost clinical composure. Learned allusions and historical examples deepen the apparent realism while also delaying the supernatural explanation. The result is a characteristic blend of documentary precision and metaphysical horror: the impossible is narrated as though it were a case history, and the final irony is that self-knowledge arrives only when it becomes fatal.

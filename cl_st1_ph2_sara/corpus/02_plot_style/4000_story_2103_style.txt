The story is written as an extended political or social allegory in the form of a nautical fable. Its surface world is concrete and self-contained—a harbour, an old brig, captains, crews, anchors and charts—but nearly every major element carries a wider institutional meaning. Stagnation is represented by ships that remain at anchor, reform by sailing, entrenched authority by the skipper and steersman, and public passivity by a crew habituated to inaction. The allegory is lucid rather than cryptic: the maritime fiction remains vivid in its own right, while words such as “initiative,” “evolution,” “juvenile agitators,” and “logic of events” expose the political vocabulary being mocked.

The narrative voice is omniscient, assured and apparently traditional, with the cadence of a storyteller recounting an instructive old tale. It opens with the formulaic “Once there lay,” establishing a fable-like distance, and frequently uses broad moral observations such as “Men are not so good as they ought to be.” The narrator’s composure is central to the humour. Absurd conduct is reported in a sober, reasonable tone, while ironic qualifications quietly undermine the characters: rumours unfavourable to the skipper are “most probably a lie,” his habitual incompetence is presented as experience, and the brig’s lack of achievement becomes evidence of excellent seamanship. The narrator often adopts the authorities’ assumptions just long enough to reveal their ridiculousness.

The dominant tone is dry, genial and satirical, gradually darkening into bitterness. Early humour comes from understatement, disproportion and mock deference. The skipper is “no common man” because he painted a picture and could sing; the steersman’s monosyllabic “H’m” is treated as profound; and the leaders’ drinking and card-playing coexist with claims of vigilance and expertise. Later, the comedy becomes more openly farcical when the captain turns seasick, the officers mistake a wave for a collision, and an ordinary word is received by the crew as a mysterious weapon. The ending withdraws much of the geniality: institutional paralysis has become permanent, and the final phrase, “there she lies to this day,” gives the satire the finality of a cautionary legend.

The prose uses moderately long, balanced sentences, often built from coordinated clauses and antitheses. Phrases such as “without special damage and without special merit” and “now in storm, now in calm” create an orderly, proverbial rhythm. Paragraphs are generally compact and advance the narrative in clear stages. Transitions such as “Nevertheless,” “Therefore,” “But now,” and “As stated” lend the account a formal, almost argumentative structure. This reasoned syntax contrasts with the irrationality of events and helps parody official explanations. Occasional inversions and older constructions—“There now arose,” “none knew where,” “what think you”—give the language a nineteenth-century, translated-European flavour.

The diction combines elevated narration, seafaring terminology and colloquial speech. Nautical vocabulary—“forecastle,” “tiller,” “companion,” “port bow,” “under canvas,” “luffed up,” “islets and skerries”—supplies technical credibility and sustains the governing metaphor. Against it stand domestic and comic details such as toddy kettles, whisky, hymn books and card games. The juxtaposition reduces supposedly grand leadership to a cosy, self-protective routine. Abstract political language appears chiefly in the mouths of authority and is treated as pompous camouflage. “Initiative,” “evolution,” and “logic of events” sound impressive but are detached from practical competence.

Dialogue is sharply differentiated by status and temperament. The young sailor speaks plainly and directly, asking for action in an almost naïvely civil fashion. The skipper answers with abusive rhetoric, suspicion and self-justification. The steersman alternates between a cryptic grunt and torrents of “longest and hardest words,” making him a caricature of the adviser whose prestige depends on opacity. The cabin-boy’s literal misunderstanding of “initiative” turns jargon into broad comedy, while the crew’s repeated exclamation of the word creates a brief comic chorus. Speech therefore functions not only as characterization but as a test of honesty: practical characters use simple language, whereas evasive leaders use inflated abstractions.

Characterization is caricatural but carefully controlled. The skipper is vain, thin-skinned, ceremonious and physically unsuited to command. His changing facial colours, habitual appeal to the steersman and retreats below deck make his cowardice visible. The steersman embodies fossilized expertise: he is “glued to the tiller,” distrusts new equipment and mistakes long service for competence. The crew is characterized collectively rather than individually. Its members move from comfort and obedience to hope, amusement, division and resignation, illustrating how passivity and hierarchy sustain bad leadership. The rash young man and cabin-boy provide limited individual counterpoints, representing candour, curiosity and irreverence.

Imagery is concrete, comic and frequently personifying. The brig is “chubby as a cherub,” seems to be “in a good humour,” and raises more foam than necessary. The large wave approaches deliberately, “lie[s] down and take[s] aim,” then slaps the ship on the cheek. Such animation gives the brief voyage exuberance and makes the officers’ terror appear still more feeble. Visual incongruity is also important: the little craft has oversized rigging, painted gunports and a fashionable figurehead attached to an old-fashioned hull. Its mismatched construction acts as a physical emblem of an institution modernized only cosmetically.

The plot is linear and shaped as a progressive demonstration. It moves from general harbour-wide stagnation to the isolation of one remaining ship, then narrows further to its leaders, crew and failed attempt at departure. Exposition is leisurely and cumulative, establishing ingrained habits before the young sailor’s request creates conflict. The mysterious preparations around “initiative” build comic suspense. The actual voyage is rapid, energetic and sensory, but lasts only until the first substantial wave. The captain’s subsequent speech serves as the satirical climax: failure is reframed as vindication, responsibility is shifted downward, and retreat is presented as prudence. The short coda then shows the lasting social damage.

Overall, the style depends on sustained allegory, mock-serious narration, ironic praise, technical nautical detail, caricature and the exposure of euphemistic language. Its most characteristic method is to let authority condemn itself: the narrator rarely needs direct denunciation because the skipper’s own explanations reveal his vanity, incompetence and bad faith. The result is accessible and comic on the sentence level, but increasingly severe in implication, presenting inertia not merely as laziness but as a self-perpetuating system of fear, rhetoric and displaced blame.

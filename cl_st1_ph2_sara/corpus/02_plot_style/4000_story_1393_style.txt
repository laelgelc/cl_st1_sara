The story is written as a restrained Edwardian-style supernatural tale, combining domestic social comedy with Gothic unease and a final ghost-story revelation. Its opening is deliberately understated—“It really was most unfortunate”—and establishes a tone of dry irony. An apparently mundane household difficulty gradually becomes the mechanism that sends an isolated, nervous governess across a threatening nocturnal landscape. The supernatural is introduced obliquely rather than announced, so that ordinary inconveniences, class assumptions and local superstition slowly give way to genuine horror.

The narration uses a controlled third-person perspective centred chiefly on Miss Craig, while retaining the freedom to make sardonic observations about other characters. Mrs. Workington Bancroft is characterised through ironic narrative commentary, especially in the remark that she allows inferiors “the privilege of offering the services which it was her right to command.” This poised, socially observant voice exposes hierarchy and selfishness without overt moralising. Miss Craig’s subordinate status is similarly conveyed through circumstance and understatement: she is expected to accept an exhausting errand, and even the supposedly protective dog abandons her after discovering that she is “only the governess.”

The diction is formal, precise and distinctly period-inflected. Terms such as “jaunting-car,” “governess,” “servants’ hall,” “glebe land,” “road metal” and “shooting butts” locate the story within a rural British social world of landed households, clergy, servants and farms. The prose generally avoids extravagant Gothic vocabulary. Instead, fear emerges through exact physical details: thin rain obliterating landmarks, an empty moor road, startled grouse, a rough stone trough, a clasp knife and the distant lights of a house. This concreteness gives the supernatural ending credibility.

Sentence rhythm shifts with the emotional movement of the story. The opening uses short declarative paragraphs—“There remained Miss Craig”—to create brisk comic efficiency and to mimic Mrs. Bancroft’s process of eliminating possible messengers. Longer, more elaborately balanced sentences appear in the clergyman’s embedded narrative, particularly in descriptions of weather, landscape and spiritual depression. At moments of danger, the syntax contracts again into brief statements and rapidly sequenced sensations. The description of the assault is especially compressed and impressionistic, moving from the blow to the “flash of lightning” down the spine and the gentle, distant patter that accompanies death.

Dialogue is central to characterisation. Mrs. Bancroft’s speech is ceremoniously polite but coercive, filled with phrases such as “perhaps,” “you would not mind” and “I’d never dream of asking,” which disguise commands as requests. The cook speaks in colloquial, superstitious idiom, providing both comic relief and foreshadowing. The clergyman’s language is educated, reflective and sermon-like, while the tramp’s abbreviated vernacular—“guv’ner,” “Give us a copper”—marks class difference and introduces a harsher social register. These contrasting voices make social position immediately audible.

Structurally, the story follows a frame-and-tale pattern. The domestic opening supplies the errand; the journey builds isolation; the encounter with the clergyman leads into a substantial story within the story; and the conclusion retrospectively transforms that embedded account into a confession by a ghost. The nested narrative also delays the revelation by drawing attention toward the tramp as the apparent source of danger. Small clues are planted fairly: the clergyman speaks of an experience eleven years earlier as the “turning point” in his life, describes his own death with impossible certainty, and guides Miss Craig to the very place where it occurred. His disappearance, followed by the clock striking nine—the precise time associated with the murder—provides the final confirmation.

The atmosphere depends on gradual escalation rather than shock. Humour surrounds the governess’s attempt to imagine “the bark of a ghost,” but the joke also keeps the supernatural motif active. The moor becomes increasingly liminal: landmarks disappear, companions successively leave, and darkness reduces the world to sound, movement and isolated lights. Repetition contributes to the unease, most notably the grouse’s cry of “Go back, go back, go back,” which functions as both naturalistic detail and supernatural warning.

The clergyman’s narrative broadens the story from simple fear into theological and existential anxiety. His meditation on Job, Satan and the chessboard imagines human beings as expendable pieces in a cosmic conflict. The murderer appears to emerge from this “ocean of infinite darkness,” linking ordinary social misery with metaphysical evil. Yet the account of death is unexpectedly serene: pain dissolves into light, happiness and “twinkling lamps.” This mixture of terror, pity and transcendence complicates the ghost’s role. He does not threaten Miss Craig but escorts and reassures her, making the apparition protective even as its revelation terrifies her.

Overall, the style is economical, ironic and atmospherically precise. It contrasts polite social cruelty with supernatural kindness, comedy with dread, and material hardship with spiritual mystery. The final sentences are abrupt and visual, ending not with explanation but with flight, chattering teeth, a solid domestic shadow and the decisive stroke of nine.

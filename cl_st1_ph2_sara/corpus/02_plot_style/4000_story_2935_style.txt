The story adopts the manner of a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century psychological and moral tale. Its prose is formal, elevated, and deliberately analytical, combining melodramatic subject matter with the language of reason, ethics, and quasi-scientific inquiry. Words such as “holographic,” “perpetuation,” “manifestly,” “inalienable,” and “sensibilities” give the narration an educated, period-specific register, while repeated references to chivalry, manhood, feminine frailty, social honor, and public disgrace reflect the gender assumptions and moral conventions of its era.

The narrator is omniscient and strongly intrusive. Rather than merely reporting Randolph’s actions, the voice interprets his character, reconstructs his reasoning, and occasionally addresses the reader directly through phrases such as “Let us try to understand” and “Let us see.” This creates the atmosphere of a guided case study. The narrator generally remains close to Randolph’s consciousness, and the boundary between external narration, free indirect discourse, and interior monologue is often fluid. Rhetorical questions, exclamations, and abrupt self-corrections reproduce the movement of his mind while still allowing the narrator to frame that movement as an ethical investigation.

Characterization is primarily psychological rather than dramatic. There is almost no dialogue, and the wife and Stockton never appear directly; they exist as figures inside Randolph’s imagination. Randolph is introduced through an extensive catalogue of admirable qualities—“educated, refined, sensitive, generous, and brave”—so that his contemplated violence can be presented as a crisis of identity and civilization. His changing decisions reveal him more fully than physical action does. The story repeatedly follows the pattern of proposition, examination, revision, and replacement: he plans three deaths, spares his wife’s life, alters her punishment, forgives her publicly, reconsiders Stockton’s death, and finally engineers his own suicide as an apparent sacrifice.

The syntax is often long, cumulative, and periodic, particularly when Randolph inventories motives, consequences, or abstract moral qualities. Parallel constructions and enumerations are central to the rhythm. Lists such as “exposure, denunciation, publication, scorn, contempt, and terror” intensify emotion by accumulation, while balanced antitheses—man and brute, reason and instinct, punishment and mercy, death and dying—organize the philosophical passages. At moments of decision, the prose becomes markedly short and emphatic: “The revolver won”; “It could not be done”; “Yes; there was a way.” These compressed sentences function as structural pivots within the otherwise expansive reasoning.

Repetition is one of the story’s strongest stylistic devices. Words and formulas such as “he should die,” “like a dog,” “she was a woman,” “what good,” and “no better…than brutes” recur as Randolph tests and revises his conclusions. The repetition conveys obsession but also creates a dialectical rhythm, with each recurrence acquiring a modified meaning. Dashes, semicolons, colons, and exclamation marks further imitate a mind interrupting itself, qualifying its claims, or recoiling from its own conclusions. The punctuation is therefore both period-marked and psychologically functional.

The treatment of violence is graphic but hypothetical for most of the story. Randolph imagines the revolver’s effects in a cinematic sequence of weapon, terror, appeal, gunshot, pallor, collapse, and blood. Poison is described through clinical categories and physiological symptoms. This fusion of sensational detail with detached analysis produces a disturbing irony: his murderous frenzy initially presents itself as orderly, intelligent planning. Similar grotesque accumulation appears in his imagined future for his wife, whose degradation is projected through scandal, poverty, alcoholism, imprisonment, delirium, and death. These passages expose the vindictiveness concealed beneath his language of justice.

Structurally, the narrative is tightly compressed in time and almost entirely confined to Randolph’s office. External events are minimal: he discovers the betrayal, purchases a revolver, writes and destroys several wills and letters, and shoots himself. The real action consists of successive transformations in thought. The repeated drafting and burning of documents provides a concrete counterpart to his moral revisions, while the office clock and the anticipated dinner impose a quiet deadline. The mirror scene also serves symbolically: confronted by his distorted face, Randolph momentarily sees the physical mark of the “brute” he may become.

The story’s dominant mode is didactic and philosophical. Randolph’s personal jealousy expands into a meditation on instinct, evolution, selfishness, mercy, and the distinction between human beings and animals. The argument uses the vocabulary of natural law and hierarchical development, suggesting the influence of contemporary evolutionary thought. Humanity is defined not by freedom from animal passions but by the capacity to restrain them through self-control, compassion, charity, and reflection. The prose repeatedly turns private emotion into a generalized proposition about “man,” “animals,” or “higher mankind.”

Irony governs the ending. Randolph’s movement away from murder appears to be a moral ascent: he rejects cruelty, protects his wife’s reputation, spares Stockton, and arranges for their future happiness. Yet this apparent triumph of reason culminates in suicide. His final serenity, described through a “perfectly clear head” and a “bright and tranquil” face, makes the act seem sacrificial and controlled, but also reveals that his reasoning has never challenged the premise that he himself must die. The story therefore leaves an ambiguity between noble self-renunciation and pathological rationalization. Its characteristic style lies in this tension between moral uplift and psychological horror, between sentimental ideals of forgiveness and the cold, methodical logic through which Randolph destroys himself.

An insomniac listens as his gambler friend Barclay recounts the fate of Charlie Tavor, a brilliant but financially naïve explorer repeatedly cheated of his discoveries. After vanishing for years in Mongolia and Arabia, Tavor returns gravely ill from chewing a stimulant that kept him impervious to fatigue and heat. He knows a second fatal seizure will strike in exactly six months.

Tavor wants to spend his remaining time fulfilling his lifelong dream: living as a gentleman in an English country house with formal gardens and servants. To raise the needed money, Barclay approaches Nute Hardman, the unscrupulous man who once made a fortune by exploiting Tavor. Barclay tells Hardman that Tavor has located $300,000 in gold on a level, chalk-covered plain, reachable within several days of a coastal town, with only the final two miles presenting any difficulty.

Barclay offers Hardman an accurate map for $10,000 and stakes $20,000 in government bonds as a guarantee that every claim is true. Greedy for both the treasure and the prestige of conducting an important expedition, Hardman accepts. Tavor receives the money, realizes his dream, and dies in comfort six months later.

Hardman never recovers the treasure, yet cannot claim Barclay’s bonds because none of Barclay’s statements were false. The concealed catch is that the final two miles are vertical: the “level plain” is the Atlantic seabed, and the gold lies in the submerged hold of the Titanic.

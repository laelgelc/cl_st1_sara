The story uses an omniscient third-person narrator who moves freely between outward action, social commentary, and intimate psychological insight. The narrative voice is warm, observant, and gently ironic, often pausing to generalise about marriage, affection, fear, childhood, or human nature. These authorial intrusions create the feeling of a traditional storyteller speaking directly to the reader, while the narrator’s sympathy—especially toward Nilkanta—gives the tale strong emotional depth.

The diction is formal and literary, with a slightly old-fashioned, translated quality. Everyday domestic details such as hookahs, betel leaves, clothing, meals, bathing steps, and household boxes coexist with elevated phrases such as “the writ of Fate,” “the hot glare of publicity,” and “Mother Sleep soothed with her soft caresses.” Humorous or proverbial formulations frequently compress experience into memorable patterns: life is described as being made up of “eatings and beatings,” while Nilkanta is called “either a man too early or a boy too late.” This mixture of concrete detail, aphorism, and lyrical abstraction is central to the prose style.

Sentence structure varies according to purpose. Dialogue is brief, direct, and often combative, particularly in the playful or marital exchanges. Expository passages employ longer, balanced sentences containing qualifications, contrasts, and parallel clauses. During moments of heightened emotion or imagination, the syntax becomes more rhythmic and accumulative, as in the descriptions of river water, leaves, birds, music, jewellery, and Kiran’s idealised beauty. Repetition is used for emotional emphasis—“not a thief,” “bled and bled,” and “whining and whining”—and rhetorical questions convey confusion that the characters cannot articulate.

The tone shifts fluidly between domestic comedy, affectionate satire, lyricism, and pathos. Nilkanta’s mischief initially produces broad humour: he steals opportunities to smoke, ruins umbrellas and beds, gathers unruly boys, and helps ensure that no mango ripens. Yet these comic traits gradually acquire a darker meaning as his emotional vulnerability emerges. The narrator does not abruptly abandon humour; instead, amusement and sorrow remain intertwined, making the final tragedy feel like the natural underside of the earlier playfulness.

Characterisation is achieved through behaviour, social relationships, and psychological contrast rather than extensive physical description. Kiran is defined by vitality, impulsive kindness, and a need for amusement, but also by the unconscious carelessness of privileged affection. Sharat is practical and protective, Satish playful but suspicious, and Nilkanta is built from contradictions: child and young man, performer and servant, mischief-maker and wounded dependent, thief in action but not in his conception of himself. His emerging adolescence is portrayed indirectly through shame, jealousy, wounded dignity, and his changing response to Kiran. The story is especially attentive to feelings that cannot be named or confessed.

A major stylistic device is the contrast between external social identity and inward imaginative life. To others, Nilkanta is a troublesome, half-grown strolling player; within himself, song transports him into a realm of beauty, freedom, and heroic possibility. Nature participates in this transformation. The Ganges, sighing waves, passing boats, champak bush, rustling leaves, and calling birds create a sensuous pastoral setting that temporarily releases him from poverty and humiliation. This lyrical landscape is repeatedly set against the material world of beatings, hunger, accusation, and class hierarchy.

The plot is linear and carefully foreshadowed. It begins with Kiran’s illness and boredom, introduces Nilkanta as both refugee and entertainment, then gradually displaces him when Satish arrives. The pacing slows around Nilkanta’s inner development and accelerates toward the missing inkstand, which functions as the central symbolic object. The theft is morally ambiguous: it is an act of revenge rather than greed, but it confirms the very judgement Nilkanta cannot bear. Dramatic irony intensifies the climax because Kiran first defends him absolutely and then privately discovers the evidence.

The ending is restrained and symbolic rather than explanatory. Nilkanta’s disappearance remains unresolved, while Kiran’s act of throwing the inkstand into the river becomes a silent gesture of protection, guilt, and recognition. The abandoned dog’s hunger and repeated whining externalise the grief that the human characters cannot express. Overall, the style combines storytellerly omniscience, domestic realism, social observation, lyrical natural imagery, gentle irony, and compassionate psychological tragedy.

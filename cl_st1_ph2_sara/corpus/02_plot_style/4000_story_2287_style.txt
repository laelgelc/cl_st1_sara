The story uses a realist, omniscient narrative style that combines close psychological observation with irony, satire, and moral sympathy. The narrator moves freely between external description, historical and ecclesiastical explanation, and Father Olympus’s private thoughts. Although the narrative voice often sounds confident and detached—making broad claims about singers, actors, strong people, and public favourites—it gradually aligns itself with the deacon’s emotional and ethical awakening. This creates a double perspective: the institutional setting is viewed with comic scepticism, while the protagonist’s rebellion is treated with increasing gravity and admiration.

Characterisation is built through emphatic physical contrasts and revealing habits. Father Olympus is repeatedly described as enormous, powerful, and leonine, whereas his wife is “small, thin” and physically fragile but domestically tyrannical. His massive body, tremendous voice, gentleness, vanity, fearfulness, and emotional susceptibility form a deliberately paradoxical portrait. He is at once ridiculous and heroic: a celebrated ecclesiastical performer who worries about pitch, a compliant husband overawed by his wife, and a simple reader capable of a profound moral choice. Secondary characters are rendered more economically and often satirically, through a single physical or professional trait: the snuffling academic reader, the pedantic archbishop, the anxious choir leader, and the abusive deaconess.

The prose alternates between long, cumulative sentences and short, forceful dramatic statements. Extended sentences are especially prominent in descriptions of ritual, lists of heretics, and the mounting catalogue of curses. Their repetitions and parallel clauses imitate the ponderous momentum of liturgy and institutional authority. Against these, brief lines such as “The women had long been weeping hysterically,” “The deacon stopped suddenly,” and the deacon’s final “Well?!” produce sharp changes in pace and emotional pressure. Ellipses, broken syllables, phonetic vocal exercises, and stretched words reproduce hesitation, musical pitch, chanting, and the physical labour of singing.

Dialogue is direct, vivid, and strongly differentiated by speaker. The wife’s speech is a torrent of insults, practical fears, and colloquial abuse, while the deacon’s speech is initially hesitant and domestic, then becomes solemn, scriptural, and finally commanding. Much of his interior monologue is conversational and unsophisticated, which preserves his “elementary” nature while making his moral insight feel spontaneous rather than intellectualised. His private insults toward the choir leader also puncture the grandeur of the service and maintain the story’s comic realism.

Religious language supplies the story’s principal verbal texture. Liturgical formulas, Church titles, names of theologians and heretics, scriptural curses, and transliterated Russian chants give the narrative ceremonial density and cultural specificity. The archaism and formal rhetoric of the service contrast with mundane details such as vodka, cocaine, gargling, expectoration, tuning forks, and professional jealousy. This juxtaposition repeatedly lowers the sacred into the bodily and theatrical. The cathedral service is presented partly as worship and partly as performance, with the deacon compared to a famous baritone and his voice treated as both spiritual instrument and public spectacle.

Sound is the dominant sensory motif. The deacon’s voice vibrates through the cathedral, rattles the candelabra, governs the choir, and eventually masters the congregation. Musical terminology—“doh,” “re,” octaves, tuning forks, tones—structures both the comedy and the climax. His voice begins as an object of vanity and professional anxiety but becomes the means by which conscience is publicly expressed. The climactic substitution of “Many years” for “Anathema” is therefore not merely verbal disobedience; it transforms the story’s central physical motif into an act of moral liberation.

The imagery is bold, concrete, and often hyperbolic. Father Olympus has a chest “like the body of a motor-car,” a “lion’s roar,” swollen arteries, a mane of hair, and the monumental presence of carved stone. Comic comparisons coexist with sublime ones: townspeople stare at him as they stare into the mouth of a brass instrument, but at the climax he sounds like an archangel blowing a silver trumpet. These enlarging images track his movement from grotesque public celebrity to majestic dissenter. The recurrent candle imagery also carries symbolic weight. In the quoted Tolstoy passage, moths are rescued from a destructive flame; in the ritual, the deacon refuses to invert the candle in condemnation and instead raises it high, turning an emblem of the curse into one of affirmation.

Structurally, the narrative follows a steady progression from domestic comedy through ceremonial spectacle to crisis of conscience. The opening establishes the deacon’s physicality, marriage, voice, vanity, and recent reading. The cathedral sequence then slows to describe the setting and ritual in detail, allowing institutional pressure to accumulate. The order to anathematise Tolstoy triggers the decisive conflict. Alternation between the violent language of the curse and remembered passages celebrating compassion, freedom, and joy creates a contrapuntal pattern: official malediction is repeatedly answered by humane literary language. The climax arrives when those competing voices can no longer coexist and the deacon replaces condemnation with blessing. A brief aftermath converts the public rebellion into a personal resolution to abandon his office.

The tone is highly mobile. It begins in affectionate caricature, becomes satirical in its portrayal of clerical vanity and bureaucracy, darkens during the catalogue of punishments, and culminates in emotional exaltation. Even at the most solemn point, comedy remains present in the choir leader’s desperate plan to strike the deacon with a tuning fork. Conversely, the wife’s comic invective after the service reveals the material consequences of his action. This tonal mixture prevents the story from becoming either a simple religious polemic or an uncomplicated heroic legend.

The central stylistic tension is between repetition and rupture. Repeated rites, formulas, names, curses, and prescribed responses represent inherited authority and automatic obedience. Against them stand fragments of remembered fiction, private questions, sudden silences, and finally “wrong words.” Literature operates within the story as a counter-liturgy: Tolstoy’s simple, compassionate prose enters the deacon’s memory and displaces the elaborate rhetoric of hatred. The moral argument is consequently embodied in contrasting styles. Institutional cruelty speaks through prolix, archaic catalogues, while humane feeling speaks through images of moths, cradles, homes, animals, and joy.

The ending is visually monumental and emotionally decisive. After the explosive vocal climax, the prose returns to the deacon’s body: he removes his vestment, walks above the crowd, withstands his wife, and becomes “like a man carved out of stone.” His transformation is not presented as a rejection of faith; he explicitly retains belief in Christ, the Creed, and the Apostolic Church while refusing “malice.” The style therefore frames his rebellion as the recovery of an ethical core beneath institutional formalism. Overall, the story’s signature lies in its fusion of comic realism, ecclesiastical detail, theatrical sound effects, psychological immediacy, cumulative rhetoric, and a climactic shift from satire to moral grandeur.

The story adopts the manner of a nineteenth-century children’s animal tale, combining fairy-tale simplicity, natural-history observation, domestic comedy, and gentle moral reflection. Its opening formula, “Once upon a time,” immediately establishes an oral, timeless mode, but the setting is rendered with unusually concrete visual detail. Fern shadows, berries, patches of sunlight, nesting birds, and darting squirrels create an idyllic woodland miniature. Repeated phrases and cumulative lists give this description an abundant, animated quality, presenting the forest as a complete and bustling society rather than as passive scenery.

The narrator is omniscient, conversational, and openly present. The narration moves freely between human and animal perspectives, interprets motives, anticipates reactions, and addresses the audience directly through phrases such as “You may be sure,” “I grieve to say,” and “What would you do about it?” This creates the feeling of an adult storyteller speaking to children while retaining enough irony to amuse older readers. The closing questions turn the audience into participants and deliberately avoid a firm resolution, preserving the story’s genial moral ambiguity.

The diction is accessible but distinctly period-flavoured. Plain narrative language sits beside elevated words and phrases such as “forebodings,” “vicinity,” “veritable palace,” “rapacious creatures,” and “rural solitudes of Nature.” Formal constructions and long, carefully balanced sentences coexist with colloquial expressions including “sell out,” “no end of nuts,” and “say what you will.” The result is a flexible register that can move from lyrical description to mock-serious debate and homely anecdote. Intensifiers such as “very,” “greatest possible,” “wonderfully,” and “dear good” contribute to the emphatic warmth characteristic of writing for younger audiences.

Sentence structure is often expansive and cumulative. Long sentences join observations through repeated conjunctions, especially “and,” reproducing the sense of a gaze roaming through the forest or a storyteller adding one remembered detail after another. Parallel structures and anaphora are prominent, particularly in the chestnut tree’s speech: “Not a flower…,” “Not the least root…,” and “not a bird….” These patterns lend rhythmic authority to the tree’s complaint. Shorter sentences and exclamations punctuate moments of comedy or alarm, while sound-driven phrases such as “creep, creep, creep” and “whisk-frisk” quicken the action and imitate animal movement.

Personification is the central stylistic device. Rabbits, squirrels, birds, insects, moles, and even trees possess families, social classes, political opinions, newspapers, property concerns, and standards of respectability. Their behaviour remains recognisably animal, but their speech satirises human habits. The ant writes for the “Crickets’ Gazette”; the mole worries about falling real-estate prices; birds are dismissed as “flighty”; and Mrs. Squirrel subjects her husband to “curtain lectures.” This anthropomorphic world allows social comedy to emerge without becoming harsh. Much of the humour comes from applying solemn civic and domestic language to woodland creatures.

Dialogue is frequent and strongly characterising. Older animals speak with caution, prejudice, and self-importance, while the young squirrels use eager, repetitive questions. The chestnut tree’s long oration is grand, indignant, and rhetorically elaborate; Mother Squirrel’s speech is practical and maternal; Tit-bit and Frisky sound curious and impulsive. Names such as Tit-bit, Frisky, Father Gray, and Mother Red are transparent and affectionate, suited to a fable-like world in which personality is immediately legible.

The tone shifts among pastoral delight, mock gravity, tenderness, melancholy, and domestic farce. Human intrusion initially appears destructive, and the chestnut tree’s denunciation gives the story a serious ecological undertone: humanity is noisy, impatient, and capable of destroying in hours what nature has taken centuries to produce. Yet the human inhabitants prove gentle and attentive, complicating the animals’ fears. The story therefore contrasts destructive humanity with humane stewardship rather than condemning people categorically. Aunt Esther embodies sympathy across species, and her patience gradually replaces suspicion with trust.

The plot is episodic rather than tightly dramatic. It progresses from the building of the cottage, through woodland opposition and reassessment, to the squirrels’ visits, Frisky’s accidental death, Aunt Esther’s passing, and the later occupation of the house by another squirrel family. Transitions such as “after a while,” “the next day,” and “in time” create the loose chronology of an anecdote or remembered family story. The narrative’s emotional movement is cyclical: humans invade the animals’ home, animals enter the humans’ home, and coexistence produces both pleasure and inconvenience.

Sentiment is handled directly but briefly. Frisky’s drowning and Aunt Esther’s death are stated with restrained sorrow rather than dramatized at length. The euphemistic description of Aunt Esther going “to a land where the flowers never fade and the birds never die” introduces a consoling religious or paradisal note appropriate to children’s literature. These losses deepen the otherwise playful narrative without overturning its fundamentally warm outlook.

Overall, the style is intimate, picturesque, and gently didactic. It values kindness to animals, curiosity, patience, and tolerance, but communicates these values through charm and comic complication rather than an explicit concluding moral. The final unresolved problem—whether the noisy squirrels should be allowed to remain—captures the story’s characteristic balance: affection does not eliminate inconvenience, and harmonious coexistence is presented as an ongoing negotiation rather than a perfect pastoral ideal.

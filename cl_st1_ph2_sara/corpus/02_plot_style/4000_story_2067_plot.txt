Jefferson Briley, a timid mail-wagon driver who fantasizes about the dangers and heroism of the Wild West, carries the widowed Fanny Tobin on a cold seven-mile journey from Sanscrit Pond to North Kilby. As they talk about old disappointments, loneliness, family burdens, and Jefferson’s supposedly dangerous occupation, Fanny moves beside him for warmth. Their conversation becomes flirtatious, and each recognizes the practical and emotional advantages of marriage: Fanny wants a home of her own, while Jefferson wants companionship and domestic comfort. After some coy bargaining, Jefferson proposes and Fanny accepts. By journey’s end, the would-be defender of the mail has been humorously “taken on the road” not by robbers, but by matrimony.

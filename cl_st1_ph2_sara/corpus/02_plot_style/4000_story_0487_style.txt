The story uses a late-nineteenth-century adventure-tragedy style, combining realist social observation with melodramatic plotting and an emphatic moral stance. Its prose is direct, accessible and largely chronological, moving from biographical exposition through escalating persecution to a spectacular, fatal climax. The narrator is omniscient and openly judgmental, describing military “officialism” with irony, the Alpine Corps as brutal and isolated, and Jean’s persecutors as vain, cowardly bullies. This narrative voice does not strive for neutrality: it continually directs sympathy toward Jean and interprets events as the inevitable result of sustained injustice.

Characterisation is broad and archetypal. Jean is introduced through heroic physical scale—a man who “would have been a giant”—and is defined by strength, patience, good nature and homesickness. His nickname, Samson, supplies a biblical framework that makes his strength, broken bonds and final act feel legendary rather than merely realistic. The soldiers and officers are treated more collectively, as embodiments of institutional cruelty, while Jean’s smallest persecutor is reduced through the recurring metaphor of a “gnat.” Such contrasts are deliberately stark: giant against diminutive tormentor, victim against regiment, individual dignity against corrupt authority. Psychological complexity is limited, but Jean’s loneliness and mounting resentment are clearly traced until his violence appears both tragic and morally intelligible.

The diction mixes plain narrative language with heightened, sometimes ornate description. The mountain landscape is rendered through forceful visual and auditory imagery: snowfields are “dazzling,” the glacier has a “blue breast,” the river “roared and foamed and tossed,” and the avalanche develops a “low sullen roar.” Personification makes nature seem active and hostile, while repeated contrasts between Brittany and the Alps deepen Jean’s emotional displacement. The measured thunder of the sea signifies home, belonging and dignity; the mountains signify imprisonment, alienation and oppressive power. White snow, dark rocks and Jean’s black figure create a strongly pictorial composition, particularly during the final ascent.

Sentence structure varies according to narrative purpose. Expository passages often use long, balanced sentences containing explanatory clauses, geographical detail and authorial commentary. Moments of conflict shift toward shorter sentences, emphatic declarations and rapid actions: “Then Samson did an astonishing thing.” Dialogue is sparse but highly functional, exposing Jean’s accumulated anger and the officers’ arrogance. Exclamation, repetition and abrupt commands intensify the melodrama, especially in the cry of “An avalanche! An avalanche!!” The punctuation and phrasing retain period features, including formal constructions, rhetorical asides and the use of double hyphens to introduce speech.

Pacing is carefully staged. The opening establishes Jean’s origins, physical nature and homesickness at moderate length; the middle accumulates humiliations until the pail-of-water incident triggers a sudden chain of violence; the escape and mountain pursuit then slow the action again, creating suspense through detailed spatial description. The soldiers’ confidence is prolonged while the reader watches Jean discover that every conventional route is closed. His decision is initially withheld, and the avalanche’s meaning dawns on the characters only after it has begun. The contrast between the hours required for his ascent and the seconds of his descent produces the climax’s sharpest temporal effect.

The landscape also governs the plot with near-geometrical precision. The enclosed valley, single pass, raging stream, precipices and overhanging snow form a trap first for Jean and then, through his final act, for the soldiers. This reversal is central to the story’s design: the regiment believes the terrain guarantees Jean’s recapture, but he converts that same terrain into an instrument of retaliation. The avalanche functions simultaneously as natural catastrophe, revenge and symbolic judgment, overwhelming the institution that has abused him.

The dominant tone progresses from sympathetic and elegiac to indignant, suspenseful and finally grimly triumphant. Irony repeatedly undermines authority: France misassigns a gifted sailor to the mountains; the officers mock Jean’s intelligence while failing to understand his intention; and the men who have trapped him discover that they are themselves trapped. The ending combines horror with serenity. The persecutors die wearing expressions of terror, while Jean lies calm, imagined as resting beneath the waters of Brittany. This final return to maritime imagery gives his death emotional closure and transforms a violent act of revenge into a tragic homecoming.

Overall, the style is muscular, moralised and cinematic. It relies on elemental settings, sharp oppositions, cumulative injustice, physical action and a final reversal of overwhelming force. Its most distinctive qualities are the fusion of social protest with adventure narrative, the symbolic opposition between sea and mountain, and the elevation of an abused ordinary man into a doomed folkloric hero.

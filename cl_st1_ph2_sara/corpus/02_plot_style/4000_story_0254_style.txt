The story adopts the style of a traditional Christian literary fairy tale, combining folkloric simplicity, supernatural punishment, and an explicit moral trajectory from vanity through suffering to repentance and redemption. Its plot is linear and episodic, moving rapidly through decisive scenes rather than developing everyday life in detail. Repeated objects, places, and actions—the red shoes, the church door, dancing, looking, and the sound of the organ—give the narrative a ritual, almost incantatory structure. The escalation follows fairy-tale logic: an ordinary desire becomes a compulsion, the compulsion becomes a grotesque punishment, and sincere humility finally produces a miracle.

The narrator is third-person and omniscient, with direct access to Karen’s thoughts and motives. The voice initially resembles that of an oral storyteller, as signalled by “There was once,” but it is not neutral. It judges conduct openly through morally weighted words such as “proud,” “vain,” “pious,” “sin,” “mercy,” and “repent.” At times the narration adopts a childlike or naïve immediacy; at others it becomes stern and sermon-like. Brief ironic observations, such as the dead having “something better to do than to dance,” introduce a dark, unsettling wit without weakening the moral seriousness.

The diction is clear and concrete but marked by archaic and biblical phrasing. Expressions such as “thou art,” “Dance shalt thou,” and “thy skin” lend the supernatural speeches scriptural authority. The prose also shows the character of an older translation: some clauses are unusually ordered, conjunctions are frequent, and punctuation—especially semicolons—links long sequences of actions and perceptions. Much of the syntax is paratactic, repeatedly joining clauses with “and,” “but,” and “now.” This produces the forward-driving cadence of oral narrative while making Karen’s punishment feel relentless: she dances “over fields and meadows, in rain and sunshine, by night and day.”

Repetition is the dominant stylistic device. Karen repeatedly looks at the red shoes, repeatedly chooses them over religious or domestic duty, and later repeatedly approaches the church only to be confronted by them. The soldier’s refrain, “Look, what beautiful dancing shoes!”, changes from apparent compliment to curse. Likewise, “she danced” and “she was forced to dance” recur until movement itself becomes mechanical and oppressive. Parallel constructions reinforce the conflict between intention and compulsion: when Karen wishes to go one way, the shoes take her another. Repetition therefore supplies both fairy-tale musicality and psychological symbolism.

Dialogue is sparse, emphatic, and functional. Characters speak in short declarations, commands, pleas, and moral formulas rather than in naturalistic conversation. The old soldier’s enigmatic words activate the curse; the angel pronounces judgment in rhythmic imperatives; Karen cries for mercy and later confesses. Exclamation marks heighten wonder, terror, and religious emotion. Characters other than Karen are largely archetypal—the compassionate old lady, the tempter-like soldier, the executioner, the clergyman’s family, and the judging then merciful angel—so speech identifies their symbolic role more than their individual personality.

Description is selective and strongly symbolic. Colour carries particular weight: red signifies beauty, vanity, temptation, blood, and punishment; black belongs to mourning and religious propriety; white marks angelic purity; green and gold accompany forgiveness and divine grace. Light and darkness also trace Karen’s moral condition. The gloomy wood, fearful night, heath, graveyard, and glittering sword dominate the punishment sequence, while sunshine, roses, widened walls, music, and a golden star shape the final vision. Physical beauty is repeatedly contrasted with spiritual value: Karen’s face and shoes attract admiration, but her redemption occurs only when she no longer cares for dress, grandeur, or public display.

Religious imagery and church ritual structure the whole story. Confirmation, sacrament, psalms, prayer, burial, biblical reading, and organ music are not merely background details; they measure Karen’s spiritual attention. Early in the story she is physically inside the church but mentally absorbed by the shoes. At the end, when humility prevents her from entering, the church miraculously comes to her. This reversal is central to the style and moral design: external appearance and public respectability give way to inward devotion. The final image of her soul flying “on the sunshine to God” shifts the prose from gruesome corporeality to lyrical transcendence.

The tonal range is unusually broad. Sentimental compassion surrounds Karen’s poverty and orphanhood; visual splendour accompanies the princess and the shoes; grotesque horror enters when the shoes fuse to Karen’s feet and the executioner amputates them; devotional tenderness governs the conclusion. These tonal changes are abrupt rather than gradually modulated, which is characteristic of fairy tales and moral legends. Violence is described plainly and symbolically, without realistic medical detail, making it shocking yet archetypal.

Pacing accelerates through accumulation. The early passages pause over the origins and appearance of the shoes, while the curse sequence crosses fields, roads, bridges, churchyards, and nights in rapid succession. After the amputation, the pace slows to dwell on repeated failures, service, prayer, and inward change. The ending becomes expansive and visionary: the ceiling rises, the walls widen, music enters the chamber, and the narrow physical space opens into a spiritual one.

Overall, the style is memorable for its fusion of simple fairy-tale narration with severe Christian allegory. It relies on archetypal characterization, conspicuous symbolism, rhythmic repetition, archaic religious diction, stark contrasts, and escalating supernatural consequences. Its governing movement is from surface beauty to bodily horror and finally to spiritual radiance, with the red shoes serving as the concrete emblem that binds desire, vanity, punishment, and renunciation together.

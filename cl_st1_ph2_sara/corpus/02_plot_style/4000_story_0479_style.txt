The story is written as a first-person retrospective in the voice of Raymond, a rebellious sixteen-year-old boy. Its chief stylistic feature is the close alignment between narration and character: events are filtered through Raymond’s limited, defensive, colloquial perspective, while the reader is allowed to perceive emotional and moral developments that he himself cannot readily articulate. Expressions such as “darned stuff,” “you bet,” “lickety-cut,” “Sissy,” and “I dunno” establish a period-specific American boy’s idiom. His frequent insistences, denials, and corrections expose the gap between what he claims to feel and what he actually feels. Thus “Hope they won’t” is immediately undercut by “But I didn’t,” and his final faltering request reveals an attachment he has spent the story trying to conceal.

The prose is plain, brisk, and strongly conversational. Sentences in Raymond’s narration tend to be short or loosely coordinated, often joined by “and,” “but,” “so,” and “then,” reproducing the momentum of informal speech. Grammatical irregularities and slang are used deliberately to make the voice youthful rather than polished. Emotional passages become especially fragmented, with repetitions, dashes, stammering, and self-interruptions: “I’ll—I’ll get even,” or “You—you just stay here.” These hesitations function as indirect emotional disclosure. Raymond is most eloquent when describing action—riding, fishing, golf, defiance—and least articulate when confronting shame, admiration, or affection. The resulting style is comic on the surface but psychologically revealing underneath.

Dialogue dominates the story and carries most of its characterization and conflict. Raymond speaks provocatively, using mockery, insults, blunt refusals, and grammatical nonstandardisms. His father’s speech is more formal and censorious, filled with judgments about honor, breeding, and gentlemanly behavior, though the tutor’s diary later exposes the father’s hypocrisy and responsibility for the boy’s condition. Twigg’s dialogue is calm, economical, and strategically understated. He rarely issues theatrical threats; instead, he uses patience, literal-mindedness, humor, and consequences to neutralize Raymond’s resistance. Exchanges are often structured as contests in which Raymond tries to provoke anger and Twigg refuses to supply it. This contrast gives the dialogue both comic timing and moral force.

Twigg’s diary introduces a second register: detached, educated, analytical, and comparatively formal. Its vocabulary—“morally calloused,” “propensity,” “lamentable,” “ungentlemanly actions”—contrasts sharply with Raymond’s slang. The diary acts as an embedded external assessment of the narrator, supplying family history and psychological interpretation without abandoning the first-person frame. It also creates dramatic irony because Raymond reproduces judgments whose truth he only partly understands. Twigg’s later diary entry is especially wry: it rebukes Raymond’s spying through the very text Raymond has spied upon, turning the act of reading into a comic trap.

The characterization is largely behavioral rather than expository. Twigg’s strength is suggested through his handshake, shoulders, horsemanship, rowing background, and self-command; Raymond’s neglected but responsive nature emerges through his love of animals and outdoor pursuits, his susceptibility to praise, and his reluctant admiration for competence. Physical details are selective and functional. Objects such as the patent-leather shoes, briar pipe, books, fishing rods, golf clubs, horses, and diary become compact social and psychological markers. Twigg’s pipe, for example, is first an instrument in a battle of wills and later part of the familiar image through which Raymond recognizes his affection.

Structurally, the story proceeds episodically through a series of tests: the first meeting, failed lessons, fishing, confinement in the bedroom, diary reading, golf, riding, and the threatened departure. Each episode repeats the same basic opposition—Raymond resists, Twigg remains composed—but shifts the balance incrementally. Twigg gains authority not through a sudden conversion but by combining firmness with shared pleasures and genuine interest. The episodes therefore function as stages in Raymond’s education. Transitions are often compressed into retrospective summaries, allowing the narrative to cover a month quickly while dwelling on decisive confrontations.

The tone blends humor, irony, sentiment, and moral instruction. Much of the comedy comes from Raymond’s transparent self-deception and from Twigg’s quiet reversals, such as refusing to go fishing until the books are removed or responding to cigarette smoke by filling the room with his stronger pipe. The story does not explicitly preach through the narrator; instead, its ethical lesson emerges from contrasts in conduct. “Gentlemanliness” is presented less as social polish than as self-control, honesty, loyalty, respect for one’s word, and refusal to humiliate the weaker person. Twigg teaches these values by example, while Raymond’s father merely invokes them rhetorically.

The emotional arc is deliberately restrained. Raymond’s reform is incomplete, and he never offers a polished confession of gratitude or affection. His changing feelings appear through small concessions: wishing he had accompanied Twigg, studying despite himself, enjoying shared sports and books, feeling guilty after breaking his promise, wanting to attend Twigg’s college, and finally asking him to stay. The climactic scene gains force from understatement. Twigg frames his departure as an acknowledgment of failure, compelling Raymond to choose discipline voluntarily. Raymond’s damaged turf, twitching face, lump in his throat, and broken syntax externalize emotion without overt authorial explanation.

Overall, the style resembles an early twentieth-century American boys’ story or school-and-tutor tale: energetic, accessible, morally purposeful, and centered on masculine mentorship. Its period character appears in the vocabulary, social assumptions, educational references, servants and estate setting, emphasis on becoming a “gentleman,” and the idealization of sport as a means of character formation. Yet its strongest technique is the use of an unreliable but emotionally transparent youthful narrator. Raymond misunderstands much of what happens, but his very attempts to conceal vulnerability make his gradual attachment and moral awakening unmistakable.

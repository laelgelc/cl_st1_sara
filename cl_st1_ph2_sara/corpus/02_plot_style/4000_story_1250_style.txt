The story is written as a first-person war testimony, combining retrospective narration with the immediacy of a wounded soldier’s stream of consciousness. It opens with the insistent formula “I recollect,” establishing memory as both the narrative frame and a structural device. The perspective remains tightly restricted to Ivanof’s perceptions: events arrive through flashes of sight, sound, pain, confusion, and partial understanding rather than through an authoritative overview. Frequent shifts from past into present tense intensify this effect, making remembered suffering seem to recur as it is narrated.

The prose moves between rapid action and prolonged psychological suspense. During the battle, short clauses, repeated constructions, and accumulations of verbs create breathless momentum: men run, fire, fall, and cheer in quick succession. After the narrator is wounded, the pace slows drastically. Tiny physical details—grass blades, ants, dust, stars, insects, moonlight—expand to fill his entire field of awareness. Time is then measured through dawns, nights, thirst, bodily deterioration, and repeated attempts to move. This contrast between the battle’s confused speed and the aftermath’s agonising duration is central to the story’s design.

The syntax is often cumulative and repetitive, reproducing obsessive thought under extreme distress. Rhetorical questions, exclamations, self-corrections, fragments, and ellipses give the narration an unstable, inward rhythm: “Why did I kill him?” and “Wherein was he to blame?” are not requests for answers so much as expressions of moral shock. Repeated words such as “sleep,” “thirst,” “death,” “agony,” and “recollect” convey exhaustion and fixation. The narrator frequently argues with himself, reverses decisions, or qualifies his own perceptions, so the prose follows the mind’s movement rather than a smooth chronological account.

The diction has a distinctly nineteenth-century, translated literary quality. Forms such as “thou,” “thee,” “wherein,” “four and twenty hours,” and “it were better” coexist with concrete military vocabulary, place names, measurements, and references to weapons and equipment. This elevated or archaic register gives the moral reflections a solemn tone, while practical details—canteens, rifles, uniforms, stretcher relays, wounds, and distances—anchor the narrative in physical reality. Occasional awkward constructions and tense inconsistencies reinforce the impression of an older translation rather than polished modern English.

Sensory description is exceptionally prominent. Sound dominates the battle scenes through humming bullets, musketry, cheers, groans, hoofbeats, clanking sabres, and shouted commands. Once the narrator is isolated, silence becomes equally expressive, broken only by insects, wind, imagined whispers, and distant voices. Visual imagery repeatedly contrasts natural beauty with human destruction: the black-blue sky, moonlight, stars, mist, clouds, and sunrise remain lovely or indifferent beside blood, wounds, decay, and death. Heat, thirst, pain, and the smell of decomposition make the later passages intensely corporeal and increasingly grotesque.

The dead Turkish soldier functions as both a physical presence and the story’s central moral emblem. At first he is an enemy killed instinctively in combat; later he becomes the narrator’s “neighbor,” “preserver,” and imagined counterpart. The shift in naming reflects the collapse of national and military distinctions. Through speculation about the Turk’s mother, coercion, journey, and fear, the narrator reconstructs his humanity and recognises that both men were driven into violence by forces beyond their control. The corpse’s progressive decomposition is described with uncompromising naturalism, culminating in the explicit declaration that the grinning skeleton “is war.” Symbolism therefore emerges directly from material horror rather than from abstract allegory.

The tone develops from stunned recollection through terror, despair, guilt, bitterness, and anti-war reflection. Heroic language is repeatedly undermined by bodily helplessness and institutional anonymity. The narrator imagines his fate reduced to a newspaper phrase about “insignificant” losses or “one private,” revealing the disparity between public accounts of war and individual suffering. References to patriotism and heroism are treated with sceptical irony: the volunteer once regarded himself as acting nobly, but his experience reduces the supposed “exploit” to killing a frightened stranger and lying beside the result.

Dialogue is sparse but sharply differentiated from the introspective narration. Military commands are stretched typographically—“Fo-o-r-wa-ard!” and “Ha-a-alt!”—to reproduce their audible cadence and impersonal authority. By contrast, the narrator’s cries for help are weak, broken, or unheard. When rescue finally comes, colloquial exchanges with the corporal, hospital officer, and doctor abruptly restore a social world. Their practical, reassuring language also introduces grim understatement: the loss of a leg is called “a mere trifle” because death has been avoided.

Structurally, the story forms a survival narrative framed as a written deposition. It proceeds broadly chronologically after an initially fragmented recollection, but hallucinations, memories, moral meditations, and repeated lapses into unconsciousness interrupt linear movement. Recurring motifs—the injured dog, the mother and bride, the canteen, the corpse, and the counting of days—bind these interruptions together. The final sentence reveals that the narrator has survived to record the ordeal, retrospectively explaining the oscillation between remembered past and relived present.

Overall, the style combines psychological realism, battlefield naturalism, grotesque bodily detail, lyrical nature writing, and explicit moral inquiry. Its principal effect comes from narrowing war to one damaged consciousness and one decomposing body. Rather than presenting combat as spectacle, it renders it as confusion, prolonged pain, accidental survival, and belated recognition of shared humanity.

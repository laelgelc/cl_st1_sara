The story uses a close third-person perspective that remains tightly aligned with Reggie’s consciousness. The narration repeatedly slips into free indirect discourse, allowing his anxious, self-deprecating thoughts to colour the prose without formal attribution. Exclamations such as “Here he was!”, “Good heavens!” and “Dash it all” sound simultaneously like narration and private speech. This creates intimacy while preserving enough narrative distance for gentle irony: Reggie experiences his proposal as an almost tragic ordeal, while the reader also sees the social awkwardness, melodrama and comic incongruity of his behaviour.

The tone blends romantic pathos with comedy of manners. Reggie’s emotional pain is treated sympathetically, but his earnestness is continually undercut by small humiliations: the apparently green hair, the violently ringing bell, the check tie, the intimidating gardening scissors and his fervent claim to like doves. The comedy is affectionate rather than cruel. Even Anne’s laughter is presented as involuntary and mysterious, so the rejection remains emotionally complicated rather than simply callous. This tonal instability—moving from amusement to tenderness, embarrassment and hurt—is central to the story’s effect.

The diction is colloquial, class-marked and distinctly period-specific. Reggie’s idiom includes expressions such as “top-hole,” “dashed,” “mater,” “chap,” “governor,” “fearfully” and “cutting off.” These establish an upper-middle-class or minor-gentry English social world while revealing his boyishness and emotional limitations. His language relies on euphemism, understatement and unfinished formulations; he often cannot state what he feels directly until the proposal forces him to do so. Anne’s speech is softer and apparently more assured, but it too becomes fragmented when emotion threatens her composure. Social politeness therefore acts both as a shared code and as an obstacle to honest communication.

The syntax imitates the movement of thought. Long, accumulating sentences reproduce Reggie’s obsessive weighing of disadvantages, especially through repeated constructions such as “in spite of.” These are interspersed with fragments, abrupt questions, exclamations and ellipses that register hesitation and panic. Dashes are especially prominent, marking interruptions, qualifications, emotional recoil and thoughts that cannot be completed. Repetition intensifies feeling—“I love her, I love her,” “bowing and bowing,” “Roo-coo-coo-coo”—while also creating comic and symbolic patterns. The prose often advances by association rather than formal exposition, moving fluidly from physical action to memory, fantasy and imagined dialogue.

Characterisation is indirect and highly economical. Reggie is revealed through gestures—adjusting his tie, pressing against the chest of drawers, crushing out a cigarette, waving away his distress—as much as through explicit description. His lack of confidence appears in his bodily awkwardness and habitual self-belittling. His romantic imagination is cinematic and conventional: he pictures himself protecting Anne on a journey and imagines her ideal husband as a handsome theatrical stranger. Anne is characterised through laughter, small physical movements, shifting expressions and contradictions between what she says and what she does. Her wish to reject Reggie while keeping him emotionally unharmed exposes a mixture of affection, vanity, innocence and possessiveness.

Dialogue is naturalistic but carefully patterned around evasion and failed understanding. Characters interrupt themselves, repeat phrases, change subjects and speak past one another. Reggie interprets Anne’s refusal as final, whereas Anne cannot accept the emotional consequences of giving it. Her insistence that he must become “just as happy as” before is both naïve and controlling. The closing summons, “Come back, Mr. Dove,” converts the earlier metaphor into direct address and shows that the relationship she rejects in theory is the very pattern she recreates in practice.

Sensory description is vivid, selective and often filtered through emotion. The post-rain landscape is rendered through gleaming roads, briar-scented hedges, glowing hollyhocks, falling drops, petals and pollen. Interiors are similarly composed from a few resonant objects: a parasol on the piano, a green ashtray, a pearl ring catching flame. Colour is prominent—blue and cream, gold-brown, green, pink, wet blue—and lends the prose a delicate, impressionistic quality. These visual details are not static decoration; they flare into notice at moments of heightened consciousness. Immediately after Reggie asks his question, for example, the garden seems to “open to the light,” then fades with Anne’s answer.

Sound is equally important. Snipping scissors, the over-loud bell, Anne’s pealing laughter and the doves’ repeated cooing punctuate the narrative. These sounds frequently externalise Reggie’s unease or interrupt attempts at solemnity. Onomatopoeic effects such as “Snip,” “Ping!” and “Roo-coo-coo-coo” give the prose a playful, almost theatrical immediacy. Silence and pauses are also charged: the brief interval after the proposal expands into intense visual perception, slowing time at the decisive moment.

The story’s imagery depends on ironic correspondences between people, animals and objects. Reggie’s tie makes him resemble a cat in a picture; the mother’s Pekinese dogs appear to judge him; and the courting doves become the governing symbol. Mrs Dove runs ahead laughing while Mr Dove follows, endlessly bowing. Anne initially presents this as an image of the marriage she must avoid, yet her final call makes Reggie enact the same pattern. The symbol is therefore both comic and painful, encapsulating unequal affection, pursuit, submission and repetition.

The structure is compact and scene-driven. It begins with anticipatory self-scrutiny, moves through the comic obstacle of Reggie’s mother, opens into a bright transitional walk, and then concentrates almost entirely on the encounter with Anne. Pacing alternates between summary and sharply dramatised moments. Reggie’s history and prospects are compressed into rapid exposition, while the proposal and rejection unfold slowly through pauses, gestures and repeated attempts to leave. The ending is circular rather than conclusive: Reggie returns across the lawn, suggesting that the emotional pattern will continue despite the explicit refusal.

Overall, the style is psychologically intimate, impressionistic and delicately ironic. Its distinctive method is to place intense private feeling inside an outwardly trivial social episode, using colloquial free indirect narration, sensory precision, symbolic repetition and comic interruption. The result is a bittersweet study of mismatched love in which tenderness and ridicule remain inseparable, and in which the characters’ gestures reveal more than their explanations.

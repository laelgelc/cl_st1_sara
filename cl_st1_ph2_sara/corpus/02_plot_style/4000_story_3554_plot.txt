Three parables illustrate how societies cling to harmful practices and attack anyone who questions them.

In the first, tenants repeatedly mow a weed despite their wise master’s warning that mowing only spreads it and that it must be uprooted. The practice becomes a sacred tradition, and when a man recalls the original advice, he is accused of wanting weeds to flourish. This parallels the narrator’s advocacy of Christ’s teaching that evil should be resisted through reason, love, and goodness rather than violence; critics misrepresent him as opposing all resistance to evil.

In the second, merchants adulterate food for profit. A knowledgeable housekeeper exposes the fraud and demands wholesome replacements, but the merchants convince the public that she wants to destroy the food supply, and the crowd attacks her. The narrator compares this to his criticism of contemporary art and science, much of which he regards as counterfeit or spiritually harmful. Instead of addressing his arguments, intellectual authorities accuse him of wanting to abolish art and science.

In the third, lost travelers split into groups: one continues stubbornly in the same direction, while another rushes about trying different routes. A lone man advises everyone to stop, determine their position, and choose a course by reliable signs, but both groups ridicule him as cowardly and idle. The narrator applies this to society’s handling of labor conflict, militarism, and widespread suffering: rather than pause to reconsider its direction according to enduring moral truths, humanity intensifies its misguided activity and condemns anyone who urges reflection.

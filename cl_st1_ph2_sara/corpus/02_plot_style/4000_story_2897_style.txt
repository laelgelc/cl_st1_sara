The story adopts the form of a mock-ethnographic maritime sketch rather than a conventionally plotted narrative. A first-person seafaring narrator presents his “memoranda” as practical information about the “‘Gees,” moving from supposed racial history and physical description to employment practices, methods of selection, anecdotes, and closing advice. This catalogue-like organization mimics a natural-history entry, sailors’ handbook, or travel account. Its apparent purpose is explanatory, but the accumulating exaggerations turn the account into grotesque social satire.

The narrative voice is learned, conversational, and complacently authoritative. It combines a sailor’s familiarity with the pretensions of an amateur philosopher, repeatedly treating rumor, prejudice, and casual observation as if they constituted scientific evidence. Direct addresses to readers, explanatory asides, definitions, and phrases such as “upon a scientific view” create an air of expertise that the absurd reasoning continually undermines. The narrator’s confidence is central to the comedy: he rarely recognizes the cruelty or illogic of his own claims, so much of the irony arises from the distance between his self-presentation as an informed observer and the dehumanizing assumptions embedded in his account.

The diction mixes nautical vernacular, antiquated formal English, pseudo-scientific terminology, classical and biblical allusion, commercial language, and comic colloquialism. Maritime expressions such as “green hands,” “fore and aft,” “hung fire,” and “taking in of sail” establish an authentic seafaring milieu. These sit beside Latinate phrases such as “caput mortum,” elevated formulations such as “primitive word” and “relative intensity,” and references to Fox, Belshazzar, Salamanca, Dartmouth, Hercules, and Apollo Belvedere. The contrast between high rhetoric and degrading subject matter produces a pedantic, burlesque effect. Repetition of the clipped term “‘Gee,” especially in compounds such as “green ‘Gee,” “knowing ‘Gee,” and “‘Gee jockey,” turns language itself into an instrument of classification and contempt.

The syntax is predominantly expansive and nineteenth-century in character. Long, clause-heavy sentences unfold through qualifications, parentheses, semicolons, dashes, and cumulative lists. The prose often begins with a straightforward proposition and then elaborates it into an increasingly elaborate analogy or comic logical chain. Periodic constructions delay the main point, while parenthetical interruptions imitate the digressive movement of oral storytelling. Short declarative sentences—“He comes for biscuit,” for example—are inserted for emphasis and comic timing. Exclamations, rhetorical questions, quoted commands, and stretched spellings such as “Ge-e-e-e-e!” add performative energy and reproduce the voices of sailors.

Figurative language is abundant and deliberately incongruous. Human beings are compared to animals, commodities, tools, foodstuffs, and specimens: the “‘Gee” is inspected like a horse, discussed in terms of “bone and bottom,” loaded aboard like cargo, and evaluated for labor efficiency. Extended analogies are developed with mock precision, as when “‘Gee” is said to stand to “Portugee” as attar of roses does to rosewater. Aphoristic antitheses—“a great appetite, but little imagination; a large eyeball, but small insight”—compress prejudice into polished verbal patterns. Puns and semantic reversals are frequent, including the volcano that “threw up business altogether,” wages redefined as “cuffs and buffets,” and the idea that exploitable workers receive the human title “men” only when extra labor is required.

The humor is dry, grotesque, hyperbolic, and often disturbing. Anecdotes escalate beyond plausibility, such as a captain taking an armed party to surprise a prospective sailor in his home or an incapacitated man being carried around the world for three years as unusable “lumber.” Comic understatement intensifies the brutality: violence and exploitation are reported as ordinary administrative inconveniences. The prose repeatedly deploys false balance, pretending to qualify prejudice while reinforcing it, and frames abuse through the language of efficiency, prudence, and market value.

Characterization is typological rather than psychologically individualized. The “‘Gee” is constructed as a collective category, while captains and sailors function as representatives of maritime commerce and racial hierarchy. Captain Hosea Kean is the closest thing to an individual comic character, but even he is primarily an embodiment of calculating, suspicious expertise. Dialogue is sparse and usually serves to dramatize commands, insults, or occupational attitudes rather than intimate interaction.

The dominant tonal blend is geniality, mock seriousness, and savage irony. On the surface, the narrator sounds amused, informative, and practical; beneath that surface, the story exposes a labor system in which racial prejudice permits sailors to be underpaid, beaten, objectified, and denied full human status. The satire remains deliberately unstable because the narrator reproduces racist caricature in extensive sensory and bodily detail rather than clearly repudiating it. The result is a voice that may be read as indicting sailors’ bigotry, pseudo-scientific racial classification, and capitalist exploitation, while also forcing the reader to confront how readily comic rhetoric can participate in the dehumanization it depicts.

Overall, the style is best described as digressive maritime burlesque: encyclopedic in shape, pseudo-scholarly in method, anecdotal in movement, and rhetorically ornate. Its signature effects come from the collision of elevated diction with coarse prejudice, elaborate logic with absurd premises, and comic geniality with institutional cruelty.

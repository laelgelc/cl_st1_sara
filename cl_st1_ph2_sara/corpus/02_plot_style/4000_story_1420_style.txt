The story adopts the form of a nineteenth-century personal essay or character sketch, narrated in the first person by an educated, self-conscious observer. The narrator addresses the reader directly and presents his account as a sober investigation, repeatedly insisting on its truthfulness even as the subject grows increasingly fantastical. This mock-documentary posture creates dramatic irony: the reader quickly understands that Monsieur du Miroir is the narrator’s reflection, while the narrator continues to discuss him as an autonomous and mysterious gentleman. The sustained pretence supplies both the story’s humour and its philosophical reach.

The narrative voice is urbane, ceremonious, and playfully pedantic. It combines the manner of a gentlemanly memoirist with that of an amateur philosopher or investigator of human nature. Formal assurances, qualifications, and appeals to the public lend absurd gravity to commonplace observations about mirrors. The narrator is perceptive but comically prone to overinterpretation, converting ordinary optical effects into questions of genealogy, supernatural mobility, personal loyalty, and metaphysical identity. His unreliability is therefore rhetorical rather than factual: he accurately records what he sees but deliberately frames it through an impossible personification.

The prose is highly periodic and syntactically elaborate. Long sentences accumulate subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, balanced phrases, and extended sequences of examples before arriving at their principal point. Semicolons, dashes, and rhetorical interruptions reproduce a mind continually refining its claims. This expansive syntax is punctuated by short exclamations and questions—“Is he really dumb?” and “Ha! What is yonder?”—which heighten comic surprise or Gothic apprehension. Parallel constructions and antitheses are frequent, especially in contrasts between appearance and substance, self and image, youth and age, visibility and spiritual reality.

The diction is elevated, Latinate, and slightly archaic, with words such as “aug​​ht,” “wherefore,” “betokening,” “veritable,” and “pertinacity.” French honorifics and references to genealogy, classical mythology, Don Quixote, witchcraft, philosophy, and theology give the narration a learned cosmopolitan surface. This refinement is repeatedly brought into comic contact with colloquial or mundane details: toothache, old coats, mud puddles, warming-pans, swollen cheeks, and hardware-store kettles. Puns and idiomatic twists are central to the style, particularly those built around “reflection,” “liquor,” “outward show,” and the reflection’s inability to offer tangible help or physical contact.

The governing device is an extended conceit in which a mirror image becomes a lifelong companion. The conceit is developed with remarkable literal consistency. Reversed gestures become evasiveness; imitation becomes sartorial dependence; appearances in reflective objects become magical transportation; reflections in water become amphibious habits; silence becomes dumbness; intangibility becomes social slipperiness; and the image’s disappearance at death becomes a question of fidelity and extinction. Each episode discovers another comic implication of the same premise, producing an episodic structure held together by conceptual variation rather than external plot.

The pacing depends on delayed disclosure and tonal escalation. The story begins as a whimsical catalogue of eccentricities, gradually introduces supernatural language, and finally turns toward mortality, disappointment, conscience, and the limits of human knowledge. Transitional passages explicitly prepare the reader for “stranger things,” parodying the suspense mechanisms of Gothic fiction. Yet the central mystery is never truly concealed. Suspense comes less from discovering Monsieur du Miroir’s identity than from seeing how far the narrator can extend the fiction and what moral significance he can derive from it.

Tonally, the piece moves between affectionate satire, fanciful comedy, melancholy, and metaphysical awe. Early passages treat the reflection as a ridiculous social acquaintance and expose the narrator’s vanity. Later passages contemplate ageing through thinning hair, wrinkles, sunken eyes, and the “tranquil gloom of a disappointed soul.” In the closing movement, the mirror becomes a figure for self-knowledge, memory, conscience, fate, and humanity’s tendency to seek transcendent answers in projections of its own reason. The tonal shifts are gradual enough that the comic conceit can bear genuine emotional weight without losing its irony.

Imagery is predominantly visual, as suits the subject, and repeatedly invokes surfaces, light, shadow, water, glass, polished metal, and duplicated forms. Reflections appear in mirrors, eyes, brass objects, puddles, wells, lakes, and lamplight. The imagery broadens from domestic and urban settings to Niagara, the Nile, northern lights, Alpine spectres, and the spiritual world, enlarging a familiar optical phenomenon into a cosmic emblem. Light and darkness carry moral and existential implications: youthful brightness gives way to age, disappointment, death, and “the dark realm of nothingness.”

The story’s humour arises from personification, disproportion, and double meaning. A passive reflection is credited with vanity, loyalty, mockery, cowardice, occult powers, and intrusive intimacy. The narrator’s grandiose reasoning is consistently undercut by the reader’s simpler explanation, while understated punch lines close many paragraphs. The final sentence offers the sharpest example: Monsieur du Miroir’s “whole business is reflection,” yet it remains doubtful whether reflection has made him wise. This epigram compresses the story’s central irony—the distinction between merely looking at oneself and achieving genuine self-understanding.

Overall, the style is ornate, discursive, allegorical, and self-reflexive. It fuses the familiar essay, comic character portrait, Gothic fantasy, and moral meditation. Its signature effect comes from sustaining a light absurdity until it opens into serious questions about identity, vanity, ageing, conscience, spiritual perception, and the inadequacy of human reason before ultimate mysteries.

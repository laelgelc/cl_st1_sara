The story adopts the manner of an early twentieth-century social comedy, combining domestic realism, romantic imagery and persistent irony. Its setting is sharply localized in the industrial Five Towns, where smoke, factories, provincial society and newly acquired wealth coexist with aspirations toward elegance and romance. Contrasts drive the style: stars hang above industrial fires, a luxurious boudoir overlooks factories and golf links, and a fashionable Christmas gathering becomes enthralled by a working-class fortune-teller. Material details—clothing, furnishings, food, architecture and commercial products—give the narrative a solid social texture while exposing distinctions of class and taste.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, but it frequently moves into the attitudes of particular characters, especially Vera and Stephen. The narrator is conspicuously present, addressing the reader through asides, parenthetical comments and mock-confidential observations such as “I doubt” or “I regret to have to say it.” This creates an intimate, conversational relationship with the audience. The voice pretends to offer sober explanation while quietly undermining the characters, often presenting absurd behaviour in the language of reasoned analysis. Foreshadowing is similarly playful: apparently incidental details are given comic emphasis because the narrator knows how they will later matter.

The dominant tone is affectionate satire rather than cruelty. Characters are mocked for vanity, superstition, self-interest and marital gamesmanship, yet they remain lively and sympathetic. Vera’s “bird-like soul,” Stephen’s emphatic practicality and Charlie’s self-conscious romanticism are rendered through compact, humorous character sketches. Social pretension is a major target: the guests reject belief in fortune-telling while eagerly submitting to it, and the humble tea-leaf reader gains authority precisely because everyone wishes to be entertained and frightened. The repeated insistence that no one believes—“Oh no!”—produces comic irony by making the opposite obvious.

The prose alternates between richly descriptive sentences and brisk comic statements. Longer passages often accumulate details through parallel clauses, lists and repeated constructions, as in the opening sequence of “there was” phrases or the catalogue of fashionable subjects unknown to the fortune-teller. These inventories create rhythm while defining class and milieu. They are punctuated by short verdicts—“Bosh!”, “No romance in Stephen!”, “No. Stephen could not understand it.”—which sharpen the timing and give the narration a theatrical snap. Repetition is used extensively for comic escalation: the news “stopped” one activity after another, people “went thither” and “came back,” and Vera answers each objection to the balcony in a progressively more dismissive fashion.

The diction moves flexibly between elevated, romantic phrasing and colloquial bluntness. Phrases such as “clear dark velvet sky,” “soft splendour” and “the wonders of the night” evoke sentimental romance, but they are undercut by practical references to golf links, manufacturing profits and architectural inconvenience. Mock-heroic language enlarges trivial domestic matters: Vera is a “sweet enemy,” disagreement becomes a “pitched battle,” and Stephen’s survival appears to stand between “England and anarchy.” This inflation of ordinary married life is central to the humour. Occasional archaic or biblical allusions, notably “the witch of Endor,” lend comic grandeur to a provincial party entertainment.

Dialogue is concise, idiomatic and strongly characterizing. Stephen speaks in curt, practical dismissals; Vera uses enthusiasm, emotional pressure and sudden contradiction; Charlie contributes amused romantic suggestions. The exchanges resemble stage comedy, with quick reversals and carefully placed punch lines. The fortune-teller’s Staffordshire dialect is reproduced selectively and followed by a translation, marking both regional authenticity and the social distance between her and the guests. Elsewhere, the dialogue remains accessible and lightly period-specific, with expressions such as “What fun!”, “all rot” and “one never knows.”

The story’s four-part structure is tightly engineered around setup, complication, revelation and reversal. The balcony is introduced as a seemingly minor desire, displaced by the fortune-telling episode, then reintroduced after Vera’s fear has transformed her conduct. Her exaggerated solicitude creates a sustained comic mystery until the prophecy is disclosed. Stephen’s revelation that he has supposedly been promised a life of ninety years resolves one conflict but destroys his tactical advantage, allowing the balcony dispute to return with greater force. The final sentence functions as a dry epilogue and punch line: Vera has already begun planting trees, so her victory is implied through action rather than explicitly narrated.

Overall, the style is urbane, observant and mischievously intrusive. It treats marriage as a contest of temperament and tactics, provincial society as both ridiculous and endearing, and superstition as a force people deny while allowing it to govern them. Its signature effect comes from the collision of romance with practicality, elevated narration with trivial concerns, and apparent logic with emotionally motivated absurdity.

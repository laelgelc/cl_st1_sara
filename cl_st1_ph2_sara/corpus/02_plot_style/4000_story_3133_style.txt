The story adopts a late-Victorian or Edwardian sentimental-gothic style, combining an affectionate portrait of childhood with a restrained supernatural tale. Its language is polished, decorous, and deliberately old-fashioned, with elevated expressions such as “not prepossessed,” “pellucid eyes,” “materialistic gaze,” and “prodigal in their expenditures.” Archaic exclamations and constructions—most notably “Alack!” and “what was, was!”—reinforce the period character. This formality is softened by intimate diminutives such as “little godchild,” “tiny boys,” “wee path,” and “dear child,” which create a tender, almost devotional tone.

The narration is retrospective and first-person, delivered by an unmarried older woman who is self-deprecating, educated, affectionate, and emotionally guarded. She refers to herself as a “foolish old creature” and an “old maid,” while her retreat into Egyptian history and the Ptolemies functions as a dryly comic defence against grief. Her voice balances wistful sentiment with mild irony, allowing strong emotion to emerge without becoming wholly uncontrolled. Although she presents herself as rational and worldly, she is receptive to wonder; this tension between scepticism and belief gives the supernatural events their credibility.

Elsbeth is characterised through idealising, almost sacred imagery. Her physical imperfections are introduced first, then transformed by sustained observation into signs of charm and spiritual distinction. She is repeatedly associated with light, fragrance, crystal, beauty, wisdom, and secret knowledge. Rather than being psychologically complex in a realist sense, she functions as an emblem of imaginative childhood and as a “medium” through which the narrator perceives what is loveliest in life. The child’s fairy lore foreshadows her later intimacy with the “Unknown,” linking imaginative play, death, and the supernatural.

The prose alternates between long, flowing descriptive sentences and short, emphatic statements. The longer sentences accumulate sensory detail—dew, perfumes, green shadow, birdsong, breaking waves—and produce an enchanted, immersive rhythm. Short sentences and fragments are reserved for emotional pressure: “Two is very little!” “Alack!” “Only the empty rooms.” Repetition is especially important. Phrases such as “two stockings and two piles of toys,” “only,” “farewell,” and “very still” turn grief into a rhythmic refrain. Exclamation marks, dashes, and rhetorical questions heighten the oral, emotionally responsive quality of the narration.

Dialogue is simple, musical, and strongly differentiated by age. Elsbeth’s speech is imaginative and ceremonious, while the boys speak with innocent certainty and later with childlike contractions and nonstandard grammar: “cryin’,” “hadn’t no stockin’,” and “jus’ went out.” Their testimony is persuasive precisely because it is artless. Repeated questions and answers during the fairy scene create a collaborative act of belief: the children and narrator effectively imagine the fairies into visibility. This pattern anticipates the ghostly episodes, in which belief again depends less on proof than on emotional recognition.

The story’s structure is carefully symmetrical. It begins with Elsbeth alive in an enchanted woodland “place,” moves abruptly through her death into the mournful Christmas visitation, and concludes with a second Christmas in which the narrator consciously provides for the absent child. The fairy wand, fairy horns, and “lost path to Arcady” in the first half are echoed by the vanished toys, the autoharp, and the child’s distant laughter in the second. These correspondences transform the opening fantasy into supernatural foreshadowing. The plot proceeds from playful enchantment to bereavement, then to consolation and release.

Nature description is lush, sensuous, and personifying. A tulip tree “flaunted” its leaves, a snake runs “coquettishly,” and a squirrel strokes its whiskers with a “complaisant air.” Colours and textures—red hats, green garments, velvet grass, purple shadow, soft brown skin—give the world a fairy-tale vividness. By contrast, the Christmas house is characterised by darkness, silence, emptiness, and feeble candlelight. This opposition between the abundant woodland and the vacant domestic interior externalises the movement from living imagination to grief.

The supernatural is handled through ambiguity and restraint rather than horror. The ghost is delicate, sorrowful, and recognisably childlike, not frightening. Its appearance is reported second-hand through the boys, while the disappearance of the toys and the remote sound of the autoharp provide quieter corroboration. The simile “It went out as a candle goes out” is characteristic of the story’s method: simple, visual, and poignant. The final explanation—“Elsbeth was always an obedient little thing”—closes the haunting with affectionate understatement, treating death as a continuation of the child’s personality rather than a source of terror.

Overall, the style is lyrical, nostalgic, gently humorous, and openly elegiac. It idealises childhood as a state of privileged perception and presents imagination as a bridge between the ordinary world and an unseen one. Its strongest stylistic signatures are the intimate retrospective narrator, ornate yet accessible period diction, sensuous natural imagery, emotionally charged repetition, child-centred dialogue, sentimental contrasts between fullness and absence, and a supernatural conclusion whose tenderness outweighs its gothic unease.

An idealistic young teacher at a Māori school becomes fascinated by Sarah “August” Moses, a withdrawn orphan reputedly mistreated by her relatives. Hoping both to rescue her and use her life as material for a romance, he and his wife take her into their home. August initially appears transformed by their kindness, behaving gratefully and helping with the housework, and the couple plans to take her with them when they return to the city.

Soon, however, food and household goods disappear, August neglects errands and responsibilities, and she repeatedly lies about her absences. When the schoolmistress becomes ill, August grows increasingly lazy and unreliable. The teacher eventually discovers her entertaining her relatives with malicious inventions about his domestic life, which causes hostility in the community and keeps children away from school. He concludes that August’s relatives have used her to avoid supporting her while benefiting from the goods she takes from his home.

After August is expelled from the cottage, she and her relatives retaliate with an abusive letter, nighttime harassment, threats against the schoolmistress, and an apparent knife intimidation. The teacher finally responds forcefully, threatening police action and later driving away two drunken men with a gun. August subsequently spends three uneventful months as a laborer’s housekeeper before casually returning home, giving the teacher none of the grand romance he had imagined. Having abandoned his naïve faith in universal brotherhood, he remains at the school for three more peaceful years and earns the local community’s lasting respect through firmness rather than sentimental idealism.

The story uses an ornate, lightly comic romantic style associated with early twentieth-century popular fiction. Its prose combines sentimental idealisation, woodland adventure, social comedy and fairy-tale imagery. The tone is initially arch and teasing, presenting Barbara’s vanity with affectionate irony rather than outright ridicule, but it gradually becomes tender, suspenseful and bittersweet. Phrases such as “Dear little Barbara” and “Poor little Barbara” allow the narrator to patronise and protect the heroine at once, reinforcing the story’s amused, indulgent attitude toward her youth.

The narration is third-person omniscient, although it remains closely aligned with Barbara’s perceptions and fantasies. The narrator frequently steps beyond her viewpoint to comment on her limitations, as when an “observant person” would notice practical details that Barbara ignores, or when her ideas about wilderness danger are gently corrected by narratorial asides. This produces a double perspective: the forest and Stanton appear through Barbara’s romantic imagination, while the narrator quietly reveals the naïveté of that imagination. Free-indirect phrasing further blends her assumptions with the narrative voice, making her self-dramatisation part of the comedy.

Characterisation relies heavily on contrast. Barbara is rendered through diminutives, decorative details and repeated epithets: she is “petite,” “dainty,” “little,” “adorable,” butterfly-like and associated with chiffon, parasols, picture-hats and silver buckles. Stanton, by contrast, is defined by economy, competence and firm physical lines: gray eyes, a clear-cut jaw, rifle, pack, moccasins and faded flannel. Their opposition structures the story—urban femininity against wilderness masculinity, performance against practicality, ornament against utility, innocence against experience. These portraits reflect period gender conventions: Barbara’s childish caprice is disciplined by Stanton’s physical authority and outdoor competence, while his emotional reserve enhances his romantic appeal.

The diction alternates between luxuriant description and crisp colloquial dialogue. Descriptive passages favour sensuous modifiers, balanced repetitions and playful overstatement, particularly in the catalogue of Barbara’s attractions. Adverbs such as “elusively,” “teasingly,” “judicially” and “calmly” create a knowingly extravagant rhythm. The woodland vocabulary is more concrete and regionally specific, naming basswood, tamarack, hemlock, butternut, popples, moose-maples and cedar swamp. French phrases, chivalric language and references to Fontainebleau express Barbara’s borrowed romanticism, while practical camp-making terms ground Stanton’s world in physical action.

Sentences are generally fluent and moderately long, often built from coordinated clauses and cumulative descriptive lists. Repetition is a prominent rhythmic device: “straight towering trunks, saplings, undergrowth” conveys the disorienting sameness of the forest, while successive “good-by” exchanges prolong the final emotional separation. Short sentences are used for comic emphasis, sudden danger or emotional finality: “She was not on the trail at all”; “It was useless”; “Never,” he replied. Parallel constructions and deliberate reiteration also give key passages a lyrical, almost incantatory quality, especially when the forest closes around Barbara or reclaims the abandoned camp.

The dialogue is brisk, mannered and often adversarial. Barbara’s imperious declarations meet Stanton’s dry understatement, creating flirtatious comedy through mismatched assumptions. His laconic answers—“South,” “Northwest,” “Walk through the woods”—preserve mystery, while her rapid questions expose curiosity and entitlement. Their exchanges gradually shift the balance of power: Barbara begins by expecting obedience, becomes meek under his rebukes, then recovers dignity and self-command at the farewell. Much of the attraction remains implicit, conveyed through pauses, avoided glances, blushes, small gestures and things left unsaid.

Nature functions both as setting and symbolic agent. The forest changes according to Barbara’s emotional state: first it is a theatrical backdrop for romance, then a hostile green prison, then a protective and enchanted space under Stanton’s guidance. It is repeatedly personified as kind, unfriendly, enclosing, reclaiming and recalling its “son.” Light and darkness similarly mark emotional transitions. Sunlit clearings belong to social performance; twilight creates intimacy and uncertainty; firelight temporarily domesticates the wilderness; morning brings recognition and separation. The camp becomes a liminal space outside ordinary society in which Barbara’s fantasy briefly seems real.

Humour is generated through incongruity and affectionate deflation. Chiffon, high heels and a parasol are absurdly unsuited to swamp and undergrowth, while Barbara treats getting lost as though it were a minor inconvenience once a capable man appears. Her melodramatic thoughts are repeatedly undercut by practical facts, most decisively when she discovers that she has spent the night almost within sight of home. The narrator’s parenthetical judgments and mock-heroic phrasing—wolves, knights, quests, “the resignation of the man who can but die”—maintain the comic surface even as the emotional stakes rise.

The three-part structure traces a compact initiation. The first section moves from vanity and playacting to fear and helplessness. The second creates a temporary domestic idyll in the wilderness, allowing attraction to develop through food, shelter, conversation and watchfulness. The third replaces fantasy with choice and loss. The ending resists conventional romantic fulfilment: Stanton chooses his mission, Barbara’s invitation fails, and the final dropped parasol externalises an emotion she otherwise holds rigidly in check. This closing image is restrained but melodramatically resonant, converting a decorative object associated with her earlier coquetry into a sign of genuine shock and relinquishment.

Overall, the style is picturesque, sentimental, ironic and highly visual. It favours decorative femininity, heroic masculinity, romantic wilderness imagery, omniscient commentary and emotionally charged symbolism. Beneath its playful adventure plot lies a coming-of-age movement in which Barbara passes from rehearsed coquetry to authentic feeling, while the forest transforms from a stage for imaginary romance into the setting of a real but impossible attachment.

The story uses a brisk, urbane comic style built on sustained irony. Its narrator is omniscient, confident and intrusive, repeatedly interpreting events for the reader with mock seriousness. Criminal violence, vindictiveness and petty theft are described in the decorous language of duty, honour and moral principle: Buffin “corrects” enemies, regards revenge as a “Holy Mission,” and feels that attacking Keating is a private affair between gentlemen. This elevated framing of sordid behaviour creates the central comic incongruity. The narrative voice remains outwardly composed and judicial even when presenting absurd conclusions, often imitating the language of sermons, official reports, heroic history or philosophical reflection.

The tone is dry, playful and gently satirical rather than grim. Underworld figures are treated with ceremonious respect—“the great Sid,” “the prisoner,” “the paragon”—while institutions and respectable moral sentiments are lightly mocked. Bathos is pervasive: grand ideas such as crusades, sacrifice, justice and the future of the race are attached to a dim-witted pickpocket’s grudges and accidents. The humour frequently depends on understatement, euphemism and mock logic. Prison is “quiet seclusion” provided “at the Government’s expense”; Keating could have “eaten” Buffin; and the threat posed by London is presented as though supported by medical evidence. The narrator also makes knowingly excessive comparisons, as when Buffin’s opportunity is likened to Cromwell’s at Dunbar or his outrage to that of an inventor whose patent has been infringed.

The diction alternates sharply between polished, Latinate narration and colloquial criminal or working-class speech. Narrative phrases such as “foregather,” “perpended,” “ethereal natures” and “a strictly logical and impartial judge” contrast with “pinched,” “nark,” “slop,” “smitchin’” and “yer knows.” Dialogue uses dropped consonants, phonetic spelling and slang to mark class and milieu. Buffin’s repeated “Um” establishes his limited verbal and intellectual range, while Keating’s genial clichés reveal a kindly but complacent authority. The gangsters speak tersely and threateningly, with menace conveyed as much through dryness and repetition as through explicit violence.

Sentences are generally clear and rhythmically controlled, but their lengths vary for comic effect. Short declarations deliver character and premise efficiently: “His hobby was revenge.” Longer sentences accumulate qualifications, parallels and inflated reasoning until they reach a punch line. Repetition is used both structurally and rhetorically, particularly in sequences such as “For weeks…” and “He remembered…,” which magnify Buffin’s grievance while exposing its absurdity. Parenthetical qualifications and balanced clauses give the prose a conversational, essayistic polish. Ellipses, abrupt fragments and sensory lists accelerate moments of action, especially during the ambush.

Characterisation is economical and caricatural. Buffin is defined by a few governing traits: criminality, vindictiveness, physical meagreness, limited intelligence and instinctive opportunism. The narrator repeatedly reduces his mind to comic machinery, calling it a “cheap-substitute” mind and stressing the painful effort involved whenever he thinks. Keating is physically massive, professionally alert, morally conventional and benignly self-confident. Sid Marks and his associates are rendered through grotesque nicknames, ritualised menace and group hierarchy. Although the figures are types, their behaviour remains internally consistent, allowing the plot’s reversal to arise naturally from character.

The plot is tightly constructed around escalating misunderstandings and reversals. Buffin befriends Keating only to discover his off-duty vulnerability; the friendship then makes Buffin look like an informer; when he finally prepares his revenge, rival attackers pre-empt him; possessiveness drives him to rescue the very policeman he intended to assault; and his involuntary heroism makes him even less safe among his criminal associates. The closing twist completes the circle: while being praised as a public-spirited hero, Buffin steals the magistrate’s gold watch. This final detail restores his essential identity and delivers the ending without explanatory commentary.

Pacing is rapid and episodic. Expository passages summarise days or weeks, while crucial encounters unfold as scenes with dialogue. Section breaks mark clear stages in the comic progression: imprisonment and grievance, calculated friendship, confrontation with the gang, the ambush, public acclaim and escape. Suspense is genuine but always subordinate to comic inevitability. Action scenes rely on compressed auditory and physical details—whistling, feet, growls, gasps, blows—before shifting back to retrospective narration and ironic interpretation.

The setting is an urban underworld rendered through selected place names, street slang, pubs, police beats, courts, pawnbrokers and the Tube. Clerkenwell and Frith Street supply social specificity without extensive description. Weather and darkness have practical and comic functions: the cold intensifies Buffin’s sense of martyrdom, while shadowy streets enable both intended and accidental violence. The world is socially stratified but presented as a shared comic system in which criminals, police and magistrates all follow recognisable rituals and speak in characteristic registers.

Overall, the style is polished comic narration driven by verbal incongruity, mock-heroic elevation, authorial irony and exact plotting. It treats a petty criminal’s moral distortions with amused detachment, allowing the narrator’s refined language to expose the gap between Buffin’s self-conception and reality. Beneath the farce lies a satire of simplistic moral judgement: public institutions hail Buffin as an “ethereal” hero, while the reader knows that his courage arose from wounded criminal proprietorship and that he remains, to the final line, an accomplished pickpocket.

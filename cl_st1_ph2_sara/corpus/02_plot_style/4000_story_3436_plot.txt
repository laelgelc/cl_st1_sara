A city couple, dreaming of cheap and plentiful country living, borrow money to buy a cottage where they expect to grow their own food and keep livestock. Ignoring an experienced uncle’s warning that economical rural life requires practical knowledge, they spend heavily on repairs, landscaping, seeds, tools, hired labor, and an unruly cow that must eventually be sold at a loss.

Their further attempts at self-sufficiency fare no better. A newly purchased swarm of bees abandons its patent hive, hired gardeners mishandle the plantings, weeds overrun the beds, and an incompetent boy destroys vegetables and neglects his duties. Cows, hens, chicks, and pigs create additional expense and chaos, while the garden produces so little that the couple must buy their winter vegetables at market.

By year’s end, the supposed economy of country life has left them burdened with bills and unable to meet the note on their property. Their uncle, having foreseen the failure, quietly pays the debt. The narrator finally admits that his uncle was right: living cheaply in the country is possible only if one knows how.

The story uses an intimate second-person point of view, addressing the protagonist as “you” throughout. This creates both closeness and estrangement: the reader is placed inside Akunna’s experiences while the pronoun also suggests her dissociation, loneliness, and difficulty fully possessing her own life. The narration remains tightly focalized through her consciousness, filtering America, Nigeria, race, class, and romance through her observations without extensive authorial explanation. Her name is withheld until another character asks for it, reinforcing her initial invisibility and displacement.

The prose is lucid, restrained, and predominantly plainspoken. Its emotional force comes from precise concrete details rather than overt commentary: unpainted walls, crisp dollar notes, a stained carpet, a rust-eaten car roof, a blank fortune, and presents that have no practical use. Nigerian terms such as “nwanne,” “garri,” “onugbu,” “dawadawa,” and “nsi” appear naturally and are clarified by context rather than translated ceremonially. This produces a culturally grounded voice that does not treat Nigerian experience as foreign material requiring explanation. American speech, stereotypes, and assumptions are often reproduced directly, exposing ignorance through understatement rather than polemic.

Sentences are generally controlled and accessible, but they frequently lengthen into accumulative lists. Repetition and parallel syntax are central techniques: “You wanted to write,” “You did not tell him,” and “You knew” organize passages into rhythmic sequences while showing thoughts that circle around what cannot be said. Parentheses, dashes, and semicolons allow qualifications and ironic afterthoughts to enter otherwise straightforward narration. Dialogue is often embedded within paragraphs rather than staged as extended exchanges, keeping attention on Akunna’s interpretation of what is said. Short declarative sentences—“There was nothing to write about,” “Every month,” “Shit”—interrupt the longer rhythms and deliver emotional shocks with minimal embellishment.

The tone combines quiet irony, suppressed anger, melancholy, and observational wit. Irony repeatedly exposes the gap between expectation and reality: America is imagined as a place of cars, houses, and guns, yet Akunna encounters insecure work, cramped housing, exploitation, and isolation. The uncle’s phrase “give-and-take” becomes especially bitter when it is echoed after his sexual coercion. Similar tensions shape the affluent boyfriend’s fascination with “real” foreign lives, his decorative gifts, and his ability to treat travel and education as choices. The narration rarely labels these contradictions directly; it places contrasting details beside one another and allows the moral discomfort to emerge.

Imagery is economical but vivid, often based on food, bodies, color, and material objects. Extra-virgin olive oil becomes both an object of pleasure and a metaphor for the boyfriend’s green-gold eyes; his hair resembles corn tassels, while hers resembles pillow filling. Wealth and poverty are registered through usable things: money, meat, rent, shoes, cars, roofs, and food left uneaten. Similes can be sensuous, comic, or harsh, as when the father’s humiliation is compared to pigs in a marsh or pleading becomes a performance observed like pornography. Such images resist sentimentalization and preserve the protagonist’s capacity for judgment, including judgment of those she loves.

The title image functions as the story’s main psychological metaphor. The unnamed “thing” around Akunna’s neck embodies homesickness, silence, precarity, trauma, and social invisibility. It is deliberately indefinite, allowing several pressures to occupy the same bodily sensation. Its recurrence gives the otherwise realistic narrative a faintly uncanny dimension. When intimacy makes it loosen, the change is described physically rather than analyzed; the story consistently converts emotional states into bodily sensations such as choking, bruising, clogged breath, goosebumps, tears, and touch.

Structurally, the narrative is chronological but strongly episodic. It moves through compressed scenes of arrival, betrayal, work, isolation, romance, conflict, and bereavement, with transitions often marked by recurring phrases rather than explicit section breaks. Time can pass rapidly in summary and then slow around a charged encounter. Memories of Lagos interrupt the American present, creating a double perspective in which each country becomes a measure of the other. The story avoids a simple opposition between a bad America and an idealized Nigeria: both contain inequality, humiliation, intimacy, aspiration, and exploitation.

Characterization is indirect and relational. Akunna emerges through what she notices, withholds, sends home, refuses to explain, and cannot bring herself to write. The boyfriend is neither reduced to a villain nor absolved by good intentions; his curiosity and tenderness coexist with privilege, self-righteousness, and failures of understanding. Secondary characters are sketched quickly through emblematic gestures, remarks, and objects—a gold tooth, a lowered voice, a blank stare, a hand held beneath a tablecloth. This compression gives even minor encounters social significance.

The story’s central stylistic method is juxtaposition: loud farewells and private requests, apparent hospitality and sexual threat, romantic closeness and racial incomprehension, material abundance and emotional dissatisfaction, silence and the desire to write. The ending remains open and understated. Akunna’s prolonged embrace followed by release echoes the loosening of the thing around her neck, but it does not specify whether she will return. The final movement therefore preserves ambiguity while completing the story’s recurring pattern of holding on and letting go.

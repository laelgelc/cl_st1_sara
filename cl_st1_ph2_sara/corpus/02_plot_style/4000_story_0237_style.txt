The story is written as a literary fairy tale with the apparent simplicity of an oral children’s narrative and the melancholy, moral depth of an adult fable. Its central technique is sustained personification: the Fir-tree thinks, speaks, longs, suffers, remembers, and misinterprets events, while sunbeams, wind, dew, birds, and mice also possess voices or human qualities. The nonhuman protagonist nevertheless remains rooted in a recognisably natural and domestic world, so fantasy emerges without elaborate explanation.

The narration uses a warm, omniscient third-person voice that stays close to the Fir-tree’s consciousness. It moves freely between external description, direct access to his thoughts, and gently intrusive commentary, as in comparisons between a tree’s aching back and a human headache. The narrator’s tone is sympathetic but quietly ironic. Readers can perceive the value of each stage of the Tree’s life before he does, creating dramatic irony: he despises the present, idealises the future, and recognises happiness only after it has vanished. Occasional proverbial statements—such as the final observation that every tale must end—give the narration the quality of a traditional storyteller addressing a broad audience.

The diction is clear, concrete, and sensuous, but marked by an archaic, translated-folktale register. Expressions such as “were I but,” “I would fain,” “whither,” “wherefore,” “thy,” “thee,” and “‘tis” lend the prose an old-fashioned formality. This elevated language is balanced by childlike exclamations, plain emotional vocabulary, and homely details: strawberries threaded on straw, gingerbread, sugar-plums, candles, toys, bacon, cheeses, and tallow. Capitalisation of common nouns such as “Tree,” “Sunbeams,” “Wind,” “Swallows,” and “Mice” reinforces their status as characters or symbolic presences.

Sentence structure alternates between long, cumulative descriptive sentences and short questions, cries, and refrains. Semicolons frequently join successive observations, giving the prose a measured, nineteenth-century flow. Exclamation marks are abundant and communicate the Tree’s impatience, wonder, fear, and self-dramatisation. Rhetorical questions propel his fantasies—“What was to happen?” and “Where did they go to?”—while repetitions such as “grow and grow,” “something better, something still grander,” and “‘tis past, ‘tis past” reproduce the rhythms of oral storytelling and intensify his obsessive longing. Parenthetical qualifications and narrator’s asides introduce mild humour without breaking the emotional spell.

Dialogue is plentiful, economical, and character-revealing. The Tree’s speech is aspirational, emotional, and repetitive; the birds offer tantalising glimpses of distant possibilities; the mice are curious and admiring; and the rats are blunt, practical, and dismissive. Different speakers are distinguished less through individualised dialect than through their desires and social roles. Dialogue also embeds stories within the story, especially the absurdly optimistic tale of Klumpy-Dumpy, which the Fir-tree accepts literally and turns into another false promise about his own future.

The plot is linear and episodic, structured by seasons, growth, removal, display, abandonment, and destruction. Temporal transitions are simple and conspicuous—“At the end of a year,” “In winter,” “In autumn,” “When Christmas came,” “In the morning”—which gives the narrative the lucid progression of a bedtime tale. Pacing accelerates during moments of spectacle or violence, particularly the felling, Christmas celebration, plundering of the decorations, and final burning, while the loft episode slows into repetition, recollection, and loneliness. Each new setting initially appears to promise fulfilment but becomes another stage of disillusionment.

Imagery is vivid but selective. Natural imagery emphasises sunlight, fresh air, snow, birds, flowers, and seasonal renewal; domestic imagery emphasises brightness, luxury, warmth, ornament, and consumption. These two fields are deliberately contrasted. The forest’s ordinary pleasures are genuine but overlooked, whereas the drawing room’s splendour is intense, artificial, and brief. Light is a recurring motif: sunbeams represent unrecognised present life, Christmas candles represent dangerous and temporary glory, and the final fire turns the Tree’s desire to “shine” into literal consumption. The golden star similarly changes from a symbol of distinction into a discarded trinket.

The emotional mode combines tenderness, comedy, pathos, and severity. Comic touches arise from the Tree’s naïveté, vanity, and literal-mindedness, yet the narration never treats him with cruelty. His errors are recognisably human: ambition prevents attention, anticipation displaces experience, and nostalgia arrives too late. The conclusion is unsentimental and final. Rather than rescuing the protagonist, it completes the governing pattern in which every desired future becomes a lost past.

The story’s dominant themes are dissatisfaction, impermanence, belated gratitude, vanity, mortality, and the failure to inhabit the present. Its moral is conveyed through structure and irony rather than direct instruction. The repeated invitations to “rejoice” express the ethical centre of the tale, while the Tree’s inability to understand them produces the tragedy. Overall, the style is accessible, rhythmic, symbolically concentrated, and deceptively simple: a child-friendly fairy-tale surface carries a sombre meditation on time, desire, memory, and death.

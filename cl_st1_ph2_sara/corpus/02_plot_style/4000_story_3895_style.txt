The story uses an urbane, psychologically observant realist style sharpened by social satire and tragic irony. Its narrative voice is polished, controlled, and faintly amused, exposing vanity and moral hypocrisy without direct authorial condemnation. The opening establishes this method through ceremonious exaggeration: Stilling becomes the “biggest man” in a small social world, while the parenthetical “on a conservative estimate” quietly ridicules both his possessions and his need to display them. Wealth is treated not merely as background but as a language of status, appetite, and self-deception.

The narration is third-person and initially focalized chiefly through Wrayford, though it moves flexibly into conjectural accounts of what the dinner guests are thinking. This produces a form of free indirect social analysis: the narrator can enter each character’s private calculations while preserving an elegant external composure. The guests’ reactions to the motor-boat reveal their ambitions, provinciality, romantic hopes, or financial interests. Character is therefore built through minute social responses rather than explicit description. Later, as the action tightens, the perspective becomes more immediate and closely aligned with Wrayford and Isabel, reducing satirical distance and intensifying suspense.

The diction is formal, cultivated, and period-specific, combining elevated narrative prose with colloquial upper-class speech. The narration favours words such as “perorating,” “beneficence,” “fastidious,” and “dispensation,” while Stilling speaks in a blustering mixture of clichés, slang, rhetorical questions, and masculine exclamations: “Gad,” “By George,” “hang it,” and “my boy.” His repeated assertions of generosity, taste, courage, and soft-heartedness undermine themselves. Dialogue is strongly individuated and often functions as indirect revelation: Stilling’s monologues advertise the virtues he conspicuously lacks, whereas Isabel’s speech is restrained, elliptical, and weighted with what cannot safely be said.

Sentences in the social scenes are often long, balanced, and syntactically elaborate, with parentheses, qualifications, lists, and ironic afterthoughts. This creates the effect of an intelligent observer continually adjusting the moral focus. Enumerations of servants, vehicles, horses, greenhouses, and imagined social benefits reproduce the acquisitive mentality being satirized. By contrast, the boat-house scenes increasingly employ shorter exchanges, fragments, repetitions, and abrupt questions. The shift from expansive drawing-room prose to compressed nocturnal dialogue mirrors the movement from social performance to danger and confession.

Characterization depends heavily on contrast between appearance and motive. Stilling sees himself as discriminating, generous, practical, and beloved, while his behaviour reveals vulgarity, cowardice, financial irresponsibility, and an almost invincible narcissism. His wife’s stillness, lowered lids, embroidery, and controlled movements encode repression rather than passivity. Wrayford is rendered through sensory attention to Isabel and through the conflict between professional responsibility, adulterous desire, and moral disgust. Even minor characters are economically caricatured through a single aspiration or visual detail, as in Mrs. Swordsley’s resemblance to her clerical husband.

Physical settings carry symbolic and dramatic force. The bright, flower-filled drawing room is a theatre of social pretence; the dark boat-house is the concealed space where forbidden truths emerge. The abandoned sporting equipment reflects Stilling’s succession of expensive whims, while the motor-launch embodies conspicuous consumption and becomes the pretext that draws him toward danger. Darkness, unstable flooring, deep water, oily machinery, and the approaching storm create a Gothic undertone within an otherwise realist social world. The repeated sound of water is compared to a “tormented” or “rebellious” heart, turning the setting into an acoustic expression of Isabel’s suppressed emotional life.

The story is carefully paced in two broad movements. The first proceeds slowly through observation, conversation, and financial disclosure, accumulating evidence of Stilling’s character. The second narrows the space, darkens the atmosphere, and converts earlier incidental details—the key, the greased runners, the sliding floor, the launch, Stilling’s drinking—into mechanisms of suspense. Foreshadowing is conspicuous but smoothly embedded: references to deep water, danger, storms, locked doors, and Stilling’s concern for the boat all prepare the climax without fully determining its outcome.

A central stylistic device is dramatic irony. Readers understand the distance between Stilling’s self-image and his actual conduct, between Isabel’s outward duty and inward death wish, and between the apparent accident and the morally compromised circumstances surrounding it. The climax preserves ambiguity around impulse, choice, and responsibility. Isabel calls for her husband and offers the oar, yet the scene is saturated with the wish she has just confessed; Wrayford attempts rescue but dies in the act. The prose does not settle this moral ambiguity through explanation, allowing action, darkness, and fragmented perception to carry the burden.

The ending intensifies the irony by abruptly returning to Stilling’s language of public performance. His phrases—“terrible loss,” “mysterious dispensation,” “miraculous escape”—sound like rehearsed fragments of the account he will give others. The ellipses compress the transition from catastrophe to self-dramatization, showing that he has already converted Wrayford’s death into a story about his own survival. This final tonal stroke is bleakly comic and psychologically brutal: the character least capable of self-knowledge receives both rescue and narrative control.

Overall, the style combines drawing-room comedy, psychological realism, moral tragedy, and restrained Gothic suspense. Its signature qualities are sophisticated irony, socially diagnostic detail, controlled shifts in focalization, symbolically charged settings, and dialogue that exposes character through self-betrayal. Emotion is strongest when least directly narrated; the prose maintains formal composure while revealing desperation, resentment, and violence beneath the decorous surface.

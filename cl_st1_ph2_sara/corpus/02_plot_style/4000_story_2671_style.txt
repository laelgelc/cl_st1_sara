The story uses a close third-person perspective that remains tightly aligned with Monica’s consciousness. Although the narrator occasionally supplies an external description, the prose repeatedly slips into free indirect discourse, reproducing Monica’s judgments, exclamations and emotional logic without formal attribution. Phrases such as “Fatal. Fatal.” and “Oh, how terrifying Life was” blur narration and thought, making the reader experience her volatile inner life while also perceiving its vanity and self-dramatisation. This creates a subtle ironic distance: Monica’s distress is rendered sympathetically, but her habit of converting every discomfort into a personal tragedy is quietly exposed.

The prose is impressionistic and psychologically mobile. It favours momentary sensations, shifting moods and associative thoughts over explanation or background. Physical details register through Monica’s nervous sensitivity: doors slam, blinds knock, curtains flap, telephones ring and iron bangs. Fragmented sentences, repetitions, italics, exclamations and abrupt questions mimic agitation—“What was it?,” “How dared he,” “I’m free”—while longer, flowing passages accompany fantasy or emotional expansion. Dashes and ellipses reproduce hesitation, interruption and self-conscious theatricality. Onomatopoeia, including “Bang” and “Ring-ting-a-ping-ping,” makes the environment feel invasive and amplifies the heroine’s hypersensitivity.

The diction moves between refined social language and intensely sensuous description. Colours, textures, smells and movements are precisely observed: the “whitey-greyish light,” the smell of “soap and burnt paper and wallflower brilliantine,” the cold glitter of nickel fittings and the softness imagined in hair, flowers and ribbon. Similes are vivid, delicate and sometimes grotesque. A cloud resembles “a torn shirt”; Madame’s head is a powder-puff on a pin-cushion; silence descends “like flakes of snow”; George’s face crumples “like paper.” Such imagery turns objects and interiors into emotional correlatives, so that the windy morning, the shaking rooms and the cold salon reflect Monica’s unstable perceptions.

Dialogue is economical and socially revealing. Monica’s speech is mannered, elliptical and performative, full of soft drawls, dramatic understatement and carefully staged phrases. Ralph’s extravagant romantic language is quoted at length so that its artificiality becomes apparent. Marie’s brief French-inflected remarks establish the cosmopolitan, affluent setting, while George’s restrained, repetitive comments about the weather conceal grief. Much of the story’s tension arises from what characters do not say: polite formulas and trivial conversation cover emotional need, resentment and bereavement.

The structure follows a sequence of rapid emotional reversals. Monica moves from morning anguish to anger, from anger to exhilarated freedom, from exhilaration to unease, and from unease to sentimental grief. The wind serves as a recurring motif and organizing force. At first it attacks her nerves; then it symbolizes liberation; later it becomes threatening again, suggesting rootlessness and human isolation. This instability shows how readily Monica projects her current mood onto the external world.

The tone combines lyric intensity, social comedy, psychological acuity and restrained satire. Monica’s feelings are real in the moment, but their scale is repeatedly undercut by the gap between her privileged grievances and George’s actual loss. The revelation of his daughter’s death produces the story’s central contrast: Monica has treated a telephone call and luncheon invitation as existential injuries, while genuine suffering has remained hidden behind professional courtesy. Yet the ending does not resolve this contrast into a simple moral awakening. Monica immediately aestheticises the death as a “tiny wax doll” and imagines elaborate white flowers accompanied by phrases such as “From one who understands.” Her sympathy is sincere, but it is still filtered through fantasy, beauty and a desire to occupy the role of the uniquely sensitive stranger.

Overall, the style is modernist, compressed and epiphanic. It concentrates on a brief span of ordinary urban life, uses free indirect discourse to expose the instability of consciousness, and builds meaning through recurring sensory motifs rather than explicit commentary. Its characteristic effect comes from the tension between intimacy and irony: the reader is drawn deeply into Monica’s perceptions while being invited to recognize their theatricality, egotism and fragile connection to the realities of other people.

The story uses a warm, lightly satirical, old-fashioned comic-romance style. Its central technique is the affectionate contrast between romantic feeling and conspicuously unromantic people, names, occupations, and circumstances. Jedediah’s bald spot, double chin, checked suit, tin wagon, and commercial failures are repeatedly set against his conviction that his life is rich in romance. The narration never treats him cruelly, however: the humour exposes his vanity and naïveté while steadily affirming his kindness, loyalty, and emotional sincerity. The resulting tone blends amusement, tenderness, nostalgia, and mild social irony.

The narrative voice is omniscient, conversational, and conspicuously present. It moves freely between external description, communal opinion, retrospective explanation, and the private thoughts of Jed and Mattie. Occasional first-person intrusions and direct appeals to the reader—such as the opening reference to “the most exciting and tragic affair I ever knew” and the question of whether the narrator will still be believed—create the air of an amiable storyteller recounting village history. The narrator often anticipates objections, digresses briefly, qualifies claims, and supplies humorous commentary. This gives the prose an intimate oral quality while allowing the narrator to control the reader’s sympathies.

The diction mixes polished literary language with rural colloquial speech. Descriptive passages employ words and constructions such as “mésalliance,” “revel of colour,” “to all outward seeming,” and “betokening,” giving the prose a genteel, late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century flavour. Dialogue and interior monologue are more vernacular, especially in Jed’s speech: “hain’t,” “s’pose,” “persoom,” “sitooation,” and “Get up, my nag, get up.” These phonetic forms establish social setting and character without making speech difficult to follow. Mattie’s language is plainer, calmer, and firmer, while Selena’s repeated exclamations and full-name rebukes—“Mattie Adams!” and “Matilda Adams!”—make her sound censorious and melodramatic.

Sentence structure is generally expansive and balanced. Long sentences accumulate descriptive clauses, qualifications, parenthetical remarks, and comic afterthoughts, while shorter sentences deliver judgments or punch lines: “Jed was determined not to ‘persoom.’” Parallelism and repetition are important rhythmic devices. The word “romantic” and its variants recur almost obsessively, becoming both a comic refrain and the story’s thematic key. Jed’s repeated “Get up, my nag, get up” similarly punctuates his monologues, characterises his relationship with the pony, and gives his thoughts the rhythm of travel. Dashes and semicolons support the narrator’s digressive, anecdotal flow.

Description is vivid, decorative, and gently personifying. The country road “dream[s] in the haze and sunshine”; honeysuckle “riots” over the porch; the wagon clinks and flashes like a “little planetary system”; and its upright brooms make it resemble a “triumphal chariot.” Such images elevate mundane objects without erasing their comic homeliness. Colour is especially prominent: the red wagon and pony, pink necktie, amethyst pin, white house, green shutters, scarlet geraniums, golden russets, and late asters give the setting a bright, pastoral richness. Seasonal movement from midsummer to autumn quietly measures the courtship’s development, while flowers, orchards, lanes, and twilight provide a conventional romantic frame for unconventional lovers.

Characterisation proceeds through caricature softened by sympathy. Jed’s appearance, self-dramatisation, and lack of business ability are exaggerated for comic effect, but his essential honesty is repeatedly stressed. Mattie is defined by placidity, practical competence, and concealed determination; her emotional awakening is expressed through decisive action rather than lyrical introspection. Selena is the sharpest caricature: angular, pale-eyed, thin-lipped, chronically curious, and permanently stationed at a window. Her physical leanness is jokingly attributed to curiosity, and her house’s panoramic view becomes an emblem of village surveillance. Yet she also functions as an ironic agent of fate, repeatedly pushing the lovers together while trying to separate them.

Dialogue carries much of the comedy and plot. Exchanges are direct, socially observant, and often structured as verbal thrust and parry, especially between Mattie and Selena. Jed’s extended addresses to his pony operate as spoken interior monologues, revealing his romantic self-conception more fully than ordinary conversation does. A recurring comic pattern is that Jed interprets Mattie’s courtship as mere kindness, while Mattie mistakes his restraint for refusal. This sustained dramatic irony delays the resolution without creating genuine darkness.

The plot is linear but includes a substantial retrospective section explaining the lovers’ separation fifteen years earlier. It follows a clear comic-romance arc: return, recognition, renewed companionship, misunderstanding, threatened departure, forced declaration, and union. Repetition structures the middle: Jed visits weekly, Mattie buys unnecessary tinware, Selena watches, and Jed vows not to return before returning anyway. Each recurrence intensifies the emotional stakes. The climax reverses conventional courtship roles when practical Mattie effectively proposes to the timidly honourable Jed.

The ending is deliberately symmetrical and triumphant. Jed leaves in the same tin wagon that initially made him seem ridiculous, but the vehicle has become part of the romance rather than an obstacle to it. His final chant repeats the story’s major verbal motifs and confirms its governing idea: romance lies not in elegant names, wealth, youth, or conventional heroism, but in the imaginative and emotional meaning people give to ordinary life. The overall style is therefore pastoral, humorous, sentimental without being solemn, and morally generous toward commonplace characters.

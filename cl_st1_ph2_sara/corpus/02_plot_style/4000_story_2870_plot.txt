At a monthly dinner with four old friends, wealthy Parisian bachelor Joseph de Bardon recounts a strange encounter. While wandering through Montmartre Cemetery and visiting a former lover’s grave, he notices a beautiful young widow apparently overcome with grief at the tomb of her husband, an officer killed in Tonquin. When she faints, Joseph revives her, escorts her from the cemetery, takes her for tea, and accompanies her home.

The woman emphasizes her loneliness and gradually welcomes Joseph’s advances. They dine together, become lovers, and continue their affair for three weeks, until Joseph tires of her and leaves on the pretext of a journey. A month later, still puzzled by her, he returns to the cemetery.

There he sees the same woman in deep mourning, now leaning on the arm of an older, distinguished man exactly as she had once leaned on Joseph. She signals Joseph not to recognize her, revealing that her grieving-widow act is likely a scheme for seducing or exploiting bereaved men. Joseph leaves wondering whether she is a unique opportunist or one of many “huntresses of the tombs,” and whose widow she is pretending to be that day.

The story uses an intimate first-person retrospective voice, combining immediate sensory experience with the authority of someone recalling a distant, almost legendary event. The narrator moves fluidly between what he saw and felt on the revolutionary night and later reflections on its significance. This double perspective gives the account both urgency and mythic weight: domestic actions unfold moment by moment, while retrospective declarations such as the claim that “time did not exist” elevate the episode beyond ordinary history. The voice is earnest, awestruck and emotionally heightened rather than ironic or analytically detached.

The prose relies on repetition, parallel phrasing and accumulating clauses to suggest psychological transformation. Words and constructions such as “new,” “strange,” “quiet,” “transparent,” “yesterday,” and “I did not know” recur insistently, making familiar people and objects appear uncanny. Sentences often proceed through linked observations and emotional qualifications, reproducing a mind struggling to define an unprecedented experience. Short declarative sentences—“It was quiet,” “It was true then,” “I must go”—interrupt these longer passages at decisive moments. Occasional grammatical stiffness or unusual idiom gives the prose the character of an older translation, but this also reinforces its solemn, estranging effect.

The diction juxtaposes plain domestic vocabulary with abstract, visionary language. Candles, bread, aprons, beds, children and stairways coexist with freedom, eternity, time, life and the future. Ordinary objects become symbols once the revolutionary action begins: the candle flame resembles a crooked sword; stale bread embodies the family’s former life; walls appear transparent and temporary; the barricade signifies both literal resistance and the collapse of an oppressive past. The repeated contrast between solidity and dissolution—stone against air, walls against spaciousness, the city against the fields—expresses liberation as a radical change in perception.

Imagery is strongly sensory and symbolic. Darkness is pierced by candlelight, moonlight and an imagined sun, while the smoke-free spring air brings the fragrance of fields into the industrial city. Sound is especially important: the city’s unnatural silence magnifies a barking dog, a child’s cry and the cheerful blows of axes and hammers. Those blows are repeatedly likened to music, singing and constructive labour in a forest or on a river. Violence is therefore approached indirectly and transformed into an image of collective creation. The barricade builders remain shadowy and largely unseen, allowing their work to acquire an impersonal, elemental grandeur.

Dialogue carries much of the action and is spare, rhythmic and ceremonially charged. The husband and wife speak in questions, fragments and repeated formulas, often understanding more than they explicitly state. Their exchange records a rapid alteration of identity: “wife” and “husband” give way to “comrade,” while private love expands into love for humanity and the future. The recurrent questions—“Are you afraid?”, “Who are you?”, “Are you a human being?”—present political awakening as mutual rediscovery. Even the nine-year-old child speaks with an improbable gravity, extending the atmosphere of transformation across generations.

The pacing begins with abrupt alarm, slows into suspended contemplation, and then advances through a sequence of intimate farewells. External action remains minimal: the revolution is heard and sensed rather than directly depicted. This restraint keeps attention on the family’s inner conversion. Repeated pauses, silences and delays—washing, looking from the window, preparing food, visiting the sleeping children—stretch the interval before departure and make the night feel detached from chronological time. The final descent through the filthy, dark stairway restores physical confinement, but the narrator’s emotion points toward an unknown, joyous future.

Tonally, the story blends dread, tenderness, exaltation and prophetic certainty. Death is acknowledged without diminishing the characters’ enthusiasm; fear is absorbed into a larger feeling of freedom. The revolution is represented less as a political programme than as a spiritual awakening from the “sleep of grey life.” Its governing contrasts are sleep and awakening, age and youth, enclosure and openness, family attachment and collective solidarity, dead habit and living possibility. The overall style is lyrical, expressionistic and symbolist: psychological states reshape the visible world, repetition creates incantatory force, and a private household scene becomes an ecstatic parable of historical transformation.

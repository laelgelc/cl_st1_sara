The story employs a formal, early-twentieth-century adventure-romance style, with an omniscient third-person narrator and a strong emphasis on psychological suspense. The narrative remains chiefly aligned with Conyers, but withholds his history and intentions until dialogue gradually reveals them. This controlled concealment turns ordinary details—the locked drawer, the unfinished drink, the open doorway—into sources of tension. Brief explanatory passages supply social and biographical background, while the decisive emotional conflict is dramatized in real time.

The prose is atmospheric, melodramatic, and highly visual. The oppressive tropical night functions as an external expression of Conyers’s inner state: darkness hangs like a “pall,” heat has the force of a “crushing weight,” and the storm breaks as his concealed identity and desire approach exposure. Personification is frequent, with the day described as dead, darkness wrapping the earth, ghostly fingers stirring the wall hanging, and thunder speaking like a being in anguish. Similes tend to be vivid and elevated, drawing on nature, mythology, machinery, and the supernatural. Recurring images of shadow, flame, thunder, and entrapment produce a Gothic undertone within the colonial setting.

Sentence structure alternates between long, descriptive periods and short declarative statements. The longer sentences establish atmosphere or summarize history; the shorter ones mark shocks, decisions, and moments of emotional restraint: “It’s loaded,” “The struggle was over,” and “It doesn’t matter.” Repetition is used to slow time and intensify expectation, as in “Nearer came the hoofs and nearer yet” and “Slowly, very slowly.” Paragraphs are often brief, allowing pauses, gestures, and changes in expression to acquire dramatic weight. Dashes, semicolons, and interruptions reproduce hesitation, excitement, or thoughts that cannot be fully spoken.

The diction is elevated but accessible, combining literary description with period colloquialism. The narrator uses words such as “impassivity,” “vehemence,” “forthwith,” and “inscrutable,” while Palliser’s speech is full of vigorous informal expressions: “old chap,” “rot,” “by Jove,” and “lucky beggar.” His exuberant, repetitive language contrasts sharply with Conyers’s sparse, dry, cynical replies. Dialogue therefore performs much of the characterization. Palliser is impulsive, affectionate, privileged, and physically expressive; Conyers is guarded, fatalistic, self-controlled, and accustomed to disappointment. Their contrasting voices create both warmth and dramatic irony.

Character description is archetypal and symbolic. Palliser is repeatedly figured as a “young Hercules,” a Greek god, or a splendid wild animal, embodying youth, confidence, beauty, and worldly good fortune. Conyers is lean, shadowed, sphinx-like, and almost cavernous in appearance, embodying secrecy, endurance, and emotional burial. Gesture is especially important: Palliser laughs, seizes, springs up, and flings out his arms, whereas Conyers sits rigidly, turns away, locks drawers, and watches from shadow. Emotional revelation occurs through tiny physical disruptions—a dropped glass, a shifted posture, a sudden flame in the eyes—rather than through direct confession.

The pacing follows a careful pattern of delay and disclosure. The opening lingers over waiting, darkness, and approaching hoofbeats. Palliser’s arrival temporarily releases the tension through lively conversation and good news, but the revolver introduces a darker possibility. The extended discussion of the “one woman” then transforms the scene: each new detail allows the reader to infer that Conyers is Damaris’s vanished former lover before the connection is explicitly confirmed at the end. The climactic declaration is deliberately drowned by thunder, preserving uncertainty for Palliser while making Conyers’s crisis palpable. A short final section, separated typographically, returns to silence and resolves the internal struggle.

Symbolism is prominent and tightly integrated. The open door represents both expectation and the possibility of return; the lamp acts as beacon, interrogation light, and boundary against darkness. The revolver embodies despair as well as Conyers’s power to destroy himself or his rival. The reproduction of Doré’s falling Satan mirrors his sense of disgrace, exile, and lost splendour, while crushing it signals a rejection of that self-image. The repeated “wind in corn” sound is at once soothing, mocking, and ghostly. The storm supplies a moral and emotional climax, but its passing does not bring happiness—only disciplined renunciation.

The prevailing tone moves from ominous expectancy through masculine camaraderie to tragic irony and self-sacrifice. Sentiment is intense but usually contained behind reserve, understatement, and dramatic action. The central concerns are patience, fidelity, shame, lost opportunity, masculine honour, and the renunciation of personal desire for another person’s happiness. The ending preserves the story’s romantic fatalism: Conyers proves his strength not by reclaiming Damaris or confronting Palliser, but by choosing silence, endurance, and withdrawal.

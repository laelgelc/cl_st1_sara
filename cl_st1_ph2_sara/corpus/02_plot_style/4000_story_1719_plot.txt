A ten-dollar bill narrates its rapid circulation through New York, passing among workers, merchants, police, gamblers, and wealthy spenders while observing how money is used without regard to its origins. It eventually reaches Jack, a gambling-house owner whose large donation has been rejected by a church as “tainted.” Hurt and defiant, Jack spends lavishly, then gives the ten-dollar bill to a destitute woman, openly warning her that it came from gambling. She accepts it gratefully and uses it to buy food and pay rent for herself and her starving, sick child. Learning how much good it has done, the bill concludes that money itself is neither pure nor tainted; its value and moral significance depend on how it is used.

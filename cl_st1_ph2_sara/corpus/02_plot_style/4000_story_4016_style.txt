The story adopts the manner of an orally transmitted Irish folk tale, combining plain rural storytelling with mythic suggestion and lyrical symbolism. Its third-person narrator remains close to Hanrahan’s thoughts while retaining the authority of a traditional storyteller who knows his history, reputation, and supernatural entanglements. Repeated connective openings such as “And,” “But,” and “For” create a cumulative, spoken cadence, as though the narrative were being recited aloud. Time is loosely legendary rather than precisely historical, while named villages, mountains, lakes, holy days, and local families give the tale a strongly rooted sense of place.

The diction is simple, archaic, and Hiberno-English in flavour. Constructions such as “it is I know,” “what is on you,” “let you make no delay,” and “it was not long till” reproduce Irish speech patterns without making the prose difficult to follow. Formal or elevated phrases—“to the end of life and time,” “the power of the curse-making bards,” and “O Blessed Queen of Heaven”—sit beside homely details such as eggs, oaten cake, turf, sour milk, sacks, thatch, and blackthorn sticks. This mixture makes the everyday countryside feel continuous with legend, poetry, religion, and magic.

The syntax tends toward long, flowing sentences made from linked clauses. These clauses often accumulate through repetition, especially with “and,” allowing memories, physical details, and emotional associations to gather gradually. Shorter statements and exclamations then mark moments of shock or comic emphasis. Dialogue is direct and ceremonious, with speakers frequently repeating names and claims, and Hanrahan’s speech is especially expansive and self-dramatising. He speaks of personal troubles in the language of poets, queens, curses, and world history, which gives him dignity while also exposing his vanity.

Imagery is seasonal and symbolic. White hawthorn, birdsong, May blossom, wind, old trees, an ancient eagle, and a long-lived pike connect human life to the natural world. Youth is figured as blossom: fresh, white, beautiful, and destined to disappear before decay can touch it. Old age is represented through greyness, stiffness, gaps, hooks, aches, crooked limbs, and withering. The story repeatedly contrasts May’s fertility and beauty with bodily decline. The hare crossing Hanrahan’s path functions as a folkloric omen, and the landscape itself seems populated by powers capable of guiding, warning, or persecuting him.

The emotional register blends melancholy, grandeur, irony, and broad comedy. Hanrahan’s recognition of ageing is genuinely painful, conveyed through memories of social neglect and an increasingly unsparing catalogue of bodily decline. Yet his response is absurdly disproportionate: wounded by a girl’s casual comparison, he curses old age and recruits schoolchildren to spread the insult throughout the district. The old men’s retaliatory march, armed with sticks and moving like a storm, turns his poetic campaign into physical farce. The humour does not erase the sadness; rather, both arise from the same conflict between Hanrahan’s heroic self-image and his vulnerable, ageing body.

Characterisation is achieved through action, remembered reputation, and stylised speech rather than psychological analysis. Hanrahan appears as a wandering poet, teacher, flatterer, lover, and supposed possessor of bardic power. He is charismatic and imaginative but also vain, impulsive, and blind to the consequences of his words. His status is ambiguous: the community tolerates his wandering because he is a poet, yet it can quickly turn against him when his art becomes malicious. The children form a mischievous chorus that converts private resentment into public song, while the old men become both comic individuals and allegorical embodiments of “Old Age and Time and Weariness and Sickness.”

The narrative structure is circular and ironic. It begins with Hanrahan apparently settled after years of wandering, introduces an omen, then uses a small personal wound to provoke the act that destroys his new stability. His curse is effective in spreading through the countryside, but its practical effect is not the defeat of old age or Paddy Doe; it is the burning of Hanrahan’s cabin and his return to homelessness. Thus poetic power is presented as real in social terms—songs travel, shape reputations, and mobilise people—but unreliable and self-destructive when driven by pride.

The inset poem formalises the story’s central oppositions. Its rhyming couplets, repeated curses, catalogue of aged figures, and final blessing on the May blossom resemble a public bardic satire adapted into a memorable street song. The regular rhyme and list structure make it easy for children and beggars to transmit, showing poetry as communal and performative rather than private. The prose surrounding it repeatedly stresses recitation, memorisation, tunes, and circulation, reinforcing the story’s oral aesthetic.

Overall, the style is folkloric, lyrical, locally grounded, and gently tragicomic. It elevates an ordinary episode of wounded vanity into a symbolic confrontation between poetry and time, while allowing the final image of the fleeing poet and the burning thatch to puncture his grand rhetoric. The closing prayer gathers natural emblems and angry villagers into one imaginative category, ending on a characteristic fusion of mythic fear, self-pity, wit, and rueful acceptance.

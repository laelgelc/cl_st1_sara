The story is a comic coming-of-age farce rendered in a richly observant, gently satirical style. Its humour arises from the collision between adolescent self-importance and ordinary social reality: the boys attempt to imitate adult courtship through rigid etiquette, but their rehearsed formulas prove useless in a household that treats them as children. The narrative sustains affectionate mockery rather than cruelty. Abner’s pretension, Ross’s anxiety, and the adults’ amused awareness are exaggerated for comic effect, yet the boys’ humiliation remains emotionally credible.

The narration uses a flexible third-person omniscient perspective, moving freely between external description, authorial commentary, and the private fears of the two protagonists. It often inhabits the boys’ inflated understanding of events while retaining an adult ironic distance. Minor obstacles therefore acquire mock-heroic dimensions: a clean collar becomes oppressive, Aunt Missouri is as terrifying as “a lion in the way,” and an awkward visit assumes the gravity of a military expedition or religious ordeal. Occasional direct interventions—such as “Oh, youth—youth—youth, with its rash promises!”—strengthen the voice of a knowing storyteller looking back indulgently on adolescent folly.

The prose mixes elevated, literary diction with colloquial rural speech. Narrative phrases such as “pregnant silence,” “inferential invitation,” “psychological moment,” and “served his novitiate” lend ceremonious weight to trivial incidents. This is deliberately contrasted with compressed dialogue such as “I ain’t goin’,” “Fire away,” and “let’s cut and run.” The disparity between registers is one of the story’s main comic engines. Abner’s etiquette manual introduces a third idiom: stiff, prescriptive, and socially aspirational. Its formulas—“Let a rich stream of conversation flow,” “Name yours,” and “You flatter us”—sound absurd when repeated mechanically by frightened country boys.

Sentence structure is varied and generally expansive. Long, carefully balanced sentences accumulate qualifications, emotional reactions, and comic detail, while short sentences or fragments punctuate moments of alarm: “Oh, Lord!”; “Breakfast!”; “They’ve put us to bed!” Parallel constructions and repetition heighten the farce, particularly in sequences describing what the boys fail to do: “They did not rise. They did not thunder out anything.” Lists and escalating clauses allow embarrassment to build incrementally, turning each attempted solution into a new complication.

The dialogue is highly characterising. Abner speaks with borrowed loftiness whenever he feels secure, but lapses into panic and aggression when his performance is threatened. Ross is more sceptical, concrete, and honest; his literal questions expose the impracticality of the etiquette book. Aunt Missouri’s speech is warm, practical, and teasing, while the servants’ dialogue is represented through strongly marked historical Southern eye dialect. Contractions, altered spellings, nonstandard grammar, and phonetic approximations establish regional and social distinctions. This dialect treatment is integral to the period setting, though it also reflects dated racial conventions and stereotypes.

Description is selective, vivid, and frequently figurative. Physical details serve both characterisation and comedy: Ross’s freckles “advertised a rich soil,” the boys lean together like “young saplings weakened at the root,” and Babe trembles “like chocolate pudding.” Clothing and bodily gestures recur as motifs of insecurity. Collars wilt, hats are crushed or clutched, knees collapse, tongues stiffen, and faces redden or whiten. These visible signs translate inner panic into theatrical action. The repeated attention to hats, cards, collars, ties, and polished shoes also underscores the story’s concern with outward performance and social status.

The setting is a small Southern community governed by Sunday customs, family familiarity, and close communal observation. The white road, porch, arbor, dining room, servants, and plantation-style household create a specific regional world. Social privacy is almost nonexistent: mothers notice clean shirts, neighbors know one another’s children, household servants participate in the joke, and the boys fear that their failure will become village-wide knowledge. The setting therefore intensifies their predicament by making embarrassment public and inescapable.

Structurally, the story follows a sustained escalation. It begins with Abner preparing his appearance, adds Ross as an unwilling accomplice, introduces the etiquette manual as a flawed plan, and then delays the intended call through a chain of misunderstandings. The boys fail to announce themselves, are invited to supper, endure evening entertainment, are mistakenly kept overnight, and face a laughing audience at breakfast. Repeated opportunities to explain themselves create comic suspense because each chance is missed. The climax arrives when Abner’s mother publicly summons him home and he flees, after which Ross converts humiliation into decisive action by kissing Champe before everyone. The ending releases the accumulated tension and completes Ross’s development from paralysed imitator to spontaneous actor.

The dominant narrative techniques are irony, incongruity, repetition, escalation, and delayed resolution. Situational irony governs the whole plot: the boys go to demonstrate maturity but are fed, supervised, and put to bed as children. Verbal irony appears in the narrator’s grandiose labels for their enterprise and in the gap between the etiquette book’s promises and its practical effects. Running jokes—the undelivered card, the perpetually clutched hats, the inability to state the purpose of the visit—give the episode cohesion.

Thematically, the story explores the awkward transition from childhood to adulthood, the artificiality of learned social codes, and the difference between performance and genuine confidence. Abner depends on scripts, clothing, and status symbols, so interruption destroys his composure. Ross initially submits to the same “hypnotism,” but ultimately acts without a script. His impulsive kiss is socially outrageous, yet within the comic logic of the story it constitutes authentic courage. The final tone is triumphant and affectionate: embarrassment becomes initiation, and failed imitation gives way to self-possession.

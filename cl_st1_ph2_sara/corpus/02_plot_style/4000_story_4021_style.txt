The story uses a lyrical, myth-infused prose style that blends Irish folklore, Christian devotion, and fin-de-siècle symbolism. Its narrative voice is formal and elevated, with an antiquarian quality created by archaic constructions such as “whosoever,” “it were better,” and “right well I know.” Place names, native terms such as “Sidhe,” and references to hazel, quicken wood, fairy men, and ancient gods root the tale in a distinctly Irish legendary landscape. The supernatural is presented without explanatory distance: fairies, lake-dwellers, enchanted lamps, and thought-responsive hair belong naturally to the characters’ world.

The narration is in the third person and remains largely external, revealing character through speech, gesture, contrast, and setting rather than sustained psychological analysis. The two central figures are deliberately opposed. The boy speaks eagerly and impulsively, with reverence tempered by fear, while the old man speaks slowly, ceremonially, and at length, as if addressing both his servant and posterity. Their dialogue carries much of the exposition and often resembles a philosophical or moral debate. Long speeches unfold in balanced clauses and accumulative lists, giving the prose an incantatory, oral quality.

Sentences are frequently long and syntactically layered, joined by conjunctions and extended through relative clauses. Repetition supplies rhythm and emphasis: the boy repeatedly declares what he fears, while the old man repeatedly contrasts youth, manhood, age, mortality, and immortality. Parallel structures—“now spreading out and now gathering close,” or “in youth and manhood and age”—lend the language a ritual cadence. The prose nevertheless varies its pace through shorter declarations at moments of revelation, especially around the old man’s ambition and the discovery of his death.

Description is sensuous, decorative, and highly visual. The natural world is rendered through precise colours and rich materials: blue velvet, russet skin, green beryl, pale opal, ruby roses, and pearl-like lilies. Similes and metaphors often transform landscape into jewellery or sacred art, making the night seem fixed, precious, and nearly eternal. This aesthetic richness is not merely ornamental; it reinforces the story’s central tension between mortal transience and the dream of imperishability. The glow-worm is especially emblematic: amid a landscape that appears carved from precious stones, its moving flame alone seems alive and therefore perishable, “as mortal hope.”

Nature is also animated and spiritually charged. Birds, flowers, water, trees, dawn, and spring participate in the drama, often carrying meanings that the characters only partly understand. The climax arrives not through spectacle but through birdsong, sunlight, green shadows, flowers, and stillness. The “Song of the Immortal Powers” is implicitly displaced by the ordinary music of the natural world, particularly the thrush’s final song. This produces a quiet dramatic irony: the old man searches beyond life for immortality, while the story suggests that the sacred abundance he seeks has always been present in seasonal renewal, human affection, work, prayer, and lived experience.

The structure is simple and fable-like. An extended opening establishes the island, the ruined monastery, and the relationship between master and servant; a central confession reveals the old man’s lifelong quest; nocturnal preparations create expectation; and the dawn scene reverses that expectation through death rather than rejuvenation. The pacing is meditative, with little overt action. Suspense grows through atmosphere, prophecy, and ritual preparation rather than danger or conflict. The ending is restrained and elegiac, allowing the boy’s moral judgment and the thrush’s song to close the tale without an explicit authorial explanation.

The tone moves between wonder, melancholy, tenderness, and gentle admonition. Although the old man’s desire is hubristic, he is treated compassionately rather than satirically. His threadbare clothing, bodily frailty, loneliness, and immense hope make him tragic. The boy embodies practical fidelity, domestic aspiration, and religious humility; his imagined future of wife, children, cottage, and fields offers a mortal form of continuity against the old man’s solitary pursuit of endless youth. The style therefore combines fairy-tale enchantment with parable-like moral clarity, yet preserves ambiguity by granting the pagan world genuine beauty and power even as the old man’s attempt to master it fails.

Overall, the prose is ornate but controlled, symbolic without becoming abstract, and musical in both syntax and imagery. Its characteristic effects come from archaic diction, folkloric specificity, jewel-like natural description, rhythmic repetition, solemn dialogue, and an understated ironic conclusion. The result is a dreamlike literary fairy tale in which atmosphere and symbolism carry as much weight as plot.

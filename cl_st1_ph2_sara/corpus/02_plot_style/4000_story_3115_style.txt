The story is a comic bush yarn framed by a first-person motoring journey. Its narrative voice is urbane, observant and dryly ironic: the unnamed narrator presents himself as the comparatively reasonable passenger, but reports Alfred’s recklessness and casual destruction with exaggerated composure. This deadpan treatment of danger is central to the humour. Dogs are “exterminated,” a horse’s tail becomes a “trophy,” and crushed sheep are dismissed as “practically indestructible by violence.” Mock-heroic comparisons—Alfred steers “with the calm decision of a Napoleon,” while the car descends “as swiftly and noiselessly as an eagle”—inflate irresponsible driving into epic action.

The structure is that of a story within a story. The outer journey supplies movement, interruptions and comic parallels to Alfred’s account of Henery, while the embedded anecdote gradually works toward the promised elephant. Alfred repeatedly delays the revelation with background about Henery, John Bull and the ninety-horsepower car. The narrator’s impatient questions—especially about when the elephant will appear—acknowledge and exploit the leisurely conventions of oral storytelling. Suspense comes less from uncertainty about the broad outcome than from comic postponement, escalating speed and the eventual literalization of the phrase “three elephant-power.” After the apparent climax of Henery’s death, a short coda extends the joke through Scotty’s inheritance of the car and the elephant’s lasting fear.

Dialogue dominates, giving the piece the character of a performed anecdote. Alfred’s speech is rendered in strongly phonetic, colloquial Australian English: dropped initial consonants, nonstandard grammar and spellings such as “wot,” “’e,” “fust,” “districk,” “ketch” and “motorin’” establish class, region and oral rhythm. His vocabulary mixes bush slang and early motoring jargon—“wood and water joey,” “old cove,” “yokels,” “drops his clutch,” “lettin’ her out”—and assumes practical familiarity with machinery. Repetition, additive clauses and formulaic tags such as “he says” reproduce spontaneous speech. Alfred’s storytelling voice is confident, digressive and untroubled by absurdity, while the narrator’s standard English provides a polished counterpoint.

The prose alternates between Alfred’s loose, cumulative speech and the narrator’s more controlled descriptive sentences. Action passages often use long sentences packed with sequential clauses, creating the sensation of speed and barely avoided collision. Short interjections and questions interrupt that flow to sharpen timing. Mechanical detail is vivid but accessible: speed indicators, horsepower, clutch, accelerator, radiator and gears are not merely background objects but engines of plot and comedy. Cars are repeatedly personified as powerful, temperamental creatures, while animals are treated as obstacles or rival forms of transport. This reversal helps define the story’s modernizing world, where technological enthusiasm collides with the older rhythms of rural life.

Characterization is caricatural but precise. Alfred is introduced through paradox: “weirdly intelligent,” physically unimpressive, ageless in appearance, undereducated yet mechanically gifted. His professional stolidity contrasts with his ferocious driving, and his indifference to disaster is briefly complicated when he courteously salutes a little girl. Henery is Alfred’s amplified double—competitive, mechanically obsessed and incapable of resisting any challenge to his supremacy on the road. John Bull functions as the comic innocent: short-sighted, cautious and deferential to regulations, he remains unaware that his supposedly modest touring car is a racing machine. His description of Henery as “steady” and “trustworthy” after Henery’s spectacularly reckless death exemplifies the story’s sustained dramatic irony.

The humour combines understatement, hyperbole, incongruity and verbal payoff. Distances and dangers are extravagantly exaggerated, as in the cliff dropping “hundreds of thousands of feet,” while grotesque events are narrated as routine inconveniences. The central joke turns on a misunderstanding between mechanical “horse-power” and literal animal power. Further comedy comes from distorted perception: John Bull thinks overtaken cars are travelling toward him and mistakes extreme speed for a slight breach of the limit because Henery has falsified the indicator. The ending preserves this pattern of ignorance, since John Bull still does not know what car he owns and Scotty has no incentive to enlighten him.

The setting is recognizably Australian and early automotive: bush pubs, squatters, teamsters, sheep, billy-cans, home paddocks and rough mountain roads coexist with imported European racing cars. The story treats motoring as a new culture with its own status hierarchy, folklore and compulsions. Beneath the farce lies a satire of masculine competitiveness, mechanical vanity and the belief that technical skill excuses recklessness. Its moral world is deliberately elastic, however; consequences are converted into punchlines rather than occasions for solemn judgment. Overall, the style is energetic, anecdotal and theatrical, blending polished comic narration with vernacular oral performance, and using speed, repetition and delayed revelation to create the feel of a tall tale told by an unreliable but irresistibly assured raconteur.

A wealthy woman dying of consumption travels toward Italy, convinced that the climate will cure her. At a posting station, her husband and doctor privately admit that she is unlikely even to reach Moscow, but she angrily refuses to return home, unable to accept that she is dying.

At the same station, an impoverished, terminally ill driver named Theodore gives his new boots to a younger driver, Serega, in exchange for a promise that Serega will place a headstone on his grave. Theodore dies quietly that night and is buried behind the woods.

By spring, the wealthy woman has returned home and is near death. Although she receives the sacraments and briefly professes acceptance of God’s will, she still clings to the hope that a folk remedy might save her. She dies that evening and is given an elaborate funeral; within a month, a marble monument stands over her grave.

Theodore’s grave, meanwhile, remains marked only by grass because Serega has not kept his promise. Urged to provide at least a wooden cross, Serega goes into the forest and cuts down a young tree. As the tree falls and dies, the surrounding forest fills with sunlight, birdsong, and renewed life, linking the deaths of the aristocrat, the laborer, and the tree within nature’s continuing cycle.

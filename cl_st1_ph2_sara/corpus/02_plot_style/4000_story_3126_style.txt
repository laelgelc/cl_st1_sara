The story adopts the manner of an orally transmitted northern folktale, combining a communal narrative voice with the grave simplicity of legend. It opens through a frame narrative centred on Urda Bjarnason, an ancient Icelandic matriarch whose wisdom and supernatural sight establish her authority as storyteller. Her tale of the dead weaver is then presented almost without interruption, creating a story-within-a-story that links Icelandic immigrants on the North Dakota plains to the sagas and beliefs of their homeland. The frame also stresses storytelling as a collective ritual: men gather around the stove, drink coffee, listen to “pure old Icelandic,” and preserve cultural memory through speech.

The narrator is third-person and broadly omniscient, but often sounds like a member of the community rather than a detached observer. Generalisations such as “it stands to reason” and references to what “the men” know give the narration a confident, communal authority. Supernatural events are reported with the same composure as domestic facts, so prophecy and ghostly visitation never require elaborate justification. This produces a world in which folklore is not opposed to reality but forms part of ordinary experience. Mild irony appears in the treatment of modern America, fashionable habits, intellectual argument, and the “pert” Ingeborg, yet the dominant attitude toward Urda and the old traditions is reverential.

The diction blends plain storytelling with archaic and biblical colouring. Expressions such as “’tis,” “ye,” “aye,” “bade,” “wraith,” “behold,” and “having eyes ye will not see” evoke saga, scripture, and fireside recitation. In contrast, concrete domestic vocabulary—cloth, warp, woof, quilts, baskets, fish, coffee, stove, coins—keeps the tale physically grounded. Icelandic names and references to part singing, sagas, lava fields, northern seas, and immigrant settlements give the prose a strong cultural specificity. Occasional unusual compounds and older spellings, including “steelblue,” “blueeyed,” and “a-cold,” reinforce its period character.

The syntax alternates between expansive descriptive sentences and short, declarative statements. Longer sentences accumulate clauses and repeated conjunctions in a rhythm suited to oral performance, while blunt lines such as “But one day their mother died” deliver emotional turns with restraint. Repetition is a major stylistic device. Phrases such as “she knew not why,” “always they had to work,” and the repeated account of the ghost’s second appearance create incantatory momentum and suggest a tale preserved through many retellings. Parallel structures, triads, and formulaic transitions—“One day,” “All in the cold and still of that night,” “In the early morning”—further imitate traditional folklore.

Description is vivid but selective, relying especially on colour, light, cold, and movement. The landscapes are rendered through pink snow, golden flashes, blue shadows, silver wave-tips, black lava, and the deep gleam of the sea. Similes draw repeatedly on death and the northern environment: the sea resembles “the eye of a dead witch,” mist resembles blindness, the stepmother’s brow is a storm, and the ghost moves like the Northern Lights. Beauty and menace are closely joined. The spectral cloth is simultaneously “hateful” and “beautiful,” with a mystical blue warp and shining white woof; this visual contradiction captures the ghost’s role as both loving mother and terrifying judge.

Dialogue is sparse, direct, and functional. It identifies character, stages conflicts between scepticism and inherited belief, and gives moral statements memorable force. Characters tend to be types rather than psychologically intricate individuals: the wise old seer, the vulnerable children, the dead protective mother, and the cruel stepmother. Nevertheless, telling physical details—half-frozen fingers, purple feet, thin garments, the stepmother’s immobilised tongue—make their suffering and fear immediate. Emotion is usually conveyed through action and image rather than introspection.

The tone moves from affectionate ethnographic observation in the frame to melancholy, pity, dread, and eventual reassurance in the embedded tale. The pacing is leisurely at first, allowing the community and winter landscape to accumulate authority, but becomes progressively more concentrated once the children are mistreated. The haunting is narrated through repetition rather than escalation: the second visitation reproduces the first until fear compels reform. The resolution is morally clear but comparatively restrained. The stepmother is not destroyed; she is frightened into providing warmth, clothing, and peace.

Overall, the style combines local-colour realism, saga-like cadence, fairy-tale structure, and restrained Gothic supernaturalism. Its central motifs—seeing, weaving, cold, maternal care, and inherited speech—bind form to theme. Weaving becomes an emblem of both domestic love and storytelling itself: the dead mother weaves protection for her children, while Urda weaves communal memory into a tale. The result is solemn, rhythmic, visually luminous prose whose apparent simplicity conceals careful patterning and a strong moral confidence.

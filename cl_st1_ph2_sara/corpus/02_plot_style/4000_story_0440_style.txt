The story adopts the manner of a mock historical chronicle: it recounts recognisable royal and ecclesiastical events with an air of documentary authority while transforming them into a bawdy comic anecdote. Dates, titles, genealogies, place names, court offices, papal procedures, and formulas such as “the above-mentioned captain” give the narrative a pseudo-scholarly surface. This gravity is repeatedly undercut by the treatment of political captivity as chiefly a problem of sexual deprivation. The resulting style blends courtly history, fabliau, ribald Renaissance tale, and invented family legend.

The narrator is conspicuous, worldly, and mischievously partisan. He speaks as though preserving inherited national history, refers familiarly to “our” kings and country, appeals to readers who supposedly know the monarch’s character, and reserves other anecdotes for later narration. His voice combines reverence for rank with irreverence toward individual conduct. Francis is at once “his majesty,” a sacred king, and “a ribald fellow.” This tension enables the narrator to mock monarchy, clerical authority, marriage, and patriotic honour without abandoning the ceremonial language attached to them. Moral judgement is largely theatrical: the closing “morality” begins like a conventional lesson but expands into extravagant political commentary and a digression on Mary, Queen of Scots.

The diction is deliberately archaic and ceremonious. Honorifics, feudal terminology, ecclesiastical vocabulary, and old-fashioned oaths—“Sire,” “By’r lady,” “fief,” “vespers,” “legate,” “papal brief,” “high and low jurisdiction”—create an antique atmosphere. Elevated abstractions such as “the salvation of the queen’s soul” coexist with earthy euphemisms including “amorous exercise,” “warm flesh,” “soft cataplasm,” and “the sin of the thing.” Sexual acts are rarely named directly. Instead, they are rendered through culinary, medical, military, religious, and alchemical metaphors. Desire becomes an illness requiring a “specific”; lovemaking is a “terrible duel”; physical intimacy resembles kneading dough; absolution is imagined as sponging a mark from a slate. This euphemistic abundance is central to the comedy, allowing the prose to remain ornate while being unmistakably indecent.

The sentences are generally long, cumulative, and loosely periodic. Clauses are linked through semicolons, participial phrases, relative constructions, and repeated conjunctions, often carrying an idea through several comic qualifications before reaching its point. The syntax imitates oral storytelling and old chronicle prose more than modern narrative economy. Phrases such as “from one thing to another,” “for the matter of that,” and “in short” simulate a narrator thinking aloud. Repetition intensifies the mock solemnity, while occasional awkward constructions and irregular wording reinforce the impression of an antiquated or translated text. Short lines of dialogue interrupt these elaborate sentences at decisive moments and deliver the sharpest comic reversals.

The humour depends heavily on incongruity, escalation, and delayed revelation. Sacred institutions are mobilised to facilitate adultery; a pope’s indulgence is treated as practical preparation for incestuous temptation; patriotic rivalry is settled through sexual performance; and a captain risks his wife to win a French estate. Hyperbole governs both desire and description. Francis may die from abstinence, a lover may draw his soul from his body, and a desirable woman is capable of beautifying hell. Similes are vivid, physical, and deliberately undignified: the captive king is “like a silly bird,” royal confinement is compared with a cat folding lace, and the Spanish embrace resembles a baker labouring at his trough. The prose frequently builds magnificent rhetorical scaffolding around crude bodily jokes.

Characterisation is broad, energetic, and functional rather than psychologically subtle. Francis is defined by appetite, vitality, gallantry, and quick wit; the captain by poverty, ambition, honour, and opportunism; the women by sexual power, patriotic feeling, or amused practicality. Their traits are amplified to near-caricature. Female desire is presented with unusual frankness, although women are also treated as instruments in male competition and political exchange. The second Spanish woman receives a sensuous catalogue of features—white skin, long hair, velvet hands, plumpness, laughing mouth, moist eyes—whose accumulating detail pauses the plot and prepares the wager’s climax.

Dialogue is formal in address but brisk in dramatic effect. Speakers often begin with elaborate courtesy before moving into provocation, bargaining, or sexual boastfulness. Francis’s compact declaration, “Done,” abruptly converts the captain’s challenge into a feudal contract. The final exchange—“How so?” followed by “Sire, it was my wife”—is the tale’s cleanest example of comic timing: the syntax contracts as the revelation approaches, and the entire episode pivots on one understated answer. National stereotypes structure the preceding debate, with French love represented as graceful, inexhaustible, and refined, while Spanish love is figured as fervent, religiously intensified, and violent. These claims are playful rather than analytical, serving the wager and its patriotic comedy.

Structurally, the narrative proceeds by successive complications. It opens with the king’s capture and melancholy, expands into the French court’s proposed remedy, shifts to Francis’s private negotiations in Spain, introduces the captain’s wager, stages two erotic trials, and culminates in the revelation of the second woman’s identity. An etiological coda then claims to explain the origin and corruption of the family name Lara-y-Lopez into Larray. This mock explanation gives the scandalous anecdote the shape of local history. The final moralising passage is intentionally disproportionate, moving from advice to kings toward a vehement defence of royal women and a denunciation of Elizabeth I.

Overall, the style is exuberant, digressive, sensual, and satirical. It treats history as a storehouse of scandal, uses learned and courtly language to dignify appetite, and converts political and religious authority into material for comic storytelling. Its signature effect comes from the collision between elevated narration and bodily subject matter: the more solemnly the narrator invokes monarchy, theology, honour, and national destiny, the more forcefully the underlying farce emerges.

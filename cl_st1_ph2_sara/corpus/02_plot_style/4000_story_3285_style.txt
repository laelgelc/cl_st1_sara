The story adopts the manner of late-Victorian or Edwardian regional realism, combining a carefully composed literary narrator with strongly dialectal rural speech. Its setting is rendered through concrete domestic and agricultural detail: old furniture, bare boards, firelight, wet yards, farm buildings, clothing, food and household routines all establish a materially convincing world. Description is selective but substantial, often making physical surroundings carry emotional or historical significance. The opening room, for example, is personified as a face marked by generations of birth and death, while the altered emptiness of the kitchen externalises ’Lizabeth’s awareness that the household has lost its master. Fire, candlelight, darkness, rain and silence recur as atmospheric motifs, producing a restrained Gothic tension without displacing the story’s social realism.

The narrator speaks in polished, measured prose, with balanced clauses, semicolons and antithetical formulations. Sentences often begin from an observed fact and then broaden into reflection, as in the contrast between the room’s “meanness” and “distinction.” The diction has a slightly archaic, formal cast—“querulously,” “inconsequent,” “pervading,” “lachrymose,” “egregious”—and the narrator occasionally supplies dryly judgmental characterisations. These interventions create an ironic distance, especially when describing William’s shallow handsomeness or Mrs. Transom’s pretensions. Humour is therefore present, but it is usually sardonic and character-based rather than comic in a light sense. The narrator can be compassionate toward suffering while remaining unsentimental about folly, vanity and self-deception.

The point of view is third-person and broadly omniscient, though it stays closest to ’Lizabeth. Her perceptions organise much of the setting and determine the emotional weight of events, while the narrator occasionally steps beyond her knowledge to clarify motives, judge conduct or summarise later developments. Psychological states are rarely analysed at length. Instead, they emerge through bodily action, silence, domestic tasks and compressed remarks: ’Lizabeth loads the pistol, makes tea, handles the kettle, burns the will and shells peas. Such practical gestures become emotional equivalents, allowing the prose to convey repression and inner conflict indirectly. This method suits a protagonist whose reserve, endurance and moral severity are central to her character.

Dialogue occupies a large proportion of the story and is the principal engine of characterisation and conflict. Rural dialect is represented through dropped consonants, altered pronouns and verb forms, contractions and regional vocabulary: “do ’ee,” “you’m,” “main sorry,” “naught,” “ain’t,” and “reckon.” The dialect is consistent enough to establish place and class, but remains readable. Speech rhythms are blunt, repetitive and often adversarial. Characters question, interrupt, evade and reinterpret one another, so that emotional truths surface through confrontation rather than confession. ’Lizabeth’s language is usually terse and controlled, becoming more elevated and rhythmically forceful at moments of moral crisis. William’s speech, by contrast, mixes swagger, threats, coarse joking and self-serving sentiment; Mrs. Transom’s is garrulous, affected and weakly theatrical. Differences in idiom thus reveal temperament and social aspiration.

The narrative structure is strongly dramatic. It opens at a deathbed, moves into a suspense sequence built around the rattling latch, introduces William as a possible threat, and then overturns expectations by revealing first his wife and then his search through the dead man’s chest. Revelations arrive in stages, each intensifying the moral conflict. The burning of the will functions as the central symbolic and dramatic climax. Afterward, the story accelerates through summary before returning to a fully staged final encounter between ’Lizabeth and William. This later scene mirrors and revises the earlier emotional situation: she once desired him and was rejected, whereas he now proposes and is calmly refused. The ending then shifts into a comic-ironic coda in which the Hoopers misunderstand the depth of ’Lizabeth’s decision, reducing tragedy and moral self-knowledge to folk wisdom about courtship.

Pacing alternates between slow atmospheric observation and rapid dialogue. Suspense is generated through pauses, small sounds and withheld information rather than overt action. The repeated silences around the deathbed, the door and the chest are especially important. Transitions often hinge on an ordinary object—the kettle, pistol, lantern, will or pea pod—which grounds emotional upheaval in physical action. The prose becomes most compressed at decisive moments: “His lids were closed … but he was quite dead,” or the quiet finality of “Good-bye, William.” This restraint prevents the story’s melodramatic materials—death, inheritance, betrayal, concealed marriage and renunciation—from becoming overwrought.

Figurative language is vivid but controlled. Similes frequently expose character: William’s complexion recalls prize beef, his wife collapses “like a half-empty bag,” and her finery is likened to “gingerbread things.” Such images are tactile, rural and faintly cruel. Symbolism remains closely tied to the domestic world. The fire that destroys the will signifies both renunciation and self-punishment; the dead man’s silent face offers no moral guidance; the closed heart and its “clasps” suggest emotional self-sealing; and the peas passed through ’Lizabeth’s fingers during William’s proposal embody her composure and detachment. Seasonal contrasts also shape the emotional arc: the main crisis occurs during a dark, wet Christmas, while the refusal takes place on a warm June morning, when clarity has replaced confusion but renewal does not lead to romance.

The governing tone is grave, ironic and unsentimental. Moral seriousness coexists with scepticism about conventional ideas of justice, inheritance and marriage. The story admires ’Lizabeth’s integrity and strength while leaving room to question the extremity of her sacrifice. Its central effects come from dramatic irony: William mistakes moral disgust for jealousy, his wife mistakes renunciation for romantic defeat, and the Hoopers mistake hard-won self-knowledge for foolishness. The ending refuses a conventionally restorative marriage and instead closes on the gap between outward behaviour and inward truth. Overall, the style blends regional colour, domestic realism, subdued Gothic atmosphere, sharp social observation and tightly controlled melodrama, using irony and indirect psychological presentation to give a seemingly simple rural tale considerable moral complexity.

The story uses a lucid, omniscient third-person narrator who moves freely between external action, social observation, and intimate access to Phatik’s emotional life. The voice initially treats childhood mischief with amused irony, presenting Phatik as a village “ringleader” whose authority is mock-regal and whose schemes resemble miniature public dramas. As the story progresses, this light comic detachment gives way to tenderness and pathos. The narrator openly interprets characters and events rather than remaining invisible, occasionally pausing the action to offer broad reflections on adolescence, affection, exclusion, and the awkward position of a fourteen-year-old boy.

The diction is formal, literary, and slightly archaic, with phrases such as “by main force,” “carried unanimously,” “critical juncture,” and “his joy knew no bounds.” This elevated language is often applied humorously to childish behaviour: a prank becomes a “manœuvre,” Phatik’s pride becomes “regal dignity,” and the opening quarrel is called “the first act of the drama.” Such mock-heroic phrasing creates gentle comedy without wholly belittling the children. Elsewhere, the vocabulary becomes emotionally direct and elegiac, especially in descriptions of homesickness, neglect, fever, and maternal longing. The prose has the texture of an older literary translation or colonial-era English rendering, combining Indian names, kinship terms such as “Dada,” and Bengali social settings with conventional English idioms and classical allusions.

Sentence structure varies according to function. The action scenes favour clear chronological clauses and compact transitions, while reflective passages employ longer, balanced sentences, parallel constructions, and cumulative lists. The extended meditation on fourteen-year-old boys is aphoristic and generalising, repeatedly contrasting what the boy needs with how adults perceive him. Repetition strengthens the emotional logic: he is “neither ornamental nor useful,” he is condemned whether childish or grown-up, and he longs for love precisely when affection is withheld. This expository interruption slows the plot but deepens the reader’s sympathy by turning Phatik’s private suffering into a universal account of adolescence.

Dialogue is brief, natural, and socially revealing. Phatik’s early speech is defiant and laconic—“Go and find out”—while his later questions become vulnerable and repetitive: “When can I go home?” and “Have the holidays come yet?” Adults often speak in accusations, commands, or practical judgements, exposing how little attention they give to his inner state. The aunt’s contempt is conveyed through blunt labels such as “clumsy, country lout,” whereas the mother’s final repetition of “my darling” arrives too late. Dialogue therefore traces Phatik’s movement from noisy resistance to helpless dependence.

The story is structured as a sharp tonal descent. Its opening is energetic, physical, and comic, built around the rolling log, the boys’ shouting, and the brothers’ fight. The arrival of the uncle creates a hinge between village freedom and urban confinement. In Calcutta, the pace becomes slower and more oppressive, organised through recurring humiliations rather than adventurous incidents. The final section accelerates through the lost book, fever, disappearance, police recovery, delirium, and the mother’s arrival. Transitions are economical, and foreshadowing is understated: Phatik’s eagerness to leave home, his gift of treasured possessions, and his repeated longing for the holidays all acquire tragic significance retrospectively.

Setting functions symbolically as well as realistically. The village is associated with open space, riverbanks, meadows, swimming, kites, noise, and bodily freedom; Calcutta is defined by walls, cramped interiors, distant roofs, school discipline, and suffocation. This opposition mirrors Phatik’s loss of agency. Natural imagery is sensuous and expansive when he remembers home, whereas the city is enclosed and airless. Rain dominates the climactic episode, intensifying his exposure and misery. River imagery returns in his delirium through the sailor’s depth calls—“By the mark”—which become a metaphor for death as an “unfathomable sea.”

Figurative language blends humour, classical reference, animal imagery, and lyrical symbolism. Mākhan rises “blind as Fate and screaming like the Furies,” while the punished schoolboy suffers “like an overladen ass.” Phatik is compared to a stray dog, and his longing for his mother to the lowing of a calf at twilight. These animal comparisons stress instinctive need, vulnerability, and the bodily nature of attachment. The narrator’s metaphors grow progressively darker: home is “Paradise,” the strange household is “torture,” and emotional neglect becomes an atmosphere in which the boy can scarcely breathe.

The dominant emotional method is restrained sentiment gradually intensified by irony. Phatik’s mother considers him troublesome while he secretly longs for her; he interprets escape as a journey home, but the police return him to the place of his suffering; and the “holidays” he awaits finally arrive only as death approaches. The last line converts an ordinary schoolboy’s hope into a quiet euphemism for release. Because the ending avoids explicit explanation, its force comes from accumulated repetition and symbolic closure rather than melodramatic commentary.

Overall, the style combines comic realism, authorial reflection, social criticism, and tragic sentiment. It is compassionate toward adolescent awkwardness and sharply attentive to the damage caused by casual adult rejection. Its signature qualities are an intrusive but humane narrator, mock-heroic humour, formal and occasionally archaic diction, strong rural–urban contrasts, concise dialogue, lyrical homesickness, and a final symbolic convergence of homecoming, holiday, and death.

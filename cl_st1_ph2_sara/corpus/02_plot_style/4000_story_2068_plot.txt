Three middle-aged farmers and Civil War veterans—John Stover, Henry Merrill, and Asa Brown—realize that their town has never properly observed Decoration Day. Though planting season is busy and only nine of Barlow’s original soldiers remain, they organize a procession to honor their dead comrades, including neglected paupers Eben Munson and John Tighe.

On Decoration Day, eight veterans march in their worn uniforms behind a fife and drum, while the severely disabled Martin Tighe rides in a wagon. Nearly the whole town follows. The veterans salute an elderly woman who sent sons and grandsons to war, place flags and flowers on soldiers’ graves, and are overcome when Martin’s son plays taps. Their humble parade awakens an unexpected sense of patriotism, gratitude, and community throughout Barlow.

That evening, the three friends celebrate the day’s success and plan to preserve local wartime memories for younger generations. They also discover that Martha Down, who abandoned Eb Munson while he was at war and was blamed for his later decline, secretly placed flowers on his grave and now wants to pay for his headstone. Recognizing her remorse, they reconsider their harsh judgment of her. The day leaves them proud of their service and assured that Barlow has finally found its public spirit.

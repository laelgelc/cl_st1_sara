The story uses a warm, comic-realist style that punctures theatrical glamour while treating ordinary loneliness with genuine sympathy. Its opening establishes the governing contrast between the artificial “leading lady” familiar from the stage and the exhausted woman underneath the role. Repeated constructions such as “not as you have seen leading ladies” expose melodramatic conventions and replace them with awkward physical detail: swollen eyes, sniffles, a bitten pillowcase, a lumpy hotel pillow, and an unbecoming kimono. This anti-romantic method creates humor without trivializing the woman’s distress.

The narration is third-person omniscient, with a conspicuous, conversational narrator who freely comments on characters, local customs, gender relations, and the conventions of popular entertainment. The voice often addresses the reader directly through phrases such as “as you have seen” and “you take a…girl,” giving the story the air of an anecdote told by an observant, amused insider. It also shifts comfortably into a communal first-person plural—“our town,” “our girls,” “our boys”—so that the setting is filtered through the affectionate irony of a small-town resident. The narrator knows both the town’s habits and its hypocrisies, but the satire remains genial rather than cruel.

The prose alternates between brisk narrative movement and densely comic description. Rooms, clothes, and bodies are rendered through exaggerated but concrete comparisons: the green enamel bed resembles “a huge frog,” the wallpaper is “an army of tan mustard plasters climbing up a chocolate-fudge wall,” and Pearlie regards the leading lady “as a mastiff might look at a terrier.” Such similes are domestic, bodily, and deliberately unrefined. Personification adds to the effect: the pillow has a “white bosom,” the sun “smote” the heroine’s eyes, and the offending hotel room seems almost actively hostile. The imagery repeatedly contrasts heat, glare, ugliness, and confinement with shade, color, food, sociability, and evening light.

Sentence structure is varied and performance-oriented. Short fragments and abrupt qualifications deliver comic emphasis, while longer accumulative sentences catalogue clothing, behavior, or social types. Parallelism and repetition are especially important: the recurring title-like phrase “the leading lady” initially keeps the protagonist inside her public role, even while the narrator reveals her private self. As she becomes socially restored, she acquires other identities—“Miss Evans,” “Aimee Fox,” friend, woman—and the phrase becomes less constricting. Dashes, exclamations, interruptions, and suspended sentences reproduce emotional pressure and quick changes of thought. Parenthetical asides and delayed punch lines reinforce the narrator’s comic timing.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization. Pearlie’s speech is vigorous, colloquial, and practical, filled with contractions, nonstandard grammar, slang, homely comparisons, and abrupt changes of subject: “ain’t,” “steno,” “cottoned to me,” “My land!” Her language marks her social setting but, more importantly, presents her as perceptive, competent, and emotionally intelligent. The bell-boy’s description of Pearlie relies on comic vernacular and stereotypes, yet it also identifies exactly why she is the “real woman” the actress needs. The leading lady’s speech moves from hysteria and confession to animated domestic enthusiasm, showing recovery through rhythm and subject matter. Sid Strang’s polite eagerness, meanwhile, is lightly mocked through conventional courtship dialogue.

The humor depends on incongruity, reversal, and social observation. A desperate emotional crisis is relieved not by romance or grand revelation but by discussion of a corset-cover pattern, handmade lingerie, photographs, curls, and exercises for reducing the hips. This domestic exchange is presented not as trivial chatter but as life-saving intimacy. Likewise, the supposedly unsophisticated strawberry social becomes more restorative than metropolitan theatrical society. The church festival is gently satirized—particularly its strategic respectability, flirtation, and frantic fundraising—but it also functions as a genuine community.

Structurally, the story traces a clear movement from isolation to participation. It begins in a closed, overheated hotel room, expands through the arrival of Pearlie, and then opens onto the communal lawn beneath Japanese lanterns. The heroine’s transformation is shown visually as well as emotionally: tear-stained kimono gives way to white dress, pink slip, cosmetics, scarf, and public radiance. Yet the ending complicates the apparent Cinderella pattern. Instead of allowing the town’s young men to preserve their fantasy of “Ethel Evans,” the actress reveals her professional identity, age, and imminent departure. The final disclosure is both a punch line and an assertion of control: she has enjoyed being desired, but she authors the last scene herself.

The dominant tone combines affectionate satire, humane comedy, and restrained pathos. The story is alert to the limited freedoms available to women, especially women who travel alone. Men may smoke, play pool, attend shows, and approach strangers, while a woman attempting the same independence risks being judged “tough.” Female companionship therefore becomes the emotional center of the narrative. Pearlie’s practical solidarity restores the actress more effectively than male admiration does, although the latter supplies festive confirmation of her renewed vitality.

Overall, the style is socially observant, theatrical, vernacular, and strongly visual. It draws energy from early twentieth-century popular culture, fashion, hotel life, touring theatre, church socials, and small-town courtship rituals. Its signature technique is to set stage illusion against material reality, then show that ordinary communal performance—dressing up, adopting a harmless alias, selling strawberries, flirting beneath lanterns—can be both artificial and emotionally authentic.

The story adopts the style of a nineteenth-century historical moral narrative, combining documentary chronicle, devotional exemplum, and dramatic anecdote. Its subject is treated not with detached historical neutrality but through a firmly declared ethical and religious perspective. The French Revolution is presented as collective delirium: Paris becomes an “abyss of horror,” the populace a mob of “ruffians,” “butchers,” and “assassins,” and revolutionary violence a “bloody work.” Against this darkness, fidelity, charity, courage, filial devotion, and Christian constancy are made to “shine out.” The governing contrast is therefore between mass savagery and individual heroism, with suffering interpreted as a trial that reveals moral character and, ultimately, leads the virtuous toward heavenly glory.

The narrator is omniscient, authoritative, and openly interventionist. Historical events, dates, names, offices, prison populations, and locations give the account a documentary foundation, but the narrator continually interprets their meaning for the reader. Phrases such as “Think of the Swiss Guards,” “we will mention,” and “as we look into that abyss of horror” create a communal relationship between narrator and audience, guiding emotional and moral response rather than leaving judgment implicit. The voice assumes that loyalty to family, monarchy, and God is intrinsically admirable. It also occasionally anticipates objections or distinguishes report from legend, as in the discussion of whether Marie de Sombreuil actually drank blood. This lends the narrative a qualified appearance of historical conscientiousness without weakening its emotional force.

The tone is elevated, solemn, indignant, and elegiac. Horror is conveyed through repeated superlatives and intensifiers—“most fearful,” “surpassing horror,” “more dreadful,” “fearfully close,” “absolutely frantic”—while virtue is described in reverential language. The resulting emotional register is deliberately extreme: violence is infernal and degrading, whereas sacrifice is noble, pure, and spiritually transcendent. Pathos is especially concentrated around vulnerable figures such as elderly fathers, devoted daughters, persecuted priests, and loyal female friends. Grey hair, illness, youth, beauty, helplessness, and familial attachment are repeatedly foregrounded to heighten the cruelty of the threatened or actual deaths.

The diction is formal and markedly moralised. Abstract nouns such as “oppression,” “injustice,” “vengeance,” “devotion,” “heroism,” “fidelity,” and “benevolence” establish a language of ethical judgment, while religious terms—“pious,” “Scripture,” “blessings of suffering,” “commended his soul to God,” and “weight of glory”—frame history providentially. The prose also contains characteristically period-specific expressions such as “at one fell swoop,” “by and by,” “whither,” “tidings,” and “little divining.” French titles, institutional names, and place names supply historical colour, but they are usually explained for a general readership.

Sentence structure is expansive and cumulative. Long sentences link background, motive, action, and judgment through coordination and multiple subordinate clauses. This creates the movement of a narrated chronicle in which one horror or act of courage leads into another. Periodic sentences often postpone the decisive outcome, increasing suspense, while short sentences and fragments are reserved for moments of crisis: “The murderer dropped his pike”; “Swear! If not, you are dead.” Repetition also drives the rhetoric. Words such as “dreadful,” “horrible,” “murderers,” and “saved” recur insistently, and parallel formulations—particularly daughters standing between their fathers and the executioners—turn separate incidents into variations on a single moral pattern.

The structure moves from broad historical overview to a sequence of tightly focused episodes. It begins with an interpretive account of revolutionary vengeance, briefly commemorates the Swiss Guards, then narrows to the September prison massacres. From there it follows overlapping stories centred on Sicard, Monnot, Marie de Sombreuil, Elizabeth Cazotte, Madame de Fausse Lendry, and the Princess de Lamballe. Dates and transitions maintain chronology, but the selection of incidents is thematic rather than comprehensive: each episode demonstrates loyalty, self-sacrifice, piety, or mercy amid violence. The closing return to Cazotte and Sombreuil gives the story a sombre aftermath, showing that temporary rescue does not guarantee worldly deliverance.

Scenes are frequently dramatised through direct speech. Appeals, threats, declarations of loyalty, and cries from the mob are quoted at decisive moments, making ethical choices immediate and theatrical. Characters reveal themselves through concise utterances: Sicard commends himself to God, Monnot offers his own body as a barrier, Lamballe refuses to swear hatred, and Cazotte dies professing fidelity to God and king. Dialogue is less psychologically naturalistic than emblematic; it crystallises the virtue or vice each person represents. Crowds likewise speak collectively, shifting abruptly from condemnation to pardon and thereby embodying unstable, irrational popular power.

Poetic quotation and biblical allusion raise the register further. Verse appears at moments when the narrator wishes to connect historical suffering with spiritual illumination or charitable achievement. Images such as the cross brightening amid war, the “bridge of signs” joining isolated minds, and death carrying the faithful “from earth to heaven” interpret events symbolically. The final phrase “a more exceeding weight of glory” echoes Scripture and confirms the story’s providential logic: murderers can destroy the body, but their violence unwittingly magnifies the spiritual triumph of their victims.

Characterisation is idealising and economical. Virtuous figures are defined by one dominant quality—filial devotion, pastoral charity, friendship, loyalty, or faith—and are often introduced through visually affecting details such as Cazotte’s bald forehead and grey hair or Lamballe’s fair ringlets. The antagonists are largely undifferentiated members of the mob, described through bodily action, clothing, weapons, drunkenness, and animalistic excitement. Individual exceptions, especially Monnot and the sentry who warns the women, show that moral courage can emerge from within the revolutionary crowd, but the broader social portrait remains sharply polarised.

Overall, the style is didactic, emotionally charged, and commemorative. It seeks to preserve exemplary deeds rather than investigate political complexity or interior ambiguity. Historical detail provides credibility; sensational violence provides urgency; direct speech provides drama; and Christian moral commentary supplies the final meaning. The characteristic effect is that of a historical tale written to instruct and inspire, transforming episodes from revolutionary terror into narratives of martyrdom, loyalty, and self-sacrificing love.

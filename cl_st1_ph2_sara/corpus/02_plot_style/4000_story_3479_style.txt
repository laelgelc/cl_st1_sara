The story adopts the form of an eighteenth-century political and economic pamphlet, using a first-person public-minded “projector” who presents himself as rational, informed, modest, and disinterested. Its central method is sustained verbal irony: the speaker calmly advances a monstrous proposal while treating it as a practical act of benevolence. The horror lies less in explicit emotional description than in the discrepancy between the proposal’s brutality and the narrator’s composed, reasonable manner. This deadpan incongruity produces a tone that is simultaneously urbane, bureaucratic, grotesque, and savagely comic.

The narrative voice is formal and authoritative, repeatedly strengthened by assurances, calculations, alleged expert testimony, and professions of humility. Phrases such as “I humbly propose,” “I have been assured,” “I have already computed,” and “I profess, in the sincerity of my heart” imitate the conventions of serious reform writing. The persona carefully anticipates objections, acknowledges minor qualifications, cites acquaintances and authorities, and claims to have weighed alternative schemes. These gestures create an appearance of intellectual fairness, even as they expose the speaker’s moral blindness. His self-presentation as a patriot and humanitarian is therefore deeply unreliable: every claim to compassion intensifies the satire.

The diction combines elevated civic language with the vocabularies of commerce, agriculture, animal husbandry, cookery, and butchery. Human beings are progressively converted into “breeders,” “commodities,” “carcasses,” “stock,” and “dishes,” while children are discussed in terms appropriate to livestock: weight, fattening, seasonality, breeding ratios, market price, and culinary preparation. This systematic dehumanisation is the story’s most important stylistic mechanism. Euphemisms such as “provide for,” “public consideration,” “collateral advantage,” and “ease the nation” conceal violence beneath administrative politeness. Conversely, blunt material details—quarters of flesh, dressed skin, shambles, knives, and cooking methods—periodically puncture the abstract language and force the literal horror back into view.

The syntax is elaborate, periodic, and heavily subordinated. Long sentences accumulate clauses, qualifications, parenthetical remarks, numerical estimates, and causal links, creating the impression of exhaustive logical analysis. Semicolons and colons often organise chains of reasoning, while balanced constructions and parallel phrases give the prose an oratorical rhythm. The frequent use of connective terms such as “therefore,” “whereas,” “for,” “thus,” and “consequently” mimics deductive argument. Yet the apparent logic rests on an ethically absurd premise, so the precision of the reasoning becomes part of the joke. The speaker’s calculations are presented with enough specificity to sound empirical, but their pseudo-scientific exactness reveals how quantitative thinking can erase individual suffering.

Structurally, the piece resembles a formal policy proposal. It begins with a melancholy survey of poverty, broadens the scope of the social problem, estimates the affected population, dismisses existing remedies, and then unveils the central scheme. It proceeds through practical details, pricing, culinary uses, objections, refinements, and a numbered catalogue of alleged advantages. Near the end, the speaker rejects genuine reforms in an extended anaphoric sequence beginning repeatedly with “Of,” thereby allowing serious alternatives—fair taxation, domestic manufacture, moderation, patriotism, mercy, and honest trade—to emerge through ironic negation. The final declaration of personal disinterest parodies the conventional conclusion of a public-spirited tract and seals the narrator’s grotesque pose of integrity.

The humour is predominantly dark and satirical. Understatement is especially important: possible cruelty is described as merely “a little bordering upon cruelty,” and mass death from hunger and exposure is treated as a convenient release from future difficulties. Parenthetical qualifications often sharpen this effect by inserting shocking observations as if they were minor technical points. Comparisons between women and breeding animals, landlords and devourers, or infants and roast pigs create bitter figurative ironies. The line suggesting that landlords have already “devoured” the parents briefly converts an economic metaphor into the literal premise of the proposal, exposing exploitation as a form of social cannibalism.

Emotion is tightly controlled. The opening invokes pity through images of ragged mothers and children, and the narrator occasionally uses sentimental expressions such as “poor innocent babes,” but these moments are rapidly absorbed into calculation. This unstable mixture of compassion and commodification makes the voice disturbing: humanitarian language survives, but its moral content has been emptied out. The actual authorial anger is displaced into irony rather than stated directly. Readers are expected to infer the humane position by recoiling from the persona’s inhuman conclusions.

The social world is rendered through class- and sect-conscious language. “Persons of quality,” landlords, squires, merchants, tavern keepers, laborers, cottagers, beggars, Protestants, and Catholics are treated as economic or political categories. The speaker reproduces contemporary prejudices, especially anti-Catholic rhetoric, in order to reveal the viciousness of policies that reduce populations to obstacles or resources. Topical references to Ireland, England, the Pretender, emigration, rents, tithes, and absentee landlords ground the fantasy in concrete political conditions. The result is not realistic storytelling in a conventional scene-based sense, but argumentative fiction: character, conflict, and revelation are carried by a fabricated public voice.

The style is also marked by cumulative escalation. The proposal moves from an initially abstract claim to increasingly specific and repellent applications, including recipes, clothing made from skin, markets, tavern trade, and public feasts. Each new detail is delivered with the same composure, preventing tonal release and sustaining the conceit to the end. The speaker’s occasional digressions and courteous concessions make the performance feel spontaneous, yet the progression is tightly controlled: economic rationality is pushed step by step until its concealed violence becomes impossible to ignore.

Overall, the piece’s style can be characterised as mock-rational, formally polemical, numerically obsessive, and relentlessly ironic. Its signature effect comes from the collision of polished civility with moral atrocity. To reproduce this profile, a writer would use a confident but unreliable reformer as narrator, frame the work as a practical public proposal, employ long and logically signposted sentences, mix bureaucratic euphemism with disturbingly concrete details, quantify human misery, anticipate objections with ceremonious fairness, and maintain absolute seriousness while allowing the reader to perceive the argument’s ethical insanity. The archaic forms—such as “hath,” “whereof,” “thereby,” and “an hundred”—reinforce the period register, while irregular spellings in the supplied text appear partly historical and partly transmissional rather than constituting the core style.

The story uses a brisk, urbane comic-satirical style, shaped by an amused third-person narrator who moves freely between straightforward exposition, caricature, and conversational asides. The narrative voice is confident and companionable, occasionally addressing the reader directly—“If you were familiar…” or “Well, sir, if you believe me”—and thereby creating the atmosphere of an anecdote told aloud. Although the narrator generally maintains an outwardly neutral manner, the selection and phrasing of details continually expose the absurdities of the characters and their institutions.

Its dominant tone is dryly ironic rather than openly condemnatory. The magazine’s devotion to Southern heritage is treated through affectionate but sustained ridicule: professional qualifications are replaced by Confederate ancestry, social connections, and increasingly remote encounters with famous historical figures. Comic escalation is central to this effect. The staff begins with plausible family associations and proceeds to such absurd credentials as an aunt once kissed by Stonewall Jackson and a child soldier carrying “a sword in one hand and a milk-bottle in the other.” Hyperbole, mock solemnity, bathos, and incongruous comparisons repeatedly puncture claims to dignity. Phrases such as “a whole crate of Georgia peaches” and “Even Georgia stock companies sometimes realize that it takes live ones to bury the dead” give the narration a punchy, aphoristic quality.

The diction is built around a sharp contrast between two verbal registers. Colonel Telfair speaks in ceremonious, Latinate, nineteenth-century prose, using balanced clauses, abstract nouns, formal courtesies, and circumlocutions. His language reflects erudition, pride, and social rigidity: he “desires to conform,” “institutes an investigation,” and objects to “jibes and derogatory comments.” Thacker, by contrast, speaks in compressed, energetic American business slang. His idiom is full of commercial terminology, topical references, jokes, wagers, and mixed metaphors: manuscripts are “truck,” weak material is “slush,” and a successful article must “make ’em sit up.” Their dialogue is therefore not merely functional but dramatic; each man’s vocabulary embodies a competing worldview—ancestral prestige and sectional loyalty on one side, circulation, novelty, and market value on the other.

Sentences in the narrative vary between expansive descriptive periods and short comic punches. Longer sentences accumulate names, credentials, historical references, and qualifications until the sheer excess becomes humorous. Short declarative sentences then deliver reversals or punch lines: “It was a whole crate of Georgia peaches.” Lists are especially important. They mimic the magazine’s overcrowded, indiscriminate contents while allowing the comedy to build item by item. Parenthetical qualifications and precise numerical details—ten thousand volumes, a forty-minute speech, an increase of 118 subscribers, eight thousand words—lend mock-documentary authority to patently ridiculous circumstances.

Characterization is economical and external. Colonel Telfair is established through his inherited library, antiquated reading, formal appearance, punctilious manners, and fixation on pedigree. Thacker is introduced through comic physical analogy and a hybrid manner supposedly borrowed from several public figures. Neither character receives extensive psychological analysis; instead, personality emerges from clothing, gesture, speech rhythm, cultural references, and responses to manuscripts. The characters verge on types—the unreconstructed Southern antiquarian and the aggressive Northern promoter—but their consistency and verbal distinctiveness keep them vivid.

The story’s structure resembles an extended setup leading to a final reversal. The opening establishes the magazine’s provincial editorial policy and its reverence for genealogy. Thacker’s arrival creates the central conflict and turns the middle of the story into a prolonged comic review of the magazine’s contents. Suspense then gathers around the mysterious eight-thousand-word contribution. Colonel Telfair’s grand description encourages both Thacker and the reader to imagine a profound philosophical work, while his concern about the author’s sectional identity delays the revelation. The ending resolves this tension through a compact visual punch line: Theodore Roosevelt’s message to Congress is accepted only after he is presented as “A Member of the Well-known Bulloch Family, of Georgia.” The typographical imitation of a title page slows the revelation and gives the final name maximum force.

Much of the humour depends on dramatic irony. Colonel Telfair considers himself a rigorous guardian of literary quality, yet his standards are governed by kinship and regional affiliation. Thacker correctly identifies the magazine’s editorial weaknesses, but his own commercial tastes are also satirized through sensational exposés, celebrity features, recycled serial material, and calculated publicity. The story therefore does not simply oppose foolish tradition to enlightened modernity; it sets two forms of cultural absurdity against each other. One worships pedigree and the past, while the other treats literature as saleable content.

The setting is conveyed through emblematic rather than atmospheric detail. Cedar Heights, the inherited library, impoverished land, red gullies, bank-building offices, and Confederate references establish a South caught between aristocratic memory and modern commercial reality. Historical and literary allusions are abundant, but they are usually deployed for comic contrast rather than elevated resonance. Chaucer, Macaulay, Tasso, Thomas Moore, Gettysburg, Bull Run, and Fort Sumter coexist with advertising, circulation figures, breweries, waterworks, and magazine fillers. This collision of high culture, sectional mythology, and mundane business forms the story’s characteristic texture.

Overall, the style is polished, fast-moving, allusive, and epigrammatic. It favours caricature over realism, dialogue over introspection, and cumulative comic detail over elaborate plotting. Its satire remains light in surface manner but exact in design, exposing nepotism, cultural provincialism, antiquarian pride, and commercial vulgarity through verbal contrast and a carefully prepared surprise ending.

The story is a comic historical pastiche that imitates an early modern adventure memoir while deliberately exposing the artificiality and inaccuracy of that imitation. Its dominant mode is burlesque: archaic diction, famous historical figures, pirate-romance conventions and claims of heroic action are exaggerated until they become absurd. The subtitle’s suggestion that the tale has been “compiled from several eminent sources” reinforces its character as a literary collage, parodying historical romances, swashbuckling narratives, fabricated memoirs and implausibly “true” travellers’ tales.

The narration is first-person, retrospective and strongly voice-driven. John Longbowe presents himself as a “plain, blunt man” committed to brevity, truth and direct action, but his account continually disproves this self-description. He digresses, boasts, rationalises atrocities, quarrels with imagined critics and repeatedly invokes his own honesty while producing contradictions and anachronisms. His uncertainty over dates, casual conflation of historical periods and self-serving revisions make him conspicuously unreliable. Much of the humour arises through dramatic irony: Longbowe remains serenely unaware that his professed courage is cowardice, his mercy is brutality, his piety is greed and his modesty is vanity.

The language is mock-archaic rather than consistently historical. It uses forms such as “seemeth,” “hath,” “wot,” “howbeit,” “forsooth,” “mine,” “thou” and “mayhap,” alongside antiquated or pseudo-antiquated spellings including “publickly,” “complayned,” “replyed,” “idlenesse” and “Warre.” Period-flavoured nouns and insults—“cozening,” “scrivener,” “malapert wights,” “varlets,” “choler” and “behoof”—create a theatrical Elizabethan surface. That surface is intentionally unstable, however, with modern comic phrasing and historically misplaced details breaking through it. The narrator’s pedantic aside about the pronunciation of “the” and “ye” parodies antiquarian scholarship while also establishing his combative, self-important personality.

Sentences are often long, cumulative and heavily subordinated, linked by semicolons, dashes, parenthetical qualifications and strings of participial phrases. This gives the prose the breathless movement of an oral boast or deposition. Formulaic transitions such as “So to my tale,” “This much I shall say,” “But this I do know” and “I set this down” mimic the apparatus of a supposedly authentic chronicle. Repetition is central to the comic rhythm, especially the refrain “being a plain, blunt man,” which appears whenever Longbowe is about to behave hypocritically, boast extravagantly or excuse some cruelty. Capitalisation, typographical emphasis and the oversized chapter synopsis parody the sensational presentation of old adventure books.

The humour depends heavily on incongruity, reversal and understatement. Famous legends are reduced to petty accidents or reassigned to the narrator: Shakespeare appears as a horse-holding “Warwickshire lout,” the Raleigh cloak anecdote is transferred to Southampton, and the origin of the “Jolly Roger” is absurdly claimed as Longbowe’s own. Brutal acts are described in decorous moral language, as when murder is presented as a humane alternative to bloodshed or the slaughter of unarmed islanders as a measure taken “to prevent the shedding of precious blood.” Euphemism and casuistry therefore function as recurring comic techniques. Elevated phrasing is also brought into collision with physical comedy, coarse insults, seasickness, cuffing, tripping and the Queen’s flamboyant kick at an ambassador’s hat.

Characterisation is achieved almost entirely through the narrator’s idiolect and moral blind spots. Longbowe is vain, opportunistic, violent, literal-minded and socially ambitious, yet he imagines himself modest, principled and practical. Secondary characters are briskly caricatured rather than psychologically developed: nobles are swaggering gallants, courtiers are maskers and intriguers, pirates are stock adventurers, and indigenous people are filtered through the narrator’s prejudiced romance vocabulary as “savages.” Historical figures function less as realistic portraits than as recognisable cultural props whose reputations can be comically distorted.

The narrative moves episodically and at deliberately uneven speed. The opening dwells on digressions about dates, spelling, frost fairs and courtly spectacle, while “Chapters I to XX” compress years of piracy, mutiny, treasure hunting, massacre and return into a few pages. This abrupt summary mocks adventure fiction’s dependence on interchangeable exploits and padding. The omitted romantic subplot is itself a joke: Longbowe dismisses shipboard disguise, pursuit and rescue as “trifling,” even though these are precisely the conventions promised by the title’s “Realistic Romance.”

The overall tone is mock-heroic, facetious, combative and deadpan. Atrocities and absurdities are delivered with the same solemn confidence as historical corrections, while the narrator’s indignation supplies more energy than the plot’s dangers. The prose works best through sustained verbal irony rather than isolated punchlines. Its central stylistic principle is the widening gap between the heroic chronicle Longbowe believes he is writing and the portrait of boastful fraudulence, historical confusion and moral hypocrisy that the reader actually receives.

A narrator explains that American humorous storytelling differs from English comic and French witty stories because its effect depends on performance rather than subject matter. The best humorous tales are delivered gravely, wander through irrelevant details, conceal or understate their punch lines, and use carefully timed pauses.

To demonstrate, he contrasts a bluntly told anecdote about a soldier unknowingly carrying a headless wounded man with a master storyteller’s prolonged version, in which a confused old farmer repeatedly digresses and corrects himself. He then illustrates understated incongruity through an Artemus Ward joke about a toothless man who could beat a drum.

Finally, the narrator emphasizes the importance of timing by recounting a ghost story about a miser who steals his dead wife’s golden arm. Her pursuing voice follows him home and demands its return; after building suspense with a precise pause, the storyteller suddenly points at an audience member and shouts that she has it, startling her from her seat.

The story uses a refined modernist realism centred on psychological nuance rather than external action. Its premise is slight—a chance meeting between former lovers in a café—but the encounter becomes a study of memory, loneliness, self-deception and emotional power. The narrative remains close to Vera’s consciousness through free indirect discourse, moving fluidly between spoken conversation, private reaction and involuntary recollection. This limited perspective makes the man’s intentions uncertain: his warmth may be sincere, vain, manipulative or faintly mocking, and the reader shares Vera’s difficulty in interpreting him.

The prose alternates between economical narration and sensuous, figurative passages. Concrete details—the orange peel, muff, veil, cigarette case, untouched cream and paper daffodils—carry emotional significance without being heavily explained. Smell, colour, texture and sound repeatedly trigger imaginative movement: the sting of orange peel, the imagined boatman’s song and the velvet Black Sea transform conversation into vivid inner experience. Similes and metaphors are delicate but psychologically revealing. A face lights “as though he had struck a match in a dark room”; souls embrace and drown together; Vera’s reawakened longing becomes a “strange beast” that stirs, stretches, hungers and later purrs. Objects can also become symbolic extensions of the characters, most notably when Vera imagines herself as the glove in the man’s hands.

Dialogue dominates and is naturalistic in its hesitations, repetitions, interruptions and unfinished phrases. Dashes, ellipses, exclamations and brief questions reproduce the unstable rhythm of an awkward reunion. Repeated courtesies and trivialities mask emotional struggle, while interruptions reveal control: the man redirects Vera’s speech, manages the encounter and repeatedly converts her memories into versions that flatter his self-image. His manner combines lyrical reminiscence with practical fussiness, creating a comic discrepancy between romantic aspiration and petty calculation.

Memory is treated as selective, unstable and competitive. The same occasions survive differently in each character’s mind: he remembers flowers, intimacy and fairy-tale sympathy, whereas Vera recalls wasps, embarrassment, caviare and money. Under the pressure of his eloquence, however, her less flattering recollections temporarily dissolve and his romantic versions begin to feel “truer.” These embedded memories arrive associatively rather than chronologically, so the café conversation repeatedly opens into miniature scenes from the past or imagined scenes from places Vera has never visited. This produces a layered temporal structure in which present perception, recollection and fantasy continually overlap.

The tone balances tenderness, comedy and cruelty. Vera’s romantic susceptibility is rendered sympathetically, but her theatrical inwardness is also exposed. The man is likewise both attractive and absurd: worldly, polished and apparently transformed, yet still given to self-absorption, platitudes and concern over small expenses. The narration often lets a lyrical emotional ascent continue until a mundane detail punctures it. This tonal method creates understated irony rather than overt authorial judgment.

Characterisation emerges through gesture, speech habits and mismatched interpretations rather than direct exposition. Vera is imaginative, lonely and hungry for an unlived life; she projects herself intensely into the landscapes he describes. He is a practiced storyteller who enjoys being listened to and repeatedly casts himself in romantic roles. Their apparent spiritual affinity is gradually revealed as shared narcissism—or at least as his newly adopted explanation of it. His final psychological classification abruptly destroys Vera’s fantasy just as she has convinced herself that he alone understands her.

The pacing is carefully controlled. Long, lush passages of memory and imagined travel slow the scene and enlarge its emotional possibilities, while abrupt actions—buttoning a collar, returning a glove, scraping back a chair, leaving without farewell—restore the hard surface of the present. The climax is internal: Vera passes rapidly from resentment to desire, regret and renewed hope, only to be wounded by the man’s casual diagnosis. Her silent departure replaces conventional confrontation, leaving the emotional rupture unspoken.

The ending is sharply anti-romantic and circular. After Vera’s exit, the man’s astonishment gives way almost immediately to concern about being charged for untouched cream. This final mundane complaint recalls his earlier fixation on the price of caviare and proves that, despite his sophistication and travels, an essential part of him has not changed. The style therefore depends on contrast: lyricism against bathos, remembered intimacy against present estrangement, expansive fantasy against confined café manners, and claims of profound understanding against persistent failures of attention.

The story adopts the style of a nineteenth-century documentary ghost tale: a supernatural narrative presented as a sober, retrospective statement of fact. Its first-person narrator is educated, methodical, socially respectable, and determined to appear rational. He repeatedly stresses his own accuracy, the trustworthiness of witnesses, and his reluctance to infer supernatural causes. Qualifications such as “as well as I recollect,” “I think,” and “so far as it went” imitate the caution of testimony. The omission of names and dates, together with the editorial certification at the end, gives the fiction the air of an authenticated case history.

The tone is formal, measured, and circumstantial rather than sensational. Even disturbing apparitions are described with practical precision: clothing, physical defects, positions in rooms, directions of movement, lighting conditions, and possible means of entry are all carefully recorded. Fear is usually filtered through words such as “curious,” “odd,” “perplexing,” and “inexplicable.” This emotional restraint makes the supernatural material more credible and unsettling, since the narrator reports marvels in much the same manner as he reports household arrangements or architectural details. Occasional dry humour and self-reproach—most notably “stupid dog! you will say”—soften the solemnity without dispelling the mystery.

The diction is elevated, Latinate, and distinctly Victorian. Characteristic terms include “subversive,” “contrariety,” “manifested,” “discernible,” “in limine,” and “referable to natural agencies.” Everyday matters are frequently expressed through elaborate circumlocution, while period usages such as “any thing,” “bed-rooms,” “up stairs,” “inclosure,” and “apprize” reinforce the historical register. Social hierarchy is visible in phrases such as “persons in his rank of life,” though servants’ observations are treated as evidentially important. Small textual irregularities, including the opening repetition “I I” and the misspelling “rythmical,” suggest either an old printed source or lightly preserved transcription.

The syntax is expansive and heavily subordinated. Sentences often proceed through several clauses, parenthetical remarks, qualifications, and retrospective corrections before reaching their main point. Semicolons and dashes regulate long chains of information, while parentheses allow the narrator to insert explanations, credentials, or reservations. This produces a deliberate, legalistic rhythm suited to evidence-giving. Repetition is also important: phrases asserting that witnesses were awake, honest, sensible, exact, or “quite positive” accumulate throughout the account. At moments of direct description, however, the prose becomes more concrete and sequential, tracing an apparition’s movements almost step by step.

Structurally, the narrative is cumulative and episodic. It begins with an ordinary relocation and an emphatically cheerful, modern-looking house, then introduces disturbances one witness and one part of the property at a time. The wife sees a tall figure; servants encounter the one-eyed woman and an impossible shadow; the nursery produces footsteps, knocks, and further apparitions; the children see figures independently; the mare reacts with fear; and physical evidence finally appears in the form of a jawbone and teeth. Each episode follows a recurring pattern: an anomaly is reported, the witness’s reliability is established, a natural explanation is proposed, and investigation fails to confirm it. This repetition gradually overwhelms the narrator’s rational assumptions without ever producing a definitive solution.

Pacing is slow and accretive rather than climactic. Considerable space is devoted to the layout of the house, the placement of beds and doors, the movements of figures, and attempts to test ordinary explanations. These details delay resolution while giving the reader a strong spatial sense of the haunting. Suspense arises less from immediate danger than from recurrence, corroboration, and the failure of physical laws: a shadow appears beside a candle that should make it impossible, doors remain locked, intruders remove nothing, and rhythmic knocking stops when addressed. The late revelation that the apparently modern row was converted from an isolated government store retrospectively darkens the entire setting.

Characterization is functional and testimonial. People are defined chiefly by age, occupation, temperament, and credibility: the housemaid is fearless, the cook religious, the nurse sensible, the child observant, and the butler resistant to superstition. Apparitions, by contrast, are rendered through memorable visual traits—the woman’s blind eye and smallpox scars, the man’s red face and sailor-like trousers, the young woman’s loose dark hair and wounded throat. Their actions are repetitive and absorbed, as though they are reenacting fragments of past events rather than consciously haunting the living.

Dialogue is sparse and mostly indirect. Brief quotations such as “who is there,” “stop that noise,” and “come in” emphasize attempts to treat the phenomena as ordinary human activity. The longer exchange with the elderly visitor serves an expository purpose, supplying the building’s concealed history. The narrator explicitly admits that he is reproducing the “substance and purport” rather than exact words, further sustaining the rhetoric of conscientious testimony.

The atmosphere depends on a contrast between domestic comfort and unexplained intrusion. Fires, airy rooms, modern fittings, children’s play, servants’ routines, and fashionable seaside surroundings are repeatedly set against furtive figures, inaccessible noises, nocturnal visits, and buried remains. Recurring motifs include thresholds, locked doors, hidden access, defective sight, imperfect light, searching or rummaging, and failed pursuit. Seeing is central, but never entirely secure: witnesses observe distinctly while illumination remains partial, figures are usually turned away, and recognition repeatedly occurs just before disappearance.

The ending deliberately preserves ambiguity. The narrator constructs a possible murder narrative linking the three apparitions and the buried bone, but labels it only “a sort of story.” He leaves without investigating further and calls the affair “a very pretty puzzle.” The bracketed editorial coda adds another layer of pseudo-documentary authority while refusing explanation. Overall, the style creates credibility through restraint, procedural detail, corroboration, and unresolved rational inquiry, producing horror not through overt melodrama but through the steady accumulation of evidence that ordinary reasoning cannot comfortably contain.

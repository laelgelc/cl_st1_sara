During the French Revolution’s September Massacres of 1792, Parisian prisons are invaded by armed mobs intent on killing imprisoned aristocrats and clergy. Amid the slaughter, several acts of courage and devotion stand out.

The Abbé Sicard, renowned for educating deaf and mute students, narrowly escapes death when Monnot, a watchmaker who knows him only by reputation, shields him from the attackers and proclaims his good works. Recognized as “the father of the deaf and dumb,” Sicard is spared and eventually released.

Marie de Sombreuil follows her imprisoned father into the courtyard and offers her own life for his. To secure his freedom, she accepts a red drink from the murderers—rumored to be blood, though she later denies it—and leads her father to safety. Inspired by her example, Elizabeth Cazotte similarly places herself between her elderly father and the executioners, persuading the crowd to pardon him.

Others are less fortunate. Madame de Fausse Lendry’s sick uncle is murdered, and the Princess de Lamballe, refusing to swear hatred toward the king and queen, is brutally killed. The reprieves also prove temporary: Jacques Cazotte is rearrested and guillotined despite Elizabeth’s efforts, while Marie de Sombreuil’s father and brother are later executed after a mock trial, leaving her alone with the memory of her courageous attempt to save them.

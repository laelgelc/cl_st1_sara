The story uses restrained third-person narration closely aligned with Maria’s consciousness. Its dominant mode is free indirect discourse: the narrator repeatedly absorbs her vocabulary, judgments, hopes and habits of thought without formally announcing them as interior monologue. Phrases such as “What a nice evening they would have,” “but such was life,” and repeated descriptions of people as “nice” reproduce her mild, conventional outlook. The resulting perspective is intimate but not fully reliable. Readers notice loneliness, disappointment, social condescension and vulnerability that Maria herself softens, misunderstands or refuses to articulate.

The diction is plain, concrete and domestic. Everyday objects—barmbracks, mugs, tram fares, a clasped purse, cakes, boots, saucers and a prayer-book—carry much of the emotional and symbolic weight. Exact prices, routes and timings establish a carefully bounded working-class world in which small expenditures and minor mishaps matter greatly. Repeated adjectives such as “nice,” “little,” “tiny,” “genteel” and “very” imitate Maria’s limited, soothing idiom while also exposing her desire for order, dignity and approval. Occasional colloquial or socially marked expressions preserve the voices of Dublin life without elaborate exposition.

The prose generally proceeds through clear, sequential sentences linked by “and,” “but,” “then” and “so.” This paratactic rhythm gives the narration an apparently innocent, almost childlike simplicity and mirrors Maria’s orderly movement from task to task. Longer sentences gather practical details, remembered associations and quiet qualifications, while short statements create understated emphasis. Parentheses and self-corrections register social tact and suppressed unease. Repetition is especially important: Maria’s formulaic speech, her laughter, the near-meeting of nose and chin, and assurances that people are “nice” become gently comic refrains with increasingly melancholy implications.

Characterization is indirect and economical. Maria is revealed through routines, possessions, bodily gestures, calculations, evasions and the way she interprets other people. Her physical description borders on caricature, but the tone remains compassionate rather than cruel. Secondary characters appear through a few vivid social details—a hat, a moustache, a phrase, an impatient question, a threat or a toast. Dialogue is brief and naturalistic, often exposing tensions that the narration does not explain: class distinctions, religious divisions, alcoholism, family estrangement, romantic embarrassment and the precariousness of Maria’s place within other people’s homes.

The tonal method depends on dramatic irony. Maria thinks of herself as independent, useful and cherished, while the narrative quietly reveals exclusion and emotional deprivation. Her satisfaction in being a peace-maker coexists with her inability to repair the central family quarrel. Her insistence that she does not want marriage conflicts with her “disappointed shyness,” the wedding-cake misunderstanding and the Hallow Eve marriage ritual. Her belief in the tram passenger’s gentlemanliness is shadowed by the disappearance of the expensive plumcake. None of these contradictions is explicitly interpreted; the story trusts the reader to perceive the gap between Maria’s consoling account and her circumstances.

Symbolism remains embedded in ordinary action rather than being announced. Seasonal food and Hallow Eve games create a ritual framework concerned with marriage, religion and death. The unspoken contents of the first saucer—suggested through its soft, wet texture, the reference to the garden and the embarrassed removal—introduce mortality by omission. The prayer-book then supplies a socially acceptable substitute, converting an ominous moment into a harmless prediction of convent life. The missing cake similarly transforms a modest gesture of affection into a private humiliation. These objects work simultaneously as realistic props and as signs of frustrated intimacy, sterility, ageing and disappearance.

The structure is linear and tightly compressed, moving from the controlled order of the laundry through the public uncertainty of shops and trams to the unstable warmth of the family gathering. Expectations are repeatedly raised and quietly disappointed: the planned gift is lost, reconciliation fails, the festive game turns disturbing, and merriment resumes only by suppressing discomfort. Scene transitions are smooth and chronological, but memories of Joe, Alphy and Maria’s youth deepen the present without interrupting its forward motion.

Emotion is handled through understatement, pauses and omissions. Potentially painful events are followed by practical activity, laughter or renewed sociability. The prose rarely names grief directly; instead, shame appears as blushing, disappointment as nearly crying, and isolation as exaggerated gratitude for ordinary kindness. The final song provides the strongest lyrical contrast to the otherwise prosaic language. Its fantasy of wealth, ancestry and enduring love voices desires that Maria cannot state in her own limited idiom. Ending on the quoted verse leaves those desires suspended, making the close poignant without explicit authorial commentary.

Overall, the style combines social realism, close psychological focalization, colloquial simplicity, symbolic detail and compassionate irony. Its characteristic effect comes from saying less than it implies: an ordinary evening is narrated in Maria’s reassuring language while carefully chosen repetitions, missing objects, awkward silences and ritual signs reveal a much darker emotional reality.

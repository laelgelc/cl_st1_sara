The story uses polished, psychologically acute literary realism, shaped by an ironic social intelligence and heightened by symbolic, near-romantic imagery. Its surface world is one of cultivated privilege—country houses, gardens, opera, fashionable marriages—but the narrative continually probes beneath composure and material success to expose repression, frustrated idealism, and emotional hunger. The prevailing tone is poised and analytical, with an undercurrent of sympathy. Caroline’s practicality is initially presented through the envious judgments of her friends, giving the opening a satirical edge; as the narration moves into her history and private consciousness, that apparent hardness is reinterpreted as a defensive discipline forged by poverty, disorder, and the destructive self-indulgence of failed artists.

The narration is third-person omniscient but closely aligned with Caroline. It moves freely between public opinion, biographical exposition, psychological interpretation, and passages approximating her own inward language. The narrator is authoritative and often explicitly evaluative, explaining motives and qualifying judgments rather than leaving everything to implication. Parenthetical qualifications, balanced contrasts, and formulations such as “there was this to be said for her” create the sense of a discerning intelligence weighing evidence. Free indirect discourse becomes especially prominent in the lodge sequence, where Caroline’s thoughts merge with the narrator’s voice through repetitions, questions, self-accusations, and abrupt assertions. This allows her emotional collapse to remain inward and controlled in presentation even when its content becomes feverish.

The prose favors long, syntactically elaborate sentences built from accumulating clauses, parallel constructions, semicolons, and carefully placed dashes. Lists are a major rhythmic device: Caroline’s advantages, her family’s failures, the opera audience, and her history of self-denial are all rendered through cumulative catalogues. These enumerations often generate momentum while sharpening social or psychological distinctions. Repetition is used rhetorically, particularly in phrases involving “the part of one” that creates and worships an idol, or in Caroline’s insistence that she “knew.” Short sentences appear at decisive moments—“It was not enough”—and gain force from contrast with the surrounding periodic prose.

The diction is formal, cultivated, and characteristic of early twentieth-century literary prose. It combines precise social observation with abstract psychological vocabulary: “self-commiseration,” “malleability,” “self-deprivations,” “extenuation,” and “undercurrent of her consciousness.” At times it becomes deliberately elevated or archaic, drawing on mythology, religion, medieval romance, and European high culture. References to Freya, Klingsor, Icarus, Schopenhauer, Heine, Wagner, the eucharist, genies, princesses, and dungeon depths enlarge Caroline’s private crisis into a conflict between disciplined modern life and the ancient seductions of art, fantasy, and desire. These allusions are not ornamental alone; they form the conceptual language through which idealism is shown as both sustaining and dangerous.

Imagery is sensuous, symbolic, and increasingly atmospheric. The opening May landscape is bright, floral, and erotically charged: snowy apple boughs, riotous bloom, blue water, and floating music surround a woman reputedly incapable of surrendering to them. Later, the hot night and delayed storm externalize Caroline’s suppressed passion. Still water, heavy roses, heat-lightning, massing clouds, breathless gardens, and oppressive darkness create an extended pathetic fallacy in which weather and landscape enact emotional pressure. The storm breaks at the moment her restraint collapses, while dawn restores the ordinary world without undoing what she has learned. Light is especially important: moonlight makes the lodge enchanted and unreal; lightning reveals her in stark fragments; dawn exposes the physical aftermath of the dream.

Music functions as both subject and structural metaphor. It represents the idealism Caroline has rejected, the profession through which she once survived, and the medium through which buried feeling returns. D’Esquerre is characterized less as a fully dramatized individual than as an imaginative catalyst or cultural phenomenon. His power lies in suggestion, in what others desire him to embody. The lengthy description of his female audience broadens Caroline’s experience into a collective psychology: women of different classes and conditions use his performance as temporary release from labor, age, loneliness, and social limitation. The language becomes quasi-religious—“temple,” “cult,” “devoutly,” “mystic bread,” “eucharist of sentiment”—presenting art as a secular sacrament that can nourish illusion without changing material reality.

Characterization is largely retrospective and interpretive. Caroline’s past is delivered in an extended biographical movement that explains the origin of her governing traits. Her father and brother are rendered with vivid, unsparing detail: their artistic pretensions are associated with shabby rooms, unpaid bills, cigarettes, herrings, chloral, and self-pity. Material objects repeatedly puncture elevated rhetoric. “Masters and masterpieces” descend into boiled mutton and the turning of a carpet; poetic ideals coexist with soiled gloves and unpaid laundresses. This juxtaposition produces much of the story’s irony and establishes its central tension between imaginative aspiration and practical responsibility.

The structure is carefully symmetrical. It begins with the social assumption that Caroline cannot be sentimental, moves backward to explain why she has trained herself not to be, and then stages a private ordeal in which sentiment overwhelms her. The lodge acts as an enclosed psychological and symbolic space, separate from the ordered household. Within it, ordinary time weakens, memory and fantasy merge, and the self Caroline has imprisoned becomes temporarily visible. The final breakfast scene restores social comedy, brevity, and domestic order. Her decision to demolish the lodge appears to confirm her husband’s view of her, but the reader knows it is an act of containment rather than proof of emotional immunity. The ending is therefore strongly ironic: Caroline’s laughter and practicality conceal the most extravagant inward experience of her life.

Dialogue is sparse, restrained, and socially polished. It is used chiefly to frame the central private episode and to demonstrate the disparity between outward behavior and inward knowledge. Howard Noble’s lightly teasing remarks are affectionate but perceptually limited; he hopes Caroline might be “a little bit foolish” without realizing that she has already passed through a crisis far beyond the harmless sentimentality he imagines. Caroline’s replies are economical and composed, showing how quickly she resumes her public identity.

The pacing shifts from broad summary to concentrated scene. The first portion covers decades of family and personal history in compressed exposition, while the night in the lodge unfolds slowly through physical sensations, memories, musical associations, and recursive thought. This deceleration gives the emotional climax an enclosed, suspended quality. The final return to breakfast is abrupt and controlled, mirroring Caroline’s renewed discipline.

Overall, the style depends on tensions between irony and lyricism, analysis and enchantment, social realism and symbolic romance. Its signature effect comes from placing an intensely imaginative crisis inside the consciousness of a woman who has built her identity on resisting imagination. The prose neither simply condemns practicality nor celebrates surrender. Instead, it treats discipline as necessary but costly, and illusion as dangerous but inseparable from emotional life.

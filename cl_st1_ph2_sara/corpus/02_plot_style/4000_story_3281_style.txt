The story is written as a first-person retrospective confession, with Gabriel Foot recounting the events in a voice that combines criminal bluntness, emotional intensity, and vivid powers of observation. The retrospective frame allows the narrator to foreshadow later revelations—phrases such as “I can remember” and “as I write of it” repeatedly remind the reader that he survived to tell the tale—while the close alignment with his immediate perceptions preserves suspense. His account is candid about his hatred, violence, and criminality, but it is not penitential. This produces an unsettling narrator who is morally compromised yet psychologically legible.

The prose belongs to a period historical-adventure and crime tradition. Its diction is formal and literary in narration, but enriched with archaic or regional vocabulary such as “postillion,” “blunderbuss,” “gossan wig,” “gaol-fever,” and “hearthstone.” By contrast, working-class speech is rendered phonetically and idiomatically—“’Taint well,” “H’out yer goes”—to signal accent, class, and social hostility. The lawyer’s polished, exact speech sharply contrasts with Gabriel’s terse threats and growled replies, making verbal control a marker of intellectual power.

Sentence structure varies according to pace. Long, cumulative sentences dominate moments of recollection and atmosphere, especially the opening description of the courtroom, where semicolon-linked details recreate fog, candlelight, movement, smell, and sound in a dense sensory panorama. Short sentences and isolated lines then punctuate decisive moments, most notably the verdict: “Not guilty.” Dialogue-heavy passages accelerate the action, while measured exposition slows it at crucial points so that evidence can be assembled almost as if the reader were hearing a second trial. Parenthetical remarks and self-corrections give the narration an oral, conversational quality and suggest a storyteller shaping his material for effect.

The descriptive style is strongly sensory and Gothic. Fog becomes a “seething cauldron,” hangs over the town “like a shroud,” and turns candle flames into “bleared eyes.” Cold operates both literally and metaphorically: the autumn wind chills Gabriel’s body, while the lawyer’s knowing glance lays a deeper “cold” upon his heart. Moonlight, shuttered windows, barren moorland, a lonely cottage, disused lime kilns, exposed bones, and the flashing diamond create a macabre landscape poised between crime story and supernatural tale, although the plot’s revelations remain rational. Sound is equally important: murmuring crowds, shuffling feet, hammering knocks, rushing water, whistling, and especially the recurrent flute establish tension and provide ironic accompaniment.

The flute is the story’s most distinctive stylistic motif. Its melancholy popular tunes initially seem eerie and absurdly incongruous, continuing while Gabriel trespasses, eats, and prepares for violence. It also externalises the lawyer’s conscience: he claims to have been “arguing out the case” with it, and its changing tone registers his moral condition. By the ending, its “sprightly and clear” note signals his relief at having resisted temptation. The comic personification of the instrument offsets the Gothic setting and gives the lawyer’s eccentricity a memorable emblem.

Characterisation is achieved largely through gesture, speech, and recurring physical details. Gabriel is impulsive, resentful, and physically violent; he interprets the world through injury, appetite, and hatred. The lawyer is defined by composure: he trims his quill after the acquittal, plays his flute in the face of danger, smokes and drinks while reconstructing the murders, and bargains for his life without visible fear. His ruddy face, frosty humour, precise logic, and ritual destruction of Gabriel’s used glass and pipe make him at once comic, formidable, and morally ambiguous. The story repeatedly reverses apparent power: Gabriel carries the pistol, but the lawyer controls the encounter through knowledge.

Structurally, the narrative progresses through a sequence of increasingly enclosed confrontations. It moves from the public courtroom to the hostile tavern, then to the isolated cottage, the intimate upstairs room, and finally the concealed grave. Each stage strips away another layer of the official verdict. The acquittal resolves the legal case at the outset, but opens the true mystery: what did counsel see in Gabriel, and why does his glance provoke such hatred? The middle section answers this through a duel of dialogue, while the final excavation supplies both physical proof and the missing diamond. The half-hour bargain adds a clear deadline, and the return to the murder scene creates circularity.

Suspense depends less on discovering whether Gabriel is innocent than on gradually measuring how guilty he is and how much the lawyer knows. The lawyer’s reconstruction imitates courtroom rhetoric, dividing the evidence into “the case for the Crown,” “the case for the defence,” and “the facts of the case.” This gives the revelation clarity and theatrical force. Irony is pervasive: the man Gabriel most hates is the man who saved him; the lawyer who secured an acquittal knows the defendant is a double murderer; the recovered treasure buys the lawyer’s life; and Gabriel, while threatening murder, inadvertently prevents the lawyer from becoming a thief.

The dominant tone is darkly comic rather than purely grim. Murder, social ostracism, grave-digging, and threatened execution coexist with dry jokes, legal quibbles, jaunty songs, and the lawyer’s imperturbable manners. His final distinction between being “a criminal” and “merely a lawyer” crystallises the story’s sardonic humour. Overall, the style blends Gothic atmosphere, sensation-story plotting, legal mystery, sharply differentiated dialogue, and ironic moral comedy, producing a compact tale in which wit and psychological tension are as important as the criminal revelation.

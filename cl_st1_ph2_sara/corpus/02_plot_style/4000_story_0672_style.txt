The story uses a formal, late-nineteenth-century literary style marked by controlled omniscient narration, martial precision, and bitter irony. The narrator moves freely between external description, inferred motives, and retrospective commentary, often foreshadowing outcomes with phrases such as “it came to be called so.” Although the narrative remains largely detached, it occasionally intervenes with aphoristic observations about military subordination, curiosity, authority, and human behavior. This composure makes the violence more disturbing: grotesque events are reported in an exact, almost administrative voice rather than through overt emotional lament.

The diction is elevated and Latinate, combining military terminology—“caisson,” “unlimbered,” “rear-guard,” “acclivity,” “bivouac”—with vivid figurative language. Guns hide like cuttlefish, troops resemble a “torn and draggled cloud of blue smoke,” and the cannonade becomes an atmosphere of “living iron whose thoughts were lightnings and whose deeds were death.” Such metaphors animate weapons and landscapes while men are repeatedly reduced to bodies, functions, wreckage, or replaceable parts. The resulting personification and dehumanization reinforce the story’s vision of warfare as a mechanized, infernal force.

Sentence structure is varied but often elaborate. Long periodic sentences accumulate spatial detail, subordinate clauses, qualifications, and parenthetical interruptions before arriving at their main effect. Dashes are especially prominent, marking corrections, withheld information, ironic afterthoughts, and abrupt shifts in perception. Short sentences and fragments then punctuate moments of recognition: “He was serious, then”; “The spectacle which they encountered there was appalling!” This alternation between syntactic density and sudden compression controls suspense and gives revelations a sharp, terminal force.

Dialogue is restrained, hierarchical, and loaded with implication. Officers speak in formal, courteous formulas even when issuing suicidal orders or expressing contempt. Irony enters through understatement, euphemism, and discrepancies between literal language and actual intention. The general’s praise of “your brave Coulter” is a taunt; congratulations are sent into a slaughterhouse; and the colonel’s observation that the cellar is “not bomb-proof” reduces a family’s destruction to a technical judgment. Characters seldom confess emotion directly. Their pauses, pallor, broken voices, evasions, and rigid adherence to protocol convey what decorum suppresses.

The pacing begins with a carefully mapped tactical problem, then accelerates into the gun duel through kinetic verbs, sound effects, and cumulative imagery. The battle itself is partly withheld through summary, allowing anticipation to build before the narrative enters the notch and presents its concentrated horrors. There the prose becomes intensely visual and rhythmic, cataloguing stripped bodies, bloodied tools, ruined guns, and endlessly replaced men. Repetition and parallel construction create the impression of an automatic cycle: when the gun is sponged, it is loaded; when loaded, it is aimed and fired; when one man falls, another takes his place.

Violence is rendered through grotesque, sensory detail but is rarely sentimentalized. Smoke, noise, blood, splinters, exposed flesh, and mutilated bodies create a hellish spectacle. The most memorable images fuse human beings with military machinery, particularly the “bleeding” gun whose sponge is dipped in a comrade’s blood. Yet the narrator also introduces bleakly comic or mundane details—the staff officer thinking of supper, the orderly thinking of cellar casks—which expose the persistence of appetite and routine beside catastrophe.

Characterization is indirect and economical. Coulter is first established through appearance, bearing, and anomalous hesitation; the colonel through pride, obedience, dawning moral anger, and eventual compassion; the general through irony, immobility, and abuse of authority. Information about Coulter’s past is delayed and filtered through rumor, so his reluctance initially appears cowardly before being reinterpreted. This dependence on partial knowledge is central to the story’s method: characters judge one another from surfaces while the decisive truth remains concealed.

The structure is tightly organized around delayed revelation and retrospective reversal. An apparently tactical episode gradually acquires the shape of private vengeance, and Coulter’s inexplicable fixation on the plantation gun becomes legible only at the end. The final scene mirrors and transforms earlier imagery: the apparently dead man rises, his blackened face echoes the demonic gunners, and the house under bombardment is revealed as his own home. The last plain declaration—“I am Captain Coulter”—functions as both identification and devastating reinterpretation of everything preceding it.

Overall, the style combines realism, gothic horror, military satire, and tragic irony. Its emotional power comes from contrast: ceremonious speech against savagery, tactical abstraction against bodily ruin, public courage against private agony, and narrative composure against an appalling final truth. The story’s characteristic effect is not cathartic heroism but a cold recognition of how pride, hierarchy, rumor, and personal malice can turn military duty into senseless sacrifice.

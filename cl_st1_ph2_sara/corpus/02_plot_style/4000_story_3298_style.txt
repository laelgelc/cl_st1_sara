The story uses a poised, ironic, late-Victorian realist style enriched by biblical allegory, symbolic landscape, and touches of fable. Its central conceit reverses the story of Eden: the released prisoners are named Adam and Eve, the outside world becomes an overwhelming “paradise,” and prison offers the enclosure and security from which freedom has expelled them. This symbolism is clearly signalled but remains integrated into concrete social observation rather than turning the narrative into pure allegory.

The narrator is omniscient, urbane, and gently intrusive. Phrases such as “We may know them as Adam and Eve” establish a storyteller who interprets events while maintaining an emotional distance from the characters. The narrative attitude combines compassion with dry amusement. Adam and Eve are never mocked for their fear; instead, the irony exposes the inadequacy of conventional ideas about liberty, benevolence, and progress. Understatement is especially important: extraordinary suffering is disclosed in flat, casual remarks, while the conclusion presents a return to prison as though it were a homecoming.

The prose alternates between economical action and richly visual description. Sentences often have balanced clauses, semicolons, participial phrases, and carefully controlled accumulations. Descriptive passages expand rhythmically through lists, as in the changing colours of shop lights or the sequence of constellations, whereas moments of shock are delivered in abrupt statements and terse dialogue. This contrast reaches its comic and emotional climax in the astronomer’s torrents of astronomical figures followed by Adam’s blunt exclamation, “Then damn Groombridge Eighteen-thirty!”

The diction mixes elevated literary phrasing with precise everyday detail and colloquial speech. The narrator uses terms such as “opaque,” “heterogeneous,” “descant,” and “measureless,” while the characters speak in clipped, ungrammatical vernacular: “‘Cumulated jobs,” “less’n,” and “what I’ve took.” Their dialogue is sparse and indirect, revealing history and temperament without explanatory monologues. Adam’s laconic defensiveness and Eve’s tentative questions suggest people accustomed to institutional restraint. Repetition—especially Eve following “at his heels”—quietly establishes dependence before the final reversal, when Adam follows her.

Setting functions psychologically as well as physically. Fog, darkness, railings, crowds, and vast open spaces embody the pair’s disorientation. The fog is repeatedly personified or rendered through textile and maritime images: it is a “tent-cloth,” has a “frayed edge,” and surrounds them with a sound like the sea. The city is dense, noisy, greasy, and anonymous; the park becomes a “limitless plain”; the clear night sky is more terrifying still because it exposes cosmic immensity. Successive environments therefore enlarge from street to park, road, and universe, making freedom feel progressively less habitable.

The imagery repeatedly opposes confinement and boundlessness. Bars initially signify imprisonment, but later promise safety; the park railings make the fugitives look like “caged beasts,” although they are technically inside a garden; and the heavens become the ultimate open prison. Animal comparisons—the bird’s eyelid, the hare-like eyes, the puppy in the crowd, the caged beasts—give the characters an instinctive vulnerability. Their white hands, short hair, unworn boots, and uncertain movements provide unobtrusive physical evidence of recent imprisonment.

The structure is episodic and westward-moving. A coin toss begins the journey almost arbitrarily, after which each episode tests a larger form of freedom: the crowded city, enclosed nature, the open road, private charity, and finally cosmic knowledge. Asterism breaks give the story the cadence of a compact moral tale. Motifs recur with variation: following, gates, food, fear, and the word “big.” The repeated complaint that the world is “too big” supplies the emotional and philosophical spine.

The tone shifts from whimsical observation through pathos and claustrophobic unease to darkly comic existential terror. Humour comes from incongruity rather than jokes: ex-prisoners cast as humanity’s first couple, a public park mistaken for a vast gaol, and astronomical sublimity answered with profanity. The closing biblical imagery—paradise, a blue-clad warder, and the absent flaming sword—completes the inversion with restrained poignancy. Overall, the style is lucid, pictorial, socially observant, symbolically patterned, and humane, using controlled irony to suggest that institutional life can so deform perception that freedom itself becomes unbearable.

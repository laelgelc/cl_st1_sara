The story uses a polished, economical realist style shaped by comic observation, affectionate satire, and an eventual trick ending. Its third-person narrator is broadly omniscient, moving between external description, social commentary, and selective access to the characters’ attitudes. The narrative voice remains poised and urbane: it smiles at Major Talbot’s vanity, impracticality, and devotion to the antebellum South, yet also treats his courtesy, pride, and emotional vulnerability with sympathy. This balance prevents him from becoming merely a caricature and supports the story’s mixture of humour and pathos.

Description is concrete, selective, and strongly characterising. The boarding house’s pillars, trees, blossoms, and box-bordered walks immediately evoke the Southern world that attracts the Talbots, while the major’s extraordinary clothing makes his historical displacement visible. Recurring details—the “Father Hubbard” coat, slipping string tie, elaborate bow, manuscript, and ritual preparation of mint juleps—function as comic motifs. They first establish the major’s personality, then become material for Hargraves’s theatrical impersonation, and finally help make the deception involving Uncle Mose plausible. Objects and gestures therefore carry structural as well as decorative weight.

The diction alternates between the narrator’s lucid literary prose and sharply differentiated character voices. Major Talbot speaks in ceremonious, euphemistic, grandiloquent sentences, favouring phrases such as “place of occupation,” “financial adjustment,” and “quitting the apartment.” His language embodies an antiquated code of gentility and often creates humour by treating mundane problems as matters of dignity and honour. Miss Lydia’s speech is plainer, shorter, and practical, revealing her role as the family’s financial realist. Hargraves uses brisk, colloquial theatrical language—“I put it all over ’em,” “I had my inning”—but can also argue intelligently and expose the major’s double standard. The speech of Uncle Mose is rendered through heavy eye-dialect and racial stereotypes typical of the story’s historical period. This representation is central to the plot’s disguise and to the text’s sentimental image of plantation loyalty, but it also reflects the paternalistic assumptions and racial prejudices of its era.

Sentence structure is generally clear and moderately elaborate. Descriptive passages use balanced clauses, accumulative lists, and rhythmic parallelism, especially when cataloguing the remembered pleasures of plantation life or detailing the making of a julep. Short sentences and compact exchanges appear at moments of financial pressure, offence, or revelation. Parenthetical remarks and narrator’s asides add conversational wit, while occasional mock-elevated phrasing magnifies small actions for comic effect. The prose often shifts smoothly from summary to scene: months of friendship may be compressed into a few paragraphs, whereas a theatrical performance, confrontation, or apparent rescue is presented through detailed action and dialogue.

The humour arises chiefly from incongruity, understatement, repetition, and dramatic irony. Major Talbot’s aristocratic self-conception is repeatedly set against unpaid bills, an unsaleable manuscript, and the practical world of boarding houses and popular entertainment. His indignation at being stereotyped is juxtaposed with his own sweeping stereotype of Northerners. The narrator rarely condemns him directly; instead, contradictions emerge through action and dialogue. Miss Lydia’s dry remarks provide corrective wit, as when she insists they remain in the theatre rather than “advertise the copy by exhibiting the original coat.” Hargraves’s final impersonation creates retrospective irony, revealing that the apparently providential return of a loyal former slave was another performance.

The plot is tightly constructed around observation, imitation, pride, and money. The opening establishes the major as a living relic; the middle shows Hargraves gathering the speech, stories, gestures, and mannerisms required for his role; the theatrical sequence turns those details into public caricature; and the financial crisis gives Hargraves a motive to use his talent a second time. The false resolution arrives when Uncle Mose repays the supposed debt, satisfying the major’s pride because the money can be understood as both legitimate payment and proof of old-regime loyalty. The postscript—“How did I play Uncle Mose?”—delivers the characteristic reversal, forcing the reader to reinterpret the preceding scene. Miss Lydia’s immediate concealment of the truth supplies a quiet final comic beat and confirms her protective pragmatism.

The pacing is deliberate but efficient. The opening third builds character and social setting; the theatre episode provides the major dramatic set piece; the confrontation articulates the story’s ethical conflict; and the final sections move rapidly through crisis, rescue, and revelation. Foreshadowing is conspicuous in retrospect: Hargraves is introduced as an expert dialect comedian, possesses a strong memory, questions the major closely, studies his mannerisms, and knows about the Talbots’ financial trouble. These details make both impersonations feel prepared rather than arbitrary.

Tonally, the story combines nostalgia, satire, sentiment, and irony. The remembered South is rendered with sensuous charm and ceremonial splendour, but the narrative also exposes the major’s nostalgia as selective, economically useless, and entangled with slavery and class prejudice. Hargraves represents modernity, commerce, popular theatre, and adaptive intelligence; the major represents inherited identity, honour, and resistance to change; Miss Lydia mediates between them through common sense and discreet compassion. The ending does not fully resolve their moral conflict. Hargraves’s deception is generous but manipulative, while the major’s principles are dignified but sustained by illusion. The resulting style is humane yet sharply ironic: characters are allowed their follies, and practical kindness succeeds by accommodating—rather than destroying—the stories they tell about themselves.

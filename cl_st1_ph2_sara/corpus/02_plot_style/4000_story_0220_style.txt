The story adopts the manner of a nineteenth-century comic realist tale, combining domestic farce, social observation, and affectionate caricature. Its setting is established with documentary precision—an exact hour, date, street, and seaport town—but this apparent historical seriousness is immediately undercut by playful commentary. The plot itself is compact and theatrical: a servant secretly marries, returns home burdened by guilt, and is exposed when her drunken young husband arrives at the front door demanding his wife. Suspense rests not on whether a crime has occurred, but on when an awkward social secret will be discovered.

The narrator is conspicuously present, urbane, intrusive, and amused. Rather than relating events neutrally, the voice addresses or anticipates the reader, as in observations about what “the least imaginative reader” may conjecture and the decision “not to prolong a mystery.” First-person plural formulations such as “we refer,” “we repeat,” and “we hold” create the air of a witty raconteur speaking for a civilized consensus. The narrator freely pauses the action to pass judgment, provide background, qualify a metaphor, or deliver an aphorism. This produces a leisurely storytelling rhythm and establishes a comic distance from the characters without making them wholly unsympathetic.

The prevailing tone is mock-serious and gently satirical. Ordinary events are described in elevated, legalistic, classical, or romantic language: Margaret’s secret marriage resembles an “indictable offense”; her kitchen becomes the “penetralia”; her departure with Larry echoes the expulsion of Adam and Eve; and the final apostrophe to Hymen contrasts aristocratic love’s costly incense with the “pitiful penny-dip” illuminating a back-street romance. Such grand comparisons deliberately exceed the scale of the action. The comedy arises from this discrepancy between rhetorical magnificence and mundane reality, as well as from the narrator’s habit of treating absurd conduct with solemn analytical care.

The diction mixes formal literary vocabulary with vivid colloquial speech. Narrative passages use words such as “surreptitiously,” “untenable,” “chronometer,” “retrospectively,” and “effrontery,” along with biblical, classical, fairy-tale, and art-historical allusions. These are offset by homely concrete details—broiled mackerel, buttered toast, a cream pitcher, a door scraper—and by the phonetic rendering of Irish speech. Larry and Donnehugh use forms such as “me wife,” “betther,” “howld,” “dhrunk,” “owld counthry,” and “begorra.” The dialect sharply differentiates social voice and supplies much of the scene’s energy, though it also reflects the period’s tendency to turn immigrant speech into comic spectacle. Margaret’s limited literacy and servant status are likewise presented through paternalistic period assumptions.

Sentences are often long, balanced, and elaborately subordinated, especially in description and authorial commentary. Parentheses, dashes, semicolons, appositions, and qualifying clauses allow the narrator to digress while preserving syntactic control. Comic timing frequently depends on delayed disclosure or a sentence-ending reversal. Short statements then provide emphasis: “But the coffee was bad.” Dialogue grows increasingly rapid during the doorstep confrontation, with brief questions, repetitions, and misunderstandings accelerating the pace after the more expansive opening narration.

Description is highly visual and hyperbolic. Margaret’s outfit is rendered through saturated color—light green, blazing red, yellow, azure, orange, and magenta—so that her attempt at secrecy conflicts comically with her dazzling appearance. The prose repeatedly animates objects and surroundings: sunshine “fingered” brass knockers and “drained” dew; a clock “lisped”; the knocker extends a “grim appeal.” These personifications lend the town a whimsical storybook atmosphere. Portraiture proceeds by accumulation of selected physical details, often capped by an interpretive joke, as when Larry’s curls, beard, mouth, eyes, uniform, and drunkenness are assembled into a mock “pre-Raphaelite portrait.”

Characterization is external, economical, and caricatural, but softened by humane touches. Margaret is defined by her flamboyant dress, guilty stealth, illiteracy, loyalty, and emotional dependence. Mrs. Bilkins appears formidable yet charitable, waging “guerrilla warfare” against peddlers while remaining compassionate toward sailors because of an old family loss. Larry is ridiculous, intoxicated, and immature, but also good-natured rather than threatening. Minor objects and habits—the dissipated cat, the umbrella grasped as a weapon, tears shed into the cream—function as comic extensions of character.

The story’s structure moves from concealment to revelation. The opening creates a visual mystery around Margaret’s dawn excursion, which the narrator soon resolves for the reader while keeping it hidden from the Bilkinses. The breakfast scene sustains dramatic irony, and the triple knock initiates the central farcical encounter. Repetition of Larry’s demand, “Me wife,” drives the dialogue toward the climactic naming of Margaret. The ending is swift and theatrically symbolic: husband and wife are expelled, embark on a parodic “wedding journey,” and disappear into a shabby riverside dwelling. The final flourish enlarges their comic misfortune into a deliberately tawdry version of romance.

Overall, the style is polished, digressive, allusive, and theatrically comic. Its signature effects come from an intrusive narrator, mock-heroic elevation, social and verbal contrast, ornate sentence architecture, vivid personification, dialect-heavy dialogue, and the affectionate exposure of human vanity and folly. Beneath the satire lies a mild sentimental current: the characters are laughable, but their weaknesses are treated as ordinary and recognizably human rather than cruelly condemned.
